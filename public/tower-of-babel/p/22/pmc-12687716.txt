Emerging Advances in Nanomedicine for Targeted Cancer Photoimmunotherapy and Photo‐Immunometabolic Therapy
Authors: Alexander Chota, Paromita Sarbadhikary, Heidi Abrahamse, Blassan P. George
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTImmunotherapy has advanced quickly as a promising new approach for cancer treatment, but its therapeutic efficacy and clinical translation have lagged expectations, because of its inability to directly target tumors, low response rates, and nonspecific immune reactions. Anticancer therapies like photodynamic therapy (PDT) show promise over traditional modalities, as they can induce a specific type of tumor cell death known as immunogenic cell death (ICD) to boost the immune response against solid tumors. However, successful therapeutic outcomes cannot be achieved with PDT‐driven ICD‐based single‐modal cancer immunotherapy alone, which necessitates the combination of ICD‐based PDT with other immunotherapies. Therefore, to achieve effective PDT‐driven immunotherapy, it is imperative to enhance the in vivo delivery efficiency of photosensitizers and immuno agents, along with reducing the off‐target side effects, to develop comprehensive therapeutic strategies like photo‐immunotherapy and photo‐immunometabolic therapy. Herein, this review discusses the recent advances in the field at the intersection of PDT, immunoconjugates, nanotechnology, and ICD, majorly focusing on their molecular design and nanoengineering, and their induced synergistic effects. Finally, the opportunities and current challenges encountered in this field, along with the significant advancements made in recent years, have been highlighted.This article is categorized under: Therapeutic Approaches and Drug Discovery > Nanomedicine for Oncologic DiseaseTherapeutic Approaches and Drug Discovery > Emerging TechnologiesToxicology and Regulatory Issues in Nanomedicine > Toxicology of Nanomaterials

Introduction

With an estimate of ~20 million new cases and 9.7 million deaths attributed to cancer in 2022 itself, the WHO declares cancer as the second leading cause of mortality worldwide. The rapidly increasing incidence of cancer worldwide is indicated by the reports of one in five people getting cancer at some point in their lives, showing a projected 77% increase in new cases in 2050 (WHO 2024). Over the past few decades, research has completely changed our understanding of the cellular and molecular alterations responsible for tumor development and growth; however, radiation, surgery, and chemotherapy remain the primary cancer treatment options. Typically, undoubtedly, these modalities have been shown to reduce tumor burden and induce remission; however, they typically do not offer a reliable means of attaining long‐term remission. Moreover, their nonspecific targeting and consequent suppression of the host immune system lead to compromised innate and adaptive immunity in patients treated with radiation therapy and chemotherapy, thus worsening the progression of cancer (Debela et al. 2021; Monaco et al. 2022; Schirrmacher 2019).

In recent decades, significant progress has been made in cancer treatment, with immunotherapy revolutionizing the field. By stimulating the body's natural immune system, immunotherapy helps prevent tumor metastasis and recurrence while inhibiting primary tumor growth. Compared to traditional treatments like radiation and chemotherapy, it enhances progression‐free survival in cancer patients with fewer side effects (Tan et al. 2020; Waldman et al. 2020). The most common immunotherapy techniques are adoptive cellular immunotherapy (ACI), tumor vaccination, immune checkpoint‐blockade (ICB) therapy, and chimeric antigen receptor T cells (CAR‐T) therapy (Liu, Li, et al. 2023; Wang, Wu, and Sun 2022). Undoubtedly, immunotherapy can potentially treat cancer by establishing long‐lasting antitumor immunity, but as with any other treatment modality, it also carries its limitations. For instance, it poses a significant risk to the host system in the form of autoimmunity and nonspecific inflammation. Furthermore, only a small percentage of patients have benefited from the current immunotherapy techniques (Riley et al. 2019; Taefehshokr et al. 2022; Tan et al. 2020). Consequently, to unleash the full potential of immunotherapy in long‐term cancer management strategies involving improved delivery technologies, increased immunogenicity, and boosted antitumor efficacy needs to be explored.

The effectiveness of immunotherapy has been shown to be improved by combining it with other treatment modalities, including radiation therapy, chemotherapy, and photodynamic therapy (PDT), due to the targeting of multiple survival pathways or blocking cell death escape mechanisms and having non‐overlapping toxicities. However, the introduction of the concept of immunogenic cell death (ICD) has bridged the gap between conventional and novel treatment modalities by showing the role of host immunity in cancer treatment. Among all others, PDT has emerged as a revolutionary approach for ICD‐based synergistic therapeutics, due to its potential to trigger an antitumor immune response (Lu et al. 2023; Warszyńska et al. 2023; Zhang, Wang, et al. 2022). However, studies have shown that PDT‐driven ICD‐based single‐modal cancer immunotherapy is insufficient to achieve a good therapeutic result. Consequently, to achieve effective PDT‐driven immunotherapy, it is imperative to enhance the in vivo delivery efficiency of photosensitizers (PSs) and develop combined therapeutic strategies (Zhang, Wang, et al. 2022).

The second strategy involves the development of innovative methods for immunotherapy delivery to allow better targeting and accumulation within tumor tissues and lessen side effects that occur off‐target. Delivery platforms are superior to therapeutic agents alone, whereby they protect the therapeutic cargo before targeted delivery, as well as being sensitive to stimuli like pH, light, or ultrasound; they can allow for spatiotemporal control over therapeutics. Thus, they allow safe and effective delivery of cancer immunotherapy while lowering toxicities (Riley et al. 2019). In this regard, the use of nanoparticle‐based drug delivery systems as an effective vehicle for the delivery of anticancer medications has been extensively studied due to their significant benefits in improving tumor targeting and accumulation, reducing systemic toxicity, and enhancing therapeutic outcomes (Elumalai et al. 2024; Goldberg 2019; Hak et al. 2021; Marchal et al. 2015). However, because of their unpredictable drug release characteristics and nonspecific biodistribution, conventional nanomedicines frequently cause systemic side effects. Therefore, advanced, smart, and activable nanomedicines have gained interest in recent years as they can be specifically activated in a controlled manner at desired locations to reprogram tumor metabolic profiles and reactivate the immune system against tumors. Interestingly, light‐activated PSs and photothermal agents themselves allow for photoactivation of immunotherapeutic agents, as well as elicit photothermal therapy (PTT) and PDT‐induced enhanced antitumor immunity; thus, they offer an advantage for combinational photo‐immunotherapy (PIT) of cancer (Yu et al. 2021). In view of the growing interest in this area, in this review, we have summarized the most recent and distinct developments in the field of PDT‐induced immunotherapy with a special focus on combining PDT with immune agents as simple conjugates to photoactivable nanotherapeutics.

In this review, we first address the function of PDT for the induction of ICD. This is followed by a detailed discussion on the latest design tenets, benefits, and difficulties with each strategy, which can pave the way toward the rationale for further development of ICD‐based photo‐nanotherapeutics for diagnostics, precision medicine, and clinical applications. Furthermore, this perspective concludes with a discussion of the challenges of the present and the outlook for the future.

Photodynamic Therapy of Cancer

PDT is a clinically approved, less invasive, and highly effective form of cancer treatment that employs the use of light energy and photosensitizing drugs, also known as photosensitizers (PS), to destroy tumor cells (Agostinis et al. 2011). This form of treatment has great potential as an alternative therapy for various types of solid tumors. It has also been reported to produce excellent cosmetic outcomes (Farias et al. 2012). The effectiveness of this therapy is highly dependent on the localization of PS within the targeted tumor cells, light source, and the interactions of the excited PS with molecular oxygen (O2; George et al. 2022). Within the tumor cells, the interactions of the excited state of PS with O2 lead to the generation of cytotoxic reactive oxygen species (ROS). Additionally, high levels of ROS in any living cell have been reported to induce oxidative damage on various cellular organelles (e.g., mitochondria) and nucleic acids such as deoxyribonucleic acid (Figure 1; Murphy 2009). Oxidative damage to the above‐highlighted cellular organelles and macromolecules may lead to cell cycle arrest and eventually cell death (Lange et al. 2019). This review aims to present the recent advances and benefits of photo‐immunoconjugates in targeted PDT.

Schematic representation of mitochondrial and caspase cascade‐mediated tumor cell death in cancer photodynamic therapy. After the administration of the photosensitizer, the photosensitizer will preferentially co‐localize within various organelles of the cell. When irradiated at a specified wavelength, the administered photosensitizer will move from its ground state to the excited state. The interactions of the excited state of the photosensitizer with molecular oxygen via the Type II pathway will lead to the generation of cytotoxic singlet oxygen (1O2) molecules. Increased levels of 1O2 during PDT may lead to the activation of caspase‐mediated cancer cell death. Additionally, increased levels of 1O2 may alternatively lead to the expression of transformation‐related protein 53 (p53), which in turn prevents the formation of tumor cells by blocking the activities of anti‐apoptotic proteins (Bcl‐w, Bcl‐2, Bfl‐1, and Mcl‐1), thus promoting the initiation of apoptotic activities.

Mechanism of PDT‐Mediated Cytotoxicity

PDT is a novel treatment modality that is widely employed in the treatment of cancer (Mansoori et al. 2019). Its photodynamic action is dependent on the incorporation and interactions of three fundamental components: a PS, molecular O2, and a light source (Sai et al. 2021). The photochemistry of PDT involves two main reactions, Type I and Type II photochemical reactions. In the Type I reaction, the triplet state of the PS (3PS*) can interact with nearby biomolecules and lead to the generation of intermediate free radicals such as superoxide anions (O2˙−), hydroxyl radical (OH˙), and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). In this type of photochemical reaction, electrons (e −) are transferred between 3PS* and nearby biomolecules. In addition, the generated free radicals may further interact with molecular O2 and lead to ROS generation (Ortiz‐Rodríguez and Crespo‐Hernández 2020; Chota et al. 2021). In the Type II photochemical reaction, 3PS* directly interacts with molecular O2 and leads to the generation of cytotoxic singlet oxygen (1O2). This type of photochemical reaction involves the transfer of energy from 3PS* to molecular O2 (Kubrak et al. 2022). The mechanism of action behind PDT can be well explained by using the Jablonski diagram illustrated in Figure 2.

Illustration of the Jablonski diagram describing the underlying photochemistry and photophysics of photodynamic therapy. Upon laser light illumination, the administered photosensitizer gets excited and moves from its initial ground state to a singlet state; it is at this state that the photosensitizer is in its unstable state. However, during this state, the excited photosensitizer undergoes a series of photochemical reactions which eventually lead to the production of cytotoxic reactive oxygen species (ROS) via the Type I and Type II photochemical reactions.

Photosensitizing Agents

Photosensitizing agents are drugs that are pharmacologically inactive but get activated upon exposure to sunlight or ultraviolet (UV) radiation (Baskaran et al. 2018). These drugs can be administered topically or systemically depending on the disease being treated. Post‐PDT, patients must be advised to avoid exposure of PS‐treated sites to direct sunlight. Exposure of treated sites to direct sunlight has been reported to induce undesired phototoxic side effects (di Stasio et al. 2018). Photosensitizers used in the treatment of various diseases are divided into different generations and are typically classified based on the time of development and the photochemical characteristics they exhibit (van Straten et al. 2017). PSs can be categorized into three generations (first, second, and third generation; Escudero et al. 2021). Despite significant progress over the last decade, the development of next‐generation PSs is still in the stage of development (Mfouo‐Tynga et al. 2021). Hematoporphyrin and its derivatives (HpD) were the first group of PSs to be classified as first‐generation PSs (Figure 3; Plekhova et al. 2022). Porfimer Sodium (Photofrin) is one example of a clinically approved PS from this class; it was widely used in the treatment of skin, lung, brain, bladder, esophageal, and gastric carcinomas. However, clinical investigations have a number of limitations, such as composition complexity, low chemical purity, and poor light absorption (i.e., triggered by wavelengths less than 640 nm; Niculescu and Grumezescu 2021). First‐generation PSs were improved to overcome these restrictions and broaden their light absorption range. Second‐generation PSs were created as a result of chemical alterations to first‐generation PSs (Gulati et al. 2022).

Chemical structures of first‐ and second‐generation photosensitizers (PSs). First‐generation PSs include hematoporphyrin and porphyrins, while second‐generation PSs comprise chlorins, bacteriochlorins, benzoporphyrins, and phthalocyanines.

Second‐generation PSs are a group of PSs that consist of purely synthesized aromatic macrocycle compounds (e.g., phthalocyanines; Figure 3; Niculescu and Grumezescu 2021). These PSs were developed with the aim of overcoming limitations exhibited by second‐generation PSs as well as improving their chemical and therapeutic properties (Plekhova et al. 2022). These compounds get excited after absorbing the near infrared region wavelengths (650–800 nm). The second‐generation PSs exhibit a higher chemical purity and a higher quantum yield of singlet oxygen during PDT compared to first‐generation PSs (Kwiatkowski et al. 2018). However, one of the disadvantages of second‐generation PSs in cancer therapy is their low water solubility (Makhseed et al. 2013). Furthermore, their low water solubility causes PS aggregation, increased self‐quenching, and the search for new drug delivery methods (Hu et al. 2018; Kwiatkowski et al. 2018).

Third‐generation PSs are a type of PS designed to address specific therapeutic demands such as tumor specificity, localization, and toxicity. These PSs are the result of chemical modifications, for example, antibody conjugation or carrier molecule packaging of certain second‐generation PSs for enhanced tumor cell targeting (O'Connor et al. 2009). Liposomes, micelles, polymers, and fullerenes are the most common drug delivery vesicles employed in the delivery of hydrophobic PSs into a tumor cell (Avci et al. 2014). Since tumor cells possess surface antigens that are different from those on the surface of normal cells, active and passive targeting play an essential role in tumor cell targeting, for example, the use of monoclonal antibodies (mABs) to passively target specific surface antigens expressed on tumor cells (Mfouo‐Tynga et al. 2021). Although third‐generation PSs have increased tumor selectivity, localization, and toxicity, research into their application in theranostics and PDT is ongoing (Gulati et al. 2022).

Tumor Cell Targeting in PDT

Tumor cells can be passively or actively targeted in PDT. The discovery of these two strategies has improved the efficacy of PDT and changed the landscape of cancer therapy (Hodgkinson et al. 2017). The application of these strategies in cancer therapy has demonstrated several advantages over conventional treatment modalities. Some of the advantages of these strategies include enhanced specificity, reduction of dose‐dependence, and dose‐associated adverse effects (Attia et al. 2019). In addition, these strategies are often used when a therapy targets either the intrinsic or extrinsic apoptotic pathways, thus making them ideal drug delivery systems for effective PDT (Rosenblum et al. 2018).

Passive Targeting

Passive therapies, also known as adoptive immunotherapy, are immunology‐based targeted therapy. This therapeutic approach can either be tumor‐specific or non‐specific and involves the administration of anti‐tumor mABs. The anti‐tumor mABs can be classified into two types: naked antibodies (nABs) or conjugated antibodies (cABs). nABs are basically mABs that are not conjugated to an active factor but can inhibit tumor cell growth and induce apoptosis via their interactions with tumor surface markers. In addition, their interactions with tumor surface markers can result in endocytosis and the activation of the complement system. Active therapies, as opposed to passive therapies, entail the infusion of active components (e.g., photosensitizing agent or chemo‐toxin) conjugated to an anti‐tumor antibody that is utilized as a delivery mechanism for specific tumor cells (Figure 4).

Passive and active targeting of tumor cells in cancer therapy and PDT using PS‐immunoconjugates with the help of immune cells such as T cells. After the intravenous administration of PS‐immunoconjugates, the administered PS‐immunoconjugates may passively attach to the receptors found on the surface of tumor cells. Alternatively, the administered PS‐immunoconjugate may be internalized by cells via a process known as endocytosis. Post PDT, the generation of both extracellular and intracellular ROS occurs.

Active Targeting

Active PDT targeting is a type of therapy in which tumor cells are precisely targeted via direct interactions with antibodies or overexpressed tumor receptors located on the surface of tumor cells (Yao et al. 2020). This therapy involves the administration of nano‐based immuno‐conjugated NPs, which promote molecular recognition of overexpressed tumor cell receptors (Hodgkinson et al. 2017). It has also been reported that interactions of immuno‐conjugated NPs with tumor cell surface receptors increase endocytic activity, allowing NPs to be internalized (Figure 4; Yu et al. 2012). Other than mABs targeting moieties used in active targeted therapies include amino acids, carbohydrates, peptides, and vitamins (Yao et al. 2020). In addition, epidermal growth factor receptors (EGFRs), glycoproteins, folate receptors (FRs), tumor necrosis factor (TNF)‐related apoptosis‐inducing ligand (TRAIL), and transferrin receptors (TfRs) are examples of the most studied surface receptors in targeted PDT (Figure 5; Johnstone et al. 2008; Wang, Zhou, et al. 2022).

The illustration depicting the expression of surface receptors involved in both passive and active tumor cell targeting in cancer immuno‐photodynamic therapy (immuno‐PDT). The magnified view illustrates the tumor microenvironment and vascular system, emphasizing receptor‐mediated targeting mechanisms (a). The intracellular signaling pathway is shown, where structural proteins, including death receptors (DR), transferrin receptors (TfRs), epidermal growth factor receptors (EGFRs), folate receptors (FRs), tumor necrosis factor (TNF)‐related apoptosis‐inducing ligand (TRAIL), and glycoproteins are translated and expressed onto the surface of the cancer cell membrane (b). The activation of these receptors facilitates selective tumor cell recognition, enhancing the therapeutic efficacy of immuno‐PDT by promoting targeted apoptosis while minimizing damage to normal cells.

PDT and Immune Responses

PDT has been shown to significantly activate both the innate and adaptive arms of the immune system in several pre‐clinical and clinical studies. Its immune‐stimulatory capabilities (e.g., induction of inflammatory responses) can boost the therapeutic benefits, thus allowing it to be used in clinical practices (Reginato et al. 2014). Cancer cell death mechanisms after PDT have been extensively investigated in recent years. These mechanisms are classified into two types: programmed mechanisms and unprogrammed mechanisms (Carafa and Altucci 2020; Jan and Chaudhry 2019). As per most studies suggested to date, conventional PDT induces cell death pathways of apoptosis, necrosis, and autophagy. The origin of the tumor, the PS's photophysical and photochemical characteristics, its tissue distribution, the uptake of the PS by cancer cells, and its subcellular localization, and the dose of irradiation all play a significant role in the fate of cancer cells exposed to PDT which can subsequently trigger, one or more modalities of cell death (Mishchenko et al. 2022). In recent decades, it has been discovered that other “non‐conventional” cell death modalities, such as necroptosis, ferroptosis, pyroptosis, parthanatos, and mitotic catastrophe, can be triggered by photodynamic reactions (Mishchenko et al. 2022).

Not only as a clinically approved cancer treatment modality, PDT has also garnered interest since the late 1900s as a means of stimulating the immune system and as a standalone anti‐tumor therapy (Mishchenko et al. 2022). It is worth mentioning that the generated ROS post‐PDT can indirectly or directly activate both the innate and adaptive immune systems, leading to the induction of the highlighted cell death mechanisms. This type of cell death is known as ICD, which creates long‐term memory for the immune system. The antigenicity and adjuvanticity of dying cancer cells releasing damage‐associated molecular patterns, or DAMPs, a class of immunostimulatory molecules, determine the immunogenicity of cancer cell death. These include heat shock proteins (HSPs) 70 and 90, calreticulin (CRT), high‐mobility group Box 1 (HMGB1), and cytokines/chemokines (Alzeibak et al. 2021; Mishchenko et al. 2022). Different ICD modalities, such as apoptosis, necroptosis, and pyroptosis, and to a lesser extent, certain nonconventional cell death modalities, can be induced by PDT. One of the most effective regulatory pathways to induce ICD is to facilitate PDT‐induced apoptosis, whereby apoptosis‐related proteins like caspase‐3, caspase‐7, GRP78, and CHOP play a crucial role in inducing ICD. Certain factors, such as intracellular localization of PSs, mitochondria‐mediated pathway, oxidative stress‐induced DNA damage, and endoplasmic reticulum stress, are frequently linked to the apoptosis‐initiated cell death. Another type of ICD is necroptosis, which shows stronger antigenicity that elicits an antitumor immunogenic response. PDT‐induced necroptosis triggers a range of inflammatory responses, which include the release of various DAMPs from dying cancer cells (such as ATP, HMGB1, CRT, ERp57, HSP90, and vasostatin), upregulation of inflammatory chemokines (CXCL1‐3, 10, 12, and 13), constant activation of death receptors such as TLR4 that detects bacterial lipopolysaccharide (LPS), interferon‐gamma (IFN‐γ), and tumor necrosis factor‐alpha (TNF‐α), and suppression of CD47, or the “don't eat me” signal. In contrast to cell apoptosis and necroptosis, pyroptosis is more likely to cause profound inflammation via the release of DAMPs (e.g., HMGB1, ATP, mtDNA) and DAMP‐like molecules, for example, ASC specks, as well as intracellular proinflammatory mediators such as lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), IL‐18, and IL‐1β (Figures 6 and 7). Other than these major ICDs, PDT‐induced mitotic catastrophe, ferroptosis, and pyroptosis also exhibit several ICD hallmarks, which may increase the efficacy of anti‐cancer therapy (Lu et al. 2023; Mishchenko et al. 2022).

Depicts various pathways involved in cell death, including apoptosis, necroptosis, and pyroptosis, which are regulated forms of cell death, while necrosis occurs without regulation. In apoptosis (a), TNF‐α binds to TNFR1, leading to the recruitment of TRAF2/5, TRADD, cIAP1/2, and RIP1, which activate caspase 8. This results in RIP1 cleavage and the formation of Complex IIa, which includes RIP3, FADD, and TRADD, ultimately activating caspase 3 and inducing programmed cell death. In necroptosis (b), when caspase 8 is inhibited (e.g., by z‐VAD), RIP complex activation leads to the formation of Complex IIb, consisting of RIP1, RIP3, TRADD, and FADD. This complex phosphorylates MLKL, which disrupts membrane integrity, leading to necrotic cell death. In pyroptosis (c), DAMPs/PAMPs activate Toll‐Like Receptors (TLRs), which induce NF‐κB activation in the nucleus, upregulating inflammasome components such as Nlrp3, Nlrc4, Pycard, and Il1b. This activates caspase 1, which cleaves Gasdermin D (GSDMD) into N‐GSDMD, forming membrane pores that release inflammatory molecules such as HMGB1 and IL‐1β. This results in pyroptotic cell lysis and the release of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) as a marker of pyroptosis. These pathways regulate different forms of programmed cell death, playing crucial roles in inflammation, immune responses, and disease progression.

PDT as a localized treatment can also induce localized inflammation and the release of cytokines at the site of irradiation (Nath et al. 2019). In response to the release of pro‐inflammatory cytokines such as interleukins (IL; e.g., IL‐1β, IL‐6), and tumor necrosis factor alpha, the innate immune system is activated and results in the recruitment of innate immune cells, for example, macrophages, neutrophils, and natural killer (NK) cells (Nath et al. 2019; Vanderwall and Milligan 2019). In addition, subsequent studies have reported that PDT has the potential to induce the release of inflammatory mediators (i.e., cytokines) and other complement‐activating proteins. Beltrán Hernández et al. (2020) evaluated the potential of nanobody‐targeted PDT on human epidermoid carcinoma (A341) and human head and neck carcinoma (ssc‐U8) cell lines in vitro. They discovered elevated levels of pro‐inflammatory cytokines such as IL‐1β and IL‐6 from cancer cells post 24 h treatment with nanobody‐targeted PDT. Suárez‐Valladares et al. (2018) investigated differences in the intralesional inflammatory cytokine pattern in a group of 18 basal cell carcinoma (BCC) patients post intralesional PDT treatment with a 630 nm laser. It was found in their study that intralesional PDT increased the release of IL‐1β, IL‐6, and IL‐8 in all treated patients.

In addition to the induction of acute inflammation, PDT also plays an important role in the maturation, activation, and migration of dendritic cells (DCs) into draining lymph nodes (Hwang et al. 2018). Furthermore, the migration of DCs and other innate immune cells (e.g., neutrophils) to the draining lymph nodes has been reported as inducing and/or modulating the activation of the adaptive immune system post‐inflammatory activities via the major histocompatibility complex (MHC; Figure 7; Castell et al. 2019; Hwang et al. 2018; Nath et al. 2019). The cells of the adaptive immunity that are mobilized during this process include B and T lymphocytes (Stögerer and Stäger 2020). In many studies, T‐cell‐mediated anticancer immunity is reported to be tumor‐specific. In a report by Zhang, Xie, et al. (2021), it was found that both PSs used in their study, IR700DX‐6T‐ and IR700DX‐6T‐mbc94‐induced remarkable immunological responses post‐PDT. They further concluded that the combination of PDT with immunotherapeutic drugs has great potential for improving therapeutic efficacy in cancer treatment.

Based on the above‐mentioned studies, PDT has shown notable immunostimulatory effects by triggering several immunogenic cell death (ICD) pathways, such as apoptosis, necroptosis, and pyroptosis, and by stimulating both innate and adaptive immune responses. However, research gaps still exist in determining the predominant ICD pathways, maximizing PDT‐induced immunological memory, and understanding its variability among various cancer types. Other areas that require attention include elucidating the PDT‐activated specific immune pathways, improving its synergy with immune checkpoint inhibitors, personalizing treatment strategies through biomarker discovery, and integrating nanotechnology for enhanced therapeutic efficacy.

Apart from the previously mentioned ICD, other pathways activated during PTT and PDT include the cGAS–STING pathway (Fang et al. 2024; Yuan et al. 2025). The cGAS–STING pathway plays a significant role in inhibiting the proliferation of certain cancers via the secretion of anti‐proliferative molecules with the potential of promoting senescence. Likewise, the cGAS–STING pathway is regarded as the primary cellular cytosolic double‐stranded DNA (dsDNA) sensor that enables the innate immune system to react to infections, inflammation, and malignancy (Zhou et al. 2023). It can be activated by both extrinsic and intrinsic self‐DNA sensing. It is evident that the STING pathway is crucial for detecting self‐DNA produced by tumor cells and dying cells, in addition to being critical for detecting pathogens (Zhou et al. 2023; Kwon and Bakhoum 2020).

Mechanistically, cancer cells often accumulate significant levels of cytosolic dsDNA, in contrast to healthy cells. This abnormal DNA comes from a variety of sources, including exogenous components like viral sequences, mitochondrial DNA, and nuclear genomic fragments. Although the exact biological effects of ectopic cytosolic dsDNA in cancer are still not fully known, new research suggests that it has both pro‐tumorigenic and anti‐tumorigenic effects and that its effects vary greatly depending on the context and stage of the disease (Kwon and Bakhoum 2020).

During the early phases of malignant transformation, cytosolic dsDNA can function as a danger signal, activating innate immune sensing pathways, most notably the cGAS–STING axis to trigger type 1 IFN production, immune surveillance, and in some cases, cell‐intrinsic senescence, thereby suppressing tumor development (Wan et al. 2020; Jiang et al. 2020). However, as tumors progress, the loss or dysfunction of critical cell‐cycle regulators and immune checkpoint mediators can shift the balance, allowing persistent cytosolic dsDNA to drive chronic, low‐grade inflammatory signaling. This sustained inflammation may paradoxically foster cell survival, proliferation, and metastatic potential by shaping an immunosuppressive TME and promoting epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT). A major contributor to cytosolic dsDNA accumulation in cancer is chromosomal instability (CIN), a defining hallmark of many human malignancies. CIN is associated with aggressive tumor phenotypes, therapeutic resistance, and distant metastatic spread (Hosea et al. 2024; Bakhoum and Cantley 2018). Genomic instability predisposes tumor cells to chromosome missegregation events during mitosis, leading to the formation of micronuclei—small, aberrant nuclear bodies that encapsulate missegregated chromosomal fragments. These micronuclei, which are particularly unstable, frequently rupture in a cell‐cycle‐dependent manner, releasing nuclear‐derived DNA into the cytosol and further amplifying the pool of immunostimulatory (and potentially tumor‐promoting) DNA substrates. Ultimately, cytosolic dsDNA in cancer represents a double‐edged sword capable of activating potent anti‐tumor immune responses in some settings, while in others fueling inflammation‐driven tumor progression and metastatic dissemination (Levine and Holland 2018; Kwon and Bakhoum 2020; Fukuda 2023).

Recent research has demonstrated a significant connection between cellular senescence, a condition of irreversible cell‐cycle arrest, and the cGAS‐STING cascade. According to Fang et al., photothermal, photodynamic, and sonodynamic cancer treatments minimize unfavorable side effects while offering prospects for accurate tumor ablation. They pointed out that a possible target for boosting the immune system and producing a long‐lasting antitumor immune response in patients is the cyclic GMP‐AMP synthase stimulator of IFN genes (cGAS‐STING) pathway. Their review indicates that the combination of these phototherapies and cGAS‐STING agonists is a novel therapeutic strategy that exhibits impressive innovation in immune function modulation (Fang et al. 2024).

Since cGAS recognizes DNA based on its length rather than its specific sequence, DNA from any origin, whether self‐derived or foreign, has the potential to activate the cGAS‐STING pathway and initiate an immune response. According to Yang et al., STING activates the NLRP3 inflammasome to cause pyroptosis in primary murine hepatocytes, and STING deletion reduces hepatic pyroptosis (Xiao, Zhao, et al. 2023). More interestingly, Abe et al. highlighted that although the precise mechanisms by which cells recognize cytosolic DNA, including DNA from microbial pathogens, to initiate host defense gene activation remain incompletely understood, their study provided important insights into this process. They demonstrated that STING, a transmembrane protein associated with the endoplasmic reticulum translocon, plays a critical role in detecting cytoplasmic DNA. According to their findings, STING homodimers can form complexes with both self‐derived DNA, such as that released during apoptosis or necrosis, and pathogen‐associated single‐stranded or double‐stranded DNA. Furthermore, STING was shown to be essential for herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV 1) mediated transcriptional activation of a broad spectrum of innate immune and proinflammatory genes, including type I IFN (Abe et al. 2013).

PDT Combined With Immunotherapy

Successful eradication of cancer by any therapeutic approach not only depends on specifically destroying the primary tumor mass but also on activating and priming the host immune system to recognize and eliminate residual tumor cells and prevent metastasis (Baghban et al. 2020; Gun et al. 2019; Mantovani et al. 2022). Anticancer immunotherapy, which has emerged as one of the novel therapeutic pillars in oncology, has revolutionized the field of cancer treatment through the discovery and development of new approaches to enhance the body's antitumor immune functions to specifically target and eliminate cancer cells (Dobosz and Dzieciątkowski 2019; Farkona et al. 2016; Yang et al. 2022; Zhang and Zhang 2020). The specificity of cancer immunotherapy for eradicating cancer cells and inhibiting metastasis makes it a promising approach over conventional modalities like chemotherapy and radiotherapy (Bondhopadhyay et al. 2020; Gupta et al. 2022). Moreover, cancer immunotherapy stimulates the host immune system, which is usually suppressed by conventional treatment. Thus, cancer immunotherapy has advanced greatly in clinics, with various effective techniques including immunomodulatory antibody‐based treatments, immune checkpoint blockers, adoptive cellular therapy, and cancer vaccines (Ventola 2017a; Waldman et al. 2020; Zhang and Zhang 2020). Even though cancer immunotherapy strongly induces a systemic antitumor immunity to remove residual tumor cells and inhibit tumor metastasis and recurrence, it is relatively ineffective in ablating solid primary tumors (Liu and Sun 2021; Zhang, Li, et al. 2022). Furthermore, it suffers from several other limitations, which include subject‐dependent responses, dose‐limiting systemic auto‐immune side effects, and restricted host antitumor efficacy (Sakowska et al. 2022; Taefehshokr et al. 2022; Valencia et al. 2019). This restricts the application of cancer immunotherapy as an adjuvant therapy and in combinatorial approaches to improve clinical outcomes for both well‐established tumors and tumor micro‐metastasis (Liu, Yang, et al. 2022; Ott et al. 2017; Vanneman and Dranoff 2012).

Several factors influence the efficacy of combinatorial therapy, including suitable timing, dose, and administration schedules of anticancer agents. Although targeted therapies offer great potential for enhancing anticancer responses with immunotherapy, this is entirely dependent on achieving an acceptable antitumor outcome without a corresponding rise in significant side effects (Vanneman and Dranoff 2012). Therefore, this necessitates the use of a therapeutic approach that can selectively destroy the primary solid tumor with lesser induced side effects, alongside activating the host immune response to eradicate the metastatic lesions and prevent tumor recurrence.

As discussed in the earlier section, ICD during PDT also plays an important role in enhancing the immunotherapeutic efficacy of cancer to improve the therapeutic outcome by combining PDT with immunotherapy. Recently, researchers have started to explore the potential benefits of the introduction of PDT in combination with immunotherapy to augment some of the inherent shortcomings of immunotherapy by (1) inducing PDT‐mediated ICD of tumor cells to eradicate the primary tumor; (2) releasing tumor‐specific antigens which act as the substrates for an in situ autovaccine; (3) boosting the immunostimulatory properties of native tumor antigens by the release of DAMPs concurrently; and (4) elevation of proinflammatory cytokines. Eventually, immunotherapy increases the recruitment of DCs to the tumor site by enhancing the immunogenicity of the TME and also promoting tumor infiltration of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells and memory T cells by reducing immunoregulatory suppression (Figure 8). Thus, the synergistic effects of combining PDT with immunotherapy improve antimetastatic effects and permit effective abscopal responses by effectively destroying the primary tumor alongside the removal of residual tumor and targeting the metastatic tumor sites (Moloudi et al. 2023). Thus, this combinatorial therapeutic strategy can have the potential to improve the overall treatment outcome, along with preventing tumor recurrence to advance toward complete recovery (Hao et al. 2022; Lu et al. 2023).

The schematic illustration of the mechanism of action of PDT in combination with immunotherapy to enhance antitumor immune responses. PDT induces immunogenic cell death (ICD) of tumor cells, leading to the eradication of the primary tumor while simultaneously releasing tumor‐specific antigens that act as substrates for an in situ autovaccine. Additionally, PDT promotes the release of DAMPs, which enhance the immunostimulatory properties of native tumor antigens, alongside the elevation of proinflammatory cytokines that contribute to a favorable tumor immune microenvironment (TME). Immunotherapy further augments this process by increasing the recruitment of dendritic cells (DCs) to the tumor site, enhancing antigen presentation, and stimulating the infiltration of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells and memory T cells. This synergy not only improves local tumor eradication but also triggers abscopal effects, effectively targeting metastatic tumor sites and reducing immunoregulatory suppression, leading to improved antitumor immune responses and long‐term immunity against cancer recurrence.

Recent research has revealed that elevated ROS in the tumor microenvironment (TME) has an adverse effect on ICD‐induced immune response by reducing the proliferation, activation, and antitumor function of tumor‐infiltrating T lymphocytes and making them completely ineffective (Aboelella et al. 2021; Salemme et al. 2021). Thus, relieving oxidative stress in TME is an imperative strategy to overcome the immunosuppressive environment at the tumor site (Zhang, Jiang, et al. 2023; Zhao et al. 2021). In the tumor site, TME acidity results in the de‐shielding of PEG surface coating and exposes the ECM targeting peptide for ECM anchoring. This allows continuous scavenging of extracellular ROS by the targeted nanoscavenger to relieve the immunosuppression and increase CD8+ T cells infiltration (Deng et al. 2020). However, during PDT, a high amount of generated ROS usually inhibits the overall PDT‐mediated antitumor immune response. Also, PDT‐mediated upregulation results in adaptive immune resistance. It has been demonstrated that IFN‐γ can improve the immunocompetence of tumor treatments, which can enhance the expression of programmed death‐ligand 1 (PD‐L1) on cancer cells. Given that PDT may increase IFN‐γ expression, it is possible that PDT, to a certain extent, is responsible for the expression of PD‐L1 on tumor cells. The interaction of PD‐L1 on the tumor cell membrane with programmed cell death‐1 (PD‐1) on the surface of T cells, then inhibits T‐cell invasion, activation, proliferation, and cytotoxic effects to promote immune escape. The resultant immune suppression is responsible for tumor recurrence and metastasis (Warszyńska et al. 2023; Xiong, Qi, et al. 2021). Thus, methods for immune suppression regulation should also be taken into account to improve PDT's effectiveness. Fortunately, the application of advanced nanotechnology in medicine has opened novel, promising options for combining PDT with immunotherapy to maximize the full potential of both therapies.

Unfortunately, PDT‐induced antitumor immunity is frequently insufficiently strong; thus, in several preclinical models, combining PDT with immunotherapy has demonstrated synergistic effects (Alzeibak et al. 2021; Anand et al. 2021; Tan et al. 2022; Warszyńska et al. 2023). However, the clinical research of this combination strategy is still in the initial stages since the first clinical trials assessing the combination of PDT with immune checkpoint blockers are only now getting started (Penetra et al. 2023). The first clinical case illustrated by Santos et al. (2018) described the use of immune checkpoint inhibition with an anti‐PD1 antibody, nivolumab, after PDT with Redaporfin, as a successful treatment option for locally advanced head and neck squamous cell carcinoma progressing after surgery, external beam radiotherapy, and chemotherapy. Some of the ongoing clinical trials include Porfimer sodium with nivolumab, or pembrolizumab (NCT03727061), 5‐ALA with nivolumab (NCT04400539), and Sinoporphyrin sodium with pembrolizumab (NCT05386056; Penetra et al. 2023). Lobo et al. investigated the immunological effects of vascular PDT using redaporfin in a CT26 murine tumor model. PDT involves administering a PS and, after a defined drug‐to‐light interval (DLI), illuminating the tumor with light at a wavelength absorbed by the PS. While insufficient light doses generally fail to eliminate solid tumors and very high fluence rates have been linked to weak immunogenicity, earlier studies showed that vascular‐targeted redaporfin PDT delivering a low total light dose at a high fluence rate eradicated primary tumors and reduced metastasis formation, indicating a systemic antitumor immune response. In the present work, the authors show that this PDT regimen induces marked immune activation: pronounced neutrophilia within 2–24 h, a systemic surge in IL‐6 at 24 h, elevated proportions of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells expressing IFN‐γ or CD69 within 2–24 h, and an increased CD4+/CD8+ T‐cell ratio in the same period. At the tumor site, T‐cell infiltration was initially lost after PDT but returned the following day at significantly higher levels. Functional studies revealed that the therapeutic efficacy of redaporfin PDT critically depends on neutrophils and CD8+ T cells, but not on CD4+ T cells (Lobo et al. 2020).

In 2020, the Japanese government approved cetuximab saratolacan, the first antibody‐photosensitizer conjugate, for the treatment of advanced and recurrent head and neck cancer. It involves the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved monoclonal antibody (mAb) cetuximab conjugated to the water‐soluble silicon‐phthalocyanine derivative IRDye700DX, also referred to as IR700 (Gomes‐da‐Silva et al. 2020). Another, PS‐immunoconjugate RM‐1995, a bioconjugate of IRDye 700 to a monoclonal antibody specific for the cell‐surface IL‐2 receptor α‐chain CD25, received its FDA approval in 2022 for photoimmunotherapy treatment of patients with advanced cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma or head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (Inc 2021). Further, two clinical trials (NCT04305795 and NCT05265013) and one clinical trial (NCT05220748) are presently being conducted to assess the efficacy of cetuximab saratolacan in conjunction with Pembrolizumab or Cemiplimab and RM‐1995 combined with Pembrolizumab, respectively (Penetra et al. 2023).

Thus, by addressing the drawbacks of each technique, PDT and immunotherapy together offer a viable way to improve cancer treatment. This strategy has demonstrated promise in enhancing antimetastatic effects, facilitating efficient abscopal responses, and overcoming immunosuppression in the TME. However, severe challenges remain, such as PDT‐induced oxidative stress, which paradoxically suppresses immunological responses and encourages immune escape by upregulating PD‐L1. Further, expanding studies beyond head and neck cancers to other solid tumors, coupled with personalized approaches, could pave the way for PDT‐immunotherapy to become a mainstream oncological treatment modality.

Photoimmunotherapy of Cancer

Photoimmunotherapy is a form of therapy that employs the conjugation of PS's to a tumor‐specific antibody or any protein‐based targeting moiety. Few examples of photo‐immunoconjugates include Cetuximab–Phthalocyanine, Pembrolizumab–Porphyrin, Trastuzumab–Chlorin e6, Rituximab–Phthalocyanine conjugates, and so forth. These examples showcase the potential of photo‐immunoconjugates to combine the specific targeting abilities of antibodies with the phototoxic effects of photosensitizers. However, it is important to note that the development of these conjugates is an active area of research, and their clinical applications might vary based on factors such as the specific disease, the choice of antibody and photosensitizer, and the delivery methods used. Mew et al. first proposed the concept of photoimmunotherapy in the 1980s using the approach of conjugating a monoclonal antibody with PS (photo‐immunoconjugate, PIC) to synthesize more tumor‐specific photochemical drugs to minimize the PDT‐induced side effects, by increasing the tumor‐specific drug delivery and lowering the effective therapeutic dose. They further reported that the administration of PIC anti‐M1‐hematoporphyrin (HpD) conjugates directed against tumor‐specific antigens on the DBA/2J rhabdomyosarcoma M1, resulted in significant tumor suppression in M‐1 tumor‐bearing mice at lower doses, compared to HpD or antibody treatment alone (Mew et al. 1983).

Monoclonal Antibodies and Photo‐Immunoconjugates for Enhanced PDT and PIT

Development of immuno‐conjugated PS that target tumor cell growth and survival regulators, such as EGFRs and other surface receptors expressed on the surface of tumor cells, shown in Figure 5, is important in cancer research (Abu‐Yousif et al. 2012; Johnstone et al. 2008). The idea of covalently conjugating PSs to mAbs for fluorescence imaging and targeted therapy started back in the 1980s, with the first reports by Julia Levy and colleagues when HpD‐anti‐M1 antibody conjugates were used in animal models. Later on, three patients with advanced ovarian cancer were involved in the first clinical trial of PIT using antibody‐targeted phthalocyanine, which took place in 1992, whereby Fluorescein isothiocyanate‐folate assemblies with the potential to target folate receptor‐α, were used for intraoperative fluorescence imaging of tumor nodules (Huang et al. 2018). Cetuximab, panitumumab, rituximab, and trastuzumab are examples of mAbs employed as scaffolds for the conjugation of therapeutic agents such as chemo‐toxins, PSs, and immunotherapeutic medicines (Etrych et al. 2022; Fernandes et al. 2019).

Cetuximab

Passive therapies such as Cetuximab are anticancer recombinant chimeric mABs employed as immunotherapeutic drugs for targeted PDT (Juarranz et al. 2020). It belongs to a subclass of immunoglobulins (IgG), immunoglobulin 1 (IgG1). In 2004, Cetuximab was first approved for the treatment of colorectal cancer by the FDA (Goulet et al. 2022). Mechanistically, the Cetuximab antibody exerts its anti‐tumor effects by targeting the EGFR through multiple mechanisms. It binds to the extracellular domain of EGFR, preventing ligand binding (e.g., EGF, TGF‐α) and inhibiting receptor dimerization and phosphorylation, thereby blocking key oncogenic pathways such as RAS–RAF–MEK–ERK (inhibiting cell proliferation), PI3K‐AKT–mTOR (suppressing cell survival and metabolism), and JAK–STAT (modulating immune response and inflammation; Ohishi et al. 2020; Vincenzi et al. 2010).

Additionally, Cetuximab mediates antibody‐dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) by recruiting immune cells, including NK cells and macrophages, to enhance tumor cell destruction (Ochoa et al. 2017). It also induces EGFR endocytosis and degradation, reducing receptor availability and downstream signaling (Sung et al. 2023). With the growing emphasis on efficiently degrading disease‐related proteins like EGFR for therapeutic purposes, targeted protein degradation approaches, including proteolysis‐targeting chimeras and lysosome‐targeting chimeras, have garnered significant attention in recent years (Békés et al. 2022; Kim et al. 2024). Furthermore, cetuximab exhibits anti‐angiogenic effects by inhibiting VEGF production, leading to reduced tumor angiogenesis (Ciardiello and Tortora 2008). Furthermore, Cetuximab can also initiate cell cycle arrest in the G1 phase, thus promoting inhibition of proliferating cell nuclear antigen (Okuyama et al. 2021). To target and drive more tumor cells into apoptosis, many researchers have conjugated cetuximab to a phototoxin, for example, IRDye700DX.

In PDT, Cetuximab enhances therapeutic efficacy by selectively targeting EGFR‐overexpressing tumors, improving PS delivery and tumor destruction (Bhuvaneswari et al. 2015). By conjugating Cetuximab to PSs such as chlorin e6 or IR700, the accumulation of these agents in cancer cells can significantly increase while minimizing off‐target toxicity (Wang et al. 2021). Additionally, Cetuximab‐mediated endocytosis can enhance intracellular retention of a PS, thereby leading to higher ROS generation upon light activation (Komatsu et al. 2024). Moreover, Cetuximab blocks EGFR signaling, making tumor cells more vulnerable to PDT‐induced oxidative stress, which promotes apoptosis, necrosis, and immune activation. In PIT, Cetuximab plays a key role by acting as a targeting agent for IR700, a near‐infrared (NIR) PS. Upon binding to EGFR‐expressing cancer cells, NIR light activation induces rapid membrane damage, thus causing immediate tumor cell death (Kobayashi et al. 2021). PIT also triggers ICD, stimulating an anti‐tumor immune response by releasing tumor antigens that enhance dendritic cell activation and T‐cell recruitment (Moriya et al. 2022). Furthermore, when combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors like anti‐PD‐1/PD‐L1 antibodies, PIT can amplify anti‐cancer immunity, making Cetuximab‐based PDT and PIT promising strategies for targeted cancer therapy (Quaye and Obaid 2024).

Different research groups have already reported pioneering examples of photo‐immunoconjugates for the treatment of different types of cancer. A recent study by Tahara et al. (2021) described the use of RM‐1929, a light‐activated drug consisting of EGFR‐directed mAB cetuximab and phthalocyanine dye, IRDye700DX (Cetuximab‐IRDye700DX), having manageable photo‐immunotherapeutic effects in Japanese patients with recurrent head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (rHNSCC). Since the majority of photo‐immunoconjugates (PICs) used in cancer research employ the use of randomized conjugation techniques, many researchers have resorted to enhancing the transportation of chemo‐toxins and PICs across tumor cell membranes by the use of different nanocarriers. Likewise, de las Heras et al. (2022) reported the photo and chemotoxicity of EGFR‐targeted nanocarrier, IRDye700DX–cetuximab–doxorubicin–mesoporous silica NPs (IRDye700DX‐Cet‐DOX‐MSNP) on human pancreatic adenocarcinoma (AsPC‐1) and human pancreas carcinoma (MIA PaCa‐2) cell lines. In addition, it was reported that the conjugation of cetuximab to PEG‐coated MSNPs loaded with IRDye700DX improved the aqueous dispersibility of the nanocarrier.

Research has demonstrated that when combined with photosensitizers like IRDye700DX, cetuximab‐based PIT and PDT have shown great promise in specifically targeting EGFR‐overexpressing tumors by taking advantage of its capacity to block important oncogenic pathways, inducing ADCC, and promoting tumor destruction, thus showing success in treating a range of malignancies, including colorectal and head and neck squamous cell carcinoma. Its therapeutic efficacy is further increased when combined with nanocarriers, which promote drug transport and dispersibility. However, its full therapeutic potential is hindered due to the limitations of heterogeneous EGFR expression among several cancer types, randomized conjugation procedures, and possible off‐target effects. Panitumumab

Panitumumab (Vectibix) is a recombinant, fully human anti‐EGFR IgG2 isotype mAB approved by the FDA for the treatment of colorectal cancer (Rösner et al. 2019). Just like Cetuximab, Panitumumab targets and binds to the extracellular EGFR of tumor cells (Fernandes et al. 2019). This process eventually results in the inhibition of receptor dimerization and auto‐phosphorylation, thus preventing the activation of the downstream signaling cascade responsible for tumor cell proliferation and growth (Mamo et al. 2013). Panitumumab also inhibits tumor cell proliferation via inhibition of angiogenesis and induction of cell cycle arrest (Salimi‐Jeda et al. 2022). The binding epitope of Panitumumab contains the EGFR residues (P349, P362, D355, F412, and I438), which are all required for a 50% binding affinity (García‐Foncillas et al. 2019).

The mechanism of action of Panitumumab photo‐immunoconjugate in cancer photoimmunotherapy involves targeted binding, for example, the selective binding of Panitumumab‐IR700 to EGFR‐expressing tumor cells (Railkar et al. 2017). When exposed to NIR light (typically at 690 nm), IR700 is activated, triggering immediate membrane damage (Wei et al. 2022). This process results in ICD, releasing DAMPs that stimulate an immune response and contribute to selective tumor elimination while sparing surrounding tissues and minimizing off‐target effects (Ahmed and Tait 2020). The advantages of Panitumumab‐based PIT include high precision, as the targeted antibody‐IR700 conjugates ensure selective tumor destruction with minimal impact on normal tissues (Mohiuddin et al. 2023). PIT induces rapid and targeted tumor cell death within minutes, enhances anti‐tumor immunity, and potentially prevents recurrence. Additionally, PIT is non‐toxic in the absence of NIR light activation, reducing side effects compared to chemotherapy and radiation (Paraboschi et al. 2021). However, challenges include heterogeneous EGFR expression, which allows some EGFR‐negative tumor cells to evade PIT‐induced destruction, and limited NIR light penetration, which restricts treatment to superficial or endoscopically accessible tumors (Hua et al. 2024; Paraboschi et al. 2021). To overcome these limitations, PIT often requires a combination with other therapies such as immunotherapy, chemotherapy, or radiation for complete tumor eradication (Peng et al. 2022). Comparatively, Panitumumab has approximately an 8‐fold higher binding affinity for EGFR than Cetuximab (Izzedine and Perazella 2017).

In addition, the therapeutic properties of Panitumumab may also be enhanced through conjugation with a phototoxin such as IRDye700DX, forming a photo‐immunoconjugate; Panitumumab‐IRDye700DX (Pan‐IRDye700DX; Okada et al. 2022). Railkar et al. (2017) demonstrated the photo‐immunotherapeutic (PIT) effects of pan‐IRDye700DX immunoconjugate for the treatment of bladder cancer. According to their results, pan‐IRDye700DX induced PIT selectively in EGFR‐expressing bladder cancer cells. In another study, Mitsunaga et al. (2012) evaluated the in vivo target specificity of mAB‐IR700 conjugate (pan‐IRDye700DX) on EGFR‐expressing stable luciferase‐transfected cells, MDA‐MB‐468‐luc post near infrared (NIR) PIT. It was established that the pan‐IRDye700DX conjugate improved PIT, which resulted in rapid tumor cell death. In an attempt to enhance drug delivery efficiency and improve therapeutic outcomes, many researchers have resorted to the use of liposomes and other polymeric and inorganic NPs to facilitate increased drug transportation across tumor cell membranes (Liu, Chen, and Zhang 2022; Mitchell et al. 2021).

Compared to Cetuximab, Panitumumab exhibits stronger EGFR binding, enhancing its therapeutic efficacy, and has demonstrated its potential in treating bladder and breast cancers, with evidence of immune activation through ICD. However, similar to Cetuximab‐mediated PIT, Panitumumab also suffers from heterogeneous EGFR expression, restricted NIR penetration, and possible off‐target phototoxicity.

Rituximab

Rituximab is a chimeric anti‐CD20 mAB that was created by Professor Ronald Levy specifically for the treatment of malignant B cells. His first mAB tested on cancer patients in 1982, which quickly led to the establishment of the pharmaceutical company IDEC Corporation (Pierpont et al. 2018). The interactions of Rituximab with the CD20 B‐cell antigen, found on the surface of B‐lymphocytes, have been reported for initiating the activation of transmembrane signals that produce auto‐phosphorylation and activation of serine/tyrosine protein kinases (Kurozumi et al. 2016). Advancements in nanotechnology have tremendously changed the hallmark of cancer diagnostics and therapeutics, ranging from basic research to clinical applications. Previous studies have shown that when Rituximab is coupled with an active factor, it can induce the activation of different apoptotic cascades through hyper‐cross‐linking of CD20 (Song et al. 2020). In refractory tumors, Rituximab can also be conjugated to a nanoparticle such as mesoporous silica NPs for targeted drug delivery (Zhou et al. 2017). Chu et al. (2015) demonstrated that nano‐sized therapeutic conjugates have greater tumor penetration. Subsequently, Song et al. (2019) demonstrated that Rituximab‐conjugated magnetic nanoprobe (Fe3O4@DMSA@Ab) can induce apoptosis in CD20+ Raji cells via calcium (Ca2+) influx, and the mitochondrial signal cascade. With a similar standpoint, Rancan et al. (2007) reported the synthesis and in vitro anticancer effects of a pyropheophorbide‐a‐fullerene hexakis adduct immunoconjugate for targeted PDT. Their confocal microscopy studies demonstrated that the immunoconjugate Rituximab‐pyropheophorbide‐a [5:1] fullerene hexaadduct 5 conserved its affinity toward CD20 receptors expressed by CD20+ B‐lymphocyte cells. In addition, their results showed that the antibody‐free complex was unbound. Figure 9 shows the chemical structure of pyropheophorbide‐a [5:1] fullerene hexaadduct 5.

Rituximab‐based PDT immunoconjugates have revolutionized B‐cell malignancy treatment by inducing apoptosis through CD20 hyper‐crosslinking, activating downstream signaling pathways, and triggering apoptosis via mitochondrial and calcium‐dependent cascades, as well as the selective targeting of CD20+ cells. However, limitations such as off‐target effects, heterogeneity in CD20 expression, and potential resistance mechanisms remain challenges in Rituximab‐based therapies. Additionally, the immunogenicity of chimeric antibodies and the need for site‐specific conjugation methods to improve stability and targeting precision require further investigation.

Trastuzumab

In 1998, trastuzumab was approved by the US FDA as the first human epidermal growth receptor 2 (HER2)‐targeted medication for the treatment of HER2‐positive breast cancer (Maadi et al. 2021). It is a recombinant IgG1 Kappa humanized mAB employed in the treatment of breast and gastric cancer cells with overexpressed HER2 receptors (Sheikh et al. 2022). Binding of trastuzumab onto the extracellular domain of HER2 receptors has been reported to mediate the activation of antibody‐dependent cell‐mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC), which involves the recruitment of immune cells, for example, NK cells (Mandó et al. 2021). The therapeutic effects of ADCC induced by various immunotherapeutic mABs such as anti‐EGFRs (e.g., cetuximab and trastuzumab), and anti‐CD20 (e.g., rituximab), among others, have been evaluated in both pre‐ and clinical models, thus highlighting the rationale for the aforementioned mABs in cancer treatment (Zahavi and Weiner 2020). Despite some amazing clinical advances in immunotherapy, therapeutic resistance remains a significant obstacle to effective therapy (Ventola 2017b). However, there has been a substantial surge in studies focusing on overcoming therapeutic resistance in recent years. This has resulted in the development of novel therapeutic strategies such as the conjugation of active components (e.g., drugs, dyes, and phototoxins) and NPs to a mAB for enhanced drug delivery and therapeutic efficacy (Sarbadhikary et al. 2021; Shepard et al. 2017; Villela Zumaya et al. 2022).

In this regard, Korsak et al. (2017) developed porphyrin‐modified Trastuzumab for improved HER2‐targeted PIT for gastric cancer cells. Their findings indicated that the use of Trastuzumab as a scaffold for the immunoconjugate Trastuzumab‐porphyrin may be useful for the treatment of gastric cancer. Further, the in vitro findings from their study proved that the porphyrin conjugate (Trastuzumab‐porphyrin) was able to bind to receptors of HER2‐positive cells, and was intracellularly localized within the lysosomes of gastric cancer cells. They further observed that the immunoconjugate was able to induce both necrosis and apoptosis post‐irradiation. In another recent study, Deken et al. (2020) studied the in vitro and in vivo anticancer efficacy of the HER2‐targeted nanobody‐PS (IRDye700DX) conjugate for effective PDT. This nano‐formulation selectively accumulated in HER2‐overexpressed cancer cells in vivo and induced significant tumor regression post‐irradiation. As a result, nanobody‐targeted PDT has been proposed as a potential therapeutic option for HER2‐positive breast cancer, with a particular focus on Trastuzumab‐resistant HER2‐positive breast cancer cells.

Photo‐activation of Trastuzumab‐IRDye‐700DX conjugate showed tumor reduction in an ovarian mouse model (Sato et al. 2015). Enhanced therapeutic efficacy has also been reported using the anti‐EGFR antibody (ZEGFR: 3115‐IR700DX) conjugate in subcutaneous glioma xenografts (Burley et al. 2018).

Research has shown that trastuzumab‐based immunoconjugates effectively target HER2‐positive cancer cells, leading to substantial tumor regression upon photoactivation. Despite these advancements, challenges such as tumor heterogeneity, variations in HER2 expression, and potential off‐target effects limit their broader application.

In addition, several EGFR‐targeted PICs (e.g., Cetuximab‐IRDeye700 and Panitumumab‐IRDye800) are currently in clinical trials for PIT or fluorescence‐guided surgery (FGS; NCT02422979, NCT03384238). A PIC system was previously developed that comprises an FDA‐approved anti‐EGFR monoclonal antibody (Cetuximab, Cet) and a clinically used benzoporphyrin derivative (BPD) photosensitizer to target cancer cells (Abu‐Yousif et al. 2012; Huang et al. 2018; Inglut et al. 2019; Savellano and Hasan 2003; Spring et al. 2014). The highly self‐quenched BPD photosensitizer molecules conjugated to Cet can be de‐quenched (activated) by cancer cells via lysosomal proteolysis of the antibody (Huang et al. 2018; Savellano and Hasan 2003; Spring et al. 2014).

The integration of antibodies with PICs along with nanocarrier systems has shown promising potential for enhancing tumor‐targeted treatment; thus, it has prompted the development of innovative therapeutic strategies such as PIT and antibody‐drug conjugates. Several targeted PICs, such as cetuximab‐IRDye700 and panitumumab‐IRDye800, are being explored for PIT and fluorescence‐guided surgery, showing potential clinical applications. Despite these promising advancements, the optimization of conjugation techniques, along with limitations such as tumor‐specific heterogeneity in receptor expressions and potential off‐target effects, hinders widespread application. Additionally, the depth of light penetration in PDT remains a concern for effective tumor eradication. Future research should focus on optimizing PICs to enhance targeting specificity, reduce systemic toxicity, and improve the penetration of these conjugates into solid tumors. To overcome resistance, future research should emphasize enhancing nanocarrier formulations for deeper tissue penetration, combining PIT with immune checkpoint inhibitors or combinatorial therapies, and examining patient‐specific biomarker expression patterns to better customize treatment plans for enhanced tumor penetration, exploring combination therapies to overcome resistance, and developing multi‐targeted approaches integrating immunotherapy and advanced nanoparticle delivery systems to improve clinical outcomes in trastuzumab‐resistant cancers. Future research should also prioritize refining nanoparticle formulations to achieve superior biodistribution and designing fully humanized or bispecific antibodies to address resistance in the treatment of refractory B‐cell malignancies.

Nanotechnology for Targeted Photoimmunotherapy

Nano‐based drug delivery systems are of major interest in cancer therapies, which make use of nanostructures as delivery vehicles by encapsulating or attaching therapeutic drugs and delivering them to target tissues with specificity and selectivity alongside the ease of various routes of administration (Edis et al. 2021; Mitchell et al. 2021; Patra et al. 2018). Similarly, the application of NPs in PDT has been a major stride forward in solving some of the major challenges associated with conventional PSs and classical PDT (Chota et al. 2024). In general, nano‐PS delivery systems improve the solubility and stability of PSs to promote the targeted delivery, cellular absorption, and biodistribution of PSs in tumors, which potentiates effective PDT outcomes by minimizing the collateral damage to surrounding healthy tissues (Winifred Nompumelelo Simelane and Abrahamse 2021). Besides this, several novel PS‐NPs are being designed to provide great prospects to overcome the unsuitability for deep tumor treatment, tackle the tumor hypoxic problem, as well as combine with other treatment modalities for PDT‐based synergistic therapy (Hong et al. 2022; Nkune and Abrahamse 2023). Nanotechnology also connects PDT with immunotherapy, combining effective primary tumor destruction with the host immune system to recognize and eliminate the residual tumor lesions at the local site and/or metastatic site, thus preventing metastasis and recurrence.

Compared to conventional PDT and immunotherapy, targeted PIT offers an improved “therapeutic index,” by balancing the potential benefits with the potential risks in the management of cancer. However, successful PIT depends on several different factors: decrease or loss of immunoreactivity after conjugation, internalization and intracellular accumulation, and intracellular and phototoxic efficacy (Pereira et al. 2015). Thus, the introduction of nanotechnology into PIT suggests overcoming such impediments. Nanomaterials, due to their high surface area‐to‐volume ratio, enable a large drug payload, as well as can be decorated with large amounts of antibodies for tumor targeting (Cheng et al. 2021). These facts were well demonstrated by Huang et al., whereby PIC‐ NPs were synthesized by the click chemistry coupling of benzoporphyrin derivative (BPD)‐Cetuximab PIC onto FKR560‐dye‐containing poly(lactic‐co‐glycolic acid) NPs. The study showed that in comparison to PIC alone, PIC‐NP increased intratumoral PIC accumulation by two in xenograft OVCAR‐5 mouse models. Five days after treatment, mice treated with PIC‐NP‐PDT showed a mean tumor volume reduction of 55%, whereas only 30% tumor inhibition was seen in mice treated with PIC‐PDT. These findings suggest that, in comparison to using PIC alone, the intracellular PIC accumulation and light‐activated PIT are significantly enhanced in PIC‐NP‐based PIT (Huang et al. 2018).

Moreover, increased blood flow and permeability of tumor vessels following PIT result in the extravasation of relatively high concentrations of NPs specifically into the treated tumor mass with minimal uptake in untreated regions, causing super‐enhanced permeability and retention (Hanaoka et al. 2015; Nagaya et al. 2016; Sano et al. 2013). This effect was demonstrated by Hanaoka et al., whereby administration of nanosized IR800‐albumin‐bound paclitaxel immediately after PIT with IR700 conjugated anti‐Glypican‐3 (YP7) showed increased accumulation of NPs in the tumor mass of a hepatocellular carcinoma animal model, thus improving the overall efficacy of nano‐drugs (Hanaoka et al. 2015).

To achieve a satisfactory therapeutic outcome, single‐modal cancer immunotherapy based on PDT‐driven ICD is insufficient. Thus, several approaches have been investigated recently to improve PDT and cause tumor cells to undergo ICD to trigger the host immune response against cancer. Multimodal cancer immunotherapy requires the combination of other immunotherapies with ICD‐based PDT, such as conventional specific and non‐specific immunotherapies that target immune checkpoints or rely on systemic administration of antibodies and cytokines, which seems especially intriguing (Warszyńska et al. 2023; Zhang, Wang, et al. 2022). Nanotherapeutics with photosensitizers combined with immune response modifiers for photoimmunotherapy.

To overcome the limitation of monoclonal antibodies, smaller antibody fragments like nanobodies have drawn a lot of interest as substitute targeting carriers because of their increased specificity, binding affinity, and tissue penetration. Nanobodies are the smallest naturally occurring binding domains with a nanometric size, of 15 kDa in molecular weight, and dimensions of 4 nm in length and 2.5 nm in width, which are roughly 10 times smaller than conventional antibodies. They refer to the variable domain of a specific type of antibody, known as heavy‐chain‐only antibodies. Despite their nano size, nanobodies have the same ability to bind very specifically and tightly (low nanomolar affinities) to their antigens as monoclonal antibodies. On the other hand, by binding to epitopes that mABs are unable to reach, the long CDR domain of nanobodies can aid in the identification of new pharmaceutical targets. On the other hand, complement‐dependent or immune cell‐mediated cytotoxicity is absent in nanobodies due to the absence of Fc domains (Bao et al. 2021; Heukers et al. 2014; Xiong, Peng, et al. 2021). For example, a study showed that compared to the anti‐EGFR monoclonal antibody (cetuximab), the EGFR‐targeted nanobody 7D12 conjugated with the NIR fluorophore IRDye800, CW demonstrated a faster accumulation in the tumor mass, a more homogeneous distribution within the tumor, and a faster clearance of unbound molecules (Oliveira et al. 2012). Several targeted nanobodies‐PS PICs are reported for targeted PDT (Deken et al. 2020; Heukers et al. 2014; Xiong, Peng, et al. 2021). But to obtain these PICs, either a single PS was conjugated to a nanobody site‐specifically, albeit with unsatisfactory efficacy, or multiple PSs were conjugated to a nanobody randomly to yield heterogeneous conjugates, which are known to have a profoundly negative effect on the stability and pharmacokinetics of PICs (Xiong, Peng, et al. 2021). Therefore, the effectiveness of using nanoparticulate drug delivery systems as a vehicle for delivery has been used as a promising strategy for efficient design and delivery. An HER‐2‐Nanobody‐conjugated human serum albumin integrated with catalase (CAT) and chlorin (Ce6; Nb@HCC) was synthesized to investigate the synergistic effect of Nb@HCC‐mediated PDT and anti‐CTLA‐4 therapy in an SK‐OV‐3 tumor‐bearing murine model. When exposed to a 660 nm laser, Nb@HCC‐mediated PDT induced the release of DAMPs that showed ICD and dendritic cell maturation. Nb@HCC under NIR radiation reduced HIF‐1α expression and diminished hypoxia. Further, Nb@HCC‐mediated PDT and anti‐CTLA‐4 therapy prevented distant tumor growth and stimulated T‐cell infiltration (Zhang, Wu, et al. 2021).

To overcome the drawbacks of therapeutic antibodies of very long half‐life, immunogenicity, restricted tissue and tumor penetration, and high production costs. Zhang et al. reported non‐antibody‐based Ce6/BMS‐202 NPs. BMS‐202 is a strong nonpeptidic inhibitor of the PD‐1/PD‐L1 complex, and the Ce6/BMS‐202 NPs offer the dual purpose of utilization as immunologic checkpoint blockade for tumor immunotherapy and PDT for direct cell death. By using 660 nm laser irradiation with Ce6/BMS‐202 NPs, the tumor inhibition rate at the endpoint was reported to be 91.1% in the BALB/c 4 T1 tumor model, compared to 57.6% and 65.1% with Ce6 NPs and BMS‐202 NPs, respectively, demonstrating a strong synergistic effect (Zhang et al. 2019). Another example includes a 2‐in‐1 nanoplatform IR775@Met@Lip, by incorporating Metformin (Met) and IR775, a novel NIR hydrophobic ICG analog, into liposomal carriers. Met was used to inhibit the expression of PD‐L1 on the tumor cell membrane by degrading endoplasmic reticulum‐associated proteins through pAMPK (Figure 10a,b). Moreover, it can lower the oxygen consumption rate by blocking complex I in the respiratory chain of the mitochondria to reverse tumor hypoxia, offering a novel method of enhancing PDT effectiveness by regulating tumor oxygenation. Thus, PDT with IR775@Met@Lip was shown to increase ROS production to cause more tumoral damage by reversing tumor hypoxia, as well as successfully down‐regulating the PDT‐mediated overexpression of PD‐L1. These therapeutic advantages, which included reduced T‐cell exhaustion, reversed tumor hypoxia, and decreased PD‐L1 expression, effectively suppressed the growth of both the primary and abscopal tumors in bladder and colon murine tumor models (Xiong, Qi, et al. 2021). Furthermore, He et al. reported a self‐assembled nanosystem PTX/ICG‐NVs@Au@CAT that produces a TME‐triggered oxygen nanogenerator to overcome tumor hypoxia for self‐enhanced PDT. Under NIR laser irradiation, ICG simultaneously produces heat and ROS to induce PDT and PTT. Hereby, tumor hypoxia was alleviated by catalase‐mediated conversion of H2O2 to oxygen, which resulted in effective immunotherapy by promoting the polarization of tumor‐associated macrophages from M2 to M1 type (He et al. 2019).

Certain cancers, such as the majority of triple‐negative breast cancer, pancreatic cancer, colon cancer, and prostatic cancer, rarely respond to immunotherapy due to their immunosuppressive microenvironment, resulting from immunosuppressive immune cell infiltration rather than antitumor immune cell infiltration (Labani‐Motlagh et al. 2020; Tormoen et al. 2018). Furthermore, the hypoxic TME, a major feature of most solid tumors, seriously impairs PDT's effectiveness, as well as contributes to immune resistance and immune suppression/tolerance (Du et al. 2022; Noman et al. 2015). Therefore, without the need for checkpoint inhibitors or immune adjuvants, the local PDT treatment with oxygen carriers and catalyst‐based oxygen generators could generate strong and systemic anti‐tumor immunity by relieving tumor hypoxia.

Following these facts, PDT and antitumor immunity may likely be enhanced simultaneously by a combination of PDT and hypoxia relief through upregulating catalase expression in TME, which will aid in the breakdown of H2O2 to produce oxygen locally. To realize this strategy, a multifunctional pH‐sensitive sheddable, polymer‐based nanomedicine incorporated with a plasmid DNA encoding the catalase gene (pDNA‐cat) and Ce6 as PS (SPM‐P/C), was structured by Huang et al. O2 production from tumor‐enriched H2O2 was catalyzed in situ by catalase expressed by tumor cells transfected with pDNA‐cat, reducing hypoxia to help Ce6 produce ROS for a successful PDT. Subsequently, PDT‐induced ROS generation in response to NIR laser irradiation resulted in ICD and potent antitumor immunity in an in vivo tumor model by promoting dendritic cell maturation and antitumor T‐cell tumor infiltration (Huang et al. 2022). Another study (Figure 10c,d) reported a bioinspired oxygen‐augmented hybrid protein oxygen nanocarrier, by hybridizing human serum albumin with hemoglobin and loading Ce6 (C@HPOC) to facilitate oxygen self‐sufficiency PDT. ICD was shown to be induced by oxygen‐boosted PDT with C@HPOC with increased release of DAMPs (CRT, ATP, HMGB1), which encouraged the maturation of DCs, in turn activating NK cells and T cells. As shown in Figure 11a C@HPOC‐mediated immunogenic PDT demonstrated eradication of primary tumors and successfully prevented lung metastases and distant tumors by inducing systemic anti‐tumor immunity in a metastatic triple‐negative breast cancer murine model (Chen et al. 2018). In another work, investigators have shown the administration of a water‐soluble phthalocyanine derivative (PcN4) concurrently with the hypoxia‐activated prodrug AQ4N. PcN4 selectively binds to albumin dimers in vivo to form supramolecular complexes, which most likely adds to its potent tumor‐targeting ability. Following combined treatment, the primary tumor mass was significantly reduced with minimal distant lung metastasis by significantly activated CD8+ T cells in the murine triple‐negative breast cancer model. Further, an improved abscopal effect was seen in both distant and metastatic tumors, with an additional combination of activated PcN4‐AQ4N with anti‐PD‐L1 antibody (Figure 11b; Li, Jeon, et al. 2021).

Wan et al. developed a photoactivatable polymeric toll‐like receptor 7/8 (TLR7/8) nanoagonist (PNA) system made up of pyropheophorbide‐a as PS and TLR7/8 agonist resiquimod conjugated by a ROS‐sensitive linker, that induces ICD in dying tumor cells in synchrony with the spontaneous release of a potent immunoadjuvant. Local NIR irradiation generates ROS and promotes bond cleavage, which releases active resiquimod at the tumor sites on demand, spontaneously. Moreover, the in situ generation of ROS by local PDT triggered IL cascades, which in turn induced the synthesis of proinflammatory cytokines, DC activation, and the presentation of antigens to immune cells. Specifically, the conjugation of the TLR7/8 agonist resiquimod into a biodegradable PNA system allowed for the optimization of delivery efficiency in mice and pharmacokinetic tuning of small‐molecule agonists. PNA treatment, combined with NIR tumor irradiation, demonstrated notable tumor regression and inhibited postsurgical tumor recurrence and metastasis in several syngeneic tumor models, such as orthotopic 4T1 breast tumor models, MC38 colorectal carcinoma models, and B16F10 melanoma models (Wan et al. 2023).

In addition, conventional type 1 DCs (cDC1s) play a pivotal role in anti‐tumor immunity by orchestrating both the initiation and amplification of cytotoxic T‐cell responses. Their unique capacity to migrate to tumor‐draining lymph nodes, cross‐present tumor antigens, secrete pro‐inflammatory cytokines, and recruit effector T cells enables them to both prime de novo immune responses and enhance local T‐cell cytotoxicity within the TME (Böttcher and Reis e Sousa 2018). Clinically, cDC1 abundance correlates with improved patient survival, and therapeutic strategies that expand or activate cDC1 populations represent a promising approach to augment immunotherapy efficacy and overcome resistance (Nagarsheth et al. 2017). In addition, the presence of cDC1s within tumors is shaped by mechanisms involving both their chemokine‐driven recruitment and the chemokine‐dependent retention and spatial organization within the TME. Cancer cells themselves are a key source of these chemokines, producing factors such as CXCL1, CCL2, and CCL20, which preferentially recruit immune cell populations that promote tumor growth, including monocytes, macrophages, T helper 22, and Tregs cells (Nagarsheth et al. 2017; Böttcher and Reis e Sousa 2018).

Nanotechnology‐based PIT is rapidly evolving as a highly precise and powerful integration of PTT, PDT, and immunotherapy using metallic NP‐based platforms, predominantly AuNPs and AgNPs. PIT can also enhance conventional PDT and immunotherapeutics by enabling targeted tumor delivery and provoking potent anti‐tumor immune responses (Liu et al. 2021; Thiruppathi et al. 2024). In addition, NPs‐mediated PTT, such as AuNPs, which include nanorods, nanoshells, nanocages, and nanostars, enables the conversion of NIR light into localized heat via surface plasmon resonance. This localized hyperthermia facilitates targeted tumor cell ablation while sparing adjacent healthy tissue through precise control of irradiation parameters and NP distribution (Kumar and Lim 2023). NPs can cause ICD and heat the TME when exposed to NIR radiation. Tumor‐associated antigens, heat shock proteins, and DAMPs, such as calreticulin and HMGB1, are released during this process. These signals stimulate cytotoxic T lymphocytes to target tumor cells systemically by activating DCs (Li et al. 2023).

Wang et al. designed and prepared bovine serum albumin (BSA)‐coated Au nanorods (AuNRs) and applied them for the photothermal ablation of breast tumor cells. They found that the BSA‐coated AuNRs exhibited high photothermal conversion efficiency and demonstrated strong tumor‐cell‐killing ability. Furthermore, the authors explained that the ablated tumor cells were co‐cultured with immature dendritic cells (DCs) using two approaches: a direct cell‐to‐cell contact model and a diffusion model. These setups allowed them to assess the stimulatory effects mediated either by physical cell contact or by soluble factors released from the ablated tumor cells. Their results indicated that photothermally ablated tumor cells could activate DCs through both mechanisms: direct interaction and soluble factor release (Wang et al. 2018).

Reduced graphene oxide (rGO) is another interesting carbon‐based nanomaterial used in cancer PTT and PDT. It is derived from graphene oxide (GO) through chemical, thermal, or other reduction processes that decrease its oxygen content (Dash et al. 2021). Owing to its outstanding photothermal conversion efficiency in the NIR region and large specific surface area for drug loading, it shows considerable promise for applications in drug delivery and tumor therapy. Functional modifications can further augment its drug delivery performance by improving biocompatibility, enabling targeted delivery, and enhancing its photothermal therapeutic effects (Xu et al. 2025).

Wei et al. reported the synthesis of a multifunctional reagent capable of both PTT and PDT based on diazotized rGO (rGO–C6H4–COOH). They described how rGO–C6H4–COOH was first conjugated with polyethyleneimine (PEI) to improve water solubility, after which tetrakis(4‐carboxyphenyl) porphyrin (TCPP) was attached to impart PDT functionality. According to the authors, the resulting rGO–PEI–TCPP nanocomposite demonstrated excellent stability in various biological media and showed acceptable cytotoxicity profiles. Their results indicated that CBRH7919 cancer cells could be effectively driven into apoptosis when treated with rGO–PEI–TCPP under laser irradiation. This therapeutic efficacy was attributed to the strong synergistic effects of localized photothermal heating and singlet oxygen generation. Furthermore, it was observed that the treatment induced cell cycle arrest at the G2 phase recognized as the most vulnerable stage to radiation and other cytotoxic damage, thereby enhancing the therapeutic outcome (Wei et al. 2016).

Likewise, another study by Weng et al. reported that PIT has recently gained significant interest for the treatment of metastatic cancers and highlighted the need for smart nanocomposites capable of imaging‐guided therapy to enhance treatment efficacy. They described the development of a PEGylated nanocomposite for photothermal‐immunotherapy, synthesized by hybridizing Fe3O4 NPs (FNPs) with rGO via electrostatic interaction, followed by surface modification with PEG‐NH2. According to the authors, the resulting FNPs/rGO‐PEG nanocomposites served as highly effective agents for PTT when exposed to 805 nm laser irradiation. The study further revealed that the incorporation of FNPs reduced tumor‐associated macrophages (TAMs), thereby enhancing host anti‐tumor immune activity. In their experiments, it was observed that FNPs/rGO‐PEG‐mediated PTT induced ICD, accompanied by the release of DAMPs. They also reported activation of DCs in tumor‐draining lymph nodes following treatment (Wang et al. 2019).

Nanotechnology in Photo‐Immunometabolic Therapy

Although new developments in the field of cancer immunotherapy have led to the development of potent new cancer management modalities, however, 60% to 80% of patients with solid tumors either stop responding or develop resistance to the most widely used immunotherapy, like immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI). A growing body of research has revealed that metabolic reprogramming in tumor cells and infiltrating immune cells plays a critical role in the development of ICI resistance, which paved the way for tumor immunometabolism, a completely new frontier. Whereby, the depletion of nutrients essential for immune cell functions within the TME occurs due to over‐exploitation of nutrients like glucose, amino acids, and fatty acids by rapidly proliferating tumor cells that have significant requirements for biosynthesis. This not only results in a nutrient‐deficient TME for immune cells but also speeds up the production of harmful metabolic waste in tumor cells, such as lactate and methylglyoxal byproducts, which further promote an immunosuppressive TME. This hostile environment pushes immune cells to adapt their metabolic programs as a mode of survival. This leads to an increase in the immunosuppressive function of suppressor cells or tumor‐promoting cells, while anti‐tumor immune cells differentiate or polarize into immunosuppressive phenotypes and even lose their anti‐tumor functions in the process. In the end, this results in the development of a potently immunosuppressive TME that inhibits immunosurveillance and promotes tumor immune evasion and metastasis. Consequently, immune cells infiltrating the TME with highly dysregulated metabolism have a significant impact on the effectiveness of ICI immunotherapy for cancer. This led to a promising strategy known as immunometabolic cancer therapy, which involves manipulating cancer metabolic pathways with immunotherapeutic drugs to reprogram the tumor immune microenvironment (Guo et al. 2019; Li, Wenes, et al. 2019; Lian et al. 2022; Xiao, Xiong, et al. 2023; Zeng et al. 2021; Zhang and Pu 2020).

Majorly immunometabolic cancer therapy makes use of small‐molecule inhibitors for immuno‐metabolic intervention and enzyme immunotherapeutics, which are highly selective in that they can catalyze changes in the local availability of immunostimulatory and immunosuppressive signals (Farhadi et al. 2018; Sheridan 2015). In contrast to small‐molecule inhibitors, enzyme therapy offers the advantages of avoiding the risks of mutations, drug resistance, and unfavorable side effects (Farhadi et al. 2018; Salami and Crews 2017; Zeng et al. 2021). However, due to rapid clearance enzyme degradation via proteases and insufficient accumulation at target tissues, the clinical success of enzyme immunotherapeutics often depends on achieving sustained biocatalysis over pertinent time scales at the designated site (Farhadi et al. 2018). Therefore, a promising approach to deal with these limitations involves photoactivatable nanoplatforms to combine immunometabolic agents with PS and/or photothermal agents for photo‐immunometabolic therapy (Moloudi et al. 2023; Zhang and Pu 2020).

An NIR photoactivatable immunotherapeutic action of an organic semiconducting pro‐nanostimulant (OSPS) for photo‐immunometabolic therapy was reported by Li et al. The OSPS is composed of a semiconducting polymer nanoparticle (SPN) core coupled to an Indoleamine 2, 3‐dioxygenase (IDO) inhibitor NLG919, through a 1O2 cleavable linker. These OSPS allowed the production of heat and 1O2 in response to NIR photoirradiation at 808 nm, which in turn caused tumor damage and ICD through released antigens linked to the tumor. More significantly, the cleavage of 1O2‐linkers triggered by NIR irradiation, in turn, causes the remote release of immunostimulants (NLG919) from OSPS to modify the immunosuppressive TME by reducing the Kyn production, which in turn encouraged effector T‐cell activation and proliferation. OSPS‐mediated photo‐immunometabolic therapy entirely inhibited the growth of both primary and distant tumors and significantly reduced lung metastasis in 4T1 tumor‐bearing BALB/c mice (Li, Cui, et al. 2019).

Zheng et al. reported the synthesis of the first of its own an organic semiconducting polymer nanoenzyme known as SPNK, which self‐assembled from a PEGylated 1O2‐cleavable linker connecting the immunomodulating enzyme kynureninase (KYNase) to the NIR light‐absorbing SPN core. Mechanistically, the SPN core produces 1O2 in response to NIR photoirradiation with an 808 nm laser, which triggers the PDT‐induced ICD as well as initiates immunometabolism modulation by activating the KYNase enzyme, which reverses Kyn‐induced immune suppression by catalyzing the breakdown of immunosuppressive Kyn into immunologically inert alanine and anthranilic acid. The study involved the use of both in vitro B16F10 melanoma cancer cell line and in vivo B16F10 tumor‐bearing C57BL/6 mice model to investigate SPNK‐mediated photodynamic immunometabolic therapy, as shown by an 86% and 46% depletion in immunosuppressive Kyn in B16F10 cells and in the main tumors of B16F10 tumor‐bearing mice, respectively. Consequently, compared to other groups, the proliferation and infiltration of cytotoxic T lymphocytes in mice treated with SPNK were significantly higher, which resulted in complete suppression of primary tumors and efficient inhibition of distant tumors (Zeng et al. 2021).

In an interesting study (Figure 12), authors reported the designing of photoactivable erythrocyte membrane camouflaged organelle targeted nanozymes (BIONs) made up of light‐responsive 1,2‐Distearoyl‐sn‐glycero‐3‐phosphoethanolamine‐Poly(ethylene glycol) analog conjugated to protoporphyrin IX (PpIXER) targeted by the endoplasmic reticulum, and 1‐methyl‐dl‐tryptophan (1MT) as an IDO inhibitor. Ex vivo organotypic glioblastoma mouse brain slices and 3D printed “mini‐brains” using RAW 264.7 macrophages and U87 spheroids were used to characterize and assess BIONs in order to confirm their immunometabolic, catalytic activity modulating, and organelle targeting qualities. Studies showed that the stealth property of BIONs allowed their efficient tumor accumulation by evasion from systemic clearance and macrophage uptake after being administered systemically. Exposure to 630 nm light causes the on‐demand, customizable release of 1MT and PpIXER, which generates ROS in situ, resulting in ER stress and photoinduced ICD‐mediated regression of tumors. In the meantime, 1MT modifies the TME's T‐cell response. Activated BIONs were shown to be able to elicit a robust immune response, as evidenced by activation of APCs, T‐cell priming, and proliferation. The antigen‐pulsed DCs showed migration into the draining lymph node through chemotaxis and transendothelial migration, to present the antigenic cargo necessary for T lymphocyte proliferation. Additionally, loaded ceria nanocubes allowed for controlled catalytic activity with pro‐oxidant behavior inside the tumor, enhancing the effectiveness of photo‐immunometabolic therapy and antioxidant behavior in healthy tissues like the kidney and preventing AKI in the process to minimize collateral damage (Sunil et al. 2022).

For synergistic NIR‐II photothermal immunometabolic therapy of cancer, Xu et al. reported a photoactivatable semiconducting polymeric nanoantagonist (ASPA), obtained by linking vipadenant, VIPA (adenosine A2A receptor antagonist) to the light‐absorbing semiconducting polymer via an azo‐based thermolabile linker. Findings both in vitro and in vivo consistently confirmed that ASPA can produce a strong photothermal effect for tumor ablation and ICD in response to NIR‐II photoirradiation. It can also photothermally cleave the thermosensitive linker in situ to release and activate the antagonist VIPA, which intensifies the antitumor immune response by modulating the adenosinergic pathway, which in turn promotes the infiltration of cytotoxic T cells and counteracts the immunosuppressive regulatory T lymphocyte cells by preventing the secretion of immunosuppressive cytokines. Furthermore, due to long‐term and systemic immunological memory, the synergistic therapeutic action of ASPA allowed for the total eradication of the primary tumors, effective prevention of distant pulmonary metastasis, and tumor recurrence (Xu et al. 2021).

The potential effects of PDT in inducing cytotoxic cell death in T cells using PD‐1/PD‐L1‐targeted PSs are gaining attention in immunotherapy (Warszyńska et al. 2023). Likewise, attaching or conjugating PSs to PD‐L1‐specific molecules, PDT can selectively localize within PD‐L1‐expressing T cells, particularly those that are exhausted or dysfunctional in the TME (Delcanale et al. 2023). Upon exposure to light, the PS produces ROS, thus triggering oxidative stress, membrane damage, and ultimately leading to apoptosis or necrosis of the targeted T cells. Several studies have explored the combination of PDT with PD‐1/PD‐L1 inhibitors to enhance antitumor immune responses. A study by Kaneko et al. demonstrated that combining a novel heat shock protein 90‐targeted PDT with PD‐1/PD‐L1 blockade induced potent systemic antitumor efficacy and an abscopal effect against breast cancers. This combination therapy enhanced tumor infiltration by activated CD8+ T cells and improved overall survival in murine breast cancer models (Kaneko et al. 2022). Additionally, a case report by Jingyu et al. discussed the successful application of PDT combined with a PD‐1 inhibitor in treating advanced bronchial tumors. The patient achieved complete remission and remained free of recurrence and metastasis during a 14‐month follow‐up (Jingyu et al. 2022). Li et al. reported that immune checkpoint inhibitors are promising in treating various cancers, including head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), though a significant number of HNSCC patients do not respond to PD‐L1 antibody therapy. To address this, they described a PDT approach aimed at enhancing the anti‐tumor effects of the anti‐PD‐L1 antibody. They further assessed the phenotypic and functional maturation of immature DCs (imDCs) induced by PDT through flow cytometry and ELISA. Using a mouse model established with the HNSCC cell line SCC7, they evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of a PDT‐DC vaccine in enhancing the anti‐tumor immunity of the PD‐L1 antibody. Their findings indicated that PDT at 0.5 μM of m‐THPC with a light dose of 5 J/cm2 induced ICD in SCC7 cells, which in turn stimulated the maturation of imDCs. Furthermore, in vivo assays suggested that the combination of the PDT‐DC vaccine and anti‐PD‐L1 monoclonal antibody synergistically promoted anti‐tumor immunity and suppressed tumor progression (Li et al. 2022). In addition, a literature review indicates that rationally designed nanosystems for achieving synergistic photodynamic/photothermal‐immunometabolic therapy have been minimally explored. Notably, photoactivatable nano‐sensitizers for combinational photo‐immunometabolic cancer therapy remain an emerging research area with limited studies (Table 3).

Photoactivatable nano‐sensitizers for combinational photo‐immunometabolic cancer therapy.

Besides the IDO pathway, several other metabolic pathways influence PDT‐induced immunotherapy by shaping immune cell activity and altering the TME. Modulating these pathways with metabolic inhibitors or immune checkpoint blockade presents a promising strategy to enhance PDT effectiveness and counteract tumor immune evasion. For example, Tryptophan 2,3‐dioxygenase (TDO), like IDO, catalyzes the breakdown of tryptophan (Trp) into kynurenine (Kyn), leading to immune suppression in the TME. Unlike IDO, TDO is constitutively expressed in certain tumors (e.g., glioblastoma, liver cancer) and contributes to immune evasion by suppressing cytotoxic T cells (CTLs) and NK cells, activating aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR), which further promotes immunosuppressive phenotypes and promotes the development of regulatory Tregs that inhibit antitumor immunity. On the other hand, PDT‐induced oxidative stress can increase TDO activity, leading to increased Kyn production. More interestingly, high Kyn levels weaken PDT‐induced immune responses by promoting an immunosuppressive TME.

While specific studies combining TDO inhibitors like LM10 with PDT are currently lacking, existing research indicates that inhibiting TDO can enhance antitumor immune responses. For instance, a study by Pilotte et al. demonstrated that systemic administration of a TDO inhibitor restored the ability of mice to reject TDO‐expressing tumors, highlighting the potential of TDO inhibition in cancer therapy (Pilotte et al. 2012). Additionally, PDT can activate the immune system by generating ROS, which may induce tumor destruction through direct cell killing, vascular disruption, and/or immune stimulation. Combining PDT with immunotherapeutic strategies has shown promise in enhancing antitumor immunity (Anzengruber et al. 2015). Although we have so far reviewed the IDO/kyn and TDO pathways only, we would like to report on glycolysis and the Warburg effect and arginine metabolism (ARG1/NOS pathways) as the final example of the effects of photo‐immunometabolics in cancer therapy.

Glycolysis is a central metabolic pathway that breaks down glucose into pyruvate, generating ATP and metabolic intermediates essential for cellular function (Kierans and Taylor 2024). In normal cells, pyruvate is primarily directed into the mitochondria for oxidative phosphorylation under aerobic conditions (Tufail et al. 2024). However, cancer cells often exhibit the Warburg effect, a metabolic adaptation in which they preferentially rely on glycolysis for energy production, even in the presence of oxygen (Vaupel and Multhoff 2021). This metabolic shift results in increased glucose uptake and lactate production, contributing to an acidic TME that suppresses immune cell function (Jaworska et al. 2023). Conceptually, the Warburg effect supports rapid tumor proliferation by providing biosynthetic precursors for nucleotides, amino acids, and lipids while also promoting immune evasion by impairing T‐cell activation and antigen presentation (Mao et al. 2024; Zheng et al. 2024). Furthermore, lactate accumulation inhibits dendritic cell maturation and enhances the immunosuppressive activity of Tregs and myeloid‐derived suppressor cells (MDSCs; Hayes et al. 2021). Targeting glycolytic pathways through inhibitors of LDH or glucose transporters, in combination with immunotherapies or PDT, is being explored as a strategy to enhance antitumor immunity and overcome metabolic barriers in the TME (Chelakkot et al. 2023).

A critical component of enhancing cancer treatment efficacy lies in deepening the mechanistic understanding of the photo‐immunometabolics, particularly by examining the metabolic vulnerabilities that phototherapy can exploit. One such vulnerability is the tumor's reliance on aerobic glycolysis, which leads to excessive lactate production and accumulation in the TME (Jedlička et al. 2022; Jiang et al. 2024; Sharma et al. 2022). This build‐up of lactate not only acidifies the TME but also suppresses the activity of effector immune cells such as CD8+ T cells and NK cells (Jedlička et al. 2022). The resulting acidic niche impairs cytokine production, disrupts T‐cell receptor signaling, and diminishes overall immune functionality, creating conditions that tumors can leverage to evade immune surveillance (Jiang et al. 2024). Interventions aimed at disrupting lactate dynamics either by inhibiting LDH, blocking monocarboxylate transporters (MCT1 or MCT4), or neutralizing acidic pH have been shown to restore antitumor immunity and improve responses to immunotherapies (Bader et al. 2020; García‐Cañaveras et al. 2019; Jiang et al. 2024). Additionally, MCT1 is responsible for absorbing lactate into tumor cells and tumor endothelial cells, whereas MCT4 promotes lactate release in hypoxic environments (Jiang et al. 2024). Furthermore, lactate build‐up has important paracrine effects on non‐malignant cells in the TME in addition to paracrine effects between tumor cell subtypes. For instance, it has been demonstrated that when fibroblasts and tumor cells co‐culture, the fibroblasts initiate aerobic glycolysis and MCT4‐dependent lactate excretion, while the tumor cells initiate MCT1‐mediated lactate uptake and oxidation (Schwörer et al. 2019).

Currently, there are many studies that have explored numerous nanoplatforms in tumor immunotherapy, including liposomes, dendrimers, and polymeric NPs in targeting the glycolytic pathway in cancer PDT. A comprehensive review by Gu and Yu discusses how nanomaterials with controlled chemical compositions and structures can modulate tumor glycolysis. These nanomaterials serve as platforms for drug delivery, biochemical modulation, and combination therapies, effectively disrupting the glycolytic processes that cancer cells rely on for energy (Gu and Yu 2022). Lei et al. demonstrated that liposomes encapsulating IR780 and the glycolysis inhibitor lonidamine (LND) enhanced the efficacy of photodynamic and photothermal therapy in hepatocellular carcinoma. The combination led to increased ROS production and energy depletion in cancer cells, resulting in suppressed tumor growth (Lei et al. 2023). A comprehensive review by Chelakkot et al. highlighted that targeting enhanced glycolysis in cancer cells is a promising strategy to increase their susceptibility to conventional treatments, including immuno‐PDT. By inhibiting key glycolytic enzymes or altering glucose transport, these strategies disrupt the energy supply of cancer cells, enhancing treatment efficacy (Chelakkot et al. 2023).

With regards to enhancing phototherapy and immunotherapy through glycolysis inhibition, a study by Fu et al. demonstrated that mannose‐modified liposomes loaded with ICG could inhibit glycolysis in triple‐negative breast cancer cells. This inhibition reversed tumor hypoxia and suppressed heat shock protein expression, thereby enhancing the efficacy of photothermal and photodynamic therapies. The metabolic reprogramming induced by glycolysis inhibition also promoted an immunogenic TME, potentially improving the response to immunotherapy (Fu et al. 2023). While liposomes offer high biocompatibility, dendrimers provide greater surface functionalization for multi‐drug loading. A study by Chu et al. investigated how drug loading levels influence the performance of NP drug delivery systems, a factor often underexplored despite its central role in NP formulation. Using particle replication in Non‐wetting Templates (PRINT), a lithographic technique that enables precise control over particle size, shape, and surface chemistry, they developed two docetaxel‐loaded nanoparticles that were physically identical but differed in drug content. In a murine cancer model, the formulation with lower drug loading (9%‐NP) demonstrated improved pharmacokinetics and therapeutic efficacy compared to the higher‐loaded version (20%‐NP). Specifically, the 9%‐NP led to increased docetaxel concentrations in plasma and tumor tissue, while simultaneously reducing accumulation in off‐target organs such as the liver, spleen, and lungs (Chu et al. 2013).

Merino et al. developed an innovative class of immunoliposomes (ILs) by functionalizing the surface of liposomes with mAbs targeting cancer‐associated markers such as PD‐L1, a key immune checkpoint implicated in tumor immune evasion. These antibody‐decorated ILs are designed to specifically bind PD‐L1 overexpressed on tumor cells, thereby blocking its interaction with PD‐1 and reactivating antitumor immune responses. This approach represents a novel application of ILs in immunotherapy. The team engineered ILs conjugated with either full anti‐PD‐L1 antibodies or their Fab fragments and systematically optimized factors like PEGylation density, ligand size, and conjugation strategy. In vitro, Fab'‐modified ILs exhibited the strongest PD‐L1 binding affinity, which correlated with enhanced tumor accumulation in vivo. This targeted delivery led to an increase in cytotoxic CD8+ T‐cell infiltration at the tumor site and resulted in tumor shrinkage, with complete tumor regression observed in 20% of treated mice. These findings support the potential of ILs as a versatile immune‐nanoplatform for delivering immunotherapeutic agents and facilitating combination cancer therapies (Merino et al. 2019).

Currently, there are already a few passively targeted anti‐cancer nanomedicines available on the market, including DaunoXome (liposomal daunorubicin) and Doxil (liposomal doxorubicin). Several others, including polymeric conjugates including Opaxio for paclitaxel administration and micelles such as NK105 for paclitaxel delivery, are undergoing advanced clinical trials (Hu et al. 2015). de Oliveira Silva et al. investigated the therapeutic potential of a long‐circulating, pH‐sensitive, folate‐modified liposomal formulation of doxorubicin (SpHL‐DOX‐Fol) for targeted breast cancer treatment. Recognizing that folate receptors are frequently overexpressed in various epithelial tumors, including breast cancer, the researchers enhanced the liposomal surface with folic acid to promote receptor‐mediated uptake by tumor cells and improve intracellular drug delivery. The study assessed both in vitro and in vivo antitumor efficacy using the 4T1 breast cancer model, along with biodistribution and cardiac safety profiles. Notably, radiolabeled liposomes (99mTc‐SpHL‐Fol) demonstrated significantly higher tumor accumulation 4 h post‐injection compared to their non‐targeted counterparts. In vivo results revealed that SpHL‐DOX‐Fol treatment led to a 68% inhibition of tumor growth and a marked reduction in lung metastases (de Oliveira Silva et al. 2019).

The dual metabolic disruption strategy represents a promising and innovative approach in cancer therapy, targeting the metabolic flexibility that allows tumor cells to survive and thrive under diverse conditions. Cancer cells typically rely on both mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and anaerobic glycolysis for energy production and biosynthetic demands. OXPHOS provides sustained ATP generation and supports anabolic processes under normoxic conditions, while glycolysis—especially in the form of the Warburg effect—is upregulated even in the presence of oxygen, offering rapid energy supply and metabolic intermediates critical for proliferation (Burns and Manda 2017; Zhang et al. 2025). By simultaneously disrupting both metabolic pathways, this strategy undermines the tumor's ability to adapt to microenvironmental stresses such as hypoxia or nutrient deprivation. It effectively starves the cancer cells, induces metabolic collapse, and enhances apoptotic signaling. Moreover, dual metabolic targeting can help overcome resistance to single‐pathway inhibitors, reduce the likelihood of tumor relapse, and sensitize tumors to other therapeutic modalities. This comprehensive interference with cancer metabolism holds significant potential to improve treatment efficacy, particularly in aggressive or treatment‐resistant cancers. Likewise, a study by Zhang et al. developed a self‐assembled copper complex NPs targeting both mitochondria and anaerobic glycolysis. These NPs enhanced mitochondrial respiration and increased intracellular copper ion accumulation, promoting cuproptosis—a form of regulated cell death. The induction of cuproptosis, coupled with glycolysis inhibition, led to robust antitumor immunity, suggesting a synergistic effect with immunotherapy approaches (Zhang et al. 2024).

Finally, we continue exploring how other metabolic pathways influence PDT‐induced immunotherapy, with l‐Arginine metabolism serving as a key example. l‐Arginine plays a crucial role in modulating the TME by enhancing T‐cell function and nitric oxide (NO) production (Geiger et al. 2016). When combined with PDT, l‐Arginine metabolism can boost immune responses, thus promoting better tumor eradication and improving the efficacy of immunotherapy (Geiger et al. 2016; Liu, Zhang, et al. 2023). In addition, l‐Arginine serves as a substrate for two key enzymes: arginase (ARG1) and nitric oxide synthase (NOS), which have opposing effects on immune function. ARG1 metabolizes L‐arginine into ornithine and urea, leading to T‐cell suppression by depleting arginine, an essential amino acid for T‐cell proliferation and activation (Caldwell et al. 2018). In contrast, NOS catalyzes the conversion of l‐arginine to NO, a signaling molecule that can enhance immune responses by promoting T‐cell activation and cytotoxicity (Förstermann and Sessa 2012). However, excessive NO production may also contribute to tumor progression by promoting angiogenesis and immune evasion. In the TME, cancer cells and MDSCs often upregulate ARG1, leading to l‐arginine depletion and impaired antitumor immunity (Kumar et al. 2016). Strategies targeting arginine metabolism, such as ARG1 inhibitors or l‐arginine supplementation, are being explored to restore T‐cell function and enhance immunotherapy efficacy, particularly in combination with treatments like PDT (Hua et al. 2021; Mussai et al. 2019). Several studies have investigated the role of the l‐arginine pathway in enhancing PDT and immunotherapy.

As stated above, Gong et al. developed NPs co‐delivering doxorubicin, indocyanine green, and valsartan. Upon light activation, these arginine‐rich peptide‐modified NPs enhanced drug penetration and synergistically combined chemotherapy, PDT, and immunotherapy. This approach effectively inhibited tumor growth and promoted immune cell infiltration in tumor‐bearing mice (Gong et al. 2023). Likewise, Zhang et al. developed a novel nanosystem, Arg‐PCN@Gel, designed to target and synergize cascade PDT with nutritional immunotherapy. This nanosystem comprises PCN‐224 as a PDT platform for generating ROS, along with Arg as a NO donor. The system was coated with gelatin, serving both as a targeting agent and a sustained provider of Arg. The integration of these components enables an efficient antimicrobial effect against MRSA biofilms. In this study, they demonstrated that Arg‐PCN@Gel effectively suppressed MRSA biofilms in vivo, thus leading to significant bacterial eradication. The combined effects of PDT, NO release, and immune modulation provided a potent and promising strategy against biofilm‐associated infections. This dual approach enhances bacterial killing while simultaneously boosting host immune responses, offering a novel therapeutic avenue for tackling antibiotic‐resistant infections (Zhang, Wu, et al. 2023).

With regards to strategic NP engineering for TME modulation coupled with activated photoacoustic‐fluorescence guidance to potentiate precision chemo‐immunotherapy, a study by Zeng et al. reports a synergistic theranostic strategy that integrates targeted chemotherapy with real‐time immune response monitoring to improve glioblastoma management and patient outcomes. Conventional systemic temozolomide (TMZ) administration and imaging techniques fall short in enabling precise drug delivery or dynamic tracking of tumor‐immune interactions in vivo. To address this, the authors developed an all‐in‐one theranostic nanoprobe (PEG/αCD25‐Cy7/TMZ) capable of both targeted TMZ delivery and photoacoustic‐fluorescence imaging of immune responses. This nanoprobe encapsulates TMZ and an αCD25‐Cy7 optical tracer specific to regulatory T lymphocytes (Tregs) within a glutathione‐responsive DSPE‐SS‐PEG2000 shell, achieving a remarkable targeting efficiency of 92.3% toward Tregs. Upon activation by elevated glutathione levels in the tumor microenvironment, the nanoprobe facilitates site‐specific TMZ release while delineating glioblastoma margins via photoacoustic‐fluorescence imaging. When combined with IDO inhibitors post‐chemotherapy, the approach augments antitumor immunity by reducing Treg infiltration, thereby prolonging survival. Photoacoustic imaging enables noninvasive, micrometer‐scale visualization of immune dynamics, revealing an initial post‐chemotherapy upregulation of Treg infiltration followed by downregulation after IDO inhibition. This multifunctional theranostic platform offers a powerful means to achieve precise TMZ delivery and longitudinal in situ monitoring of regulatory T lymphocytes, paving the way for more effective and personalized glioblastoma chemo‐immunotherapy (Zeng et al. 2023).

The integration of nanotechnology into photo‐immunometabolic therapy offers a promising new approach in cancer treatment, PDT, metabolic reprogramming, and immune modulation. Research has shown that photoactivatable nanoplatforms, such as organic semiconducting pro‐nanostimulants (OSPS), organic semiconducting polymer nanoenzymes (SPNK), and erythrocyte membrane camouflaged organelle‐targeted nanozymes (BIONs), can induce tumor‐specific cytotoxicity while also reversing the immunosuppressive TME through targeted metabolic modulation, thus boosting anti‐tumor immunity. However, despite substantial advancements, challenges such as rapid enzyme degradation, limited tissue penetration, and insufficient accumulation at target sites continue to pose significant barriers to their clinical application. Additionally, while some metabolic pathways, like the kynurenine and glycolytic pathways, have been widely studied, others, such as L‐arginine metabolism and TDO inhibition, remain relatively unexplored. Future research should focus on developing more efficient and stable nanocarriers to address these challenges, expanding the investigation of additional metabolic pathways, and optimizing combination therapies to improve tumor targeting, enhance immune responses, and minimize off‐target effects.

Emerging Biomarkers and Patient Stratification for Photoimmunotherapy

Integrating tumor‐intrinsic, immunological, and systemic biomarkers is necessary to determine which patients are most likely to benefit from PIT or PDT in conjunction with immune regulation. Tumor mutational burden (TMB) and neoantigen load are two potential tumor‐intrinsic predictors that are correlated with antigenicity and the probability of effective T‐cell responses (Dudzik et al. 2024; Thiruppathi et al. 2024). The presence and activity of cDC1, baseline CD8+ tumor‐infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) density, and an IFN‐γ–dominant transcriptional signature are examples of immune microenvironment markers that show an underlying immune contexture that photo‐triggered ICD can amplify. Indicators of adaptive resistance, such as PD‐L1 expression and inducible checkpoint upregulation after IFN‐γ, aid in predicting whether PD‐1/PD‐L1 blockage will be necessary in combination (Gao et al. 2024; Meiser et al. 2023).

Targeted DNA sequencing for TMB/neoantigens, multiplex immunohistochemistry or immunofluorescence for CD8 and cDC1 markers, bulk or single‐cell RNA sequencing for IFN‐signatures, and measurement of circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) for early pharmacodynamic response are examples of practical assays for stratification (Ahmed et al. 2023; McGrail et al. 2021). After therapy, metabolic or immunological alterations can be spatially mapped using functional imaging (FDG‐PET or new immune PET tracers). Anatomical (RECIST/iRECIST), metabolic (FDG‐PET), and immunological readouts (change in CD8+ TILs, ctDNA kinetics, cytokine profiling) should be combined for response evaluation in order to capture both systemic immune activation and local control (Ryssel et al. 2025; Wahl et al. 2009). For trials, we advise using a tiered stratification workflow: Assign to PIT/PDT alone when compared to PIT/PDT combined with checkpoint blockade; assess baseline immune infiltrate and cDC1 markers; screen for tumor antigenicity (TMB/neoantigen) and PD‐L1; and track response using ctDNA decline and early immune imaging with confirmatory tissue sampling at predetermined timepoints. This multimodal strategy can be tailored to certain tumor types and trial stages while striking a balance between practicality and mechanistic relevance.

Outlook, Challenges, and Future Perspectives

At the moment, one of the most promising treatment modalities is cancer immunotherapy, but issues like poor response rates and unfavorable immune‐related side effects restrict its clinical use. Conventional immunotherapies only work by stimulating the immune system rather than actually killing cancer cells. However, a more ideal cancer treatment should eliminate the main tumor along with inducing the immune system to identify, locate, and eliminate any other tumor cells, whether they are located near the original tumor or in distant micrometastases. In this regard, PDT has such advantageous characteristics of triggering an immune response and causing tumor cells to undergo ICD. As a result, efficient PDT makes it possible to eliminate tumor cells as well as reverse the immunosuppressive TME and create the desired synergy with additional immunotherapeutics. Within cancer immunotherapy modalities, PIT has advanced rapidly over the recent years, reaching significant preclinical and clinical stages. Compared to PIT, photo‐immunometabolic therapy further increases the scope of immunotherapy by modifying the metabolic programs of cancer and/or immune cells to improve the therapeutic window.

The field of PIT basically started with photoimmunoconjugates by conjugating specific antibodies with PSs, which increases PDT selectivity. Despite exhibiting encouraging outcomes in clinical trials, the major challenge with PICs lies in their design principle and chemistry, as the final conjugate should have the ability to target, along with the phototoxic potential. The limited number of antigens on the surface of each cancer cell, the limited PS–to–antibody ratio of PIC (1–7), and the poor tumor accessibility and accumulation of antibodies in vivo are the other major causes of limitation. Moreover, the conjugates' large size (150 kDa) and long half‐life prevent them from penetrating deeply into tumor tissues and restrict the quantity of PS to be delivered.

PIT‐based agents further face numerous obstacles, including the short half‐life and aggregation‐caused quenching (ACQ) effect of the majority of PSs, the hypoxic microenvironment surrounding the tumor, the ineffective accumulation of PSs in the tumor, and the photobleaching issue. To overcome these restrictions, the use of a nanotechnology approach has proven to improve PIC PSs' physicochemical characteristics and accumulation in solid tumors and prolong blood circulation time to strengthen PDT‐induced ICD and immunotherapy. For example, studies have shown that phototoxic and photothermal nanosystems targeting the endoplasmic reticulum, modulating the immunosuppressive TME and inducing mild hyperthermia‐mediated increases in CAR‐T‐cell infiltration and activation in solid tumors, show great potential in phototherapy‐induced immunotherapy. Thus, nanomedicine offers great potential in enhancing targeted PIT and photo‐immunometabolic therapy. These innovative strategies harness the combined effects of phototherapy, immunotherapy, and metabolic reprogramming to selectively eliminate tumors while reducing systemic toxicity. Although substantial progress has been made in preclinical studies and early clinical trials, several obstacles remain that must be overcome to enable broader clinical implementation and improve therapeutic efficacy.

A major challenge in advancing nanomedicine‐based therapies to clinical application is ensuring the long‐term safety and biocompatibility of nanomaterials. Although various nanocarriers, such as liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles, and inorganic nanostructures, exhibit favorable biodistribution, their potential immunogenicity, systemic toxicity, and clearance mechanisms require thorough investigation through long‐term clinical studies. Another critical obstacle is the heterogeneity of the TME, which complicates the ability to achieve consistent therapeutic responses. Differences in tumor oxygenation, receptor expression, metabolic pathways, and immune cell infiltration can influence the effectiveness of photoimmunotherapies. Personalized treatment approaches that account for patient‐specific tumor characteristics may improve therapeutic outcomes. Optimizing light penetration and energy deposition is also a significant concern. The success of PIT depends on the efficient activation of PSs at tumor sites, yet the limited penetration of light, especially in deep‐seated tumors, remains a challenge. Techniques such as upconversion nanoparticles, two‐photon excitation, and NIR light sources are being explored to enhance treatment efficacy.

One promising direction is the development of smart and multifunctional nanoplatforms. Future research should prioritize the engineering of nanosystems that integrate imaging, targeted drug delivery, immune modulation, and phototherapy into a single platform. Stimuli‐responsive nanoparticles that release therapeutic agents in response to tumor‐specific signals could further enhance treatment specificity and efficacy. Synergistic combination therapies offer a compelling strategy to improve the effectiveness of photoimmunotherapies. These approaches demonstrate greater therapeutic impact when combined with conventional cancer treatments, such as immune checkpoint inhibitors, metabolic regulators, and radiotherapy. The synergy between PDT and immune checkpoint inhibitors like nivolumab and pembrolizumab is being studied in ongoing trials, but clinical translation is still in its early stages despite encouraging preclinical evidence. The recent approval of antibody‐photosensitizer conjugates like cetuximab saratolacan (RM‐1929) in Japan for the treatment of unresectable locally advanced or recurrent head and neck cancer and RM‐1995 by FDA for patients with advanced cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma or head and neck squamous cell carcinoma represents a step toward clinical implementation, but more research is required to optimize treatment parameters and patient selection for maximizing therapeutic efficacy.

The shift toward personalized and precision medicine will also be crucial in advancing nanomedicine‐based photoimmunotherapies. Biomarker‐guided approaches that tailor treatments based on individual tumor characteristics have the potential to improve patient outcomes significantly. Innovations in liquid biopsy, molecular profiling, and theranostic nanoparticles can facilitate real‐time monitoring and adaptive treatment strategies. Lastly, addressing regulatory and ethical challenges will be critical for translating nanomedicine‐based therapies from research to clinical practice. As the regulatory landscape evolves, establishing clear guidelines for safety assessments, ethical considerations, and clinical trial protocols will be essential. Strong collaboration between scientists, pharma companies, industry stakeholders, and regulatory agencies will be necessary to navigate these challenges and accelerate clinical adoption.

Conclusions

In conclusion, several novel nanotherapeutics as discussed in this review can broaden the biomedical applications of nano‐photosensitizers for their potential use in clinical settings down the road for phototherapy‐synergized immunotherapy. Nanomedicine‐driven targeted cancer PIT and photo‐immunometabolic therapy offer promising prospects for transforming cancer treatment. Despite existing challenges, advancements through interdisciplinary collaboration, technological innovation, and personalized treatment strategies will drive progress toward successful clinical application. Ensuring the safety and efficacy of nano‐PIT across various cancer types and regulatory compliance will be crucial in fully harnessing the potential of these emerging therapeutic approaches in oncology.
