Textured piezoelectric ceramics with reduced grain size for high-frequency transducer applications
Authors: Yizhou Xiao, Shuai Yang, Mingwen Wang, Yaolin Wang, Tao Li, Chunchun Li, Jinglei Li, Fei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTextured piezoelectric ceramics are considered promising next-generation piezoelectric materials, offering single-crystal-like high piezoelectric performance and low production cost. However, the large grain size of textured ceramics (15 ~ 40 μm) lead to significant decrease in piezoelectricity as the thickness of samples approaches the scale of grain size, limiting their application in high-frequency transducers (>20 MHz, corresponding thickness <100 μm). Here, we address this issue by reducing the grain size through template shortening. A modified topochemical microcrystal conversion method was developed to fabricate BaTiO3 templates with a much smaller length of 2.7 μm, compared with conventional templates (>7 μm). Using these reduced-size developed templates, we obtained <001 > -textured Pb(Mg1/3Nb2/3)O3-PbTiO3 ceramic with an average grain size of 7.8 μm, being significantly smaller than previously reported textured Pb(Mg1/3Nb2/3)O3-PbTiO3 ceramics (~20 μm), while achieving a high piezoelectric coefficient d33 of 1330 pC N-1. Notably, the adverse thickness scaling effect on piezoelectric performance was greatly mitigated: at a thickness of 100 μm, the piezoelectric response was found to reduce only 8% for our textured ceramics with reduced grain size, compared with almost 30% in conventional-grain-size textured ceramics. This strategy provides a practical route to high-performance textured ceramics suited for next-generation high-frequency ultrasonic transducers.Subject terms: Ferroelectrics and multiferroics, Materials for devices

Introduction

Ultrasonic transducers are widely utilized in biomedical diagnostics, ultrasound therapy and industrial nondestructive testing due to their high spatial resolution and real-time imaging capabilities. Operating at higher frequencies, ultrasonic transducers enable precise detection of internal anatomical structures, making them particularly suitable for applications requiring detailed visualization1,2. As the core functional component of ultrasonic transducers, piezoelectric materials play a critical role in determining bandwidth and sensitivity of transducers through their electromechanical properties, making the selection and optimization of piezoelectric materials essential for ultrasonic transducer design3,4.

In recent years, textured PbTiO3-based piezoelectric ceramics have gained considerable attention owing to their high electromechanical properties comparable to those of relaxor-PbTiO3 single crystals, along with a relatively cost-effective fabrication process akin to polycrystalline ceramics5–7. Despite these merits, state-of-the-art textured ceramics usually possess large grain sizes, typically ranging from 15 to 40 μm, being much larger than those of nontextured ceramics (2~10 μm). Such an intrinsic microstructural feature presents a fundamental obstacle for high-frequency ultrasonic applications, where the thickness of the piezoelectric active layer must be reduced below ~100 μm to achieve resonance frequencies above 20 MHz8. Numerous studies have reported that the electromechanical performance of piezoelectric ceramics/single crystal and thin film deteriorates rapidly upon thinning to such thicknesses, which is widely attributed to the so-called “thickness scaling effect”, arises from the interplay between surface damage layers, residual stresses and constrained domain dynamics9–11. For textured ceramics with large grains, their ferroelectric domain size (domain size is proportional to grain size)12, becomes comparable to or larger than the thickness of the damaged surface layer introduced during mechanical thinning and polishing. As a result, domain switching and rotation are strongly impeded, leading to incomplete polarization and a pronounced reduction in piezoelectric response. Similar limitations have also been observed in relaxor-PbTiO3 single crystals with large domain structures, underscoring that domain size plays a decisive role in thickness-dependent electromechanical degradation11. Consequently, refining grain size offers a promising solution to this challenge. First, fine grains typically lead to smaller domain sizes that are less susceptible to be affected by defects compared with larger domain size13–15. Second, the stress distribution in fine-grain ceramics tends to be more homogeneous, which can alleviate the residual stress concentration induced from the surface damage layers16,17. These characteristics collectively enhance domain switching or rotation behavior during poling, enabling more effective polarization and better electromechanical responses in the ceramic sections with reduced thickness. Therefore, to fully exploit the performance of textured ceramics for high-frequency transducer applications, the grain size of textured ceramics is highly desired to reduce.

Achieving smaller grain size in textured ceramics is predominantly achieved by tailoring both the size and content of the templates, as they play a crucial role in determining the final microstructure of textured ceramics. Generally, the textured grain size (xT) satisfies the following relationship: xT=(6πfT)1/3, where fT is the ratio of templates content to the template size5. Based on this equation, reducing the template size or increasing its content can effectively decrease the grain size for textured ceramics. While increasing template content has been shown to suppress grain growth, this approach inevitably introduces trade-offs, excessive template content may deteriorate piezoelectric property of textured ceramics, since commonly used templates such as BaTiO3 (BTO) and SrTiO3 possess lower piezoelectric coefficients (d33) than that of the ceramic matrix. For instance, increasing BTO template content to 7 mol.% could reduce the grain size to 11 μm for 0.72Pb(Mg1/3Nb2/3)O3-0.28PbTiO3 (PMN-28PT) textured ceramics, but resulted in a significant drop (~45%) in d33 compared to the 1 mol.% counterpart18. Despite recent advances in template engineering, including the use of PbTiO3 templates in PMN-PT systems and alternative template-dissolution strategies (such as Ba(Zr, Ti)O3 in Pb(Zr, Ti)O3-based textured ceramics), it remains challenging to fabricate templates that are fully identical in composition to the ceramic matrix7,19,20. Consequently, the degradation of piezoelectric properties associated with increased template content is unlikely to be fundamentally resolved under the current template-engineering framework. In contrast, reducing template size offers a more attractive pathway toward grain refinement without sacrificing intrinsic piezoelectric performance, for example, reducing the template size from 50 to 8 μm has enabled a decrease in grain size from 150 to 10 μm in PMN-PT textured ceramics21–23. Nevertheless, despite extensive efforts over the past decade, the grain size of textured piezoelectric ceramics reported to date has rarely fallen below ~10 μm. This persistent limitation stems largely from the inherent constraints of traditional topochemical microcrystal conversion method, in which template coarsening during molten-salt treatment is difficult to suppress while maintaining crystallographic orientation and phase purity24. In addition, we noticed that relatively few studies have systematically investigated the influence of template size in textured piezoelectric ceramics. For instance, Zata et al. employed a mixture of conventional-size and fragmented templates to fabricate Pb(Mg1/3Nb2/3)O3-PbZrO3-PbTiO3 textured ceramics; however, a systematic correlation between grain size and electrical properties was not established25.

In this work, we report a route to solve this long-standing problem by developing reduced-size BTO templates with an average length of 2.7 μm using a modified topochemical microcrystal conversion method. These developed templates are substantially smaller than their counterparts through the conventional method (~7.8 μm) and enable the fabrication of <001>-textured PMN-30PT ceramics with grain sizes reduced by more than 50%. In contrast to conventional-grain-size textured ceramics, which suffer a pronounced degradation upon thinning, our reduced-grain-size textured ceramics exhibit markedly enhanced domain switching and rotation capabilities, effectively mitigating thickness scaling effects. As a result, a high piezoelectric coefficient d33 exceeding 1100 pC N-1 is retained at a thickness of 75 μm, corresponding to only a 12% decrease, compared with a ~35% reduction of the conventional-grain-size counterpart. The potential application of this reduced grain size textured ceramics for high-frequency ultrasonic transducers is further demonstrated by measuring the electrostrains at reduced thicknesses.

Results

Preparation of textured ceramics with reduced grain size

A modified topochemical microcrystal conversion method was used to synthesize reduced-size templates, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Molecular dynamics simulations suggest that suppressing ionic diffusion during molten salt synthesis is crucial to limit excessive seed growth24,26. Accordingly, two strategies were implemented in this study to suppress ionic diffusion by minimizing the amount of molten salt and reducing both the sintering temperature and duration. Firstly, to suppress crystal coarsening of Bi4Ti3O12 precursor microplates, the weight ratio of Bi2O3 + TiO2 to KCl was reduced to 1:1, which is significantly lower than the traditional ratio used previously (1:1.5). Furthermore, by modifying the sintering time and temperature (detailed in “Methods” section), we obtained the reduced-size template with an average length of 2.7 μm and a thickness of 0.32 μm (thickness data is listed in Supplementary Fig. 1). Using this reduced-size templates, we fabricated PMN-30PT textured ceramics with different template contents, named as “reduced-grain-size textured ceramics”. To investigate the influence of grain size on textured ceramic properties, we also prepared templates with an average length of 7.8 μm and a thickness of about 0.66 μm using a conventional topochemical microcrystal conversion method5,27 (detailed in “Methods” section). These textured ceramics are referred as “conventional-grain-size textured ceramics”.

Characterization of microstructure and electromechanical properties of textured ceramics

Figure 2a shows the relationship between the average textured grain size and template content for two types of templates, consistent with the relationship xT=(6πfT)1/3 reported in previous study6,22. As the template content increases from 0.3 to 3 mol.%, the grain size of reduced-grain-size textured ceramics decreases from 15.2 to 6.2 μm (close to the size of 4~6 μm for nontextured ceramics), while that of the conventional-grain-size textured ceramics decreases from 28.5 to 12.6 μm. The primary difference between these two is that the reduced-grain-size textured ceramics achieve grain sizes approximately 50% smaller than those of conventional-grain-size counterparts at the same template content. Figure 2b, c presents cross-section scanning electron microscope (SEM) images of these two with 1.2 mol.% template content, the additional microstructural and density data for other template contents are provided in Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 1. Both samples exhibit well-developed grains without excessively untextured grains, suggesting uniform and successful template-induced grain growth with high density. Compared to nontextured ceramics, textured ceramics exhibit markedly enhanced (001) and (002) diffraction peaks, as shown in Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 3, confirming a strong preferential growth along the (001) direction. At low template content (0.3 mol.%), the reduced-grain-size textured ceramic exhibits a higher Lotgering factor (F001), while when the template content exceeds 1.2 mol.%, the F001 factors exceed 99% for two different textured ceramics, as depicted in Fig. 2f. This implies that reduced-size templates disperse more uniformly in the matrix when the template content is relatively low, facilitating more efficient directional growth of the grains. Electron backscattered diffraction maps (EBSD, Fig. 2e, g) further confirm the excellent <001>-oriented texture alignment in both types of ceramics.

Figure 3a illustrates the variation of the piezoelectric coefficient d33 as a function of template content. Compared to the 340 pC N−1 of nontextured ceramic, both two types of textured ceramics reach approximately 1050 pC N−1 at 0.3 mol.% template content. As the template content increases, d33 continues to rise, reaching a maximum at 1.2 mol.%, being 1330 pC N−1 and 1310 pC N−1 for the reduced-grain-size and conventional-grain-size textured ceramics respectively. Beyond this point, d33 gradually decreases and stabilizes around 1180 pC N−1 at 3 mol.%. Likewise, the room-temperature dielectric permittivity ɛr also shows a maximum value at 1.2 mol.% template content, with values of 3850 and 3750 for two textured ceramics, respectively, as shown in Fig. 3b. The coercive field EC of textured ceramics are the same of 4.05 kV cm−1 (Supplementary Fig. 4), similar to that of nontextured ceramic, while higher than that of single crystals (EC ~ 2.5 kV cm−1)1, indicating improved operating voltage in practical applications. Meanwhile, the remanent polarization Pr of textured ceramics (~26.5 μC cm−2) is lower than that of nontextured ceramics (29.8 μC cm−2), this is because that due to the alignment of spontaneous polarization along the (111) direction in the rhombohedral phase, thus the polarization along (001) direction exhibits the lowest value among all other orientations28,29. Regarding electrostrains (Fig. 3c), both textured ceramics exhibit strain of approximately 0.4% under 30 kV cm−1 at 1.2 mol.% template content, representing a 250% increase over the nontextured ceramics. This increase in strain is consistent with the enhancement in d33 observed in Fig. 3a.

Figure 3d presents the temperature-dependent dielectric properties of PMN-30PT ceramics. For template content below 1.2 mol.%, the textured ceramics show a clear Trt (rhombohedral-tetragonal phase transition temperature) peak near 103 °C, resembling the behavior of single crystals30. Comparing templates of different sizes, it can be found in Fig. 3e that the Trt and TC (Curie temperature) of reduced-grain-size and conventional-grain-size samples are almost the same. Notably, compared with previously reported PMN-PT textured ceramics (detailed in Supplementary Fig. 5 and Supplementary Table 2), which typically exhibit grain sizes of 10~40 μm and corresponding d33 in the range of 780~1180 pC N−1, our reduced-grain-size textured ceramic demonstrates a smaller grain size while simultaneously achieving a higher piezoelectric coefficient, which is approximately 13% higher than previous, likely due to the improved crystallographic orientation and lower template content in our present work18,19,23,31–35. Moreover, Amorin et al. reported the fabrication of textured ceramics using templates with sizes ranging from 50 to 10 μm. However, their results indicated that a 27 μm size template yielded a higher piezoelectric coefficient than a 10 μm size template, which we believe was attributed to the reduced F001 associated with smaller template sizes in their work (35%~78%)36. These factors suggest that the microstructure in our samples more closely resembles that of single crystals, thereby contributing to the enhanced piezoelectric and dielectric properties.

Factors affecting piezoelectric activity of textured PMN-PT ceramics

Previous studies on textured piezoelectric ceramics have shown that variations in grain size can influence piezoelectric properties; however, such effects are typically achieved by increasing the template content, which introduces additional variables and complicates the systematic investigation of factors governing piezoelectric performance. Since we propose an alternative strategy in this work, that is, reducing the template size to achieve grain size refinement, thereby enabling a more comprehensive discussion of the underlying mechanisms. As summarized in Fig. 4a, the piezoelectric properties of textured ceramics fabricated with the templates with two different sizes are nearly identical when the template contents are the same. Therefore, we conclude that template content is the primary factor influencing the piezoelectric and dielectric properties of textured ceramics.

Once the template content is fixed, the influence of grain size on piezoelectric and dielectric performances can be explored. Typically, under the assumption of constant grain boundary thickness, reducing grain size could increase the volume fraction of grain boundaries. Given the amorphous nature of grain boundaries, an increased grain boundary fraction is generally detrimental to piezoelectric and dielectric performance. To examine this, impedance spectroscopy was performed for qualitative analysis. As shown in Fig. 4b, c, for samples with 1.2 mol.% template content (which gives the highest piezoelectric performance), we measured |Z|-θ curves across different temperatures for both reduced-grain-size and conventional-grain-size textured ceramics, then normalized and fitted the data. The results showed that an Arrhenius plot of the temperature dependance of the bulk conductivity σ, fitting well to be divided into two parts, confirming that the influence of the two parts on electrical properties at high temperatures was successfully separated.

For the grain region, in Fig. 4d, the activation energies for conduction Ea within the grain region are 1.08 and 1.07 eV for two samples, indicating identical grain conductivity. However, the Ea for the grain boundary region of the reduced-grain-size textured ceramic is 20% higher than that of the conventional-grain-size one (Fig. 4e), suggesting that grain boundaries in reduced-grain-size textured ceramic are more resistive to electron conduction37,38. This implies a higher volume fraction of amorphous grain boundaries in the reduced-grain-size textured ceramic. However, the grain boundary regions are generally known to adversely affect piezoelectric performance, thus a key question arises: why do its piezoelectric/dielectric properties show no significant difference compared to the conventional-grain-size textured ceramic? To address this issue, we provide three experiments, as discussed in the following.

First, Fig. 5a, b shows the Rayleigh analysis results for reduced-grain-size and conventional-grain-size textured ceramics. Small-signal hysteresis loops were measured under various electric field amplitudes to determine the piezoelectric response, which is subsequently divided into intrinsic and extrinsic contributions based on the relationship SE=(dinit+αE0)±α(E02−E2)/2, (a detailed description of Rayleigh analysis is listed in the Supplementary Note 1). The extrinsic response of the reduced one is 179E0, slightly higher than the 92E0 of conventional one. At an applied electric field of 1.5 kV cm−1, the ratio of extrinsic contribution to total piezoelectric response αE0/(αE0+dinit) for reduced-grain-size textured ceramic is 18.3%, compared with 9.9% in conventional counterpart, indicating a markedly stronger contribution from extrinsic mechanisms in small grain size. Since the chemical compositions of both textured ceramics are identical (PMN-30PT with 1.2 mol.% template content), the enhanced extrinsic response in reduced-grain-size textured ceramic can be attributed to the stronger domain wall motion39. Additionally, in Fig. 5c, piezoresponse force microscopy (PFM) further confirms that reduced-grain-size textured ceramic has a stronger domain wall switching ability when compared to the conventional one (threshold voltage for former is ±8 V, much lower than ±16 V for the latter). Moreover, through a.c. poling (Supplementary Fig. 6), we found that reduced-grain-size textured ceramic achieves a d33 of 1330 pC N−1 after one cycle and stabilizes at 1360 pC N−1. In contrast, the conventional-grain-size one reaches only 1250 pC N−1 after the first cycle and requires three cycles before its impedance phase angle surpasses 89°, at which d33 stabilizes at 1350 pC N−1. This suggests that reduced grains may facilitate the polarization switching process.

In conclusion, although reduced-grain-size textured ceramics have a larger volume fraction of grain boundaries, which is unfavorable for piezoelectric performance, they exhibit comparable piezoelectric and dielectric properties to conventional-grain-size counterparts. This is primarily due to the enhanced domain wall motion or domain switching ability in the reduced-grain-size textured ceramics.

Thickness dependent properties of PMN-30PT textured ceramics

To verify the potential feasibility of reduced-grain-size textured ceramic in high-frequency ultrasound transducers, the thickness scaling effects on textured PMN-30PT ceramics were investigated. We selected reduced-grain-size textured ceramic with 1.2 mol.% template content as our experimental subjects, which exhibited d33 of 1330 pC N−1 at a thickness of 500 μm. Meanwhile, conventional-grain-size textured ceramic with a d33 of 1310 pC N−1 was used as a comparison. Both samples were mechanically thinned from 500 to 75 μm by the same procedure. Notably, the thickness of the surface-damaged layer induced by the thinning process is estimated to be in the range of 0.3~1.0 μm (see Supplementary Note 2 for details), the corresponding impacts on electric performance are discussed in a subsequent section.

As shown in Fig. 6a, the d33 value of the conventional-grain-size textured ceramics begins to decline at 200 μm and drops to 880 pC N−1 at 75 μm (a 34.6% decrease). In contrast, the reduced-grain-size textured ceramics maintain stable performance at the thickness down to 125 μm and retain 1130 pC N−1 at 75 μm, corresponding to only 12.8% decrease. A similar trend is observed in ɛr (Fig. 6b), where a slight decrease occurs at the thickness of 125 μm for the reduced-grain-size textured ceramic, while conventional-grain-size counterpart shows an obvious decline at 200 μm. At 75 μm, compared with the conventional-grain-size one, whose ɛr decrease by 18.9%, the reduced-grain-size textured ceramic only decreases 11.8%, indicating well stability in dielectric performance. Meanwhile, the thickness dependence of single crystal electromechanical performance as reported by Lee et al. was also examined; the relevant data were extracted and incorporated into Fig. 6b. The results indicate that PMN-PT single crystals exhibit a more pronounced degradation in performance with decreasing thickness (ɛr decrease by 34.8% at 75 μm), which has been attributed to its larger domain wall size. In contrast, ternary Pb(In1/2Nb1/2)O3-Pb(Mg1/3Nb2/3)O3-PbTiO3 single crystals with finer domain wall width can effectively mitigate this behavior11. This conclusion is further supported by the findings of Qiu et al. and is consistent with the enhanced domain wall motion or domain switching ability observed in our reduced-grain-size textured ceramic, which arises from its reduced grain size40. Additionally, the dielectric loss of reduced-grain-size textured ceramic at 75 μm is only 0.8% (no significantly different from that of 0.6~0.8% under 500 μm), whereas the conventional-grain-size textured ceramic reaches 1.8%. We believe that this increase in dielectric loss in conventional-grain-size sample arises from the clamping effect imposed by the surface damage layers, which is considered the primary factor responsible for degradation of sample performance. P-E loops at different thicknesses reveal further distinctions (Fig. 6c, d), polarization of the reduced-grain-size textured ceramic maintains nearly unchanged across thicknesses, while that of the conventional-grain-size counterpart decreased from 26.5 μC cm−2 at 500 μm to 21.3 μC cm−2 at 75 μm. These indicate that the thickness scaling effect is more significant for the samples with larger grain size. In conventional-grain-size textured ceramic, the larger grains exacerbate the hindrance of polarization rotation and domain switching by surface defects, which makes the sample not fully poled, thus amplifying performance degradation. In contrast, small grains are more resistant to such effects.

Moreover, at the same thickness, the conventional-grain-size textured ceramic generally shows a lower maximum of dielectric permittivity ɛmax than the reduced one, especially at 75 μm in Fig. 6e. As shown in Fig. 6f, with decreasing thickness, electromechanical coupling factor k33 of the reduced-grain-size textured ceramic remains stable, fluctuating only slightly between 90.17% to 89.76%. In contrast, the k33 of conventional ones shows a decrease from 89.63% to 84.74%. This is consistent with the observed decrease in d33 and Pr and suggests clamping of domain walls motion is evident. In addition, reduced-grain-size textured ceramic exhibits a more stable kt (shown in Supplementary Fig. 7), while the conventional one shows a slight decrease, consistent with the trend of nontextured ceramic. Since kt is directly influenced by the resonance frequency, which is governed by the elastic modulus and thickness, a stable kt in the reduced-grain-size sample implies consistent elastic properties and minimal clamping/pinning of domain wall, even at the thicknesses of 75 μm. Therefore, with the smaller domain size and stronger domain wall motion, reduced-grain-size textured ceramics show a more stable properties, with minimal influence from thickness scaling effects.

Further differences in electric field-induced strains verify the advantage of reduced-grain-size textured ceramics. Firstly, it is important to note that due to potential measurement errors with commercial strain test system at thinner samples, bipolar S-E loops may exhibit different shapes when positive and negative voltages are applied41,42. To mitigate this, we first checked the bipolar S-E loops (Supplementary Fig. 8), no such discrepancies were observed for electrostrictive strain at 75 μm, indicating that measurement errors are negligible in our data.

As shown in Fig. 7a, b, the electric field-induced strains in the reduced-grain-size textured ceramics remain constant at approximately 0.42% under 30 kV cm−1, regardless of thickness. In contrast, conventional-grain-size samples exhibit increased strain at 200 μm, with thinner samples showing larger strain, stabilizing around 0.59% between 125 and 75 μm (about a 50% increase compared to 500 μm). Furthermore, an unexpected variability in strain is observed in conventional-grain-size samples. With decreasing thickness, the proportion of the surface damage layer increases (assuming its absolute thickness remains unchanged during the thinning process), thereby making it likely to affect the performance of the samples. For conventional-grain-size textured ceramic, the smaller proportion of grain boundaries may lead to a decrease in its mechanical strength. Moreover, defects in the surface damage layer are easier to pin larger domain walls, hindering the release of residual stress, as illustrated schematically in Fig. 7c. Discussion

In this study, we proposed a grain-size reduction strategy for <001>-oriented PMN-30PT textured ceramics, achieving a substantially reduced average grain size of 7.8 μm, significantly smaller than that of previously reported textured piezoelectric ceramics, while maintaining a high d33 value of 1330 pC N−1. Compared with conventional-grain-size textured ceramics counterparts, which typically suffer from pronounced degradation of dielectric and piezoelectric properties upon mechanical thinning because of the thickness scaling effect, our reduced-grain-size textured ceramics exhibit markedly electric performance stability. This improvement is primarily attributed to the enhanced domain switching and/or rotation ability in reduced-grain-size textured ceramic, making it could be easily polarized after mechanical thinning and thus maintaining stable dielectric/piezoelectric properties at reduced thicknesses. This is particularly beneficial for the development of compact, high-frequency piezoelectric transducers, where thinner active layers are critical for achieving higher operating frequency and enhanced resolution. As a result, these demonstrate that grain-size engineering provides an effective route to decouple high piezoelectric performance from thickness-induced limitations, enabling the miniaturization of textured piezoelectric ceramics without sacrificing functional properties, which is an essential requirement for high-frequency ultrasonic transducers that rely on thin active layers to achieve improved resolution and bandwidth.

Based on these findings, the microstructural design strategy presented in this work is expected to be readily extendable to emerging ternary/quaternary relaxor-PbTiO3-based piezoelectric textured ceramics (such as Pb(In1/2Nb1/2)O3-Pb(Mg1/3Nb2/3)O3-PbTiO3, Pb(Mg1/3Nb2/3)O3-PbZrO3-PbTiO3, Pb(In1/2Nb1/2)O3-Pb(Sc1/2Nb1/2)O3-PbTiO3), which typically possess higher Curie temperatures and finer ferroelectric domain structures. By combining these intrinsic material advantages with the present grain-size refinement approach, further thinning of piezoelectric layers is anticipated while maintaining dielectric and piezoelectric responses. Overall, we believe such synergy highlights the pivotal role of microstructural engineering in enabling both miniaturization and high-performance operation of piezoelectric devices, offering a promising route for incorporating textured ceramics into next-generation ultrasonic transducers.

Methods

Material preparation

Preparation of templates and textured ceramics: The conventional-size <001>-oriented BTO templates, synthesized via the topochemical microcrystal conversion method, following by these reaction formulas:

2Bi2O3+3TiO2→Bi4Ti3O12

Bi4Ti3O12+BaCO3→BaTiO3+Bi2O3+CO2

Firstly, raw materials Bi2O3, TiO2 and KCl were mixed (the weight ration of Bi2O3 + TiO2 to KCl was 1:1.5) and placed in Al2O3 crucible for 4 h at 1080 °C. The obtained molten salt was cleaned with de-ionized water, and an AgNO3 solution is used to detect whether there is residual Cl-, then dried to obtain the Bi4Ti3O12 precursor. In the second step, Bi4Ti3O12/BaCO3 precursors with a molar ratio of 1:10 were mixed with NaCl and KCl (1:1 molar ratio) and sintered at 1000 °C for 2 h. The resulting product was washed with hot deionized water and diluted acid to obtain <001>-oriented BTO templates. As for reduced-size BTO templates, the weight ration of Bi2O3 + TiO2 to KCl was reduced to 1:1, and the reaction condition was also decreased to 950 °C for 2 h; in the second step, the sintering temperature was reduced to 980 °C.

The PMN-30PT powder was prepared through a two-step traditional solid-state reaction. First, MgO and Nb2O5 powders were ball-milled for 24 h and then calcined at 1200 °C for 6 h to obtain pure MgNb2O6. Second, Pb3O4, MgNb2O6 and TiO2 powders were mixed by ball milling for 6 h, followed by calcination at 850 °C for 2 h to yield pure PMN-30PT matrix powder. Finally, the sintered powder was ball milling for 12 h and subjected to high-energy milling to achieve a uniform fine particle size, less than 150 nm (Supplementary Fig. 10).

For the tape casting, the slurry was prepared using PMN-30PT matrix powder, BTO templates (0.3, 0.6, 0.9, 1.2, 1.5, 1.8, 3 mol.% content), CuO (0.25 wt.%), ethanol/xylene co-solvents (30 wt.%), KD-1 dispersant (2.5 wt.%), polyvinyl butyral binder (2 wt.%), and polyalkylene glycol/butyl benzyl phthalate plasticizer (1.2 wt.%). After 24 h of ball milling, the slurry was tape-cast at a rate of 0.7 cm s−1 using a stainless-steel blade with a thickness of 120 μm for reduced-grain-size textured ceramics, and 220 μm for conventional-grain-size textured ceramics. The dried green tapes were cut, stacked and uniaxially pressed at a pressure of 30 MPa at 70 °C for 30 min. The green pellets were preheated in air to 600 °C for 2 h to remove the binders, then sintered into a PbO-rich atmosphere at 1200~1230 °C for 3~5 h to obtain textured samples. Notably, textured ceramic samples with diameter of 7 mm and thickness of 0.5, 0.3, 0.2, 0.15, 0.1, 0.075 mm were used for electrical measurement.

Material characterization

Phase structures were detected using X-ray diffraction (Smartlab, Rigaku, Japan) with Cu Kα1 radiation, confirming the formation of perovskite-type phases with no evidence of any impurities. Data were collected in the 2θ range of 15–75° with a step of 0.02°. The microstructures of the templates and textured ceramics were imaged using scanning electron microscopy (TM3030plus, Hitachi, Japan). The cross-sections SEM images of sintered samples were obtained by annealed at 1050 °C for 20 min in a PbO-rich atmosphere. Grain size and the distribution were statistically analyzed using ImageJ software. The average grain size and the distribution were determined from at least 100 randomly selected grains. Briefly, three representative regions within the same sample were imaged to evaluate the microstructural grain size, and the averaged results are summarized in Fig. 2a. Electron backscattered diffraction (Oxford EBSD System, UK) were employed to characterize the grain orientation of the textured ceramics.

Dielectric properties were measured on pellets with silver electrodes printed and fired at 600 °C for 20 min, then samples were poled under electric field of 30 kV cm−1 for 3 min at room temperature. The piezoelectric coefficient (d33) was measured using a quasistatic piezo-d33 meter (ZJ-6AN, Institute of Acoustics, China). The temperature-dependence of the dielectric behavior was measured using an LCR meter (E4980AL, Keysight, USA), connected to a computer-controlled temperature chamber, from room temperature to 200 °C with 2 °C min−1 heating rate. Hysteresis loops and related data were obtained through a ferroelectric analyzer (TF analyzer 2000E, aixACCT, Germany). |Z|-θ test at varying temperature was measured using a precision impedance analyzer (4294A, Agilent, USA) in the frequency range of 100 Hz to 3 MHz, connected to a custom-made chamber. The domain of polarity structures was observed with the PFM mode of an atomic force microscope (MFP-3D, Oxford Instruments, USA) in the air environment. In vector piezoresponse force microscope (VPFM) characterization, the vertical applied a.c. drive amplitude was 0.5 V, with a corresponding drive frequency of approximately 340 kHz. And the surface topography information maps of the samples were listed in Supplementary Fig. 11.
