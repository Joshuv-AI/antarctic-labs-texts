The effect of mixed fatigue on knee biomechanics and muscle activation during sidestep cutting in elite soccer players
Authors: Kuiyu Chen, Dong Sun, Diwei Chen, Bojie Xuan, Dongxu Wang, Yang Song, Xuanzhen Cen, Monem Jemni, Gusztav Fekete, Yaodong Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFootball is one of the most popular sports in the world, and it is also a sport with a high rate of injury. The study aims to investigate the effects of physical and mental mixed fatigue (PMF) on knee biomechanics during sidestep cutting maneuvers in elite male soccer players, thereby assessing the potential mechanisms underlying non-contact knee injuries.MethodsThirty-six elite male soccer players were recruited (age: 21.61 ± 1.22 years; body mass: 75.16 ± 6.34 kg; height: 175.8 ± 3.53 cm; shoe size: 41–44 EUR). Following a targeted fatigue induction protocol, key lower limb biomechanical data were acquired during anticipated sidestep cutting maneuvers both pre- and post-PMF. Statistical analyses were performed utilizing paired sample t-tests and one-dimensional Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM1d).ResultsFollowing PMF, knee valgus increased at initial contact (P = 0.022). Kinetic analysis, supported by SPM1d, revealed a marked transition from an extensor-dominant to a flexor-dominant pattern in sagittal knee moments (P = 0.007), alongside elevated knee valgus moments (P = 0.039). Neuromuscularly, quadriceps and lateral gastrocnemius activation (iEMG/RMS) significantly decreased, whereas compensatory increases were observed in the hamstrings and medial gastrocnemius (all P < 0.001).ConclusionWhile PMF preserved most kinematics, the statistically significant increase in knee valgus, though small in magnitude, suggests an impaired frontal-plane control that may elevate Anterior Cruciate Ligament (ACL) strain. The shift from quadriceps to hamstring dominance reflects a compensatory neuromuscular strategy. These findings emphasize the importance of incorporating cognitive load into injury-prevention programs and monitoring mental fatigue to reduce non-contact knee injury risks.

Introduction

In soccer and other contact ball sports, sidestep cutting is a movement involving rapid deceleration and re-adjusting the body’s spatial position. Sidestep cutting represents a prevalent sidestep cutting maneuver in soccer and constitutes a critical technical component for offensive penetration, defensive play, and ball interception [1]. While athletes may perform over 700 sidestep cutting during a single match [2], the frequency of these maneuvers is positively correlated with increased lower extremity joint loading and the incidence of non-contact injuries [3, 4]. Susceptibility to severe injuries, particularly Anterior Cruciate Ligament (ACL) ruptures, is heightened during rapid deceleration or landing phases when the knee is positioned near full extension [5].

Sustained competitive match-play results in the gradual accumulation of various forms of fatigue, thereby compromising technical performance and elevating the risk of injury. Physical fatigue is widely documented to alter lower extremity kinetics at initial contact, specifically shifting knee valgus and rotational moments towards high-risk patterns [6]. Conversely, mental fatigue impairs technical execution, characterized by the adoption of a more erect posture during sidestep cutting and heightened uncertainty in decision-making, potentially precipitating motor control errors [7–9]. Although the effects of physical or mental fatigue have been reported separately, most experiments often use a single fatigue source or unanticipated direction conditions, thereby failing to adequately replicate the complex motor environments characteristic of actual match-play.

In fact, elite athletes need to process a lot of tactical and environmental information while performing high-intensity running in matches, so they are often in a state where physical and cognitive loads coexist, known as physical and PMF(physical and mental mixed fatigue) [10, 11]. Preliminary evidence suggests that dual-task paradigms or combined fatigue states exacerbate knee valgus and rotational moments, thereby shifting joint mechanics towards high-risk profiles [6]. This explains that there may be a synergistic interference mechanism between physical and cognitive fatigue. In addition, compared with physical fatigue, mental fatigue affects motor control ability more significantly in unanticipated sidestep cutting situations [12, 13]. This further explains that single fatigue models may not be able to explain neuromuscular control methods under real conditions. Therefore, exploring the biomechanical characteristics of sidestep cutting under PMF conditions becomes an important research direction.

Employing a typical anticipated 45° sidestep cutting maneuver as the experimental paradigm, this study analyzed knee joint angles, moments, and angular velocities, concurrent with the acquisition of electromyographic (EMG) signals from major lower extremity muscles, to investigate the impact of PMF on key kinetic and neuromuscular control indices. We hypothesized that even during anticipated sidestep cutting maneuvers, PMF would induce a shift in knee kinetic parameters towards high-risk profiles. While external kinematic patterns may remain largely unaltered, internal joint kinetics and muscle activation strategies would exhibit characteristics distinct from isolated fatigue states, thereby elevating the potential risk of joint injury.

Methods

Participants

An a priori power analysis was performed using G*Power (version 3.1.9.7, Franz Faul) based on an effect size 𝑑 = 0.50, a significance level α = 0.05, and a statistical power(1 − β) of 0.80, yielding a required sample size of 34 participants [14]. Ultimately, thirty-six healthy male participants were recruited (age: 21.61 ± 1.22 years; body mass: 75.16 ± 6.34 kg; height: 175.8 ± 3.53 cm; shoe size: 41–44 EUR), all classified as National Level I Athletes in accordance with the Technical Classification Standards for Athletes (2025 Revision) issued by the General Administration of Sport of China (www.sport.gov.cn). All subjects had no severe lower limb injury within 6 months before the experiment, did not participate in vigorous exercise 24 h before the experiment, and had no other musculoskeletal or neurological diseases that might affect sports performance. Given the implementation of a fatigue induction protocol, participants were required to abstain from ingesting caffeine, nicotine, or alcohol for 24 h prior to testing. Prior to the study, all participants provided written informed consent following a comprehensive explanation of the objectives, procedures, and associated risks. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of Ningbo University (TY2025065).

Experimental protocol

Data collection was conducted within the controlled environment of the Biomechanics Laboratory at Ningbo University, with the ambient temperature maintained at 26 ± 1.5 °C. The experiment used a single-group pre-test and post-test design, including two stages: baseline test (Pre-test) and post-fatigue test (Post-test). Prior to data collection, all participants were confirmed to be right-hand dominant, with the right leg identified as the preferred kicking limb and the left leg as the preferred stance limb. The non-dominant (stance) limb was selected for analysis, as it typically bears greater support and braking loads during sidestep cutting, thereby incurring a higher risk of injury [15].Given that all participants were right-dominant and performed 45° sidestep cuts to the right, data were acquired from the left (stance) limb, consistent with the concept of cross-symmetry [16].

Prior to each data collection session, participants were outfitted with standardized tight-fitting attire and artificial turf (TF) soccer shoes provided by the laboratory, and received comprehensive instruction regarding task execution and safety protocols. The formal test process is shown in Figure (Fig. 1), and subjects first performed a 5-minute standardized warm-up on a treadmill. Subsequently, subjects were instrumented with 38 retro-reflective markers and 8 sEMG sensors (Fig. 1A and B). Anatomical markers, including the sternum, V.Sacral, and bilateral ASIS, were used to define the torso and pelvis segments. To determine joint centers and axes, medial and lateral markers were placed on the femoral epicondyles (Knee.Lat/Med) and malleoli (Ankle.Lat/Med). Segment motion during dynamic trials was tracked using non-collinear clusters (comprising Upper, Front, and Rear markers) positioned on the lateral aspect of the thighs and shanks. This configuration was specifically designed to minimize soft tissue artifacts (STA) during high-intensity maneuvers. The foot was modeled using a multi-point set (Heel, Midfoot, and Toe markers) to accurately capture foot-ground interactions. All markers were synchronized with the 2392 musculoskeletal model for subsequent simulation [17]. To minimize experimental error and ensure marker stability, all markers were placed by a single experienced investigator. For locations where markers were attached over tight-fitting compression garments, high-elasticity fabric was selected to ensure a snug fit to the skin, and all markers were further secured with medical-grade adhesive wrap to prevent displacement due to garment motion during the task.

Participants were instructed to execute the sidestep cutting maneuver along a 10-m runway, maintaining an approach velocity of 4.5 ± 0.2 m/s as monitored by timing gates (Fig. 1C) [18, 19]. Upon completion of the baseline assessment, participants underwent the fatigue induction protocol until predefined fatigue criteria were met (Fig. 1D); subsequently, they immediately replicated the identical sidestep cutting task under fatigued conditions.

Fatigue induction protocol

During the latter stages of match-play, tactical decision-making depletes cognitive resources; thus, subsequent physical fatigue accumulates against a backdrop of cognitive impairment. This aligns with previous literature, which indicates that mental fatigue precipitates the onset of physical fatigue by elevating the rating of perceived exertion (RPE) [20]. Therefore, in this study, PMF is defined as a composite fatigue state characterized by the superimposition of physical load upon either sustained cognitive load or the prior depletion of cognitive resources.

The study utilized a mixed fatigue induction protocol comprising time-constrained dual-tasks to induce mental fatigue and Burpee exercises to target physical fatigue.

The Time Load Dual Back (TLDB) task was implemented to induce mental fatigue (MF), as Staiano et al. [21] demonstrated that a shorter-duration, adaptive TLDB protocol exhibits superior efficacy in inducing MF compared to the classic Stroop task. The protocol entailed the alternating execution of a primary n-back memory task and a secondary odd/even number judgment task, lasting 15 min, during which participants were instructed to perform at maximum capacity. Subjective mental fatigue was assessed via the National Aeronautics and Space Administration Task Load Index (NASA-TLX) [22].

The Burpee task consists of a compound whole-body movement sequence involving a transition from a standing stance to a squat thrust, followed by a push-up, and culminating in a vertical jump from the squat position. This exercise is capable of eliciting rapid and substantial physiological responses; notably, acute neuromuscular fatigue indices suggest that the Burpee protocol imposes a greater physiological load and necessitates higher perceived exertion compared to sprint tasks [23]. Continuous verbal encouragement was provided by the research team throughout the protocol, instructing participants to execute the maximum possible number of Burpee repetitions within a 90-second interval. Throughout the induction protocol, fatigue levels were monitored utilizing a Polar H9 heart rate sensor (Polar Electro Oy, Kempele, Finland) and the Borg Rating of Perceived Exertion (RPE) scale. Upon conclusion of the 90-second interval or in instances of technical failure, participants were instructed to cease exercise and immediately reported their subjective mental fatigue and perceived exertion levels [14] (Table 1).

Data acquisition

Motion data were collected by a 10-camera Vicon 3D motion capture system (Oxford Metrics Ltd., Oxford, UK) at a sampling frequency of 200 Hz, tracking the movement trajectories of 38 reflective markers on the subjects. Ground reaction force (GRF) data were collected synchronously by Kistler 3D force platform (Kistler Instrumente AG, Winterthur, Switzerland) at 1000 Hz sampling frequency, recording changes of GRF in sagittal, coronal, and horizontal three directions.

sEMG signals were recorded from eight target muscles: the rectus femoris (RF), vastus medialis (VM), vastus lateralis (VL), biceps femoris (BF), tibialis anterior (TA), medial gastrocnemius (MG), lateral gastrocnemius (LG), and semitendinosus (ST). Prior to electrode attachment, skin preparation involving shaving and cleaning with 75% alcohol was performed to minimize impedance; sEMG signals were subsequently acquired via the EMGworks system (Delsys, Boston, USA) at 2000 Hz, with sensors aligned parallel to the muscle fiber orientation.

For each trial, the stance limb (left) was required to make full contact with the force platform; ultimately, five valid data were retained for subsequent analysis.

Data analysis

Data were processed in Visual 3D (C-Motion, Germantown. Raw EMG signals were band-pass filtered (10–450 Hz) using a zero-lag Butterworth filter to minimize artifacts, followed by full-wave rectification. Following rectification, signals were smoothed using a low-pass filter with a 5 Hz cutoff frequency to generate a linear envelope. The stance phase was defined as the interval extending from initial contact (IC) to toe-off (TO) of the left limb. Contact events were identified based on a vertical ground reaction force (vGRF) threshold of ≥ 20 N acquired from the force platform [24]. EMG normalization employed a dynamic task-specific method, utilizing the pre-fatigue peak activation as the baseline. Conducted within a single session with unchanged electrode placement, this approach allowed for the precise quantification of relative percentage changes in muscle activation post-fatigue.

Joint angles were calculated using a Cardan X-Y-Z rotation sequence, corresponding to the medial-lateral, anterior-posterior, and vertical axes, respectively. Ankle, hip, and knee joint angles were defined as the orientation of the distal segment relative to the proximal segment. The pelvis was defined utilizing the CODA model, with pelvis and trunk angles computed with respect to the global coordinate system; internal joint moments were resolved into the proximal segment coordinate system. The analysis focused primarily on kinematic, kinetic, and sEMG data acquired during the stance phase (extending from initial contact to toe-off). Outcome measures included: (1) Kinematics: knee angles at initial contact, peak knee angles, knee angles at the instant of peak lateral ground reaction force (LGRF), knee range of motion (ROM), and tri-planar angular velocities. (2) Kinetics: peak tri-planar ground reaction forces (GRF), peak knee joint moments, knee joint moments at the instant of peak LGRF, and Tri-planar Knee Joint Reaction Forces (JRFs). To control for multiple comparisons across discrete biomechanical and mean EMG variables, a Bonferroni correction was utilized, adjusting the alpha level to α = 0.05/n (n= number of dependent variables).

In accordance with prior literature, knee flexion, valgus, and external rotation were designated as negative (-), whereas extension, varus, and internal rotation were defined as positive (+) [25].

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (M ± SD). All discrete variables were first tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test; subsequently, paired sample t-tests were utilized to evaluate differences between pre- and post-fatigue conditions. Statistical analysis for discrete variables was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 27, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). For time-series data, paired t-tests using one-dimensional Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM1d) were implemented in Python (version 3.13.0, Python Software Foundation) to compare knee angles, moments, and joint reaction force trajectories across the 0–100% stance phase. Statistical significance was established a priori at α = 0.05 with a target effect size of d = 0.50.

Figure 2 depicts the SPM1d analysis of knee joint angles pre- and post-PMF. In the sagittal plane, no supra-threshold clusters were identified (P > 0.05); however, knee flexion angles exhibited a non-significant reduction throughout the entire stance phase following fatigue. In the frontal plane, a significant supra-threshold cluster was identified spanning 0%-78% of the stance phase (P < 0.001), and the knee valgus angles were significantly greater post-fatigue compared to pre-fatigue values. In the transverse plane, no significant supra-threshold clusters were detected (P > 0.05); however, the overall trend indicated that knee internal rotation angles were marginally greater post-fatigue compared to the pre-fatigue condition.

EMG activity

The iEMG analysis (Table 5) revealed that fatigue significantly altered lower extremity muscle activation strategies during the sidestep cutting maneuver. Under PMF conditions, iEMG values for the rectus femoris (RF), vastus medialis (VM), and lateral gastrocnemius (LG) exhibited significant reductions (all P < 0.001), while the remaining muscles showed no significant changes (P > 0.05). RMS analysis (Fig. 3) indicated that activation levels of the vastus lateralis (VL), lateral gastrocnemius (LG), and vastus medialis (VM) were significantly reduced post-fatigue (VL:P = 0.004; LG: P = 0.011; VM: P < 0.001). Conversely, the semitendinosus (ST) and medial gastrocnemius (MG) demonstrated significantly increased activation levels (ST:P = 0.039;MG:P = 0.043).

Discussion

This study investigated the impact of PMF on knee joint kinematics, kinetics, and muscle activation strategies in elite athletes during a 45° sidestep cutting maneuver. The findings partially supported our hypothesis: while external kinematic patterns remained largely unaltered during sidestep cutting under PMF, distinct alterations were observed in both internal joint kinetics and lower extremity neuromuscular control strategies. These alterations potentially elevate the risk of non-contact lower extremity injuries, particularly involving the knee joint.

Impact on kinematics

The results indicated that under PMF conditions, kinematic alterations of the knee joint were predominantly concentrated within the frontal plane. Key kinematic indicators such as sagittal knee flexion angle and coronal valgus/varus angle can sensitively indicate potential limb injury risk, and in the cushioning and landing stage, the degree of sagittal knee flexion is positively correlated with the energy absorption of ground reaction forces [26]. Although sagittal plane knee angles did not yield statistically significant differences, mean flexion angles exhibited an overall decreasing trend, characterized by a more erect, extended posture at IC; This coupled kinematic profile, characterized by sagittal extension and frontal plane valgus, is frequently associated with compromised shock absorption capacity and a shift in joint line loading distributions [26]. Consequently, we postulate that under PMF conditions, athletes may be exposed to an elevated risk of knee injury at the instant of IC. Furthermore, SPM1d analysis of the knee valgus angle revealed a significant supra-threshold cluster spanning 0%-78% of the stance phase. This suggests that PMF may induce a sustained deviation in frontal plane kinematics throughout the early-to-mid stance phase. Furthermore, under PMF conditions, increases were observed in both frontal plane valgus and transverse plane external rotation angular velocities, accompanied by a significant delay in the timing of peak internal rotation angular velocity. These results are similar to existing research results [6–9].

These observed phenomena may be attributed to diminished quadriceps recruitment following PMF, which subsequently contributed to increased knee valgus angles, indicative of a compromised ability of the musculoskeletal system to maintain joint stability [27, 28]. Ultimately, this decline in dynamic knee stability, coupled with the rapid increase in valgus angular velocity, heightens the potential risk of knee joint injury [29, 30]. From current literature, in terms of kinematics, the influence of fatigue on sidestep cutting is complex and results are inconsistent; some studies show fatigue increases knee valgus and internal rotation angles, but has no effect on flexion degree [31–33]; There are also studies finding that fatigue decreased flexion angles and internal rotation angles [34, 35]. This study believes that different fatigue modes and fatigue induction methods may be one of the reasons for these result differences. The physical fatigue induction method adopted in this study may have stimulated the quadriceps to a greater extent, thus forming the research results of this experiment.

Clinically, even minimal deviations are significant: during landing, every 1° increase in valgus angle elevates ACL load by 10–15%. Surpassing critical thresholds may amplify the risk of non-contact injury by roughly 6.7-fold [36]. Thus, these angular shifts suggest that PMF drives athletes closer to injury-prone biomechanics, implicitly signaling a heightened potential for non-contact injuries [37].

Impact on kinetics

Kinetic analysis revealed that following the PMF intervention, the sagittal plane knee moment shifted from an extensor-dominant pattern to a significant flexor-dominant moment. In conjunction with the SPM1d analysis of knee flexion-extension moments (Fig. 2d), we postulate that this shift is attributable to quadriceps fatigue-induced deficits in recruitment, necessitating a compensatory increase in hamstring force production to preserve knee joint stability [38]. This is consistent with the trend revealed by the aforementioned kinematic results. In addition, the significant increase in the frontal plane valgus moment after PMF suggests that the knee joint bore a greater valgus moment load [39, 40]. However, the absence of statistically significant differences in peak GRF magnitudes suggests that PMF did not fundamentally alter the gross kinetic characteristics of the sidestep maneuver, implying an attempt by participants to preserve motor consistency under fatigued conditions; nevertheless, significant alterations were evident in lateral shear joint reaction forces during the early stance phase (0–20%). Early-phase GRFs and JRFs, particularly lateral shear forces, are critically linked to dynamic limb stability and braking capacity during the landing phase [41]. The reduction in shear forces implies that under PMF conditions, athletes adopted a more cautious landing strategy [41]. However, this observation stands in contrast to the aforementioned kinematic results, which indicated a significantly more erect posture. This paradoxical pattern likely stems from PMF-induced dual deficits in muscle activation and neuromuscular control, resulting in a conflicting motor strategy characterized by a dichotomy between subjective caution and an objectively erect posture.

Given the identical motor task where external loading remained consistent, the observed redistribution of joint moments and forces implies that PMF primarily altered the strategy of load absorption and transfer across the joints, rather than merely modifying the magnitude of the external load itself. Similarly, Dempsey et al. [42] postulated that under fatigue conditions, compromised synergy between proximal joints and knee musculature typically manifests as elevated loading moments within the frontal and transverse planes. Our kinematic and kinetic findings align with those of Belkhelladi et al. [43], suggesting that increased biomechanical loading at the knee is intrinsically associated with reduced knee flexion angles.

These findings are of significant clinical relevance, given that increased knee valgus loading is a well-established predictor of non-contact ACL injury risk in athletic populations [43]. Prospective research demonstrates that athletes who went on to suffer ACL injury displayed significantly elevated valgus moments—in some cases, up to 2.5-fold greater than uninjured controls [36]. The augmented knee valgus angle during PMF indicates a potential impairment in the medial musculature’s capacity to resist external valgus moments, necessitating a shift in dependence towards passive ligamentous restraints.

Impact on neuromuscular control

Variations in sEMG indices indicate that PMF induced a shift in sidestep cutting control strategies: from a pattern characterized by knee extensor (RF, VM) and lateral plantarflexor (LG) predominance, towards a reliance on hamstrings (BF, ST) and medial ankle musculature (MG, TA). A generalized reduction in activation was observed across the quadriceps (RF, VM, VL) under fatigued conditions, consistent with Wang et al. [44], who indicated that fatigue compromises both quadriceps contractile capacity and neural drive. Given the pivotal role of the quadriceps in the deceleration phase and in maintaining sagittal and frontal plane stability, the observed reduction in activation is identified as a primary biomechanical factor contributing to the compromise of dynamic knee stability [43–45]. In an attempt to preserve stability, athletes may exhibit compensatory hyper-activation of alternative muscle groups, a strategy that potentially exacerbates biomechanical loading on the lower extremity joints. Specifically, the observed increase in semitendinosus (ST) and medial gastrocnemius (MG) activation reflects, to some extent, a shift in lower extremity compensatory recruitment patterns during sidestep cutting under PMF. In the presence of quadriceps fatigue, hamstring contraction is hypothesized to increase in order to constrain anterior tibial translation and mitigate excessive anterior knee shear forces [46]. This suggests that under PMF conditions, athletes employ this compensatory pattern as a protective mechanism for the knee joint.

Our findings highlight that within the quadriceps complex, the reduction in vastus medialis (VM) activation appears to be particularly critical. The VM plays a pivotal role in maintaining dynamic knee stability within the frontal plane. Our findings revealed that following PMF, both the RMS amplitude and relative iEMG contribution of the VM decreased significantly, indicative of a marked compromise in the medial control capabilities of the knee joint [47, 48]. This medial deficit is clinically critical; a weakened VM cannot adequately counter the external valgus load, leading to the valgus posture frequently observed in ACL injury mechanisms. This alteration directly accounts for the increase in knee valgus moments observed in the kinetic analysis [47, 48].Specifically, the relative iEMG contribution of the MG increased from 14.1% pre-PMF to 17.5%, while that of the TA increased from 9.5% to 13.6%. To compensate for diminished proximal stability, athletes may employ distal strategies, facilitating the maneuver via increased stiffness of the shank and ankle complex [49, 50]. To preserve joint integrity under PMF, the Central Nervous System likely redistributes muscle forces by prioritizing flexor contribution for active stabilization. This shifts the net joint moment polarity, reflecting a protective yet inefficient mechanical strategy rather than a change in external load magnitude [51]. While the increased activation of the hamstrings and gastrocnemius might be interpreted as a compensatory mechanism to enhance joint stability, it may also signify neuromuscular coordination deficits or inefficient co-contraction patterns resulting from the mixed fatigue state. Such alterations could potentially compromise joint loading distribution during the cutting maneuver [33].

Limitations and future direction

First, the one-group pretest-posttest design lacks a control group, presenting potential confounds despite familiarization efforts. Second, analyzing only the left leg relies on bilateral symmetry assumptions that require further verification under fatigue. Finally, soft tissue artifacts from surface markers remain an inherent source of minor kinematic error despite rigorous controls.

The laboratory setting limits ecological validity by failing to fully replicate the physical and psychological demands of match-play. Additionally, the restriction to elite male players limits generalizability to other demographics, while the exclusive focus on the knee may overlook compensatory adaptations at the hip and ankle. Future research should leverage AI-driven protocols for high-fidelity simulation, recruit diverse cohorts, and extend analysis to multi-joint systems to elucidate biomechanical coupling under PMF.

Conclusion

This study shows that although PMF did not significantly affect kinematics or external metrics during a 45° sidestep cutting, it notably reduced quadriceps activation and impaired dynamic knee stability. These deficits increased frontal-plane valgus moments and may heighten knee-injury risk. Therefore, athletic conditioning should emphasize proximal stability and neuromuscular fatigue resistance. Reinforcing quadriceps–hamstring coordination and incorporating dual-task change-of-direction drills under fatigued conditions are recommended to optimize knee biomechanics and reduce injury risk.
