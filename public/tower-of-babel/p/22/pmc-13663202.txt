FOSL1 upregulation contributes to ischemia/reperfusion-induced acute kidney injury by regulating GPX4-dependent ferroptosis
Authors: Xuesong Yu, Jiaqing Peng, Yundong Zou, Liguo Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fos-like antigen 1 (FOSL1) has been implicated in the pathology of acute kidney injury (AKI), but its specific mechanism remains unclear. In this study, we investigated the role of FOSL1 in the regulation of ferroptosis and inflammation in the context of ischemia-reperfusion (I/R)-induced AKI. Our results showed that renal I/R resulted in renal injury as evidenced by renal tissue damage and decreased renal function accompanied by increased FOSL1 expression, all of which was effectively restored by FOSL1 knockout (KO) in mice. FOSL1 KO downregulated the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines and the number of CD4+ T cells in I/R mice model. Furthermore, FOSL1 KO decreased ROS, MDA, and Fe²⁺ levels, elevated GSH levels, and normalized the expression of the acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4)/glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) axis. Consistent with the in vivo results, FOSL1 inhibition suppressed HK-2 cell ferroptosis induced by oxygen-glucose deprivation and reoxygenation (OGD/R), erastin, or co-culture with CD4+ T cells. Notably, GPX4 knockdown (KD) in HK-2 cells reversed the ferroptosis-resistant effect mediated by FOSL1 KD, suggesting that GPX4 is a key downstream molecule of FOSL1. Besides, dual-luciferase reporter (DLR) and chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays further confirmed that FOSL1 directly binds to the GPX4 promoter to repress its transcription. Collectively, these results suggest that FOSL1 inhibition alleviates I/R-induced AKI by enhancing ferroptosis resistance through GPX4 transcriptional regulation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13062-026-00899-w.

Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a common clinical condition, with an estimated 13.3 million cases and 1.7 million mortalities worldwide per year globally [1]. AKI affects approximately 10–15% of hospitalised patients and more than 50% of patients in the intensive care unit [2]. It is characterized by a sudden decline in renal function and is associated with increased mortality, prolonged hospital stays, and high healthcare costs [3, 4]. Renal ischemia-reperfusion (I/R) injury is one of the most prevalent triggers of AKI, which is caused by the transient blockage of renal blood flow followed by blood reperfusion [5]. The pathological processes underlying AKI are complex, encompassing ferroptosis, inflammation, microcirculatory dysfunction, tubular cell toxicity, vasoconstriction and oxidative injury [6]. thereby hindering the advancement of novel therapeutic strategies for AKI [3]. Recent multi-omics studies have systematically prioritized novel AKI therapeutic targets and highlighted inflammatory regulation and biomarkers as core intervention directions [7, 8], underscoring the need to characterize regulators of cell death-inflammation crosstalk in AKI.

Ferroptosis is a non-apoptotic cell death, featured by intracellular iron accumulation, decreased glutathione (GSH) levels, and iron-dependent lipid peroxidation [9]. Ferroptosis has been demonstrated to be involved in the pathophysiological mechanisms of AKI. Ferroptosis not only leads to considerable renal cell death, triggering the initiation of AKI, but also acts as an intermediary in the AKI progression [10]. Recent studies applying an artemisinin-based probe have achieved early detection of AKI through Fe2+-targeted MRI, identifying renal injury 24–48 h prior to an elevation in creatinine levels [11]. Besides, it has been reported that renal tubular cells isolated in vitro displayed increased sensitivity to ferroptosis by over-expression of acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4), a contributor to ferroptosis [12]. In addition, ACSL4 deficiency could protect the kidneys from I/R-induced injury in mice [13]. Moreover, ferroptosis inhibitors have shown ameliorative effects in AKI. Ferrostatin-1, an inhibitor of ferroptosis, has been reported to promote antioxidant activity and ameliorate AKI in mice subjected to cecal ligation and puncture [14]. Cpd-A1, a ferrostatin-1 analog, has shown the effects on improving kidney function, mitigating renal tubular injury, and abrogating inflammation in mice model of AKI [15]. These results collectively suggest a strong association between ferroptosis and AKI.

It is worth noting that ferroptosis and inflammation are closely related, collaboratively executing both physiological and pathological roles [16]. Numerous studies have indicated that ferroptosis is immunogenic and contributes to inflammatory processes [15], since it facilitates the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and lipid peroxides, and propagates cellular death in a synchronized manner [17]. Ferroptosis triggers widespread cellular death, induces subsequent inflammatory responses, and ultimately leads to the deterioration of renal function [18]. Inhibition of ferroptosis in the proximal tubules contributes to renal function repair by alleviating inflammation and enhancing proximal tubular plasticity [19]. Conversely, the activation of the inflammatory response also facilitates ferroptosis. The biological process of ferroptosis is complex and is modulated by numerous inflammatory signaling pathways [16]. For example, the activation of the nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-, leucine-rich repeat-, and pyrin domain- containing receptor 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome could induce oxidative stress, lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis [20]. Suppressing the nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) pathway can enhance the expression of critical ferroptosis factors, such as glutamate cysteine ligase (GPX4), which ultimately protects cells from oxidative stress and ferroptosis [21]. Therefore, regulating the crosstalk between ferroptosis and inflammation could be a potential strategy for the restoration of renal function in AKI.

Fos-like antigen 1 (FOSL1) is a transcription factor that belongs to the Fos family of transcription factors [22]. Recent studies have revealed the relationship between FOSL1 and AKI. FOSL1 can regulate the expression of genes involved in the pathological processes related to AKI, such as differentiation, proliferation, and cell death [23]. It has been found that the mRNA expression of FOSL1 was increased in kidneys in both preclinical and clinical AKI [24]. FOSL1 is also related to ferroptosis and inflammation [25]. Knockdown (KD) of FOSL1 could alleviate the inflammatory response and ferroptosis, which could be rescued by erastin, a ferroptosis activator [26]. Besides, FOSL1 could regulate the expression of neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL) in macrophages [27], and inhibition of FOSL1 activity could reduce the expression of inflammatory factors, thereby attenuating lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced renal injury [28]. Notably, GPX4, a pivotal inhibitor of ferroptosis and a key player in inflammation regulation, could potentially be modulated by FOSL1 [29, 30]. FOSL1 KD could reduce the expression of GPX4 and decreased the levels of ferroptosis-related key markers, such as reactive oxygen species (ROS) and malondialdehyde (MDA) [31]. Considering these findings, we thus proposed a hypothesis in this study that the FOSL1/GPX4 axis might mediate the regulation of renal ferroptosis and inflammation in AKI, and targeting FOSL1 may represent a promising potential strategy worthy of further preclinical investigation for AKI intervention.

Materials and methods

Animals

Eight- to ten-week-old male C57BL/6 wild-type (WT) and renal proximal tubule-specific FOSL1 conditional knockout (KO) mice were obtained from Liaoning Changsheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Benxi, China, License No. SCXK (Liao) 2020-0001). The FOSL1 KO mice were generated using the classic Cre-LoxP conditional knockout system [32]. Briefly, homozygous FOSL1loxP/loxP mice were first crossed with Ggt-Cre mice [33]. The Ggt-Cre line labels proximal tubule and shows no recombination in other renal compartments. The heterozygous offspring carrying both FOSL1-flox allele and Ggt-Cre transgene was back-crossed with homozygous FOSL1loxP/loxP mice. Mouse genotyping was performed by using a standard PCR protocol with custom-designed primers targeting FOSL1 floxed locus and Ggt-Cre transgene at postnatal week three. FOSL1 knockout efficiency in renal tubules was verified via RT-PCR and Western blotting, which confirmed nearly complete depletion of FOSL1 mRNA and protein.

Male WT and FOSL1 KO mice were housed in specific pathogen-free environment under 12/12 h light/dark cycle with free access to food and water. WT mice were randomly divided into 3 groups (Sham, I/R 24 h and I/R 48 h). FOSL1 KO mice were divided into Sham and I/R groups. Mice were anesthetized with 3% isoflurane via inhalation induction and maintained with 2% isoflurane during the surgical procedure. The lateral ventral incision was made to expose the bilateral renal pedicles. Then, an atraumatic microaneurysm clamp was placed on the renal pedicle for 45 min to induce ischemia. Mice in sham group underwent the same surgery without clamping [34]. Mice were warmed with a heating blanket (37 °C) throughout the surgery. Finally, mice were sacrificed at 24–48 h after reperfusion via an intraperitoneal injection of an overdose of sodium pentobarbital. The blood samples and renal tissues were collected for further examination. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the China Medical University (KT20252650) and conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH).

Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining

The kidneys were quickly removed on ice and fixed in 4% formalin for 24 h followed by embedding. The embedded kidney tissues were sectioned into slices of 5 μm thickness. Then, the sections were dewaxed and stained with hematoxylin for 10 min. After a blue color appeared, the sections were then counter-stained with eosin for 3 min. Next, the sections were sealed with neutral gum [35]. Finally, the sections were observed under a light microscope (Olympus, CX21, Tokyo, Japan) at magnifications of 200x.

Biochemical parameter measurement

The levels of interleukin-1β (IL‑1β), interleukin-6 (IL‑6) and tumor necrosis factor α (TNF‑α) were quantified using matched commercial enzyme‑linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits following the manufacturers’ instructions (Liankebio, Hangzhou, China). Contents of creatinine (CTN), urea nitrogen (UN), MDA, GSH and intracellular Fe2+ were determined via dedicated biochemical assay kits in accordance with the respective manufacturer protocols (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Kidney tissue sections were dewaxed, rehydrated, and subsequently blocked with 10% normal goat serum for 10 min at room temperature. Sections were then incubated with anti-FOSL1(1:300, Proteintech, 12663-1-AP), anti-NGAL (1:300, Proteintech, 30700-1-AP) and kidney injury molecule-1 (KIM-1, 1:400, Proteintech, 30948-1-AP) antibodies at 4 °C overnight. After washing with PBS solution, the sections were incubated with secondary antibodies (1:200, ASPEN, AS1107) for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were visualized under a light microscope (Olympus, CX21, Tokyo, Japan) at magnifications of 200x [36].

Flow cytometry (FC)

The lymphocytes in the kidneys and blood of the mice were initially isolated. After isolation, the lymphocytes were washed and suspended with PBS, and suspensions containing 1 × 10^6/mL lymphocytes were stained with FITC anti-mouse CD3 antibody (Invitrogen; 11-0032-82). Then, the CD4+ T cells were sorted using FC (CytoFLEX, Beckman, Brea, CA, USA) [37].

Cell culture, transfection, and oxygen-glucose deprivation and reoxygenation (OGD/R) model

The human renal proximal tubular epithelial cells (HK-2 cells) were obtained, and incubated in DMEM/F12 (D8437, Sigma) cell culture medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA) at 37 °C incubator (5% CO2). OGD/R was performed as follows: HK-2 cells were placed into a hypoxic incubator (37 °C, 1% O2, and 5% CO2, Thermo Fisher) for 3 h in a glucose-free and serum-free medium. Then, the cells were re-oxygenated for 24 h via transferring to a normal atmosphere (37 °C, 21% O2, and 5% CO2), and replaced with complete medium. In order to inhibit the activity of FOSL1, HK-2 cells were transfected with oe-FOSL1/oe-NC or siFOSL1/siNC, and then subjected to OGD/R. Ferrostatin-1 was used to inhibit the ferroptosis, and erastin was used to induce ferroptosis in the OGD/R model.

For FOSL1 knockdown rescue assay, we obtained siRNA-resistant FOSL1 plasmid (pcDNA3.1-siRes-FOSL1 plasmid) with synonymous mutations at the siFOSL1 target site from GemPharmatech (Jiangsu GemPharmatech Co., Ltd., China) and HK-2 cells were co-transfected with siFOSL1 and pcDNA3.1-siRes-FOSL1 plasmids to rescue FOSL1 expression and confirm the phenotypic specificity of FOSL1 knockdown.

Co-culture of HK-2 cells with CD4+ T cells

HK-2/CD4+ T cells co-culture experiment was performed as described previously [38]. Human CD4+ T cells were purchased from Zeyi Primary Cells (Shenzhen, China) and activated with Dynabeads Human T-Activator CD3/CD28 following the manufacturer’s instructions (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). For co-culture, HK-2 cells were transfected with si-FOSL1 or si-NC negative control. After 24 h of transfection, HK-2 cells (5 × 10^5 cells/well) were seeded into the Transwell lower chamber (0.4 μm pore size membrane), which allowed the exchange of soluble cytokines but prevented direct cell-to-cell contraction. Abovementioned activated CD4+ T cells (5 × 10^5 cells/well) were resuspended in complete medium and seeded into the upper chamber. The cells were co-cultured for a total of 24 h and the HK-2 cells were collected to measure ferroptosis-related indicators.

Cell viability assay

The cell viability was assessed using a CCK-8 assay. Logarithmically growing HK-2 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 4 × 10^4 cells/mL. After discarding the supernatant, 100 µL of fresh medium containing 10 µL of CCK-8 working reagent (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) was added. The medium was gently shaken and subsequently incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 2 h. The absorbance of each well was detected at 450 nm using a microplate reader [39].

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from renal tissues or HK-2 cells using Trizol reagent and reversed transcribed to cDNA using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit. qRT-PCR was performed using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix on a CFX96 real-time PCR detection system. The data were analyzed using the 2‑ΔΔCt method, glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as internal control [40].

Western blot

The RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) containing 1% protease inhibitor (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) was used to extract the proteins in kidney tissues or HK-2 cells. Proteins were separated by sodium lauryl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to the polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, USA, IPVH00010). After blocking with 5% fat-free milk for 2 h, the PVDF membranes were then incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C for 24 h. Next, the membranes were washed with 1×Tris-buffered saline Tween-20 (TBST) and incubated with secondary antibodies (1:5000, ASPEN, AS1107, AS1106) under ambient condition for 2 h. An enhanced chemiluminescence substrates (ECL) kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) was used for detection of the protein bands, and the optical density was analyzed using Image J [41].

The antibodies used were as follows: Anti-FOSL1 (1:1000, ABclonal, A5372), Anti-ACSL4 (1:5000, Proteintech, 22401-1-AP), Anti-GPX4 (1:1000, Proteintech, 67763-1-Ig) and Anti-β-actin (1:10000, TDY BIOTECH, TDY051).

Immunofluorescence (IF)

After fixation, renal tissue sections and HK-2 cells were incubated in 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 2 h at room temperature to eliminate non-specific binding. The sections were then incubated with the primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. After washing with PBS, the sections were incubated with a secondary antibody in the dark for 2 h at room temperature and counterstained with DAPI [41]. The antibodies used were as follows: anti-CD4 (1:100, ABclonal, A0362), anti-ACSL4 (1:200, PTG, 22401-1-AP), anti-GPX4 (1:200, PTG, 67763-1-Ig), goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (1:100, Servicebio, GB21303) and goat anti-mouse IgG H&L (1:100, Aspen, AS1111) antibody.

Detection of intracellular ROS

The fluorescent probe dihydroethidium (DHE; Keygen Biotech, KGA7502-5) and 5-(and-6)-carboxy-2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA; Beyotime, S0033) were used to detect intracellular ROS in kidney. For DHE staining, cryosections of kidneys were incubated with 10 µM DHE at 37 °C for 30 min. For the detection of ROS in HK-2 cells, the cells were plated in a 96-well plate and treated with 10 µM DCFH-DA at 37 °C for 30 min. Then, the sections or cells were observed under an inverted fluorescence microscope (IX51, OLYMPUS, Tokyo, Japan) [42].

Detection of lipid ROS

Briefly, lipid ROS levels were measured by C11-BODIPY 581/591 probe. HK-2 cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 10^4 cells per well for 24 h. The cells were then incubated with 10 µM boron-dipyrromethene (BODIPY, λex = 581 nm, λem = 591 nm) probe at 37 °C for 1 h. Fluorescence intensity values in the FITC channel were measured by FC (CytoFLEX, Beckman, Brea, CA, USA) [43].

Detection of Fe2+ by FerroOrange

FerroOrange was used to detect intracellular Fe2+ levels. Briefly, cells were stained with FerroOrange dye (1 µmol/L) in the dark at 37 ℃ for 30 min. Images were acquired by an inverted fluorescence microscope (IX51, OLYMPUS, Tokyo, Japan). Fluorescence intensity was analyzed using Image J software [44].

Transmission electron microscope (TEM)

HK-2 cells were fixed using the TEM-specific fixed solution and dehydrated using a graded series of acetone solutions (50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, and 100%). Then, the cells were soaked in a mixture of acetone and resin and subsequently embedded in pure resin before being sectioned on an ultramicrotome. Finally, the sections were examined under a TEM to observe ferroptosis [45].

Dual-luciferase reporter (DLR) gene assay

The wild-type (WT) and mutant (MUT) reporter plasmids targeting the GPX4 promoter were generated by inserting the corresponding WT or mutated sequences of the predicted FOSL1 binding sites within the GPX4 promoter region into the pGL4-basic luciferase vector (Genomeditech, Shanghai, China). HK-2 cells were seeded into 24-well plates and incubated at 37˚C for 48 h. Subsequently, cells were co-transfected with reporter plasmids and oe-FOSL1/oe-NC using Lipofectamine 2000. Following transfection for 48 h, the luciferase activity was detected using the DLR assay system and normalized to Renilla luciferase activities [46].

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

The ChIP assay was conducted following the manufacturer’s instructions of the ChIP kit (Beyotime Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Briefly, HK-2 cells were treated with 1% paraformaldehyde for cross-linking of endogenous protein-DNA complexes. The HK-2 cells were then lysed and chromatin was sheared into appropriate fragments (500–1000 bp). Immunoprecipitation was carried out in the chromatin fragments and antibodies against FOSL1 or IgG in protein A/G agrose beads. Purified DNA was subjected to SYBR Green-based RT-PCR to detect the enrichment of the GPX4 promoter. GPX4 primers: GPX4 forward, 5’-ACAAGAACGGCTGCGTGGTGAA-3’; GPX4 reverse, 5’-GCCACACACTTGTGGAGCTAGA-3’.

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as mean ± SD using GraphPad Prism 8.0. Prior to the statistical comparison, the normality of data distribution was verified using the Shapiro-Wilk test. The homogeneity of variance was assessed by Levene’s test. For datasets conforming to normal distribution and homogeneous variance, the unpaired Student’s t-test was applied for comparisons between two groups, while one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used for multiple-group comparisons. Appropriate non-parametric tests were adopted for data that failed normality or homogeneity of variance. P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

FOSL1 expression was increased after renal I/R injury

As shown in Fig. 1A-C, the mRNA and protein expressions of FOSL1 were significantly increased in both the I/R (24 h) and I/R (48 h) groups compared to the WT-Sham group. Moreover, as reperfusion time was extended, the expression levels continued to increase. HE results revealed that in the WT-Sham group, the size of the glomeruli was normal, the morphology was regular, and no inflammatory cell infiltration was observed in the renal interstitium. In the I/R (24 h) group, I/R injury led to inflammatory cell infiltration in the renal interstitium, partial necrosis of renal epithelial cells, the absence of a brush border, and enlargement of the glomerular sac cavity. More severe damage was observed in the I/R (48 h) group, manifested as inflammatory cell infiltration of renal interstitium, extensive necrosis and vacuolation of renal epithelial cells and absence of brush border (Fig. 1D). Besides, FOSL1 was weakly expressed in the cytoplasm and nucleus of renal tubular epithelial cells with negligible glomerular staining and renal I/R injury markedly and specifically upregulates FOSL1 in injured proximal and distal tubular epithelial cells at 24 h and 48 h reperfusion (Fig. 1E-F). In addition, NGAL, and KIM-1 are key indicators that reflect the severity of renal dysfunction. ELISA results showed that the levels of CTN and UN were significantly elevated in the I/R groups compared to WT-Sham group (Fig. 1G-H). As shown in Fig. 1E-F, the expressions of NGAL and KIM-1 were increased in the I/R groups compared with WT-Sham group. Overall, the renal damage caused by 48 h reperfusion was more severe than that caused by 24 h reperfusion.

FOSL1 KO alleviated inflammation after renal I/R injury

In turn, we generated FOSL1 KO mice to investigate the role of FOSL1 in renal I/R injury (Fig. 2A-C). Most notably, the severe renal injury evidenced by elevated levels of creatinine CTN, urea nitrogen UN, NGAL and KIM-1, and pathological alterations observed in WT mice, was effectively abolished in FOSL1 KO mice (Fig. 2D-I). The inflammatory response is a crucial pathological factor in AKI [41]. Compared with the WT-Sham group, the WT-I/R group showed significantly elevated mRNA levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α in renal tissues (Fig. 2J). Similar increases were observed in the protein levels of these inflammatory cytokines in both serum and renal tissues (Fig. 2K-L). However, these changes were effectively reversed by FOSL1 KO.

FOSL1 KO suppressed ferroptosis after renal I/R injury

The levels of ROS and MDA were increased (Fig. 3A-C), whereas GSH was decreased in the WT-I/R mice compared to the WT-Sham one (Fig. 3D). Higher Fe2+ levels following I/R injury were also observed WT mice exposed to I/R injury (Fig. 3E). Besides, FOSL1 KO apparently reversed these changes of these ferroptosis-related indicators. GPX4 and ACSL4 are two recognized biomarkers of ferroptosis. The protein expressions of ACSL4 and GPX4 were dysregulated in the WT-I/R group, with an increased expression of ACSL4 and a decreased expression of GPX4. FOSL1 KO effectively balanced the expressions of ACSL4 and GPX4 (Fig. 3F-H), which was confirmed by IF detection (Fig. 3I-L).

FOSL1 KD inhibited ferroptosis in OGD/R-treated HK-2 cell model

As depicted in Fig. 5A-B, the mRNA and protein expressions of FOSL1 were significantly increased in OGD/R-treated HK-2 cells compared to the control, indicating that OGD/R elevated FOSL1expression in HK-2 cells. To exclude potential off-target effects of siRNA interference and confirm the specific regulatory role of FOSL1, we further performed rescue experiments with siRNA-resistant FOSL1 plasmids (pcDNA3.1-siRes-FOSL1 plasmids). As presented in Supplementary Fig. 1, the anti-ferroptotic phenotype triggered by FOSL1 knockdown was fully abolished upon pcDNA3.1-siRes-FOSL1 plasmid transfection, verifying that these biological changes are specifically caused by FOSL1 depletion. As shown in Fig. 5C, OGD/R induced significant cell death of HK-2 cells, as evidenced by reduced cell viability. FOSL1 KD effectively enhanced cell viability, with an effect comparable to that of ferrostatin-1. Besides, the expression of ACSL4 was increased, and the expression of GPX4 was decreased in HK-2 cells following exposure to OGD/R, which were reverted by FOSL1 KD or ferrostatin-1 treatment (Fig. 5K-M).

Discussion

The current study elucidated the role of FOSL1 in mediating ferroptosis and inflammation in renal tubular cells during I/R-induced AKI. Consistent with the latest multi-omics framework prioritizing inflammation and cell death regulators as high-priority AKI therapeutic targets [7, 8], our results demonstrated that I/R impairs renal function by triggering ferroptosis and inflammation via FOSL1 activation, whereas FOSL1 inhibition alleviates AKI and confers resistance to ferroptosis and inflammation in renal tubular cells through the transcriptional regulation of GPX4 in the disease progression of I/R injury.

Emerging evidence has linked FOSL1 to AKI, with this molecule regulating the transcription of genes driving critical AKI-related pathological events such as cell differentiation, proliferation and death [23]. As expected, I/R caused significant renal injury, as evidenced by renal tissue damage and elevated levels of CTN, UN, NGAL and KIM-1, well-validated renal injury biomarkers highlighted as core diagnostic indicators in latest AKI clinical research [47]. Besides, our results confirmed that I/R markedly increased the expression of FOSL1 at both transcriptional and translational levels. FOSL1-KO obviously mitigated renal injury induced by I/R injury, suggesting that the inhibition of FOSL1 may have a nephroprotective effect on I/R injury.

It has been revealed that ferroptosis and inflammation play important contributory roles in most AKI models [48]. The activation of inflammatory responses is closely associated with the occurrence of ferroptosis [16]. During the process of ferroptosis, dead cells recruit macrophages and release inflammatory factors, such as IL-6, IL-1β and TNF-α, thus triggering a robust inflammatory response [49]. Of note, this inflammatory reaction plays a crucial role in iron metabolism disorders and redox system imbalances, and the pro-inflammatory cytokines can regulate ferritin synthesis, thereby affecting iron storage in cells and tissues [50]. The cross-link between ferroptosis and inflammation has also been demonstrated in a murine nephrotoxic AKI model, where ferroptosis leads to the recruitment of inflammatory cytokines, which in turn triggered a wave of necroptosis, resulting in persistent renal injury and renal dysfunction [51]. Therefore, the inhibition of ferroptosis and inflammation may effectively mitigate renal function damage in AKI. In the present study, I/R injury resulted in obvious inflammation as indicated by increased levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines in serum and kidneys, and by increased number of CD4+ T cells in spleen and peripheral blood. Besides, I/R aggravated lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis, manifested by increased levels of ROS, MDA and Fe2+, decreased levels of GSH, and the characteristic mitochondrial morphological alterations associated with ferroptosis. FOSL1 KO efficiently reversed these changes. ACSL4 and GPX4 are key regulatory factors involved in the process of ferroptosis. ACSL4 facilitates the incorporation of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) into phospholipids, thereby increasing their vulnerability to oxidation by lipoxygenase, which leads to the deterioration of the lipid bilayer and the induction of ferroptosis. In contrast, GPX4 inhibits ferroptosis by converting lipid hydroperoxides into non-toxic lipids by utilizing GSH, effectively reducing oxidative stress and preventing ferroptosis [52]. In our study, I/R injury apparently increased the expression of ACSL4 and decreased the expression of GPX4, which could be restored by FOSL1 KO. These findings suggested inhibition of FOSL1 could ameliorate the ferroptosis and inflammation induced by I/R injury.

To further explore the potential mechanisms underlying the regulatory effects of FOSL1 on ferroptosis and inflammation, we applied an OGD/R-treated HK-2 cell model in this study. OGD/R significantly increased the expressions of FOSL1 in HK-2 cells. In addition, OGD/R decreased cell viability, aggravated lipid peroxidation and induced ferroptosis, which could be restored by FOSL1 KD or by an inhibitor of ferroptosis, ferrostatin-1. Moreover, erastin, a specific inducer of ferroptosis [53], also triggered ferroptosis in HK-2 cells, and FOSL1 KD exhibited significant inhibitory effects on ferroptosis in the erastin-treated cell model. Erastin was specifically selected for our experiments as a canonical inhibitor of system Xc⁻. It blocks cellular cystine uptake and subsequently depletes intracellular GSH, thereby initiating GPX4-dependent ferroptosis. Given that our core focus is the FOSL1/GPX4 regulatory axis, erastin serves as an ideal tool to stably and specifically induce ferroptosis and validate the underlying molecular mechanism. These findings reinforced the protective role of inhibiting FOSL1 activity. In sequence, we conducted HK-2 and CD4+ T cell co-culture to investigate the correlation between inflammation and ferroptosis, as well as the potential role of FOSL1 in this process. Our results showed that, after co-culture with CD4+ T cells, the viability of HK-2 cells was significantly decreased. In addition, co-culture with CD4+ T cells induced obvious lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis in HK-2 cells. These results suggested that the inflammatory response could likewise facilitate the onset of ferroptosis, highlighting the cross-link between ferroptosis and inflammation. Of note, aforementioned changes were all effectively restored by FOSL1 KD in vitro, unveiling the crucial role of FOSL1 in linking inflammation and ferroptosis.

Recent studies have highlighted the crucial role of GPX4 in regulating ferroptosis. Additionally, GPX4 is also implicated in mediating the inflammatory response [54]. It has been demonstrated that activators of GPX4 can suppress ferroptosis, decrease the production of pro-inflammatory lipid mediators, and inhibit the activation of the NF-κB pathway [55]. However, the direct involvement of GPX4 in FOSL1-mediated regulation of ferroptosis and inflammation remains to be elucidated. In this study, we found that the activity of GPX4 was decreased by over-expression of FOSL1, and increased by FOSL1 KD. Besides, DLR assay and ChIP-qPCR results showed that FOSL1 can directly bind to the GPX4 promoter thereby inhibiting the GPX4 activity. Furthermore, FOSL1 KD could significantly increase cell viability and inhibit ferroptosis induced by OGD/R in HK-2 cells, which was abolished by the dual inhibition of FOSL1 and GPX4, suggesting that GPX4 may play a protective role against ferroptosis downstream of FOSL1. Notably, although our experimental data verified that FOSL1 KD reversed the upregulation of ACSL4 triggered by I/R injury, FOSL1 does not regulate ACSL4 directly. Mechanistically, I/R stress induced the upregulation of FOSL1, which directly suppressed GPX4 transcription at the transcriptional level. The deficiency of GPX4 impaired lipid hydroperoxide clearance and induced redox imbalance, meanwhile, the accumulated oxidative stress secondarily upregulated ACSL4 to aggravate lipid peroxidation and ferroptotic injury. Therefore, the alteration of ACSL4 was the downstream secondary effect caused by GPX4-mediated oxidative imbalance, rather than a direct effect of FOSL1. Overall, FOSL1 primarily modulated I/R-triggered ferroptosis via the FOSL1/GPX4 axis, impairs lipid hydroperoxide clearance and induces redox imbalance.

Despite the solid findings in the present study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, our study employed male FOSL1 kidney knockout mice, precluding analysis of potential sex-dependent effects. In addition, we only assessed short-term renal pathological alterations following I/R injury, with no long-term outcome observation performed to evaluate sustained renal injury progression. Although FOSL1 small-molecule inhibitors and PROTAC-based FOSL1 degraders are currently under development, the therapeutic potential and pharmacological mechanism of FOSL1 inhibitors in AKI remain unclarified. Therefore, further studies utilizing other cell-type-specific FOSL1 knockout mice and pharmacological FOSL1 inhibitors will be required to validate the translational value of FOSL1-targeted therapy for ischemic AKI. Additionally, all results in this study were obtained from murine and cell line models without using patient biopsy samples. Further validation using human AKI biopsy samples is needed to verify the clinical relevance of the FOSL1/GPX4 axis in human ischemic AKI.

Taken together, our findings suggested that inhibiting FOSL1 could suppress ferroptosis and inflammation through the enhancement of GPX4 activity, thereby restoring renal function in AKI. Inhibiting FOSL1 may be a promising new therapeutic strategy for preventing ferroptosis and inflammation after AKI.
