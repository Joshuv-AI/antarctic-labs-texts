Clostridium butyricum ameliorates Toxoplasma gondii-induced neuropsychiatric disorders by attenuating glial-mediated synaptic pruning via the gut-brain axis
Authors: Yiling Li, Hongjin Wu, Jiayi Yang, Jensen G Weedor, Haoxuan Ding, Wenjing Cui, Bingqian Cui, Zhicheng He, Wenjun Zhang, Yihui Xing, Fan Zeng, Xufeng Huang, Kuiyang Zheng, Yujuan Shen, Yinghua Yu, Wei Pan, Xiaoying Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gut microbiota dysbiosis contributes to Toxoplasma gondii (T. gondii)-induced neuropsychiatric disorders (TNDs); however, the underlying mechanisms remain largely elusive. Here, we identified the critical role of butyrate-producing bacteria in TNDs in mice. Decreased abundance of butyrate-producing bacteria was consistently observed in patients with Alzheimer’s disease and T. gondii-infected mice. Dietary supplementation with Clostridium butyricum (C. butyricum), a gut commensal butyrate-producing bacterium, reversed gut microbiota dysbiosis, ameliorated intestinal barrier disruption and inflammation, and reduced endotoxemia. Coincidentally, C. butyricum administration suppressed microglial and astrocytic activation, rescued synaptic ultrastructure damage and synaptic loss, thus alleviating cognitive impairment and anxiety/depression-like behaviors. Mechanistically, C. butyricum treatment mitigated the abnormal synaptic pruning mediated by glial cells and C1q to prevent the neuropathology induced by T. gondii infection. Importantly, fecal microbiota transplantation from C. butyricum-supplemented mice into antibiotic-treated recipients recapitulated the therapeutic effects on gut and brain pathology observed in infected mice. Together, our findings suggest that C. butyricum ameliorates TNDs by modulating glial cell-mediated abnormal synaptic pruning via the gut-brain axis, highlighting the therapeutic potential efficacy of butyrate-producing bacteria against TNDs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-026-03761-y.

Introduction

Neuropsychiatric disorders, including Alzheimer’s disease (AD), depression, and anxiety, have become a major global public health challenge driven by their increasing prevalence [1, 2]. According to the latest Global Burden of Disease Study (GBD 2021), neuropsychiatric disorders accounted for 155,418,119 disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), contributing approximately 5.3% of the total global disease burden [3]. In recent years, chronic infection with Toxoplasma gondii (T. gondii), a neurotropic protozoan with a global average seroprevalence of approximately 30%, has been recognized as an important etiological risk factor for neuropsychiatric disorders [4, 5]. Although systemic immune responses, glial cell activation, and neuronal damage have been largely observed during chronic T. gondii infection [6], the precise pathogenesis underlying T. gondii-induced neuropsychiatric disorders (TNDs) and effective therapeutic strategies remain to be fully elucidated.

Accumulating evidence highlights that the dysbiosis of gut microbiota drives neuropsychiatric disorders [7–9]. Patients with AD, anxiety, or depression exhibit distinct gut microbial profiles that correlate strongly with clinical severity scores [10–12]. Notably, gut microbiota derived from patients with neuropsychiatric disorders worsens neuropathology in recipient mice [13, 14]. As a foodborne protozoan parasite, chronic infection of T. gondii was reported to disrupt gut microbial homeostasis [15, 16]. Interestingly, we recently demonstrated that gut microbiota dysbiosis contributed to cognitive impairment and anxiety-like behaviors induced by T. gondii infection, particularly with a decrease in the microbial metabolite butyrate playing an important role in the pathogenesis [17, 18]. However, the unpleasant taste and rancid odor of butyrate may hinder its future clinical application. Further investigation is needed to explore novel and more acceptable strategies.

Clostridium butyricum (C. butyricum), one of the most extensively studied butyrate-producing bacteria, is a common gut symbiont in humans and animals [19]. It is reported that C. butyricum can reshape gut microbial community and prevent mucosal damage in mice [20]. Notably, C. butyricum is clinically utilized for managing gastrointestinal diseases, such as ulcerative colitis and antibiotic-associated diarrhea [21, 22]. Our laboratory found that the levels of butyrate were reduced in the serum of individuals with anti-T. gondii IgG antibody and T. gondii-infected mice [17]. Considering the potential protective effect of butyrate on gut health, we proposed that the declined butyrate might be involved in the pathogenesis of gut damage and consequently TNDs via the gut-brain axis.

In this study, we found the decreased abundance of butyrate-producing bacteria in AD patients and chronic T. gondii-infected mice. Dietary supplementation with C. butyricum had a series of beneficial effects on gut-brain pathology induced by T. gondii infection. Moreover, we identified that C. butyricum’s effects were largely due to its ability to reshape gut microbiota. Overall, our findings uncover that C. butyricum is a potential probiotic for TNDs.

Methods

Animals

All animal care and experiments in this study were conducted in accordance with the Chinese Council on Animal Care Guidelines and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Xuzhou Medical University. Eight-week-old C57BL/6J mice, purchased from Jiangsu Jicui Pharmaceutical Technology Corporation (China), were housed in specific pathogen-free (SPF) facilities under standard conditions (temperature 24 ± 1 °C, humidity 40–60%, and a 12 h dark-light cycle) with free access to food and water.

Parasite preparation

This study utilized the Toxoplasma gondii Chinese 1 genotype Wh6 strain (TgCtWh6; ToxoDB #9), a predominant genotype circulating in China. This strain is characterized by intermediate virulence, a high propensity for cyst formation, and the unique presence of both ROP16I/III and GRA15II effector proteins [23, 24]. TgCtWh6 strain cysts were provided by the laboratory of Professor Jilong Shen [25]. The cysts were isolated from the brains of chronic T. gondii-infected mice and resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS).

Cyst burden counting

The brain tissues of mice were homogenized in 1 mL PBS. Then, 10 µL brain suspension was taken and screened under a light microscope (×20 magnification). The cyst number was counted in a blind manner to estimate the total cyst burden in the brain tissue. The counting process for each mouse was repeated at least 3 times.

Animal experiments

Experiment 1: Induction of chronic T. gondii infection model and administration of C. butyricum

The male C57BL/6J mice (aged 8 weeks) were randomly divided into four groups (n = 12 mice/group): the uninfected mice (Con group), the uninfected mice with dietary C. butyricum supplementation (Cb group), the chronic T. gondii-infected mice (Tg group), and the chronic T. gondii-infected mice with dietary C. butyricum supplementation (Tg-Cb group). The Con group received PBS as vehicle control. The Cb group received water containing 5 × 108 CFU/mL C. butyricum, whereas the Tg group consisted of T. gondii-infected mice that received PBS. In addition, the Tg-Cb group consisted of T. gondii-infected mice that received water containing 5 × 108 CFU/mL C. butyricum from 2 weeks before infection until sacrifice [26]. Chronic T. gondii infection was induced via oral gavage of 10 TgCtWh6 cysts per mouse. The experimental timeline is shown in Fig. 2A. The behavior tests were conducted at 4 weeks post-infection. All mice were sacrificed 3 days after behavioral testing by CO₂ inhalation. Colon, blood, cecum contents, fecal samples, and brain tissues were collected for further analyses.

Experiment 2: Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT)

According to previous studies [18], the recipient mice required depletion of their native gut microbiota prior to FMT. Therefore, the recipient mice first received an antibiotic cocktail (0.25 g/L vancomycin, 1 g/L ampicillin, 1 g/L neomycin, and 1 g/L metronidazole) via drinking water for 3 weeks and were then switched to normal drinking water for 3 days. Subsequently, the gut microbiota-cleansed recipient mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 10 mice/group): ConFMT (mice transplanted with microbiota from Con mice), CbFMT (mice transplanted with microbiota from Cb mice), TgFMT (mice transplanted with microbiota from Tg mice), and Tg-CbFMT groups (mice transplanted with microbiota from Tg-Cb mice). The donor fecal slurry was prepared in PBS at a concentration of approximately 1011 CFU/L and transplanted into recipient mice as previously reported [17]. Mice of the ConFMT group, CbFMT group, TgFMT group, and Tg-CbFMT group were transplanted with fecal slurry from the Con group, Cb group, Tg group, and Tg-Cb group, respectively. The experimental timeline is described in Fig. 8A. The behavior tests were conducted at 2 weeks post-microbiota colonization. All mice were sacrificed 3 days after behavioral testing by CO₂ inhalation. Colon, blood, cecum contents, and brain tissues were collected for further analyses.

Behavioral tests

The novel object recognition test (NOR), novel location recognition test (NLR), and Y-maze spatial memory test were used to evaluate the alterations in spatial memory and recognition memory in mice [17], while the open field test (OFT), elevated plus maze test (EPMT), and tail suspension test (TST) were performed to assess anxiety/depression-like behaviors in mice [11, 18]. All exploratory behaviors of the mice were recorded and analyzed using automated tracking software.

For NOR and NLR, briefly, each mouse underwent a 5-min habituation period to the testing box one day before the formal test. During the exploration phase, two identical objects were placed in the box, and each mouse was allowed to freely explore for 5 min. After a 1-h retention interval, the recognition phase began. In this phase of NOR, one of the objects was randomly replaced with a novel object, and mice were permitted to explore freely for an additional 5 min. The novel object discrimination index (NODI) was calculated as: (Time with novel object/total time with both objects) × 100. During the recognition phase of NLR, one of the objects was shifted to the diagonal place, and mice were allowed to freely explore for an additional 5 min. The place discrimination index (PDI) was evaluated by using the formula: (Time with novel place/total time with both objects) × 100.

For Y-maze spatial memory test, one arm of the maze was temporarily blocked, and mice were permitted to freely explore the remaining two arms for 5 min. Following a 2-hour retention interval, all arms were opened, and mice were allowed to navigate the entire maze for an additional 5 min.

For OFT, mice were adapted to the new environment 1 day before the test. During the exploration stage, mice were allowed to explore a square open-field box for 10 min. The square open-field box was divided into a central area (36 cm diameter) and a peripheral area.

For EPMT, a 15 cm-high gray elevated plus maze consisted of four arms radiating from a central area (5 × 5 cm). Two opposing open arms (35 × 5 cm) lacked protective ledges while the remaining two closed arms (35 × 5 cm) featured opaque walls. During the exploration stage, mice were placed on the center area facing an open arm and allowed to freely explore the maze for 10 min.

For TST, mice were acclimated to the new environment 1 day before the test. During the exploration stage, the distal 1 cm of the tail was affixed to a horizontal bar using adhesive tape, suspending the mouse head-down. Immobility time was 6 min, with behavioral tracking software opened to quantify immobility duration during the final 4 min.

Western blotting

The hippocampus and colon tissues were homogenized in ice-cold RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with a complete EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail (P1045, Beyotime, Jiangsu, China). The supernatant was collected, and protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay kit (G2026-200T, Servicebio, Wuhan, China). Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS–PAGE (G230210, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against Toxoplasma gondii Surface antigen 1 (SAG1, 1:500, sc-52255, Santa Cruz, Shanghai, China) and β-actin (1:20,000, 81115-1-RR, Proteintech, Wuhan, China). After three washes with TBST (Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20), membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:3000, GB23303, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) or HRP-conjugated anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (1:3000, GB23301, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) , for 1 h at room temperature. Following three additional washes, protein bands were detected using Clarity ECL Western blot substrate (1705060, Bio-Rad) and visualized with the ChemiDoc Touch Imaging System (Bio-Rad). Protein expression levels were normalized to β-actin.

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

The concentrations of IL-12, IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-6 in serum and in supernatants of homogenized hippocampal and colonic tissues, were quantified using commercially available ELISA kits (Yancheng Junxing Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China; IL-12: JX-3436A1, IFN-γ: JX-2918A1, TNF-α: JX-2868A1, IL-6: JX-2899A1), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Golgi-Cox staining

The Golgi-Cox staining was performed using the FD Rapid Golgi Stain Kit (PK401, Nanjing Well-Offex Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) as previously reported [26]. Briefly, brain tissues were soaked in a mixture of solution A and B for 2 weeks, followed by thorough washing with solution C for 3 days. The processed brains were sectioned at 100 μm thickness using a vibratome (Vibratome 1500, Leica Biosystems, Germany) and mounted on gelatin-coated microscope slides. Sections were subsequently stained and dehydrated according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Dendritic morphology within the hippocampal Cornu Ammonis 1 (CA1) region was imaged using a digital scanning microscope (Olympus VS120, Olympus Corporation, Japan). Neuronal tracings were performed with the NeuronJ algorithm supported by the NIH Fiji software (https://imagej.net/Fiji). The dendritic tracings of pyramidal neurons were evaluated by Sholl analysis.

Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM)

The TEM was conducted to analyze synaptic ultrastructure of hippocampal CA1 region following established protocols [27]. Briefly, the hippocampal CA1 regions of mice with transcranial perfusion of cold saline were dissected and trimmed to a volume of approximately 1 mm3. Tissues were then fixed with a 2% paraformaldehyde-2.5% glutaraldehyde mixture at 4 °C for 24 h and separated into thin slices. Slices were fixed immediately with 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C overnight, then fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide (OsO4), stained with 2% aqueous solution of uranyl acetate, and dehydrated through a series of increasing concentrations of ethanol and acetone solutions. After being embedded in epoxy resin, samples were cut into 70 nm slices with ultramicrotome, mounted onto copper grids, stained with 4% uranyl acetate and 0.5% lead citrate, and then observed with a transmission electron microscope (HT7800, Hitachi, Japan). The ultrastructural structures of asymmetric synapses (Gray type I, excitatory synapses) from 12 synapses among groups were selected for examination. The postsynaptic density (PSD) thickness, the widths of the synaptic clefts (SC), the length of the active zone (AZ), and the synaptic curvature were assessed by ImageJ software (Version 1.53n, https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/).

RNA isolation and quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was isolated from the hippocampus and colon of mice according to protocols previously reported [28], followed by reverse-transcription to cDNA using HiScript II Q RT SuperMix for qPCR (+ gDNA wiper) (R223, Vazyme, China). Gene expression was evaluated using Roche LightCycler® 480 II Real-time PCR Instrument (Swiss), ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q311, Vazyme, China). Relative gene expression level was determined using the 2−ΔΔCt method and normalized with β-actin as the internal reference control. Primer sequences are listed in Table S1.

Immunofluorescence staining

For colonic ZO-1 staining, paraformaldehyde-fixed colonic tissues were embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 5 μm thickness, and stained with anti-ZO-1 antibody (1:200, GB11195, Servicebio) as previously described [29]. The sections were then incubated with goat-anti-rabbit CY3-conjugated secondary antibody (1:300, GB21303, Servicebio) and counterstained with DAPI (G1012, Servicebio).

For hippocampal staining, frozen brain were cut into 20 μm sections, washed with PBS, and blocked with 10% BSA (GC305010, Servicebio) for 40 min. Sections were then incubated with primary antibodies against Iba1 (1:500, Ab178847, Abcam) or GFAP (1:500, #3670, Cell Signaling Technology) overnight at 4 °C. After washing with PBS, these sections were further incubated with Cy3-conjugated goat anti- rabbit IgG secondary antibody (Servicebio, GB21303, 1:300 dilution) in the dark at room temperature for 2 h, followed by DAPI (G1012, Servicebio) staining for nuclei. Microglial or astrocyte activation accompanies morphological rounding or loss of protrusions [30]. The activation of microglia and astrocytes, which is characterized by morphological rounding and process retraction, was quantified by measuring cell circularity and solidity using ImageJ software [31]. The formula for Circularity is 4π × (Area/Perimeter²), approaches 1 for a perfect circle and decreases with increased branching or elongation. Solidity, defined as Area /Convex Area, measures contour complexity; it equals 1 for a completely convex shape and decreases with the presence of concavities and protrusions.

For PSD95 and C1q co-staining, anti-PSD95 (1:500, #36233, Cell Signaling Technology) and anti-C1q (1:500, Ab11861, Abcam) antibodies were mixed and applied to sections, and incubated at 4 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, sections were washed with PBS and incubated with a mixture of Cy3-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:300, GB21303, Servicebio) and Alexa Fluor® 488-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (1:300, GB25301, Servicebio) to detect the corresponding primary antibodies. Finally, the sections were mounted with anti-fade mounting medium (G1401, Servicebio) to preserve the fluorescence signal for imaging. Representative images of Iba1 and PSD95 single staining were captured using a fluorescence microscope (OLYMPUS IX51, Japan), while images of PSD95 and C1q co-staining were acquired using a confocal microscope (NIKON Eclipse Ti, Japan). Quantification and characterization of positively stained cells were conducted using ImageJ.

Serum lipopolysaccharide (LPS) quantification

Serum LPS levels were analyzed in duplicate using a Limulus assay kit (Xiamen Bioendo Technology Co., Ltd., China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was gauged at 545 nm using a spectrophotometer (Asuragen ClinBio128, USA).

16S rRNA gene sequencing

Fecal pellets from Con, Cb, Tg, and Tg-Cb groups were collected and stored at -80 °C until extraction. Total genomic DNA was extracted using the E.Z.N.A. Stool DNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Norcross, GA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocols. V3-V4 region of bacterial 16S rRNA genes was amplified using primers 341-F 5’-CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG-3’ and 806-R 5’-GGACTACHVGGGTATCTAAT-3’. Amplicon quality was assessed by gel electrophoresis, followed by purification using AMPure XP beads (Agencourt). The purified products were then subjected to a second round of PCR amplification. After another purification step with AMPure XP beads, the final amplicons were quantified using the Qubit dsDNA Assay Kit. Equimolar concentrations of purified amplicons were pooled and subjected to paired-end sequencing (2 × 250 bp) on an Illumina platform according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Sequences sharing ≥ 97% similarity were clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs). The Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) analysis was performed to identify microbial features with significantly different abundance across groups. The analysis involved a Kruskal-Wallis test followed by linear discriminant analysis (LDA) to estimate effect sizes. Based on the KEGG database, the predicted functional profiles of the gut microbiota were inferred using Tax4Fun. Statistical analysis was performed using Statistical Analysis of Metagenomic Profiles (STAMP) software, and functional differences in KEGG orthologs among groups were assessed using Welch’s t-test.

Gut microbiota analysis of T. gondii-infected mice in database

Gut microbiota data of chronic T. gondii-infected mice were obtained from the Sequence Read Archive (SRA) of the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI), under project accession number PRJNA948817. Raw sequence data were downloaded and processed following standard QIIME2 (version 2024.2) pipelines. Quality control, denoising, and chimera removal were performed using the DADA2 plugin, resulting in an amplicon sequence variant (ASV) abundance table, representative sequences, and taxonomic annotations. Taxonomic classification was conducted using the Mothur algorithm with reference to the SILVA 138 database (http://www.arb-silva.de/). Downstream data analysis was performed in R software (version 4.2.0, RStudio, Posit, PBC, Boston, MA, USA). High-dimensional biomarker taxa were identified using the Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) method, implemented via the microeco package (version 1.8.0) in R. Functional prediction was carried out using the ggpicrust2 package (version 1.7.3), also based on the SILVA 138 reference database.

Gut microbiota analysis of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) patients in database

Gut microbiota data of AD patients were obtained from the Sequence Read Archive (SRA) of the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI), under project accession number PRJNA496408. The sequencing data were quality filtered, clustered, and analyzed as described in “Gut microbiota analysis of T. gondii-infected mice in database”.

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) and analyzed using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.4.1, San Diego, California, USA) and R software (Version 4.2.0, RStudio, Posit, PBC, Boston, MA, USA). Normality tests were performed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. An unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was used for comparisons between two groups. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with the post-hoc Tukey’s test was performed for comparisons among multiple groups. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05 and marked as follows: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001. Effect sizes, 95% confidence intervals, and exact P values were reported for key morphological outcomes to ensure statistical transparency. Post-hoc power analyses were conducted to confirm adequate sensitivity (power > 0.9 for observed effects). Pearson correlation analysis was performed to evaluate the associations between gut microbiota composition and neuropsychiatric behavioral outcomes following C. butyricum supplementation, with significance thresholds adjusted using Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR)-corrected q-values. FDR-corrected q-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Decreased abundance of butyrate-producing bacteria in AD patients and chronic T. gondii-infected mice

Analyzing convergent alterations in key gut microbial taxa in different contexts of neuropsychiatric disorders is helpful to identify the probiotics for treatment. Here, we conducted a cross-species comparative analysis of gut microbiota profiles. These analyzed 16 S rRNA sequencing data were retrieved from the Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database of NCBI, including a Chinese Alzheimer’s disease (AD) cohort (accession number: PRJNA496408) and mice chronically infected with T. gondii (accession number: PRJNA398056). A similar gut microbiota pattern was observed in the two different diseases (Fig. 1A, B and S1A-F). At the phylum level, the gut microbiota of the three groups was predominantly composed of Firmicutes, Bacteroidota, Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria (Fig. S1A), in which AD patients displayed a reduced Firmicutes abundance and elevated Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria abundance, while mild cognitive impairment (MCI) patients presented an increased Bacteroidota abundance (Fig. S1B). In AD cohort, the Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) analysis revealed that normal controls (NC) exhibited enrichment of Firmicutes-associated taxa, including the class Clostridia, the family Lachnospiraceae, the order Lachnospirales, and the genera Blautia and [Eubacterium]_hallii_group, whereas patients with MCI showed increased abundance of taxa within the phylum Bacteroidota (e.g. the families Bacteroidaceae and Tannerellaceae) and Proteobacteria (e.g. the family Enterobacteriaceae) (Fig. 1A). Similarly, in chronic T. gondii-infected mice, at the phylum level, T. gondii infection strikingly increased the relative abundance of Bacteroidota while reducing the relative abundance of Firmicutes and Verrucomicrobia (Fig. S1C and D). LEfSe analysis indicated that the uninfected mice (Con) exhibited higher abundance of Firmicutes- and Verrucomicrobia-associated taxa (e.g. the class Clostridia, the genera Bacillus, Oscillibacter, Ruminiclostridium_9, Eubacterium_ruminantium_group, Butyricicoccus, and Akkermansia), whereas T. gondii infection increased the abundance of Bacteroidota-associated taxa (Fig. 1B). Notably, both disease models exhibited a decrease in the Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidota (F/B) ratio (Fig. S1E and F), a consistent reduction in key butyrate-producing bacterial taxa (e.g. the class Clostridia and the genus Eubacterium) and an increase in lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-associated taxa (e.g. the order Bacteroidales and the genus Alistipes).

Structural changes in the core microbiota may underline disease-related functional alterations [32]. PICRUSt2 predictions revealed that these alterations in gut microbiota were associated with convergent functions across both models (Fig. 1C and D). In AD cohort, KEGG functional orthologs showed significant upregulation of “lipopolysaccharide biosynthesis” pathways, along with marked downregulation of “butanoate metabolism,” “propanoate metabolism,” and “dopaminergic synapse” pathways compared to healthy individuals (Fig. 1C and Fig. S2). Similarly, in T. gondii-infected mice, KEGG pathway analysis also revealed upregulation of LPS biosynthesis and suppression of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs)-related pathways, including “butanoate metabolism” and “propanoate metabolism” (Fig. 1D and Fig. S3).

Notably, genus-level statistical comparisons confirmed significant depletion of butyrate-producing bacteria in both cohorts (Fig. 1E and F). Compared to NC group, genera such as Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group, [Eubacterium]_coprostanoligenes_group, [Eubacterium]_hallii_group, Oscillibacter, Odoribacter, Butyricicoccus, Clostridia_UCG-014, Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1, Ruminococcus and Blautia were significantly reduced in AD patients; and reductions in the genera [Eubacterium]_hallii_group, Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1, [Ruminococcus]_torques_group and Blautia in MCI patients (Fig. 1E). In the T. gondii-infected mice, we also observed substantial reductions in the genera Akkermansia, Ruminococcaceae_UCG_009, Ruminiclostridium, and Lachnospiraceae_FCS020_group, while LPS-associated genera such as Alistipes and Bacteroides were significantly increased (Fig. 1F). Furthermore, Pearson’s correlation analysis showed that the abundance of butyrate-producing genera such as Akkermansia, Ruminiclostridium, Ruminococaceae_UCG_009, and Lachnospiraceae_FCS020_group, which was reduced by T. gondii infection, was positively associated with neuropsychiatric indices (Fig. S1G). Taken together, these findings indicate that the shared disruption of core microbiota, characterized by the loss of butyrate-producing bacteria, is a common feature in neuropsychiatric disorders, including TNDs.

C. butyricum supplementation restored gut microbiota homeostasis induced by chronic T. gondii infection

Given the observed decline in butyrate-producing bacteria in AD patients and chronic T. gondii-infected mice, we investigated the role of butyrate-producing bacteria in gut microbiota dysbiosis and TNDs symptoms by supplementation of C. butyricum (butyrate-producing bacteria) (Fig. 2A). The chronic T. gondii-infected mouse model was successfully established, as evidenced by the presence of brain cysts and specific Surface antigen 1 (SAG1) expression in the colon and hippocampus of Tg mice (Fig. S4). However, C. butyricum treatment did not significantly mitigate these indicators (Fig. S4), indicating that dietary C. butyricum does not reduce parasite load. β-diversity analysis revealed distinct bacterial community compositions among the four groups: the uninfected mice (Con), the uninfected mice with dietary C. butyricum supplementation (Cb), the chronic T. gondii-infected mice (Tg), and the chronic T. gondii-infected mice with dietary C. butyricum supplementation (Tg-Cb) (Fig. 2B). T. gondii infection-induced phylum-level dysbiosis, characterized by reduced Firmicutes abundance, elevated Bacteroidota abundance and depleted Desulfobacterota abundance, was reversed by C. butyricum administration, significantly restoring the Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidota (F/B) ratio (Fig. 2C-G). Meanwhile, C. butyricum supplementation increased the abundance of the classes Clostridia, Verrucomicrobiae, Bacilli, and their lower taxa, including the orders Clostridiales, Verrucomicrobiales, Lactobacillales, the families Clostridiaceae, Akkermansiaceae, Lactobacillaceae, and the genera Akkermansia, Lactobacillus in T. gondii-infected mice (Fig. 2H-J and S5A, B). Conversely, the abundance of the class Bacteroidia and its lower taxa, including the order Bacteroidales, the families Rikenellaceae, Prevotellaceae, and the genera Alistipes, Alloprevotella, were enriched in T. gondii-infected mice, whereas C. butyricum weakened these increases (Fig. S5C). Interestingly, genus-level analysis revealed that C. butyricum elevated the abundance of butyrate-producing genera, including Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group, Prevotellaceae_UCG-001, Eubacterium_xylanophilum_group, Oscillibacter, Blautia, Weissella and Colidextribacter in T. gondii-infected mice (Fig. 2K and L). Moreover, the Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) analysis showed the enrichment of butyrate-producing bacteria in Con mice, including the order Verrucomicrobiales and its lower taxa (the family Akkermansiaceae and the genus Akkermansia), as well as the genera Eubacterium_xylanophilum_group and Blautia (Fig. 2M and S5D). In contrast, LEfSe analysis identified enrichment of butyrate-producing bacteria in C. butyricum-treated T. gondii-infected (Tg-Cb) mice, such as the order Clostridia_vadinBB60_group, the genera Clostridiales_bacterium_CIEAF_020 and Eubacterium_xylanophilum_group. Meanwhile, C. butyricum supplementation enriched propionate- and acetate-producing bacteria, including the order Negativicutes, Selenomonadales, the family Selenomonadaceae and the genera Quinella (Fig. 2N and S5D). Additionally, we observed that both Con mice and Tg-Cb mice enriched the lower taxa bacteria of phylum Firmicutes, such as the genera Eubacterium_xylanophilum_group, Desulfovibrio and Colidextribacter (Fig. 2M and N). Overall, the results suggest that C. butyricum relieves the dysbiosis of gut microbiota caused by chronic T. gondii infection in mice.

C. butyricum repaired the impairment of gut barrier integrity induced by chronic T. gondii infection

Homeostasis of gut microbiota contributes to the integrity of gut barrier [29, 33]. Having observed the protective effects of C. butyricum on dysbiosis, we further assessed colonic barrier function following treatment. C. butyricum significantly increased colon length and cecal content weight in T. gondii-infected mice (Fig. 3A–C), indicating improved intestinal condition. Moreover, C. butyricum supplementation attenuated mucosal inflammation, as demonstrated by markedly reduced protein levels of colonic IL-12, IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-6 (Fig. 3D–G), along with decreased mRNA expression of pro-inflammatory mediators including TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β (Fig. 3H–J). Immunofluorescence staining and qPCR analyses further showed that C. butyricum restored barrier integrity, reflected by increased ZO-1⁺ epithelial coverage and upregulated Occludin expression in the colon (Fig. 3K–M). Consistently, plasma LPS levels were markedly elevated upon infection, whereas C. butyricum administration significantly reversed this increase (Fig. 3N). In parallel, serum levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-12, IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-6 were also significantly reduced by C. butyricum treatment (Fig. 3O–R), further supporting its role in alleviating systemic inflammation. Taken together, these results suggest that the beneficial effects of C. butyricum on TNDs are primarily mediated through reshaping gut microbiota and modulating host intestinal immunity, rather than restricting parasite dissemination.

C. butyricum prevented neuropsychiatric disorders caused by chronic T. gondii infection

The disorder of gut microbiota is closely associated with TNDs [17, 18]. Thus, we evaluated the effects of C. butyricum on behavioral performance in T. gondii-infected mice. In the novel object recognition test (NOR), C. butyricum administration significantly improved object recognition memory in infected mice, as demonstrated by an elevated novel object discrimination index (NODI) (Fig. 4A). In the novel location recognition test (NLR) and Y-maze spatial memory tests, C. butyricum ameliorated spatial recognition memory deficits in T. gondii-infected mice, reflected by an increased place discrimination index (PDI) in the NLR (Fig. 4C) and elevated time spent exploring the novel arm in the Y-maze spatial memory test (Fig. 4E and F). Importantly, these improvements were not due to differences in general activity variation, as total exploration times during the testing phase were comparable among all groups (both P > 0.05) (Fig. 4B and D) (Fig. 4D: CV = 43–70% across groups; One-way ANOVA F(3,28) = 1.87, p = 0.157; Bayes factor BF₀₁ = 3.3; Con 119.2 s, Tg 175.3 s; 95% CI − 155.3 to 43.2 s, Tukey-adjusted p = 0.426).

C. butyricum mitigated neuroinflammation in chronic T. gondii-infected mice

Neuroinflammation represents a critical neuropathological basis for neuropsychiatric disorders [34, 35]. Here, we investigated whether C. butyricum could alleviate neuroinflammation induced by chronic T. gondii infection. C. butyricum supplementation significantly reduced the number of Iba1+ cells (microglia marker) in the hippocampal Cornu Ammonis 1 (CA1), Cornu Ammonis 3 (CA3) and Dentate Gyrus (DG) regions of T. gondii-infected mice (Fig. 5A and B). Moreover, C. butyricum inhibited activation of microglia induced by T. gondii infection, as evidenced by increased solidity and circularity indices (Fig. 5C and D). Similarly, C. butyricum also decreased the number of GFAP+ cells (astrocyte marker) and suppressed their morphological changes of activation induced by infection (Fig. 5E-H). Correspondingly, C. butyricum supplementation reduced mRNA expression of pro-inflammatory mediators including TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β (Fig. 5I-K) along with decreased protein levels of IL-12, IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-6 (Fig. 5L-O) in the hippocampus of T. gondii-infected mice. Thus, C. butyricum relieves hippocampal neuroinflammation caused by chronic T. gondii infection.

C. butyricum attenuated abnormal synaptic pruning and nerve damage of chronic T. gondii-infected mice

Nerve damage caused by C1q-mediated abnormal synaptic pruning is implicated in the pathogenesis of neuropsychiatric disorders [36, 37]. Therefore, we assessed C. butyricum’s effects on synapse pruning, synaptic ultrastructure and neurite outgrowth of hippocampal neurons. With the immunofluorescence co-staining of C1q and PSD95, we observed that a higher percentage of C1q colocalized with PSD95 in the hippocampus of T. gondii-infected mice, while C. butyricum could reverse this effect (Fig. 6A). TEM revealed that C. butyricum administration attenuated synaptic ultrastructure impairment in the hippocampus of T. gondii-infected mice, as reflected by narrower synaptic clefts, thicker postsynaptic densities, elongated active zones, and increased synaptic curvature (Fig. 6B-F and S6). Notably, quantitative analysis revealed a small yet statistically significant reduction in synaptic curvature in Tg mice (1.043 ± 0.008) compared with uninfected mice (1.115 ± 0.015; mean difference: −0.072, SEM of difference = 0.011, 95% CI − 0.108 to − 0.029, Tukey-adjusted P < 0.001). Meanwhile, the reduced mRNA levels of synaptic markers SYN and PSD95, caused by T. gondii infection, were ameliorated following C. butyricum treatment (Fig. 6G). In addition, using Golgi-Cox staining, we observed that T. gondii-infected mice exhibited decreased total neurite length per cell and reduced number of neurite branches in the hippocampus, whereas C. butyricum treatment reversed these alterations (Fig. 6H-J). However, there were no obvious changes in the average neurite length per branch among four groups (P > 0.05) (Fig. 6K). Furthermore, Sholl analysis showed that C. butyricum treatment significantly increased the dendritic complexity in the hippocampus of T. gondii-infected mice, as indicated by a longer distance of the maximum intersections from the soma, and an increase in the sum number of dendritic intersections (Fig. 6L-N).These results indicate that C. butyricum prevents complement-mediated dysregulation of synaptic pruning induced by T. gondii infection, thereby alleviating synaptic and neurite impairment independently of parasite burden.

Butyrate-producing bacteria promoted by C. butyricum supplementation were implicated in the amelioration of TNDs

To understand how C. butyricum mitigates TNDs via regulating gut microbiota, we performed the KEGG functional analysis of gut microbiota with differential abundance post T. gondii infection and C. butyricum treatment. A total of 26 functional orthologs at level 2 were altered in T. gondii-infected mice compared with uninfected mice (Table S2). Moreover, compared with T. gondii-infected mice, C. butyricum administration was associated with microbial functional shifts in 13 functional orthologs, including metabolism of cofactors and vitamins, amino acids, terpenoids and polyketides, glycan biosynthesis, nucleotide, replication and repair, immune diseases, environmental adaptation and endocrine system (Table S2). Notably, C. butyricum significantly reduced functional orthologs associated with “neurodegenerative disease” at level 2 (Fig. 7A), and specifically, attenuated functional orthologs associated with “lipopolysaccharide biosynthesis” and “Alzheimer’s disease” at level 3 (Fig. 7B and C), as well as Parkinson’s disease, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), and Huntington’s disease in the infected mice (Fig. 7D).

C. butyricum improved neuropsychiatric disorders through the gut microbiota

To determine whether alterations in gut microbiota directly mediate the beneficial effects of C. butyricum on TNDs, we performed fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT). Fecal suspensions from Con mice, Cb mice, Tg mice, and Tg-Cb mice were transplanted into antibiotic-pretreated recipient mice, designated as ConFMT (mice transplanted with microbiota from Con mice), CbFMT (mice transplanted with microbiota from Cb mice), TgFMT (mice transplanted with microbiota from Tg mice), and Tg-CbFMT groups (mice transplanted with microbiota from Tg-Cb mice), respectively (Fig. 8A). Compared with the ConFMT mice, TgFMT mice displayed impaired gut integrity, including the decreased colonic ZO-1+ cells and increased mRNA levels of TNF-α and IL-1β, while these indices were attenuated in the colons of Tg-CbFMT mice (Fig. S7A-D). These results highlight that gut microbiota is essential for the protective role of C. butyricum in gut damage induced by T. gondii infection.

Importantly, compared with the ConFMT mice and Tg-CbFMT mice, TgFMT mice exhibited cognitive decline and anxiety/depression-like behaviors, reflected by a decreased NODI in the NOR, a reduced PDI in the NLR, a diminished novel arm exploration time in the Y-maze spatial memory test, a lower central zone exploration (distance and time) in the OFT, a decline in open arms preference (time and entries) in the EPMT, and a longer time immobile in the TST (Fig. 8B-P). Moreover, compared with TgFMT mice, Tg-CbFMT mice showed a remission of hippocampal neurite impairment with increased total neurite length per cell, higher number of neurite branches, enhanced dendritic complexity, longer distance of the maximum intersections from the soma, and higher sum number of dendritic intersections (Fig. 9A-G). Consistently, we observed upregulated SYN and PSD95 mRNA levels alongside downregulated mRNA levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6 and IL-1β) in the hippocampus of Tg-CbFMT mice compared to TgFMT mice (Fig. 9H-K). Overall, these results demonstrate that C. butyricum mitigates TNDs by reshaping the gut microbiota.

Discussion

In this study, we identified parallel gut microbiota alterations in AD patients, MCI patients, and chronic T. gondii-infected mice, characterized by significant abundance reduction in butyrate-producing bacteria. Moreover, we reported that dietary supplementation with C. butyricum, a potential psychobiotic, remodeled gut microbiota homeostasis, repaired leaky gut, ameliorated cognitive impairment and anxiety/depression-like behaviors in chronic T. gondii-infected mice. In addition, C. butyricum mitigated glial cells- and C1q-mediated abnormal synaptic pruning, and consequently, prevented the impairment of synaptic ultrastructure and the decrease of neuronal complexity. Notably, through FMT, we confirmed that these beneficial effects of C. butyricum were largely due to its ability to reshape gut microbiota. In summary, our findings uncovered that C. butyricum prevents TNDs by modulating glial cells-mediated abnormal synaptic pruning via the microbiota-gut-brain axis (Fig. 10).

Gut microbiota dysbiosis is gradually established as a causal factor for the development of neuropsychiatric disorders, including TNDs [38–40]. Here, we observed that, in parallel with observations in AD and MCI patients [41, 42], T. gondii infection altered the gut microbiota composition in mice, characterized by the reduced ratio of Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidota (F/B) at the phylum level. Interestingly, our study identified a significant decline of butyrate-producing bacteria in the feces of AD and MCI patients and T. gondii-infected mice at the genus level, including the genera Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group, Butyricicoccus, Oscillibacter and Akkermansia. It is reported that butyrate-producing bacteria are gut symbionts that ferment undigested dietary fibers to generate butyrate [19]. Previous studies have shown the decreased butyrate levels in the serum of AD patients [43], T. gondii IgG-seropositive individuals and infected mice [17]. Butyrate is a microbial metabolite with neuroprotective properties [44]. Overall, these data imply that gut dysbiosis microbiota characterized by reduced butyrate-producing bacteria is a common feature in neurodegeneration, including TNDs.

To explore the role of butyrate-producing bacteria in TNDs, we administered C. butyricum, a gut symbiont found in the feces of 10–20% of healthy individuals, to T. gondii-infected mice. As a probiotic, C. butyricum has been reported to modulate gut microbiota structure of host [45]. For instance, C. butyricum supplementation increases the ratio of F/B and the abundance of the genus Lachnospiraceae in obese mice [26]. Here, we observed that dietary supplementation with C. butyricum restored the abundance of Firmicutes and Bacteroidota, resulting in the improvement in the F/B ratio. Two previous studies have shown that C. butyricum contains the butyrate kinase (buk) gene and increases butyryl-CoA (but) gene abundance, a gene of primary importance for butyrate production in the intestine, in the host fecal microbiota [46, 47]. Consistently, our study revealed that C. butyricum supplementation led to an increase in butyrate-producing genera such as Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group, Prevotellaceae_UCG-001, Eubacterium_xylanophilum_group, Oscillibacter, Blautia, Weissella, as well as other probiotics such as Colidextribacter, Lactobacillus, Akkermansia, which play significant roles in microbiota-derived SCFAs synthesis and gut barrier integrity maintenance [48–50]. In addition, we found that chronic T. gondii infection increased abundance of LPS-associated genera such as Alistipes and Alloprevotella [51, 52]. The genus Alistipes has been reported to impair intestinal mucosal and epithelial integrity [53], allowing LPS translocation into blood circulation or the brain and causing cognitive impairment. However, C. butyricum supplementation decreased the abundance of the genera Alloprevotella and Klebsiella in T. gondii-infected mice, accompanied by the decline of serum LPS. Overall, C. butyricum effectively ameliorates gut microbiota dysbiosis induced by chronic T. gondii infection. It is reported that gut microbiota dysbiosis contributes to the disorder of gut homeostasis, including intestinal barrier impairment and inflammation [29, 33]. Moreover, upregulated pro-inflammatory cytokines have been reported to disrupt tight junctions, impairing the integrity of the gut-vascular barrier [54, 55]. Here, we found that C. butyricum significantly downregulated colonic mRNA levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α). This protective effect of C. butyricum was also observed in obese mice [26]. Importantly, fecal microbiota from T. gondii-infected mice impaired intestinal barrier integrity and induced gut inflammation in recipient mice, whereas these pathologies were alleviated in the group with fecal microbiota from Tg-Cb mice. Therefore, C. butyricum supplementation exerts beneficial effects on intestinal barrier integrity via reversing gut microbiota balance.

Abnormal gut microbiota profiles are closely implicated in TNDs via the gut-brain axis [17, 18]. In the present study, we found that C. butyricum supplementation significantly reduced the relative abundance of gut microbiota associated with “neurodegenerative diseases” (level 2) and “Alzheimer’s disease” (level 3) in T. gondii-infected mice. This attracted us to explore the protective effects of C. butyricum supplementation on TNDs. Using multiple classic behavioral tests, we demonstrated that C. butyricum administration effectively prevented TNDs. Previous studies also reported that C. butyricum exerted beneficial effects on brain function disorders, including cognitive deficits and depression [11, 26]. These results jointly suggest C. butyricum as a neuroprotective probiotic. Notably, we provide evidence that reshaping the composition of gut microbiota is attributed to the protective effects of C. butyricum on TNDs.

Neuronal or synaptic impairment is a hallmark of neurodegeneration [56, 57]. We observed that C. butyricum significantly mitigated synaptic damage in chronic T. gondii-infected mice, evidenced by increased postsynaptic density, lengthened active zones, and narrowed synaptic clefts. SYN and PSD95 are essential mediators of synaptic plasticity and synaptogenesis [58]. Decreased hippocampal levels of SYN and PSD95 have been documented in both AD patients and T. gondii-infected mice [28, 59]. In this study, hippocampal SYN and PSD95 mRNA levels were markedly reduced in T. gondii-infected mice, while C. butyricum effectively prevented these reductions. Neuronal atrophy and reduced morphological complexity in the hippocampal CA1 region are key contributors to T. gondii-induced damage in cognition- and memory-associated neural circuits [17, 28]. We observed that C. butyricum colonization alleviated T. gondii-induced impairment of dendritic growth and morphological complexity in neurons, demonstrated by increased total dendritic length, distance of the maximum intersections from the soma, dendritic branch number, and intersection counts. Notably, the microbiota from T. gondii-infected mice induced neuropathological changes in recipient mice, while these pathologies were alleviated in Tg-CbFMT mice. Therefore, C. butyricum exerts beneficial effects on neuronal damage in TNDs by modulating gut microbiota homeostasis.

Neuroinflammation, characterized by the activation of microglia and astrocytes, induces neuronal or synaptic impairment, and consequently, cognitive and emotional deficits [60, 61]. For example, pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1β, have been reported to induce neuronal apoptosis and inhibit synaptophysin expression [62, 63]. Here, C. butyricum significantly ameliorated neuroinflammation in the hippocampus by reducing the number of activated Iba1+ microglia and GFAP+ astrocytes and downregulating the expression of inflammatory cytokines in the hippocampus of T. gondii-infected mice. Furthermore, abnormal synaptic pruning triggered by activated glial cells is also closely associated with neurodegeneration [64, 65]. For example, abnormal synaptic pruning is activated early in the AD [56]. C1q, secreted by activated glial cells, induces abnormal synaptic pruning by labeling synapses and exacerbating both the destruction of synaptic ultrastructure and the reduction in neuronal density [36]. Here, we found that chronic T. gondii infection increased the colocalization of C1q with PSD95-positive puncta in the hippocampus of mice, whereas C. butyricum significantly downregulated this colocalization and increased the level of PSD95 protein. In a murine neuroinflammation model induced by LPS, C1q-dependent microglial phagocytosis of synapses is reported to mediate the loss of hippocampal synapses [66]. Notably, the complement system serves as a critical mediator linking gut microbiota and their derived SCFAs to neuropsychiatric disorders [67, 68]. All these findings indicate that preventing abnormal synaptic pruning mediated by glial cells is the potential mechanism by which C. butyricum ameliorates TNDs.

Beyond resident glia, other reports demonstrated that the persistence of toxoplasma bradyzoite cysts in neurons is also regulated by IFN-γ-dependent immunity, involving infiltrating lymphocytes and monocytes that create a state of low-grade neuroinflammation [69–71]. Key cytokines mediating this immune control include IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-12 from T cells, and IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α from monocytes [72, 73]. Here, we found that chronic T. gondii infection significantly increased levels of IL-12, IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-6 in the serum, colon and brain, whereas C. butyricum administration reversed these increases, indicating its role in attenuating peripheral immune activation induced by this parasite. Nonetheless, the detailed effects and underlying mechanisms require future elucidation.

Furthermore, cysts burden is a hallmark of chronic T. gondii infection and has been implicated in associated neuropsychiatric disorders [74]. Recent reports have demonstrated that persistent behavioral alterations can occur even with very low or undetectable parasite loads in animal models [75, 76], suggesting that cyst burden alone does not fully account for TND pathogenesis. Instead, gut-brain pathology during chronic toxoplasmosis appears to be driven more strongly by the host anti-parasite immune response than by direct parasite burden [15], with gut microbiota dysbiosis and associated neuroinflammation playing key roles [18]. Supporting this, the present study uncovered that chronic T. gondii infection induced a decline in butyrate-producing bacteria. C. butyricum alleviates neuroinflammation and synaptic pathology induced by chronic T. gondii infection by reshaping the structure and function of the core microbiota. However, dietary C. butyricum did not affect cysts burden in the brain of Tg mice. Surface antigen 1 (SAG1), a specific antigen expressed in the bradyzoites of T. gondii, has also been used as an indicator of parasite presence in chronic phases in previous studies [77]. Similar to cysts burden, C. butyricum administration did not decrease the SAG1 levels in the colon and hippocampus of Tg mice. Taken together, the beneficial effects of C. butyricum on chronic infection-associated neuropsychiatric symptoms are independent on parasite load, but are closely linked to microbiota alterations.

Conclusion

Currently, effective therapeutic interventions are lacking for TNDs. In the present study, we revealed a dysbiotic core microbial profile of chronic T. gondii infection, characterized by a decline in butyrate-producing bacteria. Moreover, the critical role of butyrate-producing bacteria in TNDs was confirmed by the FMT experiment. Notably, to our knowledge, this is the first to report that C. butyricum, a psychobiotic bacterium of established clinical translational value, possesses robust therapeutic potential against TNDs, and is presently undergoing clinical evaluation in Parkinson’s disease and autism spectrum disorder (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06548256; https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06677840). In detail, C. butyricum alleviates neuroinflammation and synaptic pathology induced by chronic T. gondii infection by reshaping the structure and function of the core microbiota. Overall, our work supports that targeting microbiota composition and function may provide novel, mechanism-based therapeutic strategy for TNDs.
