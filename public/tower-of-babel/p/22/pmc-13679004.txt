Concordance between large language models and orthodontists in photo-based orthodontic assessment
Authors: Feridun Abay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe widespread use of digital platforms for preliminary health information has increased interest in artificial intelligence (AI)-based tools in dentistry. Large language model (LLM)-based systems are increasingly used for patient education and preliminary clinical guidance. However, their clinical consistency in orthodontic assessments based solely on intraoral photographs remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the clinical appropriateness of LLM-generated orthodontic assessments compared with blinded expert orthodontist evaluations.MethodsThis blinded comparative agreement study included 104 patients aged ≥ 12 years undergoing orthodontic evaluation. For each case, three standardized intraoral photographs and basic demographic data were submitted to two LLM-based systems (ChatGPT-4o and Grok 4.1) using a standardized prompt. Responses to five clinical questions were independently evaluated by three expert orthodontists using a 5-point Likert scale. Consensus scores were obtained by averaging the ratings of the three evaluators. Inter-system comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Bonferroni and Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) corrections were applied for multiple comparisons.ResultsMean ratings were generally ≥ 3, indicating moderate clinical acceptability. After correction for multiple comparisons, the most robust finding was the significantly higher rating assigned to ChatGPT-4o for the recommended orthodontic treatment approach in Dental Class I patients. In the 12–15-year subgroup, ChatGPT-4o also received higher ratings for treatment recommendation; however, statistical significance was retained only after FDR correction and not after Bonferroni adjustment. No significant differences were observed across most other evaluated parameters, particularly malocclusion classification. In patients older than 15 years, paired differences between the two systems were highly similar, resulting in an insufficient number of non-zero differences for reliable Wilcoxon signed-rank testing.ConclusionsLLM-based systems may provide clinically acceptable outputs in selected orthodontic assessment domains when evaluations are based solely on intraoral photographs. However, variability persists in complex decision-making tasks, particularly treatment planning. Introduction

Orthodontic anomalies are common developmental conditions observed during childhood and adolescence, and if left untreated, they may lead to esthetic, functional, and psychosocial problems. Impairments in mastication and speech, increased susceptibility to periodontal complications, and temporomandibular joint disorders are among the potential long-term consequences of these conditions [1]. Therefore, early diagnosis and timely intervention are critical, as they allow clinicians to utilize the individual’s growth potential to reduce treatment complexity and duration. Current evidence suggests that orthodontic interventions implemented during the developmental period may help limit the progression of skeletal discrepancies and improve oral health outcomes [2, 3].

In recent years, the way individuals seek information about orthodontic treatment has changed substantially. The internet, social media platforms, and digital content-sharing tools enable patients to obtain preliminary information regarding treatment options, duration, and expected outcomes [4]. However, the accuracy and reliability of orthodontic content available in digital environments may vary considerably, potentially leading to unrealistic expectations and communication challenges during treatment [5, 6]. Accordingly, the extent to which information obtained from digital sources aligns with clinical reality has become an important area of investigation.

Parallel to this evolving digital information ecosystem, the use of artificial intelligence (AI)-based systems in healthcare has increased rapidly. Large language model (LLM)-based systems have emerged as potential tools for patient education, clinical decision support, and preliminary assessment [7]. The literature indicates that these systems are capable of generating comprehensive and context-aware responses to medical queries; however, their performance may vary in scenarios requiring complex clinical reasoning [8]. Furthermore, differences in accuracy across systems when responding to clinical research questions have also been reported [9].

In orthodontics and dentistry, most existing AI-related studies have focused on radiographic datasets, cephalometric analyses, or structured diagnostic inputs. Deep learning-based systems have demonstrated promising results in malocclusion classification and radiographic assessment tasks [10]. In the field of radiology, large language models have shown competence in structured report generation, yet they have not consistently achieved expert-level performance in contextual clinical decision-making processes [11, 12]. Despite these advancements, the clinical consistency of general-purpose LLM systems in orthodontic assessments based solely on intraoral photographs and limited demographic data remains largely unexplored. In particular, there is a notable gap in the literature regarding the extent to which photo-based evaluations conducted without radiographic or comprehensive clinical examination data align with expert orthodontic judgments. At the time of the study, ChatGPT-4o (OpenAI) and Grok 4.1 (xAI) were among the most widely accessible multimodal large language model systems available to the general public. Both platforms were capable of processing image-based inputs and generating detailed clinical explanations, making them suitable candidates for evaluating the potential role of general-purpose LLMs in orthodontic assessment. Furthermore, the two systems were developed by different organizations and trained using distinct architectures and optimization strategies, allowing a comparative evaluation of their clinical consistency under identical assessment conditions.

Given the increasing tendency of patients to seek preliminary assessments through digital tools, determining the degree to which AI-generated evaluations correspond with expert clinical decisions is essential for patient safety and expectation management. It is therefore necessary to establish, through scientific evidence, whether such systems should be positioned as independent decision-makers or as supportive tools under professional supervision.

The aim of this study was to comparatively evaluate the clinical consistency of large language model-based AI systems in orthodontic assessments performed using limited intraoral photographic data, against blinded reference evaluations provided by expert orthodontists. In doing so, the study seeks to clarify the potential role and limitations of photo-based AI assessments in clinical orthodontic practice.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was designed as a blinded, comparative agreement study evaluating the clinical appropriateness of large language model (LLM)-based artificial intelligence systems in orthodontic assessments based on intraoral photographs. The extent to which AI-generated evaluations were clinically appropriate was analyzed through independent assessments conducted by three expert orthodontists. The study was observational in nature and aimed to assess clinical appropriateness and evaluation consistency rather than diagnostic accuracy.

Ethical approval

The study protocol was approved by the Giresun University Medical Research Ethics Committee (Approval Date: 18 February 2026; Decision No: 12). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All patient data were anonymized prior to analysis. Clinical trial registration number: Not applicable.

Sample and power analysis

A total of 104 individuals who presented for orthodontic evaluation and met the inclusion criteria were included in the study. The minimum required sample size was calculated using G*Power software during the planning stage. Because no previous study with an identical design was available to provide a precise non-parametric effect size estimate for the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, a conventionally assumed medium effect size was used for preliminary sample size estimation. Based on 95% statistical power and a 5% significance level, the minimum required sample size was determined to be 55 participants. The final sample size exceeded this requirement.

Expert evaluation and blinding

Three expert orthodontists independently evaluated each case based on the intraoral photographs and demographic information. The experts were unaware of the AI-generated responses when forming their own clinical judgments. These preliminary clinical judgments were established to minimize potential bias and ensure objective case assessment. They were used for evaluator calibration purposes and were not included in the statistical analyses. Subsequently, AI-generated responses were presented to the experts; however, the identity of the system (ChatGPT-4o or Grok) was concealed. The experts were asked to rate the clinical appropriateness of each AI response using a 5-point Likert scale.

The Likert scale was defined as follows:

1 = Clinically inappropriate

2 = Poor

3 = Moderately acceptable

4 = Good

5 = Highly clinically appropriate

Importantly, the evaluation was not based on direct comparison between AI responses and the experts’ own answers, but rather on the perceived clinical appropriateness of the AI outputs.

For each patient and each evaluation question, the ratings assigned by the three orthodontists were averaged to obtain a single consensus score for each AI system. Statistical analyses were performed using these mean scores, thereby preserving the patient as the unit of analysis and avoiding non-independence of repeated ratings.

Reliability analysis

To assess intra-rater reliability, 10% of the cases were randomly selected and re-evaluated by the same experts after a defined time interval. Test–retest reliability was calculated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) based on a two-way random-effects model. In addition, inter-rater reliability was evaluated to determine the consistency among the three orthodontists who rated the AI-generated responses. Inter-rater agreement was also assessed using ICC values derived from a two-way random-effects model with absolute agreement. ICC values were interpreted according to commonly accepted thresholds as follows: <0.50 = poor reliability, 0.50–0.75 = moderate reliability, 0.75–0.90 = good reliability, and > 0.90 = excellent reliability. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Although Likert-scale ratings are ordinal variables, mean ± standard deviation (SD) values were reported for descriptive purposes to facilitate comparison with previous studies and to summarize central tendency and variability. However, because of the ordinal nature of the data and the non-normal distribution of paired differences, inter-system comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test.

Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Because multiple subgroup comparisons were performed, post-hoc multiple comparison corrections were applied using both the Bonferroni method and the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. Adjusted p values were calculated and reported alongside unadjusted p values. Subgroup analyses were considered exploratory and were interpreted cautiously. To facilitate interpretation of the clinical relevance of statistically significant findings, effect sizes (r = Z/√N) were calculated and interpreted as small (0.10), medium (0.30), and large (0.50) effects.

Findings according to dental class

Among patients with Dental Class I malocclusion, ChatGPT-4o received significantly higher ratings than Grok for Q1 (recommended orthodontic treatment approach) (Table 2). This difference remained statistically significant after both Bonferroni and FDR correction (Table 9). No statistically significant differences were observed for Q2–Q5. Mean scores for both systems were generally ≥ 3, indicating moderate to good clinical appropriateness. In Dental Class II and Dental Class III patients, no statistically significant differences were identified between ChatGPT-4o and Grok across any of the evaluated parameters (Tables 3 and 4). Mean scores for all questions were generally ≥ 3, suggesting comparable performance between the two systems.

Findings according to age groups

In the 12–15-year age group, ChatGPT-4o received significantly higher ratings than Grok for Q1 (Table 5). This finding remained statistically significant after FDR correction but not after Bonferroni correction (Table 9). Therefore, this result should be interpreted cautiously as an exploratory finding. No significant differences were observed for the remaining parameters. For patients older than 15 years, paired differences between the two systems were limited and symmetrically distributed. Consequently, an insufficient number of non-zero differences was available for reliable Wilcoxon signed-rank testing. Descriptive analyses demonstrated comparable performance between systems, with mean scores exceeding 3 across all evaluated parameters (Table 6).

Findings according to sex

Among female and male patients, a small number of subgroup comparisons reached nominal statistical significance before adjustment for multiple comparisons (Tables 7 and 8). However, none of these findings remained statistically significant after Bonferroni or FDR correction (Table 9). Therefore, these results should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Overall, both AI systems demonstrated comparable performance across most evaluation domains. After adjustment for multiple comparisons, the most robust finding was the higher Q1 rating achieved by ChatGPT-4o in Dental Class I patients, whereas the remaining subgroup differences should be interpreted with caution.

Discussion

The present study evaluated the clinical appropriateness of two large language model (LLM)-based artificial intelligence systems, ChatGPT-4o and Grok 4.1, in orthodontic assessments performed using only intraoral photographs and limited demographic information. Overall, both systems generated clinically acceptable responses across several assessment domains. However, variability was observed in parameters requiring more complex clinical reasoning and individualized decision-making, particularly treatment planning and treatment duration estimation.

The impact of orthodontic anomalies on oral health-related quality of life has been well documented [1, 13], and timely intervention during periods of active growth remains a key determinant of successful treatment outcomes [2]. Consequently, evaluating the ability of emerging digital technologies to provide clinically appropriate orthodontic recommendations is increasingly important. Previous studies have reported considerable variability in the quality and reliability of orthodontic information available through social media platforms, online resources, and digital communication channels [4–6, 14]. As AI-based conversational systems become increasingly accessible to the general public, patients may use these tools as preliminary sources of orthodontic information, making the evaluation of their clinical appropriateness particularly relevant.

Previous investigations have demonstrated that LLM-based systems perform well in structured information retrieval and content generation but exhibit greater variability when tasks require advanced clinical reasoning and individualized decision-making [8, 9]. Similar observations have been reported in radiology, where LLMs have shown promising performance in structured reporting yet have not consistently achieved expert-level performance in complex diagnostic and treatment-related decisions [14, 15]. The findings of the present study are consistent with these observations and suggest that AI performance may decline as the complexity and multidimensional nature of the clinical task increase.

In image-based applications, artificial intelligence has demonstrated performance approaching that of human experts in several diagnostic classification tasks. Deep learning algorithms have shown high accuracy in orthodontic image classification and cephalometric landmark identification [16, 17]. However, these applications generally involve predefined classification problems with clearly defined outputs. In contrast, orthodontic treatment planning requires the integration of multiple biological, skeletal, dental, behavioral, esthetic, and prognostic factors. Consequently, generating individualized treatment recommendations represents a substantially more complex challenge than image classification alone.

The subgroup analyses performed in the present study revealed differences between the two AI systems, particularly regarding the recommended orthodontic treatment approach. After correction for multiple comparisons using Bonferroni and Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate procedures, the most robust finding was the significantly higher rating assigned to ChatGPT-4o in Dental Class I patients. This observation may reflect the greater heterogeneity of treatment alternatives frequently encountered in Class I malocclusions, where multiple treatment strategies may be considered clinically acceptable. Conversely, the relatively limited differences observed in Class II and Class III malocclusions may be associated with the presence of more distinct morphological characteristics and more predictable treatment pathways.

Age-related findings also deserve consideration. The 12–15-year age group represents a critical period for growth modification and orthopedic intervention. Accurate assessment of growth potential, skeletal maturity, and treatment timing typically requires radiographic and cephalometric evaluation, which were intentionally excluded from the present study design. Consequently, the variability observed in AI-generated recommendations within this subgroup may reflect the restricted information available to the systems. Furthermore, because statistical significance in this subgroup was retained only after false discovery rate correction and not after Bonferroni adjustment, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and regarded as exploratory rather than confirmatory.

An interesting observation was that the responses generated by ChatGPT-4o and Grok 4.1 were highly similar in patients older than 15 years. The limited number of non-zero paired differences prevented reliable Wilcoxon signed-rank testing in this subgroup. Rather than representing a methodological weakness, this finding may indicate substantial agreement between the two systems when evaluating patients whose treatment decisions rely less heavily on growth-related considerations and more heavily on relatively stable dental characteristics.

Sex-related subgroup analyses yielded nominally significant findings before correction for multiple comparisons. However, none of these findings remained statistically significant after adjustment procedures. Therefore, the present results do not provide sufficient evidence to support clinically meaningful sex-related differences in AI performance. Although previous studies have suggested that LLM outputs may be influenced by contextual cues and patterns present within training datasets [18], larger studies are required before definitive conclusions regarding sex-related variability can be drawn.

Notably, only minimal differences were observed in parameters such as malocclusion classification, which depend primarily on visual pattern recognition. This finding is consistent with previous reports indicating that AI systems generally perform more consistently in structured classification tasks than in individualized treatment planning scenarios [16]. The distinction between image classification and comprehensive clinical decision-making is particularly important when interpreting the capabilities and limitations of contemporary LLM-based systems.

Overall, the findings suggest that AI performance should not be evaluated solely on a binary correct-versus-incorrect basis. Rather, performance appears to vary according to clinical complexity, developmental considerations, and the degree of individualized reasoning required [19]. Tasks involving visual categorization and standardized outputs appear more suitable for current AI systems than tasks requiring integration of multiple clinical variables and long-term prognostic considerations.

Furthermore, studies investigating the influence of LLMs on clinical diagnostic reasoning have suggested that AI-generated outputs may both support and inadvertently bias clinician decision-making processes [20–22]. Orthodontic treatment planning requires integration of growth potential, biomechanics, patient compliance, facial esthetics, periodontal health, and long-term stability considerations. Consequently, text-based systems operating solely from intraoral photographs would be expected to demonstrate greater variability than specialized diagnostic systems trained for specific orthodontic tasks.

Recent studies evaluating large language model-based artificial intelligence systems in dentistry have reported promising performance in generating clinically relevant responses and supporting evidence-based information retrieval [23]. However, these studies have also emphasized that AI-generated outputs may contain inaccuracies, inconsistencies, or unsupported statements, highlighting the continued necessity of professional oversight [23, 24]. Similarly, previous investigations have suggested that artificial intelligence offers considerable opportunities in dental diagnosis, patient communication, and treatment planning, while simultaneously emphasizing that concerns related to reliability, transparency, explainability, and accountability continue to limit its independent use in routine clinical practice [25].

An important consideration when interpreting the present findings is that the AI systems were provided only with intraoral photographs and limited demographic information. Comprehensive orthodontic diagnosis routinely requires integration of cephalometric analyses, radiographic examinations, facial photographs, skeletal growth assessment, periodontal evaluation, and detailed clinical findings. Therefore, the present study should not be interpreted as an assessment of the ability of LLM-based systems to perform comprehensive orthodontic diagnosis. Rather, it evaluates their capacity to generate clinically appropriate preliminary assessments under restricted information conditions.

Taken together, the findings suggest that although LLM-based systems may generate clinically acceptable responses in selected orthodontic assessment domains, they do not yet demonstrate the consistency, contextual understanding, or diagnostic comprehensiveness required for independent clinical decision-making. Current evidence supports their use as supportive decision-assistance tools operating under professional supervision rather than as substitutes for clinical expertise. The ultimate responsibility for diagnosis, treatment planning, informed consent, and patient outcomes remains with the clinician, emphasizing the continued importance of professional judgment, ethical accountability, and evidence-based practice.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of the present study.

First, the evaluated AI systems were provided only with intraoral photographs and limited demographic information. Comprehensive orthodontic diagnosis and treatment planning typically require integration of radiographic examinations, cephalometric analyses, facial assessment, growth evaluation, periodontal status, and detailed clinical findings. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as reflecting the performance of ChatGPT-4o and Grok 4.1 in photo-based preliminary assessment scenarios rather than their ability to perform comprehensive orthodontic diagnosis or definitive treatment planning.

Second, all analyses were conducted using the specific versions of ChatGPT-4o and Grok 4.1 available at a single point in time. Because large language model systems undergo continuous updates and optimization, their performance characteristics may change over time, limiting the generalizability of the present findings to future model versions. In addition, only a single response was generated for each case using a single standardized prompt. Therefore, the inherent response variability of LLMs and the potential influence of alternative prompt formulations were not evaluated.

Third, clinical appropriateness was assessed using expert-based Likert ratings. Although evaluator blinding procedures were implemented and both intra-rater and inter-rater reliability analyses demonstrated good-to-excellent agreement, some degree of subjectivity is inherently associated with expert judgment. While evaluator consistency was found to be satisfactory, residual evaluator-related variability cannot be completely excluded.

Fourth, multiple subgroup analyses were performed. Although Bonferroni and Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate corrections were applied, the possibility of residual Type I error cannot be entirely eliminated. Consequently, subgroup-specific findings should be interpreted cautiously and considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence.

Finally, only two general-purpose LLM systems were evaluated. Therefore, the findings may not be directly generalizable to other AI platforms, specialized orthodontic AI systems, or future multimodal models specifically developed for dental applications.

Overall, the present results should be regarded as preliminary evidence regarding the potential role of multimodal large language models in orthodontic assessment. Future studies incorporating comprehensive diagnostic records, prospective clinical validation, larger multicenter datasets, and comparisons with additional AI systems will be necessary to establish the clinical utility, reliability, and generalizability of these technologies.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this study, large language model-based artificial intelligence systems demonstrated clinically acceptable performance in several orthodontic assessment domains when evaluations were based solely on intraoral photographs and limited demographic information. However, performance varied according to the complexity of the clinical task, with greater variability observed in individualized treatment planning and treatment duration estimation.

The findings suggest that contemporary multimodal LLM systems may serve as useful adjunctive tools for preliminary orthodontic assessment, patient education, and clinical decision support. Nevertheless, they do not currently demonstrate the diagnostic comprehensiveness, contextual understanding, or consistency required for independent clinical decision-making. Therefore, their use should remain under professional supervision, with final diagnostic and treatment-related decisions made by qualified orthodontists.

Future research incorporating comprehensive diagnostic records, multimodal data integration, prospective clinical validation, and larger multicenter datasets will be necessary to further define the clinical utility, reliability, and safe implementation of artificial intelligence systems in orthodontic practice.
