Airway management analysis in the rescue environment of the emergency service Zug: a retrospective real-world evaluation
Authors: Felix Brinkmann, Gian Cajoeri, Sebastian Daniel Sahli, Philipp Karl Buehler, Benedikt Florian Scherr
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAirway management is critical in prehospital care. This study quantified first pass success (FPS) for endotracheal intubation (ETI) in a Swiss emergency medical service (EMS) and identified factors associated with success.MethodsAll adult (≥ 18 years) ETI attempts documented in the EMS database between 1 January 2021 and 31 December 2024 were reviewed. Devices [direct laryngoscopy, standard-geometry videolaryngoscope (VL), hyperangulated VL], adjuncts [bougie, stylet], operators [critical-care paramedic (CCP), emergency physician (EP)] and setting were analyzed. The primary outcome was FPS; predictors were evaluated with univariate and multivariable logistic regression.ResultsETI was attempted in 270 patients (mean age 67.9 ± 16.4 year; 64.8% male); 55.6% intubations occurred during cardiopulmonary resuscitation. Overall FPS was 92.6% (250/270). Bougie use achieved 100% (49/49) FPS versus 91.0% (201/221) with a stylet (p = 0.03). Device-specific FPS was 94.3% (33/35, direct laryngoscopy), 94.0% (173/184, standard-geometry VL) and 86.3% (44/51, D-BLADE VL). FPS was significantly associated with D-BLADE VL use in patients with an anticipated difficult intubation (89,7% FPS, p < 0.001). CCP showed higher odds for FPS for CCP compared to EP (OR 3.61, 95% CI 1.14 to 11.45).ConclusionsBougie-assisted laryngoscopy was significantly associated with higher first pass success rates. When a difficult airway was anticipated, the hyperangulated D-BLADE videolaryngoscope showed higher odds for first pass success. Critical-care paramedics achieved first pass success rates comparable to those of emergency physicians.Trial registrationClinical Trials.gov: NCT06653166 (19 October 2024).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13049-025-01489-x.

Background

Airway management is pivotal in prehospital emergency care. Despite a growing range of supraglottic devices, endotracheal intubation (ETI) remains the gold standard because it best secures the airway, safeguards against aspiration, and enables reliable oxygenation and ventilation [1–4].

Rapid, first pass ETI is therefore crucial for emergency medical services (EMS) personnel; failure markedly increases morbidity and mortality [5–10]. First-attempt failure occurs in up to 20% of intubations in emergency departments and up to 40% in the prehospital setting, precipitating severe hypoxemia, aspiration, cardiac arrest, and death. Prehospital conditions—restricted space, poor lighting, and time pressure—exacerbate these risks and demand high levels of technical competence and appropriate adjuncts.

Videolaryngoscopy (VL), particularly with a Macintosh-style blade, is now widely recommended as the first-line approach for endotracheal intubation [1]. A growing body of evidence demonstrates that VL generally outperforms conventional direct laryngoscopy by providing improved glottic visualization and modestly increasing first pass success rates—especially in less experienced hands [11–15].

Among VL techniques, hyperangulated blades have been shown to further enhance glottic view, particularly in anatomically difficult airways. However, their use is technically more demanding and associated with a steeper learning curve, requiring specific training and greater operator experience. In contrast, Macintosh-style VL offers a smoother transition for clinicians familiar with direct laryngoscopy, serving as a practical bridge between traditional and video-assisted techniques.

Adjuncts such as stylets and bougies are routinely employed to facilitate tube placement. While several studies suggest that bougie-assisted intubation may improve first pass success, especially when used with VL, the interaction between device type—particularly hyperangulated VL—and bougie use remains poorly defined and underexplored in clinical practice [16–19]. Existing literature presents conflicting evidence regarding the impact of routine bougie use on first pass success, and comprehensive real-world data—particularly from European prehospital settings—are lacking.

Moreover, while differences in intubation outcomes between physicians and paramedics have been reported, large-scale cohort studies that systematically compare these provider groups under operational field conditions are rare [20–23].

This retrospective analysis of the Zug Emergency Medical Services aims to address these gaps by evaluating success rates, number of attempts, complication profiles, and user-reported device usability. By focusing on the role of bougie-assisted videolaryngoscopy across differently trained provider groups, this study seeks to generate evidence-based insights to inform future strategies for improving out-of-hospital airway management.

Methods

This retrospective observational cohort study analyzed endotracheal intubation by Zug Emergency Medical Services (RDZ) between 1 January 2021 and 31 December 2024.

This retrospective study of fully pseudonymised airway-management data was approved by the Ethics Committee Northwest & Central Switzerland (EKNZ; Project ID 2024–02051, 17 Oct 2024) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Swiss Human Research Act. The committee granted a waiver of individual informed consent under Art. 34 HRA because no intervention was performed, and participants cannot be reidentified.

Data were extracted from the electronic quality-management database maintained by Zug Emergency Medical Services (RDZ). This database retrospectively logs all prehospital missions, including patient demographics, clinical interventions, airway management details, and procedural outcomes. All videolaryngoscopy recordings were initially reviewed by the quality management team for inclusion in the database. For this retrospective analysis, encrypted, non-genetic routine data were accessed and pseudonymized prior to evaluation to ensure patient confidentiality.

Adult patients (aged ≥ 18 years) were eligible for inclusion if endotracheal intubation was attempted by RDZ personnel—either a board-certified emergency physician or a critical-care paramedic—during a primary EMS mission between 1 January 2021 and 31 December 2024. The only exclusion criterion was a documented refusal of data use in accordance with Swiss data protection regulations. No matching, sampling, or data imputation was applied. All eligible cases within the defined period were included.

In Switzerland, certification as an emergency physician (EP) requires postgraduate training across anesthesiology and intensive care, completion of advanced life support courses, at least 50 supervised prehospital missions, and a final examination. Critical-care paramedics (CCP) are dual-qualified, holding both the paramedic diploma (dual pathway combining college instruction and ambulance-based practice) and the postgraduate diploma in nurse anesthesia (qualified for anesthesia and advanced airway management under delegated supervision), and possess several years of prehospital and in-hospital anesthesia experience. Details of the required qualifications are provided in Supplemental Data 1. The RDZ operates three ambulances during daytime and two at night, supported by a dedicated rapid response vehicle staffed at all times by either an EP or a CCP. During the observation period, 11 EPs and 11 CCPs were actively involved in service provision. All vehicles are equipped with identical airway devices and drugs, ensuring system-wide consistency. The local difficult airway algorithm is derived from national and institutional guidelines and is provided as Supplemental Fig. 2.

Airway-related data were reviewed in detail, including number of attempts, devices used (e.g. videolaryngoscopy, bougie), and complications. The American Society of Anesthesiologists defines that a “difficult airway includes the clinical situation in which anticipated or unanticipated difficulty or failure is experienced by a physician trained in anesthesia care” [24]. “Anticipated difficult airway” was assessed using institutional criteria (MOANS, LEMON, Mallampati; Suppl. Figure 1). Exact reasons were not retrievable due to the retrospective design. “Definitive difficult airway” was defined as any case requiring more than one intubation attempt by the most experienced provider, as documented in the airway registry form and by video review, reflecting mainly visual obstacles.

The primary outcome of this study was first pass success (FPS), defined as successful endotracheal intubation on the initial attempt. Three different airway techniques were analyzed: videolaryngoscopy using the C-MAC® with a standard Macintosh blade, videolaryngoscopy with a hyperangulated C-MAC® D-BLADE (both Karl Storz SE & Co. KG, Germany), and conventional direct laryngoscopy. Each technique was used in combination with either an S-Guide bougie (VBM Medizintechnik GmbH, Germany) or a malleable stylet or the preformed guidewire of the D-BLADE, depending on provider preference and clinical situation.

Secondary outcomes included the occurrence of mechanical complications such as dental injury or soft-tissue trauma, oxygen desaturation defined as SpO₂ < 90% or a decrease of more than 5% points from baseline, and the provider category performing the procedure (emergency physician vs. critical-care paramedic). Additional variables included patient and mission characteristics, such as age, sex, presence of cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) at the time of intubation or drug-assisted ETI (DAI), intubation location (on-scene vs. in-ambulance), and time of day. DAI was performed according to the clinical assessment of the responsible CCP or EP. Standardized anesthesia protocols are available (see Supplemental Figs. 3 and 4); protocol “red” was applied in six cases, protocol “blue” in four cases, protocol “yellow” in 62 cases, an individualized alternative plan in 47 cases (as the plans were established only in 2021), and in one case no medication was required.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, categorical variables as counts and percentages. Two-sided Fisher’s exact tests (α = 0.05) assessed associations between categorical variables. A multivariable logistic-regression model examined predictors of FPS, providing odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals. Missing values for pre-oxygenation method or peri-intubation SpO₂ were managed by complete-case analysis. All analyses were conducted with Stata MP 18.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX) and GraphPad Prism 10.5 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA).

Results

Patient characteristics

Between 1 January 2021 and 31 December 2024, a total of 270 adult patients required prehospital endotracheal intubation (Fig. 1). The mean age was 67.9 ± 16.4 years, and 64.8% were male. Critical-care paramedics (CCPs) performed 237 intubations (87.8%), while emergency physicians (EPs) conducted 33 (12.2%). Cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) was the indication in 55.6% of cases. The remainder included post-ROSC coma, suspected stroke, seizures, trauma, and intoxications. Most procedures were performed during the day (79.6%) and at the scene (78.1%). Airway visualization was frequently impaired by secretions (in 39%), blood (in 14%), or vomitus (in 4%). The median NACA score was 6, with an on-scene mortality rate of 35.6%. Primary outcome: first pass success

First pass success (FPS) was achieved in 250 of 270 cases (92.6%). FPS was significantly higher when a bougie was used during the first attempt (100%) than with a stylet (91.0%, p = 0.03; corresponding to an absolute risk difference of 9%). All first pass failures occurred in the stylet group.

Unadjusted analysis revealed no significant differences in FPS between direct laryngoscopy (94.3%) and videolaryngoscopy (92.3%, p = 1.00), nor between the conventional direct laryngoscope, C-MAC and D-BLADE configurations (p = 0.176). However, anticipated airway difficulty (assessed according to the internal guidelines and algorithms, supplemental Fig. 1) was associated with reduced FPS: patients with predicted easy airways achieved 95.9% FPS versus 86.7% in the difficult group (p = 0.008). Within the difficult airway subgroup, FPS varied across techniques: 75% with direct laryngoscopy, 86.9% with C-MAC, and 89.7% with D-BLADE (p < 0.001).

Multivariate logistic regression (Fig. 2) confirmed that intubation by CCPs was independently associated with higher FPS (OR 3.61, 95% CI 1.14–11.45, p = 0.029), whereas an anticipated difficult airways reduced the odds of success (OR 0.29, 95% CI 0.10–0.83, p = 0.021). Neither intubation devices (C-MAC or D-BLADE) nor intubation location (scene vs. ambulance) were independent predictors.

Failed FPS correlated strongly with established difficult airway characteristics: patients with a suspected difficult airway had markedly lower FPS (34% vs. 65%, ASD = 0.65), as did those with a confirmed difficult airway (55% vs. 12%, ASD = 1.01). A higher mean Cormack–Lehane grade (2.0 ± 1.0 vs. 1.5 ± 0.6; ASD = 0.60) was also associated with failed FPS. Use of a C-MAC was more frequently observed in successful first attempts (69% vs. 55%, ASD = 0.40), whereas the D-BLADE was more often applied during second attempts following initial failure (35% vs. 18%).

Among procedural factors, pre-intubation bag-mask ventilation was associated with a moderate benefit (86% vs. 70%, ASD = 0.38), as did daytime operations (80% vs. 70%, ASD = 0.24). Conversely, CPR-related intubations (54% vs. 70%, ASD = 0.33) and on-scene intubations (77% vs. 90%, ASD = 0.35) were associated with lower FPS. The data regarding tools, devices and intubation characteristics is presented in Table 2.

Secondary outcomes and complications

Twenty patients (7.4%) required more than one attempt. Nineteen were successfully intubated on the second attempt, and one on the third. Optimization strategies included head repositioning (n = 8), use of a bougie (n = 5), switch to D-BLADE (n = 1), Suction assisted Laryngoscopy and Airway Decontamination (SALAD) technique (n = 2), and sedation adjustment (n = 1). No patient required a supraglottic airway or front-of-neck access.

Mechanical complications were rare, with soft tissue injury documented in three patients (1.1%). Oxygen desaturation (SpO₂ < 90% or decrease > 5% points from baseline) was observed in 11 of 118 intubations with available oxygenation data (excluding cases of CPR); six occurred after failed first pass attempts.

While differences in FPS related to time of day or intubation setting did not reach statistical significance in univariate analysis, trends favored in-ambulance intubations (96.6% [57/59] vs. 91.5% [193/211], p = 0.263) and procedures performed during daytime hours (93.5% [201/215] vs. 89.1% [49/55], p = 0.258).

Discussion

In our analysis, videolaryngoscopy was not associated with improved first pass success, whereas the hyperangulated D-BLADE was used successfully in the most challenging cases. BVM was associated with a moderate benefit, while there was a trend towards reduced success in CPR-related and on-scene intubations. These results highlight the value of early recognition of difficult airway, careful device selection, vigorous pre-oxygenation and bag-mask ventilation techniques – reinforced through training and simulation – may further mitigate the adverse impact of challenging environmental and procedural contexts on intubation outcomes.

Overall, video and direct laryngoscopy showed no significant difference in FPS in univariate analysis, but device choice mattered when a difficult intubation was anticipated. In the exploratory multivariate analysis, the subgroup with anticipated difficult airway the D-BLADE achieved the highest FPS and outperformed the standard geometry videolaryngoscope and conventional direct laryngoscopy. In a randomized trial regarding difficult airway, D-BLADE reached 97% FPS, outperforming a standard Macintosh-style video blade and direct laryngoscopy highlighting the advantage emerges in truly challenging situations [25]. Yet a prehospital meta-analysis covering 35.489 intubations found Macintosh-geometry VL superior in seven of ten studies, whereas hyperangulated systems were advantageous in just three of seven and often prolonged intubation time [26]. Taken together, the evidence suggests that hyperangulated VL blades confer their benefit when a difficult airway is predicted, whereas in the broader out-of-hospital setting success is more dependent on operator experience and contextual factors. A meta-analysis of 26 randomized trials likewise showed no overall FPS advantage for VL (RR 1.05, 95% CI 0.97–1.13); benefits appeared mainly in in-hospital settings or with inexperienced operators, while VL actually reduced FPS under prehospital conditions [27]. These findings underscore that VL advantages surface when novice clinicians work under in-hospital conditions, whereas in the austere prehospital environment direct laryngoscopy may remain a reliable choice. Dutch prehospital registry data echoed this pattern, with VL significantly improving first pass intubation rates but not improving the performance of highly experienced physicians [23]. Taken together, hyperangulated VL seems most useful when difficulty is foreseen; otherwise, operator skill and setting dominate outcomes.

Bougie use as primary adjunct during prehospital laryngoscopy produced a clear benefit in our cohort, raising FPS by an absolute 9% without any first pass intubation failures when using a bougie. This mirrors the result of the BEAM trial (FPS 96% vs. 82% with stylet) conducted in a single emergency department [16], but contrasts with the multicenter BOUGIE RCT (FPS 80.4% vs. 83.0% with stylet) enrolling critically ill adults across different emergency departments and intensive-care units [28]. Our data also supports a meta-analysis of 9.151 patients favoring bougie (RR 1.11, 95% CI 1.06–1.17), though with low certainty of evidence [29]. Although prolonged placement times have been reported [30], our zero-failure experience suggests that routine bougie deployment by trained prehospital teams can reduce the morbidity of repeated attempts. While Bougie use was associated with high FPS, selection bias (e.g., preferential use in anticipated easy airways or by more experienced users) may influence this finding in our retrospective study.

Intubating inside the ambulance did not differ from on-scene attempts, matching Broms et al., who reported ~ 89% vs. ~86% (at the scene) FPS [31]. Providers can achieve high intubation success in either setting and therefore choose either location according to logistics or patient condition.

FPS rates remained high during both day (93.5%) and night (89.1%) shifts despite challenges such as darkness or fatigue, consistent with air-ambulance data showing stable performance through 24 h shifts compared to 12 h shifts [32]. In contrast to this, a review regarding prehospital intubations found that nighttime reduces the rate of FPS [33]. Hospital studies have observed 17% lower odds in night-time FPS, especially in obese patients [34], underscoring the influence of context, team-configuration and case mix. Reduced FPS during CPR align with stress-induced technical challenges.

CCP achieved high FPS rates (FPS 93.7% vs. 84.8%; OR 3.61, 95% CI 1.14–11.45), comparable to EP in this cohort. However, the width of confidence intervals (reflecting the limited EP sample size, indicating reduced precision of the estimates and warranting cautious interpretation of the results), and late introduction of EP precludes definitive superiority conclusions and confounding by experience (uneven operator experience) cannot be excluded. Earlier meta-analyses reported higher intubation success rates for physicians over non-physicians [20], those did not account for team composition or provider training level. Recent data have demonstrated no significant difference in first pass success between EP and well-trained CCP. Earlier European HEMS data showed a comparable result in which physicians out-performed paramedics [21], yet our results are concordant with more recent UK data demonstrating equivalence when paramedics undergo extended training and practice within a structured airway protocol [22]. Importantly, airway management performance reflects not only the skills of the individual provider but also the functioning of the entire prehospital team and system. Taken together, the literature suggests that experience, training intensity, and system design may be stronger determinants of FPS than professional title per se.

Anticipating a difficult airway reduced first-attempt success to ~ 86%, echoing a systematic review linking difficult airway anatomy, contamination, restricted jaw and neck mobility and other “difficulty features” to first pass failure in the prehospital environment [33]. Structured pre-intubation assessments such as HEAVEN and early adoption of additional tools like the bougie or videolaryngoscopy therefore remain essential.

This study has several limitations: inherently, this retrospective analysis from a single emergency medical service restricts causal inference and limits generalizability beyond our specific organizational context. Reporting and selection bias as well as missing data may have influenced our results. Also, there is a relevant risk for selection bias as bougie and D-BLADE use was non-randomized, and operators likely selected tools based on perceived difficulty and experience. Provider type was not randomized, and EP entered the service late in the observed period, so temporal trends and accumulating experience could confound the apparent profession effect. This might be reflected in the conflicting outcomes between univariate and multivariate analysis concerning profession with a wide confidence interval in this analysis. Relevant patient factors such as BMI, comorbidities and validated airway-difficulty scores could not be recorded, leaving residual confounding possible. The retrospective design precluded detailed documentation of reasons for “anticipated difficult airway.” The “definitive difficult airway” classification was video‑based and may not capture non‑visual factors. Due to the small number of cases in some subgroups, there is also a relevant risk of overfitting in the multivariate linear regression analysis, and the results should be interpreted as exploratory. The study focused solely on immediate procedural outcomes without capturing longer term or in-hospital morbidity or mortality. Finally, counts in several strata were small, producing wide confidence intervals and increasing the risk of type-II error, particularly for device-specific subgroup analyses.

Nevertheless, together with Ångermann et al. [35], our series forms one of the largest European EMS datasets on bougie use and provides a benchmark for future prospective work.

Conclusions

Routine bougie-assisted videolaryngoscopy may optimize first pass success in prehospital settings, particularly with hyperangulated videolaryngoscopes when a difficult airway is anticipated. Advanced-trained critical-care paramedics can deliver airway management comparable to physicians in structured EMS systems. Neither intubation site, CPR context, nor time of day substantially affected FPS. While our data support a bougie-first strategy to maximize FPS, prospective studies should determine whether this procedural gain translates into reduced morbidity and mortality in the prehospital environment.
