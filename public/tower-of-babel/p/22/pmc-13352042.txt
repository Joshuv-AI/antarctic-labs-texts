Glutathione metabolism-linked ferroptosis in human seminoma: a spatial multi-omics mapping study
Authors: Chenyao Deng, Jiaxing Li, Zhongkai Guo, Jun Zhu, Qiancheng Zhao, Zishui Fang, Jiachen Zheng, Lingxiang Ran, Ruiyu Li, Xuesong Li, Kan Gong, Jing Peng, Aixiang Wang, Jingtao Guo, Yuzhuo Yang, Hui Jiang, Zirun Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTesticular cancer is the most common solid tumor in young men aged 15-44 years, with seminoma being a major subtype. To elucidate its poorly understood molecular mechanisms, we performed integrated spatial transcriptomics (ST) and spatial metabolomics (SM) on fresh-frozen seminoma tissues from four patients. ST identified 16 distinct cell clusters and spatially resolved transcriptional programs in tumor, invasive, tubular, and stromal regions, revealing tumor-enriched pathways like glycolysis, oxidative phosphorylation, and ferroptosis. Pseudotime analysis suggested a branched trajectory of malignant progression. SM showed pronounced dysregulation of glutathione metabolism in tumor areas. Integrated analysis pinpointed glutathione metabolism and ferroptosis as convergent regulatory pathways in seminoma. Functional validation in TCam-2 cells and a xenograft mice demonstrated that pharmacologic inhibition of glutathione synthesis (using buthionine sulfoximine, BSO) or genetic knockdown of xCT induced ferroptosis and suppressed tumor cell growth both in vitro and in vivo. This study establishes glutathione metabolic reprogramming as a key adaptive mechanism in seminoma that maintains redox homeostasis and protects against ferroptosis, suggesting that targeting this pathway or promoting ferroptosis represents a novel therapeutic strategy.Keywords: Seminoma, Spatial transcriptomics, Spatial metabolomics, Glutathione metabolism, Ferroptosis

Introduction

Testicular cancer is the most common tumor among men aged 15 to 44 in 62 countries and regions worldwide [1]. The highest incidence rates are observed in Western, Northern, and Southern Europe, as well as Oceania, with age-standardized rate (ASR) exceeding 7 per 100,000 population [2]. In the United States alone, approximately 10,000 new testicular cancer cases are diagnosed annually [3]. Germ cell tumors (GCTs) account for 90–95% of all testicular cancers and are histologically classified into seminoma and non-seminomatous GCTs (NSGCTs), based on their embryonic origin and differentiation pathways. Seminomas account for 2%-3% of male malignancies and 3%-9% of genitourinary tumors [4,5]. While early-stage seminoma is highly curable with surgery, radiotherapy, or chemotherapy, these treatments can damage spermatogenic cells, potentially leading to permanent hypogonadism or infertility [[6], [7], [8]]. Known risk factors for seminoma include Klinefelter syndrome and gonadal hypoplasia syndrome [9], however, the molecular mechanisms underlying its pathogenesis remain poorly understood.

Advances in high-throughput genomic technology have revolutionized our understanding of tumor biology. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) enables comprehensive mapping of cellular transcriptome map within tumor tissue and reveals intricate cell–cell interaction networks in the tumor micro-environment (TME) [10,11]. Nevertheless, scRNA-seq lacks spatial resolution, limiting its ability to capture interactions between neighboring cells. Spatial transcriptomics (ST) overcomes this limitation by generating transcriptome-wide expression profiles directly from tissue sections while preserving the spatial architecture of individual cells—albeit with potential confounding from multicellular contributions at each measurement spot [12]. Robust cell type decomposition (RCTD) leverages scRNA-seq-derived cell type signatures to deconvolve mixed signals in ST data, enabling accurate cell type assignment while correcting for technical variation across platforms [13,14]. This integrative approach allows precise spatial mapping of cell types, defining the spatial organization of cellular identities and uncovering novel architectural principles in biological tissues. By using scRNA-seq data to infer cellular composition and occupancy in ST datasets, researchers can perform targeted analyses of dominant cell populations and dissect regional variations in the cellular microenvironment, thereby achieving spatially resolved co-localization of cell types.

Metabolic reprogramming (MR), a hallmark of cancer, enables tumor cells to adapt to the hypoxic and nutrient-deprived conditions of the TME, supporting their energetic, biosynthetic, and redox demands [15]. However, conventional metabolomics approaches often fail to fully elucidate the functional roles and underlying molecular mechanisms of metabolites during tumorigenesis and progression [16]. Mass spectrometry imaging (MSI) has emerged as a powerful tool for characterizing the spatial distribution of metabolites in heterogeneous tumor tissues [17]. When applied to spatial metabolomics (SM), MSI facilitates in situ identification of metabolic biomarkers associated with tumor initiation, progression, and metastasis, thereby reconstructing the metabolic landscape of the TME [18]. Despite these advances, the complexity of metabolic networks and intercellular crosstalk poses significant challenges for comprehensive visualizing reprogrammed metabolism and its regulatory interactions across multiple molecular layers [19]. Integrating high-resolution ST and SM on matched tissue sections offers a multidimensional view of tumor metabolism and gene expression [20]. Concurrent profiling of metabolites and transcripts enhances our understanding of dynamic changes in the TME and disease evolution [21]. Moreover, coupling SM with single-cell transcriptomic data has significantly advanced insights into tumor metabolic mechanisms and cell–cell communication at the transcriptional level [22]. Together, these multimodal approaches hold promise for identifying novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets across diverse cancer types [23].

In this study, we systematically characterized the transcriptomic and metabolomic features of seminoma through an integrated ST and SM framework, revealing distinct biological programs associated with seminoma progression. ST analysis delineated the spatial distribution of immune cells within TME and uncovered a prominent ligand-receptor interaction between macrophages and seminoma cells. SM profiling revealed widespread metabolic reprogramming in seminoma, with marked dysregulation of glutathione metabolism. Integrated analysis of SM and ST data, combined with functional validation, highlights the roles of glutathione metabolism-linked ferroptosis in seminoma pathogenesis.

Results

The spatial transcriptomic landscape of seminoma reveals intertumoral heterogeneity

To characterize spatial multi-omics architecture of seminoma and decipher key molecular events during tumorigenesis, we collected fresh testicular tissue samples from four patients with clinically diagnosed seminoma (Table S1). Frozen sections were prepared for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining as well as spatial multi-omics profiling. Given the maximum detectable area of the spatial transcriptomics (ST) platform (6.5 × 6.5 mm), representative regions encompassing tumor, invasive fronts, seminiferous tubules and non-tubule stromal areas were selected from each sample. Two adjacent serial sections were used for ST and spatial metabolomics (SM) analyses, respectively. Each ST capture area contains approximately 5000 barcoded spots (55 μm in diameter, spaced 100 μm center-to-center), with each spot carrying a unique oligonucleotide barcode. H&E staining revealed well-demarcated tumor boundaries, clearly distinguishing tumor tissue from surrounding normal testicular parenchyma (Fig. S1). In contrast to the highly organized structure of normal seminiferous tubules, tumor regions exhibited a relatively uniform cellular distribution but distinct architectural disarray. Tumor cell nests were separated by fibrovascular stroma infiltrated by variable numbers of lymphocytes, along with occasional granuloma formation, findings suggestive of an active host immune response. These histopathological features are consistent with classical seminoma pathology (Fig. S1). Residual dysplastic seminiferous tubules were observed within tumor regions in patients 1 (P1) and 2 (P2), whereas more extensive invasive growth patterns were identified in patients 3 (P3) and 4 (P4). In P3, tumor cells appeared more scattered, exhibiting larger, hyperchromatic nuclei, prominent nucleli, and frequent mitotic figures (Fig. S1B), indicative of higher proliferative activity.

ST and SM analyses were performed concurrently across all four samples. The overall workflow, which includes sample collection, sectioning, and spatial multi-omics profiling, is illustrated in Fig. 1A. Using the 10× Genomics Visium platform, a total of 18,303 spots were captured, with an average of 72,601 unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) per spot mapping to 2685 detected genes. Dimensionality reduction and clustering were conducted using principal component analysis (PCA) followed by Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) for visualization [24]. Batch effects across samples were corrected using the Harmony algorithm [25], resulting in the identification of 16 transcriptionally distinct clusters (Fig. 1B). Cluster annotation was based on the top ten differentially expressed marker genes (Fig. S2). Key molecular signatures: C1: Enriched for Sushi domain-containing protein 2 (SUSD2), a gene associated with tumor progression [26]; C2: Defined by Cytochrome P450 Family 11 subfamily a member 1 (CYP11A1) and Hydroxysteroid 3-β-dehydrogenase 2 (HSD3B2), encoding key enzymes in steroid hormone biosynthesis [27]; C3: Characterized by immunoglobulin heavy constant gamma 4 (IGHG4) [28] and C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 9 (CXCL9) [29], both implicated in immune cell recruitment and modulation of the tumor microenvironment; C4: Marked by Thrombospondin 1 (THBS1), which regulates tumor cell migration and invasion [30]; C5: H4 clustered histone 3 (HIST1H4C), a core component of nucleosomal histones [31]; C6: Enriched for Chemokine (C–C motif) ligand 19 (CCL19) and Chemokine (C–C motif) ligand 21 (CCL21), chemokines known to guide dendritic and T-cell trafficking, shaping anti-tumor immunity [32]; C7: Highlighted by Testis-expressed protein 47 (TEX47), a gene potentially involved in spermatogenesis [33]; C8: Expressing Steroidogenic acute regulatory protein (STAR) and Cytochrome P450 family 17 subfamily A member 1 (CYP17A1), critical regulators of androgen synthesis [34]; C9: Featuring claudin 6 (CLDN6), a tight junction protein involved in epithelial barrier function [35]; C10: Marked by dynein axonemal heavy chain 11 (DNAH11), a dynein family member essential for ciliary and flagellar motility [36]; C11: Enriched for Transmembrane 4 superfamily member 1 (TM4SF1), a transmembrane protein involved in cell adhesion and signal transduction [37]; C12: Defined by TATA-box binding protein-associated factor 7-like (TAF7L), a transcription factor implicated in spermatogenesis-related gene regulation [38]; C13: Expressing Cyclin D2 (CCND2), a key regulator of G1/S phase transition in the cell cycle [39]; C14: Dominated by B-cell–associated immune markers including complement C3d receptor 2 (CR2), Fc receptor like A (FCRLA), Fc receptor like 1 (FCRL1), Activation induced cytidine deaminase (AICDA) and B-lymphocyte kinase (BLK), indicating active humoral immune responses [[40], [41], [42]]; C15: Co-expressing SRY-box transcription factor 9 (SOX9), a master regulator of testicular development [43], and epididymal peptidase inhibitor (EPPIN), involved in sperm maturation [44]; C16: Showing elevated expression of CatSper channel auxiliary subunit zeta (CATSPERZ), an auxiliary subunit of the CatSper channel critical for sperm motility and capacitation [45].

Based on the spatial distribution of spots across tissue sections, clusters 1 (C1) and 10 (C10) were annotated as tumor subtypes, which were located exclusively within tumor regions. Clusters 4 and 5 (C4–C5) were assigned to the invasive front and classified as invasion subtypes. Cluster 12 (C12) was identified as seminiferous tubule subtypes, corresponding to intact tubular structures, while clusters 2, 8, 11, and 15 (C2, C8, C11, C15) were localized in stromal regions outside seminiferous tubules and designated as non-seminiferous tubule subtypes. The spatial distribution of all clusters across samples is shown in Fig. 1C and Table S2. Cell identity was further supported by the top 10 differentially expressed marker genes per cluster, revealing distinct transcriptional profiles across subtypes (Fig. 1D and Fig. S2). Notably, tumor subtypes exhibited elevated expression of PMAIP1 (encoding p53 apoptosis-stimulating protein 1), a regulator of apoptosis that may influence seminoma cell survival and disease progression [46]. Invasion subtypes showed upregulation of LTBP2 (latent transforming growth factor-β binding protein 2), which can activate TGF-β signaling and potentially promote epithelial-mesenchymal transition, thereby enhancing tumor cell migration, invasion, and metastatic potential [47].

To integrate transcriptomic data with histopathological features, all spots were reclassified into four regional categories, namely tumor, invasion, tubule (seminiferous tubule), and non-tubule (stromal), based on their anatomical location in H&E-stained sections (Fig. 1E and F). Both regional composition (Fig. 1E) and global transcriptomic patterns (Fig. 1F) closely recapitulated the underlying histology. UMAP stratified clusters according to these regional annotations (Fig. S3A) and functional characterization was performed using top marker genes (Fig. S3B). Region-specific gene expression included: PPP1R14C (involved in glycogen metabolism) and KIT in the tumor region; SFRP4 (a Wnt pathway modulator) and CDH11 (cadherin 11) at the invasive front; CYCT1 (a spermatogenesis-associated gene) in seminiferous tubules; and STAR (steroidogenic acute regulatory protein) in stromal regions (Fig. 1G and Fig. S3B). Of note, KIT, frequently mutated or overexpressed in seminoma, drives oncogenic signaling that enhances proliferation and suppresses apoptosis, contributing to tumorigenesis [48]. CDH11, a calcium-dependent adhesion molecule, may mediate homotypic tumor cell interactions, tumor-stromal crosstalk, and local tissue infiltration [49].

We next defined invasion, tubule, and non-tubule regions collectively as non-tumor areas to identify differentially expressed genes between tumor and non-tumor compartments. This comparison revealed 406 upregulated and 528 downregulated genes in tumor regions (Fig. S3C). KEGG enrichment analysis highlighted several key pathways enriched in tumors, including Glycolysis/Gluconeogenesis, Oxidative phosphorylation, and Ferroptosis (Fig. S3D). Seminoma cells exhibit a Warburg-like metabolic phenotype, favoring glycolysis for rapid ATP generation to support proliferative demands [50]. Gluconeogenesis may sustain glucose supply under nutrient stress, while oxidative phosphorylation contributes substantial energy for tumor growth, invasion, and metastasis. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated during this process serve as signaling molecules, with tumor cells tightly regulating redox balance to ensure survival [51]. Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death, plays a context-dependent role in cancer development and therapy response [52]. Together, these pathways shape seminoma pathobiology through coordinated regulation of energy metabolism and cell death mechanisms, offering novel insights into disease mechanisms and potential therapeutic vulnerabilities.

In summary, we established a comprehensive spatial transcriptomic map of human seminoma, covering representative tumor, invasive, tubular, and stromal regions. Analysis revealed 16 transcriptionally distinct clusters, whose identities were validated through integration of gene expression profiles, spatial context, and histopathological features. This framework provides a foundation for further studies on cellular heterogeneity, malignant evolution, and intercellular communication in seminoma.

Spatial-temporal transcriptomic evolution of seminoma

To investigate the spatiotemporal dynamics of malignant transformation in seminoma, we performed pseudotime analysis on a subset of clusters, namely C1, C2, C4, C5, and C8–C12, C15, representing tumor and invasive regions (Fig. 2A and B and Fig. S4A–B; Table S2). Clusters C3, C6, C7, and C13–C14 were excluded due to their sparse and spatially dispersed distribution, which could confound trajectory inference. The pseudotime trajectory was constructed based on histopathological zonation, with the earliest state anchored in seminiferous tubule (C12) and non-tubular stromal (C15) regions, representing putative origins of tumorigenesis. From this common initiation point, the trajectory bifurcated into two distinct branches (Branch 1 and Branch 2), reflecting divergent transcriptional programs during tumor progression. Notably, C12 and C15 cells were present in both branches, suggesting a shared precursor pool. In contrast, tumor-enriched clusters C1 and C10 appeared at intermediate stages in Branch 1 and later stages in Branch 2, indicating differential timing of clonal expansion. C4 and C5 predominantly shaped the directionality of Branch 1 and Branch 2, respectively (Fig. 2A–B and Fig. S4A–C), highlighting their roles in driving branch-specific evolutionary paths. The distinct regional compositions, namely tumor, invasive, tubule, and stroma, across the two branches suggest differences in microenvironmental cues and cellular trajectories during tumor development.

We next conducted Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment analysis on differentially expressed genes (DEGs) across pseudotime phases (Fig. 2C). Genes highly expressed in the pre-branch phase were enriched in cell division, cell cycle regulation, protein ubiquitination, meiotic processes, and spermatogenesis, consistent with heightened proliferative and developmental activity during early transformation. Further annotation of DEGs along each branch revealed functionally coherent gene modules. In the pre-branch phase, genes such as GPX4, SPATA33, YBX1, PRM2, and SOX9 were implicated in spermatogenesis, transcriptional/translational regulation, and redox homeostasis. In Branch 1, tumor-associated genes including VEGFB and IGF1 pointed to intercellular signaling and paracrine communication, while COL4A1 and APOD suggested contributions to extracellular matrix remodeling and microenvironment construction. In contrast, Branch 2 upregulated genes linked to proliferation (HSP90B1), ribosomal function (RPLP1, RPS3), translation initiation (EIF4A1), and drug resistance, underscoring its association with aggressive phenotypes (Fig. 2D and Fig. S4D).

To complement spatial transcriptomics, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) was used to resolve cellular heterogeneity, identifying tumor cell states including stress, hypoxia, active cell cycle, partial epithelial-mesenchymal transition (pEMT), and two differentiation states, as well as cancer-associated fibroblast subsets (CAF1, CAF2) and immune-related populations, namely T cell inflammation, T cell exhaustion, MHCII signaling, cytotoxicity, and chemokine signaling [[53], [54], [55]]. Spatial mapping of these subsets revealed that CAF1, CAF2, stress, and hypoxia states were primarily localized in non-tumor regions (seminiferous tubules and stroma), whereas the cell cycle-active subset was enriched in tumor areas (Fig. 2E and Fig. S5). Notably, T cell inflammation, MHCII signaling, and chemokine-expressing cells were concentrated at the invasive front, suggesting an organized but spatially restricted immune response. These findings indicate that tumor regions are dominated by proliferative activity, while immune surveillance is compartmentalized, particularly at the invasion margin. The presence of MHCII signaling implies antigen-presenting capacity, and chemokine expression may facilitate immune cell recruitment to the invasive zone. Collectively, the distinct cellular composition and functional specialization across tumor, invasive, and non-tumor regions reflect a dynamically evolving ecosystem during seminoma progression.

The spatial transcriptomic atlas reveals immune cell distribution and cell–cell interaction networks in seminoma

Seminoma is composed of eight major cell types, including seminoma cells (POU5F1+, TFAP2C+ and NANOG+), T cells (CD3D+, CD3E+), B cells (CD79A+, MZB1+), NK cells (CD160+, FCGR3A+), endothelial cells (CDH5+), macrophages (CD68+, LYZ+), plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs; LILLRA4+), and smooth muscle cells (ACTA2+, NOTCH3+) [23]. Despite this characterization, the functional roles of these cell populations in tumor progression remain poorly defined. Here, we leveraged spatial transcriptomics (ST) to map the spatial organization and interactions of these cell types across four seminoma tissues. Our analysis revealed that immune-related subsets were predominantly localized in non-tumor regions, with B cells, T cells, and macrophages enriched at the invasive front (Fig. 3A). Using robust cell type decomposition (RCTD) [14], we confirmed that the spatial distribution of seminoma cells closely matched histological annotations on H&E-stained sections. Moreover, immune cell proportions, particularly T cells and macrophages, were highest in non-tumor areas (Fig. 3B). When comparing average cell type abundances across the 16 transcriptional clusters (C1–C16), we observed an inverse relationship between the proportion of seminoma cells and smooth muscle cells, while other cell types showed relatively uniform distributions (Fig. 3C and Fig. S6A). This reciprocal pattern suggests that expanding tumor clones may disrupt normal tissue architecture and impair stromal cell function during disease progression. These findings imply potential crosstalk among tumor cells, immune cells, and stromal components through surface ligand–receptor interactions, contributing to microenvironmental remodeling.

To explore such intercellular communication, we performed ligand–receptor interaction analysis using CellPhoneDB [56]. This revealed a prominent signaling axis between macrophages and seminoma cells (Fig. 3D and E). Among the top 10 significant ligand–receptor pairs, the MIF–(CD74+CD44) interaction stood out (Fig. 3F), which was further validated by immunofluorescence co-staining (Fig. 3G–I). Spatial mapping based on H&E-guided region annotation (Fig. S6B–D) showed that MIF was broadly expressed but particularly enriched at the invasive margin, whereas CD74 and CD44 were specifically upregulated in both tumor and invasion zones. Functionally, MIF, which signals through CD74 and CD44, may act as an immunomodulatory factor that suppresses T cell activation and proliferation by inducing anti-inflammatory cytokines such as transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) [57]. By dampening local immune responses, this pathway could enable tumor immune evasion. Furthermore, MIF-mediated signaling has been implicated in enhancing tumor cell motility, invasion, and metastatic potential [58]. Together, these results suggest that seminoma cells exploit the MIF-driven network to reshape the immune landscape and promote aggressive behavior.

The spatial metabolomic atlas reveals metabolic reprogramming in seminoma

Using the AFADESI-MSI platform, we constructed a comprehensive spatial metabolomic (SM) atlas of seminoma. Each of the four fresh-frozen tissue samples was pixelated at a resolution of 100 × 100 μm, yielding mass spectrometry imaging data for 56,913 m/z features (Fig. S7A–B). Due to inherent differences in chemical structure, metabolites ionize preferentially as either positive or negative ions; therefore, both ionization modes were employed with optimized acquisition parameters to ensure broad coverage and accurate discrimination of metabolite profiles. Metabolites were clustered using a spatial shrunken centroids method, grouping them into 15 distinct clusters based on differential expression patterns across tissue pixels. The top 100 metabolites driving cluster separation were identified (Fig. S7C–D). High-dimensional SM data were visualized via UMAP dimensionality reduction, revealing sample-wide metabolite distribution trends and inter-cluster relationships (Fig. 4A and B). Spatial mapping of these clusters showed precise alignment with histologically defined regions on matched H&E-stained sections (Fig. 4C), confirming that metabolic heterogeneity mirrors tissue architecture. We next compared metabolite profiles between tumor and adjacent para-tumor regions across all samples. Orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) revealed significant metabolic divergence between these regions (Fig. S8A). Volcano plots highlighted sample-specific differentially abundant metabolites (Fig. 4D), with representative metabolites enriched in tumor or para-tumor areas shown in Fig. 4E. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis identified the top altered pathways, including the TCA cycle, glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism, arginine and proline metabolism, alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism, biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids, and glutathione metabolism (Fig. 5A–D).

To identify consistent metabolic alterations in seminoma, we focused on overlapping differentially expressed metabolites across all four cases, detecting 72 commonly dysregulated metabolites (Fig. S8B). In tumor regions, upregulated pathways included the citrate cycle (TCA cycle), glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism, amino acid metabolism, particularly arginine and proline, and glutamate metabolism (Fig. S8C–E). Additionally, pathways such as the FoxO signaling pathway, lipid metabolism (including biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids and linoleic acid metabolism), and ferroptosis were significantly enriched. Spatial visualization of representative metabolites from these pathways confirmed their region-specific accumulation in either tumor or para-tumor tissues (Fig. 5E). Notably, glutathione (GSH) metabolism emerged as a central node in metabolic adaptation. As a tripeptide composed of glutamate, cysteine, and glycine, GSH functions as a major intracellular antioxidant, playing critical roles in detoxification, redox homeostasis, and biosynthetic processes such as amino acid transport, protein, and DNA synthesis. In seminoma, we observed elevated levels of (5-L-glutamyl)-l-amino acid (m/z 146.16488) in tumor regions, suggesting that seminoma cells may exploit glutamine-derived amino acids to fuel energy production and support rapid proliferation (Fig. 5E). Similarly, increased spermidine (m/z 259.09343) in tumors implies a role in promoting cell growth and disease progression (Fig. 5E) [59]. Key intermediates of glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism, such as succinic acid, fumaric acid, and malic acid, are known to feed into the TCA cycle [60]. Here, higher levels of citrate (m/z 226.99629) and malate (m/z 269.13515) were detected in tumor tissues compared to normal counterparts, indicating enhanced tricarboxylic acid cycling and widespread metabolic reprogramming [61]. Furthermore, glutamate (m/z 146.04573) and glutathione (m/z 306.07632) were both enriched in tumor regions and are directly implicated in the regulation of ferroptosis [62,63]. Together, these findings suggest that seminoma cells undergo coordinated metabolic rewiring to counteract ferroptotic stress while sustaining high biosynthetic and energetic demands. These reprogramming increases flux through glutamate-related pathways, supporting redox homeostasis and anabolic growth, critical adaptations for tumor survival and progression [64].

Glutathione metabolism and ferroptosis are functionally linked in seminoma

Integrated analysis of spatial transcriptomics (ST) and spatial metabolomics (SM) enables a systems-level reconstruction of molecular regulatory networks by aligning gene expression with metabolic activity [65]. As previously noted, patient 3 exhibited a scattered tumor distribution, which compromised the spatial registration between ST and SM datasets. To ensure analytical rigor, this sample was excluded, and integrative analysis was performed on the remaining three cases. For robust comparison, six matched regions, spanning both tumor and adjacent para-tumor areas, were selected for downstream integration (Fig. S9A). UMAP visualization revealed clear separation between tumor and non-tumor metabolic-transcriptional states (Fig. S9B). Pearson correlation analysis was applied to assess concordance between gene expression and metabolite abundance across spatially aligned spots. Differentially expressed genes and metabolites meeting stringent criteria (p value ≤ 0.01, correlation≥0.99) were retained for further analysis. The top 20 correlated gene–metabolite pairs are shown in Fig. S9C. Notably, 3-Oxopentanoic acid (m/z 161.04545) displayed strong positive correlations with multiple genes. This metabolite can be converted into acetyl-CoA, serving as a substrate for the TCA cycle and contributing to cellular energy production. Further analysis identified 71 overlapping KEGG pathways between the ST and SM datasets (Fig. S10A–B), with the top 10 enriched pathways per sample subjected to detailed evaluation (Fig. S10C–H). Among these, glutathione metabolism, glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism, and ferroptosis emerged as consistently shared pathways across all samples. All three are mechanistically linked to redox regulation and lipid peroxidation control, implicating them as potential nodes of vulnerability in seminoma biology [66].

Glutathione metabolism plays a central role in maintaining cellular redox homeostasis and modulating ferroptosis susceptibility [67]. Our earlier spatial transcriptomic data revealed upregulation of GPX4 at the onset of malignant differentiation (Fig. 2D), suggesting its involvement in early seminoma evolution. To validate the functional relevance of ferroptosis in seminoma, we integrated the expression patterns of ferroptosis-related genes and glutathione pathway enzymes with corresponding metabolites in situ. Using patient 1 as an illustrative example, we mapped the spatial co-distribution of key molecules (Fig. 6A). We observed increased levels of glutamate, glutamine, cystine, GSH, GSSG, γ-glutamylalanine, and pyroglutamic acid in tumor regions, whereas glycine was reduced (Fig. 6B). At the transcriptional level, GPX4 expression was lower in tumor areas. GPX4 utilizes GSH to reduce lipid hydroperoxides, thereby preventing lethal lipid peroxidation and suppressing ferroptosis. We further assessed the protein expression of core ferroptosis regulators (GPX4, FTH1, FTL, ACSL4, SLC7A11) in seminoma tissues. Strikingly, GPX4 protein levels were significantly decreased in tumor regions compared to para-tumor tissues and showed preferential localization around residual seminiferous tubules (Fig. 6C and D). In contrast, no significant changes were observed for the other four proteins (Fig. 6E–H). Collectively, these findings highlight glutathione metabolic reprogramming as a hallmark of seminoma. The coordinated shift in metabolite abundance and downregulation of GPX4 suggest a finely tuned adaptation that balances redox stress and ferroptotic vulnerability. Rather than inducing cell death, this state may confer a survival advantage under metabolic stress, positioning the glutathione–ferroptosis axis as a potential therapeutic target in seminoma.

Glutathione metabolism reprogramming-linked ferroptosis supports TCam-2 cell survival

To investigate the functional role of glutathione metabolic reprogramming-linked ferroptosis in seminoma pathogenesis, we employed the human seminoma-derived cell line TCam-2 as an in vitro model. Buthionine sulfoximine (BSO), a specific inhibitor of γ-glutamylcysteine synthetase (γ-GCS), was used to block de novo glutathione synthesis [68]. Treatment with 5 mM BSO led to a significant reduction in TCam-2 cell viability, by approximately 50% compared to control cells (Fig. 7A and Fig. S11A). Consistently, EdU incorporation assays revealed markedly decreased DNA synthesis in BSO-treated cells, indicating impaired proliferative capacity (Fig. S11B). Concomitant with reduced proliferation, BSO treatment resulted in a substantial increase in reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels and lipid peroxidation (Fig. 7B–D), hallmark features of ferroptosis. Western blot analysis showed that the expression of the proliferation marker PCNA was significantly downregulated following BSO exposure, and protein levels of the antioxidant enzymes SOD1 and SOD2 was significantly downregulated (Fig. 7E). Notably, expression of SLC7A11 (also known as xCT), a key subunit of the cystine/glutamate antiporter critical for glutathione synthesis, and GPX4 levels were significantly reduced (Fig. 7E).

Functionally, total SOD activity was significantly decreased while GPx activity was unexpectedly increased, likely reflecting a compensatory response to oxidative stress. Malondialdehyde (MDA), a product of lipid peroxidation, was significantly elevated, while intracellular H2O2 levels were reduced, likely due to its consumption in redox reactions. Further assessment of cellular redox status showed significantly reduced T-AOC and GSH/GSSG ratio, indicating severe oxidative stress and disrupted redox homeostasis (Fig. 7F–K and Fig. S11C–D). Targeted metabolomic profiling confirmed these findings, showing a significant accumulation of oxidized glutathione (GSSG) in BSO-treated cells (Fig. S12), consistent with impaired glutathione recycling and heightened oxidative burden.

To further validate the role of glutathione metabolism in seminoma cell survival, we performed siRNA-mediated knockdown of SLC7A11 (si-xCT) in TCam-2 cell [69]. Efficient silencing was confirmed at both mRNA and protein levels (Fig. S11F–G). Similar to pharmacological inhibition, knockdown of SLC7A11 did not reduce EdU labeling (Fig. 7L), but significantly increased ROS production (Fig. 7M), enhanced lipid peroxidation (Fig. 7N) and increased ferrous (Fe2+) (Fig. 7O). Western blot analysis confirmed reduced SLC7A11, GPX4, SOD1/2 and PCNA expression (Fig. 7P). Moreover, si-xCT cells exhibited decreased T-AOC, elevated SOD activity, increased H2O2 and GSSG levels (Fig. S11H–L), elevated ferrous (Fe2+) (Fig. S11M − N) collectively confirming a state of redox imbalance and ferroptotic stress.

In summary, our data demonstrate that glutathione metabolic reprogramming plays a critical role in maintaining redox equilibrium and supporting proliferation and survival in seminoma cells. Disruption of this pathway, either through pharmacological inhibition or genetic silencing of SLC7A11, induces oxidative stress, triggers ferroptosis, and suppresses tumor cell growth. These findings underscore the therapeutic potential of targeting glutathione metabolism in seminoma.

Inhibition of seminoma tumor growth through glutathione metabolic reprogramming in vivo

To interrogate the therapeutic relevance of glutathione (GSH) metabolism as a redox vulnerability in seminoma, we employed buthionine sulfoximine (BSO) in an orthotopic-like TCam-2 xenograft model established in immunodeficient NSG mice. TCam-2 cells were implanted subcutaneously; upon confirmation of palpable tumors, mice were randomized and treated with BSO or vehicle daily for 14 days. Longitudinal monitoring revealed no significant body weight loss or behavioral abnormalities (Fig. S13A), indicating favorable tolerability. At endpoint, tumors were excised for integrated phenotypic, biochemical, and molecular analyses (Fig. 8A). Gross morphological assessment demonstrated robust antitumor efficacy: representative photographs of harvested tumors (Fig. 8B and Fig. S13B-C) visually corroborated quantitative reductions in both tumor volume (Fig. 8C) and final tumor weight (Fig. 8D) in BSO-treated animals versus controls. Pharmacodynamic target engagement was confirmed by time-resolved measurement of hepatic GSH, which declined progressively over the treatment course (Fig. S13D–J), without evidence of hepatocellular injury or systemic toxicity. Serum iron parameters, including ferritin, transferrin saturation, and hepcidin remained unchanged (Fig. S13K), confirming that systemic iron homeostasis was preserved.

Within tumor tissue, BSO induced profound redox remodeling. Total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC) and enzymatic activity of superoxide dismutase (SOD) were significantly attenuated (Fig. 8E and F), reflecting global impairment of antioxidant buffering. As anticipated, intratumoral GSH was markedly depleted while oxidized glutathione (GSSG) accumulated, resulting in a significantly decreased GSH/GSSG ratio (Fig. 8H–J), a highly sensitive and quantitative indicator of redox imbalance and oxidative stress severity. Notably, downstream lipid peroxidation markers, including malondialdehyde (MDA), 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), and the NADP+/NADPH ratio showed no statistically significant differences between groups (Fig. 8K–N), suggesting that redox collapse preceded overt peroxidative damage under these experimental conditions, a feature consistent with early-stage ferroptotic priming rather than terminal execution. Metabolically, BSO treatment significantly reduced intratumoral cysteine (Fig. 8O), the rate-limiting precursor for GSH synthesis, and concurrently elevated labile ferrous iron (Fe2+) (Fig. 8P), while ferric iron (Fe3+) levels remained stable (Fig. 8Q), yielding a significantly increased Fe2+/Fe3+ ratio (Fig. 8R), a recognized metabolic signature of ferroptosis-susceptible states. At the proteomic level, Western blot analysis revealed downregulation of PCNA, indicating suppressed proliferative capacity. Expression of both cytosolic (SOD1) and mitochondrial (SOD2) superoxide dismutases was diminished, underscoring broad suppression of redox-regulatory networks. Critically, key ferroptosis gatekeepers, SLC7A11 (the functional subunit of system Xc−) and GPX4 were significantly downregulated (Fig. 8S–T), providing mechanistic coherence between GSH depletion, iron dysregulation, and ferroptosis sensitization. Collectively, these findings position glutathione metabolism as a therapeutically exploitable redox node in seminoma, wherein its pharmacologic inhibition initiates a cascade of redox collapse, iron dyshomeostasis, and ferroptotic reprogramming in vivo.

Pharmacological modulation of ferroptosis confirms glutathione metabolism as a non-redundant determinant of seminoma cell viability

To directly test whether the antitumor effects of glutathione depletion are mediated through ferroptosis and to establish causal involvement of this pathway in seminoma pathobiology, we performed in vivo rescue using ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1), a potent and selective inhibitor of lipid peroxidation-driven cell death. TCam-2 xenograft-bearing NSG mice were co-treated with BSO (to induce glutathione crisis) and Fer-1 or vehicle control for 21 days. Strikingly, Fer-1 administration fully abrogated BSO-induced tumor suppression: Fer-1–treated mice developed significantly larger tumors than vehicle controls (Fig. 9A), with both final tumor volume (Fig. 9B) and weight (Fig. 9C) restored to near-baseline levels. Longitudinal monitoring confirmed stable body weight and absence of treatment-related morbidity (Fig. S14A–B); hepatic GSH remained suppressed throughout the study (Fig. S14C–E), confirming that Fer-1 acted downstream of GSH depletion without interfering with BSO's pharmacodynamic action.

Consistent with its mechanism as a radical-trapping antioxidant, Fer-1 markedly improved intratumoral redox homeostasis (Fig. 9D–K and Fig. S14F–G): the level of cystine and cysteine as well as the GSH/GSSG ratio increased significantly (Fig. S14F–G and Fig. 9E), and the NADP+/NADPH ratio decreased (Fig. 9H), indicating enhanced reducing capacity and reductive biosynthetic potential. In contrast, total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC), GPx activity, and total SOD activity showed no significant differences between groups (Fig. 9D–F, G), underscoring that Fer-1 selectively intercepts lipid peroxidation propagation rather than broadly augmenting enzymatic antioxidant defenses. Paradoxically, levels of MDA and 4-HNE, canonical markers of lipid peroxidation, were decreased in Fer-1–treated tumors (Fig. 9I–K). Ferroptosis rescue was accompanied by normalization of iron metabolism: Fer-1 significantly lowered the Fe2+/Fe3+ ratio in tumor tissue (Fig. 9L and Fig. S14H–I), consistent with attenuation of labile iron pool expansion and diminished Fenton reactivity. At the molecular level, Fer-1 restored expression of key ferroptosis regulators: PCNA, GPX4, and xCT (SLC7A11) to levels comparable to vehicle controls (Fig. 9M), reinforcing functional linkage between glutathione status, system Xc− activity, and GPX4-dependent peroxide detoxification.

To dissect the centrality of glutathione per se, we conducted orthogonal in vitro rescue experiments in TCam-2 cells. Cells were treated with erastin (a canonical system Xc− inhibitor) with or without exogenous reduced glutathione (GSH), titrated to achieve maximal rescue without off-target effects (Fig. S14J–K). Erastin induced rapid GPx inactivation (Fig. 9N), compensatory SOD upregulation (Fig. 9O), and accumulation of MDA and H2O2 (Fig. 9P–Q) all fully reversed by GSH supplementation. Direct redox imaging corroborated these findings: DCFH-DA fluorescence (total ROS), C11-BODIPY oxidation (lipid peroxidation), and EdU488 incorporation (proliferation) each showed erastin-induced perturbations that were completely prevented by GSH (Fig. 9R–S and Fig. S14L). Notably, erastin triggered a paradoxical rise in T-AOC (Fig. S14N), likely reflecting acute adaptive induction of non-enzymatic antioxidants (e.g., thioredoxin, bilirubin) in response to cystine starvation. Erastin increased the GSH/GSSG ratio (Fig. S14M) and exogenous GSH partially normalized T-AOC toward baseline (Fig. S14N), confirming that extracellular GSH replenishes the reduced pool and dampens redox stress signaling. Western blot analysis revealed erastin-mediated downregulation of SOD1, SOD2, and GPX4 protein levels, and these changes partially rescued by GSH (Fig. 9T), suggesting transcriptional or post-translational regulation secondary to sustained redox imbalance. Together, these integrated in vivo and in vitro interventions demonstrate that glutathione is not merely a biomarker but a causal linchpin: its depletion initiates a cascade wherein impaired GPX4 function, dysregulated iron speciation, and unchecked lipid peroxidation converge to execute ferroptosis in seminoma. The fidelity of rescue both phenotypic and molecular by Fer-1 and exogenous GSH establishes glutathione metabolism as a therapeutically actionable redox node, with the SLC7A11–GPX4 axis serving as its core effector pathway.

Discussion

In this study, we employed an integrative spatial multi-omics approach, which combines spatial transcriptomics (ST) and spatial metabolomics (SM), to comprehensively dissect the molecular architecture of seminoma with high spatial resolution. Our analyses revealed key features of cellular heterogeneity, malignant evolution, immune microenvironment organization, and metabolic reprogramming, ultimately identifying the glutathione metabolism–ferroptosis axis as a central and therapeutically targetable pathway. ST profiling enabled the construction of a spatially resolved transcriptomic map of seminoma, delineating 16 transcriptionally distinct clusters that correspond to specific anatomical regions, including seminiferous tubules, stromal compartments, invasive fronts, and tumor cores. These clusters exhibited region-specific gene expression programs, with multiple oncogenic pathways significantly enriched in tumor areas. Pseudotemporal reconstruction uncovered dynamic functional shifts along the trajectory of malignant progression. Furthermore, we identified non-random spatial distributions of immune cells within the tumor microenvironment, along with prominent ligand–receptor interactions between macrophages and tumor cells. Integrated SM analysis revealed extensive metabolic reprogramming in seminoma, marked by altered glutathione metabolism and ferroptosis pathways, highlighting a critical redox-cell death regulatory link in this malignancy.

Tumor ecosystem heterogeneity is increasingly recognized as a critical driver of cancer progression and therapeutic response [53,70]. In line with this paradigm, our spatial transcriptomic (ST) analysis identified 16 transcriptionally distinct cell clusters within seminoma tissues, each localized to specific anatomical niches. Genes upregulated in tumor and invasive regions, such as PMAIP1 and LTBP2, implicate their roles in evading apoptosis and promoting metastasis [71,72]. Notably, the tumor-enriched cluster C10 exhibited elevated expression of PHKG1, a regulator of glycogen metabolism, and HIST1H4C, a core histone gene involved in chromatin organization, both of which have been linked to tumor growth and dissemination in other malignancies [73,74]. Pseudotime analysis revealed a bifurcated trajectory of malignant evolution, with one branch characterized by inflammatory and angiogenic signaling and the other dominated by proliferative and stress-adaptive programs. This divergence suggests that seminoma progression is shaped by distinct microenvironmental cues, such as immune infiltration and metabolic stress, which may drive parallel evolutionary paths during tumorigenesis [48].

The immune landscape of seminoma, characterized by a prominent accumulation of macrophages at the invasive margin, is consistent with emerging evidence of stromal-mediated immune regulation in germ cell tumors [75]. The spatial distribution of immune populations, including T cells, B cells, and macrophages, suggests their active involvement in tumor invasion, while simultaneously indicating that tumor cells may co-opt these interactions to evade immune surveillance [76]. For instance, the spatial patterning of natural killer (NK) cells within the tumor microenvironment has been linked to tumor immune escape mechanisms [54]. Together, these spatially defined immune architectures and intercellular communication networks reveal potential vulnerabilities that could be exploited therapeutically [77]. Our ligand–receptor interaction analysis identified robust crosstalk between macrophages and seminoma cells, particularly via the MIF–CD74/CD44 signaling axis [78]. MIF has been previously implicated in promoting immune evasion and tumor cell migration in multiple cancer types [79], and its spatial enrichment at the invasive front suggests a localized role in shaping immunosuppressive microenvironments in seminoma [57,80]. Furthermore, activation of the MHC II signaling pathway in this region may reflect dysregulated antigen presentation, potentially contributing to impaired anti-tumor immunity [81]. These observations align with recent insights into immunosuppressive mechanisms operating within solid tumors [82], and collectively suggest that targeting tumor-associated macrophages or disrupting the MIF signaling cascade could represent a promising strategy to enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy in seminoma [83].

A central finding of our study is the extensive reprogramming of glutathione metabolism in seminoma, robustly supported by integrated spatial transcriptomics (ST) and spatial metabolomics (SM) data [84]. Glutathione-related metabolites were consistently elevated in tumor regions, reflecting an enhanced antioxidant capacity that likely serves to mitigate oxidative stress and sustain proliferative activity [85,86]. This metabolic adaptation aligns with the development of nanotherapeutic strategies aimed at depleting glutathione to sensitize cancer cells to oxidative damage [87]. Moreover, we observed broad alterations in cellular metabolite profiles, which may reflect the heightened bioenergetic demands of tumor cells. Notably, changes in TCA cycle intermediates underscore the functional importance of mitochondrial metabolism in cancer progression [88], further supporting a metabolic phenotype consistent with the Warburg effect, wherein cancer cells favor glycolysis for ATP production despite oxygen availability [89]. Strikingly, our multi-omics integration revealed coordinated dysregulation of ferroptosis pathways and glutathione metabolism in seminoma. Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death driven by lipid peroxidation, was transcriptionally and metabolically upregulated across tumor regions. Crucially, this analysis establishes a direct link between glutathione metabolism and ferroptotic regulation. We detected downregulation of GPX4 protein in tumor tissues, despite high glutathione levels, suggesting that seminoma cells fine-tune ferroptosis sensitivity to balance redox homeostasis and survival under metabolic stress. Functional validation confirmed this vulnerability: pharmacological inhibition of glutathione synthesis or genetic silencing of key pathway components triggered ferroptosis and significantly impaired tumor growth both in vitro and in vivo. These findings resonate with the growing recognition of ferroptosis induction as a promising anticancer strategy [90] and highlight glutathione metabolism as a critical metabolic dependency in seminoma.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Although the sample size is adequate for spatial profiling, it may not fully capture the breadth of seminoma heterogeneity. Moreover, while key findings have been validated in both in vitro and in vivo models, the exact regulatory mechanisms connecting glutathione metabolism to ferroptosis execution require further elucidation. Future studies using organoid or orthotopic PDX models are warranted to better recapitulate tumor heterogeneity and the native tumor microenvironment. The Visium spatial transcriptomics platform has a spot size of 55 μm, which may contain multiple cells. Although we used RCTD and SPOTlight for cell-type deconvolution, true single-cell spatial techniques will be required to achieve higher cellular resolution in future studies.

In conclusion, our spatial multi-omics framework reveals the molecular landscape of seminoma and identifies the glutathione metabolism–ferroptosis axis as a potential therapeutic target. These results advance the understanding of seminoma pathobiology and provide a rationale for exploring metabolic interventions in patients with this malignancy.

Material and methods

Clinical seminoma samples

Four seminoma tissue samples were obtained from patients undergoing surgical resection at the Department of Urology, Peking University First Hospital. This study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Peking University First Hospital (approval number: 2024-701) and conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to radical orchiectomy for testicular tumor. Histopathological diagnoses were independently confirmed by two senior pathologists. Tumor staging was performed according to the 8th edition of the TNM classification system established by the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) and the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC). Each sample included regions of tumor tissue, adjacent non-tumor tissue, and distant non-tumor tissue. Fresh specimens were embedded in pre-cooled optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound, rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C until processing for spatial transcriptomics (ST) and atmospheric flow-assisted desorption electrospray ionization mass spectrometry imaging (AFADESI-MSI). Detailed clinical and pathological characteristics of the seminoma samples are provided in Table S1 (Supporting Information).

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

Frozen tissues were sectioned at 10 μm thickness and mounted onto glass slides. Sections were air-dried and fixed in fixative for 30 s, followed by a brief rinse with distilled or tap water (2 s). Nuclear staining was performed using hematoxylin solution at 60 °C for 60 s, followed by washing with running water for 5 s. Differentiation was achieved by immersion in 1% hydrochloric acid ethanol for 3 s, then briefly rinsed with water (2 s). Sections were subsequently incubated in bluing buffer for 10 s to enhance nuclear contrast, followed by a 30-s rinse with running water. Cytoplasmic and extranuclear structures were stained with 0.5% eosin solution for 60 s, then briefly washed with distilled water (2 s). Following staining, sections were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (80%, 95%, and 100%) with 2-s immersions in each. Clearing was performed using carbol xylene for 2 s (with absolute ethanol as an optional substitute), followed by two sequential 2-s incubations in xylene I and xylene II. Finally, sections were mounted with neutral balsam and coverslipped. Histological imaging was performed using a digital panoramic scanner (WS-10, WISLEAP, Zhiyue Medical Technology Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China).

ST barcoded microarray slide information

The spatial transcriptomics (ST) barcoded microarray slides were obtained from 10x Genomics (https://www.10xgenomics.com/). Each spot on the array had a diameter of 55 μm and was spaced 100 μm center-to-center, arranged across a total area of 6.5 × 6.5 mm2. Each slide contained four distinct capture areas, with each area comprising approximately 5000 unique barcoded spots capable of capturing gene expression information, enabling high-resolution spatial profiling of tissue sections.

Tissue preparation, frozen sectioning and scanning imaging for ST

Tissue samples from seminoma were dissected and gently cleaned with cleanroom wipes to remove surface blood contamination. Fresh tissues were embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound and immediately snap-frozen at −80 °C to preserve molecular integrity. Cryosectioning was performed at a thickness of 10 μm, and sections were carefully transferred onto spatial transcriptomics (ST) barcoded arrays. Prior to histological staining, sections were dehydrated in isopropanol for 1 min, followed by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining to visualize tissue morphology. After staining, slides were mounted in 80% glycerol to maintain optical clarity and prevent photobleaching during imaging. Whole-slide scanning was performed using a 3D HISTECH Pannoramic MIDIFL scanner at 40× magnification, enabling high-resolution digital reconstruction of tissue architecture for precise spatial alignment with transcriptomic data.

Tissue optimization

To optimize tissue permeabilization, frozen sections were transferred onto the capture area of a Visium spatial gene expression slide, fixed with methanol, and subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining for morphological assessment. Excluding the built-in positive and negative control regions, six additional tissue sections were serially processed with permeabilization enzymes for incrementally increasing durations. During permeabilization, released mRNA was captured by poly-dT primers immobilized on the array surface. A master mix containing reverse transcription reagents and fluorescently labeled nucleotides was then applied to generate fluorescently tagged cDNA. Following enzymatic removal of residual tissue, fluorescence microscopy was performed to visualize cDNA signal distribution. Optimal permeabilization time was determined by coregistering H&E-stained images with fluorescence signals, selecting the condition that maximized fluorescence intensity while minimizing spatial diffusion of signal. In cases where signal intensity was comparable between two time points, the longer duration was selected to ensure complete RNA release. Detailed procedural protocols follow established guidelines in the literature [91].

Reverse transcription, spatial library construction, and sequencing

Tissue sections were mounted onto the capture areas of the Visium spatial gene expression arrays, where they underwent fixation, H&E staining, and controlled permeabilization to release cellular mRNA. Released transcripts were captured by oligo (dT) primers conjugated to spatially barcoded spots, ensuring that all cDNA synthesized from a single spot shared a unique spatial barcode. Full-length cDNA was then amplified and used to construct sequencing libraries. Paired-end sequencing was performed on an Illumina platform, with the spatial barcodes enabling precise alignment of sequencing reads to their original locations on the tissue section, thereby facilitating high-resolution spatial gene expression mapping. Detailed experimental procedures followed the manufacturer's protocol and have been previously described in the literature [92].

Bioinformatic analysis pipeline

The bioinformatics workflow consisted of two main phases. In the first phase, raw sequencing data in FASTQ format were processed using Space Ranger (v2.0.1; 10x Genomics), which aligned reads to the human reference genome (GRCh38), demultiplexed samples based on spatial barcodes, and generated feature-barcode matrices for downstream analysis. Quality metrics, including total counts, number of detected genes per spot, and proportion of mitochondrial reads, were evaluated to assess sample integrity. In the second phase, further processing was carried out using Seurat (v4.1.0). Data were normalized and scaled using the SCTransform function to correct technical noise and improve detection of biological variation [93]. Highly variable genes (top 3000) were identified and subjected to principal component analysis (PCA). Dimensionality reduction and visualization were performed using uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) based on the significant principal components. Clustering was conducted using graph-based methods (FindNeighbors, FindClusters) at a resolution optimized for biological interpretability. Marker genes for each cluster were identified using the FindAllMarkers function, with significance defined as adjusted p-value <0.05 and log2FC > 0.585 (∼1.5-fold). Functional enrichment analyses for Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathways were performed using the hypergeometric test. Visualization of marker gene expression was achieved via VlnPlot and FeaturePlot functions. To deconvolve cell type composition within each spatial spot, we applied SPOTlight (v0.1.2), a non-negative matrix factorization (NMF)-based algorithm that integrates single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) reference profiles with spatial transcriptomic data. This enabled estimation of cell type proportions across the tissue architecture, which were visualized using spatial-scatterpie plots. Additionally, robust cell type decomposition (RCTD) was employed to refine spatial cell identity assignments, particularly in morphologically complex regions.

Cell type annotation using single-cell reference data

A publicly available scRNA-seq dataset of seminoma [23] (https://bigd.big.ac.cn/gsa-human/browse/HRA004404) was used as a reference for cell type annotation. The Seurat pipeline was applied to preprocess the scRNA-seq data, including normalization, integration to remove batch effects, dimensionality reduction (PCA), clustering (FindClusters), and visualization (UMAP). Cell clusters were annotated based on canonical marker genes derived from the FindMarkers function and validated against known lineage-specific markers. These annotated cell types were then mapped onto the spatial transcriptome data using SPOTlight and RCTD to infer the spatial distribution of immune, stromal, and malignant populations within the tumor microenvironment [14].

Spatial pseudotime analysis using stLearn

Spatial trajectory inference was performed using stLearn (v0.4.11) [94]. Gene expression data were first globally normalized using the Spatial Mean Expression (SME) method to account for spatial autocorrelation and technical variability. Initial topology inference was conducted using PAGA (Partition-based Graph Abstraction) to identify potential developmental relationships among cell subpopulations. Subsequently, diffusion pseudotime (DPT) was applied to compute a continuous pseudotemporal ordering of spots, modeling transitions as a random walk process across the inferred manifold. The root node was selected based on biological relevance—specifically, regions corresponding to early-stage or precursor phenotypes. To incorporate physical tissue architecture into the trajectory model, pseudo-space-time distance (PSTD) was calculated by integrating gene expression similarity with Euclidean distances between spots. A directed minimum spanning tree algorithm was then used to optimize the trajectory graph, identifying the most parsimonious path and branching structures representing divergent differentiation programs.

Cell–cell interaction (CCI) analysis using stLearn

We performed ligand–receptor interaction analysis using the stLearn framework. Following quality filtering and normalization of gene expression data, putative cell–cell interactions were inferred by integrating the CellPhoneDB database. The st.tl.cci.run function was applied with default parameters (min_spots = 20, distance = None, p-adjust cutoff = 0.05) to identify statistically significant ligand–receptor pairs across cell types. To refine interaction probabilities at the spatial level, we incorporated local cell type abundances using st.tl.cci.spatial_cci (min_spots = 3, cell_prop_cutoff = 0.2), which models interactions within individual spots based on co-localization and expression levels. Results were visualized using interaction network graphs, circos plots, and heatmaps to illustrate key communication axes within the seminoma microenvironment.

Airflow-assisted desorption electrospray ionization-mass spectrometry imaging (AFADESI-MSI)—slide preparation

Seminoma tissue samples embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound were stored at −80 °C prior to sectioning. Cryosections of approximately 10 μm thickness were prepared using a cryostat (Leica CM 1950, Leica Microsystems, Germany), transferred onto positively charged conductive slides (Thermo Scientific, USA), and immediately returned to −80 °C for storage until analysis. Prior to mass spectrometry imaging (MSI), sections were dried under controlled conditions: 1 h at −20 °C followed by 2 h at room temperature. After drying, the slides were loaded directly onto the AFADESI source for MSI analysis. Concurrently, adjacent serial sections were reserved for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining to enable histological correlation.

Sample detection

AFADESI-MSI was performed as previously described [95], using an integrated platform developed by Beijing HuaZhiTaiKe Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China), coupled to a Q Exactive Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). In negative ion mode, the spray solvent consisted of acetonitrile/water (8:2, v/v); in positive ion mode, the solvent was modified with 0.1% formic acid. The flow rate of the electrospray solvent was maintained at 5 μL/min, with a sheath gas flow rate of 45 L/min. A spray voltage of 7 kV was applied, and the distances between the sample surface and the sprayer tip, and between the sprayer and the ion transfer tube, were both fixed at 3 mm. The mass spectrometer was operated at a resolution of 70,000 (at m/z 200), with a mass range of 7–1000 Da. The automatic gain control (AGC) target was set to 2 × 106, maximum injection time to 200 ms, S-lens RF level to 55 V, and capillary temperature to 350 °C. For MSI scanning, the stage moved at a speed of 0.2 mm/s in the X-direction, with a step size of 100 μm in the Y-direction, corresponding to the spatial resolution used in the study.

Data processing

Raw data files (.raw) were converted to the open imzML format using imzMLConverter [96]. Background subtraction and spectral processing were performed using the Cardinal R package [97] Processed data were imported into MSiReader, an open-source MATLAB-based platform, for ion image reconstruction and visualization. Total ion current (TIC) normalization was applied to all pixels to correct for signal intensity variations across the tissue section [98]. High-resolution H&E images were coregistered with MSI data to precisely delineate tumor, stromal, and adjacent non-tumor regions for region-specific spectral extraction. To identify endogenous metabolites differentially expressed between tissue microregions, orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was employed. Variables were ranked by their variable importance in projection (VIP) score from the OPLS-DA model, which reflects the contribution of each m/z feature to group separation based on the first two latent components. Features with VIP >1.0 and p < 0.05 (two-tailed Student's t-test) were considered statistically significant differential metabolites. Given the high dimensionality and spatial structure of MSI data, unsupervised dimensionality reduction techniques, including t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) and uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP), were applied to visualize metabolic heterogeneity at single-pixel resolution. Metabolic zonation was further analyzed using spatial shrunken centroids clustering (SSCC), which clusters pixels based on ion abundance patterns while preserving spatial coherence. Metabolite annotation was performed using the pySM pipeline and an in-house database (SmetDB, Lumingbio, Shanghai, China) [99], supported by accurate mass, isotopic patterns, and retention time matching where applicable.

Immunofluorescence staining

Frozen tissue sections were fixed and permeabilized with saponin. Antigen retrieval was carried out using 10×EDTA buffer (SL1860, Coolaber, China) at 60 °C for 30 min. Sections were blocked with 10% donkey serum in 0.1 M PBS containing 0.3% Triton X-100 for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C in 1% donkey serum. After three PBS washes, sections were incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature: Cy3-conjugated donkey anti-mouse IgG (H + L) and Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (both 1:500; Jackson ImmunoResearch, PA, USA). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (100 ng/mL, Beyotime, Jiangsu, China) for 10 min. Slides were mounted with Gel-Mount medium and imaged using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Zeiss LSM880, Jena, Germany) with excitation wavelengths at 405 nm (blue), 488 nm (green), and 555 nm (red). Primary antibody details are provided in Table S3 (Supporting Information).

Western blot analysis

Testicular tissues were homogenized in ice-cold RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime) supplemented with 1 mM PMSF. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay kit (Pierce/Thermo Scientific). Equal amounts of protein (40 μg per sample) were denatured, separated by SDS-PAGE on 10% gels, and transferred to PVDF membranes (Bio-Rad, CA, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Primary antibodies included: rabbit polyclonal antibody to PCNA (Proliferating Cell Nuclear Antigen, 1:2000, 10205-2-AP, Proteintech), SOD1/SOD2 rabbit polyclonal antibody (Superoxide Dismutase, 1:2000, #37385, #13141, Cell Signaling Technology), rabbit polyclonal to Ferritin Light Chain (FTL, 1:1000, ab69090, Abcam); rabbit monoclonal anti-Ferritin Heavy Chain (FTH1, 1:1000, 4393S, Cell Signaling Technology); rabbit monoclonal to Glutathione Peroxidase 4 (GPX4, 1:1000, ab125066, Abcam); rabbit monoclonal to ACSL4 (1:1000, ab155282, Abcam), rabbit polyclonal to xCT (1:1000, ab37185, Abcam) and mouse monoclonal to β-actin (1:3000, YM3028, ImmunoWay Biotechnology, Plano, TX, USA), respectively. After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit (1:5000, Biodragon) or anti-mouse IgG (1:5000, Biodragon) for 1 h. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (New Cell & Molecular Biotech) and detected with a Tanon 5200 system (Shanghai, China). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ (NIH).

Cell culture and reagent treatment

The TCam-2 cell line (ml-CC1371, mlbio, Shanghai, China) was cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Hyclone), 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. Cells were grown in standard medium or treated with 5 mM buthionine sulfoximine (HY-106376, MedChemExpress) for 48 h unless otherwise specified. For SLC7A11 knockdown, cells were transfected with 20 nM siRNA using Lipofectamine™ 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer's protocol. siRNAs were purchased from GenePharma (Shanghai, China); sequences are listed in Table S4. Knockdown efficiency was assessed 48 h post-transfection.

Erastin-induced ferroptosis and exogenous GSH rescue assay

The ferroptosis-specific inducer Erastin (HY-15763, MedChemExpress) was dissolved in sterile dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, Sigma-Aldrich, D2650) to prepare a 20 mM stock solution, aliquoted at 100 μL per tube, and stored at −20 °C protected from light to avoid repeated freeze-thaw cycles. It was diluted to working concentration with complete medium immediately before use. Reduced glutathione (GSH, HY-D0187, MedChemExpress) was dissolved in serum-free RPMI-1640 medium to prepare a 100 mM stock solution, sterilized by filtration through a 0.22 μm sterile filter, stored at 4 °C, and prepared freshly for each use. Vehicle group: Cells were treated with complete medium containing an equal volume of DMSO. Erastin group: Cells were treated with complete medium containing 20 μM Erastin (final concentration). Erastin + GSH combination group: Cells were treated with complete medium containing 20 μM Erastin (final concentration) followed by 10 mM GSH (final concentration). Cells in all groups were cultured for 48 h at 37 °C under 5% CO2 and saturated humidity, then harvested for subsequent functional and molecular biological assays.

Cell viability assay

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at appropriate density and allowed to adhere before treatment. CCK-8 reagent (CK04, Dojindo) was added to each well and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Flow cytometry analysis

Cell proliferation was assessed using the BeyoClick™ EdU Cell Proliferation Kit (C0071S, Beyotime) according to the manufacturer's instructions, followed by flow cytometric detection. Intracellular ROS levels were evaluated using DCFH-DA (5 μM, Beyotime) or C11 BODIPY 581/591 (2 μM, Invitrogen) or FerroOrange (1 μM, MedChemExpress) with probes loaded at 37 °C for 30 min. Cells were harvested, washed twice with PBS, and analyzed on a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter). At least 5000 events were collected per sample. Data were processed using BD FACSDiva Software v7.0 (Becton Dickinson).

Oxidative stress assessments

Intracellular oxidative stress markers were quantified using commercial kits (Beyotime Biotechnology) following the manufacturer's protocols. Assays included total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC, cat# S0119), glutathione peroxidase (GPx, cat# S0058), superoxide dismutase (SOD, cat# S0109), reduced/oxidized glutathione (GSH/GSSG, cat# S0053), and malondialdehyde (MDA, cat# S0131 M), as previously described [100].

Animals

Male NCG mice (NOD-Prkdcem26Cd52Il2rgem26Cd22/NjuCrl, 5 weeks old) were obtained from Gempharmatech (Jiangsu, China). Animals were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with a 12-h light/dark cycle, ambient temperature of 23 ± 3 °C, and humidity of 40–70%. They had ad libitum access to irradiated food, bedding, and 0.22-μm-filtered water. All procedures were approved by the Medical Science Research Ethics Committee of Peking University First Hospital (approval number: J2024048). For xenograft modeling, 5 × 106 TCam-2 cells were subcutaneously injected into the flanks of male NCG mice. When tumors reached ∼100 mm3, mice were randomized into two groups: one received daily intraperitoneal injections of BSO (20 mg/kg), and the other received PBS vehicle. Tumor volume was monitored regularly. According to institutional guidelines, animals were humanely euthanized upon reaching a tumor burden exceeding 1000 mm3.

Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1, HY-100579, MedChemExpress), a specific ferroptosis inhibitor, was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare a 100 mM stock solution, aliquoted into 50 μL/tube, and stored at −80 °C in the dark to avoid photodegradation. The xenograft model was established as described above using 5 × 106 TCam-2 cells subcutaneously injected into the right flanks of male NCG mice. When tumors reached ∼100 mm3, mice were randomized into two groups to ensure balanced baseline tumor volumes. Vehicle group: received daily i.p. injection of BSO (20 mg/kg/day) plus an equal volume of 5% DMSO in PBS; Fer-1 group: received daily i.p. injection of BSO (20 mg/kg/day) plus Fer-1 at a dose of 10 mg/kg/day.

Targeted amino acid metabolomics

Cells in logarithmic growth phase were harvested, washed with PBS, and pelleted by centrifugation. Pellets were stored at −80 °C until analysis. For metabolite extraction, cells were resuspended in pre-chilled methanol/water (80:20, v/v), vortexed, and sonicated on ice for 15 min. Samples were centrifuged at 12,000×g for 15 min at 4 °C, and supernatants were collected. Extracts were dried under a gentle nitrogen stream and reconstituted in 100 μL of 0.1% aqueous formic acid prior to LC-MS analysis.

Targeted amino acid profiling was performed using ultra-performance liquid chromatography coupled to triple quadrupole mass spectrometry (UPLC-TQ-MS). Chromatographic separation was achieved on a C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm) maintained at 40 °C. Mobile phases consisted of (A) 0.1% formic acid in water and (B) 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile, with gradient elution at 0.3 mL/min. Detection was performed in positive electrospray ionization mode using multiple reaction monitoring (MRM). Raw data were processed using instrument-specific software (e.g., MassHunter). Metabolites were identified by matching retention times and MRM transitions to authentic standards. Peak areas were normalized to total protein content (measured by BCA assay). Statistical and multivariate analyses were conducted using platforms such as MetaboAnalyst [101]. Additional amino acid profiling was performed by MetWare (Wuhan, China) using the AB Sciex QTRAP 6500 LC-MS/MS platform.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R (v4.2.1) and GraphPad Prism (v9.0). Spatial transcriptomic data were log2-transformed and normalized prior to downstream analysis. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation, with a minimum biological replicate number of n = 3. For normally distributed data, comparisons between two independent groups were made using two-tailed Student's t-tests; paired samples were analyzed using paired t-tests. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to assess linear associations between variables. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.
