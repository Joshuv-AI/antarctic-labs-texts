Reduced cytokine levels among people living with HIV (PLWH) in ghana co-infected with human coronaviruses
Authors: Esimebia Adjovi Amegashie, Caleb Koranteng Kwayisi-Darkwah, Franklin Yengdem Nuokpem, Mildred Adusei-Poku, Jones Amo Amponsah, Emmanuel Ayitey Tagoe, Elijah Paintsil, Kwasi Torpey, Osbourne Quaye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCytokines play crucial roles in regulating immune cell interactions, and in influencing innate and adaptive immune responses. People living with HIV (PLWH) on antiretroviral therapy (ART) show reduced inflammation and immune activation but with partial immune recovery. Interactions between HIV and human coronaviruses (hCoVs), with respect to immune modulation and ART impact on immune recovery, are not well understood. In this case-control cross-sectional study, we investigated cytokine profiles among three groups: PLWH co-infected with hCoVs (HIV+/hCoVs+), HIV mono-infected individuals (HIV+/hCoVs-), and HIV-negative without hCoVs control group (HIV-/hCoVs). A total of 300 PLWH were screened for hCoVs in three hospitals by subjecting their nasopharyngeal and oropharyngeal swabs to RNA extraction and PCR. Sixty-seven (67) PLWH with hCoVs were age- and sex-matched with HIV mono-infected and HIV negative control groups, and plasma samples were collected to assess cytokine levels using a Luminex multiplex bead-based immunoassay. Lower cytokine levels were observed among the HIV co-infected and mono-infected groups compared to HIV-negative control group. Granzyme B, TNF-α, IL-17 A, IL-3, IL-15 and VEGF were significantly reduced in the HIV co-infected group. Participants who had been on ART for less than a year had higher TNF-α levels than those who had been on ART for more than 5 years. The study demonstrates that HIV co-infection with hCoVs is associated with significantly reduced levels of key cytokines, which may be due to immune dysregulation and/or exhaustion associated with chronic HIV infections. Introduction

Cytokines, including interleukins (ILs), chemokines, interferons (IFNs), and tumour necrosis factors (TNF) are key inflammation modulators produced in response to pathogens. Cytokines help control response to infection and inflammation, with imbalances affecting inflammatory, autoimmune diseases and pathophysiology of infections1. Cytokines are released by various cells such as macrophages, lymphocytes, mast, stromal, and natural killer (NK) cells2.

Cytokines are essential signaling molecules that are secreted early in inflammation and play vital roles in immune response against viruses3. Immune responses to viral infections are associated with increased levels of IL-6, TNF-α and IFN-γ. These cytokines promote an antiviral state as well as a virus-specific response via activation of adaptive immune responses by enhancing antigen presentation and T cell differentiation into memory phenotypes, promoting B cell antibody production, and expanding cytotoxic CD8 + T cells4.

A good immune response involves early IFN response to limit viral replication while allowing locally restricted pro-inflammatory cytokine, T cells and neutralizing antibodies to clear viral infection with regulatory cytokines reducing inflammation and speeding up tissue repair5. However, excessive immune activation, such as through the over-expression and release of some cytokines, can lead to increased inflammation, cytokine storm, cell degeneration, organ dysfunction, and death6. While anti-retroviral therapy (ART) usage inhibits viral replication and results in lower inflammation, chronic inflammation persists in PLWH, even in long-term ART usage. HIV-ART experienced people tend to show reduced inflammation and immune activation, though not to the same extent as HIV negative people4. In chronic HIV infection, CD4:CD8 T-cell ratio of 0.5–0.7, which is a hallmark of HIV pathogenesis, does not recover even with long-term ART usage. Some of these immune pathologies have been shown to be triggered by cytokines such as IFNs and IL-77.

Seven human coronaviruses have been identified as of 2026 which includes hCoVs-229E, OC43, NL63, HKU1, MERS-CoV, SARS-CoV and SARS-CoV-2. The endemic hCoVs made up of 229E, OC43, HKU1 and NL63 causes mild to moderate respiratory tract flu-like symptoms while MERS-CoV, SARS-CoV and SARS-CoV-2 are more virulent and cause pneumonia8,9.

Human coronavirus infection of alveolar epithelial cells can amplify immune activation10, leading to excessive cytokine release11. SARS-CoV infection of dendritic cells can dysregulate antiviral cytokines such as IFN-α, IL-12p40, IFN-γ and IFN-β, alongside an increase in TNF-α and IL-612. SARS-CoV indirectly induces T cell apoptosis through inflammatory cytokines released by infected monocyte-macrophages and DCs, contributing to cytokine storm13.

In severe SARS-CoV-2 and HCoV-229E infections, cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6 are significantly elevated. High levels of these cytokines have been demonstrated to be major players of the cytokine storm pathology associated with these infections14,15. For hCoV-OC43 infection, upregulation of cytokine mRNA contributes to inflammatory immune-pathologies and has been shown to alter IL-6 and TNF-α expression in infected astrocytes, suggesting a robust inflammatory response16. While cytokine responses have been extensively studied in individual infections, there is limited understanding of how HIV and hCoVs together influence immune regulation.

This study compared cytokine profiles among three groups: (HIV(ART+)/hCoVs+ infected, HIV(ART+)/hCoVs- and a control group of HIV-/hCoVs-) to elucidate how co-infection with hCoVs affects cytokine dynamics in HIV positive individuals.

The three study groups were then established from these PCR results. The 67 co-infected participants formed the HIV+/hCoVs+ group. Cytokine Levels among the Three Groups

Cytokine levels as obtained in the three groups - the HIV and human coronavirus co-infected (HIV+/hCoVs+), the HIV mono-infected (HIV+/hCoVs-), and the HIV and human coronavirus negative control (HIV-/hCoVs-), are reported as follows: The negative control (HIV-/hCoVs-) group recorded significantly higher cytokine levels compared to both the co-infected (HIV+/hCoVs+) (p = 0.0007) and the mono-infected (HIV+/hCoVs-) groups (p = 0.009), and no significant difference between the co-infected (HIV+/hCoVs+) and mono-infected (HIV+/hCovs) groups (p = 1.0) was observed (Fig. 1A). To distinguish functional cytokine classes, analytes were grouped into pro-inflammatory (IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-15, IL-17 A, IFNα2) and anti-inflammatory (IL-10, IL-1ra, IL-4) cytokines and compared within each group. Total anti-inflammatory levels were higher than total pro-inflammatory levels in all three groups (Mann-Whitney U, p < 0.0001 in all groups); this contrast is influenced by the high baseline abundance of IL-1ra and we report this as a descriptive comparison. Also, anti-inflammatory cytokine levels did not differ across the three groups (Kruskal-Wallis p = 0.60) (Fig. 1B). In contrast, pro-inflammatory cytokine levels differed significantly across groups (Kruskal-Wallis p < 0.0001), being higher in the negative control group than in both the co-infected (Dunn’s p < 0.0001) and mono-infected (Dunn’s p = 0.01) groups, with no significant difference between the co-infected and mono-infected groups (Fig. 1C).

Cytokine levels and age

The age groups 15–32 years, 33–50 years, and > 50 years did not differ significantly (p = 0.90) when compared for the overall cytokine levels (Fig. 3Ai). Similar results were observed when age-stratified cytokine levels were compared within the three infection groups: co-infected (Fig. 3Aii), HIV-mono-infected group (Fig. 3Aiii), and the negative control group (3Aiv). For the individual cytokines, significant differences were observed for IL-33 and TNF-α. IL-33 levels were significantly lower in the 15–32 years group compared to both 33–50 years group (p = 0.03) and > 50 years group (p = 0.02) (Fig. 3Bi), while the 33–50 years group had significantly higher TNF-α levels than the > 50 years group (p = 0.02) (Fig. 3Bii). ART drug combination and duration on cytokine levels

HIV positive study participants were categorized into 3 groups based on their current ART drug combinations. Ten participants were on abacavir, lamivudine and dolutegravir, 120 were on tenofovir disoproxil, lamivudine and dolutegravir, and 4 patients were on zidovudine, lamivudine and nevirapine. There was no significant difference in cytokine levels across the groups (p = 0.85) (Figure S2).

Study participants were categorized into four groups based on the duration on ART: less than 1 year, 1–2 years, 3–5 years, and greater than 5 years. The overall cytokine levels did not differ across the ART duration groups (p = 0.91) (Fig. 4A), however, for the individual cytokines, TNF-α levels were significantly higher in participants on ART for less than 1 year compared to those on ART for greater than 5 years (Fig. 4B) (p = 0.031).

Discussion

People living with HIV tend to exhibit lower levels of inflammatory markers and experience less severe disease when admitted to the hospital for COVID-1917. HIV infection results in an impaired immune system, which may elevate the risk of contracting SARS-CoV-218. There is limited information available regarding the interplay of cytokine levels among individuals with HIV co-infected with hCoVs. This study investigated cytokine levels in individuals with HIV, who are co-infected with human coronaviruses.

Granzyme B, TNF-α, IL-17 A, IL-3, IL-15 and VEGF were significantly lower in the co-infected group, which suggests an impaired immune response in the co-infected group due to HIV induced immune suppression and negative impacts the cytokines’ abilities to control hCoVs replication19. Because the HIV-negative controls represent an immunologically intact comparator, their comparatively higher levels of these cytokines reflect a preserved capacity for immune activation rather than any greater exposure to the pathogens in question. The reductions observed in the HIV mono- and co-infected groups should therefore be read against this intact baseline, and are consistent with the immune dysregulation and exhaustion that accompany chronic HIV infection. Granzyme B reduces viral spread and aids in the elimination of damaged cells, however, lower levels impair effective antiviral responses20, whereas hCoVs infection activate regulatory pathways that suppress TNF-α and evade immune response21. Elevated IL-17 A22, IL-323 and VEGF24 have been associated with severe, ICU-requiring hCoV disease; the reduced levels of these analytes in the co-infected group are consistent with the generalized suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokines observed in this cohort25. Decrease IL-15 levels indicate a delay in NK cells entry resulting in dysregulated control of viral replication26. The continuous use of ART among PLWH co-infected with SARS-CoV-2 prevented immune deterioration compared to ART-naïve HIV-infected population with SARS-CoV-227.

When compared to older age groups, the observed drop in IL-33 levels within the 15–32 age group may reflect variations in immune modulation throughout life. The alarmin cytokine IL-33 regulates inflammation and is involved in tissue repair, and homeostasis maintenance28. Its higher levels in middle-aged and older adults may be a sign of an immune system that is more prepared for action or of tissue repair processes that are triggered as people age, which could make up for stress or cumulative tissue damage29. IL-33 activates Th2 cells and contributes to COVID-19 infection regulation, with increased levels in severe tissue damage cases30,31. Higher TNF-α levels in the 33–50 age group compared to those over 50 years may indicate a period of heightened inflammatory responses, which are known to vary with age and contribute to inflammaging, a low-grade, chronic inflammation associated with the aging process32. Immunosenescence, which is characterized by a decline in immune function as people age, and makes it difficult to fight infection, may suggest the cause of decline in pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α in older persons33.

The observed higher TNF-α levels in participants on ART for less than 1 year compared to those on ART for more than 5 years suggest an excessive release of the cytokine in regulating viral replication and microbial products in the initial stage of infection34. During the early phase of ART, despite a significant reduction in plasma HIV RNA levels, immune reconstitution and inflammation persist due to factors like microbial translocation and co-infections35. According to a study by Usoji et al., people with HIV had elevated levels of TNF-α and IL-6, which rose prior to HAART and then gradually decreased a year later, although TNF-α stayed elevated when compared to control persons36. Consistent ART for 5 + years reduces immune activation markers like TNF-α, promoting better immune system restoration, and reducing inflammation caused by viral replication or microbial translocation4 and promote immunological recovery. During HIV infection, HAART promotes viral suppression and immunological reconstitution37 and show a decline in the level of cytokines such as IL-6, IL-10 and TNF-α during treatment38,39. A significantly lower levels of IL-6 and TNF-α was observed in individuals with elevated CD4 + T cells36.

The similar levels of systemic cytokines among the groups suggest that co-infected individuals had comparable immune control or compensated inflammatory responses to mono-infected individuals when both groups are on the same ART combination. Current ART guidelines do not require routine regimen modification that is solely based on co-infection status in HIV persons, especially where stable viral suppression and CD4 + T cell counts are preserved40,41. Dolutegravir-based regimens (ALD and TLD) are associated with strong viral control and good immune recovery in many studies42,43. The absence of differences in cytokine levels by infection status may reflect effective control across all groups, and differences found in other studies often depend on the type of co-infecting pathogen, how advanced the immune system is, the ART era, as well as local treatment practices44,45. Our results suggest that when ART is uninterrupted, the systemic inflammatory profile may not be altered even in HIV co-infected individuals. It is, however, critical to monitor co-infection-specific adverse events, drug-drug interactions, and organ-specific effects, particularly in settings with differing ART access, adherence patterns, or co-morbidities46. The finding aligns with previous observations that ART regimens can be effective against viral co-infected viral infection in well-controlled PLWH with stabilized cytokine networks4,47.

HIV/hCoVs co-infection may impair systemic immune responses, as seen by the decreased cytokine levels when compared with HIV mono-infected and HIV-negative control groups, indicating immune exhaustion or dysregulation. The immune dysfunction affects key cytokines such as Granzyme B, TNF-α, IL-17 A, IL-3, IL-15, and VEGF, and highlights the intricate relationship between HIV, co-infections, and immune function.

Age-related changes in inflammatory and immune responses are observed, with IL-33 levels decreasing in 15–32 years and TNF-α peaking in 33–50 years, possibly reflecting varying immune activation states. TNF-α levels during ART treatment are elevated due to immune activation, but decline over time, emphasizing the need for long-term treatment to reduce inflammation, co-infections and promote immunological recovery.

Materials and methods

Study Design

For this analytical cross-sectional study, participants were recruited from Korle Bu Teaching Hospital, Ledzokuku Municipal Assembly (LeKMA) Hospital, and University of Ghana Hospital, in Accra, Ghana. Three hundred (300) HIV participants and 87 HIV negative control participants were recruited from January 2023 to March 2024. The sample size of HIV-infected patients was calculated based on a 17.5% prevalence from a retrospective study among PLWH in Ghana (Kotey et al., 2022), with a power of 0.80, significance of 0.05 and effect size of 0.20. The study comprised three groups: HIV co-infection with hCoVs (HIV+/hCoVs+), HIV mono-infected (HIV+/hCoVs-), and HIV/hCoVs negative (HIV-/hCoVs-) as control. Sixty-seven (67) each of HIV+/hCoVs+, HIV+/hCoVs- and HIV-/hCoVs- groups were included in the study. The criteria for sample selection of the 3 groups have been previously described13. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Boards of the Ghana Health Service (Ref. No: GHS-ERC: 007/09/22), College of Basic and Applied Sciences-University of Ghana (Ref. No: ECBAS 074/21–22), Noguchi Memorial Institute for Medical Research (Ref No: 026/22–23), and the Korle Bu Teaching Hospital (KBTH/MD/G3/23). All participants signed a consent to participate form after the objectives of the study have been explained to them.

Data and Sample Collection

All participants that were recruited had blood drawn into ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) tubes, and their nasopharyngeal and oropharyngeal swabs taken in an aseptic manner and transported to the lab in cold boxes. Using the Lymphoprep technique, plasma and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were separated as previously described (Gómez-Aguililla et al., 2023) and stored at − 80 °C.

RNA extraction and Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR)

Ribonucleic acid (RNA) extraction and subsequent polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analyses were performed on the swab samples. Briefly, primers and probes were obtained from Biolabs, (New York, USA) for the analysis of the following human coronaviruses: 229E, OC43, HKU1 and NL63. Real-time reverse transcriptase PCR was carried out using the ABI 7500 PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA). Twenty microliters (20 µL) of reaction volume, made up of 15 µL of one-step RT-PCR master mix (consisting of 2.5X buffer, 1X enzyme mix, 0.8 µM forward primer, 0.8 µM reverse primer, 0.1 µM probe and 2 µL nuclease free water) and 5 µL of extracted RNA was used. The following cycling conditions were used in the amplification of the human coronaviruses: a reverse transcription step at 55 °C for 10 min, denaturation at 95 °C for 1 min, 45 cycles of amplification at 95 °C for 10 s and an extension at 60 °C for 1 min. The PCR was done on a 96 well plate. For SARS-CoV-2, real-time reverse transcriptase PCR was performed in ABI 7500 PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA). A reaction volume of 10 µL that was made up of 2.5 µL of RNA template and 7.5 µL of one-step RT-PCR master mix (consisting of 1X of reverse transcriptase and buffer, 0.5 µM forward primer, 0.5 µM reverse primer, 0.215 µM probe and 1.75 µL Nuclease free water) was used. The following cycling conditions were used in the amplification of SARS-CoV-2 Nucleocapsid (N) protein: a reverse transcription step at 50 °C for 30 min, denaturation at 95 °C for 15 min, 40 cycles of PCR at 95 °C for 10 s, and an extension step of 72 °C for 30 s. PCR was done on a 96 well plate. Details of primers and probes used have been shown in Table 1.

Measurement of Cytokine Levels

Plasma samples were thawed and cytokine levels, including interleukin 1ra (IL-1ra), IL-1β, IFNα2, IFNγ, TNF-α, Granzyme B, IL-10, IL-1α, IL-17 A, IL-15, IL-33, IL-3, IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-7, and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), were quantified using a Luminex® Magpix analyzer (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, each microplate well received 50 µL of sample, control or standard, and 50 µL of diluted microparticle cocktail was added and re-suspended by vortexing. The microplate was firmly covered with a foil plate sealer and incubated for two hours on a horizontal orbital microplate shaker set to 800 ± 50 rpm at room temperature. The microplate was washed 3 times, and 50 µL of diluted biotin-antibody cocktail was added to each well, covered with a foil plate sealer, and incubated on the horizontal orbital microplate shaker set to 800 ± 50 rpm at room temperature for 1 h. Washing was done 3 times and 50 µL of diluted Streptavidin-phycoerythrin was added to each well, and covered with a foil plate sealer and incubated at room temperature for 30 min on a shaker set to 800 ± 50 rpm. Hundred (100) µL of wash buffer was added and incubated for 2 min on a shaker set to 800 ± 50 rpm to resuspend microparticles, and Luminex® Magpix analyzer was used to quantify the cytokine levels within 90 min.

Statistical Analyses

Cytokine concentrations were interpolated from a 4-parameter logistic regression curve generated using an assay standard cocktail and were log10-transformed to reduce skewness. Using Shapiro-Wilk test, the log-transformed cytokine concentrations were evaluated for normal distribution, with a significant threshold of p < 0.05. Based on the normality test results, parametric tests or non-parametric tests were selected for downstream analyses. Kruskal-Wallis test was utilized to compare non-normally distributed data that involved three or more groups. Post-hoc analysis was carried out using the multiple comparisons Bonferroni correction and Dunn’s test. For every test, statistical significance was considered as p < 0.05.
