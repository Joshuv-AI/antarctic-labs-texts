Rhesus Antagonism Is Associated With Non‐Anastomotic Biliary Strictures After Orthotopic Liver Transplantation From Brain‐Death Donors
Authors: Leonard D. (Niels) Broekman, Danny van der Helm, Stefanus G. T. Janson, Eveline S. M. de Jonge‐Muller, Maarten E. Tushuizen, Bart van Hoek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundBiliary non‐anastomotic strictures (NAS) are among the most severe complications of liver transplantation (LT). Risk factors include donation after circulatory death (DCD) as compared to donation after brain death (DBD) and prolonged ischemia times. Immunological factors are also believed to play a role, as ABO blood group incompatibility is associated with NAS. This study aimed to assess the effect of rhesus (Rh) antagonism (RhA) on development of NAS after LT.MethodsAll 678 orthotopic LTs performed at the LUMC between January 2000 and August 2023 (DBD = 422, DCD = 191, and machine perfused = 65) were included in this observational cohort study. Data were locked on October 1st, 2023. The primary endpoint was NAS that required cholangiographic intervention. RhA was defined as a Rh negative recipient receiving a Rh positive donor graft. Kaplan‐Meier curves and Cox regression models were used for survival and risk factor analysis. Immunohistochemistry and Western blot were performed on human biliary tissue to assess the presence of the RhD antigen on biliary epithelial cells.ResultsTotal incidence of NAS was 142 (20.9%), of which 14.9% for DBD, 35.6% for DCD, and 16.9% for machine‐perfused grafts (p < 0.001). RhA was identified in 52 (7.7%) cases. In LTs with DBD, RhA was associated with higher incidence of NAS (HR 2.70; 95% CI = 1.41–5.19). This effect was absent in the overall cohort and in LTs with DCD specifically. No expression of RhD on biliary epithelium was found.ConclusionRhA was associated with a threefold risk of NAS development after LT with DBD.

Introduction

Biliary problems can complicate liver transplantation (LT), and include anastomotic (AS) and non‐anastomotic biliary strictures (NAS), biliary leaks, biliary sludge and casts, and bacterial cholangitis [1, 2, 3]. Distinction between AS and NAS is made on the basis of the respective localization of the biliary strictures: NAS is typically defined as stenosis of the donor bile ducts at 1 cm or more proximal from the biliary anastomosis [4, 5]. Clinical features of both AS and NAS include cholestasis‐related pruritis, jaundice, and cholangitis [4]. NAS is diagnosed with cholangiography, usually starting with magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) [4, 5, 6, 7]. Treatment of biliary strictures consists of endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) or percutaneous transhepatic cholangiography (PTC) with interventions, such as balloon dilatation, stenting, or a combination of both [8].

Between 11%–31% of patients develop NAS after LT, and up to 16% of these patients eventually need re‐transplantation (re‐LT), illustrating its debilitating nature [3, 4, 6]. Risk factors for NAS development include donation after cardiac death (DCD) compared to donation after brain death (DBD), the presence of pretransplant primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC), and both prolonged cold (CIT) as well as warm ischemia times (WIT) [3]. Moreover, hepatic artery thrombosis (HAT) can also lead to NAS [3]. Apart from ischemic injury, it is believed that donor (e.g. age)‐, immunological (e.g. ABO incompatibility (ABOi))‐, and cytotoxic (e.g. bile salt composition) factors might play a role in the development of NAS as well [1, 2, 4, 7].

ABO blood group‐related antigens are expressed on biliary epithelium, and ABOi between donor and recipient was found to increase the risk of biliary strictures and other biliary complications after LT [9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. Knowledge on the role of rhesus (Rh) blood group incompatibility as a risk factor for NAS is highly limited, and evidence of Rh antigen expression on biliary epithelium is lacking [14, 15]. Given the previous evidence on ABO antigen expression and the effect of ABOi on biliary stricture formation, and the limited research on Rh incompatibility, we hypothesize that Rh antagonism (RhA) too might be a possible driver in the development of NAS after LT.

Therefore, the aim of this study is to examine the role of RhA in the development of NAS after LT and to evaluate expression of the RhD antigen on biliary epithelium.

Materials and Methods

Study Population and Data Source

We included all consecutive deceased donor orthotopic LTs (n = 678) performed from January 2000 until August 2023 at the Leiden University Medical Center (LUMC). This included both LTs with DBD (n = 422) and DCD (n = 191), and both with (D‐HOPE = 29, NMP = 27, NRP = 9) and without the use of machine perfusion. Relevant recipient, donor, and surgery characteristics were retrieved from electronic patient files. Discovery (2000–2018) and validation cohorts (2019–2023) were defined, and data was analyzed accordingly. For additional sensitivity analysis, LTs with DBD were grouped together with all LTs with machine perfusion.

Study Parameters and Outcomes

Liver grafts were followed until either re‐LT or patient death, or the latest known doctor's visit or hospital admission. Study data were locked on October first, 2023, and times from LT to the endpoints defined below were calculated accordingly.

We defined the primary endpoint of NAS as stenosis of the donor biliary system at more than 1 cm proximal from the biliary anastomosis that required biliary intervention, also referred to as symptomatic NAS. The date of diagnosis was defined as the date on which the first cholangiographic intervention (ERCP, PTC) was performed. We defined RhA as a case in which a Rh negative recipient received a liver graft from a Rh positive donor, where a Rh positive or negative state refers to the presence or absence of the RhD antigen, respectively. Controls are defined as cases in which RhA is absent.

Since HAT is an independent risk factor for NAS development after LT, cases of HAT were identified and documented. HAT was defined as total occlusion of the hepatic artery that required thrombolytic treatment, endovascular or surgical revascularization, re‐LT, or a combination of the former. For a robustness check, we additionally performed all analyses on a sub‐cohort in which cases of HAT were excluded.

Secondary endpoints included acute cellular graft rejection (ACR) within 1‐year posttransplantation, and 1‐year graft and patient survival. ACR was documented along with corresponding rejection activity index (RAI) scores. The date of rejection was defined as the date on which antirejection therapy was initiated.

Antigen Expression

Liver tissue from patients who underwent LT was obtained to perform immunohistochemistry (IHC), and pretransplant collected biliary tract samples from donor grafts were obtained to perform Western blots (WB) for detection of the RhD antigen on biliary epithelium. Extensive methodological approaches for both techniques are described in Supplemental Materials 1 and 2.

Statistics and Ethics

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 for Windows (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). Continuous data were compared between LTs with DBD, LTs with DCD, and LTs with machine perfusion using the ANOVA test for means and the Kruskal‐Wallis test for medians. Proportions were compared between LTs with DBD, LTs with DCD, and LTs with machine perfusion using the Chi‐square test (χ 2). Kaplan‐Meier survival analysis was performed for the primary and secondary endpoints, and survival curves are compared using the Log‐Rank test. For all statistical tests, p‐values of less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analysis (Wald) was used for risk factor analysis, and hazard ratios are presented accordingly with 95% confidence intervals (CI). A multivariate model evaluating risk factors for NAS development within the entire cohort included recipient age, donor age, presence of pretransplant PSC, presence of RhA, and ischemia times. Since no confounding factors were identified assessing the potential association between RhA and NAS development, for subgroups of LTs with DBD and LTs with DCD, respectively, we only included the presence of RhA in univariate models.

The study protocol was approved and deemed exempt by the local medical ethics committee. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and reported using the STROBE checklist for observational research (Supplemental Material 3).

Results

Baseline

All 678 orthotopic LTs with deceased donor grafts performed between January 2000 and August 2023 were included. This population consists of 422 (62.2%) LTs with DBD, 191 (28.2%) LTs with DCD, and 65 (9.6%) LTs using machine perfusion techniques (D‐HOPE: 29; NMP: 27; NRP: 9). Of the perfused grafts, 48 (73.8%) were derived after DCD. Due to overlapping and corresponding results, findings are presented for the entirety of the study cohort (2000–2023), and separate analyses for discovery (2000–2018) and validation cohorts (2019–2023) are provided in Supplemental Material 4. Mean recipient age at LT was different between LTs with DBD (52.0 years), LTs with DCD (55.0 years), and LTs with machine perfusion (55.3 years; p = 0.005). The most prevalent primary indications for LT were alcoholic liver disease (ALD), viral liver disease, and PSC (Table 1). The number of recipients diagnosed with PSC and the number of RhA cases did not differ significantly between groups. Median CIT (555 vs. 484 vs. 411 min; p = 0.018) was different for LTs with DBD, LTs with DCD, and LTs with machine perfusion. While median donor WIT was different for LTs with DCD with and without use of machine perfusion techniques (15 vs. 18 min; p = 0.021), the median recipient WIT was not different between study groups (Table 1).

Follow‐Up

The incidence of NAS was 20.9% overall and significantly higher for LTs with DCD compared to LTs with DBD and to LTs with machine perfusion (35.6% vs. 14.9% and 16.9%; p < 0.001). The median time to NAS was numerically slightly shorter for LTs with DCD, but this difference was not statistically significant (174 vs. 120 days; p = 0.081, Table 2). No differences were observed between the three patient groups in terms of the occurrence of HAT and cellular rejection within 1‐year posttransplantation. Re‐LT rates were significantly higher for LTs with DCD compared to DBD and machine perfusion (17.8% vs. 9.7% and 12.3%; p = 0.018). Both 1‐year graft‐ and patient survival did not differ between patient groups (Table 2).

As compared to LTs with DBD.

In LTs with DBD, cases of RhA demonstrated a significantly lower NAS‐free survival (Figure 1 and Table 1). For those patients undergoing LT with DBD, NAS‐free survival 1 year after OLT was 69.5% in the case of RhA compared to 88.8% for controls. In this group, the overall risk of NAS development increased threefold for cases of RhA (HR 2.70; 95% CI = 1.41–5.19, Table 3). This effect holds when grouping LTs with DBD together with LTs with machine perfusion, when separately analyzing discovery and validation cohorts (see Supplemental Material 4), and when adjusting for clinical parameters in a multivariate model (see Supplemental Material 5). In addition, exclusion of cases in HAT (n = 44) did not yield different results (see Supplemental Material 4). Last, supplemental Material 5 provides all univariate estimates for LTs with DBD.

In LTs with DCD, the presence of RhA was not significantly associated with worse 1‐year (54.5% vs. 65.9%) and overall NAS‐free survival (HR 1.10; 95% CI = 0.44–2.71, Supplementary Material 5).

In addition, we compared the incidence of NAS not only for LTs with DBD with and without RhA specifically, but also for DBD cases with and without Rh incompatibility in general. While in total 106 cases of Rh incompatibility were identified in this group, the development of NAS was not significantly different compared to Rh compatible cases (p = 0.103, χ 2 test), contrary to our findings for cases of RhA (n = 33; p = 0.002, χ 2 test).

Antigen Expression

We evaluated the presence of RhD antigens on biliary epithelium in donor liver rest material following two distinct approaches (see Supplementary Materials 1 and 2). Both IHC and WB experiments showed no evidence for expression of the RhD antigen on biliary epithelium (see Supplementary Materials 1 and 2).

Discussion

We demonstrate that RhA is associated with significantly higher rates of NAS following orthotopic LT with DBD. The predominant share of this effect was observed within the first year after LT. The median time to development of NAS was not shorter (p = 0.450) for cases of RhA (168 days, n = 16) compared to Rh compatible LTs (136 days, n = 216). While RhA significantly increases the risk of NAS development in LTs with DBD (HR 2.70; 95% CI = 1.41–5.19), this effect does not hold in LTs with DCD (HR 1.10; 95% CI = 0.44–2.71). Since DCD is a major and independent risk factor for NAS development [2, 3, 5], we believe that the effect of DCD therefore overshadows the effect of RhA on the development of NAS in this group of patients. Based on the distribution of several other independent risk factors for the development of NAS, it is unlikely that those factors could account for the association we found. For instance, no differences were observed in the baseline prevalence of PSC in recipients and the incidence of HAT after LT between LTs with DBD and DCD. As the results presented here introduce a potential second dimension to the domain of incompatibility in LT medicine, next to ABOi, the clinical implications might be substantial, given the ongoing scarcity of available liver grafts worldwide.

The total incidence of NAS after LT found in the present study (20.9%) is in concordance with findings in previous literature [4, 5]. In line with earlier findings, NAS is more prevalent in LT with DCD compared to LT with DBD. Increased ischemia‐related injury in LT with DCD due to the donor warm ischemia time is thought to be the main driver of this phenomenon [5, 6, 16]. Although 73.8% of grafts preserved using machine perfusion techniques were retrieved after DCD, the incidence of NAS in LT with machine perfusion (16.9%), regardless of the type of machine perfusion, was not different from the incidence of NAS in LTs with DBD (14.9%), and both of those numbers are lower compared to the NAS rate in LTs with DCD without machine perfusion (35.6%). This finding is in agreement with findings from a systematic review and meta‐analysis by Schlegel et al. (2023), concluding with moderate certainty that both HOPE and NMP are likely to reduce NAS after LT with DCD [17]. A similar conclusion was drawn by Van Rijn et al. (2021), demonstrating a significantly lower risk of NAS development when using D‐HOPE compared to controls that were preserved with static cold storage in a randomized controlled trial [5].

Median CIT was significantly longer for LTs with DBD (555 min) as compared to LTs with DCD (484 min) and LTs with machine perfusion (411 min; p = 0.018). Naturally, LTs with DCD and LTs with machine perfusion are amongst graft modalities that gained more ground later in time, thereby profiting from recent logistic and surgical efforts to reduce ischemia times.

Evidence on the effect of RhA on biliary complications after LT in general is scarce and exclusively observational in nature. Also, historically, RhA has not served as an allocation criterion for organ transplantation. Busquets et al. (2007) aimed to assess the effect of Rh incompatibility on a range of biliary complications in a cohort of 345 patients and, in doing so, found a higher incidence of biliary complications (e.g., biliary leaks and biliary strictures) in LT with nonidentical Rh status between donor and recipient. Contrary to our research, the study by Busquets et al. did not make a distinction between Rh incompatibility in general, including cases of either a Rh positive donor and Rh negative recipient or a Rh negative donor and Rh positive recipient, and RhA in particular, defined as the combination of a Rh positive donor and Rh negative recipient. However, we hypothesize that the risk of stricture formation is elevated only in procedures in which a Rh negative recipient receives a liver graft from a Rh positive donor. After all, such an immunological reaction might just exclusively take place in case of the introduction of a foreign antigen. In addition, separate analysis of biliary outcomes might have made it easier to assess the effect of both types of Rh incompatibility on biliary stricture formation [14]. The case report by Turiño‐Luque et al. (2012) discusses the development of biliary strictures in light of a Rh incompatible LT. Since this was a case of a Rh positive recipient and Rh negative donor, the applicability of this report to our research remains low. Also, a potential passenger lymphocyte syndrome was believed to have been a pathophysiological mechanism of action, which lies outside the scope of the current paper [15].

We evaluated the presence of RhD antigens on biliary epithelium in donor liver rest material following two distinct approaches (see Supplementary Materials 1 and 2). While both Okada et al. (1988) and Nakanuma et al. (1989) observed ABO antigen expression on biliary epithelial cells at several levels of the biliary tree, expression of RhD antigens on biliary epithelium had not been subject of investigation before [9, 10]. Although the presence of ABO blood group antigens on biliary epithelium as well as endothelial cells has been demonstrated, available literature is also clear on the absence of ABO blood group antigens on hepatocytes [18, 19]. As suggested by Sanchez et al. (1993), expression of ABO antigens may result in bile duct injury of an immunological nature in ABOi transplantation procedures [11]. However, and contrary to evidence on the expression of ABO antigens, our experiments assessing RhD expression on biliary epithelium yielded negative results. Mouro‐Chanteloup et al. (2002) have suggested that RhD expression on non‐erythroid surfaces is conditional on the presence of RhAG, a non‐erythroid Rh glycoprotein and ammonia transporter [20]. RhAG, in contrast to RhBG and RhCG, is not expressed in liver or biliary tissue, which may explain the absence of RhD expression we found [21]. Accordingly, it is suggested that indirect mechanisms might be driving the association between RhA and NAS. Unfortunately, no data were available on other potential RhA‐related complications, including alloimmunization, hemolysis, or passenger lymphocyte syndrome.

The Rh blood group system is characterized by antigens displayed on the surface of erythrocytes. While this system is known to have well over 50 distinct antigens, a Rh positive or Rh negative state specifically refers to the presence or absence of the RhD antigen, respectively. In contrast to the ABO blood group system, no spontaneous agglutinins exist within the Rh blood group system. Therefore, a Rh negative individual will not develop a hemolytic reaction upon first contact with another individual's Rh positive tissue. Only after sensitization and subsequent production of IgG anti‐Rh antibodies can severe reactions take place. Examples are hemolytic disease of the newborn and alloimmune hemolytic transfusion reactions [22]. It can be suggested that Rh negative recipients of Rh positive liver grafts who have been sensitized before (e.g., during pregnancy), are more prone to developing RhA‐related biliary complications after LT. Data on previous pregnancy and/or transfusion are absent in our cohort, and the only proxy we can use for previous pregnancies is female recipient sex. The 33 cases of RhA in LTs with DBD included 14 female recipients and 19 male recipients. The share of female recipients who developed NAS, 35.7% (n = 5), did not differ from the share of male recipients who developed NAS (31.6%, n = 6) (p = 0.803). Nevertheless, it stands out that this potential mechanism needs to be explored in further research. An equal argument can be made with regard to the role of desensitization therapy and enzymatic treatment during machine perfusion, as is the case for cases of ABOi, albeit in a research setting for the latter.

The observational nature of this cohort study is a limitation. However, strong ethical and logistical objections exist regarding experimental clinical research on this matter. In efforts to reduce information and selection bias to a minimum, we included all consecutive LTs that were performed in our center from 2000 onwards and ensured the use of consistent definitions for primary and secondary endpoints for all patients. We reported our efforts accordingly, using the widely accepted STROBE checklist (see Supplemental Material 3). Extensive documentation in patient files throughout the follow‐up period made the former particularly possible. Still, time‐related effects due to the great deal of developments that took place in the field of LT throughout the follow‐up period are not entirely ruled out. Also, variations between cases might still exist in the time from actual development of NAS to the date of diagnosis confirmation by biliary intervention.

Conclusion

We show that RhA is associated with a threefold increase in NAS development after LT with DBD. We hereby add a potential third dimension to the field of incompatibility in transplantation medicine, next to ABO and HLA incompatibility. This may implicate that health gains with respect to biliary stricture formation and graft survival can be expected if future allocation criteria for liver grafts are expanded with Rh blood group compatibility. In individual cases, avoiding RhA must be balanced against the need for rapid LT, and could be considered in patients with NAS listed for re‐LT.
