Quality of Antenatal Care Service in Public Health Facilities in Southern Ethiopia: Retrospective Study
Authors: Misganu Endriyas, Biruk Tinsou, Agegnehu Gebru, Abraham Alano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsThe provision of quality service demands continuous monitoring and evaluation of ongoing service provision and improving organizational processes. Hence, the aims of this study were to assess facility readiness to provide quality of antenatal care and quality of antenatal care in public health facilities in southern Ethiopia.MethodsA health facility‐based cross‐sectional and retrospective studies were done in 2018. Multi‐stage sampling was used to select 131 facilities of different levels and 2237 records. To assess the quality of ANC, all three dimensions (number of visits, early initiation and services provided) were considered. Factors associated with getting ANC components were assessed by using multi‐level mixed effects linear regression.ResultsFrom the total health facilities assessed, only 47.3% had at least one HIV test kit, 30.5% had iron, 23.7% folic acid, and 51.9% had iron and folic acid combined. From hospitals and health centers, 77.1% had syphilis testing kits, and 34.3% could perform hemoglobin level tests. Of 1222 records with gestational period, only 15.5% started ANC at or before 16 weeks of gestation, and only 2.7% started at or before 12 weeks. On average, women in rural areas received 2.5% points lower ANC components than those in urban areas (p = 0.04). Compared to women attending at hospitals, women attending at health centers receive 5% fewer ANC components (p = 0.001) while those attending at health post receive 20% lower (p < 0.001) ANC components.ConclusionThe readiness of facilities to provide quality ANC was suboptimal, and clients' medical records demonstrated poor performance in all programmatic quality indicators. Women living in rural areas, women attending health centers, and health posts receive lower ANC components. Close monitoring of facility readiness and supporting service provision is vital to ensure the quality of the service.

Background

Globally, complications during pregnancy, childbirth, and the postnatal period are the leading causes of death and disability among women of reproductive age [1]. Pregnancy‐related preventable morbidity and mortality remain unacceptably high, and in 2020, an estimated 287,000 women died globally, of which a large majority (95%) occurred in low‐ and middle‐income countries [2]. The maternal death in Ethiopia is high, and in 2020, it was estimated to be 267 per 100,000 [3].

Antenatal care (ANC) is a healthcare service provided by skilled healthcare professionals to women throughout their pregnancy and is one of the main services to reduce maternal death. It comprises screening and risk identification, prevention and management of pregnancy‐related or concurrent diseases, and health education and promotion [4, 5]. The focused ANC of four visits was recommended by WHO in 2002 [6]. In 2016, the WHO revised four visits to at least eight contacts as increased visits are linked to improved maternal satisfaction and outcomes [7].

Essential interventions in ANC include assessment and management of nutritional problems, maternal and fetal assessment, screening and managing common diseases (such as HIV and syphilis), assessment and management of common physiological symptoms, and health system interventions to improve utilization and quality of ANC [8]. ANC is the foundation for healthy motherhood as it provides a platform for health promotion, disease screening, and prevention [8]. ANC is also an opportunity to promote the use of skilled attendance at birth and healthy behaviors such as breastfeeding, early postnatal care, and planning for optimal pregnancy spacing [9]. In this way, quality ANC improves maternal health, decreases risks of anemia and pregnancy‐induced hypertension [10, 11, 12], and promotes positive pregnancy outcomes (like reduced risk of low birth weight and preterm babies) [11, 12, 13].

Despite its crucial benefits, ANC is an under‐utilized platform for maternal and perinatal health, and many of the opportunities continue to be missed [9, 14]. According to the 2019 Ethiopian demographic and health survey, only 43% of the women with live births in the 5 years before the survey had at least four or more ANC visits [15]. ANC service in general can be affected by both facility and community‐related factors. In Ethiopia, suboptimal service provision has been linked with urban–rural variations, poor readiness of facilities, shortages of supplies and trained staff, and levels of health facilities like health posts [16, 17]. The community‐ related factors reported from Ethiopia include maternal educational status, occupation, residence, age, income (wealth index), number of children, access to health services, religion, and participation in local women's networks [18, 19, 20].

The quality of ANC is measured by three dimensions: number of visits, timing of initiation of care, and inclusion of all recommended components of care [21]. These components greatly influence its effectiveness and might also affect women's decisions regarding the time of initiation and continuity of care [22, 23]. The Donabedian's systems‐based model of quality health care encompasses a three‐part approach to quality assessment, in which “good structure increases the likelihood of good process, and good process increases the likelihood of a good outcome” [24]. This implies that the health system should be equipped with the required resources to provide quality service.

The provision of quality service demands continuous monitoring and evaluation of ongoing service provision, and improving organizational processes by applying scientific methods is key to a better quality [25]. However, evidence on the status of health facilities' readiness and quality of ANC in the Southern Nations Nationalities and People's Region (SNNPR) of Ethiopia was scarce. Hence, the aims of this study were to assess facility readiness to provide quality antenatal care and quality of antenatal care in public health facilities in SNNPR, Ethiopia.

Methods and Materials

A health facility‐based cross‐sectional study and retrospective studies were conducted in 2018. The study was conducted in SNNPR, which at the time of the study represented about 20% of the country's population. SNNPR was the most diverse region in the country in terms of culture, language, and ethnicity. In 2017, there were 64 hospitals of all types, 720 health centers (HCs), 694 private clinics, and 3885 health posts (HPs). Currently, the region is administratively subdivided into four regions. These are Sidama, South Ethiopia, Central Ethiopia, and Southwest Ethiopia.

Ethiopia's health service provision is organized into threetiers: primary, secondary, and tertiary levels of care [26]. The primary level comprises HPs, HCs, and primary hospitals. The secondary level includes general hospitals, and the tertiary level includes referral hospitals. All levels provide ANC. However, it is recommended that the first visit should be provided at HC or higher level so that all tests can be done.

In this study, public health facilities providing ANC service for at least 2 years were considered for sampling. The sample size for the facility was estimated as suggested by measure evaluation [27] for the facility survey. n=[zα/2]2fqV2p

Where n is the sample size, f is the design effect, p is the anticipated proportion of facilities with the attribute of interest (quality ANC), q is 1 − p, V 2 is the relative variance, and [zα/2]2 is the reliability coefficient, which is 3.84 at 95% level of confidence.

Assuming p (proportion of facilities providing good quality ANC) 50% at 95% level of confidence and considering 20% relative error, design effect of 1.5, and after correcting for finite population, the final sample size was 131. Distribution of sample size to facility types considered the health center to hospital ratio and pairing HC with HP. For every selected HC, one HP in its catchment was selected. This yielded 9 hospitals, 61 HCs, and 61 HPs, giving a total of 131 facilities.

All sampled facilities were assessed for readiness to provide quality ANC as per their standard. The checklists were filled by interviewing the maternal health case team coordinator and by observation. For document review, ANC clients' cards of the most recent completed quarter (January–March 2017) were reviewed. Considering client flow, random samples of 30 cards from hospitals, 25 cards from HCs, and 10 cards from HPs were planned to review. Cards were selected from the ANC registration book by systematic sampling. To get sample cards, the total number of ANC fourth visitors on the ANC register in the past complete quarter was divided by the sample size allocated. When the number of ANC fourth visits was below the allocated sample size, cards from April (the most recent month) were considered. In cases where these sampling sources couldn't yield planned samples, cards from the previous quarter were considered. After selecting samples from the ANC registration book, the medical record number was used to identify cards. To compensate for non‐readable records and incorrect cards (like incorrect medical record number or ANC visits of less than four), the next case was reviewed.

To assess the quality of ANC given, all three dimensions (number of visits, early initiation, and services provided) were considered. However, the first dimension (number of visits) was used to select samples rather than to measure service quality. At the time of the study, the national ANC guideline recommended focused ANC (four visits). As the ANC's fourth visit shows a full course of care, these cards were identified and reviewed. Early initiation and services provided were assessed from these identified cards.

While components of ANC are diverse, key indicators selected for quality assessment were early initiation of ANC (before Week 16), syphilis test, tetanus toxoid vaccination (two or more dose), iron for at least 90 tablets, nutritional assessment (at least one time), HIV test, counseling on infant feeding (at least one time), counseling on danger signs (at least one time), counseling on family planning (FP) (at least one time) and counseling on maternal nutrition (at least one time). ANC service provided in HP was assessed as per standard using referral linkage, that is, if HP provided ANC, assessments, and lab results by referral linkage should be attached to women card at health post. If services provided were not written, it was recorded as zero (not given). However, early initiation of ANC was estimated out of valid records (missing cases were excluded for this specific quality component).

BSc holder clinicians (nurses or health officers) with prior experience in maternal health service and data collection were trained and collected the data. Daily onsite supervision was done by supervisors and investigators. Data was collected using SurveyCTO application on android based smart phones. Data was downloaded as an Excel file from SurveyCTO and exported to SPSS version 20.0 to clean, transform, and analyze data. Descriptive statistics like frequencies, percentages, and mean with standard deviation were performed to describe facility readiness and quality of ANC. The results were also compared between levels of health facilities and regions. Since facilities were nested in districts, considering district as cluster, the intracluster correlation coefficient (ICC = 32%) showed significant differences at the cluster level, indicating the need for multilevel modeling [24]. Therefore, factors associated with getting the proportion of ANC components were assessed by using multi‐level mixed effects linear regression. After fitting, the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) reduced from 19550.47 to 18918.39. The model was significantly different from simple linear regression (X 2 = 569.71, p < 0.001) (Supporting Information S1).

Ethical clearance was obtained from the Ethical Review Committee of SNNPR regional health bureau (Ref. የወ6‐19/22591). An official support letter was written to the study facilities. The record keepers were informed about the purpose, benefit, risk, and confidentiality of the study and provided written informed consent. The findings were presented in the regional annual review meeting to decision‐makers and other partners at different levels.

Results

A total of 131 public health facilities were included in the study, from which nine (6.8%), 61 (46.6%), and 61 (46.6%) were hospitals, HCs, and HPs, respectively.

Inventory

From the total health facilities assessed, only below half (47.3%) had at least one HIV test kit. From hospitals and HCs, about three‐fourths (77.1%) were testing syphilis while only one‐third (34.3%) were testing hemoglobin level. More than three‐fourths of facilities had a functional adult weight scale (75.6%), functional stethoscope (77.9%), and examination bed (89.3%). Regarding medicines, only less than one‐third had iron (30.5%) and folic acid (23.7%) while about half had iron and folic acid combined (51.9%) and TT antigen (57.3). Document Review

A total of 2237 cards were retrieved, and 10 ANC components were reviewed. The assessment of early initiation showed that only about one out of eleven clients (15.5%) started ANC at or before 16 weeks of gestation, and only 2.7% started at or before 12 weeks. About half (51.3%) of clients received TT vaccination two or more times that can protect current pregnancy, and about two out of five (40.6%) were tested for syphilis. Very few records indicated that mid‐upper arm circumference (MUAC) was measured (3.3%) at least one time over the follow‐up period, and only 9.3% of clients received iron tablets for 90 and above. About three‐fifths (63.2%) of clients were tested for HIV, and 61.7% were counseled on danger signs (Table 2). In summary, out of 10 items assessed, cards had an average of 3.4 (SD = 2.2) services that met national standard. Overall, no record demonstrated complete service as per programmatic standard, and as much as 12.3% of cards did not demonstrate any of items assessed, which when disaggregated becomes as much as 30.5% at health post level.

Factors Associated With Getting ANC Components

In adjusted multilevel mixed effects linear regression, residence and level of facilities had a statistically significant association. On average, women in rural areas received 2.5% points lower ANC components than those in urban areas (95%CI: −5.01 to −0.06; p = 0.04). Compared to women attending hospitals, women attending health centers receive 5% fewer ANC components (95%CI: −8.42 to −2.11; p = 0.001) while those attending health post receive 20% lower (95%CI: −24.02 to −16.40; p < 0.001) ANC components. Although older women received lower components, it was not statistically significant. Both district‐level and residual variances are significant (Table 3).

Discussion

The facilities included demonstrated suboptimal readiness in terms of availability of laboratory tests, crucial medical equipment, medicines, and items for precautions. Clients record with four ANC visits showed poor performance in all programmatic quality indicators.

This study presents suboptimal readiness of facilities with regional disparities that have the potential to affect quality service delivery. In general, hospitals' readiness was relatively better, and health posts were worse, but the availability of iron/folic acid was poor at hospital levels. However, the national survey conducted in the same year (2018) showed higher availability of iron/folic acid in hospitals than in other facility types [28]. A possible reason for the difference could be the small sample size of hospitals in this study. A study in low‐ and middle‐income countries indicated that there is a significant association between facility readiness and provision of care across countries [29].

Due to high priority given by the government to syphilis testing, more than three‐fourths (77.1%) of hospitals and HCs had kits for testing syphilis, but only 34.3% of hospitals and HCs had any method to test hemoglobin level. The document review indicated that only 9.3% of clients received at least 90 iron tablets. Low hemoglobin level testing capacity and poor iron/folic acid provision, thus, compromise the quality of ANC and may open room for anemia, one of the major causes of maternal death.

While early initiation of ANC is essential for the early detection of pregnancy‐related problems and unfavorable pregnancy outcomes [30], the record review showed that only 15.5% of clients started ANC at or before gestational week of 16, and only 2.7% started at or before 12 weeks. This finding was much lower than other studies conducted in Ethiopia [30, 31, 32]. Differences could be attributed to sources of data and data collection methods. Studies that used interviews with women report higher numbers. Both methods have their own limitations to consider. Community surveys are prone to recall bias, while record review is subject to registration (not recording services provided or false registration) errors.

Another component of ANC with poor performance was nutritional assessment, in which only 3.3% had recorded service provision. Possible justification could be that in the study settings, nutritional assessments are done at community level, usually in a campaign approach. Overall, as much as 12.3% of records did not meet any of the standards, and no record had complete (100%) components and the result was worse than reports of other studies [33, 34].

During the survey, it was noted that some hospitals do not provide comprehensive ANC service to general clients other than referred clients and due to this reason, getting ANC records with four visits was challenging (30 records within past 6 months). Another point that should be considered is that HPs in some zones were not providing ANC. It was reported that the HPs were only identifying clients and referring to HCs and such few HPs were replaced during sampling.

Women living in rural areas, women attending health centers, and health post receive lower ANC components. In addition, the differences exist between districts and individuals within districts. Although the government of Ethiopia has been struggling to improve access to health facilities, a lot has to be done to ensure universal access to sexual and reproductive healthcare services and to maintain quality, which is the aim of the Sustainable Development Goal agenda 3.7. The disparities among districts and service delivery levels need political commitment to address equity.

Even though we replaced incomplete and missing records and reviewed cards with four ANC visits, the poor recording system might have affected the document review. In addition, this study focused on the quality of programmatic indicators of ANC and some important clinical aspects like BP measurement, urine tests and others are missing.

Conclusion

The readiness of facilities to provide quality ANC was suboptimal in terms of availability of laboratory tests, crucial medical equipment, medicines, and precautions. Clients' medical records demonstrated poor performance in all programmatic quality indicators. Availing essential supplies and trained staff could improve service provision. Close monitoring of facility readiness and supporting service provision is vital to ensure the quality of the service.
