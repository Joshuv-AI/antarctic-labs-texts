Clinical and epidemiological factors affecting donor corneal endothelial cell density
Authors: Eden Amir, Dean David Lichter, Netanel Corem, Abraham Solomon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/aimsThe limited availability of corneal donors poses a significant challenge to meeting transplantation demands. Factors such as advanced donor age and prior cataract surgery are known to reduce donor corneal quality, yet a predictive system for resource optimisation is lacking. This study aimed to identify clinical and epidemiological factors affecting donor corneal endothelial cell density (ECD) in an Israeli eye bank, focusing on the impact of short death-to-retrieval time (DRT).MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 611 donor corneas retrieved at Hadassah Medical Center between 2018 and 2022. Donor age, sex, medical history, cause of death and DRT were evaluated for their association with adequate ECD (defined locally as ≥2400 cells/mm²), using univariate and multivariable analyses.ResultsThe study included 611 corneas from 310 donors, with a mean ECD of 2624 cells/mm² (SD 519); 76.3% (n=466) met the local adequacy threshold. The mean donor age was 64.4 years (SD 15.3), and median DRT was 2 hours and 29 minutes. In univariate analysis, lower ECD was associated with advanced age, prior cataract surgery and systemic conditions such as diabetes mellitus, hypertension and ischaemic heart disease. In multivariable analysis, only advanced age (adjusted OR 0.96 per year) and phakic lens status (adjusted OR 14.25) remained significant. A non-significant trend toward higher adequacy was observed in corneas retrieved within 4 hours of death (82.5% vs 75%; p=0.12).ConclusionsAdvanced donor age and prior cataract surgery were the strongest predictors of lower ECD. The potential benefit of shorter DRT on corneal quality warrants further investigation. These findings are subject to limitations, including reliance on a local adequacy threshold (≥2400 cells/mm²) and missing DRT data in part of the cohort.

Introduction

Corneal disease is the fifth leading cause of preventable blindness in the world, following cataract, refractive error, glaucoma and age-related macular degeneration. Approximately 2.9 million individuals worldwide experience vision impairment attributed to corneal opacity.1 Among these cases, some can be treated by corneal transplantation, representing the most common and effective tissue transplantation in medicine. Over 50 000 cornea transplantations are carried out in the USA alone every year.2 Surgery can involve the replacement of the entire thickness of the cornea or the specific diseased corneal layer. To date, no effective artificial substitute for corneal tissue has been developed.3

For a cornea to be defined as suitable for transplantation, it must meet specific criteria. Unlike organs, tissue typing is not required, but a thorough assessment of the donor’s medical history, serological tests and an evaluation of the tissue itself is essential. The tissue evaluation involves slit-lamp biomicroscope to detect signs of pathology, in particular endothelial disease. In addition, a specular microscope is used to examine the density and morphology of the corneal endothelial cells.4

A pivotal criterion, providing a quantifiable measure of tissue health and accounting for a substantial portion of corneas disqualified for transplantation, is the endothelial cell density (ECD). In most eye banks, a cornea is considered suitable for transplantation if its central ECD does not fall below 2000–2500 cells/mm2.5 6 The threshold may vary between penetrating and endothelial keratoplasty due to differences in postoperative endothelial cell loss,7 and is ultimately determined at the discretion of the Medical Director within each institute.8 For instance, at our Eye Bank in Hadassah Medical Center, corneas with an ECD of at least 2400 cells/mm2 are considered suitable for transplantation. In practice, a substantial number of corneas fail to meet the above-mentioned criteria and are eventually discarded. Since corneal donation is a sensitive issue involving legal, ethical, religious and cultural considerations, minimising the extent of post-procurement rejections is imperative. Moreover, in a world where the need for corneal donation far exceeds the supply,9 it is important to consider how resources can be allocated more efficiently.

Various studies have endeavoured to identify the most influential factors affecting the ECD of donor corneas. Findings indicate that donor age and a history of cataract surgery are the most significant negative factors.10 11 However, the Cornea Donor Study reported no significant difference in graft survival between older and younger donors when ECD was controlled.12 This has not led to the definition of specific criteria or cut-offs within the international guidelines of major eye banking institutions such as the Eye Bank Association of America or the European Eye Bank Association.8 13 Additionally, exploration into other donor characteristics, including medical history or cause of death, has yet to yield unequivocal conclusions.

The Eye Bank at Hadassah Medical Center stands out as one of the largest and leading eye banks in Israel. Eye banks in Israel distinguish themselves with an exceptionally brief death-to-retrieval time (DRT) compared with their global counterparts. Typically, this interval does not exceed 3 hours, attributed to the location of eye banks within hospital premises. Furthermore, religious considerations, rooted in Jewish tradition regarding burial with a minimal delay, contribute to this expedited process.

This article aims to identify clinical and epidemiological factors associated with donor corneal ECD, with particular emphasis on the impact of the relatively short death to retrieval interval at our eye bank. This article is the first of its kind conducted in an Israeli eye bank.

Materials and methods

This retrospective study included all corneal tissue retrieved at Hadassah Medical Center between the years 2018 and 2022. Demographic and clinical data were extracted for all cornea donors from the Hadassah eye bank records, and the medical records of the donors were evaluated for missing data.

Between these years, the database included 725 corneas from 367 donors. Of these, 57 donors were excluded due to incomplete data. The study was conducted on 611 corneas from 310 donors for whom complete data were available (except for DRT).

Among these 611 corneas, records for DRT were missing for 201 corneas, as this data collection began only from May 2019 onward. Consequently, data completeness for DRT is ensured for records obtained from that date onward. To evaluate potential bias related to missing DRT data, donor age, lens status, mean ECD and the proportion of corneas with adequate ECD (≥2400 cells/mm²) were compared between corneas with and without available DRT values.

The study was conducted according to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Storage and assessment

Corneas were preserved in Eusol-C storage solution (Alchimia, CTC 001–01) at 4°C, and kept at room temperature for approximately 2 hours before examination with a Konan CellChek EB-10 specular microscope. ECD was assessed with the Flex-Center method. In accordance with our eye bank practice, at least 30 cells manually marked in each of three different fields per cornea.

Variables

We gathered relevant donor and harvesting data, including donor age, sex, cause of death, hospitalisation department, time elapsed from death to retrieval and the presence of systemic diseases such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, cancer and ischaemic heart disease. Systemic diseases were identified based on diagnoses documented in the donor’s medical records.

In this study, the dichotomous dependent variable is defined as the adequacy of corneal tissue for transplantation. ECD was categorised into two groups based on a threshold of 2400 cells/mm²: adequate (≥2400 cells/mm²) and inadequate (<2400 cells/mm²). This threshold reflects the local criterion currently used at Hadassah Medical Center’s eye bank.

Statistical methods

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 27.0. To examine the association between two categorical variables, the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test was used. The linear-by-linear test was applied to assess a trend for ordinal categorical variables. Comparison of a quantitative variable between two independent groups was performed using the two-sample t-test. A multivariable logistic regression model was applied to simultaneously assess the effect of several variables on the dichotomous dependent variable. This was done using the stepwise, forward, likelihood ratio method. All tests applied were two-tailed, and a p value of 0.05 or less was considered significant.

Results

The mean donor age was 64.4 years (SD 15.3) ranging from 4 to 85 years. Among the donors, 56.3% were male (344) whereas 43.7% were female (267). As for medical history, 23.7% had diabetes mellitus (145), 42.7% had hypertension (261), 20.5% had ischaemic heart disease (125) and 59.7% had cancer (365). The most common causes of death were cancer (51.6%) and sepsis (13.7%). The most common departments for harvesting were oncology (34.7%), intensive care unit (25.2%) and internal medicine departments (21.4%).

In terms of DRT, most corneas were retrieved between 2 and 3 hours (33.9%), followed by 1–2 hours (24.6%). The mean DRT was 2 hours and 52 minutes (SD 1.72 hours) (table 1).

Corneas without DRT data were derived from older donors (67.9±14.7 vs 62.7±15.3 years, p=0.006), were less often phakic (73.2% vs 86.4%, p<0.001) and exhibited lower mean ECD (2548±564 vs 2662±493 cells/mm², p=0.025). The proportion of corneas with adequate ECD (≥2400 cells/mm²) was also lower in this group (65.7% vs 81.5%, p<0.001).

The mean ECD was 2624 (SD 519) cells/mm2. The mean donor age of corneas assessed to be suitable was 62 years (SD 16), whereas the mean donor age of those corneas assessed to be unsuitable for transplantation was 72 years (SD 10) (p<0.001).

ECD significantly decreased with increasing donor age, from a mean of 3104 cells/mm² (SD 598) in donors younger than 40 years to 2308 cells/mm² (SD 604) in those aged 80 years or older (p<0.001) (figure 1).

Of the corneas harvested, 82.0% originated from eyes with natural lenses (phakic), whereas 17.7% were from eyes with artificial lenses (pseudophakic), and only 0.3% from eyes without lenses (aphakic). A substantial 87% of corneas from donors with phakic lenses had an adequate ECD (≥2400 cells/mm²), contrasting with only 25.5% of those with pseudophakic and aphakic lenses meeting this criterion (p<0.001). This result highlights that pseudophakic and aphakic lenses are risk factors for lower ECD.

Discussion

This study examined clinical and epidemiological factors related to cornea donors that might negatively affect ECD, a key parameter for determining transplant eligibility. Factors such as age, sex, history of cataract surgery, systemic diseases, cause of death and DRT were investigated. The aim was to identify predictors of lower ECD and thereby support improved tissue selection and reduce unnecessary harvesting of corneas unlikely to meet local adequacy criteria. The study was conducted at the Hadassah Medical Center eye bank in Israel, which is characterised by particularly short DRT intervals and a relatively older donor population compared with many other eye banks worldwide.

Donor age was found to be a major factor associated with lower ECD, with older donors tending to have lower counts. Studies in eye banks around the world have shown similar results, such as Armitage and Easty10 in the UK, who demonstrated that donor age is a major determinant of ECD. Furthermore, previous studies have established that ECD decreases progressively throughout life.14 However, in our cohort, a substantial proportion of corneas from older donors still exhibited adequate ECD (≥2400 cells/mm²), 80.2% up to age 69 and 65.3% up to age 79. A notable decline in adequacy was only observed above age 80 (52.1%). Since the definition of ECD adequacy in this study is based on a local threshold of 2400 cells/mm², and some medical centres accept lower thresholds (eg, 2000 cells/mm²), these findings support the approach of avoiding a strict age cut-off in international eye banking guidelines.

Corneas from pseudophakic donors were found to be at much higher risk of being unsuitable for transplantation compared with corneas from phakic donors, 74.5% versus 12.6%, respectively. This result is consistent with the study by Conrad-Hengerer et al, which indicated damage to the corneal endothelium after cataract surgery by standard phacoemulsification or femtosecond laser-assisted phacoemulsification reflected in an 8.1–13.7% decrease in ECD on average 3 months after surgery.15 The study by Kwon et al also showed a significantly lower ECD in corneas from donors who underwent refractive surgery compared with donors who did not,11 although the effect of refractive surgery was not examined in our study.

A longer DRT was associated with a non-significant trend toward lower ECD. The lack of statistical significance may be attributed to the particularly short DRT range in our cohort, influenced by local practices and religious considerations rooted in Jewish tradition that advocate prompt burial. In this study, the average DRT was 2 hours and 52 min (SD 1.72 h), whereas previous studies examined intervals of 6, 12 or even 24 hours. Some of these studies demonstrated a statistically significant negative effect of prolonged DRT on ECD, although the clinical relevance of this effect was limited.10 16 17 Additional negative effects of longer DRT have been reported, including increased epithelial defects,18 morphological changes in endothelial cells19 and a higher likelihood of positive serology results.20

The average age of donors in the study was 64.4 years (SD 15.3), with an average ECD of 2624 (SD 519). The age of donors in this study is relatively high for eye banks. Nonetheless, the average ECD remained relatively high. The relatively high ECD observed in this study in older donors may also be attributed to the particularly short retrieval times at our eye bank.

The study examined the effect of donors’ clinical status on ECD. Clinical status was assessed based on cause of death, medical background and indirectly, the hospital department in which the donor was hospitalised. Death due to infection was significantly associated with lower ECD. Armitage and Easty reported the opposite effect for cardiovascular or cerebrovascular causes of death,10 a pattern also observed in our data.

Among background diseases, diabetes mellitus (DM), ischaemic heart disease (IHD) and hypertension (HTN) were associated with lower ECD in univariate analysis. These findings align with previous studies reporting reduced ECD in donors with IHD,21 22 23DM and, in some cases, HTN,24 though other studies found no such association.11 Olsen25 hypothesised a shared mechanism between endothelial dystrophy and atherosclerotic disease. However, in our study, these associations did not remain significant in multivariable analysis, possibly due to confounding by donor age.

Being a multi-organ donor was not associated with a significant difference in the proportion of corneas with adequate ECD (≥2400 cells/mm²). In contrast, several previous studies reported a higher likelihood of adequate ECD among organ donors.10 20 26 This discrepancy may be attributed to confounding factors, such as the relatively younger age of organ donors in those studies. In our cohort, the number of organ donors was relatively small, which may have limited the ability to detect a similar effect. Additionally, no significant differences in ECD adequacy were observed between male and female donors, aligning with the prevailing evidence in the literature.

This study has several limitations. First, it relied solely on ECD as a measure of corneal tissue quality. Although ECD is widely regarded as a key indicator of tissue health, it does not encompass all factors that could disqualify a cornea for transplantation. Other important factors, such as tissue quality assessed via slit lamp examination, positive serology or suspected infection, were not comprehensively evaluated. Future studies should integrate these additional factors to provide a more comprehensive assessment of donor tissue quality.

Second, this study focused on the adequacy of ECD based on a local threshold (≥2400 cells/mm²), rather than on long-term transplant outcomes. The actual success of transplantation depends on multiple recipient-related and procedure-related factors, which were beyond the scope of this study.

Third, DRT data were unavailable for 201 corneas, as systematic documentation of this parameter began only in May 2019. Corneas lacking DRT values were derived from older donors and were less often phakic, with correspondingly lower mean ECD and adequacy rates (≥2400 cells/mm²). This may have limited the ability to fully assess the impact of DRT on ECD.

In summary, this study examined donor-related and process-related factors that may influence corneal ECD, a key determinant of graft eligibility. Advanced donor age and prior cataract surgery emerged as the most significant predictors of lower ECD in multivariable analysis. The cohort was characterised by particularly short DRTs compared with other eye banks worldwide. Even within this narrow window, a trend was observed suggesting that shorter retrieval times may contribute to higher ECD. These findings may help inform donor selection and retrieval practices in eye banks.
