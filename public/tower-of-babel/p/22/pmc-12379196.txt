Identification of potential drug targets and possible drugs for esophageal carcinoma from genetic insights: a comprehensive two-sample Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Juan Wang, Ji Sun, Chen Liang, Haibin Ou, Jiong Liu, Aoqing Ye, Qiuji Wu, Yu Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEsophageal carcinoma (EC) is the eighth most common cancer, which is a major health problem on a global scale. Comprehensive treatments such as surgery, radiotherapy and chemotherapy have demonstrated efficacy in certain patients with early-stage esophageal carcinoma. However, it is crucial to explore improved treatment modalities for individuals with advanced, recurrent and metastatic esophageal carcinoma. MethodsThe eQTL datasets were obtained from Drug-Gene Interaction Database (DGIdb V.4.2.0) and an interesting review about druggable genes. The two-sample MR was used to identify potential drug targets. Co-localization analysis was applied to evaluate if the genes expression single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) drove and was associated with EC risk. And drug prediction and molecular docking were used to verify the pharmaceutical value of potential clinical drugs. ResultsThe present study found thirty potential drug targets that were remarkably correlated with the risk of developing esophageal carcinoma at least in two eQTL datasets by two-sample MR analysis. Five genes colocalized with esophageal carcinoma were identified through colocalization analysis and the medicinal value of four potential clinical drugs was testified by molecular docking.ConclusionsWe identified five drug targets by MR analysis and co-localization analysis and four drugs by drug prediction and molecular docking analysis respectively, which provide new insights for treatment of esophageal carcinoma.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-02777-9.Keywords: Esophageal carcinoma; Mendelian randomization; Drug targets; CCR1, SCAP, SETD2

Introduction

Esophageal carcinoma is the eighth most common cancer in the world. According to statistics from the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) in 2020, there were approximately 604,100 new cases and 544,100 deaths from esophageal cancer worldwide, with China accounting for over half of these cases. Moreover, esophageal carcinoma is the sixth leading cause of cancer-related mortality globally, representing a significant public health challenge [1]. The main types of esophageal cancer are esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) and esophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC), with esophageal squamous cell carcinoma accounting for 85% of esophageal carcinomas worldwide [2, 3].

In current medical practice, the treatment strategies for esophageal cancer primarily include surgical resection, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and the emerging field of immunotherapy. Despite significant advancements in early diagnosis and therapeutic techniques for esophageal cancer in recent years, the prognosis for patients in advanced stages remains challenging. A key statistical finding indicates that only about 20% of esophageal cancer cases are diagnosed at an early stage, where the 5-year survival rate can be as high as 85%[4]. In contrast, the majority of patients diagnosed at a later stage have a significantly lower 5-year survival rate, dropping to around 20%. Traditional treatment methods have played a role in improving patient outcomes, but as the disease progresses, the responsiveness of patients to existing treatment options diminishes, and the choices for effective treatments become more limited. Faced with this harsh reality, there is a need to explore new pharmacological targets for treatment. Moreover, drug repurposing strategies, which involve investigating new applications of known drugs in the treatment of esophageal cancer, offer an economically viable and potentially efficient therapeutic approach. This method not only has the potential to reduce the high costs associated with the development of new drugs but may also lead to treatment plans that are more aligned with patient needs.

Mendelian randomization (MR) can be applied to predict drug efficacy as a novel genetic statistical method equivalent to randomized controlled trials [5]. The SNPs as genetic variations are correlated to the expression quantitative trait Loci (eQTLs) associated with gene expression difference, which possess an advantage in evaluating the correlations between long-term exposure and drugs targeted to proteins [6]. The EC dataset from genome-wide association studies (GWAS) can be used to identify SNPs associated with EC as outcome [7]. We can testify the casual associated relationship between gene expression and EC by two-sample MR analysis.

Additionally, gene co-localization analysis can exclude potential confounding factors as a validation method of MR analysis results and phenome-wide association study (PheWAS) analysis can recognize possible side effects of target gene expression products as a supplement to MR analysis [8]. Also, gene enrichment analysis and protein–protein interaction (PPI) network construction as traditional bio-informatics research methods can help reveal biological characteristics and functions of the underlying druggable targets for EC [8]. Moreover, drug prediction discovers more potential medications targeted to proteins encoded by genes based on the Drug Signatures Database (DSigDB) and molecular docking further verifies the binding ability of drugs to targeted proteins for EC treatment [8].

In light of the limited effectiveness of current treatments for advanced esophageal carcinoma, this study undertakes an extensive mendelian randomization (MR) analysis. By utilizing the rich resources of Genome-Wide Association Studies (GWAS) databases, the research aims to uncover the causal relationship between esophageal cancer and the expression levels of genes. The ultimate goal is to identify new therapeutic targets that could potentially enhance the pharmaceutical development for esophageal cancer (EC). Furthermore, the study seeks to broaden the clinical use of existing medications by repurposing them for EC treatment, thereby improving the overall therapeutic landscape for esophageal cancer patients.

Methods and materials

Study design

The present study undertakes an extensive Mendelian Randomization (MR) analysis to uncover the causal relationship between esophageal cancer and the expression levels of genes. The instrumental variables (IVs) were extracted based on the following three criteria[8]. First, the SNPs are directly linked to exposure traits associated with at least one gene expression. Second, the SNPs are not associated with exposure-outcome confounders. Third, the SNPs affect outcome through exposure solely. This research was conducted in strict adherence to the STROBE-MR guidelines, which are designed for studies employing Mendelian Randomization (MR) methods. For this study, all MR analyses were performed using summary statistics that are openly accessible to the public, eliminating the need for extra ethical approval or consent. The analytical principles and procedures are illustrated in Figure S1.

Data sources

We tried to search druggable genes by conducting previous reviews and database searches. Fortunately, we took a list of druggable genes from a study, in which authors got a clue from the Drug-Gene Interaction Database (DGIdb V.4.2.0, https://maayanlab.cloud/Enrichr/) and an interesting review about druggable genes [9]. For two-sample MR analysis, For the two-sample MR analysis, the eQTL datasets used as exposure data were obtained from blood and esophageal mucosa samples of European ancestry individuals. We obtained the blood eQTL datasets from eQTLGen (https://www.eqtlgen.org/cis-eqtls.html) that contains cis-eQTLs of 16,987 genes from 31,684 blood samples of healthy European ancestry individuals and from the GTEx project V.8, which includes cis-eQTLs from 755 European blood samples[10, 11]. The esophageal mucosa eQTL dataset, whic is a part of the GTEx project V.8 (https://gtexportal.org/home/) involved cis-eQTLs collected from 555 esophageal mucosa European samples[11]. The esophageal cancer (EC) GWAS dataset, used as outcome data, was obtained from the GWAS Catalog database with a study accession GCST90041891 (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/downloads/summary-statistics), which consists of 456,276 UK Biobank participants with 196 clinically diagnosed esophageal carcinomas and 456,080 controls. The details are presented in Table S1.

Two-sample MR analysis

We conducted all analyses using the R (v4.4.0) statistical software and the MR analyses were based on R package Two-Sample MR (version 0.5.6) and MR_PRESSO mainly. The eQTLs of druggable genes pooled from three independent cis-eQTL datasets were regarded as exposure data collected from three cis-eQTL datasets separately. The instrumental variables (IVs) were extracted according to the following three criteria [8]. First, the SNPs were directly linked with exposure traits which were associated with at least one gene expression. Second, the SNPs are not associated with exposure-outcome confounders. Third, the SNPs affect outcome through exposure solely. Consequently, SNPs within 100 kb of each gene's transcriptional start site (TSS) and with a P-value < 5 × 10^−8 were included. These SNPs were clumped at r2 < 0.2 using a reference panel from the 1000 Genomes Project and were identified as instrumental variables (IVs) if their F-statistic was > 10 [F = (Beta/Se)2] [8]. Furthermore, to validate the reliability of our findings, we employed the mRnd instrument for a power analysis, setting a benchmark of at least 80% power, a standard widely recognized in genetic epidemiology. This approach ensures that our research possesses the necessary statistical power to identify genuine causal links between gene expression and esophageal cancer. Finally, to resolve issues with missing information and discrepancies when merging the exposure and outcome data, we eliminated incompatible genetic variants and unclear palindromic SNPs to avoid strand confusion. Only those SNPs that were present in both datasets after standardization were considered in the MR analysis. For the MR analysis, inverse variance weighted (IVW) method under a random-effects model and Wald ratio method were counted as primary methods of causal judgment between druggable genes and EC. The former one was used for genes with more than one SNP and the other was for genes with only one SNP. The false discovery rate (FDR)-corrected P values (P-FDR) was used as the criteria for detecting more significant P-values, calculated with the Benjamini–Hochberg method. FDR-corrected p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Heterogeneity and pleiotropy were tested by the Cochran’s Q statistic and MR-Egger intercept methods respectively [8]. The MR-PRESSO global test was applied further in significant outlier detection and horizontal plural effect revision by excluding outliers [8].

Co-localization analysis

On the basis of R package colco, the co-localization analysis was introduced to validate potential genetic associations between EC and gene targets discovered at least in two datasets [8]. The test area should be determined with gene’s transcriptional start site (TSS) and distance parameter before starting a co-localization analysis. The harmonized SNPs were identified for relevance exploration with genetic expression in test area, EC risk and both with default priori probabilities p1 = 1 × 10–4, p2 = 1 × 10–4 and p12 = 1 × 10–5. The posterior probability (PP) was induced for hypotheses support quantification, which includes PPH0 to PPH4. PPH0 indicates no association with either gene expression or EC risk; PPH1 means only association with gene expression; PPH2 means only association with EC risk; PPH3 means association with EC risk and gene expression for distinct causal variants; and PPH4 means association with EC risk and gene expression for a shared causal variant. Some possible distance parameters including 1 Mb, 500 kb, 100 kb, 50 kb and 25 kb were included in our study to maximize the sensitivity of the co-localization analysis and the significant threshold for co-localization analysis was defined as PPH4 > 0.75 in at least one dataset.

Enrichment analysis and protein–protein interaction network construction

To explore the potential functional characteristics and biological relevance of the therapeutic target genes of EC significant in two-sample MR analysis and co-localization analysis, enrichment analyses and protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks were introduced [8]. Enrichment analyses were on account of R package clusterProfler and org.Hs.eg.db and PPI networks were constructed by GeneMANIA (https://genemania.org/) [8]. The Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment provided druggable genes information of biological process (BP), molecular function (MF), and cellular component (CC) and the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) provided metabolic pathway detail. Furthermore, PPI networks indicated the possible genetic expressions interacting with druggable targets and potential patterns of interaction.

Gene-level phenome-wide association analysis

The phenome-wide association study (PheWAS) was used for assessing the possible side effects and horizontal pleiotropy of identified druggable targets of EC, with around 450 thousand UK Biobank participants’ exome sequencing, which was based on AstraZeneca PheWAS Portal (https://azphewas.com/) [8]. The P-values of significant and suggestive thresholds were considered as 1 × 10–6 and 1 × 10–8 individually.

Candidate drug prediction and molecular docking

To discover potential drugs of EC treatment, candidate drug prediction and molecular docking were induced. First, the Drug Signatures Database (DSigDB, http://dsigdb.tanlab.org/DSigDBv1.0/) contained 17,389 distinct compounds correlating to 22,527 genes and was applied according to the identified druggable genes previously [8]. Then, the interaction of druggable gene expression product and drug candidates from DSigDB was further evaluated and testified by molecular docking on the level of atoms in which interaction pattern and the binding energy take a significant role. Based on AutodockVina (v.1.2.2, http://autodock.scripps.edu/), the top 3 drug candidates of every gene target identified by two-sample MR and co-localization analyses were used for molecular docking. Macromolecular protein structure data with corresponding PDB IDs were acquired from Protein Data Bank (PDB, http://www.rcsb.org/) and micromolecular drug structure data with PubChem IDs were downloaded from PubChem Compound Database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). The details can be acquired from Yu et al.’s study [8]. The flow chart of the study is shown in Figure S1.

Results

Screening for druggable genes associated with esophageal carcinoma

Our list of druggable genes was from DGIdb v4.2.0 and a review. The DGIdb contains the information of 3953 druggable genes shown in Table S2 and there were 4463 genes enumerated in the review (Table S3). We integrated the former into a list in the same way as a study for discovering more druggable genes, presented in Table S4 [9].

Candidate druggable genes for EC-related traits

There was a total of 5883 druggable genes used for MR analysis with EC by searching SNPs relative from three datasets, including blood eQTL, blood GTEx and esophageal mucosa GTEx. Firstly, the 122,050 SNPs, 4664 SNPs and 3817 SNPs relevant to 3420 genes, 1373 genes and 1332 genes were extracted from blood eQTL, blood GTEx and esophageal mucosa GTEx as the IVs respectively (Tables S5-7). Additionally, the characteristics of these SNPs are shown in Tables S8-S10. We detected 497 genes, 29 genes and 18 genes from blood eQTL, blood GTEx and esophageal mucosa GTEx with P-FDR < 0.05 associated causally with EC risk, after two-sample MR analysis (Tables S11-13). To improve the accuracy of MR results, we extracted an intersection of each two datasets according to P-FDR < 0.05. Twenty-four genes were significantly associated to EC in blood eQTL and blood GTEx datasets, including CCR1, THBS3, CCR3, ACP2, UGGT2, SETD2, SCAP, P2RX4, CSNK1G1, PPIL3, NDUFA6, BAZ2B, TNFSF12, SHMT1, RXFP2, PRDX5, CLEC4 C, SIRT1, QSOX1, BTN3 A2, HLADQA2, SENP6, CDKN2B, NBL1 (Fig. 1A). Heterogeneity analysis and pleiotropic analysis of drug targets for esophageal carcinoma and side effect assessment of candidate drugs

Five potential druggable gene targets (CCR1, CCR3, SCAP, RXFP2 and SETD2) were identified by two-sample MR and co-localization analysis. We testified the possible side effect, heterogeneity or pleiotropy as the medicability evaluation, based on AstraZeneca PheWAS Portal database, Cochran's Q statistic and MR-Egger intercept test. Verifying predicted candidate drugs by molecular docking

We tried to verify these predicted candidate drugs by introducing molecular docking, which tests the stability of the binding site between compounds and proteins produced by gene expression. The top 3 compounds of each five druggable targets significant for P-value and found in PubChem Compound Database were used for molecular docking (Table 1). Then, we applied Autodock Vina (v.1.2.2) that detects binding sites and binding energy by interactions of proteins and compounds for molecular docking. Finally, we totally identified four potential drugs for five druggable targets with binding energy < −5, including CCR1-A-987306, CCR1-CYPROTERONE ACETATE, CCR3-wortmannin and CCR3-diphenylpyraline (Table 2). The Fig. 5 displayed some visible strong electrostatic interactions like hydrogen bonds and target in proteins occupied by the potential drugs.

Discussion

Principal findings

The present study identified thirty potential drug targets that were remarkably correlated with the risk of developing esophageal carcinoma at least in two eQTL datasets through two-sample MR analysis. Subsequently, we identified five genes colocalized with esophageal carcinoma through colocalization analysis and testified drug prediction results of four potential clinical drugs (A-987306, CYPROTERONE ACETATE, wortmannin and diphenylpyraline) by molecular docking. In addition, five potential therapeutic targets were provided for personalized treatment of esophageal carcinoma which need to be explored in subsequent mechanism studies. In the meantime, the four drugs that we offer to patients with esophageal cancer may be validated in a randomized controlled trial in the future.

The five potential druggable gene targets (CCR1, CCR3, RXFP2, SCAP, SETD2) were identified by two-sample MR and co-localization. CCR1 and CCR3 are surface proteins belonging to G protein-coupled receptor family, which can bind to multiple ligands, such as CCL15 (MIP-1α), CCL5 (RANTES), CCL23 (Eotaxin-2) and so on. CCR1 expression in the tumor microenvironment can attract immunosuppressive cells [13, 14]. These cells secrete immunosuppressive factors, such as TGF-β and IL-10, which suppress anti-tumor immune response and promote tumor growth and metastasis [15], while expression on tumor cell surface can promote tumor cell migration and invasion by binding to ligands [16]. In addition, the combination of CCR1 and CCL14 can promote vascular endothelial cell migration and angiogenesis [14, 17]. Althouh the expression of CCR1 has not been previously link to esophageal carcinoma, previous studies have shown that the expression of CCR1 in hepatocellular carcinoma tissues is significantly lower than that in adjacent liver tissues [18], which is consistent with our findings. A review by Gilchrist et al. discussed CCR1 antagonists suitable for studying multiple myeloma [19], which show that CCR1 has great potential in treatment. Although there is limited direct evidence supporting the association of CCR3 with EC, the mRNA expression level of CCR3 in the skin of anaplastic large cell lymphoma lesions is significantly higher than that in normal skin [20]. RXFP2, also known as relaxin/insulin-like family peptide receptor 2, is a class of G-protein-coupled receptors with leucine-rich repeats. Most of research found that RXFP2 is associated with cryptorchidism and resistant hypertension (rHTN) in men [21]. The research of Sabine et al. shows that INSL3 and its receptor RXFP2 have tumor-promoting activity in thyroid cancer [22], while our study indicate that RXFP2 expression was inversely proportional to the of occurrence EC. SREBP cleavage-activating protein (SCAP) is a vital protein in endoplasmic reticulum that interacts with SREBPs (sterol regulatory element-binding proteins) to regulate the expression of genes related to lipid metabolism [23]. Numerous studies have shown that SCAP is highly expressed in various tumors and promotes tumor growth [24–27], which is consistent with our study. In addition, increased SCAP activity may impair the anti-tumor response of CD8+T cells, thereby promoting tumor growth [28]. Given its role in lipid metabolism and tumor growth, SCAP is a potential target for cancer therapy. SETD2 (SET domain-containing 2) is a histone lysine methyltransferase that is mainly responsible for the trimethylation of lysine 36 th (H3 K36 me3) of histone H3, a modification that plays a crucial role in gene expression regulation [29]. Currently, an increasing number of tumors have been shown to be associated with mutations or abnormal express of SETD2, such as prostate cancer, clear cell renal cell carcinoma, pancreatic cancer, lung cancer, gastric cancer and so on [29–34], which is related to the occurrence and development of tumors. The mutation status of SETD2 has been identified as a new potential therapeutic biomarker for immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) immunotherapy in various refractory cancers. Furthermore, SETD2 inactivation promotes chemokine and programmed cell death protein-1 expression through the NR2 F1-STAT1 signaling pathway and enhances antigen presentation, thereby sensitizing cancer cells to immune checkpoint blocking [35]. As an epigenetic modification enzyme, SETD2 has shown great potential in the treatment of tumors. With the deepening of research, SETD2 may also be expected to become an important target for the precision treatment of esophageal cancer.

At the same time, we identified four potential drugs (A-987306, CYPROTERONE ACETATE, wortmannin and diphenylpyraline) for the treatment of EC by DSigDB database prediction and molecular docking verification according to five drug targets above. All four drugs bind to the drug target CCR1, and we searched for information on these four drugs at https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cder/daf/#apphist. We found that wortmannin and diphenylpyraline have been approved by the American Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for clinical practice, while A-987306 and CYPROTERONE ACETATE have not been approved. A-987306 is A potent and selective H4 receptor antagonist used primarily in scientific research, which can be used to block pain responses against hyperalgesia [36]. CYPROTERONE ACETATE is a synthetic steroid compound used to treat severe acne and androgenization symptoms in women. However, whether A-987306 and CYPROTERONE ACETATE can be used to treat cancer needs further research. Wortmannin is an irreversible inhi bitor of phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3-K). Wortmannin has been shown to block the proliferation of tumor cells and induce apoptosis by blocking the P3 K signaling pathway, thus exerting anti-tumor effects [37]. In addition, wortmannin can also be used in combination with other drugs to improve the efficacy of anti-tumor therapy [38, 39]. Diphenylpyraline is an antihistamine used to treat allergic rhinitis, hay fever, and other allergic skin disorders [40]. However, the use of Diphenylpyraline in cancer treatment requires more in-depth research. In summary, these four drugs may be used for the treatment of esophageal cancer in future clinical practice. Additionally, no significant side effects, pleiotropy, or heterogeneity were detected in any of the four drugs according to the MR-Egger intercept test, Cochran’s Q heterogeneity test, MR-PRESSO global test, and AstraZeneca PheWAS Portal database. The PPI network construction was applied to detect the biological significance of these five drug targets. And we made a molecular docking between the drug target and the potential drug, which further verified the druggable possibility of the target genes.

According to BP category of GO enrichment analysis, significant pathways of five target genes were related to chemokines and calcium ions. Chemokines are a class of chemotactic cytokines that can mediate immune cell transport and target tumor cells, thereby indirectly or directly affecting tumor immunity and biological phenotype, as well as cancer progression, treatment and patient prognosis [41]. In addition, chemokines are highly expressed in esophageal carcinoma cells, which can promote the proliferation, invasion and metastasis of tumor cells [42]. A study by the School of Basic Medicine of Guangzhou Medical University found that KCTD4 destroyed its dimerization by binding with CLIC1, increased the intracellular Ca2+ level and promoted the metastasis of esophageal carcinoma [43]. The MF category of GO enrichment showed that the significant pathways were associated with cytokines and immune receptors. Immune receptors, such as programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) inhibitors, have shown good efficacy and safety in the treatment of esophageal carcinoma. According to our results, the genes we have screened have great medicinal potential and can be considered for further research in the future.

Strengths and limitations

There are some strengths in this study. First of all, it is can be a primary attempt that searching for potential druggable targets based on a comprehensive MR analysis with three eQTL datasets and one GWAS dataset as exposure and outcome respectively. Second, a rigid rule was introduced for targets identification that the targets must be significant in at least two eQTL datasets and validated by co-localization at least in one eQTL datasets for EC. Third, enrichment analysis and PPI network construction were applied to support possible biological functions and characteristics of identified targets in risk of EC. Finally, we provided four practical drugs with a possibility of clinical treatment of EC based on drug prediction and molecular docking.

Notedly, there are also a few limitations in our study. Firstly, it is lack of biological verification after providing five possible druggable targets and four potential drugs for EC treatment. Secondly, we only paid attention to the expression level of targets, but the time or other regulatory factors may play an important role between exposure and outcome on the basis of MR analysis. Thirdly, there seem to be unmeasured elements or pleiotropy biased the results because of the introduction of MR analysis. Fourthly, the validation of potential drugs based on molecular docking was relying on digital emulation of interaction between protein structures and ligands which needs to be verified by real data. Fifthly, the current study did not perform formal subtype-stratified GWAS due to limited sample size and incomplete clinical subtyping in our cohort, we will look for more appropriate databases and further Subtype-specific analyses of esophageal cancer. Finally, our current conclusions are drawn from the analysis results of the European population and are only applicable for promotion within the European population.

Conclusion

In the present study, we identified five drug targets (CCR1, CCR3, RXFP2, SCAP, SETD2) by MR and co-localization analysis and four drugs (A-987306, CYPROTERONE ACETATE, wortmannin and diphenylpyraline) by drug prediction and molecular docking analysis respectively, which provide new insights for treatment of esophageal carcinoma. However, it is indispensable to verify by mechanism researches or clinical trials.
