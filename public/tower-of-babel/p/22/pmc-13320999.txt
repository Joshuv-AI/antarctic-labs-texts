Disease-Site Specific Peer Review Implementation: From Chart Rounds to the Treatment Couch
Authors: Leslie Chang, Chad Caraway, Mario Duhon, Daniel Song, Russell Hales, Ana Kiess, Akila N. Viswanathan, Jean L. Wright
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe impact of disease site-specific peer review on radiation treatment planning recommendations is not well understood. We implemented a standardized peer review checklist to evaluate factors influencing recommendations and their implementation during disease site-specific peer review.Methods and MaterialsA 7-item checklist was completed prospectively during weekly or biweekly meetings in 4 disease site-specific peer review groups, including (1) radiation approach, (2) dose/fractionation, (3) prescription accuracy, (4) organ-at-risk contours, (5) target volumes, (6) plan quality evaluations, and (7) setup imaging. Review comments were divided into (A) education/discussion without change recommendation, (B) minor suggestion/optional modifications, or (C) major comment requiring plan modification. Rates of comment types and implementation of plan changes were tracked. Statistical analysis was conducted using the 2-proportion Z-test, Welch’s t-tests, and analysis of variance.ResultsA total of 1311 peer reviews in 914 unique patients were recorded over 6 months. The average review duration was 2 minutes 32 seconds, with significant differences across disease site groups (P < .01). There were 286 total comments: 259 (21.8%) were category A, 26 (2%) were category B, and 1 (<1%) was category C. The proportion of cases generating comments varied by disease site (10%-34%), with only a subset resulting in implemented plan changes (0%-25.8% of comments). Most implemented changes involved target volumes, organ-at-risk contours, and patient setup. Cases undergoing multiple reviews were more likely to generate comments than cases with a single review (25.0% vs 13.0%, P < .01). Furthermore, cases with multiple reviews were significantly more likely to have implemented plan changes compared with single-review cases (28.9% vs 1.2%, P < .01).ConclusionsWe successfully implemented a peer review checklist in the context of disease site-specific peer review meetings. There is significant variability in comments and their implementation across checklist items and disease site groups. Iterative review significantly increased the probability of treatment plan changes.

Introduction

Peer review or “chart rounds” is an important quality-control tool within radiation oncology. Guidance from the American Society for Radiation Oncology (ASTRO) Safety White Paper differentiates the role of quantitative quality assurance (QA) checks versus the role of peer review, which focuses more on qualitative and potentially subjective treatment planning decisions and parameters.1 Prior work has shown that careful review of radiation treatment delivery is important, as guideline-concordant care is highly correlated with improved outcomes,2,3 yet there is often a range of reasonable treatment approaches for a given patient’s case. Discussion of these approaches and assurance that a treatment plan falls within the range of those reasonable options is the primary benefit of peer review, with the inherent recognition that some comments or recommendations may not require action; some comments may be educational, stylistic, or “for consideration,” whereas others may be deemed mandatory for the safety of the treatment plan. In recognition of the benefits of the peer review process, several professional organizations mandate peer review as a requirement for accreditation, including the ASTRO, American College of Radiation Oncology, and American College of Radiology.4, 5, 6, 7 The seminal ASTRO Peer Review White Paper1 and Safety Is No Accident document7 clearly lay out the rationale and priorities of a peer review program in radiation oncology, without making stringent rules about how peer review is conducted.

How best to carry out peer review, therefore, has been debated, and implementation is variable across practices. Peer review is a time-intensive endeavor, requiring multidisciplinary input from members of a radiation oncology team, and is inherently challenging to schedule with other demands on clinical time. In addition, there is wide variation in both the reported rate of change recommendations and the rate of implementation of those recommendations, with reported ranges of 1.8% to 40%.8, 9, 10 One approach to streamlining the process has been the transition from review of all cases in 1 larger group to disease site-specific peer review groups, with a subset of physicians reviewing only those cases in the designated disease group(s). Disease site-specific peer review has been found to improve concordance of care with national guidelines or institutional recommendations.11 In addition, disease site specialists can improve accuracy of contoured structures, which can change the coverage of planning target volumes.9 The frequency and type of peer review comments and recommendations, as well as a detailed follow-up of how often recommendations actually lead to plan changes, are not well understood, particularly in the setting of disease site-specific reviews. For example, Walburn et al12 found that compliance decreased as recommendation magnitude increased and compliance differed among treating physicians. However, a separate analysis found that major deviations were more likely to be addressed when plans were reviewed earlier in the planning process.13

Our department made the transition from full team to disease-site peer review groups in 2018. In this analysis, we sought to quantify peer review comments and recommendations and analyze factors affecting the implementation of recommendations through a prospective peer review checklist that collects data on recommendations and subsequent plan changes in the context of the disease site-specific approach. We hypothesized that a peer review checklist would ensure that all aspects of peer review have been completed and provide data to assess which treatment planning decisions have the highest rate of plan review recommendations and plan changes.

Methods and Materials

During a 6-month period from January 2023 to June 2023, we implemented an institutionally developed web browser platform, which housed a peer review checklist program within disease site-specific peer review conferences in 1 multicenter radiation oncology department. The disease site groups included in this study were: (1) breast; (2) head and neck (HN), and central nervous system (CNS); (3) genitourinary (GU) and gynecologic (GYN); and (4) thoracic. These categorizations facilitated targeted analysis and comparison across different types of cancer treatments.

Using the ASTRO Safety White Paper,1 we identified 7 questions that comprised the checklist, addressing critical aspects of radiation therapy planning, including (1) the appropriateness of radiation approach based on the clinical scenario such as medical history, (2) radiation dose/fractionation, (3) prescription accuracy, (4) organs at risk (OAR) contours, (5) target volumes, (6) plan quality evaluations (dose goals vs achieved), and (7) patient setup imaging. Pretreatment review was encouraged for proton and short-course (≤5 fractions) or high-dose (≥5 Gy per fraction) treatments. Our approach to pretreatment review of selected cases has been previously described.14 Briefly, cases selected for pretreatment review underwent case discussion (items 1-2) and slice-by-slice contour review (items 4-5) with or without plan quality review (item 6), depending on plan readiness at the time of review. All pretreatment review cases were reviewed in the treatment planning system, with a second review including all remaining items completed after treatment setup imaging was available. We have defined this approach of reviewing available checklist items when ready and bringing the case back for subsequent review when remaining checklist items are ready for review as “iterative.” Iterative review during the planning process results in some cases undergoing multiple reviews, addressing items available at the time of review. In contrast, “routine” case review comprised a single review of all items after treatment setup and only in PDF form, with cases being brought back for iterative review only if modifications were implemented. A designated individual participating in all recorded peer review sessions completed the checklist, noted whether comments were made, and noted the outcome of each case review; duration of each case review was also recorded. We did not collect information regarding which checklist item a comment referred to. Peer review sessions were attended by a minimum of 2 physicians with disease-site expertise. Other members of the radiation therapy team, including physics, dosimetry, clinical/physics residents, and therapists, attended whenever possible. The breast and thoracic peer review groups met twice weekly and dedicated 2 hours cumulatively per week to formal peer review, whereas the GU/GYN and HN/CNS groups met once weekly and dedicated 90 minutes weekly to peer review meetings, with cases also discussed in other meetings not formally designated as peer review, such as tumor boards or other disease site conferences. For example, the CNS group completes offline contour and plan review with neurosurgeons participating in stereotactic radiosurgery cases, and the HN group holds a weekly planning conference, which is not formally designated as peer review. The recorded outcomes reflect only the outcomes of designated peer review meetings. Review outcome was categorized as either no comment or recommendation beyond basic case presentation and information-based questions, versus qualitative comments or suggestions made. The comments were then further divided into (A) education/discussion of alternatives without change recommendation, (B) minor suggestions with optional modifications, or (C) major comments requiring plan modification. Example of category A includes discussion of dose/fractionation for breast reirradiation; example of category B includes recommendation for an additional dose constraint of the brachial plexus when planning stereotactic body radiation therapy to the shoulder, and example of category C includes incorrect contouring of target volume for a prostate case. Minor documentation errors requiring correction were noted separately. Only documentation corrections or major comments were deemed mandatory, and minor suggestions could be considered by the treating physician and implemented at their discretion. A notation was made in the review outcome checklist item when plan changes were made in response to comments/suggestions. Data collection for this study was approved by the institutional review board.

We evaluated the frequency of comments and comment types, as well as implemented plan changes for each checklist item, and compared them by disease site group and timing of review. Continuous variables were analyzed using 1-way analysis of variance or 2-sided Welch’s t-tests, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using 2-proportion z-tests. All tests were 2-sided, and P values < .05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 1311 peer reviews involving 914 unique patients were recorded during the 6-month period. Across all reviews, there were 286 recorded comments (21.8% overall). Education/additional discussion without a direct recommendation (category A) occurred in 259 cases (19.8%), minor suggestions/recommendations for optional modifications (category B) occurred in 26 cases (2%), and a major recommendation requiring plan change (category C) was made in only 1 case (Table 1). Examples of category A comments included discussion of alternative dose/fractionation for central stereotactic radiation in thoracic cases, or discussion of a new systemic therapy to treat locally recurrent squamous cell carcinoma of the skin as an alternative to radiation. Examples of category B included the addition of a boost for a breast-conserving radiation treatment or consideration of expanding or shrinking margin width because ofassessment of the anatomy or setup considerations. Category C included a plan signed with an incorrect prescription. In addition to qualitative comments, 47 instances of minor documentation errors requiring correction were found during the peer review process. We compared the frequency and type of review comments and outcomes across the 4 disease site groups, as well as the average duration of each review, shown in Table 1. The percentage of cases generating any comments was variable across disease site groups, ranging from 10% to 41%. Changes were implemented most frequently in the breast (25.8%) and thoracic groups (14%). The average duration of case review in each session was 2 minutes 32 seconds, with the longest time spent per case in the thoracic group (3 minutes 44 seconds); differences in peer review duration were statistically significant (P < .01). Reviews that included comments were significantly longer than reviews with no comments (P < .01), but there was no significant difference in the duration of peer review with comments resulting in implemented change versus no changes (P > .05).

To investigate the effect of the iterative peer review process, we stratified the data between patients with a single “routine” review and those with iterative reviews, which includes both cases selected for pretreatment review and those brought back for a second review after recommended plan changes. In our data set, approximately half (53%) of the patients had iterative reviews, the vast majority of which were iteratively reviewed as a part of the pretreatment review algorithm (Table 3). In the breast group, 298 of 431 cases (69%) were reviewed more than once, most commonly reflecting the use of 5-fraction protocols, warranting pretreatment review. In the H&N/CNS group, 60 of 229 cases (26%) were reviewed iteratively in peer review (noting that many cases are reviewed in other settings, not documented as formal peer review, as described in methods). In the GU/GYN group, 112 of 317 cases (35%) were reviewed iteratively, and in thoracic, 226 of 334 cases (68%) were reviewed iteratively, reflecting the common use of stereotactic body radiation therapy. Iterative reviews had a significant positive effect on the frequency of discussion and plan changes; notably, a higher percentage of cases with multiple reviews generated comments, 25.0%, versus 13.0% of cases with a single peer review (P < .01). Discussion

A detailed recording of peer review meetings across 4 different disease site groups in a regionally integrated radiation oncology department reveals marked differences in peer review practice, case review outcomes, and implementation of changes following review. As radiation oncology practices expand across larger geographic areas, group review of all cases becomes increasingly cumbersome, and many groups have transitioned to a disease site-specific approach to peer review.15 Acknowledging that the more narrow focus of site-specific peer review may limit input from a broader group of participants, there are many potential benefits of this approach. Zaki et al15 have found that, compared with practice site peer review, disease site peer review resulted in increased frequency of requested changes; notably, they also found that this structure did not compromise education. Ali et al16 found that disease site-specific peer review fostered standardized and reliable peer review. Our findings provide additional support for the feasibility of using a checklist to support an iterative, disease-site approach and highlight the positive impact of early peer review, while also demonstrating marked differences across disease site groups and identifying potential limitations of disease site-specific approaches.

The differential types and rates of comments and implemented changes across disease sites may represent the distinct considerations inherent in different groups and treatment paradigms. The structure of disease-site meetings, including peer review, can vary. As noted above in methods, the breast and thoracic peer review groups meet more frequently for formal peer review sessions than the GU/GYN and HN/CNS groups. Conversely, significant portions of peer review elements may occur “offline” in other focused meetings in those sites. For example, HN plans are also discussed in a separate planning conference where cases are presented between the attending physicians for better concordance of care, which is not captured in our data set. Planning decisions are also made outside the formal peer review setting in discussion with nonradiation oncologists participating in stereotactic radiosurgery for many CNS cases. In addition, there are different numbers of disease-site experts in each group, and those with smaller groups may be more likely to have a higher initial alignment, minimizing recommended modifications. There is also variability in the duration of case reviews; thoracic cases, which had the longest duration and a larger percentage of iterative “prereview” cases because of a high volume of stereotactic cases, which may involve more complex decision-making or require more comprehensive reviews because of the anatomic and treatment decision complexities. There was also a significant increase in duration for reviews resulting in comments or discussion, underscoring the additional time required for thorough discussion of treatment plans. There was no significant difference in duration of reviews with completed versus not completed plan changes, suggesting that the increased discussion may be educational or impactful for future cases, rather than related to a significant clinical deviation in care. Although peer review is primarily used in the clinical workflow for patient safety, there can be significant educational benefits for team members,16 and educational discussion should be a core goal for the conference.

In this study, 21.8% of cases resulted in noted comments, and the vast majority of these comments were in categories A (discussion only) or B (minor/optional suggestions), with <1% considered major (category C). These findings are similar to those reported by Ali et al,16 in which about 25% of cases resulted in comments, with <3% requiring major changes. According to Zaki et al,15 disease site-specific peer review increased change recommendations from 1.4% to 5.2%, although the significance (major vs minor) was not reported. The implementation of change recommendations is not reported in either analysis,15,16 making our data set of disease site-specific peer review unique in showing how frequently comments are actually acted on: the implementation of changes following discussion or commentary in our analysis was relatively low, occurring in 16.8% of cases. This falls within the range reported in the literature, which spans from 1.8% to 40%.8, 9, 10 This is likely reflective of the culture at our institution, and we would expect these statistics to vary with other centers. In addition, all peer reviews were conducted virtually, and although video-based peer review can be effective, other centers have also commented on lower rates of major recommendations using a virtual platform as compared with face-to-face peer review.17 As an academic institution with a robust disease-site infrastructure across regional campuses and recognized leaders in many disease areas, most plans are acceptable at baseline, and institutional practice is generally well-aligned within groups. At the same time, our culture is to respect physician autonomy, recognizing that many factors may contribute to a treatment or planning choice, such as patient performance status, comorbidities, and preferences; thus, it is uncommon to mandate a change unless the plan is objectively unsafe or low quality. These findings could also be construed as a limitation or downside to disease site-specific review, from the perspective that increased alignment within teams limits more robust educational discussion.

The finding that changes were more commonly made in the setting of iterative review suggests that there are fewer barriers to implementing changes before the effort of planning, documenting, or performing QA is done, consistent with previous studies.12,13 Traditional approaches to peer review are often static, 1-step processes, but our data suggest that the iterative processes may be more effective in enhancing treatment plan quality, as this provides multiple opportunities for critical evaluation and refinement. The impact of iterative tools in quality improvement has been shown to improve quality in health care organizations through regular feedback mechanisms, particularly positive, or “asset-based” mechanisms.18 In addition, quantitative models of peer review have demonstrated better communication and interpretation from treating physicians.19,20 Future studies will be needed to assess the benefit of pretreatment peer review in all cases of radiation treatment21 versus a select group that may derive the greatest benefit.

The finding that comments and changes occurred in all 7 of the checklist items at low and variable rates suggests that all components of the review process are important and may benefit from review and discussion. Notably, treatment setup imaging exhibited the highest effectiveness in translating comments into completed changes, although the impact was greatest in the breast group, where 3D conformal planning is commonly used. Although setup is often not reviewed in institutional peer review practices,8 we have found that peer assessment of the clinical setup, particularly in the context of portal films for 3D treatment, can lead to changes in setup and direction to therapy teams,8 and we therefore continue to review setup imaging as part of peer review at this time. Notably, the prescription itself generated the lowest rate of comments, suggesting that initial prescriptions are generally well-aligned with established protocols, making significant alterations less likely unless new information prompts a reassessment. Once a dose/fractionation approach has been agreed on (a qualitative decision), prescription accuracy is a primarily quantitative aspect of peer review that can also be streamlined using machine learning algorithms that can quickly detect anomalous prescriptions. Several tools have been developed to use artificial intelligence or automated tools to assist the treatment team in detecting anomalies in prescription entry and could be implemented in the treatment planning workflow for efficiency and accuracy.22, 23, 24 These automated tools can also be used to assist in decreasing documentation errors, which were also found to affect 3.5% of plans within this implementation period.

There are several important limitations to our study. First, we recognize the unique structure of this regionally integrated academic department, which may limit applicability to other settings. Second, although the elements of peer review are standardized for all review groups, the meeting frequency and format vary across groups, and some aspects of peer review are addressed in supplemental meetings or offline. Thus, it is possible that some recommendations and plan changes are not captured in this data set, particularly in the HN/CNS group. At the same time, this reality can also be used to support the role of disease site peer review groups, allowing for the inherent differences in the structure of plan quality evaluation, including peer review, across teams. Further improvement in contour review can include adding other specialties to peer review, such as radiologists. A systematic review found that peer review with a radiologist resulted in greater changes in contour targets.3 Finally, we do not have data on the unique number of treatment plans reviewed, only the number of patients and the number of reviews. Although we did not capture variables regarding the treating radiation oncologists who participated in peer review, all of the treating radiation oncologists were highly experienced with >5 years of independent practice. In addition, previous studies have found that neither physician nor patient, age, sex as noted in the medical record, or race significantly affects compliance with recommendations.12

Based on the results of this study, we have further refined our peer review checklist by streamlining the number of questions on the checklist, combining items 2 and 3 (radiation dose/fractionation and prescription accuracy) and items 4 and 5 (OAR contours and target volumes), and having a dedicated QA manager whose role is to prepare charts for peer review, record recommendations, and track follow-up on plan changes. Pretreatment case discussion is encouraged at all stages of radiation treatment planning.

Conclusion

We successfully implemented a peer review checklist to quantify treatment planning recommendations in the context of disease site-specific peer review meetings and found significant variability in comments and implementation across checklist items and disease site groups. Using the checklist to support iterative review, with cases reviewed more than once, focusing on previously unreviewed items, significantly increased the probability of treatment plan changes. Peer review checklist implementation continues to be an integral quality improvement resource and one that can be modified and adopted by other practices for continual quality improvement. Our findings provide additional support for the consideration of disease site-specific peer review in regionally integrated radiation oncology practices.
