Peptide Epitopes of NC16A BP180 in the Diagnostics of Bullous Pemphigoid
Authors: Simon D. Lytton, Christine Wagger, Damian Meyersburg, Birgit Mussnig, Roland Lang, Roberto Maglie, Florian Anzengruber, Emiliano Antiga, Russell P. Hall, Johann W. Bauer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Bullous pemphigoid is a severe autoimmune blistering skin disorder causing significant morbidity and mortality among the elderly. Improved diagnostic strategies rely on the identification of autoantigen epitopes recognized by pathogenic autoantibodies. Continuous (linear) peptide epitopes, unlike conformational epitopes in recombinant proteins, offer advantages such as chemical stability, reproducibility, and lower production costs, making them attractive for diagnostic assay development. To identify relevant linear epitopes, a microarray of overlapping peptides spanning the NC16A domain of BP180 and the C-terminus of BP230 autoantigens was screened with sera of 13 patients with bullous pemphigoid and 2 control sera. A 25-mer peptide of NC16A, termed Pep7-NC16A, was evaluated by ELISA in 2 independent patient cohorts (n = 35 and 26 bullous pemphigoid cases, n = 48 and 53 controls). Pep7-NC16A ELISA showed sensitivity of 71% and 62% and specificity of 92% and 98%, with area under the curve values of 0.92 and 0.754 (P < .001). Reactivity strongly correlated with commercial BP180 NC16A ELISA (r = 0.72, P < .0001). In this study, we demonstrate the diagnostic potential of synthetic peptide in bullous pemphigoid. The identified epitope could also serve as a potential biomarker for BP180-associated conditions or future peptide-based immunotherapies.

Introduction

Bullous pemphigoid (BP) is characterized by a broad clinical presentation, primarily featuring large fluid-filled, tense, subepidermal blisters accompanied by intense pruritus (Bernard and Antonicelli, 2017; Schmidt and Zillikens, 2013a; Zillikens et al, 1997a and b). The disease affects predominantly the elderly, with an increasing global annual incidence of 8.2 per 1,000,000 people (Persson et al, 2022). The underlying dogma of BP autoimmune pathology includes an age-related breakdown in tolerance to 2 hemidesmosome autoantigens present at the dermal–epidermal basement membrane, the transmembrane BP180 (alias BPAG2, collagen XVII), and intraepidermal BP230 (BPAG1), with an associated imbalance of T regulatory cells (Akbarialiabad et al, 2025; Antiga et al, 2014; Genovese et al, 2019).

The confirmatory diagnosis of BP usually relies on detecting IgG autoantibodies directed against BP180 and BP230 in the patient’s skin. These autoantibodies have been shown to be directed against the NC16A domain of BP180 and the C-terminus of BP230 and can be detected in the serum of patients with BP using ELISA (Di Zenzo et al, 2004; Mariotti et al, 2004). Anti-BP180 IgG1 and IgG3 autoantibodies activate complement-dependent cell membrane lysis and facilitate the neutrophil- and eosinophil-mediated destruction of the basement membrane. Although 85% of patient’s sera recognize the immunodominant region NC16A, circulating autoantibodies may also target several other antigenic regions across the BP180 molecule (Akbarialiabad et al, 2025; Matsumura et al, 1996; Tegtmeyer et al, 2024; Zillikens et al, 1997b). The compelling evidence supporting immunodominant epitopes within the BP180 transmembrane NC16A, driving BP autoimmune pathology, includes the strong and positive correlation between anti-NC16A IgG titers and BP disease activity and between anti-BP180 IgG levels and the functional decline in patients with PB (Chou et al, 2024; Patsatsi et al, 2012).

The recent evidence of elevated levels of soluble BP180 ectodomain, resulting from proteolytic cleavage and shedding in patients with cancer (potentially serving as a noninvasive biomarker), prompted questions about whether peptide fragments of, for example, BP180 are similarly released owing to inflammatory processes (Crespo-Bravo et al, 2023). The concept of proteolyzed noncollagenous domain epitopes was initially pursued by screening antibodies from patients with epidermolysis bullosa and bullous systemic lupus erythematosus for reactivity to the noncollagenous domain of type VII collagen using 8 mer peptides (Gammon et al, 1993). The findings highlight that cryptic epitopes within the intact protein become exposed in peptides (neoepitopes) and may potentially modulate the immune pathology in the extracellular matrix.

Linear peptides of BP180 induce anti-BP180 antibodies (Chiriac et al, 2013; Liu et al (1993, Wada et al, 2016). The passive transfer of mAb against a 23-amino-acid peptide of NC16A but not the passive transfer of mAb against a peptide of C-terminal BP180 causes blistering disease in animal models of BP. These results strongly support the role of NC16A peptide epitopes in the pathogenesis and pathophysiology of BP (Chiriac et al, 2013; Liu et al, 1993; Wada et al, 2016).

The use of linear peptides in ELISA screen for the detection of IgG autoantibodies against continuous peptide epitopes within the BP180 and BP230 may have value to replace recombinant NC16A proteins in the routine confirmation of BP diagnosis. BP180 peptide epitopes and/or anti-peptide antibodies are foreseen as biomarkers to assess the responses, recurrences, and long-term prognosis in BP. Their broader application in patients with diabetes and cancer with drug-related BP (de Nicolas-Ruanes et al, 2023; Huttelmaier et al, 2023; L'Orphelin et al, 2023) as well as for predicting the risk of neurological diseases (Messingham et al, 2019; Wang et al, 2021) and malignancies (Crespo-Bravo et al, 2023; Meng et al, 2021) may yield valuable insights into the involvement of extracellular matrix in selected age-associated diseases.

This study focuses on peptides derived from the NC16A domain because it contains immunodominant epitopes that are unequivocally the targets of pathogenic anti-BP180 autoantibodies. We identified a 25-amino-acid linear peptide of NC16A BP180 (Pep7-NC16A) and set out to determine the peptide ELISA’s sensitivity and specificity in the diagnosis of BP and its correlation with the performance of conventional BP180 NC16A recombinant protein ELISA.

To develop peptides and investigate their feasibility to replace recombinant proteins in confirmatory diagnosis of BP, a peptide microarray of 726 different peptides printed in duplicate (totaling 1452 spots) was developed from segments of BP180 (NCBI NP_000485.3, amino acids 490–562 and amino acids 1190–1497) and BP230 (UniProt Q03001, amino acids 2319–2649). Those sequences were converted into linear 15-amino-acid peptides with 14-amino-acid overlap. The 15mer is the optimized length for efficient high-density and duplicate printing of the peptide onto glass microarrays with fluorenyl methoxycarbonyl solid-phase peptide synthesis technology and PepPerPrint’s unique amino acid toner formulation of solid polymer microparticles. The 14-amino-acid overlap in the peptide microarray allowed fine resolution mapping of the NC16A epitopes and led directly to a stable biotinylated peptide-streptavidin ELISA design without recombinant proteins.

The IgG reactivities of 16 BP—6 of site 1 and 7 of site 2—and 2 control serum of patients with systemic sclerosis without blistering disease were screened by fluorescence (Figure 1a). The PepSlide Spotxel Microarray Image and Data Analysis Software (Sicasys Software) identified 10 peptides containing epitopes of interest on the basis of the criteria of common reactivity across different BP serum samples and the highest relative intensity of their fluorescence signals.

Seven of these 10 peptides, including a 25-mer NC16A peptide found to encompass 2 epitopes, designated peptide 7 (Pep7-NC16A), were prioritized for further diagnostic performance testing and were batch synthesized with a C-terminal PEG-biotin linker for conjugation to streptavidin microtiter plates. The 25mer Pep7-NC16A was constructed after screening to include the entirety of 2 overlapping immunodominant peptides, identified through the array, giving a 15-amino acid core plus 10 additional amino acid for complete epitope coverage.

In a second evaluation, using 26 BP samples and 53 dermatitis herpetiformis controls of site 3, the ROC analysis of the Pep7-NC16A ELISA showed 62% sensitivity and 98% specificity with an area under the curve of 0.754 (P < .001) (Figure 2c). The cutoff value OD450–620 nm > 0.19 and the upper limit of normal OD450–620 nm of 0.114 for the non-BP blistering disease controls were shown. The ROC analysis of the MBL BP180 ELISA gave 65% sensitivity and 92% specificity with an area under the curve of 0.787 (P < .001) at cutoff value 11.3 U/ml. The Pep7-NC16A ELISA positively correlated with the MBL BP180 NC16A ELISA by the Pasing–Bablok method comparison regression analysis with Spearman R-value of 0.727 (P < .0001) (Figure 2d). Discussion

This study demonstrates that peptides derived from the adhesion molecule of BP180, neither part of larger protein domains nor that of recombinant fusion proteins, comprise stable continuous epitopes suitable for diagnostic applications in BP. This is, to our knowledge, previously unreported.

Rapid growth has been observed in peptide-based diagnostic systems in the last few decades after a delineation of antigenic epitopes and the identification of autoantibody targets in the noncollagenous domain of type VII collagen in the autoimmune blistering disease epidermolysis bullosa acquisita (Gammon et al, 1993). Today, peptide-based diagnostic systems are widely used not only in the diagnosis of cancer, cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, and autoimmune disease but also in that of viral and bacterial infections owing to their high purity profile, chemical modifiability, and specificity (Pandey et al, 2021; Talucci and Maric, 2024).

The diagnostic performance of the Pep7-NC16A ELISA, assessed through 2 independent evaluations, yielded sensitivity of 71 and 62% and specificity of 92 and 98% with ROC analyses of high statistical significance. These results confirm the established immunodominance of the NC16A domain in anti-BP180 autoantibody formation (L'Orphelin et al, 2023; Mariotti et al, 2004; Pandey et al, 2021) and indicate that 1 or 2 epitope(s) within the 25mer peptide of NC16A are likely sufficient for accurate BP diagnosis.

The slightly lower sensitivity of Pep7-NC16A ELISA in the second evaluation than that of the recombinant protein ELISAs is attributed to sera BP22 and BP23, which tested negative in Pep7-NC16A ELISA but positive in both BP180 NC16A ELISAs: 137 U/ml and 38 U/ml (Euroimmun) and 197 U/ml and 12 U/ml (MBL). The 6% (3 of 53) positivity of dermatitis herpetiformis controls in Pep7-NC16A ELISA is consistent with the anti-NC16A BP180 antibody positivity rate of MBL BP180 NC16A ELISA (9%; 5 of 53) in this study and 6.5% (3 of 46) reported in a previous study (Nätynki et al, 2020).

The lot-to-lot reproducibility and stability of the Pep7-NC16A on streptavidin microtiter plates is underscored by the peptide being stored in solution at 4 °C for over 6 months prior to 2-hour room temperature coating onto streptavidin microtiter plates and the storage of coated plates 1 week to 4 months at 4–8 °C prior to performing the interday runs.

The ability of Pep7-NC16A to neutralize anti-BP180 autoantibodies in patients with BP is evidenced by a 10–50% inhibition of NC16A BP180 ELISA activity, reaching saturation at approximately 100 μg/ml of peptide. The residual reactivity in NC16A BP180 ELISA is likely attributed to polyclonal autoantibodies, which target epitopes outside the 25mer peptide, having questionable relevance in BP diagnosis. Although the 2 BP sera testing positive in MBL ELISA and negative in Pep7-NC16A ELISA were not assessed for their neutralization by Pep7-NC16A, these 2 BP cases presumably express autoantibodies, which bind the NC16A recombinant protein but not the epitopes of the Pep7-NC16A peptide.

In a previous report, synthetic peptides of the NC16A domain were conjugated with keyhole limpet hemocyanin for rabbit immunization, generating antibody responses against the peptide carrier (Nishie et al, 2015). Notably, rabbit polyclonal IgG fraction did not bind the complementary peptides in immunoblot nor ELISA, although it recognized the cleaved NC16A from cell extracts transfected with human BP180 cDNA. In another report, the ELISA of peptide RIRRSILPYGDSMDRIEK gave positive reactivity with serum of 2 dogs with BP and none from 5 normal dogs. However, human BP serum was not tested for reactivity against the NC16A peptides in this study (Xu et al, 2000). Investigations with BP patient sera have subsequently used glutathione S-transferase and hexa-histidine-tagged fusion proteins of BP180 domains for epitope mapping rather than synthetic peptides (Nätynki et al, 2020; Sitaru et al, 2007).

In another study, BP180 peptide fragments were expressed on the surface capsid of lambda bacteriophage and bio-panned with the sera of 10 patients with BP, identifying 15 epitopes (20–40 amino acids). Eight of these mapped to the BP180 intracellular collagenous domain, and 7 mapped to the BP180 extracellular domain, including NC16A. Sera reactivity was detected in crude dot blots, None of these peptides were validated on diagnostic test platforms; instead, a glutathione S-transferase fusion protein containing NC16A amino acids 490–562 was used for ELISA development (Di Zenzo et al, 2007).

Our research was motivated by the work of Nishi et al (Nishie et al, 2010; 2015), who demonstrated that the helical coil–coil motif within the NC16A domain plays a vital role in regulating BP180 ECT cleavage and shedding. On this basis, we presumed that short peptides may be naturally generated and preserve stable epitopes in a coiled conformation.

Two major limitations in our study need acknowledgement. First, the diagnostic performances of the ELISA were only evaluated using serum collections of site 3. Testing on an increased sample size of BP serum is needed to confirm whether the apparent differences in sensitivity between the ELISA are of statistical significance. The current data indicate that the diagnostic performance of the Pep7-NC16A ELISA is equivalent but not superior to that of the recombinant protein ELISA. Second, Pep7-NC16A ELISA is subject to background reactivity, which can affect the cutoff value of OD between runs. This technical issue can be overcome in the future by inclusion of internal calibrators or run controls, to convert the OD to units of activity as reported by the MBL and Euroimmun ELISA.

According to our knowledge, the use of a synthetic BP180 NC16A peptide in the diagnosis of BP has not been previously described. Although it does not necessarily close the seronegative gap in the diagnosis of BP, the Pep7-NC16A peptide ELISA demonstrated performance comparable with that of the recombinant BP180 NC16A protein ELISA. The Pep7-NC16A, along with other peptide epitopes of C-terminal BP180 and BP230 (to be determined), offers a cost-effective alternative to commercial ELISAs containing recombinant protein. The potential of these peptides for monitoring antipeptide reactivities associated with autoimmune neurological disorders among the elderly (Messingham et al, 2019; Wang et al, 2021) and in patients on antidiabetic dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors or checkpoint inhibitors warrants further investigation (de Nicolas-Ruanes et al, 2023; Huttelmaier et al, 2023; L'Orphelin et al, 2023; Messingham et al, 2019). Interestingly, a study on seronegative patients with Alzheimer’s disease by salt-split skin indirect immunofluorescence revealed 48% (23 of 49) positivity in the range >9 U/ml to 30 U/ml by MBL BP180-NC16A ELISA compared with 8% (4 of 50) positivity of geriatric controls. Immunoblot reactivity was detected in the Alzheimer’s disease serum and not in control serum against an extract of human brain area tissue having high BP180 expression and well-known predilection for disease-specific lesions (Messingham et al, 2019). These findings underscore the neurological relevance of NC16A autoantibodies and the need for more data on NC16A peptide-based testing such as 25mer NC16A pep7.

Synthetic peptide-based diagnostic platforms offer a significant advantage over recombinant protein-based tests, when the autoantibodies directly tackle linear epitopes not dependent on the conformation of folded protein structure or post-translationally modified epitopes (Carlton et al, 2023; Lytton et al, 2013; Sitaru et al, 2007; Talucci and Maric, 2024).

By avoiding the expenses of protein expression and purification and owing to the stability of biotinylated Pep7-NC16A on streptavidin microtiter plates, peptide-based assays represent a promising and potentially cost-effective alternative to commercial ELISAs containing recombinant proteins. In addition, the versatility and adaptability of peptide autoantigen epitopes make them suitable for coating onto latex beads or gold nanoparticles, enabling their use in lateral flow immunochromatography for rapid point-of-care testing. These properties of peptides have also proven favorable in the stabilization of the hemidesmosome (Walko et al, 2015) and the development of therapeutic peptides (Wang et al, 2022), including targeted B-cell depletion therapy by autoantigen peptide-engineered chimeric antigen receptor T cells (Ellebrecht et al, 2016) or T-cell engagers, which create an immunological synapse between autoreactive B cells bearing peptide autoantigen-specific surface Ig+ and CD3+ T cells (Perico et al, 2024).

Materials and Methods

Serum samples

Serum samples were prospectively collected from patients with BP and control geriatric individuals undergoing antinuclear antibody immune fluorescence-negative testing at the University Clinic for Dermatology and Allergology (Salzburg, Austria) (site 1) as well as from patients with BP at the Department of Dermatology, University of Florence (Florence, Italy) (site 2). In addition, sera from patients with dermatitis herpetiformis were retrospectively obtained from the biobank at the Department of Dermatology, Duke University, North Carolina, with diagnosis based on clinical, histopathological, and immunological findings (site 3). All of the abovementioned collections had institutional review board approvals. Written, informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Immunoassays

The circulating antibasement membrane zone IgG was confirmed by indirect immunofluorescence either on the epidermal side by 1 M sodium chloride-split human skin or Novalite monkey esophagus screen assay (catalog number 708330, Inova). Autoantibodies BP180 and BP230 were measured by ELISA (catalog number RG-M7612 and catalog number RG-M7613, MBL Life Science) with binding activity ≥10 U/ml determined as positive.

Peptide microarray

PEPperCHIP Custom Peptide Microarray 003412_01 - 003412_04 was prepared in 5 array copies for one-by-one assays (PepPerPrint, Heidelberg, Germany). Segments of BP180 (NCBI NP_000485.3, amino acids 490–562 and amino acids 1190–1497) and BP230 (UniProt Q03001, amino acids 2319–2649) were linked and elongated with neutral GSGSGSG linkers at the C- and N-termini to avoid truncated peptides. Those antigen sequences were converted into linear 15 amino acid peptides with a peptide–peptide overlap of 14 amino acids. The resulting BP180 and BP230 peptide microarrays contained 726 different peptides printed in duplicate (1452 spots) and were framed by additional HA (YPYDVPDYAG, 46 spots) and polio (KEVPALTAVETGAT, 44 spots) control peptides. Printed peptides were blocked with Rockland MB-070 buffer for 30 minutes and then incubated with 13 BP and 2 control autoimmune disease sera (1:500 in PBS, pH 7.4, 0.05% Tween 20, 10% blocking buffer) for 16 hours at 4 °C with orbital shaking (140 r.p.m.).

Peptides were washed 3 times for 10 seconds with PBS (pH 7.4, 0.05% Tween 20). Goat antihuman IgG (Fc) DyLight680 (0.1 μg/ml) staining for 45 minutes at room temperature in incubation buffer or the control mAb HA-CA5 Dylight800 was followed by thrice repeated washings.

Fluorescence was detected using an Innopsys InnoScan 710-IR Microarray Scanner (20 μm resolution; 680 nm laser, gain 50; 800 nm laser, gain 10). Signal intensities were quantified, and epitope sequences were identified using a PepSlide@Analyzer (Sicasys Software) (patent application EP AG 4276 filing in progress, priority date September 12, 2024).

Peptide synthesis

Peptides were synthesized using a standard solid-phase fluorenyl methoxycarbonyl protecting group chemistry with a C-terminal Polyethylene glycol-biotin linker. Quantities of 1 mg/ml or 10 mg/ml peptide were obtained after high-performance liquid chromatography purification, achieving >90% purity as confirmed by matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization-mass spectrometric analysis. Pep7-NC16A of BP180 was synthesized in 2 batches: batch 1 (PepPerPrint) used in the evaluations of samples collected from all 3 sites and batch 2 (Intavis peptide services, Tübingen, Germany) for precision testing compared with batch 1.

Peptide ELISA

Biotinylated peptides (diluted to 2 μg/ml in coating buffer, Rockland MB070S, Lot 38323) were added (250 μl/well) to streptavidin-coated, 96-well plates (Greiner bio-one, catalog number 655990, or Biomat, catalog number MT0STF4-SA5/200) and stored at 4–8 °C inside sealed aluminum envelopes for 48 hours or up to 1 month prior to usage. Before use, plates were warmed to room temperature, washed thrice with BPS-Tween 20 (Medizym Wash Buffer 1:10), and incubated with 100 μl human serum (1:600 dilution in Medizym Dilution Buffer, 3505 lot 147665, Medipan) for 1 hour. After 5 washes, 100 μl antihuman IgG-horseradish peroxidase conjugate (catalog number 609-4303) diluted 1:20,000 in wash buffer were added to each well for 1 hour. After 5 repeated washings, 100 μl of tetramethylbenzidine peroxidase substrate (Biomat, 500-1-100) were added for up to 10 minutes, stopped with 100 μl of 0.5 M sulfuric acid solution, and read at OD450 nm to OD620 nm using a Multiskan 96-well reader (lot 357-706872, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Life Technologies, Darmstadt, Germany). Values of Skax output files (Skanlt Software 6.1.1. RE) were transferred to Excel sheets, and the OD450–620 nm of each sample was corrected for background by subtraction of the mean OD450–620 nm of triplicate blank wells. For Pep7-NC16A imprecision testing, 2 peptide batches (2 μg/ml in coating buffer) were coated onto streptavidin plates, incubated for 2 hours at room temperature, washed 5 times, and assayed with serum samples in singlet using the described ELISA protocol.

Statistical analysis

Patient characteristics were compared between BP and control groups using the Mann–Whitney test or chi-square analysis, and mean OD450–620 nm values were compared with an independent t-test (P ≤ .05 considered statistically significant). Spearman’s correlation coefficients r > 0.4 or r < −0.4 with P < .05 indicated strong associations. Data analysis, including box-whisker plots, ROC analyses, and Passing–Babok method comparisons, was performed using MedCalc, version 14 (MedCalc Software, Mariakerke, Belgium). Binding curves were evaluated using GraphPad Prism, version 10.

Pep7 NC16A ELISA imprecision was expressed as CV%. Intraday CV% was the average ratio of the SD to the mean OD450–620 nm for n samples across 2 runs of 2 batches of Pep7-NC16A run on separate microtiter plates on the same day. Interday CV% was the average ratio of SD to mean OD450–620 nm for batch 2 Pep7-NC16A tested on 5 days over a period of 2 hours and 1 week to 4 months after coating on to separate microtiter plates.

Neutralization of anti-BP180 autoantibody activity by peptide NC16A

The ability of Pep7-NC16A to neutralize the autoantibody binding in BP patient serum was assessed by preincubating BP sera without Pep7-NC16A or with Pep7-NC16A at concentrations ranging from 3 μg/ml to 100 μg/ml prior to adding them to the BP180 NC16A ELISA. Pep7-NC16A concentrations between >10 μg and 100 μg/ml resulted in a 10–50% decrease in autoantibody binding activity (U/ml), depending on the individual patients with BP (Figure 3).
