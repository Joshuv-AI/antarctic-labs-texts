Possible Vestibulocerebellar Control of Posterior Canal Afferents: Implications From Central Positional Vertigo and Nystagmus
Authors: Hyunji Kim, Eun-Hyeok Choi, Jeong-Yoon Choi, Ji-Soo Kim
Published: 2026

The flocculus modulates the vestibulo-ocular reflex (VOR), primarily via signals from the anterior and horizontal canals.1 This has been demonstrated in an experiment where electrical stimulation of floccular Purkinje cells suppressed excitatory VOR activity from the ipsilateral anterior and horizontal canals, while minimally affecting posterior canal activity.2 These findings underpin the prevailing view that posterior canal activity is modulated indirectly via the effects of Purkinje cells on the paired anterior canal pathway.12 Here we investigated whether the flocculus can directly modulate the posterior canal signals in a clinical case of paroxysmal central positional vertigo and nystagmus (CPVN).

A 53-year-old male presented with positional vertigo after the resection of right-sided cerebellar hemangioblastomas. Preoperative brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed two solid cystic masses involving the right cerebellar hemisphere and the midline vermis (Fig. 1A and B). MRI performed after tumor resection demonstrated tissue loss in the right flocculus, biventer and inferior semilunar lobules, and the right inferior vermis (Fig. 1C and D). Various examinations revealed 1) spontaneous downbeat nystagmus with a clockwise torsional component, 2) direction-changing gaze-evoked nystagmus, 3) bidirectional hypermetric horizontal saccades, and 4) bilaterally impaired horizontal pursuit that was more pronounced on the right. These observations are compatible with oculomotor dysfunction in floccular lesions. Video head-impulse tests produced normal findings. The right and left Dix-Hallpike maneuvers provoked paroxysmal downbeat nystagmus with counterclockwise and clockwise torsional components, respectively. Supine head-hanging (SHH) elicited counterclockwise torsional and downbeat nystagmus (Fig. 1E). These findings led to the patient being diagnosed with paroxysmal CPVN.

Paroxysmal CPVN has been explained as a postrotatory response triggered by head motion. Cerebellar lesions, particularly those involving the nodulus/uvula, exaggerate the postrotatory rebound response of secondary vestibular neurons to canal signals, accompanied by impaired central compensation—rotational feedback—that normally counteracts postrotatory cues to minimize errors in gravity estimation.345 While nodulus/uvula dysfunction plays a crucial role in the development of CPVN, the directionality of CPVN is determined by how semicircular canal afferents are modulated within the secondary vestibular neurons, which is influenced by the flocculus. The rotational axis of the triggered nystagmus typically aligns with the vector sum of the inhibited semicircular canal axes during a positional change.345 In this context, the downbeat and torsional nystagmus observed during the Dix-Hallpike and SHH maneuvers, which activate the posterior canals while inhibiting the pairwise anterior canals, can be explained by increased rebound excitation of the anterior canal pathway or increased rebound inhibition of the posterior canal pathway. If floccular Purkinje cells do not directly modulate the posterior canal pathway, nystagmus is more likely to reflect increased rebound excitation of the anterior canal pathway.

Counterclockwise torsional and downbeat nystagmus in the right-sided Dix-Hallpike maneuver likely reflects an inhibitory rebound from prior excitation of the right posterior canal and an excitatory rebound from prior inhibition of the left anterior canal cues. This finding does not necessarily indicate posterior canal modulation, since it could also result from increased left anterior canal activity induced by a midline vermal lesion. Likewise, clockwise torsional and downbeat nystagmus during the left Dix-Hallpike maneuver may be explained by the mirror mechanism involving the left posterior and right anterior canals; with again the nystagmus direction potentially attributable to rebound excitation of the right anterior canal associated with the right-sided cerebellar lesion. However, in the SHH position, counterclockwise torsional and downbeat nystagmus cannot be accounted for by enhanced anterior canal pathways. Both anterior canals are inhibited, and rebound hyperactivity can produce downbeat nystagmus; the torsional component should therefore be clockwise because of a lesion-induced predominance of right-over-left anterior canal overactivity, which manifested as spontaneous downbeat and clockwise nystagmus. Both posterior canal pathways are excited during positioning, and their subsequent inhibitory rebound can likewise generate downbeat nystagmus. This results in rebound inhibition of the right posterior canal predominating over the left to produce counterclockwise nystagmus. The vector of paroxysmal CPVN during SHH is closely aligned with the inverse direction of the preferential activation vector of the right posterior canal (Fig. 1F). This reasoning implies modulation of the posterior canal pathway by the floccular Purkinje cells.

The possibility of the flocculus modulating posterior canal signals can be inferred from 1) increased firing of floccular Purkinje cells during downward eye movements—which may be related to downward pursuit or VOR involving anterior and posterior canals—and 2) the presence of floccular Purkinje neurons receiving convergent anterior and posterior canal inputs, thereby suggesting that posterior canal signals can be represented in floccular output.67 Our reasoning supports this possibility because paroxysmal CPVN is provoked by the VOR.
