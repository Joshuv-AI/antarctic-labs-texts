The CBS/H2S axis regulates intestinal stem cell homeostasis and radiation-induced intestinal damage
Authors: Tianyu Wu, Zhiyuan Zheng, Jiong Ren, Zhifan Yang, Ning Zhang, Kenan Guo, Dong Chen, Meijiao Gao, Yinglian Chen, Xi Liang, Yong Wang, Wensheng Chen, Yongping Su, Jianming He, Fengchao Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe cycling intestinal stem cells (ISCs) exhibit radiosensitivity, and their death or impaired regenerative capacity following irradiation may result in intestinal barrier dysfunction. The cystathionine-β-synthase (CBS)/H2S axis plays a critical role in regulating cell proliferation, reactive oxygen species scavenging, and the DNA damage response. However, it remains unclear whether the CBS/H2S axis modulates ISC homeostasis and tissue radiosensitivity.MethodsIntestinal epithelium specific conditional CBS knockout mice were generated by crossing CBSfl/+ mice with Villin-CreERT2 mice. CAGGCre-ER™ mice were crossed with CBSfl/fl mice to achieve CBS knockout in multiple tissues and cell types. The Lgr5-Tdtaomato-Flag mice were generated by CRISPR/Cas9 system. The CBS inhibitor AOAA or the H2S donor GYY4137 was used to treat mice or intestinal crypt organoids. Hematoxylin and eosin, immunohistochemistry, immunofluorescence, Western blot, qRT-PCR, et al. were employed to investigate the role of the CBS/H2S axis in ISCs homeostasis and radiation-induced intestinal damage.ResultsLgr5 + ISCs and progenitor cells expressed higher levels of CBS than differentiated cells. The cecum and colon expressed significant higher CBS levels than the small intestine. Treatment with the H2S donor GYY4137 enhanced the proliferation of intestinal organoids in vitro, while inhibition of CBS by AOAA reduced this effect. Genetic knockout of CBS in the intestinal epithelium or global downregulation of CBS driven by CAGG-CreER™ in vivo did not affect ISC proliferation or differentiation under physiological conditions. Pharmacological regulation of the CBS/H2S axis in vitro failed to protect organoids from radiation-induced damage. Interestingly, administration of AOAA in vivo reduced radiation-induced atrophy of the intestinal mucosa. Furthermore, global downregulation of CBS significantly promoted ISC recovery after irradiation exposure. However, intestinal epithelium-specific CBS knockout did not confer radioprotective effects.ConclusionsOur findings suggest that the CBS/H2S axis contributes to the regulation of ISC homeostasis and represents a potential target for radiation protection, mediated through the intervention of non-epithelial cells.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-025-04704-9.Keywords: CBS, H2S, Intestinal stem cells, Radiation, Radiation enteritis

Introduction

Currently, radiotherapy is employed in at least two-thirds of cancer treatment regimens, either as a standalone modality or more commonly in combination with surgery, chemotherapy, and other interventions. It remains one of primary curative or essential treatment options for abdominal and pelvic cancers, including rectal cancer, cervical cancer, pancreatic adenocarcinoma, and their metastases [1, 2]. In intra-abdominal and pelvic malignancies, the healthy bowel is inevitably exposed to the radiation field, leading to radiation-induced enteritis [1–3]. Additionally, radiation enteritis can occur in individuals exposed to accidental or intentional radiation in nuclear accidents or acts of terrorism. Damage to the mucosal barrier plays a critical role in radiation enteritis, resulting in severe consequences such as nutrient absorption disorders, bleeding, and systemic inflammation [2, 3]. The integrity of the mucosal barrier is maintained by the rapid cell turnover of the intestinal epithelium, which is driven by intestinal stem cells (ISCs). Extensive research has been conducted to prevent or treat radiation-induced intestinal barrier dysfunction by targeting ISCs directly or indirectly [4–6]. Unfortunately, few effective treatments are currently available, and their efficacy remains unsatisfactory [2–4].

Elimination of excessive reactive oxygen species induced by radiation is fundamental to protect ISCs from injury [2, 4, 7, 8]. Free hydrogen sulfide (H2S) exhibits weak antioxidant properties under biological conditions and may mediate antioxidant activity by inducing other cellular antioxidant defense systems [9, 10]. Moreover, H2S may play a role in the DNA damage response by modulating ATR kinase activity [7, 11]. Cystathionine-β-synthase (CBS), a key enzyme responsible for H2S biosynthesis [9, 10], has been shown to directly regulate cell proliferation and exerts anti-inflammatory effects [9, 12]. Following irradiation exposure, surviving or dedifferentiated ISCs must initiate a rapid regeneration process within an inflammatory microenvironment [2, 13]. However, it remains unclear whether targeting the CBS/H2S axis is a viable strategy for maintaining ISC homeostasis and alleviating radiation enteritis. In this study, conditional CBS knockout mice, CBS inhibitors, and H2S donors were employed to systematically investigate this possibility.

Materials and methods

Reagents and antibodies

Reagents: Trition X-100 (Solarbio; Beijing China; T8200), aminooxyacetic acid (AOAA; MCE; USA; 2921-14-4), morpholin-4-ium 4-methoxyphenyl (morpholino) phosphinodithioate (GYY4137; Selleck; USA; 106740-09-4), Citrate Antigen Retrieval Solution (SolelyBio; Beijing China; ZLI-9065), Alcian blue kit (SolelyBio; Beijing China; G1562), DAPI (Beyotime; Shanghai China; C1006; 1:500).

Antibodies: Brdu (BD pharmingen; USA; 555627; 1:300), Olfm4 (Cell Signaling Technology; USA; 39141 S; 1:300), Ki67 (immunohistochemistry) (Cell Signaling Technology; USA; 12202 S; 1:300), Goat anti-Mouse IgG(H + L) (MULTISCIENCES(LIANKE); Hangzhou China; GAM007; 1: 300), Anti-rabbit IgG (Cell Signaling Technology; USA; 7074 S; 1:300), CBS (Santa Cruz Biotechnology; USA; sc-133154; 1:1000), CSE (Santa Cruz Biotechnology; USA; sc-374249; 1:1000), Tubulin (Proteintech; Wuhan China; HRP-66031; 1:5000), GAPDH (Proteintech; Wuhan China; HRP-60004; 1:5000), β-Actin (Beyotime; Shanghai China; AA128-1; 1:5000), Ki67 (immunofluorescence) (Thermo Scientific; USA; MA5-14520; 1:400), E-cadherin (Thermo Scientific; USA; 14-3249-82; 1:800), Alexa Fluor® 488 Goat Anti-Rabbit antibody (Beyotime; Shanghai China; 1:400), Alexa Fluor® 555 Goat Anti-mice antibody (Beyotime; Shanghai China; 1:400), Alexa Fluor® 647 Goat Anti-Rabbit (Beyotime; Shanghai China; 1:400).

Animals

C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Beijing Hua-Fu-Kang Biosciences (Beijing, China). Mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free animal facility in plastic cages in a temperature-controlled room (22 ± 1 °C) with a daylight cycle from 6 AM to 6 PM as previously described [14]. Mice had free access to a standard laboratory rodent chow diet and water. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Third Military Medical University and the work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.

Mouse lines

CBS-floxed mice were generated by Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc., (Shanghai, China). Briefly, Exon 3–7 of murine CBS was flanked with two loxp sequences, and a neomycin resistance cassette (Neo) flanked with two FRT sites was used for positive selection in mice embryonic stem cells (ES) clones. After expansion, positive ES cell clones were injected into C57BL/6J blastocysts to generate the chimeric offspring. The CBS floxed heterozygous mouse CBSfl/+ were generated by mating the chimeric mice with C57BL/6J mice. Floxed CBS mice CBSfl/+ and Villin-CreERT2 mice were crossed to generate CBSfl/fl;Villin-CreERT2. To delete CBS expression in the intestinal epithelium, CBSfl/fl;Villin-CreERT2 mice and control litters were intraperitoneally injected with tamoxifen at 50 mg/kg once per day for 5 consecutive days. Further more, Floxed CBS mice CBSfl/+ and CAGG-CreERT2 mice were crossed to generate CBSfl/fl;CAGG-CreERT2. CBS deletion in multiple tissues was induced by similar tamoxifen treatment. The Lgr5-Tdtaomato-Flag mice were generated by CRISPR/Cas9 system with the help of Beijing Biocytogen Company (Beijing, China).

Mice model and drug treatment

Eight to twelve week-old, male, 20–30 g mice were randomized into groups indicated in figure legends through a table of random numbers. For CBS/H2S axis regulation under homeostasis, GYY4137, AOAA and vehicle were given by intraperitoneal injection at different doses with the strategies illustrated in given figures. For TBI, mice were received a single dose of TBI with 10 Gy using a 60Co γ-ray source (Irradiation Center, Third Military Medical University), and the dose rate is approximately 90 cGy/min. For BrdU incorporation assay, 100 mg/kg body weight of BrdU (Sigma, St. Louis, MO) was administrated by intraperitoneal injection 3 h before the sacrifice. Then tissues were collected and processed as previously described [8, 15]. If body weight of a mouse decrease by more than 20% within 1 week during this period, euthanasia by CO2 is suggested. All experimental mice were sacrificed by CO2 or by cervical dislocation. Animal anaesthesia was not used in this study.

Intestinal organoid culture

Small intestinal crypts, cecum crypts and colonic crypts were isolated and cultured as previously described [4, 16]. Briefly, intestines were obtained from 8- to 10-week-old given strain mice and the fecal matter was flushed with ice-cold DPBS (Hyclone). After opening by cutting longitudinally and washing by ice-cold DPBS, the intestine was cut into 2–3 mm pieces and incubated in the dissociation buffer (DPBS with 30 mM EDTA). Small intestine (SI) tissues were incubated for 20 min on ice. Cecum tissues were incubated for 2 h and colonic tissues for 2.5 h. After replacing dissociation buffer with DPBS, pieces were incubated in a 50 ml tube for another 15 min on ice. To release SI villus-crypt unit from the basement membrane, the tube was shaken for about 30–60 s. The mixture was passed through a 70 mm cell strainer, and crypts were isolated and purified through centrifugation at 4 °C. To release cecum and colonic tissues, the tube was shaken for about 2–3 min, isolated crypts were enriched through centrifugation at 4 °C. Enriched intestinal crypts were mixed with Matrigel (Corning) and seeded in 96-well flat-bottom plates (40–60 crypts/100µl/well). Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 17 min. And the culture medium, IntestiCult™ Organoid Growth Medium (Mouse) (Stem cell Technologies, Cat: 06005) was added into wells. Growth factors (R-spondin 1, EGF and Noggin) were added every other day and the entire culture medium was replaced 4 days after seeding. Y-27,632 was then withdrawn and not included in subsequent culture maintenance.

Organoids treatment and imaging

For drug treatment in organoids, after 24 h culture, vehicle, GYY4137 or AOAA was added with indicated dosages, respectively. Organoids in 96 or 48 well plates were imaged and analyzed at different time points as previously described [16]. For irradiation, organoids in plates were received a single dose of irradiation with 6 Gy using a 60Co γ-ray source (Irradiation Center, Third Military Medical University) at the dose rate about 90 cGy/min.

Immunohistochemistry and Immunofluorescence

Intestines were fixed with 10% formalin/PBS and embeded in paraffin. Samples were cut into 6 µM and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) as previously described [14]. Immunohistochemistry was conducted according to the manual of the LAB-SA double-staining kit (Streptavidin-Biotin) (Beijing Zhongshan Jinqiao biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) as previously described [17].

Oganoids or dehydrated samples were embedded with OCT and were cut into 10 µM. Slides were incubated with antibodies. Antibody-antigen complexes were visualized by incubation with secondary antibodies and counterstained with DAPI (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) as previously described [4].

Cell sorting

Single cell pellets were resuspended in staining medium (Advanced DMEM/F12, 100 µg/mL penicillin streptomycin, 2 mmol/L Glutamax, 10 mmol/L HEPES, 10 mmol/L Y27632, 0.5 mmol/L N-acetylcysteine) at a concentration of 5 × 106/mL. Cells were stained with antibodies and prepared for cell sorting as previous descripted [18]. The following antibodies were used. CD24 (1:200, BD Pharmingen™ PE-Cy™7 Rat Anti-Mouse CD24 ,Cat: 560536) ; CD44(1:100, BD Pharmingen™ PerCP-Cy™5.5 Rat Anti-Mouse CD44, Cat༚560570) ; CD45 (1:1000) Biolegend™ Alexa Fluor® 647 anti-mouse CD45 ,Cat༚103124); FVD (1:2000): eBioscience™ Fixable Viability Dye eFluor™ 660, Cat༚65-0864-14). Cells were sorted using a 100 μm nozzle on the MA900 Multi-Application Cell Sorter (Sony). For western blot assay, 20,000 cells per group were collected.

Western blot analysis

Protein was resolved by SDS/PAGE and blotted on nitrocellulose membranes (Bio-Rad, Richmond, CA, USA) as previously described [14, 17]. Nitrocellulose membranes were incubated with specific primary antibodies overnight. After incubation with secondary antibodies, immunoreactive proteins were visualized by the enhanced chemiluminescnet substrate (Thermo Scientific, Pittsburgh, PA, USA).

Quantitative reverse transcription PCR analysis

Total RNA was isolated using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen Corporation, Carlsbad, CA, USA), and treated with DNase I (RNase-free, Promega). The RNA was used for reverse transcription to produce cDNA by kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) was performed using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) to measure the expression of a specific gene versus a control reference with the formula 2−△△CT. TBP was evaluated as the control gene [19].

Primers used were as follows:

Lgr5-F: CGTTGTCAGCCGTGGTCATAGC.

Lgr5-R: GCCCTGCGTTTGAGCACTGAA.

Olmf4-F: AAGTGACCTTGTGCCTGCCAC.

Olmf4-R: CACCACGCCACCATGACTACAG.

Krt20-F: TGAGCAGGAGATCGCCACCTAC.

Krt20-R: CGACCTTGCCGTCTACCACTTC.

Bioinformatic analysis

Dataset GSE92332 was obtained from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE92332). It included scRNA data of cell type and gene expression TPM matrix from the SI of seven to ten weeks old female or male wild-type C57BL/6J or Lgr5-EGFP-IRES-CreERT2 mice [20].

Based on the TPM expression matrix data of different types of intestinal cells and genes, Spearman correlation analysis by R 4.4.0 was used to obtain the expression correlation between the Cbs gene and all genes, and genes with a significance level less than 0.05 were selected as the set for next GO enrichment analysis. Relative genes were performed GO annotation analysis, including biological process (BP), molecular function (MF), and cellular component (CC). Functional annotations of these genes were performed and visualized for clustering analysis by R 4.4.0 and using “clusterProfiler v4.12.0” and “enrichplot v1.24.0” packages. KEGG database (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/) was exploited to classify the correlated genes with Cbs and analyze important pathways by using same method and tools. p value < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant enrichment. The expression of the Cbs gene in different intestinal cells was processed and plotted using the R 4.4.0 and “ggplot2 3.5.1” package.

Statistics

The data shown represent the mean ± standard error. Statistical differences between groups were analyzed by Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant [14].

Results

Lgr5 + ISCs along the whole intestinal tract express higher CBS than differentiated cells

H2S is produced by three major enzyme systems, CBS, cystathionine c-lyase (CSE) and 3-mercaptopyruvate sulfurtransferase (3-MST) in a cell type-dependent manner [7, 9, 10, 12]. Results from the GEO dataset (GSE133691) based on Lgr5-GFP high and Lgr5-GFP low cells sorted from 8 to 10 weeks of Lgr5-EGFP-IRES-creERT2 mice [20] revealed that all three genes were expressed in ISCs. Notably, Cbs exhibited significantly higher expression in Lgr5-high ISCs compared to progenitor cells, whereas the other two genes did not show such differences (Fig. 1A). Lgr5 + ISCs were enriched by sorting CD44+CD24low crypt cells and Western blot was employed. A significant higher level of CBS was observed in the Lgr5 + ISC-enriched population, whereas it was nearly undetectable in the differentiated population, as indicated by CD44+CD24neg and CD44−CD24neg phenotypes (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Fig. 1). To further investigate CBS expression along the intestinal tract, the intestinal epithelium was isolated from the duodenum to the distal colon, and the protein level of CBS was assessed. Western blot analysis confirmed the presence of CBS in SI crypts, where Lgr5 + ISCs are located and its low expression level in differentiated villous cells. Additionally, CBS levels were markedly higher in the cecum and colon compared to the SI (Fig. 1C, Supplementary Fig. 2). Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence analyses demonstrated a consistent expression pattern of CBS in both the SI and colon (Fig. 1D, E). The strongest staining intensity was observed in the lower base of colonic crypts, which overlapped with Lgr5-EGFP, indicating robust CBS expression in Lgr5 + ISCs (Fig. 1E). In contrast, a weaker signal was detected at the bottom of SI crypts, and notable CBS expression was also identified in the lamina propria and submucosa of the SI (Fig. 1D, E). Collectively, these findings suggest that CBS is expressed in Lgr5 + ISCs throughout the entire intestinal tract, albeit at varying levels, and that Lgr5 + ISCs exhibit a higher CBS protein level than differentiated crypt and villous cells.

High-dose AOAA/GYY4137 affects the homeostasis of ISCs under physiological conditions in vivo

To investigate the role of the CBS/H2S axis in the homeostasis of ISCs, mice were treated with either the CBS inhibitor AOAA or the H2S donor GYY4137 (Supplementary Fig. 3; Fig. 2). AOAA effectively inhibited CBS function across multiple tissues at dosages ranging from 5 mg/kg to 20 mg/kg [9, 10, 21]. Treatment with AOAA at a dose of 20 mg/kg did not significantly alter mouse body weight, intestine length, crypt depth, or the number of Ki67 + cells and Olfm4 + cells (an ISC marker) within the crypt (Supplementary Fig. 3B-C; Fig. 2). However, high-dose AOAA treatment (80 mg/kg) resulted in rapid weight loss and reduced SI length, colorectal length, crypt depth, as well as the number of Ki67 + and Olfm4 + cells within the crypt (Supplementary Fig. 3B-C; Fig. 2). Considering the multiple targets of AOAA and the complexity of niche signals, it remains possible that other essential signals required for ISC self-renewal were affected by high doses of AOAA.

As a long-acting H2S donor, GYY4137 at a dosage of 50 mg/kg has been reported to protect various tissues [9, 10, 22]. While 50 mg/kg GYY4137 exhibited minimal effects on mouse body weight, SI length, colorectal length, crypt depth, and the number of Ki67 + and Olfm4 + cells within the crypt (Supplementary Fig. 3B-C; Fig. 2), a higher dose of 200 mg/kg GYY4137 slightly increased crypt depth, the number of Ki67 + cells, and the number of Olfm4 + cells within the crypt (Supplementary Fig. 3B-C; Fig. 2). Collectively, these findings suggest that basal level of H2S may be requisite to maintain normal homeostasis of ISC pool proliferation in vivo.

Pharmacological regulation of CBS/H2S axis affects intestinal organoid growth

Considering the potential effects of complex niche microenvironments on the regulation of ISCs in vivo [6, 23], intestinal crypt organoids were utilized to investigate the role of the CBS/H2S axis in ISCs. Initially, CBS expression in SI organoids was assessed. Immunofluorescence staining revealed that high CBS expression was predominantly localized in the budding regions of organoids, where most Ki67 + proliferating cells were concentrated (Fig. 3A). Western blot analysis demonstrated that protein levels of CBS and CSE increased in organoids three days after crypt seeding. The addition of GYY4137 at a concentration of 1.5 mM significantly decreased both protein levels (Fig. 3B, Supplementary Fig. 4). Given that intestinal crypts are routinely exposed to high levels of exogenous H2S produced by microbes in vivo [9, 10], these findings suggest that the elevated protein levels of CBS/CSE in vitro may be regulated by an intrinsic mechanism as a compensatory response to the lack of exogenous H2S. To investigate the impact of CBS inhibition on ISCs, the expression levels of stemness and differentiation markers in SI organoids were assessed. Treatment with AOAA significantly reduced the expression levels of ISCs markers, Lgr5 and Olmf4, while promoting the expression of the differentiation marker Krt20 in a time-dependent manner (Fig. 3C). Subsequently, effects of varying doses of AOAA or GYY4137 on organoid growth were evaluated. A low dose of 1 mM AOAA inhibited the growth of SI organoids, while a high dose of 4 mM resulted in the death of most colonies (Fig. 3D-F). Long-term inhibition of the CBS/H2S axis using 0.5mM AOAA reduced the colony-forming efficiency of SI organoids (Supplementary Fig. 5). Conversely, low doses of 0.5-1 mM GYY4137 slightly promoted the growth of SI organoids, whereas a high dose of 4 mM had minimal impact on cell survival (Fig. 3D-F). Long-term treatment of 0.5mM GYY 4137 did not change the colony-forming efficiency of SI organoids (Supplementary Fig. 5). These data indicate that H2S is essential for intestinal epithelial crypt cells and that SI crypt cells may sense the concentration of exogenous H2S to adjust CBS/CSE expression as a compensatory response.

Effects of AOAA and GYY4137 on the growth of cecal and colonic organoids were similar as in SI organoids (Supplementary Fig. 6A). To directly examine effects of AOAA and GYY4137 on ISCs, cecal organoids derived from Lgr5-Tdtomato mice were utilized. Compared with the vehicle control group, the fluorescence intensity of Lgr5-Tdtomato was decreased in the 0.5 mM AOAA-treated group and increased in the 0.5 or 1.0 mM GYY4137-treated groups. However, high concentrations of GYY4137 also led to a reduction in the fluorescence intensity of Lgr5-Tdtomato (Supplementary Fig. 6B-C). Collectively, these findings suggest that the CBS/H2S axis plays a direct regulatory role in the stemness of ISCs.

Cbs knockout does not affect the homeostasis of ISCs in vivo but affects intestinal orgnoid growth in vitro

Postnatal mortality of CBS-/- mice was approximately 90% within the first five weeks, potentially attributable to liver failure [24]. To accurately assess the role of intestinal epithelial CBS in the maintenance of ISCs under physiological conditions, CBSfl/+ mice were crossed with Villin-CreERT mice to specifically knockout Cbs in the intestinal epithelium. High efficiency of CBS protein loss in the SI and colon epithelium was confirmed in tamoxifen-induced CBSfl/fl;villin-CreERT mice via immunohistochemical staining (Supplementary Fig. 7A). However, conditional knockout of CBS in the intestinal epithelium did not significantly affect villus length or crypt depth (Supplementary Fig. 7B). No significant difference was observed in the number of Olfm4 + cells or BrdU + cells in the crypt between CBSfl/fl; Villin-CreERT mice and CBSfl/fl controls (Supplementary Fig. 7C-D). These findings suggest that intestinal epithelial CBS does not play a dominant role in ISC homeostasis under physiological conditions in vivo.

Furthermore, CAGGCre-ER™ mice were crossed with CBSfl/fl mice to achieve CBS knockout in multiple tissues and cell types. High efficiencies of CBS protein loss in the SI and colon epithelium were confirmed in CBSfl/fl;CAGGCre-ER™ mice by Western blot (Supplementary Fig. 8A, Supplementary Fig. 9A.). Additionally, high efficiencies of Cbs knockout were observed in the kidney and spleen, while relatively lower efficiencies were noted in the liver and brain (Supplementary Fig. 8B, Supplementary Fig. 9B). Consistent with these results, global CBS knockout did not significantly alter villus length, crypt depth, the number of Olfm4 + cells, Ki67 + cells, or goblet cells (Supplementary Fig. 8C-G). Collectively, these data indicate that CBS does not play a dominant role in ISC homeostasis under physiological conditions in vivo.

To further exclude the influence of complex niche microenvironments in vivo and to accurately assess the role of the CBS/H2S axis in ISC homeostasis, crypts isolated from CBSfl/fl or CBSfl/fl;villin-CreERT mice were cultured as organoids. Compared to intestinal organoids from CBSfl/fl mice, the number of buds in intestinal organoids derived from CBSfl/fl;villin-CreERT mice was significantly reduced (Supplementary Fig. 10A-B), suggesting a decline in cell proliferation. Sustained growth of CBSfl/fl;villin-CreERT organoids implies the existence of alternative pathways what compensate for CBS deficiency. Additionally, the inhibitory effect of AOAA on organoid growth was observed in the CBSfl/fl;villin-CreERT group (Supplementary Fig. 10C-D), indicating that AOAA may also suppress potential compensatory mechanisms. Consistently, the proliferation-enhancing effect of GYY4137 on organoids was partially diminished (Supplementary Fig. 10C-D). Collectively, these findings indicate that CBS/H2S play a critical role in regulating ISC homeostasis, and other H2S sources or non-CBS/H2S-related signaling pathways may reduce the impact of Cbs knockout in vivo.

Low-dose AOAA reduces the radiation induced damage of intestinal mucosa in vivo

Because ISC homeostasis plays a critical role in radiation enteritis, the function of the CBS/H2S axis in radiation-induced intestinal injury was investigated. Mice were treated with either vehicle or AOAA both before and after exposure to 10 Gy whole-body γ-ray irradiation (Fig. 4A). Contrary to our expectations, AOAA treatment resulted in an increase in body weight of mice (Fig. 4B). A macroscopic examination of intestines revealed that AOAA administration reduced intestinal fluid accumulation compared to the vehicle group (Fig. 4C). Histological analysis using H&E staining and immunohistochemistry demonstrated epithelial damage, inflammatory cell infiltration, and regenerative colonies (Fig. 4D–F). Compared to the vehicle group, AOAA significantly enhanced crypt regeneration, increased crypt depth, raised the percentage of Olfm4 + crypts per field, and elevated the number of BrdU + cells in the SI and colon on day 4 post-irradiation. These findings suggest that inhibition of CBS promotes ISC regeneration and alleviates radiation enteritis in vivo.

The impact of AOAA on organoid regeneration following radiation exposure was investigated. In comparison to the vehicle control, higher doses of AOAA significantly reduced both organoid survival and regeneration. Conversely, low-dose AOAA treatment had no observable effect on the regeneration of irradiated intestinal organoids (Fig. 4G). These findings suggest that the in vivo alleviation of radiation enteritis by the CBS inhibitor is unlikely to be mediated through a direct effect on intestinal epithelial cells.

CBS knockout in intestinal epithelia does not affect the radiation induced damage of intestinal mucosa in vivo

To further investigate the role of the epithelium-derived CBS/H2S axis in radiation-induced intestinal injury, CBSfl/fl;villin-CreERT mice and CBSfl/fl mice were utilized as experimental models. Following 10 Gy TBI, no significant differences were observed in body weight loss induced by irradiation (Fig. 5A) or in the length of SIs (Fig. 5B) between CBSfl/fl;villin-CreERT mice and CBSfl/fl mice. H&E staining results demonstrated that the morphology of SIs or colons in CBSfl/fl;villin-CreERT mice was comparable to that in CBSfl/fl mice (Fig. 5C). Additionally, BrdU staining revealed no significant difference in proliferation of small intestinal epithelial cells or colonic epithelial cells between the two groups (Fig. 5D). Collectively, these findings suggest that CBS knockout in intestinal epithelia does not substantially affect the intestinal damage caused by irradiation.

CBS knockdown in multiple tissues reduces the radiation induced damage of intestinal mucosa in vivo

Considering the inhibitory effect of AOAA on CBS/H2S in multiple tissues and cells, CBSfl/fl;CAGGCre-ER™ mice were utilized in the irradiation model (Fig. 6A). The macroscopic examination of intestines following irradiation exposure revealed a reduced degree of intestinal swelling (Fig. 6B) and significantly longer SIs and colons in CBSfl/fl;CAGGCre-ER™ mice compared to control CBSfl/fl mice (Fig. 6C). H&E staining results demonstrated that the pre-irradiation knockout of CBS in multiple tissues markedly increased villus length and crypt depth in the jejunum, ileum, and colon on day 4 post-irradiation (Fig. 6D). Immunohistochemistry was performed, and the number of Olmf4 + cells per sight was quantified. Notably, there were more Olmf4 + cells in the crypts of CBSfl/fl;CAGGCre-ER™ mice than in control CBSfl/fl mice (Fig. 6E). Collectively, these findings consistently suggest that the downregulation of the non-epithelium-derived CBS/H2S axis promotes ISC regeneration and alleviates radiation enteritis in vivo.

Discussion

H2S, akin to carbon monoxide and nitric oxide, has been established as a gasotransmitter that exerts its effects on various systems [9, 10]. It exhibits a modest antioxidant role under biological conditions and may mediate antioxidant activity through the induction of cellular antioxidant defense mechanisms, such as enhancing cystine uptake, as well as increasing cellular glutathione (GSH) and cysteine concentrations [9, 10, 25]. On the other hand, H2S is toxic [9, 10]. Consequently, even within a single organ, conflicting effects of H2S have been reported in different studies. For instance, Luo et al. demonstrated that excessive H2S promoted inflammation in the brain, leading to brain damage, whereas Yang et al. reported that H2S attenuated inflammation in the brain and reduced damage following ischemia/reperfusion [26, 27].

The effect of H2S on the intestine remains ambiguous, and its role in intestinal epithelial cells requires further elucidation. Unlike other organs, intestinal epithelial cells are routinely exposed to high levels of exogenous H2S produced by microbes [9, 10]. In this study, intestinal organoids, which more closely mimic in vivo conditions compared to immortalized cells or cancer cell lines cultured in 2D, were utilized [4, 16, 28]. The data indicate that under physiological conditions, the expression levels of CBS and CSE, the two primary endogenous H2S-producing enzymes [9, 10], are elevated in intestinal crypt organoids without supplemental H2S. The addition of an H2S donor significantly decreases CBS and CSE expression levels (Fig. 3B). Combined with findings that H2S deprivation via CBS inhibition or CBS knockout inhibits growth or induces death of ISCs in vitro (Fig. 3D-F; Supplementary Fig. 6A; Supplementary Fig. 10), these results suggest that H2S is essential for intestinal crypt cells under physiological conditions.

In intestinal crypts, ISCs are positioned at the base of the crypt, fully differentiated cells are located at the apex of the crypt, and transit-amplifying cells reside in the intermediate region between fully differentiated cells and ISCs [5, 6, 13, 23]. The self-renewal of intestinal crypts is primarily maintained by Lgr5 + ISCs [4, 5, 13]. CBS exhibits a significantly positive correlation with Lgr5 + ISCs (Fig. 1, 3 A). Under physiological conditions, H2S donors or downregulation of the CBS/H2S axis via AOAA treatment or knockout affects the proliferation of crypt cells, the number of ISCs within the crypt and stemness of ISCs both in vivo and in vitro (Fig. 2 C-F; Supplementary Fig. 6; Supplementary Fig. 10). These findings suggest that the CBS/H2S axis promotes ISC proliferation under physiological conditions. However, the role of the CBS/H2S axis in ISC homeostasis is complex. First, low concentrations of H2S stimulate ISC proliferation, while higher concentrations inhibit it (Fig. 3D-F; Supplementary Fig. 6A), which aligns with observations made by other researchers [9, 10]. Second, in vivo, H2S produced by CBS in ISCs is unlikely to be the predominant source of H2S because neither intestinal epithelial CBS knockout nor global CBS knockout affects ISC self-renewal or crypt cell proliferation under physiological conditions in vivo (Supplementary Fig. 7–8). As previously noted, intestinal epithelial cells are routinely exposed to high levels of exogenous H2S generated by microbes [9, 10]. Due to its high lipophilicity, H2S can rapidly diffuse into surrounding tissues, freely penetrate biological membranes, and readily enter cellular interiors [9, 10]. Consequently, ISCs in vivo may predominantly acquire H2S from external sources.

Since the damage and repair of ISCs play a critical role in radiation enteritis [2, 8, 13], and given that the level of activation of the CBS/H2S axis contributes to ISC maintenance under physiological conditions, we hypothesized that downregulation of the CBS/H2S axis would exacerbate radiation-induced intestinal injury. However, in vivo experiments contradicted our expectations. Specifically, global CBS downregulation reduced radiation-induced intestinal damage (Figs. 4A–F and 6). This effect does not appear to depend on a direct influence on the radiosensitivity of intestinal epithelial cells, as in vitro data demonstrated that AOAA did not significantly decrease the radiosensitivity of ISCs (Fig. 4G). Although AOAA, a CBS inhibitor, also targets CSE and other pyridoxal phosphate (PLP)-dependent enzymes [9, 12], global CBS knockout promoted intestinal tissue regeneration (Fig. 6), suggesting that effects of AOAA in the irradiation model should be attributed to its inhibition of the CBS/H2S axis. Therefore, the conclusion that global downregulation of the CBS/H2S axis reduces radiation-induced intestinal damage while the CBS/H2S axis promotes ISC proliferation under physiological conditions is reliable. One plausible explanation for the observed difference is the alteration in ISC niche microenvironments. It is widely acknowledged that ISC niche microenvironments play a critical role in maintaining proliferation, differentiation, self-renewal, and homeostasis [6, 23]. Following radiation exposure, these microenvironments differ significantly from those under physiological conditions. For instance, a substantial chemotaxis of immune cells around ISCs occurs, thereby integrating them as components of the ISC niche microenvironment [2, 6, 13, 23]. Various immune cell types, including macrophages, neutrophils, innate lymphoid cells (ILCs), and regulatory T (Treg) cells, have been reported to regulate ISC proliferation and differentiation [6, 13, 23, 29]. Given the established role of CBS/H2S as an endogenous regulator of the immune system (Fig. 7) [10, 12, 23, 30], it is conceivable that immune cells contribute to the mechanism by which downregulation of the CBS/H2S axis promotes ISC regeneration and reduces radiation-induced intestinal damage. This hypothesis is supported by data demonstrating that conditional knockout of CBS in intestinal epithelial cells does not alter radiation-induced intestinal damage in vivo (Fig. 5). Futher single-cell omics and multi-omics technologies may help identify the types of immune cells affected by the CBS/H2S pathway and the related functions.

CBS is significantly upregulated in various types of cancer, including colorectal, ovarian, lung, breast, and kidney cancers [9, 30, 31]. The CBS/H2S axis has been reported to promote carcinogenesis, facilitate tumor progression, enhance cancer cell proliferation, increase resistance or induce multidrug resistance to chemotherapeutic agents, and drive metastatic dissemination [9, 30, 31]. These findings, combined with our results demonstrating that downregulation of the CBS/H2S axis reduces radiation-induced intestinal damage, suggest that the CBS/H2S axis may serve as a potential therapeutic target for both cancer treatment and protection of the intestine from radiation-induced injury. A low dose of the CBS inhibitor AOAA (no more than 20 mg/kg), intestinal epithelial CBS knockout, and global CBS knockout did not affect ISC self-renewal or crypt cell proliferation under physiological conditions in vivo (Figs. 2 and 3, Supplementary Fig. 7–8). However, a low dose of AOAA (20 mg/kg) and global CBS knockout significantly reduced radiation-induced intestinal damage (Fig. 4). These results indicate that targeting CBS to protect the intestine from radiation-induced damage may be both effective and safe.

Conclusions

In summary, our identification of the CBS/H2S axis as an endogenous regulator of ISC stemness and homeostasis contributes significantly to the organoid culture system, thereby enhancing our understanding of ISC biology. The well-defined and specific role of the CBS/H2S axis in radiation enteritis in vivo renders it a promising and safe target for radiation protection strategies. However, further investigation is necessary to identify the direct target niche cells and crucial pathways that contribute to the radioprotective effect regulated by the CBS/H2S axis in vivo. This will likely bring significant benefits to cancer radiotherapy in the future.
