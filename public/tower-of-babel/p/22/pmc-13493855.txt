Stable high-valent iridium single atoms for high-temperature CO2 electrolysis
Authors: Shaowei Zhang, Shuo Wang, Hewei Liu, Geng Zou, Yige Guo, Wenwen Zhang, Lina Yu, Haolin Liu, Xiaomin Zhang, Mingrun Li, Peng Zhang, Runsheng Yu, Xusheng Zheng, Yuefeng Song, Guoxiong Wang, Xinhe Bao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSingle-atom catalysts (SACs) offer high atomic efficiency and catalytic activity but are prone to aggregation and degradation under high-temperature conditions. Here, we propose a thermally and electrochemically stable high-valent iridium single atom synthesis strategy based on strong metal-support interactions (SMSI) to enhance high-temperature CO2 electrolysis performance in solid oxide electrolysis cells (SOECs). The SMSI effect, in situ induced during high-temperature cell fabrication and operation, stabilizes the high-valent iridium single atom and simultaneously modulates the surface electronic structure of the La0.6Sr0.4FeO3−δ (LSF) cathode by weakening the Fe−O hybridization, finally promoting oxygen vacancy formation and enhancing CO2 adsorption and activation. This approach boosts the CO2-to-CO electrolysis current density by 80.8% relative to the pristine LSF cathode, achieving 3.02 A cm−2 at 800°C and 1.5 V with nearly 100% Faradaic efficiency and excellent stability over 600 h. These findings provide a viable strategy for designing thermally and electrochemically robust SACs for high-temperature catalytic reactions.Subject terms: Electrocatalysis, Fuel cells, Electrocatalysis

Introduction

Single-atom catalysts (SACs), characterized by maximal utilization of metal atoms, well-defined coordination environments, and highly uniform catalytic sites, have emerged as a frontier in energy-related catalysis1,2. In contrast to conventional nanosized catalysts, SACs afford atomic-level control over catalytic processes and enable substantial improvements in both activity and selectivity through rational modulation of electronic structures3. Despite the extensive investigation of SACs in low-temperature (<150 °C) and medium-temperature (150–400 °C) reactions, such as the oxygen reduction reaction4–6, oxygen evolution reaction7,8, hydrogen evolution reaction9, carbon monoxide oxidation10, C–O hydrogenation11, and C–H dehydrogenation12, their application under high-temperature conditions remains significantly constrained, particularly in solid oxide electrolysis cells (SOECs), which typically operate at temperatures exceeding 600 °C13. SOECs feature the efficient utilization of electricity (from renewable sources, such as solar and wind power) and thermal energy (from industrial waste heat and nuclear reactions) to convert CO2 into valuable CO at the cathode, while generating pure O2 at the anode, thereby providing a sustainable integrated platform for carbon neutrality and energy circularity13–15. Nevertheless, the cathodic CO2 reduction reaction (CO2RR) exhibits slow kinetics, primarily due to the limited adsorption and activation of CO2 on the cathode surface, which significantly restricts the overall performance of SOECs15,16. SAC-based strategies offer a promising approach to enhance CO2 adsorption and activation sites on the cathode2. However, their application in high-temperature CO2 electrolysis remains challenging, as the cell fabrication process involves multiple high-temperature annealing steps (typically above 1000 °C)17, and the SOEC cathode generally operates at cathodic reduction potentials15. Under such harsh thermal and cathodic polarization environments, isolated metal atoms are prone to migrating, aggregating, or undergoing dynamic valence state transitions, ultimately leading to structural degradation and catalytic deactivation1,2. These issues severely constrain the practical deployment and long-term stability of SACs in high-temperature CO2 electrolysis. Consequently, the precise design of thermally robust and catalytically efficient SACs is critical for overcoming this bottleneck and ensuring their reliable operation under harsh electrochemical conditions.

To harness the reactivity of single-atom sites and mitigate their structural instability, engineering strong metal-support interactions (SMSI) has emerged as a key strategy in SAC design18. This approach enhances the electronic coupling between isolated metal atoms and reducible supports, which not only anchors the active atoms effectively by preventing migration and aggregation under thermal or electrochemical conditions, but also tailors the electronic structure of the support to improve their catalytic activity19,20. For instance, single gold atoms (Au1) supported on FeOx exhibit stronger resistance to sintering than gold nanoparticles, attributed to the formation of robust Au–O–Fe covalent interactions with surface oxygen on the reducible FeOx support, which enhances the thermal stability of Au1 and results in improved activity and durability in CO oxidation19. More importantly, the role of SMSI extends beyond structural stabilization, as it can also modulate the catalytic reactivity of the support through synergistic effects20,21. In the Pt1-TiO2 system, SMSI leads to electron enrichment on the TiO2 surface and the generation of oxygen vacancies, which facilitate the adsorption and transformation of reaction intermediates, thereby enhancing catalytic performance in hydrogenation reactions20. This dual function of SMSI, simultaneously stabilizing single-atom structures at elevated temperatures and activating the support by altering the surface electronic structure, offers valuable guidance for the rational design of highly active and stable SAC systems for high-temperature electrocatalytic reactions in SOECs.

Herein, we propose a urea-assisted method to uniformly disperse Ir species onto the La0.6Sr0.4FeO3 − δ (LSF) perovskite cathode, induced by the SMSI effect during the high-temperature fabrication conditions of SOECs. Aberration-corrected high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM), X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS), and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) confirm that the SMSI effect stabilizes the dispersed Ir species as isolated single atoms on the cathode surface, with interfacial electron transfer from the Ir species to the LSF driving the oxidation state of Ir single atoms toward a nearly hexavalent configuration. Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR), positron annihilation lifetime spectroscopy (PALS), quasi-in situ XPS, quasi-in situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS), C18O2 isotope-labeled time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (ToF-SIMS), and in situ surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS), combined with density functional theory (DFT) calculations, reveal that these high-valent Ir single atoms exhibit structural stability and competitive activity for CO2 adsorption, while simultaneously activating the LSF perovskite by weakening Fe−O hybridization, resulting in promoted oxygen vacancy formation and increased CO2RR kinetics. This strategy provides a blueprint for designing thermally robust SACs with competitive activity and durability for high-temperature CO2 electrolysis.

Results

Atomic-scale dispersion of Ir species

The temperature-dependent synthesis pathway of atomically dispersed Ir species on the LSF support is illustrated in Fig. 1a. H2IrCl6 was initially loaded onto the LSF substrate via a urea-assisted method (Supplementary Fig. 1), subsequently decomposed into IrO2 at approximately 300 °C (denoted as IrO2-LSF), and ultimately transformed into isolated Ir single atoms anchored on the LSF surface (Ir1-LSF) as the temperature increased to 1000 °C (Fig. 1a). This thermally driven dispersion process is evidenced by the X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns in Fig. 1b, c, where the characteristic peaks of IrO2 (PDF#86-0330) at 28.0° (110) and 34.7° (101) are observed at 300 °C but disappear at 1000 °C, indicating the complete dispersion of Ir species on the LSF support upon heating. The evolution of Ir dispersion was further confirmed by aberration-corrected HAADF-STEM images combined with atomic-resolution EDS elemental maps. As shown in Fig. 1f, IrO2 nanoparticles aggregate on the LSF surface after treatment at 300 °C, consistent with the XRD results. Upon heating to 1000 °C, the IrO2 nanoparticles become uniformly dispersed on the LSF support (Fig. 1g and Supplementary Fig. 4). Furthermore, the brightness contrast between Ir atoms and the LSF support in aberration-corrected HAADF-STEM images further confirms that the Ir species are atomically dispersed, stably anchored, and exist as isolated single atoms without aggregation (Fig. 1h, i).

Since IrO2 particles exhibit negligible solubility in dilute nitric acid solutions, only atomically dispersed Ir species can be fully dissolved22. Accordingly, the dispersity of Ir species can be quantitatively characterized by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES), using dilute nitric acid as the dissolution medium. As evidenced in Fig. 1d, the content of atomically dispersed Ir species in Ir1-LSF (83.3 wt%) is significantly higher than that in IrO2-LSF (17.8 wt%). Furthermore, the H2-temperature-programmed reduction (H2-TPR) results in Fig. 1e show that the reduction temperature of Ir species increases from ~150 °C for IrO2-LSF to ~450 °C for Ir1-LSF, demonstrating a strong interaction between Ir single atoms and LSF support23,24. Even at elevated temperatures under a CO2 atmosphere, no diffraction peaks of IrO2 (PDF#86-0330) or metallic Ir (PDF#87-0715) are observed in the in situ high-temperature XRD patterns (Fig. 1j and Supplementary Fig. 5), indicating the thermal stability of the atomically dispersed Ir species. XRD refinement further reveals that the LSF support retains its rhombohedral perovskite structure (Supplementary Fig. 6 and Supplementary Table 1), with only a slight increase in cell volume (Supplementary Fig. 7). Taken together, these results demonstrate that high-temperature calcination effectively promotes the atomic dispersion of Ir species on the LSF surface, rendering it highly suitable for high-temperature CO2 electrolysis in SOECs.

Chemical states and coordination environments

The chemical states and coordination environments of dispersed Ir species on the LSF surface were systematically investigated using XPS, X-ray absorption near edge spectroscopy (XANES), and extended X-ray absorption fine structure spectroscopy (EXAFS). The XPS results in Fig. 2a show that the Ir 4f peak intensity in Ir1-LSF is significantly increased after calcination at 1000 °C, suggesting that Ir species are mainly dispersed on the surface and have not diffused into the inner bulk of LSF25,26. Meanwhile, the Ir 4f peak in Ir1-LSF shifts to higher binding energy compared with IrO2-LSF, indicating that the valence state of Ir increases after dispersion. The normalized Ir L3-edge XANES spectra in Fig. 2b demonstrate that the white-line region intensity (∼11,215–11,225 eV) exhibits pronounced enhancement, while the corresponding first-derivative profiles in Supplementary Fig. 8 reveal a positive shift in the excitation energy (E0) after Ir dispersion, indicating that the Ir valence in Ir1-LSF is higher than IrO2 (+4)27, consistent with the XPS results (Fig. 2a). Furthermore, XANES fitting results in Fig. 2c reveal a high Ir oxidation state of +5.57 in Ir1-LSF. In contrast, Fe 2p XPS spectra demonstrate a reduced Fe valence in Ir1-LSF (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Table 2)28. The reduction in Fe valence is further corroborated by Fe K-edge XANES spectra in Fig. 2g, where the excitation energy undergoes a negative shift following Ir dispersion. In addition, the scattering path of Fe–O decreases in the Fourier-transformed Fe K-edge EXAFS spectra (Fig. 2h). These results collectively demonstrate SMSI between the dispersed Ir species and the LSF support, enabling directional electron transfer from the Ir species to the LSF27. The coordination environment of Ir was resolved through Fourier-transformed and wavelet-transformed Ir L3-edge EXAFS spectra. As shown in Fig. 2d, e, the scattering path at 1.95 Å is ascribed to the Ir–Cl coordination in the Ir-LSF-RT sample29, originating from the use of H2IrCl6 as the precursor under room-temperature synthesis conditions. Following 1000 °C calcination, Ir dispersion results in the formation of an Ir–O scattering path at 1.62 Å and an Ir–O–Fe scattering path at 2.95 Å29–31, attributed to the SMSI effect and electron transfer between Ir and Fe, as demonstrated above.

Activation effects of high-valent Ir single atoms

Figure 3a displays the O K-edge XAS spectra of LSF and Ir1-LSF, where the adsorption peaks at ~528.9 eV and ~530.4 eV are attributed to the electron transition to the unoccupied O 2p-Fe 3d (eg) and O 2p-Fe 3d (t2g) hybridized states in the octahedral crystal field, respectively32,33. The attenuated pre-edge intensity at ~528.9 eV demonstrates that the dispersion of Ir weakens the Fe−O hybridization and reduces the oxidation state of Fe species33, consistent with the elevated Ir oxidation state in Ir1-LSF as mentioned above, indicating electron transfer from Ir species to the LSF support due to the SMSI effect. In addition, the O 1s XPS spectra in Fig. 3b reveal surface oxygen species in LSF and Ir1-LSF, including lattice oxygen (O2−, ~528.4 eV) and adsorbed oxygen species (O−, O22−, OH−, and CO32−, ~531.0 eV)34. Upon Ir dispersion, the proportion of adsorbed oxygen increases from 46.0 to 57.6% (Supplementary Table 3), and such favors enhanced surface reactivity toward CO2 activation34.

The attenuation of Fe–O hybridization is expected to facilitate the formation of oxygen vacancies, as evidenced by EPR measurements35. As shown in Fig. 3c, Ir1-LSF exhibits a higher peak intensity at g = 2.003 compared to LSF, indicating more oxygen vacancies in Ir1-LSF36,37. Furthermore, the vacancy-type defects in LSF and Ir1-LSF were further characterized using positronium annihilation lifetime spectroscopy (PALS) technique with the spectra shown in Fig. 3d. The PALS spectra can be resolved into three annihilation components (C1, C2, and C3), corresponding to distinct lifetime intervals (τ1, τ2, and τ3) and relative intensities (I1, I2, and I3) for both LSF and Ir1-LSF26,38, as summarized in Supplementary Table 4. The shortest component (τ1) is attributed to delocalized positron annihilation in defect-free crystal regions, associated with small isolated Fe3+ sites and oxygen vacancy-related environments38. The intermediate component (τ2) reflects positrons captured by larger defects, such as oxygen vacancy clusters, while the longest component (τ3) corresponds to orthopositronium annihilation in nanoscale voids38. The PALS fitting results in Supplementary Table 4 reveal that the intensity ratios of I1 and I2 dominate over I3, confirming oxygen vacancies as the predominant defect type in both LSF and Ir1-LSF26. Moreover, the annihilation lifetime τ1 increases from 102.7 ps for LSF to 120.0 ps for Ir1-LSF, while τ2 rises from 185.1 ps for LSF to 200.1 ps for Ir1-LSF, indicating the formation of additional isolated Fe3+ and oxygen vacancy associates due to the dispersion of Ir species26,38.

Oxygen vacancies and metal sites are generally considered as active sites for CO2 adsorption and activation15,16. Accordingly, Ir dispersion significantly enhances CO2 adsorption capacity as confirmed by CO2-TPD experiments in Fig. 3e. Specifically, the chemisorbed CO2 amount, as quantified in Fig. 3f, increases by 55.2% from 1.71 μmol g−1 for LSF to 2.69 μmol g−1 for Ir1-LSF. To further elucidate the CO2 adsorption properties at elevated temperatures, quasi-in situ spectroscopic analyses were conducted. As shown in Fig. 3g, the quasi-in situ XPS spectrum reveals a more prominent carbonate signal at ~289 eV for Ir1-LSF compared to LSF26. Similarly, the quasi-in situ DRIFT spectra acquired at 500 °C (Fig. 3h) display a significantly stronger carbonate absorption band at ~1440 cm−1 for Ir1-LSF37, demonstrating enhanced CO2 adsorption under high-temperature SOEC operating conditions. As a result, Ir dispersion significantly accelerates CO2RR kinetics determined by the electrical conductivity relaxation (ECR) method17,34. ECR fitting results in Fig. 3i reveal that the chemical oxygen surface exchange coefficient (kchem) increases by fivefold, from 2.13 × 10−4 cm s−1 for LSF to 1.11 × 10−3 cm s−1 for Ir1-LSF at 800 °C. These results highlight that high-valent Ir single atoms activate the LSF perovskite by weakening Fe–O hybridization, thereby promoting oxygen vacancy formation and significantly enhancing CO2 adsorption and CO2RR kinetics.

Mechanistic insights into Ir-induced enhancement of CO2RR kinetics

To investigate the CO2 electroreduction pathway at the macroscopic level, single cells with LSF and Ir1-LSF cathodes were operated at 800 °C and 1.0 V using isotopically labeled C18O2 as the feed gas, with detailed procedures provided in Supplementary Fig. 9a, b. Subsequently, the cross-section of the cells was analyzed using time-of-flight secondary mass spectroscopy (ToF-SIMS) to collect 18O− and 16O− signals shown in Supplementary Fig. 9c–f. On the cathode side, Ir1-LSF has a stronger 18O− signal intensity and a higher 18O−/(16O− + 18O−) ratio than LSF (Supplementary Fig. 9g), indicating that the Ir1-LSF cathode has a competitive C18O2 adsorption ability relative to LSF. In addition, the 18O−/(16O− + 18O−) ratio on the electrolyte for Ir1-LSF cell is also significantly higher than that of LSF (Supplementary Fig. 9g), indicating that more C18O2 is dissociated into 18O2− at the cathode surface and subsequently migrates into the electrolyte, providing direct evidence of the stronger C18O2 dissociation ability of Ir1-LSF.

To further reveal the CO2 electroreduction mechanism under SOEC operating conditions, in situ surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS) was performed on LSF and Ir1-LSF cells, as illustrated in Fig. 4a–c and Supplementary Fig. 10. For the LSF cell, the peak at ~1070 cm−1 in Fig. 4b is attributed to carbonate intermediate species, which begin to appear as the applied voltage increases to 1.5 V. As a comparison in Fig. 4c, the carbonate species in Ir1-LSF emerge at a relatively low voltage of 1.1 V and gradually intensifies as the voltage increases to 1.2, 1.3, 1.4, and 1.5 V, which strongly indicates that the dispersion of high-valent Ir single atoms promotes the adsorption and activation of CO2 to generate more carbonate intermediates, thus enhancing the CO2RR kinetics.

DFT calculations were systematically performed to shed light on the possible mechanism of CO2RR based on the above experimental results. Experimental characterizations revealed that high-valent Ir single atoms effectively activate the LSF perovskite surface, generating abundant oxygen vacancies. To systematically evaluate the tendency of vacancy formation, oxygen vacancy formation energies (EVO) were calculated at four distinct lattice sites as illustrated in Fig. 4d. The surface site-1 oxygen in pristine LSF exhibits the lowest EVO of 3.11 eV, which is reduced to 1.55 eV at the same site upon Ir dispersion. Furthermore, subsurface site-3 oxygen on Ir1-LSF exhibits an EVO of 0.94 eV, markedly lower than 3.78 eV in pristine LSF. These computational results are consistent with the interpretation that Ir dispersion substantially weakens both surface and subsurface metal-oxygen bonds, thermodynamically favoring vacancy generation, in agreement with XAS, XPS, EPR, and PALS results.

Building on these findings, we tentatively propose a possible CO2 electroreduction pathway as shown in Fig. 4e. Initially, the CO2 molecule is adsorbed on the LSF surface with an adsorption energy of −0.12 eV. However, the subsequent cleavage of the C–O bond requires a substantial energy input of 2.73 eV, and the final desorption of CO is also endothermic, with an energy uptake of 0.40 eV. In the case of Ir1-LSF, CO2 undergoes chemisorption with a significantly lower adsorption energy of −1.16 eV. The adsorbed CO2 forms a covalent bond between the carbon atom and lattice oxygen, leading to the formation of a monodentate carbonate species, consistent with the configuration observed in the in situ SERS analysis. Although C–O bond cleavage remains an endothermic process with a thermodynamic energy barrier of 2.34 eV, this value represents a 14.3% reduction compared to the pristine LSF system (2.73 eV). Furthermore, CO desorption exhibits an exothermic characteristic (ΔE = −0.48 eV) based on thermodynamic analysis. Therefore, the DFT results suggest that Ir incorporation could enable dual functionality: (1) enhancing CO2 chemisorption via optimized d-orbital alignment (−1.16 vs. −0.12 eV) and (2) lowering C–O bond dissociation energy. Simultaneously, the exergonic CO desorption (−0.48 eV) facilitates active-site regeneration. This synergistic effect accelerates CO2 activation and turnover, collectively boosting CO2RR kinetics.

To gain mechanistic insight into the catalytic enhancement induced by Ir dispersion, the density of states (DOS) was further adopted to investigate electronic structure modifications. As shown in Fig. 4f, the Fe–O coordination in LSF induces a wide band gap, indicating diminished conductivity at active sites. The detailed DOS distribution shows predominant occupancy of O 2p orbitals below the Fermi level, whereas Fe 3d orbitals exhibit spin-polarized electronic configurations with prominent spin-down peaks. This pronounced electronic localization at Fe centers, as evidenced by the asymmetric DOS distribution, suggests restricted electron delocalization that potentially impedes interfacial charge transfer kinetics. In contrast, Ir1-LSF displays pronounced Ir–O orbital hybridization and a reduced band gap (from 0.94 to 0.50 eV), as shown in Fig. 4g. Further analysis reveals that the electronic states below the Fermi level (−2.00 to 0 eV) are predominantly contributed by Ir 5d orbitals, displaying a broad energy distribution. This electronic configuration is consistent with a strengthened SMSI effect in the model, which may facilitate charge transport and optimize CO2 chemisorption through improved orbital overlap. Furthermore, distinct unoccupied spin-up Ir 5d states appear above the Fermi level (0.50–1.68 eV), enabling efficient electron acceptance of O from CO2 breaking (Bader charge transfer: 0.24 e, Supplementary Fig. 13), thereby lowering the CO desorption barrier from 0.40 eV for LSF to −0.48 eV for Ir1-LSF. Detailed computational data, including the optimized atomic coordinates (Supplementary Data 1), are available in the Supplementary Information.

Electrochemical performance and operational stability

A systematic comparison was conducted between pristine LSF and LSF-based cathodes containing 2 wt%, 4 wt%, and 6 wt% Ir, all synthesized using the same urea-assisted dispersion method. ICP-OES quantification shows that the 2 wt% and 4 wt% samples exhibit high dispersion efficiencies of 83.8% and 83.3%, whereas the efficiency decreases sharply to 55.6% at 6 wt% as the dispersion capacity approaches saturation (Supplementary Fig. 14). Consistently, CO2 electrolysis current density increases with Ir loading up to 4 wt% and then reaches a plateau under the same operating conditions (Supplementary Figs. 15 and 16), with the enhancement clearly originating from atomically dispersed Ir rather than IrO2 particulates (Supplementary Fig. 17). Taken together, these results identify 4 wt% Ir as the optimal loading, maximizing both atomic dispersion and catalytic performance (Supplementary Fig. 18). BET measurements confirm that Ir incorporation has a negligible effect on the surface area (Supplementary Fig. 19). Cross-sectional SEM reveals nearly identical electrode microstructures across all samples, and quantitative image analysis confirms comparable porosity levels (Supplementary Fig. 20). These observations indicate that the enhanced performance arises from the atomically dispersed Ir single-atom sites, rather than from morphology-related effects.

Symmetrical cells were fabricated to evaluate the CO2RR mechanism, with the testing principle illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 21. The dispersion of high-valent Ir single atoms greatly reduces the interfacial polarization resistance (Rp) across the evaluated temperature of 650–800 °C (Supplementary Fig. 22). At 800 °C, for instance, Rp decreases by 84.1% from 0.243 Ω cm2 for LSF to 0.132 Ω cm2 for Ir1-LSF (Fig. 5a). To reveal the performance enhancement mechanism, the distribution of relaxation time (DRT) method was adopted to deconvolute EIS spectra into individual CO2RR-associated processes39. Ir dispersion exerts negligible influence on high-frequency (P1) and intermediate-frequency (P2) processes, but significantly reduces the low-frequency (P3) peak intensity (Fig. 5b). According to the ALS model and prior studies17,34,40,41, the decrease in the P3 peak is associated with accelerated CO2 adsorption and dissociation processes on the electrode surface, which can be attributed to the surface activation effect of Ir single atoms. Additionally, Ir dispersion decreases the apparent activation energy (Ea) from 91.9 to 85.4 kJ mol−1 (Fig. 5c).

To further evaluate the electrocatalytic performance and stability under practical operating conditions, single cells were fabricated for CO2 electrolysis, with the testing setup illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 23. Remarkably, the Ir1-LSF cathode exhibits significantly higher CO2 electrolysis performance than LSF across 650–800 °C (Supplementary Fig. 24a–d). For the pristine LSF cathode, the current densities at 1.5 V are 0.30, 0.57, 1.00, and 1.67 A cm−2 at 650, 700, 750, and 800 °C, respectively. After Ir single-atom dispersion, the values increase substantially to 0.42, 0.88, 1.67, and 3.02 A cm−2 under the same conditions (Supplementary Fig. 24e). Consistently, the polarization resistance decreases markedly across the entire temperature range (Supplementary Fig. 24f). Notably, at 800 °C and 1.5 V, the Ir1-LSF cathode achieves a high current density of 3.02 A cm−2 (Fig. 5d), representing an 80.8% enhancement over the 1.67 A cm−2 for the pristine LSF cathode, and significantly surpassing various representative cathodes (Supplementary Table 6). To elucidate the enhanced electrocatalytic performance, EIS spectra were measured at 1.2 V. As evidenced in Fig. 5e, Ir dispersion shows minimal influence on the ohmic resistance at 800 °C (RΩ ≈ 0.13 Ω cm2). The slight differences observed at lower temperatures (Supplementary Fig. S24c, d) are likely attributable to the reduced CO2RR activation energy and the enhanced low-temperature conductivity together with faster interfacial charge transfer of Ir1-LSF relative to pristine LSF. In contrast, the polarization resistance (Rp) at 800 °C decreases substantially from 0.25 Ω cm2 for LSF to 0.12 Ω cm2 for Ir1-LSF, clearly indicating the enhanced electrode catalytic activity17. Since the SOECs are composed of identical anodes and electrolytes, this performance gain is unequivocally ascribed to high-valent Ir-induced cathode activation. In addition, the Ir1-LSF cathode demonstrates high stability (Supplementary Fig. 25) and near 100% Faradaic efficiency (Fig. 5f), delivering a high CO production of 24.4 mL min−1 cm−2 at 800 °C and 1.5 V.

While low-temperature electrolysis offers practical advantages, such as reduced thermal energy consumption, extended SAC lifetimes, and a broader product distribution42, our work demonstrates that the Ir1-LSF cathode achieves good durability even at 800 °C, operating with slight performance decay for more than 600 h at both 1.0 and 1.5 V (Fig. 5g and Supplementary Fig. 26), showing competitive stability (Fig. 5h and Supplementary Table 5) and performance (Supplementary Table 6). In addition, the Ir1-LSF cathode exhibits excellent stability under practical operating conditions at 800 °C and a constant current density of 1.0 A cm−2 (Supplementary Fig. 27), underscoring its strong suitability for integration into industrial high-temperature CO2 electrolysis systems.

Discussion

Experimental and theoretical investigations jointly demonstrate that high-valent Ir single atoms effectively activate the LSF perovskite surfaces, facilitating efficient and durable CO2 electrolysis. Remarkably, even after the stability test for more than 600 h, the Ir1-LSF cathode maintains a well-preserved microstructure (Supplementary Fig. 28), with no detectable carbon deposition via Raman spectroscopy measurements (Supplementary Fig. 29)43. In addition, no Sr segregation is detected, as evidenced by the Sr 3d XPS spectra (Supplementary Fig. 30) and HAADF-STEM coupled with EDS elemental maps (Supplementary Fig. 31), in agreement with DFT calculations (Supplementary Fig. 32) showing that Ir dispersion increases the Sr-vacancy formation energy and thus suppresses Sr migration to the surface. Furthermore, atomic scale aberration-corrected HAADF-STEM, coupled with EDS elemental maps, confirms homogeneous dispersion of Ir without aggregation (Fig. 6a–c and Supplementary Fig. 31). Moreover, Ir L3-edge XANES and EXAFS analyses reveal that the Ir species undergo no notable changes after the stability test (Fig. 6d, e), and subsequent fitting confirms that Ir retains a high oxidation state of +5.50, closely matching the pre-reaction value of +5.57 (Fig. 6f). Additionally, Fe K-edge XANES spectra indicate a modest valence reduction of Fe (Supplementary Fig. 33). These results collectively demonstrate that the SMSI effect promotes the formation of high-valent Ir single atoms, which activate the LSF cathode by lowering the Fe valence state and attenuating Fe–O hybridization. Simultaneously, the resulting high-valent Ir single atoms exhibit high stability under cathodic polarization conditions.

Although Ir is a precious metal, its atomically dispersed form enables high atomic efficiency, delivering an 80.8% increase in CO2 electrolysis current density with a mere 4 wt% loading while sustaining over 600 h of stable operation at 800 °C. The urea-assisted dispersion strategy also exhibits broad generality, as Ir is uniformly dispersed not only on perovskite LSF but also on fluorite-type Gd0.1Ce0.9O2 − δ without forming secondary phases or aggregates (Supplementary Figs. 34 and 35). Comparative studies further show that earth-abundant alternatives, such as Fe, Co, and Ni, can also be uniformly dispersed on LSF using the same urea-assisted method (Supplementary Figs. 36–39); however, none of these metals can activate the LSF support or enhance the CO2 electrolysis activity (Supplementary Figs. 40 and 41), underscoring the special catalytic promotion provided by high-valent Ir single atoms.

In summary, we present a strategy to activate the LSF perovskite cathode with high-valent Ir single atoms through the SMSI effect, in situ induced at the cell fabrication process. This high-temperature process facilitates electron transfer from the Ir species to the LSF cathode, elevating the oxidation state of Ir close to +6. The high-valent Ir single atoms not only provide additional active sites for CO2 adsorption and activation but also enhance stability under high temperatures and cathodic potentials. Importantly, the dispersion of high-valent Ir activates the LSF perovskite cathode, significantly reducing the oxygen vacancy formation energy from 3.78 eV to 0.94 eV, thereby promoting oxygen vacancy formation and improving CO2 adsorption and conversion into carbonate intermediates. As a result, the dispersion of Ir increases the chemical oxygen surface exchange coefficient (kchem) for CO2RR by a factor of ~5.0, reaching 1.11 × 10−3 cm s−1, while reducing the polarization resistance (Rp) by 84.1% to 0.132 Ω cm2 at 800 °C. The CO2 electrolysis performance is enhanced by 80.8%, reaching 3.02 A cm−2 at 800 °C and 1.5 V, with a CO production rate of 24.4 mL min−1 cm−2 and a Faradaic efficiency approaching 100%. Moreover, the high-valent Ir single-atom cathode demonstrates reliable durability over 600 h of continuous operation. This strategy offers valuable insights for the development of thermally and electrochemically stable SACs.

Methods

Materials preparation

La0.6Sr0.4FeO3 − δ (LSF) powder was synthesized using an ethylene diamine tetraacetic acid (EDTA) and citric acid (CA) assisted combustion method. First, La(NO3)3·6H2O (AR, 99.9%), Sr(NO3)2 (AR, 99.5%), and Fe(NO3)3·9H2O (AR, 99.99%) were dissolved in a dilute nitric acid solution in stoichiometric ratios. Then, EDTA (AR, ≥99.5%) and CA (AR, ≥99.5%) were added, maintaining a molar ratio of total metal ions, EDTA, and CA at 1:1:2. Subsequently, dilute ammonia solution was added dropwise to adjust the pH of the mixture to 7.0. After stirring on a magnetic stirrer for 12 h, the solution was transferred to an evaporating dish and gradually evaporated until spontaneous combustion occurred. Finally, the black combustion products were collected and sintered at 1000 °C for 2 h in air to obtain the target cathode powder. La0.6Sr0.4Co0.2Fe0.8O3 − δ (LSCF) anode powder was prepared using the same EDTA-CA method as the LSF cathode, with the final sintering performed at 950 °C for 3 h. Sm0.2Ce0.8O2 − δ (SDC) was synthesized by glycine-nitrate process with Ce(NO3)3·6H2O (AR, 99.95%) and Sm(NO3)3·6H2O (AR, 99.99%) as precursors, finally sintered at 600 °C for 2 h44. Ir atomically dispersed LSF powder was prepared using a urea-assisted dispersion method. H2IrCl6 (Jinbolan Fine Chemical Co., Ltd.) was first dissolved in deionized water to prepare a 0.0074 g mL−1 precursor solution. Urea was then added at a urea-to-Ir molar ratio of 2:1, followed by impregnation of the resulting Ir–urea mixture onto the LSF support according to the desired loading. The mixture was thoroughly ground for 2 h, transferred to a muffle furnace, and calcined in air at 1000 °C for 2 h to obtain the Ir-dispersed LSF catalyst powder. The Ir loadings were controlled at 2 wt% (2Ir-LSF), 4 wt% (4Ir-LSF), and 6 wt% (6Ir-LSF); among them, the 4 wt% Ir atomically dispersed LSF catalyst powder is also referred to as Ir1-LSF. The same dispersion method was applied to Ni, Co, and Fe, with the corresponding nitrate salts employed as metal precursors. All the nitrates mentioned above were sourced from Aladdin Industrial Corporation, while all the chelating agents were obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co. Ltd.

Cell fabrication

La0.8Sr0.2Ga0.8Mg0.2O3 − δ (LSGM, Fuel Cell Materials) was employed for the preparation of symmetrical cells (approximately 330 μm in thickness) by dry pressing and single cells (approximately 200 μm in thickness) by tape casting. The green bodies were sintered at 1450 °C for 6 h to obtain dense LSGM electrolyte pellets. Ir-dispersed LSF powder and organic binder (containing 94 wt% α-terpineol and 6 wt% ethyl cellulose) were thoroughly ground in a mortar at a mass ratio of 1:2 to prepare the cathode slurry. LSCF and SDC were mixed at a mass ratio of 6:4, followed by the addition of the organic binder and thorough grinding to form the LSCF-SDC anode slurry. For the preparation of symmetrical cells, the Ir-dispersed LSF slurry was symmetrically printed on both sides of the LSGM electrolyte pellets, fully dried, and sintered at 1000 °C for 2 h. For the preparation of single cells, the Ir-dispersed LSF cathode slurry was first screen-printed onto one side of the LSGM electrolyte pellet, while the LSCF-SDC anode slurry was printed on the opposite side. To prevent potential performance degradation caused by elemental diffusion, an SDC barrier layer was applied between the electrolyte and the cathode in the durability testing. The assembled cells were subsequently sintered at 1000 °C for 2 h. The resulting single cell consists of a porous cathode (~40 µm), an SDC barrier layer (~6 µm), a dense LSGM electrolyte (~200 µm), and a porous LSCF-SDC anode (~30 µm). The active electrode area used for testing was 0.2376 cm2. For comparison, the LSF and non-dispersed IrO2-LSF electrodes were fabricated using the same preparation procedures and processing conditions.

Electrochemical measurements

Electrochemical measurements were carried out using custom-built equipment. Symmetrical cells were fully exposed to a 50% CO-50% CO2 atmosphere (30 mL min−1) for EIS measurements, which were conducted using an electrochemical workstation with an AC perturbation amplitude of 20 mV (PGSTAT302N, Metrohm, Switzerland). EIS data were analyzed using the DRT function to distinguish the reaction steps of CO2RR based on RelaxIS 3 software. Single cells were sealed with glass rings at the top of a corundum tube for CO2 electrolysis. Prior to testing, the cathode was not pre-reduced in H2. During operation, pure CO2 (30 mL min−1) was directly fed to the cathode, while the anode was exposed to static air. J–V polarization curves were recorded by linearly scanning the voltage from open-circuit voltage (OCV) to 1.6 V at a sweep rate of 10 mV s−1, and EIS were recorded under an applied voltage of 1.2 V in the temperature range of 650–800 °C. All voltages were reported as measured without iR correction, and that the data reflected the full‑cell voltage under operating conditions. Quantitative analysis of cathode gas products was performed using an online gas chromatograph (GC, Agilent 490). LSF and Ir-dispersed LSF bar samples were prepared for ECR characterization by rapidly switching the atmosphere from 50% CO-50% CO2 to 33.3% CO-66.7% CO2 at a flow rate of 200 mL min−141. Gold paste and gold wire were used as current collectors. All gas flow rates (including CO2 and any other gases) were measured and controlled using calibrated mass flow controllers. Structural, morphological, and compositional analyses

Phase structures were analyzed using X-ray diffraction (XRD, PANalytical X’pert PPR diffractometer). Microstructures of LSF and Ir-dispersed LSF samples were observed by a scanning electron microscope (SEM, JSM-7800F). Aberration-corrected HAADF-STEM combined with EDS was used to visualize the dispersion state of the single atoms on the oxide support (STEM, JEOL-ARM 300F). The specific surface area was investigated using nitrogen adsorption-desorption measurements based on the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method with an automated analyzer (Quadrasorb evo). Fourier transform infrared spectra of urea and urea-Ir complex were obtained using attenuated total reflection (ATR) mode (FTIR-ATR, Bruker INVENIO S). ICP-OES was used to determine the content of dispersed Ir species on an Optima 7300DV instrument (ICP-OES, PerkinElmer Instrument Corporation).

Valence state and coordination environment analyses

Valence states of LSF and Ir-dispersed LSF samples were analyzed using XPS with an Al Kα X-ray source (XPS, Thermofisher Escalab 250 Xi+). Soft XAS at the O K-edge of LSF and Ir1-LSF were obtained from the BL10B beamline station of the National Synchrotron Radiation Laboratory in Hefei, China. Hard XAS at the Fe K edge and Ir L3 edge of the LSF and Ir-dispersed LSF samples were recorded at the BL11B beam line of SSRF, China. The XAS data were analyzed by the Athena software package.

Defect and oxygen vacancy characterizations

EPR spectra of LSF and Ir1-LSF samples were obtained with a Bruker A200 spectrometer. PALS tests for LSF and Ir1-LSF samples were carried out at the Institute of High Energy Physics, CAS. To ensure statistical reliability, over 2 million counts were accumulated for the lifetime spectrum, which was subsequently analyzed using Lt9.0 software. The measurement system offered a temporal resolution of ~210 ps and employed standard NIM modules (EG&G, USA).

Redox properties and CO2 adsorption analyses

The reduction behavior of LSF and Ir-dispersed LSF samples was investigated by H2-TPR equipped with a TCD detector, using 10% H2/90% Ar as the reducing gas at a flow rate of 50 mL min−1 and a heating rate of 10 °C min−1 (H2-TPR, AutoChem II-GSD350). The CO2 desorption process of LSF and Ir1-LSF samples was determined by a temperature-programmed chemisorption apparatus equipped with a mass spectrometry detector (CO2-TPD, AutoChem II-GSD350 OmniStar). First, the sample was heated to 900 °C in He to remove the adsorbed H2O and other species, then cooled to 25 °C in CO2. Subsequently, the sample was swept with He to remove CO2 from the gas path. Finally, the sample was heated from 25 to 900 °C at a ramp rate of 10 °C min−1, and the m/z = 44 signal was collected for analysis.

In situ and quasi-in situ characterizations

In situ XRD was carried out in CO2 atmosphere from 25 to 800 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C min−1. Quasi-in situ DRIFT spectra of LSF and Ir1-LSF samples were collected at 500 °C on a Bruker spectrometer (VERTEX 80 v FTIR). First, the sample was heated to 500 °C in He and held for 30 min, followed by feeding CO2 for 30 min to allow adsorption. Subsequently, He was introduced to remove residual gaseous CO2, and finally, the spectral signals were collected. In the quasi-in situ XPS experiment, LSF and Ir1-LSF powders were pre-treated with a pure CO2 atmosphere at 600 °C for 2 h, followed by transfer to the sample chamber for measurement. ToF-SIMS analysis was conducted using a vacuum-integrated nanoscale workstation operated in fast imaging mode with a 30 kV Bi ion source (ToF-SIMS 5–100, Germany). Single cells of LSF and Ir1-LSF were identically pretreated in an electrochemical reactor at 1.0 V and 800 °C, using C18O2 (10 mL min−1) as cathode reactant gas. In situ SERS experiments were conducted in a custom-built reactor, as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 10. Single cells of LSF and Ir1-LSF were sealed at the center of the reactor using ceramic glue. Prior to the experiment, an equal amount of SiO2-coated Au (Au@SiO2) was added to the cathode side to enhance the Raman signal. CO2 was continuously supplied to the cathode, and in situ Raman measurements were performed at 600°C on a LabRAM HR 800 Raman spectrometer using a 532 nm laser under constant voltages (1.0–1.5 V) applied via an electrochemical workstation (Ivium, Vertex).

DFT calculations

Spin-polarized DFT calculations were performed using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP 6.2.0)45. The exchange-correlation interactions were treated with the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof functional under the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) framework46. Core-electron interactions were addressed through the projector augmented wave method47, with a plane-wave kinetic energy cutoff of 520 eV systematically applied across all simulations. To account for strong electronic correlations in transition metal oxides, a Hubbard U correction (GGA+U) was implemented with effective parameters U_eff = 4.0 eV for Fe 3d orbitals and 5.5 eV for La 4f orbitals, consistent with prior studies of perovskite oxides48. Geometry optimizations employed a Γ-centered Monkhorst-Pack k-point grid with 2 × 2 × 1 sampling density for surface models49, maintaining energy convergence thresholds of 10−5 eV and force convergence criteria below 0.05 eV/Å. A three-layer slab model of the LSF (110) facet was constructed, wherein the bottom atomic layer remained fixed to mimic bulk substrate behavior. A vacuum spacing of 15 Å normal to the surface plane effectively mitigated periodic image interactions. Post-processing analyses, including adsorption energy calculations and electronic DOS extraction, were conducted using the VASPKIT toolkit50. Thermal corrections and zero-point energy contributions for adsorbed carbon intermediates were evaluated through harmonic frequency calculations at the Γ-point. The Sr segregation energies (eV) were calculated based on the formula: Eseg = Esur − Ebulk. Here, the Esur represents the segregated Sr located at the surface, and Ebulk is the energy of Sr in the bulk lattice.
