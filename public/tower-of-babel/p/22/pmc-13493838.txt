Food and garden organics (FOGO) compost application improves post-harvest chemical and sensory profiles of conventionally grown tomatoes
Authors: Jia Wang, Joseph Robert Nastasi, Sangeeta Prakash, Buddhi Dayananda, Nicole Robinson, Susanne Schmidt, Marlize Zaretha Bekker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The incorporation of food and garden organics (FOGO) compost into horticultural systems is promoted to improve soil health and advance circular economy goals; however, its impact on flavour quality and consumer perception remains poorly understood. In particular, most studies assess fruit quality at harvest, overlooking post-harvest processes critical to consumer experience. Here, we investigated the influence of FOGO compost on the flavour of tomatoes (Solanum lycopersicum cv. Ninja) under conventional commercial production conditions using integrated sensory and chemical approaches. Tomatoes grown with and without FOGO compost were evaluated at harvest maturity and following post-harvest storage. Consumer sensory analysis revealed no differences in preference at harvest maturity. However, after 7-day storage, FOGO tomatoes were preferred and described as sweeter, more aromatic, and less bland. These sensory differences were associated with higher total soluble solids, lower malic acid, elevated concentrations of umami-associated amino acids, and treatment-specific differences in aroma-active volatiles.

Introduction

Tomatoes lead global horticultural production, valued at US$116 billion annually1. Consumer acceptance and market value depend on sensory attributes, particularly sweetness, acidity, aroma, and overall flavour intensity, which drives consumer purchasing behaviour and willingness to pay2. These sensory attributes arise from complex interactions among sugars (fructose, glucose, sucrose), organic acids (citric, malic)3, volatile organic compounds (e.g. cis-3-hexenal, β-ionone, and 2-isobutylthiazole contribute green, fruity, and tomato-like notes)4, and lycopene and β-carotene (characteristic red colour properties)5, whose relative abundance and balance determine perceived quality. Tomatoes with vibrant red hues, firm textures, pleasant flavours, and aromas often command premium prices6, but commercial varieties often lack sweetness due to an inverse relationship between fruit size and sugar content7. However, the loss of sensory quality in modern tomatoes is largely attributable to conventional breeding programmes, which, until recent decades, prioritised yield and requirements of large-scale distribution rather than consumer preferences8.

Conventional growers rely predominantly on mineral fertiliser to maximise production and yield stability, and some also use organic materials such as composts and manures9–12. Despite the well-documented benefits of organic fertilisers and soil amendments, variability in composition and functionality across products limits the ability to standardise application strategies. Nonetheless, organic fertilisers derived from animal or plant sources, or mixtures of both, have been reported to increase tomato yield when compared with chemical fertilisers13. However, the effects on tomato quality varied: a similar sugar-to-acid ratio was observed with chemical and organic fertilisers, but a higher soluble sugar content with organic fertiliser13. Composts are widely used as organic fertilisers and soil amendments, and contribute to improved nutrient recycling and enhanced circularity within agricultural systems. By combining compost with chemical fertiliser, global crop yields increased on average by 16% compared to chemical fertiliser alone14.

Food and garden organics (FOGO) compost, derived from the organic fraction of municipal solid waste, represents a rapidly expanding resource within circular economy and food-waste valorisation frameworks. FOGO compost also aligns with national food waste strategies, preventing landfill disposal and supporting circularity15,16. Beyond diverting organic waste from landfills, FOGO compost can accelerate tomato ripening17 and improve fruit quality by lowering undesirable cadmium and nitrate levels and increasing soluble sugar content18. Compost from pineapple waste+poultry litter increased tomato size, sugar content and shelf life19, and compost from agricultural wastes increased ascorbic acid content20.

Despite such promising findings, the effects of FOGO compost on tomato quality have not been thoroughly investigated, and its use in horticulture remains limited. Most studies assess tomato quality at Time 1 (5 days postharvest), assuming that compost-mediated effects on flavour arise primarily during fruit development. Flavour qualities, however, are not fixed at harvest. Post-harvest storage induces continued metabolic activity, including dynamic changes in organic acids, amino acids, and volatile compounds that can substantially alter sensory properties. Whether organic soil amendments influence these post-harvest metabolic trajectories, thereby modifying when and how flavour differentiation becomes sensory-relevant, has not been systematically examined.

In this study, we address this knowledge gap by investigating whether incorporating FOGO compost into a conventional fertilisation programme influences tomato flavour perception at harvest as well as after post-harvest storage. FOGO and Control compost treatments were implemented under commercial production conditions, and tomatoes harvested from each treatment were subjected to sensory evaluation and comprehensive chemical profiling to assess tomato flavour at harvest maturity (5 days postharvest, Time 1) and following post-harvest storage (12 days postharvest, Time 2). By combining sensory evaluation with targeted analysis of sugars, organic acids, amino acids, and volatile organic compounds, this study investigates the relationship between circular organic fertilisation practices and tomato flavour perception.

Results and discussion

Sensory evaluation reveals time-dependent effects of FOGO compost on tomato flavour perception

Consumer sensory evaluation was conducted at two defined time points to determine whether incorporating FOGO compost influenced tomato flavour perception at Time 1 (5 days postharvest) or after post-harvest storage (Time 2). This two-time-point approach enabled comparison of sensory profiles at harvest maturity (5 days postharvest; Time 1) and under conditions that represent when consumers typically consume the product (12 days postharvest; Time 2).

At Time 1 (5 days postharvest), sensory evaluation was conducted with 47 consumers who assessed FOGO and Control tomatoes using a 24-attribute CATA questionnaire. Chi-square screening of individual attributes did not identify any statistically significant differences between treatments at α = 0.05. Citation frequencies indicated moderate differences for several attributes, including I like its appearance (FOGO 57.4% vs Control 68.1%), Bland/no taste (29.8% vs 38.3%), Pleasant aroma (61.7% vs 55.3%), and Not enough sweet (51.1% vs 53.2%), while Beautiful colour was highly cited for both treatments (80.9% for both) (Fig. 1a and Figure. S1a). Multivariate exploratory analysis of the CATA data using principal coordinate analysis (PCoA; Jaccard distance) suggested differences in the sensory attributes of the FOGO and Control tomatoes despite the absence of significant univariate effects. The first two PCoA dimensions explained 20.6% (PCoA1) and 12.7% (PCoA2) of the variance, as indicated by the positive eigenvalues (Fig. 1b). As expected for binary CATA data analysed using the Jaccard distance, the first two PCoA dimensions explained a relatively modest proportion of the total variance (Time 1: 32.7%; Time 2: 32.6%). The ordination plots should therefore be interpreted as exploratory representations of multivariate patterns rather than definitive separation between treatments. The attributes contributing most strongly to the ordination included Pleasant aroma (PCoA1, r = −0.66), Not enough sweet (PCoA1, r = 0.66), Beautiful colour (PCoA2, r = −0.72), I like its fleshy appearance (PCoA2, r = −0.62), and I like its appearance (PCoA2, r = −0.51). Correlation signs depend on axis orientation and should be interpreted in terms of relative alignment rather than directionally (Fig. 1b). At Time 2, 41 consumers evaluated FOGO and Control tomatoes using the same CATA attribute question set. In contrast to Time 1, chi-square screening identified a statistically significant difference between treatments for the attribute Sweet, which was cited more frequently for FOGO than for Control tomatoes (36.6% vs 14.6%, p = 0.043). Several additional attributes showed notable directional differences, including I do not like its appearance (FOGO 9.8% vs Control 29.3%, p = 0.051), Bland/no taste (19.5% vs.5%, p = 0.055), Too pale colour (9.8% vs 22.0%, p = 0.227), and Pleasant aroma (58.5% vs 39.0%, p = 0.122), although these did not reach the α = 0.05 threshold individually (Fig. 1c and Figure. S1b). The PCoA of the Time 2 data showed more apparent separation between products, with PCoA1 and PCoA2 explaining 22.8% and 9.8% of variance, respectively (Fig. 1d). Attributes most strongly associated with I do not like its appearance (PCoA1, r = 0.69), Bland/no taste (PCoA1, r = 0.56), Too pale colour (PCoA1, r = 0.51), Pleasant aroma (PCoA1, r = −0.50), and Sweet (PCoA1, r = −0.48). Collectively, these patterns indicate that FOGO tomatoes were more closely associated with positive eating-quality descriptors, whereas Control tomatoes were more frequently associated with less desirable appearance- and flavour-related attributes following storage.

Taken together, multivariate ordination suggested patterns of separation between FOGO and Control tomatoes at both time points, although these differences were not statistically significant at the univariate level at Time 1 and were limited to Time 2. At Time 1, sensory differences were modest and distributed across multiple attributes, whereas at Time 2, differentiation was more apparent, with Sweet significantly favouring FOGO tomatoes, and several undesirable attributes showing directional (but not significant) trends. Although the experimental design did not test a formal time × treatment interaction, the stronger separation at Time 2 suggests that treatment-related contrasts become more salient under post-harvest conditions. Overall, FOGO compost did not markedly influence sensory perception at harvest but was associated with a more favourable sensory profile following storage.

Colour measurements and °Brix:TA ratio indicate comparable ripeness between treatments

Colour analysis was performed to assess ripeness and ensure that differences in fruit maturity did not confound sensory differences. Comparison of CIELab parameters (L*, a*, b*) between Control and FOGO tomatoes revealed no significant between-group differences at either Time 1 or Time 2 (Table S1). Within-treatment analysis showed a substantial increase in redness (a*) from Time 1 to Time 2 for both treatments (Fig. 2a), consistent with continued ripening during storage. To further validate ripeness equivalence, the °Brix:TA ratio was evaluated as an established indicator of tomato maturity21. No significant differences in the °Brix:TA ratio were observed between Control and FOGO tomatoes at either Time 1 or Time 2 (Fig. 2b). This finding is consistent with the colour measurements and provides independent support for the comparable ripeness across sampling points. The absence of treatment effects on both colour parameters and °Brix:TA ratio indicates that sensory differences observed at Time 2 are unlikely to be attributed to differences in fruit maturity or harvest timing.

Physicochemical parameters indicate differences in soluble solids, while ripeness indicators remain comparable between treatments

Moisture content, pH, and TA did not differ significantly between Control and FOGO tomatoes at either time point (Table S2). Within-group analysis indicated a small but statistically significant change in pH for FOGO tomatoes from Time 1 to Time 2; however, the magnitude of this change was minor and unlikely to have had a meaningful impact on sensory perception.

In contrast, TSS, expressed as °Brix, were consistently higher in FOGO tomatoes at both Time 1 and Time 2 (Fig. 2c). Because the °Brix:TA ratio and a* colour values, two independent indicators of ripeness, remained comparable between treatments at each time point (Fig. 2a, b), the elevated °Brix values in FOGO tomatoes are consistent with differences in soluble composition that are not attributed to advanced maturity. These findings are consistent with the sensory data, supporting the interpretation that perceived sweetness differences were not attributed to differences in ripeness.

Volatile organic compound profiles differ between treatments following post-harvest storage

A total of 114 volatile organic compounds (VOCs) were identified across all tomato samples, including aldehydes, alcohols, esters, ketones, and terpenoids (Table S3). These compound classes are well-established contributors to tomato aroma and collectively shape flavour perception. While the overall qualitative VOC composition was similar across treatments, quantitative differences in the relative abundances became more evident after post-harvest storage.

To provide context for the observed treatment- and storage-dependent differences in volatile composition, the relative abundance of the most prominent VOCs was first examined. Eighteen compounds contributed ≥ 1% of the total volatile signal in at least one treatment at Time 2 ( > 1% of total peak area; Figure. S2). These included lipid-derived aldehydes and alcohols associated with green and fresh notes (e.g. hexanal, trans-2-hexenal, trans-2-hexen-1-ol, cis-3-hexen-1-ol, octanal, trans-2-octenal, 1-hexanol, and 1-pentanol), esters linked to fruity and sweet aromas (e.g. methyl hexanoate, propyl butyrate, and methyl palmitate), and terpenoid or apocarotenoid-derived compounds contributing floral and citrus notes (e.g. α-phellandrene, p-cymene, 6-methyl-5-hepten-2-one, and trans-geranylacetone). Additional high-abundance compounds characteristic of tomato aroma, including 2-pentylfuran, 3-octanone, 2-isobutylthiazole, and phenylacetaldehyde, were also detected. Collectively, these compounds are consistent with established tomato aroma profiles reported in the literature22–24, providing context for the subsequent discrimination analysis.

To identify the volatile compounds that were most strongly associated with treatment-related differences between FOGO and Control tomatoes, variable importance in projection (VIP) scores derived from PLS-DA were examined (Fig. 3). The VIP scores provide a relative measure of the contribution of each variable (volatile compound) to the separation observed in the PLS-DA model. Compounds with VIP scores ≥1.5 were therefore considered to contribute to the observed differences between Control and FOGO. Compounds with VIP scores greater than 1.5 comprised of several esters (isopropyl butyrate, (Z)-3-hexenyl butyrate, methyl hexanoate, and butyl benzoate), aldehydes ((Z)-2-decenal, (E)-2-decenal, 2,4-heptadienal, and benzaldehyde), alcohols (1-hexanol, 1-heptanol, 1-octanol and 1-octen-3-ol), and the terpenoid alcohol citronellol as well as 3-methyl-2(5H)-furanone (Fig. 3). The relative abundance patterns associated with the VIP-ranked compounds indicate compound-specific, time- and treatment-dependent shifts, with discrimination arising from coordinated variation across multiple volatile classes rather than from a single compound. In particular, several ester compounds, including isopropyl butyrate, (Z)-3-hexenyl butyrate, methyl hexanoate, and butyl hexanoate, as well as the terpenoid alcohol citronellol, showed higher relative abundance in FOGO tomatoes after storage (Fig. 3). This pattern is consistent with the sensory outcomes observed after storage, where FOGO tomatoes were more frequently associated with aroma-related attributes and less frequently with blandness.

Organic acid profiles differ between treatments following post-harvest storage

Analysis of organic acids revealed treatment- and time-dependent differences following post-harvest storage (Fig. 5). Malic and citric acids were the dominant organic acids detected, occurring at substantially higher concentrations than low-abundance acidic metabolites such as acetic, propionic and pyroglutamic acid. Acetic acid and propionic acid were present only at low concentrations and were comparable between FOGO and Control tomatoes at both time points (Fig. 5a, 5b), suggesting limited contribution to overall acid balance. Malic acid concentrations were significantly lower in FOGO tomatoes at Time 2, whereas no between-treatment differences were observed at Time 1 (Fig. 5c). Citric acid concentrations decreased significantly from Time 1 to Time 2 in both treatments, with no significant inter-group differences (Fig. 5d). Malic and citric acids are the primary contributors to the sour taste in tomatoes and can mask the perception of sweetness22. The decline in major organic acids during storage (Fig. 5), together with the significantly lower malic acid content in FOGO tomatoes at Time 2, may have contributed to the enhanced sweetness perception reported by consumers. The concentrations and prominence of malic and citric acids described in this study are consistent with previous studies identifying these as key organic acids contributing to fruit flavour balance25,26. Storage-associated changes in organic acid profiles, particularly citric acid decline and associated effects on sourness, have also been reported in cherry tomato25,26. FOGO tomatoes also exhibited higher concentrations of pyroglutamic acid at Time 2 (Fig. 5e). Although present at much lower concentrations than malic acid and citric acid, pyroglutamic acid has been associated with umami perception in tomato products27 and may therefore have contributed to the sensory differences observed at Time 2.

Ascorbic acid-related compounds were evaluated as part of the broader organic acid profile. Concentrations of l-AA and T-AA were lower in FOGO tomatoes compared with Controls, whereas DHAA was significantly higher in FOGO samples (Fig. 5f–h). This pattern indicates a shift in the distribution of ascorbate forms rather than a uniform change in total ascorbic acid content, with a relative increase in the oxidised form (DHAA), suggesting changes in the ascorbate redox status in FOGO fruit. Total ascorbic acid concentrations were broadly comparable to values previously reported for tomatoes28. The relative increase in DHAA is consistent with previous work linking changes in ascorbate redox balance with stress-associated physiological responses in tomato tissues28. Considering the central role of the ascorbate–dehydroascorbate pool in antioxidant metabolism and oxidative stress responses, this change may reflect altered post-harvest redox regulations28. However, this was not directly tested in the present study. While ascorbic acid is unlikely to be directly associated with the flavour effects observed in this study, altered ascorbate redox balance may indicate additional physiological responses associated with the FOGO treatment.

Aspartic and glutamic acids account for approximately 80% of the total free amino acid content in tomatoes and are key contributors to umami flavour29. Their increased concentrations in FOGO tomatoes at Time 2 are consistent with the sensory differences observed after storage, including the decreased perception of blandness. Although THR has been reported to contribute modestly to sweetness30, its concentrations were unlikely to be flavour-active relative to sugars, explaining why differences at Time 1 were not reflected in sensory sweetness perception. Overall, these results suggest that treatment-associated differences in amino acid composition are more evident after post-harvest storage rather than at harvest maturity (5 days postharvest, Time 1). The change in amino acid concentrations measured in this study is consistent with previous reports showing that the free amino acid composition changes substantially during tomato ripening, with implications for flavour development and nitrogen metabolism31.

Antioxidant capacity and carotenoid composition are largely unaffected by FOGO compost

There were time-dependent differences in antioxidant capacity at Time 1, which were not maintained during storage. At Time 1, FOGO tomatoes showed significantly greater DPPH radical scavenging activity and FRAP values than the Control, whereas TPC did not differ between treatments (Table S4). By Time 2, there were no significant differences in DPPH, FRAP, or TPC values between FOGO and Control tomatoes. This shows that the differences in antioxidant capacity measured at Time 1 were not maintained during storage. These findings are broadly consistent with previous reports showing that changes in antioxidant capacity during tomato ripening can be transient and not necessarily be maintained throughout storage31.

The concentrations of lycopene, lutein, and β-carotene were comparable between treatments at Time 1 (Figure. S3). At Time 2, lutein and β-carotene concentrations remained similar, while lycopene concentrations were significantly lower in FOGO tomatoes. This decrease was not reflected in colour measurements or sensory perception, suggesting that visual colour was maintained despite modest compositional differences. Given that tomato colour is influenced by multiple pigments and matrix interactions, this discrepancy is plausible and does not undermine the sensory findings. The carotenoid concentrations were broadly consistent with previous reports for ripe tomatoes, and the relative abundance of lycopene together with comparatively lower lutein and β-carotene concentrations aligned with established ripening-related carotenoid patterns in tomatoes31. Because freeze-drying may influence carotenoid stability through oxidation or isomerisation, lycopene data are interpreted comparatively across treatments, all of which were processed under identical conditions.

In summary, this study shows that incorporating FOGO compost was not associated with pronounced sensory differentiation between FOGO and Control tomatoes at harvest maturity (5 days postharvest, Time 1), but was associated with sensory differences after post-harvest storage (12 days postharvest, Time 2). Sensory differences at Time 1 were subtle and distributed across multiple attributes, whereas at Time 2 the FOGO tomatoes were more frequently described as Sweet and less frequently associated with undesirable eating-quality descriptors, although most individual attributes did not reach statistical significance. These sensory results coincided with the compositional differences observed at Time 2, including higher soluble solids, decreased malic acid concentrations, increased umami-associated amino acids, and distinct volatile profiles, while colour, antioxidant capacity, and overall visual quality remained comparable between treatments. However, no direct statistical relationship between sensory attributes and compositional parameters was assessed. Future studies incorporating correlation or multivariate approaches would be valuable to further explore these relationships. As this study was conducted as a commercially embedded field comparison, treatments were implemented under real-world production conditions; however, further multi-site and multi-season studies would be valuable to assess the broader generalisability of the observed effects. As measurements were not conducted at the point of harvest (day 0), early postharvest changes could not be directly assessed and should be considered when interpreting temporal effects.

Taken together, the findings suggest that post-harvest flavour-related composition may be an important factor contributing to the observed differences in sensory perception between treatments. This suggests that the use of circular organic inputs, such as FOGO compost, may be associated with sensory outcomes alongside their agronomic and environmental benefits under conventional commercial production conditions.

Methods

Tomato production and collection

Tomato plants (Solanum lycopersicum cv. Ninja) were cultivated on a commercial farm in the Stanthorpe region, Queensland, Australia, on sandy granite-derived soils under standard grower practice. The study was designed as a commercially embedded comparative assessment, in which all rows received identical baseline fertilisation and crop management, with the sole experimental contrast being the addition of FOGO compost to a designated treatment row.

The field experiment was part of a commercial tomato farm. The field had 110 m long rows with 3 m spacing between rows, ~37 cm raised planting beds and 40 cm spacing between plants. According to the farm’s practice, all rows (control and FOGO compost) received the same amount of NPK granular mineral fertiliser prior to planting, liquid multitrace starter (Nutrien, Ag Solutions) at planting and regular fertigation (including calcium nitrate and potassium sulfate) throughout the growing season to support an expected tomato yield of ~8 kg/plant ( ~ 10 tonne/ha). In accordance with the practice at this farm, all rows (control and FOGO compost) received a small amount of mushroom compost produced from spent mushroom substrate (0.37 kg/plant, 100 kg/row) before planting as soil conditioner. The research centred on FOGO compost and the FOGO treatment row receiving an additional 600 kg FOGO compost per row (2.2 kg/plant). Compost use is not currently standard practice in Australian soil-based tomato production, motivating this study. The chemical composition of the compost is provided in Table S5 and fell within ranges characteristic of these materials.

FOGO compost was incorporated into the top 20 cm of soil using a tractor-mounted rotary hoe before transplanting (Figure. S4). Aside from compost addition, all agronomic practices (irrigation, pest management, fertilisation regime) were identical across rows to isolate the effects of FOGO compost on the food quality of tomatoes.

Tomatoes were harvested at commercial maturity (¾ colour stage12) during routine farm harvest. To account for spatial variability within rows, fruit were collected evenly along the full length of each row and pooled by treatment. Samples from each treatment contained fruit from 30–40 plants to account for potential within-site-variability (total of ~20 kg per treatment), and transported to The University of Queensland. Fruit were stored at 20 °C under natural light for five days to allow ripening to full red colour (Time 1; 5 days postharvest), at which point 13 tomatoes per treatment were randomly selected for sensory and chemical analysis. While this sample size is consistent with similar experimental studies, it represents a relatively limited number of fruit and should be considered when interpreting the results. The remaining fruit was stored for an additional seven days under the same conditions and re-analysed at Time 2 (12 days postharvest). For analytical measurements, each biological replicate corresponded to a single fruit. As a result, replicates were derived from a mixed field collection rather than from predefined independent sampling points. While this approach provides a representative sample of commercially harvested fruit and decreases localised sampling bias, it limits the independence of biological replicates at the field level. Accordingly, the treatment row is considered the primary experimental unit, and the results are interpreted with this in mind. A schematic overview of field setup, sampling, and post-harvest handling is provided in Figure. S5.

Sensory analysis

Tomatoes were randomly selected from each treatment group, washed under hygienic conditions, and halved longitudinally. Tomatoes from each treatment were pooled, and individual portions (approximately one-eighth of a tomato) were randomly selected from multiple fruits to account for fruit-to-fruit variability. One-half of each tomato was allocated to sensory evaluation and the other to chemical analysis. For sensory testing, tomatoes were cut into uniform pieces, with one-eighth of each fruit served to panellists.

Samples were labelled with random three-digit codes generated by sensory software and presented according to a randomised block design to minimise order and carry-over effects. A total of 47 healthy, untrained panellists (aged 18–50 years; 28 female, 22 male) from diverse ethnic backgrounds participated in the sensory evaluation at Time 1. Due to participant availability, 41 panellists completed the sensory evaluation at Time 2.

Panellists first completed a check-all-that-apply (CATA) questionnaire assessing appearance, aroma, and taste attributes (Table S6), followed by a paired preference test comparing Control and FOGO samples. Sensory evaluation was conducted at both Time 1 and Time 2 under controlled conditions with standardised instructions.

Reagents and chemicals

Ultrapure water (18.2 Ω) was prepared on-site using a Milli-Q purification system (Millipore, North Ryde, NSW, Australia). High-purity solvents, including acetonitrile (HPLC-grade, 99.9%), ethanol (HPLC-grade, >99.8%), and methanol (HPLC-grade, >99.9%) were obtained from Merck (Frenchs Forest, NSW, Australia). Sodium chloride (99.5%) was also obtained from Merck (Frenchs Forest, NSW, Australia), and formic acid (98%) from Rowe Scientific (Lonsdale, SA, Australia). The following chemicals were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (Darmstadt, Germany): acetic acid (glacial, >99.9%), acetone (ACS reagent, 99.5%), α-aminobutyric acid (98%), l-ascorbic acid ( > 98%), butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT, >99%), citric acid (99%), 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH, >90%), dl-dithiothreitol (DTT, 99%), ethyl acetate ( > 99.8%), ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid tetrasodium salt (EDTA, 99%), ferrous sulfate heptahydrate (FeSO₄·7H₂O, >99.5%), 9-fluorenylmethyl chloroformate in acetonitrile (FMOC reagent), Folin-Ciocalteu reagent, formic acid (98%), gallic acid, hydrochloric acid (6 M, ACS reagent grade), iron (III) chloride (FeCl₃, >97%), iodoacetic acid in NaOH (used as alkylating agent), lithium hydroxide monohydrate ( > 98%), lycopene ( > 98%), malic acid ( > 99%), 3-sulfanylpropionic acid in borate buffer, metaphosphoric acid (MPA, 99%), norvaline ( > 98%), o-phthalaldehyde in 3-sulfanylpropionic acid (OPA reagent), petroleum ether (boiling point 40-60 °C), propionic acid ( > 99.5%), pyroglutamic acid ( > 98%), sarcosine ( > 98%), a set of seventeen amino acid standards ( > 98% each), sodium acetate ( > 99%), sodium azide (NaN₃, >99.5%), sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃, >99.5%), sodium hydroxide ( > 95%), sodium phosphate dibasic (Na2HPO4, >99%), sulfuric acid (H2SO4, ACS reagent, 95.0%), tert-butyl methyl ether ( > 99%), 2,4,6-trimethylpyridine (99%), 2,4,6-tripyridyl-s-triazine (TPTZ, >98%), Trizma base ( > 99.9%), and Trolox ( > 97%).

Sample preparation and extraction

Tomato samples were homogenised using a high-speed stick mixer (Cuisinart, China). An aliquot (0.5 g) of the homogenate of each biological replicate was subsampled and stored at −80 °C for volatile compound analysis. The remainder of the homogenate was freeze-dried using an Alpha 1–2 LSC freeze dryer (Martin Christ, Germany), then ground into a homogeneous powder using a TissueLyser II (QIAGEN, Tokyo, Japan) and stored at −80 °C for subsequent chemical analysis.

For methanolic extractions, 0.5 g of homogenised tomato powder (n = 6) was extracted with 80% methanol (v/v), sonicated for 15 min (Soniclean 160TD, Australia), and centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min (Heraeus Multigue X1R, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The supernatant was filtered through 0.45 µm mixed cellulose ester filters and stored at −80 °C until further analysis.

Physiochemical analysis

Instrumental colour parameters (L*, a*, b*) were measured on whole tomatoes (six replicates per treatment) using a handheld colour reader (FRU Precise Colour Reader, ShenZhen Wave Optoelectronics). Five measurements per fruit were averaged.

pH was measured potentiometrically (TPS Pty Ltd, Australia). Total soluble solids (TSS; °Brix) and titratable acidity (TA) were measured on homogenised samples diluted 100-fold using a pocket Brix-Acidity Meter (PAL-BX | ACID F5, Atago, Japan). Moisture content was determined gravimetrically according to AOAC methods32.

Volatile compound analysis

Volatile compounds were analysed by headspace solid-phase microextraction gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (HS-SPME-GC-MS) using a Shimadzu TQ8050NX, following Ma et al. 33 with minor modifications. Homogenised tomato pulp (5 g, n = 4) was placed in 20 mL headspace vials with 2,4,6-trimethylpyridine (10 µL, 10 mM) as internal standard. Volatiles were extracted using a DVB/CAR/PDMS fibre, desorbed at 250 °C, and separated on an SH-Rxi-5Sil MS column. Acquisition mode was conducted under multiple reaction monitoring mode (MRM), and compounds were tentatively identified using retention indices and the Shimadzu Smart Aroma Database, a database consisting of approximately 500 compounds. Data processing was performed using LabSolutions Insight (v4.0 SP6) and statistical analysis on the web-based platform MetaboAnalyst. Peak areas were normalised to the internal standard and expressed as relative abundance (% of total normalised peak area).

Organic acids

The lyophilised tomato powder was extracted by adding 2 mL of 10 mM H2SO4 to 0.5 g of tomato powder, briefly vortexing, and then centrifuging for 5 min at 15,000 rpm. The supernatant was collected, and the extraction process was repeated 2 more times. The tomato extracts were filtered through a 0.45 µm × 25 mm Mixed Cellulose Esters (LabCo Scientific Pty Ltd, Brendale, QLD, Australia) filter before injection. Organic acids were quantified using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) equipped with a diode array detector (DAD) at 210 nm. Separation was performed using an Agilent HiPlex H column (H + 8%, 300 × 7.7 mm) and a Phenomenex Security Guard Carbo-H column (Security Guard Carbo-H, 4 × 3 mm) guard column using the method described by Wood et al.34. Identification and quantification were achieved using authentic standards.

Ascorbic acid

l-Ascorbic acid, dehydroascorbic acid, and total ascorbic acid were quantified using a modified method of Spínola et al.35. Briefly, the freeze-dried and ground tomato powder was extracted using an extraction solution containing 3% metaphosphoric acid (MPA), 8% acetic acid, and 1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA). l-Ascorbic acid (l-AA) was quantified directly after buffering and acidification, while dehydroascorbic acid (DHAA) was determined following DTT reduction of extracts. Samples were filtered (0.22 µm GHP) prior to analysis. Separation was performed by UHPLC-PDA on a Waters Premier HSS T3 column (150 × 2.1 mm, 1.8 µm) at 25 °C using 0.1% formic acid in water and acetonitrile as the mobile phases (0.2 mL/min; 5 µL injection), with detection at 245 nm. Quantification was achieved using external calibration with authentic ascorbic acid standards (2–95 mg/L).

Amino acids

Free amino acids were quantified by HPLC with pre-column derivatisation following established methods36,37. Briefly, freeze-dried tomato powder (~20 mg) was acid-hydrolysed (6 M HCl containing 0.1% phenol, 110 °C, 16 h), neutralised, centrifuged and filtered (0.22 µm). Digested samples were spiked with internal standards and analysed by HPLC-FLD using an Agilent Zorbax Extend C18 column (4.6 × 150 mm, 3.5 µm) with OPA and FMOC pre-column derivatisation. Amino acids were separated using phosphate buffer and acetonitrile/methanol/water mobile phases and detected fluorometrically under established conditions. Quantification was performed using external calibration with mixed amino acid standards (1.95–500 µM).

Total phenolic content and antioxidant capacity

Total phenolic content was determined using the Folin-Ciocalteu assay following the method from Ainsworth and Gillespie and expressed as mg gallic acid equivalents per g dry weight (mg GAE/g DW) using external calibration38. Antioxidant capacity was assessed using FRAP39 and DPPH radical scavenging assays40. FRAP was conducted using acetate–TPTZ–FeCl₃ reagent with absorbance measured at 593 nm and results expressed as mmol Fe(II) equivalents per g dry weight. DPPH radical scavenging activity was determined spectrophotometrically in cuvettes, with absorbance measured at 520 nm and results expressed as mg Trolox equivalents per g dry weight (mg TE/g DW). Quantification was based on external calibration using gallic acid, FeSO₄ and Trolox standards, respectively.

Carotenoids

Carotenoids (lycopene, lutein and β-carotene) were quantified by HPLC-PDA following established extraction protocols for tetraterpenoids41,42. Briefly, freeze-dried tomato powder (0.5 g) was sequentially extracted with acetone and methanol:ethyl acetate:petroleum ether (1:1:1, v/v/v, containing 0.1% BHT) until colourless, combined extracts were dried under nitrogen, reconstituted in MTBE:methanol (1:1, v/v), and analysed using HPLC-PDA on a C30 carotenoid column with MeOH–MTBE–water gradient elution (1.2 mL/min, 15 °C, 10 µL injection). Carotenoids were monitored at their characteristic wavelengths and quantified using authentic external standards. Given the susceptibility of carotenoids to oxidation and isomerisation during processing, concentrations are interpreted comparatively across treatments, all of which were processed under identical conditions.

Statistical analysis

Unless otherwise stated, chemical analyses were performed with n = 4 biological replicates per treatment per time point; instrumental colour was measured on n = 6 fruit per treatment per time point. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. For physicochemical and compositional measurements, two-way ANOVA (factors: treatment and time) was used to test main effects and interactions, followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparisons test where appropriate (GraphPad Prism 10). Volatile profiles were analysed in MetaboAnalyst. Peak areas were normalised to the internal standard, and data were log10-transformed and Pareto-scaled prior to partial least squares discriminative analysis (PLS-DA).

Statistical analysis of the sensory analysis was conducted in R. CATA attribute citation frequencies were compared between treatments within each time point using Chi-square tests (α = 0.05). Multivariate relationships among samples were explored using principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) on a Jaccard distance matrix computed from the binary CATA responses, and attribute associations with the ordination were assessed by correlating attribute responses with PCoA coordinates.
