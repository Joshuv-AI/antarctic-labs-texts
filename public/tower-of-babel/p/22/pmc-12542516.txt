Epidemiological analysis of human papillomavirus infection in senior women: a hospital-based retrospective study
Authors: Ming Chen, Yue Wang, Binhua Dong, Yuhang Zhang, Lulu Feng, Huachun Zou, Ye Li, Yuxuan Huang, Guanxiang Huang, Xiaodan Mao, Yafang Kang, Shuxia Xu, Hao Lin, Pengming Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCervical cancer (CxCa) is associated with the persistent infection of various high-risk types of Human Papillomavirus (HPV). Currently, the infection rate of HPV and the incidence of cervical lesions in senior women remain significant, becoming a new focus of public health and clinical medical research.MethodsIn this study, we focused on the population of senior women aged 60 years and older, enrolling a cohort of 2,566 participants from the Fujian Maternity and Child Health Hospital for an epidemiological analysis. We characterized the baseline demographics of the participants using descriptive statistics and frequency distributions. To assess differences in infection rates, we utilized chi-square (χ2) tests for larger sample sizes and Fisher’s exact tests for smaller groups. Furthermore, logistic regression was applied to investigate the relationships between various independent factors and the persistence of infection.ResultsOur research found a 37.6% (966/2,566) HPV infection rate among women aged 60 years and older, with 92.9% of cases attributed to High-Risk HPV(HR-HPV). There was a significant downward trend in HR-HPV positivity with age (p < 0.001). And a waning vulnerability to persistent HPV infection, suggesting reduced susceptibility to persistent infections in senior women, though this did not achieve statistical significance, indicating a need for further investigation. Patients with CxCa in this population, HPV-16 was the predominant genotype, followed by HPV-52. This pattern contrasted with that observed in women with Low-grade Squamous Intraepithelial Lesions (LSIL) or High-grade Squamous Intraepithelial Lesions (HSIL). Specifically, HPV-52, HPV-53, HPV-18, and HPV-45 infections were associated with a lower risk of HSIL relative to HPV-16. Moreover, HPV-58 and other genotypes were linked to a significantly lower CxCa risk, underscoring the genotype-specific dynamics in cervical cancer development. Furthermore, the study identified a significant prevalence of HPV-81 and other Low-Risk HPV(LR-HPV) types in CxCa and HSIL, suggesting a potential role in disease progression that requires further investigation.ConclusionOur findings align with prior studies, indicating a consistent relationship between age and HPV infection rates among senior women. We observed that the persistence of HPV, along with the incidence of HSIL and CxCa, was not significantly influenced by age. Introduction

The “Global Cancer Statistics Report 2020” indicates that the incidence and mortality rates of Cervical Cancer (CxCa) are on a double rise, posing a serious threat to women’s life and health. Persistent infection with Human Papillomavirus (HPV) is an important risk factor for various cancers, mainly CxCa. Persistent infection with high-risk HPV (HR-HPV) is the main etiological factor for malignant tumors of the cervix [1, 2]. Although HPV infection is more common in younger women, recent studies have found that the epidemiological characteristics of HPV infection in the elderly female population are also worthy of attention [3]. Especially among senior women over the age of 60, the dynamics of HPV infection, the chances of persistent infection, and the relationship with CxCa have become new focal points in the field of public health and clinical medical research [4]. Globally, although the incidence of CxCa in senior women varies across different countries, the mortality rate is high. The standardized mortality rate for CxCa in women over 60 years old is 6.9 per 100,000, and it is as high as 28 per 100,000 for those over 70 years old [5]. With increasing age, the treatment effect and prognosis of CxCa are poor, and 9.91% of senior women experience delayed treatment due to late detection [6]. At the same time, with the intensification of the aging population process and the extension of life expectancy, the importance of addressing health issues in the elderly population is highlighted, and CxCa screening for senior women cannot be ignored [7].

According to the “Guidelines for Cervical Cancer Screening in China” released in 2023 [8], HR-HPV nucleic acid testing is recommended as a primary screening method for cervical cancer and has been gradually incorporated into routine laboratory testing. Screening starts at age 25, and women aged 25 to 64 are advised to undergo HPV nucleic acid testing every 5 years or cytology testing every 3 years. For women over 65, screening can be discontinued if they have had adequate negative screening results in the past. This underscores the necessity for tailored screening strategies for senior women, particularly those over 65.

Good screening strategies and preventive HPV vaccination can effectively reduce infections caused by vaccine-targeted HPV genotypes and significantly reduce the incidence of HPV-related cancers [9, 10]. Therefore, investigating the prevalence and distribution of HPV genotypes in specific populations is crucial for formulating HPV vaccine strategies for specific regions [11]. However, the current upper limit for HPV vaccination is 45 years old, and routine CxCa screening is usually conducted before the age of 65, leaving a gap in CxCa prevention for senior women, especially those over 45 and particularly over 65 [12]. Moreover, existing studies mostly focus on women under 65, especially those under 60, which makes our understanding of the epidemiological characteristics of HPV infection in senior women insufficient, and this lack of data limits the ability to formulate targeted prevention strategies for women in this age group.

This study is based on the HPV data from women who attended Maternal and Child Health Hospital in Fujian Province, China. By analyzing the HPV infection data of female patients aged over 60, the study aims to thoroughly investigate the epidemiological characteristics of HPV infection in senior women and elucidate the potential age-related differences. The findings not only provide valuable data support for managing HPV infection in senior women, formulating regional screening strategies, and evaluating the effectiveness of HPV vaccines, but also help to fill the gap in the existing literature regarding the characteristics of HPV infection in elderly women. Moreover, the study holds significant implications for optimizing CxCa prevention measures and improving the health of the senior female population.

Materials and methods

Participants and study design

This retrospective study was conducted at Fujian Maternity and Child Health Hospital between February 2012 and December 2019. The study included women aged above 60 years who had undergone at least one HPV test as part of their routine gynecological evaluations. Participants who met the following exclusion criteria were excluded from the study: 1) pregnancy or breastfeeding, 2) history of total hysterectomy or partial hysterectomy, and 3) receipt of radiotherapy or chemotherapy treatment. Cervical HPV testing involved HPV genotyping, and women were eligible for inclusion regardless of their cytological testing results.

A total of 2,566 women were included in the baseline epidemiological research, as shown in Table 1. Among them, 612 women had valid pathological results. Among the 192 women who tested positive for HPV at baseline, a subset had at least one follow-up visit with repeat testing after 6 months. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Fujian Maternal and Child Health Hospital.

Cervical specimen collection

Cervical cells were obtained from the cervical canal of all participants using plastic brushes. The collected cells were then placed into two separate vials: one containing 20 mL of ThinPrep® PreservCyt® solution (Hologic Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) for cytological analysis, and the other containing 2 mL of preservation solution for HPV DNA testing. Specimens intended for cytology were stored at 4 °C.

HR-HPV DNA genotyping test

The HR-HPV genotyping test was performed using the PCR-RDB HPV genotyping assay for 23 types (Yaneng® Biosciences, ShenZhen, China) [13]. This assay can identify 18 h-HPV types (16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 53, 56, 58, 59, 66, 68, 73, 82, and 83) and 6 Low Risk (LR-HPV) types (6, 11, 42, 43, and 81) in cervical exfoliated cells. The assay utilized the L1 consensus HPV PGMY09/PGMY11 primer set [14] to amplify 5 µL of extracted HPV DNA or control (positive or negative) in a 24-µL reaction system. All detection procedures were performed according to the manufacturer’s instruction provided by the kit. HPV DNA Extraction: The cervical cell suspension was centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 10 min. The pellet was resuspended in 50 µL of lysis buffer, heated at 100 °C for 10 min, and then centrifuged again at 13,000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant containing the extracted DNA was used for PCR amplification. The amplification process involved thermal cycling with the following conditions: 50 °C for 15 min, 95 °C for 10 min, denaturation at 94 °C for 10 s, annealing at 45 °C for 90 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s, repeated for a total of 40 cycles. After amplification, HPV genotyping was performed by hybridization and RDB (reverse dot blot) on strips that were fixed with 23 different type-specific probes. Positive results were indicated by blue spots on the strip, which could be visually determined.

Histopathology

The auto-imaging system (Hologic, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) was used for Thinprep Cytologic Test (TCT). The cytological results were classified according to the Bethesda system [15, 16]. Specimens were classified as: Negative for Intraepithelial Lesion or Malignancy (NILM); Atypical Squamous Cells of Undetermined Significance (ASC-US), Low-grade Squamous Intraepithelial Lesion (LSIL); Atypical Squamous Cells, not possible to exclude High-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (ASC-H); High-grade Squamous Intraepithelial Lesion (HSIL); Squamous cervical cancer; Atypical Glandular Cells (AGCs); and Adenocarcinoma In Situ (AIS). In clinical practice, the referral for colposcopy and histopathological examination is guided by the 2012 National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) Cervical Cancer Screening Guidelines [17]. The collected specimens were fixed in 10% formalin and underwent standard processing for paraffin embedding. Subsequently, 4-µm-thick histological sections were cut and stained with hematoxylin and eosin using standard protocols. Then evaluated by two experienced pathologist according to the standard of the 2014 World Health Organization (WHO) Classification of Tumors of the Female Genital Tract and the Cervical Intraepithelial Neoplasia (CIN) system [18]. Discrepancy specimens were re-evaluated by another pathologist until the consensus was reached.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 25.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, New York, USA). The baseline characteristics of the study population were analyzed and presented as frequencies or means with standard deviations (SDs). Infection rates were compared using χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests. Logistic regression analysis was conducted to investigate the associations between independent variables and persistence. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study population

A total of 2,566 women participated in this study, with a mean age of 64.96 years (SD: 4.954, range: 60–92 years). In order to analyze the characteristics of HPV infection in the elderly in different age groups more carefully, and provide the basis for formulating more accurate prevention and screening strategies. The participants were divided into three age groups: 60–64 years old (mean age: 61.7 ± 1.402), 65–70 years old (mean age: 66.51 ± 1.329), and ≥ 70 years old (mean age: 74.26 ± 3.925).

HPV prevalence and genotype distribution

All 23 HPV genotypes were detected (Figure S1). The overall HPV infection rate in this population was 37.6% (966/2,566), encompassing both HR-HPV and Low-Risk (LR-HPV) infections. Among these infections, the vast majority, specifically 92.9% (897/966), were attributed to HR-HPV, with the remaining 7.14% (69/966) being attributed to LR-HPV. The prevalence of single-type infection was 16.7% (428/2,566), while multi-type infection was observed in 21.0% (538/2,566) of the participants (Table 1). Among the cases of multi-type infections, the most prevalent HPV types were HPV-52, HPV-58, and HPV-16. The HR-HPV infection rates in the different age groups were as follows: 38.3% (569/1,486) for 60–64 years old, 34.3% (231/673) for 65–69 years old, and 23.8% (97/407) for ≥ 70 years old (Table 2). There was a significant age-related decrease in HR-HPV positivity (χ2 = 36.469; p < 0.001). Similar trends were observed for both single and multiple infections (both p < 0.001). Figure 1 illustrates the prevalence of HR-HPV in the overall population, which was found to be 35.0% (879/2,566). Among HPV-positive senior women, the three most common genotypes were HPV-52, HPV-16, and HPV-58. These three types accounted for the majority of multi-type infections, highlighting their significant role in the context of multiple infections. The remaining genotypes, in descending order, were HPV-53, HPV-81, HPV-51, HPV-56, HPV-33, HPV-42, HPV-68, HPV-18, HPV-43, HPV-66, HPV-35, HPV-59, HPV-31, HPV-11, HPV-39, HPV-45, HPV-73, HPV-83, HPV-82, and HPV-6 (Fig. 1A). Similarly, among the age groups of 60–64 and 65–70 years old, the five most common infected genotypes were HPV-52, HPV-58, HPV-16, HPV-53, and HPV-81. However, in the age group of ≥ 70 years old, HPV-68 replaced HPV-81 among the five most commonly infected genotypes. Notably, the infection rate of HPV-16 showed the most pronounced decrease with increasing age in the elderly population, while HPV-52 and HPV-58 did not exhibit the same age-related trend (Fig. 1B).

HR-HPV infections in patients with different cervical lesions

The histopathology results were categorized as Low-grade Squamous Intraepithelial Lesion (LSIL), High-grade Squamous Intraepithelial Lesion (HSIL), and CxCa. Among the senior women included in this study, 80.1% (490/612) were diagnosed with LSIL, 15.2% (93/612) with HSIL, and 4.7% (29/612) with CxCa (Table 1). Among them, the infection rate of HR-HPV in elderly women diagnosed with LSIL was 41.0% (207/490), in those with HSIL was 86.0% (80/93), and in those with CxCa was 82.8% (24/29) (Fig. 2A). The prevalence of HSIL and CxCa decreased with age in different age groups, although the differences were not statistically significant (Table 2).

Similar to the overall population, the three most common HPV genotypes in women with LSIL and HSIL were HPV-52, HPV-16, and HPV-58, however in women with CxCa, HPV-16 was the most prevalent genotype, followed by HPV-52 and HPV-81 (Fig. 2B-2).

Furthermore, Table 4 demonstrates that there were no statistically significant differences were found in the risk of HSIL or CxCa among different age groups. Women infected with HPV-52, HPV-53, HPV-18, and HPV-45 had a significantly lower risk of HSIL compared to those infected with HPV-16. However, the differences were not statistically significant for the risk of CxCa. Notably, women infected with HPV-58 and other genotypes had a significantly lower risk of CxCa.

Discussion

Currently, epidemiological studies on HPV infection and cervical lesions in senior women are relatively scarce, and research on the characteristics of HPV infection and its impact on CxCa in women over 60 years old is particularly rare, leading to the health needs of this key population often being overlooked [19]. Our study reveals a concerning phenomenon: although the mortality rate of CxCa increases with age, the number of senior women participating in HPV testing has significantly decreased. This contradiction points out the current deficiencies in the management of HPV infection and cervical lesions in senior women. Therefore, this study selects senior women visiting the Fujian Provincial Maternity and Child Health Hospital in China as the research subjects, aiming to deeply analyze the epidemiological characteristics of HPV in senior women and explore potential risk differences related to age. Through this study, we hope to provide a scientific basis for the current CxCa screening strategies, especially references for whether the age boundary for screening needs to be adjusted, in order to better serve the senior women population and improve the prevention and management effects of CxCa.

In our study, the overall prevalence of HPV infection in this population was 37.6%, which is significantly higher than the reported rate of 20.57% in Fujian Province [13]. Specifically, the prevalence of HR-HPV infection among HPV-positive patients was 92.9%. The higher infection rates may be attributed to cohort effects related to age-related decline in immunity or reactivation of underlying HPV infections [20]. Additionally, it could be influenced by bias resulting from research conducted in hospital populations. Previous studies have reported that senior women are less likely to undergo screening compared to younger women, which may contribute to the observed bias [21]. Our previous research has shown a bimodal trend in HPV infection rates with age [13]. Building on this foundation, this study further investigated the population of women aged 60 and above, employing a detailed age-stratified analysis to uncover distinct epidemiological profiles of HPV genotypes and their differential risks associated with cervical cancer across various age groups within the senior cohort.

While there are some interregional differences in the relative prevalence of HPV genotypes in China, we found that the most common genotypes among senior women in Fujian Province (HPV-16, HPV-58, and HPV-52) were consistent with those observed in other regions of China [22, 23]. And we found that HPV-52, HPV-58, and HPV-16 were the most prevalent types in cases of multi-type infections. This finding underscores the complexity of HPV infection dynamics in senior women and highlights the need for targeted screening and management strategies, especially in populations with high prevalence of multiple infections. However HPV-52 and HPV-58 did not have the clear downward trend like HPV-16 with age [10, 24]. The findings align with those of Yi B et al. [25], suggesting that the dynamics of HPV-52 and HPV-58 infections may differ from those of HPV-16. This highlights the need for tailored management strategies for these specific genotypes, particularly in the elderly population.

Our study’s findings indicate a notable prevalence of HPV-81, a LR-HPV genotype, among CxCa patients. This observation, coupled with the detection of HPV 43 in a small percentage of CxCa cases and the presence of HPV 6, HPV 42, HPV 43, and HPV-81 in a proportion of HSIL cases, highlights the frequent co-infection of LR-HPV types with HR-HPV. While these LR-HPV types are generally linked to benign or low-grade lesions, their detection in HSIL and CxCa cases indicates a potential role in disease progression that necessitates further exploration. The co-occurrence of LR-HPV with HR-HPV in patients raises questions about whether this co-infection might affect the pathogenicity of HR-HPV or alter its mechanisms of disease. However, the impact of LR-HPV co-infection on the pathogenicity of HR-HPV and whether it changes the disease mechanism remains unclear and requires further observation and mechanistic studies. Our results highlight the importance of investigating the role of LR-HPVs, especially HPV-81, in CxCa development, hinting that their impact could be more considerable than previously acknowledged. There is a possibility that these LR-HPV types could contribute to cervical cancer progression, through mechanisms that remain to be fully understood.

Persistent HR-HPV infection plays a crucial role in the development of cervical malignancy. Although there is no consensus on the definition of HPV persistence, most studies define it as a minimum duration of 6 to 12 months [26]. Although most women infected with the virus become negative within 2 years, women with persistent HR-HPV infections are at greatest risk for developing CxCa [27]. Because persistence of HPV infection is more likely to progress into CIN or CxCa, we elected to monitor HPV infection persistence at 12 and 24 months. We found that HPV persistence at 12 and 24 months’ time point was independent of the age factor. In addition, we compared the ORs for the occurrence of persistent infection across different HPV genotypes and found no significant difference in persistent infection of each genotype.

The proportion of HSIL and CxCa cases was significantly higher in our study. However, we found that unlike HPV infection, the prevalence of HSIL and CxCa was not significantly correlated with age. Although it might be related to sample size distribution bias, it did not preclude the need to consider raising the screening age cut-off to 70 years. HPV-16, the most prevalent HR-HPV genotype, was also the most common genotype among senior women with CxCa in our study. This finding is consistent with previous studies indicating that HPV-16 single subtype infection is a major contributor to cervical precancerous lesions (CPLs) and CxCa [28]. We also observed a significantly lower risk of HSIL in women infected with HPV-52, HPV-53, HPV-18, and HPV-45 compared to those infected with HPV-16. These observations suggest that while HPV-16 is a dominant factor in CxCa, other genotypes also play significant roles, and the dynamics of HPV infection in cervical cancer development may involve both single and multiple infections. Future research should explore the specific contributions of different HPV genotypes, including the potential role of multiple infections in the progression to CxCa. Additionally, the risk of CxCa was significantly lower in women infected with HPV-58 compared to HPV 16 infection. Although HPV-18 is known to be highly prevalent and persistent among Colombian women [29], we did not observe significant persistence of HPV-18 in our study. These variations in results may be attributed to regional differences and variations in study methodologies. For instance, a systematic review and meta-analysis of HPV prevalence in vaccinated and unvaccinated women globally found that the prevalence of non-vaccine HR-HPV types, including HPV-18, varied significantly across different regions [30]. In China, a study in Changsha revealed that HPV-18 accounted for 10.06% of cervical cancer cases among screened women aged 35 to 64 [31], which is lower than the global average of 15.7% [30]. This suggests that the prevalence and impact of HPV-18 may vary significantly across different regions. Future research should explore the specific contributions of HPV-18 and other high-risk types to cervical cancer development, considering regional and global data to better understand these differences.

There was study found that among all investigated strategies, HPV screening once every 5 years with vaccination was the most cost-effective strategy and should be the prioritized strategy for CxCa prevention in China. This study provides valuable epidemiological data on HPV infection and cervical lesions in senior women above 60 years of age, a population that has not received HPV vaccination. These findings can serve as useful control data for implementing effective HPV infection management, developing regional screening strategies, and evaluating the effectiveness of HPV vaccines. However, it is important to acknowledge the limitations of this retrospective hospital-based study. The study population was derived from a single hospital, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other regions or populations. Additionally, the follow-up period was relatively short, which may not be sufficient to fully assess the long-term persistence of HPV infections and their progression to cervical lesions. Future research with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up periods, including community-oriented population research or prospective studies, is warranted for further analysis of the elderly population. Another significant limitation is that we did not consider the vaccination history of the enrolled participants regarding HPV vaccination. This omission prevents us from analyzing how prior HPV vaccination may affect the epidemiological characteristics of different HR-HPV genotypes and their associated cancer risks in the elderly population. Understanding the impact of vaccination on HPV prevalence and its relationship with cervical cancer outcomes is crucial for developing effective public health strategies. In China, the coverage of HPV vaccination remains relatively low, falling significantly short of the WHO 2030 target [32]. Given the current situation, it is crucial to explore more effective approaches to improve vaccination coverage and tailor strategies to specific populations. This underscores the necessity for personalized vaccination strategies that consider individual risk factors and medical history. Our study provides valuable insights into the epidemiological characteristics of HPV infection in older women and can provide a theoretical basis for screening and vaccination strategies for older women.
