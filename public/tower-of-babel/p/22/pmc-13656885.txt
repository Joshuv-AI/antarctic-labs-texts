Variation on Toxic and Inflammatory Potential of Coal Smoke of Different Sources From a Region With High Lung Cancer Risk
Authors: Yongliang Zhang, Wei Hu, Nathanial Rothman, Dingyu Liu, Jihua Li, Jun Xu, Bofu Ning, Roel Vermeulen, Qing Lan, Flemming R. Cassee, George S. Downward
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTExcess lung cancer risk in Xuanwei and Fuyuan, China, has been attributed to household air pollution from use of a locally sourced smoky (bituminous) coal. However, the carcinogenic mechanisms remain unclear. Given the important role of inflammation in lung cancer development, this study compared the proinflammatory potentials of the particulate phase of smoky coal emissions with those of a locally sourced smokeless (anthracite) coal, which poses a lower lung cancer risk. A co‐culture model of lung epithelial (Calu‐3) and macrophage (differentiated THP‐1) cells was exposed to aerosolized particulates, followed by analysis at 24 h exposure (R0) and after 24 h of recovery (R24). Cytotoxicity was minimal, with lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release consistently under 15% of the total LDH content. Particulate exposure reduced transepithelial electric resistance (TEER) at R0, with partial recovery by R24, indicating sustained epithelial barrier damage. IL‐6 release occurred at R0 in a dose‐dependent manner but normalized by R24 for most samples. No significant IL‐8 release was observed. The inflammatory and toxic potential (TEER reduction and IL‐6/IL‐8 changes) were similar across coal types and ventilation conditions. The observed potencies showed no correlation with polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) content and the observed lung cancer risks of the coal samples. In summary, transient inflammation and lasting barrier damage were observed with little contrast between different coal samples. As these results do not explain the variation of lung cancer risks, further studies examining alternative cellular pathways and biological effects are needed to elucidate the mechanisms driving lung cancer in this population.

Introduction

Household air pollution (HAP), generated from the domestic usage of solid fuels, such as biomass, coal, and kerosene, contributed to as many as 3.2 million pre‐mature deaths worldwide in 2020, among which 6% were lung cancer [1]. In Xuanwei and Fuyuan, China, the domestic combustion of smoky coal (a locally sourced bituminous coal) has been linked to some of the highest lung cancer rates in the world, especially among non‐smoking women [2, 3, 4]. Previous exposure assessment studies from the region have revealed that individuals using smoky coal experience higher levels of fine particulate matter (PM) and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) compared to smokeless coal (a locally sourced anthracite coal and is of lower risk for lung cancer) [5, 6, 7]. Recent epidemiological analyses have suggested that exposure to PAHs, especially methylated PAHs, was the most likely driving agent for lung cancer in these regions [7, 8]. However, despite the growing evidence regarding environmental exposures, the biological pathways driving the etiology of lung cancer in this population have not been well understood.

Inflammation has been suggested to play an important role in carcinogenesis, from tumor initiation to metastasis [9, 10, 11]. Results from in vivo and in vitro studies suggest that fine particulate matter (PM2.5) and its bound parent and substituted PAHs may lead to cytotoxicity and inflammatory responses, including elevated interleukin (IL)‐6 [12, 13, 14]. Earlier studies in Xuanwei have also revealed polymorphisms in innate immune genes associated with an increased risk for lung cancer in Xuanwei [15]. It has also been reported that exposure to smoky coal emissions led to changes in six immune markers in plasma [16].

The mechanistic investigations for carcinogenesis in the lung often involve studies in which in vitro lung models have been used and researchers have utilized samples of PM to approximate exposures. Filter‐based sampling for PM provide one approach to collect materials for toxicological testing but are limited in that there is a risk of losing chemical components, especially (semi‐) volatile compounds, during the extraction, drying, and resuspension steps that are required before toxicity testing can be performed. By contrast, the versatile aerosol concentration enrichment system (VACES), which is being increasingly utilized in studies of PM, can preserve the physical and chemical properties of PM much better while also effectively concentrating collected aerosols, though the collection efficiency drops when particle size drops below 50 nm [17, 18, 19, 20, 21]. However, to the best of our knowledge, the coal combustion products from Xuanwei and Fuyuan are seldom being tested for their biological impacts in in vitro studies.

To achieve more realistic exposures, air‐liquid interface (ALI) exposures were used to overcome the limitations of traditional submerged exposures. This approach allows particles to reach cell models via deposition of aerosolized particles, mimicking exposures happening in the lung and reducing the uncertainty in submerged settings, where not all particles in the suspension reach the cells and potential physiochemical changes when interacting with cell culture media [22, 23].

The main hypothesis of the current study was that a major difference can be observed in the toxic and inflammatory potential of combustion products of coal from different sources using different stoves. These differences are expected to be modulated by the ventilation conditions of the stoves from which PM was collected. As a secondary objective, we tested how the response of lung cells to PM would differ when collecting PM using the VACES system versus the traditional way on filters.

Materials and Methods

Sample Collection

Samples of HAP were collected via a series of water boiling tests (WBT) described elsewhere [24]. For each WBT, a pot containing 2.8–3.5 L of water (16°C–25°C) was quickly brought to boil (95°C) before being left to simmer for 45 min in a traditional Xuanwei kitchen using coal from different sources and different stoves. Ten smoky and two smokeless coal samples from coal mines throughout Xuanwei and Fuyuan were used to reflect geographic variation in lung cancer rates [25]. These coal samples were collected recently from 2010 to 2017 and supplemented by samples collected in the 1980s to evaluate temporal changes in coal characteristics as parameters such as mining depth changed. Two stoves were used for the WBTs, including a traditional unventilated firepit and a pre‐existing ventilated stove with a dedicated chimney, to reflect the approximately 50% reduction in lung cancer rates among those who adopted improved cookstoves [2]. One coal sample from a region with high esophageal cancer (outside Yunnan Province, China) was used as an external comparison and herein referred to as “Linxian coal”.

During each WBT, household air samples (coal combustion products) were collected with the VACES system and Teflon filters. For VACES sampling, air was drawn through a VACES system at a flow rate of 33 L/min where it was saturated by warm water vapor, condensed by cooling before concentrated by passing through a virtual impactor and collected as aqueous suspension, particles larger than 2.5 μm within the air stream were lost by impaction and deposition to the walls of the inlet and saturator [21]. The aqueous suspensions (herein referred to as water‐based samples) were then frozen and stored at −80°C. Household air samples were also collected onto 37 mm Teflon filters via a Harvard Low Volume Impactor with an aerodynamic diameter cutoff of 10 μm and a flow rate of 3.3 L/min. All the equipment was calibrated and maintained after each WBT. Air samples from Teflon filters were extracted via immersion in 2 mL of methanol (HPLC grade, Sigma) in a petri dish and sonicated in a water bath (Sonorex RK‐52, 60/120 KW, 35 KHz, Bandelin, Berlin, Germany) for 1 min on each side. This process was repeated three times before the extracts from one filter were pooled into a 4 mL cryogenic vial, dried overnight at 30°C and weighed before being stored at −80°C (herein referred to as filter‐based samples). The filters were also weighed before and after extraction to calculate the total collected mass of PM as well as the extraction efficiency.

Co‐Culture Model

Human bronchial epithelium Calu‐3 cells (ATCC, Rockville, US) were cultured with minimal essential medium (MEM) + GlutaMAX (Gibco, US), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, US), 1% non‐essential amino acid (NEAA, Gibco, US) solution and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Pen/Strep, Gibco, US). Human monocyte cell line THP‐1 cells (ATCC, Rockville, US) were cultured in RPMI‐1640 medium, supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% Pen/Strep. Both cell lines were maintained in an incubator with 95% humidity, 5% CO2, and 37°C (ThermoFisher, Germany). THP‐1 cells were differentiated into THP‐1‐derived macrophages (TDMs) by addition of phorbol 12‐myristate 13‐acetate (PMA, 30 ng/mL) for 24 h and harvested with Accutase (Gibco, US) for co‐culture seeding.

In vitro lung models were prepared as per a previous study, with slight modifications [23, 26]. Briefly, at 80% confluency, the Calu‐3 cells were enzymatically detached with 0.05% Trypsin–EDTA (ThermoFisher, US) from the culture flask and seeded (0.5 mL 2.5 × 105 cells/mL) onto the apical side of 12 mm transwell inserts (0.4 μm pore size, 1.12 cm2 surface area of polyester membrane, Corning Inc., Germany) with 1.0 mL of culture medium added to the basolateral side for nutrient supply. After two‐weeks (14 days) of submerged culture to achieve confluency, the apical medium was removed for one‐week (7 days) for culture at the ALI. The culture medium was refreshed every 2–3 days and the apical side of the insert was only washed (with culture medium) to remove waste when refreshing medium. After one week of ALI culture, the basal medium was replaced with low‐serum MEM supplemented with 1% FBS, 1% NEAA and 1% Pen/Strep. In addition, TDM cells (0.1 mL, 1.4 × 105 cells/mL) were seeded on top of Calu‐3 cells and co‐cultured for 24 h to allow attachment.

Air‐Liquid Interface Exposure

Prior to exposure, the extracted PM samples from Teflon filters were resuspended in Milli‐Q water to 0.5–1.0 mg/mL using probe sonication (Branson digital Sonifier 250, Marshall Scientific LLC.) with amplitude at 10% for 15 min in an ice water bath. The water‐based samples were sonicated using the same setup as filter‐based samples. The particle size distributions of the filter‐based and water‐based samples were determined using dynamic light scattering (DLS) method (Zetasizer Ultra, Malvern Panalytical Ltd.).

ALI exposure was performed using the VITROCELL Cloud Exposure system (Vitrocell, Waldkirch, Germany) at 37°C. The exposure chamber of the system houses 8 cavities for 12 mm inserts and one cavity for a microbalance to determine the particle deposition. Filter‐based and water‐based samples were aerosolized into the exposure chamber via ultrasonic nebulization, resulting in a dense cloud of suspension droplets sized 4.0–6.0 μm. The cells were exposed to the air samples via sedimentation of the suspension droplets, and the deposition doses were measured using the built‐in microbalance. Three doses were designed for each filter‐based and water‐based sample and are arbitrarily referred to as “low”, “medium” and “high” based on the ascending order of the deposition doses, the exact doses are presented later in the results. After exposure, the co‐culture models were cultured in an incubator for 24 h (herein referred to as “R0”), media samples were collected and the deposited air samples were washed off with fresh low‐serum MEM media. The cells underwent a further 24 h recovery (herein referred as “R24”) before media collection.

Trans‐Epithelial Electric Resistance (TEER)

The membrane barrier function of the co‐culture models was assessed before ALI exposure (background level, herein referred to as “B”), at R0 and at R24 by measuring the transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) with the EVOM2 Voltmeter with 4 mm chopstick electrodes (World Precision Instruments Inc., FL, USA) [23]. For these measurements, 0.5 mL low‐serum MEM media were added to the apical side of the inserts and left to stabilize for 10 min. After taking TEER measurements at R0 and R24, the inserts were incubated for another 30 min to harvest media samples from both the apical and basolateral chamber. All TEER values were corrected for the resistance of cell‐free inserts and insert surface area.

Lactate Dehydrogenase (LDH) Measurements

The leakage of total LDH in culture media was measured with an LDH cytotoxicity detection kit (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany). For this, 100 μL apical or basolateral media and 100 μL reaction reagents were added into 96‐well flat bottom plates and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 20 min, followed by adding 50 μL of stop solution (HCl, 1.0 M, Sigma, the Netherlands). All LDH values were corrected for the total amount of LDH in cells (LDHmax), which was collected by lysing cells of a co‐culture model in an insert with 2% triton X‐100 (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., the Netherlands) for 5 min. In addition, low‐serum MEM culture medium was tested as a blank control.

Cytokines

The inflammatory response of the in vitro lung models was evaluated by measuring cytokine levels, including IL‐6, IL‐8, IL‐10 and TNF‐α. At R0 and R24, both apical and basal media samples were collected and centrifuged at 150g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatants were stored at −80°C until analysis. The cytokine levels were measured using LEGENDplex Human Essential Response Panel (BioLegend Inc., US) according to the manufacturer's instructions, with concentration values derived using the LEGENDplex Data Analysis Software Suite (BioLegend Inc., US). Cytokine concentrations were assessed in duplicate, and the average values were presented.

Chemical Analysis

Chemical analysis of the filter‐based samples was described in an earlier publication [27]. Briefly, the extracted and dried PM samples were prepared with 1 mL of 1:1 dichloromethane:hexane and sonicated for 5 min, this process was repeated five times and the extracts from the same sample were pooled and subsequently concentrated under nitrogen and solvent exchanged to hexane to a final volume of 1 mL. Concentrated extractions were examined via gas chromatography tandem mass spectrometry to determine concentrations of 83 PAHs, and the quantity of each PAH was measured in nanograms (ng/g PM samples).

Statistical Analysis

For each of the filter‐based and water‐based samples, the Benchmark dose (BMD) together with 95% confidence intervals [lower limit (BMDL), upper limit (BMDU)] were estimated for TEER and cytokine responses using Power and Exponential models in the US EPA's BMDS tool (version 3.3) [28]. Results from Power models were preferentially reported, unless those models failed to converge or fit appropriately. To compare the overall potency between samples that were collected from the same type of coal (smoky coal vs. smokeless coal vs. Linxian coal), the original data were pooled to model the overall BMD, BMDL and BMDU. The derived BMDs were then used to compare the relative biological potencies of the different samples. Next, the BMDs were linked to Benzo[a]pyrene (BaP) equivalent concentrations, which are obtained by aggregating concentrations of 12 measured PAHs for filter‐based samples using potency equivalency factors as reported earlier [29]. In addition, to explore if the potency differences as represented by BMDs were associated with the observed lung cancer risks, the BMDs were plotted against the lung cancer risks as observed in human observational studies that showed large differences in risks (odds ratio ranged 7.49–33.40) between different coal mining deposits (representing the geologic variations of lung cancer risks) [25].

Tukey's Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) test was used to determine differences between different exposure groups and Kendall's non‐parametric tests were performed to examine if a monotonic trend relationship exists between response levels (cytokine production, TEER, and LDH) and exposure dosage. Tukey's HSD and Kendall's non‐parametric tests were performed in RStudio (version 4.2) [30, 31, 32].

Results

A total of 44 air samples collected during 25 WBTs were examined, 20 via Teflon filters and 24 via VACES (water‐based, Table S1). Of the four cytokines measured, IL‐10 and TNF‐α were below the limit of detection (LOD) for over 90% of the samples and therefore not reported. For 19 of the WBTs, filter‐based and water‐based samples were examined with full measurements for TEER, IL6, and IL8. Results of those samples are presented here, with the rest available in the supplements. Those 19 WBTs were performed in a traditional firepit, of which 16 were WBTs with smoky coal from different deposits, two were with smokeless coal, and one with Linxian coal.

LDH Content and Trans‐Epithelial Electric Resistance

The measured LDH levels for the filter‐ and water‐based samples were within 15% of LDHmax (Figures S1 and S2). Therefore, despite statistical differences for some samples, we conclude that no meaningful cytotoxicity is seen for those samples at the tested doses and the cell models were viable after exposure. For example, filter‐based samples from WBT SY9 (smoky coal from deposit 13) resulted in slightly higher LDH release as compared to the control group at R0 (p < 0.05), the LDH levels for the two exposed groups (1.19 and 1.72 μg/cm2) were both around 6% of LDHmax as compared to 3% for the control group.

In general, a reduction in TEER was observed at R0 after exposure to all the samples (Figures S3 and S4), irrespective of fuel sources and stove types. For 20 out of 21 water‐based samples from smoky coal, such reduction was seen in a monotonic dose‐dependent trend (p < 0.05, Table S2). Similar trends were also observed for 14 out of the 16 filter‐based samples from smoky coal and the three filter‐based and two water‐based samples from smokeless coal (p < 0.05). At R24, the reductions in TEER were partially recovered for both water‐based and filter‐based samples (Figures S3 and S4) and the trend reduction sustained for most of the samples. Reductions in TEER were observed for samples collected from WBT with firepit and ventilated stove. For example, water‐based samples from WBT SY1 and SY18 (smoky coal from deposit 9 tested in firepit and ventilated stove respectively, Figure S3) both reduced TEER at R0 in a dose‐dependent manner. These reductions lasted at R24 without showing monotonic dose response. Both filter‐based samples and water‐based samples from Linxian coal (WBT LX1) reduced TEER at R0 in a dose‐dependent manner, and these effects were attenuated at R24.

Overall, the BMDs for 10% reduction in TEER at R0 were slightly lower for samples from smokeless coal as compared to smoky coal (Table 3, 0.71 vs. 0.96 μg/cm2 for filter‐based samples and 0.97 vs. 1.35 μg/cm2 for water‐based samples), such differences were also observed at R24. However, these differences were non‐significant considering the overlapping confidence intervals. Variations in BMDs were observed when examining closer for air samples from smoky coal and smokeless coal from different sources (Figures 1, 2, and S5). For example, filter‐based samples from WBT SY1 and SY2 (smoky coal from deposit 9 tested in firepit) showed consistently lowest BMDs for TEER among filter‐based samples from smoky coal and lower than those from smokeless coal at R0 and R24. In addition, filter‐based samples from WBT SL1 (smokeless coal from deposit 20 tested in the firepit) had slightly lower BMDs than many other smoky coal samples at R0 and R24.

Cytokines

Exposure to all the water‐based samples and filter‐based samples increased IL‐6 secretion on the apical side immediately after exposure (at R0) in a monotonic dose‐dependent manner (Figures S6 and S7), these observations were also observed for IL‐6 secretion on the basal side at R0 (Results not shown). The increased IL‐6 secretion was attenuated after 24 h recovery (at R24) with monotonic trend effects observed for six out of the 20 filter‐based samples and 13 out of the 24 water‐based samples (Table S2).

For the filter‐based samples from smoky coal, the overall BMDs for increasing IL‐6 by 10% at R0 were 0.10 (0.07–0.17) μg/cm2, which is lower than those from smokeless coal 0.31 (0.13–1.22) μg/cm2 (Table 3). However, the water‐based samples from smoky coal resulted in relatively higher BMDs than smokeless coal [0.29 (0.22–0.40) vs. 0.19 (0.13–0.37) μg/cm2]. Such differences remained at R24. Variations in the BMDs for apical IL6 among samples from smoky coal were also observed, but they were not as prominent as those observed for TEER (Figures 1 and 2). The secretion of IL‐8 was also increased for some of the samples, although the effect was less consistent with that of IL‐6 (Figures S8 and S9). The BMDs for Linxian coal were 0.59 (0.36–1.96) μg/cm2 and 0.89 (0.24–2.45) μg/cm2 for filter‐based sample and water‐based sample respectively, both of which were relatively higher than those observed for samples from smoky coal and smokeless coal.

When comparing the 19 pairs of filter‐based and water‐based samples, the filter‐based samples seem to be more potent in increasing apical IL6 [0.10 (0.07–0.17) vs. 0.29 (0.22–0.40) μg/cm2] and apical IL8 [0.20 (0.14–0.50) vs. 1.18 (0.20‐Infinite) μg/cm2] as compared to water‐based samples (Table S3). While the reverse comparison was observed when examining the BMDs for smokeless coal, for example, the overall BMDs for apical IL6 were 0.31 (0.13–1.22) μg/cm2 and 0.19 (0.13–0.37) μg/cm2 for filter‐based and water‐based samples respectively. As with the stove comparison observed for TEER, the BMDs for 10% increase in apical IL6 were similar for samples from WBTs with firepit and ventilated stoves (Table S4).

Matching to BaP Content Within PM and Lung Cancer Risks

Twelve PAHs with available BaP potency equivalent factor [29] were detected in the filter‐based samples: 5‐methylchrysene, 7,12‐dimethylbenz[a]anthracene, benz[a]anthracene, benzo[a]pyrene, benzo[b]fluoranthene, benzo[j]fluoranthene, benzo[k]fluoranthene, chrysene, dibenzo[a,h]anthracene, dibenzo[a,e]pyrene, dibenzo[a,l]pyrene, and indeno[1,2,3‐cd]pyrene. These 12 PAHs were aggregated into BaP equivalent content (BaPeq). The derived BaPeq for the filter‐based samples was also applied to water‐based samples, considering PAH measurements for water‐based samples were not available.

When matching the calculated BMDs to the lung cancer risk profile as observed in population studies, the point estimates from filter‐based samples and water‐based samples do not correlate with the observed risks (Figure 3 panel A and C, Figure S10). Discussion

The current study represents one of the first in vitro studies examining the toxic and inflammatory potential of coal combustion products from Xuanwei and Fuyuan, sampled under various realistic indoor exposure settings and using water‐based (VACES) and filter‐based PM sampling techniques. For both smoky and smokeless coal samples, we observed decreased membrane integrity and pro‐inflammatory effects within a dose range of up to 6 μg/cm2, represented by decreased TEER, increased IL6 and IL8 production. We observed variations in membrane disruption and proinflammatory effects for samples from smoky coal of different sources. Potency differences between smoky coal and smokeless coal were noted, but such differences might be minor considering the overlapping confidence intervals. Stove ventilation also does not seem to influence the potency of PM in reducing TEER and increasing cytokine production. Filter‐based samples tend to have smaller BMDs than water‐based samples from coal of the same source, which can result from different PM chemical composition due to different sampling approaches. The BMDs for filter‐ and water‐based samples did not correlate with the observed lung cancer risks in this population nor with the BaP equivalent content within the PM samples. Collectively, the differences in toxic and pro‐inflammatory potential were rather limited and do not explain the major difference in lung cancer seen in observational studies from the regions where we have collected PM.

The integrity of the epithelial membrane is an important index for barrier functions, including in the lung and intestine. Reduction of membrane integrity as measured by TEER and pro‐inflammatory response as measured by IL6 secretion was observed in a dose‐dependent manner for coal combustion products in the current study. Those findings are in line with previous observations where coal mine dust exposure was associated with cell membrane damage and structural changes in tracheal wall and alveolar [33], which may direct or indirectly be linked to (at least partially) reduced membrane integrity. The observed reduction in TEER in the current study may also have reciprocal interactions with the observed inflammatory response. Inflammatory responses would regulate tight junctions between epithelial cells and increased inflammation would lead to increased barrier permeability or loss of the barrier function, which in turn would allow foreign chemicals to pass the epithelial membrane and the infiltration of inflammatory cells from the circulation, possibly leading to an amplification of the inflammation in the lung.

Activated nuclear factor kappa B (NF‐κB) promotes the production of pro‐inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF‐α, IL‐6, IL‐1β), chemokines, and growth factors. These molecules create a pro‐tumorigenic microenvironment. As discussed above, we observed increased production of IL‐6 and IL‐8 following exposure to the coal smoke samples, while IL‐10 and TNF‐α levels remained unchanged. This pro‐inflammatory response may suggest the involvement of reactive oxygen species mediated activation of the NF‐κB pathway, in which the IκB kinase (IKK) phosphorylates the inhibitor protein IκB, triggering its degradation and enabling NF‐κB (p65) to translocate to the nucleus and drive the downstream IL‐6 and IL‐8 production [34, 35, 36]. The absence of TNF‐α and IL‐10 may suggest that the activation of NF‐κB was sufficient for IL‐6 and IL‐8 production, but did not reach the threshold to activate broader cytokine responses. The pronounced IL‐6 and IL‐8 response has potential implications for carcinogenesis, as chronic inflammation and NF‐κB driven cytokines can promote DNA damage, cell proliferation, and tumorigenic signaling. However, it is important to note that only these four cytokines were measured, which limits our ability to fully substantiate the proposed mechanism.

The observed pro‐inflammatory responses are also in line with previous studies where inflammatory responses were also linked with exposure to coal‐combustion related air pollutants, including the NF‐κB/NLRP3 pathway, which regulates IL6 and IL8 production and polymorphisms in immunity genes [37, 38]. Coal fly ash, an industrial byproduct of coal combustion, has been shown to induce oxidative stress—a known trigger for inflammatory responses. Notably, the concentration of PAHs reported in those studies was approximately one fifth to half of those in the samples analyzed in our current study [27, 39].

Experimental data further supports this, both coal mine dust and coal fly ash have been shown to increase IL6 and IL8 secretion in Calu‐1 cells and alveolar macrophages (MH‐S) [38, 40, 41]. In addition, coal fly ash was found to enhance nitric oxide production and initiate inflammatory responses via NF‐κB and MAPK signaling pathways, including activation of NLRP3, ERK, JNK, and p38. In population‐based studies, polymorphisms in immunity genes were linked to increased or decreased susceptibility for lung cancer depending on phenotypes, including genes for chemokine, complements and cytokines in Xuanwei and Fuyuan [15, 42].

Coal emissions have a complex chemical composition, including PM, quartz, organic carbon, and PAHs (and its derivatives). We have previously reported abundant particle‐bound PAHs in coal smoke from this area, including BaP, which is known for its mutagenic and carcinogenic potency [7, 27, 43]. Earlier studies indicated that air pollutants' PAH and alkylated PAH components were associated with DNA damage, cytotoxic effects, and oxidative stress [44]. Exposure to BaP can lead to inflammatory responses, including promoting IL‐1, IL‐6, and CXCL‐13 secretion. The 7,12‐dimethylbenz[a]anthracene (DMBA), a group I carcinogen found in the emission of smoky coal, was also reported to increase cytokine production [45]. Recent research emphasizes the role of aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) in PAH‐driven lung carcinogenesis. AhR activation may upregulate immune‐regulatory factors and contribute to a pro‐tumorigenic microenvironment, including non‐genomic activation of EGFR, genomic activation of NF‐κB, promotion of angiogenesis, etc. [46] Given the abundant PAHs within coal emissions, further investigations into AhR signaling in this context are warranted.

In the current study, combustion products from smoky and smokeless coal increased production of IL‐6, while the differences between samples from these coals were minor. IL‐6 and IL‐8 are important cytokines for PAH‐associated inflammatory responses and toxicity. In the current study, we observed a dose‐dependent pro‐inflammatory response, such pro‐inflammatory effects were eased after removing the particles from the cells for a 24‐h recovery. However, the BMDs for TEER, IL‐6, and IL‐8 were also not associated with BaP content or BaPeq content in samples within these two types of coals, indicating similar potencies between smoky and smokeless coal [27]. In an earlier case–control study, six plasma cytokines were differentiated by up to two‐fold between smoky coal users and smokeless users, including chemokines, cytokines, and complements [16]. However, such differences did not seem to be as large as the lung cancer risk differences between these two groups. The concentrations of plasma IL‐6 and IL‐8 were also similar between groups using different coals in this region, which seem to support our current findings where null differences of BMDs were observed for apical IL‐6 and IL‐8 between different coal samples.

The sampling approach plays an important role in the hazard assessment of environmental pollutants. Sampling via VACES has several advantages in that it limits extra steps in sample preparation and is assumed to be able to preserve active chemicals while also concentrating air samples. However, in the current study, the BMD results indicated that findings from filter‐based samples did not discriminate significantly from water‐based samples. One possible explanation is smaller particles (< 50 nm) were not captured efficiently when sampling with VACES. An additional potential explanation is that the type of samples collected here consisted of relatively high levels of hydrophobic chemicals in both filter‐based samples and water‐based samples, making it challenging to prepare a homogenous resuspension upon exposure of lung cells. Despite the differences in dosages, the observed effects among filter‐ and water‐based samples were similar and very modest for the different PM samples.

The current study has several strengths. First, samples were collected in a consistent manner, while still reflecting “real world” practices. We examined a variety of fuel sources, aiming to explore the real‐world variations in exposures. Second, different sampling approaches were used attempting to minimize losses of active compounds during downstream preparation steps. However, we are also bound by limitations; first, despite the co‐culture model being well established and suitable for use in ALI exposures, Calu‐3 cells are very resilient with well‐formed tight junctions and the production of thick mucus which may limit their sensitivity. Second, the BMD modeling suggested estimates that were outside the tested concentrations. While this is commonly regarded as a benefit of using BMD, it may also result in unreliable estimations where training data is lacking. Third, the tested doses were not fine enough to better capture the biological effective doses in the in vitro models that could improve BMD modeling. Finally, both sampling techniques used in the current study may not fully represent the chemical background of coal emission people are exposed to in the study region considering filter‐based samples had extensive extraction and preparation steps and water‐based sampling is less effective in capturing smaller particles.

In conclusion, samples of coal smoke showed similar toxic and inflammatory potential (represented by decreased TEER and increased IL‐6 and IL‐8) in the current study, regardless of coal source or ventilation conditions. The adverse effects (lung cancer) of exposure to indoor coal smoke PM may be a result of persistent inflammation which in turn is triggered by pro‐inflammatory mediators and impaired epithelial barrier function. We did not see major differences in the potency of PM due to stove operating conditions or the type of coal used despite the observed marked chemical differences [27]. The observed differences in membrane toxicity and immunotoxicity between coal samples do not match the large contrast in lung cancer rates in population studies, meaning that alternative biological pathways may be involved. We only noted marginal difference in effect on lungs cells between PM collected directly in water versus on filters which does not support the idea that filter based PM collections may result in loses of potent chemicals that would otherwise not occur when using water‐based sampling methods. Alternatively, losses of the chemicals that are causing lung cancer may have occurred for both PM sampling strategies, which would argue in favor of performing direct online exposure using ALI exposure systems that can continuously expose lung cells to freshly generated smoke at levels representative for the actual exposure levels.
