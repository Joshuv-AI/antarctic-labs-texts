Experimental performance and economic analysis of finned solar receiver for parabolic dish solar collector
Authors: Senthil Kumar Vishnu, Ramalingam Senthil
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe solar receiver is a vital component of concentrated solar collectors that absorbs solar radiation and converts it into heat. One of the challenges the research community faces is minimizing heat loss from the receiver at higher temperatures to maximize the thermal performance of parabolic dish collectors and achieve the system's cost-effectiveness. Cavity receivers have a complex design that makes them more challenging to manufacture and entails higher costs for improved thermal performance. Implementing innovative receiver designs is essential to maximize the absorption of solar radiation and minimize heat losses. In this experimental study, a cost-effective solar receiver was fabricated with fins to study heat transfer. The solar receiver is examined using water as heat transfer fluid with three flow rates of 0.097 kg/s, 0.125 kg/s, and 0.152 kg/s. The residence time of water is increased by adopting integrated fin receiver designs. The provision of fins in the solar receiver enhances heat transfer by increasing the turbulence in the fluid flow and results in higher thermal efficiency. The average energy and exergy efficiencies are 67.81 % and 8.93 %, respectively, with a 0.152 kg/s flow rate. At the highest water flow rate (0.152 kg/s) considered in this study, a lesser heat loss of about 3776.2 W occurred due to the effective heat transfer. The cost metrics, like levelized cost of electricity, net present value, and the payback period, are about 0.21 $/kWh, 923 $, and 3.38 years, respectively, at 0.152 kg/s flow rate. The proposed solar receiver produces optimal thermo-economic performance and lower initial investment for steam generation than other receiver designs. The current experimental study's findings could benefit the entire solar industry by presenting an effective solar receiver design for solar collectors.Keywords: Solar energy, Parabolic dish, Solar receiver, Fins, Energy and exergy efficiency, Economic analysis

Introduction

The steadily increasing demand for energy is being witnessed worldwide. Environmental concerns about using fast-depleting fossil fuels have also necessitated the adoption of renewable resources to meet our energy needs. Effectively utilizing solar energy could help address the energy crisis and mitigate environmental pollution [1]. Solar collectors play a significant role in providing thermal energy through concentrating and non-concentrating collectors [2]. Thermal storage with solar collectors helped minimize the demand and supply gap [3]. Non-concentrating collectors are deployed for low-temperature applications, while concentrating collectors are utilized for high-temperature applications. In concentrating collectors, solar rays are focused on the receiver. The present experimental study focuses on utilizing solar radiation to meet energy needs in an endeavor to work towards the United Nations' Sustainable Development Goals 7 (Affordable and Clean Energy) and 13 (Climate Action). Parabolic dish collectors (PDC) deliver higher temperatures and collector efficiency than other concentrating collectors [4]. The PDC performs effectively regarding energy and exergetic factors because of high concentration ratios [5]. The PDC concentrates sunlight at a fixed focal point. The receiver's aperture is significantly smaller than the PDC, which aids in achieving the maximum concentration ratio and higher heat generation [6]. PDC-based Stirling engine produced power to drive prime movers [7,8].

Loni et al. [9] investigated cubical, cylindrical, and hemispherical solar receivers with a PDC. The hemispherical receiver with nanofluids enhanced thermal performance by about 12 %. The thermal efficiency of cubical receivers was about 65.1 % [10]. Compared to cylindrical, rectangular, spherical, and conical receiver systems, the cylindrical-conical solar receiver design yielded a thermal efficiency of 67.95 % at 300 °C [11].

Soltani et al. [12] investigated a cavity receiver with a PDC and reported an enhanced thermal performance of about 65 %. Avargani et al. [13] examined a PDC and a cavity receiver with a spiral base, and the receiver performed well under a realistic non-uniform solar flux in windy climatic conditions. Sinha and Gulhane [14] investigated a three-coil cavity receiver and a PDC, concluding that convection was responsible for up to 97 % of heat losses. Eteraf et al. [15] demonstrated a thermal performance enhancement of 11.92 % using a cavity receiver with a glass cover. Kurşun [16] investigated sinusoidal-shaped fins and found that the maximum attained temperature increased from about 66 °C to 164 °C. The focus change of the cavity receiver increased the hot water temperature by about 20 °C [17].

Zheng et al. [18] studied natural convection by increasing the length and number of aluminum alloy fins. They observed an enhanced heat transfer by adding fins. The spiral flow path receiver with fins improved thermal efficiencies as the fluid's residence time was longer [19]. The solar receiver with perforated rectangular fins produced exergy efficiency of about 59.21 % [20]. Li et al. [21] investigated the geometrical parameters of square parallel fins. The parallel fin geometry decreased surface fouling. Karami and Kamkari [22] investigated the conversion efficiency of perpendicular shell-and-tube heat exchangers. A heat exchanger with perforated fins was 7 % faster than solid fins. Researchers investigated various solar receivers for PDC [23], conical cavity receivers [24], and trough collectors [25]. A significant issue is the heat loss from solar receivers at higher receiver temperatures.

From an extensive review of available literature, the performance of solar receivers is effectively improved by increasing solar rays’ concentration, using thermal storage, and employing rectangular, annular, longitudinal, spiral, perforated, plate, and radial fins. Several solar receiver designs were investigated, including square, spherical, conical, and hemispherical geometries. Most studies have only discussed the thermal performances of different cavity receivers, which are more challenging to fabricate and involve a higher investment outlay. Vengadesan et al. [25] investigated a flat-surface rectangular solar receiver with zigzag fins for parabolic collectors. The heat transfer fluid (HTF) passed horizontally at the line focus absorber. Several studies established that solar receivers with fins enhance heat transfer. However, the fin orientation and perforations vary in the fluid flow path to increase residence time, and such aspects have not been investigated much by researchers so far. Improving heat absorption in the receiver is crucial for maximizing energy conversion.

Earlier studies did not use fins to investigate the point-focus type flat-surface receiver for PDC with an improved flow path and increased fluid residence time. HTF flow upwards against gravity along with discontinuous fins and point-focus absorber with the PDC was not investigated much in earlier studies. The present experimental study investigates the thermo-economic performances of a flat-surface receiver with discontinuous rectangular fins to improve flow path, fluid residence time, and heat exchange. The receiver with fins increases surface area and improves the turbulence (better mixing of fluid), resulting in more heating of the passing fluid. Solar receivers come in different shapes; cavity receivers are challenging to fabricate and have a shorter HTF residence time. This study focuses on a flat-surface receiver with rectangular fins for improving thermal performance. The receiver geometry is simple to fabricate. Flow turbulence is enhanced by the improved HTF flow mix resulting from placing the receiver's fins, which raises heat transfer. Finned solar receivers can increase fluid residence time. HTF is passed to the receiver at flow rates of 0.097 kg/s, 0.125 kg/s, and 0.152 kg/s to analyze the PDC's performance. The parameters studied are Nusselt number (Nu), convective heat transfer coefficient (CHTC), heat loss coefficient (HLC), energy efficiency, and exergy efficiency. The primary results obtained are compared with earlier research studies.

The present experimental work is structured comprehensively and summarized in four sections. Section 1 provides the background and needs for solar receiver designs that offer optimal performance. Section 2 deals with experimental research methodology and the calculations for the thermo-economic performance assessment. Section 3 details the present study's significantly enhanced thermo-economic performance analysis. Section 4 summarizes the important conclusions derived from the study and provides recommendations for further extending the study.

Materials and methods

Experimental work

The experiment setup consists of a PDC of 16 m2 aperture and a finned solar receiver. The selected PDC facility is tested at a site location with a latitude of 12° 59′ N and longitude of 79° 58′ E. This Scheffler-type PDC consists of about 900 solar-graded mirrors. The PDC is constructed using an oval-shaped hardened steel frame. Water is the selected HTF because of its availability and good thermal properties. The HTF storage tank has a capacity of 50 L, and a hot water pump for recirculation. Solar receiver is kept at a fixed focus. Scheffler-type parabolic dish reflector is tracked by the two-axis tracking, which operates on a gear mechanism for daily tracking and a slot and lever mechanism for seasonal tracking. The black paint-coated receiver is kept at a focus of 2.75 m and the dish reflector tracks the sun. Solar receiver does not move during solar tracking and is kept at 13° tilt to harness maximum solar rays. The actual aperture area varies 14.8–16 m2 with the sun declination angle. Experimental procedure

Outdoor experiments are conducted at three mass flow rates. A control valve is used to regulate the fluid flow rate. HTF inlet, outlet, and ambient temperature are measured using K-type thermocouples. A digital infrared laser thermometer with a temperature range of −50 °C–550 °C (an accuracy of ±0.5 °C) is used to measure the receiver's surface temperature. A weather station records solar radiation, wind speed, and ambient temperature. Flow rate is measured using a digital flow meter. HTF circulation pipes are insulated using glass wool of 50 mm thickness to minimize heat losses. Experimental investigations were conducted in April 2023. Hot water slightly causes scaling on inner surfaces after prolonged usage. This scaling can be removed using selective cleaning methods to decrease the receiver's flow resistance and improve heat exchange. The readings are measured and recorded every 10 min. Results and discussion

The experiments are conducted over five days with three flow rates: 0.097 kg/s, 0.125 kg/s, and 0.152 kg/s. Solar radiation, wind speed, inlet, outlet, ambient, and receiver surface temperatures are recorded during outdoor experiments. The measured solar radiation and ambient temperature range from about 400 to 1000 W/m2 and 30–37 °C, respectively. Useful heat transferred to HTF, heat loss, CHTC, overall HLC, Nu, energy efficiency and exergy efficiency are evaluated and discussed.

Water temperature

Water is circulated to the receiver using a pump. The storage tank is emptied daily and refilled with water at room temperature. For the performance calculation, the inlet, outlet, ambient temperature, and solar radiation are measured at an interval of 10 min. Fig. 5(a-c) show the ambient temperature and solar radiation variation at the selected flow rates of 0.097 kg/s, 0.125 kg/s, and 0.152 kg/s, respectively. The maximum outlet temperature of about 99 °C is recorded at 0.152 kg/s flow rate. The peak water outlet temperature at 0.097 kg/s and 0.125 kg/s flow rates is about 88.5 °C and 95 °C, respectively. A peak solar radiation is recorded only at noon. The fluid temperature increases throughout the test because of constant recirculation until the boiling point. Heat transfer is maximized by more solar radiation on the receiver and quick heat absorption by water. Tests are conducted for water to reach its boiling point. The receiver with fins enhances the fluid residence time, creates turbulence, and increases heat transfer [41].

Variation of solar radiation and ambient temperature with time for flow rates: (a) 0.097 kg/s, (b) 0.125 kg/s, (c) 0.152 kg/s.

Fig. 6(a-b) show the HTF inlet/outlet temperatures, fluid temperature difference and heat transfer over time for three selected flow rates of 0.097 kg/s, 0.125 kg/s, and 0.152 kg/s, respectively. The increasing inlet temperature also increases the HTF outlet temperature with increasing solar radiation. Water temperature rises progressively till midday. Therefore, greater HTF circulation is needed to bring water to a boil. Hence, the time taken for the HTF to boil decreases by adding fins and higher receiver surface temperature [42]. Heat transfer and temperature difference are about 12725 W, and 28 °C at 0.152 kg/s. The values are higher than other flow rates. Similarly, the maximum heat transfer is about 6498 W, and 9101 W using 0.097 kg/s and 0.125 kg/s flow rates, respectively.

Variation of: (a) Outlet temperature, (b) Heat transfer with temperature difference at selected flow rates.

At the higher flow rate, the less time is taken to reach the boiling point. Hence, heat transfer is determined based on HTF temperature difference. An increased HTF temperature difference indicates increased heat transfer [43]. A higher flow rate increases the HTF outlet temperature and enhances heat transfer with fins in solar receivers.

Heat loss

Receiver heat loss includes convective and radiative heat losses from wind and radiation. Fig. 7(a–c) indicate heat loss and HLC at different flow rates of 0.097 kg/s, 0.125 kg/s, and 0.152 kg/s, respectively. The peak heat loss is 5817.2 W, 5464.3 W, and 4896.7 W at 0.097 kg/s, 0.125 kg/s, and 0.152 kg/s flow rates. Similarly, the average heat loss is about 4728.3 W, 4548.6 W, and 3776.2 W for 0.097 kg/s, 0.125 kg/s, and 0.152 kg/s, respectively. Thus, an increased flow rate decreases heat loss. The HLC is determined based on heat loss.

Heat loss and HLC for flow rates: (a) 0.097 kg/s, (b) 0.125 kg/s, (c) 0.152 kg/s.

Fig. 8 shows the average heat loss and HLC for three flow rates. The HLC depends on heat loss. The average HLC is 34.5 W/m2K, 33.2 W/m2K, and 28.5 W/m2K at 0.097 kg/s, 0.125 kg/s, and 0.152 kg/s flow rates, respectively. So, the HLC peaks at higher heat loss. Hence, an increased fluid flow rate reduces heat loss and the HLC. In the absence of wind, radiative heat loss outweighs convective loss. The insulated receiver's back surface reduces conduction loss. The receiver absorbs the maximum possible radiation because of high absorbance and low emissivity [44]. A low-iron tempered glass will help prevent intense winds from directly contacting the receiver's incident surface.

Average heat loss and HLC variation with flow rates.

For flow rates of 0.097 kg/s, 0.125 kg/s, and 0.152 kg/s, the maximum CHTC is about 170.3 W/m2K, 323.9 W/m2K, and 451.2 W/m2K, while the Nu is 5.09, 9.6, and 13.4, respectively. The maximum CHTC and the Nu are obtained at the highest flow rate. The Nu is calculated using CHTC due to increased solar radiation on the receiver.

Energy and exergy efficiencies

The receiver is fitted with fins to extend the HTF's residence time and expand heat transfer area to maximize convective heat transfer—fins generate turbulence to enhance heat transmission from the surface to water. Fig. 11(a-c) indicate energy and exergy efficiency variations for selected flow rates of 0.097 kg/s, 0.125 kg/s, and 0.152 kg/s, respectively. The energy and exergy efficiency figures are noted to have a similar trend. The maximum energy and exergy efficiencies are about 41.2 %, 71.3 %, and 87.3 %, and 5.7 %, 9.2 %, and 16.4 % at the respective flow rates of 0.097 kg/s, 0.125 kg/s, and 0.152 kg/s.

Thermal efficiency and exergy efficiency for: (a) 0.097 kg/s, (b) 0.125 kg/s, (c) 0.152 kg/s flow rates with time.

Fig. 12 shows the average energy and exergy efficiencies are about 67.81 % and 8.93 %, at the flow rate of 0.152 kg/s. This is a higher value than the other flow rates. Similarly, at flow rates of 0.097 kg/s and 0.125 kg/s, the average energy and exergy efficiencies are 31.2 %, 3.23 %, and 49.42 %, 5.14 %, respectively. It shows that an increased water flow rate enhances heat transfer, improves energy and exergy efficiencies by adding fins to a solar receiver. Compared to energy efficiency values, exergy efficiency is incredibly low. The high exergy concentration of the sunlight input and the temperature differences between HTF's inlet and outlet temperatures are two probable causes of exergy destruction in the solar collector [46]. At a lower flow rate, the HTF absorbs more heat initially, but the HTF recirculation is lower. This could affect the receiver's thermal efficiency at lower flow rates. Further, heat transfer is done quickly at a higher flow rate, and HTF recirculation is much higher than the lower flow rates.

Economic analysis

The economic analysis is carried out for cost assessment to determine profitability by examining the values of financial indicators, such as levelized cost of electricity (LCOE), NPV, and PBP for the solar receiver at three flow rates. The calculated LCOE for power generation from PDC receivers is compared to other benchmark energy prices. The LCOE values for 0.097 kg/s, 0.125 kg/s, and 0.152 kg/s flow rates are 0.189 $/kWh, 0.197 $/kWh, and 0.21 $/kWh, respectively. Similarly, the NPV is 414 $, 654 $, and 923 $ for respective 0.097 kg/s, 0.125 kg/s, and 0.152 kg/s flow rates. The 0.152 kg/s flow rate is optimal based on economic considerations. The discount rate, lifetime equipment cost, and capital cost salvage values in LCOE and NPV calculations are on par with the available literature [55]. Fig. 14 indicates NPV and LCOE for the three flow rates. The PBP is evaluated using the PDC with receiver feasibility analysis [56]. The PBP of the PDC is also calculated based on economic analysis. The flow rate of 0.152 kg/s has the lowest PBP of about 3.38 years calculated. At flow rates of 0.097 kg/s, and 0.125 kg/s, the PBP is recorded to be about 5.3 and 3.36 years, respectively.

LCOE and NPV variation with flow rates.

The economic analysis reveals that the finned solar receiver at a 0.152 kg/s flow rate is a cost-effective investment since the predicted profits exceed the capital costs, as indicated by the maximum NPV and shorter PBP [57]. As per ASHRAE and ASTM test standards, the current study is limited to producing hot water up to 100 °C. Future studies on steam generation could focus on improving solar receiver designs for multi-phase flows. The design of the finned solar receiver investigated here performs well from thermal and economic perspectives. Using treated water in the finned design could have long-term effects of minimizing corrosion and fouling inside the receiver. However, using periodic cleaning methods like a chemical or high-pressure jet ensures that the solar receiver's heat exchange surface is free from fouling. Evaluating the cost-effectiveness and long-term viability of flat-surface solar receivers in PDC and improving system's thermal efficiencies before considering large-scale deployments is essential. Further research can focus on adaptive control strategies, tracking algorithms, and optimization models to achieve maximum solar energy utilization, improved reliability, and efficient operation under varying weather conditions.

Conclusion

The present experimental study examines the thermal performances of a finned flat-surface receiver with a PDC with three fluid flow rates of 0.097 kg/s, 0.125 kg/s, and 0.152 kg/s. The experiments are carried out under actual weather conditions. The proposed solar receiver design with fins enhances the HTF's residence time and creates more turbulence, resulting in higher heat transmission. The parameters analyzed based on experimental data are heat transfer, heat loss, energy efficiency, and exergy efficiency. Also, the economic analysis of the PDC is made.
