Spinal Castleman disease: An imposter–A rare case from Ethiopia
Authors: Fasikawit Kinde Getu, Taye Jemberu Robele, Abebe Mekonnen Woldeyohannes, Tadesse Mitiku Alemu, Milkias Befekadu Balcha, Seid Mohammed shobe, Worku Furi Kabeto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Castleman disease is a rare lymphoproliferative disorder that presents significant diagnostic challenges, particularly when involving the spine. Its radiologic features often mimic more common pathologies such as lymphoma, meningioma, metastatic disease, and infection like spinal tuberculosis—conditions frequently encountered in Ethiopia. We report a case of a 62-year-old Ethiopian female who presented with progressive paraparesis. MRI revealed a homogeneously enhancing epidural mass with mildly expansile posterior vertebral elements involvement at the T4 level showing diffusion restriction on DWI/ADC. Differential diagnoses included lymphoma, Plasmacytoma and metastasis. Histopathologic analysis following laminectomy and excision of the epidural mass confirmed hyaline-vascular Castleman disease. Spinal Castleman disease remains a diagnostic imposter, especially in resource-limited settings. Awareness of its imaging features and mimickers is essential to avoid misdiagnosis and guide appropriate management.

Introduction

Castleman disease is a rare, lymphoproliferative disorder first described by Benjamin Castleman in the 1950′s [1,2]. Based on anatomic distribution and number of lymph node involved and presence or absence of systemic symptoms, it can be classified as unicentric (UCD)or multicentric disease (MCD) which also differ in clinical manifestation, imaging appearances, treatment options, and prognosis [3]. UCD typically manifests as a solitary enlarging mass or confined to a single lymph node or a localized cluster of nodes within one nodal station. Most patients with UCD are asymptomatic, only discovered after development of compression-type symptoms. MCD, on the other hand, involves multiple lymph node regions and is frequently accompanied by systemic manifestations and organ impairment [4,5]. UCD is more common and has been reported to occur in younger individuals than MCD [6,5,7].

Histologically, it is classified into hyaline-vascular, plasma cell, and mixed cellular variant [6,5]. The hyaline-vascular type accounts for 85%-90% of cases [8,9] and it is the predominant histologic subtype (70%) in unicentric Castleman disease (UCD) [1]. The plasma cell variant is more commonly seen in multicentric Castleman disease, while the mixed cellular type is rare.

A systematic review involving 404 patients with unicentric Castleman disease (UCD) identified the most commonly affected anatomical regions as the mediastinum (29%), neck (23%), abdomen (21%), and retroperitoneum (17%) [8,10]. Extranodal involvement—especially of the central nervous system (CNS)—is exceptionally uncommon [8]. Spinal Castleman disease is even more uncommon, with fewer than 50 cases reported globally according to authors literature review. Radiologically, such lesions often resemble more prevalent pathologies including lymphoma, Plasmacytoma, multiple myeloma, meningioma, metastatic disease, and spinal tuberculosis, complicating diagnosis—particularly in resource-constrained environments such as Ethiopia. Here we present a 62-year-old woman with thoracic spine cattleman disease causing compressive myelopathy.

Case presentation

A 62-year-old Ethiopian female presented with progressive lower limb weakness over one month, accompanied by unsteady gait and repeated falls. Neurological examination revealed paraparesis, with signs of compressive myelopathy and a sensory level at T7. On Lymphoglandular system, no palpable Lymphadenopathy appreciated. No palpable abdominal organomegaly identified.

Laboratory investigations—including CBC and liver function tests were within normal limits. HBV, HCV, and HIV were all negative.

MRI of the thoracic spine (Fig. 1) revealed an approximately 4.5cmx2cmx1cm (Craniocaudal, Mediolateral, Anteroposterior dimension) measuring posterior epidural mass that had a T1 iso-hypointense and T2/STIR hyperintense signal as compared to the cord, causing significant spinal canal stenosis and cord compression at T3–T4 and T4–T5 levels. Associated T2/STIR hyperintense cord signal changes were noted, suggestive of compressive myelopathy. The lesion demonstrated diffusion restriction on DWI/ADC and avid post-contrast enhancement with dural tail sign. Additionally, there was mild expansion, similar marrow signal abnormality and contrast enhancement involving the T4 spinous process, bilateral lamina, bilateral transverse process and its right pedicle, with minimal extension to the right posterior superior corner of the vertebral body and the bilateral posterior paravertebral muscles. Radiologic differential diagnoses included lymphoma, plasmacytoma and metastatic disease. Discussion

Castleman disease—also known as angiofollicular hyperplasia, localized nodal hyperplasia, giant lymph node hyperplasia, angiomatous lymphoid hamartoma, follicular lymphoreticuloma, benign giant lymphoma-is a lymphoproliferative disorder of uncertain etiology. Spinal involvement is exceptionally rare but in most reported cases it typically presents as an epidural mass causing myelopathy [8,11], as in our case. Most reported spinal cases of Castleman disease describe lesions that are iso- to hypointense on T1, hyperintense on T2, and show homogeneous enhancement post-contrast [9,11]. Although diffusion restriction has been reported as a characteristic of Castleman disease in other anatomical regions such as the chest and abdomen, documentation of this feature in spinal involvement remains rare according to the authors’ review; in our case, both the epidural lesion and the affected posterior elements demonstrated restricted diffusion.

A mildly expansile, lytic lesion involving the posterior elements of the T4 vertebra initially raised suspicion for metastasis; however, systemic evaluation with abdominopelvic CT, chest CT, and mammography was negative. Castleman disease has been reported with abnormal spinal signals and lytic destructive changes affecting both anterior and posterior elements [11], like our case. Considering the patient’s age and imaging features, plasmacytoma and spinal lymphoma were strong possibilities. Spinal lymphoma, though rare, accounts for 1%-2% of extranodal lymphomas and approximately 10% of epidural tumors. It typically involves the thoracic spine, appearing hypointense on T1, hyperintense on T2/STIR, with marked diffusion restriction and robust enhancement, and vertebral involvement often demonstrates a lytic pattern [12], closely mirroring our findings. Lytic destruction with an associated extra-osseous soft tissue mass is a common imaging feature in these entities including plasmacytoma, though spinal lymphoma usually arises in the setting of systemic disease. Other differentials included epidural meningioma and spinal tuberculosis. Epidural meningiomas, while rare in this location, may present with homogeneous enhancement and a dural tail sign [13,9], both of which were observed here. Nevertheless, the expansile lytic lesion with cortical destruction and diffusion restriction argued against meningioma. In Ethiopia, spinal tuberculosis with a phlegmonous epidural component is an important consideration given the high prevalence of TB. However, the predominant posterior vertebral involvement, expansile nature, and pronounced diffusion restriction were atypical for TB [14,15].

Castleman disease of the spine being rare and imaging features mimicking these relatively common pathologies, Imaging alone cannot establish a definitive diagnosis; histopathologic confirmation is essential. Histologically, the hyaline-vascular type is characterized by atrophic germinal centers with hyalinized vessels and numerous follicular dendritic cells. The mantle zones show concentric rings of small lymphocytes in an onion-skin pattern, and the interfollicular zone lacks prominent sinuses. In contrast, the plasma cell variant features hyperplastic germinal centers and abundant plasma cells in the interfollicular region [8,6]. Marked vascular proliferation in the interfollicular region is present in both CD variants [16].

Management of unicentric Castleman disease (UCD) is typically surgical [5,7]. Complete surgical excision is usually curative with an excellent prognosis [5]. This approach is further endorsed by the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) 2025 guidelines, which highlight surgical resection as the primary treatment, offering excellent long-term outcomes and low recurrence rates.

Our patient underwent successful T4–T5 laminectomy and gross resection of the epidural mass. This management is also consistent with similar cases reported in the literature, including Kachur et al., which support surgery as first-line therapy for localized disease [17].

For unresectable or incompletely resected cases, systemic therapy—often rituximab with prednisone or cyclophosphamide—is recommended. Radiation therapy remains an option for severe disease but is used cautiously due to long-term risks [18].

Postoperatively, our patient showed satisfactory response though there is still evidence of residual in the post op image. NCCN recommends observation after complete resection, but rituximab is a valid alternative, particularly when residual disease or symptoms persist. Its use is supported by case reports, including treatment of extramedullary spinal UCD. Radiation therapy (RT) is also an option, particularly for severe disease, though it's typically reserved due to long-term risks [5,19].

Our patient remains asymptomatic and is functioning well. In this case, adjuvant rituximab is aimed to manage residual disease. Involved-site radiotherapy (ISRT) is planned as the next therapeutic step, with close clinical and radiologic follow-up to assess for disease control or potential resolution.

Ultimately, the choice of treatment for UCD must be individualized. While surgery remains the cornerstone, adjuvant or alternative therapies like rituximab and radiation play a crucial role in cases where complete surgical resection is not possible or the patient remains symptomatic. The successful outcome in our patient highlights the effectiveness of this multimodal approach.

Conclusion

This case contributes to the limited literature on spinal Castleman disease in sub-Saharan Africa and underscores the importance of considering it in the differential diagnosis of spinal epidural masses. Recognition of its imaging features and histopathologic confirmation are crucial, especially in settings where more common conditions dominate clinical suspicion.
