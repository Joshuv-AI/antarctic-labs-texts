To the Editor:

Breathlessness is a common presentation to primary care yet one where a delay in appropriate investigation and diagnosis may result in adverse patient outcomes [1, 2]. The NHS England Adult Breathlessness Pathway facilitates a structured approach to the evaluation of breathlessness [3]. The common conditions responsible for breathlessness appear to disproportionately affect those most at risk from healthcare inequality [4, 5]. Evidence is emerging regarding the role of local football clubs in improving the health of communities, especially in targeting groups traditionally deemed “hard to reach” when accessing mainstream healthcare services [6, 7]. Furthermore, as healthcare systems globally struggle to balance capacity – including the provision of trained medical staff – with increasing service demands, there is increasing emphasis on technology in aiding the appropriate investigation of breathlessness [8]. We report on the feasibility of implementing clinical decision support system software in order to enhance uptake of the NHS England Adult Breathlessness pathway in the setting of a weekly “one stop-shop” clinic at “Everton In the Community”, the official charity of Everton Football Club.

A programme of engagement was conducted by a health awareness coordinator from Everton In the Community through an existing framework of health and well-being events as well as through primary care teams and social media. Individuals aged ≥40 years were invited to attend the “Beat Breathlessness” hub, a clinic held at “The People's Place” – a mental health and wellbeing hub purpose-built by Everton In the Community. Subjects undergo key observations including blood pressure (BP), body mass index (BMI), pulse and rhythm check using a 30-second six-lead ECG (AliveCor Kardia) followed by review with nursing staff using the “Cardio-LungHealth” software (LungHealth Ltd). Cardio-LungHealth is a clinical decision support system in the form of a Clinical Guided Consultation (CGC) intelligently integrating a structured history, physical examination with results from key investigations such as NT-ProBNP (N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide), spirometry, exhaled nitric oxide fraction, and glycated haemoglobin/HbA1c, thus generating a final diagnosis or alerting the operator to the possibility a significant cardio-respiratory condition. The CGC also interprets spirometry, stages COPD and incorporates validated heart failure (HF) symptom screening (BEAT-HF questions) [9]. Each patient consented to CGC use, to records being held electronically and data being used anonymously for reporting. Patients identified by the CGC review with a risk factor (e.g. smoking history) and respiratory symptoms (breathlessness, chronic cough, regular sputum production, recurrent chest infections) undergo Spirometry (ArtiQ). Patients with elevated finger-stick (Lumira-Dx) NTproBNP≥400 nG·l−1 undergo artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted echocardiography (US2.AI) performed by a trained HF specialist nurse/general practitioner (GP) with images reviewed by a cardiologist, with HF treatment initiated as appropriate (“Beat to Treat” pathway developed by UK's Heart Failure patient charity – “The Pumping Marvellous Foundation”) followed by referral for standard echocardiography.

The patient report from the CGC review along with any patient investigations are electronically sent by the CGC securely from the “Beat Breathlessness” hub direct to the patient's GP practice. The CGC is linked to an electronic dashboard populated in real-time enabling identification of clinically “high risk” patients, accurate oversight of clinical activity and highlighting key pathway pressure points thus facilitating service review. The system is password protected enabling Caldicott principles and General Data Protection Regulations to be satisfied [10]. Medical oversight is provided by a GP, consultant cardiologist and respiratory physician. The clinical pathway in current use is outlined in figure 1. 261 patients (mean age 61 (sd 18) years; 55% female; mean BMI 27.58 (sd 8.62)) underwent CGC review from 16 April 2024 to 19 March 2025. 112 patients complained of breathlessness (96 reporting modified Medical Research Council score ≥2) and spirometry was performed in 61 of these who had no previous respiratory diagnosis. Of these, 38% (23 of 61) had spirometry consistent with COPD (Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease staging by CGC software: 5=1A, 6=1B, 1=1E, 3=2A, 6=2B, 2=2E). CGC review determined one case of haemoptysis whilst a further seven patients were found to have abnormally low peripheral oxygen saturations ≤93% with the CGC alerting the operator and recommending urgent medical review. 168 patients had point of care NT-ProBNP levels checked and of these, 30 (18%) were elevated ≥400 ng·L−1 including 5 patients ≥2000 ng·L−1 (all underwent AI echocardiography and specialist assessment). 47 patients were highlighted as having clinically relevant hypertension (systolic BP ≥140 mmHg and diastolic BP ≥90 mmHg). All these cases were flagged by the software prompting the user to trigger further investigation. CGC review determined that 32 subjects were “current smokers”, 76 “ex-smokers” and 152 were “never smokers” and prompted the operator to administer smoking cessation support and offer community referral in all tobacco users. 38% (12 of 32) of smokers accepted community smoking cessation service referral. CGC review determined 37% (97 of 261) to be clinically obese with all highlighted for weight management advice. Targeted Hba1c checking has since been introduced following pathway launch and to date, 20 subjects were found to have an abnormal value between 42–47 mmol·mol−1 and no prior history of diabetes mellitus with the CGC alerting the operator for further action.

In traditional medical practice, the extent to which a given patient receives guideline-standard care is dependent on the level of knowledge held by the healthcare professional conducting the review. The advantage in using a CGC is that it ensures that every aspect of what constitutes “guideline standard” practice in evaluating breathlessness becomes compulsory during every review. This is further enhanced by the intelligent interpretation of investigations entered into the CGC, the automated staging of COPD, the incorporation of key safety alerts and embedding the “learning” of multiple specialists into its programming. The CGC thus functions more than just an electronic template but rather as an intelligent medical “sat nav” taking the healthcare professional from the history and examination then onto investigations subsequently arriving at a diagnosis before finally prompting appropriate management. Critics have previously cited low use as a barrier to implementation of such technology [11]. In order to aid uptake in this pathway, each CGC review is completed within 15 min with the CGC being fully integrated into the primary care server carrying the functionality to electronically deliver patient reports directly to the respective primary care practice. We have previously reported on the clinical validation and implementation of this technology in the diagnosis and management of COPD, asthma and obstructive sleep apnoea [12–14]. Here, the diagnostic yield of COPD was 38% whilst 18% of individuals were found to have a raised NT-proBNP level. The CGC is also suitable for identifying the presence of comorbid conditions coexisting in a breathless patient e.g. COPD combined with heart failure, atrial fibrillation or diabetes mellitus that may be diagnosed late or go unrecognised in standard practice and which may carry an adverse prognosis [15]. The functionality of the CGC in this pathway also extends beyond diagnosis e.g. in smoking cessation and weight management referral thus promoting a holistic model of care. Importantly, it should be stressed that this is a cohort who may not have presented to traditional healthcare services until a more advanced clinical presentation, typically as an urgent presentation to the Emergency Department, highlighting the importance of football clubs in improving the health of communities through early diagnosis and healthcare promotion, particularly in areas of socio-economic deprivation, thus truly addressing healthcare inequality. This model therefore demonstrates the feasibility of using clinical decision support software with medical oversight to enable guideline-based evaluation of breathlessness outside traditional healthcare settings. Further studies are required to determine applicability of this pathway incorporating other staffing models thus enhancing scalability taking into account training requirements e.g. healthcare assistants using the CGC working under senior medical oversight with the aid of Artificial Intelligence.
