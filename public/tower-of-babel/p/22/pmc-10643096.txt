A comparative study on ITS (intelligent transport system) standardization policies in the U.S. and Europe
Authors: Run Duan
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractStandardization is an essential link in the process of moving a technological system from research and development to application. As more international standards shift from “post-standard” to “pre-standard”, policies are playing an increasingly significant role in the process of standardization. In this study, we sorted out the ITS (Intelligent Transport System) standardization policy in the United States and Europe in chronological order from 1991 to 2012, comparing the context of policy documents and extracting four dimensions to discuss the influence of policy on technical standardization, which are the policy priorities, the role of government, policy intervention time, and cooperation. The results show that policy priorities and government roles influence each other and influence intervention time and cooperation. Policy priorities driven by infrastructure construction and the leadership role of the government have made the government more actively involved in the standardization process at an early stage and promoted top-down cooperation. Policy priorities driven by strengthening technological competitiveness and a supportive government role have had the opposite effect. Policy intervention time and cooperation have a direct impact on technology research and development, standardization, and integration. The results also confirm the importance of policies that strengthen the development of common technologies and mobilize stakeholder participation, as well as actively facilitate the transformation of domestic standards into international ones, providing insight for latercomers.Keywords: Intelligent transport system, Standardization, Technology policy

Introduction

Standardization is the process of developing and implementing technical standards. It can help maximize the compatibility, interoperability, security, repeatability, and quality of a new product or service. It can also be used as an effective means to quickly capture the market by addressing the connectivity of technical interfaces. As a result, it has become a competition for companies.

As technological systems have become more complex and larger, the process of setting technical standards has changed. Businesses prefer to reach a consensus before, rather than after, the completion of R&D to reduce investment and risk. The company’s research and development strength, position in the industry, and the national power behind the business, such as discourse power, are key factors in reaching a consensus. The standardization policy of a country or region plays an essential role in this context. In this research, we compared the standardization policy of ITS (Intelligent Transport System) in the United States and Europe from 1991 to 2012 to discuss the influence of different policies on technical standardization.

Literature review

Standard and standardization

Standard means a system that can be used in common [1]. Standardization is a series of processes that establish standards. It can be considered as the “activity of establishing and recording a limited set of solutions to actual or potential matching problems, directed at benefits for the party or parties involved, balancing their needs, and intending and expecting that these solutions will be repeatedly or continuously used, during a certain period, by a substantial number of the parties for whom they are meant” [2].

Some scholars classify standards as de facto, de jure, and consensus based on the standardization process. De facto standards are those that arise as a consequence of market mechanisms. De jure standard is those standards established by committees and political deliberation [3]. Consensus standards comply with the principle that all parties must participate in the setting process [4]. It is the result of a compromise between the parties. Shintaku and Eto [5] classify the consensus standards into three types, which are international standards, national standards, and industry/forum/consortium standards. International standards are those agreed upon at international standardization conferences and endorsed by international standardization organizations. National standards (so-called domestic standards) are standards created by national standardization organizations. Industry standards are created by industry groups and are public standards. These standards can be transformed among each other. For example, national standards may become international standards, due to the nation’s strong voice in international standardization organizations [6].

Blind [7] classifies the types of standards based on their economic functions. Compatible/interoperable standards have positive effects, such as network externalities and efficiency in the supply chain, but can lead to monopolies. Minimum quality/safety standards may avoid adverse selection and reduce transaction costs, but raise costs for competitors. Variety reduction criteria promote economies of scale and critical mass in start-up industries while reducing choice and leading to market concentration. Information standards and criteria facilitate trade and reduce transaction costs, but raise costs for competitors.

Most ITS standards need to address the issue of connectivity between devices, which belongs to the compatible/interoperable standard. They are formulated through discussion and adjustment of the interests of all parties and are subject to consensus standards. Different organizations, countries, and international organizations have participated in the development of ITS standards, the consensus standards include industry standards, national standards, and international standards.

The relationship between innovation and standard

Standardization is an essential link in the chain of technological innovation. Standards are the output results of previous technological innovations and the basis for the implementation of the next technological innovation [8]. Scholars differ on the impact of standards on innovation.

The traditional view has always been that standards and innovation contradict each other [9]. The existence of a minimum quality setting regulator may discourage the adoption of quality-cost-reducing innovations, which may reduce firm incentives to innovate [10]. The dominant design, which may be selected due to the strategic advantages of being technologically inferior [11], can not only hamper innovation by locking in users into inferior technology and make technology developers unable to gain useful feedback but also lead to monopoly since only a few companies could act as platform leaders and promote their standards by investing in the technical aspects of innovation while shaping the network of alliances [12].

Academics’ understanding of standards has gradually changed due to intensive research on the relationship between standardization and innovation. Allen & Sriram [13] point out that although standards can inhibit innovation by codifying inefficient or obsolete technology, they also spur innovation directly by codifying accumulated technological experience and forming a baseline from which new technologies emerge. Techatassanasoontorn & Suo [14] refuted the view that the winner-take-all outcome in the competition of de facto standards. Their results suggest that the user network structure has a significant impact on the dynamics and outcomes of standard competition. Brem et al. [15] point out that a dominant design stimulates process innovation but has a negative influence on radical innovation. Xie et al. [16] focus on standardization as a process of knowledge codification, knowledge transfer, or recombination, and consider that knowledge codification fosters incremental and architectural innovations and, at the same time, hinders modular and radical innovations.

Recently, scholars have focused more on the positive role of standardization, especially for innovation in developing countries, open innovation, and innovation involving the public interest. Friedrich [17] confirms the positive role of standardization in the integration of technologies and the acceptability and adoption of new and innovative technologies by providing open information. Pīlēna et al. [18], demonstrate that both the standardization process and outcomes can be compared to the processes of open and responsible innovation, and can be characterized as a contributor for creating the environment for the achievement of sustainable development and fostering open and responsible innovation. Kim et al. [19] demonstrate that standards guide the direction of the technological trajectory by promoting technological convergence and creating a new path for catch-up firms. Featherston et al. [20] propose a structured approach grounded in technology road mapping for exploring the potential standardization needs of emerging technologies. Their research shows how standards can enhance and catalyze innovation by codifying and disseminating knowledge among innovation actors.

The relationship between policy and standard

The relationship between policies and standards is extremely close. Standards Development Organizations (SDOs) conduct consensus among partners and competitors, users, interest groups, and governments in defining technical specifications [21,22]. The government represents the public interests in standardization. With the emergence of concepts such as open, responsible, sustainable, and big data, the role of the government has become increasingly significant [18,[23], [24], [25]].

Earlier studies focused on the impact of policies on the market competition of technologies and considered them to be a hindrance to technology development. Besen and Johnson [4] take the HDTV standards, which are developed by the Federal Communications Commission, as an example. They show that government attempts to speed up standardization have led to frequent lobbying, making it more difficult to set standards. David [26] criticizes that the government had a huge influence but could not predict the standard appropriately and might implement the technology early or late. Policy intervention may lead to a “narrow window” or “blind giant” effect. Cabral & Salant [27] states that mandated standard results in lower consumer surplus and social welfare than standards competition. And standard regulation leads to slower innovation than market-based standards. Based on the above issues, scholars have proposed to improve the standardization procedure. Gandal [28] confirms the positive role of policies in shortening standard-setting time and coordinating different technologies. He pointed out that the government or the commission should avoid rent-seeking activities before setting standards. Shintaku & Eto [5] summarized the successes and failures of the consensus-building process and noted that it was critical to creating an environment through competition policies, intellectual property rights policies, certification policies, and industry-academia government collaboration policies, etc.

As technology becomes increasingly complex and large-scale, and researchers focus more on the relationship between technology and society, the role of policies in standardization has been re-recognized. Blind et al. [29] construct a Heckman selection model and find out that in the case of low uncertain markets, formal standards lead to lower innovation efficiency while regulation has a positive influence. In the case of highly uncertain markets, this relationship is inverted. De Vries & Verhagen [30] also point out that for some conservative industry sectors, such as the construction sector, government standards can foster innovations for achieving societal goals, in this case reducing energy consumption. Zhang et al. [31] describe the U-shaped relationship between government subsidies for R&D with national/industry standardization for sustainable development. Zoo et al. [32] depict standardization in developing countries as a dynamic process to shape the innovation path and point out that the government and the technology/industry support organizations should complement the relatively weak technological capabilities of other actors.

Based on the above literature, we find that policies play an increasingly important role in the standardization process, and the positive role of standardization in innovation is gradually being discussed by scholars. However, most studies focus on the indirect impact of policies on standardization [31], or consider standardization as a strategy for latecomers and promoted through policies [33,34]. There has been relatively little discussion of how policies directly affect standardization in the initial stages of technology development. In this study, we focus on the early and middle stages of ITS technology development to gain insight into how governments directly participate in and facilitate the development of technical standards. It provides a different framework and approach for understanding the relationship between policy and standardization and also provides recommendations for developing countries and latecomers to promote technology development and enhance standard competitiveness through policies.

Background and methodology

The technological development path of ITS

ITS is an advanced and complicated technological system that refers to the addition of information and communication technologies to transportation infrastructure and vehicles to manage factors such as vehicles, roads, and routes to improve safety and reduce vehicle wear, travel times, and fuel consumption. It requires many devices to be interconnected, and it is crucial to ensure standards for interconnectivity between devices. The technological development of ITS can be divided into three phases according to the changes in the technological paradigm.

In the first phase, from the 1990s to 2000, researchers focused on collecting and integrating road information and providing it to vehicles, now known as navigation technology. García-Ortiz et al. [35] summarized technologies developed in the 1990s, including Digital Maps, Computers, Path Planning, Human Factors, Sensors, Communications, Vehicle Control, and Traffic Control.

In the second phase, from 2000 to 2010, the technological paradigm was attributed to two categories. They are cooperative driving and autonomous driving. Cooperative driving refers to vehicles obtaining road and route information from wireless communication with infrastructure and other vehicles. Autonomous driving refers to vehicles that recognize their environment through cameras, radars, and sensors installed in themselves.

In the third phase, from 2010 to the present, the technology paradigm shifted to automated driving with the involvement of IT technology and IT enterprises. Automated driving during this period refers to a vehicle driving itself in certain situations or spaces. The calculation and correction of driving paths are carried out through a series of technologies, such as satellite navigation, sensors, big data, and algorithms. From the perspective of technology development, it is a combination of cooperative driving and autonomous driving (Fig. 1), and an advanced level of autonomous driving. The representative technical system is Google's self-driving car, which began road testing in 2009. This sparked a race to commercialize autonomous driving technology, and other IT companies and car manufacturers have since introduced their autonomous driving systems, such as Apple Car, BMW’s Personal Pilot Pro system, Toyota’s Teammate Advanced Drive system, and Tesla’s Full Self-Drive system.

Policy selection

In this study, we focus on standardization policies in the United States and Europe from 1991 to 2012, including Acts, programs, projects, plans, white papers, etc. based on three reasons. First, this period corresponds to the first and second phases of technological development, when the development of technology has gone through a process of going from one to many. Numerous core technologies related to cooperative driving and autonomous driving have been introduced, and standardization has become a crucial aspect to ensure connectivity between devices. We selected this period to study the impact of standardization on technological development.

Second, this period corresponds to the early days of ITS policy in the United States and Europe, when governments were directly involved in R&D and standardization. Since 2012, companies have been heavily involved in R&D, while the government has tended to provide research funding and support, due to changes in the technological paradigm and the role played by the government in developing technologies. To clarify the direct impact of policy on standardization and the role of government in promoting the development and standardization of technology, we have selected the period 1991 to 2012.

Third, as the governments considered ITS as a technical means to improve public transportation at the early stage, there is technological content in the public policy documents. As the technological content of ITS is increasing and gradually becoming more complex, and the dominance of technological development and technical practicality is gradually shifting to enterprises and markets, the technological content is not easily accessible. In this study, we use text analysis to gain insights into technology and policy changes. Due to the limitations of this methodology, we selected the policies from 1991 to 2012. To analyze the technology and policy beyond 2012, it is insufficient to rely solely on policy documents. A combination of corporate annual reports, research papers, news, and interviews is necessary.

Methodology

This study mainly uses a literature analysis approach to categorize public policy documents in chronological order and gain insight into the changing trends of ITS-related policies. By comparing ITS-related policies in the United States and Europe from 1991 to 2012, we extract four dimensions to discuss the impact of policies on technical standardization, which are policy priorities, government role, time of policy intervention, and cooperation. The dimensions of policy priorities and cooperation are extracted from the policy text. There are descriptions of policy objectives, ITS positioning, and forms of cooperation in public policy documents. The role of government in standardization and the policy intervention time are summarized in the overall understanding of policy based on the timeline.

To avoid the subjectivity of dimensionality, we first sorted the policy documents chronologically to gain insight into the policy changes and the reasons driving them. Second, we extracted six dimensions through comparison and analysis by reading the policy text and timeline. After establishing the dimensions, we consulted experts in transportation policy, communication policy, and ITS-related technologies to explore the rationality of the six dimensions. Following the advice of experts and considerable discussion, we finally merge, reduce, and revise the six dimensions and identify them as four dimensions.

In Sections 4, 5, we analyze the development and changes in ITS standardization policies in the United States and Europe. In Section 6, we summarize and compare the differences between the United States and Europe from the four dimensions. In Section 7, we stated the impact mechanism of the four dimensions on standardization. Last but not least, we have the conclusion and some policy recommendations.

The development of ITS standardization policy in the U.S.

Since NASA’s Electronics Research Center transferred to the Department of Transportation (DOT), where it began applying NASA’s electronics expertise to transportation [37], ITS, a military technology, has officially been transformed into civilian use. Before 1991, the DOT was the main developer of related technologies. The Federal Highway Administration (FHWA) in the DOT has engaged in two programs, one was Electronic Route Guidance System (ERGS) terminated in 1971, and the other was IVHS beginning around 1990 [38]. Each program consisted of a diverse collection of development projects for such systems as electronic navigation consoles to guide drivers through city street networks, mobile communication systems to warn drivers of traffic congestion and suggest alternate routes, and automated merging systems smooth traffic flows at highway on-ramps [38]. Neither technical system was very mature, however, they set the technological paradigm for later ITS.

From 1991 to 2012, the federal government was the main promoter of R&D and standardization. It launched a series of Acts and national programs. The Intermodal Surface Transportation Efficiency Act (ISTEA) in 1991 is the first Act to include the R&D costs of ITS in the national budget. The National Economic Crossroad Transportation Efficiency Act (NEXTEA) in 1997, the Transportation Equity Act for the 21st Century (TEA-21) in 1998, the Safe, Accountable, Flexible, Efficient Transportation Equity Act-A Legacy for Users (SAFETEA-LU) in 2005, and the Moving Ahead for Progress in the 21st Century (MAP-21) in 2012 extended the budget every fiscal year. Based on each Act, the federal government initiated a national ITS program. The program focuses on the implementation and standardization of ITS-related technologies.

Since 2012, most R&D projects and road tests have been transferred to the states, facilitated by the collaboration between local governments and enterprises. The federal government is not directly involved in R&D but has built an architecture framework named Architecture Reference for Cooperative & Intelligent Transportation (ARC-IT) Training, and encouraged all parties to participate. Control of the standardization process has been transferred to industry standards organizations. The federal government still extended the ITS budget at the end of the MAP-21 fiscal year, with Fixing America’s Surface Transportation Act (FAST) in 2015. The following ITS strategic plan 2015–2019 emphasized that “interoperability will be more critical than ever before with the implementation of CV systems and the introduction of automated transportation systems as system interdependencies increase”. The Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act (IIJA), also known as the “Bipartisan Infrastructure Law” in 2021 strengthened infrastructure construction to address climate change and environmental impacts. The ITS Strategic Plan 2020–2025 emphasizes interoperability between national and international standards, between industry-based standards and architectures, and between existing and new standards, especially in the ICT domain (Fig. 2).

The timeline of ITS standardization policy in the U.S.

ITS has gone through a transition from military technology to civilian technology, from department-developed technology to national-supported technology to private-developed technology. At the same time, the federal government has undergone a transformation from a developer to a supporter to a facilitator. In the period from 1991 to 2012, although the federal government continued to play a leading and supportive role, the focus and goals of policies were changing. To better understand the changes, we divide the development of standardization policy from 1991 to 2012 into four periods, namely the standard-setting period, the standard revision period, the standard application period, and the international collaboration period based on the policy objectives.

Standard-setting period (1991–1997)

In 1991, ISTEA launched the IVHS program, which aims to promote the development and commercialization of electronics, telecommunications, or information technology to enhance the efficiency and security of land transportation system. In contrast to ERGS, IVHS is more integrated, complex, and institutionalized. It integrated multiple technologies, such as information processing, communications, control, and electronics, to establish seven subsystems and conducted more than 20 driving experiments in regions [39]. During the experiments, Congress recognized the necessity for compatible standards and protocols of IVHS and worked with the DOT to promote ITS compatibility at the national level. ITS standardization-related work was carried out by a specialized committee named IVHS America [40]. It transformed into ITS America and has been managing projects related to ITS and coordinating with participants and organizations. It also serves as the secretariat of ISO/TC 204 until today.

National ITS Program Plan during the ISTEA period defined 30 user service items and advocated the construction of a national architecture. The first national ITS architecture was announced in 1995. It was designed as an open system architecture to facilitate competition among multiple vendors. Each component was modular, could be interconnected, and information could be exchanged between each component [41].

ITS Standards Development Plan in 1996 classified standard compatibility at three levels, namely, national level, regional level, and product level. All of the standards should be under the national architecture, but their development could be prioritized by urgency and necessity [42]. In addition, the plan analyzed the benefits and risks of standardization and called on standardization organizations, product developers, service providers, or public authorities for cooperation [42]. Taking international standards into account, the plan points to the problem of limited market size if compatibility with international standards is low. Therefore, it is important to consider aligning national standards with international standards in terms of compatibility, level of technology, etc. If there was no domestic technology competition, it was better to cooperate with international standardization organizations at the beginning [42]. As for the role of government, the plan emphasizes that public sector support is essential in the early stage of standardization. It noted the need to clarify the functions of DOT, ITS America, and the standardization organization with the standardization process. For programs that are already in the implementation phase, it is important to maximize the use of existing standards and involve existing standardization organizations. In addition, it was important for DOT to frequently exchange information with state and local governments, and provide technical assistance to unify standards under the national architecture [42].

Standard revision period (1998–2002)

NEXTEA, established in 1997 as the successor to ISTEA, continues to support all ISTEA programs with a greater emphasis on system integration and advocating for a leadership role for the federal government. According to NEXTEA, the federal government should recognize the role of leadership and work with the public transport sector, academic institutions, and private sector to establish consensus, and support technological development, evaluation, demonstration, or training [43].

TEA-21 in 1998 combined the seven programs from the ISTEA period into four, those are Metropolitan Infrastructure, Rural Infrastructure, Commercial Vehicle Infrastructure, and the Intelligent Vehicle Initiative. The ITS structure during this period was centered on the construction of a road information network that integrates urban and rural areas [44]. National ITS Program Plan of 2000 explains the dynamic development of the standardization described in TEA-21. It is a single or a series of standards that are used periodically over a lifetime. The life cycle refers to three phases, those are development, evaluation and demonstration, and deployment. In the first stage, a standard is created or issued for initial use. In the second phase, the standard is required to be implemented in practice. In the final stage, the standard is proven to be widely available through testing and implementation in commercial products and public infrastructure. The plan pointed out that coordination between the operators of the telecommunication equipment, the traffic management department, the transportation department, and other management entities was essential at various stages [45]. Building on this, the plan called for national-level interoperability by June 1, 1999, and interim standards for some significant technologies by January 1, 2001.

National ITS Program Plan in 2002 noted problems during the standard promotion. First, although there have been many successful cases of consensus standards due to collaboration between the public and private sectors, consensus is still difficult to achieve, leading to delays in the market introduction. Second, standardization slowed down as federal funds shifted from standard development to implementation, coupled with the economic downturn. To address this issue, the plan states that it is important to establish and implement policies that allow the private sector to participate in the construction of public facilities. Third, as international standardization competition gradually shifts to disadvantaged areas of the U.S., the program requires the industry to seek standards with high interoperability and low life-cycle costs. To achieve this goal, automakers and primary suppliers needed to cooperate and make an agreement [46].

Standard application period (2003–2008)

SAFETEA-LU 2005 facilitated driving experiments in urban, rural, and commercial areas. It noted that interim standards can be established in cooperation with the DOT and related parties. The period of validity of interim standards is until the appropriate standard is adopted or issued by standardization organizations.

The subsequent National ITS Program Plan of 2007 established nine technical systems based on the SAFETEA-LU requirements. This classification is still in use today. In addition to ITS-JPO, the strategic planning group, and the management council, the plan has added two related organizations for ITS standardization, which are the ITS advisory committee and the ITS standards expert panel. The ITS advisory committee plays a role in assessing the progress of the program and the market value of the technology. The expert panel evaluated the life cycle from ITS technology development to implementation and endeavored to simplify the standard development process early [47]. It also extended ITS responsible agency to the Federal Highway Administration (FHWA), National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA), Federal Transit Administration (FTA), and Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration (FMCSA). FHWA was responsible for the maintenance of the road network and the application of the system to the roadside. NHTSA is responsible for the research and development of basic technologies such as bus transportation, electronic fare collection systems, rail and road crossing systems, standardization, architecture design, cost-benefit analysis, and development of enforcement strategies. FMCSA cooperated in the development and commercialization of automotive technologies [47].

International collaboration period (2009–2012)

In January 2009, European DG INFSO and U.S. RITA announced a joint EU-U.S. Declaration of Intent on Research Cooperation in Cooperative Systems, followed by a joint statement in November of the same year. The joint declaration aims to create cooperative systems and global open standards to ensure interoperability and eliminate redundant standards. At the same time, a Joint ITS Technology Task Force was established to support standard coordination and provide opportunities for future joint development. In June of the following year, contracts for five projects had been concluded. In 2011, it extended the existing joint declaration and advocated cooperation with the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport, and Tourism of Japan and the Ministry of Transportation of Canada.

Standards Program Strategic Plan for 2011–2014 allows modes and related devices developed in various countries to be easily interoperable, minimize development costs, and participate in the global ITS equipment market. To increase the number of vendors, ITS-JPO integrated the ITS standardization program of the U.S. into the European program for harmonization of standards centered on vehicle platforms under the development of European standards [48].

MAP-21 in 2012 highlighted the application of ITS and specifically expected ITS to contribute to traffic operations, emergency response, and traffic accident reduction. The following ITS Strategic Research Plan 2010–2014 decided to invest 100 million annually in ITS research, especially $ 49 million in autonomous driving technology. It established a consortium centered on the U.S. automotive industry, European standardization organizations, the European Telecommunications Standards Institute (ETSI), and the European automotive industry to design and produce common hardware and software [49]. ITS Strategic Research Plan (2015–2019) continued to invest in autonomous driving technology and emphasized the development and implementation of advanced automation technology to develop autonomous vehicles [50].

The development of ITS standardization policy in Europe

The development and standardization of ITS can be divided into two tracks (Fig. 4). The R&D track for automotive technology is mainly facilitated by two programs. One is an official technology development project named Framework Program (FP), and the other one is a bottom-up project named Europe-wide Network for Market-Oriented Industrial Research and Development (EUREKA) program. The infrastructure maintenance is promoted by the Trans-European Transport Network project (TEN-T).

The two tracks of ITS standardization policy in Europe.

FP was established in 1984 to streamline the adoption of Community research programs. With the subsequent iterations of the process and Treaty modifications, it became a financial and strategic tool to support and implement EU research and innovation policies. Starting from FP 2, each FPs has ITS-related R&D projects. They are the Dedicated Road Infrastructure for Vehicle Safety in Europe (DRIVE I 1989–1991) project in FP2 (1987–1991), DRIVE II (1992–1994) project, and the Advanced Transport Telematics (ATT) project in FP 3 (1990–1994), the Telematics Applications Program (TAP) and the Keystone Architecture Required for European Networks (KAREN) program in FP 4 (1994–1998).

Since FP5, it inclined to support cooperative driving and its applications. FP5 (1998–2002) has not set up ITS-related projects. FP6 (2002–2006) supported eSafty-related projects, the latter aims to develop a vehicle-based intelligent safety system. FP7 (2007–2013) emphasized collaboration with Competitiveness and Innovation Framework Programme (CIP) and launched two large-scale demonstration experiments.

Since 2014, the EU projects have shifted again towards supporting autonomous driving. The European Roadmap for Smart Systems for Driving in 2015 proposes a strategy for the development of autonomous driving in Europe. The Collaborative Intelligent Transport Systems (C-ITS) strategy in 2016 aims to promote the integration of investment and regulatory frameworks across Europe. The Horizon 2020 (2014–2020) following FP7 supported several projects related to cooperative and automated transport and vehicle, such as AV-Ready, Drive2TheFuture, PAsCAL, SUaaVE, Trustonomy, and TransAID. Horizon Europe (2021–2027) planned to allocate about 500 million euros for the research of autonomous vehicles.

EUREKA was established in 1985 as an agreement between 18 countries and the European Commission to foster competitiveness and market integration and to encourage R&D cooperation. Compared with FP, EUREKA aimed to construct a bottom-up innovation system [51]. It supported an automatic driving technology development project named PROMETHEUS (1987–1995) focused on the development of autonomous driving technology [52], laying the foundation for the next stage of technology integration and road testing.

TEN-T is a project aimed at integrating national networks and transport modes, linking the EU's periphery, and improving the safety and efficiency of the network. The first guidelines were published in 1992 when the Maastricht Treaty was signed. It establishes a general framework covering the objectives, priorities, and broad thrust of the contemplated measures, as well as projects of common interest in the trans-European transport network area. The 2001 revision covered access to seaports, inland ports, and intermodal terminals in the network and specified criteria for identifying projects of common interest relating to these parts of the infrastructure. The 2004 revision was intended to consider the expected changes in traffic flows as a result of EU enlargement.

In 2008, the EC recognized problems with current ITS, such as a greater focus on technological development rather than application, fragmented and uncoordinated deployment, minor continuity of service, and low level of intermodality. To better fulfill the European integration objectives, the EC issued an Action Plan and Directive that combined the achievements of TEN-T and Telematics into one implementation to accelerate and coordinate technologies and services, increase interoperability, establish efficient cooperation, and address privacy and liability issues. In the following, we present the development of the standardization of ITS in Europe in terms of two-track periods and the integration of two-track periods.

The R & D track

PROMETHEUS (1987–1995) is an automatic driving technology development project of EUREKA. It aims to build a common technology platform that provides intermediate technologies before companies commercialize them, making them environmentally friendly, improving safety, and creating efficient road system concepts and solutions. Among them, in-vehicle systems for vehicle monitoring and driver assistance, vehicle-to-vehicle communication networks, and road-to-vehicle communications for traffic control have been developed by the industry. Electronic components such as sensors, computer systems, artificial intelligence vehicles and related software, inter-vehicle/in-vehicle/road-to-vehicle communications, and environmental impact assessments have been developed by a dedicated research group. Demonstration experiments in 11 fields were conducted throughout Europe until 1994. The research funding came from major automakers in France, Germany, the United Kingdom, Italy, and Switzerland, with more than 749 million euros by 1995.

To realize the solution of PROMETHEUS, the Dedicated Road Infrastructure for Vehicle Safety in Europe (DRIVE I 1989–1991) project was launched in FP2. DRIVE I is a community-based collaborative research and development program. It supported 72 projects to provide information to the driver through the development of advanced road-vehicle communication systems [53]. The affiliated project Advanced Transport Telematics (ATT) aimed at advanced information technology that facilitated communication between intelligent vehicles and road infrastructure. During this period, the European Road Transport Telematics Implementation Coordination Committee (ERTICO) was established to coordinate projects and strategies to promote the commercialization of research results. Its basic functions are similar to those of ITS America.

DRIVE II (1992–1994) project in FP3 aimed to promote the practical use of the results of DRIVE I. It launched 67 projects and a series of demonstration experiments. The experimental result stated that the ATT project had an effect of improving public transportation in urban areas, however, it was difficult to be accepted by the market [54]. Some common standards and protocols were established during DRIVE, such as protocols for digital wireless transmission, protocols for direct withdrawal devices to operate full-time, cellular wireless network standards, digital map standards, smart card, and payment message standards, etc. From this time on, the purpose of ITS-related projects had been changed from “Exploring Options” to “Validation of Results” [55].

The Telematics Applications Program (TAP) was implemented in FP4 from 1994 to 1998. This program supported 110 projects for the application of information technology. The Transport sub-program TAP-T has established several development programs to develop technologies for more efficient, safer, and environmentally friendly telematics applications. The System Architecture and Traffic Control Integration (SATIN) program aims to develop technologies and architectures for satellite interactive multimedia IP networks and services and provide users with high-speed internet and multicast services for kiosks at frequently attended places such as village centers, bus stations, rest areas, deploying a return channel satellite system.

In 1998, the Guidelines for the Development and Assessment of Intelligent Transportation System Architecture were launched. It serves as a guideline for the field of telematics and aims to integrate traffic-related telematics programs and promote a system architecture called CONVERGE. CONVERGE proposes methods for the demonstration and evaluation of projects suitable for various architectures. As for standards, the guidelines considered them as a prerequisite to gaining market share and pointed out that standards could learn from existing standards [56].

Keystone Architecture Required for European Networks (KAREN) project in 1999 unified SATIN and CONVERGE, and constructed the architecture of ITS with three layers of functional, physical, and communication, providing the basis for deploying multiple systems. The architecture is composed of many ethnic groups with cross-border interoperability and multilingual support [56]. As the outcome of the KAREN project, the FRAME Architecture provides ITS engineers with a common approach, or “language”, for use throughout the EU. It covers 9 areas, including electronic payment facilities, safety and emergency facilities, managing traffic, managing public transport operations, advanced driving assistance systems, traveler journey assistance, support for law enforcement, managing freight and fleet operations, and support for cooperative systems.

FP6 from 2002 does not list transportation development separately. It placed “Transport” under the chapter “Sustainable Development, Global Change and Ecosystems” and decided on the theme “Sustainable Surface Transport” with two thematic areas which are “environmentally friendly and competitive transport systems” and “safer, more effective, and competitive rail and maritime transport” [57]. FP6 supports an application program called eSafety. The eSafety was established by the European Automobile Manufacturers Association (ACEA) in April 2002 and aims to formulate and examine the policies and concepts of European ITS. Participants include the EC, EU member states, car manufacturers, parts suppliers, highway management operators, telecom operators, service providers, the insurance industry, traffic safety/automobile user organizations, road management departments, emergency service providers, and others. In the following year, the eSafety Forum established 7 working groups. The introduction and development of autonomous in-vehicle systems is the achievement of the working group [58].

FP7 from 2007 emphasized the collaboration with the Competitiveness and Innovation Framework Programme (CIP) and launched two large-scale demonstration experiments. One is the Large-scale European Field Operational Test on Active Safety Systems (euroFOT) aimed to evaluate the short-term and long-term socioeconomic impact of the intelligent vehicle system on safety, environment, and driver efficiency. The evaluation consists of the functionality and performance of the advanced vehicle, as well as the interaction between the driver's behavior and the vehicle. The results are expected to be widely accepted by consumers and facilitate a global strategic network with the United States and Japan. Since 2009, euroFOT has established operational centers in France, Germany, Italy, and Sweden and has installed smart vehicle systems in 1000 vehicles. The other one is the Field Operational Tests of Aftermarket and Nomadic Devices in Vehicles (teleFOT) which is a large-scale field test of convenience, safety, efficiency, and environmental applications offered by aftermarket and in-vehicle nomadic devices. There are two types of tests, large-scale and detailed, and they were conducted at test sites in 8 countries in southern Europe [59].

The infrastructure track

The trans-European networks (TENs) is to create a modern and effective infrastructure to link Europe’s regions and national networks. It includes the Trans-European Transport Networks (TEN-T), the Trans-European Energy Networks (TEN-E), and the Trans-European telecommunications networks (e-TEN). The first one focuses on the transportation areas and covers projects of common interest to create new transport infrastructure or to upgrade the existing infrastructure, to close gaps, remove bottlenecks, and eliminate technical barriers to transport flows between EU Member States. In 1996, based on the Maastricht Treaty, the first guidelines of TEN-T were proposed by the European Parliament and the Council [60]. The guidelines define priorities, intention measures, infrastructure planning, etc. to achieve the common interests of the European region through the implementation of TEN-T policies under a common framework. The funding of TEN-T came from the EU budget, European Regional Development Fund (ERDF), Cohesion Fund, and the loan from the European Investment Bank, and could be divided into three stages (Fig. 5) [61]. The investment ratio of member countries has gradually increased [60].

The funding source of the TEN-T (Unit: billion euro). Source: DG for Energy and Transport (2005) [60].

The updated version in 2001 included covered ports (outside ports), inland ports, and inter-modal terminals, and emphasized settling the criteria for identifying projects of the joint benefit. The 2004 version developed 30 priority axes, based on projections of increased traffic as the EU expanded. The TEN-T priority axes and projects 2005 launched the specific contents of the 30 priority axes. It admitted the delay of the project due to the lack of funds and advocated the investment of member countries and Public Private Partnerships [61]. Since then, there has been a growing trend of widening disparities in progress in each country. The 2009 guidelines require member states to set minimum standards to be met. However, several projects, hampered by a lack of funding and difficult terrain, are already in jeopardy of being completed by the 2020 target. The 2013 guidelines proposed a two-tiered structure for the TEN-T network. The core network, consisting of nine corridors reflecting major transport routes, was decided to be completed by 2030. Six of them penetrated Germany. The entire network was originally expected to be completed by 2050.

In 2006, the Trans-European Transport Network Executive Agency (TEN-T EA) was established by the EC aimed to solve the technology and financing problems. In 2003, EA was replaced by the Innovation and Networks Executive Agency (INEA). INEA managed technology and funds for TEN-T, Marco Polo 2007–2013, Connecting Europe Facility (CEF) project, and partial projects in the transportation and energy fields at Horizon 2020.

Integration of two tracks

Recognizing the various issues associated with the current development of ITS, the European Commission announced in 2008 an Action Plan for the Deployment of Intelligent Transport Systems in Europe. It has issued 24 action directives in six priority areas to facilitate practical applications. For functional, technical, organizational, or service purposes, specifications should be adopted for a period of seven years, the EC stated. It aims to articulate a solution that is compatible, interoperable, and continuous across Europe.

To accelerate the trend, the EC announced a framework for the coordinated and effective deployment and use of intelligent transport systems in 2010. It emphasized centralizing the necessary specifications to ensure compatibility, interoperability, and continuity for the deployment and operation of ITS services in priority areas [59].

WHITE PAPER Roadmap to a Single European Transport Area-Towards a Competitive and Resource-Efficient Transport System 2011 calls for standardization to avoid technological fragmentation and to benefit from the rapid formation of the trans-European transport market. To ensure participation in the standardization process, the EU should adopt a flexible strategy. Regarding the application, DG MOVE presented a vision for a competitive and sustainable transportation system by 2050. It included 10 goals and 40 actions and proposed to complete a functional multi-modal TEN-T core network within Europe by 2030 and a single European transportation area by eliminating barriers between transportation modes and national systems [59]. In 2014, Horizon 2020, as the successor to the FP7, launched the goal of the transportation sector was to build a smart and green integrated transportation system using technologies such as low carbon technology and clean automobiles to deal with the problem of traffic congestion that is becoming more serious every day.

Comparison

Throughout sorting out the timeline of the ITS policy in the U.S. and Europe from 1991 to 2012 and repeatedly reading policy documents, we summarize four dimensions to compare the differences between ITS standardization policies in the United States and Europe. They are policy priorities, the role of government, the timing of policy intervention, and cooperation.

Policy priorities

The dimension of policy priorities is summarized from the policy content. In the United States, the federal government considers ITS to be a crucial component of the surface transportation system and lists it in the public budget. The ISTEA in 1991 proposed to “develop a national intermodal surface transportation system, to authorize funds for the construction of highways, for highway safety programs, and for mass transit programs, and other purposes”. It pointed out the function of ITS as “improving surface transportation for public benefit by providing equipment which can improve traffic flow and provide for enhanced safety”, and included the R&D fund of ITS in the federal budget for the first time. The TEA-21 in 1998 confirmed the ISTEA’s positioning of ITS, and emphasized to “accelerate the rate” of incorporating ITS “into the national surface transportation network”. The SAFETEA-LU in 2005 proposed ITS as “a component of the surface transportation systems” and emphasized the application. The MAP-21 in 2012 stated to “develop and maintain a national ITS architecture” and emphasized the integration of ITS. Each of the Acts extends the ITS R&D budget for the respective fiscal year.

In Europe, the R&D of ITS-related technologies has been placed under FP and EUREKA as separate programs to enhance European industrial competitiveness. Decision of 90/221/Euratom, EEC of the FP3 planned the “development of telematic systems”. The term “telematic” is the joining of telecommunications and informatics, which refers to technologies that collect and transmit data on vehicle use, maintenance requirements, and automotive servicing [62]. Decision No 1110/94/EC of the FP4 proposed to develop information technologies including “intelligent systems in areas such as cars, telecommunications”. Decision No 182/1999/EC of the FP5 placed the development of “advanced intelligent systems” under the key action of “systems and services for the citizen”. Decision No 1513/2002/EC of the FP6 proposed the concept of “ambient intelligence” and promoted the development of “interactive and intelligent systems for mobility”. Decision No 1982/2006/EC of FP7 proposed to develop “intelligent ICT-based transportation systems”, and “vehicles and intelligent service solutions for tourism enabling people and goods to move safely, ecologically, comfortably and efficiently in the mobility field”.

The role of the government

The dimension of the government’s role can be inferred from the policy timeline and content. In the United States, the federal government has taken the lead in the standardization process. The ISTEA in 1991 was required to submit a report addressing “federal design standards”, and to “conduct a survey to identify current State standards relating to geometric design, traffic control devices, roadside safety, safety appurtenance design, uniform traffic control devices, and sign legibility and directional clarity for all Federal-aid highways. The NEXTEA in 1997 addressed the federal government to work with the public transport sector, academic institutions, and private sector to establish consensus and support technological development, evaluation, demonstration, or training [43]. The TEA-21 in 1998 states that “the Secretary shall prescribe technical and operational standards”. The SAFETEA-LU in 2005 promoted an agreement between federal and local by “the standards that are appropriate for each territory and approved by the Secretary” and “implement standards for traffic operations and uniform traffic control devices that are approved by the Secretary”. MAP-21 in 2012 encourages the Secretary to “develop standard public-private partnership for the development, financing, construction, and operation of transportation facilities”, and assists “the development of consensus-based standards and best practices by the public transportation industry”. As the federal government realized that technology was shifting to areas where the United States was not as good as it had been in the past, it sought out international technology alliances to ensure its active participation in the development of international standards.

The European Union has been a supporter and guide for the development and standardization of ITS. It provides research funding and calls on European research institutions and companies to participate in research projects. During the period from 1987 to 1998, it supported projects on developing communication and automotive driving-related technologies, such as PROMETHEUS, DRIVE, ATT, and Telematics Applications Program (TAP). During the period from 1998 to 2011, it supported numerous system frame-building projects aimed to integrate a unified standard for in-vehicle, vehicle-vehicle, and road-vehicle communication into the architecture framework, such as the System Architecture and Traffic Control Integration (SATIN) program, the CONVERGE, which is a guideline for telematics and aims to integrate traffic-related telematics programs and promote a system architecture, and the KAREN project. KAREN is the prototype of the European ITS framework architecture, now known as the FRAME architecture. Since FP5, the EU has focused on supporting application projects and demonstration experiments.

Policy intervention time

The dimension of the policy intervention time can be inferred from the policy timeline. In the United States, standardization requirements were introduced at an early stage. During the ISTEA, Congress recognized the need for compatible standards and protocols and worked with DOT to promote ITS compatibility at the national level. Work-related to ITS standardization was carried out by a dedicated committee named IVHS America, which was later renamed ITS America. The National ITS Program Plan 1995 defined 30 user service items and advocated the construction of a national architecture. The national ITS architecture is an open system architecture to facilitate competition among multiple vendors. The ITS Standards Development Plan 1996 classified standard compatibility into three levels, national, regional, and product level, and required that all standards be under a national architecture. It also emphasizes maximizing the use of existing standards and involving existing standardization organizations in programs already in the implementation phase, with public sector support. The National ITS Program Plan 2000 describes a standard life cycle that points to a standard being created or issued for initial use, followed by implementation in practice, and proven to be widely available through testing and implementation in commercial products and public infrastructure. It encouraged the establishment of interim standards and called for national-level interoperability.

During the actual operation, however, several problems surfaced. The National ITS Program Plan 2002 acknowledged the difficulty of achieving consensus, which led to delays in introducing the market. Also, standardization has slowed because of a shift in federal funding from standard development to implementation, combined with the economic downturn. It points to the need to encourage the industry to seek standards with high interoperability and low life cycle costs, including cooperation with Europe, as international standardization competition gradually shifts to disadvantaged areas of the United States.

Europe promotes an open architecture and encourages separate standards for in-vehicle and out-of-vehicle technologies to be developed at an early stage. The Decision of the FP3“requires research and technological development work in the fields of standardization and interconnection protocols”, and emphasized that “this work should take into account the development of a new European regulatory environment on open architecture”. It placed the standardization of ITS-related technologies in separate projects. A number of common standards and protocols were established during DRIVE, such as protocols for digital wireless transmission, protocols for full-time operation of direct withdrawal devices, cellular wireless network standards, digital mapping standards, smart cards, and payment message standards. However, the requirement for standard integration was not raised until 1998.

The CONVERGE program of 1998 proposed methods for the demonstration and evaluation of projects suitable for various architectures and noted the importance of standards for gaining market share. The EC has attempted to place ITS-related technologies within the EU framework by supporting a series of framework projects during FP4 in conjunction with the introduction of EU standards. It involves more member states adopting EU standards through support application programs and demonstration experiments during FP6 and FP7. However, the standardization requirement was not explicitly mentioned in the EU document until 2008. The European Commission launched an Action Plan for the Deployment of Intelligent Transport Systems in Europe in 2008, proposing to seek a solution that is compatible, interoperable, and continuous across Europe. WHITE PAPER Roadmap to a Single European Transport Area-Towards a Competitive and Resource-Efficient Transport System 2011 formally mentions the need for standardization. It called for standardization to avoid technological fragmentation and facilitate the rapid formation of a trans-European transport market, noting that the EU should adopt a flexible strategy to ensure participation in the standardization process.

The cooperation

Cooperation patterns are extracted from policy timelines and contexts and can be verified from project documents. In the United States, cooperation is led by the federal government and includes state governments, standardization organizations, R&D agencies, industry, and the private sector. The 1991 ISTEA encouraged the federal government to work with states on public affairs, with industry on strengthening manufacturing capabilities, and with international agencies for potential benefits. The TEA-21 in 1998 authorized the Secretary to “cooperation with foreign public sector entities in research, development, demonstration, training, and other forms of technology transfer and exchange of experts and information” in addition to the existing cooperations. The 2005 SAFETEA-LU maintained the cooperation structure and was implemented as a project. The following National ITS Program Plan in 2007 extended ITS responsible agency to the Federal Highway Administration (FHWA), the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA), the Federal Transit Administration (FTA), and the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration (FMCSA), and promoted the establishment of the ITS advisory committee and the ITS standards expert panel, in addition to existing ITS-JPO, the strategic planning group, and the management council to accelerate standardization of technology. The ITS Advisory Committee plays a role in assessing the progress of the program and the market value of the technology. The expert panel assessed the life cycle from ITS technology development to implementation and worked to simplify the standard development process at an early stage.

In Europe, collaborations were established as projects progressed and disbanded as they ended Participants were experts from research institutions and companies. The PROMETHEUS project (1987–1995) assembled engineers from major automakers such as Daimler and Mercedes Benz. The DRIVE I program (1989–1991) launched 71 projects with the collaboration of 1000 experts from more than 300 European companies [63]. The TAP program has funded 413 projects involving hundreds of researchers. Researchers act as boundary spanners between enterprises, academia, projects, and standardization organizations, facilitating the transition between technical standards, industry standards, national standards, and international standards. In other words, project-based cooperation is conducive to a flexible transformation of standards based on Europe’s industrial strengths and technological R&D advantages, as well as its participation and voice in international standardization organizations.

Findings

Comparing ITS policies in the United States and Europe based on four dimensions, we find that (1) policy priorities are influenced by, and at the same time influence, the role of government; (2) policy priorities and government’s role influence policy intervention time, and the latter one influences standardization; (3) policy priorities and government’s role influence cooperation pattern, and the latter one influences standardization (Fig. 6).

The relations between four dimesions and their influence mechanism to standardization.

For the first finding, policy priorities are influenced by the role of government, which in turn reinforce the government's positioning of itself. In the United States, the federal government recognized ITS as a component of the national surface transportation system and a military-to-civilian technology, since the DOT became a major force in ITS research and development with NASA’s Electronics Research Center, which was the ITS research facility in the past, merged into the DOT. The policy then began to include R&D budgets in national spending and to emphasize the federal government's leadership role. In Europe, ITS has been recognized as a fundamental technology for enhancing regional industrial competitiveness since its technological direction was introduced by FP and EUREKA. The latter two are top-down and bottom-up projects aimed at strengthening Europe's industrial competitiveness. Based on these policy priorities, the R&D has been internalized into projects, and the EU has taken on the role of supporter, providing funding and institutional assistance (Table 1).

For the second finding, the policy intervention time is influenced by policy priorities and the role of government and affects standardization. In the United States, due to policy priorities and the leadership role, the federal government tends to and can lead the process of technological development and standardization of ITS at an early stage. From the launch of the first ITS prototype, IVHS, in the 1990s, to the introduction in 2007 of a classification of nine technical subsystems that continue to be used by other countries and international standardization organizations, the federal government has been actively driving the development of related technologies and technology paradigms at an early stage. Government leadership and early intervention may reduce disputes among parties and facilitate the formation of a technological paradigm that gains a first-mover advantage. The United States thus gained international discourse on ITS standardization, and ITS America serves as the secretariat for ISO/TC 204 to this day. However, since ITS is a large-scale technical system consisting of many subsystems and components, policy documents tend to emphasize connectivity standards from a strategic perspective. The government's leadership role and early intervention may have overlooked technological diversity and supported inferior technologies due to its completion of technology integration and encapsulation at a time when technology development was immature and technology competition was insufficient.

In Europe, due to delays in infrastructure integration, policy priorities, and the supportive role of the EC/EU, the EC/EU did not intervene in standardization but encouraged the formation of project-centric standards at an early stage. Limited government involvement and belated intervention have resulted in a competition of diverse technologies and participation from industry and academia, which has facilitated auto companies to develop their autonomous driving technologies. However, these advantages in standardization are built on its strong automotive industry and R&D capabilities. The assistant role of the government and belated policy intervention may have resulted in advanced technologies being blocked within closed technical subsystems that fail to serve public concerns and make it difficult for subsystems to integrate with the infrastructure (Table 2).

For the third finding, cooperation patterns are influenced by policy priorities and the role of government, and affect standardization. In the United States, the federal government promoted national cooperation at an early stage, involving relevant departments and local governments. The central and local cooperation makes “many states revamped their planning and program development processes” which “formed the basis for shifts in other functional areas” [64]. It needs to be based on the authority of the central government and the provision of funds. The authority of the government has somewhat reduced the conflict among stakeholders. The funds make transportation stakeholders want to “participate in the planning process” [64]. However, conflicts of interest can erupt in other forms as time passes and funding dwindles. In the middle stage, the difficulty in achieving formal consensus standards led the government to switch to promoting interim standards, which only required the agreement of an expert committee, rather than a standardization organization as in the 2005 SAFETEA-LU. It then changed its cooperation strategy to incorporate more participation from departments and expert committees in the subsequent 2007 National ITS Program Plan. Around 2010, the U.S. acknowledged that technology had shifted to areas the U.S. was not good at and that corporate involvement was inadequate, and started to seek cooperation with Europe.

In Europe, project-centric cooperation involves participants from across EU member states and facilitates a diversified path of technological development. It provides technological niches for new technologies to grow through project iteration. Experts in the project may also be involved in international standardization organizations, which gives the number of European experts an advantage in some international standardization organizations that have individuals as members. In addition, Europe also has an advantage in terms of membership in some international standardization organizations with countries as participating units. As a result, standards that gain a competitive advantage in the European region can easily be transformed into international standards. However, the lack of top-down administrative enforcement to promote standardization may result in a loss of first-mover advantage. In addition, separate technology development may result in redundant development and waste of R&D resources, which is not conducive to technology integration (Table 3).

Conclusion

Policy priorities driven by infrastructure construction and the leadership role of the government allowed the government to become more actively involved in the standardization process at an early stage, and facilitated top-down cooperation. Policy priorities driven by increased technological competitiveness and a supportive government role make it difficult for governments to intervene in the process of setting connectivity standards and promoting top-down cooperation. In contrast to early policy intervention and government-centric cooperation, which are the result of proactive government action, late policy intervention and project-centric cooperation are the result of passive government acceptance. That said, the government’s lack of ability to control the overall situation leaves time and niche space for diverse technologies to develop.

From a theoretical point of view, this study is complementary to the quantitative study of standardization policies. Existing research has typically focused on the impact of standards on technological innovation in a market economy, treating government influence on standardization as an external factor affecting market competition. This study explains the internal mechanisms of government influence on standardization from a policy perspective and revisits the role of government and standardization policies as endogenous factors in technological innovation. The inherent relevance of policy dimensions hints at the strategic positioning and policy orientation of standardization in the United States and the European Union, and also reveals the role of standards in a national innovation system, thus broadening the scope and content of standardization research. From a practical point of view, this study provides three recommendations for policy formulation.

First, the government should not overestimate its predictions about technology and carry out detailed technology planning. Instead, it should strengthen its R&D capabilities and common technology accumulation from a strategic perspective, ensuring that the industry can construct and develop integrated technology systems at any time.

Second, the government should make the R&D funding more effective to attract researchers and industry stakeholders to participate, rather than shifting money from one pocket to another. Investing in infrastructure is an easy way for the central government to control and stimulate economic growth, but it may not promote technological innovation without supporting the development of industries and technologies.

Last but not least, the government should take a more active and important role in the process of converting national standards to international ones. Whether it is the United States establishing the discourse power of international standards by occupying a first-mover advantage and expanding international cooperation, or the European Union strengthening its ability to transform regional standards to international standards by leveraging its membership advantage and voting power in international organizations, it should be recognized that developed countries attach great importance to the competition for international standards of each technology, as international standards are a prerequisite for gaining access to the international market and a means to black-box the advantageous technologies, protect the enterprises, and obtain Externality.

For the latecomers, the government should actively participate in the construction of discourse forces in the field of international standardization, and encourage domestic agents to participate in the process of pre-standardization through cooperation with foreign advanced technology enterprises and academia to gain a first-mover advantage in the formulation of international standards.

Limitation and plans

There are three main limitations of this study. First, although we collected ITS standardization policies in the United States and Europe from 1991 to 2012 and analyzed the impact of policies on standardization through content in chronological order, we still lacked material from enterprises to assist in the analysis. Second, we only compared ITS related policies in the U.S. and Europe, and lacked more countries, especially those from behind, for comparison. After 2012, more and more countries joined the technological race in ITS, such as China, South Korea, India, and Lebanon. Many countries are also committed to the application of ITS to solve transportation, environmental, social, and other issues, such as Dominica [23]. Last but not least, this study cannot be used for policy evaluation. Relying on the EU’s dominant auto industry and a strong voice in international standardization, which partly hedge the sunk costs of redundant technology and institutions, EU policies appear to be better than those of the U.S. However, if the U.S. had prioritized the right technological path in today’s view, such as automated driving, in the first place, the standardization competition for ITS could have been another scenario. As a result, it is difficult to assess which policy is good or bad based on this paper.

For the future study plan, nowadays, research and development of ITS technology have shifted to the use of algorithms and artificial intelligence to further optimize path planning, item recognition, and energy consumption. Competition for interconnectivity standards has shifted from manufacturing to the protocol and service industries, such as algorithms, frameworks, and controls. In addition, with the application of technologies involving ethical and social factors such as big data, artificial intelligence, and facial recognition, the competition for minimum quality/safety standards has also shifted from product quality to ethical standards, environmental standards, and so on. It is necessary to adopt a new research framework to address these issues and to construct a comprehensive understanding between technology and society. Some studies have made progress in this area, such as social technology systems and sustainability. We hope to refine and apply them to policy formulation and evaluation.
