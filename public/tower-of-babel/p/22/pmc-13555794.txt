Anatomical Feature of Nasal Cavity for Endoscopic Endonasal Skull Base Surgery in Patients With GHoma
Authors: Tomoharu Suzuki, Noritaka Komune, Yusuke Miyamoto, Daisuke Kuga, Yuhei Sangatsuda, Ryota Higuchi, Yuichi Saito, Daisuke Murakami, Koji Yoshimoto, Takashi Nakagawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEndoscopic endonasal skull base surgery (EESS) is widely performed for pituitary tumors. In growth hormone–secreting adenomas (GHomas), the procedure is technically more demanding than for other pituitary neuroendocrine tumor (PitNET) subtypes due to restricted instrument maneuverability. Few studies have evaluated the influence of nasal cavity morphology on this difficulty.ObjectiveTo identify anatomical factors contributing to the technical challenges of EESS for GHomas by comparing nasal cavity structures between GHoma and non‐GHoma PitNET patients.Study DesignRetrospective chart review.SettingTertiary referral center with a collaborative skull base team of neurosurgery and otolaryngology.MethodsComputed tomography (CT) images of 28 GHoma and 104 non‐GHoma PitNET patients who underwent EESS between August 2018 and April 2024 were analyzed. Measurements included inter‐vidian canal distance, nasolacrimal duct opening width, inferior turbinate width, piriform aperture width, sphenopalatine foramen width, inter‐carotid artery distance, skull base–to–hard palate distance, anterior nasal spine–to–clivus distance, and anterior sphenoid wall–to–clivus distance. Comparisons were performed using Student's t‐test.ResultsGHoma patients showed significantly greater nasolacrimal duct opening width, piriform aperture width, and anterior nasal spine–to–clivus distance (P < .05). Vertical‐to‐horizontal ratios did not differ significantly. However, the inter‐carotid artery distance and the ratios of inter‐carotid distances to the anterior nasal spine–to–clivus distance were significantly smaller in GHomas.ConclusionGHoma patients possess generally larger sinonasal dimensions, yet a narrower paraclival inter‐carotid space limit surgical maneuverability. These CT‐based anatomical differences may have potential implications for preoperative anatomical assessment in patients with GHomas.Level of EvidenceIV.Keywords: Endoscopic endonasal approach, GHoma, PitNET, pituitary tumor

As first‐line surgical treatment for pituitary tumors, endoscopic endonasal skull base surgery (EESS) is the preferred approach owing to its minimally invasive nature and clear visualization, thus allowing for enhanced tumor resection while minimizing complications. 1 , 2 At our institution, endoscopic transsphenoidal surgery for pituitary tumors is performed collaboratively by the departments of neurosurgery and otolaryngology since August 2018. 3 The otolaryngologists create the surgical corridor along which the neurosurgeons perform tumor resection and rigid reconstruction. Finally, the otolaryngologists reconstruct the bony and dural defect with a nasoseptal flap, if needed, and perform postoperative nasal packing. Most pituitary tumors are pituitary neuroendocrine tumors (PitNETs), according to terminology revised in 2021. 4 In terms of surgical maneuverability, we have experienced that growth hormone‐producing adenomas (GHomas) are more challenging than other PitNET subtypes. Clinically, patients with GHomas often require more extensive bone drilling to secure an adequate operative field within a constrained, deep surgical corridor—specifically, drilling the bone between the bilateral vidian canals to their anatomical limits—which increases the technical complexity of EESS for GHoma. Patients with GHomas frequently present with characteristic physical features such as frontal bossing, nasal enlargement, and hypertrophy of the lips or mandible. However, few studies have detailed the anatomical differences within the nasal cavity of patients with GHomas compared with those with other PitNETs. 5 , 6 , 7 This paucity led to our study. In this study, we retrospectively analyzed computed tomography (CT) images to investigate such differences.

Methods

Patients were included if they met the following criteria: (1) diagnosis of a GHoma or non‐GHoma PitNET, (2) primary EESS performed collaboratively by our neurosurgery and otolaryngology teams between August 2018 and April 2024, and (3) availability of preoperative high‐resolution maxillofacial CT scans. Patients were excluded if they had a history of previous sinonasal or skull base surgery, severe craniofacial trauma, congenital craniofacial anomalies, or were under 15 years of age, as these factors could significantly alter normal sinonasal anatomy (Table 1). Anatomical assessments were conducted using the AquariusNET system® (TeraRecon Inc.) for all CT measurements. All measurements were performed by a single evaluator, and the average of multiple measurements was used. Images were reconstructed at a 1.0‐mm slice thickness and a 1.0‐mm reconstruction interval. A high‐resolution bone reconstruction algorithm was employed to provide maximal detail of the craniofacial and sinonasal bony structures, ensuring the accuracy of the Hounsfield unit (HU) measurements and anatomical dimensions. Nine anatomical landmarks were evaluated—3 measurements each on coronal, axial, and sagittal CT images. Specifically, on coronal CT images, we measured the distance between the bilateral vidian canals, the lateral edges of the nasolacrimal duct opening, and the maximum width of the inferior turbinate mucosa. When the left and right inferior turbinates had different maximum mucosal widths, the larger value was used for analysis (Figure 1).

On axial CT images, we measured the distance between the piriform aperture edges, the inter‐sphenopalatine foramen distance within the nasal cavity, and the minimum distance between the internal carotid arteries (ICAs) in the paraclival region (Figure 2).

(A) Piriform aperture width. (B) Sphenopalatine foramen width. (C) Minimum paraclival internal carotid artery distance.

On sagittal CT images, the following distances were measured: from the skull base to the posterior edge of the hard palate, from the anterior nasal spine to the clivus, and from the anterior wall of the sphenoid sinus to the clivus (Figure 3).

(A) Skull base–palatine bone distance. (B) Anterior nasal spine–clivus distance. (C) Anterior sphenoid wall–clivus distance.

The distances between the internal carotid arteries, between the sphenopalatine foramen, and between the vidian canals were measured as the shortest distances between their respective inner bony walls.

These specific measurements were chosen to comprehensively characterize the anatomical corridor used in endoscopic skull base surgery. Landmarks along the coronal, axial, and sagittal planes reflect the width of the nasal cavity, the spatial relationship of critical neurovascular structures such as the internal carotid artery, and the anteroposterior depth of the sinonasal tract. By analyzing these parameters, we aimed to clarify potential anatomic factors that could contribute to surgical difficulty in GHoma cases.

To further assess morphological differences, we calculated ratios by comparing the distance between the piriform aperture edges, distance between the lateral edges of the nasolacrimal duct opening, maximum width of the sphenopalatine foramen, maximum distance between the ICAs, and distance between the vidian canals to the distance from the anterior nasal spine to the clivus. The anterior nasal spine to clivus distance was used as a proxy for the overall sagittal dimension of the surgical field to normalize anatomical measurements, as it represents the fundamental working depth during endoscopic endonasal surgery. Comparisons between the 2 groups (GHomas and non‐GHoma PitNETs) were performed using the t‐test in JMP Pro 18®, a statistical software program developed by SAS Institute Japan Ltd. A P < .05 was considered statistically significant. To address the issue of multiple comparisons among the 14 anatomical parameters, P‐values were adjusted using the False Discovery Rate (FDR) method (Benjamini‐Hochberg procedure). In addition to P‐values, effect sizes (Cohen's d) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to evaluate the clinical magnitude of the observed differences. Furthermore, to determine whether GHoma was independently associated with anatomical features while accounting for sexual dimorphism, multivariable linear regression analysis was performed for key parameters, adjusting for sex. Statistical analyses were conducted using JMP Pro 18®. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Kyushu University Hospital (Approval No. 23003‐00).

Results

In the GHoma group, patient ages ranged from 17 to 78, with a median age of 56. In the non‐GHoma group, ages ranged from 15 to 84, with a median age of 58. The GHoma group consisted of 13 male and 15 female patients, whereas the non‐GHoma group included 49 males and 55 females. The groups did not significantly differ in terms of sex (Table 1).

On coronal CT images, no significant differences were observed in the inter‐vidian canal distance (Cohen's d = 0.18, 95% CI: −0.78 to 1.98, adjusted P = .4435) or the width of the inferior turbinate (Cohen's d = −0.15, 95% CI: −1.1 to 0.52, adjusted P = .4754) between the 2 groups. However, the distance between the lateral edges of the nasolacrimal duct opening was significantly greater in the GHoma group than that in the non‐GHoma group (Cohen's d = −0.56, 95% CI: −0.55 to −0.38, adjusted P = .0209). On axial CT images, no significant differences were observed in the width of the sphenopalatine foramen (Cohen's d = −0.41, 95% CI: −2.87 to 0.04, adjusted P = .0878). However, the distance between the piriform apertures was significantly greater in the GHoma group than that in the non‐GHoma group (Cohen's d = −0.78, 95% CI: −2.54 to −0.76, adjusted P = .0017). In contrast, the inter‐carotid artery distance was considerably shorter in the GHoma group than that in the non‐GHoma group (Cohen's d = 0.83, 95% CI: 1.11‐3.39, adjusted P = .0014). On sagittal CT images, no significant differences were observed in the distance from the skull base to the posterior edge of the hard palate (Cohen's d = −0.41, 95% CI: −3.83 to 0.07, adjusted P = .0878) or the distance from the anterior wall of the sphenoid sinus to the clivus (Cohen's d = −0.20, 95% CI: −2.25 to 0.82, adjusted P = .4435). However, the distance from the anterior nasal spine to the clivus was significantly greater in the GHoma group than that in the non‐GHoma group (Cohen's d = −0.59, 95% CI: −5.64 to −0.95, adjusted P = .0185). No significant differences were observed in the ratios of the sphenopalatine foramen width (Cohen's d = −0.02, 95% CI: −0.02 to 0.01, adjusted P = .918), distance of the edges of the nasolacrimal duct opening (Cohen's d = −0.23, 95% CI: −0.03 to 0.01, adjusted P = .3503), or distance of the edges of the piriform aperture (Cohen's d = −0.30, 95% CI: −0.02 to 0.00, adjusted P = .2602) to the anterior nasal spine to clivus distance. The ratios of the inter‐vidian canal distance (Cohen's d = 0.47, 95% CI: 0.00‐0.03, adjusted P = .074) and the inter‐carotid artery distance (Cohen's d = 1.07, 95% CI: 0.02–0.05, adjusted P < .0001) to the distance from the anterior nasal spine to the clivus were both significantly smaller in the GHoma group than those in the non‐GHoma group. To account for potential confounding by sex, multivariable linear regression analyses were performed with each CT‐based parameter as the dependent variable and group and sex as independent variables. After adjustment for sex, all CT‐based parameters that were significantly different between the GHoma and non‐GHoma groups in the unpaired t‐test remained significantly associated with GHoma status. In particular, the narrower IC–IC distance remained strongly associated with GHoma status (P = .0002), whereas sex was not significantly associated with this parameter (P = .3726). These findings indicate that the CT‐based anatomical differences observed in patients with GHoma were not solely explained by sex‐related skeletal differences.

Discussion

In this study, we retrospectively analyzed CT images to investigate anatomical differences between patients with GHomas and those with non‐GHoma PitNETs, all of whom underwent EESS at our institution. The analysis revealed that the inter‐carotid artery (ICA) distance was significantly shorter in patients with GHomas than in those with non‐GHoma PitNETs. In addition, the ratios of the inter‐carotid artery distance to the anterior nasal spine‐to‐clivus distance were smaller in patients with GHomas. These results suggest that although patients with GHomas exhibit absolute anatomical enlargement (wider and deeper structures) compared with those with non‐GHoma PitNETs, their relative sinonasal morphology remains comparable. A wider nasal cavity may intuitively appear advantageous for maneuverability; however, true surgical limitation arises in the deeper operative field where the ICA distance is significantly narrower, restricting safe instrument manipulation.

Despite the greater anterior nasal spine‐to‐clivus distance in patients with GHomas, the absence of a significant difference in the inter‐vidian canal distance implies that the extent of the anterior sphenoid wall opening is similar between groups, potentially contributing to reduced maneuverability during surgery.

The multivariable analysis clarified that the anatomical differences observed in GHoma were not fully explained by sex‐related skeletal differences. In particular, IC–IC distance remained significantly associated with GHoma after adjustment for sex, suggesting that narrowing of the deep surgical corridor is a characteristic CT‐based feature associated with GHoma. Meanwhile, ANS–CL and the IC–IC/ANS–CL ratio were associated with both GHoma status and sex, indicating that both disease‐related and sex‐related factors may contribute to the overall shape of the surgical corridor.

Regarding transverse dimensions, the distance between the lateral edges of the nasolacrimal duct opening on coronal CT images was significantly greater in patients with GHomas. Although the width of the sphenopalatine foramen tended to be larger in GHomas, the difference was not statistically significant. No significant differences were observed in the ratios of these transverse parameters to the anterior nasal spine‐to‐clivus distance, further supporting comparable overall nasal morphology. For vertical dimensions, the distance from the skull base to the posterior edge of the hard palate showed no significant difference between the 2 groups, which aligns with previous reports indicating that vertical nasal structures are only slightly elongated in GHoma patients. 7

In terms of anteroposterior dimensions, the anterior nasal spine‐to‐clivus distance was significantly longer in patients with GHomas than in those with non‐GHoma PitNETs, consistent with previous findings that demonstrated a greater distance from the external nasal aperture to the clivus in acromegalic patients. 8 , 9 , 10 Although a prior study reported a shorter distance from the anterior sphenoid wall to the sella turcica in GHomas, 7 our analysis revealed no significant difference in the anterior sphenoid wall‐to‐clivus distance between groups. The position of the ICAs is a critical consideration in pituitary surgery. Consistent with previous studies, 7 , 8 , 10 , 11 , 12 our findings confirm that the inter‐carotid artery distance is significantly shorter in GHomas, resulting in a narrower surgical corridor at the skull base and thereby increasing the technical difficulty of endoscopic manipulation.

Notably, this is the first report to demonstrate that the ratios of the inter‐carotid artery distances to the anterior nasal spine‐to‐clivus distance is significantly smaller in patients with GHomas than in those with non‐GHoma PitNETs. These ratios serve as surrogate indicators of relative sinonasal morphology independent of absolute dimensions, and their disproportionate reduction may contribute to the limited surgical maneuverability observed in GHomas. In such patients, the anterior wall opening of the sphenoid sinus is relatively narrower at the level of the vidian canals, necessitating maximal enlargement of the surgical corridor. To achieve adequate exposure, resection of the anterior sphenoid wall should be extended to the vidian canal level, with careful management of the palatovaginal artery. When required, the vidian artery and nerve may also need to be sacrificed. 13

Because endoscopic pituitary surgery requires precise manipulation in deep, narrow spaces, GHoma resections are inherently more technically demanding. ICA injury, though rare, is a potentially fatal complication, with an incidence of approximately 0.5%. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 While direct evidence linking GHoma to an elevated risk of ICA injury remains lacking, the narrower ICA distance in these patients may theoretically increase such risk. Therefore, meticulous preoperative CT evaluation, intraoperative navigation, and Doppler‐based localization techniques are essential. Recently, indocyanine green video angiography has been developed for ICA visualization, and its selective application should be considered. 19

In summary, the present findings provide novel quantitative evidence that GHoma‐specific anatomical disproportions—particularly the reduced inter‐carotid artery distance relative to sinonasal depth—may contribute to the increased technical difficulty of EESS. These insights will be valuable for neurosurgeons and otolaryngologists in improving preoperative planning and minimizing the risk of ICA injury. Not only in skull base surgery but also in routine sinus surgery, including procedures for chronic rhinosinusitis and sphenoidotomy, surgeons should be aware that a narrower inter‐carotid artery distance may limit the maneuverability of surgical instruments. Therefore, careful attention to these anatomical features is essential during surgery. The present findings have educational significance not only for surgeons engaged in skull base surgery but also for otolaryngologists in general. Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, while we emphasized the clinical necessity for extensive bone removal within the constrained, deep surgical corridor—specifically the maximal drilling between the bilateral vidian canals—to secure an adequate operative field, our current CT measurements did not specifically quantify the volume or extent of this required bone drilling. This unmeasured factor, combined with the narrower inter‐carotid space and reduced relative inter‐vidian distance identified in our study, likely contributes cumulatively to the overall surgical difficulty in GHoma cases. Second, although we have reported typical CT parameters and provided effect sizes with 95% confidence intervals to enhance the robustness of our findings, some clinical data were unavailable. Specifically, operative metrics such as operative time, blood loss, complication rates, and the extent of resection were not included. Future studies incorporating both radiological measurements and standardized operative metrics are warranted to clarify the clinical and surgical significance of these anatomical findings. Third, although we performed multivariable analyses adjusted for sex, other clinically relevant variables, including tumor size, Knosp grade or invasiveness, and biochemical severity of acromegaly, were not uniformly available for the entire cohort and therefore could not be incorporated into the multivariable models. Moreover, our cohort may be subject to selection bias inherent to a tertiary referral center, and the study exclusively involves patients deemed suitable candidates for EESS. Finally, further multi‐institutional studies involving larger, ethnically diverse populations and incorporating comprehensive operative metrics are warranted to validate and generalize these findings.

Conclusions

In this study, we retrospectively analyzed anatomical differences between patients with GHomas and those with non‐GHoma PitNETs. Our findings suggest that patients with GHomas have a broader and deeper nasal cavity and a significantly shorter inter‐carotid artery distance. These anatomical characteristics may be relevant to preoperative assessment and surgical planning for EESS.
