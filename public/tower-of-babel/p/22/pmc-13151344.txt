Maternal and infant predictors of early mother-to-child transmission among HIV-exposed infants in the Karamoja region, Northeastern Uganda 2020–2023, a retrospective cross-sectional study
Authors: Saadick Mugerwa Ssentongo, Susan Alwedo, Bonniface Oryokot, Ronald Opito, Lameck Bukenya, Simon Peter Ijala, Robin Chepukurui, Peter Sekiranda, Timothy Otaala, Rogers Kyagulanyi, Yunus Miya, Baker Bakashaba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite Uganda making significant progress in reducing mother-to-child transmission (eMTCT), from 20% in 2000 to 2.8% in 2021, regional differences in MTCT rates exist. The drivers of these differences remain unclear in some nomadic populations of the Karamoja region. This study aimed to determine the maternal and infant predictors of HIV infection among HIV-exposed infants (HEI) under 18 months of age in the Karamoja region, Northeastern Uganda.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cross-sectional study across twelve public health facilities in Karamoja, Uganda. Data on mothers and their HEI who had their first HIV nucleic acid amplification test at 4 to 6 weeks of age by either dry blood test or point of care testing were abstracted from the Early Infant Diagnosis (EID) register. The primary outcome was an HEI PCR test result (Positive or Negative). Firth’s penalized likelihood regression was used to determine factors associated with Infant HIV positivity. Data was analyzed using STATA version 15, and a p-value of < 0.05 was considered significant.ResultsA total of 223 mother-baby pairs were included in the analysis, with 52.9% (n=118) of the HEI being female, and the maternal median age was 27 (interquartile range 23-30). The Infant HIV positivity was 2.2% (n=5) and was significantly lower among mothers with no advanced HIV disease (stage 1 or 2)[adjusted odds ratio (aOR): 0.008, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.0002- 0.35 P=0.012], Mothers having a suppressed viral load (<1000 copies/ml) [aOR: 0.019, 95% CI: 0.0011- 0.36, P=0.008] and infants who received Nevirapine [aOR: 0.038, 95% CI: 0.0015 - 0.922, P=0.044].ConclusionThe results showed that maternal viral suppression, absence of advanced HIV disease, and infant receipt of nevirapine prophylaxis were associated with lower infant HIV positivity. Ensuring mother-baby pairs receive all the eMTCT services can avert new HIV infections among the HEI.Clinical trialNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13155-3.

Introduction

Mother-to-child transmission continues to be a global challenge in achieving HIV epidemic control. In 2023, an estimated 120,000 infants acquired HIV within 18 months postpartum, and 86% of Infants positive infants live in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) [1]. Furthermore, women and girls account for 63% of new HIV infections in SSA, highlighting the high risk of 25 − 24% of MTCT if no intervention is implemented [2].

Uganda is among the top ten countries with the highest burden of vertical transmission. Despite 78,482 pregnant women receiving antiretroviral Therapy (ART) for Prevention of Mother-to-Child Transmission (PMTCT), over 4,700 infections occurred among infants in 2023 [3].

To eliminate MTCT (eMTCT), the Uganda Ministry of Health transitioned from PMTCT Option A to Option B+, which offers superior operational and programmatic benefits. This transition has averted over 19,000 New HIV infections through PMTCT [3]. However, according to the national PMTCT evaluation report 2017–2019, the final vertical transmission rate, including during pregnancy, remains high at 2.2% for early MTCT, which is still above the expected United Nations Programme on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS) targets of < 2% transmission rates [3, 4].

Although Uganda has made remarkable progress in reducing the MTCT rates from 20% in 2000 to 2.8% in 2021 [5], several challenges continue to drive new infections among infants. These include gender based violence, incomplete antenatal care (ANC) attendance, Maternal seroconversions, and Mothers not being on ART [6–8]. These issues are more prevalent in the Karamoja region due to poor security attributed to cattle rustling, limited skilled human resources, and long distances to the facilities [9].

While several studies in Uganda have focused on understanding the PMTCT interventions and their impact on maternal and child health outcomes [10–13]few or no studies have investigated both infant and maternal determinants of early MTCT (Infant HIV positivity at 4 to 6 weeks of age) in this region [13, 14]. Understanding the contextualised drivers of these of HIV MTCT in this population is essential for achieving the global goal for the elimination of mother-to-child transmission of HIV [15]. This study aimed to determine infant HIV positivity (early MTCT rates) and its predictors among HIV-exposed infants in four districts of southern Karamoja, North Eastern Uganda.

Materials and methods

Study design

We conducted a retrospective cross-sectional study between 1st March 2025 to 14th March 2025 on all HEIs that had received the first polymerase chain reaction (PCR) test in the study sites. The findings were reported following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [16].

Study setting

The study was conducted in all twelve (12) accredited ART health facilities located in four districts of Southern Karamoja (Amudat, Napak, Nakapiripirit, and Nabilatuk Districts). The facilities were: Apetolim HC III, Irriri IV, Karita HC III, Lokales HC II, Lolachat HC III, Lorengedwat HC III, Nabilatuk HCIV, Nabulenger HC II, Nakapiripirit HC III, Namalu HC III, Tokora HC IV, and Lorengor HC III. These districts are home to pastoralist communities characterized by frequent movement and limited access to healthcare due to long distances and migratory behavior within the region [17].

These health facilities provide high-quality TB/HIV care and treatment services in accordance with the national HIV care treatment guidelines. The services provided at facilities are supervised by trained district HIV focal persons. The Karamoja region has an estimated population of 1.4 million people, with a growth rate of 2.4% to 4.2% [17]. The teenage pregnancy rate is at 3.4%, and it has the lowest HIV prevalence rate in Uganda at 2.1% [17, 18]. The region faces health-related challenges, including limited access to health services due to long distances, inadequate health infrastructure, and inadequate human and financial resources for health [19].

The study sites were purposively selected because they are the only accredited centers to offer HIV care treatment and PMTCT services in the Karamoja region. Each facility has a mentor mother and midwives trained to offer eMTCT services guided by the national comprehensive PMTCT curriculum [20]. In addition, each of these sites is an established mother-baby care point with a dedicated PMTCT focal person (clinic in charge) who is supervised by the Assistant District Health Officer in charge of maternal and child health.

Study population

This study was conducted among HIV exposed infants and their corresponding mothers receiving care at the respective study sites.

Inclusion criteria and exclusion criteria

We included all mother-infant pairs in the cohorts between 1st April 2020 and 30th September 2023, and the HEIs with a first DNA PCR result at 4 to 6 weeks of age. HEIs with no first DNA PCR test results were excluded from the analysis.

Minimum sample size estimation

The minimum sample size of 168 was calculated using Kish Leslie’s formula (1965) for the single population proportion formula, with a 95% confidence interval, a 5% margin of error, 10% non-response rate, and an expected 11% MTCT rate of HIV among exposed infants, based on the 2022 WHO prevalence of vertical transmission estimates [21]. To improve the precision of our estimate, we reviewed 228 records. After excluding 5 records, a final sample of 223 was included in the analysis.

Sampling procedure

We conducted a census of all mother-baby pairs within the selected health facilities during the study period.

Study variables

Exposures

The independent variables were divided into two main domains: maternal characteristics, including age, baseline ART regimen, WHO clinical staging, the most recent viral load before the first PCR done, and ART status during antenatal, delivery, and postnatal. Infant characteristics included sex of Infant, age at first PCR, Nevirapine prophylaxis uptake, type of test for the first PCR done, infant feeding practice, and cotrimoxazole received [22].

The dependent outcome was Infant HIV positivity at first PCR within 4 to 6 weeks of age (early MTCT). The HEIs were classified as positive or negative based on the documented testing result done either with dry blood spot or point of care EID testing, and with a confirmatory repeat Deoxyribonucleic Acid (DNA) PCR test done centrally at the National Central Public Health [23, 24].

According to Uganda national guidelines for Prevention and Treatment of HIV 2022, all HEIs are to be tested for HIV by DNA PCR using dried blood samples or point of care (POC) testing, for early infant diagnosis (EID) at 4 to 6 weeks of age, at 9 months of age, and 6 weeks after cessation of breastfeeding [23]. A confirmatory DNA PCR using DBS is required for all infants who test positively on the day they start treatment. HEI testing negative at first, second, and third PCR receives a final rapid HIV antibody test at 18 months of age [23, 24]. This waiting period reduces the possibility of false positives from maternal antibodies, which may occur in younger children [25].

Data collection

Data were extracted using a designed data abstraction tool, on both the independent variables and the dependent outcome variables, by the facility-level data clerks from the national EID registers at 12 health facilities in the four districts of southern Karamoja. Data on the socio-demographic and clinical variables for both mother and their HEI were collected and submitted to the Central Strategic Information Unit in secure, encrypted Excel files for analysis.

Data analysis

The collected data were entered into a Microsoft Excel sheet and then exported to Stata Statistical Software: Release 15 (College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC), where it was cleaned, validated, and analyzed [26]. Descriptive data were summarized as proportions for categorical variables, means and standard deviations for normally distributed continuous variables, and medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) for skewed continuous variables.

The association between HEI positivity at 1st PCR and categorical variables was determined using the Chi-square (Mother newly tested during breastfeeding and Mother newly enrolled on ART during breastfeeding) or Fisher’s exact test for all other variables. The associations between HEI positivity at 1st PCR and numerical variables were assessed using Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U. The Independent Variables with a p < 0.2 from bivariate analysis were included in a multivariate Firth’s penalized likelihood regression to establish predictors of HIV infant positivity. Due to evidence of quasi-complete separation and small sample size, we employed Firth’s penalized likelihood regression, which provided more stable estimates than standard logistic regression [27]. A p < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Recruitment of participants in the study

Of the 228 identified HEI, 5 had not undergone the first PCR due to loss to follow-up and death. A total of 223 HEIs were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1).

Socio-demographic characteristics of the mother of the HEI

The median age of the 223 mothers was 27 years (interquartile range 23–30), with the largest proportion (34.5%, n = 77) being aged 25–29 years. Over half of the mothers (57.4%, n = 128) were on a TDF/3TC/EFV regimen, and 99.2% (n = 221) were in WHO clinical staging 1 or 2. Most mothers (61.9%, n = 138) were not newly tested during breastfeeding. The vast majority (98.7%, n = 220) were on lifelong ART during antenatal, delivery, and postnatal, and 60.1% (n = 134) had initiated ART before the Breastfeeding period. Overall, 93.7% (n = 209) of the mothers had achieved recent viral suppression before the first PCR test was done.

Socio-demographic characteristics of the HEI

Among the 223 HEIs, the majority (52.9%, n = 118) were female and had received Nevirapine prophylaxis (95.1%, n = 212). The majority of HEI (92.4%, n = 206) were on exclusive breastfeeding, as shown in Table 1.

The prevalence of HEI positivity

An overall Infant HIV positivity at first PCR was 2.2% (n = 5) (0.93% − 5.3%).

Association between maternal clinical characteristics and infant HIV positivity at first PCR

In bivariate analyses, mothers not on ART during antenatal, delivery, and postnatal significantly had a higher proportion of HEI testing positive (66.7% versus 1.4%). Mothers with non-viral load suppression had more infants testing positive as compared to those with suppressed viral load (21.4% versus 1.0%), as shown in Table 2.

Association between infant clinical characteristics and Infant HIV positivity at first PCR

In bivariate analyses, HEI Infants who did not receive Nevirapine prophylaxis tested more positive as compared to those who received it (27.3% versus 0.9%). A significantly higher proportion of HEI who were on mixed/complementary feeding tested positive as compared to those on exclusive breastfeeding (17.7% versus 1.0%), as shown in Table 2.

Discussion

In this study, we aimed to determine the Maternal and Infant predictors of early mother-to-child transmission among HIV-exposed infants in the Karamoja region, northeastern Uganda, 2020–2023. We found that early mother-to-child transmission (Infant HIV Positivity) at first PCR was associated with advanced maternal HIV disease, maternal viral load suppression, and receiving Nevirapine by the HEI. These findings add to the existing literature on the drivers of mother-to-child transmission and the need to optimise both community and facility-based approaches for maternal ART adherence and MTCT prophylaxis interventions for mother-baby to close the quality gap in the PMTCT program to reduce the early transmission rates.

In this study, we found an early HEI positivity at first PCR of 2.2%, which is above the UNAIDS target of < 2% [3]. This is consistent with findings by the Uganda national PMTCT evaluation, which reported the early MTCT rate of 2.1% (95% CI: 1.4–3.2) at 4–12 weeks [28]. and across sub-Saharan countries [29]. This finding highlights the need to scale up PMTCT interventions and implement them with fidelity.

We also found that HEI born to mothers without advanced HIV disease had lower odds of HEI positivity as compared to those with it. This is similar to findings in studies done in East Africa [30], Ethiopia [31, 32] and Tanzania [33]. This is because mothers with advanced clinical stage have an impaired immunity system characterised by different opportunistic infections such as Tuberculosis, and lower CD4 count [34]. Consequently, they have a high viral load, which increases the risk of MTCT [35].

In this study, we found that HEI born to mothers with a suppressed viral load (< 1000 copies/ml) had lower odds of testing positive for HIV at 4 to 6 weeks of age. This is similar to the findings of a systematic review done in 41 sub-Saharan countries [33]. This is because when the mother is virally suppressed before conception, and maintained throughout pregnancy and breastfeeding, the baby is exposed to low volumes of viruses whilst in the uterus, during birth, and breastfeeding, making the risks for mother-to-child HIV transmission extremely low [36].

The HEI that had received Nevirapine had 96% lower odds of testing positive for HIV. These findings are consistent with a review done in East African countries, Ethiopia, and India, which found that HIV-exposed infants who were enrolled in care and had ARV prophylaxis were less likely to get HIV infection [30, 37] This could be due to the benefits of ARV acting as pre- and postexposure prophylaxis against HIV infection in infants, thereby reducing the risk of HIV transmission from mother to child [35].

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations, including the use of retrospective data, which is associated with incompleteness and missing variables, including some maternal sociodemographic variables. This analysis did not include data on the outcome of the infants at 18 months, and some of the infants could have seroconverted along the way. The small sample size and rarity of HEI positivity undermined the power of the study. However, this study has notable strengths, first being a multicenter study, done in all ART clinics offering PMTCT services in southern Karamoja, and despite the low number of WLHIV in this region due to low HIV prevalence, we included all mother-baby pairs in the analysis for over three years (2020–2023), strengthening the generalizability of the findings. Secondly, this study is one of the few studies done in a nomadic population, adding to the existing literature on predictors and determinants of early mother-to-child transmission of HIV in resource-limited rural settings.

Conclusion

Our study found that the absence of advanced HIV disease, a suppressed maternal viral load, and infant Nevirapine prophylaxis were associated with a low rate of early MTCT. To further reduce the MTCT, there is a need to implement multi-pronged strategies, including strengthening supply chain systems to ensure the uninterrupted availability of Nevirapine and ART, improved maternal ART initiation through scaling up differentiated service delivery models such as mobile EID clinics and community drug distribution points to reach mobile populations directly. Improving viral load suppression by enhancing ART adherence through peer support groups and using direct observed therapy models for pregnant and lactating mothers. We also recommend qualitative studies to understand barriers to nevirapine uptake. Implementing these tailored interventions is essential to overcome the challenges of resource-limited settings and reduce mother-to-child transmission of HIV to less than 2%.
