Sarcopenia and Cachexia in Older Patients with Cancer: Pathophysiology, Diagnosis, Impact on Outcomes, and Management Strategies
Authors: Efthymios Papadopoulos, Brian A. Irving, Justin C. Brown, Steven B. Heymsfield, Schroder Sattar, Shabbir M. H. Alibhai, Grant R. Williams, Richard F. Dunne
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Sarcopenia and cachexia are two common and overlapping but distinct muscle wasting syndromes that predict adverse outcomes and undermine quality of life among older adults with cancer. Despite their prognostic value and negative effects on older patients’ well-being, sarcopenia and cachexia are not routinely or adequately assessed and managed in clinical oncology practice. However, efforts to recognize and manage sarcopenia and cachexia at diagnosis and during follow-up may have beneficial effects on muscle mass, physical function, and quality of life among older adults with cancer, although evidence on long-term clinical outcomes in response to targeted interventions has yet to be established. This comprehensive review attempts to (i) delineate the differences in the pathophysiology and clinical manifestations between sarcopenia and cachexia, (ii) clarify how sarcopenia and cachexia are defined in the geriatric oncology literature, (iii) describe methods for assessing sarcopenia and cachexia in clinical practice, (iv) review the prognostic value of sarcopenia and cachexia among older patients, particularly those undergoing systemic cancer treatment, and (v) discuss evidence-based strategies aimed at managing sarcopenia and cachexia for older adults with cancer.

Key Points

Sarcopenia and cachexia are highly prevalent among older adults with cancer.Older adults with sarcopenia or cachexia undergoing systemic cancer treatment may have worse disease outcomes than those without sarcopenia or cachexia.Assessing and managing sarcopenia and cachexia in clinical practice is essential for optimizing patient care and potentially improving disease outcomes.

Introduction

Sarcopenia and cachexia are two distinct syndromes with significant overlap [1, 2] that have gained major attention in clinical oncology research over the past few decades [3–5]. In 1988, Dr. Irwin Rosenberg coined the term “sarcopenia” (from the Greek word “sarx/σαρξ,” meaning flesh, and “penia/πενία,” meaning poverty) to highlight the strong association between age-related declines in muscle mass, strength, and function to raise awareness within both the scientific and clinical communities [6, 7]. Over the last three decades, researchers and clinicians have feverishly investigated both the underlying pathophysiology and clinical implications of sarcopenia, leading to its recognition as a clinically relevant muscle disease (International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-10CM code M62.84) characterized by reduced muscle mass, muscle strength, and function [8]. Similar to osteoporosis, primary sarcopenia is characterized as the age-related loss of muscle mass, strength, and function, while secondary sarcopenia is due to secondary causes such as physical inactivity, malnutrition, including inadequate caloric and protein intake, and diseases such as acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, renal disease, and most notably, cancer [9]. However, some researchers argue that secondary sarcopenia in the context of cancer should be diagnosed as cachexia [1]. Cachexia (from the Greek word “kakos/κακός,” meaning “bad,” and “hexis/έξις,” meaning “state/condition”) is a disease-related metabolic disorder characterized by involuntary weight loss, including declines in muscle mass, and in many cases loss of fat mass, as well as impaired physical function [1, 4, 10]. Therefore, low muscle mass and function are key features of both sarcopenia and cachexia despite differences in the pathophysiology behind the two conditions [2, 4].

Studies in geriatric oncology report wide estimates of the prevalence of sarcopenia depending on the population and definition used [11–15], while ~ 52–66% of older patients have cachexia, which is predominately associated with upper gastrointestinal and lung cancer [16] and late-stage disease [16, 17]. Thus, the number of older patients suffering from sarcopenia and cachexia is expected to increase given the rapid growth of the aging population [18] and the high disease prevalence in older adults [19]. A preponderance of evidence suggests that sarcopenia [12, 20–23] and cachexia [16, 17] are each independent predictors of adverse outcomes in older adults with cancer, underscoring the need for effective management strategies [4, 5, 24].

In light of the exponential growth and interest in sarcopenia and cachexia research in oncology [3–5, 15, 25], in addition to the inconsistent and often confusing literature on the differences between these conditions among patients with cancer, the present review attempts to (i) provide an overview of the pathophysiology of sarcopenia and cachexia, (ii) describe how sarcopenia and cachexia are defined in the aging cancer population, (iii) review methods to assess sarcopenia and cachexia in clinical practice, (iv) discuss the prognostic value of muscle wasting syndromes among older adults with cancer, and (v) describe evidence-based strategies for the management of sarcopenia and cachexia after a cancer diagnosis.

Pathophysiology and Characteristics of Sarcopenia and Cachexia

The clinical manifestations of sarcopenia and cachexia on the skeletal muscle appear to be comparable relative to healthy controls. By definition, lean soft tissue (LST) mass (i.e., fat- and bone-mineral-free mass measured via dual X-ray absorptiometry), appendicular LST mass (i.e., the amount of LST mass in the upper and lower extremities), and muscle cross-sectional area (CSA) are lower in patients with sarcopenia or cachexia compared with healthy age-matched controls [26–29]. For example, older adults with sarcopenia exhibited ~ 25% smaller CSA of the thigh muscle using computed tomography (CT) compared with their non-sarcopenic counterparts [26]. Similarly, patients with cancer cachexia displayed ~ 12–39% smaller CSA of the quadriceps muscle than healthy controls as determined by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [27–29]. Moreover, these reductions in muscle mass likely contribute to impairments in physical function among patients with sarcopenia or cachexia. Indeed, compared with controls, individuals with sarcopenia or cachexia have lower grip strength [26, 30, 31], lower body strength [30, 32, 33], and slower gait speed [33–35]. Despite the comparable effects of sarcopenia and cachexia on skeletal muscle mass and function, the pathophysiology behind each condition is different. Below, we further characterize the similarities and differences between the sarcopenic and cachectic skeletal muscle and describe key physiological factors implicated in the development of muscle-related impairments. Composition of the Skeletal Muscle in Sarcopenia Versus Cachexia

At the myocellular level, the human skeletal muscle consists of three primary muscle fiber types: type I (slow-twitch, oxidative), type IIa (fast-twitch, oxidative-glycolytic), and type IIx (fast-twitch, glycolytic), which are classified on the basis of their myosin isoform expression, myosin ATPase activity, metabolic properties, size, and type of motor neurons innervating them [36–38]. Phenotypically, type I fibers express myosin heavy chain (MHC) I, have high mitochondrial and capillary density, high myoglobin content and oxidative capacity, are resistant to fatigue, exhibit slow speed of contraction, low specific tension, low power output, and smaller diameter compared with type II fibers [36, 37]. Type IIa fibers have high mitochondrial and capillary density, medium myoglobin content, intermediate speed of contraction, high specific tension, intermediate power output, high glycolytic capacity, are less resistant to fatigue and have a larger diameter compared with type I fibers [36, 37]. Type IIx fibers have low mitochondrial and capillary density, low myoglobin, high glycolytic capacity, very fast speed of contraction, high specific tension, high power output, exhibit the lowest resistance to fatigue, and have the largest diameter compared with type IIa and type I fibers [36, 37].

In the third decade of life, muscle mass starts to decline by approximately 3–8% per decade [39]. By the age of 70, it is estimated that muscle mass is decreased by 20–40% [40, 41]. Longitudinal studies suggest that the annual loss of leg lean mass among older men and women > 70 years old is approximately 0.8% and 0.7%, respectively [42, 43]. According to small-scale cross-sectional studies in humans, the skeletal muscle of older adults is characterized by a preferential atrophy of type II fibers [44–49]. Although both type IIa and type IIx fibers are affected by aging, when compared with type I, type IIx are the most adversely affected by age-related atrophy [47, 50]. The profound reduction in the CSA of type II muscle fibers may, in part, explain the reductions in muscle strength, speed, and power output [51, 52] observed in sarcopenia, as type II fibers contain more myosin cross-bridges per CSA of fiber and a higher maximum velocity of unloaded shortening and isometric force than type I fibers [53]. With respect to phenotypic characteristics with aging, it has been observed that the skeletal muscle is characterized by large groups of fibers that exhibit the same histochemical properties, a phenomenon known as fiber type grouping [54]. For example, it was demonstrated that 28.5% of muscle fibers in the vastus lateralis of older adults co-expressed MHC I and MHC IIA [48], a phenomenon that has been corroborated by additional work [54] and is primarily attributed to altered innervation as described below. In addition to muscle atrophy of type II fibers, older adults exhibit a lower number of muscle fibers (type I and type II) relative to younger adults [46]. This was observed in early studies where a 25–39% difference in the total number of muscle fibers was found between young and old subjects [44–46], suggesting that sarcopenia is a result of both muscle atrophy and loss of muscle fibers [52].

Cancer cachexia is associated with atrophy of both type I and type II muscle fibers [28, 29, 55–57]. Johns et al. found significantly smaller CSA of the rectus abdominis in patients with cachexia (mean age 63 years) with upper gastrointestinal cancer without evidence for preferential loss of a specific fiber type compared with non-cachectic controls with cancer (mean age 67 years) [55]. These observations have also been replicated when examining the vastus lateralis where patients with cancer cachexia demonstrated atrophy of both type I and type II muscle fibers compared with aged-matched healthy controls [29] and non-cachectic patients [56]. Even when younger patients with cachexia (mean age 52.5 years) were compared with slightly older healthy controls (mean age 57.9 years), the CSA of the vastus lateralis was smaller in the presence of cachexia irrespective of muscle fiber type [28], which could indicate that the underlying mechanisms driving muscle atrophy in cancer cachexia differ from sarcopenia. However, others corroborated declines in the CSA of type II but not type I muscle fibers in patients with cancer cachexia [58]. Regarding muscle fiber distribution, most studies did not observe a fiber type shift in patients with cancer cachexia [28, 55, 56, 58, 59], contrary to Taskin et al., who found increased MCH IIa and a smaller MCH I:IIa ratio in patients with cancer cachexia relative to non-cachectic and healthy control patients [60]. These disparate findings regarding non-selective versus preferential reductions in type I versus type II muscle fibers may be due to differences in patient populations, exposure to cytotoxic therapies, and/or interaction with sarcopenia-associated changes in muscle fiber atrophy in patients with underlying sarcopenia as cancer cachexia develops. Future studies employing single muscle fiber proteomics and phenotyping will likely lead to a better understanding of the independent and combined effects of sarcopenia and cachexia on muscle fiber characteristics.

Neural Factors in Sarcopenia Versus Cachexia

Neuronal innervation is essential for the structural and functional integrity of the skeletal muscle. Alpha motor neurons innervate muscle fibers of the same type, forming the following three types of motor units: (i) slow-twitch fatigue resistant (type S), (ii) fast-twitch fatigue resistant (type FR), and (iii) fast-twitch fatigue sensitive (type FF) [61, 62].

Motor unit loss appears to be an inevitable consequence of aging that alters the morphology and function of the skeletal muscle [63], thus contributing to sarcopenia. Compared with young individuals, older adults have approximately 40–50% fewer motor units by their seventh decade of life [64]. The aging-associated loss of motor neurons results in lateral sprouting of adjacent motor neurons in an effort to re-innervate denervated muscle fibers [64]. Denervated type II muscle fibers can be re-innervated by a type S motor neuron, which will result in co-expression of MHC isoforms (hybrid fiber), a phenomenon that has been demonstrated in human studies [48, 65]. However, not all denervated type II fibers will be re-innervated by neighboring motor neurons, resulting in atrophy and subsequent loss of these fibers [63, 66, 67], a bona fide characteristic of the sarcopenic muscle [66]. The neuromuscular junction (NMJ), which is the site of communication between alpha motor neurons and muscle fibers, is also involved in the development of sarcopenia [68–72]. Specifically, remodeling of the NMJ with aging includes altered morphology of the pre- and postsynaptic regions, decreased number of pre-synaptic vesicles containing acetylcholine receptors at the post-synaptic muscle membrane, decreased safety factor, endplate fragmentation, and impaired neuromuscular transmission [68–72], thus leading to impaired muscle fiber innervation and marked reductions in muscle strength and physical function.

Denervation-induced atrophy genes and muscle wasting have also been found in animal models of cachexia [73], which have been confirmed in humans [74, 75]. According to Daou et al., patients with cancer with stable weight and those with cancer cachexia displayed an increased number of muscle fibers in the rectus abdominis with central myonuclei by 34% and 50%, respectively, compared with cancer-free participants [74]. Displaced myonuclear positioning may be suggestive of muscle-related pathological conditions but may also be present during muscle regeneration [74]. However, in this study, patients with cancer cachexia had upregulated expression of molecular markers of denervation (e.g., neural cell adhesion molecule (NCAM) and histone deacetylase (HDAC)) in their muscle compared with patients with stable weight, with no evidence of muscle regeneration [74]. These findings suggest that altered innervation or loss of motor neurons contributes to muscle wasting and impaired muscle function in patients with cancer cachexia. Further work in this area found that tumor-derived factors, such as activin A and interleukin-6 (IL-6), induce expression of Noggin in the skeletal muscle (rectus abdominis), which subsequently inhibits bone morphogenetic protein (BMP), leading to impairments at the NMJ, denervation, and muscle wasting in humans with cancer cachexia [75]. However, Boehm et al. found that although patients with cancer cachexia had significantly higher muscle atrophy of the rectus abdominis compared with non-cachectic patients with cancer and age-matched cancer-free controls, no morphological differences were found at the NMJ among the three groups, suggesting that muscle-specific pathological manifestations in cancer cachexia are independent of alterations in motor neurons [76]. Nonetheless, it should be noted that patients in this study were eligible for definitive surgery, and thus, we cannot exclude the possibility that denervation and impairments at the NMJ are more profound in palliative patients with cachexia, particularly in response to systemic therapy [77].

Loss of Proteostasis in Sarcopenia Versus Cachexia

The ability to maintain protein homeostasis (i.e., proteostasis) declines with aging [78]. Two major systems that control protein degradation include the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) [79] and the autophagy-lysosome system [80], both of which are implicated in the development of sarcopenia and cachexia. The UPS facilitates the degradation of myofibrillar proteins through ubiquitination, a process that is orchestrated by specific enzymes including ubiquitin-activating enzymes (E1), ubiquitin-conjugating enzymes (E2), and ubiquitin ligases (E3) [81]. Autophagy also results in proteolysis via the transport of cytoplasmic damaged proteins and organelles to the lysosomes for degradation [82].

Data from human studies present mixed findings on the involvement of the UPS in age-related sarcopenia. Specifically, no differences were found in the expression of ubiquitin ligases, such as muscle-RING-finger protein-1 (MuRF-1) and muscle atrophy F-box (MAFbx) in the vastus lateralis between older (mean age 72.8 years) and younger (mean age 21.5 years) subjects [83], which was also corroborated by additional work in rats [84]. In another study, older women (aged 85 years) had higher mRNA levels of MuRF-1 and forkhead box (FOXO) 3, a transcription factor implicated in muscle atrophy [85], compared with younger women (aged 23 years) [86]. However, physically inactive, frail older women exhibited decreased expression of ubiquitin ligases and markers of autophagy by approximately 40% compared with their physically active counterparts [87]. Inhibited or defective autophagy/mitophagy is a hallmark of the sarcopenic muscle [80, 82, 88], leading to mitochondrial dysfunction, excess production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and inflammation, which collectively impact the function of satellite cells (SCs), and therefore, the regenerative ability of the skeletal muscle [80, 89]. However, basal muscle protein breakdown does not appear to be increased in older adults compared with their younger counterparts [90–92], suggesting that inhibition of autophagy may promote sarcopenia through other pathways, some of which are described below. For example, skeletal muscle protein synthesis is often lower in older adults compared with younger adults in the post-absorptive state [52, 93–95], but likely even more so in the post-prandial state [96, 97]. Unfortunately, reductions in protein synthesis with no change or slight reductions in protein breakdown can slow down skeletal muscle protein turnover, which likely leads to an accumulation of oxidatively damaged proteins that contribute to poor myocellular function [98]. Collectively, defective autophagy rather than a hyperactive UPS appears to contribute to sarcopenia in the absence of underlying disease.

This differs from cancer cachexia, where the UPS system appears to promote muscle catabolism [99–101]. Several studies in patients with cancer cachexia found increased expression of MURF-1 [58, 75, 102, 103] or atrogin-1 [58] in the skeletal muscle, as well as mRNA levels of ubiquitin [104] and higher chymotrypsin-like activity [105], which was correlated with disease severity, poor nutritional status, and higher age [105]. However, unchanged levels of MURF-1 [106, 107] and atrogin-1 [75, 102, 103, 107] have also been reported in cancer cachexia, suggesting the expression of specific atrophy-related genes as well as the potential involvement of other proteolytic pathways in the skeletal muscle breakdown among patients with cancer cachexia [108]. The autophagy lysosome system also appears to be involved in muscle wasting in patients with cancer, even prior to treatment initiation [109]. Specifically, proteases cathepsin B and L, both of which are involved in proteolytic degradation in the lysosome, were found to be increased by 90% and 115%, respectively, in the vastus lateralis of patients with esophageal cancer awaiting surgery compared with controls [109]. In line with these findings, patients with lung cancer prior to surgery exhibited higher mRNA levels cathepsin B but not cathepsin D relative to weight-stable controls [110]. Several studies have shown increased expression of autophagy-related genes in the skeletal muscle of patients with cancer cachexia compared with control participants [55, 103, 107, 111, 112], whereas others were not able to replicate these findings [58]. The divergent findings along with the observation of an increase in some but not all autophagy-related genes [55, 111] may be suggestive of increased autophagosome formation but impaired autophagosome elimination [111, 113]. Collectively, impairments in the UPS and autophagy are present in cancer cachexia and may contribute to the observed declines in muscle protein synthesis, as previously shown in humans [114, 115].

Mitochondrial Dysfunction and Oxidative Stress in Sarcopenia Versus Cachexia

Mitochondrial dysfunction is also implicated in the development of age-related sarcopenia [116, 117]. Skeletal muscle mitochondria are imperative for adenosine triphosphate (ATP) synthesis via oxidative phosphorylation, regulation of intracellular calcium levels [118], oxidative stress [119], redox balance [120], and apoptotic signaling for skeletal muscle remodeling [121]. The aged muscle is associated with lower rates of mitochondrial ATP production [122], oxidative capacity [123], and an accumulation of oxidatively damaged proteins [124]. Moreover, the severity of age-related impairments in mitochondrial bioenergetic function has been associated with lower physical function [125, 126]. Mitochondria content and quality are regulated by mitochondrial dynamics (fusion and fission) and autophagy/mitophagy [127]. Mitochondrial fusion is essential for maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis under metabolic and environmental stress, whereas mitochondrial fission is critical for quality control and mitochondrial division [127]. Autophagy/mitophagy is responsible for the clearance of damaged and dysfunctional mitochondria, a process that is important for maintaining muscle mass [82] and function [128]. Aging impairs mitochondrial fusion and fission, making mitochondria susceptible to damage due to metabolic and/or environmental stimuli [129, 130]. Additionally, inhibited or defective autophagy/mitophagy is a hallmark of the sarcopenic muscle, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and excess production of reactive ROS and oxidative stress [80, 82, 88].

Mitochondrial dysfunction is also present during cancer cachexia, as demonstrated in preclinical models [131–133] and humans [112]. Collectively, cancer cachexia is associated with lower expression of fission and fusion genes, potentially suggesting compromised mitochondrial biogenesis and function. Impairments in mitochondrial respiration, mitochondrial coupling, and mitochondrial content have been demonstrated in cachectic animal models [134], while disruption of mitochondrial structure has been observed in the skeletal muscle of animals and humans with cancer cachexia [112, 135]. As a result of mitochondrial dysfunction and impaired mitophagy, oxidative stress is increased in the skeletal muscle of patients with cancer cachexia, as demonstrated by higher levels of carbonylation and lower expression of antioxidant enzymes [58, 136]. In turn, oxidative stress can promote muscle atrophy and declines in muscle function [137]. However, higher levels of some antioxidant proteins have also been reported in the presence of cancer cachexia, which may be indicative of a balancing mechanism to offset the excess production of ROS within the mitochondria of the skeletal muscle [58, 113].

Satellite Cell Function in Sarcopenia Versus Cachexia

SCs reside beneath the basal lamina of the myofiber in a quiescent state. Perturbations to the sarcolemma and muscle damage stimulate SCs to proliferate, differentiate, and fuse with existing myofibers to support muscle growth and regeneration [138]. Aging is associated with a decline in SC content in type II muscle fibers in humans [139, 140], which appears to promote muscle atrophy as demonstrated in mechanistic work [141], thus contributing to sarcopenia. Additionally, the remaining SCs within the sarcopenic muscle appear to lose their regenerative capacity in response to muscle damage [142], as they transition from a reversible quiescent state to irreversible pre-senescence [143].

Muscle regeneration also appears to be impaired in cancer cachexia. As demonstrated in animal and humans, activated SCs are unable to differentiate in response to muscle damage, leading to incomplete muscle regeneration [144–146]. Additionally, it was demonstrated that overexpression of Pax7 by SCs was mediated by the nuclear factor kappa-beta (NF-κΒ) [144], a transcription factor with potent catabolic effects on the skeletal muscle, as further discussed below [147].

Inflammation in Sarcopenia Versus Cachexia

Aging is associated with chronic low-grade inflammation characterized by an increase in circulating pro-inflammatory cytokines such as C-reactive protein (CRP), IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) [148, 149]. Chronic low-grade inflammation is associated with a higher risk of several chronic conditions, including sarcopenia [148, 150]. Higher levels of CRP have been associated with lower fat-free mass [151] and muscle strength among older adults [152]. Similarly, elevation in IL-6 levels is associated with a decline in muscle strength [152] in older individuals and the presence of sarcopenia [153, 154]. However, the associations between IL-6 and fat-free mass, a proxy for muscle mass, were not corroborated by others [151]. The deleterious effects of IL-6 on the skeletal muscle have further been exemplified by mechanistic work, where treatment of IL-6 transgenic mice with anti-mouse IL-6 receptor antibody abolished the observed muscle atrophy and associated perturbations in proteolytic pathways [155]. Circulating levels of TNF-α are significantly higher in dwelling older adults with sarcopenia compared with their non-sarcopenic counterparts [153], and this increase in TNF-α has been associated with longitudinal declines in grip strength and thigh muscle area in older adults [156]. Proinflammatory cytokines activate NF-κΒ [157], which promotes muscle catabolism via proteolytic gene expression [81, 158, 159], inflammation, and impaired myogenic differentiation [147].

Inflammation has gained attention as a hallmark of cancer cachexia [160–162] while its deleterious effects on muscle mass and function have been recognized by several studies in clinical [56, 107, 163, 164] and preclinical [165] models. Evidence from a systematic review of 17 studies found elevated circulating TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-8 in patients with cancer cachexia compared with healthy controls, in addition to higher levels of IL-6 relative to non-cachectic patients with cancer [166]. Elevated CRP has also been reported in patients with cancer cachexia compared with non-cachectic patients [107] and healthy individuals [167]. In another systematic review, higher levels of inflammation, mainly measured via circulating CRP and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio were associated with low muscle mass in cross-sectional and longitudinal studies of patients with cancer [163]. Notably, elevated circulating proinflammatory cytokines in patients with cancer cachexia appear to co-exist with lower muscle strength [107] and higher inflammatory signaling in the skeletal muscle as demonstrated by higher mRNA of IκBα [107], TNF-α [168], and IL-6 [168, 169] compared with healthy controls. Mechanistic studies have revealed a direct effect of inflammation on the skeletal muscle, demonstrating IL-6-mediated atrophy of type IIb fibers with concomitant expression of mRNA atrogin-1 [165], in addition to downregulation of MHC in skeletal muscle induced by TNF-α and IFN-γ [170]. A plausible mechanism behind the catabolic effects of proinflammatory cytokines on the skeletal muscle involves the activity of NF-κB, which has repetitively been shown to induce muscle wasting, thereby contributing to cachexia [158, 159, 171–174]. The proteolytic effects of NF-κB primarily involve expression of atrophy-related genes (e.g., atrogin-1, MuRF-1, MAFbx) that facilitate proteolysis via the UPS [162, 175].

Hormonal Alterations in Sarcopenia Versus Cachexia

Age-related changes in growth factors and sex hormones are also involved in the development of sarcopenia in older adults [176]. Compared with younger individuals, older adults exhibit declines in circulating levels of anabolic hormones, such as insulin-like growth factor-I (IGF-I) [177, 178], testosterone [179], and estrogen [180]. IGF-I is a polypeptide hormone that promotes cell growth, survival, and proliferation in various tissues including the skeletal muscle [181]. The anabolic effects of IGF-I that promote muscle hypertrophy are mediated by the activation of the PI3K/Akt/mTOR [182–184], a pathway that is downregulated in states of muscle atrophy [185], including sarcopenia [186, 187]. Testosterone, a potent anabolic sex hormone, has repetitively been shown to increase muscle mass via activation of the Akt/mTORC1 pathway and proliferation of SCs [188–192]. Thus, the positive association between circulating testosterone and lean body mass [193] may be attributed to increased muscle protein synthesis and improved muscle repair mechanisms. In line with this notion, exogenous administration of testosterone improves lean body mass and may improve muscle strength in hypogonadal middle-aged men [194–196]. Estrogen deficiency has also been implicated in the development of sarcopenia in post-menopausal women [197]. Similarly with testosterone, estrogen stimulates proliferation of SCs [197, 198]. However, the age-associated decline in estrogen levels is associated with a reduction in SCs in both humans and animals, undermining skeletal muscle repair [197, 199] and muscle strength [200].

Hormonal alterations are also present in patients with cancer cachexia, and may explain, in part, the observed declines in muscle mass and function. Similarly with sarcopenia, lower circulating levels of IGF-I have been found in patients with cancer cachexia relative to non-cachectic or healthy controls [201, 202]. IGF-I was also found to be lower in the skeletal muscle of patients with gastric cancer irrespective of weight loss [203]. Additionally, cancer cachexia is associated with altered activation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, which may be precipitated by the aberrant inflammatory response [204–206] due to tumor- [207] and/or treatment-related factors [208, 209]. Overactivation of the HPA axis leads to higher secretion of glucocorticoids, through the action of corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) and adrenocorticotropin-releasing-hormone (ACTH), promoting metabolic and hormonal dysregulation [210–212] which negatively regulates skeletal muscle mass and strength [213, 214]. Glucocorticoids appear to directly induce muscle atrophy, primarily of fast-twitch glycolytic fibers studies (type IIb/IIx) in animal models [215–217], while in humans, glucocorticoids increase net protein catabolism [218] and inhibit muscle protein synthesis [219]. The catabolic effects of circulating glucocorticoids on the skeletal muscle are mediated via overexpression of atrophy-related genes followed by upregulation of the proteolytic UPS [217, 220], while the suppressive effect on muscle protein synthesis is explained by inhibition of mTORC1 [221], along with reductions in IGF-I [222, 223]. Moreover, glucocorticoids increase the expression of myostatin [217, 224, 225], a myokine that blocks myogenesis [217, 226–228] and facilitates muscle wasting via induction of the UPS in an NF-κB independent manner [229], while inhibition of myostatin enhances muscle hypertrophy [227, 230, 231] and function [227, 231]. Another hormone that appears to negatively regulate the skeletal muscle in an indirect manner is growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF-15), also known as macrophage inhibitory cytokine-1 [232]. GDF-15, whose levels rise in response to inflammation [233, 234] and cancer cachexia [235, 236], is associated with anorexia [237], thereby reducing energy intake leading to weight loss [235, 238] and muscle atrophy [238]. Notably, the catabolic effects of GDF-15 on the skeletal muscle may also be mediated via activation of the HPA axis and overexpression of glucocorticoids [10, 239].

Is it Sarcopenia or Cachexia? Diagnostic Criteria and Key Considerations for Older Adults with Cancer

Several reviews have recognized the significant overlap between sarcopenia and cachexia, as both conditions are characterized by low muscle mass and function [1–5]. The main difference between sarcopenia and cachexia is that the former is mainly a result of aging whereas the latter is caused by an underlying illness [1, 4]. Many older adults are already sarcopenic prior to a cancer diagnosis, after which treatment- and disease-related factors further worsen muscle mass and function promoting the development of cachexia [1, 240]. Theoretically, a non-cachectic older patient with a T2 stage cancer may present with physiologic aging-associated sarcopenia at diagnosis. Subsequently, cancer and its treatments can further decrease muscle mass and function [241] in the absence of cancer progression or cancer cachexia. However, cachexia can develop during the course of the disease, further exacerbating muscle wasting and function. In other words, a cancer diagnosis does not necessarily imply cachexia-induced muscle loss [242]. Collectively, low muscle mass and function in older adults with cancer can be attributed to several factors that include, but are not limited to, aging, physical inactivity, medications (e.g., corticosteroids), malnutrition, cancer and its treatments, or disease-related cachexia. Well-defined diagnostic criteria for sarcopenia and cachexia are essential for the accurate diagnosis and clinical management of each condition.

In the field of sarcopenia, the availability of several definitions and associated diagnostic cutoffs [9, 34, 243] may hinder the ability of clinicians and researchers to better understand the prognostic value of sarcopenia and develop effective therapeutic strategies [244]. The first definition of sarcopenia by the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People (EWGSOP) included the presence of low muscle mass in addition to either low muscle strength or physical performance [245]. In an effort to improve the understanding of sarcopenia on accumulating evidence, the same group (EWGSOP2) met again in 2019 and revised the definition of sarcopenia as follows: (i) low muscle strength via the grip strength test (men < 27 kg; women < 16 kg) or the chair stand test ( >15 s for five rises) is suggestive of probable sarcopenia; (ii) low muscle strength along with low muscle quantity (mass) or quality (often assessed through radiographic muscle attenuation) are confirmatory of sarcopenia; and (iii) sarcopenia and low physical performance (an objectively measured whole body function related with mobility [246]) are indicative of severe sarcopenia [9]. Some of the reasons for changing the order of the criteria from the original EWGSOP to EWGSOP2 (i.e., low muscle strength first followed by low muscle quantity or quality) is that low muscle strength is a stronger predictor of adverse outcomes than muscle mass [247, 248], in addition to the non-linear relationship between muscle mass and function as previously shown [42]. To promote awareness of clinicians and address sarcopenia across Asia, the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) defined sarcopenia as the presence of low muscle mass in addition to low grip strength (men < 28 kg; women: < 18 kg) or low physical performance, whereas deficits in all three characteristics is suggestive of severe sarcopenia [243]. The Sarcopenia Definitions and Outcomes Consortium (SDOC) developed a definition of sarcopenia that can be used by clinicians to identify older adults at risk for adverse outcomes [249]. The SDOC defined sarcopenia as the combination of muscle weakness via the grip strength test (men < 35.5 kg; women < 20 kg) and slowness (< 0.8 m/s), regardless of lean body mass, as lean body mass is a proxy of skeletal muscle mass and does not appear to be a predictor of adverse outcomes in older adults [34, 249]. The lack of international consensus on the definition of sarcopenia [244, 250], led to the Global Leadership Initiative in Sarcopenia (GLIS), whose purpose was to establish a global conceptual definition of sarcopenia via a Delphi study comprising 107 experts from sarcopenia societies and organizations [251]. According to the expert panel, the components of sarcopenia included (i) muscle mass (agreement: 89.4%), (ii) muscle strength (agreement: 93.1%), and (iii) muscle-specific strength (i.e., muscle strength/muscle size) (agreement: 80.8%) [251]. Notably, muscle quality commonly measured via fat infiltration in the muscle or muscle density was not included in the definition of sarcopenia, contrary to the EWGSOP2 diagnostic criteria of sarcopenia. The exclusion of morphological characteristics of muscle tissue from the definition of sarcopenia suggests that myosteatosis (i.e., the infiltration of fat in the skeletal muscle) should be recognized as a separate condition despite its association with sarcopenia [252] and its prognostic value in healthy and clinical populations [253–256]. Additionally, according to the GLIS, physical performance is an outcome but not a component of sarcopenia [251]. The next phase of GLIS will involve operationalizing a definition of sarcopenia that can be utilized by clinicians and researchers [251].

In geriatric oncology, several studies have assessed sarcopenia opportunistically solely on the basis of low muscle mass, often via computed tomography (CT) scans without considering muscle strength or physical performance [15]. The diagnosis of sarcopenia solely based on muscle mass may be insufficient given the prognostic value of muscle strength and physical performance in geriatric oncology. Older patients who exhibit low muscle strength and/or low physical performance have a higher risk of all-cause mortality [257] and treatment modification defined as reduced treatment intensity or transition from active treatment to supportive care [258]. Additionally, declines in muscle mass may not occur in a linear manner with declines in physical function [42]. Therefore, the assessment of muscle strength and physical performance, in addition to muscle mass, can further aid risk stratification, treatment decisions, and management strategies in geriatric oncology. Other studies in older adults with cancer have assessed sarcopenia using the EWGSOP2 [259, 260], AWGS [261, 262], or the SDOC criteria combined with CT-based muscle mass or density [263]. Overall, the term sarcopenia in the cancer literature has widely been used to describe the presence of low muscle mass and often function, regardless of the mechanistic cause behind these impairments. However, an attempt to understand the cause and relative contribution of each factor that negatively regulated skeletal muscle health in older adults with cancer, particularly those who had received or were receiving systemic treatment in these studies and in the absence of weight loss data, would be extremely challenging.

Unlike sarcopenia, a single definition of cachexia has prevailed in the geriatric oncology literature, which according to Fearon et al., includes the following criteria: (i) weight loss > 5% over the past 6 months; or (ii) > 2% weight loss in addition to either body mass index (BMI) < 20 kg/m2 or low muscle mass [264]. Several studies where the mean age of participants was ≥ 65 years adopted this definition to identify the presence of cancer cachexia [16, 17, 259, 265, 266]. To better characterize cachexia and identify downstream management strategies, the international consensus definition and classification of cancer cachexia recommends the assessment of the following factors: (i) anorexia and food intake; (ii) catabolic drive; (iii) muscle mass and strength; and physical and psychological functioning [264]. Assessing sarcopenia and cachexia in older adults with cancer may help clinicians with treatment personalization and downstream management strategies to foster patient care and support. Geriatric assessment (GA) may present an excellent opportunity to assess sarcopenia and cachexia at diagnosis [4]. Clinical practice guidelines by the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) recommend that all patients aged ≥ 65 years should have a GA to identify impairments in seven domains (physical function/performance, functional status, cognitive function, emotional health, comorbid conditions, nutrition, and social support) followed by GA-guided management [267]. GA domains overlap with measures that are used to diagnose sarcopenia (e.g., muscle strength) or characterize cachexia (e.g., food intake, weight loss, muscle strength, physical and psychological function). The assessment of sarcopenia should include both muscle strength and muscle mass, in line with EWGSOP2 [9] and the GLIS [251]. Physical performance may also be used to aid risk stratification given its prognostic value for older patients [257]. Cachexia can be assessed using the Fearon’s criteria while taking into considerations GA domains and inflammation to characterize cachexia [264]. While both sarcopenia and cachexia can be assessed at diagnosis and prior to treatment, oncology clinicians should be evaluating cachexia at follow-up visits by measuring changes in body weight. The next section describes common methods for assessing sarcopenia and cachexia in clinical practice.

Tools to Assess Sarcopenia and Cachexia in Geriatric Oncology

Several methods/tools are available to objectively assess sarcopenia and cachexia in clinical practice. However, each method has its own advantages and disadvantages, as presented in Table 2. Body composition, which is central to the assessment of sarcopenia and cachexia, can be assessed using various methods, from simple anthropometric measurements (e.g., height, weight, and BMI) to more advanced techniques that can differentiate and quantify different tissues. Dual X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) is considered the gold standard for assessing body composition in clinical practice [268, 269]. Using DXA, skeletal muscle mass can be estimated through total and appendicular LST mass in kilograms, which does not include fat or bone mineral content. Appendicular LST mass (ALST) can then be divided by the patient’s height in meters squared to derive an ALST index (ALSTI). Subsequently, clinicians can identify patients with low muscle mass using cutoffs per the EWGSOP2 [9] and AWGS [243]. Notably, the EWGSOP2 [9] and AWGS [243] use the term appendicular skeletal muscle (ASM) mass, which denotes ALST mass. Importantly, LST mass should be distinguished from the widely used term “lean body mass,” which comprises LST mass and bone mineral content [270]. Instead, lean body mass should be replaced with the term fat-free mass (FFM), which reflects the estimated mass of all non-fat molecules in the body [270]. Often it is unclear whether studies that examined “lean body mass” as an outcome measured FFM or LST. Therefore, we have retained the term “lean body mass” to describe the results of studies in the literature. However, future studies should use the terms FFM or LST instead of lean body mass depending on the outcome of interest. DXA also provides an assessment of fat mass and bone mineral density, both of which are of clinical relevance for older patients with cancer. A key consideration when using DXA is the patient’s hydration status, which has been shown to affect LST in healthy and clinical populations [271, 272]. Additionally, it is important to note that DXA-derived LST approximates muscle mass, unlike other methods for assessing body composition, which may explain, in part, the lack of an association between DXA-derived measures of lean mass and adverse health outcomes in older adults [34, 254]. In the absence of DXA-derived ALSTI, other methods can be used to assess muscle mass to diagnose sarcopenia and identify muscle depletion for patients with cachexia. However, such methods are lacking clinical consensus and well-defined cutoffs despite their wide use in research settings. A multitude of studies have assessed muscle mass using available CT scans that are part of standard of care for diagnostic and disease monitoring purposes [15, 273]. CT scans offer an opportunistic and accurate method for assessing muscle mass and muscle density, representing muscle quantity and quality, respectively. A common strategy to assess low muscle mass via CT scans involves quantifying the entire CSA of the skeletal muscle (cm2), most commonly at the midpoint of the third lumbar vertebra (L3), and subsequently calculating a skeletal muscle index (SMI) by dividing the CSA (cm2) of the skeletal muscle by the patient’s height in meters squared (m2) [274]. The SMI can then be used to identify patients with low muscle quantity or mass on the basis of previously established [275, 276] or new cutoffs using the receiver operating characteristic curve. It is important to note, however, that assessing the CSA of the skeletal muscle at the L3 level will only be possible for patients with an available abdominal CT scan (e.g., gastrointestinal and genitourinary cancers, as well as some patients with lung cancer). Other anatomical sites that have been used to assess low muscle mass include the twelfth thoracic vertebra (T12) and the third cervical vertebra (C3) for patients with lung cancer [277] and head and neck cancer [278], respectively, suggesting that low muscle mass is predictive of worse disease-free survival (DFS) [277] and overall survival (OS) [277, 278]. Quantifying skeletal muscle mass can also be performed using MRI, a method that strongly correlates with CT-based muscle mass measurement [279]. Bioelectrical impendence (BIA) is another method to assess FFM [280]. Although BIA is cost-effective, easy to use, and predicts adverse outcomes in oncology [281], its accuracy is largely influenced by hydration status, edema, and other factors [5, 281, 282]. Lastly, ultrasonography (US) is gaining attention as a valid and reliable tool for measuring muscle mass in older adults [283], including those with cancer [284]. The most common measures of muscle mass using US include muscle thickness and CSA of one or more muscle groups, including the rectus femoris, vastus intermedius, vastus lateralis, gastrocnemius medialis, and biceps brachii [284]. However, the accuracy of US largely depends on the technician’s experience [285, 286], while it has not become widely utilized for evaluation of muscle mass in clinical settings. An alternative and more novel approach for estimating whole-body skeletal muscle mass is the D3-creatine dilution method. The assessment of whole-body skeletal muscle mass using this method relies on the estimation of total creatine pool size following ingestion of an oral tracer of D3 creatine, as expertly reviewed elsewhere [287, 288]. In oncology, a cross-sectional study among 119 patients with colon cancer (mean age 56.6 years) demonstrated that whole-body skeletal muscle mass via the D3 creatine dilution method was highly correlated with CT-based CSA (m2) at the L3 level (r = 0.73) and DXA appendicular lean mass (kg) (r = 0.76) among men [289]. Among women, whole-body skeletal muscle mass based on the D3 creatine dilution method was moderately correlated with CT-based CSA (m2) at the L3 level (r = 0.45) and DXA appendicular lean mass (kg) (r = 0.50) [289]. However, adjustment for age, height, and weight decreased the strength of these correlations. D3-creatine-based whole-body skeletal muscle mass was also moderately correlated with gait speed (r = 0.43) and grip strength (r = 0.32) among men but not among women [289]. Although the D3 creatine dilution method has gained attention in research settings as an accurate and noninvasive technique for assessing whole-body skeletal muscle mass, its application in real-world clinical settings is currently limited. Muscle strength is included in the definition of sarcopenia [9, 243] and is also used to characterize cachexia [264]. Muscle strength in older adults with cancer is largely assessed using the grip strength test, which is an inexpensive, brief, and valid method for assessing clinically relevant weakness [290] and has been endorsed by the EWGSOP2 [9] and the AWGS [243]. A detailed description of how to conduct the grip strength test is described elsewhere [291]. The 5-times sit-to-stand test (5-STS) [292] is another test that is recommended by the EWGSOP2 for assessing muscle strength of the knee extensors by measuring the time required for a patient to rise from a chair five times without using their arms [9]. However, the AWGS classifies the 5-STS as a physical performance measure rather than a measure of muscle strength [243]. This is supported by evidence in older adults where the 5-STS exhibited stronger correlations with measures of physical performance and weaker correlations with grip strength [293, 294] and knee extension strength [292], suggesting that the grip strength test may be a more appropriate measure of muscle strength in clinical practice than the 5-STS. Isokinetic lower extremity strength can be accurately assessed using various isokinetic dynamometers [295, 296]. However, the use of isokinetic dynamometers for direct assessment of lower extremity strength is not practical, particularly in clinical settings, which has popularized the use of grip strength as a proxy for assessing whole-body strength.

Physical performance can easily be assessed in clinical practice using various tests, such as the 4-meter gait speed, the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB), and the Timed Up and Go test (TUG). The 4-meter gait speed is a rapid test that measures the time required for a patient to walk 4 m at their usual pace [246]. According to the EWGSOP2, a gait speed of ≤ 0.8 m/s is indicative of low physical performance [9], while a similar cutoff (< 0.8 m/s) has been proposed by the SDOC [34]. A recent report found that slow walking speed is predictive of several impairments in GA among older patients with cancer [290]. The SPPB is another widely used test of physical performance that includes three components: (i) balance; (ii) gait speed; and (iii) 5-STS [297]. Each SPPB component is scored on a scale from 0 to 4 (maximum total SPPB score: 12 points), with higher scores representing better physical performance. In geriatric oncology, a total SPPB score of ≤ 9 out of 12 points is conventionally used to identify older adults with low physical performance [258, 298]. However, a total SPPB score of ≤ 8 has been recommended by the EWGSOP2 [9]. The TUG is another brief test of physical performance. In short, the patient, from a seated position, is instructed to stand up from a chair, walk for 3 m at their usual speed, walk back to the chair, and sit down again [299]. According to the EWGSOP2, a cutoff of ≥ 20 s on the TUG indicates low physical performance [9].

In addition to objective measures of body composition, muscle strength, and physical performance, patient-reported outcomes may be used to aid clinicians with identifying patients at risk for sarcopenia or cachexia. The SARC-F is a brief, five-item questionnaire that can be used to screen for sarcopenia per the EWGSOP2. However, although the SARC-F is correlated with the SPPB [300] and other health-related impairments [301] in older adults with cancer, its performance in identifying sarcopenia is suboptimal (sensitivity: 28.9–55.3%; specificity: 68.9–88.9%) [302]. Another questionnaire that correlates with the SPPB and identifies functional limitations (sensitivity: 82%; specificity: 80%), and fallers (sensitivity: 63%; specificity: 76%) in geriatric oncology is the SarcoPRO [303], a 14-item questionnaire that was developed through interviews with patients with sarcopenia [304]. The SarcoPRO may be useful for evaluating impairments in physical function due to sarcopenia when the objective assessment of physical performance is not feasible [303]. In cancer cachexia, the Mini-Nutritional Assessment (MNA) [305] or its short-form (MNA-SF) [306] can be used to assess malnutrition, an important characteristic of cachexia, in addition to changes in body weight and BMI that can collectively aid diagnosis while informing patient care. To stage cachexia, Argiles et al. developed the CAchexia SCOre (CASCO) [307], which considers the following components: (i) body weight loss and composition; (ii) blood markers indicative of inflammation, metabolic abnormalities, and immunosuppression; (iii) self-reported physical performance; (iv) anorexia; and (v) quality of life [307]. The CASCO calculates a score that ranges from 0 to 100 to determine the severity of cachexia, which can inform treatment decisions [307]. A few years later, the same group developed the MiniCASCO (MCASCO), a more streamlined version of the original CASCO to allow for rapid staging of cachexia given its high correlation with the original version (r = 0.964) [308].

Prognostic Value of Muscle Wasting Syndromes Among Older Adults with Cancer

Sarcopenia and cachexia are muscle wasting disorders of clinical relevance among older adults with cancer. Sarcopenia defined per expert groups (EWGSOP [245], EWGSOP2 [9], AWGS [243]) has been shown to predict grade 3–4 adverse events with systemic therapy [260], postoperative complications [262], and 1-year mortality [20, 261] among older adults with solid cancers undergoing different cancer treatments. Most studies, however, have assessed sarcopenia using CT scans on the basis of low SMI. As mentioned earlier, the term sarcopenia in the oncology literature has widely been used to characterize low muscle mass, often combined with low muscle function, where the differentiation between aging-related sarcopenia and cachexia or the presence of the latter is obscure. Several studies suggest that CT-based sarcopenia per low SMI predicts any [309] and severe [310, 311] postoperative complications, DFS [312, 313], progression-free survival (PFS) [309], as well as short- and long-term survival [309, 310, 314, 315] in older adults in response to various cancer therapies. Similarly, cachexia using Fearon’s criteria [264] has been shown to be associated with dependence in instrumental activities of daily living [17] and mortality [16, 17] among older patients with cancer undergoing different treatments.

The impact of sarcopenia and cachexia may be more pronounced in older adults undergoing systemic therapy. As previously described, low muscle mass may alter the pharmacokinetics of chemotherapeutic drugs, which may increase the risk of toxicity while undermining treatment outcomes [5, 316–318]. Additionally, systemic therapies further deteriorate low muscle mass and function [241], thereby increasing the risk of adverse outcomes. Below we describe the impact of muscle wasting syndromes among older adults with cancer undergoing different types of systemic treatments with an emphasis on chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and targeted therapy.

Chemotherapy

Although several studies have examined the associations between muscle wasting syndromes and chemotherapy outcomes [316, 318–320], very few have exclusively focused on older adults with cancer. Evidence from a systematic review of primarily younger patients (< 65 years) suggested that patients with sarcopenia undergoing chemotherapy had more toxicities than their non-sarcopenic counterparts, which could be explained, in part, by the impact of low muscle mass on the pharmacokinetics of chemotherapeutic agents [316]. This is in line with a more recent systematic review where indices of low muscle mass via DXA or CT were associated with a higher risk of dose-limiting toxicities in adults with colorectal cancer receiving chemotherapy [321]. Promising evidence from the LEANOX trial revealed that dose-adjusting based on lean body mass led to a lower incidence of grade ≥ 2 oxaliplatin-induced peripheral neurotoxicity with no changes in OS compared with body surface area (BSA)-based dosing in adults (median age: 63 years) with low lean body mass undergoing adjuvant chemotherapy for colon cancer [322]. However, additional research is required to understand whether dose-adjusting based on LST mass leads to better chemotherapy outcomes than BSA-based dosing in older adults.

In geriatric oncology, a few studies have demonstrated associations between muscle wasting syndromes and adverse outcomes in response to chemotherapy. In a retrospective cohort study of 80 older adults (aged ≥ 75 years) undergoing palliative chemotherapy for pancreatic cancer, CT-based low SMI alone was not predictive of early treatment discontinuation or other outcomes [323]. However, cachexia per Fearon’s criteria was associated with a shorter time to treatment failure and worsened PFS [323] in the same cohort. Moreover, in a sub-analysis of this study, older patients with cachexia receiving gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel had a greater incidence of grade 4 neutropenia than those without cancer cachexia [323].

In addition to pretreatment sarcopenia or cachexia, the rate at which older adults lose muscle mass during chemotherapy appears to be predictive of adverse outcomes. Evidence from a retrospective cohort study of 150 older adults (mean age 71.1 years) undergoing neoadjuvant chemotherapy for locally advanced esophageal cancer suggested that major muscle loss defined as a 12% reduction in CT-based SMI was associated with significantly shorter OS without differences in disease recurrence compared with older patients with minor muscle loss [324].

Muscle wasting syndromes are also predictive of adverse outcomes in older adults receiving chemotherapy combined with other treatments. For example, low SMI based on a CT scan prior to treatment was associated with more treatment toxicities [325], worse DFS [315], PFS [326], and OS [315, 325, 326] among older adults undergoing chemoradiation. Additionally, CT-based SMI prior to treatment was found to be prognostic of PFS and OS among older adults (mean age 78 years) with large B-cell lymphoma receiving chemotherapy and rituximab [327]. Specifically, compared with older patients with normal CT-based SMI, those with low SMI were approximately two times more likely to experience shorter PFS and OS [327].

Most studies that used CT to assess muscle mass prior to chemotherapy did not examine objective measures of muscle function that appear to have prognostic value in older patients undergoing chemotherapy. For example, in a study of 103 older patients (mean age 70 years) receiving palliative chemotherapy, pretreatment grip strength but not muscle mass was positively associated with OS [328]. However, grip strength and/or muscle mass were not predictive of chemotherapy toxicity [328]. In another study of 85 older adults (mean age 79.1 years) with cancer, pretreatment low grip strength and/or low SPPB combined was associated with chemotherapy delay, but no associations were found with other outcomes such as dose reduction or chemotherapy completion [298]. Puts et al., however, found that low grip strength was a significant predictor of toxicity in older patients (mean age 74.1 years) receiving chemotherapy alone or combined with other treatments [329]. Additionally, gait speed was associated with a higher risk of grade 3–5 toxicities in a pilot study of older adults (median age 74.5 years) undergoing chemotherapy [330].

Collectively, low muscle mass along with objective measures of muscle strength and function due to sarcopenia or cachexia appear to be prognostic of clinically relevant outcomes in older adults receiving chemotherapy. However, the dearth of studies in the geriatric oncology population, along with the heterogenous definitions and cutoffs used, limit the understanding of the impact of sarcopenia and cachexia on specific clinical outcomes during chemotherapy, underscoring the need for further research. Future studies should integrate measures of strength and function when assessing muscle wasting, as muscle mass alone may not be sufficient to understand whether and how muscle wasting syndromes impact outcomes in older adults.

Immunotherapy and Tyrosine Kinase Inhibitors

Meta-analytic evidence suggests that CT-based low muscle mass is associated with worse disease control, PFS, and OS than normal muscle mass among adult patients receiving immunotherapy [331, 332]. However, evidence regarding the associations between muscle wasting syndromes and clinical outcomes in older adults with cancer undergoing immunotherapy is scarce. For example, of the 26 studies that were included in a meta-analysis on the impact of low muscle mass during treatment with immune checkpoint inhibitors [332], only one comprised mostly older adults (median age 74 years) [333]. The results of this study suggested that CT-based sarcopenia using low SMI at the L3 level was associated with lower objective response rate and worse PFS than normal SMI among older adults receiving pembrolizumab for advanced urothelial carcinoma [333]. Similarly, a recent study of 130 older men (mean age 74.9 years, range 70–86 years) undergoing programmed death-1 inhibitors for advanced squamous lung cancer demonstrated that sarcopenia based on CT-derived low SMI was a significant predictor of worse PFS and OS compared with non-sarcopenic patients [334]. Additionally, severe adverse events (i.e., grade ≥ 3) were more common in the sarcopenic group relative to older patients without sarcopenia (75.3% versus 64.9%) [334]. It is important to note, however, that the participants of this study had received prior treatment(s) and the average BMI of the overall cohort was 20.7 kg/m2 [334]. Therefore, it is possible that some participants of this study, in addition to aging-related sarcopenia, were also suffering from cachexia [334]. A lack of studies that formally assessed cachexia in older adults receiving immunotherapy is evident in the literature. In a recent meta-analysis that demonstrated the negative impact of cachexia on the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors, none of the included studies (n = 12) focused exclusively on older adults, although the average age among participants was >60 years [335]. A recent study of 3285 patients receiving immunotherapy, of whom 54% were aged ≥ 65 years, found that cachexia per Fearon’s criteria was associated with disability, hospitalization, and all-cause mortality, with age (≥ 65 years) being a significant predictor of disability in this cohort [336].

An emerging body of evidence suggests that low muscle mass is associated with treatment-related adverse events [337], toxicity-related dose reduction [338], shorter PFS [339–341], and OS [342] among adults treated with tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs). In older adults with cancer, however, the impact of muscle wasting syndromes on clinical outcomes during treatments with TKIs is poorly understood due to scarcity of evidence. In a study of 437 patients, of whom most were older, low muscle volume based on a CT scan prior to treatment was predictive of appetite loss (any grade), PFS, and OS [343].

Overall, these findings suggest that sarcopenia and cachexia may be prognostic of adverse outcomes in older adults receiving immunotherapy or targeted therapy, emphasizing the importance of future research in geriatric patients.

Evidence-Based Strategies for the Management of Sarcopenia and Cachexia in Older Adults with Cancer

Lifestyle interventions including exercise and/or diet have widely been used to manage sarcopenia [344, 345] and cachexia [346]. However, as previously described, the benefits of such interventions are more pronounced in individuals with sarcopenia but are less clear in patients with cancer cachexia [1, 346]. Additionally, several clinical trials aim to identify a pharmacological strategy for managing sarcopenia and cachexia, as described below [347, 348]. Exercise Training

Resistance training has gained attention as an effective strategy for improving muscle mass and function in sarcopenic individuals [349]. Recent global consensus recommendations suggest resistance training and power training 2–3 times per week at 40–80% of the one-repetition maximum (1-RM) for the management of sarcopenia [350]. Meta-analytic data suggest that exercise, particularly resistance training, improves appendicular skeletal muscle index, grip strength, gait speed [351], and quality of life [352] in older adults with sarcopenia. However, these observations come mostly from non-oncologic studies. The value of resistance training alone or combined with aerobic training in preserving or increasing muscle mass and strength has also been corroborated in studies of older men undergoing androgen deprivation therapy for prostate cancer, a population at risk of muscle wasting [353–356]. However, whether exercise training can improve muscle-related outcomes (e.g., mass and function) in the presence of cancer cachexia, particularly in older patients, is not well described, and overall, the effectiveness, acceptability, and safety of exercise during cancer cachexia require further research in high-quality trials [346, 357]. Additionally, whether resistance and/or aerobic training can improve long-term clinical outcomes among older adults in the presence of sarcopenia or cachexia is poorly understood due to the scarcity of evidence.

Nutrition

Several meta-analyses have examined the effects of various dietary interventions on muscle-related outcomes in oncology. Meta-analytic data in patients with pancreatic cancer demonstrated that omega-3 fatty acid supplementation may increase body weight [358]. However, as demonstrated by another meta-analysis, oral supplementation of omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids had no impact on body weight, lean body mass, and quality of life in patients with cancer undergoing different treatments [359]. Adequate protein intake is essential for promoting muscle protein anabolism, particularly in older adults with cancer undergoing systemic treatment [360]. The ESPEN guidelines recommend > 1 g protein/kg/day up to 1.5 g protein/kg/day for patients with cancer [361]. Recent meta-analytic data from 3701 patients with mixed cancers undergoing different treatments demonstrated that high-protein supplementation (≥ 10 g or protein per serving) mitigated weight loss (MD = 1.45 kg, 95% CI 0.42–2.48 kg), while 11 out of 13 studies found that declines in muscle mass were less pronounced in experimental participants receiving protein compared with controls [362]. Additionally, high-protein supplementation had a beneficial effect on grip strength, with mixed findings on quality of life or OS [362]. High-quality trials are needed to better understand the effects of protein and other dietary strategies that include but are not limited to omega-3 fatty acids, vitamins, and minerals on muscle-related outcomes in patients with cancer, particularly those with cancer cachexia [363, 364].

Exercise Training and Nutrition Combined

Multimodal lifestyle interventions consisting of exercise and diet are gaining attention due to their potential synergistic effects on muscle wasting syndromes in patients with cancer [365–367]. Results from the MENAC trial demonstrated that the combination of exercise (aerobic and resistance), an oral nutritional supplement with fish oil, and ibuprofen, stabilized body weight but had no impact on muscle mass or objective physical activity compared with usual care among patients with advanced lung or pancreatic cancer [367]. The MIRACLE trial also investigated the effects of a multimodal intervention on cachexia-related outcomes in patients receiving palliative chemotherapy [365]. The intervention involved ibuprofen (400 mg thrice/day), omega 3-fatty acids (1g twice/day), bojungikki-tang (a herbal formula, 3.75 g twice/day), an oral nutrition supplement (9.8 g of protein, 27 g carbohydrates, 6 g of fat, and 2 g of fiber twice/day), weekly aerobic and resistance training, psychiatric assessment, and nutritional counseling [365, 368]. The results revealed no differences in lean body mass and grip strength between groups [365]. However, it is important to note that this trial did not reach the target sample, which negatively impacted the statistical power of the study [365]. Additionally, challenges with studying nutrition interventions and recording exercise adherence may have masked the potential benefits of multimodal interventions for patients with cachexia, highlighting the importance of future adequately powered trials.

Pharmacological

Pharmacological interventions for managing sarcopenia [348, 369, 370] and cachexia [347] are currently being investigated by several trials. As thoroughly reviewed elsewhere [348, 369, 370], several pharmacological strategies aimed at preventing or treating sarcopenia have been examined, including myostatin inhibitors, angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, as well as hormonal therapies such as growth hormone, testosterone, dehydroepiandrosterone, and selective androgen receptor modulators (SARMs). Nonetheless, FDA-approved pharmacologic strategies for the treatment of sarcopenia are not currently available.

SARMs gained research attention in both sarcopenia and cachexia due to their skeletal muscle-selective anabolic effects via binding to the androgen receptor [348, 371]. In a 12-week phase II RCT of healthy older men and postmenopausal women (mean age 63.3 years), enobosarm improved lean body mass and physical function compared with placebo [372]. Evidence from another phase II RCT among patients with cancer experiencing weight loss showed that enobosarm led to increases in lean body mass from baseline [373]. Similarly, two phase III [374] RCTs of patients with advanced non-small lung cancer (NSCLC) found that enobosarm improved lean body mass relative to placebo with a comparable incidence of adverse events between groups [375]. Nonetheless, enobosarm did not reach FDA approval likely due to the lack of clinically meaningful improvements in functional outcomes [376]. The ability to stimulate appetite and increases in body weight is of paramount importance for patients with cachexia due to malnutrition and involuntary loss of muscle mass, which is often accompanied by loss of adipose tissue. Thus, ghrelin receptor agonists have gained attention in oncology due to their appetite-stimulating effects and increases in body weight [377]. Evidence from two randomized, double-blind, placebo controlled trials (ROMANA 1 and 2) demonstrated that oral administration of 100 mg of anamorelin (a ghrelin receptor agonist) for 12 weeks improved lean body mass and anorexia-cachexia symptoms with no differences in grip strength or 1-year OS compared with placebo among patients with advanced NSCLC [378]. Study participants from these two trials had the opportunity to participate in ROMANA 3, which involved an additional 12 weeks of the previously assigned treatment (anamorelin versus placebo) [379]. Results from ROMANA 3 showed that participants in the anamorelin group sustained the improvements in body weight and anorexia-cachexia symptoms without significant improvements in grip strength, in line with the findings of ROMANA 1 and 2 [379]. Importantly, adverse effects between anamorelin and placebo were not significantly different, although hyperglycemia appears to occur in ≤ 1% of patients taking anamorelin [378–380]. Anamorelin did not receive FDA approval for the management of cachexia, likely due to the lack of an effect on grip strength and OS [378, 381, 382]. Another pharmacologic agent with promising effects against cachexia is ponsegromab, a monoclonal antibody that inhibits GDF-15 [347, 383]. Recent findings from a phase II RCT revealed a dose-response relationship between ponsegromab and body weight, with higher doses inducing greater weight gain relative to placebo among patients with cancer cachexia [383]. Additionally, the highest dose of ponsegromab (400 mg) improved appetite, cachexia symptoms, lumbar SMI, and physical activity compared with placebo. Moreover, adverse events were not different between ponsegromab and placebo [383]. Lastly, recent evidence from a phase II RCT demonstrated that tocilizumab, an anti-IL-6 receptor antibody, significantly decreased muscle loss among patients with advanced pancreatic cancer receiving gemcitabine/nab-paclitaxel [384]. Although tocilizumab attenuated muscle loss and improved OS at 18 months, it was associated with a higher incidence of grade ≥ 3 treatment-related adverse events [384]. While progress on the development of pharmacological agents for managing cachexia has been made, no FDA-approved therapies are currently available.

Conclusions

Sarcopenia and cachexia are common muscle wasting syndromes with significant overlap among older adults with cancer. The assessment of both at diagnosis and during follow-up may assist clinicians with treatment decisions and downstream management strategies. Future studies should comprehensively assess the presence of sarcopenia and cachexia and their prognostic value, particularly in older adults with cancer undergoing systemic treatment. Additionally, high-quality trials aiming to identify effective pharmacological and non-pharmacological (e.g., exercise and diet) strategies for preventing or mitigating muscle wasting syndromes following a cancer diagnosis and during treatment are of clinical relevance to optimize patient care and support.
