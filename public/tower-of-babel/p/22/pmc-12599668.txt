Explore the relationships between gut microbiota and cardiomyopathies with the potential mediating effect of cytokines: A univariable and multivariable Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Ling Guo, Congying Ni, Jingtao Ma, Zhenli Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Emerging evidence suggests a potential link between gut microbiota and cardiomyopathies, but the causal relationships and the mediating role of cytokines remain unclear. We performed a two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis using genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary data for 211 gut microbiota taxa, 41 cytokines, and cardiomyopathy subtypes. Instrumental variables were selected at a genome-wide significance threshold (P < 1 × 10−5). Primary causal estimates were derived via inverse-variance weighted (IVW) regression, supplemented by sensitivity analyses. Multivariable MR was utilized to reduce bias from confounders. Mediation analysis tested whether cytokines linked gut microbiota to cardiomyopathies. Genetic liability in gut microbiota showed significant associations with cardiomyopathy risk. For dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM), genus Ruminococcaceae (OR = 1.24, 95% CI: 1.00–1.54, P = .045) and genus Flavonifractor (OR = 1.66, 95% CI: 1.15–2.35, P = .0067) increased risk, while family Peptostreptococcaceae was protective (OR = 0.71, 95% CI: 0.51–0.99, P = .041). For hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM), genus Enterorhabdus raised risk (OR = 1.53, 95% CI: 1.00–2.33, P = .049), whereas order Methanobacteria lowered it (OR = 0.69, 95% CI: 0.52–0.93, P = .013). For myocarditis, genus Faecalibacterium reduced risk (OR = 0.53, 95% CI: 0.33–0.85, P = .009), but genus Family XIII UCG001 increased it (OR = 2.06, 95% CI: 1.15–3.69, P = .015). Cytokines including IL-9 and MIP-1β showed protective effects for DCM, while pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and VEGF elevated myocarditis risk. Mediation analysis revealed cytokines did not mediate gut microbiota-cardiomyopathy pathways. Specific gut microbiota and cytokines exert causal effects on cardiomyopathy subtypes, independent of cytokine mediation. These findings highlight potential therapeutic targets and underscore the need to explore alternative mechanistic pathways.

1. Introduction

Cardiomyopathies consist of primary (genetic, mixed, or acquired) and secondary categories, which result in varied phenotypes including dilated, hypertrophic, and restrictive patterns, which often lead to progressive heart failure with significant morbidity and mortality.[1,2] Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is the most common primary cardiomyopathy, with a prevalence of 1:500 persons and associated with sudden cardiac death.[3,4] Moreover, Dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) is the leading indication for heart transplantation, with a prevalence of 1:2500. Myocarditis, peripartum cardiomyopathy and takotsubo cardiomyopathy are also known as acquired cardiomyopathies. The gut microbiome modulates host metabolism, immune responses, and cytokine production, which may indirectly influence cardiac remodeling and dysfunction.[5] However, establishing causal relationships remains challenging due to confounding factors inherent in observational studies.

Gut microbiota in the intestinal microecosystem can not only participate in the physical process of digestion and absorption, but regulate various physiological process of the host.[6] The gut microbiome also regulates the health of cardiovascular ecology. The dysbiosis of gut-heart axis, which a complex network between the gut microbiota, their metabolites, and the cardiovascular system, may result in systemic inflammation and endothelial dysfunction.[5] The cytokines also play an important role in gut-immune system crosslink, and they can be regulated by metabolites produced by gut microbiota, such as short-chain fatty acids and trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO).[5,7] At present, most investigators are trying to use gut hypothesis to explain the relationship between gut microbiota and cardiomyopathies.[8] However, research studies supporting the “gut hypothesis” thus far are associative in nature. In addition, the current association was mainly based on observational studies with limited sample size and the presence of confounders. As a class of small proteins with broad biological activity, cytokines play an important role in the inflammatory and anti-inflammatory process.[9] They have been explored in the pathological process of cardiomyopathies in several studies, in which the key role of TNF-α, INF-γand IL-βwere demonstrated.[10–12] Some gut microbiota may affect pro-inflammatory cytokines to promote the development of myocardial inflammation.[5] From this perspective, there is a necessary to clarify the possible mediating effect of the cytokines on the relationship between the gut microbiota and cardiomyopathies.

Mendelian randomization (MR), regarding genetic variants as instrumental variables (IVs), offers a robust framework to infer causality between different phenotypes.[13] Genetic variation follows the random segregation of alleles from parents to offspring and is determined at conception by genetic variation, thus MR analysis owns less confounding than traditional observational studies. Recent MR studies have explored gut microbiota’s role in several cardiovascular diseases (e.g., atrial fibrillation and heart failure), yet its direct impact on cardiomyopathies is still underexplored.[14–16] Similarly, cytokines, key mediators of inflammation, are hypothesized to link microbial dysbiosis to cardiac pathology, but causal pathways remain unvalidated. This study aims to evaluate causal effects of gut microbiota and cytokines on cardiomyopathy subtypes and bi-directional relationships using MR. Moreover, we explored the potential mediation mechanisms of cytokines under the circumstance that gut microbiota and cytokines were both significantly related to cardiomyopathies. By processing sensitivity analysis, we provide novel insights into the gut-heart axis, potentially informing preventive and therapeutic strategies for cardiomyopathies.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design

The study design was illustrated in Figure 1. The two-sample MR (TSMR) analysis was performed to detect the causal effects of gut microbiota and cytokines on the risk of multiple subtypes of cardiomyopathies, following by a reverse TSMR analysis. Subsequently, we performed univariate TSMR to detect significant risk factors for subtypes of cardiomyopathies, and performed multivariable MR (MVMR) to reconfirmed the significant gut microbiota and cytokines selected by the TSMR by confounding the significant risk factors of each cardiomyopathy. Potential mediation analysis of whether cytokines played a role in the pathway from gut microbiota to cardiomyopathies was also conducted. Sensitivity analysis was processed to assess MR results and detect their reliability at the meantime.

Flow chart of the study design.

2.2. Data sources

The gut microbiota taxa from the IEU Open GWAS database (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/) were utilized, involving ebi-a-GCST90016908 to ebi-a-GCST90017118 entities. Initially, there are 418 traits related to the gut microbiota, among which exist overlap. Thus, we selected the traits from the year of 2021, which was widely used. There is a total of 211 bacterial traits, including 131 genera, 35 families, 20 orders, 16 classes, and 9 phyla and deriving from large-scale, multi-ethnic genome-wide association study (GWAS) with a total of 18,340 participants.[17] The genetic data for 41 cytokines came from a dataset containing the meta-analysis summary statistics for a GWAS of inflammatory cytokines, which was without fitting body mass index as a covariate in the model.[18] We downloaded this dataset from https://data.bris.ac.uk/data/dataset/3g3i5smgghp0s2uvm1doflkx9x. The GWAS summary data of cardiomyopathies was from IEU Open GWAS database (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/), including hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (ebi-a-GCST90018861), dilated cardiomyopathy (ebi-a-GCST90018834), myocarditis (ebi-a-GCST90018882) and other or unspecific cardiomyopathies (finn-b-I9_CARDMYOOTH_EXNONE). We provided the detailed information in Table S1 (Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q555). The present study is based on publicly available GWAS summary statistics. Ethical approval has been granted for each GWAS studies.

2.3. IVs selection

For MR studies, the selection of IVs must satisfy the following 3 assumptions to obtain a reliable conclusion: genetic variants must be strongly associated with the exposure; they must not be related to any confounding factors of the exposure–outcome relationship; they must influence the outcome solely through the exposure, not directly or via other pathways.[19,20] Firstly, we selected the SNPs with genome-wide significance (P-value < 1 × 10–5) for gut microbiota, which is used for several studies.[21,22] In terms of cytokines, we tried different thresholds to extract significant SNP. After processing a threshold of 1 × 10–7, there existed no SNP for some cytokine traits; thus, we finally selected the SNPs with a threshold of 5 × 10–6 for genome-wide significance (P-value) so as to obtain enough instruments for each cytokine. To minimize the effect of linkage disequilibrium, only SNPs meeting the following 2 filter conditions will be involved: the distance between 2 SNPs > 10,000 kb; the r2 for linkage disequilibrium should < 0.001. 2.4. Bi-directional causality analysis

As Figure 1 displays, to estimate the causal effects of gut microbiota/cytokines on different types of cardiomyopathies, we performed TSMR analysis respectively. We regarded the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) approach as the main analysis method, which were expressed as odd ratios (ORs) with the 95% confidence intervals (CI).[24] Without horizontal pleiotropy, IVW can provide unbiased causal estimates. However, when the heterogeneity existed, we utilized the random-effect IVW as the analysis method, which can provide more conservative and robust estimates.[22] To evaluate bi-directional causation effects between gut microbiota/cytokines and each type of cardiomyopathy, we processed the reverse MR by using multiple types of cardiomyopathies as exposures and gut microbiota/cytokines as outcomes. In the reverse MR analysis, we selected the SNPs significantly (P-value < 1 × 10−5) associated with myocarditis, dilated cardiomyopathy, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, and other or unspecific cardiomyopathies respectively as IVs.

2.5. Risk factor identification for cardiomyopathies and MVMR analysis

To estimate the causal effects of 9 risks factors, related to body mass index, life styles, blood lipids and endocrinopathies (Fig. 1), on different types of cardiomyopathies, we performed TSMR analysis respectively. The detail of the data source can be found in Table S1 (Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q555). After screening the significant risk factors that may confound the effect of gut microbiota/ cytokines, we performed MVMR analysis, by adding the significant confounding risk factors, to reevaluate the causal effect of gut microbiota/cytokines on each subtype of cardiomyopathies, which could help us better understand the intricate interplay between the risk factors and gut microbiota/cytokines.

2.6. Potential mediation analysis

After the primary analysis (Section 2.4), we have explored whether there laid significant causal effects of different types of gut microbiota/cytokines on cardiomyopathies respectively. The primary analysis helped us identify cardiomyopathies-associated (positive) gut microbiota and cardiomyopathies-associated (positive) cytokines. Moreover, we identified the risk factors which may confound the results and processed MVMR to reconfirmed the significance and direction of the primary analysis. Based on the above analysis, we further processed a TSMR analysis to detect the causal effects of positive gut microbiota on positive cytokines. On the condition that gut microbiota had a causal effect on cytokines, we would subsequently detect whether cytokines acted as the mediation factors in the pathway from gut microbiota to cardiomyopathies.

2.7. Statistical analysis

As for heterogeneity, we utilized Cochran Q test by performing IVW and MR-Egger methods. In addition, we used MR-PRESSO and MR-Egger regression to test the horizontal pleiotropy. If there were significant outliers, MR-PRESSO was used to correct the horizontal plural effect. Scatter plots were displayed to visualize MR results and leave-one-out analysis was utilized to evaluate the effect of each SNP. A two-side P-value that less than the Bonferroni correction P-value was defined as statistically significant, which is .0012195 (.05/41) for cytokines and .000226 (.05/211) for gut microbiota. Bonferroni-corrected threshold < P-value < .05 was considered as suggestive association. Both P-value of IVW < .05 and the direction of IVW consistent with MR-Egger were selected as the condition of statistically significance.[25] All analyses were carried out using R software (version 4.4.1). The study was performed using the R-based packages, such as “TwoSampleMR.”

3. Result

3.1. Causal effects of gut microbiota and cytokines on multiple cardiomyopathy types

3.1.1. Dilated cardiomyopathy

A total of 6 gut microbiota types were significantly associated with DCM (Table S4, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q555, and Fig. 2). The details of 69 SNPs for 6 gut microbiota types are displayed in Table S5 (Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q555). As shown in Figure 2, MR analysis revealed that 4 gut microbiota types were associated with an increased risk of DCM from the genetic perspective, while 1 bacterial trait decreased such risk. For example, genus Ruminococcaceae (OR = 1.24, 95% CI = 1.00–154, P = .045) and genus Flavonifractor (OR = 1.66, 95% CI = 1.15–2.35, P = .0067) significantly increased the risk of DCM, while family Peptostreptococcaceae (OR = 0.71, 95% CI = 0.51–0.99, P = .041) decreased such risk. The traits named class Negativicutes and order Selenomonadales shared the same SNP group with a significant OR of 1.50. Among the 41 cytokines, IL_9 (OR = 0.80, 95% CI = 0.64–1.00, P = .047) and MIP_1B (OR = 0.89, 95% CI = 0.79–0.99, P = .032) significantly decreased the incidence risk of DCM by the method of IVW (Tables S8 and S9, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q555 and Fig. 3).

Mendelian randomization results of causal effects between gut microbiota and subtypes of cardiomyopathies.

3.1.2. Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy

A total of 8 gut microbiota types were significantly associated with HCM (Table S4, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q555, and Fig. 2). The information of 105 SNPs for 8 gut microbiota types is shown in Table S5 (Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q555). As shown in Figure 2, MR analysis revealed that one of the gut microbiota (genus Enterorhabdus) was associated with an increased risk of HCM from the genetic perspective. Genus Enterorhabdus (OR = 1.53, 95% CI = 1.00–2.33, P = .049) significantly increased the risk of HCM. Moreover, other 7 bacterial traits were found to decrease such risk, among which class Methanobacteria, family Methanobacteriaceae, and order Methanobacteriales shared the same SNP group with a protective effect on HCM (OR = 0.69, 95% CI = 0.52–0.93, P = .013). As for cytokines, G_CSF (OR = 0.56, 95% CI = 0.37–0.85, P = .005) significantly decreased the incidence risk of HCM in TSMR analysis (Tables S8 and S9, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q555, and Fig. 3).

3.1.3. Myocarditis

A total of 3 gut microbiota types were significantly associated with myocarditis (Table S4, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q555, and Fig. 2). The information of 34 SNPs for 3 gut microbiota types is shown in Table S5 (Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q555). As shown in Figure 2, MR analysis revealed that genus Ruminococcaceae UCG004 (OR = 0.52, 95% CI = 0.33–0.82, P = .005) and genus Faecalibacterium (OR = 0.53, 95% CI = 0.33–0.85, P = .009) were associated with a decreased risk of myocarditis from the genetic perspective. However, genus Family XIII UCG001 (OR = 2.06, 95% CI = 1.15–3.69, P = .015) increased such risk. As for cytokines, M_CSF (OR = 1.24, 95% CI = 1.01–1.53, P = .040), TNF_A (OR = 1.66, 95% CI = 1.09–2.54, P = .018), and VEGF (OR = 1.21, 95% CI = 1.02–1.44, P = .033) significantly increased the incidence risk of myocarditis (Tables S8 and S9, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q555 and Fig. 3).

3.1.4. Other and unspecified cardiomyopathies

A total of 6 gut microbiota types were significantly associated with other and unspecified cardiomyopathies (Table S4, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q555 and Fig. 2). The information of 91 SNPs for 6 gut microbiota types is displayed in Table S5 (Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q555). As shown in Figure 2, MR analysis identified 3 gut microbiota types which were associated with an increased risk of cardiomyopathies (other and unspecified) from the genetic perspective. Correspondingly, other 3 bacterial traits decreased such risk. For example, genus Paraprevotella (OR = 1.37, 95% CI = 1.06–1.77, P = .018) and family Porphyromonadaceae (OR = 1.87, 95% CI = 1.10–1.85, P = .039) significantly increased the risk of cardiomyopathies (other and unspecified). As for cytokines, IL_13 (OR = 1.16, 95% CI = 1.02–1.32, P = .026) and VEGF (OR = 1.16, 95% CI = 1.02–1.32, P = .024) significantly increased the incidence risk of cardiomyopathies (other and unspecified) (Tables S8 and S9, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q555 and Fig. 3).

3.2. Causal effects of cardiomyopathies on gut microbiota and cytokines

A reverse MR analysis was processed with multiple types of cardiomyopathies as exposures and 211 types of gut microbiota as the outcomes. When regarding DCM as the exposure, it displayed significant associations with the genetic-predicted quantity of 9 bacterial traits, among which it showed an almost positive effect instead of genus Coprococcus3 (OR = 0.94, 95% CI = 0.89–0.99, P = .014) and genus Holdemania (OR = 0.93, 95% CI = 0.87–0.99, P = .017). When regarding HCM as the exposure, it also displayed significant associations with the genetic-predicted quantity of 5 bacterial traits, among which it showed an almost negative effect instead of genus Oscillospira (OR = 1.06, 95% CI = 1.01–1.12, P = .017) and genus Terrisporobacter (OR = 0.95, 95% CI = 0.91–0.99, P = .010). When it comes to myocarditis, it displayed significant associations with the genetic-predicted quantity of 3 bacterial traits, among which the myocarditis showed negative effect on genus Oscillospira id.2064 (OR = 0.95, 95% CI = 0.91–1.00, P = .040), genus Parasutterella id..2892 (OR = 0.93, 95% CI = 0.87–0.99, P = .034), and genus Peptococcus id.2037 (OR = 1.06, 95% CI = 101–112, P = .017). At last, other or unspecific types of cardiomyopathies were identified to be associated with 10 bacterial traits including 6 negative association. The detail can be found in Tables S6 and S7 (Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q555), Figs. S1, S11 to S16 (Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q554).

Subsequently, a reverse MR analysis was processed with multiple types of cardiomyopathies as exposures and 41 types of cytokines as the outcomes. The results indicated that the DCM decreased the genetically predicted level of SCF (OR = 0.95, 95% CI = 0.91–1.00, P = .048), whereas the HCM increased the genetically predicted level of IL_7 (OR = 1.0411, 95% CI = 1.0028–1.0808, P = .035), IL_8 (OR = 1.0407, 95% CI = 1.0030–1.0799, P = .034), and IL_13 (OR = 1.04, 95% CI = 1.00–1.08, P = .044) (Fig. 3). As for other and unspecific types of cardiomyopathies, the outcomes of cytokines named IL_2, IL_8, MIP_1B, and SDF_1A were found to be negatively related to them in the reverse MR analysis. The detail can be found in Tables S10 and S11 (Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q555), Figs. S2, S19, and S20 (Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q554). After the above analysis, no bi-directional causal effects were observed between gut microbiota and cytokines, and cardiomyopathies.

3.3. Risk factor identification for cardiomyopathies and MVMR analysis

As the Figure 4 displays, the univariable MR analysis suggested a negative correlation between triglycerides/ T2DM and an increased risk of HCM. Hyperthyroidism was not included for the risk factor of myocarditis in the further mediation analysis for the direction of IVW was not consistent with MR-Egger, though it displayed significance in the IVW method. As for other subtypes of the cardiomyopathies, no risk factors were found associated with them. Subsequently, we performed MVMR analysis to reevaluate the positive gut microbiota and positive cytokines of HCM from the previous TSMR analysis by confounding triglycerides and T2DM. The result suggested phylum Proteobacteria, phylum Euryarchaeota, class Methanobacteria, order Methanobacteriales, and family Methanobacteriaceae were still significantly associated with HCM, and directions were consistent with the TSMR (Table 1). However, G_CSF lost significance after confounding triglycerides and T2DM.

3.4. Potential mediation analysis

As the previous steps showed, part of gut microbiota and cytokines had causal effects on different cardiomyopathy types, which indicated that cytokines may play a mediating role in the pathway from gut microbiota to different cardiomyopathy types. Such association can only be established on condition that gut microbiota was significantly associated with cytokines. The only significant cytokine of HCM – G_CSF – lost significance after confounding. Thus, we analyzed the mediation role of cytokines for myocarditis and DCM. However, no causal effects between gut microbiota and cytokines were found for myocarditis and DCM in the present study (Table S12, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q555), which indicated that the assumption of cytokines served as a mediator in the pathway from gut microbiota and these cardiomyopathies was invalid.

3.5. Sensitivity analysis

P values < 0.05 indicated the presence of heterogeneity when estimated by Cochran Q. For horizontal pleiotropy assessment using the Egger-intercept method, P > .05 indicated the absence of horizontal pleiotropy. The results of heterogeneity and horizontal pleiotropy tests of the primary analysis are presented in Tables S13 to S16 (Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q555). As displayed, we found no heterogeneity in our MR analysis results. It shows no evidence of horizontal pleiotropy, and MR-PRESSO method reassured such significance.

4. Discussion

Up to now, Luo et al[26] focused on 2 gut microbiota types and demonstrated shigella was significantly associated with risk factors for heart failure, including myocarditis and HCM, but not for DCM. Moreover, Qi et al[27] explored but found no significant causal relationship between DCM and IL-18 as well as IL-27. However, there are no study processing systematic analysis towards the relationship between cardiomyopathies and gut microbiota traits (cytokines traits) of a large amount. As we have known, there are no studies exploring the causal relationship between cytokines and myocarditis. Moreover, we explored how the abundance expression of gut microbiota taxa was associated with cardiomyopathies and assumed that cytokines may mediate the pathway between gut microbiota and cardiomyopathies to clarify the hidden mechanism.

In a recent study, specific microbial-metabolite features have shown promising diagnostic utility in discriminating DCM.[28] In our study, we analyzed the potential relationships between DCM and gut microbiota, and have identified several significant bacterial traits, such as genus Ruminococcaceae and genus Flavonifractor. However, the scientific researches of relationships between DCM and gut microbiota are still rare. Thus, further studies were necessary to validate such associations. In our study, high level of IL_9 and MIP_1B were evaluated as protective factors for DCM. Correspondingly, IL-9 was previously proved to be an increased risk for coronary heart disease and a protective factor for viral myocarditis, which is generally reported to mediate allergic and autoimmune diseases.[29,30]

HCM is known as a genetically heterogeneous cardiac muscle disorder.[31] Several studies[32,33] suggested that obesity and AF are causally associated with an increased risk of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy by the method of MR analysis. However, there are less studies focusing on the causal relationship between the gut microbiota taxa and HCM. The present study initially revealed that 7 bacterial traits owned negatively causal relationship with HCM based on a relatively large scale of gut microbiota, while 1 performed a positive relationship. More importantly, this study revealed the critical role of gut microbiota in HCM. Clinically, the findings offer new approaches for early warning and intervention of developing HCM through the modulation of gut microbiota. As for cytokines, only G-CSF was identified as a protective factor towards HCM. However, it was detected to be increasing among HCM patients in the previous study.[34] Consistently, the G-CSF lost significance in the MVMR analysis.

In our study, we identified triglycerides and T2DM were negatively associated with the risk of developing HCM. Correspondingly, a recent study revealed both cardiac troponin T and myosin heavy chain mutant may cause lower level of triglycerides in hearts of HCM mouse models, which could contribute to impairment of energy metabolism and diastolic dysfunction.[35] Moreover, Hajouli et al[36] demonstrated T2DM is associated with worsened clinical outcomes in hypertrophic HCM patients and Badran et al[37] demonstrated T2DM is linked to more deterioration of cardiac mechanics and contributes to unfavorable consequences in HCM. However, these studies didn’t reveal the relationship between T2DM and HCM from the gene level and the toxicity of high blood glucose may have led to these consequences. The prospective clinical study is still needed to demonstrate whether the susceptibility to T2DM will decrease the incidence of HCM.

In terms of myocarditis, Ozkan et al[38] revealed that gut microbiota were also associated with it. MR analysis in the present study indicated that genus Ruminococcaceae UCG004(id.11362) and genus Faecalibacterium (id.2057) could decrease the risk to develop myocarditis. Correspondingly, Warmbrunn et al revealed genus Ruminococcaceae was protective against heart disease, which is also protective factors for pulmonary arterial hypertension.[39,40] Moreover, our first discovered genus Faecalibacterium and genus Family XIII UCG001 can affect the myocarditis significantly from the genetic perspective. Three cytokines were found to increase the risk of myocarditis. TNFα, identified as a risk factor for myocarditis in the study, is a potent inflammatory cytokine implicated in cardiovascular inflammation by inducing multiple inflammatory cytokines via NFkB mediated mechanism, whose level was ever found increasing among myocarditis patients.[34] Moreover, M-CSF induction causes excessive macrophage infiltration leading to inflammation. Correspondingly, Matsumori et al[34] found an increasing of M-CSF in patients with myocarditis, which also displayed a positive relationship with myocarditis in the present study. Though there’s less study involving the association between VEGF and myocarditis, the MR analysis provided a potential to further explore the detail pathway about the above cytokines.

We also explored the causal relationships between gut bacterial traits and other or unspecific cardiomyopathies. After screening, order Selenomonadales, family Porphyromonadaceae, family Oxalobacteraceae, and genus Paraprevotella may increase the incidence of cardiomyopathies, whereas genus Bifidobacteri, class Actinobacteria, family Veillonellaceae were protective factors. Among the above traits, shift in family Porphyromonadaceae was previously found to be associated with cardiac hypertrophy in hypoxic rats. We further identified the risk role of IL_13 on cardiomyopathies, which is signaling in cardiac regeneration and repair.[41]

Recently, the direct roles of the diet, type 2 diabetes mellitus and physical activity in the composition of gut microbiota has been demonstrated.[42–44] However, there’s no study exploring the effect of cardiomyopathies on gut microbiota. In terms of cytokines, it may be much more complex to identify the specific factors that may directly affect the expression of them. In the present study, the reverse causal analysis screened out both significant effects of cardiomyopathies on gut microbiota and cytokines using the reliable IVs. As we have known, we firstly demonstrated cardiomyopathies may cause the potential change of gut microbiota and cytokines from the genetic perspective. Nevertheless, such relationships are still needed to be validated in the feature researches.

Though the present study has explored the causal effects of subtypes of cardiomyopathies on gut microbiota and cytokines by reverse MR, there existed no bi-directional causal relationship between cardiomyopathies and gut microbiota (cytokines) according to the results. What’s more, we found no mediating effect of cytokines between the gut microbiota and 3 subtypes of cardiomyopathies. The limitations of our study are as follows. Firstly, the cases of cardiomyopathies, especially HCM, were insufficient. Moreover, population of our study analyzed was mainly European for cardiomyopathies, which may limit the generalizability of conclusions. Secondly, gene-environment interactions (G × E), the interplay of genetic variation with environmental factors, have a pivotal impact on human complex traits and diseases.[45] Thus, external factors—such as diet, environment, or life style—may influence both genotypic and phenotypic data, possibly confounding the results of our analysis. However, we identified the significant risk factors for subtypes of cardiomyopathies and processed the MVMR to reconfirmed the primary results, which may reduce part of bias in the present study. To be noticed, MR analysis can’t fully eliminate confounding or reverse causation in the same way as randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and cannot entirely rule out the potential impact of horizontal pleiotropy on our results.[46] Nonetheless, it does provide an approach that can reduce bias to a certain extent. Future studies combining MR with environmental exposure data (e.g., fecal metabolomics, longitudinal diet records) may disentangle genetic versus modifiable risk factors. Thirdly, the range of gut microbiota traits involved in the study is not exhaustive, indicating the need for further exploration using more gut microbiota taxa and cytokines with more depth and breadth. Lastly, the mechanisms of how gut microbiota and cytokines affected the cardiomyopathies remained to be further explored though no cytokines were identified as a mediating factor in this study. However, when it comes to the SCFA produced by gut microbiota, reviews from Zeng et al[47] and Hu et al[48] stated SCFA are widely acclaimed beneficial effect against cardiovascular diseases (CVD), including atherosclerosis, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and obesity. Moreover, genetic perturbations may inadvertently affect SCFA production, potentially confounding the proposed cytokine-centric mechanism. Thus, it’s necessary to further explore whether metabolite, especially SCFA, plays a mediating role between the gut microbiota and the presence of the cardiomyopathies.

5. Conclusion

In this study, we comprehensively explored the causal effects between gut microbiota, cytokines, and cardiomyopathies. We identified both positive and negative causal effects between gut microbiota and subtypes of cardiomyopathies, as well as cytokines and subtypes of cardiomyopathies. However, we found no bi-directional causal effects between the gut microbiota, cytokines and cardiomyopathies. Triglycerides and T2DM were negatively associated with HCM. Moreover, the cytokines perhaps did not play a mediating role in the pathway from gut microbiota to myocarditis, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and dilated cardiomyopathy. Future studies need to be implemented to validate these findings and exploring additional factors that may mediate the relationship between gut microbiota and cardiomyopathies.
