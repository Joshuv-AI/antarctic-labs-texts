Microbial biomarkers and sex-associated gut microbiota characteristics of thyroid cancer
Authors: Juxiang Gou, Ziyi Hu, Ming Cai, Shirong Zhang, Shiyu Zhou, Xianqiong Feng, Yali Tian, Ka Li, Xiuying Hu, Xinxia Liu, Xiaofang Pei, Lvbo Tian, Haojiang Zuo, Mei Rosemary Fu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Microbial biomarkers have emerged as promising tools for early cancer detection and disease monitoring. This study aimed to investigate microbial biomarkers and sex-associated differences in gut microbiota associated with thyroid cancer (TC). A total of 268 participants were recruited: 60 females with TC, 26 males with TC, and 182 healthy controls. The patient and healthy groups were comparable in terms of age and sex ratio. We first compared the gut microbiota between the patients and healthy participants, followed by sex-specific analyses: female patients vs. female controls, and male patients vs. male controls. Predictive models were used to explore the role of gut microbiota in predicting TC. Correlations between gut microbiota and T stage (i.e., T1 to Tx) were also investigated. The alpha and beta diversities differed between the patients and healthy participants. Sex-specific analysis demonstrated that Blautia and Alistipes were the dominant bacteria shared by both female and male patients. A 25.0% overlap in dominant bacteria was found between the male and female patients. The unique dominant bacteria in female patients constituted 43.8% (7/16) and mainly included Schaalia, Moraxella and Alicyclobacillus. In male patients, the unique dominant bacteria constituted 55.6% (10/18) and included Holdemanella, Clostridium_sensu_stricto, and Senegalimassilia. Regardless of sex, Catenibacterium was the distinguishing feature between patients and healthy participants (area under the curve [AUC] = 0.911). When combined with Aquabacterium and Dialister, the predictive accuracy increased (AUC = 0.992). Male and female patients exhibited both shared and distinct sex-specific microbiota compositions, potentially influenced by hormonal regulation, immune responses, and metabolic differences. These variations may contribute to differences in TC susceptibility and progression. The findings underscore the need for further research to confirm the functional significance of the identified microbiota and explore their potential clinical applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-14986-0.

Background

The prevalence of thyroid cancer (TC) continues to increase worldwide [1, 2]. According to 2022 data from the National Cancer Center of China, TC was the second most frequently occurring cancer in people aged 15–44 [3]. Notably, while early-stage TC is More commonly diagnosed in women with a female-to-male ratio of approximately 4:1 [4], the incidence of late-stage TC is nearly equal between female and male patients [5]. However, the reasons behind this sex-based disparity in early-stage TC remain unclear [4].

Several factors may account for this disparity. Differences in access to and use of healthcare may partially explain the higher frequency of early-stage TC diagnoses in women [6]. Physiological factors, such as genetic [7, 8], hormonal variations [9], and a significantly higher incidence of thyroid nodularity (38.5% in women vs. 26.0% in men, may also contribute [10]. Furthermore, enhanced immune surveillance in women is assumed to act as a protective shield against TC development [11, 12], serving as a target for prevention [13]. This highlights a possible link between sex-specific cancer susceptibility and host immunity, which may intersect with gut microbiota.

Recent advances in 16S rRNA gene sequencing have enabled investigations into the microbiota-thyroid axis [14, 15]. Gut microbiota can influence thyroid hormone levels by regulating iodine uptake, degradation, and enterohepatic cycling through the hypothalamic-pituitary axis [16, 17], and laso shape immune response to TC [18, 19]. Yet, sex-specific differences in TC-associated gut microbiota remain poorly understood. Increasing evidence indicates that such differences may modulate host immunity, hormone metabolism, and cancer susceptibility, thereby contributing to TC risk and progression in a sex-specific manner [20, 21]. Identifying microbial biomarkers and sex-associated gut microbiota characteristics of TC may therefore provide insights for effective prevention, early detection and management.

To address these gaps, this study aimed to: 1) determine the shared and unique gut microbiota characteristics of female and male patients with TC; 2) examine the correlations between gut microbiota and clinical characteristics; and 3) identify potential microbial markers for TC detection.

Methods

Study design, setting, and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted at West China Hospital, a Class A tertiary general hospital in Sichuan, Southwest China, from March 2020 to March 2021. Participant recruitment is detailed in Fig. 1. We consecutively recruited adult patients suspected of having TC before biopsy or surgery.

Study Flowchart. TC: thyroid cancer

The inclusion criteria for patients with TC were: (1) diagnosed of TC with the primary tumor site in the thyroid gland and scheduled for surgery; (2) able to understand the informed consent process; and (3) willing to participate voluntarily. Exclusion criteria included: (1) severe mental illness or cognitive impairment; (2) oral diseases such as gum inflammation or ulcers; (3) recent dental restoration (within 6 months); (4) antibiotic or microbiota-regulating drug use within 2 weeks [22]; (5) recent gastrointestinal infections (within 2 weeks); (6) history of digestive tract surgeries, such as esophageal, gastrointestinal, or gallbladder surgeries; (7) other severe systemic diseases, such as malignancies or uremia. The TC removal criteria were: (1) patients who did not undergo surgery; (2) patients who withdrew from the study during the study; or (3) those in whom the postoperative thyroid-related pathological diagnosis did not confirm the diagnosis of TC.

The inclusion criteria for the healthy controls (HCs) were: (1) no systemic diseases (e.g., hypertension, diabetes, or other chronic conditions); (2) no thyroid disease or prior TC history; (3) no history of oral surgeries or oral diseases (e.g., periodontal disease, dental caries); and (4) no other concurrent tumor diseases. The exclusion criteria for the HCs were as follows: (1) individuals who were pregnant or breastfeeding; (2) individuals with histories of antibiotic, prebiotic, or probiotic intakes within the preceding month; (3) individuals with histories of gastrointestinal infections within the preceding month; (4) individuals who had undergone esophageal, gastrointestinal tract, gallbladder, or other gastrointestinal surgeries; and (5) individuals with severe mental illnesses or cognitive impairments.

Initially, a total of 102 patients with suspected TC were recruited. Based on histopathological results, 86 patients were confirmed to have primary TC, while 16 were excluded owing to benign nodules confirmed by biopsy. We selected 182 HCs from the broader community as controls, ensuring a posteriori matching after the TC group was identified. The two groups had similar age and sex distributions, with participants aged 30–50 years and females comprising approximately 65–75% of the total population.

Ethical consideration

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (IRB Number: 2021 − 0362). The study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Rules of Good Clinical Practice. All participants provided written informed consent.

Stool sample collection

Trained healthcare staff collected stool specimens from the participants. Stool specimens were collected from patients with TC prior to their surgical procedures [25]. Fresh fecal samples were collected, temporarily stored at − 20 °C, and transferred to − 80 °C for long-term storage [26].

DNA extraction and 16S rRNA gene sequencing

The Mag-Bind® Soil DNA Kit (M5635; Omega Bio-tek, GA, USA) was used to extract total DNA from the samples, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Extracted DNA was quantified using a fluorescence spectrophotometer (E6090 QuantiFluor; Promega, Madison, WI, USA). The integrity and purity of the DNA were determined using 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. Amplification of bacterial 16 S rRNA gene fragments (V3–V4) was performed using the universal primers 338 F (ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA) and 806R (GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT) [27–29]. PCR was performed using the ABI 2720 Thermal Cycler (Life Technologies Corporation), which was selected for its reliability and precision in thermal cycling, crucial for high-fidelity DNA amplification. We used Q5 High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (NEB, MA, US) to ensure accurate amplification, executing approximately 25 cycles in the first round of PCR and approximately 10 cycles in the subsequent round.

After gel recovery and purification using an AxyPrep DNA Gel Extraction Kit (AP-GX-500, Axygen, MA, US), each specimen was quantified using a BioTek microplate reader (Flx800; BioTek, VT, US) in conjunction with a Quant-iT PicoGreen dsDNA Assay Kit (P7589; CA, US). The Illumina TruSeq DNA library creation protocol was followed to construct the required library. The diluted and quantified mixed library (10 nM) was processed on an Illumina Novaseq 6000 PE250 sequencing platform, using the standard procedure of Shanghai Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Subsequent sequencing data were analyzed using an -omics analysis platform (Biostack, Qingdao, China) with an integrated pipeline tailored for swift amplicon data analysis [30–32].

We employed effective procedures that our team has used successfully to prevent potential contamination during microbial testing [33–35]. All personnel were trained to strictly follow standardized protocols to reduce variability and prevent contamination in 16 S rRNA sequencing. All personnel strictly followed protocols, including using sterile equipment and reagents, employing high-quality DNA extraction kits, setting negative controls, and separating workspaces for DNA extraction, PCR setup, and post-PCR analysis. We also routinely decontaminate equipment and surfaces with appropriate cleaning solutions, and UV-treat rooms and equipment to destroy contaminant DNA [36].

Data analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± SD and categorical variables as frequencies (%). Fisher’s exact test was used to analyze categorical variables. The Student’s t-test and Mann-Whitney U test were used to estimate continuous variables. The threshold for statistical significance was set at a P < 0.05 with a 95% confidence interval (CI). In the context of microbiota-related data analysis, P values were adjusted to account for the false discovery rate (FDR), with statistical differences deemed significant at an FDR value of < 0.05 [25].

After demultiplexing, the resulting sequences were quality-filtered using fastp (version 0.19.6) [37] and merged with FLASH (version 1.2.11) [38]. Then, the high-quality sequences were de-noised using the DADA2 [39] plugin in the Qiime2 (version 2020.2) [40] pipeline with recommended parameters, which obtains single-nucleotide resolution based on error profiles within samples. DADA2-denoised sequences are usually called amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). To minimize the effects of sequencing depth on alpha and beta diversity measures, the number of sequences per sample was rarefied to 31,770 (Figure S1). Taxonomic assignment of ASVs was performed using the Naive Bayes consensus taxonomy classifier implemented in QIIME2 and the SILVA 16 S rRNA database (v138).

Alpha diversity metrics, including Chao1, ACE, Shannon, and Simpson, were used to assess and compare species richness and evenness. The sample size of each group was determined using cumulative curve analysis. Microbial composition analysis was depicted using a Venn diagram and a structure diagram at the genus level. To estimate the similarities among groups, beta diversity was measured via principal coordinate analysis (PCoA). The discriminatory taxonomic characteristics between patients with TC and HCs were investigated using linear discriminant analysis (LDA) coupled with effect size measurement (LEfSe). Microbial taxa with LDA scores of > 2 and P values of < 0.05 were considered significantly different. A random forest model was constructed to explore the potential discriminatory power of the gut microbiome for differentiating patients with TC from HCs. Model performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The ASV database was randomly split into two subsets for two-fold cross-validation, with one subset used for model training and the other for validation (testing). Correlations between gut microbiota and patient characteristics in the context of TC were analyzed using Spearman heatmaps [25]. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 23.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, United States) and R software (version 4.0.3).

Results

Participant characteristics and gut microbiota sequencing depth

The study included 86 TC patients (69.8% female) and 182 HCs (75.3% female). There were no significant differences in age, sex ratio, or body mass index (BMI) (Fig. 1; Table 1). For each fecal sample, 31,770 reads were randomly selected for downstream analysis.

Patients with TC showed significantly reduced microbial richness (Chao/ACE indices, adjusted P < 0.001; Figs. 2A-B), whereas diversity indices (Shannon/Simpson) between groups (Figs. 2C-D) compared to HCs. These patterns were consistent in sex-specific analyses (Figs. 2E-L).

A sex-specific analysis showed that Blautia and Alistipes were the common dominant bacteria shared by both female and male patients with TC. The dominant bacteria shared between female patients with TC and all patients with TC was 56.3% (9/16), and between male patients with TC and all patients with TC was 44.4% (8/18). The overlap in the dominant bacteria between female and male patients with TC was 25.0% (4/16). The unique dominant bacteria in female patients with TC comprised 43.8% (7/16) and included Schaalia, Moraxella and Alicyclobacillus. In male patients with TC, the unique dominant bacteria comprised 55.6% (10/18) and included Holdemanella, Clostridium_sensu_stricto, and Senegalimassilia (Fig. 4).

Gut microbiota for thyroid cancer prediction

To explore the role of gut microbiota in predicting TC, we randomly divided the patients with TC and the HCs into two equal groups. One half was used as the training set and the other was the testing set. Catenibacterium alone could distinguish patients with TC from HCs, regardless of sex (AUC = 0.911, 95% CI = 0.857–0.964; Fig. 5A). Catenibacterium differentiated female patients from female HCs (AUC = 0.902, 95% CI = 0.844–0.960; Fig. 5B) and male patients from male HCs (AUC = 0.938, 95% CI = 0.870–1.000; Fig. 5C). Catenibacterium, Aquabacterium and Dialister together distinguished patients with TC from HCs (AUC = 0.992, 95% CI = 0.982–1.000; Fig. 5D). Catenibacterium, Gemmobacter and Dialister together differentiated female patients from female HCs (AUC = 0.993, 95% CI = 0.977–1.000; Fig. 5E). Catenibacterium, Kroppenstedtia and Holdemanella together distinguished male patients from male HCs (AUC = 0.993, 95% CI = 0.977–1.000; Fig. 5F).

Discussion

The relationship between TC and gut microbiota remains unclear [41–43], particularly regarding microbial biomarkers and sex-specific features. This study is the first to identify specific microbial markers associated with TC in a Southwest Chinese population.

Regional variations in gut microbiota of patients with TC

We observed decreased microbial richness in all patients with TC, as shown by the Chao and ACE indices. However, no significant variations were observed in the Shannon and Simpson diversity indices. A similar study from Shanghai, in Southeast China, concluded that patients with TC had reduced gut microbiota richness and diversity [43]. Conversely, two previous studies from the Shandong Peninsula [42] and Harbin [41], in Northeast China, reported findings that were inconsistent with our results. These studies reported higher microbial richness in patients with TC compared to HCs [41, 42]. It is well known that dietary habits can substantially influence the microbiome [44]. Regional dietary differences may explain the conflicting results, while unassessed iodine status could also be a potential influencing factor [45]. Our participants were recruited from Sichuan Province, a representative inland region in Southwest China [46, 47]. Historically, Sichuan was iodine-deficient [48], however, following the implementation of universal salt iodization (USI) in 1995, the iodized salt coverage rate has reached 97.21%, and the adult population in Sichuan now has a median urinary iodine (MUI) level of 172.1 µg/L [49], indicating that Sichuan meets the WHO standards for iodine sufficiency (100–199 µg/L) [50]. In comparison, northeastern provinces have MUI levels ranging from 137.9 to 164.65 µg/L, and southeastern cities Like Shanghai report an MUI of 164.15 µg/L [49]. All regions mentioned are considered iodine-sufficient, yet they differ in regional and environmental factors [44, 51]. Future research should further explore the influence of dietary and iodine status on gut microbiome.

TC-associated microbial signatures

Tumor stage correlations

In this study, we observed increased levels of Blautia, Neisseria, Peptostreptococcus, Alistipes, Bacteroides, and Streptococcus in patients with TC. These bacteria were also positively correlated with T stage. However, bacteria enriched among our HCs, such as Catenibacterium and Akkermansia, were negatively correlated with T stage. Similar results were found in previous studies, for example higher levels of Blautia were found in patients with TC, and were positively linked to lipid metabolites [41]. Increased levels of Neisseria, Streptococcus, and Bacteroides were also observed in TC group [42, 43]. Notably, Blautia has also been linked to autoimmune thyroid diseases [52] and breast cancer [53]. Collectively, patients with TC in higher T stage in our cohort had increased levels of Blautia, Neisseria, and Peptostreptococcus, indicating their potential involvement in TC progression.

Sex-specific microbiota

Regarding sex-specific bacteria among patients with TC, gut microbiota alterations have been increasingly recognized as potential biomarkers for cancer diagnosis and prognosis, given their role in immune modulation, metabolic pathways, and disease progression [54–56]. In our exploratory analysis, Blautia and Alistipes emerged as the dominant bacteria common to all of our patients with TC. Only a 25% overlap in dominant bacteria was observed between female and male patients. Female patients exhibited distinct enrichment of Schaalia, Moraxella and Alicyclobacillus, while male patients showed higher abundance of Holdemanella, Clostridium_sensu_stricto, and Senegalimassilia. Previous studies have reported Schaalia in female-predominant urinary microbiomes [57], and linked Holdemanella to male-specific immune responses [58], and androgen modulation in prostate cancer [59]. Larger and longitudinal studies are needed to validate sex-related microbiota differences and clarify their potential role in TC pathogenesis.

Diagnostic potential of microbial biomarkers

Our study demonstrated that Catenibacterium showed high diagnostic accuracy for TC (AUC = 0.911 overall; 0.902 in females, 0.938 in males). Catenibacterium, scientifically known as “Catenibacterium mitsuokai”, is a Gram-positive bacterium. This bacterium is routinely detected in the gut microbiota of various fauna, including humans [60]. Catenibacterium’s ability to utilize glucose to generate short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) has been demonstrated in previous research [60]. Yu et al. inferred that a reduction in SCFA-acid-producing bacteria may promote the progression of TC [43]. Notably, gut microbiota-derived SCFAs can regulate the immune system and trigger cell death in colon cancer cells [61, 62].

It should be noted that in real-world clinical practice, many thyroid lesions are classified as follicular thyroid cancer (FTC) or indeterminate lesions, for which accurate preoperative diagnosis remains challenging. To evaluate microbiota as a universal diagnostic marker for TC, it is essential to clarify whether these microbial changes also occur in FTC and indeterminate lesions. A recent large-scale Mendelian randomization study [63] provided robust evidence for causal associations between specific gut microbes and both PTC and FTC. Specifically, Bifidobacterium longum was identified as the primary causal taxon for PTC, while Bacteroides was Linked to FTC. Key metabolites, including sphingomyelin and 2-hydroxysebacate, were also found to mediate these associations. Future research is needed to ascertain whether distinct microbial signatures may be related not only to PTC but also FTC.

Additionally, we found that the inclusion of Aquabacterium and Dialister along with Catenibacterium improved the predictive power for TC diagnosis, achieving an AUC of 0.992. The use of three bacterial genera for prediction is an effective strategy, as the addition of multiple biomarkers can increase the robustness of the model. In practical applications, having a larger number of microbial biomarkers could complicate predictive modeling. Fewer microbial markers, Like the combination of 1–3 genera, could facilitate more efficient and cost-effective methods, such as multiple quantitative PCR testing, to assess TC risk. Furthermore, Holdemanella was identified as a significant genus unique to male patients with TC, underscoring its potential role in male-specific microbiota alterations related to TC. Although research on Catenibacterium and its potential roles in immunity and cancer continues, our study provides initial evidence for a link between decreased levels of SCFA-producing bacteria, such as Catenibacterium, and TC. These findings suggest that targeted modulation of gut microbiota may become a promising therapeutic approach for TC. Although sex-stratified models showed high AUC values, independent validation cohorts are required to confirm their generalizability. The findings of our study demonstrate that gut microbiota may serve as a potential biomarker for TC in Southwest China.

Microbiota - PTC causality

Findings of our study demonstrated significant differences in gut microbiota between patients with PTC and HCs. Nevertheless, the causal relationship between gut microbiota dysbiosis and PTC development remains a long-standing “chicken-or-egg” dilemma. A recent Mendelian randomization study identified Bifidobacterium longum as a causal protective taxon for PTC, mediated by sphingolipid metabolism, suggesting that gut microbes may influence oncogenesis [63]. Pro-inflammatory microbiota (e.g., Clostridium enrichment) could promote chronic inflammation and DNA damage, creating a tumor-permissive microenvironment [64]. Tumor-derived factors (e.g., thyroid hormones) may alter gut ecology, as observed in advanced PTC with reduced microbial diversity [43]. Moreover, iodine intake has been shown to influence both thyroid function and gut microbiota composition, indicating that alterations in iodine levels may contribute to gut microbial shifts in patients with PTC [65]. While microbial dysbiosis likely both precedes and results from PTC, the strongest evidence currently points to a feedforward loop, in which early dysbiosis may contribute to tumor initiation, and tumor progression further exacerbates microbial imbalance.

Strengths and limitations

Our study has several notable strengths. First, to the best of our knowledge, this study represents the largest comprehensive profiling of TC in Southwest China and demonstrates the abundance of specific microbes that correlate with T stage in both male and female patients with TC. Our study used a considerably larger sample size compared to comparable previous studies (268 vs. 65 [41], 74 [42], and 180 participants [43]). Second, our study focused on the differences between female and male subgroups to verify the efficiency of our established microbial models, thereby enhancing the reliability of our findings and strengthening the capacity of the models to differentiate between male and female patients from their respective HC counterparts. The default rarefaction depth is generally set at 10,000 reads per sample [66]; however, we randomly selected 31,770 high-quality reads from each sample (Figure. S1) for our analysis, which potentially offers a higher-sensitivity platform for assessing microbiota [67].

It should be noted that our study had some limitations. First, this was a single-center cross-sectional study, which limited its ability to investigate temporal changes in the microbiota or alterations that may occur after the development of cancer. Therefore, a multicenter and longitudinal study with a larger sample size is warranted. Second, some of our findings related to alpha diversity contradict those of two previous studies in Northeast China [41, 42], but align with the results of another study conducted in Southeast China [43]. Such discrepancies may be due to different geographic locations and dietary habits of the study populations. To counter these limitations, the use of shotgun metagenomics may prove beneficial. In addition, we applied stringent exclusion criteria to minimize confounding factors across both the TC and HC groups. Participants from both groups were excluded if they had recently used antibiotics or gut microbiota-regulating supplements, or had a history of gastrointestinal or other severe diseases to ensure a homogenous study population. Despite these efforts, other variables such as healthcare system utilization, dietary patterns, and socioeconomic conditions could still have influenced the gut microbiota. Future research should take these considerations into account.

Conclusions

Patients with TC demonstrated different alpha and beta diversities in their gut microbiota compared to HCs. Blautia and Alistipes were the dominant bacteria found in all patients with TC, regardless of sex. However, only 25.0% of the dominant bacteria were common between female and male patients with TC in our study cohort. Specifically, females had unique strains including Schaalia, Moraxella and Alicyclobacillus, whereas males had Holdemanella, Clostridium_sensu_stricto, and Senegalimassilia. Notably, Catenibacterium effectively differentiated patients with TC from healthy individuals. This distinction was consistent across the sexes. Our study advances the understanding of the characteristics of the TC microbiome, its heterogeneity across different sexes, and its relationship with clinical variables.
