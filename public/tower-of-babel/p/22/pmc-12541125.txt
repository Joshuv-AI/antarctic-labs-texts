Subglottic Stenosis After COVID‐19 Mimicking Bronchial Asthma, Treated by Bronchoscopic Resection
Authors: Suzuka Matsuoka, Yuki Takigawa, Ken Sato, Jun Nishimura, Hiromi Watanabe, Kenichiro Kudo, Keiichi Fujiwara, Takuo Shibayama
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA woman developed dyspnea following COVID‐19 without prior intubation. The patient was initially treated for asthma; however, her symptoms persisted. Evaluation revealed subglottic stenosis due to a membranous structure. Bronchoscopic resection and balloon dilation resulted in symptomatic improvement. This is the first reported case of membranous subglottic stenosis following COVID‐19.Keywords: bronchoscopic resection, COVID‐19, subglottic stenosis

A 44‐year‐old woman was evaluated for tracheal stenosis, presenting with a four‐year history of dyspnea following COVID‐19 without prior tracheal intubation. She was diagnosed with bronchial asthma based on wheezing, dyspnea, and elevated FeNO levels, and treatment was initiated; however, her dyspnea remained poorly controlled during this period. Upon physical examination, her vital signs were within normal limits, with an oxygen saturation of 97%, and mild stridor was auscultated. The flow–volume curves of pulmonary function testing indicated central airway obstruction (Figure 1A). Chest computed tomography revealed subglottic stenosis caused by membranous structures (Figure 2). Flexible bronchoscopy revealed a membranous structure tethered on the left side (Figure 3A). Under local anaesthesia and conscious sedation, the membranous structure was repeatedly incised using a loop cutter, followed by balloon dilation via flexible bronchoscopy (Figure 3B,C). After the procedure, the stridor resolved, and pulmonary function testing demonstrated an improvement in the flow–volume curve (Figure 1B). Several cases of tracheal stenosis with and without intubation for severe COVID‐19 have been reported [1, 2]; however, this is the first case of membranous tracheal stenosis following non‐intubated COVID‐19. Tracheal stenosis should be considered in patients with persistent dyspnea after COVID‐19, regardless of intubation history.
