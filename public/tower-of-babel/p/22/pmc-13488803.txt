Evaluation of the prevalence of overactive bladder syndrome in patients with rheumatoid arthritis
Authors: Derya Cirakoglu, Ahmet Yuce
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe aim of this study was to evaluate the frequency of overactive bladder syndrome (OAB) in patients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and its relationship with clinical parameters and central sensitization (CS).MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 95 patients diagnosed with RA according to the 2010 ACR/EULAR criteria and 95 healthy controls. OAB symptoms were assessed using the Overactive Bladder Questionnaire–Version 8 (OAB-V8). Pain intensity was assessed using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS), disease activity using the Disease Activity Score in 28 joints (DAS-28), functional status using the Health Assessment Questionnaire (HAQ-DI), quality of life using the Quality of Life–Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA-QoL), and CS Central Sensitivity Inventory Section A (CSI-A).ResultsOAB was detected in 54.7% of patients with rheumatoid arthritis, while this rate was 17.9% in the control group (p < 0.001). Multivariable logistic regression analysis showed that RA was independently associated with OAB after adjustment for age, BMI, and smoking status (adjusted OR: 5.37, 95% CI: 2.75–10.53, p < 0.001). When RA patients were evaluated according to the presence of OAB, age (p = 0.019), education level (p = 0.007), pain intensity (VAS) (p = 0.046), functional status (HAQ-DI) (p = 0.002) and CS scores (p < 0.001) were significantly higher in patients with OAB. Correlation analyses showed significant associations between OAB-V8 scores and pain (p < 0.001), functional status (p = 0.001), quality of life (p = 0.021), and CS scores (p < 0.001). In the regression analysis performed among RA patients, CS was independently associated with OAB (OR: 1.048, 95% CI: 1.009–1.089, p = 0.017).ConclusionThe prevalence of OAB is significantly higher in patients with RA. CS appears to be independently associated with OAB in this population, suggesting that mechanisms related to central sensitization may contribute to bladder symptoms in RA. Therefore, OAB and CS should be evaluated together in RA patients regardless of disease duration or disease activity.

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic and systemic inflammatory disease that usually has symmetric joint involvement. It not only affects joints but is also associated with various non-joint organ involvements that may lead to mortality and morbidity [1]. The current literature provides increasing evidence that lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS)—a broad term that includes storage, voiding, and post-micturition urinary symptoms—can be observed in patients with rheumatoid arthritis. While these symptoms are usually not life-threatening, they may adversely affect the general quality of life and daily activities of patients to a significant extent [2].

Overactive bladder syndrome (OAB) is a subtype of LUTS characterized primarily by urinary urgency, usually accompanied by frequency and nocturia, with or without urge urinary incontinence [3]. Its global prevalence is estimated to be approximately 20%. It is seen more prevalently in women, and its prevalence increases with age [4]. Myogenic, neurogenic, and urothelial mechanisms are dominant in the pathophysiology of OAB. The etiology of OAB has not been completely clarified yet, whereas multiple factors considered to contribute to this condition have been defined. Bladder ischemia, advanced age, metabolic syndrome, psychological disorders, urinary microbiome, and inflammation are among these factors [5]. OAB represents the storage-symptom spectrum of LUTS. OAB symptoms can also be seen in various rheumatic and autoimmune diseases. For example, it was argued that in Sjögren’s Syndrome (SS), the autoantibodies binding with the M3 muscarinic receptor in the etiology of OAB could lead to the stimulation and contraction of the detrusor muscle [6]. Bladder storage symptoms were reported to be more prevalent compared to control groups, especially in RA patients with secondary SS [7]. The high prevalence of LUTS in RA patients was also reported in previous studies [8]. However, studies on the prevalence of OAB and etiological factors in RA patients are limited, and the topic is still under debate.

The results of recent studies have suggested that central sensitization (CS) may play a significant role in the etiology of OAB [9, 10]. CS is a condition where the pain response to normal or subthreshold afferent stimuli increases as the nociceptive neurons in the central nervous system (CNS) become hyperexcitable [11]. Similarly, the neurogenic hypothesis in the pathophysiology of OAB is an abnormal increase in afferent signals coming from the bladder or a decrease in the capacity of the CNS to modulate these signals. Studies have shown that, as in CS cases, silent C-fibers can also be spontaneously activated or develop excessive sensitivity to low-intensity stimuli in OAB [12]. These similarities suggest that the concept of CS may have an important role in explaining the pathophysiology and clinical symptoms of OAB [13]. Studies have revealed that CS may have an important role in the etiology of pain in RA cases [14]. For instance, it was reported that CS could affect the disease activity in RA cases and reduce satisfaction with treatment [15]. In a study conducted with 240 RA patients by Saitou et al., it was emphasized that CS should be considered in patients with chronic pain or neuropathic complaints independent of inflammation [16]. However, in the current literature, the presence of OAB in RA patients and its potential association with CS have not been examined.

This study comparatively evaluates the prevalence of OAB in RA patients along with a control group and investigates the relationships between OAB presence and disease activity, functional capacity, and other clinical parameters. Additionally, the hypothesis that OAB symptoms in RA may be associated with CS is proposed.

Methods

This cross-sectional study was conducted between August 2025 and January 2026 at the Physiotherapy and Rehabilitation and Urology Outpatient Clinics of the Faculty of Medicine, Ordu University. The study sample consisted of 95 patients diagnosed with Rheumatoid Arthritis according to the American College of Rheumatology/European League Against Rheumatism ACR/EULAR 2010 Classification Criteria for Rheumatoid Arthritis who had been followed up as outpatients for at least one year. Patients were consecutively recruited from the outpatient clinic during the study period. The control group consisted of 95 age-matched healthy volunteers without any known chronic disease, recruited as a convenience sample from individuals accompanying patients in the outpatient clinic and from individuals attending the hospital for routine health check-ups. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Non-Invasive Scientific Research Ethics Committee of Ordu University (approval number: 2025/228). Due to the retrospective design of the study and the use of previously recorded clinical data, the requirement for individual informed consent was waived by the ethics committee.

Demographic data (age, disease duration, and education status) and relevant laboratory parameters were recorded. Clinical examinations were carried out in accordance with a standardized and predefined protocol: an experienced Physiotherapy and Rehabilitation specialist performed the examinations related to pain, disease activity, functional status, quality of life, and CS, while an experienced urologist conducted the examinations of OAB. Pain severity was determined using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS), scored on a scale ranging from 0 to 10. Disease activity was evaluated using the Disease Activity Score in 28 joints (DAS-28), calculated based on tender and swollen joint counts, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), and the global health assessment of the patient [17]. Functional status was assessed using the 20‑item Health Assessment Questionnaire (HAQ-DI), which covers activities of daily living, with higher scores indicating poorer functional status [18]. Quality of life was evaluated using the disease‑specific Rheumatoid Arthritis Quality of Life Questionnaire (RA‑QoL), with higher scores indicating lower quality of life [19]. CS symptoms were assessed using Part A of the Central Sensitization Inventory (CSI‑A). The maximum possible score on this scale is 100, and scores of 40 or above were accepted to indicate CS [20]. OAB symptoms were evaluated using the Overactive Bladder Questionnaire–Version 8 (OAB-V8). The questionnaire was administered to both RA patients and controls under the supervision of a single urologist to ensure standardized data collection; participants completed the form with guidance when necessary, and responses were verified through direct questioning to minimize potential misunderstandings. The OAB-V8 has a maximum score of 40, with higher scores indicating greater symptom severity, and scores ≥ 8 were considered consistent with OAB in this study [21]. The same standardized assessment procedure was applied to both groups to ensure methodological consistency and minimize potential information bias.

Patients receiving conventional synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (csDMARDs) were categorized as a single group due to the heterogeneity of treatment regimens, including both monotherapy and combination therapies. The csDMARD group consisted of patients treated with methotrexate (n = 12), hydroxychloroquine (n = 1), sulfasalazine (n = 1), or leflunomide (n = 7) as monotherapy, as well as combination therapies including methotrexate + sulfasalazine (n = 4), hydroxychloroquine + sulfasalazine (n = 2), leflunomide + sulfasalazine (n = 4), leflunomide + hydroxychloroquine (n = 5), methotrexate + hydroxychloroquine + sulfasalazine (n = 2), and methotrexate + hydroxychloroquine + leflunomide (n = 5).

Patients receiving biologic therapy were treated exclusively with tumor necrosis factor (TNF) inhibitors, including etanercept, adalimumab, infliximab, golimumab, and certolizumab. Although a small number of patients were receiving other biologic agents or Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors, these patients were not included due to their limited number, which was insufficient for meaningful statistical analysis. Therefore, advanced therapy in this study was represented solely by TNF inhibitors. Due to the relatively small sample size within individual treatment subgroups, treatment categories were not analyzed separately in the regression models.

Patients with diabetes mellitus, thyroid disease, neurological or psychiatric disorders, rheumatic diseases other than RA, or a diagnosis of secondary SS associated with RA were excluded. Individuals with known acute or chronic lower or upper urinary tract disease (urinary tract infection, neurogenic bladder, urolithiasis, bladder cancer, interstitial cystitis/bladder pain syndrome), those with a history of prior urinary tract or intra‑abdominal surgery, those using medications affecting the urinary system such as diuretics or opioids, and patients who had difficulty communicating were also excluded. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS for Windows (version 20.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), whereas categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages. For comparisons between independent groups, Student’s t-test was used for normally distributed variables and the Mann–Whitney U test was used for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables were compared using the Pearson chi-square test.

To determine factors independently associated with OAB, a multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed including age, body mass index, and smoking status as covariates. In addition, a binary logistic regression model including demographic and clinical variables was conducted to evaluate factors associated with OAB among patients with rheumatoid arthritis. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The primary endpoint for the sample size calculation was the prevalence of overactive bladder (OAB), defined as an OAB-V8 score ≥ 8. Secondary analyses included comparisons of demographic and clinical characteristics and evaluation of factors associated with OAB. A power analysis was conducted based on the literature regarding lower urinary tract symptoms in female patients with rheumatoid arthritis (Abushamma et al., 2021). Using an effect size of 0.64 and a statistical power of 90%, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 51 participants per group, resulting in a total of 102 participants.

Results

The demographic and clinical characteristics of the RA patients are summarized in Table 1. The mean age of the patients was 47.85 ± 12.69 years, while the mean age of the control group was 45.54 ± 10.67 years (p = 0.175). OAB was identified in 54.7% (n = 52) of the patients, whereas this rate was 17.9% (n = 17) in the control group, and the difference between the groups was statistically significant (p < 0.001). The presence of CS was detected in 23.2% (n = 22) of RA patients and 6.3% (n = 6) of controls, and this difference was also significant (p = 0.002) (Table 2). A multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to evaluate factors associated with OAB. After adjusting for age, BMI, and smoking status, rheumatoid arthritis remained independently associated with OAB. RA patients had a 5.37-fold higher odds of having OAB compared to the control group (adjusted OR: 5.37, 95% CI: 2.75–10.53, p < 0.001). Age showed a borderline association with OAB (p = 0.054), whereas BMI and smoking status were not significantly associated with OAB (Table 3).

When the RA patients were divided into two groups according to their OAB‑V8 scores, as those with OAB (score ≥ 8) and those without OAB (score < 8), the mean age of the patients with OAB was 50.71 ± 10.46, while the mean age of those without OAB was 44.40 ± 14.33. The difference between the OAB subgroups was significant (p = 0.019). Significant differences were also observed between the subgroups in terms of education level (p = 0.007), VAS‑pain scores (p = 0.046), and HAQ-DI scores (p = 0.002). However, no significant differences were found in terms of disease duration (p = 0.467), disease activity (DAS‑28, p = 0.136), or RA‑specific quality of life (RA‑QoL, p = 0.424). The mean CS scores were 30.23 ± 19.25 in the patients with OAB and 15.79 ± 18.99 in those without OAB, and the difference between these scores was significant (p < 0.001) (Table 4).

A binary logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with OAB among patients with rheumatoid arthritis. Variables included in the model were age, BMI, disease duration, VAS pain, DAS28 score, steroid use, smoking status, HAQ-DI score, and treatment groups. The analysis demonstrated that CS was independently associated with OAB (OR: 1.048, 95% CI: 1.009–1.089, p = 0.017), while the other variables were not significantly associated with OAB (Table 6).

Discussion

The results of our study demonstrated that OAB was observed at a high rate in patients with RA, independent of disease duration and disease activity. In addition to this, the rate of CS was found to be significantly higher in patients with OAB. These results suggested that bladder symptoms in RA patients may be associated not only with joint‑related and inflammatory processes but also with neurological and CS-related mechanisms. Studies systematically examining the relationship between OAB and CS in RA patients are quite limited. Therefore, this study may provide a new perspective to the literature in this field.

LUTS are frequently encountered in inflammatory rheumatic diseases. For example, in a study by Erdik et al. involving 72 patients with primary SS, OAB symptoms were evaluated using OAB‑V8, and OAB was detected in 54.2% of patients [22]. Similarly, in a cross‑sectional study comparing patients with SLE to controls, patients were reported to have higher OAB symptom scores and lower quality of life [23]. Nonetheless, studies investigating the frequency of OAB specifically in RA patients are very limited. Existing studies have generally evaluated LUTS, and none have used OAB‑V8 as a measurement instrument. In a study examining LUTS and hematuria in RA patients, the prevalence of LUTS was found to be 77.3%, and high rates of symptoms such as nocturia, urge incontinence, urinary frequency, and nighttime urinary leakage were reported [24]. Likewise, a recent prospective study showed that LUTS were common in RA patients, and symptoms such as daytime frequent urination, urgency, nocturia, and urge incontinence negatively affected quality of life [12]. Therefore, it was recommended that LUTS be routinely assessed during the follow‑up of RA patients and that treatment be initiated when necessary to improve quality of life. In our study, the prevalence of OAB in RA patients was found to be 54.7%, which was significantly higher than that in the control group.

Inflammation in rheumatic diseases is not limited to joint involvement and may also play an important role in the development of LUTS. Studies have shown that inflammation may also contribute to the etiology of OAB [25]. In a recent study, it was reported that levels of pro‑inflammatory cytokines such as IL‑6 were higher in patients with OAB compared to controls [26]. Wei et al. demonstrated a correlation between OAB and the systemic immune‑inflammatory index (SII), an indicator of inflammation [27]. Additionally, some studies have revealed increased numbers of inflammatory cells in the bladder in OAB cases. Ongoing inflammation may impair bladder function and predispose individuals to OAB [3, 24]. In our study, the prevalence of OAB in RA patients was 54.7%, significantly higher than the rate in controls. As in other rheumatic diseases, the high prevalence of OAB in RA may be associated with inflammation. However, similar to previous studies, OAB was not found to be associated with disease activity or disease duration in this study [12]. This result indicated that the intensity and duration of inflammation in RA did not directly influence the presence of OAB and supported the hypothesis that different etiological mechanisms may play a role in OAB.

In addition to the potential role of inflammation in the pathogenesis of OAB, mechanisms originating from the CNS have recently been proposed to contribute to this process. In this context, the presence of CS and its clinical implications in RA patients have attracted increasing interest. In our study, the rate of CS in RA patients was 23.2%. Previous studies have shown that, in addition to inflammation, CS may also play a role in the etiology of pain in RA. For example, in a study involving 113 RA patients, CS was detected in 59.29% of patients, and CS was reported to be associated with disease activity and pain. In the same study, it was emphasized that CS may be present in RA patients even in the absence of conditions such as fibromyalgia, which are typically associated with CS [28]. In contrast, another recent study involving 220 RA patients showed a CS rate of 7.7% and concluded that CS negatively affected satisfaction with treatment [29]. The variability in CS rates reported in studies conducted in different countries may be due to not only methodological differences but also racial and sociocultural characteristics of the populations studied.

Recent studies have provided increasing evidence that CS may also play a role in the etiology of OAB. In a study by Celenay et al. involving 144 women diagnosed with OAB, CS was detected in 47.9% of patients, and the presence of CS was shown to be associated with OAB symptom severity and reduced quality of life [30]. In our study, CS scores were significantly higher in RA patients with OAB in comparison to those without OAB, and OAB‑V8 scores showed a positive correlation with CS scores. According to this result, CS may play a role in the etiology of OAB in RA patients. Furthermore, although OAB scores were not associated with disease activity, their significant positive correlation with VAS‑pain scores was worth noting. This may indicate that the pain experienced by patients and the discomfort perceived in the bladder may be related more to CS mechanisms than to inflammation. In addition, the multivariable binary logistic regression analysis demonstrated that CS was independently associated with OAB among RA patients. The positive association between OAB symptom severity and CS highlights the need for a multidisciplinary evaluation approach in this patient group. Moreover, no significant association was found between RA treatment groups and OAB in the multivariable analysis, suggesting that OAB symptoms in RA patients may not be directly related to specific antirheumatic therapies. In patients with accompanying CS, incorporating approaches targeting pain modulation and central mechanisms into the treatment plan, rather than focusing solely on urological or inflammatory treatments, may contribute to the improvement of quality of life. Although CS is not a disease-specific parameter of RA, its independent association with OAB supports the hypothesis that central mechanisms may contribute to urinary symptoms beyond inflammatory pathways. However, prospective studies with larger patient populations are needed to clarify this relationship.

This study is a pioneering study evaluating OAB in RA patients using OAB‑V8 and examining the relationship between RA and CS. However, some limitations of the study should be acknowledged. The study was conducted at a single center, and the sample size was limited. This restricts the generalizability of the results. Additionally, the cross‑sectional design does not allow causal inferences regarding the relationship between RA and OAB/CS. Considering these limitations, the results of the study should be interpreted with caution, and future multicenter studies with larger samples and longitudinal designs are recommended to support and expand upon these results.

In this study, the prevalence of OAB was found to be high in patients with RA, and this condition appeared independently of disease activity and disease duration. Furthermore, CS rates were significantly higher in patients presenting with OAB symptoms. These results highlight the importance of systematically evaluating OAB and CS together during the routine clinical follow‑up of patients. Integrating this approach into clinical practice may contribute to a more comprehensive management of patients and improvements in their quality of life.
