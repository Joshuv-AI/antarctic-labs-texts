Finite element analysis of chloride ion penetration and service life prediction in concrete with supplementary cementitious materials
Authors: Pooria Dashti, Faramarz Moodi, Ali Akbar Ramezanianpour, Rasoul Banar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates chloride ion penetration and service life (SL) prediction in concrete, highlighting the role of supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs), such as silica fume (SF) and ground granulated blast furnace slag (GGBFS). Finite element (FE) simulations using COMSOL Multiphysics were performed with both constant and time-dependent chloride diffusion coefficients obtained from experimental data. Four concrete mixtures with varying water-to-binder (w/b) ratios and SCM contents were tested. Chloride diffusion coefficients, measured from samples exposed to a 165 g/L NaCl solution for 3, 6, 9, and 12 months, were incorporated into the simulations. The results showed that concretes with a higher w/b ratio (0.55) experienced greater chloride penetration, with diffusion coefficients ranging from 8.574 × 10⁻¹² m²/s (at 3 months) to 5.635 × 10⁻¹² m²/s (at 12 months), whereas concretes with a lower w/b ratio (0.45) exhibited superior resistance, with diffusion coefficients decreasing from 6.494 × 10⁻¹² m²/s (at 3 months) to 4.401 × 10⁻¹² m²/s (at 12 months). SCMs significantly reduced chloride penetration in both w/b ratios; SF-containing concrete achieved the lowest diffusion coefficient (1.563 × 10⁻¹² m²/s at 12 months), due to its ability to densify the concrete matrix. Time-dependent diffusion modelling improved SL prediction accuracy, reducing simulation errors to below 30%, compared to 48–75% for constant models. SL predictions further highlighted the durability benefits of SCMs: concrete with GGBFS demonstrated a potential SL of 62 years (variable diffusion model), compared to 10 years (constant model), while SF-enhanced concrete achieved even greater longevity, with SL extending up to 106 years using variable models. These findings underscore the critical importance of SCMs, appropriate w/b ratios, and dynamic diffusion modelling in enhancing durability and ensuring the longevity of infrastructure in chloride-rich environments.Keywords: COMSOL, Finite element simulation, Ground granulated blast furnace slag, Silica fume, Constant and variable chloride ions diffusion coefficientSubject terms: Civil engineering, Software

Introduction

Concrete, a fundamental material in civil engineering, is renowned for its versatility, durability, and widespread application in construction. Reinforcement in concrete plays a crucial role in enhancing the structural capacity and longevity of concrete elements, particularly in large-scale infrastructure projects. The integration of steel reinforcement helps counteract the inherent weakness of concrete in tension, thereby improving its performance under various loading conditions over time. However, the presence of steel reinforcement also introduces potential durability concerns, particularly the risk of corrosion over time.

Reinforcement corrosion, induced by chloride ion ingress from environmental sources such as seawater, deicing salts, and marine atmospheres, poses a significant threat to the durability of reinforced concrete structures (RCSs). It is considered one of the most common causes of deterioration. Two primary mechanisms protect steel reinforcements embedded within concrete. First, physical protection is provided by the concrete cover, which acts as a barrier to chloride ions; the depth of penetration increases proportionally with concrete porosity1. Second, chemical protection arises from the high alkalinity of concrete, which forms a passive oxide layer on the steel surface, shielding it from corrosion2. Despite these mechanisms, chloride ions can penetrate the concrete over time, compromising the protective layers and initiating the corrosion process.

However, due to the inherent porosity of concrete, chloride ions, along with moisture and oxygen, gradually infiltrate over time. This compromises the protective oxide layer, accelerating the corrosion process2–7. The expansion of corrosion products further exacerbates the situation, inducing internal stresses that contribute to concrete cracking6,8. Moreover, corrosion-related deterioration can lead to the separation and cracking of the concrete cover, which decreases the reinforcement’s effective cross-sectional area and weakens its bond with the concrete matrix3,7,9.

As corrosion persists, the structural integrity of concrete is progressively compromised, potentially resulting in severe loss of serviceability and significant reduction in load-bearing capacity, sometimes leading to sudden structural failure4. Given these critical implications, addressing reinforcement corrosion and ensuring long-term concrete durability remain paramount challenges in civil engineering practice7,10.

Numerous factors influence the durability of CSs, including environmental conditions, material quality, construction practices, and maintenance protocols. Ensuring durable concrete requires meticulous attention to various measures, including optimal mix design, the selection of appropriate materials, precise batching and mixing procedures, effective transportation and placement practices, and adequate curing techniques.

Furthermore, alongside strategies aimed at enhancing the concrete durability, it is imperative to assess the resistance of concrete to environmental challenges initially. Following the analysis of penetration mechanisms in concrete, accurately predicting the SL of various concrete mixes becomes essential. Such predictions help select optimal mix designs and define maintenance intervals and costs20. Several definitions of SL have been proposed in the literature, each emphasising different durability criteria. Zhang and Ba23 define SL as the time until chloride ions reach a critical threshold near the reinforcement. Chai et al.24 and Vořechovská et al.25 consider the breakdown of the passive layer as indicative of SL, while Parameswaran et al.26 describe it as the period during which a structure maintains its efficiency. Martin-Perez and Lounis27 define SL as the time until damage becomes unacceptable, and Abu-Tair et al.28 define it as the period until significant damage requiring costly repairs occurs. Teplý29 refers to SL as the period during which the structure can operate under intended conditions. Although numerous studies have focused on SL prediction of concrete structures30–36. Challenges remain due to the heterogeneous microstructure of concrete11. A critical parameter in estimating SL is the chloride threshold level (CTL)10,34. This represents the chloride concentration at the steel surface needed to initiate corrosion. For accurate SL prediction, the total chloride content near the reinforcement must exceed the CTL34,37.

As previously noted, the CTL is a crucial factor in modelling the SL of RCSs. Nevertheless, there is presently no widely accepted approach for establishing the CTL38. Accurately assessing the chloride concentration that initiates corrosion on the surface of reinforcements requires meticulous examination. The CTL is affected by several factors, including the amount and type of binder, the reinforcement’s electrochemical potential, the chloride binding capacity, the hydroxyl ion concentration, the presence of oxygen, the surface condition of the reinforcement, and temperature10,38. Consequently, the CTL is typically specific to each concrete type and its exposure conditions38. Nonetheless, past studies have commonly reported CTL values of 0.4–0.8 w.t% of cement/binder10,34,38–42 or 0.05–0.1 w.t% of concrete20,39–41,43.

Extensive research has been conducted on simulating chloride penetration and predicting the SL of RCSs using various FE software tools. These tools represent a significant advancement in durability design by providing predictive models to estimate SL34. Generally, if simulation models are well-calibrated against experimental data, they can accurately and efficiently predict the long-term behaviour of concrete structures19,44. Furthermore, simulations can reveal phenomena not captured in experiments, offering more profound insight into chloride transport mechanisms11,19. Life-365 is one of the most widely used tools in this domain, applied over the past 15 years to estimate the SL of concrete exposed to chlorides45. COMSOL Multiphysics is another advanced software that offers a broad range of simulation capabilities. It solves differential equations for nonlinear systems in one, two, or three dimensions using the FE method and excels at integrating multiple physical phenomena into unified models. Originally developed at the Royal Institute of Technology in Sweden4. COMSOL has demonstrated high accuracy when validated against experimental data and MATLAB results4,11,19,46. For example, studies have shown that using COMSOL to simulate chloride transport and binding in cracked concrete provides valuable insights, confirming its utility for assessing chemical ingress11,47.

Two critical parameters influencing the durability of RCSs against chloride ion attack, and consequently the corrosion of the reinforcement, are the w/b ratio and the incorporation of SCMs. The w/b ratio directly affects the pore structure and permeability of concrete, which are key factors in chloride ingress and subsequent corrosion48,49. A lower w/b ratio results in a denser matrix with fewer and smaller pores, thus reducing permeability and improving resistance to chloride-induced corrosion49–51. Conversely, a higher w/b ratio increases porosity, facilitating chloride penetration and thereby increasing the corrosion risk. Badalyan et al.52 demonstrated that lower w/b ratios significantly enhance durability, while Mehta and Monteiro53 noted that high w/b ratios make concrete more susceptible to deterioration. SCMs, such as SF and GGBFS, also play a crucial role in enhancing resistance by modifying the microstructure and chemical properties of concrete. SF, a by-product of silicon and ferrosilicon production, exhibits high pozzolanic reactivity and reacts with calcium hydroxide to form additional C-S-H gel, refining pores and reducing permeability54–56. GGBFS, a by-product of iron and steel manufacturing, is another SCM that contributes to improved durability. Similarly, GGBFS, a byproduct of steel production, reacts with the alkaline components of cement to produce more C-S-H, thereby enhancing chloride binding and reducing permeability. Recent work has confirmed that GGBFS incorporation lowers chloride diffusion and improves resistance by forming a stable, less porous microstructure46,57–59.

Previous studies have employed various methods, including Fick’s second law and FE simulations, to model chloride ion diffusion in concrete. Recent studies60–62 have demonstrated that incorporating SCMs, along with time-dependent diffusion and surface chloride concentration, significantly enhances the accuracy of SL predictions for concrete exposed to chlorides. Although some researchers have utilised FE software, such as COMSOL Multiphysics, for these simulations, most of these studies assume a constant chloride diffusion coefficient, failing to account for the time-dependent evolution of concrete’s microstructure and permeability. While such models provide valuable insights into initial chloride ingress, they fall short in accurately capturing the long-term behaviour of concrete, where diffusion properties are influenced by ongoing hydration, pore refinement, and the beneficial effects of SCMs, such as SF and GGBFS.

The research gap lies in the lack of comprehensive studies that integrate time-dependent chloride diffusion coefficients with the effects of SCMs in numerical simulations, particularly for predicting the SL of RCSs. Most existing models neither incorporate experimental data for variable diffusion coefficients nor consider the combined effects of SCMs over extended exposure periods. Furthermore, there is limited research employing COMSOL Multiphysics to simulate chloride diffusion under realistic conditions, accounting for these critical variables.

This study addresses these gaps by utilising COMSOL Multiphysics to simulate chloride ion penetration using both constant and time-dependent diffusion models. The simulations are informed by experimental data on chloride diffusion coefficients, measured from concrete specimens exposed to a 165 g/L NaCl solution for 3, 6, 9, and 12 months. By explicitly incorporating different SCMs (SF and GGBFS), this research systematically evaluates their effects on chloride resistance and develops a more accurate framework for SL prediction in concrete structures. Unlike previous studies that relied on simplified assumptions, this work demonstrates the importance of combining time-dependent diffusion behaviour with the influence of SCMs to improve the prediction of long-term durability in chloride-laden environments.

Research program

The workflow depicted in Fig. 1 outlines a systematic approach to investigating chloride ion penetration in concrete, using both experimental and numerical methods. The process begins with Step 1: Mixture Design, where four concrete mixtures are prepared. These mixtures vary in w/b ratios and the inclusion of SCMs. The mixtures include OPC with w/b ratios of 0.45 and 0.55, as well as mixtures containing SF and GGBFS, each with a w/b ratio of 0.45. This mixture design allows for the analysis of how different compositions affect chloride ion diffusion and overall concrete durability.

The research framework.

In Step 2: Experimental Program, the compressive strength of the concrete is tested at 28 days, followed by chloride ion penetration tests at 3, 6, 9, 12 and 24 months. These tests provide essential empirical data on how chloride concentrations change over time and depth within the concrete, serving as a foundation for further analysis. In Step 3, the D is calculated using two models. In Step 3.1, a constant D is calculated for the 3, 6, 9, and 12-month time points using MATLAB’s Curve Fitting Toolbox. In contrast, Step 3.2 calculates a variable D using a time-dependent equation that accounts for changes in diffusion rates over time, which is crucial for simulating more realistic chloride ingress behaviour.

Next, in Step 4: FE Simulation, the calculated Ds are applied in numerical models to simulate chloride ion penetration through the concrete. This simulation allows for the visualisation and prediction of chloride ion concentration profiles over time. Step 5: Validation compares the simulation results with experimental data at 24 months of age, using the average error formula to quantify the model’s accuracy. Finally, in Step 6: SL Prediction, the validated models are used to estimate the concrete’s SL based on a chloride threshold limit (0.4% by weight of the binder) and a predefined concrete cover of 7.5 cm. This step is critical for determining how long the concrete will remain resistant to chloride-induced corrosion under real-world conditions.

Experimental program

Materials

In this study, OPC, SF, and GGBFS were used as binders in the concrete samples, with their physical and chemical properties detailed in Table 1. The cement had a compressive strength of 35.8 MPa at 28 days, as measured on standard mortar cubes in accordance with ASTM C109. The strength activity index of the SCMs was approximately 77% for GGBFS and about 115% for SF, indicating their compliance with ASTM standards and confirming their effectiveness in enhancing the mechanical and durability properties of concrete. Additionally, crushed sand and gravel, conforming to ASTM C3363 for granularity, were utilised. The fine aggregate had a fineness modulus of 2.68 and a water absorption of 3.2%, while the coarse aggregate had a water absorption of 2.55%. To achieve workability due to the high fineness of the particles, a polycarboxylate ether-based superplasticiser (SP) with a specific gravity of 1.05 was added to the samples. The SP had a pH of 7 ± 1 and a solid content of approximately 35%.

Mixing and specimen Preparation

The concrete samples were prepared following the ASTM C19266. For all specimens, a SP was added to a portion of the mixing water to enhance workability. The freshly mixed concrete was then placed into 100 and 150 mm cubic moulds and cured for 24 h at ambient temperature in a humidity chamber. After curing, the samples were demolded and transferred to a chamber with saturated lime water maintained at 21 ± 3 °C, where they remained until the time of testing. For each mixture and each testing age, three specimens were prepared and tested, and the average results were reported.

Test methods

Compressive strength test

A 2000 kN concrete compression testing equipment was used to determine the compressive strength of 100 × 100 × 100 mm concrete specimens at a loading rate of 0.5 N.mm− 2.s− 1. This experiment was conducted after 28 days of curing on three samples of each mixture.

Chloride ions penetration test

In a thoroughly structured experimental program, 150 mm cubic samples were prepared using four distinct concrete mixtures. After curing in a moist environment for 7 days, epoxy resin was applied to all but one surface of each sample, allowing unidirectional chloride penetration. The specimens were then immersed in a 165 g/L NaCl solution and left to soak for 2 years67. At the testing stage, once the specimens were dried, powder samples were collected by drilling successive layers of approximately 2 mm thickness from the exposed surface. The extracted powders were mixed with nitric acid and distilled water to prepare solutions based on ASTM C115268. These solutions were subsequently heated, filtered, and analysed by potentiometric titration to determine the chloride content, following ASTM C1152. Figure 2 illustrates the experimental procedure. It is important to note that while chloride profile testing was performed at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months for modelling purposes, additional data at 24 months were also collected and used solely for validating the simulation results, as discussed in Sect. 4. This procedure was repeated at least five times to assess the chloride ion penetration profile of each specimen at various exposure ages (3, 6, 9, 12, and 24 months). Finally, MATLAB software was utilised to calculate the diffusion coefficient (D), facilitating the development of a numerical model. Several approaches have been proposed for estimating the time-dependent diffusion coefficient, including regression analysis of experimental data or adopting fixed m values as suggested in previous studies. While the regression-based integrated approach — which simultaneously fits D(t) and Cs(t) into a unified model62 — is considered rigorous and comprehensive, Eq. (3) was employed in this study with m values reported in the literature16,22,69, specifically 0.62, 0.30, and 0.71 for the S, O&P, and G mixtures, respectively, under submerged conditions. This decision was due to the limited number and range of experimental data at different ages, which constrained the reliability of the regression. Furthermore, the m-based method remains a well-established and widely accepted approach in the literature for comparative studies.

FE simulation program

For a comprehensive long-term assessment, laboratory test outcomes were incorporated as input factors for the computational simulation. COMSOL Multiphysics software was employed for this task. This software is particularly useful for modelling various durability and mechanical-related factors in RCSs due to its diverse modules. In this project, several key inputs for the COMSOL model included the bulk diffusion coefficient, surface chloride concentration, geometry of the cross-section, sealant properties, and time-varying changes in the D. These parameters are discussed in more detail below. Additionally, based on the specific simulation conditions, further detailed information, such as variations in moisture and temperature content, sorption rates, diffusion coefficients within voids and concrete porosity can be provided to the software.

In COMSOL, the transport of chemical species is modelled by solving transport equations that account for both convection and diffusion processes. The equation for the conservation of mass for chemical species is stated as follows:

Where Ri​ denotes the reaction rate (mol/(m2⋅s)), Di​ is the diffusion coefficient (m2/s), Ci represents the species concentration (mol/m³) and u is the mass-averaged velocity vector (m/s).

This equation accounts for diffusion and convection as the primary transport mechanisms. The relative mass flux Ji​ (mol/(m²⋅s)) is applied in boundary condition calculations and flux analysis and linked to the mass balance equation. The Transport of Diluted Species interface inherently handles molecular diffusion, where the diffusive flux Ji​ is defined as per Eq. (5).

The model allows for direct input of the diffusion coefficient, including the option to specify an anisotropic diffusion tensor. The convective term, which depends on velocity u, can be defined analytically or derived by coupling the model with a fluid flow interface, such as Laminar Flow. The right portion of Eq. (5) includes Ri​, which accounts for sources or sinks, generally caused by chemical reactions or desorption from a porous medium. The Transport of Diluted Species interface also supports additional nodes, such as the Reaction node, which allows for the specification of reaction terms using the variable names of the species involved. In cases where the fluid is incompressible, the term u.∇c becomes zero, leading to the simplified conservative formulation.

When modelling in COMSOL Multiphysics, the problem and its environmental conditions must first be defined. The software supports simulations in one, two, or three dimensions. The next step involves determining the physics of the problem, which may include structural mechanics, fluid dynamics, electrochemistry, chemical species transport or solid mechanics. Afterwards, the analysis type is selected within the Study section. Following the definition of relevant units, parameters, and functions, the problem’s geometry is configured. The dimensions, shapes, and coordinates are defined in Geometry. Materials are allocated in the Materials section, either from the software’s library or manually. Boundary conditions, model type, and initial conditions are specified within the Solid Mechanics section. The model is then meshed, either automatically or manually. To perform the analysis, the settings within the Study section, including linearity, non-linearity, physics selection, and boundary conditions, must be finalised. The simulation is then executed, and results such as graphs and visualisations can be generated in the Results section.

In this research, a 2D model was used to simulate chloride ion penetration from the concrete surface (Fig. 3). The Transport of Diluted Species physics module was applied to model the diffusion process, with a time-dependent analysis approach. Laboratory results, including diffusion coefficients and surface chloride concentrations, were entered as input data into the software. The modelled geometry consisted of a cross-sectional square column with sides of 15 cm. The mesh consisted of 6,282 domain elements and 200 boundary elements. Time steps for analysis were set at 60-day intervals thereafter. Following the simulation, various outputs, including chloride concentration profiles at different depths and concentration changes over time at specific locations (e.g., at the rebar), were generated. In this study, changes in chloride concentration along a horizontal line at the midpoint of the section were examined over time.

Experimental test and FE simulation results

Chloride ions penetration profile

The results of the penetration depth test of chloride ions, obtained after placing the samples in NaCl solution for 3, 6, 9, and 12 months, are shown in Fig. 4. At 3 months, the penetration depth of all samples, except for the P mixture, was observed to be less than 20 mm. The P mixture, with a w/b ratio of 0.55, exhibited higher chloride penetration compared to the O mixture with a w/b ratio of 0.45, indicating that a higher w/b ratio facilitates chloride ingress. At later ages, a similar trend was observed, with the SF-modified concrete (S) showing the lowest penetration depth at all four ages, followed by the GGBFS-modified concrete (G). These findings confirm that the incorporation of SCMs, specifically SF and GGBFS, substantially reduces the chloride diffusion coefficient and penetration depth compared to plain concrete. Our results align with those reported by Shittu et al.56 and Basha et al.70, who observed superior performance of SF over GGBFS in mitigating chloride ingress. Furthermore, Banar et al.55 noted that the denser microstructure and refined pore system of SF concretes enhance resistance to chloride penetration, which supports our observations. Similarly, Liu et al.71 and Karein et al.72 also documented significant durability improvements and extended SL in SCM-modified concretes exposed to marine environments. Ramezanianpour et al.73 highlighted that both SF and GGBFS effectively inhibit chloride diffusion, though their efficiency depends on dosage and exposure conditions. Additionally, the duration of exposure significantly influenced chloride ingress; as shown in Fig. 4, samples exposed to NaCl solution for 12 months had penetration depths approximately 150% greater than those measured at 3 months.

Chloride ions penetration test results.

Table 3 presents the D calculated for concrete mixtures at various exposure durations (3, 6, 9, and 12 months). The results show a clear trend of decreasing diffusion coefficients over time, which is consistent with the expected ageing process of concrete, where the pore structure typically becomes denser as hydration continues. However, it is also noted that the surface chloride concentration increases significantly over the same period, more than doubling from 3 to 12 months. This observation warrants a deeper analysis, as it suggests a complex interaction between the diffusion process and the surface chloride concentration.

The increase in surface chloride concentration despite the decrease in diffusion coefficient may be due to several factors. As concrete ages, its pore structure refines, reducing the overall permeability and slowing down chloride ion ingress. However, the chloride ions that have already penetrated the surface may remain trapped near the surface or within the near-surface layers due to the reduced mobility of chloride ions in the denser microstructure. This could explain the increased concentration near the surface, as the material resists further chloride ingress while holding onto existing chloride ions.

The higher w/b ratio in Sample P consistently results in greater chloride penetration which expected because a higher w/b ratio generally leads to a more porous CS, allowing for easier chloride ingress, highlighting the importance of a lower w/b ratio in mitigating such risks. GGBFS is known to enhance the durability of concrete by refining the pore structure. The hydration of GGBFS produces additional calcium silicate hydrate (C-S-H), which fills the capillary pores, reducing porosity and permeability. This refinement leads to reduced chloride ion ingress. Additionally, the pozzolanic reaction of GGBFS consumes calcium hydroxide (Ca (OH)2), further densifying the microstructure. In addition, GGBFS-modified concrete has a higher chloride binding capacity due to the formation of Friedel’s salt, which binds free chlorides and reduces their mobility. This chemical binding reduces the concentration of free chloride ions that contribute to corrosion risk, as reflected by the lower chloride concentrations at deeper levels compared to non-SCM concrete. SF is characterised by its high pozzolanic activity and extremely fine particle size, which significantly enhances the concrete’s microstructure. SF reacts with calcium hydroxide to form more C-S-H, leading to a denser and more impermeable matrix compared to GGBFS-modified concrete. Additionally, the ultra-fine particles of SF fill the micro-pores within the cement paste, effectively reducing chloride ion diffusion. In addition, the high surface area of SF leads to a rapid pozzolanic reaction, consuming CH more effectively than GGBFS and producing a more substantial amount of C-S-H. The combination of noted effects contributes to a more pronounced reduction in chloride ion diffusion and lower concentrations, which is consistent with the trends seen in Fig. 4.

Service life prediction

SL prediction utilises validated models to estimate the durability of concrete based on a chloride threshold limit (0.4% by weight of the binder) and a predefined concrete cover (7.5 cm), with the results summarised in Table 5. The predicted SL for concrete specimens based on chloride ion penetration varies significantly depending on the assumed D, reflecting the influence of both the modelling approach and the evolving properties of the material. In this analysis, the D values were derived from experimental tests on concrete samples exposed to NaCl solution for 3, 6, 9, and 12 months, providing a realistic representation of how chloride ingress changes over time as the microstructure develops. This variability is influenced by the interaction between modelling assumptions, mixture composition, and age-dependent permeability, which collectively determine the concrete’s resistance to chloride ingress75,76. A comparison between constant and variable diffusion models clearly highlights these effects, particularly in SCMcontaining concretes such as those with GGBFS and SF. For OPC concretes (O and P), the differences between the two models are moderate. For example, in concrete O, the constant D model estimated SLs increasing from 3.27 to 5.42 years as the exposure duration changed from 3 to 12 months, whereas the variable D model predicted slightly higher SLs, increasing from 6.00 to 6.83 years. This reflects the ability of the time-dependent model to account for the progressive densification of the microstructure due to ongoing hydration during prolonged NaCl exposure77. In concrete P, the constant D model estimated shorter SLs, ranging from 2.79 to 4.23 years, while the variable D model predicted modest improvements, starting at 4.80 years and stabilising at 4.82 years by 12 months. This modest improvement is attributed to the limited resistance of OPC concrete with higher water content, which results in a more permeable matrix and reduced long-term performance48,49. The use of SCMs significantly improved the SL predictions, especially when variable D values were considered. Concrete G demonstrated a marked improvement in SL under the variable diffusion model. While the constant D model estimated SLs between 5.77 and 10.72 years, the variable model dramatically increased these values, predicting an initial SL of 202 years at 3 months of exposure, decreasing to 62.13 years at 12 months. As observed, the predicted SL based on diffusion coefficients determined at lower reference ages, such as 3 months, yields substantially higher values compared to those at higher reference ages. This phenomenon can be explained by the nature of Eq. (3), in which the time-dependent diffusion coefficient at the target age t is calculated based on t₀ and the exponent m. When a smaller t₀ (e.g., 3 months) is chosen, the ratio (t₀/t)m becomes significantly lower than at larger t₀, although the exponent m < 1 partially mitigates this effect. As a result, the computed diffusion coefficient at the target age becomes much smaller, leading to an unrealistically large predicted SL. Therefore, the remarkable increase in SL associated with lower reference ages is due to the exaggerated reduction in chloride ingress predicted at early ages, which explains the inverse trend observed in Table 5 for the time-dependent model compared to the constant model. In addition to this mathematical effect, the observed improvement is further supported by the progressive refinement of the pore structure and reduction in permeability provided by GGBFS under prolonged chloride exposure22,57,58. Similarly, concrete S showed substantial differences between the two models. The constant diffusion model estimated relatively high SLs (7.71 to 15.14 years), reflecting the known benefits of SF in reducing chloride ingress. However, the variable diffusion model projected much higher SLs, from 169.11 years at 3 months to 106.62 years at 12 months. This significant enhancement is attributed to the ultra-fine particles of SF, which continue to refine the pore structure and densify the matrix over time, thereby greatly limiting chloride penetration52,55,56. Similar trends have been reported in recent studies60–62, which also demonstrated that the combined effects of SCMs and time-dependent parameters substantially enhance concrete durability and extend SL predictions in aggressive environments. These findings emphasise the necessity of adopting time-dependent diffusion models and incorporating mixture composition and microstructural evolution into SL predictions to achieve reliable estimates of long-term durability for reinforced concrete exposed to chlorides.

In light of the above findings on the significant influence of SCMs and time-dependent diffusion on SL of concrete, the following engineering recommendations are suggested. Incorporating SF or GGBFS as partial replacements for cement in concrete mixtures is strongly recommended for structures exposed to chloride-rich environments, such as those in marine or de-icing conditions. Specifically, using around 8% SF or 20% GGBFS by weight of binder, as investigated in this study, was shown to significantly reduce chloride ingress and improve the SL of reinforced concrete. Among the two SCMs, SF demonstrated superior performance due to its finer particle size and greater ability to densify the microstructure. Therefore, when maximum durability is desired, the use of SF is preferred, whereas GGBFS remains an effective alternative, particularly when longer setting times or improved workability are beneficial. These recommendations can guide engineers in selecting optimal mix designs to enhance the long-term performance of concrete in aggressive chloride-laden environments.

Conclusions

This study investigated chloride ion penetration in concrete using COMSOL Multiphysics simulations, focusing on mixtures with different w/b ratios and the incorporation of SCMs such as GGBFS and SF. SL predictions were performed using both constant and time-dependent diffusion coefficients, the latter derived from experimental tests conducted at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months. The predictive accuracy of these models was evaluated against 24-month experimental data. The following outlines the main findings of this study.

The results clearly demonstrate that concrete durability against chloride ingress is strongly influenced by both the w/b ratio and the incorporation of SCMs. Concrete with a higher w/b ratio of 0.55 (P) exhibited significantly deeper chloride penetration compared to the 0.45 (O) mixture, indicating that reducing the w/b ratio improves impermeability. Furthermore, SCM-containing concretes—particularly those with SF (S)—showed the lowest penetration depths among all mixtures, highlighting the effectiveness of SCMs in refining the pore structure and substantially limiting chloride ingress.

Furthermore, SCM-containing concretes—particularly those with SF (S)—showed the lowest penetration depths among all mixtures, highlighting the effectiveness of SCMs in refining the pore structure and substantially limiting chloride ingress.

The SL predictions demonstrate the substantial influence of both SCMs and the choice of diffusion model. In mixtures without SCMs (O and P), the constant diffusion model estimated SLs of 2.79–5.42 years, which increased slightly to 4.80–6.83 years using the variable model, highlighting the moderate impact of time-dependent modelling in OPC concretes. However, SCM-containing mixtures showed dramatic improvements under variable diffusion modelling. For GGBFS concrete (G), SL increased from 5.77 to 10.72 years (constant model) to 155–62.13 years (variable model), while SF concrete (S) improved from 7.71 to 15.14 years to 180–106.62 years. These results underscore the significant long-term benefits of SCMs, particularly when their effects on microstructure refinement and chloride resistance are captured through time-dependent modelling.
