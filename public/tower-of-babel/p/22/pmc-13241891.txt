Protocol to generate microglia-containing cerebral organoids to model HIV neuroinflammation
Authors: Janet P. Zayas, David Gagliardi, Stephanie Gambut, Sam Acors, Nathalia Almeida, Michael H. Malim, Luis Apolonia, Lena Al-Harthi, Srinivas D. Narasipura, João I. Mamede
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryCerebral organoids (COs) are valuable for studying neurodegenerative diseases and pathogens, but their limited microglia pose challenges. Here, we present a protocol to generate organoids with physiologically relevant microglia (CO-iMs). We describe steps for differentiating induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) into mesoderm, seeding embryoid bodies (EBs), and harvesting hematopoietic progenitor cells (HPCs). We detail procedures for co-culturing HPCs and iPSCs to generate CO-iMs, followed by their infection and treatment. The model provides a platform to study viral infections and neuroinflammation.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Narasipura et al.1

Before you begin

Several established strategies exist for generating brain organoids from human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs). In guided approaches, exogenous patterning factors are applied at defined times points to direct differentiation toward specific brain regions resulting in region-specific organoids such as forebrain, midbrain, hindbrain, retina, choroid plexus and hypothalamus models. In contrast, unguided approaches rely on the intrinsic developmental potential of human pluripotent stem cells to self-organize and spontaneously differentiate into diverse neural lineages generating heterogeneous cerebral organoids that recapitulate early stages of human brain development.2,3

The protocol here presented is based on the unguided cerebral organoid methodology established in 2013,2 and has been adapted to support and promote enrichment and long-term maintenance of microglial populations within the organoid environment. This stepwise protocol details the generation of hematopoietic progenitor cells (HPCs) from iPSCs using a custom media formulation. Microglia-containing cerebral organoids (CO-iMs) are then generated by co-culturing HPCs and iPSCs derived from the same donor line at the onset of embryoid body (EB) formation as described in.1 By introducing HPCs at an early stage, the protocol supports concurrent differentiation of HPCs into microglia, and iPSCs into neural lineage cells, including neurons and astrocytes; modeling in vivo developmental processes and yielding CO-iMs with physiologically relevant, functional microglia populations.1

To ensure successful implementation of this protocol, iPSC lines must exhibit healthy colony morphology and maintain a normal karyotype. Regular monitoring under a microscope is critical. Colonies should have well-defined tight borders and contain less than 5% differentiated cells. Phenotypic validation should include flow cytometric analysis confirming the expression of key pluripotency markers, such as OCT-4 and TRA-1-60, along with high proliferative activity indicated by Ki-67 expression in over 90% of the cells. Genetic integrity can be assessed using qPCR-based screening kits. Additionally, karyotype analysis is recommended for approximately every 12 passages to monitor genomic stability and detect chromosomal abnormalities. Alternative methods for karyotyping include G-banding, metaphase chromosome staining, and whole-genome sequencing.

The iPSC lines used in this study were derived from healthy donors and obtained from the Rutgers University Cell and DNA Repository (RUCDR) Infinite Biologics (iPSC-Ru; male; 60 years old) and King’s College London (iPSC-R6; male; 24 years old). These lines exhibit normal chromosomal copy numbers. iPSCs are cultured as small colonies on Matrigel-coated 6-well tissue culture plates in mTeSR Plus medium, maintained at 37°C with 5% CO2. Media are changed every other day until colonies reach approximately 80–90% of confluency, defined by the point at which colonies begin to merge. For passaging, colonies are typically dissociated as small aggregates using ReLeSR, following the manufacturer’s instructions, with one confluent well usually split into four to five wells. Alternatively, when single cell colony formation is preferred, iPSCs may be dissociated into single cells using Accutase, according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Innovation

Cerebral organoids (COs) typically contain a minimal proportion of innately developed microglia (often <1.5%),4,5 or depend on the grafting of pre-infected microglia.3,6 The following protocol outlines the steps to generate cerebral organoids enriched with physiologically relevant levels (≥7%) of microglia, which are triple-positive for CD45, CD11b, and Iba-1, hereafter referred to as microglia-containing cerebral organoids (CO-iMs).1 These organoids contain different types of cells, including microglia, astrocytes, different neuron subtypes, Schwann cells, oligodendrocyte precursors, and others that were characterized by single-cell RNAseq and protein levels.1 The innate differentiation of microglia in CO-iMs increased cellular complexity and is an advancement to accurately reflect the human central nervous system (CNS) environment, establishing CO-iMs as a robust cell culture platform for investigating infections, including HIV-1, and other neurological disorders involving microglial dysfunction.

Microglia are the resident immune cells of the CNS, playing critical roles in brain development, maintenance of neuronal networks, resolving infections, and promoting repair following injury.4 As the primary cellular targets of HIV-1 in the brain, microglia contribute to sustained neuroinflammation and the development of HIV-associated neurocognitive disorders (HAND).4,5 However, the study of HIV-1 infection and its impact on the CNS has been limited by the inadequacy of existing cell culture, ex vivo, and in vivo models. Previous cerebral organoids systems particularly limit 3D CNS modeling due to the scarcity of functional microglia.5 The low abundance of these cells restricts the ability to accurately model HIV-1 neuropathogenesis and the complex interactions between infected microglia and other brain cell types. CO-iMs address this limitation by offering a reproducible system with physiological levels of microglia. CO-iMs support long-term HIV-1 infection and mimic key features of chronic viral persistence in the CNS. This platform enables detailed investigation of interactions between CNS-residing cells (e.g., microglia, astrocytes, neurons), mechanisms of viral persistence, and the evaluation of potential therapeutic interventions within a human CNS-like environment.

Expected outcomes

Using this protocol, high-quality HPCs are generated from iPSCs in a custom X-Vivo-based medium (Figure 1). HPCs are validated by flow cytometry for the expression of CD45+ and CD43+ markers (Figures 4A and 4B) and by Calcein violet-AM staining to confirm high cell viability (Figures 4C and 4D). EBs cultured in this medium begin releasing HPCs at around day 12, with continuous release observed up to +60 days.

These HPCs are subsequently used to generate CO-iMs through co-culture with matched iPSCs as described in Figure 1. Ru CO-iMs exhibit ∼7–10% microglia population (please refer to Narasipura & Zayas et al1), while R6 CO-iMs generated in this study exhibited ∼10%–14% microglia population, as measured by CD45 and TMEM119 double positive cells via flow cytometry (Figure 4E). Analysis for CD45/IBA1 double positive cells in the same samples yielded slightly lower percentages (∼6%, Figure 4F). TMEM119 is a hallmark signature of homeostatic microglia and supports identification of brain-resident microglia within the organoid.

Importantly, neuroepithelial rosette organization was preserved after HPC incorporation, indicating that microglia differentiation conditions do not disrupt early neural architecture (see Figures 5C–5E for reference).

Although CO-iMs can be analyzed after 40 days of maturation, we recommend using them beyond day 60 to ensure optimal cellular maturity.1 In this study, HIV infection experiments were conducted with CO-iMs over 120 days old. Prolonged maturation allows for a more robust representation of mature glial populations, including astrocytes and specific neuronal subtypes that emerge only in long-term cultured organoids.7 Alternatively, HPCs generated using the custom X-Vivo medium (Figures 1 and 4) can also be differentiated into iPSC-derived microglia (iMGL) or macrophages using commercially available kits or custom differentiation media (Figure 1).

Here, we used two separate iPSC lines generated from different donors (iPSC-R6 and iPSC-Ru) and successfully modeled chronic HIV infection followed by its replication control under combination antiretroviral therapy (cART). In the R6 CO-iMs, we observed maximal viral replication at ∼10 days post-inoculation and overall lower p24Gag levels with or without cART when compared to Ru CO-IMs (Figures 7A and 7B). We observed a significantly lower p24Gag levels following cART treatment in R6 CO-iMs compared to untreated controls (Figure 7A).

Interestingly, in a similar experiment using Ru CO-iMs, we observed peak viral replication at ∼20∼25 days post-infection, followed by a significant reduction in p24Gag levels upon initiation of cART at day 34 post-infection (Figure 7B, green and blue legends). One treatment group received cART as early as day 3 post-infection, which completely prevented viral replication with undetectable p24Gag levels throughout the experimental timeline (Figure 7B, purple legend) indicating that early cART exposure to microglia or preloading microglia with antiretrovirals can completely abolish their ability to serve as HIV reservoirs. Overall p24Gag levels differ between CO-iMs derived from the two iPSC lines, with an approximately 10-fold difference in peak output, and earlier viral replication kinetics in R6 CO-iMs, reflecting donor-dependent differences in infection dynamics and the intrinsic ability to support productive HIV infection. These differences are to some extent expected and are experimentally informative. Thus, CO-iMs provide a robust and generalizable platform that effectively recapitulates microglial differentiation and integration within a 3D brain-like environment and enables modeling of chronic HIV-1 infection and therapeutic suppression.

Beyond viral quantification, CO-iMs also support investigation of neuroimmune interactions following HIV exposure, including microglial inflammatory activation, astrocyte reactivity, neuronal injury, and potential therapeutic rescue. Previous studies using this model have demonstrated neuroimmune signaling changes in response to infection, and the protocol described here allows for reproducible generation of CO-iMs suitable for such downstream analyses.

Detailed neural lineage characterization of CO-iMs, including neuronal and astrocytic populations, has been established.1 The present protocol focuses on reproducible generation and maintenance of CO-iMs and their application to chronic HIV infection studies rather than comprehensive developmental phenotyping.

Additionally, the protocol is designed to model persistent HIV exposure, rather than to resolve discrete microglial activation states. Detailed characterization of activation phenotypes in intact organoids remains challenging, as inflammatory readouts cannot always be attributed to a single type population within a heterogeneous 3D tissue. Approaches, such as single-cell RNA sequencing or spatially resolved transcriptomics, may help address these limitations. Nevertheless, previous reports have indicated that HIV-infected microglia undergo morphological activation and expresses inflammatory gene programs consistent with reactive states observed in HAND. In addition, infected macrophage-like cells can display prolonged survival associated with induction of anti-apoptotic signaling pathways.11,12,13,14,15 Together, these observations support the biological relevance of modeling chronic HIV infection in CO-iMs even when detailed activation phenotyping is not routinely performed.

This experimental framework is particularly relevant when interpreting the persistent detection of viral output under cART. p24Gag levels do not decline to zero because the first-line TRIUMEQ regimen consists of one integrase strand transfer inhibitor (INSTI) and two nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NRTIs) (dolutegravir, abacavir, and lamivudine), which prevent HIV-1 spread, but does not block viral particle production from reservoir cells harboring already integrated HIV-1 proviruses. This persistence likely arises from two factors: (1) enhanced survival of HIV-infected microglia exhibit compared to uninfected cells,12,13,14 and (2) the ability of cART to primarily inhibit new rounds of infection without blocking transcription, translation, assembly, or release of HIV-1 from long-lived reservoir cells, such as microglia.15,16,17,18 Together, these findings highlight the relevance of CO-iMs as a valuable platform for studying HIV neuropathogenesis and evaluating therapeutic responses.

Limitations

Microglia are recognized as critical mediators of HIV infection and neuroinflammation within the CNS, due in part to their surface expression of CD4, CCR5, and CXCR416,17 along with other factors which make them susceptible to HIV. Moreover, as they can remain latently infected and long lived, they represent an important HIV reservoir.16,18 HIV-infected microglia become activated and secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines and neurotoxic factors that exacerbate the inflammatory cascade and contributes to neurocognitive dysfunction and ultimately play a significant role in the development of HAND.16,19,20,21,22,23 However, modeling these complex interactions in cell culture has been challenging due to the low abundance and variability of microglia in conventional human CO models. This protocol addresses these limitations by generating organoids with physiologically relevant proportions of microglia (∼7%). Achieving this level of microglia representation in CO-iMs requires the incorporation of high quality HPCs (characterized as CD43hi and CD45hi) alongside iPSCs from day 0, as illustrated in Figure 1.

Another challenge for organoid models is performing microglia-specific functional assays such as phagocytosis or cytokine responsiveness which are commonly used to assess microglial activity in 2D cultures. Microglia functional assays within intact cerebral organoids are technically challenging. Within organoids, astrocytes may also exhibit phagocytic behavior and respond to inflammatory signals/stimuli, complicating cell type-specific interpretation. Moreover, particulate probes (i.e., pHrodo-labeled particles) have limited diffusion through the dense organoid parenchyma and may not reliably access microglia located in deeper regions.

Importantly, the limitation on routine functional assays within intact organoids does not indicate lack of microglial competence. Human HPC-derived microglia associated with cerebral organoids have been shown to display canonical surveillance behavior and mount injury response after in vivo transplantation, including direct migration toward lesion sites and phagocytic activity.24 In addition, human iPSC-derived microglia exhibit cytokine responsiveness and phagocytic capacity in defined experimental systems.25 Together these studies support that organoid-associated microglia retain functional properties, while the present protocol is focused on reproducible generation and quality control validation of microglia-containing organoids rather than detailed functional phenotyping. However, within this system, HIV infection itself provides a biological relevant inflammatory stimulus, enabling study of microglial activation and downstream neuroinflammatory signaling in a physiologically brain-like complex environment.

Although CO-iMs incorporate microglia along other CNS cell types, replicating additional aspects of brain physiology including vascularization, interactions with peripheral immune cells, and full maturation of all CNS lineages remains a challenge to model in culture. Future efforts may focus on integrating these components and extending culture duration to more accurately model long-term disease processes and chronic immune activation. Finally, from a technical perspective, another limitation of this method is its labor-intensive nature, particularly during the Matrigel embedding stage where EBs must be embedded individually in Matrigel prior to CO-iM expansion. Automating this step could significantly increase the yield of CO-iMs per batch and reduce their variability in size, cell content, and morphology. Nonetheless, CO-iMs offer a versatile and reproducible platform for studying microglia-neuron interactions, viral infections, and neuroinflammatory processes in culture, enabling mechanistic studies and therapeutic screening in the context of neurodegeneration.
