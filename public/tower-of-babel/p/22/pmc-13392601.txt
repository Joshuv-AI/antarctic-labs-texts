Head injury and guideline awareness: A cross-sectional survey of Australian community cricket players
Authors: Anna E Saw, Alex Kountouris, Adam Smith, Martin Love
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSafety in community cricket, particularly head injury, is essential. This study utilised an online, cross-sectional survey open to all community players in Australia. Among 467 respondents (86% male; ages 12 to 60+ years), 36% reported being hit on the head, helmet, or neck at least once in the past 12 months. Of those hit, 81% were wearing a helmet and 35% reported a traumatic head injury. 80% of respondents always wore a helmet when batting against pace. Awareness of Australian Cricket's guidance varied: 80% were aware of the Helmet Recommendations, whereas 49% were aware of the Community Cricket Concussion and Head Trauma Guidelines. These findings add to evidence of a risk of traumatic head injury associated with participation in community cricket and suggest that, while helmet recommendations are generally known and often followed, there remains scope to improve consistency of use, optimal protection, and concussion-guideline awareness and application.Keywords: Brain injuries, Traumatic, Brain concussion, Helmet

Introduction

Cricket is widely participated in across Australia, with approximately 600,000 registered participants across junior, senior, school, social, and inclusion [1]. The scale of this participation highlights the importance of ongoing attention to evidence-based safety practices within community environments.

Traumatic head injuries, including concussion, have been reported in elite [[2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7]] and community cricket cohorts [[8], [9], [10], [11], [12]]. In these studies, head injury more often results from a ball strike while batting or fielding, and less commonly collisions, ground contact, and bat impact.

Safety initiatives at the elite level include: i) requirement for players to wear a helmet which meets the industry standard when batting and fielding at more at-risk positions, ii) implementation of a concussion substitute rule, iii) medical servicing at matches and training, and iv) player, staff, and umpire education [13]. These safety initiatives are not readily replicated at the community level due to resource constraints, with efforts often limited to guideline provision.

The Australian Cricket Helmet Recommendations published by Cricket Australia strongly recommend community cricket players wear an industry compliant helmet when batting, wicket-keeping up to the stumps and/or fielding close to the stumps [14]. The Australian Cricket Concussion and Head Trauma Guidelines provide information on the early management of concussion and guidance on return-to-play, supported by resources for participants and clubs [15].

Although guidelines are available, it is not known whether players are aware of, understand, or follow them. A previous survey of Australian community cricket players and officials during the 2018–19 season demonstrated that up to one-in-three players lacked understanding of helmet recommendations and approximately one-in-three players were unaware of the concussion guidelines [10].

The purpose of this study is to i) gain insight into the scale of head injury risk in community cricket players in Australia, and ii) assess current awareness of, and adherence to, Australian Cricket helmet recommendations and concussion guidelines.

Methods

Institutional ethics approval was obtained (La Trobe University HEC23350). Survey questions were developed by the authors, with input and review from other key stakeholders within Cricket Australia. The cross-sectional survey was administered online via QuestionPro.

Participation was invited through posts on Cricket Australia social media and an advertisement shared by State/Territory Cricket Associations to their members via email or newsletter during the 2023–24 season. Potential participants who clicked on the link to find out more about the study were presented with the Participant Information Statement, with continuation to the survey indicating consent.

Participant eligibility, confirmed by the first questions, included living in Australian in the last 12 months, participating in organised cricket at any level in the last 12 months in Australia, and aged 12 years or over (under 16 required parent/guardian assistance, under 18 required parent/guardian consent). The survey could only be completed once.

The survey included general and cricket-specific demographics, and questions about helmet use, head impacts, and concussion with both fixed response and free-text questions. Data was exported to a spreadsheet (MS Excel) for analysis using descriptive statistics. Proportions were calculated with a 95% confidence interval (CI).

Results

The survey was completed by 467 community cricket players of which 401 (86%) were male and 56 (12%) female. The largest proportion of respondents lived in New South Wales (53%), followed by Victoria (17%), South Australia (10%), Western Australia (9%), and Queensland (8%). Respondents were diverse in age (12–19 years (34%), 20–33 years (16%), 34–46 years (20%), 47–59 years (22%), and 60+ years (8%)) and number of years participating in organised cricket (1–5 years (20%), 6–10 years (27%), 11–15 years (11%), 16–20 years (6%), and 21 or more years (36%)). Level(s) of cricket participation in the last 12 months included school (24%), community club (85%), association/representation (35%), and premier (24%).

Thirty-six percent (95% CI 31–40%) of respondents reported being hit in the head, helmet, or neck one or more times whilst participating in cricket training or a match in the last 12 months. The median number of hits reported by all respondents was 0 (interquartile range 0–1). Of those hit, 57% (49–64%) were hit in a match and 55% (47–62%) were hit in training; 81% (74–86%) were hit on the helmet, 32% (25–39%) were hit on the head without a helmet, and 7% (4–12%) were hit on the neck or neck protector.

Thirty-five percent (28–42%) of respondents who were hit sustained a concussion, symptoms consistent with concussion, or another injury such as a cut, fracture, or bruise as a result of being hit (Fig. 1). Concussion, or symptoms consistent with concussion, was experienced by 20% (14–26%) of respondents who were hit. Free text responses providing details on concussion revealed varied mechanisms across batting, fielding, wicketkeeping and training. Concurrent facial injuries were common when no helmet was worn. Concussion recognition and management was inconsistent with several respondents “playing on” despite symptoms and time-loss ranging from none to weeks or more.

Proportion of respondents by gender and age who reported being hit in the head, separated by helmet use and whether any injury (concussion or other) was sustained.

Eighty percent (76–83%) of respondents reported always wearing a helmet when batting facing a pace bowler in the last 12 months (79% of males, 88% of females). This proportion was higher for younger players and lower for older male players (Fig. 2). Of the remaining 20% of respondents, 5% reported wearing a helmet most of the time, 4% wore a helmet sometimes, and 11% did not wear a helmet.

Proportion of respondents by gender and age who reported wearing a helmet batting when facing pace bowling.

Across all respondents, 80% (76–83%) were aware of the Australian Cricket Helmet Recommendations. Of those who were aware of the guidelines, 80% (75–83%) wore a helmet all of the time. 81% (72–88%) who were unaware of the guidelines wore a helmet. 49% (45–54%) were aware of the Australian Cricket Concussion and Head Trauma Guidelines. Approximately half gained awareness through official resources (Play Cricket website or cricket organisation) whilst the other half were made aware through word-of-mouth.

Discussion

This study surveyed community cricket players in Australia to gain insight into head injury risk and the translation of safety initiatives. Approximately one-in-three players were hit in the head, helmet, or neck and approximately one-in-ten players sustained a traumatic head injury in the last 12 months whilst playing organised cricket. This is consistent with the 9% injury incidence reported by a sample of Australian players in a similar study during 2018–19 [10]. Whilst both survey studies are limited by a potential sampling bias and recall bias, the occurrence of head injury whilst participating in cricket at the community level is evident.

Approximately one-in-five players reported experiencing a concussion or symptoms consistent with concussion while playing cricket in the past 12 months. This proportion cannot be interpreted as an incidence estimate due to the aforementioned sampling and recall biases, as well as the question wording not limiting responses to medically diagnosed concussion. The inclusion of undiagnosed symptoms was a deliberate decision, as players at the community level do not always seek medical attention following a head impact. While the presence of symptoms alone does not allow confirmation of a concussion diagnosis, symptoms are a key diagnostic criterion, and their reporting is therefore meaningful from a risk assessment perspective.

Both this study and the previous study by Kodikara et al. [10] noted that injuries occurred whilst wearing a helmet. The surveys did not discern whether the players were wearing an industry standard helmet [16] including grille and neck protector, or other potential contributing factors such as use of ill-fitting, modified, or damaged helmets. Specific research is needed to evaluate whether cricket helmets reduce the incidence or severity of traumatic head injuries.

This study only assessed awareness of guidelines. Awareness of the existence of concussion guidelines does not equate to having read, understood, and if applicable, applied the guidelines [10]. Further research into the implementation of helmet and concussion guidelines would provide further insight and direction for improvement.

In conclusion, this study provides insight into the occurrence of traumatic head injury in community cricket. While helmet recommendations are generally known and often followed, there remains scope to improve consistency of use and optimal protection. Greater awareness, understanding, and on-field application of concussion guidelines are also needed. Meaningful progress will require a coordinated, multi-level approach spanning the national body, state/territory associations, clubs, and schools. Future work could evaluate equipment advances (including helmet/neck-protector technology and fit-checking), targeted player skill development to reduce exposure to head-height impacts, playing-condition levers such as restrictions on close-in fielding and head-height bowling, and ensuring a safe training environment.
