Spontaneous asymptomatic superficial femoral artery pseudoaneurysm associated with stent fracture
Authors: Theja Bhamidipati, Joseph Yoo, Ann Yufa, Nadia Awad, Evan Deutsch
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractStent fractures of the peripheral arterial system are a documented phenomenon with the most common location being areas of high mobility such as behind the knee or groin. Concurrently, pseudoaneurysm formation of the superficial femoral artery is a rare complication most frequently linked to trauma, infection, or iatrogenic injury. We describe a unique case of a noninfectious, atraumatic, pseudoaneurysm of the superficial femoral artery after stenting. Previous reports have identified pseudoanerysmal degeneration from a stent fracture managed with open repair; however, management of these cases with endovascular repair is rare. This case additionally outlines the endovascular repair of a spontaneous pseudoaneurysm without symptomatology.Keywords: Stent fracture, Pseudoaneurysm, Superficial femoral artery, Endovascular repair

Stent fractures of the peripheral arterial system have been long documented with literature outlining risk factors specifically within the superficial femoral artery (SFA). These risk factors include length of stent (>150 mm), multiple stents, and severe stenosis. Concurrently, pseudoaneurysm formation of the SFA is a rare complication most frequently linked to trauma, infection, or iatrogenic injury. This case outlines a 70-year-old man with a history of hypertension, tobacco abuse, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease who is followed by the vascular surgery team. The patient's vascular surgery history is relevant for peripheral arterial disease, life-disabling left lower extremity claudication recalcitrant to exercise therapy, and nonpalpable pulses. After being initiated on goal-directed medical therapy and smoking cessation, the patient underwent angiography, laser atherectomy, balloon angioplasty, and deployment of a paclitaxel-eluting nitinol stent. This was performed at an outside hospital for a long-segment lesion of approximately 120 mm, treated with two 6 mm × 100 mm stents. Fig 1 showcases pre- and postintervention angiograms. At the patient's immediate follow-up visit, there was resolution of his claudication with return of palpable pedal pulses. The patient was discharged on aspirin, Plavix, and a lipid-lowering agent.

(A) Pretreatment intraoperative angiography during the index intervention demonstrating complete total occlusion of the left superficial femoral artery (SFA). (B) Deployment of two overlapping stents in the left SFA (Eluvia; Boston Scientific). (C) Completion angiogram demonstrating appropriate revascularization with no apparent technical inadequacy.

During the patient's future follow-up approximately 2 years after his initial operation, surveillance ultrasound imaging demonstrated discontinuity within the stent concerning for a stent fracture vs stent dissociation (Fig 2, A). However, the patient had no signs of any claudication. He had normal ankle brachial index (1.08, 1.05). There was no pulsatile mass appreciated nor was there any tenderness. There were consistent lower extremity pedal pulses. Given the ultrasound findings, computed tomography angiography of the left lower extremity was performed, which showed the presence of a stent fracture and a concurrent pseudoaneurysm of the SFA measuring 1.1 cm (Fig 2, B and C). All imaging was performed in anatomic position.

(A) Surveillance duplex ultrasound imaging demonstrating stent fracture with associated aneurysmal changes to the left superficial femoral artery. (B) Axial computed tomography angiography of the left lower extremity with clear stent fracture and associated pseudoaneurysm. (C) Coronal computed tomography angiography redemonstrating pseudoaneurysm and minimal overlap between the two prior stents.

The patient was subsequently taken for lower extremity angiography. Initial fluoroscopic imaging of the left lower extremity demonstrated the presence of the previous stents that had fractured in the mid-SFA and distal above-the-knee popliteal segment. Also noted was that there was significantly less overlap between the two stents. Contralateral retrograde access was obtained, and diagnostic left lower extremity angiography was performed, which demonstrated a pseudoaneurysm at the location of the stent fracture. The stents were crossed using a wire, and a balloon was used to ensure that the wire did not traverse the open cells of the stent. After confirming that the wire was within the stent throughout the entirety of the lesion, the stents were relined using a 7 mm × 100 mm and 8 mm × 150 mm covered nitinol stent with a postdilation balloon angioplasty. Sizing was confirmed with angiographic measurements intraoperatively. The stents overlapped by approximately 2 cm. Post-treatment angiogram demonstrated complete resolution of the pseudoaneurysm, appropriate apposition of the stents to the vessel wall, and two-vessel runoff to the foot, as shown in Fig 3. Since his procedure, the patient has remained asymptomatic and has undergone follow-up imaging at 2-month and 6-month intervals. He is currently maintained on aspirin, Plavix, and a lipid-lowering agent.

(B) Pretreatment angiography demonstrating a patent superficial femoral artery with an associated pseudoaneurysm. There has been an exponential increase in endovascular stent placement in the past 20 years. Device-specific trials are typically followed for 1 to 2 years and rarely capture the late complications from stent placement. In the case of the Eluvia stent, the initial IMPERIAL trial in 2018 demonstrated one fracture in 309 stents.1,2 Long-term follow-up of 3 years through the MAJESTIC trial showed no stent fractures among the studied population.3 Various other stents report a stent fracture rate of between 0% and 20%. Still there remains a paucity in the clinical relevance of these arterial stent fractures. Literature regarding drug-eluting stents does associate stent fractures with increased rates of in-stent restenosis or stent thrombosis. Indeed, certain literature reflects these findings in the peripheral arterial domain though data remain somewhat controversial.

In our case report, we outline an instance of an atraumatic late stent fracture leading to a pseudoaneurysm without any clinical symptoms. Typical teachings are that pseudoaneurysms ≤2 cm of the peripheral arterial system may be managed without any operative intervention. Given that this pseudoaneurysm stemmed from a stent fracture rather than from an access-site complication, and that the risk for further stent migration, stent thrombosis, and acute limb ischemia would be elevated with progressive pseudoaneurysm growth, our institute proceeded with endovascular therapy.4,5

There is a significant paucity of data regarding patient demographics and intraoperative details of patients with stent fracture after endovascular peripheral procedure.6,7 Therefore, it is difficult to identify patient or procedural risk factors to mitigate future stent fractures. Given that there is a growing body of case reports describing late stent fractures that require significant endovascular and open intervention, continued observational data for those with and without stent fracture are prudent. This may ultimately lead to the development of adjusted clinical guidelines for surveillance in a population with asymptomatic stent fractures, something that has not yet been developed. Furthermore, procedural data will be useful in identifying whether adjuncts such as intravascular ultrasound imaging, which would confirm an intraluminal course of the wire, would help mitigate the risk of stent fracture.
