Single-position surgery with intraoperative digital subtraction angiography via a transpopliteal approach for complex spinal vascular lesions: illustrative cases
Authors: Guan-Tze Liu, Li-Yu Fay, Ping-Chuan Liu, Tsung-Hsi Tu, Jau-Ching Wu, Wen-Cheng Huang, Chao-Hung Kuo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDIntraspinal vascular lesions, including dural arteriovenous fistulas (DAVFs) and arteriovenous malformations (AVMs), require accurate intraoperative localization and complete obliteration to prevent irreversible neurological deficits. Conventional transfemoral digital subtraction angiography (DSA) is often limited by patient repositioning during spinal surgery. This study describes a single-position technique integrating microsurgical resection with intraoperative DSA via a transpopliteal approach in the prone position.OBSERVATIONSThree patients (1 AVM, 2 DAVFs) underwent microsurgical treatment with intraoperative DSA through ultrasound-guided left popliteal artery access in a hybrid operating room. Angiography enabled precise localization before durotomy and confirmation of complete obliteration after lesion disconnection without repositioning. Real-time visualization of feeding arteries and draining veins facilitated accurate microsurgical intervention. Intraoperative neurophysiological signals were preserved or improved in all cases. Postoperative MRI and DSA confirmed complete lesion removal and regression of spinal cord edema. No access-related complications were observed, and all patients demonstrated favorable neurological recovery.LESSONSThe single-position transpopliteal intraoperative DSA is a safe and efficient approach for complex spinal vascular lesions. Standardized positioning, risk-based access site selection, and integration of intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring enhance precision and safety. A spinal vascular lesion is an abnormality of the blood vessels within or around the spinal cord, most commonly including spinal dural arteriovenous fistulas (DAVFs),1 arteriovenous malformations (AVMs),2 and cavernous malformations.3 These lesions may be congenital or acquired and can cause irreversible neurological injury, manifesting as progressive weakness, gait disturbance, sensory impairment, sphincter dysfunction, or acute deterioration due to hemorrhage. In DAVFs, abnormal arteriovenous connections lead to venous hypertension and gradual myelopathy. AVMs are typically congenital, often occurring in younger patients, and may present with hemorrhage or slowly progressive neurological deficits. Cavernous malformations are vascular hamartomas prone to recurrent microhemorrhages, which can accumulate and result in neurological decline.1–3

MRI is the first-line modality for evaluation, often revealing cord edema, flow voids, or hemorrhagic changes,2 whereas digital subtraction angiography (DSA) remains the gold standard for definitive diagnosis and therapeutic planning.4 Treatment depends on lesion type and angioarchitecture: DAVFs are best managed by surgical disconnection of the draining vein or, in selected cases, endovascular embolization; AVMs may require microsurgical resection, targeted embolization, or combined approaches; and cavernous malformations are generally observed unless symptomatic, in which case microsurgical excision is considered.5

Traditionally, transfemoral angiography has been the most commonly used method. However, the conflict between the supine position required for transfemoral angiography and the prone position needed for spinal surgery often necessitates a two-stage procedure with repositioning, or awkward femoral access in the prone position, increasing the risk of contamination and catheter kinking.6–8 Intraoperative transfemoral access may require sheath placement before positioning or lateral thigh access, both of which add procedural complexity and risk disrupting the sterile field.6,8 Surgical intervention without intraoperative DSA carries a higher risk of incomplete lesion obliteration, as residual shunting may go undetected intraoperatively. Without angiographic confirmation, surgeons must rely solely on visual inspection and preoperative imaging, which may not adequately reveal complex or deep-seated vascular anatomy, especially in recurrent or anatomically challenging lesions.9,10 In this article, we integrated intraoperative DSA via a transpopliteal approach for the management of intraspinal vascular lesions in a single position. This method provides a streamlined and effective technique that minimizes the need for repositioning and enhances both the safety and accuracy of the procedure. This case series details the application of this technique in managing challenging intraspinal vascular lesions.

Illustrative Cases

Surgical Workflow and Procedure

In the hybrid operating room, the patient was placed in the prone position on a translucent table under total intravenous anesthesia. The intraoperative neuromonitoring (IONM) was set up before surgery, including motor evoked potentials (MEPs) and electromyograms (EMGs) over four limbs. The surgical field, including the posterior spine and left popliteal region, was prepared and draped in a sterile fashion. Prior to puncture, ultrasound evaluation of the popliteal fossa was performed to assess the neurovascular relationships. The optimal puncture zone was selected—typically section 3, which is characterized by greater neurovascular separation and favorable compressibility (Fig. 1). Under ultrasound guidance, a Jelco needle was inserted into the left popliteal artery, followed by placement of a 5-Fr working sheath for diagnostic angiography. A B2 100-cm angiographic catheter (Bentson-Hanafee-Wilson 2, Terumo) was advanced over a 0.035-inch guidewire. Contrast angiography was then performed, targeting the adjacent arterial segments to delineate the vascular architecture and identify the feeding artery and detailed structure of the vascular lesion (Fig. 1).

Anatomical segmentation and procedural guidance for popliteal artery puncture. A: Schematic illustration of the anatomical segmentation of the popliteal region for arterial access. The popliteal fossa is divided into four distinct sections based on surrounding muscular and neurovascular landmarks. Section 1 lies at the superior border, bounded by the biceps femoris and semimembranosus muscles. Section 2 occupies the upper quarter of the fossa, where the popliteal vein typically crosses superficially over the artery. Section 3 represents the central zone, characterized by greater separation among neurovascular structures. Section 4 forms the inferior quarter, where the tibial nerve divides into multiple branches. Each section presents unique technical considerations, including access difficulty, hemorrhage risk, hemostasis control, and potential complications such as pseudoaneurysm or arteriovenous fistula. B: Patient positioning for popliteal artery puncture. C: The patient is placed in the prone position with adequate cushioning to achieve approximately 10°–15° of knee flexion and mild internal rotation of the leg, optimizing anatomical exposure for arterial access. Excessive flexion (> 30°) should be avoided to prevent compression of the popliteal vein. The boundaries of sections 1–4 are also marked. D: Sonography- and angiography-guided medial approach to popliteal artery puncture, demonstrating successful arterial access from the medial aspect of the fossa using combined ultrasound and angiographic guidance for precise and safe sheath placement.

A midline incision was made over the target spinal level and deepened to the fascial layer. The paraspinal muscles were dissected along the spinous processes and lamina to expose the bony anatomy under anteroposterior imaging guidance. After laminectomy of one level above and below the target site, intraoperative DSA was performed before durotomy. The abnormal vascular structures were identified both macroscopically and radiographically.

Under microscopic visualization, the lesion was carefully dissected. The feeding artery and draining vein were temporarily clipped with aneurysm clips and subsequently ligated in sequence after observing MEP and EMG signal changes on IONM and corresponding hemodynamic changes on angiography. The vascular lesion was then obliterated by bipolar coagulation to eliminate high-flow shunting and prevent further venous congestion. The dural sac, which had been compressed and displaced by the lesion, was decompressed, relieving pressure on the spinal cord and nerve roots.

Intraoperative DSA was repeated to confirm complete obliteration, with no residual vascular lesion detected within the territory of the previously feeding artery. A watertight duraplasty was performed, reinforced to ensure proper sealing of the dura. The surgical field was irrigated with sterile saline, and an Exudrain was placed for postoperative drainage. The wound was closed in layers using no. 1 Vicryl and 2-0 Polysorb sutures, and skin staples were applied for final closure. After sheath removal, hemostasis was achieved with 15–20 minutes of manual compression at the popliteal puncture site, followed by application of a sandbag and pressure bandage, with 4 hours of leg immobilization.

Case Illustrations

Three patients with complicated intraspinal vascular lesions underwent single-position surgery with intraoperative DSA.

Case 1

A 48-year-old male presented with progressive bilateral lower limb numbness and urinary retention. MRI and conventional DSA identified a vascular lesion at the left T9 level. Intraoperative transpopliteal DSA confirmed an AVM at the left T9–10 intervertebral foramen, supplied by the left ninth radicular artery with intradural venous drainage. Excision with IONM was performed, and the lesion was completely removed, as confirmed by intraoperative DSA (Fig. 2).

Case 2

A 71-year-old female presented with acute bilateral lower limb weakness and numbness. T2-weighted MRI revealed serpentine intradural vessels and edematous changes of the spinal cord at the T8–11 levels. Conventional DSA confirmed a spinal DAVF supplied by the right eighth thoracic segmental artery. The patient underwent lesion obliteration and spinal cord decompression according to the established protocol (Fig. 3).

Case 3

A 53-year-old female presented with intermittent left lower limb weakness that had recently worsened. MRI revealed serpentine intradural vessels, spinal cord edema, and intradural extramedullary flow voids at T11–L1. Conventional DSA confirmed a spinal DAVF originating from the left L1 lumbar artery with engorged perimedullary veins. The patient underwent T12–L1 laminectomy for decompression and DAVF obliteration following our protocol (Fig. 4).

Outcomes and Follow-Up

All 3 patients demonstrated preserved or improved intraoperative neurophysiological signals. Transpopliteal intraoperative DSA enabled accurate lesion localization before durotomy and confirmed complete obliteration, with restoration of normal distal cord arterial flow and no residual shunting. Postoperative MRI and DSA verified complete lesion removal, resolution of serpentine intradural vessels, and regression of spinal cord edema.

Neurological function improved in all patients: the Nurick grade improved from 1 to 0 in case 1 (with resolution of urinary retention), from 5 to 1 in case 2 (near-complete motor recovery), and from 3 to 1 in case 3. Mild sensory numbness persisted in cases 1 and 3. No access-related complications were observed, including hematoma, pseudoaneurysm, arteriovenous fistula, limb ischemia, or nerve injury, and no procedure-related complications occurred, such as spinal cord infarction, embolic events, or contrast-induced nephropathy. Radiation exposures were 1093, 220, and 310 mGy for cases 1–3, respectively (mean 541 mGy).

Discussion

Observations

Management of complex spinal vascular lesions centers on microsurgical resection and endovascular embolization, with the choice of modality determined by lesion type, angioarchitecture, and patient factors. Microsurgery is generally the most definitive curative option, achieving higher rates of complete obliteration and lower recurrence than embolization, especially for lesions with simple angioarchitecture, small size (< 3 cm), nonmetameric features, and absence of anterior sulcal artery supply.11,12 For spinal DAVFs and epidural arteriovenous fistulas with intradural venous drainage, microsurgical disconnection provides durable cures and low recurrence rates, with most patients achieving neurological stabilization or improvement.13–16 However, microsurgery carries risks, including treatment-related neurological deficits (up to 16% in some series) and higher perioperative morbidity in complex or deeply situated lesions.11,12 Technical challenges are pronounced in high-grade or metameric AVMs, lesions with multiple draining veins, or those supplied by critical anterior spinal arteries, for which microsurgery may not confer superior outcomes and may be less safe than endovascular approaches.11,12

Endovascular embolization provides a less invasive alternative with lower immediate risk and faster recovery, particularly suitable for fistulous lesions, AVMs in surgically inaccessible areas, or high-risk surgical candidates.12,17 Limitations include the complexity of spinal vascular anatomy, which may prevent complete occlusion and result in higher recurrence or retreatment rates than microsurgery.14,16,18 Embolization is often used palliatively to reduce shunt flow and hemorrhagic risk, or adjunctively to facilitate later resection.12,17 AVMs are generally more suitable for advanced multimodal or intraoperative imaging–guided treatment, as their complex nidus and multiple feeders require precise localization and complete obliteration. In contrast, DAVFs typically involve a single, readily identifiable fistulous connection, where simple disconnection of the draining vein is often sufficient for durable cure.19 Treatment should thus be individualized, integrating both modalities as appropriate, with microsurgery favored for lesions amenable to total resection and embolization reserved for select or adjunctive cases.

Intraoperative DSA has become an increasingly valuable adjunct in the surgical management of spinal vascular lesions. Unlike indocyanine green videoangiography, limited to the exposed field, DSA provides a comprehensive view of the spinal vasculature, including feeding arteries, nidus, and draining veins, enabling precise intraoperative assessment of lesion architecture and treatment completeness. The ability to identify residual shunting allows immediate correction, reducing incomplete obliteration and recurrence.6,8,10 Intraoperative DSA has been reported to alter surgical strategy in up to 26% of cases, correlating strongly with postoperative angiographic results. The main limitations are increased operative time (approximately 45 minutes on average), the need for specialized equipment and personnel, and the invasiveness of arterial catheterization. Nonetheless, intraoperative DSA is technically feasible and safe, with no significant access-related complications reported in most series.6–8,10

Prior studies have established the safety and feasibility of transpopliteal intraoperative angiography for spinal vascular lesions.7,9,20 Favorable outcomes have also been reported in a systematic review, with no access-related complications.7 Thus, in this study, standardized prone positioning with 10°–15° knee flexion and mild internal leg rotation facilitated popliteal fossa exposure and increased artery-vein separation in preoperative preparation. Although neurovascular relationships may vary with knee positioning, targeting section 3 of the popliteal fossa allowed safe sheath placement with minimal puncture attempts (Fig. 1).9,21,22

Access refinement is key to optimizing intraoperative DSA. The traditional transfemoral approach allows reliable catheter manipulation but is impractical in the prone position, often requiring prepositioned sheaths or lateral thigh access, both of which complicate workflow. Transradial access is a safe and practical alternative, especially when a radial arterial line is already in place, but it may be limited by aortic arch navigation and catheter length. Transbrachial access is less common and carries a higher neurovascular risk. The transpopliteal approach offers several advantages: linear antegrade access similar to femoral navigation; direct accessibility in the prone position without repositioning; a larger vessel diameter (mean 7 mm) compared with the radial artery (2.4–2.6 mm), potentially reducing puncture-related complications; and the ability to achieve hemostasis by manual compression against bone.23,24

Integration of IONM with real-time DSA enabled stepwise surgical decision-making: temporary clipping was maintained until both stable neurophysiological signals and angiographic confirmation were achieved before permanent ligation. This workflow provided complementary verification of treatment safety and completeness. The radiation dose (mean 541 mGy) was also comparable to reported median values for spinal DSA (1300 mGy) in the literature.25

Lessons

The transpopliteal approach has recently emerged as particularly advantageous for prone position spinal surgery. Under ultrasound guidance, it enables atraumatic puncture, maintains a sterile field, and eliminates the need for repositioning. This method has low complication rates and is technically feasible for confirming intraoperative lesion obliteration, although catheter length may limit access to lesions above T5–6. Overall outcomes are favorable, with most patients demonstrating stable or improved neurological function.

Single-position surgery combined with intraoperative DSA via a transpopliteal approach represents a promising advancement in the management of complex spinal AVMs and related lesions. This surgical strategy provides a safe, efficient, and effective means of treating these challenging pathologies, potentially expanding the therapeutic options available for patients with spinal vascular anomalies.
