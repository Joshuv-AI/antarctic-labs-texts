Metabolomic Biomarkers Are Independently Associated With Secondary Adverse Cardiovascular Events in Patients With Coronary Artery Disease
Authors: Jeffery Osei, Pradeep Tiwari, Chang Liu, Zakaria Almuwaqqat, Arshed A. Quyyumi, Peter W. F. Wilson, Yan V. Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study evaluated the potential of plasma metabolites as novel biomarkers for secondary major adverse cardiovascular event (MACE) development and risk prediction in patients with coronary artery disease.MethodsWe analyzed data from 10 175 UKB (UK Biobank) participants with coronary artery disease and metabolites (n=249) measured through nuclear magnetic resonance at baseline. Cross‐validated elastic net regression models were used in the training cohort (n=7122) to select metabolites to be included in the metabolomic risk score (MRS). Hazard ratios (HRs) were estimated to examine the association between MRS and MACEs (composite of nonfatal myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke, and cardiovascular death) in the testing cohort (n=3053). Predictive performance of MRS was evaluated with C‐index and calibration plots.ResultsOver a median follow‐up of 10 years, 1624 (16%) MACEs occurred. Elastic net regression identified 26 metabolites for construction of the MRS. Participants in the highest 20% of the MRS had significantly increased risk of MACEs (HR, 2.19 [95% CI, 1.61–2.99]) and cardiovascular death (HR, 3.69 [95% CI, 2.35–5.78]), compared with the lowest 20%, after adjusting for traditional risk factors. Adding MRS to a traditional risk factor model modestly improved the 10‐year MACE prediction (ΔC‐index: 0.012 [95% CI, −0.003 to 0.025]); calibration slopes (0.91 [95% CI, 0.79–1.03] versus 0.88 [95% CI, 0.74–1.02]).ConclusionsMetabolomic biomarkers are independently associated with and predict MACEs, particularly cardiovascular death, in middle‐aged individuals with coronary artery disease, providing valuable insights into the biological pathways underlying MACEs in high‐risk populations.

Major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) are the leading cause of morbidity and death in patients with coronary artery disease (CAD), with an estimated incidence between 30% and 40% over an average follow‐up of 15 years. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 Effective modification of traditional risk factors and guideline‐directed antithrombotic therapy have played a pivotal role in reducing MACEs in this high‐risk population over time. 8 , 9 However, risk assessment remains challenging due to the heterogeneity of risk within this population and the complex, multifactorial mechanisms driving secondary MACE development. 8 , 10 Despite significant advancements, many patients continue to experience recurrent MACEs, even with a lower traditional cardiovascular risk factor profile. This underscores the need to identify novel biomarkers to enhance risk prediction and enable early personalized preventive strategies for secondary MACEs.

Metabolomic profiling, an emerging technology for measuring small molecules (metabolites) in body fluids, provides a unique opportunity to identify the combined influence of genetic and environmental factors. 11 , 12 This analytic approach is particularly relevant in populations with CAD for secondary MACEs, where the interplay between metabolic factors may indicate key drivers of disease progression. Despite its potential, the relation between plasma metabolites, including metabolomic risk scores (MRSs) derived from multiple metabolites, and MACE development in patients with CAD has been explored in only a few studies. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 However, published reports have been limited by either a small number of metabolomic biomarkers, a small sample size, or a short follow‐up duration. 13 , 14 , 15 Furthermore, the contribution of MRS to MACE risk prediction in this high‐risk population is not well understood. Given that plasma metabolites are more proximal to disease phenotypes than other omics (genomics, transcriptomics, and proteomics) and can be measured from a single biosample, unlike many traditional cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk factors, deriving MRS presents a promising approach to identifying intermediate metabolites associated with MACEs. 11 , 12 This approach could enable more precise monitoring as well as the development of novel diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for this high‐risk group.

This study aimed to evaluate the potential of circulating metabolites as novel biomarkers for MACE development and risk prediction in patients with CAD. Using a machine learning approach, we constructed an MRS and investigated its association with MACEs in a middle‐aged population with CAD. Additionally, we assessed the incremental predictive value of the MRS when added to the updated Secondary Manifestations of Arterial Disease (SMART2) model, a traditional risk factor–based model designed to estimate the 10‐year risk of MACEs in individuals with established CVD. 17

Methods

Data Availability

This research was conducted using data from the UKB (UK Biobank; Application ID: 34031), which is an open‐access resource for public health research. All supporting data, including statistical/analytic code, are available upon reasonable request.

Study Population and Definition of the Outcome

The study population was drawn from the UKB, a large prospective study with >500 000 individuals aged 40 to 72, recruited between 2006 and 2010. A detailed description of the UKB has been reported previously. 18 In this study, we included a subset of 10 175 participants who, at baseline, (1) underwent nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) metabolic profiling of plasma samples, providing data on 249 metabolomic biomarkers, and (2) had a documented history of CAD (all subtypes), identified using the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth (ICD‐9) and Tenth Revisions (ICD‐10), and Office of Population Censuses and Surveys Classification of Interventions and Procedures (OPCS‐4) codes (see Supplemental Material). The derivation of the final study cohort is shown in Figure 1. Although a formal power calculation was not performed, the sample size was determined by data availability following the application of the study’s inclusion and exclusion criteria and was considered adequate to address the study objectives. The study was approved by the Northwest Multicenter Research Ethics Centre in the United Kingdom and reported in accordance with Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines. 19 All participants provided informed consent before participation.

CAD indicates coronary artery disease; and MRS, metabolomic risk score.

The primary end point of this study was secondary MACE, defined as the first occurrence of a composite of nonfatal myocardial infarction (MI), ischemic stroke, or cardiovascular death after baseline in participants with preexisting CAD. Individual MACEs were used as secondary end points in this study. Events were identified using ICD‐9, ICD‐10, and OPCS‐4 codes (see Supplemental Material). Follow‐up time was defined as the period between enrollment and the date of first recurrent MACE, death, or end of follow‐up as of September 2023 (last day of data access).

Metabolomic Profiling

Metabolic biomarker quantification was conducted on baseline EDTA plasma samples from ≈275 000 randomly selected UKB participants using a high‐throughput, targeted NMR platform (Nightingale Health Plc). 20 This platform simultaneously quantifies 249 metabolic biomarkers per sample, including 168 directly measured and 81 derived ratios, comprising amino acids, fatty acids, lipids, glycolysis‐related metabolites, and ketone bodies. Most biomarkers were reported in absolute concentration units (mmol/L). Further methodological details of the NMR platform have been described previously. 21 , 22 , 23

Construction of the Metabolomic Risk Score

The study population, including participants with established CAD at baseline who underwent metabolomic profiling, was randomly divided into 2 groups: 70% formed the training cohort for feature selection and estimation of β coefficients (weights) used to construct the MRS, while the remaining 30% served as the testing cohort for applying and evaluating the MRS. To identify the most informative metabolites associated with secondary MACEs, an elastic net Cox regression model was used for feature selection. This method integrates both least absolute shrinkage and selection operator and ridge penalties, enabling efficient variable selection and enhancing predictive performance.

In the training cohort (n=7122), age and sex‐adjusted elastic net Cox proportional hazards regression was performed using all 249 metabolomic biomarkers as predictors and secondary MACEs as the outcome. The optimal α for the elastic net model was chosen by a grid‐search approach. A series of α values ranging from 0 to 1, with increments of 0.1, was generated. For each α, a self‐generated sequence of 100 λ values was used to construct an internal 10‐fold cross‐validation model. The evaluation covered all possible combinations of α and λ values within the specified range. The best α and λ combination was selected on the basis of the minimum model error. Metabolomic biomarkers with top frequencies (present in at least 8 of the 10 cross‐validated models) were selected for the calculation of MRS (Table S1).

An unpenalized Fine and Gray competing risk model was then fitted in the training cohort, incorporating all selected metabolomic biomarkers to assess their association with 10‐year incident secondary MACEs (treating non‐CVD death as a competing event). 24 Briefly, the Fine and Gray model directly models the subdistribution hazard function, allowing for the estimation of the cumulative incidence function of the event of interest in the presence of competing risks. 24 We assessed the proportional subdistribution hazard assumption for the Fine and Gray competing risks model using Schoenfeld residuals. The results indicated no violation of the proportional hazards assumption (Figure S1). The resulting coefficients from this model were used to calculate a weighted MRS in the testing cohort (n=3053) by summing the metabolomic biomarkers, each weighted by its corresponding β coefficient.

Traditional Risk Factors and SMART2 Risk Model

We used a refitted version of the updated SMART2 model, a widely used traditional risk factor–based model designed to estimate 10‐year MACE risk in individuals with established CVD, as the reference model in our study. 17 The variables used to calculate the SMART2 risk model were age at enrollment (in years), sex, type 2 diabetes, current smoking, systolic blood pressure, non–high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (derived from total cholesterol and high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol), CRP (C‐reactive protein), estimated glomerular filtration rate, 25 years since first atherosclerotic CVD event, self‐reported history of angina, self‐reported history of heart attack, and self‐reported history of stroke. 17 Detailed description of ascertainment of these variables and other risk factors, including body mass index and alcohol status, is provided in the Supplemental Material. Missing data for systolic blood pressure (6%), total cholesterol (5%), high‐density lipoprotein (12%), CRP (5%), and estimated glomerular filtration rate (5%) were imputed using multiple imputation by chained equations. Coefficients for the SMART2 risk score were derived using the Fine and Gray competing risk model in the training data set and subsequently calculated for participants in the testing data set.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics were described for the overall cohort and compared between the training and testing cohorts using χ2 tests for categorical variables and Student t tests for continuous variables. MRS was categorized into high‐ (top 20%), intermediate‐ (middle 60%), and low‐risk (bottom 20%) groups. Using the middle quintiles (eg, quintiles 2–4) to define the intermediate‐risk group better reflects the overall risk distribution in the general population, as most individuals will fall within this range. This method allows for meaningful public health inferences by highlighting the risk at the extreme ends of the distribution, where individuals in the top and bottom 20% represent distinct risk profiles. Crude cumulative incidence curves for secondary MACE (composite and individual components) stratified by MRS groups were generated.

We assessed the independent associations between each standardized (mean center and scaled by the SD) metabolomic biomarker included in the MRS and secondary MACEs in the testing cohort using age‐ and sex‐adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs, estimated with a Fine and Gray competing risk model. To evaluate the relation between MRS categories and secondary MACEs, both unadjusted (model 1) and adjusted (models 2 and 3) HRs were calculated, using the low MRS group as the reference. Fine and Gray’s competing risk model was used for composite MACE outcomes to account for competing events (non‐CVD death). HRs for nonfatal MI and ischemic stroke were estimated using Cox proportional hazards models, whereas HRs for CVD‐related death were estimated using Fine and Gray competing risks models, with non–cardiovascular death treated as a competing event. Additionally, we modeled MRS as a continuous variable using standardized MRS (mean center and scaled by the SD) values. Covariate adjustment was performed sequentially in 2 models: Model 2 adjusted for age and sex, while model 3 further adjusted for type 2 diabetes status, smoking status, systolic blood pressure, non– high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, estimated glomerular filtration rate, and the number of years since the first atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease event. We assessed the proportional hazards assumption for both the Fine and Gray competing risks model and the Cox model using Schoenfeld residuals. The results indicated no violation of the proportional hazards assumption (Figures S2 through S6). Cubic spline regression was used to evaluate the pattern of association between MRS and secondary MACEs. Statistical significance for linear or nonlinear patterns was assessed with a P value cutoff of 0.05.

We evaluated the incremental value of the MRS in predicting 10‐year secondary MACEs by comparing the discrimination and calibration of 2 models: (1) the SMART2‐only model and (2) the SMART2+MRS model. Model discrimination was evaluated using Harrell’s C index, 26 , 27 with improvement from MRS defined as the difference in C indices (ΔC) between model 1 and model 2. Bootstrapping was used to estimate the 95% CI. Calibration, measuring how close the predicted probability is to the observed risk, was evaluated visually with calibration plots (deciles of predicted versus observed risk of MACEs) and quantified by the calculation of calibration slopes. A slope of 1.0 indicates perfect calibration, while a slope>1.0 suggests higher observed risk than predicted, and a slope<1.0 indicates lower observed risk than predicted.

Results

The overall cohort had a mean age of 62.1±6.0 years, including 7237 (71.1%) men and 2938 (28.9%) women (Table 1). In the training cohort, there were 5095 (71.5%) male and 2027 (28.5%) female participants with a mean age of 62±6.0 years. Over a median follow‐up time of 10 years, there were 1144 (16.1%) total MACEs, 560 (7.9%) nonfatal MIs, 401 (5.6%) ischemic strokes, and 904 (12.7%) cardiovascular deaths in the training cohort. The prevalence of diabetes and hypertension in the training cohort was 19.3% and 10.5%, respectively (Table 1). The average interval from the first atherosclerotic CVD event for participants in the training cohort was 6.5±7.0 years. In the testing cohort, the incidence of MACEs during a median follow‐up of 10 years was lower, compared with the training cohort (480 [15.7%] total MACEs; 171 [5.6%] nonfatal MI cases; 95 [3.1%] ischemic stroke cases; and 288 [9.4%] cardiovascular deaths). The distribution of demographic characteristics, clinical characteristics, and traditional cardiovascular risk factor burden in the testing cohort were similar to the training cohort (Table 1).

Association Between Metabolomic Biomarkers and Secondary MACEs

The elastic net Cox proportional hazard regression models identified 26 metabolites in the training cohort (n=7122) for construction of the MRS. HRs estimating the association of the 26 metabolomic biomarkers and secondary MACEs in the testing cohort are shown in Table 2. Fatty acid degree of unsaturation showed a statistically significant negative association with secondary MACEs (HR per 1‐SD increase: 0.78 [95% CI, 0.63–0.97]). Three other metabolomic biomarkers, including glucose, creatinine, and glycoprotein acetyls had statistically significant positive associations with MACEs (Table 2).

Association Between MRS and Secondary MACE

The incidence of secondary MACEs, including both composite and individual components, was greatest in the high MRS (top 20%) group compared with the intermediate‐ (middle 60%) and low‐risk groups (bottom 20%) (Figure 2). This association was strongest for composite MACEs and CVD‐related deaths. Individuals in the high MRS group had a >3‐fold increased risk of composite MACEs compared with those in the low‐risk group after adjusting for age and sex (HR, 3.08 [95% CI, 2.31–4.12]). With further adjustment for cardiovascular risk factors and clinical phenotypes, the risk persisted (HR, 2.19 [95% CI, 1.61–2.99]). In comparison, the intermediate‐risk group did not show a statistically significant increase in MACE risk (Table 3) compared with the low‐risk group.

On the continuous scale, each SD increase in MRS was associated with a 35% to 50% higher risk of MACEs, even after adjusting for demographic factors (HR, 1.54 [95% CI, 1.43–1.67]), cardiovascular risk factors, and clinical phenotypes (HR, 1.37 [95% CI, 1.25–1.50]). Among MACE outcomes, the association between MRS and cardiovascular death was the strongest, with the high‐risk group having a 3‐ to 5‐fold greater risk than the low‐risk group after adjustment for demographic and cardiovascular risk factors and clinical phenotypes (model 2: HR, 5.26 [95% CI, 3.45–8.03]; model 3: HR, 3.69 [95% CI, 2.35–5.78]). Although MRS was significantly associated with nonfatal MI, the effect sizes were lower compared with composite MACEs and CVD death (Table 3). No statistically significant association was observed between MRS and ischemic stroke. Furthermore, the relation between MRS and secondary MACEs was largely linear for composite MACEs and CVD‐related death, while nonfatal MI and ischemic stroke showed nonlinear associations (Figure 3).

MRS Performance for MACE Risk Prediction

MRS performance for prediction of composite MACEs in the testing cohort was examined by comparing model performance, including discrimination and calibration, before and after addition of MRS to the SMART2 model. Modest improvement in the Harrel C‐statistic was observed after adding MRS to the SMART2 risk (C‐statistic for SMART2, 0.652 [95% CI, 0.627–0.677]; C‐statistic for SMART2+MRS, 0.664 [95% CI, 0.639–0.688]; ΔC 0.012 [95% CI, −0.003 to 0.025]). The calibration performance of the 2 models indicated an overestimation of the 10‐year risk of MACEs (Figure 4). However, adding the MRS to the SMART2 score slightly improved calibration compared with the model with SMART2 alone, as reflected in the calibration slopes (0.91 [95% CI, 0.79–1.03] versus 0.88 [95% CI, 0.74–1.02]).

Discussion

In a cohort of participants with CAD, a high MRS derived from 26 metabolomic biomarkers using penalized elastic net models was associated with an increased risk of composite MACEs, nonfatal MI, and CVD‐specific death during a 10‐year follow‐up period. Notably, the strongest effect sizes (ranging from 3‐ to 5‐fold) were observed for participants in the top 20% of MRS, compared with those in the bottom 20%, suggesting a possible threshold effect above which MRS more strongly impacts the risk of MACEs in a population with known CAD. The association was significant even after adjusting for demographic factors, traditional cardiovascular risk factors, and clinical phenotypes. The observed nonsignificant association between MRS and ischemic stroke is likely due to the lack of statistical efficiency from the relatively small number of ischemic stroke events in the cohort. Furthermore, the addition of the MRS to a traditional risk factor–based model slightly improved risk prediction, with better (but modest) calibration performance compared with the traditional risk factor–based model alone.

Previous studies have largely demonstrated the association of MRS with adverse cardiovascular events in individuals without a history of CAD, with limited research focusing on secondary event populations. 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 However, the few studies conducted in individuals with existing CVD were limited by small sample sizes, shorter follow‐up periods, 15 or fewer metabolomic biomarkers. 13 For example, Rizza et al identified independent associations between only 2 of 49 metabolites (alanine and medium‐ and long‐chain acylcarnitines) and increased MACE risk in 67 patients with existing CVD over 3.5 years of follow‐up. 13 Mehta et al analyzed >6500 metabolites in a combined cohort of 776 patients with CAD followed for 3 years and found that a metabolite risk score of 7, including creatinine, independently predicted all‐cause death. 15 More recently, Zhu et al reported significant associations between 15 metabolites (primarily amino acids and glycolysis‐related metabolites) and all‐cause death in 1606 patients with CAD over 3.5 years of follow‐up. 14 Using data from a large cohort of individuals with longer follow‐up duration, our study builds upon this evidence, providing a more comprehensive understanding of the long‐term effects of metabolomic biomarkers on the risk of secondary MACEs in a middle‐aged population with CAD. Additionally, consistent with prior findings, the novel MRS showed a stronger association with MACEs compared with individual metabolomic biomarkers, highlighting the potential utility of combining multiple biomarkers into a composite risk score. 34 , 35 Our study found a modest improvement in predictive performance when MRS was added to a clinical risk score, similar to findings from previous studies in primary event populations or individuals with type 2 diabetes. 32 , 33 , 36 , 37 , 38 This addresses a significant gap in understanding MRS performance in secondary prevention settings, where such evidence has been limited.

Several key metabolites within the MRS, such as glucose, creatinine, glycoprotein acetyls, and fatty acid degree of unsaturation, were found to be strongly associated with MACEs and provide important insights into the mechanisms underlying CVD progression in patients with CAD. Many of these biomarkers may be linked to other established risk factors for MACEs, such as inflammation and metabolic dysfunction, which could explain their association with MACEs and their contribution to the predictive value of the MRS. For instance, glycoprotein acetyls, an inflammatory biomarker, has been implicated in the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis. 39 , 40 Similarly, elevated creatinine and glucose underscore the contributions of kidney dysfunction and hyperglycemia to increased MACE risk. 41 , 42 Furthermore, the degree of unsaturation of fatty acids may indicate disruptions in lipid metabolism, promoting inflammation and oxidative stress, key mechanisms in the development and progression of cardiovascular events. 43 While the overall performance of the MRS in MACE risk prediction was modest, the use of machine learning tools helped uncover metabolites previously linked to other cardiometabolic diseases, such as type 2 diabetes, but with limited or yet‐to‐be‐established utility in patients with CAD. Importantly, combining these individual metabolites into a composite score, rather than evaluating them individually, has been shown in previous studies to enhance their association with cardiovascular outcomes and improve predictive performance, supporting the potential utility of integrated metabolomic risk models. 34 , 35

While the addition of the MRS to the SMART2 model yielded only a modest improvement in discrimination and calibration, this may still hold some clinical relevance, particularly for a severe and prevalent cardiovascular outcome like MACEs in high‐risk populations such as patients with CAD. Such a small increment in predictive performance can potentially influence clinical decision making and translate into public health benefits when applied across large populations. 44 , 45 However, we acknowledge that the costs and complexity of implementing metabolomic profiling in routine care necessitate further research to objectively evaluate the clinical utility of the MRS, including its cost effectiveness, robustness, and generalizability across diverse populations. Also, the ability to capture additional insights into the biological pathways underlying MACEs through a single plasma‐based metabolomic profile may support the development of novel therapeutic targets. Since plasma metabolites are more proximal to disease phenotypes, they may reflect underlying pathophysiological processes more directly than traditional risk factors.

A major strength of this study is the use of machine learning algorithms for model development rather than traditional statistical approaches. Specifically, we used penalized elastic net regression, a regularization method that integrates penalties from both least absolute shrinkage and selection operator and ridge regression to optimize variable selection. This method enhances predictive accuracy while addressing multicollinearity and overfitting, challenges often encountered in conventional statistical models that prioritize statistically significant associations in variable selection over predictive performance. Our study has some limitations. First, the participants in the UKB are predominantly of European ancestry, and the absence of an independent external validation cohort further limits the generalizability of our findings to broader and more diverse populations. Future studies are necessary to validate these findings in more diverse cohorts. Second, the UKB exhibits a healthy volunteer bias, potentially influencing comparisons between our findings and the true prevalence of disease in the general population. 46 However, because exposure–disease relationships do not necessarily require a representative population, and our study focused on individuals with established CAD, the impact of this bias on our results is likely minimal. Furthermore, although our study included a larger number of metabolomic biomarkers compared with previous studies, the MRS was still based on a limited panel of 26 biomarkers. Future research could refine and improve the predictive performance of the MRS by incorporating a more comprehensive panel of metabolomic biomarkers.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that a 26‐metabolomic biomarker score is significantly associated with adverse cardiovascular events in middle‐aged individuals with established CAD. While the addition of the MRS to a traditional risk factor–based model resulted in modest improvements in MACE risk prediction, its clinical utility and incremental value in real‐world settings warrant further evaluation. As metabolomics becomes more accessible in clinical practice, its ability to assess a broad range of metabolic markers from a single specimen at 1 time presents a distinct advantage over traditional risk assessment measures, which often require multiple tests over time. These findings, along with insights from prior studies leveraging machine learning and metabolomics, have the potential to advance our understanding of complex biological pathways and improve risk stratification in patients with CVD, ultimately contributing to more efficient and personalized clinical decision making.
