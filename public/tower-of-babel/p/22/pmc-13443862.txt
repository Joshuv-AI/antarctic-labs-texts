Diagnostic and potential prognostic associations of long noncoding RNAs LIFR-AS1 and SLCO4A1-AS1 in multiple myeloma
Authors: Mahmood Khosravi, Behjat Kalantari Khandani, Alireza Farsinejad, Elham Jafari, Hajar Mardani Valandani, Amirhossein Sahebkar, Ali Afgar, Roohollah Mirzaee Khalilabadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThere is growing evidence for the involvement of LncRNAs in the development of multiple myeloma (MM). We aimed to investigate the possible diagnostic and potential prognostic associations of two LncRNAs with oncogenic potential, LIFR-AS1 and SLCO4A1-AS1, in MM. Using qPCR, expression levels of both LncRNAs were quantified in 30 newly diagnosed patients with MM and 30 healthy controls. Their associations with clinicopathological parameters including serum protein electrophoresis, immunofixation, bone marrow, biochemical, hematological indices and the expression of IL-17 A and RORC, downstream adaptors of the IL-17/NF-κB signaling pathway, were assessed. Diagnostic applicability was sought via ROC curve analysis. Both LncRNAs were significantly downregulated in MM patients and showed inverse correlations with IL-17 A, RORC expression, and serum IL-17 levels. Their reduced expression was also negatively correlated with β₂-microglobulin, LDH, and ESR, but positively correlated with albumin and hemoglobin (all P < 0.05). ROC analysis demonstrated potential diagnostic utility, yielding an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.691 for LIFR-AS1 (cut-off: 0.065-fold; sensitivity: 30%; specificity: 100%) and 0.711 for SLCO4A1-AS1 (cut-off: 0.805-fold; sensitivity: 80%; specificity: 100%). Our findings suggest that LIFR-AS1 and SLCO4A1-AS1 may serve as potential diagnostic relevance and prognostic associations biomarkers in MM. Further studies and warranting functional investigation are recommended to evaluate their pathogenic roles and downstream molecular interactions. Introduction

Multiple myeloma (MM) is a malignancy arising from the post-germinal center transformation of plasma cells. Atypical clonal proliferation of immunoglobulin-producing plasma cells (PCs) in the bone marrow is the usual defining feature of this condition1,2. MM is an uncommon form of cancer, representing a mere 1–2% of all cancer cases and somewhat over 10% of hematologic neoplasms3. The majority of patients exhibit indications or symptoms associated with the invasion of plasma cells into the bone or internal organs4. Recent progress in therapeutic approaches have greatly enhanced patient survival, but MM is still regarded as an untreatable condition, with a median overall survival of 6 years5. Therefore, targeted therapies are essential for these patients, and ongoing efforts aim to discover new biomarkers with diagnostic and therapeutic implications6,7.

Dysregulation of long non-coding RNAs (LncRNAs) has been linked to several type of cancers. LncRNAs, defined as transcripts exceeding 200 nucleotides and transcribed by RNA polymerase II, are key regulators of gene expression8,9. They affect transcriptional and post-transcriptional levels of gene expression, including mRNA splicing and stability, and act as molecular sponges for microRNAs (miRNAs)10. Emerging evidence indicates that lncRNAs play critical roles in MM pathogenesis by regulating key signaling pathways, such as IL-17 and NF-κB11.

Two lncRNAs of recent interest are LIFR-AS1 and SLCO4A1-AS1. LIFR-AS1 has been reported to regulate tumor behavior, at least in part via the IL-17 A/NF-κB pathway, acting as an oncogene in pancreatic and colorectal cancers12,13, but as a tumor suppressor in glioma and lung cancer14,15. SLCO4A1-AS1 is upregulated in several malignancies, including colorectal cancer, gastric carcinoma, and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), where it promotes cell proliferation and carcinogenesis16–18. LncRNAs can also function as competing endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs) for miRNAs, which are master regulators of post-transcriptional gene expression. miRNAs, which typically repress gene expression, are intimately linked to cancer biology and have been shown to mediate crucial functions in MM19,20.

The selection of LIFR-AS1 and SLCO4A1-AS1 for investigation in multiple myeloma was based on converging evidence. First, bioinformatics analysis of a public transcriptomic dataset (GEO: GSE6477) revealed significant dysregulation of both lncRNAs in malignant versus healthy samples.

Second, both lncRNAs have been implicated in cancer pathogenesis through highly relevant mechanisms: LIFR-AS1 regulates NF-κB signaling via miR-4262 modulation, and NF-κB activation is a hallmark of glioma progression and drug resistance14, SLCO4A1-AS1 interacts with the NF-κB pathway through miR-223-3p in NSCLC18. Third, preliminary screening in a small patient cohort confirmed significant dysregulation and downregulation of both lncRNAs in MM. These observations suggested that LIFR-AS1 and SLCO4A1-AS1 might play important roles in MM biology and could serve as potential diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the diagnostic and prognostic relevance of these lncRNAs in newly diagnosed MM patients.

ROC curve analysis

The AUC values for LIFR-AS1 and SLCO4A1-AS1 were found to be 0.691 (95% CI, 0.558 to 0.824; P-value = 0.011) and 0.711 (95% CI, 0.580 to 0.843; P-value = 0.004), respectively. Additionally, the optimal cut-off for LIFR-AS1 was determined to be 0.065, yielding the sensitivity and specificity rates of 30% and 100%, respectively. The optimal cut-off for SLCO4A1-AS1 was established at 0.805, with sensitivity and specificity rates of 80% and 100%, respectively (Fig. 5). Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate the expression of two lncRNAs, LIFR-AS1 and SLCO4A1-AS1, in newly diagnosed MM patients and to investigate their diagnostic potential and associations with clinical and laboratory features.

We observed significant downregulation of both lncRNAs in MM patients compared to healthy controls. This pattern is compatible with a potential tumor suppressor role, although functional validation is required to confirm this hypothesis.

Future studies should directly test whether restoring the expression of these lncRNAs inhibits the proliferation or survival of malignant plasma cells.

Furthermore, the downregulation of LIFR-AS1 and SLCO4A1-AS1 correlated with elevated levels of established poor potential prognostic association markers in MM, including erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and serum levels of IL-17 A, beta-2 microglobulin (B2M), and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH).

LIFR-AS1, an antisense lncRNA transcribed from the LIFR gene on chromosome 5p13.1, was downregulated in our MM cohort. This finding aligns with reports of reduced LIFR-AS1 expression in other malignancies, such as breast and serous ovarian carcinoma21,22. In those cancers, lower LIFR-AS1 expression has been associated with poor prognosis, higher risk of progression, larger tumor size, advanced stage, and metastasis22,23.

Functional studies support a tumor-suppressive role; for example, in hepatocellular carcinoma, LIFR-AS1 downregulation correlates with poor prognosis, while its overexpression inhibits tumor growth and induces apoptosis24. Similarly, LIFR-AS1 overexpression suppresses proliferation and metastasis in breast cancer21. In glioma, LIFR-AS1 exerts its tumor-suppressive function by sponging miR-4262, thereby inhibiting the NF-κB pathway and suppressing cell proliferation, migration, and invasion14.

In our study, reduced LIFR-AS1 expression in MM patients was accompanied by increased expression of IL-17 A and its key transcription factor RORC, suggesting a potential role to inflammatory signaling. Given the crucial role of the NF-κB pathway in plasma cell biology regulating proliferation, apoptosis, and drug resistance25. it is plausible that LIFR-AS1 may influence MM pathogenesis through similar mechanisms, possibly via competitive regulation of NF-κB components such as p65 and IκBα14.

The second lncRNA investigated, SLCO4A1-AS1, is transcribed from the antisense strand of the SLCO4A1 gene on chromosome 20. Emerging evidence indicates that SLCO4A1-AS1 is involved in tumor development and progression by regulating miRNAs and signaling pathways16,26.

In our cohort, its downregulation was associated with elevated IL-17 A/RORC expression and higher serum levels of IL-17 A, B2M, and LDH markers of adverse prognosis in MM or observed correlations may reflect an overall association with more advanced disease.

The role of SLCO4A1-AS1 appears context-dependent across cancers. It has been reported to act as an oncogene, promoting cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and cisplatin resistance in lung cancer17, and its silencing reduces viability and proliferation while accelerating cell death in acute lymphoblastic leukemia27. Conversely, in colorectal cancer (CRC), SLCO4A1-AS1 suppression enhances apoptosis and impedes cell growth, potentially via the Wnt/β-catenin pathway28.

Reports of its overexpression in melanoma, bladder cancer, and ALL further highlight its variable expression patterns27,29,30. In non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), SLCO4A1-AS1 activates the NF-κB pathway by interacting with miR-223-3p, leading to IKKα amplification17.

The observed inverse correlations between SLCO4A1-AS1 expression and inflammatory markers (IL-17 A, ESR) in our study suggest its potential involvement in IL-17 or NF-κB signaling in MM, though this remains speculative without functional validation.

The IL-17 axis is prominent in MM pathophysiology, influencing osteoclast activation and myeloma cell growth31. Th17 cells, which require the transcription factor orphan nuclear receptor RORγt (encoded by RORC) for differentiation, are a key source of IL-1732.

We found elevated serum IL-17 A levels and increased RORC expression in newly diagnosed MM patients, with both parameters showing significant negative correlations with LIFR-AS1 and SLCO4A1-AS1 expression. While these observations implicate the IL-17 axis, they do not definitively identify the cellular sources of IL-17 A. The increased RORC expression suggests potential involvement of Th17 cells, but direct cellular profiling was beyond this study’s scope. Future research is warranted to investigate the specific immune cell populations contributing to IL-17 A production in the MM bone marrow microenvironment and to elucidate whether the observed lncRNAs directly modulate these cells or their cytokine production.

In our exploratory analysis, SLCO4A1-AS1 showed promising diagnostic potential with high specificity (100%) and acceptable sensitivity (80%), though its moderate AUC (\:0.711) indicates a need for further optimization and validation.

The role of lncRNAs as diagnostic biomarkers in hematologic malignancies, including MM, is an area of active investigation33. Previous studies have reported LIFR-AS1 overexpression in gastric and pancreatic cancers, with ROC analysis supporting its diagnostic utility12,34.

Similarly, elevated SLCO4A1-AS1 has been proposed as a diagnostic biomarker in CRC and malignant glioma28,35. The negative correlations we observed between LIFR-AS1/SLCO4A1-AS1 expression and general disease burden markers (LDH, B2M, ESR) should be interpreted with caution. While statistically significant and suggestive of an association with more advanced disease, these correlations do not establish causality. LDH, B2M, and ESR are broad indicators of tumor burden, renal function, and systemic inflammation, respectively. Therefore, these associations may primarily reflect overall disease severity rather than direct molecular interactions. Future studies with larger cohorts and more specific molecular profiling are needed to determine whether these lncRNAs directly influence these biomarkers or whether the observed associations are secondary to their relationship with disease progression.

Our findings should also be considered within the broader context of the bone marrow microenvironment (BME), a complex network of stromal and immune cells that supports MM survival, drug resistance, and progression.

Our findings on LIFR-AS1 and SLCO4A1-AS1 downregulation should be considered within the broader context of the bone marrow microenvironment (BME) that shapes plasma cell disorders. The MM BME comprises a complex network of stromal cells (including mesenchymal stem cells, fibroblasts, and osteoblasts) and immune components (such as T cells, macrophages, and myeloid-derived suppressor cells) that collectively support tumor survival, promote drug resistance, and drive disease progression36. Non-coding RNAs, including lncRNAs, have emerged as critical regulators of these cellular interactions. While our study focused on correlations with systemic inflammatory markers (IL-17 A) and transcription factors (RORC), future investigations should explore how LIFR-AS1 and SLCO4A1-AS1 might directly influence or be regulated by specific BME components. For instance, these lncRNAs could modulate cytokine production by stromal cells, affect immune cell recruitment or polarization, or participate in feedback loops that sustain the pro-tumorigenic niche. Understanding such networks may reveal novel mechanisms underlying the transition from precursor conditions to overt myeloma and identify microenvironment-targeted therapeutic strategies. In summary, we report, for the first time, the downregulation of LIFR-AS1 and SLCO4A1-AS1 in newly diagnosed MM patients and their association with markers of disease severity and the IL-17 axis. These lncRNAs may represent novel tumor suppressor candidates and regulators of inflammatory signaling in MM. However, given the exploratory nature of this study and its limited sample size, these findings require validation in larger, independent cohorts. Future functional studies are essential to elucidate the precise molecular mechanisms and therapeutic potential of these lncRNAs in MM pathogenesis.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that the lncRNAs LIFR-AS1 and SLCO4A1-AS1 are downregulated in newly diagnosed multiple myeloma (MM) patients. This downregulation correlates with elevated expression of IL-17 A and its transcription factor RORC, as well as with established clinical and laboratory markers of disease severity. These findings suggest that LIFR-AS1 and SLCO4A1-AS1 may function as tumor suppressors in MM, potentially influencing disease pathogenesis through modulation of the IL-17 A/NF-κB inflammatory axis. Furthermore, both lncRNAs, particularly SLCO4A1-AS1, show preliminary diagnostic potential. However, these observations require functional validation to establish causality. Future studies with larger, independent cohorts and longitudinal designs are necessary to confirm these associations and to elucidate the precise molecular mechanisms involved.

Limitations of study

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the cohort size, while adequate for an initial exploratory analysis, is relatively small. Future validation in larger, independent cohorts is essential. Second, the cross-sectional design prevents assessment of the prognostic value of LIFR-AS1 and SLCO4A1-AS1. Longitudinal studies are needed to determine their association with treatment response, progression-free survival, and overall survival. Third, our analysis was performed on RNA extracted from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs). While this captures a composite signal from both circulating tumor cells and the systemic immune response, it does not distinguish between tumor-specific and immune cell-specific expression. Future studies employing cell-sorting techniques would clarify the cellular source of these lncRNAs. Fourth, due to severe resource constraints resulting from regional conflict and economic sanctions, we were unable to perform several key analyses. These include: cytogenetic risk stratification (e.g., for t(4;14), t(14;16), del17p, or 1q amplification); detailed cellular profiling of IL-17 A-producing immune subsets (e.g., Th17 cells) by flow cytometry; and investigation of these lncRNAs in precursor conditions like monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS) or smoldering myeloma. Consequently, we cannot determine whether expression patterns vary across molecular subtypes or disease stages, nor can we identify the precise cellular source of IL-17 A. Fifth, the statistical framework relied on non-parametric comparisons and correlation analyses, which do not account for potential confounding by variables such as age, renal function, or inflammatory status. While the observed correlations with markers like lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and beta-2 microglobulin (B2M) provide clinical context, they may reflect overall disease burden rather than direct molecular causality. Finally, the proposed regulatory roles of LIFR-AS1 and SLCO4A1-AS1 in the IL-17 A/NF-κB pathway are based on correlative data. Direct functional validation through in vitro and in vivo experiments is required to establish mechanistic causality.

Future studies

To build upon these findings, future research should address the following priorities:

Validation and Clinical Translation: The diagnostic and prognostic potential of LIFR-AS1 and SLCO4A1-AS1 must be validated in larger, multi-center cohorts that include diverse patient populations and incorporate cytogenetic risk stratification. Their performance should be compared against established diagnostic and prognostic markers, such as serum free light chains and bone marrow plasma cell percentage, to determine their additive clinical value.

Mechanistic Investigation: Direct functional validation is required to establish causality. Gain- and loss-of-function experiments in myeloma cell lines are needed to determine whether these lncRNAs influence cell proliferation, apoptosis, drug sensitivity, and the activity of the IL-17/NF-κB pathway.

Cellular and Molecular Context: Future studies should employ cell-sorting techniques to delineate the expression of these lncRNAs specifically in malignant plasma cells versus various immune cell subsets within the bone marrow microenvironment. Concurrent detailed immunophenotyping (e.g., of Th17 cells) would clarify the cellular source of IL-17 A and its relationship to lncRNA expression.

Disease Evolution and Prognosis: Investigating these lncRNAs in precursor conditions (MGUS, smoldering myeloma) and in longitudinal samples from patients undergoing therapy is crucial to understand their role in disease progression and their utility as dynamic biomarkers of treatment response and survival.

Materials and methods

Study participants

Thirty patients with newly diagnosed multiple myeloma (MM) and 30 age- and sex-matched healthy controls were enrolled from the Hematology-Oncology Ward of Afzalipour Hospital, Kerman, Iran, between July 2023 and December 2024. The diagnosis of MM was established according to the International Myeloma Working Group (IMWG) diagnostic criteria. The sample size was calculated using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7), with the following parameters: two-tailed alpha (Type I error) = 0.05, power (1 – beta) = 0.80, and an effect size of 0.43. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Kerman University of Medical Sciences (Approval No.: IR.KMU.REC.1402.304) and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The required sample size was calculated a priori using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7) Based on preliminary data from our pilot cohort (n = 8 per group) comparing lncRNA expression levels between MM patients and healthy controls, an effect size of 0.43 was estimated. Using a two‑tailed alpha of 0.05 and a desired power of 0.80, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 28 participants per group. To account for potential technical failures or sample exclusion, we enrolled 30 patients and 30 controls.

Clinical data collection

The baseline clinical data included the following: (a) Demographic factors such as age and gender; (b) levels of immunoglobulins in serum protein electrophoresis (SPEP) and immunofixation electrophoresis (IFE), (c) bone marrow aspiration (BMA) and biopsy (BMB) results (e.g., cellularity, plasma cells percent); (d) bone marrow flowcytometry results (CD38, CD56, CD138), (e) clinical symptoms and signs (e.g., bone pain, bone lytic lesions), (f) renal function, (g) biochemical and hematological profile (i.e., erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), hemoglobin (Hb), white blood cell count, and platelet count), and (h) clinical stage as determined by the Durie-Salmon (DS) staging and international staging system (ISS). The diagnosis of MM was verified by an experienced pathologist oncologist.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All MM diagnoses were confirmed by an experienced hematopathologist/oncologist. Patients were included if they met the following criteria: (1) newly diagnosed (de novo), symptomatic MM as defined by the International Myeloma Working Group (IMWG) criteria37 and (2) age ≥ 18 years. Patients were excluded if they had: (1) asymptomatic (smoldering) or relapsed/refractory MM; (2) concurrent amyloidosis, other hematologic malignancies, or active solid tumors; (3) active infection with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), hepatitis B virus (HBV), or hepatitis C virus (HCV); (4) any prior history of chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or stem cell transplantation for any condition; or (5) were pregnant or lactating. Healthy control subjects were required to be ≥ 18 years of age. Their health status was verified through clinical examination and review of medical history to confirm the absence of hematologic disorders, solid tumors, chronic infectious diseases (HIV, HBV, HCV, syphilis), and significant cardiac, hepatic, or renal dysfunction. Routine biochemical and hematological profiles were also reviewed to confirm the absence of clinically significant abnormalities.

Total RNA extraction

Before receiving any chemotherapy medications, peripheral blood samples were collected from patients with MM, as well as healthy individuals. Isolation of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) was achieved by Ficoll Hypaque lymph-prep density gradient centrifugation. In MM patients, circulating plasma cells were detectable in the peripheral blood, as is commonly observed in newly diagnosed, untreated patients with active disease. Total RNA was extracted from the PBMC fraction, which included both immune cells and circulating plasma cells. The extraction of total RNA was performed using the YTzol Pure RNA Kit (Yekta Tajhiz Azma Co., lot: AZ0211). Quantification of RNA concentration was performed using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer manufactured by Thermo Fisher Scientific in Waltham, MA, USA. We were used RNA samples with A260/A280 ratios between 1.8 and 2.0 and A260/A230 ratios > 1.8. RNA integrity was visually assessed by 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis, examining for distinct 28 S and 18 S ribosomal RNA bands; samples showing clear rRNA bands and minimal smearing were deemed suitable for downstream analysis. Samples exhibiting degraded RNA profiles were excluded from further analysis. All RNA samples were stored at −70 °C to maintain their stability for subsequent analyses.

cDNA synthesis quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA (1000 ng) was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using a cDNA synthesis kit (Yekta Tajhiz Azma) and stored at −20 °C until use. SYBR Green Real-Time PCR was used to quantify LIFR-AS1, SLCO4A1-AS1, IL-17 A, and RORC (Table 4). The qPCR reactions were conducted using a mixture containing 12.5 µL of SYBR Green Master Mix. The thermal cycling conditions consisted of an initial five-minute denaturation at 94 °C, then 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 70 s, annealing at 58.5 °C for 40 s, and extension at 72 °C for 50 s. Validation of the real-time PCR products was achieved by melting curve analysis.

Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as the internal reference gene for normalization. The Livak method (2^-ΔΔCT) was used to calculate changes in mRNA expression . Each sample was analyzed in triplicate.

Primer design and specificity assessment

Primer sequences for LIFR‑AS1, SLCO4A1‑AS1, IL‑17 A, RORC, and GAPDH were designed using (NCBI Primer-BLAST) and are listed in Table 4. The primers melting temperatures (Tm) were optimized using Gene Runner software version 6.5.52, developed by Frank Buquicchio and Michael Spruyt. All primer pairs were further validated empirically by melt curve analysis following each qPCR run, confirming the generation of specific amplification product.

Biochemical and hematological assays

Biochemical parameters were measured in serum samples from all participants. Serum concentrations of beta-2 microglobulin (B2M), albumin (ALB), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), calcium, and creatinine were quantified using commercially available assay kits on a BS-480 Chemistry Analyzer (Mindray, China). Serum interleukin-17 A (IL-17 A) levels were determined using a human IL-17 A enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (Karmania Pars Gene Co., Kerman, Iran) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. All assays were performed in duplicate. A complete blood count (CBC), including hemoglobin concentration, white blood cell count, and platelet count, was performed for all samples using a Sysmex XN-1000 automated hematology analyzer (Sysmex Corporation, Japan).

Statistical analysis

SPSS software (version 26.0) was employed to conduct statistical analyses, and GraphPad Prism 8 was employed to generate graphics. The normality of all continuous variables was evaluated using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistical test. Comparative analysis between patients and controls was conducted using Mann-Whitney and Independent Samples t-test. Correlational analysis was conducted by calculating the Spearman Correlation Coefficient. In addition, the diagnostic potential of LIFR‑AS1 and SLCO4A1‑AS1 expression levels was explored using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The area under the curve (AUC) with its 95% confidence interval (CI) was calculated for each lncRNA. The optimal cutoff value, along with the corresponding sensitivity and specificity, was determined using the Youden index. Statistical significance was defined as a P-value of less than 0.05.
