Effect of a digital resilience-based course-game sequential intervention on social participation in stroke survivors with limb dysfunction: a randomised controlled trial protocol
Authors: Xuan Zhou, Yijia Li, Songqin Gao, Runlin Liu, Ying Wang, Lanshu Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionImpairments in social participation are highly prevalent after stroke and are strongly associated with diminished quality of life. Existing interventions have demonstrated limited effectiveness, often focusing predominantly on physical functional training while neglecting the critical psychological and behavioural processes necessary for a successful transition from physical recovery to community reintegration. Furthermore, traditional models relying on face-to-face delivery suffer from poor accessibility, particularly in contexts with unevenly distributed rehabilitation resources. This study aims to evaluate the effect of a digital resilience-based course-game sequential intervention on improving social participation among stroke survivors with limb dysfunction.Methods and analysisThis study is a single-centre, assessor-blinded, two-arm randomised controlled trial. A total of 152 stroke survivors with limb dysfunction will be recruited from three rehabilitation wards of a single tertiary rehabilitation hospital and randomly allocated in a 1:1 ratio to either the intervention group or the control group. Participants in the intervention group will receive a digital resilience-based course-game sequential intervention, which is designed to first impart knowledge and skills through the course, followed by their application and reinforcement in the game context to facilitate behaviour transfer, over a 7-week period. The control group will receive a structured, attention-matched control condition comprising digitally delivered general health education materials. The primary outcome is social participation assessed immediately post-intervention using the Frequency subscale of the Utrecht Scale for Evaluation of Rehabilitation-Participation (USER-P). Secondary outcomes include resilience, exercise adherence, physical function, social network and life-space mobility. All outcomes will be measured at baseline, immediately post-intervention and at 3-month post-intervention. All analyses will follow the intention-to-treat principle.Ethics and disseminationThis study has been approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai First Rehabilitation Hospital (YK-2025-03-011). Trial findings will be disseminated via peer-reviewed publications and conference presentations. Introduction

Stroke remains a leading cause of long-term adult disability worldwide, with a substantial proportion of survivors facing persistent limb dysfunction.1 The consequences extend beyond physical impairment, profoundly disrupting an individual’s social participation—defined as their involvement in life situations.2 Impairments in social participation are highly prevalent post-stroke and are robustly linked to diminished quality of life, increased psychological distress and mortality.3 4 Consequently, promoting social reintegration is a priority in stroke rehabilitation.

Our prior systematic review identified limitations in the evidence base for social participation interventions, which is not only sparse but also indicates limited efficacy.5 Current rehabilitative efforts to enhance social participation have predominantly focused on physical functional training or facilitating support within specific community programmes.6,8 While valuable, these approaches frequently overlook the critical psychological and behavioural processes that mediate the transition from physical recovery to successful community reintegration. Many individuals with limb dysfunction retain the physical capacity for greater engagement but are impeded by psychological barriers, including fear of negative evaluation, loss of confidence and a reduced ability to adapt to new challenges.9 10 This underscores a significant gap in care: the absence of interventions that systematically target the intrinsic psychological resources required to navigate the complex social environment after stroke.

The problem of limited efficacy is compounded by poor accessibility. The prevailing model of delivering psychosocial interventions relies on face-to-face sessions,11 12 which is particularly unsuitable for the healthcare context in China. Given the vast population of stroke survivors and the unequal distribution of specialised rehabilitation resources across regions, such a model is unsustainable and fails to reach the majority of patients in need.13 There is thus an urgent and dual gap: a lack of potent interventions that target the psychological mechanisms of social participation, and a lack of scalable delivery models to make such interventions widely accessible.

To address the first gap of intervention potency, we first identified resilience as a promising theoretical construct. Compared with other psychological factors frequently targeted in rehabilitation, such as self-efficacy or motivation, resilience encompasses a broader, dynamic process of positive adaptation in the face of adversity.14 In the context of stroke recovery, resilience can be conceptualised as a dynamic process through which individuals leverage internal strengths and external resources to maintain or regain social engagement despite disability. An initial cross-sectional study found a significant positive association between resilience and social participation in this population.15 A subsequent investigation delineated the underlying mechanism, revealing that resilience influences social participation both directly and indirectly through the serial mediation of improved exercise adherence and enhanced physical function. This evidence provides a clear psychological-behavioural pathway for intervention: enhancing resilience can catalyse a positive cascade leading to greater social participation.

To ensure this scientifically grounded intervention can bridge the second gap of accessibility, we designed it as a digital complex intervention delivered via a ubiquitous WeChat mini-programme. This digital delivery model is not merely a matter of convenience; it is a strategic necessity to ensure that an effective intervention can transcend geographical and resource constraints,16 thereby achieving widespread impact across China. Guided by the Kumpfer resilience framework,17 18 we developed a novel resilience-based course-game sequential intervention designed to address both identified gaps. Its innovation lies in integrating universal resilience training with contextualised application: a course module systematically builds psychological skills, while a subsequent game module immerses users in simulated real-world social scenarios to practise applying these skills, thereby directly targeting the transition from knowledge to behaviour19 . This design moves beyond general psychoeducation to offer targeted, context-specific training for social participation.

The primary objective of this randomised controlled trial (RCT) is to evaluate the effect of this digital resilience-based course-game sequential intervention on social participation among stroke survivors with limb dysfunction. We hypothesise that the intervention group will demonstrate significantly greater improvement in social participation at post-intervention compared with an attention-matched control group. Furthermore, we hypothesise that the intervention group will also show significantly greater improvements in the following secondary outcomes at post-intervention: increased resilience, better exercise adherence, enhanced physical function, a larger social network and greater life-space mobility. Exploratory objectives include investigating the potential mediating roles of these variables in the intervention’s effect on social participation.

Methods

This study is a two-arm randomised, controlled, assessor-blinded clinical trial conducted at Shanghai First Rehabilitation Hospital, Shanghai, China. As an initial RCT evaluating this novel digital intervention, the single-centre design allows for rigorous control and intensive monitoring of the implementation process with available resources, which is pivotal for establishing proof of concept and informing the design of future multi-centre studies. Participant recruitment will be conducted across five specialised rehabilitation wards within the hospital to ensure a heterogeneous and representative sample of the target population. The study protocol is reported in consistent with the recommendations of the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) 2013 statement.20 An overview of the study design is shown in figure 1. According to the protocol, participant recruitment is scheduled to run from November 2025 to June 2026. The entire study, from recruitment through final analysis, is planned to be completed by December 2026.

Study population

Participants will be included in the study according to the following inclusion criteria: (1) meeting the diagnostic criteria for stroke established by the Fourth National Cerebrovascular Disease Conference, confirmed by cranial CT or MRI, including both ischaemic and haemorrhagic stroke,21 (2) patients with a disease duration of ≥3 months since the last stroke onset, (3) aged between 18 and 80 years, (4) presence of limb dysfunction, with a modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score between 1 and 4, (5) stable vital signs, (6) a score of ≥26 on the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), indicating normal cognitive function, (7) no significant auditory or linguistic communication barriers and (8) voluntary participation in the study with a signed informed consent form. Participants will be excluded if they (1) are unable to use a smartphone or complete digital interventions, (2) report comorbid conditions that could confound outcomes (eg, severe dysphagia, major depressive disorder, advanced cancer, end-stage organ failure) and (3) participate in another concurrent clinical trial.

Recruitment

Participant recruitment will be conducted in the wards. A responsible nurse will distribute paper-based recruitment flyers to potentially eligible stroke patients, providing a preliminary introduction to the study’s purpose and procedures. Patients who express interest will be contacted by a researcher to complete a preliminary screening. This screening is designed to assess key eligibility criteria, such as the patient’s score on the mRS and their ability to use a smartphone and application mini-programmes. Eligible candidates who pass the screening will then be invited for a face-to-face interview. During this interview, the researcher will ensure the patient fully comprehends the study details, time commitments and potential obstacles. A 1-week period will be allowed for participants to decide whether or not they wish to participate in the study. Participants’ reasons for non-participation will be recorded. Patients who voluntarily provide written informed consent will be formally enrolled into the study and will subsequently proceed to the baseline assessment and randomisation phase. Recruitment will continue until the target sample size has been reached.

Sample size estimation

The sample size for this trial is determined through an a priori calculation in G*Power software (V.3.1.9.4),22 targeting the primary outcome of social participation at the post-intervention assessment. This decision was informed by a critical synthesis of the current evidence base. While our recent network meta-analysis (manuscript in preparation), along with other published network meta-analyses (NMAs) on post-stroke social participation,23 has reported very large standardised mean differences (SMDs>2.7), these estimates are often accompanied by significant heterogeneity and are potentially susceptible to inflation from small-study effects and methodological limitations of the aggregated trials. Concurrently, a large-scale trial published in JAMA Network Open reported a non-significant, minimal effect on community participation,8 highlighting the risk of over-optimism. While other studies have reported large Cohen’s d values (eg, d>0.9),24 we deemed it prudent to adopt a conservative approach to ensure the robustness of our findings. Consequently, a medium-to-large effect size (Cohen’s d=0.6) was selected as the target. This value aims to balance the optimistic upper estimates against the null findings in the literature, ensuring the study is adequately powered to detect a meaningful effect without being underpowered by overly optimistic assumptions. With a two-sided α of 0.05 and 80% power, the calculation (using a t-test for means between two independent groups) indicated a requirement of 128 participants (64 per group). Based on previous studies on social participation in stroke survivors, the attrition rate ranged from approximately 0%7 to 15.2%.25 Therefore, allowing for a 15.2% attrition rate, the final target sample size required is 152 participants.

Randomisation, concealment and blinding

Participants will be randomly allocated to either the intervention or control group. The randomisation sequence will be generated by an independent statistician using a computer-based random number generator. To ensure balanced distribution of participants’ characteristics across groups, the randomisation will be stratified by rehabilitation wards with a block size of six. The allocation sequence will be concealed using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. After obtaining written informed consent and completing the baseline assessment, the research assistant who is not involved in patient enrolment will open the next envelope in sequence to reveal the group assignment. Another research assistant will receive names and contact details of the participants allocated to the intervention group and be responsible for following the intervention. This study is single-blinded in view of the nature of the intervention. Intervention staff and participants are not feasible to be blinded to the group allocation. The researchers collecting baseline and follow-up data will be kept blind to participant allocation.

Intervention protocol

All participants will continue to receive their routine standard care throughout the study period. Following baseline assessment and randomisation, participants will be assigned to one of the two parallel groups receiving distinct study interventions.

Intervention group

Participants allocated to the intervention group will receive a digital resilience-based course-game sequential intervention. The development and description of this intervention are detailed below.

Theoretical basis and intervention development

The intervention was developed primarily based on the first four steps of the Intervention Mapping methodology: needs assessment, definition of objectives, selection of theory-driven intervention strategies and programme development.26 27 Rooted in Kumpfer’s resilience framework,17 18 this intervention is designed to systematically cultivate five key internal protective factors. Specifically, cognition refers to an individual’s capacity for rational thinking, which helps shield them from negative thoughts; emotion entails the ability to appropriately recognise and process negative feelings; spirituality reflects a firm belief in one’s own capacity to improve circumstances and achieve goals; behavioural skills such as problem-solving enhance self-efficacy in coping with adversity; and physical well-being implies that individuals with a solid physical foundation are more inclined to internalise it as a positive psychological state. The intervention designed aims not only to teach universal resilience skills but also to deliver targeted application within specific social participation contexts.

Intervention content and implementation details

The intervention content is delivered via two sequential modules on the WeChat mini-programme: the course learning module and the gamified experience module. The course learning module forms the core of the intervention, consisting of 7 weekly thematic sessions (detailed in online supplemental table 1). These sessions are systematically designed, with each session aligning with a resilience factor, aiming to progressively achieve psychological empowerment. Each theme is presented as a detailed, cohesive content system integrating text, images, videos, case studies and interactive questions within a narrative storyline. Users progress through the material simply by sequentially clicking a ‘Next’ button. To further reduce cognitive load and enhance accessibility for stroke survivors, all textual content is accompanied by professional voice-over narration, allowing users to choose between audio-only or combined audio-text learning. Each session is designed to last approximately 60 min and is intended to be completed once per week over 7 weeks. Crucially, to enhance feasibility and reduce burden, participants are not required to complete a weekly session in one continuous sitting. The platform allows the content to be accessed multiple times within the week, with progress automatically saved, enabling users to split the session into shorter segments according to their own pace and availability. After finishing all seven course sessions, participants unlock the gamified experience module. This module serves as the practical application component, using a supermarket shopping journey to simulate various obstacles encountered from home to checkout. Users are guided to apply the resilience skills learnt in the course to navigate these challenges. The game’s narrative begins with a stroke patient with mild-to-moderate right-sided limb dysfunction preparing a birthday dinner for their spouse, unfolding across five core levels: leaving home, the community park, the supermarket entrance, item selection and checkout. Each level is designed with distinct obstacles and problem-solving mechanics that directly reinforce the five intrinsic protective factors of the Kumpfer resilience framework. Each gaming session lasts approximately 20 min. In addition to the core functionalities, the mini-programme also provides a ‘Social Participation Elements’ query page, which allows participants to dynamically track and monitor their social participation goals, facilitators, barriers and strategies identified throughout the courses. The mini-programme also offers the functions of browsing knowledge related to stroke and engaging in conversations with the backend.

Control group

Participants allocated to the control group will receive a structured, attention-matched control condition comprising digital delivery of general health education materials. To precisely match the intervention group’s schedule for contact time and engagement, the control participants will receive these materials in 7 weekly electronic instalments over the subsequent 7 weeks. Each instalment will consist of PDF documents or a structured web page containing educational text and illustrative graphics on topics such as stroke basics, complication management, nutrition and general exercise advice. Crucially, the content of these materials is strictly informational and is carefully designed to exclude any components of psychological resilience training, behaviour change techniques or guided social participation practice that constitute the active ingredients of the experimental intervention. Apart from this distinct informational provision, both groups will receive identical routine medical treatments, rehabilitation therapies and nursing care.

Outcome measurement

The researchers masked to the participant allocation will collect follow-up data through face-to-face interview. Prior to the study, all outcome assessors will undergo standardised training to ensure consistent understanding and application of the assessment scales. Throughout the trial, regular meetings will be held to review assessment procedures and resolve any discrepancies to maintain rating consistency. Participants will be assessed at baseline (T0), immediately post-intervention (T1) and 3-month post-intervention (T2). Primary outcome

Social participation

Utrecht Scale for Evaluation of Rehabilitation-Participation (USER-P) will be used to assess social participation.28 It consists of 32 items across three subscales: Frequency, Restrictions and Satisfaction. The Frequency subscale has two parts: Part 1 (4 items) and Part 2 (7 items), both rated on a 6-point scale (0=never to 5=≥36 hours per week or≥19 times). The Restrictions subscale (11 items) is rated from 0 (impossible) to 3 (no difficulty). The Satisfaction subscale (10 items) is rated from 0 (very dissatisfied) to 4 (very satisfied). No total score is calculated; instead, raw subscale scores are converted to a normalised score (0–100). Higher scores indicate greater frequency, fewer restrictions and higher satisfaction. The Chinese version shows good internal validity, with Cronbach’s α values of 0.704, 0.861 and 0.841 for the subscales, and test-retest reliability coefficients of 0.734, 0.832 and 0.824.29 30

Secondary outcomes

Resilience

The 10-item Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale, originally developed by Connor and Davidson and later simplified by Campbell-Sills and Stein, will be used to evaluate the resilience of participants.31 Responses are recorded on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (‘not true at all’) to 4 (‘true nearly all the time’). The total score ranges from 10 to 40, with higher scores indicating greater psychological resilience. The scale has shown good reliability and validity across diverse populations, with reported Cronbach’s α of 0.94, split-half reliability of 0.89 and item-total correlations between 0.74 and 0.81.32 33

Exercise adherence

Exercise adherence will be measured using the Exercise Adherence Questionnaire (EAQ), developed by Lin Beilei et al. in 2013.34 The EAQ includes 14 items across three dimensions: engagement in physical exercise, monitoring of exercise effects and active seeking of exercise advice. Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (‘cannot do at all’) to 4 (‘can do completely’), yielding a total score between 14 and 56. The EAQ demonstrates good psychometric properties, including content validity (0.95), structural validity, criterion validity, test-retest reliability (ranging from 0.778 to 0.850) and a Cronbach’s α of 0.923.35

Physical function

The Motor subscale of the Functional Independence Measure (FIM-motor) will be applied to measure physical function.36 It contains 13 items rated on a 7-point scale from 1 (‘complete dependence’) to 7 (‘complete independence’). The total score ranges from 13 to 91, with higher scores indicating better motor function. The FIM-motor shows good validity and is significantly correlated with the Barthel Index (r=0.955, p<0.001).37

Social network

The Lubben Social Network Scale will be used to evaluate social networks.38 It comprises six items (three on family network, three on friend network), each rated on a 6-point scale from 0 to 5. The total score ranges from 0 to 30, with higher scores indicating a better social network. The Chinese version has an internal consistency reliability of 0.83, with Cronbach’s α values of 0.90 for the family network and 0.95 for the friend network.39

Life space

The Life-Space Assessment will be used to assess participants’ life space.40 It assesses mobility through five concentric zones (from bedroom to beyond one’s town), considering the frequency of movement and independence level. Scores for each zone are calculated by multiplying the zone level, frequency score and independence score. The total score ranges from 1 to 120, with higher scores indicating a larger life space and scores<60 indicating restricted life space. The Chinese version has a test-retest reliability of 0.76 and demonstrates a good construct validity.41

Potential covariates

Socio-demographic characteristics and clinical-related data

Socio-demographic characteristics and clinical-related data will be collected at baseline from participants. Patients will be asked about age, gender, educational level, marital status, family monthly income, disease duration, stroke type, stroke side and stroke location.

Stroke severity

Stroke severity will be evaluated using the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale .42 This scale includes 15 items that systematically assess key neurological functions such as level of consciousness, gaze, visual fields, facial palsy, motor function in the arms and legs, sensation, ataxia, language, dysarthria and sensory neglect. Each item is scored on an ordinal scale, and the total score ranges from 0 (no neurological deficit) to 42 (severe stroke), with higher scores indicating more severe neurological impairment.

Disability rating

mRS will be used to assess disability level.43 It is a 7-point ordinal scale ranging from 0 (no symptoms) to 6 (death). A score of 0 indicates no neurological symptoms; 1 indicates minor symptoms without significant functional impairment; 2 reflects slight disability that prevents some activities but allows self-care; 3 denotes moderate disability requiring assistance with certain tasks yet retaining the ability to walk independently; 4 corresponds to moderately severe disability characterised by the inability to walk without assistance and requiring help with daily activities; 5 represents severe disability where the patient is bedridden, incontinent and requires constant nursing care; and 6 indicates death.

Cognitive function

MoCA will be used to assess participants’ cognitive function.44 It assesses multiple cognitive domains, including visuospatial abilities, executive function, memory, attention, language and orientation, with a total possible score of 30. A score of ≥26 is generally considered indicative of normal cognitive function. For individuals with 12 years or fewer of formal education, a correction of +1 point was added to the raw score to adjust for educational background.45

Psychological distress

Psychological distress will be measured using the Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K10).46 Originally developed by Kessler et al in 1992, the K10 is designed to rapidly assess the level of psychological distress experienced by individuals over the past 4 weeks46 . It consists of 10 items focusing on anxiety and depressive symptoms, each rated on a 5-point scale (from ‘never’ to ‘always’). The total score ranges from 10 to 50, with higher scores indicating more severe psychological distress. The K10 demonstrates good reliability and validity, with Cronbach’s α typically ranging between 0.85 and 0.95.47

Intervention fidelity and adherence

To ensure the validity and replicability of this digital intervention study, intervention fidelity will be assessed and enhanced in accordance with the recommendations of the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Behaviour Change Consortium,48 focusing on the following aspects. All intervention content has been standardised based on the Kumpfer Resilience Framework and embedded in the mini-programme backend prior to study initiation. Delivery is automated and consistent via the WeChat mini-programme, ensuring all participants receive identical core components of the intervention. The courses are released on a weekly unlock schedule, supported by a standardised escalating reminder system (automatic reminders sent 3 days, 1 day and on the day of unlocking) to optimise participant adherence. To evaluate intervention receipt, the backend system will automatically track and record each participant’s course access, dwell time and task completion status. For the gaming module, key monitoring metrics include overall task completion and, critically, participants’ behavioural choices at key decision points, which serve to assess their ability to apply theoretical knowledge. The research team will conduct weekly reviews of aggregated adherence data (eg, overall course completion rates). For participants who fail to engage for two consecutive weeks, a research coordinator will place a supportive and standardised follow-up call to understand potential difficulties and provide technical assistance. This process aims to enhance engagement without offering guidance on specific course content. In the post-intervention follow-up assessment, qualitative methods will be used to understand how participants set social participation goals, as well as their experiences and barriers in applying the strategies learnt in real-world social contexts.

Intervention discontinuation

Intervention for a participant will be discontinued for any of the following reasons: voluntary withdrawal of consent, the occurrence of a serious adverse event or significant clinical deterioration warranting discontinuation for safety reasons. Participants who discontinue the intervention will be encouraged to complete all subsequent follow-up data.

Data and safety monitoring

All participant data will be initially collected using standardised paper-based Case Report Forms at the assessment sites. These data will be subsequently transcribed into a centralised electronic database. A double-data entry process with built-in logical and range checks will be employed to ensure accuracy. All data will be de-identified using unique study codes and stored on secure, password-protected servers. Only the research team will have access to the servers. On study completion and after all data have been verified for accuracy, the electronic database will be formally locked under the instruction of the Principal Investigator. This locked dataset will serve as the definitive basis for all final analyses in this study. The Principal Investigator and the research team will be responsible for continuous safety monitoring. This includes the systematic collection, documentation and assessment of all adverse events. Any serious adverse events will be reported to the Institutional Review Board within the required timeframe.

Statistical analysis

All analyses will adhere to the intention-to-treat principle. Data will be analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics (V.26.0) for descriptive and basic inferential statistics, R software (V.4.2.2) with the Geepack package for generalised estimating equation (GEE) models and Mplus (V.8.4) for mediation analysis. Descriptive statistics will summarise participant characteristics. Continuous variables will be presented as mean (SD) or median (IQR), and categorical variables as frequency (percentage). Baseline comparisons will use independent t-tests, Mann-Whitney U tests or χ2/Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate.

The primary analysis will assess the effect of the intervention on social participation at the immediate post-intervention time point, with the USER-P frequency subscale serving as the sole primary endpoint. Analyses of all other social participation measures (the remaining USER-P subscales) will be considered secondary. An analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) will be conducted with the post-intervention score as the dependent variable, group as a fixed factor, and the corresponding baseline score as a covariate. This model provides an adjusted estimate of the between-group difference.

To examine the trajectory of outcomes across all assessment points (baseline, post-intervention and 3-month follow-ups), GEE will be employed. This longitudinal model will include group, time and the group-by-time interaction as factors, with an autoregressive correlation structure assumed for repeated measures. The model will adjust for baseline values of the outcome to enhance precision.

An exploratory mediation analysis using a bootstrapping approach in Mplus V.8.4 will test whether immediately post-intervention resilience, exercise adherence, physical function, social network and life space mediate the intervention’s effect on the primary outcome. The model will control for baseline levels of all variables, and significance of the indirect effects will be determined via 5000 bootstrap samples with 95% bias-corrected CIs.

The distribution of outcome variables and model assumptions will be checked. If substantial missing data (>10%) occurs, multiple imputation (Markov chain Monte Carlo method, preferred) will be considered. All statistical tests will be two-sided with a significance level of α=0.05.

Discussion

Although social participation is crucial for the long-term quality of life of stroke survivors,49 current interventions have limitations in both effectiveness and accessibility. Predominant approaches primarily focus on physical functional training or rely on resource-intensive, face-to-face models, often overlooking the critical psychological and behavioural processes necessary for transitioning from physical recovery to successful community reintegration.50 To address this dual challenge, we developed a digital resilience-based course-game sequential intervention, delivered via a WeChat mini-programme, designed to systematically empower patients in overcoming psychosocial barriers to social participation. This RCT will rigorously evaluate whether this theoretically grounded, highly accessible digital solution can improve social participation among stroke survivors with limb dysfunction, which could, if effective, address a critical gap in current clinical practice.

The primary innovation of this study lies in the deep integration of universal resilience skills and targeted applications within specific social participation contexts. Many existing resilience interventions are highly general and lack contextual specificity.51 52 For instance, while they often teach universal stress management techniques, they typically lack specialised training on how to initiate and apply these skills in specific high-pressure social situations.53 This gap frequently leads to a dilemma, where individuals understand the principles but struggle to apply them effectively in real-world social stressors. The core innovation of this study is its shift from universal resilience education to contextualised resilience training. We not only focus on individuals’ intrinsic psychological qualities but also anchor the intervention precisely within the concrete context of social participation. On the one hand, through the course learning module, we provide scenario-based training targeting different resilience factors, such as fostering positive cognitions about social participation, guiding participants to use emotion regulation techniques to cope with negative emotions related to social participation, and helping them set goals and develop action plans for social participation. On the other hand, the serious game module highly simulates real-world social participation barriers, such as public questioning and environmental obstacles, enabling individuals to naturally activate learnt resilience skills when facing actual social pressures, thereby forming efficient situation-response associations. The RCT aims to examine the effectiveness of this resilience-based course-game sequential intervention, thereby providing a testable theoretical model for future research.

The second innovation of this study is the development of a dual-module digital intervention delivery model driven by course-game integration. This model ues two complementary media forms to optimise the efficiency of knowledge acquisition and behavioural transformation, respectively. The course module is meticulously designed with a coherent narrative thread running through each instructional topic, transforming abstract psychological theories into a learning system integrating text, images, videos, case studies and interactive exercises. Participants can progress seamlessly by simply clicking ‘Next’, significantly reducing operational barriers while maintaining learning focus and continuity. Furthermore, the module supports repeated access to specific topics, balancing the depth of personalised review with the breadth of standardised knowledge delivery. The game module serves as a practical field for applying knowledge gained from the courses.54 It ingeniously embeds learning objectives within a simulated supermarket shopping scenario. This context acts as a concrete behavioural cue, guiding participants to proactively apply theories and techniques learnt in the courses within a high-fidelity, low-risk environment. This facilitates the transition from cognitive understanding to behavioural application, effectively promoting the transfer of knowledge into practical competence.55 Ultimately, by leveraging the synergy between these two media forms, this study is designed to enhance the intervention’s appeal and effectiveness, while contributing to methodological innovation for future psychological and behavioural intervention research.

In addition, the study design incorporates key considerations for real-world applicability and scientific rigour. First, the use of a WeChat mini-programme for delivery addresses the challenge of unevenly distributed rehabilitation resources in China,56 aiming to ensure low cost, standardised delivery and potential for future large-scale dissemination.57 Second, regarding the control condition, this study employs routine health education as the comparator. Given that the intervention contains complex, theory-driven psychosocial components, designing a perfectly matched, inert ‘placebo’ programme poses substantial ethical and practical difficulties.58 The present design thus aims to maximise the attribution of any observed effects to the core active ingredients of the intervention. Finally, concerning outcome assessment, to mitigate potential bias associated with subjective measures like social participation, we will employ validated instruments such as the USER-P,59 supplemented by objectively recorded adherence data (eg, course completion rates, simulation task performance) automatically captured by the programme backend, thereby constructing a multi-dimensional evidence chain to cross-validate intervention effects.

Looking ahead, should the intervention prove effective, its implementation is designed to be low-burden. It can be seamlessly embedded into discharge planning or community rehabilitation via a simple QR code, empowering healthcare providers to support psychosocial recovery without significant additional workload. A key consideration for sustained impact, however, will be maintaining long-term engagement beyond the initial structured programme. Additionally, technology fatigue could affect long-term adherence, particularly in populations with lower digital literacy. Future iterations may address this by optimising the user interface for simplicity and exploring adaptive content or periodic digital prompts to support habit formation.

This study also acknowledges several limitations. First, due to the nature of the intervention, blinding of participants and intervention providers was not feasible, which may introduce expectation bias. To mitigate this, outcome assessments will be conducted by research staff unaware of group allocation, using standardised scales. Second, the primary outcome relies on patient self-report, which carries a risk of subjective bias. To address this, we will use USER-P, assessing multiple dimensions including frequency, restrictions and satisfaction. Furthermore, we will incorporate objective or behaviour-related secondary outcomes (eg, exercise adherence, physical function). Third, the requirement for participants to use a smartphone may systematically exclude older individuals or those with more severe disabilities or lower digital literacy. This raises an issue of equity and may limit the generalisability of findings to the broader stroke population. Although we will provide technical support, future iterations of the intervention should consider including alternative access modes (eg, with caregiver assistance) to enhance inclusivity. Fourth, the participant sample will be recruited from a single rehabilitation centre, which may limit the generalisability of the findings. Future research should be conducted in multi-centre settings. Additionally, while the supermarket scenario is culturally intuitive for our setting, its context may require adaptation for implementation in different cultural regions. Notwithstanding these limitations, this study protocol is rigorously designed to evaluate a novel, feasible and theoretically grounded solution to the challenging problem of post-stroke social participation. The findings will determine whether this digital intervention can offer a crucial practice model and evidence base for the large-scale implementation of evidence-based psychosocial interventions within resource-constrained healthcare systems in China.
