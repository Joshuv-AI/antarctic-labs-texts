Parasitological prevalence of scabies and secondary bacterial infections among scabies suspected patients at Borumeda General Hospital, Northeast Ethiopia
Authors: Habtu Debash, Agumas Shibabaw, Hussen Ebrahim, Mihret Tilahun, Abdurahaman Seid, Getnet Shimeles, Birhanu Kassanew, Ermiyas Alemayehu, Yeshimebet Kasa, Sisay Desale, Amanuel Mengesha, Alemu Gedefie
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundScabies disproportionately affects people in resource-poor areas. Clinical diagnosis risks misdiagnosis due to resemblance to other skin diseases, but laboratory confirmation improves accuracy. Scabies allow for secondary bacterial infections. Associated bacteria exacerbate scabies and antibiotic resistance. Ethiopian scabies diagnosis relies solely on clinical exams without confirming parasites or investigating secondary bacterial infections. This study aims to identify parasites via scraping, isolate secondary bacteria, and evaluate their antibiotic susceptibility profiles.MethodsA hospital based cross-sectional study was conducted from September 2022 to July 2023 among scabies suspected patients who visited the dermatology clinic at Borumeda General Hospital in Northeast Ethiopia. A systematic random sampling technique was used to select 422 study participants. Socio-demographic, hygiene, and clinical characteristics data were collected via face-to-face interviews and observation. Skin scrapings for parasitological investigations and swab samples for microbiological investigations were collected and transported for analysis and drug susceptibility testing. Descriptive statistics and logistic regression analysis were employed to assess risk factors.ResultAmong 422 skin scraping samples, 156 (37.0%) cases of scabies were microscopically confirmed. Bed-sharing and having contact history were independent predictors of microscopically confirmed scabies. The prevalence of secondary bacterial infections among scabies-confirmed patients was 35.9% (56/156). The most prevalent bacterial species were Staphylococcus aureus, coagulase-negative staphylococci, and Streptococcus pyogenes. Tetracycline for Gram-positive bacteria and ampicillin for Gram-negative bacteria showed the highest rate of resistance. In both Gram-positive and Gram-negative strains, multidrug resistance was also observed.ConclusionThis study found that over one-third of skin scrapings tested positive for scabies. Additionally, more than one-third of scabies cases were complicated by secondary bacterial infections. Improved scabies diagnosis and consideration of secondary bacterial infections are important for better controlling this neglected tropical disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-024-09977-8.Keywords: Scabies, Parasitological diagnosis, Secondary bacterial infection, Drug resistance

Introduction

Scabies is one of the most common dermatological diseases, accounting for a significant number of skin diseases in underdeveloped nations. It estimates that scabies affects 158 million people worldwide per year [1], and its prevalence ranges from 0.2 to 71% [2]. In many resource-poor tropical environments, the estimated average prevalence among children is 5–10% [3]. Its prevalence in Sub-Saharan Africa ranged from 4.7 to 33.7% [4–6], while in Nigeria, it ranged from 4.7 to 65% [7]. Scabies is one of the most prominent public health problems among communicable diseases in Ethiopia, with a prevalence ranging inconsistently from 2.5 to 78% [8]. The highest incidence of scabies infestation occurs in nations with hot, tropical climates, particularly in areas where overcrowding and poverty coincide and treatment is limited [9].

Scabies severity is not only related to the quantity of mites but also to the immune response of the patient [10, 11]. Clinical diagnosis is the most common diagnostic approach for scabies. The International Alliance for the Control of Scabies (IACS) in 2020 has agreed upon criteria for clinically diagnosing scabies without laboratory confirmation [12]. However, the cutaneous manifestations of scabies can be similar to those of other infectious and non-infectious skin conditions. Furthermore, given the shortage of trained dermatologists in Ethiopia, clinical diagnosis alone can lead to the misdiagnosis of scabies. It has previously been noted that there is a 45% possibility of misdiagnosing scabies as other skin disorders [13]. Misdiagnosis exposes patients to the possible negative effects of unwanted medication, such as the expense of unneeded treatment and drug resistance [14].

Regarding confirmatory diagnosis, the results showed that dermoscopy had the highest diagnostic sensitivity at 97.5% compared to 75% for skin scraping and 62.5% for the adhesive tape test. Dermoscopy also had higher specificity (91.7%) than skin scraping (83.3%) or the adhesive tape test (70.8%). The authors concluded that dermoscopy was the most accurate field-applicable method for diagnosing scabies in resource-limited settings [15]. However, in our setting, due to the lack of dermoscopy equipment, skin scraping examined under a microscope serves as the laboratory diagnostic method for confirming scabies infections. Therefore, the diagnosis of scabies relies heavily on an accurate clinical and laboratory diagnosis based on the criteria developed by the International Alliance for the Control of Scabies (IACS) in 2018 [11].

Scratching and parasite induced suppression of the local immune response can result in secondary bacterial infection [16], most commonly caused by Streptococcus pyogenes (Group A streptococci (GAS)) and Staphylococcus aureus. These bacteria were identified in skin burrows, implying that mites may directly contribute to the transmission of bacteria. This secondary bacterial infection has the potential to develop antibiotic resistance [17]. Scabies-induced secondary bacterial infections lead to severe, unnoticed morbidity [18]. Regular monitoring of the causative bacteria, strains, and antibiotic sensitivities in communities is important to ensure options for resistant strains.

Despite some reports conducted on clinical prevalence, Ethiopia’s scabies prevention and control programs as well as researchers have not emphasized confirmatory parasitological analysis of scabies and its association with secondary bacterial infections. Therefore, the aim of this study was to determine the prevalence of scabies, and its association with secondary bacterial infections and their drug susceptibility patterns in Borumeda General Hospital.

Materials and methods

Study area and period

This study was conducted in Borumeda General Hospital, Northeast Ethiopia, from September 2022 to July 2023. This hospital is one of the oldest hospitals in Ethiopia, established in 1954, and it is located in the center of the country, 410 km from Addis Ababa, the capital city. The hospital was initially established by missionaries mainly for treating leprosy and its complications. Later, the hospital expanded its health services to provide diagnosis and treatment of different diseases in the surrounding areas. Currently, the dermatology clinic has two offices, staffed with two dermatologists and two health officers with dermatology training.

Study design

An institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted among patients clinically suspected of having scabies in Borumeda General Hospital, Northeast Ethiopia.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Patients of all ages who visited the dermatology clinic at Borumeda Hospital during the data collection period, suspected of having scabies, and who consented to participate, were included in this study. On the other hand, patients who had taken anti-scabies drugs for the last 4 weeks and antibiotics for the last one week were excluded from the study. There were no criteria for choosing a particular lesion and body parts affected from which to take the skin scrapings and swabs for parasitological and bacteriological analysis, respectively.

Sample size determination

The sample size was calculated using a single population proportion formula to determine parasitologically confirmed prevalence of scabies from clinically scabies suspected patients. By considering the 95% confidence limits (Zα/2 = 1:96) and 5% margin of error (d) with a maximum proportion of 50%, the sample size is calculated as follows: sample size = Z(α/2)2 P (1- P)/d2, (1.96)2*0.221 (1–0.221)/(0.05)2 =384. Therefore, the final sample size was 422 considering a 10% contingency to account for the non-response rate.

Sampling technique and procedures

Simple systematic random sampling technique was used so as to select a representative sample of study participants. The number of study participants was allocated based on the flow of scabies-suspected patients during the same season in the previous year at Borumeda General Hospital. Study participants were selected by every other eligible attendant (K = 2) and a lottery method was used to get the first participant. Finally, skin scrapings and swabs were collected from patients suspected of having scabies for respective parasitological and microbiological analysis.

Data collection and laboratory methods

Socio-demographic and other factors

Data were collected using a pre-tested, interviewer-administered questionnaire that was developed from different literatures [8, 9, 11, 12, 18] (Supplementary file 1). The questionnaire was used to gather socio-demographic information, as well as hygiene and sanitation practices. Additionally, clinical variables such as the duration of scabies, severity, affected region, and number of lesions were collected by dermatologists.

Laboratory methods

Parasitological investigation

Skin scrapings for parasitological investigation and swabs for microbiological investigation were collected by experienced laboratory technologists. There were no defined criteria for selecting specific lesion types or affected body parts during sampling for parasitological analysis. Furthermore, a full examination of the entire skin surface was not performed in order to confirm findings across the whole body. A definitive diagnosis was done based on the identification of mites, eggs, eggshell fragments, or mite fecal pellets in skin scrapings under microscopy. One or two drops of mineral oil were applied to the lesion, which is then scraped or shaved by a scalpel blade. The specimens were examined after clearing with 2 drops of 10% potassium hydroxide (KOH). A drop of the sample was placed on a clean, grease-free slide and covered by a cover slip. The smeared specimen was then examined using a compound microscope with low (10x) and high (40x) power objectives [11, 13, 15].

Microbiological investigation

Sample collection, handling and transport

Swabs were simultaneously collected from patients who provided skin scrapings for parasitological analysis of scabies. As with the parasitological analysis, there were no defined criteria for selecting specific lesion types or affected body parts during microbiological sampling. Swabs were collected after contaminants were cleansed off with sterile normal saline-moistened gauze. The collected samples were immediately transported to the Medical Microbiology Laboratory of Wollo University College of Medicine and Health Sciences, Department of Medical Laboratory Sciences, for processing. Tryptic soy broth was used as a transport medium following standard procedures. Using one of the swabs, a smear was prepared on a clean microscopic slide. The smear was allowed to air dry, heat fixed, and stained with Gram stain. Then the smear was examined for the presence of bacteria and cellular elements under a microscope [19].

Bacterial culture and identification

Swabs/skin scrapes were inoculated onto a blood agar plate (5% sheep blood), MacConkey agar, and chocolate agar by rolling the swab over the agar and streaking from the primary inoculum using a sterile inoculating wire loop. The plates were incubated at 35–37 °C. All plates were aerobically incubated and initially examined for growth after 24 h. Plates without growth were further incubated for up to 48 h [19]. Bacterial isolates were characterized using colony morphology, Gram stain, and biochemical tests using catalase, coagulase, and bacitracin for identification of Gram-positive bacteria. While, glucose and lactose fermentation, sulfide-indole-motility, urease, SIM (sulfide, indole, motility), and lysine iron agar tests were used for identification of Gram-negative bacteria and performed according to standard microbiological methods [19].

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was carried out for each isolate according to the criteria of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guideline using the Kirby-Bauer disc diffusion method [19]. The inoculum was prepared from a pure culture by picking parts of the test organism with a sterile wire loop and suspending it in sterile normal saline. The density of the suspension to be inoculated was determined by comparing it with 0.5 McFarland standards. The test organism was uniformly seeded on Mueller-Hinton agar and exposed to antibiotic diffusing from antibiotic-impregnated paper disks into the agar medium, and then incubated aerobically at 37 °C for 16–24 h. After 24 h, the zones of inhibition were measured to the nearest millimeter using calipers, and results were interpreted in accordance with the CLSI interpretation chart for antimicrobial susceptibility testing [19]. The percentage frequencies of sensitivity and resistance were recorded.

The antimicrobial agents tested were Penicillin (10 µg), Ceftriaxone (30 µg), Clindamycin (10 µg), Erythromycin (15 µg), Ciprofloxacin (5 µg), Tetracycline (30 µg), Ampicillin (10 µg), Chloramphenicol (30 µg), Cefoxitin (30 µg), Gentamicin (10 µg), Amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (30 µg), Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (30 µg), Ceftazidime (30 µg), Amikacin (30 µg), Imipenem (10 µg), Cefuroxime (30 µg), Aztreonam (30 µg), Aefazolin (10 µg), and Meropenem (10 µg). These antimicrobial drug disks were selected based on the CLSI guideline and their availability and frequent prescriptions for the treatment of dermatological or wound infections in the study area [19].

Data quality assurance

The questionnaire was pre-tested before the data collection period. In scabies laboratory diagnosis, the expiration dates and impurities of KOH and normal saline reagents were checked. All slides of skin scraping smears were examined independently by two senior laboratory technologists who were working at the hospital. Discordant results were further confirmed by a third independent laboratory technologist. Bacteriological samples were collected using sterile swabs aseptically in order to avoid contamination. The sterility of culture media was checked by incubating 5% of each batch of the prepared media, plates, and tubes at 35–37 °C for 24 h. All prepared media were checked by inoculating standard reference strains, namely; Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC 25923), Escherichia coli (ATCC-25922), and P. aeruginosa (ATCC-27853) obtained from Amhara Public Health Institute Dessie branch Microbiology Laboratory as a quality control for culture, Gram stain, and antimicrobial susceptibility testing.

Data analysis

The data were entered into Epi-Data version 4.2 and exported to Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 26.0 for analysis. Descriptive statistics were computed and presented using figures and tables. Risk factors for the acquisition of scabies infestation were analyzed using bivariable and multivariable logistic regression to assess the association of different independent variables with the dependent variable. Variables with a p-value of less than 0.25 in the bivariate analysis were included in the multivariate logistic regression analysis. Adjusted odds ratios were calculated with a 95% confidence interval, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was evaluated and approved by the ethical review committee of the College of Medicine and Health Sciences, Wollo University, on the date 28/6/2022 with a protocol number of CMHS/67/2022. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant and assent from each parent/guardian. Participant’s confidentiality was maintained. Results of the laboratory examinations that had a direct benefit to the health of the study participants were directly linked to the hospital so they could get treatment as required.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of the participants

A total of 422 clinically scabies-suspected participants were included in the study. More than half (51.7%) of the participants were male. The ages of the participants ranged from 2 to 92 years, with a mean age of 27.8 ± 19.5 (mean ± SD). The majority of the participants were rural dwellers (73.9%) and had a family size greater than or equal to five (74.6%) (Table 1).

Clinical characterization and prevalence of confirmed scabies in sampled skin areas

According to Table 2, 88.2%, 76.5% and 35.5% of patients with scabies, in the restricted skin area examined, had lesions on the fingers, palms and forearms/elbows, respectively. In terms of the type of skin lesion used for scraping, the most frequent were excoriations (45.0%), vesicles (39.6%), and papules (18.0%). In this study, scabies was microscopically confirmed in 156 participants, with a prevalence of 37.0% (156/422; 95% CI: 32.5–41.9). The highest numbers of microscopic confirmation of scabies were detected on fingers (89.74%) and in patients with excoriated lesions (48.1%) (Table 2).

Secondary bacterial infection from scabies patients

A total of 156 swab samples were analyzed from microscopically confirmed scabies patients. A secondary bacterial infection was isolated from 35.9% (56/156) of scabies patients. Among the 56 secondary bacterially infected, microscopically confirmed scabies patients, 49 (87.5%) participants had a single bacterial infection, and 7 (12.5%) participants had mixed bacterial infections. From the isolated bacteria, Gram-positive bacteria were more prevalent than Gram-negative bacteria. The most commonly isolated bacterium was Staphylococcus aureus (40.3%), followed by coagulase-negative staphylococci (CoNS) (19.4%) and Streptococcus pyogenes (17.9%) (Table 4).

Antibiotic susceptibility patterns

Antibiotic susceptibility pattern of Gram-positive bacteria

The resistivity profile of isolated Gram positive bacteria showed that, the highest resistance patterns among the tested drugs were detected against Tetracycline (30.0%), Erythromycin (23.3%), and Clindamycin (16.6%). On the other hand, ampicillin, chloramphenicol, penicillin, and ceftriaxone showed high sensitivity patterns against the isolated bacteria. The resistance profile of the isolates had shown that Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pyogenes, Staphylococcus epidermidis, and CoNS were highly resistant to tetracycline, with prevalence rates of 25.9%, 33.3%, 37.5%, and 30.8%, respectively (Table 5).

Multidrug resistance patterns of bacterial isolates

Multidrug-resistant strains were common for both Gram-positive and Gram-negative isolates. Out of 67 isolates, 48 (71.6%) were found to be resistant to at least one antibiotic tested, and 19 (28.4%) isolates were found not to be resistant to the antibiotics used for the susceptibility tests. In this study, multidrug resistance (MDR) was observed in 16 (23.9%) of the bacterial isolates, with 13 (21.7%) of Gram-positive and 3 (42.9%) of Gram-negative isolates (Table 7).

Discussion

Scabies continues to pose a major global health challenge as a parasitic infection [20, 21]. The standard technique for the diagnosis of scabies consists of the identification of the mite, eggs, or feces by microscopic examination of scales obtained by skin scraping [22]. The prevalence of microscopically confirmed scabies from clinically suspected patients in the present study was 37.0%. This parasitologically confirmed scabies prevalence in this study is higher than studies conducted in Iraq (6.54%) [23], Iran (21.7%) [24] and Poland (6.7%) [25]. However, the prevalence of microscopically confirmed scabies in this study is lower than studies done in Turkey (42.9%) [26] and Korea (55.4%) [27]. Variations in reported prevalence of microscopically-confirmed scabies might be due to differences in sampling techniques, microscopist skill, populations assessed, environmental conditions, seasonal factors, and sample sizes between studies.

This study revealed that microscopically confirmed scabies infestation was eight times more likely to develop in clinically suspected participants who shared clothes with scabies cases compared to those who did not share clothes with scabies cases. This increased risk is attributed to the ability of scabies mites to survive outside their host for 1–2 days on fabric, 3–4 days on skin swabs, and up to 10 days on rough surfaces at cool room temperatures, facilitating potential transmission [28]. Likewise, the likelihood of developing a confirmed scabies infestation in clinically suspected patients with a contact history in the past two months was seven times greater than in those without such a history. The primary mode of scabies transmission is prolonged skin-to-skin contact with an infected person. This is frequently observed in close living situations, such as households, nursing homes, and crowded environments [28, 29].

In the current study, the prevalence of secondary bacterial infection was 35.9% (56/156). Similar findings were found in the studies done in Indonesia [30], Colombia [31], and Iraq [23]. In addition to the discomfort produced by the extremely pruritic lesions, untreated scabies infestations frequently develop secondary infections, particularly with group A streptococci and Staphylococcus aureus [32]. The scabies mite is a known facilitator of bacterial infection, as the mite impairs the host’s ability to defend itself locally against infection by secreting serine protease inhibitors. This impedes the proper functioning of the complement pathway, thus preventing the host’s local defense against infection [33, 34]. These possible bacterial pathogens can potentially hasten the progression of scabies and make the healing process more difficult [18].

In the current study, the predominant isolated bacteria from microscopically confirmed scabies patients were S. aureus, CoNS, and S. pyogenes. A similar finding was reported in Italy [35], Indonesia [30], Australia [36], and America [37]. Serpins from scabies mites suppress innate immune pathways, including neutrophil function, and directly stimulate the growth of S. aureus and S. pyogenes [38]. Invasins, hyaluronidase, catalase, coagulase, hemolysins, leukotoxin, and leukocidin are among the virulence factors released by S. aureus [34]. These enzymes have invasive and degenerative properties in tissues, which can accelerate the progression of skin disease. Staphylococcus aureus and S. pyogenes scabies infections can cause severe soft tissue infections and invasive illness [39]. Scabies control could be an important component of preventing staphylococcal and streptococcal skin infections [39].

The overall antimicrobial sensitivity pattern of Gram-positive bacteria revealed a low level of resistance (60%) in this study. The strongest resistance was to Tetracycline, Erythromycin, and Clindamycin. An Indonesian study found isolates resistant to tested antibiotics; however, unlike this study, clindamycin remained effective treatment for pediatric and adult patients [30]. Furthermore, Gram-negative bacteria have a high level of resistance to ampicillin (> 80%) and an intermediate level of resistance to cefazolin. This is consistent with prior research in Gondar, Ethiopia [40], Jimma, Ethiopia [41], Nigeria [42], and Indonesia [30]. On the other hand, chloramphenicol, amikacin, and aztreonam were more sensitive than the other medications examined. The effectiveness of these treatments against the tested organisms in this study might be due to the infrequency with which these drugs are prescribed by physicians, as well as the drugs being more expensive in comparison to others.

Multidrug resistance (MDR) was found in 16 (23.9%) of the bacterial isolates in this study. Drug resistance was reported in both Gram-positive and Gram-negative isolates. This result is consistent with studies conducted in Ethiopia [40, 41]. The explanation for multidrug resistance in Ethiopia could be the unregulated over-the-counter sale of antimicrobials, mostly for self-treatment of suspected infection in humans without a prescription. This would undoubtedly lead to the formation and rapid spread of resistant strains. Furthermore, the market availability of lower-cost generic medications of varying quality for the treatment of bacterial infections may contribute to the growing level of resistance.

Limitation of the study

The limitations of this study included the absence of clearly defined criteria for selecting lesion types and affected body areas for parasitological and microbiological analysis. Additionally, a comprehensive examination of the entire skin surface was not performed to validate findings throughout the body. Moreover, swab samples were primarily taken from scoriated lesions, which introduce significant bias when evaluating bacterial infections.

Conclusion and recommendations

The current study indicated that 37.0% of clinically suspected individuals had microscopically confirmed scabies. The factors that were significantly associated with microscopically confirmed scabies were bed sharing with others and having a contact history with an infested person. Additionally, the overall prevalence of bacterial infection among patients with microscopically confirmed scabies in this study was 35.9%. The most common isolates were S. aureus, CoNS, and S. pyogenes. The resistance profile of the isolates revealed that the highest resistance rates were against tetracycline among Gram-positive bacteria and against ampicillin among Gram-negative bacteria, respectively.

Therefore, the South Wollo Zone Health Office and Amhara Health Bureau should strengthen prevention efforts through awareness campaigns focused on behaviors and early testing/treatment to curb the spread of scabies and secondary bacterial infections. Moreover, most of the isolated bacteria from scabies patients showed high rates of resistance to the most commonly used drugs. As a result, the treatment of bacteremia should be based on culture and sensitivity rather than on universal guidelines.
