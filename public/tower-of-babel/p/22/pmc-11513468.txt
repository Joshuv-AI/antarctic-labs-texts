Revamping anti-cGAS-STING therapy via an injectable thermo-responsive supramolecular hydrogel for pathological retinal angiogenesis
Authors: Dan Yan, Yuqian Wang, Weijie Ouyang, Caihong Huang, Qian Chen, Jiaoyue Hu, Zuguo Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Retinal neovascularization is a leading cause of blindness. While current anti-VEGF drugs effectively inhibit pathological angiogenesis, some patients develop resistance or reduced responsiveness to treatments over time, leading to diminished effectiveness. In this study, we identified high activation of the cGAS-STING signaling pathway, which exacerbated pathological neovascularization and vessel leakage. We developed an injectable thermo-responsive supramolecular hydrogel loaded with an anti-STING drug. The hydrogel, made of Pluronic F127 (PF·127) consisting of poly(ethylene oxide) and poly(propylene oxide) units, demonstrated excellent transparency and biocompatibility. Importantly, the thermo-sensitive property allowed for precise spatial release of the drug, extending the effective treatment duration of C-176, which suppressed STING activation in the retina, reduced inflammation, and protected retinal tissue. HydroC-176 effectively inhibited microglial cell infiltration and the release of inflammatory angiogenic factors, highlighting its enhanced efficacy. While demonstrating slightly lower effectiveness compared to traditional anti-VEGF therapy, HydroC-176 exhibited more robust capabilities in regulating ocular microenvironmental inflammation. This approach may assist in enhancing the sensitivity and effectiveness of anti-VEGF therapy for reducing ocular inflammation, potentially improving patients’ response to traditional treatment. These results have suggested innovative and comprehensive strategies for the management of retinal neovascularization.

Introduction

Retinal vein occlusion, diabetic retinopathy, and retinopathy of prematurity (ROP) are ischemic retinopathies (IRs) and major causes of blindness worldwide [1,2]. These diseases are characterized by abnormal blood vessels with defective architecture and function, contributing to their pathological progression [3,4]. The compromised integrity of these vessels leads to increased vascular leakage, hemorrhage, and impaired blood flow, exacerbating retinal angiogenesis and ultimately leading to vision loss [5]. Current treatments for IRs include laser photocoagulation, vitrectomy, and intravitreal injections of anti-VEGF drugs [[6], [7], [8]]. However, the effectiveness of laser photocoagulation and vitrectomy is limited, and these procedures can cause damage to the retinal tissue and vasculature [9,10]. Despite numerous studies documenting the development and application of novel anti-VEGF medications, significant challenges persist. This complexity arises from various factors, including the heterogeneity of diseases treated with anti-VEGF therapies, individual variations in patient response, and long-term efficacy and safety concerns. Moreover, while anti-VEGF treatments have revolutionized the management of conditions like age-related macular degeneration (AMD) and diabetic retinopathy, they require frequent intraocular injections, leading to patient discomfort and increased risk of complications such as infection. Therefore, many studies explore using biomaterials for sustained drug release to extend the duration of drug action. Anti-angiogenic carbon nanovesicles (CNVs) were synthesized for loading and sustained release of bevacizumab (BVZ) and were shown to alleviate VEGF-induced angiogenesis in rabbit models [11]. Lai et al. explored nanotherapeutics designed via aminolysis of resveratrol-encapsulated polycaprolactone nanoparticles (R@PCL NPs) for sustained bioactive delivery to treat retinal diseases [12]. Additionally, the high cost and limited accessibility of these treatments remain substantial barriers for many patients worldwide [13,14]. Some individuals may experience suboptimal or no improvement in visual acuity or disease progression despite treatment [[15], [16], [17]]. Currently, aflibercept (Eylea®), ranibizumab (Lucentis®), and bevacizumab (Avastin®) are the three main anti-VEGF antibodies used to treat AMD. They are administered via intravitreal injection but unfortunately cannot completely cure these ocular diseases [18]. Therefore, while the advancements in anti-VEGF therapies represent significant progress, ongoing research is crucial to address these persistent challenges, aiming to improve patient outcomes, reduce treatment burdens, and make these therapies more accessible to a broader population.

Numerous studies have provided substantial evidence supporting the crucial role of inflammation in the development of IRs and its function as a positive feedback mediator in loop of inflammatory cascades [19,20]. Specifically, previous studies have indicated that proinflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin 1β (IL-1β) and tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α), typically promote the development and progression of retinal angiogenesis [21,22]. In studies on AMD treatment, researchers have utilized the anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties of phenol-rich poly(catechin) combined with the anti-angiogenic activity of metformin to promote disease healing, further demonstrating the role of inflammation in these processes [23]. Similarly, in the study by Chang et al., they synthesized carbonized nanomaterials using sodium alginate (SA) and 1,8-diaminooctane (DAO) to exert antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, significantly enhancing anti-VEGF treatment [24]. Therefore, effective suppression of inflammatory responses may be a crucial strategy for treating IRs [25]. Moreover, research has shown that combined anti-VEGF and anti-inflammatory therapies can significantly decrease the decreased bioefficacy observed after repeated intravitreal injections [26,27].

The roles of cGAS (cyclic GMP-AMP synthase) and STING (stimulator of interferon genes) as major regulators of inflammatory responses have been increasingly recognized in the field of inflammation [28]. cGAS recognizes and becomes activated by double-stranded DNA in the cytoplasm. Once activated, cGAS generates cyclic dinucleotide 2′,3′-cyclic GMP-AMP (cGAMP) from guanosine triphosphate (GTP) and adenosine triphosphate (ATP). As a second messenger, cGAMP binds to and activates membrane-anchored STING located at the endoplasmic reticulum. Activated STING sets off a cascade of downstream pathway activations, including those mediated by TRAF-associated NF-κB activator (TANK)-bind kinase 1/interferon regulatory factor 3, NF-κB, and Janus Kinase-signal transducer and activator of transcription [[29], [30], [31]]. Collectively, these signaling events lead to the up-regulation of cytokines and growth factors, such as IL-1β, TNF-α, and granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor, thereby eliciting innate immune responses [32,33]. Its excessive activation, particularly in myeloid cells, can contribute to tissue immunopathology and cellular senescence [34]. Several research studies have indicated that this pathway may contribute to vascular dysfunction and neovascularization, thereby presenting significant potential as a therapeutic strategy [[35], [36], [37]]. Studies have shown that the DNA-sensing cGAS-STING signaling pathway is a key driver of chronic inflammation and functional decline during aging. In the brain, STING activation has been found to trigger reactive microglial transcription states [29], while in retinal neovascularization, changes in microglial state are often associated with worsening pathological neovascularization [38,39]. Therefore, it is reasonable to speculate that cGAS-STING is associated with pathological retinal neovascularization.

We have comprehensively validated the involvement of cGAS-STING in the pathologic neovascularization of the retina, backed by transcriptome sequencing, single-cell sequencing, STING knockout (STING-/-) mice, and STING agonist application (Fig. 2, Fig. 3), revealing that the cGAS-STING signaling pathway plays a crucial role in pathological retinal neovascularization, intensifying unwanted vascular alterations. Hence, effective suppression of the anti-inflammatory responses, particularly targeting the cGAS-STING signaling pathway, may be a crucial approach for treating IRs. This approach could potentially complement existing anti-VEGF treatments, exerting a synergistic effect. To mitigate the drawbacks and obstacles associated with conventional anti-VEGF therapies, we have pioneered an innovative solution: an injectable supramolecular thermo-responsive hydrogel. This hydrogel swiftly transforms from a solution to a gel state upon injection, achieved by blending PF·127—a well-regarded biomaterial in the medical and tissue engineering fields—with deionized water at low temperatures [[40], [41], [42]]. By embedding C-176, a specific STING inhibitor, into the PF·127 mixture during the hydrogel's formation, we have ingeniously encapsulated the drug within the gel without using toxic solvents (e.g., DMSO) as C-176 is a poorly soluble drug. The hydrogelʼs architecture acted as a potent delivery system for C-176, ensuring sustained release and prolonged treatment efficacy. Upon ocular administration, the hydrogel transitioned to a gel due to the body's warmer temperature, facilitating a targeted and prolonged release of the medication [[43], [44], [45]]. Interestingly, inhibiting the STING pathway markedly mitigates these changes, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target for controlling pathological retinal changes and vascular leakage. The novel formulation, dubbed HydroC-176, has demonstrated exceptional better capabilities in preclinical models. Notably, it effectively mitigated inflammation and neutralized inflammatory cytokines, providing both anti-inflammatory and anti-angiogenic benefits. In light of these compelling results, we propose that our novel approach—leveraging a thermo-sensitive hydrogel coupled with an anti-STING agent—represents a promising and innovative treatment avenue for retinal neovascular diseases, offering a viable alternative to traditional therapies.

Materials and methods

Materials

Polyethylene-polypropylene glycol (Mn=14,600) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. TNF-α, IL-1β, Iba1 and STING antibodies were procured from Abcam (Cambridge, UK). Isolectin GS-IB4 (lectin) and 1′-dioctadecyl-3,3,3′,3′-tetramethylindodicarbocyanine (DiD) were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific (USA). The 40,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; H-1200) was purchased from Vector (Burlingame, USA). C-176 was purchased from Biovision (USA). Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated IgG and Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated IgG were obtained from Invitrogen Life Technologies Co (USA). PrimeScript RT Master Mix Kit was obtained from Takara (Japan). The SuperReal PreMix Plus (SYBR Green) Kit was procured from Tiangen Biotech (Beijing, China).

Preparation of the thermo-responsive hydrogels

The starting material for Pluronic F127 (PF·127) is composed of Polyethylene-polypropylene glycol. The PF·127 powders, with or without C-176, were dispersed in deionized water. This dispersion process occurred in an ice/water mixture bath with vigorous magnetic stirring to produce the PF·127 solutions. The viscosity of the prepared hydrogels at different temperatures was in situ characterized using a rotary viscosimeter (NDJ-5S, Shanghai Lunjie Electromechanical Instrument Co., Ltd). The hydrogel was prepared with a concentration of 20 wt%, while C-176 was used at a concentration of 100 µM. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were obtained using a PANalytical X-ray diffractometer equipped with CuKα radiation (λ = 0.15406). To examine the hydrogelʼs morphology, a Zeiss Gemini 500 scanning electron microscope was employed to obtain scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images.

Live and dead

We plated cells in a density of 2 × 104 cells/well in 24-well plates. After overnight incubation, we replaced the culture medium with either fresh medium or medium containing PF·127 and incubated the cells for an additional 2 h. Subsequently, we stained the cells using a live and dead kit for 20 min and captured images using a Carl Zeiss LSM980 confocal microscope (Germany).

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

Each subgroup consisted of six replicates. A dose of CCK-8 solution purchased from NCM Biotech was added to each well, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 4 h in the dark. To quantify the results, absorbance at 450 nm was measured and evaluated using statistical analysis.

In vitro release of C-176

At 37 °C and PBS, the gel-like HydroC-176 was facilitated. To maintain consistent sink conditions, 1 ml sink medium was taken out for the detection at each time point and replaced with 1 ml fresh PBS. The concentration of C-176 was found utilizing high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). Three technical duplicates were used to analyze all materials.

Mouse model of ischemia-induced retinopathy

In this study, C57BL/6 mice were utilized. STING-/- mice, which have a C57BL/6 genetic background, were acquired from the Model Animal Research Center of Nanjing University and Gempharmatech Company, identified by gene ID T012747. The successful elimination of the targeted genes was verified via PCR analysis of tail DNA. The conduct of all animal experiments adhered to the guidelines provided by the Association for Research in Vision and Ophthalmology regarding the use of animals in ophthalmic and vision research. The experimental protocols received approval from the Animal Ethical Committee of Xiamen University.

The oxygen-induced retinopathy (OIR) model is an established animal model used to study pathological neovascularization in retinal diseases, such as ROP and proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR). This model replicates the pathological process observed in human retinopathies [46]. The construction of the OIR model involves several steps. Initially, newborn mice (postnatal day 7, P7) are exposed to hyperoxia (high levels of oxygen) for a specific period, typically 5 d, depending on the desired severity of the model and the species. This exposure to hyperoxia induces vaso-obliteration, causing the regression of the existing retinal vasculature. After the hyperoxic phase, the animals are transferred to normal room air, resulting in hypoxia-induced retinal ischemia. The hypoxic conditions in the retina trigger pathological responses, including the release of VEGF, which promotes neovascularization [46]. During this hypoxic phase, new blood vessels start growing from the retinal vessels towards the avascular areas of the retina, forming neovascular tufts. These abnormal blood vessels are often leaky and fragile, leading to intraocular bleeding and fibrous tissue formation. This pathological neovascularization closely resembles the features observed in human retinopathies [47].

For the treatment interventions, C-176 was administered as an intravitreal injection at a concentration of 100 µM/µl. Similarly, the dosage for HydroC-176 was maintained at 1 µl for intravitreal injection. As a control for traditional anti-VEGF therapies, Conbercept was utilized, which is a novel recombinant fusion protein acting as a decoy receptor. It consists of the second Ig domain of the VEGF receptor 1 and the third and fourth Ig domains of the VEGF receptor 2, fused to the constant region (Fc) of human IgG1 [48]. The dosage was set at 0.25 mg in a 0.025 ml solution, corresponding to a 1 µl intravitreal injection.

Color fundus photography (CFP), fluorescence fundus angiography (FFA), and optical coherence tomography (OCT)

CFP, FFA and OCT imaging were conducted using an OPTOPROBE system. These imaging techniques are integral for assessing retinal structures and functions in vivo. On postnatal day 17 (P17), the mouse pups underwent anesthesia to ensure immobilization and reduce stress during the imaging process. Following anesthesia, tropicamide phenylephrine eye drops were applied to each pupʼs eyes. These eye drops serve as a mydriatic and sympathomimetic combination that effectively dilates the pupils, facilitating a clear and unobstructed view of the retina. After ensuring adequate pupil dilation, fundus images were captured for each eye. The imaging process was meticulously conducted, with particular attention focused on centering the images around the optic nerve head.

Measurement of neovascularization and vascular obliteration

At P17, the eyes of the mice were collected and immediately fixed at 4 °C for 1 h. The retinas were dissected and stained with lectin for 24 h to detect the vasculature. Subsequently, the retinas were washed with PBS and radially cut from the edge of the retina in all 4 quadrants. Once flat-mounted, a laser confocal microscope was employed to visualize the vessels (Zeiss LSM 980 with Airyscan). Vaso-obliteration and neovascularization were quantified as a percentage of the whole retina area by imageJ software (version 1.8.0., National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD). To be specific, neovascular regions were quantified by measuring the area of dense, tortuous blood vessels. the neovascular areas were manually outlined based on the presence of abnormal vessel structures. The total neovascular area was calculated and expressed as a percentage of the total retinal area. Avascular areas were identified as regions lacking isolectin B4 staining. Using ImageJ software, these areas were manually outlined and measured. The avascular area was calculated and expressed as a percentage of the total retinal area.

Immunofluorescence staining

After fixation for 10 min, the samples underwent three washes with PBS. The sections were then incubated with PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100 and washed three times with PBS for 10 min each. Next, the tissue sections were blocked with 5% BSA in PBS for 60 min and incubated with specific antibodies overnight. Following this, the slides were washed three times with PBS for 10 min each and incubated with secondary antibodies for 60 min. Counterstaining with DAPI was performed, and in the case of whole-mount retina samples, they were radially cut from the edge in all four quadrants. The samples were subsequently analyzed and captured using a laser confocal microscope. Mean intensity of staining in sections was measured using ImageJ software.

TUNEL staining

After the PBS wash, the tissues were blocked in a solution containing 5% BSA and 0.3% Triton X-100 for 1 h at room temperature. Subsequently, the tissues were treated with an in situ detection kit (MK500, TaKaRa Bio Inc, Shiga, Japan) according to the manufacturer's instructions for 1.5 h. Following the treatment, the slices were examined using a confocal microscope.

Genome-wide mRNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and bioinformatics analysis

Initially, total RNA was subjected to mRNA enrichment to isolate the poly-A tailed mRNA. The obtained mRNA was then fragmented into small pieces at elevated temperatures. Next, second-strand cDNA synthesis was performed using RNase H and DNA polymerase. The quality and quantity of the resulting cDNA library were evaluated using the Vy Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer and an ABI StepOnePlus real-time PCR system. The cDNA libraries from all specimens were pooled and sequenced using an Illumina HiSeq X-Ten sequencer. To create a comprehensive reference sequence, newly identified transcripts with protein-coding potential were added to the existing reference gene sequence. Gene expression levels were calculated using the RPKM (Reads Per Kilobase per Million mapped reads) method. Differential expression analysis was conducted using an R package, and P-values were adjusted using the Benjamin–Hochberg method. A threshold of an adjusted P-value ≤ 0.001 and a fold change ≥2 was used to identify significant differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Gene Ontology (GO), Genomes (KEGG) annotations, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes, and GSEA analysis were performed using the online platform https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn for data analysis and visualization.

Single-cell sequencing analysis

The data analyzed in this study was obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database, specifically from the dataset designated as GSE199792, which can be accessed through the link (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE199792). In the preprocessing phase, the samples were normalized using the SCTransform method. For the identification and visualization of cellular clusters, the study employed the Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) technique, integrated within the Seurat R package. The specific commands used were FindNeighbors and FindClusters (with a resolution parameter set to 1.6), followed by RunUMAP with dimensions set from 1 to 60. Following cluster identification, DEGs within each cluster were pinpointed using the FindAllMarkers function available in Seurat. After establishing the clusters for each retina sample (from both the OIR-treated and control groups), a comparative analysis was conducted. The gene lists from each cluster were contrasted against each other utilizing the ClusterMap R package, a tool designed for comparing clusters and identifying differential gene expressions. In this process, an 'edge_cutoff' parameter of 0.1 was applied, which helps in filtering out less significant connections, focusing the analysis on more substantial gene expression differences.

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Under a dissecting microscope, retinas were isolated. Total RNA was extracted from the retinas using Trizol Reagent. Reverse transcription was performed using the PrimeScript RT Master Mix Kit to synthesize cDNA from the extracted RNA. Subsequently, qRT-PCR was conducted. For quantification of gene expression, the 2-ΔΔCt method was employed. β-actin was used as the internal control for normalization. The relative expression levels of the different genes were calculated based on this method. Statistical analysis

The data obtained from the experiment were processed using GraphPad Prism version 7.0 software (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Statistical analysis was performed to determine differences between groups using either Student's unpaired t-test for two groups or one-way ANOVA for multiple comparisons. A level of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

The overactivated cGAS-STING signaling pathway in pathological retinal neovascularization model

First, we established a consistent and stable OIR mouse model (Fig. 1A). Fundus photography (Fig. 1B) shows that the retinal vascular network in the normoxia group is well-organized, covering the entire retina with high vascular density. In contrast, the OIR group exhibits incomplete retinal vascularization, with large avascular areas in the central retina, tortuous and irregularly sized blood vessels, some vascular dilation, and a pale fundus with numerous neovascular buds in the mid-peripheral region. To further illustrate the retinal blood flow status after OIR induction, we used optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA) (Fig. 1C). This revealed uniform blood flow in the normoxia group, whereas the OIR group showed significantly reduced central blood flow and markedly decreased peripheral microcirculation. To further examine the retinal condition, we performed HE staining of longitudinal retinal sections and lectin staining of whole-mounted retinas (Fig. 1D). HE staining revealed significant thickening of the inner limiting membrane. Lectin staining of the flat-mounted retinas showed reduced vascular distribution in the central region, with tortuous vessels and abundant neovascular buds. For a detailed observation of neovascular bud distribution, we performed lectin staining on longitudinal retinal sections (Fig. 1E). This staining indicated that neovascular buds were primarily located in the inner limiting membrane, with some protruding into the vitreous body. Additionally, apart from the significant increase in avascular areas and neovascularization, we observed blood-retinal barrier breakdown and vascular leakage in the OIR model (Fig. 1F). Light microscopy images clearly displayed vascular leakage points in the retina. These leakage points appeared brownish-red under the microscope due to extravasated blood cells from structurally abnormal vessels. To further investigate vascular leakage, we conducted immunofluorescence staining on retinal flat mounts (Fig. 1G). Confocal microscopy revealed numerous blood cells congested within the abnormally structured neovascular buds, with some cells extravasating from the vessels. Activation of the cGAS-STING pathway triggers a series of events that ultimately result in the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines [49]. These immune mediators play a crucial role in recruiting and activating inflammatory cells, particularly macrophages, at the site of vascular injury or inflammation [50]. Macrophages are key regulators of both inflammation and angiogenesis [51]. Excessive or prolonged activation of the cGAS-STING pathway can disrupt the delicate balance of macrophage functions and contribute to pathological neovascularization [52]. Inflammatory signals derived from the activated cGAS-STING pathway in macrophages can impair macrophage polarization and phagocytic activity, leading to the accumulation of dysfunctional macrophages within the vasculature [53,54]. Moreover, the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines induced by the cGAS-STING pathway can stimulate abnormal angiogenesis [55]. These mediators promote proliferation and migration of endothelial cells, resulting in the formation of structurally and functionally abnormal pathological blood vessels. This dysregulated neovascularization further exacerbates tissue damage, worsening the progression of various vascular-related pathologies.

As the role of cGAS-STING signaling in IRs was not yet fully understood, we conducted RNA-seq analysis to investigate the involvement of STING in OIR models. In Fig. 2A, the volcano plot displays the DEGs in the retina of normal and OIR groups. A total of 831 genes were significantly upregulated, while 192 genes were downregulated. Subsequently, we performed GO analysis to explore the pathways with significant differences in biological processes (Fig. 2B), cellular components (Fig. 2C), and molecular functions (Fig. 2D) in the pathological retinal neovascularization. By conducting GSEA analysis to explore the activated signaling pathways further, we observed significant activation of the cytosolic DNA-sensing pathway (Fig. 2E). The heatmap in Fig. 2F depicts the changes in gene expression associated with this pathway. Subsequently, we analyzed the results of inflammation by performing qRT-PCR analysis on the retinas of mice under OIR modeling (Fig. 2G) and noted a significant increase in the expression of cGAS and STING. Western Blot results (Fig. 2H & I) were consistent with previous findings, indicating significant activation of STING in the retinas with pathological neovascularization. Following this, we administered intraperitoneal injections of the STING agonist DMXAA to mice for observation. Through fundus photography and fluorescein sodium injections (Fig. 2J), we observed that after DMXAA injection, retinal vessels became more tortuous, microvasculature disappeared, and the retina appeared pale, suggesting worsened inadequate retinal perfusion. To further investigate and observe changes in retinal structure, we conducted staining of retinal cross-sections (Fig. 2K). HE staining results showed pathological neovascularization penetrated the vitreous cavity, with a worsened condition in the DMXAA group. Lectin-stained cross-sections exhibited more neovascularization. For a comprehensive assessment of retinal vascular distribution, we performed retinal whole-mount staining (Fig. 2L). The results indicated a significant increase in the area of pathological neovascularization following DMXAA injection. We also assessed retinal vascular leakage (Fig. 2M), revealing increased leakage from deep retinal vessels due to DMXAA, with a substantial number of blood cells (Ter119+) leaking outside the vessels. Thus, our experiments provided evidence of the association between STING activation and the deterioration of retinal neovascularization.

In our analysis, we examined single-cell data samples from the retinal OIR model in the study identified as GSE199792. Following meticulous cell clustering and screening processes, we identified significant differences in STING expression within microglial cells (Fig. 2N). Specifically, there was a notable increase in STING expression within the microglial cells of OIR-afflicted mice. Subsequently, we localized the expression of STING and its downstream inflammatory factors, TNF-α and IL-1β, within the cell populations (Fig. 2O). This was followed by a correlation analysis (Fig. 2P), revealing a significant linear relationship between the expressions of STING, TNF-α and IL-1β. These inflammatory factors, TNF-α and IL-1β, have been documented in the literature as being associated with the exacerbation of retinal neovascularization [56,57]. From these findings, we can preliminarily conclude that STING expression exhibited distinctive upregulation in the microglial cells of pathological neovascular retinal diseases. Furthermore, the activation of STING was likely correlated with increased inflammatory factors that contribute to vascular pathologies. This suggests that STING could play a pivotal role in the inflammatory pathways influencing the progression of neovascular retinal conditions.

Microglia, the resident immune cells of the retina, play a pivotal role in maintaining retinal homeostasis and have been extensively documented to promote pathological neovascularization in retinal diseases [56,58]. Upon activation by stress or injury, microglia can detect cytosolic DNA through the cGAS-STING pathway, which was reported in various investigations [[59], [60], [61]]. This activation leads to the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α and IL-1β, which are well-documented as key inflammatory factors involved in retinal neovascularization [[62], [63], [64], [65]]. The resulting inflammation amplifies the inflammatory response in the retina. Consequently, this inflammation can contribute to the progression of retinal diseases by promoting pathological changes, including neovascularization and neurodegeneration.

Through GO analysis (Fig. 3H), we observed that changes were concentrated in ontologies related to the regulation of the extracellular matrix organization and collagen-containing extracellular matrix, which may be involved in the regulation of the retinal vascular microenvironment. This suggests that STING inhibition leads to a decrease in vascular formation, explaining the reduction in pathological neovascularization observed in STING-/- mice.

The STING pathway is a central component of the innate immune response, primarily involved in the detection of cytosolic DNA and the induction of type I interferons and other pro-inflammatory cytokines. This pathway intersects with various other inflammatory and angiogenic pathways, influencing the overall therapeutic outcome. For instance, the STING pathway can activate the NF-κB pathway, a key regulator of inflammation and immune response [66,67]. Inhibition of STING could reduce NF-κB activation, potentially decreasing the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and angiogenic factors like VEGF, suggesting broader anti-inflammatory and anti-angiogenic effects. Additionally, by modulating the production of type I interferons, STING inhibition could influence downstream signaling cascades that regulate immune cell activation and recruitment [49,68,69]. Reduced interferon signaling might lead to decreased inflammation and a dampened immune response, impacting the efficacy of treatment in controlling neovascularization. The STING pathwayʼs induction of type I interferons can also activate the JAK-STAT signaling pathway, which is involved in cell survival, proliferation, and angiogenesis [[70], [71], [72]]. Inhibiting STING could therefore alter JAK-STAT pathway activity, potentially affecting the balance between pro- and anti-angiogenic signals in the retinal environment. Hypoxic conditions in ischemic retinopathy can activate hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs), promoting the expression of angiogenic factors [73,74]. The interaction between STING-mediated inflammation and hypoxia-induced pathways could influence the extent of neovascularization, making it crucial to understand this crosstalk for optimizing therapeutic strategies that target multiple pathways [75,76]. Furthermore, the STING pathway affects the function of various immune cells, including macrophages and dendritic cells [[77], [78], [79]]. Inhibition of STING might alter the recruitment and activation of these cells, subsequently influencing the local inflammatory milieu and angiogenic processes.

This characteristic is especially advantageous for ensuring consistent delivery and retention of therapeutic agents at the target site in the retina. It is also an effective carrier for poorly soluble drugs [96]. For instance, C-176 requires specific solvents such as ethanol and DMSO for dissolution. PF·127 undergoes a sol-gel transition with dynamic viscosity changes depicted in Fig. 4B. Notably, viscosity sharply rised in the temperature range of 17–25 °C, indicating excellent thermal responsiveness. Fig. 4C illustrates PF·127 solutions at various concentrations under 5 °C and 34 °C, showing pronounced gelation at higher concentrations, making it suitable for injectable drug delivery systems. Subsequently, we assessed the transparency of hydrogels of different concentrations at varying temperatures for ocular applications (Fig. 4D). Regardless of concentration, the sol-gel transition did not affect transparency, making PF·127 optically transparent. This transparency is crucial for vitreous injection, preserving visual acuity by allowing light transmission without distortion or scattering. The detection of elastic modulus (Fig. 4E) showed that after the temperature rised to 20 °C, G' and G'' rapidly increased, then entered a plateau phase around 25 °C. The plateau phase indicates that the material has reached a stable state and its mechanical properties remain constant within this temperature range. Fig. 4F showed the elemental composition analysis of PF-127. Fig. 4G showed the elemental distribution image, as well as the surface after gelation, revealing a smooth surface that could help minimize tissue damage post-injection. Given PF·127ʼs superior temperature sensitivity and high transparency, it is well-suited for intravitreal injections, carrying non-water-soluble drugs such as C-176 (Fig. 4H). C-176 is a small molecule inhibitor specifically designed to target and inhibit the STING (stimulator of interferon genes) pathway. C-176 inhibits STING activity by directly binding to the STING protein. Specifically, C-176 interferes with the conformational changes of STING, preventing its translocation to the Golgi apparatus, thereby inhibiting the activation of downstream signaling pathways [98]. This specificity has been demonstrated in several studies [[99], [100], [101]], showing that C-176 selectively inhibits STING-mediated responses without significantly affecting other pathways. Despite its specificity, C-176, like any small molecule inhibitor, may have potential off-target effects. These can include unintended inhibition of related proteins, as C-176 may exhibit some degree of cross-reactivity with proteins structurally similar to STING, potentially affecting their function. Additionally, by inhibiting the STING pathway, C-176 may inadvertently modulate other aspects of the immune response, leading to unintended immunosuppressive effects or altered immune regulation. Inhibiting the STING pathway could also affect cellular stress responses, particularly in cells highly dependent on this pathway for maintaining homeostasis under stress conditions. Furthermore, there is always a risk of non-specific toxicity that could affect cell viability and function, especially at higher concentrations. In our study, we have carefully optimized the concentration and administration of C-176 to minimize these potential off-target effects while effectively targeting the STING pathway.

Hydrogels are well-recognized for their ability to sustain drug release, effectively preventing premature release through their porous structure [[102], [103], [104]]. In our study, we encapsulated the STING inhibitor C-176 in PF·127, demonstrating a sustained drug release profile as shown in Fig. 4I. This result underscores the therapeutic efficacy of our approach. Despite encountering issues with burst release when incorporating poorly water-soluble drugs into hydrogel systems, our data illustrate that the PF·127 hydrogel can maintain a prolonged release of C-176, thereby enhancing its clinical utility. Addressing the burst release challenge remains a priority for us. We are committed to ongoing research efforts aimed at optimizing the hydrogel system to further mitigate burst release and improve drug delivery performance. Enhancing the stability and efficacy of this system is crucial, and we will dedicate our resources and efforts to overcoming these challenges. By doing so, we aim to advance the potential clinical application of this novel therapeutic approach, ultimately providing a more effective treatment option for retinal pathological neovascularization.

A notable advantage of hydrogel-based drug delivery is their outstanding biocompatibility, mimicking the natural tissue environment, reducing the potential for adverse reactions [102,105]. Safety evaluation on retinal cells (Fig. 4J) showed no visible dead cells, indicating PF·127ʼs safety profile. CCK-8 assay results (Fig. 4K) revealed no significant difference in proliferation compared to cells in standard culture dishes, further attesting to the hydrogel's biocompatibility. In summary, PF·127 exhibits promise as a platform for innovative retinal drug delivery, offering effective and targeted therapies for retinal diseases and improving patient outcomes.

Comparative therapeutic effects of Hydroc-176 and traditional anti-VEGF therapy

Traditional anti-VEGF therapy has shown limitations in effectively treating pathological neovascularization in the retina. Although anti-VEGF agents have demonstrated efficacy in inhibiting VEGF and suppressing angiogenesis, there are inherent deficiencies in this approach [16]. Firstly, anti-VEGF therapy primarily targets the VEGF signaling pathways, which are responsible for promoting abnormal blood vessel formation. However, it has been observed that other pro-angiogenic and inflammatory factors can compensate for the lack of VEGF, leading to persistent neovascularization despite treatment. Secondly, repeated injections of anti-VEGF agents are required due to their short duration of action. This frequent dosing regimen poses challenges both economically and logistically for patients and healthcare providers, as it necessitates regular hospital visits and increases the burden on the healthcare system. Moreover, resistance to anti-VEGF treatment has been documented in certain cases, rendering it ineffective in controlling pathological neovascularization. Mechanisms such as increased expression of alternative angiogenic factors, altered VEGF receptor signaling, or recruitment of pro-inflammatory cells can contribute to this resistance phenomenon [112,113].

These limitations underscore the need for alternative or adjunctive therapeutic strategies to address the deficiencies of traditional anti-VEGF therapy. In our study, we compared the effectiveness of HydroC-176 with anti-VEGF treatment. Fundus photography (Fig. 8A) showed that both treatment modalities significantly improved retinal morphology, restored microvasculature, and reduced the abnormal tortuosity observed in the OIR model. Retinal flat-mount images (Fig. 8B) revealed no significant difference in the areas of retinal avascular zones between the two treatments (Fig. 8C). However, in the measurement of the area of neovascularization, anti-VEGF treatment demonstrated superior performance. Subsequently, we conducted microglial cell detection (Fig. 8D) and found that compared to HydroC-176, anti-VEGF treatment did not reduce microglial cell infiltration (Fig. 8E). The levels of the inflammatory cytokine TNF-α significantly decreased with HydroC-176 compared to anti-VEGF treatment, while there was no difference in the levels of IL-1β between the two (Fig. 8F & 8G). These findings suggest that anti-VEGF therapy exhibited better therapeutic effects in reducing pathological angiogenesis. However, due to their different mechanisms of action, HydroC-176 primarily acted by improving the inflammatory level, while anti-VEGF treatment did not have a particularly significant effect on inflammation control. It has been reported in the literature that the levels of inflammatory cytokines significantly increase in pathological neovascular diseases [47,114]. Our experiment also demonstrated the feasibility of using an anti-inflammatory approach. Although anti-VEGF drugs effectively target VEGF-mediated angiogenesis, other growth factors and pathways can contribute to abnormal blood vessel formation [115]. Inhibition of VEGF alone may not completely suppress neovascularization, necessitating the exploration of additional therapeutic targets. We will apply more models subsequently to validate its therapeutic efficacy. According to studies, in refractory retinal pathological neovascularization, the levels of inflammatory cytokines are even higher, suggesting that HydroC-176 as an innovative treatment modality may be able to assist in the treatment of these patients by employing different strategies to achieve therapeutic effects and provide effective management options for pathological neovascularization in retinal diseases. Additionally, anti-VEGF therapy is expensive, particularly when considering the cumulative costs of repeated injections over an extended treatment period. Inaccessible or unaffordable treatment options may limit widespread adoption and availability while our strategy of adopting HydroC-176 is relatively cheap and easy to be manufactured. Lastly, over time, some patients may develop resistance or reduced responsiveness to anti-VEGF agents, leading to diminished treatment effectiveness. Tachyphylaxis, or rapid tolerance development, has also been reported, where subsequent injections yield diminishing therapeutic benefits [116,117]. Indeed, the use of HydroC-176 could potentially complement the treatment of patients with retinal neovascularization by targeting different pathways and mechanisms compared to traditional anti-VEGF therapy. This dual approach aims to enhance therapeutic efficacy. HydroC-176, with its controlled release system and unique properties, offers several advantages. It can provide sustained and localized delivery of C-176, promoting a consistent therapeutic concentration at the target site. This sustained release may reduce the need for frequent injections and improve patient compliance. Furthermore, HydroC-176′s supramolecular structure and biocompatibility can act as a barrier against inflammatory signals and angiogenic factors. This dual functionality helps inhibit the progression of retinal neovascularization. By combining HydroC-176 with anti-VEGF therapy, the two treatments can work synergistically to address different aspects of pathological angiogenesis. While anti-VEGF therapy primarily targets VEGF-mediated angiogenesis, HydroC-176 can assist in inhibiting inflammatory factors and pathways involved in abnormal blood vessel formation. Conclusion

In our study, we identified a significant activation of the cGAS-STING signaling pathway in models of pathological neovascularization, highlighting its critical role in exacerbating disease processes such as the formation of abnormal blood vessels and leakage. To counteract this, we developed an innovative therapeutic approach using a thermo-sensitive injectable hydrogel, composed of PF·127 and an anti-STING drug, aimed at modulating this pathway. Our hydrogel HydroC-176, is designed to be transparent, avoiding any interference with vision, and thermo-sensitive, transitioning from a liquid to a gel upon injection into the eye. This property ensures that the hydrogel remains stable within the vitreous cavity, providing a controlled and extended release of the C-176 compound. This method proved more effective in reducing microglial cell infiltration and inhibiting the STING pathway compared to traditional C-176 delivery, which can damage cells due to harsh solvents like DMSO or ethanol. Thus, HydroC-176 represents a promising supplementary treatment to conventional anti-VEGF therapies, addressing components of retinal neovascularization not typically targeted by current treatments.
