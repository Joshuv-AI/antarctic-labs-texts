The Association Between Plasma Atherogenic Index Control Level and the Onset of Hypertension in Chinese Children and Adolescents
Authors: Rong Lian, Yi‐de Yang, Xi‐jie Wang, Man‐man Chen, Jun Ma, Zheng‐he Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe association of atherogenic index of plasma (AIP) control level with hypertension onset in children was still unclear. This study aimed to investigate the association between AIP control levels and risk of childhood hypertension. We obtained data from 12,800 students participating in a national, multi‐center, school‐based health lifestyle intervention program aimed at combating obesity. AIP was calculated using the formula log10(TG/HDL‐C), and was categorized into five clusters based on baseline and 1 year follow‐up values. Logistic regression and restricted cubic spline regression models were employed to explore both linear and nonlinear associations between AIP control and hypertension risk. Of the 12,800 students, 830 (6.5%) developed hypertension within 1 year. Compared to the group with optimal AIP control (Class 1), students in Class 4 with poor AIP control (OR = 1.75; 95% CI: 1.31–2.33; p < 0.001), and those in Class 5 with the worst AIP control (OR = 2.43; 95% CI: 1.69–3.50; p < 0.001), had higher incidences of hypertension. Restricted cubic spline regression analysis revealed a nonlinear relationship between AIP control and hypertension risk overall, and in Class 4 (p for nonlinear <0.05). However, the association was linear in the other classes (p for nonlinear >0.05). In conclusion, the level of AIP control was associated with the incidence of hypertension in children and adolescents.Keywords: atherogenic index of plasma, cardiovascular disease, children hypertension, lipid

Background

Hypertension is a well‐established risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD), significantly contributing to both mortality and disability [1, 2]. Globally, over 1 billion adults are affected by hypertension [3], with the majority residing in low‐ and middle‐income countries [4]. Previous studies have demonstrated that childhood hypertension is linked to the development of essential hypertension in adulthood and to long‐term cardiovascular complications [5, 6, 7]. The prevalence of hypertension in children and adolescents has been increasing, posing a growing public health challenge worldwide [8]. In recent decades, the prevalence of childhood hypertension has been positively correlated with increasing body mass index (BMI) [9, 10]. In 2015, the prevalence ranged from 4.32% in 6‐year‐olds to 3.28% in 19‐year‐olds, peaking at 7.89% among 14‐year‐olds [8].

Beyond the well‐established risk factors for hypertension, including dietary habits, physical activity, and BMI [11], recent evidence suggests that individuals with hypertension often experience persistent dyslipidemia [12], characterized by elevated levels of low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C), total cholesterol (TC), and triglycerides (TG), alongside reduced high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C). Dyslipidemia has been well‐documented as a key contributor to atherosclerosis and an increased risk of CVD [13, 14]. Historically, LDL‐C has been considered the primary therapeutic target; however, studies have shown that approximately 50% of cardiovascular risk remains after achieving recommended LDL‐C levels [15]. This suggests that reliance on a single lipid marker is insufficient for comprehensive cardiovascular risk assessment [16].

The atherogenic index of plasma (AIP), calculated as the logarithm of the TG/HDL‐C ratio, has emerged as a strong predictor of atherosclerosis and coronary heart disease [17]. Despite this, studies exploring the relationship between AIP and hypertension were limited [18, 19, 20]. Two longitudinal studies reported a significant association between AIP and hypertension, with a stronger correlation observed in males compared to females [18, 19]. However, a large‐scale cross‐sectional study conducted in China found no significant association between AIP and hypertension in adults without type 2 diabetes mellitus [20]. These studies explored the association between AIP and hypertension in adults. However, recent studies have shown a concerning trend in the rising prevalence of hypertension among younger individuals, a demographic that was traditionally considered to be at lower risk for the condition [21]. The growing incidence of hypertension in younger populations underscores the importance of early intervention and preventive measures to mitigate long‐term health risks, such as hypertension, CVD. China has experienced a rapid nutritional transition, rising childhood obesity rates, and a high burden of hypertension, yet data on AIP and hypertension in Chinese children are scarce. Thus, the present study aims to investigate the association between AIP control levels and risk of childhood hypertension in Chinese students aged 6–17 years after adjusted for potential confounders, including demographic, dietary, and lifestyle factors.

Methods

Study Design and Population

This study is based on a national, multicenter, school‐based intervention aimed at promoting healthy lifestyles to combat obesity, conducted from September 2013 to June 2014. The details of the intervention program have been described in previous publications [22]. In brief, the program targeted participants aged 6–17 years from seven provinces across mainland China: Tianjin, Shanghai, Liaoning, Chongqing, Hunan, Ningxia, and Guangdong. Both baseline and follow‐up surveys were conducted using a standardized and uniform protocol across all selected schools in these provinces. A multistage cluster random sampling method was employed to select participants. Initially, 3–4 districts were randomly chosen from each province. Next, 12–16 schools were selected from each district. Subsequently, 2–3 classes per grade level were randomly chosen. After excluding students with severe organic diseases or those who declined to provide informed consent, all students aged 6–17 years from the selected classes were invited to participate in the survey. The intervention was a school based healthy lifestyle promotion (nutrition education, physical activity enhancement, and parental involvement) applied to all participating schools. Among these, a random sample of 16,098 students provided blood samples for analysis. A follow‐up survey was conducted 1 year later using the same methodology in 2014. With 16,098 participants and a hypertension prevalence of 6.5%, the study had >90% power to detect an OR of 1.75 for association between poor AIP control and hypertension at two‐tailed α = 0.05. The study received ethical approval from the Ethical Committee of Peking University (IRB00001052‐13034), and all participating students and their parents provided voluntary informed consent. All experiments were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

For the current analysis, a total of 16,098 students who completed blood sample collection at baseline were included. After excluding 1977 students without triglyceride or high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol measurements at the 2014 follow‐up and 1321 students with hypertension at baseline, a final sample of 12,800 students was included in the analysis (Figure 1).

Flowchart of the study population.

Definition of Hypertension

All participants underwent anthropometric measurements, including height, weight, waist circumference, hip circumference, and blood pressure. Blood pressure was assessed using a mercury sphygmomanometer (model XJ1ID, China) with an appropriately sized pediatric cuff, following the guidelines of the National High Blood Pressure Education Program (NHBPEP) Working Group for Children and Adolescents. Systolic blood pressure (SBP) was recorded at the onset of the first Korotkoff sound, and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) was recorded at the fifth Korotkoff sound. Blood pressure was measured twice, and the average was used to determine hypertension. Elevated SBP and DBP were defined as values greater than or equal to the 95th percentile for age‐, sex‐, and height‐specific reference values [23]. These percentiles were derived from the Centers for Disease Control (CDC) growth charts, as recommended by the NHBPEP Working Group [23, 24]. Hypertension was defined as either elevated SBP or DBP.

Blood Sample Collection and AIP Calculation

Venous blood samples were collected after 8 h of overnight fasting during both baseline and follow‐up surveys. Plasma lipid profiles, including Total Cholesterol (TC), Triglycerides (TG), Low‐Density Lipoprotein Cholesterol (LDL‐C), and High‐Density Lipoprotein Cholesterol (HDL‐C), were measured using enzyme colorimetric methods.

The AIP was calculated as log10 (TG/HDL‐C). In this study, AIP change from 2013 to 2014 was used as the exposure variable. The average AIP was calculated as the arithmetic mean of AIP_2013 and AIP_2014.

Ascertainment of Covariates

Based on previous studies, potential confounders were included as covariates at baseline. Demographic covariates included age (6–12 years, 13–17 years) and sex (male, female). Dietary habits were assessed using a validated food frequency questionnaire (FFQ), which has been used previously in Chinese children [25, 26]. Including frequency of breakfast consumption (7 days/week, <7 days/week), fruit (≥1 serving/day, <1 serving/day), vegetables (≥2 servings/day, <2 servings/day), meat (≥1 serving/day, <1 serving/day), milk (≥1 serving/day, <1 serving/day), beverages (<1 serving/day, ≥1 serving/day), fried food (<1 time/week, ≥1 time/week), and dessert (<1 time/week, ≥1 time/week). Physical activity (PA) level was assessed using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire short form (IPAQ‐SF) and categorized into light, moderate, and vigorous activity levels. The questionnaire please refer to the Supplementary file titled “Questionnaire”.

Statistical Analysis

‌The K‐means clustering algorithm, a widely used unsupervised learning method [27], was employed to partition the dataset into five distinct clusters, based on minimizing the distance between each data point and the corresponding cluster center (Figure 2a). In this study, when the number of clusters is equal to 5, the K‐means clustering works best [28, 29]: cluster 1, representing optimal AIP control, had average AIP ranging from ‐0.99 to ‐0.41; cluster 2, indicating better AIP control, ranged from −0.41 to −0.24; cluster 3, representing good AIP control, ranged from −0.24 to −0.06; cluster 4, indicating poor AIP control, ranged from −0.06 to 0.17; and cluster 5, representing the worst AIP control, ranged from 0.17 to 1.06. (Figure 2b).

(a) K‐means clustering method for clustering the atherogenic index of plasma; (b) The AIP clustering by k‐means clustering.

Continuous variables were summarized using mean and standard deviation, while categorical variables were described using percentages. Differences in continuous variables across clusters were analyzed using Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) or the Kruskal–Wallis test, and categorical variables were compared using the Chi‐square test. Binary logistic regression models were employed to examine the association between AIP and hypertension risk, with adjustments for confounders, such as age, sex, BMI, diet, and physical activity, which were associated with childhood hypertension reported in previous study [11]. Logistic regression results were presented as Odds Ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Additionally, a restricted cubic spline model was used to explore the nonlinear relationship between average AIP and hypertension risk, with four knots at the 20th, 40th, 60th, and 80th percentiles. Stratified analyses were performed by various demographic and lifestyle factors. A two‐sided p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.2.2 and IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.0.

Results

Baseline Characteristic of Subjects

A total of 12,800 students aged 6–17 years (Table S1), with no history of hypertension, were included in the present study. The average age of the students was 11.1 ± 3.3 years, and the average level of AIP was −0.20 ± 0.21. Following a 1‐year follow‐up period, 830 students (6.5%) developed hypertension. In comparison to Class 1 with optimal AIP control, participants in the other classes exhibited higher levels of height, weight, waist circumference, hip circumference, diastolic blood pressure (DBP), systolic blood pressure (SBP), body mass index (BMI), and average AIP (p < 0.001). Additionally, the rates of female participants, those eating breakfast less than 7 days per week, and those consuming at least two servings of vegetables per day were also higher in the other classes (p < 0.001). Furthermore, students in the higher classes had a significantly higher incidence of hypertension than those in Class 1 (p < 0.001) (Table 1).

The restricted cubic spline regression models, shown in Figure 3, revealed a nonlinear association between average AIP and hypertension (p for nonlinear = 0.022). The risk of hypertension increased significantly with higher average AIP levels when the average AIP exceeded −0.20. However, when average AIP was less than −0.20, the relationship between average AIP and hypertension risk appeared to follow an inverse “U” shape. Except for Class 4 (p for nonlinear = 0.040), the relationship between average AIP and hypertension risk in Classes 1–3 and Class 5 remained linear (p for nonlinear > 0.05).

Subgroup Analyses

A subgroup analysis was conducted to examine the relationship between average AIP and incident hypertension across various potential risk factors. As shown in Table S2, the difference of association between average AIP and hypertension risk was marginally significant in students aged 6–12 years compared to those aged 13–17 years (p for interaction = 0.050). However, no significant interactions were found between average AIP and other subgroup variables (p for interaction > 0.05).

Discussion

This study utilized data from a large‐scale national longitudinal survey to investigate the relationship between AIP control levels and childhood hypertension in Chinese students aged 6–17 years. The study found that the incidence of hypertension was 6.5%. Compared to optimal AIP control, poor AIP control was associated with a 75% increased risk of hypertension (OR = 1.75), and the worst AIP control was associated with a 143% increased risk (OR = 2.43), suggesting that AIP may serve as a potential predictor for childhood hypertension.

The mechanisms underlying the association between AIP control and hypertension remain unclear. However, hypertension and dyslipidemia are frequently observed to coexist in clinical practice [30], and this phenomenon is also evident in children and adolescents [31, 32]. Recent studies have shown a significant correlation between the progressive rise in blood pressure and elevated serum lipid concentrations [33]. As blood pressure increases, the risk of developing hypertension also rises, and this relationship appears to be closely linked to serum lipid levels. Higher concentrations of serum lipids, such as cholesterol and triglycerides, may contribute to the pathophysiology of hypertension, highlighting the importance of lipid management in the prevention and treatment of hypertension. Dyslipidemia affects both the structure and function of arterial walls, promoting the development of atherosclerosis. Abnormal lipid levels lead to lipid accumulation in the arterial walls, causing inflammation and plaque formation. This pathological process narrows the arteries, impairs blood flow, and disrupts the regulation of blood pressure [34].

In our study, students with poor AIP control had a 75% higher risk of hypertension compared to those with optimal AIP control, and students with the worst AIP control exhibited a 1.43‐fold increased risk of hypertension. These findings are consistent with previous research from both children and adults [19, 35, 36]. Zhu and his colleagues observed that children in the fourth quartile (Q4) of AIP had a 20% higher risk of hypertension compared with those in the first quartile (Q1) [36]. A cross‐sectional study in Japan, involving 15,453 participants aged 18 years or older, found that individuals in the highest AIP quartile had a significantly higher risk of prehypertension (OR = 1.15; 95% CI: 1.00–1.13) and hypertension (OR = 1.54; 95% CI: 1.16–2.04) compared to those in the lowest AIP quartile. Additionally, a 9‐year longitudinal study in Taiwan involving 7670 participants from the Tri‐Service General Hospital's health promotion center found that AIP was associated with hypertension, diabetes, and metabolic syndrome [19]. These results support the notion that suboptimal AIP levels are linked to an increased risk of hypertension, suggesting that AIP may be an important biomarker for cardiovascular health. Monitoring AIP levels may provide additional information for risk assessment, although further longitudinal studies with longer follow‑up are needed to establish its clinical utility.

Interestingly, the association between AIP levels and hypertension risk was sex‑specific, being stronger in boys than in girls, consistent with the findings of Zhu and his colleagues [36]. Zhu and his colleagues also observed a stronger association between AIP and hypertension in boys than in girls [36]. This suggests that monitoring and maintaining a healthy AIP level may be particularly important for hypertension prevention in children and adolescents, especially in males.

In this study, we noted that the non‑significant ORs for Class 2 and Class 3 (relative to Class 1), indicating that low‑to‑moderate AIP levels (less than −0.06) are not associated with increased hypertension risk, and that a clear increase in risk emerges only at higher AIP levels (Class 4 and above). Thus, −0.06 might be a threshold for the association between AIP and hypertension, the increased risk of hypertension was found only when AIP level more than this threshold. Which have clinical utility for identifying children at elevated hypertension risk.

Additionally, we employed restricted cubic spline regression models and observed a linear relationship between AIP control and hypertension risk when AIP control was greater than −0.20. However, no statistically significant association was found for AIP control levels below −0.20. This result differs from findings in adults. Min et al. reported a linear relationship between AIP control and incident CVD in middle‐aged and elderly Chinese individuals with abnormal glucose metabolism [37]. The discrepancy may be attributed to differences in AIP levels between children and adults. In our study, the mean average AIP level was −0.20 in children and adolescents, whereas in Min's study, the mean average AIP level was 1.22 in middle‐aged and elderly individuals [37].

This study utilized longitudinal data from a large‐scale national multicenter study, and found that AIP control level was significantly associated with hypertension in children and adolescents after adjusted covariates. However, several limitations should be noted. First, the participants were followed for only 1 year, and including the 2014 AIP (measured at the same time as the outcome) meaning that the AIP control level reflected only the average AIP within this period, which may have reduced the observed effect of AIP on hypertension. Moreover, averaging log‑transformed values has limited biological interpretability and that a simple average may miss dynamic changes. Second, 1977 (12.3%) participants were lost to follow‐up in the final survey. However, there were no significant differences in baseline demographic characteristics between those lost to follow‐up and those who completed the study. Third, the intervention may have attenuated or modified the observed associations, and that our findings may not generalize to non‐intervention settings. Fourth, although we had adjusted for several confounding factors, the residual confounding might exist due to several confounders were not been adjusted, such as family history of hypertension, birth weight, and pubertal development status. Finally, hypertension in this study was defined based on blood pressure measurements taken only on the visit day, which may introduce undifferentiated misclassification bias and potentially weaken the observed association between AIP control levels and hypertension risk. Future studies with longer follow‑up periods, detailed collection of family history and pubertal stage, repeated measures of AIP to capture its longitudinal trajectories, and independent validation in diverse populations are warranted to further elucidate the sex‑specific relationship between AIP and childhood hypertension.

Conclusions

The poor atherogenic index of plasma (AIP) control may increase the risk of childhood hypertension. AIP may be a useful risk marker, but causality and predictive ability need to be confirmed in longer term studies.
