Trimester-specific lipid profile reference intervals in healthy pregnant women; A cross-sectional study at Debre Markos comprehensive specialized hospital, 2022/2023
Authors: Workineh Tamir, Abtie Abebaw, Aytenew Atnaf, Alemayehu Reta, Zewdu Bishaw Aynalem, Genet Beyene, Tamiru Alene, Sisay Ayana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Reference intervals are essential for clinical decision-making in clinical chemistry, as they provide a basis for interpreting laboratory results during diagnosis, treatment, and patient monitoring. These intervals vary across populations due to differences in ethnicity, age, gender, and analytical methods. Additionally, pregnancy introduces significant hormonal changes that can alter clinical chemistry parameters, including lipid profiles. Therefore, this study aimed to establish trimester-specific reference intervals for lipid profile parameters among healthy pregnant women at Debre Markos Comprehensive Specialized Hospital in Northwest Ethiopia. An institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted from February 1, 2022, to May 30, 2023, among 459 apparently healthy pregnant women who met the inclusion criteria. Reference intervals were established using nonparametric statistical methods in accordance with the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute guideline C28-A3. The 95% reference intervals were determined using the 2.5th percentile as the lower reference limit and the 97.5th percentile as the upper reference limit. The 95% reference interval for lipid parameters was: Total cholesterol: 106–351, 119-375.6, and 110-389.5 mg/dL, Triglycerides: 54–339, 60–292, and 67-334.3 mg/dL, High density lipoprotein cholesterol: 16.7–89.3, 24-99.15, and 19.85-101.15 mg/dL, Low density lipoprotein cholesterol: 32.95–194.3, 54.25-192.95, and 59.95–199 mg/dL, for the 1st, 2nd, and 3rd trimesters respectively. Statistically significant differences were observed both within and between trimesters for nearly all lipid parameters. Furthermore, the trimester-specific reference intervals established in this study differed from the manufacturer-provided reference intervals for the lipid profile tests. The reference intervals previously in use did not align with the trimester-specific intervals established in this study. All lipid profile parameters showed distinct reference ranges across the three trimesters. These findings underscore the need for locally derived, trimester-specific reference interval guidelines rather than reliance on broad, non-specific ranges.

Introduction

A reference interval (RI) defines the range encompassing the central 95% of reference values (RVs) obtained from a well-characterized healthy population1. In accordance with international standards, including ISO 15,189 and guidelines of the International Federation of Clinical Chemistry (IFCC), the RI is established using the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the data distribution2. Depending on the clinical significance of a specific analyte, the RI may alternatively be expressed as lower and upper clinical decision limits3. Reference intervals are fundamental to disease screening, diagnosis, monitoring of disease progression, evaluation of therapeutic response, and overall patient management4, thereby enabling accurate interpretation of laboratory results and informed clinical decision-making5.

The Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) recommends that reference intervals be established with consideration of individual, population, physiological, and environmental factors, including ethnicity, climate, altitude, lifestyle, and related biological variations6. Studies conducted in Asian and African populations have demonstrated significant differences in reference values compared with those commonly applied in Western populations4,7.

Maternal physiology undergoes profound and dynamic changes throughout pregnancy, largely driven by rising levels of progesterone and estrogen, which are initially produced by the ovary during the first trimester and subsequently by the placenta8. These hormonal shifts, combined with the increased metabolic demands necessary to support fetal growth and development, elicit predictable alterations in maternal biochemical, hematological, and cardiovascular parameters9,10. A thorough understanding of these normal physiological adaptations is critical for the accurate interpretation of laboratory results and for the effective diagnosis and management of pregnancy-related conditions, including gestational hypertension, gestational diabetes, and thyroid disorders11.

During pregnancy, maternal lipid metabolism adapts to support fetal and placental growth. Rising estrogen and progesterone stimulate hepatic lipoprotein synthesis, increasing total cholesterol, LDL-C, and triglycerides12. Human placental lactogen and progesterone induce insulin resistance, promoting lipolysis and further elevating triglycerides, while HDL-C rises early due to estrogen effects on apolipoprotein A-I13. These changes result in progressive hyperlipidemia, peaking in the third trimester, providing essential substrates for fetal development, steroid hormone synthesis, and maternal energy storage14. Therefore, this study aimed to establish trimester-specific reference intervals for lipid profile parameters and to compare these intervals across trimesters among apparently healthy pregnant women at Debre Markos Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Northwest Ethiopia.

Methods and materials

Study design, period and setting

An institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted from February 1, 2022, to May 30, 2023, among apparently healthy pregnant women attending Debre Markos Comprehensive Specialized Hospital. The hospital is located in Debre Markos town, within the East Gojjam Zone of the Amhara Regional State. Debre Markos serves as the capital of the zone and is situated approximately 265 km from Bahir Dar and 299 km from Addis Ababa, the capital city of Ethiopia.

Population

The source population comprised all pregnant women attending Debre Markos Comprehensive Specialized Hospital. The study population consisted of those pregnant women who attended the hospital during the study period and met the eligibility criteria.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Apparently healthy pregnant women who tested negative for viral infections including hepatitis B, hepatitis C, and HIV as well as for malaria, and who were attending the ANC clinic at Debre Markos Comprehensive Specialized Hospital during the study period and consented to participate were included in the study.

Pregnant women with a known history of any infectious or non-infectious disease such as diabetes mellitus, renal disease, hypertension, heart disease, thyroid disorders, or liver disease as well as chronic alcohol users, smokers, whose Fasting Blood Sugar (FBS) > 140 mg/dL or those with recent viral infections were excluded. Additionally, women with a history of jaundice within the past 90 days, major surgery within the previous year, or those who were not willing to participate were excluded from the study.

Sample size determination and sampling technique

The Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute recommends the use of either parametric or non-parametric methods for establishing reference intervals (RIs). For a non-parametric 95% RI, a minimum of 153 reference individuals per subgroup is required15. Accordingly, 153 pregnant women were included in each trimester, yielding a total sample of 459 apparently healthy pregnant women. Participants from all three trimesters who met the inclusion criteria were enrolled consecutively as they presented to the institution, without prior stratification by trimester at recruitment. Trimester classification was subsequently determined during data analysis based on gestational age.

Data collection procedure

Socio-demographic and clinical data collection

Socio-demographic data were collected using a pre-tested structured questionnaire administered through face-to-face interviews. The presence of underlying chronic conditions, including kidney disease, liver disease, hypertension, and diabetes mellitus, was assessed through participant interviews and thorough review of medical records. Gestational diabetes mellitus status was screened using FBS measurements, and only participants with FBS levels below 140 mg/dL were included in the study. Participants’ hepatitis B and C virus, HIV, and malaria statuses were determined through laboratory testing performed by qualified laboratory professionals.

Laboratory sample collection and analysis

A total of 4 mL of venous blood was collected from each study participant using serum separator tubes with gel separators. Blood samples were obtained after an overnight fast of at least 8 h and were categorized into first, second, and third trimester groups based on the participants’ gestational age. Gestational age was determined based on obstetric assessment and categorized into first (≤ 13 weeks), second (14–27 weeks), and third (≥ 28 weeks) trimesters. At the time of collection, thin and thick blood films were also prepared for microscopic examination of malaria. After allowing the blood to clot, the samples were centrifuged at 2,500 RPM for 5 min to separate the serum. Lipid profile tests were measured using the FC-200 fully automated clinical chemistry analyzer. The analyzer operates based on enzymatic colorimetric principles. Total cholesterol (TC) is determined using the cholesterol oxidase–phenol aminophenazone (CHOD-PAP) method, while triglycerides (TG) are measured by the glycerol-3-phosphate oxidase–phenol aminophenazone (GPO-PAP) method. High-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) is measured using a selective direct enzymatic method based on absorbance, and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) is determined by a direct homogeneous enzymatic method. Commercial reagents such as calibrators, and control samples from Human Diagnostics (Germany) were used for the determination of lipid profile parameters.

Screening tests for HIV, hepatitis B virus, and hepatitis C virus were conducted using the STAT-PAK, HBsAg, and HCV antibody test kits, respectively. Malaria infection in participants was assessed by examining 3% Giemsa-stained blood films. Participants who were negative for all these tests were included in the study.

Quality control for data collection and laboratory analysis

The quality of socio-demographic and clinical data was ensured by initially preparing the questionnaire in English and then translating it into Amharic. A pre-testing phase was conducted on 5% of the planned sample size at the Debre Markos health center. Data collectors received thorough orientation before commencing the actual data collection. Rigorous supervision was maintained throughout the data collection phase to guarantee the accuracy and reliability of the collected information.

To ensure the quality and reliability of laboratory test results, the performance of the clinical chemistry analyzer was verified through routine internal quality control procedures. Two levels (normal and pathological) of commercially prepared control sera were used for TC, TG, HDL-C, and LDL-C. Quality control samples were analyzed daily prior to patient testing and following reagent changes. Analytical performance was monitored using Levey–Jennings charts in conjunction with Westgard multirule criteria. Throughout the study period, the coefficients of variation (CV%) for TC, TG, HDL-C, and LDL-C were maintained within commonly accepted limits (≤ 3% for TC, ≤ 5% for TG and ≤ 4% for HDL-C and LDL-C), demonstrating consistent analytical stability and reliable performance of the analyzer.

The reliability of serological test results was ensured by using known positive and negative control samples for each test category. The presence of an appropriate control line next to the sample line was verified for every test. The quality of the Giemsa stain used for malaria examination was also checked using 1 + and 2 + Plasmodium-positive samples.

Ethical approval and consent to participate

The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Debre Markos University, College of Health Sciences. A formal letter was submitted to Debre Markos Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, and permission was granted. Written informed consent was obtained from each of voluntary participant prior to sample collection. For laboratory analysis, the center’s code numbers were used on test tubes, and all information collected from participants was kept strictly confidential. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Data processing and analysis

The data were manually rechecked, organized, and arranged before entry. Afterwards, the cleaned data were entered into Epi Data version 4.2 and exported to SPSS version 23 for statistical analysis. Prior to statistical analysis, outlier detection was performed to identify and manage extreme values. Outliers were identified using the Tukey interquartile range (IQR) method, whereby observations below Q1 − 1.5×IQR or above Q3 + 1.5×IQR were classified as outliers. All identified outliers were carefully reviewed for potential analytical or data entry errors before exclusion. Subsequent analyses were conducted using the cleaned dataset. To ensure reliable results, non-parametric analytical methods were applied. Simple descriptive statistics were used to calculate the median of each lipid parameter, and the values were summarized according to trimester. The Kruskal–Walli’s test was employed to compare median parameters across trimesters, followed by post-hoc analysis to determine statistically significant differences between specific trimesters. Reference intervals were established following CLSI guidelines using percentile methods. The 95% RI was determined by the 2.5th percentile as the lower reference limit and the 97.5th percentile as the upper reference limit, depending on the clinical relevance of the analyte. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

Socio-demographic characteristics

A total of 459 apparently healthy pregnant women participated in the study. Among them, 401 (87.4%) were from urban areas, while 58 (12.6%) were from rural areas. The participants had a median age of 27 years, with ages ranging from 18 to 44 years, giving an overall age range of 26 years.

The Reference Interval of Lipid Profiles in Pregnant Women

The reference intervals for lipid profiles showed the following median values for the 1 st, 2nd, and 3rd trimesters, respectively: TC at 194, 223, and 226 mg/dL; TG at 131, 130, and 166 mg/dL; HDL-C at 51, 41, and 39 mg/dL; and LDL-C at 92, 101, and 101 mg/dL (Table 1).

Discussion

Clinical evaluation traditionally begins with the collection of symptoms and examination of physical signs to identify features that are not typically present in healthy individuals and that may be associated with specific diseases. Laboratory measurements are clinically meaningful only when interpreted in the context of values obtained from healthy populations or compared with ranges commonly observed in disease states. Reference intervals, defined as the central 95% range of values from a healthy population, are therefore essential for distinguishing between physiological and pathological conditions. They play a crucial role in clinical decision-making and in the accurate interpretation of laboratory results16. Reference intervals help clinicians determine whether a patient’s test result falls within the expected range for health or suggests possible disease or abnormality, guiding diagnosis, treatment, and monitoring of patient care16.

During pregnancy, the mother’s body requires increased energy to support the normal growth and development of the fetus17. This heightened demand, combined with hormonal changes, leads to alterations in metabolic activity and consequently affects lipid profiles14. In early pregnancy, rising levels of estrogen and progesterone, along with hyperinsulinemia, promote increased peripheral glucose utilization, greater glycogen storage in the liver, enhanced lipid storage, and reduced lipid breakdown. This metabolic shift contributes to the characteristic changes observed in maternal lipid levels during pregnancy18. Similarly, in late pregnancy, maternal lipid levels increase significantly to meet fetal energy demands19. Total cholesterol, LDL-C, and triglycerides rise due to enhanced hepatic lipoprotein synthesis, insulin resistance, and increased lipolysis, while HDL-C increases moderately20. These physiological changes reflect normal metabolic adaptations that support fetal growth and placental function.

Serum TC, TG, HDL-C, and LDL-C are routinely requested by clinicians and represent an essential component of diagnostic laboratory testing. In this study, reference intervals for TG, TC, HDL-C, and LDL-C were established for pregnant women across each trimester. The values of these lipid parameters increased during pregnancy, though at varying rates. As gestational age advances in normal pregnancy, concentrations of TC, TG, HDL-C, and LDL-C progressively rise. In line with our study findings, the study conducted in India18,21 showed that the lipid profile test increase progressively with advanced pregnancy age.

In our study, regarding statistical significance, TG showed significant differences between the 1 st and 3rd trimesters and between the 2nd and 3rd trimesters (P < 0.001). HDL-C also demonstrated significant differences between the 1 st and 2nd trimesters and between the 1 st and 3rd trimesters (P < 0.001). LDL-C showed a statistically significant difference between the 1 st and 2nd trimesters (P = 0.044). Total cholesterol levels differed significantly between the 1 st and 3rd trimesters and between the 2nd and 3rd trimesters (P < 0.001). The progressive increase in maternal lipid levels across trimesters occurs as rising estrogen and progesterone in early pregnancy stimulate hepatic lipoprotein synthesis and fat storage22. As pregnancy advances, insulin resistance and placental hormones, such as human placental lactogen, promote lipolysis and enhance hepatic production of VLDL and LDL, resulting in higher circulating cholesterol and triglyceride levels20.

In this study, serum TG levels showed a marked increase beginning in the first trimester. We also observed that serum TC, HDL-C, and LDL-C levels generally rose as gestational age advanced. The elevated lipid levels during early pregnancy are attributed to implantation of the zygote in the uterine wall and a metabolic shift in the mother from carbohydrate to lipid utilization to meet increased energy demands17. A slight decrease in LDL-C was noted during the second trimester. By the third trimester, the concentrations of serum TG, TC, HDL-C, and LDL-C were highest. This rise is associated with the rapid growth and organ development of the fetus, which increases maternal metabolic and energy requirements18.

In the present study, all obtained values differed from the reference intervals currently in use which provided by the manufacturer. This variation may be attributed to the significant anatomical and physiological changes that begin after conception and affect nearly every organ system in the pregnant mother23. These changes support fetal development, maintain maternal homeostasis, and prepare the body for birth and lactation24. Additionally, differences in reference intervals may arise from factors such as altitude, diet, genetics, and ethnic diversity. Previous studies have reported that genetics, nutritional status, and maternal lifestyle influence metabolic changes during pregnancy, although many altered analytes tend to return to normal levels by term23,25.

Limitation of the study

Although our findings meet the minimum CLSI requirements for establishing reference intervals, the study did not include a parallel group of non-pregnant women from the same reference population, which could have provided additional comparative insights.

Conclusion and recommendation

In conclusion, the lipid profile reference intervals established in this study differ from those currently in use. Each parameter showed trimester-specific variation, emphasizing the importance of considering physiological changes during pregnancy. These findings highlight the need for locally developed reference intervals rather than relying on generalized or externally derived ranges. Accordingly, patient management and interpretation of laboratory results should be based on locally established, population-specific reference intervals to ensure accurate clinical decision-making.
