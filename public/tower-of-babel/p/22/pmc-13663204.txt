Tumor cells escape from therapy-induced senescence: from molecular mechanisms to targeted intervention
Authors: Yu Han, Lie Ma, Xiangyu Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTumor recurrence represents a significant challenge in the field of tumor therapy, severely affecting patients’ survival rates and quality of life. Even after standard treatments, many patients still experience tumor recurrence. Traditional radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and targeted therapies can induce tumor cell senescence to suppress tumor growth and dissemination. However, recent studies have shown that therapy-induced senescent tumor cells can re-enter the cell cycle, acting as a key driver of tumor recurrence. Consequently, senescent tumor cells have emerged as a pivotal focus for unraveling the mechanisms underlying tumor recurrence. This review systematically elucidates the multidimensional molecular regulatory networks through which senescent tumor cells promote tumor recurrence, encompassing key processes such as cell cycle reactivation mechanisms, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), acquisition of stemness phenotypes, formation of polyploid giant cancer cells (PGCCs), fate transition from senescence to pyroptosis, and remodeling of the immune microenvironment. Additionally, it summarizes the latest advances in targeted interventions against senescent tumor cells, including senolytics-mediated clearance of senescent cells and senomorphic modulation of the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP). Furthermore, we propose that, guided by the molecular mechanisms underlying senescent tumor cell-mediated recurrence, the integration of multi-omics analyses to precisely delineate the therapeutic window, combined with artificial intelligence-driven drug screening, holds promise for developing more specific senolytic agents, thereby offering innovative strategies to improve recurrence control rates following cancer therapy.Keywords: Senescent tumor cells, Tumor recurrence, Therapy-induced senescence, Senolytics, Senomorphics, Cancer therapy

Introduction

Cellular senescence refers to the irreversible loss of proliferative capacity in cells under the influence of external environmental changes or specific internal gene activation/inactivation, leading to a relatively stable state. This process is an inevitable fate for normal cells in multicellular organisms [1]. In 1961, Leonard Hayflick and Paul Moorhead discovered that human diploid fibroblast strains cultured in vitro cease mitotic division after 50–70 population doublings, and this phenomenon of limited proliferative potential in cultured cells was termed “cellular senescence” [2]. Subsequent research revealed that this physiological senescence arises from the absence of endogenous telomerase activity. During extensive proliferation, telomeres progressively shorten, ultimately impairing division capability and resulting in cell cycle arrest and functional decline [3].

Beyond replicative senescence from telomere shortening, cellular senescence is now recognized as a stress response activated by intrinsic and extrinsic factors which include chemotherapeutic intervention, oncogene activation, oxidative or genotoxic stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and ionizing radiation [4]. A key characteristic of senescent cells is stable cell cycle arrest, primarily driven by transcriptional upregulation of cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors p21 and p16, which markedly slows cell division kinetics [4]. Although cancer cells possess unlimited proliferative capacity, various carcinogenic insults can trigger senescence programs, forming a powerful tumor-suppressive mechanism [5]. Chemotherapy, as a central pillar of cancer treatment, commonly induces senescence-like phenotypes in cancer cells. For example, docetaxel, the standard first-line chemotherapy for prostate cancer, induces tumor senescence in vivo [6]. Similarly, cisplatin and etoposide, first-line agents for small cell lung cancer (SCLC), achieve therapeutic effects by promoting SCLC cell senescence, thereby inhibiting tumor growth [7].

Recent studies have increasingly revealed the reversibility of senescent cells. For instance, non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), colorectal cancer, and breast cancer cell lines induced into senescence by chemotherapeutic agents such as etoposide or doxorubicin (dox) can escape cell cycle arrest [8], and targeting senescent tumor cells following chemotherapy significantly suppresses tumor recurrence [5]. Furthermore, research indicates that tumor cells released from senescence and re-entering the cell cycle exhibit significantly enhanced stemness characteristics and tumor-initiating capacity which further explains why recurrent tumor cells in clinical settings often manifest as tumor metastasis [9].

The aforementioned findings reveal that senescent tumor cells possess re-proliferative potential, which may pose a significant risk for tumor recurrence through senescence escape [10]. Building on current advances in tumor cell senescence-inducing factors, this review summarizes the molecular mechanisms by which tumor cells escape senescence to promote recurrence, highlights the pivotal role of therapeutic strategies targeting senescent tumor cells in suppressing tumor recurrence, and proposes future directions encompassing multi-omics analysis and artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted drug development, thereby providing scientific evidence and strategic support for improving clinical outcomes in cancer therapy.

Senescence in the context of cancer

Hallmarks of cellular senescence

Although cellular senescence induces cell cycle arrest in normally replication-competent cells, these cells remain metabolically active. Senescent cells exhibit a series of hallmarks, including morphological features, expression of antiproliferative molecules, release of senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) factors such as IL-1, IL-6, and IL-8, and large-scale chromatin remodeling [11]. Since no single marker can precisely identify senescent cells, the determination of senescence progression requires multi-marker combined detection and comprehensive analysis of multidimensional features.

Cellular morphological changes serve as a hallmark of senescence. Both normal and cancer cells undergoing senescence become enlarged and flattened, with increased vacuolization and granularity [12–14]. The flattened and enlarged cellular morphology may depend on caveolae structures and altered localization of the ROCK signaling pathway [15]. Notably, vacuolization is associated with endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress triggered by the unfolded protein response (UPR) [16]. Additionally, cellular senescence is accompanied by the upregulation of many lysosomal proteins and an increase in lysosomal content, with the activity of lysosomal enzyme senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) serving as a surrogate indicator for lysosomal content in senescent cells [17].

Senescent cancer cells typically share the same features as senescent normal cells, such as increased cell volume and elevated SA-β-gal activity. However, tumor cell senescence differs fundamentally from normal senescence in terms of morphological characteristics, cell cycle arrest patterns, epigenetic remodeling, genomic stability, and SASP composition (Fig. 1). These differences not only reflect the complexity of the genetic background of cancer cells but also directly determine the dual role of senescence in tumors, which serves as both a tumor-suppressive barrier and a tumor-promoting engine.

Distinction between normal and tumor cell senescence. Normal senescence is typically mononuclear, with cell cycle arrest mediated by the p16/Rb and p53/p21 pathways, accompanied by stable SAHF formation and SASP activation that facilitates senescent cell clearance. In contrast, tumor cell senescence is characterized by multinucleation, p53-independent cell cycle arrest, nuclear CCF-mediated cGAS-STING activation driving inflammatory responses, and heterogeneous SASP secretion that promotes tumor progression

Senescent cancer cells can acquire additional morphological features rarely observed in senescent normal cells, including micronucleus formation, multinucleation, and nuclear budding and blebbing [13]. Micronuclei have been confirmed as the origin of complex genomic rearrangements such as chromothripsis and are increasingly recognized as a common feature of aggressive cancers [18]. Furthermore, cancer cells can undergo polyploidization following treatment with DNA-damaging agents or mitotic checkpoint inhibitors [19, 20]. Extensive multinucleation has been observed in senescent HCT116 cells treated with dox, and these multinucleated cells show no signs of cell death [21]. Polyploid cancer cells can generate aneuploid or near-diploid progeny capable of undergoing normal mitosis, thereby driving cancer renewal [22].

Although cellular senescence was initially characterized as a stable G1-phase cell cycle arrest, subsequent studies have confirmed the existence of G2-phase arrest dependent on TP53 and RB1 status [23]. This distinction is particularly pronounced between normal and cancer cells. In normal replicative senescence, cell cycle gene sets are broadly downregulated, and the arrest pattern is relatively uniform, predominantly at G1 or G2/M phase [24]. However, due to the frequent mutations in TP53, RB1, and CDKN2A, cancer cells exhibit marked heterogeneity in cell cycle distribution following senescence. In TP53 wild-type cancer cells, G1, M/G1, and G1/S gene sets are moderately downregulated, whereas gene sets of other phases are strongly downregulated, consistent with the classical G1 arrest pattern. In contrast, TP53-mutant and RB1-mutant cells lack phase-specific arrest, and CDKN1A/CDKN2A mutation status is not associated with the G1 arrest mode. The cell cycle arrest in cancer cell senescence is highly dependent on the TP53/RB1 mutational background and lacks the uniformity observed in normal senescence [25].

Gene expression profiles undergo profound remodeling during cellular senescence. In normal cell senescence, stable G1/G2 cell cycle arrest is primarily mediated by the cooperative action of two canonical signaling axes: p16/Rb and p53/p21 [26]. However, in tumor cells, the frequent mutations in p53 and RB1 may disrupt these classical pathways. Recent studies have revealed that the splicing factor SF3B4 is significantly upregulated in multiple cancer types. SF3B4 directly binds to the 3′UTR of p21 mRNA and recruits NMD degradation machinery, promoting p21 transcript decay and thereby suppressing senescence. Depletion of SF3B4 upregulates p21 expression through a p53-independent pathway, ultimately inducing tumor cell senescence [27]. This finding reveals that tumor cell senescence can bypass p53 and trigger the senescence program via post-transcriptional regulation at the RNA level, reflecting the adaptive remodeling of senescence mechanisms by cancer cells upon inactivation of classical tumor-suppressive pathways.

At the chromatin remodeling level, normal cell senescence is accompanied by the stable formation of senescence-associated heterochromatin foci (SAHF). SAHF are composed of nuclease-resistant chromatin compaction, bearing typical modifications of transcriptionally silent heterochromatin, such as H3K9me3, the formation of which is dependent on the p16/pRb signaling pathway [13, 28]. However, due to the frequent inactivation of the p16/pRb pathway, cancer cells are often unable to form SAHF. Although cellular senescence is also present in human precancerous lesions, DAPI-labeled SAHF have not been detected in human lesional tissues across various stages of tumorigenesis. Specifically, analysis of human colorectal adenoma tissues revealed that SAHF formation was not observed in lesion areas that were positive for SA-β-gal activity [13, 29]. This difference results in the absence of a robust epigenetic barrier for silencing proliferation-promoting genes in senescent cancer cells, which is otherwise present in senescent normal cells.

The disparity is even more pronounced at the level of genomic stability. Normal cell senescence is characterized by stable cell cycle exit accompanied by SAHF formation. In contrast, senescent tumor cells exhibit persistent DNA damage signaling, cytoplasmic chromatin fragments (CCFs), and constitutive cGAS-STING activation, driving chronic type I interferon production that paradoxically promotes immune evasion in tumors [30, 31]. This genomic instability is not a passive byproduct but rather an active driver of pro-tumorigenic SASP production [32].

Cells undergoing senescence typically display senescence-associated phenotypic changes and trigger inflammatory cascades through the secretion of the SASP. The SASP is mediated by the transcription factors nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) and CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein β (C/EBPβ), comprising the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines interleukin (IL)-6 and IL-8, chemokines monocyte chemoattractant proteins (MCPs) and macrophage inflammatory proteins (MIPs), growth factors transforming growth factor-β (TGFβ) and granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), and proteases [33]. Under normal physiological conditions, transient activation of the SASP recruits immune cells and facilitates the clearance of senescent cells [3, 34]. In contrast, chronic SASP activation in the tumor microenvironment induces persistent inflammation, promotes tumor immune evasion, and accelerates tumor progression [35]. Early studies have revealed that when premalignant senescent hepatocytes coexist with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cells, SASP-dependent recruitment of immature myeloid cells may promote HCC progression by impairing natural killer (NK) cell function [36]. SASP factor secretion by malignant cells establishes a pro-tumorigenic local microenvironment that stimulates the migration and invasion of adjacent premalignant and malignant cells. Beyond these functions, SASP factors are also implicated in the acquisition of stem-like phenotypes by cancer cells [34].

The composition of the SASP also exhibits marked heterogeneity. IL-6 and IL-8, as canonical SASP factors, are persistently upregulated during normal cell senescence and sustain autocrine and paracrine SASP loops. However, in senescent cancer cells, IL-6 and IL-8 expression is highly dependent on the tissue of origin: baseline IL-8 expression varies significantly across lung, breast, colorectal, and liver cancers. During senescence induced by etoposide or alisertib, IL-6 is predominantly induced in lung and breast cancer cells with high baseline expression, whereas IL-8 is consistently upregulated only in breast cancer cells, showing no significant change in colorectal cancer and variable responses across lung cancer cell lines [25]. These data indicate that the SASP of senescent cancer cells displays a heterogeneity spectrum closely associated with tumor type.

Inducers of tumor cell senescence

Traditionally, cellular senescence has been recognized as an irreversible cell cycle arrest mechanism. Various stressors can induce tumor cell senescence, which inhibits the growth of malignant tumor cells and thus serves as an effective therapeutic strategy. Based on differences in the initial senescence-inducing signals, tumor cell senescence can be classified into oncogene-induced senescence (OIS), therapy-induced senescence (TIS), and stress-induced senescence.

Oncogene-induced cellular senescence

OIS represents a critical tumor-suppressive mechanism triggered by aberrant oncogenic signals, which can be initiated by overexpression of activated oncogenes (e.g., RAS and RAF) or by loss-of-function mutations in tumor suppressor genes such as PTEN [3]. The discovery of OIS dates back to 1997, when researchers first observed significant upregulation of senescence-associated markers, including p53, p21, and p16, upon overexpression of oncogenic RAS in primary human fibroblasts, thereby demonstrating that oncogenic signaling alone is sufficient to drive cellular senescence [37]. Since 2005, the pathophysiological relevance of OIS has been extended across various tumorigenesis models, including lymphoma, prostate cancer, pituitary adenoma, thyroid adenoma, and melanocytic nevi [38–41]. Among these, melanocytic nevi represent benign tumors of melanocytes; nevi induced by oncogenic BRAF mutations typically remain in a senescent state for decades, thereby preventing progression to melanoma [41]. Furthermore, PTEN loss-induced cellular senescence exerts its tumor-restraining effects in early-stage prostate cancer through activation of the AKT-mTOR signaling axis, which subsequently upregulates p53 and p16 expression. In the MDA PCa-2b prostate cancer xenograft model, pharmacological inhibition of PTEN resulted in significantly elevated p53 protein levels and increased SA-β-gal activity within tumor tissues [42].

From a temporal perspective of tumor evolution, OIS predominantly operates at the early stages of tumorigenesis. Consequently, senescent-like cells are commonly detected in precancerous lesions but are largely absent in advanced malignancies [43, 44]. Functionally, OIS restricts the growth of oncogenic stress-bearing cells, thereby confining tumors to a non‑invasive, premalignant state and is thus considered a critical barrier that prevents the malignant transformation of benign tumors [3, 45, 46].

Therapy-induced cellular senescence

Numerous anticancer therapies, including radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and cell cycle inhibitors, can induce a form of senescence known as TIS. Mechanistically, TIS arises from treatment-induced cellular stress, such as DNA damage, oxidative stress, or mitotic perturbation, which activates tumor suppressor pathways and ultimately leads to stable cell cycle arrest [47].

Radiotherapy is a widely used cytotoxic therapeutic modality effective against multiple malignancies, including skin cancer, breast cancer, head and neck cancer, bladder cancer, lung cancer, and prostate cancer, with its core mechanism being the induction of irreversible DNA damage response (DDR) in tumor cells [48]. Notably, radiotherapy not only directly eliminates tumor cells but also induces cellular senescence, a process that is highly dependent on the functional status of p53 [49]. For example, a radiation dose of 10 Gy effectively induces senescence in A549 lung cancer cells and MCF-7 breast cancer cells, whereas it fails to elicit a senescent phenotype in p53-deficient MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells [49, 50]. Moreover, this phenomenon has been corroborated in animal studies: normal mice exposed to sublethal doses of irradiation exhibit progressive progeroid phenotypes [51].

Numerous DNA-damaging antitumor agents have been extensively employed in both in vitro and in vivo studies of therapy-induced senescence, including bleomycin, cisplatin, etoposide, and dox. In vitro, treatment of MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells with 100 nM dox for 24 h results in significantly elevated SA-β-gal activity, lipofuscin, and lipid droplet accumulation on day 9 following drug withdrawal [52]. In vivo, the MMTV-Wnt-1 transgenic breast cancer mouse model exhibits significantly increased SA-β-gal activity in tumor tissues after dox chemotherapy [53]. The combination of etoposide and cisplatin constitutes the standard chemotherapy regimen for SCLC, which similarly exerts antitumor effects through senescence induction. In multiple SCLC cell lines in vitro, this combination significantly upregulates SA-β-gal activity and the expression of senescence markers including p21, p16, and IL-6. In SCLC xenograft models, this regimen not only markedly suppresses tumor growth but also elevates senescence marker levels in tumor tissues, indicating that its antitumor efficacy is at least partially attributable to TIS induction [7]. Cisplatin monotherapy has likewise been demonstrated to inhibit tumor growth via senescence induction in both in vitro and in vivo models of head and neck cancer [54]. Importantly, these phenomena are not restricted to preclinical models. In clinical specimens from NSCLC patients, the proportion of SA-β-gal-positive cells in tumor tissues is significantly increased following three cycles of neoadjuvant carboplatin plus paclitaxel chemotherapy [55]. Similarly, in malignant pleural mesothelioma (MPM) patients, the expression of the senescence marker p21 is significantly upregulated in tumor tissues after adjuvant cisplatin plus gemcitabine treatment [56].

Drugs targeting the androgen receptor (AR) signaling pathway play a pivotal role in the proliferation and survival of hormone-sensitive prostate cancer cells and may also induce cellular senescence, thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy. For instance, androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) in prostate cancer induces senescent growth arrest in prostate cancer cells exposed to the anti-androgen bicalutamide, characterized by morphological alterations and upregulation of SA-β-gal [57]. In addition, removal of estrogen signaling by tamoxifen can also induce senescence in breast cancer cells [58].

Cell cycle inhibition represents another key strategy for inducing cancer cell senescence. Cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK) 4/6 inhibitors that target the G1-S transition have been explored as potential senescence inducers. Currently, multiple CDK4/6 inhibitors, including palbociclib, abemaciclib, and ribociclib, have been approved for clinical use [59]. Palbociclib was the first CDK4/6 inhibitor approved for estrogen receptor-positive (ER⁺) advanced breast cancer [60]. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that palbociclib induces senescence in breast cancer cells through activation of immune surveillance, thereby significantly reducing tumor volume [61]. Furthermore, palbociclib has been confirmed to induce cellular senescence across multiple tumor models: in melanoma, it induces a senescent phenotype via inhibition of the mTOR signaling pathway [62]; in high-grade serous ovarian cancer [63], head and neck cancer [64], and oral squamous cell carcinoma [65], palbociclib similarly suppresses tumor growth through senescence induction in both in vitro and in vivo models. However, it is worth noting that the senescent phenotype induced by CDK4/6 inhibitors is not always stable. For example, palbociclib induces irreversible cell cycle arrest and senescence in MCF-7 breast cancer cells [66], but only reversible cell cycle arrest in bone cancer cells [67], suggesting that senescence stability is significantly tumor type-dependent.

Aurora kinases (AURKs) and Polo-like kinases (PLKs) are key regulators of mitotic progression, ensuring accurate chromosome segregation during cell division. Among these, PLK1, AURKA, and AURKB are frequently overexpressed in multiple malignancies and are subject to tight transcriptional and translational control [68–70]. In melanoma models, AURKA inhibition has been demonstrated to concurrently induce tumor cell senescence and antitumor immune responses. The AURKA inhibitor MLN8054 induces DNA damage, upregulates SA-β-gal activity, and promotes macrophage recruitment and immune surveillance via NF-κB-driven SASP, thereby achieving long-term immune control of tumors; these findings have been further validated in patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models [71]. AURKB inhibition primarily exerts antitumor effects through activation of adaptive immunity. In melanoma models, AURKB inhibitors enhance T cell-mediated antitumor immune responses and synergize with anti-CTLA-4 immune checkpoint blockade, an effect that is partially dependent on p21-mediated senescence in tumor cells, and xenograft studies have confirmed that AURKB inhibitors significantly suppress tumor growth [72]. Furthermore, AURK inhibitors (e.g., alisertib, barasertib) and PLK1 inhibitors (e.g., BI2536, BI6727) have demonstrated significant senescence-inducing capacity across in vitro tumor models and in vivo murine studies, with several agents currently advancing into clinical trials [73–81].

Studies have demonstrated that inhibition of the kinase CDC7 by small molecules XL413 or TAK931 blocks DNA replication and induces senescence in TP53-mutant liver cancer cells. Furthermore, this senescence-inducing effect of CDC7 inhibition has been further validated in multiple liver cancer xenograft mouse models, where CDC7 inhibition suppresses tumor growth, particularly in combination with mTOR inhibitors [82].

Epigenetic modifying enzymes precisely regulate tumor initiation and progression, and their selective inhibition can induce cancer cell senescence. The selective histone demethylase KDM5A inhibitor cyclopenta[c]chromen derivative 1 has been reported to induce senescence and suppress proliferation in KDM5A-overexpressing breast cancer cell lines. Mechanistically, this KDM5A inhibitor blocks H3K4me3 demethylation, promotes accumulation of p16 and p27, leading to cell cycle arrest [83]. Furthermore, the small-molecule inhibitor WM-1119 targeting the acetyltransferase KAT6A induces senescence in lymphoma cells through upregulation of p16 and p19, thereby reducing tumor burden in mouse xenograft models [84]. The DNA methyltransferase inhibitor decitabine induces senescence in MPM [85], while the histone deacetylase inhibitor vorinostat promotes senescence across multiple cancer cell types via upregulation of CDKN2A and TP53 [86, 87].

Replicative senescence is triggered by telomere shortening. Cancer cells most frequently bypass this mechanism by reactivating telomerase activity [88]. To date, a variety of compounds that inhibit the telomerase complex have been identified as candidate agents for anticancer therapy [89]. Among them, BIBR15 and GRN163L are potent telomerase inhibitors that can effectively induce senescence and suppress the proliferation of cancer cells [90, 91]. However, the application of GRN163L in senescence-promoting therapy requires further evaluation, as this compound also induces apoptosis in human pancreatic cancer cells [91].

Stress-induced cellular senescence

Even in the absence of any therapeutic intervention, senescent tumor cells exhibiting stable cell cycle arrest can still be detected in advanced-stage tumors, suggesting that cellular senescence can serve as an endogenous biological response during tumor progression. Accumulating evidence indicates that the tumor immune microenvironment is involved in driving this endogenous cellular senescence. During tumorigenesis, activation of the host immune surveillance system triggers the innate immune response, leading to robust secretion of IFNγ and TNFα to exert anti-tumor effects. A growing body of evidence has shown that, beyond their canonical anti-tumor functions, IFNγ and TNFα can also induce senescence phenotypes in a wide range of murine and human tumor models, including breast cancer, melanoma, and lung cancer [92]. NK cells, as key effector cells of innate immunity, are primary sources of these cytokines [93]. In addition to IFNγ and TNFα, NK cells secrete a diverse array of pro-inflammatory molecules, including matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and core SASP factors such as IL-6 and IL-8 [94–96]. Multiple in vitro studies have further confirmed that various SASP factors and associated signaling pathways, including IL-6 [97], IL-8 [97], TNF-α [92], CXCL1 [98], and the cGAS-STING pathway [99], can induce senescence in neighboring tumor cells through paracrine mechanisms. In summary, IFNγ, TNFα, and NK cell-derived inflammatory factors collectively secreted during tumor immune surveillance constitute a critical axis through which endogenous immune responses drive tumor cell senescence, providing key mechanistic insights into senescence occurring under non-therapeutic conditions.

In addition to immune-derived cytokines, multiple endogenous stress stimuli including oxidative stress, metabolic stress, hypoxia, and replicative stress may also drive tumor cell senescence in the absence of therapeutic intervention. With respect to oxidative stress, studies in breast cancer have demonstrated that the YPEL3 gene promotes cancer cell senescence by enhancing mitochondrial oxygen consumption, thereby elevating intracellular oxidative stress [100]; moreover, H₂O₂-induced oxidative stress has been shown to trigger senescence in cancer stem cells via the p53/p21 pathway, leading to a loss of proliferative capacity [100]. At the level of metabolic stress, ACSL5-mediated lipid oxidation in bladder cancer induces cellular senescence through upregulation of acetyl-CoA levels [101]; in breast, colorectal, and lung cancers, the key metabolic regulator citrate has been found to elicit tumor cell senescence by promoting excessive lipid biosynthesis [102], suggesting that lipid metabolic reprogramming represents an important endogenous source of senescence-inducing stress. The intrinsic oxygen partial pressure gradient within tumors establishes a sustained hypoxic microenvironment. Studies in melanoma models have revealed that SA-β-gal-positive senescent cells are predominantly distributed around necrotic/hypoxic regions, and that hypoxic treatment in vitro can induce irreversible cellular senescence [103]. Furthermore, DNA damage arising from endogenous biochemical processes, particularly DNA-protein crosslinks (DPCs), may also play a critical role in tumor cell senescence. Numerous endogenous metabolites within cells can induce DPCs formation via non-enzymatic pathways, with reactive aldehydes serving as representative examples [104]. Recent studies have further revealed that DPCs accumulation activates the cGAS-STING pathway, triggering inflammatory signaling [105]. This not only provides a potential mechanistic basis for cytokine-induced paracrine senescence in tumor cells but also suggests that endogenous replicative stressors may represent another important driver of senescence in non-therapeutic contexts. Collectively, in the absence of exogenous therapeutic intervention, the diverse endogenous stresses encountered by tumor cells may converge to constitute a complex and interconnected network that orchestrates senescence induction.

TIS: hidden risks in cancer recurrence

Initially, the scientific community was cautious about the possibility that “senescence is not a permanently growth-arrested state,” primarily due to the long-held belief that senescence is irreversible. As a result, when the restoration of proliferative capacity was observed in cells with well-defined senescence characteristics such as SA-β-gal activity in research, terms like “pseudo-senescence” and “senescence-like” emerged in the literature. However, it is crucial to emphasize that the retention of self-renewal capacity by tumor cells does not imply that they do not undergo “authentic” senescence [106]. Substantial evidence has demonstrated that cells can escape from different types of senescence, for instance OIS triggered by H-RasG12V or B-RafV600E can be circumvented through derepression of hTERT gene expression [107]. Replicative senescence (RS) can be escaped via telomere maintenance mechanisms and the loss of signaling components that sustain growth arrest in senescence, such as p53 and p16 [108].

Although cellular senescence functions as a critical tumor-suppressive mechanism by restricting the proliferation of damaged cells, it may paradoxically introduce substantial risk for tumor recurrence in cancer therapy. Current research on senescence reveals that it provides tumor cells with a pathway to evade the potentially lethal effects of anticancer drugs. senescent cancer cells, like dormant cells, reprogram following the anti-cancer treatment to adapt and resist it, survive long term, and then eventually escape to regenerate new cancer cells [109].

TIS as potential triggers for tumor recurrence

The cell cycle arrest plays a central role in the senescence phenotype. As research advances, it has been revealed in various tumor types of somatic, germline, and neural cell origins that TIS tumor cells can re-enter the cell cycle and undergo senescence escape, thereby accelerating tumor recurrence after treatment (Table 1). Senescence-escaped cells exhibit restoration of replicative capacity and disappearance of cytoplasmic lipofuscin aggregates. Furthermore, these cells gradually acquire functional genetic alterations, accompanied by aggressive biological cellular traits such as epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), stemness, and drug resistance.

TIS cell escape across different cancer types

TIS is reversible. The NSCLC cell line H460, the colorectal cancer cell line HCT116, and the breast tumor cell line 4T1 were exposed to etoposide or dox to induce senescence. Senescence-like cells were then enriched by flow cytometry after β-galactosidase staining, and subsequent replating demonstrated that these senescent tumor cells regained proliferative capacity [8]. NSCLC H1299 cells treated with chemotherapy and radiotherapy displayed flattened, enlarged senescent morphology. Within 10–21 days post-chemotherapy, a small subset of senescent cells regained proliferative ability, forming clones 3–4 weeks after drug withdrawal [55]. Human colorectal cancer HCT116 subjected to prolonged dox treatment exhibited TIS features including growth arrest and elevated SA-β-gal activity. Shortly after drug removal, a small and highly proliferative progeny population emerged, with autophagy inhibitor bafilomycin A1 (BAF A1) triggering proliferative potential in senescent cancer cells [127]. In a cisplatin-induced senescence model of p53 wild-type HN30 head and neck cancer cells, senescent HN30 cells regained proliferation 11 days post-treatment. In vivo studies confirmed that cisplatin combined with senolytic agent ABT-263 significantly delayed recurrence of oral squamous cell carcinoma in mice [54]. Furthermore, four breast cancer cell lines (MCF7, T47D, MDA-MB-231, Hs578T) treated with dox showed that 0.00001–0.00004% of cells per line successfully escaped TIS. Single-cell transcriptomics revealed that TIS cells form a distinct cellular population fundamentally different from control and re-proliferating cells [128].

Senescence-escaped cells frequently undergo significant functional alterations. Following temozolomide treatment of glioblastoma multiforme, recurrent tumor cells exhibit heightened malignancy. Although most glioblastoma multiforme cells are eradicated by temozolomide, a subset enters a senescent state characterized by cell cycle arrest. The SASP activates senescence-related signaling pathways, enabling these cells to eventually escape senescence, re-enter the cell cycle, and form aggregates with stem-like characteristics which are evidenced by elevated expression of stemness markers such as CD133, CD150, enhanced colony formation, increased invasiveness, and chemoresistance [114]. Lymphomas that relapse after senescence form more aggressive and malignant tumors when implanted into mice. Additionally, increased expression of stemness-associated genes (e.g., CD34, CD133), elevated aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH) activity, enhanced ABC transporter activity, and activated Wnt signaling in senescent cells determine their propensity for re-proliferation [9]. Thus, the reversibility of TIS in tumor cells may pose a potential risk for tumor recurrence.

Mechanisms underlying tumor recurrence driven by senescent tumor cells

Senescence escape is precisely regulated by multiple pathways, underscoring the complexity of these mechanisms, which encompass aberrant expression of key cell cycle regulators, EMT, acquisition of stemness phenotypes, cell fate conversion, polyploidy formation, and remodeling of immunosuppressive microenvironments (Fig. 2). Collectively, these molecular mechanisms enable senescent cells that persist in a dormant-like state to regain self-renewal capacity and subsequently promote tumor recurrence following therapeutic intervention.

Molecular mechanisms by which senescent tumor cells promote tumor recurrence after treatment. The rapid recurrence of senescent tumor cells after treatment is jointly driven by the aberrant expression of cyclin-related proteins, the depolyploidization of polyploid senescent cells, the EMT phenotypic transformation of senescent cells, the acquisition of stem-like phenotypes in senescent cells, the fate switch from cellular senescence to pyroptosis, and the immunosuppressive microenvironment generated by senescent cells

Senescent cell cycle re-entry and tumor recurrence

Senescence escape of tumor cells post-therapy serves as a primary driver of tumor recurrence. A hallmark of cellular senescence is cell cycle arrest, and true senescence escape necessitates overcoming this barrier. Notably, cell cycle re-entry in senescent cells often requires multidimensional synergistic actions, encompassing inactivation of cell cycle inhibitors, overexpression of CDKs, nucleolar dysfunction, and epigenetic dysregulation. The precise regulation of these mechanisms may constitute critical factors promoting tumor recurrence following chemotherapy.

The inactivation of cell cycle inhibitors in senescent cells represents one of the escape pathways from cellular senescence. During cellular senescence, the expression of key cell cycle inhibitors such as p53 and p16 is significantly elevated. Prior studies have identified p53 and p16 as critical barriers to senescence reversal [55]. Multiple lines of evidence demonstrate that genetic silencing of p53, a key mediator of cellular senescence, leads to the emergence of senescence-escaped cells exhibiting significantly enhanced tumor-initiating potential. For instance, in adriamycin (ADR)-induced lymphoma cell senescence models, p53 knockdown enables regrowth of senescent tumor cells [9, 129, 130]. During dox-induced colorectal cancer senescence, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) derived from colorectal tumors were found to promote senescence escape through the p53-p21 pathway [131]. Additionally, the inactivation of PTEN or p16 leads to the escape of melanoma cells undergoing BRAF oncogene-induced senescence [132]. In dox and SN38-induced breast and colorectal cancer senescence models, anterior gradient protein 2 (AGR2) facilitates post-chemotherapy senescence escape. Notably, intratumoral AGR2 expression is inversely correlated with the senescence marker p16 [123].

CDKs serve as the core molecular machinery regulating cell cycle progression. Among them, CDK1, encoded by the CDC2 gene, plays an indispensable role in G2/M phase transition and mitotic progression. Studies have demonstrated that in chemotherapy-induced senescent NSCLC H1299 cells, the emergence of senescence escape is accompanied by aberrant overexpression of CDK1. Application of CDK1 kinase inhibitors or knockdown of CDK1 expression via small interfering RNA significantly suppresses senescence escape; conversely, ectopic overexpression of CDK1 promotes senescence escape [55]. Similarly, in temozolomide-induced glioma cell senescence models, senescence escape is closely associated with activation of the CDK1/survivin signaling pathway, and CDK1 overexpression markedly enhances the clonogenic capacity of escaped cells [116]. CDK4, as a key protein regulating the transition from the G2/M phase to the G1 phase, experimental evidence reveals that the Cyclin D1-CDK4 complex plays a crucial role in the escape from chemotherapy-induced senescence in colorectal cancer triggered by SN38 and in breast cancer induced by dox. Moreover, the CDK4-specific inhibitor palbociclib or CDK4 siRNA can effectively block this chemotherapy-induced senescence escape phenomenon [121]. Furthermore, in breast cancer resistance models, combined inhibition of CDK2 and CDK4/6 successfully overcomes palbociclib resistance by reinforcing senescence programs [133]. Collectively, these findings establish that CDK family members act as pivotal drivers enabling senescent cells to breach cell cycle arrest and achieve escape.

Nucleolar proteins (e.g., NML, TIP5) and nuclear transcription factors (e.g., SirT1, E2F1) are intricately linked to cell cycle regulation through complex molecular networks. In dox induced senescence models of NSCLC A549 cells, deficiency in nucleolar transcription repression may accelerate tumor recurrence after chemotherapy. TIS promotes the interaction between nucleolar protein NML and SirT1, where the NML complex facilitates rDNA heterochromatin formation and represses rRNA transcription, thereby participating in senescence regulation. In senescent cells, depletion of NML reduces heterochromatin markers H3K9me3 and H3K27me3 at rDNA and E2F1 target promoters, concomitantly increasing rRNA transcription and enhancing the frequency of cell cycle re-entry. Similarly, depletion of nucleolar transcription repressor factor TIP5 promotes senescence escape [134]. These findings underscore the critical regulatory role of nucleolar dysfunction and precise epigenetic control in tumor cell cycle re-entry.

In addition, recent studies have indicated that Myc gene expression may serve as a critical regulatory factor for the re-entry of TIS tumor cells into the cell cycle. Taking breast cancer as an example, treatment with the CDK4/6 inhibitor palbociclib alone, or combined treatment with the MEK inhibitor trametinib and a CDK4/6 inhibitor, can effectively induce tumor cell senescence. However, the senescent fates induced by these two regimens are fundamentally different: upon palbociclib monotherapy washout, SA-β-gal activity was significantly reduced and tumor cells resumed proliferative capacity, suggesting that the senescence state is reversible; in contrast, combined palbociclib and trametinib treatment drove tumor cells into an irreversible senescent state. Mechanistic studies have revealed that the combined treatment activates GSK3β, which subsequently phosphorylates Myc at threonine 58 (T58), thereby reducing Myc stability and blocking cell cycle re-entry in senescent cells. Nevertheless, in breast cancer, osteosarcoma, and oral squamous cell carcinoma, forced expression of a phosphorylation-deficient Myc T58A mutant, or direct treatment with a GSK3β inhibitor under combined drug conditions, enabled the majority of senescent cells to reactivate CDK2 and resume cell cycle progression. This conclusion has been further validated across multiple senescence models: in breast cancer cells rendered senescent by γ-irradiation, the DNA-damaging agent dox, or hydrogen peroxide treatment, GSK3β inhibition similarly promoted cell cycle re-entry of senescent cells through Myc stabilization [118]. Collectively, the expression level of Myc protein may represent a core determinant of whether senescent tumor cells can re-enter the cell cycle.

EMT in senescent cells and tumor recurrence

EMT refers to a phenomenon where polarized epithelial cells lose their epithelial characteristics and acquire mesenchymal traits under specific physiological and pathological conditions. Tumors undergoing EMT develop resistance to chemotherapy and targeted drugs, leading to tumor recurrence and metastasis. In oncology, EMT plays a critical role in metastatic dissemination by enabling cancer cells to detach from the primary tumor and colonize distant secondary sites. Cellular senescence, conversely, acts as a barrier to halt oncogenic progression and suppress metastatic phenotypes. Thus, senescence and EMT appear to be opposing processes. However, recent evidence has revealed that they share key signaling pathways, tightly coupling these two processes [135].

Cellular senescence and EMT may interact to form a dynamic cellular regulatory network. As a tumor suppressor gene, Rb knockout leads to a significant increase in the expression of the senescence marker p16. Rb inactivation in breast cancer cell lines induces mesenchymal phenotype development via ZEB proteins [136]. Similarly, bone morphogenetic protein 2 (BMP-2) promotes breast cancer stem cell formation and enhances EMT signaling pathway in both breast cancer cell lines and clinical samples by activating the ubiquitination pathway to degrade the Rb protein [137]. Given Rb’s central regulatory role in cellular senescence, these findings establish a linear regulatory axis linking Rb, senescence, and EMT. Rb participates in maintaining senescent phenotypes while potentially regulating the phenotypic conversion capacity of senescent cells through EMT-related signaling pathways [135, 138]. Additionally, glucose-regulated protein 78 (GRP78) modulates cisplatin-induced ovarian cancer cell senescence sensitivity [139]. In cisplatin-induced lung adenocarcinoma senescence models, A549 senescent cells with high GRP78 expression re-enter the cell cycle after drug withdrawal [140]. In glioblastoma, ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme E2T (UBE2T) promotes cellular invasion and migration by stabilizing GRP78 and regulating EMT [141]. GRP78-related studies further tightly couple cellular senescence with EMT phenotypic transitions.

Additionally, a hallmark feature of senescent cells is the secretion of SASP factors. Studies demonstrate that SASP factors can autocrine and paracrine reinforce EMT characteristics and stemness phenotypes in tumor cells [142, 97]. Chronic exposure of breast cancer cells to senescence-associated cytokines IL6 or IL8 induces the emergence of senescent cells and significantly elevates mesenchymal markers (e.g., ZEB1, TWIST-1, TWIST-2) and stemness marker CD44 expression, thereby enhancing tumor invasiveness [97]. Conditioned medium from senescent MPM cells triggers EMT-like clone generation and chemoresistant subpopulation formation with elevated ALDH expression [143]. Thus, the regulatory role of SASP factors on EMT phenotypes provides further supportive evidence for the potential phenotypic transition of senescent cells toward an EMT-like state.

Multiple tumor cells exhibit enhanced invasiveness and distal metastasis upon recurrence, with EMT serving as a critical determinant of invasive phenotype acquisition. In nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) radiotherapy, radioresistant cancer cells concurrently display senescent and EMT phenotypes [144]. In the CDC6-mediated OIS model, cells escaping senescence manifest pronounced EMT characteristics [144]. Local recurrence frequently occurs in locally advanced cervical cancer (LACC) after radiotherapy, with studies explicitly demonstrating that radiotherapy-associated cellular senescence and EMT alterations drive disease recurrence [145]. Evolutionary analysis of colorectal cancer progression toward higher metastatic phenotypes further reveals correlations between cellular senescence and progressive phenotypic transformation of cancer cells. The evolutionary trajectory includes two types of senescent tumor cells (Type I and Type II), where EMT-phenotype Type II senescent tumor cells express metastasis-related genes such as LAMC2 and matrix metalloproteinase 7 (MMP7), exhibiting significantly higher metastatic potential [146]. Collectively, clinical reports on increased invasiveness during tumor recurrence further support that senescent cells with EMT phenotypes may serve as critical sources for post-therapy senescence escape.

Cellular senescence is not an isolated event decoupled from time; its initiation and progression are profoundly regulated by the circadian rhythm network. At the level of cellular senescence, oxidative stress-induced senescent cells have been demonstrated to exhibit significant circadian rhythm disturbances, including period prolongation, phase delay, reduced oscillation amplitude, and dysfunction of core clock proteins [147]. From a mechanistic perspective, deletion of the core clock gene BMAL1 leads to marked upregulation of p21 expression, driving cells into irreversible cell cycle arrest. More directly, BMAL1 protein can bind to the p53 promoter region and regulate its downstream transcriptional program [148]. Collectively, these lines of evidence indicate that loss of circadian function coordinately promotes the initiation of senescence at multiple nodes [149]. At the level of EMT regulation, the circadian network also serves as a critical gatekeeper. The clock-controlled genes CLOCK, BMAL1, PER2, and Timeless play indispensable roles in maintaining epithelial-mesenchymal homeostasis. Specifically, in osteosarcoma, knockdown of CLOCK or BMAL1 upregulates the epithelial marker CDH1 while downregulating mesenchymal markers CDH2, ZEB1, and VIM [150]; under hypoxic conditions, loss of PER2 upregulates OCT1-mediated EMT gene expression and exacerbates tumor malignancy [151]; and Timeless knockout drives EMT progression in colorectal cancer through a ZEB1-dependent mechanism [152]. At the clinical translation level, circadian rhythm disruption has been shown to promote tumor progression by remodeling the phenotypic plasticity of cancer cells, including driving epithelial-mesenchymal transition, aberrant dedifferentiation, and cancer stem cell (CSC) generation. Of particular note, in prostate cancer, circadian rhythm-related gene signatures have been developed as important biomarkers for predicting recurrence risk and immunotherapy response, further corroborating the close association between circadian status and tumor outcomes [153]. Collectively, the circadian network simultaneously regulates the initiation of senescence programs and the maintenance of EMT phenotypes. This implies that the phenotypic stability of EMT⁺ senescent cells, which remain after chemotherapy as a critical seed subpopulation for tumor recurrence, may be partially dependent on the regulation of the host circadian network. Therefore, modulation of circadian rhythms may attenuate the driving forces of tumor recurrence and offer a novel therapeutic strategy to overcome post-chemotherapy relapse.

Senescence-associated stemness phenotype and tumor recurrence

Persistent senescent tumor cells following chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy may exhibit a series of characteristics. After receiving anticancer therapy, senescent tumor cells can undergo reprogramming through increased expression of intracellular stemness genes (such as OCT4 and NANOG) or cell surface stem cell-related markers (such as EpCAM and CD44), or corresponding transcriptional regulation, thereby making senescent tumor cells acquire stemness characteristics [154]. Research has revealed that senescence-associated stemness promotes tumor recurrence after chemotherapy [154, 155].

Recent studies have revealed shared signaling pathways between cellular senescence and the maintenance of cancer stemness. TIS tumor cells exhibit increased expression of stemness-related genes, such as NANOG, CD44, and CD133 [156]. Research has found that lung cancer cells surviving three radiotherapy sessions strongly express stem cell-related signals (e.g., CD44, CD133, OCT4), correlated with elevated senescence biomarker SA-β-gal staining [157]. Following gemcitabine treatment, the ALDH⁺ subpopulation of senescent pancreatic cancer cells significantly expands [158]. Additionally, Milanovic et al. provided further evidence for the relationship between cancer senescence and stemness. The authors observed that chemotherapy-induced senescent tumor cells in murine lymphoma, human cancer cell lines, and patient samples displayed enhanced expression of adult tissue stem cell signatures, activation of the Wnt signaling pathway, and elevated levels of diverse stemness markers. These changes were absent in non-senescent tumor cells [9]. Following the partial inactivation of essential senescence genes, which permits senescent tumor cells to escape from senescence, these senescent tumor cells demonstrate a significantly enhanced tumor-initiating potential when compared to cell populations that have never undergone senescence. This observation suggests that the subpopulation of senescent cells that acquire stemness features may play a pivotal role in driving the escape from senescence [9].

Jun-ichi Abe et al. discovered that macrophages upregulate the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) via the ERK5-NRF2 axis to promote the production of SASP factors and the acquisition of senescence-associated stemness phenotype [159]. In cisplatin-induced lung cancer, altered localization of GRP78 contributes to the acquisition of stemness phenotype in senescent lung cancer cells. As a key factor primarily localized in the ER, GRP78 is increasingly accumulated on the cell membrane during TIS by anchoring to the transmembrane protein MTJ1/HTJ1. Following translocation, GRP78 activates the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, leading to increased expression of stemness genes CD44, CD133, Nanog, Oct4 and Sox2 in senescent cells [160]. These findings further elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the acquisition of stemness features by senescent tumor cells.

Senescence-associated stemness traits serve as a critical driver of tumor recurrence following treatment. Our previous research revealed that arginine methyltransferase PRMT5 promotes the senescence-associated stemness phenotype in SCLC post-chemotherapy. Notably, when chemotherapy is combined with the PRMT5 inhibitor GSK3326595, SCLC recurrence is significantly suppressed [7]. In a single-cell sequencing study of paired acute myeloid leukemia (AML) patients before and after chemotherapy, post-chemotherapy relapse samples were enriched with numerous stemness-related signatures compared to tumor cells at 3 weeks post-TIS, suggesting that at least some leukemia stem cells in relapsed AML patients may originate from non-CSC transformation [161]. Critically, the gain of senescence-associated stemness traits retained after senescence escape has been validated in samples from patients with relapsed diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) [9]. To support these findings, AML cells treated with chemotherapeutic agents were observed to enter a senescence-like adaptive state that mimics a diapause-like condition, enabling resistance to chemotherapy-induced genotoxic stress. Upon exiting this state, these post-senescent AML cells re-emerge as recurrent AML cells with enhanced stem cell potential [161]. Collectively, these results demonstrate that senescence-associated stemness is a cell-autonomous property that exerts its highly aggressive growth potential when escaping growth arrest, and is enriched in tumors of relapsed patients.

Polyploid senescent cells and tumor recurrence

Polyploid cells arise through cell fusion and endoreplication, with polyploidy representing a state of increased chromosomal content [20]. During cancer treatment, following genotoxic stress induced by chemotherapy or radiotherapy, cells will arrest at the G2/M checkpoint for several days, which triggers endoreduplication and ultimately leads to the formation of polyploid cells commonly termed polyploid giant cancer cells (PGCCs). PGCCs are characterized by a single enlarged nucleus or multiple nuclei. As a hallmark of chromosomal instability and diversity in tumors, polyploid giant cancer cells may exacerbate the complexity of tumor genetic architecture and development. The critical roles of PGCCs in genomic instability, tumorigenesis, metastasis, drug resistance, and post-therapy tumor recurrence are increasingly recognized [162].

Polyploidization is a common phenomenon in TIS tumor cells, primarily driven by DNA-damaging agents. When NSCLC H1299 cells are exposed to the topoisomerase I inhibitor camptothecin, it induces both senescence and polyploidy [163]. Chemotherapy treatments in human colon cancer cell lines HCT116 and SW480 using specific drugs 5-fluorouracil, irinotecan or dox consistently induce tumor cell senescence, characterized by significant increases in cell size, polyploidization, and enhanced SA-β-gal activity [124]. Temozolomide, an alkylating agent used as first-line therapy for glioma, induces cellular senescence by causing a primary DNA lesion O6-methylguanine. Mechanistic studies reveal that temozolomide does not activate the DREAM complex but instead induces incomplete G2 arrest, allowing cells to re-enter S phase, trigger endoreplication, and ultimately form polyploid giant cells, which provides an explanation for how anticancer drugs induce polyploidization and the giant cell phenotype in senescent cells [164].

The reversibility of senescent cell cycle arrest is typically coupled with polyploidization and depolyploidization in senescent cells. Research shows that PGCCs sustain self-renewal through endoreplication and undergo depolyploidization via nuclear budding or asymmetric nuclear division; these small daughter nuclei subsequently acquire cytoplasm, detach from the giant mother cell, and regain mitotic capacity [165]. In well-differentiated rat colon cancer cells, cisplatin-induced polyploidy is resolved through asymmetric division, after which tumor cells recover mitotic ability following senescence-associated growth arrest. These findings suggest that depolyploidization is a key mechanism for giant multinucleated senescent cells to escape senescent growth arrest [166, 167]. MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells undergo cellular senescence and polyploidization after dox pulse treatment. Polyploid senescent cells depolyploidize via nuclear budding, re-initiate the mitotic cycle, and enable senescence escape in breast cancer cells [52]. Dox pulse treatment induces senescence in HCT116 cells with increased ROS production. While the antioxidant Trolox reduces ROS levels to protect cells from polyploidization, it fails to prevent cellular senescence. Intriguingly, reduced ROS levels prevent senescence escape [168]. Ovarian cancer cells exposed to carboplatin exhibit reversible senescent growth arrest associated with a high proportion of polyploid cells [167]. Camptothecin-induced senescent H1299 cells frequently display polyploidy and spontaneous escape [163]. Polyploidization serves as a key factor in promoting the reversibility of senescent cells. Senescent cells that have not undergone polyploidization may be unable to evade the fate of senescence and death, nor can they re-enter the mitotic cycle [169].

Under chemotherapy-induced stress, the generation of new tumor-initiating cells may drive disease recurrence. Relapsed tumor cells often exhibit enhanced invasiveness and significantly elevated expression of stemness-related genes [170]. PGCCs commonly display mixed gene expression programs associated with EMT, enabling reversible switching between epithelial and mesenchymal states to augment migratory and invasive capacities. In breast cancer models, PGCCs-enriched populations demonstrate markedly higher invasiveness and migratory ability, accompanied by elevated expression of metastasis-related markers such as N-cadherin and vimentin [171, 172]. PGCCs are classified as tumor-initiating cells that express CSC-associated markers including CD24, CD44, CD133, ALDH, and Ep-CAM [173]. In paclitaxel-treated ovarian cancer cells, PGCC formation correlates with senescence activation, while daughter cell budding associates with senescence escape. PGCCs exhibit temporally and spatially dependent activation of embryonic stem cell markers including OCT4, NANOG, SOX2, and SSEA1 [174]. Carboplatin-induced senescent ovarian cancer cells, after depolyploidization, re-enter cell division with elevated expression of stem cell markers [167]. Furthermore, polyploid states are closely linked to activation of DDR pathways. PGCCs demonstrate enhanced genotoxic stress tolerance or augmented DNA repair capabilities, increasing their survival likelihood following DNA damage [175]. Consequently, polyploid senescent cells are widely recognized as critical cellular sources driving tumor recurrence and disease progression [176].

Fate conversion of senescent cells and tumor recurrence

Solid tumors are highly complex tissues comprising highly heterogeneous tumor cells. During tumorigenesis, molecular or genetic alterations drive differences in growth rates, invasive capacities, and drug sensitivities among distinct tumor cell populations. Such tumor heterogeneity leads to varied responses of tumor cells to drug treatment. While apoptosis and programmed cell death represent the desired outcomes following drug treatment, tumor cells may alternatively undergo cellular senescence, pyroptosis, ferroptosis, autophagy, or other distinct fate trajectories post-therapy [177].

Different concentrations of chemotherapeutic agents dictate distinct cellular fate trajectories, with potential crosstalk between these pathways. Etoposide and cisplatin can induce both tumor cell senescence, trigger necroptosis or pyroptosis. For instance, 5µM cisplatin treatment in NSCLC cell lines H460 and A549 significantly elevates SA-β-gal activity, driving cells into senescence [134]. At 20µM cisplatin, A549 cells exhibit pyroptotic bubble formation, while 60µM cisplatin activates both intrinsic and extrinsic apoptotic pathways [178]. Similarly, in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), low-dose cisplatin induces senescence, whereas high-dose cisplatin triggers lytic cell death [179, 180]. Cellular fate decisions are interdependent; for example, ferroptosis modulates telomere function to influence senescence progression, and senescence-associated ROS accumulation triggers lipid peroxidation in phospholipid bilayers, ultimately leading to ferroptosis [181]. Furthermore, unique genetic backgrounds of tumor cells may drive fate switching. Studies reveal that YAP inactivation in non-neuroendocrine SCLC shifts cisplatin- or etoposide-induced cell death from apoptosis to pyroptosis [182].

Our team’s latest research reveals that fate conversion of senescent cells mediates the recurrence process of tumor cells. Post-chemotherapy senescent SCLC cells undergo fate conversion toward the inflammatory cell death modality pyroptosis, thereby promoting tumor recurrence. Post-chemotherapy SCLC is accompanied by the generation of abundant CCF. Senescent cells bearing CCF stabilize the pyroptosis-executing protein GSDMD, mediating pyroptosis in senescent tumor cells and releasing substantial inflammatory factors, which process promotes the acquisition of stemness phenotypes in residual CCF-free senescent cells, ultimately driving SCLC recurrence post-chemotherapy [126]. Additionally, in recurrent-prone breast cancer, cell senescence and pyroptosis are similarly detected following treatment with the chemotherapeutic agent dox [183]. In tumor radiotherapy, radiation induces senescence in lung cancer cells to halt their proliferation, while concurrently activating the pyroptosis-related pathway Caspase-1-GSDMD [184]. Therefore, investigating the universality of senescence-to-pyroptosis fate conversion across different tumor types and therapeutic modalities may provide therapeutic strategies to suppress the resurgence of senescent cells and delay tumor recurrence.

Immune evasion of senescent cells and tumor recurrence

Numerous studies have revealed that senescent cells exert tumor-promoting effects by evading immune surveillance, and the immunosuppressive microenvironment generated by senescent cells provides a favorable niche for tumor recurrence [185]. Chemotherapy-induced senescence promotes the accumulation of immune cells within the tumor microenvironment by upregulating the expression of activating ligands on the surface of cancer cells. Low-dose chemotherapy induces senescence in neuroblastoma cells, and these senescent tumor cells promote the shedding of NKG2D ligands MHC class I chain-related molecules A/B (MICA/B) via the MALAT1/miR-92a-3p/ADAM10 axis, thereby facilitating the formation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment and promoting immune evasion [185]. This study reveals a novel mechanism by which chemotherapy-induced senescent tumor cells evade NK cell immune surveillance.

Additionally, during ionizing radiation-induced senescence in melanoma cells, senescent cancer cells increase programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) levels by enhancing transcription and glycosylation processes. Ribophorin 1 (RPN1) serves as a key regulator of PD-L1 glycosylation during cancer senescence. Depletion of RPN1 reduces elevated PD-L1 levels through the endoplasmic reticulum-lysosome associated degradation pathway. Moreover, therapeutic targeting of RPN1 or anti-PD-1 treatment decreases the abundance of senescent cancer cells in irradiated tumors, activates cytotoxic T lymphocytes, and suppresses cancer recurrence [186]. These findings provide critical insights into how senescent cancer cells evade T cell-mediated immunity and promote cancer recurrence following treatment.

SASP factors secreted by senescent cells can promote immunosuppressive effects. In PTEN-deficient prostate tumors treated with docetaxel, senescent cells exhibit persistent Jak2/Stat3 activation, inducing immunosuppressive SASP that leads to the accumulation of immunosuppressive myeloid cells and concurrent reduction of CD4+, CD8+, and NK cells [187]. Genetic or pharmacological inhibition of Jak2/Stat3 triggers anti-tumor immune responses and re-sensitizes tumors to docetaxel [187]. Amphiregulin, a soluble factor secreted via SASP, is activated through EGFR-mediated pathways and induces PD-L1 expression in prostate cancer cells, conferring resistance to immune surveillance [188]. In colorectal cancer, senescent tumor cells driven by oxidative stress upregulate secretion of CXCL12 and CSF1. Elevated CXCL12 levels reduce CXCR4 expression on CD8 T cells and inhibit their chemotactic migration. CSF1 promotes M2 macrophage differentiation, further impairing CD8 T cell activation [189]. Another immunosuppressive SASP factor, Galectin-9, is secreted by temozolomide-induced senescent melanoma cells via mitofusin 1 (Mfn1). Silencing Mfn1 in melanoma enhances tumor immune infiltration and improves chemotherapy efficacy [190]. Additionally, Galectin-9 has been shown to reduce cytotoxicity and cytokine production in NK cells [191], promote apoptosis of CD4+, CD8+ T cells, and CD14+ monocytes [192, 193], and increase regulatory T cells (Tregs) [194], T helper 2 cells (Th2), and M2 macrophages [195], thereby weakening anti-tumor immune responses.

The regulatory relationship between senescent cells and tumor immunity is complex, as the SASP can not only induce immunosuppression but also promote immune-mediated clearance. SASP factors secreted by senescent cells contribute to the remodeling of the tumor microenvironment (TME). In particular, through TME remodeling, the SASP enhances immune infiltration, recruits NK cells and macrophages, promotes immune-mediated cytotoxicity, and eliminates TIS cells or malignant cells [196]. Alisertib, an AURKA inhibitor, induces senescence in melanoma cells, which triggers the NF-κB signaling pathway, activates the SASP, and recruits neutrophils and macrophages. In vivo depletion of macrophages impairs immune surveillance and reduces the antitumor efficacy [71]. Furthermore, senescent human melanocytes have been shown to upregulate the expression of MHC class II antigen-presenting machinery in response to secreted IL-1β, thereby enhancing T cell proliferation in vivo, which is closely associated with a favorable prognosis in melanoma patients [197]. In HCC, senescent tumor cells secrete CCL2, which induces NKG2D ligand expression on tumor cells and activates NK cell-mediated clearance of senescent tumor cells [198]. Dexamethasone-induced senescence in lung adenocarcinoma cells leads to the release of chemokines including CCL2, CCL4, CXCL1, and CXCL2, which attract T cells and NK cells to the tumor site [199]. In the K-RASG12D/p53 null lung cancer model, senescence induced by the MEK inhibitor trametinib or the CDK4/6 inhibitor palbociclib upregulates the immunomodulatory molecule ICAM-1 and the SASP component TNF-α through NF-κB activation, thereby promoting NK cell-mediated tumor surveillance and exerting antitumor effects [200]. The NRAS-driven HCC model further supports the role of the SASP in senescence-mediated tumor suppression, in which blockade of cGAS-STING-mediated pro-inflammatory SASP production impairs immune surveillance-mediated clearance of NRASG12V-expressing hepatocytes, leading to intrahepatic tumorigenesis [201].

The complex effects of the SASP on tumors exhibit significant spatiotemporal and cell type-dependent specificity, and the balance between its antitumor and protumor activities depends on the interplay of three key dimensions. With respect to the temporal window, the SASP is not a static phenotype but rather undergoes a dynamic evolution from an “initial acute phase” to a “late chronic phase” [202, 203]: the early-phase SASP is dominated by TGF-β, displaying fibrotic and growth-inhibitory features that favor restriction of tumor outgrowth; in contrast, the late-phase SASP, driven by persistently activated NF-κB and cGAS-STING pathways, shifts toward a pro-inflammatory and matrix-degrading phenotype characterized by IL-6, IL-8, and MMPs, thereby promoting immune evasion and metastasis. The composition of SASP components largely determines the trajectory of tumor progression [196, 203]. p53 serves as a critical “gatekeeper” in this temporal switch; functional p53 prevents the transition to a late-stage malignant SASP by inhibiting NF-κB activity [204], whereas p53 loss accelerates this process, enabling surviving tumor cells to rapidly secrete a fully protumorigenic secretome. This further explains why tumor cells harboring p53 mutations or deletions, such as SCLC, are prone to relapse after chemotherapy [33]. The cGAS-STING pathway further exemplifies this temporal paradox: acute DNA damage sensing exerts antitumor effects by recruiting cytotoxic T cells via type I interferons, whereas chronic persistent signaling preferentially activates the non-canonical NF-κB pathway, leading to the secretion of SASP factors such as IL-6 and TNF-α, which drive an immunosuppressive microenvironment [205, 206].

With respect to cell type and tissue context, the same senescence-inducing stimulus can elicit markedly different SASP outputs and immune outcomes across distinct tissues. In KRASG12D-mutant, p53-null lung adenocarcinoma, T/P TIS activates an NF-κB-driven SASP that triggers NK cell-mediated tumor clearance [200]; however, in genetically similar KRASG12D-mutant, p53-null pancreatic cancer, T/P therapy induces a SASP enriched in proangiogenic factors that, although failing to activate NK cell responses, indirectly promotes T cell infiltration through vascular remodeling [207]. Moreover, when the same tumor cell line is transplanted into different tissue sites in mice and subjected to the same senescence-inducing treatment, the SASP profile undergoes significant alterations [208]. Even within the same tumor, epithelial cells and stromal fibroblasts exhibit differences in their SASP spectra [25, 209–213]; the former may secrete growth-inhibitory factors such as IL-33 [214], whereas the latter tend to establish a profibrotic microenvironment through TGF-β [203]. Single-cell transcriptomic analyses have further revealed that SASP gene expression itself is markedly heterogeneous within senescent cell populations, suggesting that distinct subpopulations contribute differentially to the overall SASP landscape [215].

With respect to therapeutic context, p53 status is a core determinant of SASP “flavor” [203], and different senogenic agents, as well as combination strategies with epigenetic modulators (e.g., EZH2 inhibitors combined with senogenic agents), can differentially reshape SASP composition, thereby altering its regulatory direction toward NK cells, T cells, and stromal components within the tumor microenvironment [216]. Collectively, the dual nature of the SASP provides a window for therapeutic intervention: identifying the specific SASP subtypes activated under particular therapy-tumor contexts, along with their temporal evolution trajectories, is a prerequisite for precisely exploiting or targeting the SASP to optimize therapeutic responses in oncology.

Targeted intervention of senescent tumor cells

Current anticancer strategies primarily rely on chemotherapy or radiotherapy, aiming to eliminate proliferating cancer cells. However, these therapies may also promote the senescence of surviving cancer cells, driving their evolution into more resistant subpopulations, thereby posing therapeutic risks for tumor recurrence, metastasis, and adverse clinical outcomes. Based on this, cancer treatment strategies are continuously being updated, such as combining cancer cell senescence induction with targeted clearance of senescent cells, or targeted therapy against SASP factors (Fig. 3).

Targeted therapy against senescent tumor cells. Two strategies to mitigate the detrimental effects of senescent cells are senolytics and senomorphic drugs targeting the secretion of SASP factors by senescent cells. Among them, senolytics target the BCL-2/XL/W family to promote apoptosis in senescent cells, while senomorphic drugs primarily alleviate the harmful impacts of SASP by inhibiting individual SASP factors or regulating core SASP signaling molecules

Direct elimination of senescent cells by senolytic agents

Senescent cells induced by DNA-damaging therapies survive through enhanced apoptotic resistance, with multiple pro-survival proteins and pathways being explored as potential targets for senolytics. ABT-263, one of the most extensively studied senolytics, is a BH3 mimetic targeting anti-apoptotic proteins BCL-2/BCL-XL/BCL-W, which has been validated to eliminate residual cancer cells in senescent states, such as inducing widespread and rapid apoptosis in dox-treated senescent breast cancer cells [217]. Preclinical evidence demonstrates that senolytic agents reduce tumor recurrence by specifically or preferentially killing senescent cells. ABT-263 selectively eliminates ionizing radiation-induced senescent glioblastoma cells in vivo, mitigating tumor recurrence [218]. In p16-3MR mice, ABT-263 clears dox-induced senescent cells. in the MMTV-PyMT orthotopic breast cancer model, tumor resection followed by dox and ABT-263 combination therapy delays recurrence and metastasis [5]. Additionally, ABT-263 conjugation with galactose generates a potent prodrug (Nav-Gal), which is preferentially activated by SA-β-gal to selectively induce senescent cell apoptosis. Nav-Gal exhibits cytotoxicity against chemotherapy-induced senescent A549 lung cancer cells, and its combination with cisplatin in vivo eliminates senescent lung cancer cells while significantly suppressing tumor growth [219]. Another BH3 mimetic, ABT-737, also demonstrates senolytic activity by dissolving gemcitabine-induced senescent pancreatic cancer cells, with gemcitabine and ABT-737 combination showing superior tumor growth inhibition compared to monotherapy in pancreatic cancer models [220]. A recent study identified Proteolysis Targeting Chimera (PROTAC) 753b, a dual BCL-2/BCL-XL degrader, that specifically clears senescent cells, effectively reducing senescence-driven chronic inflammation while minimizing damage to normal cells [221].

Numerous natural products and their derivatives exhibit potential as senolytic agents. GL-V9 (5-hydroxy-8-methoxy-7-[4-(pyrrolidin-1-yl)butoxy]-4 H-chromen-4-one), a newly synthesized flavonoid derived from wogonin (5,7-dihydroxy-8-methoxy-2-phenyl-4 H-1-benzopyran-4-one), has been demonstrated to eliminate senescent breast cancer cells induced by dox, SN-38, and paclitaxel through a ROS-dependent mechanism. Additionally, GL-V9 reduces senescence-associated features in spontaneous mammary tumors in mice treated with epirubicin [222]. The combination of dasatinib, a broad-spectrum tyrosine kinase receptor inhibitor, and quercetin, a flavonoid with broad activity that inhibits multiple kinases including SRC and PI3K, has been extensively studied as a senolytic combination therapy. Chemotherapeutic agents such as cisplatin and dox promote the proliferation of dormant breast cancer cells by inducing fibroblast senescence. However, the combination of dasatinib, quercetin, and dox enables targeted clearance of senescent fibroblasts, inhibiting the reactivation of dormant breast cancer cells after treatment and ultimately suppressing metastatic recurrence. In the etoposide-induced senescence model of breast cancer cells (MDA-MB-231), quercetin alone shows limited efficacy in clearing senescent cells. In contrast, its structural derivative QD3 exhibits favorable properties in targeted clearance of senescent tumor cells [223, 224]. Fisetin, a structurally similar flavonoid to quercetin, has been identified as a senolytic agent capable of eliminating senescent cells. Curcumin induces senescence in colorectal cancer HCT116 cells via DNA damage. Through senescence activity assays and quantification of apoptotic cell proportions, both the flavonoid components fisetin and quercetin are found to effectively clear senescent cancer cells [225].

Additionally, HSP90 inhibitors, including AT13387, Ganetespib, and 17-desmethoxy-17-N, N-dimethylaminoethyl amino geldanamycin, also exhibit potent senolytic activity [226]. FOXO4-DRI, a peptide designed to disrupt p53-FOXO4 interactions, selectively induces apoptosis in senescent cells. Studies show that FOXO4-DRI specifically triggers apoptotic clearance of senescent-like cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) following radiotherapy, thereby enhancing radiosensitivity and therapeutic efficacy in NSCLC [227]. The mTOR inhibitor AZD8055 demonstrates targeted clearance potential against senescent cancer cells induced by CDC7 small-molecule inhibitors [82]. Furthermore, recent studies reveal that conjugated polyunsaturated fatty acids, particularly α-eleostearic acid and its methyl ester derivatives, exploit the ferroptosis vulnerability of senescent cells to selectively induce ferroptotic cell death, facilitating senescent cell clearance [228].

Targeted therapy for SASP factors secreted by senescent cells

Senescent cells secreting SASP factors not only promote EMT phenotype and stemness characteristics in senescent cells but also create a conducive microenvironment for tumor recurrence. Thus, targeting the secretion of SASP factors by senescent cells represents an effective strategy to alleviate senescent phenotypes and suppress tumor relapse. Regulatory approaches targeting SASP are termed senomorphics, which primarily mitigate the deleterious effects of SASP by inhibiting individual SASP factors or modulating core signaling molecules of SASP [229].

Among SASP cytokines, IL-1 and IL-6 are pro-inflammatory factors. In the context of senescence, IL-1 and IL-6 promote inflammation and reinforce senescence-associated growth arrest, thereby facilitating the establishment and maintenance of senescent phenotypes. Anakinra and Tocilizumab antagonize IL-1R and IL-6R, respectively, effectively blocking the self-amplifying feedback loops of IL-1 and IL-6 to impede senescence progression. Analysis of patient-derived recurrent cervical cancer samples revealed that radiotherapy-induced senescence and EMT alterations determine recurrence in LACC, while the IL-1R antagonist Anakinra mitigates radiotherapy-induced senescence and EMT marker expression [145]. Furthermore, our previous studies demonstrated that post-chemotherapy SCLC exhibits significant cellular senescence and elevated IL-6 and IL-1β expression. Combined use of Anakinra with cisplatin and etoposide significantly suppresses tumor recurrence [126]. In recurrent lung adenocarcinoma patients, inflammatory pathways including IL-6 signaling are markedly upregulated, contributing to tumor recurrence [230]. PGCCs serve as critical sources for recurrence from senescent tumor cells. In paclitaxel-treated ovarian cancer, PGCC formation in senescent cells correlates with IL-6 activation; Tocilizumab-mediated blockade of the IL-6 feedback loop in PGCCs inhibits both PGCC formation and tumor growth in patient-derived high-grade serous ovarian cancer xenograft models [231].

SASP is regulated by multiple stress-responsive transcription factors and intracellular signaling pathways, including NF-κB, C/EBPβ, mTOR, JAK/STAT, and cGAS-STING pathways [232]. Targeted interventions against these pathways attenuate SASP expression and its downstream pathological effects. Marine-derived trabectedin modulates NF-κB transcriptional activity in senescent tumor cells, inhibiting SASP secretion, promoting apoptotic clearance of senescent cells, and ultimately suppressing tumor recurrence induced by therapy-resistant senescent tumor cells [233]. Flavonoids from Scutellaria baicalensis suppress SASP secretion by interrupting C/EBPβ pathway signaling [234]. Among these strategies, mTOR inhibitors, specifically rapamycin and its analogs, are the most extensively studied. These inhibitors inhibit SASP production by interfering with IL-1α translation, given that IL-1α serves as a key upstream driver of SASP cascades. Rapamycin blocks the pro-tumorigenic effects of senescent fibroblasts on prostate cancer progression in mice by suppressing senescence-associated inflammation [235]. JAK inhibitors (e.g., ruxolitinib, baricitinib) represent another major class of senomorphic agents, disrupting downstream inflammatory signaling induced by SASP components such as IL-6 and IL-8 [236]. Additionally, activation of the cGAS-STING pathway in senescent tumor cells serves as a critical upstream regulator of SASP secretion. For instance, HDAC inhibitor SAHA induces senescence in SCLC with concomitant SASP production, while EZH2 inhibitor EPZ-6438 suppresses SASP secretion by inhibiting cGAS-STING activation, thereby blocking repopulation of TIS tumor cells [99].

Therapeutic limitations of senolytics and senomorphics

Therapeutic limitations of senolytics

Although senolysis can effectively eliminate TIS tumor cells and holds promise for delaying post-treatment tumor recurrence, its inherent limitations and potential risks have become increasingly apparent with advancing research. First, senescence itself is an important defense mechanism of the host: when cellular DNA is damaged, senescence suppresses malignant transformation by preventing damaged cells from proliferating; concurrently, senescent cells play indispensable roles in wound healing and tissue repair [237, 238]. Therefore, indiscriminate elimination of senescent cells may yield unintended consequences. This concern is particularly pertinent given that elderly populations, who are inherently at high risk for cancer, may experience compromised tissue homeostasis and functional integrity upon systemic senescent cell clearance [237, 238].

Second, senolytic strategies lack universality. Currently, there are no universal senescence markers applicable to all tissue types, which fundamentally limits the widespread application of senolytic agents [239]. The responsiveness to senolytic therapy varies markedly across different tumor types: the combination of dasatinib and quercetin (D + Q) with senescence-inducing chemotherapy failed to demonstrate efficacy in HCC, whereas it enhanced the antitumor effects of radiation-induced senescence in melanoma models [240, 241]. At the cellular level, different cancer cell lines also exhibit substantial heterogeneity in their sensitivity to the same senolytic agent. For instance, ABT-263 efficiently eliminates senescent A549 NSCLC cells induced by alisertib or etoposide, but exhibits minimal clearance efficacy against senescent LoVo colorectal cancer cells, a discrepancy that may be attributed to genetic alterations in the BCL-2 family or other pro-apoptotic genes [25]. Several clinical trials have combined ABT-263 with chemotherapy or targeted agents known to induce senescence; however, this strategy has yielded limited efficacy across various solid tumors to date, accompanied by notable side effects including thrombocytopenia and neutropenia [242].

Off-target toxicity represents a core bottleneck for senolytic agents. Senolytics such as BCL-2 family inhibitors do not specifically target tumor cells or senescent cells, but rather act on apoptotic pathways common to all cells, thereby eliciting broad toxicity in patients [243, 244]. Although recently developed galactose-modified nanoparticles and prodrugs, which leverage the elevated β-galactosidase activity in senescent cells for selective drug delivery, may partially overcome off-target toxicity [219, 245–247], the clearance of physiological senescent cells, such as macrophages, megakaryocytes, and osteoprogenitor cells may still induce on-target toxicity that disrupts tissue or organismal homeostasis [248–250]. Given the aforementioned off-target effects and the potential risks associated with systemic senescent cell clearance, the development of senolytic agents capable of specifically targeting senescent tumor cells may represent a safer therapeutic strategy to inhibit tumor recurrence.

Therapeutic limitations of senomorphics

Compared with senolysis, senomorphic agents, which limit protumorigenic inflammation following TIS by eliminating or remodeling the SASP, theoretically offer a superior safety profile. Nevertheless, this strategy also faces multiple challenges. The marked heterogeneity of senotypes limits the universality of this approach. Senescent cells in different tissues, diseases, and microenvironments can form distinctly different senotypes: some are predominantly inflammatory, characterized by robust SASP factor release; others are primarily fibrotic, driving extracellular matrix deposition and tissue stiffening; and still others are metabolically aberrant, affecting lipid metabolism, mitochondrial function, and energy homeostasis. This heterogeneity of senotypes fundamentally restricts the scope of application of senescence-modulating strategies [239].

The impact of SASP inhibition on the immune microenvironment is highly context-dependent. JAK2/STAT3 inhibition has been shown to block immunosuppressive SASP programs, thereby enhancing antitumor immune responses in PTEN-deficient prostate cancer [187]. However, blockade of mTOR, cGAS-STING, or BRD4 signaling pathways may instead suppress antitumor immune surveillance and promote tumor immune evasion [201, 251, 252]. Therefore, the effects of senomorphic agents on the tumor microenvironment are not unidirectional, but rather depend on the specific signaling pathways and tumor contexts, necessitating systematic evaluation in both preclinical and clinical studies.

The narrow dose window introduces uncertainty into therapeutic strategy. Senomorphic agents are highly dose-sensitive: certain agents, such as BET/BRD4 inhibitors, mediate SASP suppression (senomorphic effects) at low doses, whereas at high doses they shift toward senolytic effects. BRD4 inhibitors have also demonstrated dose-limiting toxicities and adverse effects including thrombocytopenia in clinical trials, further narrowing their safe therapeutic window [251, 253–255].

The feasibility of long-term administration and the issue of drug resistance remain unresolved. Unlike senolytic strategies, which can be effective with intermittent dosing, senomorphic agents may require prolonged or even lifelong administration to continuously suppress the inflammatory cascade in senescent cells. The associated cumulative toxicity, reduced patient compliance, and potential development of drug resistance will all constitute critical factors affecting their clinical translation.

Therapeutic strategies targeting senescent tumor cells

Senolytic and senomorphic strategies each possess distinct advantages and limitations in cancer therapy. Senolytic strategies face core challenges including off-target toxicity, heterogeneous responses across tumor types, and unintended clearance of physiological senescent cells. Senomorphic strategies, while offering a relatively superior safety profile, are constrained by senotype heterogeneity, context-dependent immune effects of SASP inhibition, a narrow dose window, and the feasibility challenges of long-term administration. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop next-generation senescence-modulating agents capable of precisely targeting senescent tumor cells (Fig. 4).

PGCCs may represent a key targetable subpopulation

Cell cycle re-entry, EMT, and senescence-associated stemness acquisition represent three key drivers of tumor recurrence. Notably, PGCCs intrinsically possess both stemness and EMT features, and the resumption of mitosis during their depolyploidization precisely corresponds to the critical process of cell cycle re-entry. The marked phenotypic convergence between PGCCs and these three drivers of recurrence strongly suggests that PGCCs may constitute a core subpopulation driving tumor relapse. Multiple lines of evidence support this notion: metformin has been shown to exert senolytic effects potentially by preferentially disrupting the metabolic requirements of PGCCs [257], whereas agents targeting PGCCs, such as azacitidine and decitabine, can reverse drug resistance and tumor recurrence through epigenetic modulation [258]. Collectively, these findings point to the conclusion that PGCCs may represent a critical subpopulation warranting urgent intervention in senolytic strategies.

Furthermore, compared with normal cellular senescence, senescent tumor cells with PGCC features exhibit hyper-fused mitochondrial networks and abnormally elevated mitochondrial content, rendering their survival highly dependent on mitochondrial dynamics and physiological function [256]. This extreme mitochondrial phenotype, which is absent in normal senescent cells, implies that targeting the mitochondrial vulnerabilities of PGCCs may enable their precise elimination without damaging normal senescent cells, providing a compelling rationale for establishing a therapeutic window.

Following escape from dormancy, PGCCs generate invasive diploid escape cells through asymmetric cell division, which require higher metabolic energy to support proliferation and survival [259]. During this process, mitochondrial dynamics play a central role in maintaining energy homeostasis [260, 261]. Mitochondrial fission can be categorized into two functional subtypes based on the site of occurrence: midzone fission, i.e., symmetric fission, generates functional daughter mitochondria through DNM1L-MFF dynamic interactions, promoting mitochondrial biogenesis and cell survival [262]; and peripheral fission, i.e., asymmetric fission, separates damaged mitochondrial segments through DNM1L-FIS1 dynamic interactions, which are subsequently eliminated via mitophagy, also supporting cell survival [262]. Accumulating evidence indicates that multiple AMPK substrates involved in mitochondrial biogenesis, including MFF and DNM1L, are significantly upregulated under chemotherapeutic stress and nutrient deprivation, and AMPK-dependent PPARGC1 activation effectively replenishes the mitochondrial pool [256].

Proliferating PGCCs following chemotherapy often exhibit elevated ROS levels and reduced mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) due to disrupted oxidative homeostasis, a physiological state that necessitates the clearance of damaged mitochondria via mitophagy to maintain homeostasis in the hypoxic tumor core [259, 263]. To sustain the hypoxic microenvironment required for tumor growth, PGCC-derived escape cells are expected to upregulate the DNM1L-FIS1 signaling complex to selectively eliminate ROS-producing mitochondrial segments, thereby stabilizing the mitochondrial respiratory chain (MRC) [264]. Highly invasive proliferative high-grade gliomas that recapitulate the PGCC escape phenotype have been demonstrated to exhibit an association between dynamic MRC stabilization and DNM1L-FIS1-regulated mitophagy induction [265]. Thus, it can be posited that pro-survival mitophagy contributes to the maintenance of chemotherapy resistance and stemness-like features, which are essential for PGCC survival [266, 267].

On the basis of the aforementioned mechanisms, mitochondria-targeted anticancer strategies have demonstrated therapeutic advantages across multiple tumor subtypes, with several agents currently in clinical or preclinical trials [266, 268]. The mitochondrial fission inhibitor Mdivi-1 has been shown to significantly suppress EMT and stemness features in glioblastoma, lung adenocarcinoma, breast cancer, and pancreatic cancer [268]. Doxycycline, a mitochondrial biogenesis inhibitor, has advanced in clinical trials for breast/ovarian cancer (NCT02874430) and pancreatic cancer (NCT02775695) through inhibition of mitochondrial translation and biogenesis, and has also shown promise in prostate cancer, lung cancer, glioblastoma, and melanoma. Furthermore, metformin, an anti-diabetic drug targeting ETC complex I, has demonstrated significant efficacy in breast cancer (NCT04559308, NCT04387630, NCT00930579), pancreatic cancer, and glioblastoma by inhibiting oxidative phosphorylation and ATP production [268]. These mitochondria-targeted intervention strategies are highly concordant with the metabolic vulnerabilities of PGCCs, offering a feasible therapeutic avenue for the selective elimination of senescent tumor cells.

Circadian rhythm-based intervention strategies targeting EMT⁺ senescent subpopulations

Following the elimination of the majority of tumor cells by chemotherapy, the residual senescent tumor cells acquire stemness and drug resistance through their EMT phenotype, constituting a seed population for tumor recurrence [269]. However, this process does not occur independently of systemic regulation; the circadian rhythm network (BMAL1/CLOCK/PER) simultaneously governs both the initiation of senescence programs and the maintenance of EMT phenotypes, and senescent tumor cells further stabilize this EMT⁺ senescent subpopulation through SASP-derived secreted factors. Therefore, strategies targeting this subpopulation should not be limited to the direct elimination of senescent cells; rather, modulation of circadian rhythms may attenuate the potential for tumor recurrence.

Furthermore, the EMT observed in senescent tumor cells is fundamentally distinct from that occurring in normal senescent cells, and this distinction provides a feasible window for selective therapeutic targeting. The EMT associated with normal somatic cell senescence belongs to type II EMT (fibrotic/repair-type), which is functionally oriented toward tissue repair: cells transiently acquire migratory capacity to fill tissue defects, and upon completion of repair, they revert to an epithelial phenotype through the MET program, restoring tissue homeostasis without concomitant stemness acquisition. In contrast, the EMT in senescent tumor cells corresponds to type III EMT (tumor-associated type), which is pathological and persistent in nature, often maintained in a long-term hybrid E/M state, and is positively coupled with stemness acquisition, therapeutic resistance, and immune evasion, thereby driving tumor recurrence and metastasis [270]. This fundamental difference implies that senescent tumor cells develop a pathological dependence on the EMT maintenance machinery, a feature not shared by normal reparative EMT; in other words, the molecular nodes sustaining EMT persistence in tumors, such as circadian rhythm disruption and SASP chronicity, may serve as selective therapeutic targets without affecting the physiological EMT-MET cycle in normal tissues.

Extensive loss-of-function studies have demonstrated that the core clock transcriptional activators CLOCK and/or BMAL1 are in fact required for the maintenance of EMT and stemness across multiple tumor types: in a mouse model of AML, in vivo RNAi-mediated knockdown of ClOCK/BMAL1 in leukemic stem cells suppresses proliferation, promotes myeloid differentiation, and depletes the leukemic stem cell pool [271]; in glioblastoma multiforme models, knockdown of CLOCK or BMAL1 in glioblastoma stem cells induces cell cycle arrest and apoptosis, and impairs mitochondrial metabolism and tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle enzyme expression, underscoring their critical role in GSC stemness maintenance [272]; in colorectal cancer, patients with high BMAL1 expression exhibit significant enrichment of EMT and invasive gene signatures, and BMAL1 knockdown upregulates E-cadherin while downregulating TWIST, N-cadherin and Vimentin, promoting a MET-like transition and suppressing metastasis [273]. Similarly, in osteosarcoma, knockdown of BMAL1 or CLOCK upregulates the epithelial marker CDH1 while downregulating mesenchymal markers CDH2, ZEB1, and VIM [150]. Loss of PER2, a negative regulator of the BMAL1/CLOCK complex, enhances the activated expression of EMT-related genes including TWIST1, SLUG, and SNAIL in multiple breast cancer cell lines (SKBR-3, MCF-7, MDA-MB-231, and MDA-MB-157) [151]. Furthermore, the circadian rhythm transcription factor BHLHE40 promotes CDC6-induced cell cycle re-entry of senescent cells and malignant transformation of epithelial cells, further supporting the central role of the circadian rhythm network as the hub of the senescence-EMT-stemness axis [129]. On the basis of the aforementioned evidence, we propose the hypothesis that precise modulation of circadian homeostasis may selectively attenuate EMT persistence and the stemness phenotype in senescent tumor cells, thereby delaying tumor recurrence following chemotherapy.

Melatonin, an amine hormone secreted by the pineal gland, represents a potential candidate agent for achieving the aforementioned goals. Melatonin has been shown to reverse EMT at multiple levels: in human gastric cancer cells, it inhibits IL-1β-induced EMT by upregulating β-catenin and E-cadherin while downregulating Vimentin, fibronectin, Snail, MMP2, and MMP9 [274]; in UC3 bladder cancer cells, combined treatment with melatonin and valproic acid exerts synergistic cytotoxicity through activation of death-associated signaling pathways, concomitant with upregulation of E-cadherin and suppression of Snail/Slug expression [275]; in SKOV3 ovarian cancer-derived CSCs, melatonin also blocks EMT-associated migration and metastasis by inhibiting Snail/Slug and upregulating E-cadherin [276]; and in an osteosarcoma in vivo model, melatonin significantly reduces the migratory and invasive capacity of stem cells through potent suppression of SOX9 [277]. Notably, melatonin itself is a core output signal of the circadian rhythm, and its administration can directly reshape the circadian homeostasis of tumor cells, thereby intervening upstream in the CLOCK/BMAL1-mediated EMT maintenance program [278]. Currently, the therapeutic efficacy of melatonin is being evaluated in clinical trials for oral squamous cell carcinoma (NCT04137627), breast cancer CSCs (NCT02506790, NCT01965522, NCT01805089, NCT01557478), and recurrent solid malignancies (NCT01858155) [268], laying the foundation for its translational application as a targeted intervention strategy against senescent tumor cells.

Targeting the cFLIP-DR5 apoptotic escape axis in senescent tumor cells

Normal cellular senescence and tumor cell senescence are fundamentally distinct in their mechanisms of death resistance. Normal cellular senescence represents a programmed self-protective mechanism: upon completion of tumor suppression and tissue repair, senescent cells are efficiently recognized and cleared by NK cells and macrophages in the immune system, thereby maintaining tissue homeostasis [3, 34]. In contrast, tumor cell senescence drives progressive microenvironmental deterioration through the SASP, enabling long-term survival and creating opportunities for eventual relapse [35]. At the level of apoptotic regulation, normal senescent cells exhibit only modest upregulation of BCL-2 and BCL-XL, whereas senescent tumor cells simultaneously overexpress BCL-2, BCL-XL, and MCL-1, establishing a multilayered anti-apoptotic barrier that constitutes a major obstacle to their complete elimination [221, 279–282]. Importantly, this distinction also suggests that specifically targeting the unique death resistance proteins of senescent tumor cells may enable their selective clearance.

In recent years, the discovery of the death receptor inhibitory factor cFLIP has provided new insights for achieving this goal. Using CRISPR/Cas9 genetic screening in multiple senescence cancer cell models, the research team identified cFLIP deficiency as a common vulnerability of senescent cancer cells. Mechanistic studies further revealed that during senescence, NF-κB signaling is activated, which simultaneously upregulates the death receptor DR5 (TNFRSF10B) and its ligand TRAIL, placing senescent tumor cells in a state primed for extrinsic apoptosis; however, the concurrent upregulation of cFLIP blocks this apoptotic signal, thereby protecting cells from death. On this basis, screening with kinome and epigenomic libraries revealed that BRD2 knockdown alone specifically suppresses cFLIP expression and exerts synergistic lethality with DR5 activation. Accordingly, the researchers proposed a “one-two punch” strategy: activating extrinsic apoptotic signaling with DR5 agonist antibodies in combination with BRD2 inhibition to relieve cFLIP-mediated protection. This strategy, when combined with pro-senescence therapy, has been effectively validated in multiple animal models [283].

Notably, the DR5 agonist monoclonal antibody Conatumumab (AMG-655) has demonstrated a favorable safety profile in clinical trials involving patients with advanced tumors, and preliminary results from phase I/II trials also suggest its therapeutic potential in DR5-positive tumor patients [284, 285]. Collectively, the preclinical and clinical evidence supports that targeting the cFLIP-DR5 apoptotic escape axis to achieve selective clearance of senescent tumor cells holds significant promise for clinical translation.

Future challenges and research directions

Regulatory roles of senescence, dormancy, and CSCs in tumor recurrence

Although in vitro experiments have firmly established that TIS tumor cells can re-enter the cell cycle, thereby posing a potential risk for tumor recurrence, it must be acknowledged that direct in vivo evidence demonstrating senescent tumor cells as the origin of recurrence remains limited. Despite the high frequency of clinical relapse, lineage-tracing studies at the single-cell level have yet to conclusively determine whether recurrent lesions truly originate from senescent tumor cells. In fact, multiple alternative mechanisms cannot be ruled out: recurrence may be driven by pre-existing CSCs, may arise from stemness features acquired during senescence, or may result from the reactivation of proliferation in cells that have exited long-term quiescence or dormancy.

As research advances, the boundaries between senescence, cancer stemness, and dormancy are gradually blurring; they are not three isolated tumor states, but rather share overlapping molecular features and signaling networks, and even exhibit functional interplay. In multiple cancer dormancy models, the emergence of senescence traits is a striking common feature, including the expression of the SASP and β-galactosidase [161, 286–288]. Concurrently, activation of the tumor suppressor p53 is closely associated with cell cycle arrest and senescence, whereas exit from dormancy frequently requires a reduction in p53 activity [289]. Dormant cells and CSCs also exhibit significant pathway overlap: Notch and Wnt signaling are involved not only in maintaining the CSC pool but also in regulating the balance between quiescence and proliferation in dormant cells [290, 291]; senescent tumor cells that re-enter the cell cycle are often accompanied by the acquisition of stemness features; and all three states share EMT traits [259]. These overlapping relationships raise a critical unresolved question: does a tumor cell subpopulation exist that simultaneously exhibits senescent, stemness, and dormant features, thereby conferring a higher propensity for recurrence?

Furthermore, SASP factors secreted by senescent cells can trigger inflammatory cascades, and inflammation has been shown to drive dormant cells to re-enter the cell cycle [292–295] and to induce the acquisition of stemness features in tumor cells. This fact precisely illustrates that CSC-driven mechanisms, senescence-induced stemness acquisition, and dormancy escape are not mutually exclusive in the regulation of tumor recurrence; rather, they may operate synergistically through SASP as a central hub, collectively driving recurrence from multiple dimensions and accompanied by a marked enhancement of tumor malignancy. Therefore, focusing on the intersection of senescence, stemness, and dormancy may not only provide a more comprehensive perspective on the role of senescent tumor cells in recurrence but also open new directions for understanding the multi-layered regulatory mechanisms underlying tumor recurrence.

Multi-omics analysis defines therapeutic windows for precision intervention

In response to the clinical challenge of tumor recurrence following chemotherapy, the “one-two punch” strategy has been developed: first, pro-senescence therapy is employed to induce tumor cells into a senescent state, followed by senolytic or senomorphic agents to selectively eliminate senescent tumor cells or suppress senescence-associated inflammation. This strategy has demonstrated therapeutic potential in preclinical studies for inhibiting tumor recurrence. However, the marked heterogeneity of senescent tumor cells poses a significant obstacle, as senolytics or senomorphics targeting a single pathway are insufficient to achieve complete clearance of senescent tumor cells or full suppression of the SASP-driven inflammation, leaving a residual risk of recurrence. More critically, once tumor recurrence occurs, the evolutionary trajectory of the tumor undergoes a fundamental shift. Clonal evolution analyses of recurrent tumors have revealed the emergence of numerous subclonal populations, accompanied by a significant increase in subclonal diversity and characteristic mutational profiles at the time of recurrence, which in turn elevates intratumoral heterogeneity and drives the acquisition of drug resistance, severely compromising the efficacy and durability of subsequent therapeutic responses [296]. This reality imposes higher demands on subsequent clinical treatment regimens: taking the highly recurrent SCLC as an example, the initial first-line regimen consists of cisplatin combined with etoposide, whereas upon relapse, the treatment must be switched to topotecan [297]. Recurrence not only necessitates a comprehensive shift in therapeutic strategy but also signifies a substantial escalation in both patient prognosis and the complexity of clinical decision-making.

Beyond the direct elimination of senescent tumor cells, it is equally critical to seize the therapeutic window to block the awakening of senescent tumor cells. The composition of the SASP exhibits time-dependent dynamic changes, exerting dual effects at different stages of tumor progression; it can both suppress tumor growth and, under specific conditions, promote tumor recurrence. Therefore, precise identification and timely intervention targeting SASP factors hold promise for delaying tumor recurrence following chemotherapy.

The rapid advancement of multi-omics technologies has provided powerful tools for the timely intervention of the SASP. Single-cell and single-nucleus RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq/snRNA-seq) have revolutionized our understanding of the heterogeneity of senescent cells and their complex regulatory mechanisms, revealing the unique gene expression profiles and potential therapeutic targets of senescent tumor cells [210, 298–300]. Spatial transcriptomics, as an emerging technology, enables genome-wide profiling of gene expression at cellular resolution within intact tissue architecture, facilitating comprehensive genomic analysis of the senescence microenvironment [301]. Advances in proteomics and antibody array technologies have markedly enhanced the resolution of SASP component analysis [203]. Furthermore, the development of chemical biology tools such as APEX and TurboID has opened new avenues for the precise labeling and enrichment of the SASP [302, 303]. By integrating the aforementioned technologies with patient tissue or blood samples, it is feasible to achieve precise tracking and temporal monitoring of dynamic SASP changes in vivo, enabling the effective control of tumor awakening through timely drug administration at critical windows, thereby delaying post-treatment recurrence.

In addition, CDK inhibitors, particularly CDK4/6 inhibitors, have demonstrated efficacy in suppressing cell cycle escape of senescent tumor cells in vitro. Among them, palbociclib has shown inhibitory activity across multiple tumor models [304, 305] and has been approved for the clinical treatment of locally advanced or metastatic breast cancer [306], underscoring its therapeutic potential in blocking the awakening of senescent tumor cells. However, palbociclib’s in vivo application still warrants careful evaluation of dosing regimens and the safety profile associated with long-term administration to ensure clinical translatability.

AI-driven paradigm shift: from target discovery to drug design and clinical translation

Emerging diagnostic technologies, including AI-driven digital pathology and AI-assisted personalized treatment design, are driving profound transformations in therapeutic strategies. In addressing the core challenge of achieving selective targeting of senescent tumor cells, AI has emerged as a highly promising auxiliary tool.

At the level of target discovery, deep learning models trained on nuclear morphometry, DNA methylation profiles, and tissue spatial architecture hold promise for achieving accurate and rapid detection of senescent cells in routine histopathological practice [14, 307–309]. On this basis, integration with multi-omics technologies can further accelerate the screening of senescence-specific signaling pathways and predictive biomarkers, thereby establishing an actionable target database for precision therapy.

At the drug development level, machine learning algorithms can rapidly screen for active compounds targeting senescence-associated targets under the guidance of tumor recurrence mechanisms, and predict their pharmacokinetic properties, thereby accelerating the development of next-generation potent and specific senolytic agents [14]. This process directly translates “target discovery” into “drug design,” promising to bridge the critical bottleneck between basic research and clinical application. Furthermore, AI-facilitated interdisciplinary collaboration and knowledge integration may expedite the translation of research findings into clinical practice, offering new avenues to alleviate the persistent clinical challenge of post-treatment tumor recurrence.

However, in complex clinical scenarios such as end-stage patients, the predictive power of AI regarding disease progression remains markedly limited, underscoring the critical need for deep synergy between AI and clinical expertise. To fully unlock the clinical potential of AI, future efforts should focus on establishing systematic clinical case databases to expand the training data sources for large-scale AI models, thereby enhancing their analytical capacity for disease characterization and evolutionary trajectories.

Conclusion

Conventional cytotoxic therapies and targeted agents, while effectively inducing tumor regression, inevitably trigger TIS. Notably, persistently surviving senescent tumor cells acquire escape capabilities through multifaceted adaptive mechanisms including aberrant expression of cell cycle-related proteins, EMT, acquisition of stem‑like traits, formation of PGCCs, fate switching from senescence to pyroptosis, and remodeling of the tumor immune microenvironment, thereby constituting a critical cellular source of tumor recurrence. In this review, we systematically delineate the aforementioned escape mechanisms and demonstrate that senescent tumor cells are not quiescent end-stage entities, but rather dynamic entities characterized by pronounced heterogeneity and plasticity, which provides a theoretical rationale for targeting them as an intervention strategy against recurrence.

At the intervention strategy level, the “one-two punch” model combining conventional chemotherapy with senolytics or senomorphics has demonstrated potential for delaying tumor recurrence in preclinical settings; however, its clinical translation faces two major challenges. On one hand, the broad-spectrum clearance of senescent cells by senolytics may induce non-specific tissue toxicity; on the other hand, long-term administration regimens of senomorphics remain associated with substantial uncertainties regarding safety and feasibility. To address these therapeutic bottlenecks, this review systematically compares the essential differences between normal tissue-derived senescent cells and tumor-derived senescent cells in terms of surface marker profiles and survival signaling networks, based on the molecular characteristics of the escape network of tumor-derived senescent cells. Accordingly, we propose that strategies targeting the metabolic vulnerabilities of PGCCs, inhibiting the conversion of EMT-positive subpopulations through circadian rhythm modulation, and interfering with anti-apoptotic pathways may enhance the targeting specificity against senescent tumor cells, thereby offering novel therapeutic options with both high efficacy and favorable safety profiles for delaying tumor recurrence.

In terms of technical approaches, AI-assisted drug discovery is expected to accelerate the clinical translation of senolytic agents that specifically target senescent tumor cells. Meanwhile, the integrated application of single-cell and spatial multi-omics technologies enables dynamic quantification of the evolutionary trajectories of post-treatment senescent tumor cells, thereby facilitating precise intervention within the optimal therapeutic window. More importantly, the continuous deepening of fundamental theories in senescence biology, the high-throughput iteration of multi-omics technological platforms, and the establishment of AI-driven dynamic monitoring systems will collectively propel a paradigm shift in TIS research from phenomenological description to mechanistic quantification, ultimately enhancing the clinical manageability of post-treatment tumor recurrence in practice.
