Comment on “Dienogest Therapy against the Combined Oral Contraceptive Pill in Deep Infiltrating Endometriosis Cases: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis”
Authors: Peng-Hsuan Huang, Hsin-Fen Lu
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

I am writing to offer a critical appraisal of the article by Susianto et al. regarding the comparative efficacy of dienogest and combined oral contraceptive pills (COCPs) in managing deep infiltrating endometriosis.[1] While this systematic review addresses an important clinical topic, several methodological and statistical concerns must be highlighted to ensure a balanced interpretation of the results.

First, there is a notable inconsistency in study design selection. Although the authors specify the inclusion of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in their methodology, the data presented in the results and Table 1 include observational studies and retrospective cohorts. Integrating nonrandomized data into a meta-analysis alongside RCTs introduces substantial selection bias and undermines the hierarchy of evidence intended for a systematic review of interventions.

Second, the management of extremely high heterogeneity in the statistical analysis is concerning. The heterogeneity reported for the primary outcomes is exceptionally high, with I2 values of 87.432% for dysmenorrhea, 80.619% for dyschezia, and 89.991% for dyspareunia. Although a random effect model was applied, the presence of such high heterogeneity suggests that the pooled mean differences may be clinically misleading, yet the authors did not provide subgroup analyses to account for these significant variances.

Third, the quality of evidence and bias risk assessment requires further scrutiny. The analysis indicates that out of nine included studies, three are categorized as high risk of bias and two as unclear risk. Given that over 50% of the source literature lacks optimal quality or randomization details, the conclusion that the overall quality of evidence is moderate appears to be an overestimation that requires further justification.

Fourth, there is a lack of standardization in COCP formulations used as the control. The study groups various medications under the broad term COCP, yet different progestin components and estrogen dosages have distinct effects on endometriosis lesions. Comparing a specific 2 mg dose of dienogest against a nonstandardized group of COCPs limits the pharmacological precision of the study conclusions.

Finally, the omission of quantitative safety comparisons is a significant limitation. While the manuscript notes that many patients experienced side effects such as irregular genital bleeding and anemia while taking dienogest, the meta-analysis focuses exclusively on pain scores. For clinical practitioners, the safety profile and incidence of adverse events are as critical as efficacy when determining long-term postoperative therapy, and these factors should have been statistically compared against the COCP group.

In summary, while the findings support the potential of dienogest, the inclusion of lower-tier evidence and the presence of extreme heterogeneity necessitate a more cautious clinical application of these results.
