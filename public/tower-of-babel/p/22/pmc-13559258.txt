Association between early body temperature dynamic trajectory and mortality and infection rates for patients with cardiac arrest in ICU: a retrospective study
Authors: Li Liu, Bowen Li, Shuhang Wang, Ruichen Mao, Yancun Liu, Yanfen Chai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn-hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA) is a common critical event with poor prognosis. Targeted temperature management (TTM) is the only approved therapy for cerebral ischemia post-cardiac arrest (CA). Body temperature (BT) is a key physiological indicator, but the dynamic evolution of BT is usually neglected, which may be closely associated with patient outcomes. This study aimed to investigate the dynamic BT trajectories within the first 72 hours after ICU admission in CA patients, identify distinct BT trajectory subphenotypes, and evaluate their associations with mortality and infection risk.MethodsA retrospective study was conducted using data from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database and the eICU Collaborative Research Database (eICU-CRD), including 3,052 intensive care unit (ICU) CA patients. The time-series k-means algorithm combined with dynamic time warping (DTW) was used to cluster dynamic BT trajectories within the first 72 hours of ICU admission, identifying subphenotypes. To evaluate the association between BT trajectory subphenotypes and mortality, this study employed Kaplan-Meier analysis, Cox regression and logistic regression models, and sensitivity and subgroup analyses. The tabular prior-data fitted network (TabPFN) was used to construct prediction models for mortality and underwent external validation. Infection status was also compared across subphenotypes.ResultsFour BT trajectory subphenotypes were identified, including “hyperthermia”, “normothermia”, “hypothermic, slow rise” (HSR), and “normothermic, rapid decline” (NRD). The NRD subphenotype had the highest in-hospital, 7-day, 28-day, and 180-day mortality rates, which remained robust across multiple models. There was no significant difference in infection incidence among subphenotypes, but pathogen distribution varied slightly. The TabPFN models showed excellent performance in predicting mortality, with the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) values ranging from 0.840 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.688–0.979] to 0.991 (95% CI: 0.982–1.000). The above results remained consistent in external validation.ConclusionsThe NRD subphenotype is significantly associated with higher mortality in ICU CA patients. Dynamic BT trajectories can provide insights for personalized clinical management and timely intervention in ICU patients with CA.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Four early body temperature (BT) trajectories were identified in intensive care unit (ICU) cardiac arrest (CA) patients: hyperthermia, normothermia, hypothermic-slow rise (HSR), and normothermic-rapid decline (NRD).

• The NRD subphenotype carried the highest in-hospital and long-term mortality, remaining robust across multiple sensitivity models.

• Dynamic BT monitoring outperformed static measures in prognostication. The tabular prior-data fitted network (TabPFN) prediction model achieved excellent discrimination and was externally validated in the eICU Collaborative Research Database (eICU-CRD) cohort.

What is known and what is new?

• BT is a key physiological indicator post-CA. While static temperature measurements are routinely recorded, they fail to capture the dynamic physiological evolution during the early ICU stay.

• This study identified four types of BT trajectories in ICU CA patients. The results indicate that early BT dynamic changes can distinguish the risk of death and provide a deployable, high-performance TabPFN tool for personalized prognostic assessment.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Dynamic BT monitoring should be integrated into routine ICU care for CA patients to enable early identification of the high-risk NRD phenotype. Clinicians should shift from targeting fixed BT values to tracking BT trajectories for timely intervention. Future studies should standardize BT monitoring frequency and incorporate detailed resuscitation variables to refine these phenotypes and validate findings prospectively.

Introduction

In-hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA) is a common critical event in healthcare settings with an incidence of over 290,000 patients per year in the United States (1-4). Even after successful resuscitation and return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC), the prognosis for cardiac arrest (CA) patients is poor, with a survival rate of less than 15% (5-7). Targeted temperature management (TTM) is currently the only approved therapy for global cerebral ischemia (GCI) after CA (8). The guidelines published by the American Heart Association (AHA) recommend a constant temperature of 32–37.5 ℃ for at least 24 hours during TTM after CA (9). Unfortunately, TTM is associated with a variety of adverse effects, including bleeding, sepsis, tremors, cardiac arrhythmias, and the risk of electrolyte and metabolic disturbances (8,10-13).

Body temperature (BT), as an essential physiologic indicator, plays a key role in disease progression in CA patients. Different patient populations may exhibit unique temperature patterns after ROSC, which may be strongly associated with immune response, organ function recovery, and risk of infection (14,15). Some studies have found that hypothermia is associated with increased mortality, while fever correlates with decreased mortality (16,17). In addition, Iba et al. indicated that BT could affect the underlying immune status of patients (18). Meanwhile, the immune system has been proven to be involved in thermoregulation, where the pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokine levels are relevant to temperature abnormalities (19-22). Previous studies have emphasized the impact of static temperature control at a single time point or under TTM on outcomes, however, the dynamic evolution of BT over time and its potential heterogeneity were neglected (23).

In clinical practice, patients with CA experience a dynamic course involving intervention and response to intervention, where the dynamic trajectory may reflect the pathophysiological responses underlying CA and ROSC. Static characterization probably cannot fully clarify the influence of such dynamic changes on the results. Furthermore, BT is a critical vital sign. Bhavani et al. identified different temperature phenotypes of sepsis patients through dynamic trajectory models, providing a basis for refined treatment of patients (24). Therefore, this study was designed to investigate the dynamic BT trajectory within the first 72 hours among CA patients in the intensive care unit (ICU). Additionally, we sought to explore the association between distinct BT trajectory subphenotypes and key clinical outcomes, including mortality and the risk of infection. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0986/rc).

Methods

Study design and population

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This was a retrospective cohort study that utilized data from version 2.2 of the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database (https://physionet.org/content/mimiciv/2.2/) and the eICU Collaborative Research Database (eICU-CRD) (https://eicu-crd.mit.edu/gettingstarted/access/) (25). The author who retrieved data from the database (L.L.) has finished the course and got certification (No. 61529195).

Study participants were identified from the MIMIC-IV and eICU-CRD database based on two key criteria: being aged 18 years or older and having an international classification of diseases (ICD) 9 or ICD-10 code indicative of CA. In the MIMIC-IV database, ICD codes cannot reliably distinguish between out of hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) and IHCA (26). Therefore, this study cohort represents the ICU CA population and does not include the location of CA as the primary modeling variable. The flow chart showed the exclusion criteria in this study (Figure 1). For patients with multiple admission records, only data from the patient’s first admission were used. In addition, nonphysiological temperature data were excluded (temperatures <32 and >44 ℃) as per prior publications (27). Patients were required to have a minimum ICU stay of 24 hours. The 72-hour window refers to the observation period during which dynamic BT data were extracted. To ensure sufficient data density for trajectory clustering, patients were required to have at least two temperature measurements within the initial 72-hour window after ICU admission (24). Information regarding the specific implementation of TTM protocols was not collected. Consequently, the temperature trajectories identified in this study represent observed physiological dynamics, reflecting the composite result of both therapeutic interventions and spontaneous physiological responses. Eventually, 787 individuals from the MIMIC database and 2,265 individuals from the eICU-CRD database were included in this study. The MIMIC database was used for trajectory analysis and modeling, while the eICU-CRD database was used for external validation.

Data extraction

PostgreSQL was used in pgAdmin and Navicat Premium to extract clinical data for MIMIC-IV and eICU-CRD ICU CA patients. These data included dynamic BT records of patients within 72 h of admission to the ICU. In addition, clinical and laboratory variables were collected within the first 24 h after ICU admission. If a patient underwent more than one measurement of vital signs or laboratory test on the day of admission, the average value was used for follow-up analysis. The extracted patient characteristics are shown in Appendix 1. Furthermore, extract information related to infection, including whether the patient was infected during hospitalization and the type of microorganism causing the infection. The infection status was determined based on the microbial culture results obtained during ICU hospitalization. If a patient has at least one positive culture in their blood, sputum, urine, or other sterile areas, it is classified as an infection. Variables with more than 30% missing values in any databases were excluded, while all other missing values were imputed using multiple imputation (28).

Exposure and outcomes

The time-series k-means (TSkmeans) algorithm was used to analyze the dynamic BT trajectory of CA patients within the first 72 hours after ICU admission to evaluate overall BT. Before performing the TSkmeans algorithm, the elbow rule was employed to determine its optimal number of clusters. The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality, while the secondary outcome were 7-day, 28-day and 180-day mortality. Given that post-discharge outcomes were not available in eICU-CRD, assessments of 7-day, 28-day and 180-day mortality were conducted exclusively within the MIMIC-IV database.

Statistical analysis

To ensure sufficient spatiotemporal continuity of dynamic BT records, linear interpolation is used to fill missing values in the time series. The normal distribution of continuous variables was evaluated using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Data conforming to a normal distribution were summarized using the mean and standard deviation (mean ± SD), and group comparisons were performed with the independent samples t-test. For data that did not follow a normal distribution, the median with interquartile range (IQR: P25, P75) was used for description, and the Mann-Whitney U test was applied for comparative analysis between groups. Frequency and percentage (%) describe categorical variables, and chi-square (χ2) tests, corrected chi-square tests, or Fisher’s exact tests evaluate differences between groups. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were used to compare 7-day, 28-day, and 180-day survival rates between different trajectory groups based on time-series tests. Statistical significance was set at P<0.05.

To ensure the robustness and reliability of our findings, we performed sensitivity analyses using different models, including unadjusted log-rank tests, Cox models adjusted with all features, Cox models adjusted with features selected by Boruta, logistic models adjusted with all features, and logistic models adjusted with features selected by Boruta. Subgroup analyses were performed by the level of features including age, sex, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score and congestive heart failure (CHF).

Prediction model based on tabular prior-data fitted network

Covariables marked as “confirmed” by the Boruta algorithm were incorporated into the tabular prior-data fitted network (TabPFN). TabPFN is a state-of-the-art (SOTA) foundation model for tabular data, which leverages in-context learning (ICL) to generalize across diverse tabular tasks through pre-training on millions of synthetic datasets generated via structural causal models (SCMs) (29). The architecture of TabPFN is built on a Transformer encoder with a two-way attention mechanism, enabling it to capture both inter-feature (within-sample) and inter-sample (within-feature) dependencies while maintaining invariance to the order of samples and features. This allows it to demonstrate superior performance without extensive feature preprocessing and hyperparameter tuning.

The whole datasets were randomly divided into training cohort for model development and independent test cohort to validate the prediction performance of the developed model at an 8:2 ratio before feeding into TabPFN. The synthetic minority over-sampling technique (SMOTE) was employed to address the class imbalance in the training cohort. The continuous features were scaled using min-max normalization and the categorical features were converted using label encoding. A variety of models were established for the four outcomes (In-hospital, 7-day, 28-day, and 180-day mortality) of different subphenotypes obtained by the Time-series Kmeans algorithm and the overall cohort to obtain the best clinical practice. Area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was used to quantify the ability of models to distinguish between survivors and non-survivors. AUC ranges from 0.5 (random) to 1.0 (perfect discrimination), with higher values indicating better discrimination performance. Calibration curve was employed to evaluate the agreement between predicted probabilities of mortality and observed outcomes, while the Brier score was used to quantify calibration error, with lower values indicating better calibration. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to assess the clinical utility of the model by calculating the net benefit across a range of decision thresholds, helping determine whether the model adds value in guiding clinical decisions. The bootstrap resampling with 1,000 iterations was used to assess the robustness of performance evaluation.

Results

BT trajectory and subphenotypes

When the number of clusters was set to four, it exhibited a distinct “elbow” shape (Figure S1). This revealed that within four groups, the dynamic BT trajectory of patients show high internal similarity, and adding more groups does not remarkably increase intergroup differentiation. Therefore, our study divided patients into four groups to maximize the differences between groups while maintaining similarities within groups (Figure 2A). Subsequently, the group trajectory and the t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) indicate a significant separation between the four clusters, suggesting that each subphenotype exhibited significantly different BT patterns (Figure 2B).

Subphenotype trajectory analysis. (A) BT trajectories were identified as four subphenotypes; (B) t-SNE visualization of the BT trajectories. BT, body temperature; t-SNE, t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding.

Based on the clustering results of the above four BT dynamic modes, we further compared and analyzed the BT characteristics of patients with each subphenotype in detail (Table 1). Group 1 (n=171; 21.73%) was characterized by the presence of persistent high temperatures (hereafter denoted as the “hyperthermia” subphenotype). These patients had the highest mean, minimum and maximum temperatures. Group 2 (n=348; 44.22%) showed normal BT, and most patients (91.15%) did not have fever (hereafter denoted as the “normothermia” subphenotype). Group 3 (n=219; 27.83%) showed initial hypothermia followed by a slow rise in BT [hereafter denoted as the “hypothermic, slow rise” (HSR) subphenotype]. Group 4 (n=49; 6.23%) showed a rapid decline in BT followed by a sustained hypothermic state. These patients had the lowest mean, minimum and maximum temperatures, with all patients experiencing hypothermia and only 4.09% experiencing fever [hereafter denoted as the “normothermic, rapid decline” (NRD) subphenotype].

Patient infection status comparison across subphenotypes

Figure 3A indicates that 307 (39.0%) of the 787 patients were confirmed to have an infection by microbial culture and the pathogens detected from microbial cultures consisted of Gram-positive bacteria (GPB) (22.5%), Gram-negative bacteria (GNB) (7.0%) and fungi (9.5%). The most common GPB are methicillin-resistant staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and coagulase-negative staphylococci (CNS), the most common GNB are Escherichia coli (E. coli), and the most common fungi are yeast. Table S1 summarizes the infection situation between different subphenotypes. There was no significant difference in the frequency of infection between “hyperthermia”, “normothermia”, HSR and NRD subphenotypes (Figure 3B). In addition, the differences in the infection of GPB, GNB and fungi between patients in the “hyperthermia”, “normothermia”, HSR and NRD subphenotypes was not found (P=0.58, P=0.87, P=0.74) (Figure 3C and Table S1).

Outcome comparisons across subphenotypes

Kaplan-Meier curves were used to describe the survival trends of the four subphenotypes. As shown in Figure 4, patients with the NRD subphenotype demonstrated a significantly elevated risk of mortality at 7, 28, and 180 days (all P<0.001). Establishment and evaluation of TabPFN

To further quantify the prognostic value of early BT dynamic trajectories, TabPFN was used to construct prediction models for in-hospital, 7-day, 28-day and 180-day mortality for each of the four BT trajectory subphenotypes and the overall cohort (Figure 5). Table S2 indicates that the training and test cohorts exhibited no statistically significant differences. TabPFN exhibited excellent discrimination performance across all subphenotypes and outcomes, with AUC values ranging from 0.840 (95% CI: 0.688–0.979) (normothermia subphenotype) to 0.969 (95% CI: 0.906–1.000) (NRD subphenotype) for in-hospital mortality prediction, 0.871 (95% CI: 0.750–0.980) (HSR subphenotype) to 0.949 (95% CI: 0.906–0.995) (normothermia subphenotype and overall cohort) for 7-day mortality prediction, 0.886 (95% CI: 0.805–0.959) (normothermia subphenotype) to 0.991 (95% CI: 0.982–1.000) (HSR subphenotype) for 28-day mortality prediction, and 0.872 (95% CI: 0.765–1.000) (hyperthermia subphenotype) to 0.939 (95% CI: 0.893–0.970) (overall cohort) for 180-day mortality prediction, confirming the stability of TabPFN in long-term prognosis prediction.

Performance of TabPFN-based prediction models for (A-C) in-hospital mortality risk, (D-F) 7-day mortality risk, (G-I) 28-day mortality risk and (K-L) 180-day mortality risk assessed by AUC, calibration curve, Brier score and DCA. AUC, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve; CI, confidence interval; DCA, decision curve analysis; TabPFN, tabular prior-data fitted network.

Calibration curves demonstrated strong agreement between predicted mortality probabilities and observed outcomes across all models. The Brier score further validated this, ranging from 0.036 (95% CI: 0.001–1.000) (7-day mortality prediction in hyperthermia subphenotype) to 0.175 (95% CI: 0.076–0.264) (7-day mortality prediction in NRD subphenotype), indicating that the predictions are closely aligning with the actual event rates and confirming that TabPFN accurately quantifies risk magnitudes. DCA evaluated the net benefit of each TabPFN model across a range of decision thresholds, indicating consistently higher net benefit than the “treat all” or “treat none” strategies across most threshold probabilities (0.1–0.8). The normothermia subphenotype and overall cohort models exhibited the widest range of thresholds with positive net benefit, indicating their potential to guide clinical decision-making, e.g., intensive monitoring or intervention, more effectively than arbitrary strategies. Even in the NRD subphenotype with the highest mortality risk, the model maintained meaningful net benefit, supporting its value in identifying high-risk patients for targeted management. The results confirm that TabPFN could leverage dynamic BT trajectories and baseline clinical features to provide reliable and clinically actionable prognostic information for CA patients in the ICU.

External validation

To verify the generalizability and stability of the identified BT trajectory subphenotypes and the TabPFN-based prediction model, the external validation was conducted using the eICU-CDR database. Figure 6A shows that four distinct BT trajectory subphenotypes with highly consistent characteristics to those in the MIMIC-IV database were successfully identified in the eICU-CRD database. The temperature change trends, mean values, and fluctuation characteristics of each subphenotype were highly analogous to the MIMIC-IV database, confirming that the subphenotype classification based on dynamic BT trajectories possessed good external stability and reproducibility in different ICU CA patient populations (Table S3). Additionally, we analyzed baseline patient characteristics and survival outcomes in the eICU-CRD database. The NRD subphenotype exhibited a higher in-hospital mortality rate compared to the other three subphenotypes, consistent with subphenotype analysis results from the MIMIC database (Tables S4,S5). Meanwhile, in the external validation, TabPFN gave the similar discrimination and calibration to those in the internal validation, with AUC ranging from 0.825 (95% CI: 0.801–0.851) to 0.921 (95% CI: 0.878–0.962) and Brier score ranging from 0.124 (95% CI: 0.086–0.160) to 0.170 (95% CI: 0.154–0.191) (Figure 6B,6C). DCA further confirmed that the model still maintained positive net benefit across most threshold probabilities (Figure 6D). These results verified the generalizability of TabPFN based on dynamic BT trajectory subphenotypes, which could stably predict the mortality risk of ICU CA patients in different clinical populations.

Discussion

This study explored the potential association between early dynamic BT trajectories and mortality and infection in CA patients in the ICU. Four patient subphenotypes with similar trajectories were identified by the TSkmeans algorithm and labeled as NRD, HSR, “hyperthermia”, and “normothermia”. Significant differences in laboratory tests and vital signs between the trajectory groups were found, and patients with the NRD subphenotype showed higher mortality at in-hospital, 7, 28, and 180 days. Sensitivity analyses using a number of diverse models demonstrate that the results remained robust, which highlights the crucial position of dynamic temperature detection that capture fluctuations that cannot be detected by intermittent measurements (30,31).

Despite the NRD subphenotype exhibiting lower Charlson comorbidity index and a lower proportion of patients with CHF, renal disease and peripheral vascular disease compared to the other three subphenotypes, as well as higher SPO2 in the first 24 hours, its mortality was the highest among the four subphenotypes which emphasize the powerful intrinsic link between BT trajectories and the mortality risk in ICU CA patients. Meanwhile, baseline and trajectory analysis showed that the NRD subphenotype has higher levels of AST, ALT, anion gap and glucose, as well as lower levels of K+, Ca2+ and HCO3−, suggesting that the NRD subphenotype may have more severe post resuscitation pathophysiological disorders. It is worth noted that, although the median GCS score in our cohort was 15.00, this is consistent with recent MIMIC-IV literature and reflects survivor bias (32-34). Patients in the NRD subphenotype had a median GCS score of 15, but this subphenotype had the lowest GCS floor and the highest mortality rate, suggesting that dynamic temperature trajectories can identify high-risk physiological states.

Further, there was no significant difference in the incidence of infection among different BT trajectory subphenotypes, which is similar to previous research findings, but the proportion of GPB infections was higher in the “hyperthermia” subphenotype and the proportion of fungal infections was higher in the NRD subphenotype. This may be related to the increase in reactive oxygen species in mitochondria caused by high temperatures, which triggers oxidative stress and cellular damage; while low temperatures inhibit the function of neutrophils and T cells (35-38). Harmon et al. indicated a correlation between prophylactic use of antibiotics and reduced risk of infection in patients with CA (unadjusted odds ratio =0.59) (23). This provides a reference for infection prevention and control in patients with abnormal BT, suggesting that clinical practice can optimize empirical antibiotic selection and prevention strategies based on the subphenotype of BT trajectory.

The interaction mechanism between BT level and the prognosis of CA patients has been widely discussed in previous studies, however, the effect of BT on the prognosis of CA patients is still a controversial topic (39). Taccone et al. indicated that in the secondary analysis of the TTM-2 trial, hypothermia was not associated with improvements in survival and functional outcomes compared to the normal BT group (40). On the contrary, some studies have pointed out that BT is one of the important factors in predicting ICU mortality in CA patients (41,42). Fernando et al. reported a correlation between the application of moderate and deep hypothermia and an increased risk of arrhythmia in CA patients when compared to patients with normal BT (43). Although the above studies discussed the effect of different BT on the prognosis of CA patients, the focus of these studies was limited to the prognostic role of static temperature control at a single time point or under TTM. In contrast, our analysis of dynamic trajectories captures the composite result of therapeutic interventions and the patient’s intrinsic physiological response. In critically ill patients, the continuous evolution of both physiological status and medical management means that dynamic BT patterns are vital for monitoring true patient condition and therapeutic response. Integrating these trajectory analyses could significantly improve the accuracy of prognostic assessment.

Subphenotype identification based on dynamic representation may lead to more personalized management strategies (44-47). Bhavani et al. used patient temperature data to identify novel sepsis subphenotypes, with mortality in the “hypothermia” subphenotype being three times higher than in the “hyperthermia, rapid resolution” subphenotype (24). Bhatraju et al. studied the creatinine trajectory in the first 72 h of ICU stay among critically ill patients with AKI and showed that patients who have a non-resolving AKI subphenotype in that timeframe have a higher risk of mortality in comparison to patients with a resolving subphenotype of AKI (relative risk 1.68; 95% CI: 1.15–2.33) (48). Ning et al. used the dynamic trajectory of blood glucose within 48 hours after admission to identify the subphenotypes of critically ill heart failure patients and found that the mortality rate of heart failure patients with increased blood glucose was significantly increased, which provided a reference for optimizing clinical blood glucose management and improving patient prognosis (49).

A key methodological advance is the application of TabPFN, a state-of-the-art foundation model for tabular data, to quantify the prognostic value of dynamic BT trajectories (29). The results demonstrate its robustness, with high AUC (0.871–0.991) for 7/28/180-day mortality prediction across every subphenotype, strong calibration (Brier scores of 0.036–0.175) and significant net benefits in DCA. Liu et al. developed an ensemble model based on multiple ML models to predict in-hospital mortality risk in ICU patients with CA, but its AUC only achieved 0.866 (42). Meanwhile, subphenotype modelling offered more advantageous performance, with the highest AUC of 0.991 (95% CI: 0.982–1.000) and the lowest Brier score of 0.036 (95% CI: 0.001–1.000).

The results in this study suggest that the NRD subphenotype has the highest risk of death in CA patients hospitalized in the ICU and remains stable after sensitivity analysis and subgroup analysis, which highlights the crucial position of dynamic BT trajectories in predicting the prognosis of CA patients in the ICU. Dynamic temperature detection captures fluctuations that cannot be detected by intermittent measurements, allowing for more comprehensive assessment of patient temperature and providing reference for their pathological and physiological status (50). Rather than focusing solely on the achievement of a specific static target, clinicians should monitor the kinetic pattern of temperature change to identify high-risk NRD subphenotype early. By continuously monitoring BT, clinicians can identify trajectories indicating a deterioration in the pathophysiological state earlier than intermittent measurements. The discovery of this trajectory is crucial for timely intervention. Moreover, it enables clinicians to adapt interventions based on each patient’s individual BT trajectory, thereby making clinical treatment plans more personalized.

A number of limitations in this study should be noted. There is heterogeneity in the frequency of temperature monitoring. Although cases with less than two measurements within 72 hours are excluded, the inconsistency of measurement intervals among different patients may affect the accuracy of trajectory modeling. This study requires patients to stay in the ICU for no less than 24 hours and complete at least two temperature monitoring sessions within 72 hours of admission. This design may result in the exclusion of some early death or early discharge patients, thereby affecting the generalizability of the research findings to a wider CA population. Furthermore, detailed TTM protocols and sedative use were not recorded, which may confound the interpretation of temperature trajectories. Meanwhile, the lack of variables such as the cause of sudden CA, initial heart rhythm, witnessed status, and low-flow time limits the predictive ability of the model for the prognosis of patients with CA. In the future, it is necessary to further improve research by standardizing the frequency of temperature monitoring, integrating drug use, and establishing local registries with complete resuscitation data.

Conclusions

The NRD subphenotype, identified within the first 72 hours following ICU admission, is strongly linked to increased mortality among CA patients. Our results demonstrate that dynamic BT monitoring offers superior prognostic utility compared to single static measurements. This approach provides a novel strategy for early risk stratification and outcome prediction in ICU patients following CA.
