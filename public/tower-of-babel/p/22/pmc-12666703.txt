Cathepsin C-driven enhancement of neutrophil extracellular trap formation contributes to acute lung injury
Authors: Xinyu Wu, Huijun Liu, Qingwu Qin, Lin Yuan, Ye Yao, Xizi Du, Qiuyan Qin, Leyuan Wang, Qianyu Zhao, Ling Qin, Yang Xiang, Ming Yang, Xiaoqun Qin, Chi Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryAcute lung injury (ALI) is a severe pulmonary inflammatory disease with high morbidity and mortality rates. The pulmonary inflammatory phenotype of ALI is driven by the excessive aggregation and activation of neutrophils, triggering a cytokine storm. Notably, the extensive formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) has been demonstrated to play a critical role in ALI. Our results revealed significant overexpression of Cathepsin C (CTSC) in macrophages after LPS stimulation, which subsequently promoted massive NET formation through a positive feedback mechanism. Specifically, LPS stimulation markedly elevated CTSC expression in macrophages, which subsequently promoted the autocrine secretion of CTSC from neutrophils, ultimately culminating in excessive NET formation through the activation of the PR3-IL-1β-p38 pathway. Consequently, the inhibition of CTSC or PR3 significantly reduced NET formation and attenuated lung injury in ALI mice. In summary, CTSC overexpression drives excessive NET formation in ALI mice through a neutrophil-mediated positive feedback loop regulated by the PR3-IL-1β-p38 pathway.

Introduction

Acute lung injury (ALI) is a severe pulmonary inflammation triggered by both endogenous and exogenous pathogenic factors1 and can progress to acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) in severe cases.2 Globally, approximately 3 million ALI/ARDS cases occur annually, accounting for 10% of ICU admissions.3 The primary pathological hallmark of ALI is the infiltration and accumulation of peripheral neutrophils and other immune cells in the lungs. Subsequently, these infiltrating immune cells release a multitude of inflammatory mediators, triggering an “inflammatory storm” that exacerbates pulmonary edema and disrupts the ventilation/perfusion ratio, ultimately leading to severe pulmonary dysfunction.3,4 Unfortunately, despite a mortality rate as high as 40% for ALI/ARDS,5,6,7 effective therapeutic interventions at present remain limited. Therefore, there is an urgent need to elucidate the underlying pathogenesis and to develop therapeutic strategies for ALI.

Neutrophils are the most abundant leukocytes in peripheral blood and play a crucial role in the innate immune response against microbial infections.8 Notably, recent studies have shown that neutrophils can capture and eliminate pathogens through neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) during infection, thereby contributing to the pathogenesis of ALI.9,10 NETs are extracellular reticular structures composed of granule proteins, neutrophil serine proteases (NSPs), and decondensed chromatin, which capture and eliminate bacteria and fungi during the immune response.3 Clinical studies have further demonstrated that NETs are significantly enriched in the lungs of patients with ALI,5,11,12 which is closely correlated to pulmonary inflammation and tissue damage.13,14,15 Nevertheless, the molecular mechanisms driving excessive NET formation and their precise pathological role in ALI progression remain to be fully elucidated.

Notably, Cathepsin C (CTSC), a lysosomal cysteine protease, is indispensable for the activation of NSPs, thereby augmenting neutrophil activity and bactericidal function.16,17,18 In mammals, CTSC expression is principally observed in pulmonary, splenic, renal, hepatic tissues, and myeloid cell lineages, with particularly high expression in neutrophils, mast cells, monocytes, macrophages, and their progenitor cells.19 Accumulating evidence indicates that CTSC-induced NET formation is implicated in diverse inflammatory diseases and malignancies. Specifically, the modulation of CTSC activity and consequent NET suppression have been shown to ameliorate acute pulmonary inflammation and Sendai virus-triggered asthmatic manifestations.20 Moreover, marked upregulation of CTSC expression has been demonstrated to drive the generation of NETs during breast cancer metastasis.21,22 These collective findings strongly implicate CTSC in NET formation during ALI pathogenesis. Nevertheless, the precise relationship between CTSC expression dynamics and NET formation in ALI remains to be systematically investigated.

In this study, we initially identified the primary source of CTSC in the lung, with a particular focus on its spatiotemporal expression dynamics during the pathogenesis of ALI. Subsequently, we systematically examined the effects of CTSC overexpression on NET formation and ALI pathogenesis both in vitro and in vivo. Furthermore, we elucidated the fundamental molecular mechanism underlying CTSC-mediated NET formation. Ultimately, we comprehensively evaluated the targeted inhibitory effects of CTSC on NET formation and pulmonary inflammatory damage to explore potential therapeutic strategies for ALI.

Results

Overexpression of cathepsin C in the lung is closely associated with neutrophil extracellular trap formation in acute lung injury mice

Firstly, we confirmed NET formation in the lungs of mice with LPS-induced ALI. The results of H&E staining, cytokine levels, Wet/Dry weight ratios, and inflammatory cell counts verified the successful establishment of the murine model of ALI (Figures S1A–S1C). Moreover, flow cytometry analysis of single-cell suspensions from lungs revealed a significant increase in neutrophil infiltration in the lungs of ALI mice (Figure 1A), and this was further supported by classifying inflammatory cells in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) (Figures S1D and S1E). In addition, infiltrating neutrophils exhibited an enhanced inflammatory phenotype, characterized by the reduced expression of CD62L and increased expression of integrin CD11b and myeloperoxidase (MPO) (Figures 1B and 1C). Subsequently, we detected NET formation in lung tissues using immunofluorescence staining and Western blot. Of note, immunofluorescence demonstrated elevated NET components (MPO and Cit-H3) in ALI mice compared to the PBS group (Figures 1D and S1F), along with the increased expression of Cit-H3 (Figure 1E). These findings collectively indicate extensive NET formation in the lungs of ALI mice. Meanwhile, we further examined the correlation between CTSC and NET formation in the ALI mouse model. CTSC expression was significantly elevated in the lung tissues of ALI mice (Figures 1F and 1G). Correlation analysis showed that the severity of lung inflammation was positively correlated with CTSC expression in the LPS-induced ALI model (Figures 1H and 1I). Moreover, the expression of CTSC also exhibited a positive correlation with the level of NETs in the LPS-induced ALI model (Figure 1J). In addition, given that LPS and Staphylococcus aureus are two common triggers for ALI in mice, we selected datasets from studies of these two ALI models to further validate the involvement of CTSC in ALI. The GSE17355 and GSE208651 datasets were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database using the search terms including “acute lung injury,” “lung tissue,” or “mice.” Bioinformatic analysis of the selected GEO datasets identified 56 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between ALI samples and healthy controls (Figure S2A). Subsequent KEGG pathway analysis revealed that these DEGs were primarily associated with biological processes related to NET formation (Figure S2B). Representative genes involved in this module include CTSC, CAR1, FCGR3, C3, FPR2, and ITGB2. Notably, CTSC was the most significantly upregulated gene within the NET formation-related module (Figure S2C), suggesting its potential as a key regulator in NET formation-associated pathways during ALI.

To further dissect the specific role of neutrophils in CTSC-mediated host injury, we performed an adoptive transfer experiment, transferring WT bone marrow-derived neutrophils (BMDNs) into Ctsc−/− mice (Figure S3C). The adoptive transfer of WT BMDNs restored both the inflammatory response and NET formation in recipient mice (Figures S3D–S3F). Additionally, neutrophils infiltrating the lungs of Ctsc−/− mice exhibited elevated CD62L expression along with reduced CD11b and MPO levels, suggesting impaired neutrophil activation (Figures 2H and 2I). These results corroborate the notion that CTSC critically regulates neutrophil recruitment and NET formation following LPS challenge, further contributing to pulmonary inflammation in ALI mice.

To directly examine the effect of CTSC on NET formation, we isolated BMDNs from WT mice and Ctsc−/− mice and treated them with PMA, a potent NET inducer known to trigger robust NET release from neutrophils. Importantly, we normalized cell numbers across the groups to ensure equivalent BMDN counts. Strikingly, Ctsc−/− BMDNs exhibited markedly reduced morphological alterations and a significantly lower proportion of NETs compared to WT controls (Figures S4A, S4B, S4C, and S4D). These data provide compelling evidence for a direct, cell-intrinsic function of CTSC in facilitating NET formation, independent of its potential effects on neutrophil recruitment.

Previous studies have found that NET formation is dependent on a burst of reactive oxygen species (ROS).23 Therefore, we evaluated ROS levels and observed increased ROS production during PMA-induced NET formation (Figures S4E, S4F, S4G, and S4H; S4I). In addition, the antioxidant N-acetylcysteine (NAC) inhibited NET release and eliminated the difference between WT and Ctsc−/− cells. The above results strongly support that the CTSC regulates NET formation through a ROS-dependent mechanism.

Recombinant CTSC induces NET formation in vivo

To further evaluate the impact of CTSC on NET formation, we modified an ALI mouse model in which a single low-dose LPS (2 mg/kg) was administered 1 h after recombinant mouse CTSC (rmCTSC) treatment (Figure 3A). Western blot and immunofluorescence staining revealed that rmCTSC effectively induced NET formation in vivo (Figures 3B and 3C). Meanwhile, rmCTSC pretreatment enhanced the pulmonary inflammatory response to low-dose LPS, as evidenced by exacerbated lung inflammation (Figure 3D), increased neutrophil infiltration (Figure 3E), and significantly elevated NET formation (Figure 3F). Neutrophil extracellular trap formation is induced by macrophage-derived cathepsin C

Immunofluorescence staining of pulmonary cell populations in LPS-induced ALI mice revealed a significant increase in CTSC expression in macrophages and neutrophils after LPS treatment (Figures S5A and S5B), while expression remained negligible in lymphocytes, alveolar epithelial cells, and airway epithelial cells (Figures S5C–S5E). Given the sparse neutrophil population in healthy murine lungs, we postulated that the early-phase secretion of CTSC primarily originates from macrophages. To validate this hypothesis, we examined early (2 h), intermediate (6 h), and late (12 h) phases of ALI following LPS administration. The results showed that macrophage predominance during the early phase of ALI was accompanied by significantly increased CTSC expression in macrophages (Figure S5B). Subsequently, neutrophil predominance in the later stages was accompanied by substantially greater CTSC expression in neutrophils compared to macrophages (Figure S5A). Overall, these results demonstrate stage-specific CTSC expression patterns in ALI, with macrophages serving as the likely primary source of early-phase CTSC.

To further validate the impact of macrophage-derived CTSC on NET formation, THP-1 cells were differentiated into macrophages by PMA treatment. A conditioned medium (CM) model was established by culturing human polymorphonuclear neutrophils (PMNs) with supernatant from THP-1 cells (Figure 4A). Overexpression of CTSC in THP-1 cells was achieved through transfection, as determined by Western blot analysis (Figure 4B). Strikingly, conditioned media from CTSC-overexpressing THP-1 cells (OE-CTSC) enhanced both NET formation and CTSC expression in PMNs relative to the negative control (NC). AZD7986 (Brensocatib/INS1007) was identified as a highly potent, reversible, and selective inhibitor of human CTSC that exhibits cross-species activity against mouse, rat, dog, and rabbit CTSC.24 Prior studies demonstrated that AZD7986 attenuates disease severity in TNF-α-accelerated lupus nephritis in mice25 and that it suppresses lung metastasis in murine models of breast cancer by inhibiting CTSC activity,22 suggesting its systemic anti-inflammatory potential. Next, the CTSC inhibitor AZD7986 was used to investigate the role of the macrophage-secreted CTSC. In line with our in vivo results, LPS stimulation significantly increased CTSC expression in THP-1 cells, an effect that was attenuated by AZD7986 treatment (Figure 4B).26,27 As anticipated, PMNs exhibited elevated CTSC expression and increased NET formation, both of which were suppressed by AZD7986 (Figures 4C and 4D). Overexpression of cathepsin C in macrophages directly induced neutrophil extracellular trap formation through the activation of the PR3-IL-1β-p38 pathway

We next investigated the molecular mechanisms underlying the CTSC-mediated regulation of NETosis. Although PR3 is not the sole known substrate of CTSC,28 it could also be detected on the surface of quiescent neutrophils as inactive enzyme activity.29,30,31 Tumour-derived CTSC has also been shown to regulate neutrophil membrane-bound PR3,22 suggesting that CTSC-induced NET formation may require the activation of membrane-bound PR3. Following treatment with CM from macrophages or PMNs, the expression of membrane-bound PR3 on PMNs was significantly increased, an effect that was inhibited by AZD7986 (Figures S6A and S6B). In addition, the treatment of PMNs with recombinant human CTSC (rhCTSC) directly induced NET formation, while simultaneously upregulating membrane-bound PR3 expression on their surface (Figures 5A and 5B). To further validate the role of CTSC enzymatic activity in NET formation, we employed the enzymatically inactive pro-form of rhCTSC as a control to rule out any potential endotoxin-related contamination. Western blot analysis confirmed that pro-rhCTSC treatment did not induce NETs, supporting the notion that the enzymatic activity of CTSC is indispensable for NET formation (Figure S6C). CTSC and PR3 have previously been implicated in the production of mature IL-1β by neutrophils,32 and phosphorylated p38 MAPK is a well-known downstream target of IL-1β signaling.33 We therefore investigated their potential involvement in CTSC-induced NET formation. Consistent with the aforementioned findings, in vitro experiments confirmed the significant upregulation of PR3, IL-1β, and phosphorylated p38 (p-p38) protein following rhCTSC treatment (Figures 5B and 5C). Similarly, the elevated expression of these markers was observed in PMNs cultured with THP-1 conditioned medium (Figure S6D). In addition, after treatment with rhCTSC, ROS level in PMNs increased significantly (Figure 5D).

Next, we pretreated PMNs with the PR3 inhibitor Sivelestat and the p38 MAPK inhibitor SB203580 prior to rhCTSC administration and subsequently assessed NET formation. Sivelestat significantly suppressed p38 phosphorylation, NET formation, and ROS level (Figures 5E–5G). The functional relationship between CTSC and p38 MAPK activation is well-documented, particularly in promoting hepatocellular carcinoma metastasis through p38 MAPK activation in hepatocellular carcinoma cells.34 Our findings suggest that p38 MAPK activation may similarly regulate ROS-dependent NET formation (Figures 5H–5J). Accordingly, SB203580 treatment effectively attenuated CTSC-induced NET formation. Together, these results demonstrate that CTSC promotes NET formation through the activation of the PR3-IL-1β-p38 signaling pathway.

Neutrophil-secreted cathepsin C mediates an autocrine positive feedback loop driving excessive neutrophil extracellular trap formation

Given that neutrophils themselves can express CTSC, we investigated whether secreted CTSC could promote further synthesis and release of CTSC from neutrophils, thereby creating a positive feedback loop that amplifies the overproduction of NETs. To investigate the role of neutrophil-derived CTSC in NET formation, we isolated BMDNs from WT mice and Ctsc−/− mice and treated them with rmCTSC. Following rmCTSC treatment, we observed significantly elevated NET levels along with increased CTSC expression and secretion in WT BMDNs (Figures 6A–6D). In addition, when WT BMDNs were cultured with CM from WT rmCTSC-treated BMDNs, we detected enhanced NET formation and increased CTSC expression and secretion (Figures 6E–6H). Blockade of cathepsin C or PR3 inhibits neutrophil extracellular trap formation and attenuates pulmonary inflmmation and injury in acute lung injury mice

Recognizing the pivotal role of CTSC in NET formation, we sought to evaluate whether CTSC inhibition could represent an effective strategy for ALI treatment. Encouragingly, the oral administration of AZD7986 markedly ameliorated both pulmonary inflammation and edema (Figures 7A and S7A–S7C). Specifically, the infiltration of neutrophils (Figures S7D and S7E) and the formation of NET (Figures 7B–7D) in the lungs of ALI mice were substantially inhibited after AZD7986 treatment. To further investigate PR3 involvement in ALI, PR3 inhibitor Sivelestat was also administered in the ALI model. As expected, Sivelestat treatment effectively inhibited NET formation and alleviated the severity of lung inflammation in ALI mice (Figures 7E–7H and S7F–S7I). Discussion

NETs are neutrophil extracellular trap structures that participate in inflammatory conditions and antimicrobial defense mechanisms.5,35,36,37,38 Studies have shown that NETs form extensively in the lungs and are closely associated with the pathogenesis of ALI.39,40 Nevertheless, the precise molecular mechanisms governing NET formation remain incompletely understood. Our investigation demonstrates that CTSC overexpression triggers the robust formation of NETs in the lungs of LPS-stressed ALI mice via the PR3-IL-1β-p38 signaling pathway. Specifically, LPS stimulation markedly elevates CTSC expression in macrophages. This, in turn, promotes the autocrine secretion of CTSC from neutrophils, ultimately leading to excessive NET formation through the activation of the PR3-IL-1β-p38 pathway. The inhibition of either CTSC or PR3 substantially diminished NET formation and attenuated inflammatory injury in the lungs of ALI mice. Collectively, our findings elucidate the molecular mechanisms of NET formation in LPS-stressed ALI models and highlight the therapeutic potential of targeting CTSC for the treatment of NET-associated ALI.

Accumulating evidence has shown that NETs accumulate significantly in the lungs of both patients with ALI and murine models. Our study further corroborates the detrimental role of NETs’ accumulation in ALI progression. Specifically, there is a significant positive correlation between elevated levels of NETs and the severity of inflammatory lung injury in the LPS-induced ALI mouse model. Consistent with our findings, peer studies have confirmed that DNase I, a NETs-degrading enzyme, markedly attenuates lung inflammation in ALI mice.39 Neutrophils represent the predominant inflammatory cells in ALI, playing a critical role in both immunoregulation and tissue regeneration.41,42,43 While neutrophil recruitment to the lung is crucial for pathogen clearance, the excessive and prolonged presence may result in the substantial accumulation of NETs and uncontrolled release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, ultimately exacerbating lung injury.44 Particularly, NETs contribute to ALI progression through both direct and indirect mechanisms of lung damage, including the disruption of lung endothelial and epithelial barriers.45 Consequently, clinical studies have also shown that inhaling rhDnase I reduces NETs burden in COVID-19-associated patients with ARDS, thereby improving lung compliance and gas exchange.46 Therefore, strategies targeting the excessive accumulation of NETs may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for ALI treatment, as they can mitigate the harmful effects of neutrophils while preserving their beneficial effects.

To identify the potentially effective targets for inhibiting NETs, we further investigated the molecular mechanism underlying excessive NET formation in the lungs of ALI mice. Of particular note, the expression level of CTSC in the lungs is significantly positively correlated with both NET formation and ALI severity in the LPS-induced ALI mouse model. In contrast, CTSC knockout substantially attenuated NET formation and ameliorated pulmonary inflammation. Supporting these findings, the involvement of CTSC in NET formation has been validated in another Staphylococcus aureus infected model.47 CTSC, a lysosomal cysteine protease, potentiates neutrophil activity and microbicidal ability by activating NSPs.48 While cathepsin functions are typically associated with lysosomal localization, CTSC exhibits distinct expression patterns, being predominantly expressed in pulmonary, splenic, renal, hepatic tissues, and myeloid cell lineages. The expression level of CTSC is regulated through proteolytic processing by CTSL and CTSS.48 Accordingly, the activation of CTSC may exhibit tissue- and cell-type-specific variations. Importantly, the CTSC-mediated promotion of NET formation has also been documented in both patients with asthma and patients with breast cancer with lung metastases.20,22 Collectively, these findings establish CTSC as a critical regulator of NET formation in ALI, simultaneously highlighting its potential as both a diagnostic biomarker and therapeutic target for ALI intervention.

Moreover, our findings substantiate that CTSC overexpression drives excessive NET formation by activating the PR3-IL-1β-p38 signaling pathway in ALI mice. Previous research on CTSC has principally concentrated on its function as a lysosomal protease regulating NSP activity.16 In comparison with other NSPs, PR3 is predominantly localized on the plasma membrane of quiescent neutrophils in an enzyme-inactivated state.28,49 Consistent with this, our results demonstrate that the secreted CTSC could directly activate membrane-associated PR3 on neutrophils.22 The CTSC-mediated PR3 activation has also been further implicated in the leukocyte production of mature IL-1β, representing an inflammasome-independent mechanism for IL-1β activation.32 Parallel studies indicate that IL-1β facilitates NET formation in both neoplastic and inflammatory conditions.50,51,52,53 Additionally, existing literature documents that IL-1β can activate p38 MAPK54 and play an essential role in ALI/ARDS-associated inflammatory responses,55 with additional connections to ROS-dependent NET formation.23 Therefore, the CTSC-PR3-IL-1β-p38 axis constitutes a crucial regulatory mechanism for NETs generation in ALI pathogenesis.

Although CTSC overexpression has been established as a crucial inducer of NET formation and ALI pathogenesis, the primary cellular source of CTSC in the lung remains unclear. Previous studies have demonstrated that CTSC is abundantly expressed in various immune cells, including neutrophils, mast cells, macrophages, and their precursors.56,57 In agreement with these reports, our data showed that, during the early stage of ALI, elevated CTSC secretion by macrophages directly induces neutrophil activation and subsequent NET formation. CTSC has been documented to modulate neutrophil activity through membrane-associated PR3,22 suggesting that soluble CTSC can activate membrane-bound PR3, thereby enhancing CTSC expression and secretion in neutrophils. This mechanism has been further verified using both BMDNs and naive BMDNs in conditional culture models. Furthermore, during the inflammatory outburst phase of ALI, augmented CTSC secretion from neutrophils amplifies massive neutrophil infiltration, ultimately promoting additional NET formation through a self-reinforcing autocrine loop. These findings may provide novel mechanistic insights into the exacerbated inflammatory responses and tissue injury observed in ALI models. Consequently, therapeutic targeting of either CTSC or PR3-IL-1β-p38 pathway not only reduces the formation of NET but also disrupts the CTSC-mediated positive feedback cascade in neutrophils, consequently alleviating pulmonary inflammatory damage in ALI.

In summary, our study demonstrated that CTSC overexpression drives excessive NET formation and exacerbates inflammatory injury in the lungs of ALI mice. Furthermore, we established that the CTSC-PR3-IL-1β-p38 signaling pathway in neutrophils is critical for NET formation and ALI progression. Elucidation of this CTSC-mediated molecular network in NET formation may provide novel therapeutic strategies for ALI intervention.

Limitations of the study

Our findings elucidate that CTSC overexpression promotes NET formation through the activation of the PR3-IL-1β-p38 signaling pathway, which plays a pivotal role in pulmonary inflammatory injury in ALI. Nevertheless, we acknowledge several limitations in the present study. Firstly, due to the transient lifespan of neutrophils, the direct verification of CTSC overexpression at the cellular level in neutrophils remains technically challenging. Secondly, further research is required to characterize the functional properties of minor CTSC secreted by other cell types (e.g., epithelial cells) and to elucidate the precise regulatory mechanisms governing CTSC production in neutrophils. Thirdly, while we have examined exogenous CTSC-mediated activation of membrane-associated PR3 in neutrophils, subsequent studies should employ PR3-deficient neutrophils to evaluate whether membrane-bound PR3 reciprocally regulates CTSC expression or secretion. Furthermore, systemic PR3 inhibition may exert unavoidable off-target effects. We observed low-level Cit-H3 expression in the lungs of PBS-treated animals. This background signal may arise from constitutive cellular turnover or unidentified sources, though further investigation is required to confirm its origin. Finally, we used only male mice in this study in order to minimize variability associated with hormonal fluctuations and to maintain consistency with previously established ALI models. Future studies will be necessary to validate whether the observed mechanisms of CTSC-mediated NET formation and ALI pathogenesis are conserved in both sexes.

Experimental model and subject details

Mouse models

Animals were handled and housed in accordance with the local ethical guidelines and regulations. The animals were kept under standard conditions with a 12-h light/dark cycle and food and water available at all times. These animal studies were approved by the Xiangya Animal Protection and Use Committee of Central South University (CSU-2024-0181), and all the animal work took place in the Department of Laboratory Animals of Central South University. Male mice aged 6–8 weeks were used in this study unless indicated otherwise. Ctsc−/− mice were generated in 129/SvJ,58 and backcrossed to C57BL/6J for >10 generations.

Cell lines

Tohoku Hospital Paediatrics-1 cells (THP-1) was cultured in RPMI 1640 medium with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% antibiotic solution. THP-1 cells were differentiated into macrophages by 3h incubation with 100 nmol/L phorbol-12-myristate-13-acetate (PMA).59

Method details

Mouse experiments

C57BL/6J mice aged 6–10 weeks were used in all animal experiments. To establish the ALI model, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and then subjected to intranasal instillation of LPS.60 Mice were induced with 50 μL LPS (5 mg/kg, 2 mg/mL; Sigma). The control group received an appropriate volume of PBS. Twelve hours after injection, mice were sacrificed, and tissues were harvested for analysis. For recombinant mouse CTSC (rmCTSC) treatment, mice were induced with 20 μL rmCTSC (1 μg/20 μL; R&D). For AZD7986 or Sivelestat treatment, mice were administered intranasally with administered orally with CTSC inhibitor AZD7986 (5 mg/kg; MCE) or intraperitoneally treated with proteinase 3 (PR3) inhibitor Sivelestat (100 mg/kg; Selleck), respectively.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

After fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde, the lung tissues were embedded in paraffin and tissue sections of 3 μm thick were prepared for further analysis. Lung paraffin Sections were stained with Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) according to our previous publications.61 Images were captured using an optical microscope: the nucleus and cytoplasm were stained blue and red, respectively.

ELISA

ELISA kits were used to assay the levels of mouse IL-1β (Biolegend, China), mouse TNF-α (Biolegend, China), mouse IL-6 (Biolegend, China) and mouse CTSC (Camilo, China) in cell culture supernatants or BALF according to the manufacturer’s instruction. Briefly, 96-well ELISA Plates (Biolegend) was coated with capture antibody over-night at 4°C in coating buffer (15 mM of Na2CO3, 35 mM of NaHCO3, at pH 9.6). Afterfour washes in wash buffer (0.05% Tween 20 in PBS), the wells were blocked with 5% BSA in PBS for 1h at room temperature. Then, 100 μL of cell culture supernatants or sera and standard samples were added into the wells and incubated for 2 h at room temperature, and the plates were washed four times with wash buffer. Next, biotin-conjugated detection antibody in 5% BSA was added into the wells and incubated for 1h at room temperature. After five washes with wash buffer, HRP-streptavidin was incubated for 15 min at room temperature. Stop reaction by adding 100 μL of Stop Solution to each well. Then, signals were detected using TMB solution and read at 450 nm.

Lung wet/dry weight measurement

Lung samples were collected after LPS stimulation and immediately weighed (wet weight) after removal. Then, they were dried in an oven at 60°C for 48 h for dry weight measurement.62 Tissue edema was assessed by measuring the Lung wet/dry (W/D) ratio.

Immunohistochemical staining and immunofluorescence staining

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining and immunofluorescence (IF) staining were performed as previously described.63 Briefly, tissue sections were deparaffinized, hydrated, and subjected to antigenic retrieval by incubation with Tris-EDTA Buffer (10 mM TrisBase, 1 mM EDTA Solution, and 0.05% Tween 20, pH 9.0) at 98°C for 20 min. Sections were treated with 5% BSA in deionized water for 30 min and then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. The coverslips were then washed three times in PBS-T (5 min for each wash) and then incubated with both secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Then the coverslips were washed three times with PBS for 5 min each in the dark and the cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. After the coverslips were mounted with a drop of mounting media, the cells were examined under the microscope Leica/DM3000 LED. The following primary antibodies were used, respectively: CTSC (sc-74590; Santa Cruz), Cit-H3 (ab5103; Abcam), MPO (AF3667; R&D), PR3 (25278-1-AP; Proteintech), F4/80 (29414-1-AP; Proteintech), CD3 (17617-1-AP; Proteintech), SPC (DF6647; Affinity), and CC10 (sc-365992; Santa Cruz).

To visually observe the morphology of NETs, PMNs and BMDNs were incubated with 1 μM of SYTOX Green dye (BioLegend) for 15 min at room temperature in the dark.

Western blot analysis

Lung tissue samples were lysed by RIPA lysis buffer containing 1% PMSF. Cultured cells were harvested and lysed in 1× SDS Loading Buffer. The lysates were centrifuged at 13,800g for 10 min at 4°C and the concentration of protein in the supernatant was determined using BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Then, Western blot was performed according to the method previously described.61 The following antibodies were used to determine the expression of corresponding protein: CTSC (sc-74590; Santa Cruz), citrullinated histone H3 (Cit-H3) (ab5103; Abcam), PR3 (25278-1-AP; Proteintech), IL-1β (AF5103; Affinity), p38 (AF6456; Affinity), p-p38 (AF4001; Affinity). β-Tubulin (10094-1-AP; Proteintech) and GAPDH (AF7021; Affinity) were used as corresponding controls, respectively.

Flow cytometry

Single-cell suspension of lungs was prepared as previously described for flow cytometry analysis.64 At first, live/dead aqua (Zombie NIRTM Dye-APC/Cy7; 423105; BioLegend) viability dye was used to exclude dead cells. Then, cells were surface stained with the following antibody cocktail: CD45-FITC (157608; BioLegend), CD11b-PEcy7 (101216; BioLegend), Ly6G-APC (127614; BioLegend), CD62L-PE (161204; BioLegend) and isotype controls were incubated to detect different cell subpopulations. A plot of CD11b versus Ly-6G gated on CD45+ cells was used to identify mouse neutrophils (CD11b+Ly-6G+). On this gating, the activation of CD11b and CD62L was evaluated. All data were collected by a FACSCalibur cytometer (BD) and analyzed by FlowJo software. Fluorescence intensities reported are geometric mean values of each antibody corrected for the geometric mean values of respective isotype control.

Isolation of PMNs

Human polymorphonuclear neutrophils (PMNs) and bone marrow-derived neutrophils (BMDNs) from mice were isolated. PMNs were isolated from the peripheral blood of healthy volunteers using a 3-gradient Percoll separation (75%, 62%, 45%) starting from 5 mL of blood (17089101; Cyita).65 After centrifugation for 30 min (250×g) at 20°C, granulocytes, located at the interface between 75% and 62% Percoll solutions, were collected, diluted in Ca2+ and Mg2+ free HEPES-buffered saline solution containing BSA (HBSS-BSA, 145 mM NaCl, 5 mM KCl, 5 mM HEPES, 5 mM glucose, 0.2% BSA, pH 7.4), and centrifuged for 5 min at 200×g. The purity of neutrophils was confirmed to be >95% by flow cytometric analysis and Wright-Giemsa stain.

Isolation of BMDNs

BMDNs were purified from bone marrow by Percoll gradient centrifugation. Briefly, bone marrow cells were harvested from mice in ice-cold HBSS (without Ca2+ and Mg2+, containing 0.5% BSA). After centrifugation at 250×g for 30 min at 20°C, the cells were layered on a 3-step Percoll gradient (78%, 65%, 55%).66 Cells at the 78%–65% interface were collected and washed. After purification, neutrophil viability was >95%, as detected by trypan blue staining. Purity was typically >90%, as determined by morphological examination of Giemsa staining and flow cytometry based on cell surface staining of CD11b+ and Ly6G+.

Adoptive transfer of BMDNs

5.0×106 purified BMDNs resuspended in sterile 4°C PBS. BMDNs were adoptively transferred via intravenous tail injection into Ctsc−/− mice 2 h before LPS treatment.47

Cell treatment

A conditioned medium model was carried out to assess the crosstalk between macrophages and neutrophils according to the previous method with minor modifications.67 For the conditioned medium-exposure experiment, 24 h after LPS (1 μg/mL) stimulation, the culture medium of THP-1 was replaced, and cells were further cultured for another 24 h. Then, a cell-debris-free culture medium was harvested and incubated with PMNs. This enables the assessment of the effect of soluble mediators released from THP-1 on the phenotype of PMNs.

Intracellular reactive oxygen species assay

Intracellular ROS assay was measured by the reactive oxygen species (ROS) Assay Kit (S0033; Beyotime). Neutrophils were seeded into a 12-well culture plate. The fluorescence intensity of DCFH-DA was observed under an Olympus microscope, and images were captured.68

Quantification and statistical analysis

For all bar graphs, data are expressed as the mean ± SEM. Prism 9 software (graphic software) was used for statistical analysis and to generate the graphs. The specific statistical tests used are stated in each figure legend. Comparisons between the 2 groups were performed with the Mann-Whitney U test. Differences in means between multiple groups were made by one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s post hoc test. P < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant (∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, NS, non-significant).

Quantification of Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

Different variables observed in lung tissue were measured to assess the degrees of lung injury, grading four histological findings: haemorrhage, alveolar congestion, filtration, or aggregation of neutrophils in the airspace or vessel wall, and thickness of the alveolar wall/hyaline membrane formation. Next, lung injury was scored using a four-point scale: none (0), mild (1), moderate (2), or severe (3) by three pathologists blinded to the experimental conditions.

Quantification of western blot band intensities

Quantification of protein band intensities was carried out in Fiji using the Analyze->Gels command. Composed protein band and molecular weight marker images were used for quantification. Rectangular selection, big enough to include the biggest band, was used to select each protein band. Each lane was plotted using the Plot lanes command, and if necessary, a straight line was drawn to close off the area under the curve. Intensity was measured for each band. Representative blots are shown, while the quantification graphs summarize the results from all biological replicates.

Quantification of immunofluorescence

Quantification of immunofluorescence signals was performed using ImageJ software (NIH, USA). For all figures where immunofluorescence staining was quantified, fluorescence intensities of target proteins were measured within defined regions of interest (ROIs). To control for differences in cell density across samples, the integrated fluorescence intensity was normalized to the DAPI signal from the same field. For the quantification of NET formation, we determined the percentage of MPO+CitH3+ double-positive cells relative to the total neutrophil population (MPO+ cells). All quantification was carried out in a blinded manner from at least three independent experiments.
