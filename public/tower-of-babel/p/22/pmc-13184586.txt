Comparison of examination performance and test anxiety during bell‐paced and self‐paced gross anatomy laboratory examinations
Authors: Danielle C. Bentley, Angelo Sotto, Peter Lombardi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPacing during anatomy laboratory examinations may influence both student performance and test anxiety. This study compared bell‐paced (BP) and self‐paced (SP) timing structures to assess their impact on students' test performance, test anxiety, and pacing preferences. Undergraduate students (N = 35) across two academic years completed two anatomy laboratory exams under randomized crossover conditions: bell‐pacing (60 s/station, forward progression signaled by bell) and self‐pacing (balanced for total time, student‐directed forward progression). A modified State–Trait Anxiety Inventory measured three novel anxiety metrics: anticipatory test anxiety (pre‐exam), consequent test anxiety (post‐exam), and reactive test anxiety (pre‐post change). Mixed‐effects models assessed effects of pacing condition on exam performance and test anxiety (anticipatory and consequent), controlling for repeated measures. Open‐text student feedback was thematically analyzed. Exam performance was consistent between pacing conditions (BP: 82.8% ± 6.8%; SP: 81.1% ± 8.1%, β = 2.07, 95% CI [−2.45, 6.58]). Although the overall pacing effect on test anxiety was not significant, post‐hoc comparisons revealed that the BP condition significantly increased mean reactive test anxiety (M = 5.67, p < 0.01), whereas SP did not. Despite that, most students (23/35; 66%) still preferred bell‐pacing. Qualitative student feedback revealed that BP offered organization and familiarity, while SP provided flexibility and autonomy. Most students paradoxically preferred a pacing structure that made them more anxious, suggesting that preferences need not necessarily align with an associated psychological benefit. These findings underscored the complexity of assessment design and the importance of considering both academic and psychological outcomes in this process.

INTRODUCTION

Over recent years, test anxiety has become an increasingly prevalent concern for students, with estimates suggesting that over 50% of the general student population experiences moderate to severe test anxiety. 1 This has been corroborated by another study reporting that about 50% of students underrepresented in medicine experience moderate to extreme levels of test anxiety related to the Medical College Admission Test and that 95% of these students express a lack of self‐confidence in their test‐taking abilities. 2 Students who feel high levels of test anxiety are more likely to experience burnout, depression, poor academic and clinical performance, poor decision‐making, academic dishonesty, and sleep disruptions. 3

Among students in the allied health professions, test anxiety has been found to be particularly high during practical examinations, such as objective structured clinical examinations and laboratory examinations. 3 , 4 In anatomy, laboratory examinations assess a student's ability to recall factual knowledge about gross structures and their features. 4 Often called spotter exams, steeplechase exams, or infamously, bell‐ringer exams, this assessment method has a long‐standing history and is utilized across all levels of healthcare education. During a traditional bell‐ringer exam, students linearly progress through sequential question stations at a predetermined pace, starting at one station and advancing to the next, following the sound of a bell chime. Students often find the anatomy bell‐ringer exam stressful, likely due to its rigidity, and many perform poorly due to the difficulty of applying lecture content in a high‐pressure practical setting under restrictive, timed conditions. 4

Previous research among graduate‐level physiotherapy students found that an untimed anatomy laboratory examination led to a significant increase in academic performance, with a concomitant reduction in test anxiety. 5 However, the academic benefit of untimed exams is not consistent across all contexts, with medical students reporting that untimed exams did not affect academic performance. 6 While students have been found to prefer untimed exams, 5 removing time constraints from summative laboratory assessments introduces logistical limitations with respect to scheduling and space allocations and may artificially inflate grades. Further, untimed examinations fail to reflect the time‐pressured conditions essential to healthcare practice, which may undermine alignment with professional expectations.

Therefore, this research sought to experimentally assess a laboratory examination design that leveraged pacing structure with the goal of reducing test anxiety while maintaining academic rigor: an anatomy laboratory examination with a fixed total time that allowed for self‐pacing, in which students could progress through sequential question stations at their own discretion. The overall aim was to experimentally compare the self‐paced and bell‐paced timing structures among undergraduate students completing laboratory examinations as part of an advanced anatomy course. The specific research objectives were: (1) to validate the academic rigor of the self‐paced timing structure, with the hypothesis that examination performance would be consistent between self‐paced and bell‐paced conditions; (2) to compare test anxiety between the two pacing structures, with the hypothesis that self‐pacing would mitigate test anxiety; and (3) to explore student perceptions of the two pacing structures using their self‐reported preferences and associated feedback. This research is crucial for developing evidence‐based assessments that uphold rigorous academic and vocational standards while minimizing test anxiety and improving the learning experience for undergraduate students.

METHODS

Participant recruitment and previous work

Institutional research ethics board approval was obtained from the University of Toronto (REB #36496). This research was conducted over two successive academic cohorts of ANA400H1: Anatomy Dissection (Faculty of Arts & Science, University of Toronto), an advanced undergraduate course in human dissection. While all enrolled students received the research Letter of Information and were exposed to the experimental design as requisite elements of their course completion, only data from consenting students were extracted and analyzed. As such, the only inclusion criterion was enrollment in and completion of ANA400H1.

Portions of this research have been previously disseminated. Results from the first cohort of students were presented at the Experimental Biology Conference, 7 and the preliminary analysis of the combined cohorts was presented at the University of Toronto Teaching and Learning Symposium. 8 This manuscript represents the first dissemination of the full analysis of data from both cohorts.

Course description and examination structure

Nominally a fourth‐year course, ANA400H1 is a half‐year (0.5 credit) advanced anatomy dissection course offered to a limited number of undergraduate students based on academic record. The course was delivered over 12 weeks with 4 h/week organized as follows: 1 h student lecture, 1 h tutorial, and 2 h regional dissection on a human donor. Students also had access to the dissection laboratory for an additional 4 h/week of optional dissection time, utilized at their discretion. Course assessments were identical between the two cohorts and included two non‐cumulative, summative laboratory examinations: a midterm exam on the first half of the course material, weighing 35% of students' final grade, and a final exam on the second half of course material, also weighing 35%. The remainder of the course grade was allocated to a didactic lecture presentation (15%), weekly dissection completion (10%), and weekly tutorial participation (5%).

Experimental design

This research employed a randomized crossover design to experimentally compare the two pacing structures of interest, self‐paced and bell‐paced, during two laboratory examinations. Laboratory examinations consisted of 28 to 31 specimen stations, with three evenly dispersed rest stations. Each specimen station comprised two questions (A/B), primarily focused on identification of anatomical structures, with interspersed functional and application‐based extension questions. The exam set‐up, questions, and overall time limit remained identical over the two cohorts and between the two different pacing conditions. During the bell‐paced (BP) condition, students had 60 s to complete each specimen station, with their progression between stations signaled by the sound of a bell chime. During the self‐paced (SP) condition, no bell chimes or other auditory aids were used, with students permitted to progress between specimen stations at their own discretion. In either condition, total test time was standardized, and students progressed through the specimen stations linearly without the option to revisit previously completed stations.

Students arrived at their midterm laboratory examination unaware of the pacing condition they were to complete. Using a random assortment generator, half of the students were assigned to the BP condition and completed the exam first, while the remaining half of the students were assigned to the SP condition and kept in a waiting area. Once the BP‐assigned students completed their exam, they were escorted out of the examination room, allowing the SP‐assigned students to start their exam. All students then crossed over to the alternate experimental pacing condition for the final laboratory examination.

Questionnaires, measures, and timepoints of data collection

Aligned with previous research in the field, students' test anxiety was measured before (pre‐exam) and after (post‐exam) both examinations (MIDTERM, FINAL) using a modified State–Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI). 5 The original STAI, consisting of 40 items scored /160 total, uses a four‐point directional Likert scale to assess both state anxiety (20 items, scored /80), which is transient and related to momentary emotions, as well as trait anxiety (20 items, scored /80), which is more stable and related to one's trait characteristics and personality type. 9 For the present study, only items pertaining to state anxiety were assessed, with the original STAI modified in accordance with previous research in the context of anatomy education, with five of the state anxiety statements (“I feel strained,” “I am jittery,” “I feel content,” “I feel steady,” and “I feel pleasant”) eliminated due to concept overlap. 5 As such, the modified STAI used in this study, displayed in Table 1, is scored /60 and is composed of 15 state anxiety items from the original STAI.

Test anxiety has been broadly studied across academic environments, with inconsistencies in measurement strategy, timing of anxiety measurements, and associated terminology. To create much‐needed consistency, specific operational definitions were created by our research team and proposed accordingly. Anticipatory test anxiety is defined as the acute anxiety one experiences in advance of an assessment and can objectively be measured using the state‐specific portion of the STAI directly prior to beginning such an assessment. Consequent test anxiety is defined as the acute anxiety one experiences following an assessment and can objectively be measured using the state‐specific portion of the STAI following the completion of such an assessment. Reactive test anxiety is defined as the change in anxiety resulting from the assessment itself and is calculated as: reactive test anxiety=consequent test anxiety−anticipatory test anxiety

A positive reactive test anxiety score indicates an anxiety‐inducing test environment, while a negative score indicates an anxiety‐mitigating test environment. With these new operational definitions in place, key findings of this work and their broader implications can be discussed.

The modified STAI was only one component of a research questionnaire that was administered at four timepoints throughout the ANA400H1 course: pre‐MIDTERM (following random assignment of pacing condition), post‐MIDTERM, pre‐FINAL, and post‐FINAL. Other information collected at these timepoints included: student demographics (collected pre‐MIDTERM); open‐text, written feedback on the completed pacing condition (collected post‐MIDTERM and post‐FINAL); and binary selection of preferred pacing structure with an associated open‐text, written explanation (collected post‐FINAL).

Bias and positions of power differential

As is standard within our research group, the initial research design considered positions of power among members of the study team a priori. To control for unintentional bias on the part of the instructor/researcher (DCB), a research assistant was directly responsible for all research‐specific interactions with students. This included both the initial explanation of the research and the dissemination of the associated Letter of Information at course onset, as well as the process of acquiring research consent. For the acquisition of consent, the research assistant asked the prospective student participants to either provide or deny their consent for data extraction using an online survey tool (Alchemer, Louisville, CO, USA) only after final course grades were submitted to the institutional registrar. This same research assistant then extracted, transferred, and de‐identified all quantitative and qualitative data from consenting students before analysis by DCB, with additional analyses later conducted by AS. Although DCB was the ANA400H1 course instructor, the lead PI, and held the associated research grant, the only data they worked with was de‐identified student data from consenting students. These measures were taken to intentionally reduce instructor/researcher bias, minimize potential feelings of coercion to participate, and remove any potential fear of academic retribution. Ultimately, this contributes to the validity of all collected data and the rigor of the results.

In alignment with research objectives (1) and (2), which were to validate the academic rigor of the self‐paced structure and compare test anxiety between the BP and SP conditions respectively, three separate mixed‐effects models were constructed. These models included participants as a random effect to account for repeated measures between the two different exams, the four timepoints (pre‐ and post‐ each exam), and the two pacing conditions. These random effects allowed for variability in intercept estimates across participants, accounting for individual differences in baseline exam scores and anxiety levels. The three models were as follows: (A) exam scores were analyzed with assigned pacing condition (BP/SP) and exam type (MIDTERM/FINAL) as fixed effects, to assess whether self‐pacing impacted examination performance; (B) STAI scores were analyzed with assigned pacing condition (BP/SP) and timepoint (anticipatory/consequent) as fixed effects, to determine whether self‐pacing mitigated test anxiety; and (C) reactive test anxiety was analyzed with assigned pacing condition (BP/SP) and preferred pacing condition (BP/SP) as fixed effects, to explore changes in test anxiety between students' preferred and assigned pacing conditions. While Model (C) does not directly align with any of the aforementioned research objectives, it served as an exploratory model to determine whether students' assignment to their preferred pacing condition impacted their reactive test anxiety. Similarly, point‐biserial correlation (r) then assessed the relationship between preferred pacing condition (BP/SP) and reactive test anxiety for both exams. Given prior evidence that women are more vulnerable to test anxiety than men, 10 , 11 students' self‐identified gender (man/woman) was retained as a covariate in mixed‐effects models (B) and (C).

Assumptions of normality, homoscedasticity, and absence of collinearity were checked for the mixed‐effects models. Normality of residuals and random effects was assessed using Q‐Q plots. Homoscedasticity (i.e., homogeneity of variances across groups) was confirmed via residual vs. fitted value plots. Collinearity among fixed effect predictors was evaluated using variance inflation factors, with a value of VIF < 10 indicating no concern for collinearity. Outliers were identified using boxplots, and sensitivity analyses were conducted with and without outliers (defined as mean ± 2 SD) to ensure the robustness of the findings. Post‐hoc comparisons were performed using estimated marginal means with the emmeans package in R (v1.10.5). The Kenward‐Roger approximation was applied to calculate degrees of freedom, improving the accuracy of p‐values and confidence intervals for mixed‐effects models. Tukey's adjustment was used to control for family‐wise error rate in pairwise comparisons within each exam, as comparisons were made between pacing conditions at each timepoint. Quantitative analyses were conducted using R (version 4.4.1, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, with results expressed as mean ± SD unless otherwise indicated.

Qualitative analysis

In alignment with research objective (3), which was to explore student perceptions of the two pacing structures and their preferences, students were asked to provide written feedback about the pacing condition they had been assigned to, using the following prompt:In the space below, please provide open and honest feedback regarding the pacing structure of the exam. Be as detailed as possible.

In the space below, please provide open and honest feedback regarding the pacing structure of the exam. Be as detailed as possible.

Following completion of the FINAL exam, students were asked to indicate their preferred pacing structure (binary selection as either self‐paced or bell‐paced), as well as to justify their choice using the following prompt:Explain why this is your preferred pacing structure.

Explain why this is your preferred pacing structure.

To minimize researcher bias and to allow authentic reflections from participants to guide the analysis, a priori hypotheses specific to written feedback were not developed. Instead, the sequential steps of framework analysis were followed, 12 with minor modification to incorporate previous work. Specifically, students' comments were coded using a previously created codebook for a similar project that examined laboratory examination pacing conditions. 13 This codebook was created in the following way: First, open‐text written responses from students were read, in duplicate, for clarity and comprehension by DCB in advance of coding. Then, a descriptive method of coding was followed such that the names of the generated codes summarized a section of text. 14 All codes were iteratively created and refined by DCB as new ideas emerged from the dataset. The resulting codebook included the name of each code, an associated description, and instructions on how/when to apply the code. For the current project, two independent rounds of coding were completed by DCB and AS with reliability of the codebook assessed using Cohen's (inter‐coder) kappa. An overall κ > 0.8 indicates very good inter‐coder reliability, with individual codes with κ < 0.6 identified and examined. Codes were then tallied, and case counts for each code were converted into percentages. Qualitative analysis was conducted using NVivo for Mac, v15 (QRS International, Melbourne, Australia); representative quotes were extracted verbatim with [comments from authors] as required for clarity.

Author reflexivity

The two independent coders completed the reflexivity activity described by Dueñas et al. 12 , creating independent reflexivity statements that describe their own positionality and contextualize the associated qualitative analysis.

DCB: “While I continue to expand my use of qualitative methods, I was trained as a health outcomes researcher and often rely on quantitative methods within my SoTL project designs. I consider myself to hold two primary paradigms, which ebb and flow depending on the research query: constructivism and positivism. In this specific research context, I believe that objective measures of anxiety and exam performance metrics reveal truth (positivism), while simultaneously believing that students will experience the anatomy lab exam in different ways, each independently valid and worthy of further exploration (constructivism).”

AS: “I am both a researcher and a learner, shaped by training in anatomy and epidemiology, and by having once sat in the very course now under study. I approach research with a pragmatic stance, valuing rigour and structure, yet drawn to meaning and lived experiences. The dual perspective of student/emerging educator made me curious about how students articulate anxiety and engagement, while also empathetic to their challenges. I recognize that these dual roles inevitably coloured my interpretation of the data, but I embrace this as part of the richness of qualitative analysis.”

RESULTS

Thirty‐six students enrolled in the ANA400H course across the two successive academic years. Of those, one student withdrew from the second cohort. All N = 35 eligible students (100%) provided consent for research participation and associated data extraction following course completion. In addition, all 35 (100%) student participants provided at least one codable open‐text reflection for qualitative analysis with an associated initial inter‐coder κ = 0.83, indicating high inter‐coder agreement and reliability of the codebook. Further stratification revealed two codes with κ < 0.6, which were discussed and independently re‐coded. A secondary agreement value for those two codes of κ = 0.82 was achieved. Combined response length across all three reflection prompts was relatively short, averaging 117 words with a range of 33 to 379.

The majority of students self‐identified as women (n = 27, 77%) and most were in the fourth year of their undergraduate program (n = 21, 60%) (Table 2). The last mixed‐effects model assessed the effects of assigned pacing condition (BP/SP) and preferred pacing structure (BP/SP) on reactive test anxiety (defined as the difference between consequent and anticipatory test anxiety), using participants as a random effect to account for individual variability and repeated measures. The majority of students preferred the BP structure (23/35, 66%) across both Cohort 1 (14/20, 70%) and Cohort 2 (9/15, 60%) (Table 2). There was a significant main effect of preferred pacing structure (β = −13.19, 95% CI [−20.35, −6.04]) on reactive test anxiety, suggesting that students who preferred BP, overall, experienced lower reactive test anxiety than those who preferred SP when controlling for assigned pacing condition and gender. However, post‐hoc comparisons revealed that among students who preferred BP, reactive test anxiety was greater when they were assigned to the BP condition (2.14 ± 2.41) than when they were assigned to the SP condition (−5.60 ± 2.41; M = 7.73 ± 2.83, p = 0.01) (Figure 3). The same pairwise comparisons also revealed that among students who preferred SP, reactive test anxiety was marginally greater when they were assigned to the SP condition (7.59 ± 3.05) than when they were assigned to the BP condition (6.16 ± 3.05, M = 1.43 ± 3.92, p = 0.72), though this did not reach conventional significance. Lastly, when assigned to the SP condition, those who preferred SP experienced greater reactive test anxiety than those who preferred BP (M = −13.19 ± 3.74, p < 0.01) (Figure 3). These results collectively suggest that students preferred a pacing structure that made them more acutely anxious, independent of the pacing structure itself.

Open‐text feedback from 23 (66%) students who provided acclamations towards the bell‐paced structure identified two key underlying themes (Table 3). Of these 23 students, 22 praised bell‐pacing for its structure and organization, especially the clear expectations around station timing (n = 15): “it gave a good time reference of how long to spend at a station”—student AO, preferred BP; “I mostly enjoyed the [bell‐paced structure], I am able to for sure get to every station before time is up and I do not have to worry about pacing myself”—student BO, preferred BP. This external structure provided by the bell‐paced condition also allowed students to devote more focus to the exam questions (n = 5): “[I] can concentrate on questions more than worrying about the amount of time left”—student AB, preferred BP; “it allowed me to focus on the question rather than […] making sure I had time for every question”—student AE, preferred BP. However, of the eight (23%) students who criticized bell‐pacing, seven commented on its inflexibility: “I found some stations I'd be waiting for a long time and other stations I wish I had more time”—student BK, preferred SP.

Open‐text feedback from 16 (46%) students who provided acclamations towards the self‐paced structure identified two key underlying themes (Table 3). Of these 16 students, 15 praised self‐pacing for its flexibility in time management: “Have more time for harder [stations], can spend less time on easier [stations]”—student AF, preferred SP; “More control over my timing feels like a proper exam than being under control by fate”—student AS, preferred SP; “Interestingly, having to decide to move on, rather than being forced to move by [the bell], made me feel somewhat more secure and definitely stress less over answers I was unsure about”—student BM, preferred SP. However, 26 (74%) students criticized self‐pacing largely due to its added responsibility of self‐timing and the distractions of other SP writers forming a backlogged queue. Of these 26 students, 20 of them reported that the additional cognitive load associated with self‐timing interfered with their performance: “I was too rushed towards the end and I feel I didn't pace well”—student AL, preferred BP; “I focus more on the time than the question”—student AE, preferred BP; “The self‐paced [exam] was absolutely terrifying […] without that external structure that is present in bell‐ringer mode, I think students feel extremely overwhelmed”—student AT, preferred BP. The design of the self‐paced condition also meant that a queue would sometimes form as a faster student approached a slower student, a phenomenon that was reported by 11 of the 26 students who criticized SP: “scary when someone catches up so you know you're slowing down”—student AF, preferred SP; “Some students are slow and some are fast, so feeling the pressure when there are people directly behind you can affect performance”—student AS, preferred SP.

Specific to research objective (1), which was to validate the academic rigor of the self‐paced timing structure, there appeared to be perceived consistency between the two pacing conditions, as only one student even mentioned an impact to exam performance: “Better able to demonstrate knowledge [during SP]”—student AG, preferred SP. This aligns with the previously presented results quantifying the academic rigor of both pacing conditions, showing that neither condition led to an unintentional academic advantage over the other (Figure 1). Specific to research objective (2), which was to compare test anxiety between the two pacing conditions, students were very consistent between their selected pacing preference and the written feedback they provided. Although not specifically prompted to comment on test anxiety, 16 responses could be coded to these themes. There were five students who made comments about either bell‐pacing causing more anxiety and/or self‐pacing causing less, all of whom selected the self‐paced condition as their preferred timing structure: “The self‐timed structure was a lot more calming for me as I could pace myself without feeling rushed”—student BN; “the alarm is stressful in the bell‐timed”—student AF. Similarly, there were 13 students who made comments about either self‐pacing causing more anxiety and/or bell‐pacing causing less, all of whom selected the bell‐paced condition as their preferred timing structure: “I felt much more organized [during BP], less worried during the writing of the exam”—student BI; “I do not like the untimed [referring to SP], I found I was more nervous”—student AE. These consistencies provide further validation of the employed coding schemes.

DISCUSSION

We hypothesized that, compared to conventional bell‐ringer exams, providing students the choice in how much time to dedicate towards each question station through a self‐paced exam would alleviate test anxiety. Prior to this study, it was unknown if self‐pacing during an anatomy laboratory examination would affect either examination performance or test anxiety among undergraduate students. This study serves as the first to appraise the self‐paced timing structure as an anatomy laboratory examination modality. Our key findings are that: (1) examination performance remained consistent between bell‐paced and self‐paced timing structures; (2) bell‐pacing resulted in higher consequent test anxiety; and (3) bell‐pacing was preferred by a majority of the students, though interestingly, students preferred a pacing structure in which they experienced greater reactive test anxiety.

While test anxiety has been studied extensively across broad educational contexts, the lack of standardized language as well as inconsistencies around timing and operationalization of anxiety measurements have been major methodological limitations in the field. 15 Studies have varied widely in their use of instruments, ranging from single‐item self‐reports to multi‐dimensional inventories, and have differed in timing: some have measured anxiety pre‐exam, 16 , 17 , 18 others post‐exam, 5 , 16 and others during the exam itself. 17 In addition, the term “test anxiety” lacks precision and temporal granularity (i.e., whether the anxiety experienced refers to anticipatory worry, situational distress during the assessment, or concern experienced afterward). These inconsistencies pose a major barrier when synthesizing findings and developing evidence‐based educational interventions. As such, we applied newly proposed test anxiety metrics developed by our research group, including anticipatory, consequent, and reactive test anxiety, to quantify and interpret our results (see Methods for operational definitions). These novel anxiety metrics enable both consistency and reproducibility by clarifying the precise timing of measurements while reflecting the dynamic nature of test anxiety. We call on other researchers to adopt these distinct but related constructs, as doing so will enable the testing of temporally sensitive hypotheses and enhance comparability across studies, particularly given the current lack of pooled prevalence meta‐analyses on test anxiety.

As per Figure 1, students achieved similar academic outcomes regardless of the assigned pacing condition, supporting the initial hypothesis that examination performance would be consistent between the bell‐paced and self‐paced conditions. This suggests that neither bell‐pacing nor self‐pacing provides an academic advantage over the other, and that self‐pacing does not artificially inflate student marks. Therefore, alternative pacing structures, such as self‐pacing, can be confidently applied to anatomy laboratory exams in broader contexts with little concern of introducing bias.

We initially hypothesized that self‐pacing would mitigate both students' anticipatory and consequent test anxiety. Our findings report no significant differences in anticipatory test anxiety between bell‐paced (BP) and self‐paced (SP) writers, but BP writers reported a greater level of consequent test anxiety compared to their anticipatory test anxiety levels and compared to consequent test anxiety levels of SP writers (Figure 2). Our mixed‐effects model included a timepoint × pacing condition interaction that supported this finding, and while this interaction term did not reach conventional significance (β = 4.59, 95% CI [−0.19, 9.37], p = 0.06), its direction and magnitude aligned with statistically significant post‐hoc comparisons. Given the sample size (N = 35) and crossover design, this suggests a potentially meaningful effect that warrants further study with a larger sample. These findings also underscore the value of temporally disaggregating test anxiety; had we relied on a single timepoint measurement, these meaningful changes over time would have gone undetected.

Interestingly, despite the BP condition resulting in greater reactive test anxiety during both examinations, most students (23/35, 66%) still preferred this pacing structure (Figure 3). Open‐text student feedback from these 23 students revealed two primary reasons why this might be: First, working under the guidance of the bell chime provided them with structure (n = 22) and “kept [them] on task”, enabling them to focus on the question station at hand (n = 5) (Table 3). Second, there may be an underlying component of familiarity with the bell‐paced structure, as our ANA400H student sample consisted of those who had completed a prerequisite introductory anatomy course that utilizes bell‐ringer examinations. Of those who preferred the bell‐paced structure, one of them referred to this familiarity in their written explanation: “The timing of the bell‐ringer was what I was used to for this type of testing”—student BC, preferred BP. Further research from our group examined whether previous familiarity is truly confounding students' preference for bell‐pacing. This subsequent study utilized the same randomized crossover design but examined 57 junior undergraduate students in an introductory anatomy course (ANA300Y, Faculty of Arts & Science, University of Toronto) where only 20 students (35%) had previous experience with bell‐ringer examinations (Bentley et al., 2026, unpublished).

Consistent with prior literature, 10 , 11 students identifying as women reported higher levels of reactive test anxiety than students identifying as men, even after controlling for assigned pacing condition (β = 7.94, 95% CI [1.73, 14.16], p = 0.02). Although our study was not designed to specifically investigate gender differences, this pattern underscores the need to consider gendered experiences of assessment anxiety in future work.

Of note, the finding that students reported higher reactive test anxiety when assigned to a pacing condition they preferred was observed among both BP and SP writers, suggesting that students often preferred a pacing condition that made them feel more acutely anxious (Figure 3). This counterintuitive pattern suggests that preference alone need not necessarily align with what is psychologically optimal for reducing anxiety during high‐stakes assessments. One possible explanation is that students may prefer a pacing structure that aligns with their cognitive style or perceived strengths, even if it inadvertently heightens stress under timed conditions. For example, students who prefer self‐pacing may value control and flexibility, but that same flexibility might increase their cognitive load, as they must manage both the time and the question station at hand without external structure. Likewise, those who prefer bell‐pacing might enjoy not having to self‐monitor time but may be more sensitive to the bell chime or to the rigidity and abruptness of enforced transitions.

Another contributing factor may be the lack of autonomy inherent in the current study's randomized crossover design; participants were assigned to a pacing condition as opposed to choosing their pacing condition. Given that autonomy is a well‐established protective factor against performance anxiety in educational settings, 19 this disconnect between pacing preference and test anxiety outcomes may reflect a psychological reactance effect, where, in this case, being denied one's preferred pacing condition (or ironically, being forced into it) elevated anxiety regardless of the pacing condition itself. 20 These findings raise the possibility that simply granting students the autonomy to choose their pacing structure may itself mitigate test anxiety, and this idea is supported by open‐text feedback from students in our second pacing study (Bentley et al., 2026, unpublished) expressing the same sentiments. This inspired the third and final study in this sequential series of works in which students were given full autonomy to choose their examination pacing format (Sotto & Bentley, 2026, unpublished).

Limitations

The present study has several limitations. First, the modest sample size (N = 35) may have limited statistical power, particularly for interaction effects. The dual cohort design and precise operationalization of test anxiety, however, add to the reproducibility of the work. Order of assignment to pacing condition was also randomized at the individual level (half of the students were randomly assigned to BP first, and half were randomly assigned to SP first) and not fixed. This inconsistency in order of conditions (i.e., BP or SP first) may have confounded our test anxiety findings. Further, as we had relied on self‐reported STAI scores as anxiety measures, these findings were also subject to the inherent biases of self‐report instruments, including social desirability effects, extreme response, and recall bias, as well as individual differences in interpretation of questionnaire items. Lastly, the generalizability of these findings is limited to upper‐year undergraduate anatomy students at a single institution. Future work should explore these effects across diverse academic settings and student populations, using the novel test anxiety metrics established by our research team.

CONCLUSION

As academic competition intensifies, students are increasingly vulnerable to performance‐related anxiety, highlighting the need for assessment strategies that prioritize student wellbeing whilst maintaining academic rigor. This study demonstrates that modifying the pacing structure of anatomy laboratory examinations does not compromise academic rigor but can influence students' emotional responses. Interestingly, students often preferred the pacing structure that led to higher reactive test anxiety, suggesting that student preferences need not necessarily align with an associated psychological benefit. Our findings highlight the potential role of familiarity, perceived control, and autonomy, rather than the pacing structure itself, in shaping students' anxiety levels during anatomy laboratory examinations. Our work also highlights the need for consistency and precision in the field of test anxiety research. Empowering students with the choice in how they engage in assessments may be a key strategy to buffer test anxiety, especially in high‐stakes, time‐constrained settings. Future work should continue to center student agency as a cornerstone of inclusive and evidence‐based assessment design.
