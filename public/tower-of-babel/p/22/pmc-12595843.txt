Association of metabolic dysfunction–associated fatty liver disease with coronary CT angiography-derived plaque characteristics and cardiovascular events
Authors: Jin-jin Wang, Xiao-lan Huang, Yi Guo, Xin-wei Tao, Meng-su Zeng, Yi-ding Ji, Jian-jun Zhou, Meng-meng Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCoronary CT angiography (CCTA) derived pericoronary fat attenuation index (FAI) is a reliable quantitative biomarker of vessel inflammation. However, whether this novel index mediated the relationship between metabolic dysfunction–associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) remains unknown.MethodsFrom January 2019 to December 2022, consecutive patients with stable chest pain who underwent CCTA and unenhanced abdominal CT were prospectively enrolled. All participants were divided into two groups-with MAFLD and without MAFLD according to abdominal CT results. The primary end point was MACEs, defined as cardiac death, nonfatal myocardial infarction, and late revascularization.ResultsA total of 135 participants with MAFLD and 232 without MAFLD were included in our study. The multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that MAFLD was significantly associated with obstructive coronary artery disease (OR = 1.86, p = 0.024), high-risk plaque features ≥ 2 (adjusted OR = 2.34, p < 0.001), CT derived fraction flow reserve ≤ 0.8 (adjusted OR = 1.88, p = 0.006), FAI ≥ -70.1HU (adjusted OR = 3.25, p < 0.001) after adjusted for traditional risk factors. Participants with MAFLD showed a lower 36-month MACE-free survival rate than participants without MAFLD (54.0% vs. 83.5%, log-rank p < 0.001). In addition, MAFLD was significantly associated with MACEs (HR = 3.65, 95%CI: 1.83, 7.26, p < 0.001). Furthermore, mediation analysis revealed that FAI mediated 46.8% for the relationship between MAFLD and MACEs.ConclusionsMAFLD is associated with CCTA derived plaque characteristics, including geometric, physiologic and inflammatory status. The relationship between MAFLD and MACEs might be partially mediated via coronary inflammation, which could be captured by FAI.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12880-025-02000-9.Keywords: Metabolic dysfunction–associated fatty liver disease, Coronary CT angiography, Fat attenuation index, High-risk plaque

Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is the most common chronic liver disorder, affecting almost 1.7 billion individuals around the world [1]. In 2020, international experts proposed the term metabolic dysfunction–associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) as a more appropriate nomenclature for NAFLD, highlighting the pathogenic association of fatty liver disease with metabolic dysfunction [2]. MAFLD is not only associated with liver-related disease, but also with serious extrahepatic complications, especially cardiovascular disease (CVD) [3]. It has been demonstrated that CVD is the leading cause of mortality among patients with MAFLD [4]. However, the mechanisms that initiate the risk for cardiovascular events in patients with MAFLD remain unclear.

Coronary CT angiography (CCTA) has been validated as a noninvasive imaging modality for accurate assessment of luminal stenosis and plaque characteristics [5, 6]. However, the inflammatory status of coronary artery, rather than the geometrical features, could be the major determinant of cardiovascular events [7]. Pericoronary fat attenuation index (FAI) assessed by CCTA has been proposed as a reliable quantitative marker of vessel inflammation [8]. Higher FAI was revealed to be associated with an increased risk of cardiac mortality, which enhanced the cardiovascular risk prediction ability of CCTA [9, 10]. Another study also found that the pericoronary CT attenuation increased around culprit lesions compared with nonculprit lesions in patients with acute coronary syndrome (ACS) [11]. Furthermore, pericoronary FAI was reported to be related to coronary atherosclerosis independent of epicardial adipose tissue volume in patients with chronic coronary syndrome [12]. Nevertheless, it is unclear whether the association between MAFLD and future MACEs is mediated through pericoronary inflammation.

According to a cross-sectional observational study, patients with fatty liver disease showed an increased risk for vascular inflammation as measured via FDG-PET/CT even without difference in carotid intima–media thickness [13]. Thus, we hypothesized that the association between MAFLD and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) could be mediated by pericoronary inflammation. This study aimed to investigate whether FAI, determined by CCTA, moderate the relationship between MAFLD and MACEs in patients with stable chest pain undergoing CCTA scan.

Materials and methods

Participants sample

Between January 2019 and December 2022, consecutive patients >18 years of age with stable chest pain suggestive of coronary artery disease(CAD) were prospectively enrolled from three medical centers. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (a) intermediate pretest probability of CAD according to the updated Diamond-Forrester score [14]; (b) participants agree to undergo unenhanced abdominal CT before CCTA scan. The exclusion criteria were: (a) history of coronary revascularization or myocardial infarction; (b) contraindications to the usage of iodine contrast media; (c) inadequate CCTA image quality that hindered precise analysis; (d) revascularization within 90 days after the index CCTA; (e) refusal to participate in the study; and (f) loss to follow-up.

The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board or ethics committee at each participating center, and informed consent was acquired by written or telephone consent. The physicians determined the management according to the guideline, imaging reports including CT derived fraction flow reserve (CT-FFR) reports, participants’ symptoms and other examination results [15, 16].

Acquisition protocol of CCTA

Data were acquired using dual-source CT (Definition and SOMATOM Force, Siemens Healthineers), 320-detector-row CT (Aquilion ONE Vision, Canon Medical Systems; uCT 960, United Imaging), and 256-detector-row CT (Revolution, GE Healthcare). Metoprolol was administered orally [50–100 mg depending on heart rate] 1 h before data acquisition to patients with a heart rate ≥ 65 beats per minute, unless contraindicated. Additionally, sublingual nitroglycerin was administered directly before the CCTA examination. Prior to CCTA, all participants underwent a non-contrast, ECG-gated scan to quantify coronary artery calcification. The calcific burden in each epicardial vessel was expressed as the Agatston score. CCTA was performed using bolus-tracking, with the region of interest positioned in the descending aorta. A 40–50 mL bolus of contrast medium (Ultravist, 370 mg I/mL, Bayer) was administered intravenously at 4–5 mL/s via an antecubital vein, immediately followed by a 40 mL saline flush delivered with a dual-head power injector. All examinations were performed in full compliance with the Society of Cardiovascular Computed Tomography guidelines for CCTA image acquisition [17].

Image reconstruction and analysis of CCTA

Plaque characterization was performed semiautomatically using dedicated plaque analysis software (Coronary Plaque Analysis, version 5.0.3; cFFR, version 3.5.1, Siemens Healthineers, Forchheim, Germany). The plaque analysis was performed on all coronary segments with >0% stenosis using the 18-segment model. For every identified lesion, the following quantitative and qualitive plaque characteristics were analyzed: lesion length, presence of obstructive CAD (defined as luminal stenosis ≥ 50%), total plaque volume (PV), calcified PV, noncalcified PV, fibrous PV, fibro-fatty PV and necrotic core PV. For each participant, PV was assessed by adding up all lesions to reflect the cumulative atherosclerotic burden. Additionally, coronary lesions were evaluated for the following high-risk plaque (HRP) features, including spotty calcification (SC), positive remodeling (PR), low-attenuation plaque (LAP), as well as napkin-ring sign (NRS). Moreover, CT-FFR and FAI were performed at segments level where there was most severe stenosis visually assessed [18]. Detailed methods are provided in the expanded methods section in the Supplemental Material.

Two cardiovascular radiologists, with 42 and 15 years of experience in cardiac imaging respectively, independently analyzed the CCTA data, blinded to the results of unenhanced abdominal CT and clinical outcomes.

CT quantification of perivascular FAI

FAI was measured as described previously [19], with a cutoff value of −70.1 HU [9]. The length of the lesion-based perivascular FAI was defined as the length from the proximal to the distal shoulder of the lesion, where no plaque could be detected. Perivascular adipose tissue was sampled radially outward from the outer vessel wall of the plaques and measured as voxels with attenuation between – 190 HU and – 30 HU. FAI was defined as the mean CT attenuation of adipose tissue, which was within a radial distance from the outer vessel wall equal to the diameter of the target vessel. To avoid the effects of nonadipose tissues, the areas encasing small side branches or coronary veins were manually excluded.

Definition of MAFLD

MAFLD was defined as the presence of hepatic steatosis along with at least one of the following criteria: (a) overweight or obesity; (b) diabetes mellitus (DM); or (c) at least two metabolic abnormalities [1]. Hepatic steatosis was identified by unenhanced CT when hepatic attenuation was less than 40 HU [20]. The measurements of liver attenuation were performed on both right liver lobe and left liver lobe by using a prior validated method [21]. Liver Hounsfield units were obtained by drawing three circular regions of interest with an area of at least 2 cm2 at one axial level. Care was taken to sample homogeneous areas representative of the parenchyma, with avoidance of blood vessels, bile ducts, focal lesions, focal changes of fatty liver or fatty sparing, and the surface margins. The mean of these measurements was used to calculate the hepatic CT attenuation.

Clinical outcome

Follow-up clinical data were collected through a review of medical records or telephone interviews. The primary endpoint was the time to the occurrence of MACEs, defined as cardiac death, nonfatal myocardial infarction, or late revascularization. The hard MACEs was the composition of cardiac death or nonfatal myocardial infarction.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R version 3.3.0 (Vienna, Austria) and MedCalc Statistical Software (version 15.2.2, MedCalc Software bvba). The one-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was employed to assess the normality of continuous variables. Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas non-normally distributed variables are reported as median with interquartile range (IQR). Comparisons between groups were performed using Student’s t-test for normally distributed data and the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables are expressed as counts (%) and compared using the chi-square test. Interobserver agreement for all parameters was evaluated using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs). The association between MAFLD and CCTA-derived plaque characteristics was examined using univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated to visually present survival data, and the log-rank test was used to calculate the corresponding p-values. The proportional hazards assumption was verified using the Schoenfeld residuals test. The relationship between MAFLD and MACEs was investigated using univariable and multivariable Cox regression analyses. The multivariable-adjusted Cox hazards model was used to calculate the hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) for MACEs according to MAFLD status. A sensitivity analysis was conducted with hard MACEs as the clinical endpoint. A two-sided p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The causal-steps approach [22] was used for assessing whether the effect of the MAFLD on MACEs was carried through FAI (≥–70.1 HU). Then, bootstrapping mediation method [23] was applied to quantify the mediation effects mediated by FAI on the association between MAFLD and MACEs. Covariates [age, gender, current or past tobacco in use, family history of CAD, medication in use (aspirin, statin, and ACEI/ARB), obstructive CAD, total PV, CT-FFR, as well as HRP features] were included in models both in causal-steps approach and bootstrapping mediation method.

Results

Initially, a total of 549 consecutive participants were enrolled. Of these, 158 participants who did not meet the inclusion criteria, were excluded. The detailed reasons for exclusion are listed in Fig. 1. Additionally, 24 participants were lost to follow-up. Finally, 367 participants completed a median follow-up of 20 [11.5, 27.4] months and were included in the final analysis (mean age, 56.1 ± 9.3 years; 247 men). Among these participants, 135 were identified with MAFLD, while 232 did not have MAFLD. Baseline clinical characteristics, MAFLD status, and FAI were comparable between participants who completed follow-up and those who were lost to follow-up (p > 0.05 for all, Online Table 1).

Baseline CCTA findings between groups

Baseline CCTA findings between participants with and without MAFLD were summarized in Table 2. Compared with participants without MAFLD, participants with MAFLD had a higher incidence of obstructive CAD (38.5% vs. 24.1%, p = 0.005). The prevalence of LAP and NRS were significantly higher in MAFLD group than non-MAFLD group (LAP: 36.2% vs. 22.4%, p = 0.012; NRS: 32.5% vs. 20.2%, p = 0.011, respectively). There was no significant difference between the two groups with respect to total Agatston score, total lesion length, PR, as well as SC (p > 0.05 for all).

Logistic regression analysis between MAFLD and CCTA derived parameters

Univariate logistic regression analysis revealed that MAFLD was significantly associated with obstructive CAD, HRP features ≥ 2, CT-FFR ≤ 0.8, and FAI ≥ -70.1 HU (p < 0.05 for all, details in Table 3). In multivariate logistic regression analysis, the presence of MAFLD remained associated with obstructive CAD (adjusted OR = 1.86, 95%CI = 1.08, 3.18, p = 0.024), HRP features ≥ 2 (adjusted OR = 2.34, 95%CI = 1.43, 3.83, p < 0.001), CT-FFR ≤ 0.8 (adjusted OR = 1.88, 95%CI = 1.19, 2.97, p = 0.006), as well as FAI ≥ -70.1 HU (adjusted OR = 3.25, 95%CI = 1.82, 5.81, p < 0.001) after adjusted for age, gender, current or past tobacco in use, family history of CAD and medication in use.

Cox regression analysis and mediation analysis

During a median of 20 [IQR: 11.5–27.4] months follow-up, MACEs were observed in 25(18.5%, 25/135) participants with MAFLD, whereas 12(5.2%, 12/232) participants without MAFLD (details in Fig. 2). Compared with those without MAFLD, participants with MAFLD showed a significant lower 24-month MACEs-free survival rate [80.6% (95%CI: 72.3%, 88.8%) vs. 95.2% (95%CI: 91.3%, 99.0%)] and 36-month MACEs-free survival [(54.0% (95%CI: 32.2%, 75.7%) vs. 83.5% (95%CI: 72.7%, 94.2%), log-rank p < 0.001; Fig. 3A]. Similar findings were observed in the composition of MACEs (log-rank p < 0.05 for all, details in Online Table 3, Fig. 3B-D). A representative case was provided in Fig. 4.

The univariate Cox regression analysis found that MAFLD was significantly associated with MACEs (HR = 3.65, 95%CI: 1.83, 7.26, p < 0.001). This association remained significant after adjustment for traditional risk factors, medication in use, HRP features, as well as CT-FFR (adjusted HR = 1.78, 95%CI: 1.36, 2.33, p < 0.001) (details in Table 4). However, the relationship between the presence of MAFLD and MACEs was no longer significant after further adjusted for FAI (adjusted HR = 2.12, 95%CI: 0.80, 5.61, p = 0.128). The stratified models by site yielded similar results (details in Online Table 4). The primary findings remained robust in the sensitivity analysis (details in Online Fig. 1, Online Table 5). Mediation analysis revealed that FAI had a significant indirect effect (β = 0.27, 95%CI: 0.15, 0.38, p < 0.001) and mediated 46.8% (95%CI: 26.7%, 66.9%, p < 0.001) for the relationship between MAFLD and MACEs.

Discussion

In this prospective study of stable chest pain participants undergoing CCTA, we found that MAFLD was significantly associated with obstructive CAD, the presence of HRP, lower CT-FFR as well as higher FAI even after adjustment for traditional risk factors. The risk of MACEs was higher for participants with MAFLD than those without MAFLD (aHR = 1.78, 95%CI: 1.36, 2.33 p < 0.001) independent of traditional risk factors, extent of CAD, CT-FFR, and HRP characteristics. According to mediation analysis, FAI ≥ -70.1 HU quantified by CCTA, mediated the relationship between MAFLD and future MACEs occurrence. Therefore, CCTA identified FAI, which captures coronary inflammation, could provide the potential links to the observed increased MACEs incidence in subjects with MAFLD.

MAFLD is an established risk factor for coronary atherosclerosis [24–26]. A previous cross-sectional study reported that the presence and progression of MAFLD were significantly associated with higher risk of elevated carotid intima-media thickness, brachial-ankle pulse wave velocity, as well as microalbuminuria [27]. Another community-based cohort study [28] demonstrated that MAFLD was correlated with the presence of coronary atherosclerotic plaques and total atherosclerotic burden. The ROMICAT II trial further demonstrated that CCTA determined HRP features were more frequently observed in patients with NAFLD than those without NAFLD [29]. Our results were concordance with previous findings. The current research extended previous study found that lower CT-FFR and higher FAI values were also observed in the MAFLD group among patients with stable chest pain. Our study raises a possibility that patients with MAFLD are not only more prone to develop vulnerable plaque phenotype, but they are more likely to emerged with flow-limiting lesions and inflamed coronary arteries.

The association between MAFLD and the risk of CVD have been investigated by several studies. However, there remains controversial among MAFLD and MACEs. A growing body of evidence suggested that MAFLD could enhance the risk of cardiovascular events [30–32]. Additionally, the incidence of CVD gradually increased with the severity of fatty liver disease even after adjustment for established CVD risk factors [33]. Another study [34] reported that fatty liver disease is indeed associated with CAD and future need for coronary intervention. However, it does not increase mortality or cardiovascular complications. We found that the presence of MAFLD conferred almost 2-fold higher risk for MACEs. The association between MAFLD and adverse cardiac events persisted even after adjusting for CT-FFR, but not for perivascular FAI, highlighting the role of ischemia-independent inflammatory mechanisms in this risk. Previous studies have demonstrated that coronary inflammation risk provides independent prognostic information regardless of plaque vulnerability and physiologic stenosis in patients with CAD [35], which was consistent with our results.

Our mediation analysis—showing that FAI accounts for a substantial proportion of MAFLD’s effect on MACEs—further supports this pathway. Pericoronary inflammation has been reported to be important in coronary atherosclerosis independent of epicardial adipose tissue volume, indicating the existence of inflammation maybe more important than the entire amount of epicardial adipose tissue. Inflammation plays a pivotal role in the initiation and destabilization of atherosclerotic plaque [7, 36]. The community-based cohort of Framingham Heart Study [37] indicated that fatty liver is associated with multiple markers of inflammation, after accounting for obesity and other covariates. And perivascular inflammation could be associated with the progression of coronary atherosclerotic plaques [38]. Furthermore, our previous study [39] found that participants with liver fatty showed higher progression rate of coronary plaque than those without. In other words, the risk of cardiovascular events in individuals with MAFLD could be ascribed to vascular inflammation, which lead to plaque progression and prone to rupture. We suggested that FAI may identify high-risk individuals missed by ischemia-based criteria, offering a complementary tool for risk stratification in patients with MAFLD.

Clinical implication

The key novelty of our study lies not in establishing the MAFLD–ASCVD association per se, but in: (i) Identifying a potential biological mediator (FAI) that links hepatic metabolic dysfunction to coronary inflammation. While the association between MAFLD and ASCVD is known, the underlying mechanisms—particularly the role of perivascular adipose inflammation as captured by FAI—remain poorly understood. Our mediation analysis suggests that FAI may partially explain the increased ASCVD risk in MAFLD patients, providing mechanistic insight beyond mere risk association; (ii) Demonstrating incremental prognostic value of FAI in risk stratification among MAFLD patients. Not all MAFLD patients have the same ASCVD risk. Our findings show that FAI can further stratify risk within this high-risk population, potentially identifying those who may benefit from more aggressive prevention strategies. (iii) Providing a non-invasive imaging biomarker that could guide personalized management. Given that MAFLD affects ∼ 25% of the global population, a tool like FAI derived from routine CCTA, could help prioritize high-risk individuals for early intervention, thereby enhancing clinical decision-making.

Limitation

Our study had several limitations. First, it was a three-center prospective study with one ethnicity and relatively small sample size. The results might be influenced by the different geographic patient features. However, our study was a preliminary study and data on this topic were limited. Future multiethnic studies with larger sample size are needed to confirm our findings. Second, all of the patients were not followed up after the second CCTA, therefore it was not possible to determine the relationship between the changes of FAI and MACEs. Third, the current study didn’t detect the development or regression of MAFLD during follow-up period, which would be a factor to affect the occurrence of MACEs. Forth, the mediation analysis was performed using the Baron–Kenny framework with bootstrapping, which does not explicitly model the time-to-event nature of the outcome or censoring mechanism. This approach may yield biased estimates in the presence of substantial censoring. Future studies should validate our findings using more advanced causal mediation methods for survival data, such as structural nested models, accelerated failure time models with natural effects, or additive hazards frameworks under a counterfactual paradigm. Furthermore, the dynamic changes in therapy intensity were not fully available during follow-up. The potential for residual confounding by unmeasured or time-varying treatments—particularly newer agents such as GLP-1 receptor agonists and SGLT2 inhibitors with pleiotropic effects on adipose tissue and inflammation—cannot be excluded. Future studies with detailed longitudinal treatment data are needed to refine these associations.

Conclusion

MAFLD is associated with CCTA derived plaque features, including geometric, physiologic, as well as inflammatory status. The relationship between MAFLD and future MACEs occurrence, at least in part, might be mediated via coronary inflammation, detected by FAI.
