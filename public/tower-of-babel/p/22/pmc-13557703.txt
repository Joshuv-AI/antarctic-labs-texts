Predictor of Mortality in Obstructive Sleep Apnea: Results of Explainable Deep Learning-Based Survival Analysis from a 15-Year Follow-Up
Authors: Gaetano Pagano, Giorgio Castellana, Giovanni D’Addio, Lucia Palazzo, Maria Aliani, Patrizia Guido, Francesco Berloco, Pietro Maria Marvulli, Vladimiro Suglia, Simona Colucci, Donato Lacedonia, Vitoantonio Bevilacqua, Mauro Carone
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionObstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a respiratory disorder with comorbidities of several nature, from cardiovascular to renal ones. OSA is typically treated through continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP), but more investigations are needed to confirm its benefits.MethodsIn this work, survival analysis has been exploited and enhanced with eXplainable Artifical Intelligence (XAI) to investigate the impact of comorbidities and compare the model reliability. The dataset encompasses both clinical and polysomnography-based data for a total of 45 different features.ResultsA total of 1,394 OSA patients followed for 15 years were enrolled. All the selected features have been studied by means of DL-models and time-dependent XAI techniques. The variables impacting on mortality are reported below in descending order with respect their importance: (1) age, years of CPAP, renal dysfunction, COPD, body mass index categories, sex, and anemia for the CoxTime; (2) age, apnea hypopnea index, renal dysfunction, SaO2 min, years of CPAP, COPD, and anemia for the LogHazard.ConclusionAdvancing age, severity of OSA, comorbidity, including chronic kidney disease, COPD, and anemia, are crucial contributors to increased mortality.

Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a chronic respiratory disorder characterized by recurrent episodes of partial (hypopnea) or complete obstruction (apnea) of the upper airway [1], which leads to intermittent hypoxia, sleep fragmentation, hypercapnia [2, 3], marked swing in intrathoracic pressure, increased sympathetic activity, and arousal from sleep [4]. OSA is the most important pathology within the spectrum of sleep-disordered breathing and it has become of worldwide importance in the last decades [5], being widely prevalent in the general population [6] and associated with an increased risk of mortality [5] and with multiple comorbidities, such as arterial hypertension, atrial fibrillation, coronary and cerebrovascular diseases, heart failure, diabetes, renal disease, and cancer [5, 7–10]. The pathogenetic mechanisms that link OSA with these comorbidities are represented by different factors, including sympathetic activation, marked negative intrathoracic pressure swings, intermittent oxygen desaturation, hypercapnia, and arousal from sleep [11]. Cardiovascular risk is an important aspect to be considered in the management of OSA patients since OSA is associated with an increased cardiovascular mortality and incidence, which worsens with the OSA severity level, as reported by Eulenburg et al. [12].

Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) is the gold standard for the OSA treatment [4]: it opens and stabilizes the upper airways during sleep that would reduce this risk by correcting the hypoxic burden and sleep fragmentation [13]. Studies evaluating CPAP outcomes in short-term have demonstrated the improvement of sleepiness and quality of life, as well as the reduction of systematic blood pressure, particularly in hypertensive OSA patients with severe disease who presented good adherence with the device [4]. However, recent randomized controlled trials (RCTs), such as the Sleep apnea Cardiovascular Endpoints (SAVE), RICCADSA, and ISAACC studies did not show improvement in long-term cardiovascular outcomes in patients treated with CPAP [14–16]. Moreover, a case of harmful CPAP was reported in one RCT as well, thus indicating the absence of a general consensus and the need for more investigations about the benefit of this treatment [12].

This study is aimed to assess determinants of mortality in a 15-year retrospective study of patients diagnosed with treatable OSA. For this purpose, survival analysis is employed and enhanced in the context of Obstructive Sleep apnea by utilizing eXplainable Artificial Intelligence over time (XAI[t]). The study involves training and validating deep learning survival models, investigating the impact of comorbidities on OSA using XAI(t), and comparing model reliability based on XAI explanations. The authors trained multiple DL survival models, evaluated their performance metrics, and leveraged SurvSHAP algorithms for explainability. The remainder of this paper is as follows: Materials and Methods illustrates the clinical dataset, the survival models, and the XAI(t) algorithms exploited; the outcomes of this investigation are reported and discussed in Results and Discussion, respectively; final remarks about the conducted study are written in Conclusion.

Materials and Methods

In this study, survival analysis is performed with explainable DL models on preprocessed data to shed light on the determinants of the patients’ survival from OSA, which is a chronic respiratory disorder affecting the upper airway with either hypopnea or apnea [1].

Dataset

This is a retrospective study employing a dataset previously collected during the rehabilitation period of people who were diagnosed of OSA with an overnight polysomnography (PSG) at the Istituti Clinici Scientifici Maugeri IRCCS Bari hospital sleep laboratory. Such test has been used to confirm a former suspect of OSA based on the identification of such symptoms as witnessed apnea, snoring, and excessive daytime sleepiness. More specifically, the PSG test has been carried out as follows: all patients performed the baseline overnight home sleep testing (HST) (Nox-T3 monitor, Nox Medical, Inc., Reykjavik, Iceland) recording nasal pressure, snoring, rib cage and abdominal movement, pulse oximetry, heart rate, activity, and body position. HST recordings were manually scored by a sleep physician. The diagnosis of OSA was made according to the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) and International classification of sleep disorders-third edition [17–20]. The dataset encompasses both clinical and PSG-based data for a total of 1,592 samples with 45 features. There can be found demographic information, such as follow-up time, marital status, sex, profession, status, and age, together with other data, i.e., the ejection fraction (EF), the body mass index (BMI), the oxygen desaturation index, the value of the glomerular filtrate rate, the apnea hypopnea index (AHI), the minimum saturation of blood oxygen recorded (SaO2 min). Data related to comorbidities (e.g., heart disease and diabetes) are included as well.

Data Processing

The raw data, which are online available at [21], were processed before being used to train the survival models as follows:1.Exclusion of nonrelevant indexes, such as admission date, discharge date, number of health record (N CC), follow-up date, patient I.D., and profession;2.Conversion of survival time from days to months for the sake of an easier interpretation and analysis of the data;3.Imputation of null values anemia features based on both the patient’s sex and hemoglobin levels;4.Removal of oxygen desaturation index and EF features, given that 90% of samples are null values that could not be imputed for a lack of information;5.Categorization of age in 2 classes according to a threshold of 65 [22–24], as well as discretization of both BMI and glomerular filtrate rate based on related works in the medical literature [25, 26];6.Addition of a binary categorical feature, namely CPAP, for discriminating the CPAP treatment occurrence, and another scalar value years of CPAP indicating the duration in years of the CPAP treatment;7.Removal of all samples whose last follow-up date is before at least 2020 such that any biases related to the COVID-19 pandemic do not affect the outcomes.

These features are selected to remove collinearities based on the Pearson’s correlation coefficients by using a threshold of 0.6, thus finally attaining a total of 1,394 samples with 23 features, as reported in Table 1. The features with a numeric value – age, cholesterol, BMI, AHI, SaO2 min, years of CPAP, and follow-up days – are statistically compared with a nonparametric Mann-Whitney U test due to the exclusion of non-normal distributions by the Shapiro-Wilk test [27–30].

Survival Analysis

Different DL models are exploited in this work to perform SA and they are DeepSurv (DS), Deep-Hit (DH), CoxTime (CT), Piecewise Constant Hazard (PCH), and Logistic Hazard (LH). Two explanation methods are integrated in the pipeline to increase the interpretability of model predictions. Shapley additive explanations (SHAPs) and local interpretable model-agnostic explanations are used to explain the overall model behavior over time in a global and local fashion, respectively. Their extended versions SurvSHAP [31] and SurvLIME [32], which are designed for SA, are employed to provide clinicians with explanations that even consider the survival time; in this manner, the evolution of OSA can be unveiled in terms of comorbidities with SurvSHAP. SurvSHAP is extensively described in a previous work [1] and has been used for analyzing the survival probability not only for OSA patients, but also for different pathologies, such as retroperitoneal liposarcoma [23], heart failure [33], and intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma [34].

The dataset has been split by means of a holdout strategy, meaning that each model is trained with the 70% of the data and tested with the remaining 30%. This splitting strategy has already been employed in other studies analyzing survival of patients affected either by OSA [1] or other pathologies, such as retroperitoneal liposarcoma [23]; this training percentage has been chosen in related works focused on sleep staging as well [35]. Note that the observation period in the test set is ensured to lessen the one in the training set, whereas both sets are stratified by the Status index.

Three metrics have been extracted on the test set to evaluate and compare models:1.The Brier Score [36] evaluates the difference between the ground truth event and the correspondent model prediction, this latter can be regarded as random in case of a Brier Score of 0.5;2.The C-index [37] indicates the concordance between the ground truth and the model prediction, meaning that the higher its value the more the predicted order matches the order in the data;3.The Integrated Cumulative-Dynamic Area Under the Curve (C/D AUC) [38] is the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve at different time points. All the analyses have been conducted using Python (v.3.8), R (v.4.3.2), and MATLAB 2022b.

Discussion

In this study, the mortality of patients with obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) and the impact of the CPAP treatment have been retrospectively assessed through survival analysis. For this purpose, data from 1,394 OSA patients, which had been previously collected during their rehabilitative sessions throughout 15 years, have been used to feed explainable DL survival models.

The outcomes revealed that the age is the most important feature, which is in line with the outcomes of other related works [24, 39–41]. On the other hand, He et al. [42] found an increase of the mortality risk in the mild and moderate OSA group for young and middle-aged adults (<60 years), and a survival advantage was discovered by Lavie’s team in elderlies [43]; however, the sample size of these studies is halved and limited to only ≥60 years old people, respectively.

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is identified by both models, though with a different importance, as a risk factor for OSA, since its concurrence with OSA (COPD = 0) – which is known as overlap syndrome – decreases the survival probability with respect to the absence of pulmonary disease, as already found by Tondo’s team [24]. The presence of a renal dysfunction contributed to the model prediction as well Zhang et al. [44] found that more than 1/3 of people with chronic kidney disease (CKD) have comorbid OSA, which increases the risk of early death in CKD patients. Moreover, Watanabe et al. [7] found that the risk of death and cardiovascular disease is high in patients with CKD and OSA. This may be explained considering the bidirectional relation between OSA and CKD, meaning that OSA can lead to an increase in the rate of kidney function decline and an end-stage renal disease may lead to worsening of sleep apnea [45]. Remarkably, the outcomes of both models confirm that the mortality risk is reduced by the usage of CPAP, as already mentioned in other survival analyses on OSA [4, 7, 24, 46–48]. However, this result did not emerge in such RCTs as the SAVE, RICCADSA, and ISAACC studies [14–16]. This may be due to the fact that the recruited patients had an excessive daytime sleepiness, together with a low adherence to CPAP and a brief follow-up; accordingly, the impact of CPAP on OSA evolution depends on the time since the difference in mortality was found after some years after CPAP treatment [24, 48]. Accordingly, OSA patients tend to be less treated with CPAP as their age increases, which reduce the positive impact on survival [24].

Cardiovascular diseases have not been associated with mortality since they have not emerged as predictors of mortality. Studies have reported that the increase in mortality in patients with OSA from cardiovascular causes was only seen in patients under the age of 50 and when the sleep-disordered breathing was severe [49]; on the other hand, in our cohort, the average age is 63 and over 80% of the population is over 50 years old, and similar mortality rates by cardiovascular disease have been found in elderlies with and without sleep apnea. Lavie and colleagues hypothesized that an apparent decline in mortality risk with age could be explained by an adaptive protective mechanism resulting from nightly cycles of hypoxia-reoxygenation with ischemic preconditioning [49]. Moreover, Kobayashi et al. [50] suggested that cardiovascular risks are not uniformly associated with OSA among older adults. In addition, a risk factor for cardiovascular disease is given by dyslipidemia, which is under control for most patients (62% have total cholesterol <200 mg/dL) of the study population, though.

No consensus has been found in the scientific literature about the influence of AHI as a mortality factor in OSA patients, even though it proved to be predictive of mortality in some related works [46, 51, 52]. Kendzerska et al. [52] found AHI OSA-related variables to be significant independent predictors for a median follow-up of more than 68 months and in a fully adjusted model. Sleep time spent with oxygen saturation lower than 90% (9 min vs. 0; HR = 1.50, 95% CI: 1.25–1.79), the number of awakenings (35 vs .18; HR = 1.06, 95% CI: 1.02–1.10), the index of periodic leg movements (13 vs. 0/h; HR = 1.05, 95% CI 1.03–1.07), heart rate (70 vs. 56 beats per minute [bpm]; HR = 1.28, 95% CI: 1.19–1.37), and daytime sleepiness (HR = 1.13, 95% CI: 1.01–1.28). On the contrary, in the presented study the dual behavior of apnea-hypopnea index and night hypoxemia is clearly highlighted since the logistic hazard model identifies both AHI and SaO2 min as determinants of mortality.

These results are in accordance with the partial-dependence survival profiles (Fig. 3–5), which indicate how each variable influences the overall survival of the cohort keeping the other ones unaltered over time [53]. As the area of the curve increases, the importance of the related variable is higher and the survival function changes in a more relevant manner [23, 33]. For instance, COPD is more impactful than the other categorical variables on survival predictions. Moreover, a higher age, the presence of a pulmonary disease (i.e., COPD = 1), and the presence of a renal dysfunction determine a faster decay of the survival function; this confirms the negative impact of COPD on OSA patients’ survival due to the worsening effect of such phenomena as expiratory airflow resistances, systemic inflammation, and reduced ventilatory drive [24]. On the other hand, the rate of decay of the survival function is higher as BMI is lower, which is known as “BMI paradox”.

Regarding the association between mortality and anemia, no data supporting this association have been found in the literature. However, anemia is believed to be a manifestation of worse renal function related to OSA. Seifen C. et al. [54] have also shown that there is a positive correlation between AHI and ferritin: in light of this data, it can be hypothesized that there may also be a chronic inflammatory state related to OSA at the basis of the development of anemia.

Strengths of our work are the 15-year follow-up duration, the sample size and the high number of variables included. Limitations are the absence of potentially relevant covariates (nutrition, physical activity, and sleep duration), the retrospective and monocentric nature of the study, the lack of data about the actual hours of CPAP use, because the database defines CPAP use as a binary parameter, and the lack of reliable data about cause-specific deaths (e.g., CVD).

Conclusions

Advancing age, severity of OSA, and comorbidities, including CKD, COPD and anemia, are crucial contributors to increased mortality. A correct identification of these variables and a definition of their impact on mortality risk can be useful to stratify OSA patients and establish a priority in setting therapy. A better predictor of mortality in OSA patients is necessary and more studies are needed to verify which predictors work the best. The critical importance of adherence to PAP highlights the importance of strategies to improve the adherence to CPAP.
