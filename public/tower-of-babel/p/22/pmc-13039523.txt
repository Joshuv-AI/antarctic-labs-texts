Development and multinational validation of a multiple myeloma–specific comorbidity index using real-world cohorts: CAREMM-2108

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFrailty and comorbidity substantially influence clinical outcomes in patients with multiple myeloma (MM), yet existing tools such as the IMWG frailty score and Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) have limitations in real-world applicability and disease specificity.ObjectiveTo develop and externally validate a multiple myeloma–specific comorbidity index (MM-CI) using real-world data from Korean and Japanese cohorts.Materials and methodsThis retrospective study was conducted in two parts: (I) development of MM-CI using a nationwide Korean claims cohort of 17,273 MM patients diagnosed between 2007 and 2022; and (II) external validation using two independent multicenter registry cohorts: 1473 Korean patients (2010–2021) and 314 Japanese patients (2008–2023). Multivariable Cox regression was performed incorporating key clinical factors, including ECOG performance status, R2-ISS stage, and frontline treatment intensity.ResultsThe MM-CI was derived from eight variables: male sex; age 60–69, 70–79, and ≥80 years; congestive heart failure, cerebrovascular disease, hepatic disease, and cancer: 1 × (Sex: Male) + 2 × (Age: 60–69 years) + 4 × (Age: 70–79 years) + 6 × (Age ≥80 years) + 2 × (Congestive heart failure: Yes) + 1 × (Cerebrovascular disease: Yes) + 1 × (Hepatic disease: Yes) + 1 × (Cancer: Yes). The MM-CI remained independently prognostic after adjustment for ECOG, R2-ISS, and treatment intensity.ConclusionThe MM-CI provides a simple, objective, and clinically applicable tool for comorbidity-based risk stratification in MM. It outperforms existing models and may support treatment decisions in real-world practice.Subject terms: Cancer epidemiology, Myeloma

Introduction

Multiple myeloma (MM) is a clonal malignancy of plasma cells that predominantly affects older adults, with approximately 70% of diagnoses occurring in individuals aged 65 years or older [1, 2]. This demographic profile necessitates the incorporation of geriatric assessments into clinical decision-making, with the goal of tailoring treatment intensity according to patient frailty [3]. The International Myeloma Working Group (IMWG) frailty score has emerged as the benchmark model for frailty classification in MM, combining the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), the Katz Index of Independence in Activities of Daily Living (ADL), and the Lawton Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL) scale to stratify patients as fit, intermediate-fit, or frail [4]. Evidence indicates that patients categorized as frail are at increased risk of early mortality, treatment-related toxicity, and therapy discontinuation [5].

Although comorbidity profiling remains an essential component of risk assessment for frailty, the conventional CCI-based approach has several methodological limitations that compromise its clinical utility and predictive accuracy. First, the comorbidity component relies on the conventional CCI, which assigns fixed weights to 19 conditions without accounting for disease-specific relevance or covariate interactions (Table S1A) [6]. This approach may limit its predictive performance in modern statistical modeling [7, 8]. Second, the IMWG model reduces CCI to a binary indicator (one point for CCI ≥ 2; see Table S1B), potentially losing clinically meaningful gradations of comorbidity burden and overlooking clinically relevant disease burden such as renal impairment.

In addition, while the IMWG frailty score aimed to improve risk stratification by incorporating functional measures such as ADL and IADL, it presents several limitations in clinical practice. These measures are susceptible to transient impairment from acute disease-related symptoms at the time of diagnosis, reflecting acute disease burden rather than baseline functional capacity. As a result, frailty assessments based on functional status at diagnosis may overestimate patient vulnerability, potentially leading to undertreatment of patients who could tolerate intensive therapy after disease control. Furthermore, the IMWG score applies equal weight to comorbidity (CCI ≥ 2), functional decline (ADL/IADL), and age, without empirical validation of potential interactions or relative prognostic importance of each domain. This fixed-point structure may inadequately represent the clinical complexity of real-world patients, particularly those whose physical limitations are reversible after initiation of therapy [9, 10]. These limitations underscore the need for a more adaptable and disease-specific comorbidity-based prognostic model for MM.

Given these limitations and the availability of large-scale real-world datasets, we developed a refined comorbidity-based prognostic model specifically for MM patients. Our objectives were to construct a statistically robust risk score using nationwide data, demonstrate its independent prognostic value when adjusted for established staging systems, including the Second Revision of the International Staging System (R2-ISS) [11] and Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status, and validate its clinical applicability across different frontline treatment intensities.

Materials and methods

Study design and data source

This retrospective cohort study was divided into two parts: (I) development of the scoring system and (II) its validation. In Part I, a comorbidity-based scoring model, referred to as the multiple myeloma-specific comorbidity index (MM-CI), was developed using national claims data from the Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service (HIRA) database, which provides comprehensive healthcare utilization data for the entire population of South Korea. The HIRA database covers approximately 50 million Koreans and includes demographic variables such as age and sex, along with healthcare data encompassing all medical claims and mortality information [12].

In Part II, to address concerns regarding the reliability and quality of claims-based data sources [13], we externally validated the MM-CI model using multicenter registry data from both Korean and Japanese populations. For the Korean population, validation was conducted using data from the CARE-MM registry—which includes both prospectively and retrospectively collected clinical information from five university hospitals—as well as data from the Seoul National University (SNU) registry [14–16]. For the Japanese population, we utilized real-world registry data from The Jikei University School of Medicine. In addition, to examine whether the MM-CI maintained prognostic utility across clinically relevant subgroups, we performed subgroup analyses in patients with available data on R2-ISS stage, ECOG performance status, and frontline treatment intensity.

The study utilizing HIRA data was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul St. Mary’s Hospital, Seoul, Korea (No. KC21ZNSI0448). As this analysis involved publicly available, anonymized, and de-identified data, the requirement for informed consent was waived. Validation studies using data from Seoul National University Hospital and The Jikei University School of Medicine were approved by their respective institutional review boards (IRB Nos. H-2101-119-1190 and 37-003(12639), respectively). For all study components, informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the data. This research was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study population

MM patients were identified using the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10 code C90), from medical claims data in the HIRA database between January 2007 and November 2022. Patients with a primary or secondary diagnosis of MM (C90) were identified, resulting in a raw cohort of 31,769 patients. Sequential exclusion criteria were applied to refine the final development cohort: (i) patients with a single diagnosis code for MM (C90) (n = 4406), (ii) patients diagnosed before the age of 19 years (n = 12), (iii) those diagnosed in 2007 or 2008 to ensure a minimum of one year for comorbidity profiling (n = 3767), (iv) patients without the cancer-specific co-payment code (V193) (n = 2160), (v) patients who died within six months without receiving MM-targeted treatment (n = 1209), (vi) patients who did not receive MM-specific medication (n = 2900), and (vii) patients with an uncertain index date (n = 42). This resulted in a final development cohort of 17,273 MM patients (Fig. 1A). The index date was defined as the start date of first MM-targeted therapy. Specific definitions of MM-targeted therapy codes for frontline therapy, including melphalan, bortezomib, thalidomide, or lenalidomide, based on the HIRA public health insurance criteria, are provided in Table S2 [17].

For external validation in Part II of the study, two independent real-world datasets were used: (1) a Korean multicenter registry cohort comprising patients from the CARE-MM and SNU registries, and (2) a Japanese cohort consisting of patients treated at Jikei University Hospital. The same eligibility criteria were applied to both cohorts. Patients were included if they (i) had a confirmed diagnosis of multiple myeloma, (ii) were aged ≥19 years at diagnosis, and (iii) had received MM-directed frontline therapy. Exclusion criteria included (i) a diagnosis of plasma cell leukemia, solitary plasmacytoma, or AL amyloidosis; (ii) no initiation of MM-specific treatment; (iii) missing comorbidity data; or (iv) insufficient follow-up to assess survival outcomes (<6 months). A total of 1473 patients were included from the Korean registries and 314 patients from the Japanese cohort (Validation cohort I and II, respectively, Fig. 1B, C). Given the distinct treatment landscapes and healthcare systems in the two countries, the validation analyses were conducted independently within each cohort.

Definitions

For the development cohort, baseline characteristics were collected at the index date, defined as the start date of frontline MM-targeted therapy. Comorbidity profiles were identified using ICD-10 diagnostic codes classified according to the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) recorded before the index date (Table S1C) [6]. These codes were selected based on established mappings and were refined through clinical expert review to align with the structure and terminology of Korea’s national claims system [17–20].

Due to limitations inherent in claims data, several diagnostic categories were modified. The severity of liver disease and the presence of end-organ damage from diabetes could not be reliably distinguished; therefore, mild and moderate-to-severe liver disease were combined into a single composite variable, labeled “hepatic disease”. Diabetes was treated as a binary variable, without differentiating for end-organ damage. Additionally, diagnoses of leukemia, lymphoma, and solid tumors were consolidated into a unified “cancer” category. Comorbidities were considered present if listed as a primary diagnosis or if recorded as at least two separate secondary diagnoses within one year prior to the index date.

For validation cohorts, R2-ISS scoring was calculated using component weights as previously described [11]: ISS-II (1 point), ISS-III (1.5 points), del(17p) (1 point), elevated lactate dehydrogenase (1 point), t(4;14) (1 point), and 1q gain/amplification (0.5 points). Frontline treatment intensity was defined by the number of major drugs used in frontline treatment, categorized as doublet, triplet, or quadruplet regimens for the validation analyses (Table S3).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized using median with interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were presented using frequencies and percentages. Univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were employed to evaluate candidate predictors and construct a scoring model. Complete case analysis was conducted, as the proportion of missing data was less than 5% across all datasets.

For the scoring system development in Part I, candidate predictors with a p-value < 0.05 in the univariable analysis were included in the multivariable model to identify factors associated with overall survival (OS). Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors, and multivariable analysis was conducted using the Cox proportional hazard regression model after confirming the proportional hazards assumption using Schoenfeld residuals. Stepwise selection was employed to establish the scoring system from candidate predictors, with the Akaike information criterion as the metric for comparison. In addition, to balance statistical significance with selection stability, we performed 1000 bootstrap resampling iterations; variables with a mean bootstrap score ≥0.4 and a selection probability ≥50% were considered statistically significant and were retained in the final MM-CI score model. β coefficients and hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs were calculated for the final score model, referred to as the MM-CI. For clinical applicability, the β coefficients of predictors in the final model were rounded to integer values. Individual risk estimates were then derived from these weighted scores for each variable.

For validation of the MM-CI in Part II, we compared the predictive accuracy of the MM-CI, age-adjusted CCI, and CCI component of the IMWG-frailty score across multiple time points, ranging from 1 to 8 years, using time-dependent Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves. We utilized the time-dependent area under the ROC curve (AUC) at various time intervals, using the R package timeROC. The time-dependent ROC analysis is computed using the inverse probability of censoring weighting, which assigns weights to the cases and controls based on the probability of remaining uncensored, as estimated by the Kaplan–Meier curve [21]. All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) and R version 4.0.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Results

Patient characteristics

Baseline demographics and clinical characteristics of the development and validation cohorts are summarized in Table 1. The development cohort (n = 17,273) comprised 53.7% male patients, with a median age of 63 years (IQR, 53–73). The most common comorbidities in the development cohort were renal disease (22.4%), diabetes (17.1%), cerebrovascular (7.9%). In validation cohort I (Korean registry, n = 1473), patients had a median age of 63 years (IQR, 56–69), with 52.7% male patients. The most common comorbidities were renal disease (29.0%), diabetes (16.0%), and cancer (7.6%). In validation cohort II (Japanese registry, n = 314), patients had a median age of 71 years (IQR, 64–76), with 51.0% male patients. The most common comorbidities were peripheral vascular disease (40.4%), diabetes (18.8%), cancer (14.6%), and congestive heart failure (10.2%). The R2-ISS stage distribution in validation cohort I was 8.4% for stage I, 13.2% for stage II, 23.4% for stage III, and 6.2% for stage IV, with 48.8% of patients missing R2-ISS classification. In validation cohort II, R2-ISS data were available for 72 patients (22.9%), including 2.9% with stage I, 7.3% with stage II, 11.5% with stage III, and 1.3% with stage IV disease. R2-ISS staging was not available for the development cohort due to the limitations of claims data.

Development of the scoring system (MM-CI)

The univariable analysis identified sex, age and 11 comorbidity factors related to poor OS (p-value < 0.05) in the development cohort (Table S4): male (HR 1.09, 95% CI [1.05–1.14]), older age (60–69 years, HR 1.48, 95% CI [1.39–1.57]; 70–79 years, HR 2.26, 95% CI [2.14–2.40]; ≥80 years, HR 3.56, 95% CI [3.31–3.83]), myocardial infarction (HR 1.39, 95% CI [1.18–1.65]), congestive heart failure (HR 1.70, 95% CI [1.56–1.84]), peripheral vascular disease (HR 1.46, 95% CI [1.27–1.67]), cerebrovascular disease (HR 1.40, 95% CI [1.31–1.51]), dementia (HR 1.51, 95% CI [1.27–1.80]), chronic pulmonary disease (HR 1.42, 95% CI [1.30–1.55]), hepatic disease (HR 1.11, 95% CI [1.02–1.22]), diabetes (HR 1.22, 95% CI [1.16–1.29]), hemiplegia/paraplegia (HR 1.42, 95% CI [1.13–1.78]), renal disease (HR 1.16, 95% CI [1.10–1.21]), and cancer (HR 1.23, 95% CI [1.16–1.31]).

Multivariable stepwise analysis showed that six variables were significantly associated with poor OS, with all p-values < 0.001 (Table 2). Specifically, male sex (HR 1.13, 95% CI [1.09–1.18]), advanced age (HR 1.45, 95% CI [1.37–1.54] for ages 60–69; HR 2.20, 95% CI [2.08–2.33] for ages 70–79; HR 3.41, 95% CI [3.17–3.68] for ages ≥80), congestive heart failure (HR 1.46, 95% CI [1.34–1.59]), cerebrovascular disease (HR 1.13, 95% CI [1.05–1.21]), hepatic disease (HR 1.18, 95% CI [1.08–1.29]), and cancer (HR 1.18, 95% CI [1.11–1.26]) were significant predictors. Application of the MM-CI to the development cohort revealed a clear separation in OS across risk scores (log-rank p < 0.05), as demonstrated by Kaplan–Meier analysis (Fig. S2). Using optimal cutoff points determined by recursive partitioning and the median OS estimates, patients were stratified into four risk groups: low risk (0–2 points), intermediate-I (3–4 points), intermediate-II (5 points), and high risk (≥6 points), with corresponding median OS of 72.5 months (95% CI: 69.2–75.0), 43.8 months (95% CI: 42.1–46.3), 30.9 months (95% CI: 28.8–33.2), and 20.3 months (95% CI: 18.9–22.3), respectively (Fig. 2A). Similarly, MM-CI stratified time to next treatment (log-rank p < 0.05), with median value of 23.4 months (95% CI: 22.6–24.5), 18.5 months (95% CI: 17.9–19.3), 14.8 months (95% CI: 13.9–15.7), and 12.1 months (95% CI: 11.3–13.1) across the four groups, respectively (Fig. S3). The model’s internal validity and variable selection stability were confirmed through bootstrap resampling analysis (Table S5). Additionally, the MM-CI demonstrated consistent prognostic performance across all frontline treatment intensity groups, supporting its robustness and broad clinical applicability (all p-values < 0.001; Table S6, Fig. S4).

Validation of MM-CI

The developed MM-CI model was applied to both validation cohort I (Korean registry) and cohort II (Japanese registry). As shown in Fig. S5, Kaplan–Meier curves revealed significant differences in OS across the four risk groups in both cohorts (p < 0.001 for each). In validation cohort I, median OS was 97.6 months (95% CI: 92.8–110.9) for the low-risk group, 75.5 months (95% CI: 64.0–94.6) for intermediate-I, 64.5 months (95% CI: 49.7–76.1) for intermediate-II, and 43.0 months (95% CI: 37.2–53.1) for the high-risk group. In validation cohort II, median OS was not reached in the low-risk group; survival estimates were 90.6 months (95% CI: 83.7–not reached) for intermediate-I, 84.9 months (95% CI: 79.1–not reached) for intermediate-II, and 67.8 months (95% CI: 45.3–not reached) for the high-risk group (Fig. 2B, C).

Predictive accuracy over time was assessed using time-dependent ROC analysis, yielding an integrated AUC of 0.637 (95% CI: 0.610–0.665) when combining both validation cohorts (Fig. 2D). The MM-CI demonstrated superior discriminative ability compared to the conventional age-adjusted CCI (AUC: 0.613; 95% CI: 0.584–0.641) and the concise age-adjusted CCI used in the IMWG frailty model (AUC: 0.569; 95% CI: 0.546–0.593), with statistically significant differences at most time points (p < 0.001; Fig. S6).

Subgroup analysis of MM-CI across ECOG, R2-ISS, and treatment intensity

Figures S7 and 3 illustrate HRs and OS across clinical subgroups stratified by the MM-CI risk, demonstrating consistent and stepwise prognostic impact across all strata compared to the low-risk group. Among patients with ECOG performance status of ≤2 (n = 1437), intermediate-I, intermediate-II, and high-risk groups were associated with HRs of 1.37 (95% CI, 1.11–1.69), 1.88 (95% CI, 1.41–2.49), and 2.27 (95% CI, 1.65–3.11), respectively (p < 0.001). In those with ECOG ≥ 3 (n = 264), corresponding HRs were 2.23 (95% CI, 1.50–3.30), 2.05 (95% CI, 1.20–3.51), and 3.12 (95% CI, 1.86–5.24) (p < 0.001).

Prognostic trends were preserved across treatment intensity subgroups. Among patients receiving doublet regimens (n = 389), HRs for the intermediate-I, intermediate-II, and high-risk groups were 1.69 (95% CI, 1.15–2.47), 2.38 (95% CI, 1.43–3.93), and 3.92 (95% CI, 2.54–6.07), respectively. In patients treated with triplet or quadruplet regimens (n = 1383), HRs were 1.49 (95% CI, 1.21–1.83), 1.81 (95% CI, 1.36–2.41), and 2.02 (95% CI, 1.42–2.86) (p < 0.001 for both subgroups).

R2-ISS–based stratification showed similarly consistent gradients. In patients with stage I/II disease (n = 350), the intermediate-I, intermediate-II, and high-risk groups had HRs of 2.69 (95% CI, 1.50–4.83), 3.33 (95% CI, 1.52–7.33), and 3.63 (95% CI, 1.65–7.97), respectively. In those with stage III/IV disease (n = 476), corresponding HRs were 1.68 (95% CI, 1.19–2.37), 1.79 (95% CI, 1.10–2.93), and 2.84 (95% CI, 1.71–4.73), respectively (p < 0.001 for all).

In multivariable analysis (Table 3 and Fig. S8), the MM-CI retained its independent prognostic value after adjustment for ECOG performance status, frontline treatment intensity, and R2-ISS stage. Adjusted HRs for the intermediate-I, intermediate-II, and high-risk MM-CI groups were 1.95 (95% CI, 1.44–2.64), 2.07 (95% CI, 1.36–3.16), and 2.75 (95% CI, 1.78–4.25), respectively (p < 0.001 for all). Among covariates, both R2-ISS stage and ECOG performance status remained significant predictors of OS (p < 0.05), while frontline treatment intensity did not show a statistically significant association with survival (adjusted p = 0.156).

Discussion

We developed a comorbidity-based MM risk model, termed the MM-CI, by integrating patient-related factors—including male sex, advanced age, congestive heart failure, cerebrovascular disease, hepatic disease, and prior cancer—using data from a nationwide Korean claims database (HIRA). This model was externally validated in two independent real-world cohorts from Korea and Japan, and demonstrated robust prognostic performance independent of established clinical risk factors, including ECOG performance status, frontline treatment intensity, and cytogenetic abnormalities defined by the R2-ISS framework. The MM-CI outperformed the conventional CCI model and concise CCI-based model derived from the IMWG frailty score, which was developed exclusively from clinical trial populations and has limited real-world validation despite widespread clinical adoption [4]. Our model addresses this gap by leveraging multivariate modeling and real-world data to generate a streamlined, statistically robust, and clinically applicable comorbidity index.

Although the CCI has been widely used to quantify comorbidity burden and predict survival, its application in MM is limited. First, it was originally developed to estimate short-term mortality in general medical populations and may not capture the chronic course of hematologic malignancies such as MM [22]. Second, its weighting scheme is static and does not account for disease-specific relevance or population heterogeneity [23]. Third, it fails to reflect advances in treatment and prognostic paradigms that have emerged over recent decades [24, 25]. In response, alternative models such as the IMWG frailty score and the Revised Myeloma Comorbidity Index (R-MCI) have been proposed. The R-MCI incorporates variables such as renal and lung function, frailty scores, and age [7]. While these models offer enhanced precision, their implementation can be time-consuming and may require functional assessments not routinely available in clinical practice. Moreover, the R-MCI was developed using prospectively enrolled clinical trial populations, which typically exclude patients with severe comorbidities through strict eligibility criteria. As a result, these models may underrepresent patients with severe comorbidities—precisely those who pose the greatest clinical challenge in the real world.

In contrast, our model was derived from a nationwide real-world cohort of 17,273 patients and validated in a multi-institutional setting across two countries. This approach allowed inclusion of patients with a broader spectrum of comorbidities—particularly those often underrepresented in trials. The model was developed through rigorous multivariate selection from 13 candidate variables known to influence survival in MM. Six final predictors—male sex, advanced age, congestive heart failure, cerebrovascular disease, hepatic disease, and prior cancer—were independently associated with OS. Several of these variables (e.g., congestive heart failure and prior cancer) are commonly used as exclusion criteria in clinical trials but are frequently encountered in practice. Notably, although renal disease was the most prevalent comorbidity (22.4%) and retained statistical significance in full models, its incremental prognostic contribution was limited after accounting for age and overlapping cardiorenal and vascular conditions. As such, it did not meet the threshold for inclusion in the final simplified MM-CI scoring system.

This finding highlights the value of real-world data in building inclusive, generalizable risk models. Time-dependent ROC analysis demonstrated that the MM-CI consistently outperformed both the conventional age-adjusted CCI and the concise CCI used in the IMWG frailty score across all time points. Moreover, its prognostic value remained significant after adjusting for ECOG status, treatment intensity, and R2-ISS—underscoring its utility as an independent risk tool.

However, our study has limitations. First, the HIRA dataset does not capture functional assessments such as ADL or IADL, which are important but difficult to standardize in real-world settings. Moreover, comorbidities in the development cohort were ascertained from administrative ICD‑10 codes in a claims database, which may under‑ or over‑estimate the true burden of some conditions despite the use of validated coding algorithms and stringent confirmation rules. External validation in registry‑based cohorts partially mitigates this concern but does not fully eliminate the possibility of misclassification. Second, because the MM-CI was developed in a Korean nationwide cohort and validated in Korean and Japanese registry cohorts, additional studies are needed to confirm its generalizability in other ethnic groups and healthcare systems. Third, median OS differed substantially between the nationwide claims-based development cohort and the Korean and Japanese registry-based validation cohorts. This discrepancy likely reflects differences in calendar period, treatment intensity and access to novel agents, as well as referral and selection inherent to tertiary academic centers. Additionally, registry datasets may be subject to administrative censoring or incomplete death ascertainment, whereas the HIRA database includes linkage to national mortality records, enabling more accurate survival estimation. Therefore, the MM-CI is best applied for within-cohort risk stratification, and absolute survival comparisons across cohorts should be interpreted with caution. Finally, our cohorts were accrued largely during an era dominated by proteasome inhibitor- and IMiD-based doublet and triplet regimens, with the more recent introduction of quadruplets, and had limited exposure to CAR-T cells, bispecific antibodies and antibody–drug conjugates. Extrapolation of the MM-CI to modern immunotherapy-based strategies should therefore be cautious and will require prospective validation in immunotherapy-treated populations.

In conclusion, the MM-CI provides a simple, objective, and statistically robust comorbidity-based risk score that is readily applicable in routine practice. It adds independent prognostic information beyond R2-ISS, ECOG performance status and treatment intensity, and may help tailor real-world treatment decisions, including transplant eligibility and the choice between doublet, triplet, or quadruplet frontline regimens. Future studies—particularly in cohorts receiving CAR-T cells, bispecific antibodies, or antibody–drug conjugate-containing regimens—are warranted to test, refine, and, if necessary, recalibrate the MM-CI for contemporary immunotherapy-based treatment strategies.
