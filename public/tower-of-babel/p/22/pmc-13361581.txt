Prevalence and determinants of deworming uptake among under-five children in Tanzania: evidence from the Tanzania Demographic and Health Survey 2022
Authors: Md. Rayhan Kabir, Shawkatul Islam, Joya Sarker, Prionty Das, Md. Moyazzem Hossain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSoil-transmitted helminth (STH) infections remain a major public health concern in many low- and middle-income countries among children under five. Regular deworming is recommended to reduce infection-related morbidity and improve children’s health. Therefore, this study aims to determine the prevalence and determinants of deworming uptake among children aged 12–59 months in Tanzania.MethodsThis study used data from the 2022 Tanzania Demographic and Health Survey (TDHS), accounting for sampling weights, clustering, and stratification to analyze 6710 children aged 12–59 months. Descriptive statistics, Chi-square tests, and a multilevel mixed-effects logistic regression model were used to identify individual-, household-, and community-level factors associated with poor deworming uptake among children nested within survey clusters.ResultsOverall, the prevalence of poor deworming uptake among children was 50%. Compared with children aged 12–15 months, those aged 16–19 months (AOR: 0.46; 95% CI 0.39–0.55) and 20–59 months (AOR: 0.46; 95% CI 0.38–0.55) had significantly lower odds of poor deworming uptake. Vitamin A supplementation (AOR: 0.10; 95% CI 0.08–0.11), older maternal age (25–34 years: AOR: 0.78; 95% CI 0.64–0.94; 35–49 years: AOR: 0.75; 95% CI 0.58–0.97), media exposure (AOR: 0.70; 95% CI 0.60–0.81), and unmarried maternal status (AOR: 0.72; 95% CI 0.62–0.84), urban residence (AOR: 0.75; 95% CI 0.58–0.96), and residence in Zanzibar (AOR: 0.29; 95% CI 0.17–0.50), Southern Highlands (AOR: 0.49; 95% CI 0.31–0.78), and the Eastern zone (AOR: 0.38; 95% CI 0.26–0.57) were associated with significantly lower odds of poor deworming uptake.ConclusionPoor deworming uptake remains a significant public health concern among Tanzanian children aged 12–59 months. Child age, maternal characteristics, media exposure, place of residence, and regional disparities were significant predictors of poor deworming uptake. Integrated deworming into routine child health services, strengthening maternal health education, and expanding community-based outreach in underserved areas could improve deworming uptake and support Tanzania’s progress toward national child health priorities and global soil-transmitted helminth control targets.

Introduction

Soil-transmitted helminthiases (STHs) are among the most common neglected tropical diseases (NTDs), affecting over 100 countries worldwide [1]. STH infections mainly include roundworm (Ascaris lumbricoides), whipworm (Trichuris trichiura), and hookworm, which can cause severe health problems [2]. Globally, approximately 1.5 billion people are affected [3], with preschool-age children, school-age children, women of reproductive age, and pregnant women bearing a disproportionate burden of infection [4–8]. Of the 20.5 million children affected, nearly 60% live in only 10 countries: Angola, Brazil, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Ethiopia, India, Indonesia, Mozambique, Nigeria, Pakistan, and the Philippines [9]. Over 260 million children under the age of 5 live in areas where these parasites are transmitted intensively and require deworming [10]. In addition, refugees, migrants, indigenous people, individuals with disabilities, women, children, rural residents, and children from ethnic minorities face a greater risk of these infections due to inadequate healthcare access [11]. Intestinal worms inhabit the human intestine, and transmission occurs through the ingestion of eggs or larvae or by skin contact with contaminated soil [12]. STH infections are closely associated with inadequate sanitation, poor hygiene practices, and a lack of access to clean water [13]. Children under 5 are particularly vulnerable because of their developing immune systems, frequent contact with contaminated soil during play, and poorer hygiene habits [4]. To control these infections, large-scale preventive treatment, commonly known as deworming, is widely implemented [6]. Deworming involves the administration of anthelmintic drugs, primarily albendazole or mebendazole, to eliminate existing parasites and reduce future parasite-related morbidity [14].

In Tanzania, STH transmission remains highly prevalent, particularly in regions with inadequate water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) infrastructure [15]. Tanzania showed co-coverage of only 44.7% for vitamin A and deworming medication, indicating that many children remain unprotected against STH infections [16]. Despite significant economic progress, Tanzania too faces socioeconomic inequalities across population groups and geographical regions, which may limit access to essential child health services [17]. Furthermore, the prevalence and intensity of STH infections are strongly associated with access to improved WASH services [18]. As a result, WASH interventions have been recognized as an important complement to deworming programs for sustainable STH control and reduced transmission [19]. The National Neglected Tropical Diseases Control Programme (NTDCP) implements preventive chemotherapy with albendazole for at-risk populations [20]. Despite these efforts, the 2015 Tanzania Demographic and Health Survey (TDHS) reported that although 98% of pregnant women attended antenatal care (ANC), only 60.1% received deworming medication, remaining below the WHO-recommended coverage target of 75% [21].

Eliminating STH-related morbidity in children is one of the World Health Organization (WHO) global targets for 2030 [22]. To achieve this goal, WHO recommends periodic deworming of children aged 12–59 months in endemic areas, typically at six-month intervals, using albendazole or mebendazole as part of preventive chemotherapy programs [23–25]. The organization has also promoted mass drug administration and routine anthelmintic treatment as key strategies for STH control, following commitments made through the World Health Assembly resolution adopted in 2001 [26, 27]. In 2001, representatives at the World Health Assembly universally recognized a resolution urging endemic countries, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa, including Tanzania, to begin seriously tackling worms. Deworming is a cost-effective intervention that helps reduce the burden of STH infections, prevent iron loss caused by hookworm, and improve child growth and developmental outcomes [28, 29].

Previous studies have identified several factors significantly associated with poor deworming uptake, including child-related factors (e.g., children’s age, sex, diarrhea, vitamin A supplementation, birth order, and twin status), parental characteristics (e.g., age, education, employment, and marital status), and household and contextual factors (e.g., wealth status, media exposure, place of residence, and geographical region) [6, 30]. However, despite the importance of deworming in preventing soil-transmitted helminth (STH) infections, uptake remains low among children under five years of age in many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), including Tanzania. Evidence on the prevalence and determinants of deworming uptake in Tanzania remains limited, particularly using recent nationally representative data. Furthermore, previous studies have primarily focused on individual-level factors, whereas this study broadens the scope by examining both individual- and community-level determinants of deworming uptake using a multilevel analytical approach. Since health behaviors are shaped by both individual and community factors, a multilevel modeling approach provides a more comprehensive understanding of these influences than traditional regression methods. This study uses data from the 2022 Tanzania Demographic and Health Survey (TDHS) to investigate both individual- and community-level determinants of deworming uptake among children under five in Tanzania. The findings may provide important evidence to inform policy development, strengthen deworming and child health programs, and support targeted interventions to improve deworming coverage and reduce the burden of soil-transmitted helminth infections among children under five in Tanzania.

Methods and materials

Study design and data sources

This study used secondary data from the 2022 Tanzania Demographic and Health Survey (TDHS) and Malaria Indicator Survey (TDHS-MIS) in a cross-sectional design. Conducted at regular intervals since the 1990s, the TDHS-MIS provides nationally representative data on a broad range of health indicators. The final report for TDHS 2022 includes a detailed overview of the sampling process [31].

Sampling procedures

The TDHS employed a two-stage stratified cluster sampling design. In the first stage, 629 clusters (enumeration areas or EAs) were selected, comprising 211 urban and 418 rural areas. Households in each enumeration area were carefully selected for the second stage, and the second stage involved selecting 26 households systematically from each enumeration area. The TDHS comprises several datasets, including files for women (IR), children (KR), births (BR), and households (HR). This study utilized the children dataset (KR file). After restricting the data to living children aged 12–59 months, 8195 eligible children were identified. Following the exclusion of observations with missing values for the study variables, the final weighted sample available for analysis comprised 6,710 children.

Outcome variable

The dependent variable was deworming status among children aged 12–59 months. It was dichotomized as "poor" (did not receive deworming medication, coded as 1) and "good" (received deworming medication, coded as 0). This classification was made according to the DHS guidelines [32]. Identifying factors associated with non-uptake helps improve program targeting.

Independent variables

Variables were selected from the 2022 TDHS dataset based on both data availability and theoretical relevance supported by previous literature [4, 33]. A total of 15 independent variables were included in the analysis. We grouped these variables into two distinct categories: individual-level and community-level factors. The individual factors of the variables were mothers age (15–24, 25–34 and 25–49 years), maternal education (no education, primary, secondary, higher), husband’s education (no education, primary, secondary, higher), child sex (female, male), child’s age (12–15, 16–19,20–59 months), the wealth index was grouped into three categories: poor (poorest and poorer), middle, and rich (richer and richest), recent diarrhea (yes, no), vitamin A supplementation (yes, no), twin type (single birth, 1st of multiple, 2nd of multiple), birth order (1st, 2nd–3rd, 4th +), working status (working, not working), marital status (married and others) and media exposure was coded as “Yes” if the respondent used any newspaper/magazine, radio, TV, or internet; otherwise coded as “No”. Community-related factors include place of residence (urban, rural), and region (Central, Northern, Eastern, Southern, Southern Highlands, Western, Lake, Zanzibar, South West Highlands).

Statistical analysis

Data management, variable coding, and statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 17 and R programming. Sampling weights, primary sampling units, and strata were incorporated into the analysis to account for the complex survey design and obtain accurate statistical estimates. These adjustments were applied to ensure that the results accurately reflected the target population and that standard errors were estimated correctly in accordance with the survey design. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages, were used to summarize the characteristics of the study population. Chi-square tests were used to assess the bivariate association between deworming uptake and each independent variable. Variables significant at P < 0.05 were included in the multivariable analysis. To visually illustrate the flow and relationships between key categorical variables (such as residence, education, wealth, and deworming status), a Sankey diagram was constructed. A multilevel mixed-effects logistic regression model was used to identify predictors of poor deworming uptake, as the TDHS data have a hierarchical structure with children nested within households and communities. Models were fitted sequentially for each analysis as follows: Null Model (Model I): An intercept-only model was used to estimate baseline community-level variation and compute the intra-cluster correlation coefficient (ICC). Model II: Added individual-level factors (child sex, child’s age, mother’s age, maternal education, husband’s education, wealth index, recent diarrhea, vitamin A supplementation, twin type, birth order, working status, media exposure, marital status). Model III: added community-level factors (residence and region). Model IV (Full Model): included only both individual and community-level variables. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and p-values were reported for the final multilevel model. Variables with p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Model fit and parsimony were evaluated using the Akaike information criterion (AIC) and Bayesian information criterion (BIC), with lower values indicating a better-fitting model.

Results

Table 1 presents the descriptive characteristics of the study participants and the results of the bivariable logistic regression analysis. Several factors including residence, child’s age, mother’s age, maternal education, husband’s education, wealth index, recent diarrhea, vitamin A supplementation, birth order, working status, media exposure, marital status, and region, were significantly associated with poor deworming uptake (all p < 0.05). Detailed crude odds ratios (CORs) with 95% confidence intervals are presented in Table 1. The majority of children resided in rural areas (74.28%), where poor deworming uptake was more prevalent (54.60%) than in urban areas (36.82%), and 48.28% of children aged 20–59 months had poor deworming uptake. Similarly, children with young mothers (15–24 years) showed the highest prevalence of poor deworming (53.53%), and Poor households had a higher prevalence of poor deworming uptake (60.49%) compared to middle and rich households. Although the majority of children in this study had not experienced recent diarrhea (93.02%), 50.61% showed poor deworming uptake, and this proportion was higher among children who did not receive vitamin A (76.91%). Among children with poor deworming uptake, 53.10% had mothers who were not employed, and children with no media exposure had poor deworming uptake (62.02%). Discussion

This study aimed to examine the prevalence of deworming uptake and its associated determinants among children aged 12–59 months using multilevel analysis of data from the 2022 Tanzania Demographic and Health Survey (TDHS). Nearly half of Tanzania’s children aged 12–59 months remained unprotected against soil-transmitted helminth (STH) infections due to low deworming coverage. This finding highlights an important public health challenge and may reflect persistent health system barriers and inequities in access to deworming services [34]. Although limited evidence is available on deworming coverage in Tanzania, the findings of this study indicate suboptimal coverage when compared with the World Health Organization (WHO) recommended minimum target of 75%. Similar challenges in achieving adequate deworming coverage have also been reported in several low- and middle-income countries in sub-Saharan Africa, where resource constraints and implementation gaps remain key barriers [5, 15]. Substantial regional variation in deworming uptake was observed across Tanzania. Our findings indicate that, compared with children living in the Northern region, those residing in the Eastern, Southern Highlands, and Zanzibar regions were significantly less likely to experience poor deworming uptake. This suggests that a child’s place of residence plays an important role in shaping access to deworming services. Similar regional disparities have also been reported in demographic and health surveys from other low- and middle-income countries. These differences may be explained by variations in the strength of local health systems, the effectiveness of outreach activities, the level of support from implementing partners, and the availability of healthcare infrastructure [35].

The multilevel analysis identified several significant determinants at the individual, household, and community levels, consistent with findings from studies of child health interventions elsewhere [36]. At the individual, household, and community levels, child age, maternal age, maternal status, vitamin A supplementation, media exposure, and recent diarrhea were significant factors associated with poor deworming uptake. Overall, these findings suggest that deworming coverage is strongly influenced by access to preventive child health services, health information, and contextual differences in healthcare delivery [16, 37]. This result is consistent with studies showing that better maternal education improves access to preventative treatments, health literacy, and health-seeking behavior [38, 39]. The association between maternal employment and deworming uptake observed in this study aligns with findings from sub-Saharan Africa and East Africa, where maternal employment has been reported as an important factor influencing children’s use of deworming medicine [5, 33].

Children who received vitamin A supplementation were less likely to have poor deworming uptake. This may be because vitamin A supplementation and deworming are often delivered through integrated child health services, allowing children to receive multiple preventive interventions during the same healthcare visit [23, 40]. However, this finding should be interpreted with caution, as the cross-sectional design of the study does not establish a causal relationship. The association may also be influenced by other factors, such as caregivers’ health-seeking behavior, regular contact with routine child health services, or unmeasured differences among families who consistently use preventive health programmers [16]. Thus, the finding suggests that integrated child health platforms are important for service delivery, but their reach and effectiveness may vary across populations [41].

Children residing in urban areas had significantly lower odds of poor deworming uptake than those residing in rural areas, indicating better deworming coverage among urban children. This finding is consistent with predictions of increased exposure to health information in metropolitan environments, improved physical access to medical facilities, and a higher density of service providers [42]. Regional and residence-based differences may also reflect variations in health system performance, outreach coverage, medicine availability, implementation of neglected tropical disease programs, and local water, sanitation, and hygiene conditions in Tanzania [43]. This finding suggests that deworming in Tanzania may often be delivered reactively during illness-related healthcare visits rather than routinely as a preventive intervention [4]. Children presenting with diarrhea may receive deworming medication as part of syndromic management, or it may be provided alongside oral rehydration salts or zinc during facility-based diarrhea care under integrated management of childhood illness approaches [40]. This pattern is consistent with evidence from the Somali DHS investigation, where children without diarrhea were more likely not to receive deworming, suggesting a similar reactive treatment dynamic [4]. However, our results differ from evidence in other East African settings, with more established deworming programs, where treatment is often delivered systematically through campaign- or school-based platforms rather than being linked to illness episodes [5]. These differences suggest that barriers to preventive deworming in Tanzania may be greater than barriers to treatment received during illness-related care. Such barriers may include low caregiver awareness of deworming benefits, inconsistent medicine supplies for routine distribution, and the opportunity cost of seeking well-child services [44]. Strengthening deworming coverage in Tanzania, therefore, requires expanding the reach of integrated child health platforms while also supporting delivery channels that do not depend on children becoming ill before receiving treatment [45].

Limitations of the study

There are a few limitations to this study. The study had a cross-sectional design, which prevents determining a causal relationship between the identified factors and deworming uptake. Second, the use of secondary survey data may have been subject to recall bias and unmeasured confounding due to the lack of program-level data, including deworming drug availability, outreach implementation, and district-level endemicity. Third, the deworming medicines used, their dosages, and the frequency of deworming were not included in the DHS data, making it impossible to assess compliance with the WHO-recommended 6-month deworming schedule. Longitudinal and district-level studies are recommended for the future to better understand causal pathways and regions of deworming uptake. In addition, the TDHS does not collect information on the reasons for non-receipt of deworming medication, such as service availability, missed opportunity, or caregiver/community refusal. Therefore, these factors could not be distinguished or assessed in the present study.

Conclusion

This study found that poor uptake of deworming remains a substantial public health challenge among children aged 12–59 months in Tanzania, with nearly half of eligible children not receiving deworming medication. In this study, child age, mother’s age, exposure to media, vitamin A supplementation, marital status, place of residence, and geographic location were found to be significant in determining the poor uptake of deworming, suggesting that individual, household, and community factors influence the access to child health preventive services. The results highlight the importance of increasing routine and community-based deworming efforts, increasing health education outreach via mass media, and reducing disparities between regions in service delivery. Ways to increase access to preventive child health services, improve WASH interventions, and strengthen monitoring of the WHO-recommended deworming schedule may help boost coverage and mitigate inequalities. These strategies can enhance deworming uptake, mitigate the burden of the soil-transmitted helminthes, and boost child health outcomes in Tanzania when combined.
