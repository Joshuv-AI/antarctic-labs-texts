Development of migraine self-care scale
Authors: Emine Ekici, Füsun Afşar, Fatih Ozdag
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSelf-care is known to have the ability to lessen the frequency and intensity of chronic migraines, thereby enhancing the patient’s functional ability and quality of life. No scale has been found in the literature to assess self-care in chronic migraine patients. This study was carried out as an initial investigation of a scale that would be created to evaluate migraine sufferers’ self-care. 156 migraine-diagnosed patients who applied to the neurology outpatient clinic between March and June 2024 and met the sampling criteria participated in this methodological study. The research data were obtained using the Personal Information Form and the Migraine Self-Care Scale. Migraine self-care measurements were tested by performing validity and reliability analyses. Exploratory factor analysis, confirmatory factor analysis, and the content validity index were used to analyze the data’s validity. Reliability analysis was performed using Pearson’s product-moment correlation and Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient. The Migraine Self-Care Scale adapted for individuals diagnosed with migraine has a three-dimensional structure consisting of 14 items. To evaluate the stability of the scale over time, test-retest measurements were made at least two weeks apart, and there was no variation in the average scores. (p > .05). The construct validity of the Migraine Self-Care Scale was confirmed with an adjusted goodness-of-fit index (AGFI) > 0.85 and a comparative fit index (CFI) > 0.92. The content validity index of each item was 99%. The adjusted item-total correlations of the scale ranged from 0.347 to 0.599. The internal consistency Cronbach’s alpha value was found to be 0.780. The scale has a minimum possible value of 14 and a maximum possible value of 56. This study has shown that the Migraine Self-Care Scale is valid and reliable. It might be claimed that migraine sufferers’ self-care increases in tandem with their scale score. It can be used as a measurement tool to determine the self-care of migraine patients.Keywords: Migraine, Self-care, Methodological studySubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Neurology

Introduction

The most common form of migraine is frequent episodes of moderate to severe pulsating and throbbing pain localized to one side of the head. The pain is caused by the activation of nerve fibers within the wall of the cerebral blood vessels that travel within the meninges (three layers of membranes that protect the brain and)1. Recurrent headache attacks accompanied by nausea and vomiting cause serious functional impairments and reduced participation in social and recreational activities2. The World Health Organization (WHO) has listed migraine among the diseases that most severely impair people. The 2019 Global Burden of Disease study claims, that migraine ranks second as a cause of disability across all age groups worldwide and first for women aged 15–493,4. Migraine affects women three times more than men5. Migraine can lead to physical, psychological, and social consequences, affecting work, school, social interactions, and familial responsibilities6. Research has shown that migraine-related absences cost the US economy $21.3 billion annually in lost productivity7. Little is known about how migraine patients actually manage their pain and perceive the effectiveness of pain management/treatment strategies8. In addition to recognizing and avoiding triggers, lifestyle modifications play an essential role in migraine prevention9. In one study, sleep, exercise, diet, and maintaining a migraine journal were found to be the fundamental lifestyle choices associated with this disease10. Non-pharmacological therapies have been shown to be effective in some cases in managing migraines11. Consistent lifestyle choices may also help prevent the transition from episodic to chronic migraine9. Identifying and modifying lifestyle factors associated with migraine may inform the investigation of migraine pathophysiology and improve outcomes through lifestyle interventions12.

Patients’ use of self-care skills is associated with treatment adherence and lifestyle modifications. Self-care entails equipping patients with tools to manage chronic illness through shared decision-making, access to resources, and problem-solving—all critical for effective migraine management. Recent studies suggest that supporting self-care in migraine may reduce attack frequency and, severity while improving daily functioning and quality of life8,13–15.

A review of the literature indicates that no specific, valid, and reliable tool currently exists to assess self-care practices tailored to individuals with migraine. Therefore, this study aims to develop a “Migraine Self-Care Scale.”

Methods

Study design

This study employed a methodological design, which focuses on the systematic development, testing, and validation of measurement tools. In particular, it aimed to develop and psychometrically evaluate a scale measuring self-care behaviors among individuals diagnosed with migraine. The study followed established scale development procedures, including item generation, expert validation, pilot testing, item reduction, and reliability and validity analyses.

The methodological steps followed in the study were structured according to a six-step process outlined in Fig. 1: (1) Literature Review and Item Pool Generation, (2) Expert Review and Content Validity, (3) Pilot Testing, (4) Item Reduction and Exploratory Factor Analysis, (5) Confirmatory Factor Analysis, and (6) Reliability Testing. Each of these steps is elaborated in the relevant subsections of the Methods section.

Study population and sample

It is advised that a methodological study have a sample size that is five to ten times the number of items16. As suggested by Seckin (2015) and Cakir and Gungor (2016), a sample size ten times the number of items was considered in this study while determining the sample size16,17. In this study, ten times the number of each item in the Migraine Self-Care Scale was included for sample size. Migraine-diagnosed patients who visited a training and research hospital’s neurology outpatient clinic between March and June 2024 comprised the study’s population. A convenience sampling method was employed by recruiting patients who met the inclusion criteria and visited the neurology outpatient clinic during the study period. Participants were recruited sequentially until the target sample size was reached.

The study sample consisted of 156 patients who applied to the hospital’s neurology outpatient clinic between March and June 2024, who were over the age of 18 and wanted to participate in the study voluntarily, understood Turkish, had no communication barriers, and were diagnosed with migraine.

Inclusion criteria were being 18 years or older, being diagnosed with migraine by a neurologist, being literate in Turkish, and providing informed consent.

Exclusion criteria included having a severe psychiatric diagnosis, cognitive impairment, chronic use of psychotropic medication, or other neurological conditions interfering with self-report.

Data collection tools

The Personal Information Form and the Migraine Self-Care Scale were used to collect data for the study. The self-report method was used to gather data from the participants in an interview-ready room in the neurology outpatient clinic.

Personal information form

A structured questionnaire including 11 items on sociodemographic characteristics (e.g., age, sex, education) and 4 items related to migraine clinical history (e.g., frequency of attacks, duration, medication use).

Migraine Self-Care scale

The scale was developed in Turkish by researchers to determine the self-care of patients diagnosed with migraine. The initial item pool of 55 items was generated based on a qualitative content analysis of semi-structured interviews with 12 migraine patients and 6 clinical experts (neurologists and nurse specialists). Data from interviews were coded independently by two researchers using inductive content analysis. Emerging codes were grouped into themes to inform item formulation. These were then reviewed and refined by expert consensus.

There are 14 items and three subdimensions on the scale. The scale subdimensions are Nutrition and Sleep Behaviors (1–5), Migraine Triggering Behaviors (6–10), and Pain Management (11–14). The Migraine Self-Care Scale is a four-point Likert-type scale (1 “Never,” 2 “Sometimes,” 3 “Often,” 4 “Regularly”). Items 1 and 5 are reverse-scored to avoid response bias. For scoring, item responses are coded from 1 to 4, with higher scores indicating greater self-care. The total scale score is obtained by summing the scores of the 14 items. Thus, the minimum possible score is 14 and the maximum is 56.

A high score indicates high self-care in migraine. The scale’s overall Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was determined to be.780.

The three subdimensions—Nutrition and Sleep Behaviors, Migraine Triggering Behaviors, and Pain Management—were derived inductively through exploratory factor analysis and conceptually aligned with the SEEDS framework (Sleep, Exercise, Education, Diet, and Stress), which is widely used in migraine self-care literature.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 21.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and LISREL version 8.8 (Scientific Software International, Inc., USA). LISREL was used for confirmatory factor analysis (CFA).

To assess the invariance across time, the test-retest approach was used to calculate the Pearson correlation coefficient for the scale’s reliability assessment. The item-total correlation coefficient and the internal consistency coefficient were evaluated using the Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient and the Pearson product-moment correlation coefficient, respectively. Expert views were assessed for the scale’s content validity using the Lawshe technique, and the structural validity was assessed using exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) was conducted using Principal Axis Factoring with Varimax rotation. Items with factor loadings ≥ 0.40 were retained. Items with cross-loadings greater than 0.30 on multiple factors, or those with communalities < 0.30, were excluded from the final version. Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was conducted using LISREL 8.8 to evaluate the construct validity of the three-factor structure. The following fit indices and cut-off criteria were considered: Chi-Square/degrees of freedom (χ²/df) ≤ 3.00 (very good), Comparative Fit Index (CFI) ≥ 0.90, Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) ≤ 0.08, Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) ≤ 0.08, Adjusted Goodness-of-Fit Index (AGFI) ≥ 0.85. Model modifications were not performed; correlated errors were not allowed between items.

For test-retest reliability, a subsample of 40 migraine patients who were not included in the main analysis completed the scale twice, with an interval of two weeks. The Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) was calculated using a two-way mixed-effects model for absolute agreement, resulting in an ICC of 0.788 (95% CI: 0.75–0.90), indicating good reliability.

The Content Validity Ratio (CVR) was calculated using Lawshe’s formula: CVR = (Ne – N/2)/(N/2), where Ne is the number of experts rating the item as “essential” and N is the total number of experts (N = 6). Items with a CVR above the minimum acceptable threshold of 0.99 for 6 experts were retained. The Content Validity Index (CVI) was calculated by averaging the CVR values of retained items and was found to be 0.99.

In all analyses, p <.05 was considered statistically significant. The stages of the study method are shown in Fig. 1.

Content validity analysis

In the process of creating the Migraine Self-Care Scale, a literature review was used to determine the theoretical framework, and a 55-item item pool was prepared by consulting expert opinions on the subject.

The clarity of the scale items in the item pool was evaluated in detail by 6 experts in the field (neurology specialists, internal medicine and public health nursing academicians, and a migraine patient), 1 neurology professor, 3 neurology specialists, 2 internal medicine nurses, and 1 public health nursing academician in terms of quality, expression, usability, suitability for purpose, and the way the items in the scale were answered. Each item was rated by experts on a scale of 1 to 3. with 1 being “should be removed,” 2 being “appropriate but needs to be amended,” and 3 being “appropriate.” The evaluation results showed that 99% of the items received three points. The Lawshe technique was used to analyze the experts’ differences of opinion, and the Content Validity Ratio (CVR) and Content Validity Index (CVI) were used to assess the data collected from the experts. As a result of the evaluation of the experts with the content validity index (CVI), a total of 55 items in the item pool were revised and rearranged to 44 items because they expressed similar issues or were not clearly understood. Content Validity Ratio (CVR) was calculated for each item. All participants evaluated the 44 items in the scale as “appropriate,” and the CVR value was calculated as 0.99. The CVI value of the items was determined to be 99%. Although all 44 items were rated as “appropriate” by experts, 11 items were subsequently removed after pilot testing and item analysis due to redundancy, poor clarity, or low item-total correlations. Expert ratings thus ensured relevance, while item performance in practice guided final selection.

Based on participant feedback from the pilot testing (n = 40), minor wording changes were made to improve item clarity and reduce cognitive load. Items that were confusing or redundant were flagged. In addition, Likert response options were tested for comprehension. As a result, 11 items were removed or merged, reducing the item pool from 55 to 44 before factor analysis.

Following the experts’ assessments, a test study using a 44-item draft scale form was carried out. A total of 40 migraine patients who were selected by the simple randomization method from the study universe and were not included in the study were interviewed in the medical facility where the research was carried out. It was emphasized to the participants that they could ask questions and make suggestions regarding the points that were not understood in the scale items. In the pilot study conducted by the researchers, it was determined whether there were any matters that needed clarification or that were unclear, whether the instructions were understood, whether the Likert-type grading was appropriate for the items, and the adequacy of the items. The 44-item Migraine Self-Care Scale was used to do an initial statistical analysis based on the data collected from the test study, and the scale was rearranged into three subdimensions and 17 items.

Although some items such as “I keep a migraine diary” or “I know the triggers of my migraine” are considered important in the literature10they were excluded from the scale due to low item-total correlation coefficients and insufficient statistical contribution to the construct. This may be related to participants’ limited engagement with these behaviors in daily life or their overlapping nature with broader self-care items. Including such items would have negatively affected the scale’s internal consistency and factorial clarity.

Construct validity

To ascertain the Migraine Self-Care Scale’s construct validity, exploratory factor analysis was used. Principal components and varimax rotation techniques were used in factor analysis. When the initial construct validity of the Migraine Self-Care Scale, consisting of 17 items, was tested with factor analysis, it was performed with a sample size of 156, which is at least 9 times the number of items. The data’s appropriateness for factor analysis was assessed using the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) test, and the significance of the connections between the variables was assessed using Bartlett’s test. The KMO coefficient was 0.42, the chi-square value of Bartlett’s test was 613.035, df = 136, and p <.001. These results show that the data are suitable and sufficient for factor analysis (Table 5). It was determined that the 17-item Migraine Self-Care Scale had three factors with eigenvalues ​​above 1.00, explaining 45% of the total variance. The eigenvalues ​​of these factors were 4.034, 1.874, and 1.450, and their contributions to the total variance explained were 25.560%, 11.023%, and 8.535%, respectively. In the scale’s confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), item 6 (“I drink at least 7–8 glasses of water a day”) and item 9 (“‘I consume 3–5 portions of vegetables such as green leaf lettuce and spinach and fruits such as dates, bananas, and grapes every day”) were removed from the scale because their factor loading values ​​were below 0.30. In the factor analysis, item 10 (“I try to control my weight”), which had high factor loadings in many factors but was under more than one factor, was removed because the difference in loading values ​​was less than 0.10. For the final 14 items, factor analysis was repeated. The KMO coefficient in the component analysis was 0.746, and the Bartlett’s test chi-square value was χ² = 117.73 (df = 74, p =.000). These results were deemed highly significant (p <.001) (Table 5). In the exploratory factor analysis of the Migraine Self-Care Scale, consisting of a total of 14 items, it was seen that the eigenvalues ​​of the items grouped under three subdimensions varied between 3.788 and 6.073, the variances they explained varied between 22.491% and 14.030%, and they explained 71% of the total variance (Table 4).

Discussion

To assess the self-care of migraine sufferers in Türkiye, this study sought to create a valid and trustworthy assessment instrument. The scale development study began with the examination of studies on the self-care of migraine patients both globally and domestically. As a result of the literature review, no specific scale was found in our country that measures self-care for migraine. The scale provides a standardized tool for identifying gaps in self-care practices among migraine patients. It can guide healthcare providers in tailoring patient education and interventions based on specific self-care deficiencies.

It has been stated in the literature that lack of self-care is a major risk factor for migraine. A lack of self-care can frequently result in migraine attacks, and self-care interventions are the cornerstone of migraine counseling. The mnemonic “SEEDS,” which stands for “Social, Exercise, Education, Diet, and Sleep,” reminds clinicians and patients to pay attention to these basic lifestyle triggers18. In a quasi-experimental cohort study aimed to determine whether an 8-week outpatient web-based chronic migraine self-management program would reduce migraine disability and increase self-efficacy, the positive effects of self-care were demonstrated14. In the consensus report, which includes ten steps to migraine diagnosis and treatment, the importance of a patient-centered approach and education was emphasized4. To develop a scale, the need for the development of a scale in the relevant field should be revealed19. An evaluation tool for customized migraine treatment and management is required in light of the literature reviews. This scale development study was conducted in this context. The self-care theory was taken as the basis for the development of the migraine self-care scale. While creating the items of the Migraine Self-Care Scale, literature reviews and opinions of experts in the field were taken into account20.

The degree to which participants’ scores on assessments administered at several times or their group rankings based on their scores are constant determines how reliable a test is21,22. Reliability is an indispensable prerequisite for validity, so for a test to be valid, it must be reliable21–23. In the test-retest analysis of the scale we developed, a highly significant positive relationship was found between the reliability coefficients of the scale’s two measurement scores and three subdimensions, ranging from 0.74 to 0.94.

Internal consistency is a good measure of how homogeneous the questions that are assumed to measure a certain area are among themselves, whether the questions reach their intended destination, that is, whether they measure only the desired concept24. The reliability of the scale was determined using item-total correlation and Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient. It is recommended that Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient be close to 1 and that values ​​greater than 0.6 be taken into consideration25,26. In the study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of the subdimensions of the scale were determined as 0.773 for Nutrition and Sleep Behaviors, 0.766 for Migraine Triggering Behaviors, 0.670 for Pain Management, and 0.788 for the total scale. These results, when compared with the literature, show that the scale is highly reliable25,26. The construct validity of the Migraine Self-Care Scale was tested using EFA and CFA. Before the factor analysis, KMO and Bartlett’s test of sphericity were performed to test the sample size and suitability for factor analysis. The initial Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) value of 0.42 for the 17-item version was below the commonly accepted minimum threshold of 0.60, indicating insufficient sampling adequacy. Therefore, item reduction was prioritized and, following refinement, the final 14-item version yielded an improved KMO of 0.746, supporting factor analysis validity. The KMO coefficient of 0.746 indicates that the sample adequacy is good. The significance of Bartlett’s test of sphericity shows that the scale is suitable for factor analysis. The total variance rate explained should be over 50% in multidimensional scales25. After the exploratory factor analysis, the scale retained its original three-factor structure. These three factors together explained 71% of the total variance, indicating a strong and meaningful underlying construct.

The aim was to test the structure that emerged after EFA with CFA. CFA is used in adaptation studies to assess if newly gathered data supports the original framework25. With this analysis, the scale and factors are reevaluated. The CFA analysis process starts with a set of observed variables like EFA and attempts to explain the relationship between variables using a smaller number of factors27.

The confirmatory factor analysis results demonstrated acceptable fit across multiple indices (e.g., CFI = 0.93, RMSEA = 0.066). These findings confirm that the three-factor model adequately represents the observed data and support the structural validity of the scale16.

While most CFA indices indicated good model fit, the Goodness-of-Fit Index (GFI) was 0.89, slightly below the ideal cut-off of 0.90. This may reflect the relatively small sample size or item homogeneity and should be interpreted with caution.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the sample was drawn from a single tertiary care hospital in Turkey, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings. Future research should include samples from multiple centers and geographic regions to enhance external validity. Second, the sample was predominantly female and comprised participants with higher levels of education, which may impact the generalizability of the findings. While migraine is more common in women, future validation studies of the Migraine Self-Care Scale should include more diverse populations in terms of gender, educational background, and sociocultural contexts to ensure broader applicability.

Third, the study relied solely on self-reported data, which is subject to recall bias and social desirability bias. Although self-reporting is a common method in scale development studies, incorporating objective behavioral measures or clinician assessments in future research could strengthen the scale’s validity.

Fourth, the scale was not compared to other validated self-care or headache-related instruments due to the lack of a migraine-specific self-care scale. However, future studies may explore convergent and discriminant validity by comparing this scale with broader self-care, health behavior, or migraine disability instruments (e.g., MIDAS, HIT-6).

Finally, the test-retest reliability was assessed with a relatively small subgroup. While results were promising, replication with larger samples is warranted. Future research should validate the scale across diverse populations and settings, test its responsiveness to interventions, and explore correlations with clinical outcomes such as attack frequency, quality of life, or medication adherence.
