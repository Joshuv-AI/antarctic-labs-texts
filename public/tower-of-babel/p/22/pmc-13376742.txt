Coherent quantum control of nitrogen vacancy spin with nanoscale magnets
Authors: Md Fahim F Chowdhury, Adi Jung, Léa La Spina, Ausrine Bartasyte, Samuel Margueron, Jayasimha Atulasimha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCoherent control of the nitrogen-vacancy (NV) center in diamond is commonly achieved by microwave fields from conventional antennas, which suffer from scalability and thermal noise. Their spatially extended field profiles also limit their specificity in driving one NV spin without affecting other NV spins. Here, we investigate quantum control of a single NV center with microwave fields generated from a nanoscale magnet that is proximal to the NV center. Our results show nanoscale coherent control with high contrast Rabi oscillations using nearfield microwaves from shape anisotropic nanomagnets of lateral dimensions down to 200 nm, driven by surface acoustic wave (SAW) excitation. Furthermore, we show that varying the acoustic power driving such nanomagnets can achieve control over Rabi frequency. We also report spin-spin relaxation time (T2) of the NV center, measured up to 3.48±0.01 μs using microwave pulses generated by such nanomagnets. The use of the nanoscale magnets to implement highly localized coherent quantum control can replace thermally noisy microwave circuits and demonstrate a path to scalable quantum computing and sensing with NV-defects in diamond and other spin qubits.Subject terms: Electrical and electronic engineering, Spintronics, Magnetic devices

Introduction

Scalable spin-based and other microwave-driven quantum systems require local sources of microwave pulses, which are fundamental to coherent manipulation of spin defects. This is critical for applications such as quantum sensors1–5, many-qubit quantum processors, and quantum repeaters6–9. The research in quantum information science is fueled by the exponential computing capability with the increase in the number of qubits in a processor, unlike neuromorphic10,11 and non-von Neumann computing12–16. Leveraging the principles of superposition, entanglement, and quantum interference, quantum processors can tackle complex problems beyond the reach of classical computers using algorithms like Shor’s and Grover’s16–18 and, more recently, quantum AI19. Likewise, quantum sensing offers high precision in measuring magnetic fields, gravitational waves, and atomic forces, revolutionizing fields like navigation, medical imaging, and environmental monitoring5.

Among many promising candidates for quantum bits, the nitrogen vacancy center has special interest due to its exceptional stability in room temperature, high quantum coherence time, and local spin-entanglement. The coherent control of NV spins has been achieved through electron spin resonance and optical manipulation of the spin of the NV center, which often includes the application of microwave frequency excitation, typically ranging from 1 W to as high as 10 W20–22. The requirement for such high excitation power not only adds complexity to incorporating the spins within a limited space but also poses the risk of significantly disturbing the neighboring spins. The NV center is increasingly favored for quantum sensing applications, as scalable quantum information processing remains limited by challenges such as the lack of scalable entangling gates, the absence of high-fidelity single-shot readout, and difficulties in localized, energy-efficient control. Besides, despite high coherence times, slow single and multi-qubit gate operations hinder effective computation. One strategy for faster gates involves boosting microwave power or control-field amplitude. However, traditional methods, including generating electric currents in transmission lines, generate a non-local magnetic field, leading to decoherence and crosstalk issues. While injecting more current can increase microwave power, it also induces joule heating and dephasing in nearby qubits, impeding scalability. Moreover, increasing the distance of qubits to mitigate these effects complicates entanglement protocols crucial for multi-qubit gate implementation. To localize microwave power in quantum processors or sensors, various methods have been proposed, such as using mechanical resonators, applying strain23–25, leveraging the coupling of spin defects with nearby films via magnetoelasticity26, utilizing spin waves for coherent drive27, and, more recently, all-electrical control of 5 µm-sized magnetic tunnel junction (MTJ)28. The use of a magnetoelastic drive to control an NV spin may lead to different spin wave modes when magnetic films are employed29, although coherent drive has been observed under certain conditions26. Nanoscale magnet-based drive of NVs differs from spin wave27 or magnetoelastic wave26 in magnetic film-based drive of NVs, as nanomagnets are single domain states (see Supplementary Section 2.3) and tend to oscillate in a near single-domain state when driven through magnetoelastic coupling or by voltage control30, spin torques31, etc., producing a highly localized magnetic field30.

Nanomagnets offer two key advantages in coherent drive of NV and other spin qubits: (1) Fundamentally, a nanomagnet, oscillating as a large magnetic dipole, produces a strong magnetic field at the driven frequency with smaller amplitude fields at a couple of harmonics (see Supplementary Sections 4.2 and ref. 30). This behavior also applies to voltage control30, and other driving mechanisms for such nanomagnets. Thus, patterned nanomagnets can scale well to ~10 s of nanometers lateral dimensions and enable high-fidelity single qubit gates30. (2) Furthermore, without the use of nanoscale magnets, challenges persist in achieving high-power excitation for low-noise measurements, despite superior spatial decay32,33 and high-power consumption due to elevated current density or thin film dimensions, ultimately constraining scalability. Magnetoelastic interaction presents a potential solution by reducing input power requirements and generating highly efficient magnetic fields26,29 but does not offer confinement. In contrast, nanoscale magnets enable the generation of highly confined microwaves30, allowing selective driving of proximally located spin qubits (or NV centers) without disturbing the neighboring qubits. In our experiment, while the SAW IDT is positioned at a distance, we emphasize that localized control refers to the local microwave magnetic field at the NV center, which is generated through the near-field dynamics of the nanomagnet. To enable spatial selectivity, SAW IDTs and electrodes for direct strain-induced control can be designed to be highly compact, with dimensions adaptable to the requirements of the quantum system architecture with multiple NV spins.

In this work, we demonstrate coherent control of a single NV center using nanoscale magnets. We show that, under ambient conditions, elliptically shaped anisotropic cobalt nanomagnets can be driven acoustically, which strongly couple with the NV center in diamond to manipulate its spin states. The Rayleigh-mode wave generated by the interdigital transducer (IDT) induces an effective magnetic field in the nanomagnet due to magnetoelastic coupling and thus induces magnetization dynamics. Quantum information system based on an ensemble qubit requires densely packed spins34–36. Precise control over individual or ensemble spins is crucial to ensure high-fidelity multi-qubit gates, which necessitates localized control fields to prevent cross-talk, which can otherwise compromise gate fidelity34. The control field can be localized and reduce the footprint by using nanoscale magnets30,37. In this work, confining the large microwave field to nanoscale dimensions, we achieve reduced noise in the vicinity, thereby facilitating the scalability of spin/NV center-based quantum processors and sensors.

Results

We demonstrate coherent quantum control of the NV-center with microwaves generated by a nanoscale magnet proximal to a nano-diamond (ND) tip with an NV-defect. A simplified experimental set-up can be found in Fig. 1. A device under test consists of an IDT to generate the acoustic waves, and nanomagnets with different sizes and anisotropies in the acoustic delay line of the IDT. The nanomagnets are placed ~400 μm apart from the marginal IDT finger, and ~200 μm from the IDT pads where microwave voltage is applied, as shown in Fig. 1a. Two wire bonds are used on each of the IDT pads to remove the parasitic inductance, improve reliability, and ensure proper electrical contact with the transmission line for efficient coupling with the SAW transducers. The microwave voltage applied to the IDT generates a Rayleigh wave in the piezoelectric substrate, which propagates and induces a time-varying tensile and compressive stress in the nanomagnet deposited on the piezoelectric LiNbO3 substrate. Due to the magnetoelastic coupling, an effective field is induced on the magnetostrictive nanomagnet, thus converting the acoustic energy into magnetization dynamics38,39. The magnetization oscillation due to the Villari effect generates a large oscillating dipolar field in its proximity30. The NV center tip is proximal to and approximately along the easy axis of the elliptical nanomagnets such that the static global bias magnetic field is along the NV axis and the microwave field generated by the oscillating magnetization has a large component perpendicular to the NV axis.

The NV center is an electronic defect in diamond with spin −1, formed by a pair of carbon vacancy and an adjacent substitutional nitrogen atom in the crystal lattice. In NV experiments, measurements of the spin states are always a projection onto the NV axis. We used a single NV center tip made from [100] cut 12C diamond, which leads to NV orientation of ~144 ° with respect to the normal, with a nominal depth of ~10 nm. The NV center is approximately 30 nm away from the nanomagnet surface during measurement in the engaged mode of the quantum scanning microscope. The negatively charged state of the defect provides a spin triplet ground level, which can be optically initialized to ∣ms=0⟩ state and can be coherently transitioned to the excited state ∣ms=±1⟩ by the application of resonant microwave pulses, resembling a quantum bit40. In the absence of a magnetic field, the degenerate ∣±1⟩ levels and the zero level ∣0⟩ are separated by D≅2.87 GHz due to spin-spin coupling. As shown in Fig. 1, we observe a drop of intensity at ~2.87 GHz in the optically detected magnetic resonance (ODMR) plot representing the zero bias field resonance of the NV-center tip with a single defect used in this experiment. Pulsed experiments with NV centers rely on detecting Zeeman shifts in spin transitions via ODMR spectroscopy41,42. Upon the application of a magnetic field, the ∣±1⟩ states split into ∣ms=+1⟩ and ∣ms=−1⟩, which are accessible through two transition frequencies, ν±.

As shown in Fig. 1e, the Zeeman shift, defined by the effective magnetic field along the NV-axis as 2gμBBNV, alters when a 5 mT bias field is applied, causing resonances at 2.830 GHz and 2.9125 GHz. As the NV center approaches the nanomagnet, the Zeeman shift reduces, suggesting an effective static field from the nanomagnets is acting opposite to the 5 mT external bias field. We note that the error bars are on the scale of the magnitude of the data markers and are often smaller than the plotted markers.

Coherent control with nanoscale magnets: Rabi oscillation

Rabi oscillation is a fundamental step towards demonstrating coherent quantum control, along with other pulsed experiments, which represents an evolution of a spin under the constant drive of resonant microwave pulses. We first performed ODMR measurements to examine the NV- electron spin resonance (ESR) frequency between ∣0⟩ and ∣−1⟩ states in the presence of an external bias field of 5 mT applied along the NV-axis and used this frequency to drive the Rabi oscillation.

The pulse sequence for the Rabi oscillation experiment is presented in Fig. 2a. After initializing the spin at ∣ms=0⟩ state, we apply an oscillating voltage at the resonant frequency to the acoustic IDT to excite the nanomagnet to generate a MW control field at the NV-center at a variable pulse duration (τ). The NV spin oscillates periodically between the ground state, ∣ms=0⟩, and the excited state, ∣ms=−1⟩, with a Rabi frequency (ΩRabi) that is dependent on the amplitude of the applied microwave field generated by the nanomagnet at the NV-center spin. The oscillation is visualized by measuring the readout pulses at corresponding delays to measure the spin state ∣ψτ⟩ to construct the Rabi oscillation, which shows a periodic bright (high photon counts/s) and dark (low photon counts/s) PL contrast, which corresponds to the ∣0⟩ and ∣−1⟩ states, respectively. The PL contrast is defined by the relative change of photoluminescence (PL) intensity between different spin states of the NV center. In Fig. 2, the corresponding ODMR spectrum and the standard decaying Rabi oscillations are shown for the nanomagnets of sizes 2 μm x 1.8 μm, 1 μm x 0.9 μm, 500 nm x 470 nm, 200 nm x 180 nm, which demonstrates that surface acoustic wave IDTs can be applied to nanomagnets of various sizes and remain effective as the magnet dimensions are scaled down. The variation of the resonant frequency of the NV center can be attributed to the effective DC field component of the different nanomagnets’ stray fields along the NV center axis, due to the size, shape-anisotropy, and proximity to the NV center. The combined effect of this static field and global magnetic field determines the Zeeman shift and the resonant frequencies for transition to ∣0⟩↔∣−1⟩ or ∣0⟩↔∣+1⟩ states. Fast Rabi frequency ΩRabi = ~7 MHz is achieved with a nanomagnet of size down to 200 nm and almost 28% PL contrast is measured for the 500 nm nanomagnet, which are indicative of robust coherent control of the NV center with a nanoscale magnet even in the presence of the room temperature background noise. In this work, we treat the ∣ms=−1⟩ spin state of the NV center as the excited state and ∣ms=0⟩ as the ground state. However, for visualization using the Bloch sphere, we follow the standard qubit convention where the state ∣0⟩ corresponds to ∣ms=0⟩ and the state ∣1⟩ corresponds to ∣ms=−1⟩. Therefore, the ∣ms=−1⟩ state is mapped to the Bloch sphere ∣1⟩ (see supplementary).

Spin echo measurement (T2)

The spin-spin relaxation time, or transverse magnetization decay, T2 can be measured by the spin echo pulse sequence experiment. We perform spin echo measurement with 2 μm, 500 nm, and 200 nm nanomagnet devices by following the sequence π2−τ2−π−τ2−π2, where the π2-pulse and π-pulse, are defined from the Rabi oscillation at the resonant frequency. The resonant frequency (ODMR) in cases of the 2 μm, 500 nm, and 200 nm nanomagnets are 2.647 GHz at 5 mT, 2.669 GHz at 2.4 mT, and 2.605 GHz at 5 mT bias field, respectively, due to the different bias magnetic field generated by the different sizes of nanomagnets. The room temperature coherence time extracted from the 2 μm nanomagnet microwave drive is T2,2μm = 1.23±0.29 μs at 5 mT external bias field and from the 200 nm nanomagnet microwave drive is T2,200 nm = 1.13±0.17 μs at 5 mT field, while an elevated T2 of 3.48±0.01 μs is observed with 500 nm nanomagnet. This difference in the T2 time is likely due to the dephasing caused by interaction with the nanomagnet, whose magnetization fluctuates at room temperature. The pulse sequence and the experimental data of the room temperature NV-spin dynamics are presented in Fig. 4. The experimental data is fitted with a stretched exponential function (red curves). Discussion

By using a magnetostrictive nanomagnet whose magnetization is driven by SAW through magnetoelastic coupling, we have demonstrated coherent quantum control of a proximally located NV-center defect in diamond. We have performed different pulse protocols such as ODMR, Rabi oscillation, and spin-spin relaxation (T2) with the microwave drives generated by nanoscale magnets at room temperature under ambient conditions. While the present experimental results do not claim better energy efficiency compared to the current state of the art, the nanomagnet-based approach demonstrated here holds potential for significant energy efficiency based on theoretical estimates provided in Section 5 of the Supplementary Information with appropriate scaling. These theoretical estimates suggest that, in scaled devices where nanomagnets are positioned within tens of nanometers of the NV centers, this paradigm could achieve up to four orders of magnitude greater energy efficiency than microwave fields generated by conventional electromagnetic (EM) wire antennas.

The bottleneck of the NV centers becoming a viable candidate for quantum computing and sensing, is the limit of scalability due to the non-local control field and high current density required to manipulate the spin to implement fast quantum gates and other sensing protocols. Our work demonstrates that the use of nanomagnets for coherent control of proximally located NV and spin qubits has the potential to solve these problems and leads to scalable quantum computing and quantum sensing with NV centers in diamond and other spin qubits by potentially replacing inefficient antennas and waveguides currently used for such quantum control.

Thus, this work brings together the generating proximal microwaves using nanoscale magnetism/spintronics towards enabling advances in NV center-based or other spin qubit-based quantum computing and sensing by demonstrating the use of nanomagnets for coherent quantum control of an NV-defect. It can stimulate further research on the use of nanomagnets with different magnetic materials for higher magnetoelastic coupling, magnetoelectric coefficient/voltage control of magnetic anisotropy (VCMA) coefficients, higher magnetization and lower damping, etc., which can be scaled to tens of nanometer lateral dimension or driven with local strain, VCMA, and spin orbit torques instead of SAW to enable energy efficiency and downscaling.

Methods

Experiment description

The delay line was fabricated by a pair of IDTs separated by a 2 mm cavity. The IDTs and nanoscale magnets are fabricated on a 128° Y-cut, X-propagating LiNbO3 substrate. The IDTs with a wavelength of 1480 nm are made of an 80 nm thick Al film, which is designed to excite a Rayleigh wave with a nominal resonant frequency of 2.6 GHz. Each IDT contained 15 electrode pairs and an acoustic opening of 100 µm in order to attain an input impedance of 50 Ω. The devices were fabricated by e-beam lithography and metal evaporation. The nanomagnets are patterned with electron beam lithography (EBL) on a bilayer of polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA), followed by deposition of a 4 nm Ti adhesion layer and 14 nm of Co by means of e-beam evaporation technique at a pressure of 2.2 × 10−7 Torr. The free-standing nanomagnets are realized after lift-off. The nanomagnets are placed 400 µm away from the IDT fingers (see supplementary). The wire antenna is positioned about (30–50) µm away from the NV center. We note that the nanomagnet itself is proximal to the NV center (the NV is approximately ~30 nm height from the nanomagnet surface). The proximity between the NV center and the QSM antenna is limited by the manual operability of the position of the floating antenna, instead of an on-chip setup, while the NV center in the AFM tip can be brought closer to the nanomagnet with the high-precision scanner.

The quantum scanning NV microscope (QSM) is sourced commercially from Qzabre LLC with a nanofabricated pillar scanning AFM tip with a shallow NV center with a single defect, which operates under ambient conditions. The NV tips come with different crystal orientations and NV axes. All data with the nanomagnets are collected with an NV tip of orientation θ = 90°, φ = 144° and θ = 55°, φ = 180°. The QSM has a built-in vector magnet to apply a magnetic field in any orientation and was aligned with the NV center axis during the experiment. The applied magnetic field is varied between 2 mT and 8 mT to tune the NV center resonant frequency around 2.6 GHz, which is the resonant frequency of IDT. For ODMR and Rabi plots, the contrast is defined as normalized fluorescence relative to the ∣0⟩ state with no RF pulse (S∣0⟩) with fluorescence after the microwave pulse (S), such that C=(S−S∣0⟩)/S∣0⟩. For spin echo, contrast is the normalized difference in fluorescence signals measured using final +π/2 and −π/2 pulses calculated as (S+x−S−x)/(S+x+S−x). The uncertainties in the extracted T2 (spin echo) times are obtained directly from nonlinear least squares fits to the experimental decay curves. The reported values correspond to the standard deviations derived from the square root of the diagonal elements of the covariance matrix.
