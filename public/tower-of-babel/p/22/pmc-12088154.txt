Hashtag Healthcare: An Assessment of Current Educational Quality and Patient Perceptions of Scoliosis on TikTok
Authors: Reid Chambers, Anthony Yassall, Adam Mansour, Julie Balch Samora, Allen Kadado, Alexis Day, Noor Alkahlout, Kirsten Tulchin-Francis
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSignificant racial disparities exist in healthcare, including in the diagnosis and treatment of scoliosis. The social media platform TikTok is estimated to reach over 1.7 billion monthly users in 2023. It is one of the most popular social media platforms for teenagers and young adults. This study aimed to explore TikTok content metrics related to scoliosis and determine demographic differences across videos.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included TikTok posts searched using Exolyt analytics software for the top hashtags related to scoliosis from Sept 2020–Aug 2022. Data were collected for race and role of the creator and underlying themes. A staged approach was used to analyze content through familiarization, identifying thematic framework, indexing, summarizing, and interpreting data. The educational quality of videos was determined using the DISCERN scale by two independent reviewers (very poor: <26, poor: 27–38, fair: 39–50, good: 51–62, very good: 63–75).ResultsThe top five viewed hashtags #scoliosis, #scoliosisawareness, #scoliosischeck, #scoliosissurgery, #scoliosisgang yielded 220 unique, eligible posts for inclusion. Videos were grouped by the race of individuals seen in the video:176 (80%) white, 25 (11%) black, indigenous, and persons of color (BIPOC), and 19 (9%) unknown/not applicable (UK/NA). Posts by creators where race was UK/NA had significantly more views compared to other groups (P = .046). There was no difference in positive/negative tone (P ​= ​.900) between groups. BIPOC creators had a higher percentage of posts on body image (P = .023), embarrassment (P = .031), and relationships (P = .047), while white creators had more videos addressing pain (P = .012). There were no differences in the DISCERN score between racial groups (P ​= ​.711).ConclusionsIn this analysis of the most popular scoliosis-related posts on TikTok, we identified important thematic content and video analytic differences by race. Only 8% of posts involved healthcare providers, and based on the DISCERN scale, the educational quality of the videos was very poor. This study highlights that racial differences in scoliosis extend to social media, and an opportunity exists for healthcare providers and professional organizations to create more diverse, educational content.Key Concepts(1)TikTok is the most commonly used social media platform within the adolescent population.(2)Content related to scoliosis presented on TikTok is of low to no educational quality.(3)While black, indigenous, and people of color (BIPOC) represent the majority of users, they are underrepresented as content creators.(4)TikTok can be utilized as an educational platform if presented by a diverse patient population and supported by healthcare providers.(5)Continued monitoring of patient presented themes can help inform caregivers what matters most to their patient population and adjust educational content to help support these concerns.Level of evidenceLevel II

Introduction

Historically, access to medical knowledge for pediatric patients diagnosed with scoliosis was through trusted medical establishments such as physicians and/or healthcare systems. The diagnosis can be daunting and stigmatizing to a population most affected in the early teenage years, and education is integral to early identification and intervention. Now, many patients are turning to familiar platforms like social media to import and export information about scoliosis. The use of social media has become an integral part of modern society, with 72% of the public using at least one platform [1]. With new platforms constantly emerging and society becoming more electronic, this trend will likely continue.

TikTok is a video-based platform that launched in 2016 and has 834.3 million users globally. It appeals to individuals across a broad age spectrum, but more specifically, 67% of teens acknowledge using the platform, and 58% admit to daily usage [2]. Despite its widespread appeal, data suggest a skew in TikTok usage toward underrepresented minorities [3]. A recent research study revealed that among the teen demographic actively using social media, Black and Hispanic teens (at 81% and 71%, respectively) are more engaged with TikTok compared to their white counterparts (at 62%). Furthermore, daily usage reports reveal a more pronounced disparity: 26% of Black and Hispanic teens engage in social media, outpacing the 10% reported by white teens [2].

Such a widely used platform has the power to shape the perceptions of its consumers significantly. TikTok provides an unrestricted stage to anyone with an opinion, often bypassing the need for scientific accuracy. A systematic review of social media disinformation found that up to 87% of health-related content was misleading or inaccurate [4]. This prevalence of misinformation presents healthcare providers (HCPs) with not only a unique challenge to undo false narratives, but also an opportunity to create and disseminate evidence-based scientific content that can positively influence TikTok users. Notably, a recent study revealed that an academic neurosurgical presence on social media directly correlated with improved patient ratings [5]. This finding reinforces the idea that patients positively react to an increased HCPs presence on social media.

Given that TikTok's user base is heavily skewed toward adolescents, conditions relevant to this age group, like scoliosis, are of significant interest. Idiopathic scoliosis affects individuals during childhood and adolescence. Many affected individuals contend with unknowns such as treatment options (including surgery), alternative medicine, and long-term outcomes. This uncertainty often pushes them to seek, and occasionally embrace, explanations from unreliable sources. A 2022 study examined misinformation in exercise-related scoliosis videos, concluding that TikTok is not a reliable source for accurate information [6].

This study aims to evaluate scoliosis content on TikTok more broadly by analyzing not only the quality of the information presented, but also the themes and the racial and ethnic representation of the video creators themselves.

Materials and methods

This was an Institutional Review Board-approved study of scoliosis-related content on the social media application TikTok. To avoid previous user-directed content bias by searching directly in TikTok, a subscription-based, real-time AI-powered database was used to provide metrics for TikTok posts (Exolyt© TikTok Analytics Tool, Helsinki, Finland, available in multiple countries, including the U.S.). The variables collected from this database included the number of views, likes, comments, shares, total engagement (combination of all views, likes, comments, and shares for each video), engagement rate (when viewed, how often is there a view, like, comment, or share), share rate, date uploaded, username, title, video link, video duration, number of hashtags, actual hashtags used, and whether the video was promoted. The five most popular hashtags were identified as the following: #scoliosis, #scoliosisawareness, #scoliosischeck, #scoliosissurgery, #scoliosisgang. These 5 hashtags were searched in August of 2022 and included 2 years of posts. Non-English posts, content unrelated to scoliosis, and videos that were no longer available were excluded.

A staged approach was employed for content analysis, involving familiarization, identifying a thematic framework, indexing, summarizing, and interpreting the data. A survey designed using the REDCap electronic data capture tool hosted at the Nationwide Children’s Hospital [7,8] was used to assess the education content, descriptive characteristics of each video, and underlying content themes. Content themes were identified and categorized in an initial screening by the first author. There could be multiple themes identified within a single video. A gender and racially diverse team of five individuals was assigned to watch the videos and complete one or more sections of the survey. Reviewers had substantial experience in scoliosis research, with each having minimum one year of experience as research coordinators in scoliosis studies or qualifications as orthopaedic surgeons specializing in scoliosis. Any discrepancies were discussed and resolved with an orthopaedic surgeon to ensure accuracy.

The first section of the survey included the DISCERN scale (very poor: <26, poor: 27–38, fair: 39–50, good: 51–62, very good: 63–75) to assess the quality and reliability of educational content. The DISCERN tool is designed to evaluate the reliability of written and published consumer health information [9], including social media [[10], [11], [12]]. Two reviewers with doctorate-level training and scoliosis knowledge base were assigned to the DISCERN survey.

Additional qualitative survey questions were used to document the location of the video, the creator's identifying characteristics, and what was discussed in the video (a specific diagnosis, COVID, patient imaging, etc.). Video-specific questions included the overall tone of the content (positive, neutral, negative) and the type of media utilized (both visual and audio). Thematic video undertones were categorized to four areas: treatment, cause and effect, social, and activity. In addition, the race/ethnicity of any individuals present in the video and/or the content creator, if applicable, were phenotypically identified as white, Black, Indigenous, and People of Color (BIPOC), or unknown/not applicable. The diversity of the team members was intentional to reduce bias, but recognize this is a subjective categorization unless the content creator specifically self-identifies as a race/ethnicity. Any videos without unanimous agreement were reviewed again to come to a consensus on race/ethnicity of presenting individual and/or content creator. Data were exported from REDCap and analyzed using SPSS 19.0. Frequency of responses was evaluated, and nonparametric statistics were used to determine differences between groups (α ​< ​0.05).

Content was displayed in various visual formats, including video (92%), animation (6%), and still pictures (17%). Some videos employed multiple formats. Regarding the delivery of information, most individuals included text (68%) and music (66%) in their videos, while a slight minority used voiceover (44%). Almost 80% of content was filmed in a home (59%) or community (19%) setting, with only 17% filmed in a hospital or medical setting.

The specific diagnosis was discussed 73% of the time. When a diagnosis was mentioned, it was often generically defined as scoliosis (70%). In rare cases, individuals discussed specific diagnoses that led to scoliosis, including Chiari malformation, Scheuermann's Kyphosis, and Ehlers-Danlos Syndrome. In some instances, patients displayed imaging of their scoliotic curve (15%) to help illustrate their discussion. In other cases, patients showed their back either before or after a scoliosis procedure (24%).

Half of the videos conveyed a positive tone (n ​= ​112, 51%), with the remaining videos split between neutral (n ​= ​53, 24%) and negative tones (n ​= ​55, 25%). Overall, 91% of posts contained overarching social themes on various perspectives of the challenges of scoliosis in daily activity and social interactions. The four most discussed topics were patient journey (34%), empowering (31%), living with scoliosis (28%), and body image (26%). In addition, 66% of creators mentioned themes related to scoliosis treatments, with surgery (40%) and bracing (12%) being the most discussed. Cause-and-effect-themes focused predominantly on diagnosis (26%) and pain (25%). Activity-related themes including dancing, athletics and school appeared in 24% of content. Differences in content based on race

Most videos were either created by or included individuals who appeared to be white (80%). There was a significantly higher rate of views of content created by BIPOC and UK/NA creators compared to white creators (p ​= ​.046). There were differences in many aspects of the content based on race. 92 percent of BIPOC content directly included the patient, compared to only 63% among content by white creators and 32% in UK/NA videos (P ​< ​.001). Those videos, which included individuals who were White, were more likely to be in a home (66% vs 44% BIPOC, 16% UK/UA, P ​< ​.001) as opposed to in the community or at a medical facility. There was no significant difference in the percentage of videos that showed the patient's body (p-0.852) or imaging (P = .673). There were also differences in the most frequently discussed themes. BIPOC creators focused on body image (48%, P = .023) and embarrassment (24%, P = .031). There was a trend for more patient journey-themed content from White creators (34%, P = .060). The highest incidence of treatment themes was surgery, with no difference across groups (P = .209). Videos by White creators had a higher incidence of discussing pain (27%, P ​= ​.012). When the theme focused on activity, discussion included predominately dancing or athletics across all groups.

There were no differences in DISCERN scores across racial groups (P ​= ​.850).

Discussion

This study is the first to characterize not only the quality of scoliosis information presented on Tiktok but also to identify themes and racial representation in content generation. Patient education and self-advocacy are integral parts of overall patient care success [13]. Without accurate and informative content easily accessible to patients with scoliosis, misinformation, and stigmatism can negatively affect these young patients' overall health and mental well-being.

With low DISCERN scores, the scoliosis-related TikTok content reviewed for this study was not only poor quality; in some instances, it was true misinformation. It cannot be overemphasized that only 2 out of 220 videos reviewed were of fair quality, and none were deemed good or very good. As a high-volume social media platform that is used by 67% of adolescents [1], it is imperative that patients with scoliosis, particularly newly diagnosed patients, are directed to truthful and informative medical information on preferred social media platforms. With less than 10% of the content created by HCPs, facts and descriptive medical information were limited. This study highlights an important opportunity for surgeons and other HCPs to engage with their patients and peers by creating content channels that disseminate high-quality information on diagnosis, treatment, and outcomes. Given the lack of HCP-supported content in this study, there is also an opportunity for professional societies to endorse and share informative posts and to create their own stream of content.

While high-quality, HCP-supported content is needed, it is important to recognize the target consumer. There are well documented racial differences in the prevalence of scoliosis [14]. However, this disparity does not account for the fact that 80% of the scoliosis-related TikTok videos are created by White content creators.

Moreover, research has shown that representation matters significantly in healthcare communication [15]. Seeing people who look like oneself increases engagement and trust in the content being presented. It has been demonstrated that adolescents are more likely to trust and engage with content when they see individuals who share their racial or ethnic background [16]. This principle applies to health education on social media platforms as well.

This need for representation is further emphasized by differences in perceptions of body image across racial groups. A study comparing Spinal Appearance Questionnaire (SAQ) outcomes based on race revealed significant differences in how various racial groups perceive their spinal deformities. BIPOC AIS patients undergoing poster spinal fusion reported higher levels of concern surrounding body image and appearance compared to their White counterparts in both pre- and post-operative evaluation [17]. This underscores the importance of including diverse voices and perspectives in scoliosis-related content.

HCP-supported content creation should actively incorporate patients or HCPs who identify as BIPOC to improve user engagement and information dissemination. By doing so, it not only enhances the relevance and relatability of the content but also addresses the specific concerns and perceptions related to body image that are more prevalent among BIPOC individuals.

Most of the scoliosis-related content reviewed for this study was created by patients and/or families. Therefore, the underlying themes of these posts are important to evaluate. HCPs need to understand that patients or families felt strongly enough about these themes to share content about it on social media. Whether the theme of the content was their personal outcome, their treatment journey, or how scoliosis impacts their daily lives and activities, it is important to acknowledge their feelings and endorse their content when appropriate. Furthermore, HCPs should create honest and informative content that are in alignment with the themes identified as important from patients and families, using similar identifiers (hashtags) on the same platforms. Addressing concerns of body image, embarrassment, and/or posting patient-centric information on surgical techniques, outcomes, and expectations are potential themes for HCPs on social media. Providers who engage in social media should also be cognizant of which platforms are most utilized by parents (ie, adults: YouTube [>80%], Facebook [>70%]) [1] and patients (YouTube [95% of US teens, aged 13–17], TikTok [67%], Instagram [62%], SnapChat [59%]) [2], and should focus and aim their content on each platform to maximize effectiveness.

Outside of the new HCP-supported content of social media platforms, this study demonstrates the importance of patient observation beyond traditional methods of research. The ability to observe patient opinions and behaviors outside of survey studies has helped identify themes important to patients and families. There is an untapped source of patient reported outcomes and opinions within social media content and this study is a call to arms for innovative ways to monitor what matters most to the patients and families we treat.

This study has several limitations. Inclusion of only English language content, both in speaking individuals and text within the video. Similarly, only English hashtag searches were conducted. However, the utilization of the Exolyt software to query the most engaging and most shared content removed any user-directed content bias in video selection and provided objective, quantitative data on views, likes, shares, and total engagement. Another limitation is that race/ethnicity was determined based on visual assessment of the individuals in the video and/or the content creator. However, a diverse study team reviewed all videos in the attempt to best determine race based on the individuals present in the videos and/or the content creator.

In conclusion, this study illustrates the extensive reach of TikTok videos involving scoliosis related content and identifies important content themes. Despite TikTok being an open platform, most posts were made by White creators, even though consumption rates are higher among non-White populations. Additionally, only 8% of the content was created by healthcare providers. This study highlights that racial differences in scoliosis extend to social media, and an opportunity exists for HCPs to create more diverse and educational content, as well as use social media to better understand what is important to patients and their families.
