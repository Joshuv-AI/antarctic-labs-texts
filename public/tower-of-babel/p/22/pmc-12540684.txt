Central composite design optimized fluorescent method using dual doped graphene quantum dots for lacosamide determination in biological samples
Authors: Ahmed Serag, Rami M Alzhrani, Reem M Alnemari, Maram H Abduljabbar, Atiah H Almalki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLacosamide is a third-generation antiepileptic drug used for the treatment of partial-onset seizures. This study investigates the development and validation of a novel analytical method for the determination of lacosamide in human plasma and pharmaceutical formulations using boron and nitrogen co-doped graphene quantum dots (BN-GQDs). The prepared BN-GQDs exhibited excellent fluorescence properties that quenches in the presence of lacosamide, allowing for its sensitive detection. Density functional theory calculations and Stern-Volmer analysis were used to elucidate the mechanism of this fluorescence quenching, which was influenced by various factors optimized using a central composite design. The proposed method was validated according to ICH M10 guidelines displaying excellent linearity in the range of 0.1–5 µg/mL, with a limit of detection of 0.033 µg/mL. Accuracy, precision, selectivity and robustness were also evaluated, and the results met the acceptance criteria of the guidelines. Hence, the method was successfully applied to quantify lacosamide in pharmaceutical formulations and pharmacokinetic studies, demonstrating its capabilities in bioanalytical applications. Introduction

Epilepsy is a neurological disorder characterized by recurrent seizures, affecting millions of individuals worldwide1. Different antiepileptic drugs have been developed to manage this condition, including lacosamide, a recently approved third-generation anti-epileptic agent2. Lacosamide exhibits a unique mechanism of action, selectively enhancing the slow inactivation of voltage-gated sodium channels, leading to stabilization of hyperexcitable neuronal membranes and suppression of seizure activity3. Unlike other antiepileptic drugs, lacosamide has demonstrated efficacy in the treatment of partial-onset seizures, as well as potential applications in other neurological disorders4,5. To ensure the safe and effective use of lacosamide, reliable and sensitive analytical methods are required for its determination in biological matrices and pharmaceutical formulations. The literature revealed that various chromatographic techniques such as HPLC-UV6–8, LC-MS/MS9,10 and GC-MS11 have been employed for the quantification of lacosamide in biological samples and pharmaceutical preparations. Although these methods are sensitive and selective, they often require complex sample preparation, extensive instrumentation which may limit their accessibility in resource-limited settings. For example, a recent study utilized LC-MS/MS for the determination of lacosamide in human plasma, demonstrating excellent selectivity, but necessitating sophisticated instrumentation9. Additionally, the employed mobile phase contained 80% acetonitrile, a toxic and environmentally hazardous solvent. Consequently, from a green chemistry perspective, these approaches may involve the use of hazardous solvents and reagents, raising concerns about their environmental impact. In this context, the development of alternative analytical techniques that are simple, sensitive, and environmentally friendly is highly desirable.

Fluorescence-based analytical methods have gained increasing attention due to their inherent sensitivity, selectivity, and rapid response times12,13. In addition, the environmental advantages of fluorescence detection, such as the minimal use of organic solvents, as well as energy saving considerations, make it a promising approach for the analysis of pharmaceutical compounds14,15. In recent years, fluorescence-based sensors have gained increasing attention in the field of pharmaceutical analysis due to their high sensitivity and selectivity16,17. Among these sensors, graphene quantum dots (GQDs) have emerged as a versatile platform for the development of fluorescent sensors due to their unique optical properties, excellent biocompatibility, and ease of functionalization18,19. Interestingly, doping GQDs with hetero atoms can further enhance their optical properties and sensing capabilities20. For example, nitrogen doped GQDs (N-GQDs) have been reported to exhibit enhanced fluorescence emissions and improved sensitivity towards the detection of various analytes21,22. Furthermore, the incorporation of a second hetero atom such as boron into the graphene lattice can introduce new electronic and optical properties, leading to improved sensing performance23. Hence, co-doped GQDs can serve as an excellent platform for the development of sensitive and selective fluorescent sensors23–25. Consequently, these recent advancements in quantum dot technology have expanded their applications to include not only pharmaceutical analysis but also environmental monitoring, food safety assessment, and biomedical diagnostics26–30. In pharmaceutical analysis specifically, quantum dots have demonstrated increased utility for detecting drugs in complex matrices with high sensitivity and selectivity31–36.

Another area of importance in the development of analytical methods is the optimization of experimental conditions to ensure robust and reliable performance37. Traditional one-factor-at-a-time methods can be time-consuming and may not identify potential interactions between factors. On the other hand, response surface methodology, a statistical optimization tool, can effectively identify the optimal conditions for the analytical method by evaluating the effects of multiple variables simultaneously38. Furthermore, the assessment of the greenness of the analytical methods, in terms of their environmental impact, is crucial to ensure the sustainability of the developed approach39.

Given the potential advantages of fluorescence-based sensing using hetero atom-doped GQDs, the present study aims to develop a sensitive and selective method for the determination of lacosamide in human plasma and pharmaceutical formulations. Boron and nitrogen co-doped GQDs (BN-GQDs) will be employed as the sensing platform, and its spectral characteristics upon interaction with lacosamide will be investigated. The possible interaction mechanism between lacosamide and BN-GQDs will be explored using quantum mechanical calculations and Stern-Volmer analysis. The influencing factors on the fluorescence quenching process will be optimized using central composite design. Additionally, the developed method will be validated according to the ICH M10 guidelines and applied for the analysis of lacosamide in pharmaceutical formulations and pharmacokinetic studies. Finally, evaluation of different greenness, blueness, and whiteness metrics will be carried out to assess the environmentally friendly nature and sustainability of the proposed method in comparison with the reported literature.

Experimental

Chemicals and reagents

Lacosamide (purity 99.3%) was obtained from the Egyptian Drug Authority (EDA), Cairo, Egypt. Boric acid, citric acid, urea and all other reagents were of analytical grade and sourced from Piochem Co., Cairo, Egypt. HPLC grade acetonitrile was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, (St. Louis, MO, USA). Distilled water was used throughout the experiments. Lacosamet tablets, each labeled to contain 100 mg of the active ingredient, were purchased from a local pharmacy in Cairo, Egypt.

Briton Robinson buffer solutions (B-R) in the range of pH 3–10 were prepared by mixing appropriate amounts of 0.04 M acetic acid, 0.04 M phosphoric acid, and 0.04 M boric acid, and adjusting the pH with 0.2 M sodium hydroxide solution.

Instrumentation

Fluorescence measurements were performed using a Jascao 6200 spectrofluorometer equipped with a 150 W Xenon lamp as the excitation source and a quartz cuvette (1 cm path length). The monochromators were set at 10 nm bandwidth for both excitation and emission. The UV/visible absorption spectra were recorded on a Shimadzu 1800 UV/vis spectrophotometer. pH measurements were made using a Jenway 3510 pH meter. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) and zeta potential measurements were performed using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS90 instrument to determine the particle size and charge of the BN-GQDs. Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) analysis was conducted using A JEM-2100 transmission electron microscope operated at 200 kV to obtain the morphology of the BN-GQDs. Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra were recorded on a Nicolet iS5 FTIR spectrometer using the KBr pellet technique. BN-GQDs synthesis was performed using a Teflon-lined stainless-steel hydrothermal synthesis autoclave reactor with the following specifications: capacity 100 mL, stainless steel 304 construction, PTFE (Teflon) liner, maximum operating temperature 220 °C, maximum pressure rating 3 MPa (30 bar), and equipped with corrosion-resistant PTFE vessel for enhanced chemical resistance and sample integrity during hydrothermal synthesis reactions.

Synthesis and characterization of BN-GQDs

BN-GQDs were synthesized using a previously reported hydrothermal method40 with minor modifications (Fig. S1): Briefly, 0.5 g of citric acid, 0.1 g of boric acid and 0.2 g of urea were dissolved in 50 mL of distilled water and the resulting solution was transferred to a Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave. The autoclave was then heated at 180 °C for 4 h. After the reaction, the mixture was allowed to cool to room temperature, the resulting BN-GQDs were dissolved in water then subjected to centrifugation to remove any large particles. A stock solution of 100 µg/mL BN-GQDs was prepared in distilled water and stored at 4 °C for further use.

Comprehensive characterization of the synthesized BN-GQDs was performed using multiple complementary techniques. Morphological and dimensional analysis was conducted via TEM and DLS to determine particle size distribution. Zeta potential measurements were performed to evaluate the surface charge and colloidal stability of the BN-GQDs. Spectroscopic evaluation of optical properties was carried out through UV-visible absorption and fluorescence emission measurements. The surface chemical composition and functional groups of the BN-GQDs were investigated using FT-IR spectroscopy using the KBr pellet technique across the 4000–400 cm-1 spectral range.

Optimization of fluorescence quenching using design of experiments (DOE)

The effect of different variables on the fluorescence quenching of lacosamide by BN-GQDs was optimized using a central composite design: A total of 27 experiments were designed, including 3 center points, considering the following independent variables: pH of the medium (X1, 4–9), buffer volume (X2, 1–3 mL), BN-GQDs concentration (X3, 1-1.5 mL) and incubation time (X4, 2–10 min) as shown in Table S1. Axial points were set at ± 1.4 for the four variables to ensure a proper estimation of the quadratic effects. For each experiment, a fixed volume of 300 µL lacosamide stock solution (100 µg/mL) was added to the BN-GQDs in a 10 mL volumetric flask to achieve a final concentration of 3 µg/mL, and the fluorescence intensity was measured at the optimized excitation and emission wavelengths. The response variable was the quenching efficiency which was calculated as: Quenching efficiency = F0/F, where F0 and F are the fluorescence intensities of BN-GQDs in the absence and presence of lacosamide. The experimental data was analyzed using Design-Expert software (Version 11.1.2.0, 64-bit, Stat-Ease Inc., Minneapolis, MN, USA), available at https://www.statease.com/software/design-expert/) to obtain the regression equation and to identify the optimum conditions for the quenching process. The best conditions identified were pH 8.6, 3 mL of B-R buffer, 1.5 mL BN-GQDs and 2.5 min incubation time, which provided a maximum quenching efficiency. The excitation and emission wavelengths of the BN-GQDs at the optimized conditions were 361 and 439 nm, respectively.

Method validation

The developed fluorescence quenching method was validated for the determination of lacosamide in human plasma as per the ICH M10 guidelines41. For sample preparation, 5 mL of drug-free human plasma was spiked with appropriate volumes of lacosamide stock solution to achieve concentrations in the range of 0.1–5.0 µg/mL, which covers the therapeutic plasma concentration range (Cmax 2–5 µg/mL) following standard oral dosing42. Protein precipitation was performed by adding 5 mL of acetonitrile (1:1 ratio) to the spiked plasma samples, followed by vortex mixing for 2 min and centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The clear supernatant was carefully separated and dried under nitrogen stream at 40 °C. The dried residue was reconstituted in 1 mL of distilled water. For fluorescence analysis, the reconstituted sample (1 mL) was transferred to a 5 mL volumetric flask, followed by addition of 1.5 mL buffer and 0.75 mL BN-GQDs solution, then completed to volume with distilled water to maintain the same ratio as the optimized conditions, and the mixture was analyzed by the developed method. Linearity was evaluated by constructing calibration curves in the range of 0.1–5.0 µg/mL lacosamide in human plasma and the regression equation was derived. The limits of detection and quantification were determined based on the residual standard deviation and the slope of the calibration curve. The accuracy and precision were assessed at four concentration levels (0.1, 0.3, 2.5 and 4.0 µg/mL) by analyzing replicate samples on the same day (intra-day) and on three consecutive days.

Selectivity was evaluated by examining potential interferences from endogenous plasma components and commonly interferent ions as well as pharmaceutical excipients. The plasma matrix effect was assessed by analyzing three different batches of blank plasma spiked at low, mid and high concentrations of lacosamide. Recovery was investigated at the same three levels of lacosamide as well as the CV%. Robustness was evaluated by intentionally varying the pH, BN-GQDs volume and incubation time.

Theoretical investigations and environmental assessments

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed to understand the possible interaction mechanism between lacosamide and BN-GQDs. Lacosamide was optimized using Gaussian09 at the B3LYP/6-31G(d, p) level of theory to determine its ground state geometry. The frontier molecular orbitals and electrostatic potential map of the drug were computed to identify the potential binding sites. Stern Volmer analysis was also carried out to obtain the binding constants from which Gibb’s free energy was calculated to assess the thermodynamics of the interaction.

The environmental impact of the analytical method was evaluated using various state-of-the-art sustainability metrics to provide comprehensive assessment across multiple dimensions. The evaluation included: (1) The Analytical GREEnness metric approach (AGREE), which assesses the environmental, health, and safety aspects of the method based on 12 principles of green analytical chemistry43; (2) The Modified Green Analytical Procedure Index (MoGAPI), which provides both visual pentagram representation and numerical scoring for method greenness assessment44; (3) The Analytical Green Star Area (AGSA), which offers structured scoring aligned with the 12 principles of Green Analytical Chemistry45; (4) The Blue applicability grade index (BAGI), which quantifies the blueness or analytical practicability of the analytical method46; (5) The Click Analytical Chemistry Index (CACI), which evaluates method performance including validation efficiency and user-friendliness47; (6) The Carbon Footprint Reduction Index (CaFRI), which assesses the carbon emissions associated with the analytical procedure48; and (7) The RGB12 metric, which evaluates the overall whiteness or sustainability by integrating analytical performance (Red), environmental impact (Green), and practical aspects (Blue)49. The calculated values across all sustainability metrics provided a holistic environmental profile of the proposed BN-GQDs-based method, focusing on the core analytical detection methodology rather than matrix-dependent sample preparation procedures.

Application to pharmaceutical formulation and Pharmacokinetic studies

The optimized fluorescence quenching method was applied for the quantification of lacosamide in commercial pharmaceutical tablets. Five Lacosamet tablets were accurately weighed, finely powdered and an amount equivalent to 100 mg lacosamide was transferred to a 100 mL volumetric flask. The powder was dissolved in 50 mL of distilled water, sonicated for 15 min and the volume was made up to the mark with water. The solution was filtered using a 0.45 μm membrane filter and suitably diluted with distilled water before analysis using the developed method.

To evaluate the pharmacokinetic parameters of lacosamide, a single-dose pharmacokinetic study was conducted in healthy human volunteers in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Al-Azhar University, Damietta branch (approval no. DFM-IRB00012367-24-012-0017), Egypt and all the participants provided written informed consent. Five healthy male volunteers were fasted overnight and administered a single oral dose of 100 mg lacosamide tablet. Blood samples were collected at 0 (pre-dose), 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, 18, 24, 36 and 48 h after dosing. Lacosamide concentrations in the plasma samples were determined using the validated fluorescence quenching method. The pharmacokinetic parameters including Cmax, Tmax, t1/2, AUC0 − t, AUC0−∞ and MRT were calculated using PK Solver add-in for Microsoft Excel developed by Zhang et al.50 based on non-compartmental analysis approach.

Results and discussion

Characterization, spectral characteristics and interaction of BN-GQDs with lacosamide

Comprehensive characterization of the prepared BN-GQDs was performed using multiple complementary techniques (Fig. 1). DLS analysis revealed an average size of 4.15 ± 0.68 nm with highly uniform shape and size distribution of the synthesized (Fig. 1A). TEM images revealed the spherical morphology of the BN-GQDs with average diameter of 4.11 ± 0.57 nm, consistent with the DLS results (Fig. 1B). The structural characteristics of the BN-GQDs were further elucidated through FTIR (Fig. 1C). The FTIR spectrum exhibited several characteristic peaks including a broad band at approximately 3600 –3200 cm− 1 attributed to O-H and N-H stretching vibrations, peaks at around 2900 –2800 cm− 1 corresponding to C-H stretching, a distinct peak at approximately 1670 cm− 1 indicative of C = O stretching. Additionally, the spectrum showed characteristic peaks at approximately 1380 cm− 1 which can be assigned to B-N stretching vibrations, confirming the successful incorporation of boron and nitrogen atoms into the graphene quantum dot structure. Further peaks observed in the fingerprint region (1200 –500 cm− 1) correspond to various C-O stretching modes and C-O-C vibrations of the oxygen-containing functional groups on the BN-GQDs surface. The colloidal stability of the BN-GQDs was assessed through zeta potential measurements, which yielded a value of -16.7 mV (Fig. 1D). This negative surface charge is attributable to the presence of carboxyl functional groups on the BN-GQDs surface and confirms their colloidal stability in aqueous solution—a critical factor for ensuring the reproducibility and reliability of the analytical method.

The spectral characteristics of the BN-GQDs were investigated using UV-Vis and fluorescence spectroscopy. The BN-GQDs exhibited an absorption peak centered around 340 nm (Fig. S2) corresponding to the n→π* transition of the C = O and other multi-heteroatoms present in the structure. The solution of BN-GQDs appears pale yellow under daylight while showing a bright blue emission under UV light (inset of Fig. 2A). The fluorescence emission spectrum of the BN-GQDs displayed a strong band at 439 nm upon excitation at 361 nm (Fig. 2A). Such optical properties can be attributed to the quantum confinement effect and the presence of surface defects in the BN-GQDs facilitating radiative recombination of photoexcited electron-hole pairs. The quantum yield of the BN-GQDs was determined using quinine sulfate as the reference. To demonstrate the reproducibility of the synthetic methodology, quantum yield measurements were performed across three different synthesis batches of BN-GQDs, showing excellent consistency with quantum yields of 71.4 ± 1.9%, 70.6 ± 2.1%, and 71.3 ± 2.0% (n = 3 for each batch), with an overall average of 71.1 ± 2.0%. This high quantum yield, which is significantly higher than previously reported GQDs51, combined with the comprehensive physicochemical characterization, confirms the high quality of the synthesized BN-GQDs and their suitability for sensitive analytical applications. The fluorescence response was found to show less than 3% variation across different synthesis batches when tested with the same lacosamide concentration (3 µg/mL), confirming the reliability and reproducibility of the synthetic methodology. This high quantum yield, combined with the comprehensive physicochemical characterization, confirms the high quality of the synthesized BN-GQDs and their suitability for sensitive analytical applications. In the presence of lacosamide, the fluorescence intensity of the BN-GQDs decreased significantly in a concentration-dependent manner, demonstrating the fluorescence quenching phenomenon (Fig. 2B), which forms the basis of the proposed analytical method.

Sensing mechanism of lacosamide using BN-GQDs

The interaction between lacosamide and BN-GQDs was investigated based on theoretical and experimental approaches. Quantum chemical calculations were performed using DFT on the optimized structure of lacosamide to predict the possible binding sites on the drug molecule. A basis set of B3LYP/6-31G was used for geometry optimization which is a compromise between computational cost and accuracy. However, the optimization of the BN-GQDs was not feasible due to its complex structure that requires a much higher level of theory as well as greater computational resources. The frontier molecular orbitals, i.e. the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO and the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) were computed to identify the energy levels within the drug molecule. The results revealed that the LUMO energy of lacosamide is -0.214 eV, while the HOMO energy is -6.525 eV (Fig. 3A). This large HOMO-LUMO gap of 6.311 eV indicates the molecule’s stability and low chemical reactivity. The negative LUMO energy (-0.214 eV) indicates the possibility of electron transfer from the excited state of BN-GQDs to the LUMO of lacosamide. Such electron transfer could be the basis for the fluorescence quenching observed in the sensing process during the excitation cycle.

(B) Electrostatic potential map of lacosamide, illustrating the charge distribution across the molecule. Red regions indicate areas of negative potential, while blue regions represent areas of positive potential.

Furthermore, the molecular electrostatic potential map (ESP) elucidated the presence of electronegative regions on the lacosamide molecule, particularly on the carbonyl groups and positive regions on the hydrogen atoms of the N-H bond (Fig. 3B). This charge distribution in lacosamide also facilitates specific interactions with the negatively charged surface of BN-GQDs. The positively charged regions of lacosamide are attracted to the BN-GQD surface, while the negatively charged areas may orient away from it. These ESP-based interactions likely cause electronic perturbations in the quantum dots, resulting in the observed fluorescence quenching which is another possible mechanism for the sensing process.

Validation of the model was assessed by inspecting different parameters such as the adjusted and predicted R2 with values 0.9663 and 0.9548 respectively, indicating high predictive power of the model. Adequate precision of 30.7057, much greater than the desired value of 4, further confirms the high signal to noise ratio and suitability of the model. Graphical visualization of the response surface and 3D surface plots revealed the individual and interactive effects of the factors on the fluorescence quenching response (Fig. 4). A curvature is observed for both the pH and BN-GQDs concentration, indicating the presence of optimum levels for maximizing the quenching (Fig. 4A). On the hand, the buffer volume exhibits a linear effect on the response, suggesting a proportional increase in quenching with increasing volume up to the investigated range. The incubation time was found to be an insignificant factor, which may be due to the rapid adsorption and complexation of lacosamide with BN-GQDs. 3D surface plots clearly demonstrate the strong synergistic interaction between pH and BN-GQDs concentration, where both factors need to be at higher levels to achieve maximum response (Fig. 4B).

Applying the desirability function, the optimum conditions for the sensing of lacosamide using BN-GQDs were determined to be: pH 8.6, buffer volume 3 mL, BN-GQDs concentration 1.5 mL and incubation time of 2.5 min with a desirability value of 1.00 (Fig. S5A). Under these conditions, an F0/F ratio of 3.74 was achieved, indicating robust fluorescence quenching. The desirability function analysis, visualized in (Fig. S5B, top), revealed a broad optimal region, particularly at higher pH values (> 7) and BN-GQD volumes (> 1.1 mL). This trend was corroborated by the F0/F response surface (Fig. S5B, bottom), which exhibited maximum values in similar parameter ranges. The wide optimal region suggests a notable robustness of the sensing system, potentially accommodating minor experimental variations without significant performance loss. The alkaline pH optimum (8.6) may facilitate favorable electrostatic interactions between lacosamide and the BN-GQDs, enhancing sensing efficiency.

Method validation

Validation of the developed BN-GQDs-based sensing method for lacosamide was performed according to the ICH M10 guideline recommendations. The key validation parameters assessed include linearity, accuracy, precision, selectivity, limits of detection and quantification and robustness. Linearity was evaluated by constructing calibration curves using standard solutions of lacosamide in the concentration range of 0.1–5.0 µg/mL. The modelling of F0/F versus lacosamide concentration exhibited good linearity with a correlation coefficient (r2) of 0.9992, indicating the suitability of the method for quantitative determination of lacosamide (Table 2). All the back calculated concentrations were within 15% of the nominal value, demonstrating the acceptable linearity of the method. The sensitivity of the method was determined by estimating the LOD and LOQ, which were found to be 0.0333 µg/mL and 0.1000 µg/mL respectively, indicating a high sensitivity of the developed approach (Table 2). It is worth noting that these limits are significantly lower than the therapeutic range of lacosamide in biological samples, suggesting the potential utility of the presented method for pharmacokinetic studies and therapeutic drug monitoring. The proposed method exhibited higher sensitivity compared to those reported in the literature for lacosamide determination, particularly for HPLC-UV methods6, and was comparable to UPLC-MS/MS approaches9 posing the advantages of simplicity and cost-effectiveness for the developed BN-GQDs-based sensing technique.

The selectivity of the method was assessed by investigating the potential interference from common excipients and other ions as well as blank plasma matrix. The results revealed no significant interference from the studied components, confirming the high selectivity of the BN-GQDs-based sensing method (Fig. 5). Plasma matrix effect was also tested where different batches of blank human plasma were spiked with lacosamide and showed acceptable recoveries between 94.30 and 106.63 with CV% less than 7%, indicating negligible matrix effects (Table S2). The robustness of the method was evaluated by introducing small, deliberate changes in the optimized experimental conditions like pH (± 0.2 units), BN-GQDs concentration (± 0.1 mL) and incubation time (± 0.5 min). The F0/F ratios remained consistent, with % RSD values less than 2%, demonstrating the robust nature of the developed sensing method (Table S3).

Application to pharmaceutical formulation and pharmacokinetic studies

Assessment of the environmental sustainability and method greenness

The environmental sustainability of the developed BN-GQDs-based analytical method was comprehensively evaluated using multiple state-of-the-art assessment tools to provide a holistic view of the method’s environmental credentials.

AGREE assessment

The AGREE assessment was conducted following the standard 12-criteria evaluation framework, with the BN-GQDs-based fluorescence method achieving an overall score of 0.51, indicating good environmental performance (Fig. 7A). The circular assessment revealed a mixed environmental profile with specific strengths and areas requiring improvement. Green segments were observed in criteria 4, 6, and 12, indicating excellent performance in process integration, derivatization avoidance, and operator safety enhancement. Yellow segments appeared in criteria 1, 2, 5, 7, 8, and 10, representing moderate performance in sample treatment requirements, sample amount, automation level, waste management, analytical throughput, and renewable reagent use. Red segments were identified in criteria 3 and 9, highlighting limitations related to the offline measurement nature and energy consumption from hydrothermal synthesis requirements. Orange classification was assigned to criterion 11, reflecting the use of acetonitrile in the bioanalytical sample preparation protocol.

(A) AGREE assessment showing overall score of 0.51 with 12-criteria evaluation framework. (B) MoGAPI assessment displaying pentagram representation with total score of 68%. (C) AGSA evaluation presenting star-shaped visualization with score of 56.94. (D) CaFRI carbon footprint assessment demonstrating moderate performance with score of 65. (E) BAGI practical applicability assessment showing asteroid pictogram with score of 70.0. (F) CACI evaluation displaying circular representation with score of 63. The method demonstrated several environmental advantages including aqueous-based fluorescence detection, elimination of toxic organic solvents in the analytical measurement step, and reduced waste generation compared to conventional chromatographic techniques that require continuous organic mobile phase consumption. However, the assessment identified specific areas impacting the overall score: (1) the energy-intensive hydrothermal synthesis process required for BN-GQDs preparation (8 kWh per synthesis cycle), (2) the use of acetonitrile for protein precipitation in plasma sample preparation (5 mL per analysis), and (3) the offline measurement approach necessitating sample transport. Despite these limitations, the AGREE score of 0.51 represents good environmental performance and substantially exceeds typical values for conventional HPLC methods due to high organic solvent consumption and energy-intensive separations.

MoGAPI assessment

The MoGAPI evaluation was performed using both visual pentagram representation and numerical scoring, with the BN-GQDs method achieving a score of 68%, placing it in the “Acceptable Green” category below the “Excellent Green” threshold of 75% (Fig. 7B). The pentagram analysis revealed significant environmental challenges across multiple assessment areas. Green segments were observed in areas 8, 9, 10, and 13, representing good performance in additional treatment requirements (none required), reagent amounts (< 10 mL), health hazard assessment (slightly toxic classification), and occupational safety (hermetic sealing of analytical process). Yellow/orange segments appeared in areas 2, 3, 4, 11, 14, and 15, indicating moderate performance in sample preservation, transport requirements, storage conditions, safety hazards, waste generation, and waste treatment protocols. Red segments dominated areas 1, 5, 6, 7, 12, and 16, reflecting significant limitations in sample collection (offline), extraction methodology (macro-extraction required), extraction scale, solvent selection (non-green solvents), and energy consumption (> 1.5 kWh per sample).

The MoGAPI score of 68% reflects the substantial environmental challenges associated with the method while still maintaining acceptable green classification. The predominance of red segments highlights critical areas requiring improvement: (1) offline sample collection and macro-extraction requirements significantly impact sample-related scores, (2) non-green solvent classification due to acetonitrile use in protein precipitation substantially reduces environmental credentials, and (3) high energy consumption from hydrothermal BN-GQDs synthesis creates a major limitation in sustainability assessment. The positive aspects include minimal additional treatment requirements, controlled reagent volumes, and appropriate safety protocols. It is important to note that many of the environmental limitations identified (offline sampling, extraction requirements, acetonitrile use) are primarily associated with bioanalytical sample preparation protocols rather than the inherent BN-GQDs fluorescence detection methodology itself. The core analytical technique utilizes aqueous-based fluorescence measurements with minimal reagent consumption and straightforward instrumentation, representing the method’s fundamental environmental advantages.

AGSA assessment

The AGSA evaluation was conducted following the 12 principles of Green Analytical Chemistry, yielding a score of 56.94 with mixed performance across different sustainability criteria (Fig. 7C). The star-shaped representation revealed predominantly orange and red segments across the 12 principles. Green segments were observed in principles 6, 7, 11, and 12, indicating excellent performance in derivatization avoidance, waste management, toxicity reduction, and operator safety. Orange segments appeared in principles 4, 8, and 10, representing moderate performance in process integration, analytical throughput, and renewable reagent use. Red segments dominated principles 1, 2, 3, 5, and 9, highlighting limitations in sample treatment requirements, sample size, automation potential, and energy consumption from hydrothermal synthesis. The assessment demonstrated the method’s strengths in fundamental safety and waste management principles while identifying opportunities for improvement in sample handling, process automation, and energy efficiency that could significantly enhance the overall sustainability profile.

Carbon footprint assessment (CaFRI)

The carbon footprint evaluation using CaFRI demonstrated good performance with a score of 65, indicating moderate environmental impact regarding climate change implications (Fig. 7D). The assessment revealed mixed indicators across multiple categories with green segments in storage, waste management, and emission factors, yellow segments in energy consumption and transportation aspects, and red segments in recycling capabilities. The method’s carbon footprint challenges were attributed to high energy requirements from hydrothermal synthesis (> 1.5 kW), organic solvent consumption (5–10 mL acetonitrile per sample), and lack of recycling protocols for reagents. However, positive aspects included low emission factors (< 0.1 kg CO₂/kWh), minimal waste generation, normal storage conditions, and single-person operation requirements. The assessment highlighted areas for improvement including energy reduction programs, ecofriendly transportation options, and reagent recycling protocols that could enhance the method’s carbon footprint profile.

BAGI assessment

The BAGI assessment for practical applicability achieved a score of 70.0, reflecting good balance between environmental sustainability and practical implementation requirements (Fig. 7E). The asteroid pictogram representation demonstrated solid performance across most applicability criteria, with particular strengths in instrumentation accessibility through standard fluorescence equipment, reagent availability from commercial sources, and cost-effectiveness for routine analytical applications. The assessment revealed that the method offers excellent accessibility for laboratories equipped with conventional fluorescence instrumentation, avoiding the need for sophisticated and expensive LC-MS/MS systems. The good BAGI score indicates practical feasibility for widespread implementation in pharmaceutical quality control and bioanalytical laboratories, though with opportunities for enhancement in automation capabilities and method standardization protocols.

CACI assessment

The CACI evaluation was conducted to assess the practical usability and implementation feasibility of the BN-GQDs method, yielding an overall score of 63, indicating acceptable comprehensive analytical chemistry performance (Fig. 7F). The detailed assessment revealed specific strengths and areas for improvement across multiple practical criteria. The method demonstrated excellent performance in feasibility aspects, complete equipment accessibility in standard laboratories, and cost-effectiveness. Sample-related parameters showed good performance with moderate sample size requirement and rapid sample analysis time (2.5 min), contributing to efficient analytical throughput. The method achieved excellent sensitivity performance and maintained quantitative analytical capabilities across 2 different matrices including pharmaceutical formulations and biological samples. However, significant limitations were identified in automation capabilities, with the method requiring manual operation throughout the analytical procedure. The assessment revealed that conventional sample preparation protocols and lack of portability or miniaturization represented the primary constraints affecting the overall CACI score. The method’s limitation to single analyte determination per analysis also impacted the versatility score. These findings indicate that while the method provides excellent analytical reliability and cost-effectiveness, future development should prioritize automation integration, method miniaturization, and multi-analyte capabilities to enhance practical implementation and achieve higher CACI performance scores for routine analytical applications.

The comprehensive RGB12 whiteness evaluation provided the most holistic sustainability assessment, achieving an overall score of 89.3, demonstrating good integration of environmental, analytical, and practical performance (Fig. 7G). The assessment revealed mixed performance across the three critical dimensions with detailed parameter analysis providing insights into specific strengths and improvement opportunities. The red component (analytical performance) achieved an excellent score of 97.5%, demonstrating outstanding performance in scope of application, detection capabilities, precision, and accuracy metrics. This confirms the method’s superior capabilities for both pharmaceutical and bioanalytical applications with high reliability and sensitivity. The green component (environmental impact) scored 87.5%, reflecting good environmental compatibility through toxicity reduction and waste minimization, though with limitations in energy optimization due to hydrothermal synthesis requirements and organic solvent use in sample preparation. The blue component (practical aspects) reached 82.9%, with good performance in cost efficiency and time efficiency parameters, though moderate operational simplicity indicates opportunities for automation enhancement. The overall RGB12 score of 89.3 demonstrates excellent whiteness performance while identifying specific areas for improvement in energy consumption and operational streamlining to achieve optimal sustainability for routine analytical applications.

Comparative analysis with recent analytical methods for lacosamide determination

The analytical performance and practical advantages of the developed BN-GQDs fluorescence method were evaluated through comprehensive comparison with current state-of-the-art techniques for lacosamide determination. This assessment encompasses recent analytical methods, including spectrophotometric, chromatographic, and mass spectrometric approaches across pharmaceutical and bioanalytical applications (Table S4).

Spectrophotometric methods for lacosamide determination offer cost-effective solutions for pharmaceutical quality control applications, though with varying degrees of analytical performance. The UV spectrophotometric approach by Gobalakrishnan et al.52 demonstrates acceptable sensitivity with LOD of 0.329 µg/mL and LOQ of 0.999 µg/mL across a linear range of 12–40 µg/mL using detection at 230 nm, providing rapid analysis (< 5 min) suitable for routine pharmaceutical applications. However, this method suffers from selectivity limitations and potential matrix interference typical of direct UV measurements. Another kinetic spectrophotometric method was developed by Mannan & Kashif53 employs alkaline KMnO4 oxidation optimized through Box-Behnken design, achieving acceptable sensitivity (LOD: 1.31 µg/mL, LOQ: 3.98 µg/mL) with broader linearity (5–60 µg/mL) and improved correlation coefficient (r2 = 0.9994). Nevertheless, this approach requires extended analysis times (24 min) and involves potentially hazardous permanganate reagents, raising safety and environmental disposal concerns for routine implementation.

High-performance liquid chromatography methods represent significant advancement in lacosamide determination, providing excellent sensitivity and selectivity for pharmaceutical applications. Padhiyar et al.8 developed a stability-indicating HPLC method achieving superior sensitivity (LOD: 0.014 µg/mL, LOQ: 0.044 µg/mL) with efficient analysis time (6 min) across a linear range of 10–50 µg/mL using optimized C18 chromatography with phosphate buffer-acetonitrile mobile phase. This method demonstrates excellent correlation (r2 = 0.998) and stability-indicating capabilities suitable for pharmaceutical quality control, but requires 60% acetonitrile mobile phase composition and is limited to pharmaceutical matrices only. In contrast, the bioanalytical HPLC method by Kestelyn et al.6 was developed for human plasma analysis using gradient ammonium formate buffer and acetonitrile mobile phase, achieving linearity across 0.5–12.5 µg/mL range with good correlation. However, this method shows significant limitations including lower sensitivity (LLOQ: 0.5 µg/mL) and extended analysis time (25 min) for plasma samples. Overall, HPLC methods typically require substantial organic solvent consumption, complex sample preparation procedures, and sophisticated chromatographic equipment, raising operational costs and environmental sustainability concerns for routine lacosamide determination.

Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry techniques represent the current gold standard for lacosamide bioanalysis, demonstrating exceptional sensitivity and selectivity across diverse biological matrices. For example, Chen et al.54 achieved high detection capability with LLOQ of 0.002 µg/mL using UPLC-MS/MS across an extensive linear range (0.002–10 µg/mL) in rat plasma and liver microsomes with rapid analysis (2.0 min) employing C18 column and gradient 0.1% formic acid-acetonitrile mobile phase. Similarly, Bharwad et al.55 reported excellent bioanalytical performance (LLOQ: 0.02 µg/mL) in human plasma covering 0.02–20 µg/mL range with minimal analysis time (2.2 min) using C18 column and gradient formic acid-acetonitrile-methanol mobile phase, while Yadlapalli et al.9 demonstrated LLOQ of 0.076 µg/mL across 0.075–25.461 µg/mL in human plasma using C18 column with 0.1% formic acid-acetonitrile mobile phase. Specialized applications include chiral LC-MS/MS by Jalakam et al.56 achieving LLOQ of 0.1 µg/mL for enantiomeric separation using CHIRALPAK IC-3 column with 100% acetonitrile mobile phase, and GC-MS by Mouskeftara et al.11 with LOD of 0.1 µg/mL for blood samples using HP-5 column with temperature programming. However, these sophisticated techniques require substantial capital investment in expensive instrumentation, highly trained technical personnel, complex method development protocols, and specialized maintenance procedures, significantly limiting accessibility for routine analytical laboratories.

The developed BN-GQDs fluorescence method demonstrates superior practical advantages while maintaining competitive analytical performance compared to existing lacosamide determination techniques (Table S4). The method achieves excellent sensitivity (LOD: 0.03 µg/mL, LOQ: 0.10 µg/mL) that surpasses spectrophotometric approaches by 10–40 fold while requiring the shortest analysis time (2.5 min) among all compared methods. Unlike HPLC techniques demanding 6–25 min and substantial organic solvent consumption, the BN-GQDs approach operates entirely with aqueous solutions, eliminating toxic waste generation and reducing environmental impact. While LC-MS/MS methods achieve superior detection limits, the BN-GQDs technique provides optimal balance between sensitivity, speed, sustainability, and practical implementation for routine applications. The method demonstrates dual-matrix applicability across pharmaceutical formulations and human plasma with excellent linearity and minimal matrix effects, utilizing straightforward sample preparation accessible to standard laboratories. Furthermore, the fluorescence detection mechanism offers inherent advantages including enhanced signal-to-noise ratios, reduced spectral interference, and selectivity through optimized BN-GQDs-lacosamide interactions, positioning this approach as an innovative solution for sustainable lacosamide analysis in quality control and therapeutic monitoring applications.

Whiteness comparison with recent analytical methods

To demonstrate the environmental advantages of the BN-GQDs method, RGB12 whiteness assessment was applied to representative recent analytical methods for lacosamide determination in bioanalytical applications (Fig. S6). Method selection was based on matrix compatibility and analytical scope, with focus placed on techniques validated for human plasma analysis to ensure fair comparison with the bioanalytical methodology developed.

The RGB12 comparative analysis revealed substantial environmental advantages for the BN-GQDs approach, which achieved an overall whiteness score of 89.3. This superior performance was attributed to excellent analytical capabilities (R = 97.5%) through superior sensitivity (LOD 0.033 µg/mL) compared to the HPLC-UV method (LLOQ 0.5 µg/mL), rapid analysis time (2.5 min), and dual-matrix applicability. Good environmental performance (G = 87.5%) was demonstrated through aqueous-based fluorescence detection, limited organic solvent use confined to sample preparation, and minimal waste generation. Strong practical aspects (B = 82.9%) were achieved through accessible fluorescence instrumentation, cost-effective operation, and simplified analytical protocols.

In contrast, the HPLC-UV bioanalytical method by Kestelyn et al.6 achieved a significantly lower RGB12 score of 76.0, despite good analytical performance (R = 90.0%). This method was characterized by gradient mobile phase consumption (ammonium formate buffer and acetonitrile), extended analysis time (25 min), and complex chromatographic separation requirements. The environmental impact was severely compromised (G = 51.3%) due to continuous organic solvent consumption, energy-intensive column temperature control, and complex waste streams. Practical limitations (B = 56.7%) arose from sophisticated HPLC instrumentation requirements and extended analytical protocols.

The LC-MS/MS methods demonstrated varying sustainability profiles reflecting different instrumental configurations and analytical conditions. The method by Yadlapalli et al.9 achieved an RGB12 score of 79.7, with exceptional analytical performance (R = 105.0%) through superior sensitivity (LLOQ 0.076 µg/mL) and rapid analysis (2.2 min), but moderate environmental impact (G = 75.0%) due to high-energy mass spectrometry requirements and organic mobile phase consumption (80% acetonitrile). Similarly, Bharwad et al.55 scored 79.2 with outstanding analytical capabilities (R = 110.0%) achieving the highest sensitivity (LLOQ 0.02 µg/mL) among compared methods, though environmental performance (G = 67.1%) was limited by complex instrumentation and gradient acetonitrile-methanol mobile phases. Both LC-MS/MS approaches showed moderate practical aspects (B = 59.2–60.4%) constrained by expensive instrumentation, specialized maintenance requirements, and high operational costs.

The GC-MS method by Mouskeftara et al.11 demonstrated the lowest sustainability performance with an RGB12 score of 68.3. While adequate analytical performance was achieved (R = 87.5%) with reasonable sensitivity (LOD 0.1 µg/mL), the environmental impact (G = 65.8%) was compromised by high-temperature requirements (120–300 °C), helium carrier gas consumption, and energy-intensive temperature programming. Practical limitations (B = 51.7%) arose from complex sample preparation protocols, extended analysis time, and specialized GC-MS instrumentation demands.

This comprehensive whiteness comparison confirmed that the BN-GQDs method provides optimal sustainability integration (RGB12 = 89.3) while maintaining competitive analytical performance for lacosamide bioanalysis. The superior environmental balance was achieved through elimination of continuous organic mobile phase requirements, reduced energy consumption compared to chromatographic separations, and simplified instrumentation demands. These advantages support the adoption of fluorescence-based methodologies as sustainable alternatives for pharmaceutical analysis in therapeutic drug monitoring applications.

Conclusion, limitations and future perspectives

A simple, selective and sensitive spectrofluorimetric method for the determination of lacosamide has been developed using BN-GQDs as a fluorescent probe. The sensing mechanism of the quenching process was elucidated through DFT calculations, Stern-Volmer analysis and thermodynamic studies. A central composite design optimization strategy effectively identified the critical parameters (pH, concentration of BN-GQDs, and incubation time) affecting the lacosamide quantification. The developed method was comprehensively validated according to the ICH M10 guidelines and successfully applied for the analysis of lacosamide in pharmaceutical formulations. Moreover, the pharmacokinetics of lacosamide in human plasma were also characterized, demonstrating the suitability of the proposed method for bioanalytical applications.

While the described method offers several advantages, some limitations include the selectivity challenges of the fluorescent probe towards structurally similar compounds and the inability to achieve a high degree of automation. Besides, cross-validation studies with gold standard techniques like UPLC-MS/MS are required to further demonstrate the reliability of the proposed method. Future research directions may involve the exploration of molecularly imprinted polymer (MIP) coatings on BN-GQDs to enhance selectivity towards lacosamide. This approach is feasible through surface polymerization using lacosamide as template, creating specific binding sites that could dramatically improve selectivity against structurally similar antiepileptic drugs and reduce matrix interference. However, key challenges include maintaining BN-GQDs fluorescence properties during polymerization and ensuring complete template removal without surface damage. Similarly, enzyme-modified BN-GQDs offer exceptional selectivity through bioconjugation of specific enzymes to the quantum dot surface. While promising for real-time monitoring applications, this approach requires optimization of immobilization conditions to preserve both enzyme activity and quantum dot optical properties, with challenges in achieving stable conjugates and maintaining long-term stability. These advanced modifications could significantly enhance the method’s applicability for complex biological matrices and enable selective determination in the presence of multiple interfering compounds. In conclusion, the present work highlights the potential of BN-GQDs as an eco-friendly and sensitive fluorescent platform for the efficient determination of lacosamide in pharmaceuticals and biological samples, with promising applications in therapeutic drug monitoring and quality control.
