Exacerbation of bullous systemic lupus erythematosus with erythema gyratum repens-like pattern following dupilumab
Authors: Kathyana P Santiago Mangual, Amanda Truong, Joshua Mo, Jennifer Liu, Mazdak A Khaligi, Alana Haussmann, Amir A Razmjou, Vanessa Holland, Kyle Cheng
Published: 2025

Introduction

Bullous systemic lupus erythematosus (BSLE) is a rare autoantibody-driven blistering eruption observed in less <5% of patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).1 We report a unique initial presentation of SLE, characterized by BSLE that worsened with an erythema gyratum repens-like pattern following dupilumab administration.

Case

A 50-year-old previously healthy female presented to the emergency department (ED) with a 2-month history of a worsening erythematous rash on the trunk and extremities. She was previously treated with prednisone over the prior month with some improvement, however, as prednisone was tapered and a concurrent 2-week course of minocycline was completed, the rash progressed to tense palmoplantar bullae and erythematous papules and vesicles on the upper extremities and abdomen (Fig 1, A and B) prompting her ED visit. Two days prior, she was evaluated by an outside dermatologist with biopsies revealing subepidermal bullae with numerous eosinophils and positive direct immunofluorescence of the basement membrane zone suggestive of bullous pemphigoid (BP), however serum ELISA for BP antibodies, BP180/230, were negative and indirect immunofluorescence showed dermal staining on salt split skin. Prednisone was continued and dupilumab was initiated with some improvement, One week later, her rash acutely worsened, prompting hospital re-admission, where a second dose of dupilumab was given. On physical exam, she had painful tense bullae at the dupilumab injection sites, new lesions on the vermillion lips and oral mucosa, and progression of existing lesions on the trunk and extremities (Fig 1, C and D). Two days later, she also developed polycyclic annular plaques on the abdomen, back, and arms (Fig 2, A and B).

Polycyclic annular erythematous plaques with EGR-like pattern on the back (A, left) and abdomen (B, right). EGR, Erythema gyratum repens.

Biopsy of the back showed subepidermal bullae with numerous neutrophils at the base and mild interstitial mucin as well as linear staining of the basement membrane zone on direct immunofluorescence (Fig 3, A and B). Salt-split biopsy revealed “floor” localization of linear C3 and IgG. IIF confirmed dermal pattern IgG basement membrane zone reactivity with split skin substrate, and was negative for p200, laminin 332, and type VII collagen. Additional serum studies were negative for BP180/230 and collagen type VII antibodies and positive for antinuclear antibody immunofluorescence (titer 1:640), dsDNA (titer: 1:1280), lupus anticoagulant, anti-cardiolipin IgM, and anti-beta2 glycoprotein 1 IgM. C-reactive protein was elevated. Creatinine and complement levels (C3, C4) were within normal limits. The patient remained hemodynamically stable and afebrile. Imaging for chest and intra-abdominal malignancies were negative.

Biopsy of the back showing subepidermal bullae with neutrophils and eosinophils (A, 100×), and direct immunofluorescence salt split showing 3+ linear continuous staining for IgG (B) and C3 (C) (200×).

These findings were consistent with BSLE, and the patient met criteria for SLE, although she did not exhibit any systemic features of SLE at that time or historically.2 Dupilumab was discontinued given its previous association with the development of SLE.3 She was started on intravenous methylprednisolone, dapsone, rituximab, hydroxychloroquine, and extensive wound care, with significant improvement. Intravenous immunoglobulin was added 1 week later due to development of new blisters. The following week, her course was complicated by methemoglobinemia secondary to dapsone requiring supplemental oxygen. Colchicine was initiated as an alternative. The patient was discharged 1 week later on colchicine, hydroxychloroquine and a slow taper of prednisone with improvement of her skin disease (Fig 4, A-C).

Significant improvement of BSLE of the palms (A), back (B), and abdomen (C) approximately 3 weeks after initial presentation. BSLE, Bullous systemic lupus erythematosus.

Discussion

BSLE, a rare manifestation of SLE, is characterized by a transient vesiculobullous eruption, typically involving the face (vermillion border), trunk, and extremities, that resolves without scarring. While BSLE as the first clinical manifestation of SLE is exceedingly rare, few cases have been reported.4,5 Erythema gyratum repens is a rare paraneoplastic eruption of centrifugally migrating pink plaques with fine scale trailing the leading edge. However, erythema gyratum repens-like rashes have also been reported in association with infectious, inflammatory, and autoimmune disease, including in BSLE, and are not typically associated with malignancy.6,7

The clinical features, histology, and immunopathology of BSLE are distinct from other forms of cutaneous lupus. BSLE commonly features positive ANA and dsDNA, and frequently, anti-collagen VII.1 Characteristic histopathologic features of BSLE include subepidermal vesiculation, neutrophilic microabscesses in the dermal papillae, dermal edema, and mucin deposition in the reticular dermis.1 It is important to note that collagen VII-negative cases of BSLE occur,8 as in this case, and in our experience, can be negative in one-third of cases.

BSLE should be differentiated from bullous pemphigoid, as both conditions exhibit linear basement membrane zone staining on DIF. However, BSLE typically presents with a predominance of neutrophils, occasionally accompanied by eosinophils. Notably, this case initially demonstrated a biopsy characterized by a predominance of eosinophils, with subsequent biopsies revealing more neutrophilic features consistent with BSLE. Therefore, as in our case, further testing, including salt-split skin testing and/or more extensive basement membrane zone antibody testing, should be considered, especially in cases where the clinical presentation is atypical.

BSLE treatment typically involves dapsone, which targets neutrophil-mediated inflammation.1 However, hematologic complications, such as methemoglobinemia, can limit its use, as observed in this patient.1 For severe refractory cases or those with concomitant systemic SLE symptoms, systemic corticosteroids, immunosuppressive agents, or biologics such as rituximab may be necessary.1 This patient ultimately required a multimodal treatment approach, with gradual and sustained improvement of symptoms.

Minocycline and dupilumab are considered the most likely drug culprits for this patient’s worsening presentation. Although there is a known association with drug induced lupus, minocycline has not previously been reported to exacerbate BSLE, specifically, and she had a period of improving skin symptoms after stopping minocycline, making dupilimub the more likely culprit. Dupilumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting IL-4 and IL-13 receptors, is indicated for patients with moderate to severe atopic dermatitis, prurigo nodularis, and, most recently, bullous pemphigoid.9,10 However, it has been postulated that inhibition of T-helper (Th)2-type immune responses by dupilumab may accelerate the pathogenesis of Th1-related inflammatory disorders such as SLE.3

In conclusion, this case emphasizes the complexities of diagnosing BSLE, particularly in patients with no prior history of SLE. The overlap in clinical and histological features between BSLE and other bullous dermatoses presents a diagnostic challenge. However, the combination of biopsy with immunofluorescence and serum studies evaluating for autoimmunity, in conjunction with the clinical presentation, can help guide toward the correct diagnosis. While BSLE as a first manifestation of SLE is rare, this case underscores the importance of considering this autoimmune etiology in patients with bullous eruptions.
