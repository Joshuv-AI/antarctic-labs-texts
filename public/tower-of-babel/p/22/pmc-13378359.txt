Phospholipid flippases ATP8A1 and ATP8A2 regulate GABAA receptor trafficking through distinct mechanisms in hippocampal neurons
Authors: Muneyuki Kawase, Shu Tokunaga, Yuta Umemura, Hossam H Shawki, Jumpei Omi, Ayana Hoshiai, Tomoha Ohshima, Tsuzumi Nakajima, Takao Kohno, Takashi Sakurai, Junken Aoki, Hisashi Oishi, Mitsuharu Hattori
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryPhospholipid asymmetry in cellular membranes is maintained by flippases. ATP8A1 and ATP8A2 are the principal flippases of phosphatidylserine (PS) in the mammalian nervous system; however, their specific physiological roles and potential functional redundancy remain incompletely characterized. Here, we show that ATP8A1/ATP8A2 double-knockout mice displayed more severe growth retardation than ATP8A2 knockout mice, indicating that ATP8A1 and ATP8A2 play redundant roles. In hippocampal neurons, ATP8A2 was highly enriched at inhibitory post-synapses. Surface biotinylation assays revealed increased surface GABAA receptor levels of hippocampal neurons deficient for ATP8A1 or ATP8A2. Mechanistically, our data support a model in which ATP8A2, but not ATP8A1, regulates local membrane lipid asymmetry, thereby facilitating dynamin2 recruitment and promoting GABAA receptor endocytosis. Collectively, ATP8A1 and ATP8A2 share overlapping roles in postnatal growth, while they contribute to neural function by modulating inhibitory synaptic transmission through distinct mechanisms.Keywords: Phospholipid flippase, inhibitory synapse, GABAA receptor, phosphatidylserine, dynamin

Introduction

Virtually all biological membranes exhibit lipid asymmetry, wherein lipid components are unequally distributed between the extracellular or luminal leaflet and the cytoplasmic leaflet of the bilayer. Among these, phospholipid asymmetry has been recognized for over five decades1 and remains a subject of extensive investigation. It is now well established that this asymmetry is maintained, at least in part, by flippases that actively translocate phospholipids from the extracellular or luminal leaflet to the cytoplasmic leaflet.2,3,4,5,6 Among the asymmetrically distributed phospholipids, phosphatidylserine (PS) plays particularly critical roles, primarily in two biological contexts. First, its externalization to the outer leaflet of the plasma membrane serves as a signal to neighboring cells, most notably functioning as an “eat-me” signal.7,8 Second, PS in the cytoplasmic leaflet acts as an anchoring lipid for the various membrane-associated proteins in the cytoplasm.9,10,11,12 Both of these functions are essential in the nervous system, but how the localization and concentration of PS is regulated in the neurons remains incompletely understood.

Among the P4-ATPase family of flippases in mammals, ATP8A1 and ATP8A2 are reported to be two major subtypes in the adult mouse brain.13 Mutations in ATP8A2 in humans are causative of cerebellar ataxia, mental retardation, and disequilibrium syndrome (CAMRQ).14,15,16,17,18 ATP8A1 knockout (ATP8A1 KO) mice exhibit abnormal hippocampal electrophysiological activity and impaired memory formation,19,20,21 whereas ATP8A2 deficiency in mice leads to structural and functional abnormalities in the femoral nerve, spinal cord, and retina.22,23 Although a brief mention has been made that mice lacking both ATP8A1 and ATP8A2 (ATP8A1/ATP8A2 DKO) die within several hours after birth,22 detailed characterization of the ATP8A1/ATP8A2 DKO mice is not reported.

In most cell types examined to date, ATP8A1 protein localizes primarily to intracellular compartments such as the Golgi apparatus and endosomes,10,24,25,26 and it is predominantly found in synaptic vesicles in hippocampal neurons.20,21 ATP8A2 protein localizes in the inner segments of photoreceptors and in PC12 cells,23 and in the plasma membrane of overexpressing lymphoma cells.27 Direct evidence supporting the localization of endogenous ATP8A1 and ATP8A2 to the plasma membrane remains limited, and it is still unclear which flippase(s) are primarily responsible for maintaining PS asymmetry at the neuronal plasma membrane.

In this study, we address four key questions: (1) Do ATP8A1 and ATP8A2 contribute to brain development? (2) To what extent do they serve redundant or distinct functions? (3) What are their subcellular localizations in neurons? (4) How does the loss of these flippases affect synaptic protein expression and function? The findings presented herein enhance our understanding of the physiological roles of ATP8A1 and ATP8A2 in the mammalian central nervous system and provide novel insights into the functional significance of phospholipid asymmetry in neuronal cells.

Results

ATP8A1/ATP8A2 DKO mice exhibit slower growth and more severe systemic symptoms compared to ATP8A2 KO mice

To elucidate the respective roles of ATP8A1 and ATP8A2 in brain structure and function, we generated ATP8A1 and ATP8A2 KO mice using the CRISPR/Cas9 system (Figures 1A and 1B). Genome sequencing confirmed that the introduced mutations resulted in frameshifts predicted to abolish protein expression in both lines. We subsequently crossed these mice to produce ATP8A1/ATP8A2 DKO mice. Western blot analysis of brain lysates from ATP8A1 KO mice confirmed the absence of ATP8A1 protein (Figure 1C). We generated two monoclonal antibodies against ATP8A2, each recognizing distinct epitopes, and employed them in combination; however, they failed to detect endogenous ATP8A2 in brain lysates (Figure 1D, upper panel, lanes 1 and 2). We thus performed immunoprecipitation using an anti-TMEM30a antibody prior to western blotting. TMEM30a is an essential β-subunit that forms a 1:1 complex with most P4-ATPase phospholipid flippases.28,29,30 The anti-TMEM30a antibody successfully precipitated ATP8A2 from wild-type (WT) brains (Figure 1D, upper panel, lane 3), but no ATP8A2 protein was detected in the immunoprecipitate from ATP8A2 KO mice (Figure 1D, upper panel, lane 4). These results confirmed that our ATP8A2 KO mice express no functional ATP8A2 protein.

We noted that the amount of TMEM30a protein in brain lysates differed between WT and ATP8A2 KO mice (Figure 1D, lower panel, lanes 1 and 2). A previous study reported a reduction in TMEM30a protein in the retina of ATP8A2 KO mice.23 TMEM30a undergoes glycosylation in the Golgi apparatus following complex formation with its partner flippase.31,32 In contrast, TMEM30a that fails to associate with a flippase remains trapped in the endoplasmic reticulum and is ultimately degraded.31,33 Therefore, the total amount of mature TMEM30a serves as a reliable indicator of the total amount of functional P4-ATPase family proteins. We thus compared the levels and glycosylation states of the TMEM30a protein in various brain regions among WT, ATP8A1 KO, ATP8A2 KO, and ATP8A1/ATP8A2 DKO mice at postnatal day 0 (P0). In flippase-deficient mice, the amount of glycosylated TMEM30a was reduced compared to WT mice (Figure 1E), though the extent of reduction and glycosylation patterns varied depending on the genotype and brain region. In the cerebral cortex, ATP8A1 deficiency (Figure 1E, upper panel, lane 2) had a greater impact on glycosylated TMEM30a levels than ATP8A2 deficiency (Figure 1E, upper panel, lane 3). In ATP8A1/ATP8A2 DKO mice, glycosylated TMEM30a in the cortex was markedly reduced (Figure 1E, upper panel, lane 4), suggesting that ATP8A1 and ATP8A2 together constitute the majority of flippase activity in this region. A similar pattern was observed in the hippocampus (Figure 1E, upper panel, lanes 5–8). In the cerebellum, ATP8A1 deficiency caused a substantial reduction in glycosylated TMEM30a (Figure 1E, upper panel, lane 10), whereas ATP8A2 deficiency had little effect (Figure 1E, upper panel, lane 11). Quantitative analysis of glycosylated TMEM30a in the cerebral cortex and hippocampus (Figures 1F–1H) indicated that ATP8A1 and ATP8A2 together account for more than 70% of the flippase complexes in these regions. We also investigated the relative mRNA levels of flippases in the brain. From a public single-cell RNA-seq database (CZ CELLxGENE Discover - Cellular Visualization Tool: https://cellxgene.cziscience.com/gene-expression), we confirmed that ATP8A1, ATP8A2, ATP11A, and ATP11C are expressed in the mouse brain. We then performed qRT-PCR analyses using cDNA synthesized from total RNA extracted from the E18.5 mouse brain. The results indicated that at mRNA level, ATP8A1 is the major flippase and together with ATP8A2 accounts for approximately 70–80% of the total (Figure S1; Table S1). These data were generally consistent with previously published proteomics datasets.13 Taken together, ATP8A1 and ATP8A2 are the major flippases in neurons of the mouse brain, particularly in the cerebral cortex and hippocampus.

Our ATP8A1 KO mice exhibited normal growth, showed no overt behavioral abnormalities, and were fertile in both sexes, consistent with the previous report.19 In contrast, ATP8A2 KO mice displayed impaired growth, abnormal gait, and spontaneous death around one month of age, findings that align with previous studies.22,23 Although it was previously reported that ATP8A1/ATP8A2 DKO mice are embryonically lethal,22 our ATP8A1/ATP8A2 DKO mice were born alive and survived for more than two weeks. Notably, while WT, ATP8A1 KO, and ATP8A2 KO mice grew similarly up to P14, DKO mice exhibited clear growth retardation soon after birth (Figure 1I). In addition, both ATP8A2 KO and ATP8A1/ATP8A2 DKO mice progressively developed abnormal hindlimb reflexes (hindlimb clasping) indicative of motor dysfunction, beginning around P14. By P21, nearly all ATP8A2 KO and ATP8A1/ATP8A2 DKO mice exhibited hindlimb clasping and abnormal gait (Figure 1J), with ATP8A1/ATP8A2 DKO mice displaying more severe symptoms than ATP8A2 KO mice. These observations strongly suggest that ATP8A1 and ATP8A2 possess partially redundant functions in vivo.

There are no major abnormalities in the brain structure of ATP8A1/ATP8A2 DKO mice

In patients with ATP8A2 dysfunction, various structural brain abnormalities are reported, including cerebellar atrophy and ventricular enlargement.16,18 However, in mice lacking ATP8A2, no major structural malformations of the brain have been observed.22 Given that ATP8A1 and ATP8A2 are the two most highly expressed flippases in the mouse brain13 and can functionally compensate for each other during postnatal development (Figure 1I), we presumed that ATP8A1 might compensate for ATP8A2 during mouse brain development. We thus analyzed the brain structure of ATP8A1/ATP8A2 DKO mice. Contrary to our expectations, no abnormalities were observed in the cerebellar laminar structure at P0 (Figures 2A–2D) or at P21 (Figures 2E–2H) across all genotypes. Similarly, the hippocampal structure appeared grossly normal at both P0 (Figures 2I and 2J) and P21 (Figures 2K and 2L). These results indicated that the observed neurological dysfunctions in ATP8A2 KO and ATP8A1/ATP8A2 DKO mice are not due to abnormal neuronal development, but rather to impaired neuronal network function.

No change in the phospholipid profile in the hippocampus of ATP8A1/ATP8A2 DKO mice

A pioneering work by Pomorski and colleagues in yeast showed that the loss of aminophospholipid flippase caused a significant decrease in the levels of PS and PE while the level of phosphatidylcholine (PC) increased.34 The same tendency was observed in the retina of ATP8A2 KO mice.23 Although the mechanism of these changes remains unsolved, we reasoned that, if such global changes in phospholipids were also observed in the brain of ATP8A1/ATP8A2 DKO mice, this would be highly significant and would require further investigation and discussion beyond PS exposure alone. Therefore, to evaluate the effect of flippase deficiency on phospholipid composition in the hippocampus, we performed comparative lipidomic analyses between ATP8A1/ATP8A2 double heterozygous (DHEs, e.g., ATP8A1+/−; ATP8A2+/−) and ATP8A1/ATP8A2 DKO mice. Neonatal mice at P0 were used for this analysis, as ATP8A1/ATP8A2 DKO mice at this stage appeared phenotypically normal, thereby minimizing the confounding effects of delayed growth. The analysis revealed that the relative abundance of phospholipid polar headgroups was nearly identical between these groups (Figure 2M). Furthermore, the fatty acid compositions of both PS and PE showed no significant differences between them (Figures 2N and 2O, respectively). These results suggest that the loss of ATP8A1 and ATP8A2 has minimal impact on the overall phospholipid species in the neonatal hippocampus, and the phenotypes observed in the ATP8A1/ATP8A2 DKO mice are likely due to the change in the asymmetric distribution of PS and/or PE.

ATP8A2, but not ATP8A1, localizes to inhibitory post-synapses in cultured hippocampal neurons

We sought to investigate how ATP8A1 and ATP8A2 contribute to the functional expression of synaptic proteins in hippocampal neurons for three reasons. First, although ATP8A1 has been shown to influence hippocampal transmission and memory formation,19,20 the potential involvement of ATP8A2 in these processes has not been examined. Second, ATP8A1 and ATP8A2 regulate intracellular trafficking and the functional expression of membrane proteins in cerebral cortical neurons,10 but it remains unclear whether the same applies to hippocampal neurons. Third, although the importance of local PS externalization in hippocampal neuronal synapses has been demonstrated,35 the specific flippase involved in the PS internalization in the hippocampus has not yet been identified.

In the primary-cultured hippocampal neurons from WT mice, ATP8A1 exhibited strong co-localization with the excitatory presynaptic marker VGLUT1 (Figure 3A, 40.5 ± 1.9%, n = 9 neurons), while minimal co-localization with the inhibitory presynaptic marker VGAT (Figure 3B, 6.4 ± 1.8%, n = 9 neurons). ATP8A1 showed little co-localization with PSD95 (Figure 3C, 6.4 ± 2.6%, n = 9 neurons), an excitatory postsynaptic scaffolding protein, or with inhibitory post-synaptic proteins GABAA receptor β2/3 subunits (Figure 3D, 5.1 ± 1.1%, n = 8 neurons), and gephyrin (Figure 3E, 5.3 ± 1.6%, n = 8 neurons). The specificity of the ATP8A1 signal was confirmed by its complete absence in ATP8A1 KO neurons (Figures 3F–3J). These observations indicate that ATP8A1 is selectively localized to excitatory presynaptic terminals and is present only at very low levels at excitatory postsynaptic sites and inhibitory presynaptic and postsynaptic sites in our cultured hippocampal neurons, consistent with previous reports.20,21

Next, we tried to clarify the localization of ATP8A2, but signals obtained by using our anti-ATP8A2 antibodies did not reflect the localization of endogenous ATP8A2 protein since the obtained signals did not differ between WT and ATP8A2 KO neurons (Figures S2A and S2B, respectively). We thus expressed hemagglutinin (HA)-tagged ATP8A2 in hippocampal neurons and stained them with anti-HA antibody. HA-ATP8A2 co-localized partially with VGLUT1 (Figure 3K, 21.9 ± 5.4%, n = 6 neurons) while minimally with VGAT (Figure 3L, 9.2 ± 3.5%, n = 6 neurons) or PSD95 (Figure 3M, 5.3 ± 1.9%, n = 6 neurons). Notably, HA-ATP8A2 co-localized with the GABAA receptor β2/3 subunits (Figure 3N, 23.0 ± 4.6%, n = 6 neurons) and gephyrin (Figure 3O, 18.0 ± 3.8%, n = 6 neurons). These findings suggest that ATP8A2 localizes to both excitatory presynaptic vesicles and inhibitory post-synapses.

To confirm the localization of endogenous ATP8A2, we examined TMEM30a localization in primary hippocampal neurons derived from flippase KO mice. In WT neurons (i.e., neurons expressing both ATP8A1 and ATP8A2), TMEM30a immunostaining revealed strong somatic signals and numerous puncta along the neurites (Figure 3P). In ATP8A1 KO neurons (i.e., neurons expressing ATP8A2), the overall TMEM30a signal intensity was reduced; however, punctate signals remained detectable in both the soma and neurites (Figures 3Q and S2C). In ATP8A2 KO neurons (i.e., neurons expressing ATP8A1), somatic TMEM30a signal intensity was comparable to that in WT neurons (Figure 3R). In ATP8A1/ATP8A2 DKO neurons, TMEM30a signals were markedly diminished, with only a few puncta detected along the neurites (Figures 3S and S2D). Punctate TMEM30a signals were retained in neurites of ATP8A1 KO neurons but greatly reduced in ATP8A1/ATP8A2 DKO neurons, indicating that a subset of endogenous ATP8A2 forms a complex with TMEM30a and targets to neurites. Then, TMEM30a localization was compared between ATP8A1 KO and ATP8A1/ATP8A2 DKO neurons (Figures 3T–3Y). TMEM30a partially co-localized with VGLUT1 in both ATP8A1 KO (Figure 3T) and ATP8A1/ATP8A2 DKO neurons (Figure 3W), with no significant difference in co-localization efficiency (Figure 3Z), suggesting that these signals arise from other flippases present in excitatory presynaptic vesicles. In contrast, co-localization of TMEM30a with GABAA receptor β2/3 subunits was often observed in ATP8A1 KO neurons (Figure 3U) but significantly reduced in ATP8A1/ATP8A2 DKO neurons (Figures 3X and 3Z). Although TMEM30a also co-localized with gephyrin (Figures 3V and 3Y), the extent of co-localization was comparable between genotypes (Figure 3Z). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the ATP8A2-TMEM30a complex is present at inhibitory postsynaptic sites and is preferentially enriched near extrasynaptic GABAA receptors that are not associated with gephyrin.

Surface expression of GABAA receptors is increased in ATP8A1 KO and ATP8A2 KO hippocampal neurons

The distinct subcellular localization patterns of ATP8A1 and ATP8A2 suggest that these flippases fulfill specialized roles at the synapse. One well-established biological function of PS asymmetry is to facilitate the formation of transport vesicles.10,12,36 We thus hypothesized that ATP8A1 and ATP8A2 regulate the vesicular trafficking of synapse-associated proteins through the modulation of PS asymmetry. To test this hypothesis, we examined the effects of flippase deficiency on the cell surface expression of neurotransmitter receptors in primary cultured hippocampal neurons using the surface-biotinylation technique.10

We first compared WT and ATP8A1 KO neurons. The total amount of the GABAA receptor (whole-cell lysate; Figure 4A, top panel, lanes 1 and 2) and its intracellular pool (unbiotinylated fraction; Figure 4A, top panel, lanes 3 and 4) were comparable between them. By contrast, the surface expression of GABAA receptor (biotinylated fraction; Figure 4A, top panel, lanes 5 and 6) was elevated in ATP8A1 KO neurons compared to WT neurons (Figure 4B). No significant differences were observed in either the total or surface expression levels of the AMPA receptor subunit GluR1 between the two groups (Figure 4A, middle panel; Figure 4B). We next compared WT and ATP8A2 KO neurons. In ATP8A2 KO neurons, the total amount (Figure 4C, top panel, lanes 1 and 2) and intracellular pool (Figure 4C, top panel, lanes 3 and 4) of the GABAA receptor were unchanged compared to WT neurons, whereas its surface expression was significantly increased (Figure 4C, top panel, lanes 5 and 6; Figure 4D). Again, no differences were detected in the total or surface expression of GluR1 (Figure 4C, middle panel; Figure 4D). These results suggested that the individual loss of ATP8A1 or ATP8A2 produced similar effects on GABAA receptor localization, revealing a previously unrecognized role for flippases in the regulation of inhibitory neurotransmission.

We finally compared ATP8A1 KO and ATP8A1/ATP8A2 DKO neurons. For the GABAA receptor, the total amount (Figure 4E, top panel, lanes 1 and 2), intracellular pool (Figure 4E, top panel, lanes 3 and 4), and surface expression (Figure 4E, top panel, lanes 5 and 6) were all comparable between the two groups, with no significant difference in surface expression rates (Figure 4F). Interestingly, ATP8A1/ATP8A2 DKO neurons exhibited a modest but detectable increase in GluR1 surface expression compared with ATP8A1 KO neurons (Figure 4E, middle panel, lanes 5 and 6; Figure 4F). These results indicate that a complex process is occurring in ATP8A1/ATP8A2 DKO neurons, which cannot be accounted for by a simple additive effect or redundancy of the individual deficiencies of ATP8A1 and ATP8A2. We confirmed that the contamination of glial cells in our primary-cultured neurons was minimal (Figure S3), and neither the number of excitatory nor inhibitory synapses differed among genotypes (Figure S4).

ATP8A2, but not ATP8A1, facilitates endocytosis of GABAA receptors

The increase in the surface expression of the GABAA receptor may result from either inhibition of endocytosis or enhancement of transport to the plasma membrane. Since dysfunction of flippases has been reported to inhibit the membrane budding,10,36 we hypothesized that the increase in GABAA receptors on the plasma membrane was due to a decrease in endocytosis. We thus examined the contributions of ATP8A1 and ATP8A2 by the endocytosis assay of GABAA receptor.37,38 When WT neurons were fixed immediately after cell-surface labeling, they exhibited only background levels of intracellular signal (Figure 5A). When they were fixed after 90 min incubation, intracellular signals (i.e., endocytosed GABAA receptor) increased (Figure 5C). When ATP8A1 KO neurons were employed, the signals appeared approximately the same as those of WT neurons (Figures 5B and 5D), and quantitative analysis revealed no significant differences between WT and ATP8A1 KO neurons (Figure 5E). We next compared WT and ATP8A2 KO neurons in the same way. Both showed background signals immediately after labeling (Figures 5F and 5G), but endocytic signals were markedly lower in ATP8A2 KO neurons than in WT ones (Figures 5H–5J). Collectively, ATP8A1 and ATP8A2 exert distinct effects on GABAA receptor surface expression, with ATP8A2, but not ATP8A1, promoting its endocytosis.

We next aimed to elucidate the molecular mechanism by which ATP8A2 contributes to GABAA receptor endocytosis. Because ATP8A2, unlike ATP8A1, is abundantly located in the vicinity of inhibitory postsynapses (Figure 3), we hypothesized that the local PS asymmetry maintained by ATP8A2 may contribute to GABAA receptor endocytosis. Numerous studies indicated that GABAA receptor endocytosis is mediated by clathrin-coated pits and requires its interaction with the AP2 adaptor complex.39,40,41 Dynamin (DNM) GTPase are essential for vesicle scission during this process, and disruption of DNM function leads to increased surface levels of GABAA receptor.39,40,41 Among the three types of DNMs, DNM2 is distributed along dendrites of hippocampal neurons,41 making it the most likely candidate that regulates GABAA receptor endocytosis. However, whether DNM2 binds PS remained unknown, although DNM1 binds PS.42 Therefore, we performed liposome-binding assays using the pleckstrin homology (PH) domains of DNM1, DNM2, and DNM3 (Figure 5K). The PH domain of evectin-2 (Evt2-PH-WT) and its mutant form (Evt2-PH-KE) were used as positive and negative controls, respectively.43 Evt2-PH-WT exhibited robust PS binding, whereas Evt2-PH-KE showed no PS-dependent association (Figure 5K, lanes 12–15), confirming the validity of our assay conditions. Under these conditions, DNM1, DNM2, and DNM3 all displayed substantial PS binding (Figure 5K, lanes 6–11) with affinities comparable to that of Evt2-PH-WT (Figure 5L). These results supported the idea that the disruption of PS asymmetry caused by ATP8A2 deficiency impairs DNM2-mediated GABAA receptor endocytosis.

Finally, we investigated whether the loss of ATP8A2 affects the co-localization of DNM2 and GABAA receptors. GABAA receptors co-localize with AP2,39 and AP2 and DNM2 are nanoscale neighbors,44 suggesting that GABAA receptors/AP2/DNM2 form a complex before their internalization. The co-localization rates of DNM2-GABAA receptor were approximately the same between WT and ATP8A1 KO neurons (Figures 5M–5O). On the other hand, the co-localization rate in ATP8A2 KO neurons was significantly lower than that in WT neurons (Figures 5P–5R). Together, these findings support a model that ATP8A2, but not ATP8A1, promotes DNM2 recruitment, likely through the maintenance of local PS asymmetry, thereby facilitating GABAA receptor endocytosis (Figure 6).

Proposed model for ATP8A2-mediated regulation of GABAA receptor endocytosis

(1) ATP8A2 localizes near GABAA receptors at inhibitory postsynapses, whereas ATP8A1 is largely absent. ATP8A2 maintains PS asymmetry by translocating PS to the cytoplasmic leaflet, potentially creating local PS enrichment around receptor-containing membrane domains.

(2) Local PS enrichment may contribute to dynamin recruitment to these sites, potentially facilitating membrane invagination and vesicle scission.

(3) The resulting endocytic vesicles are efficiently internalized, reducing GABAA receptor levels at the cell surface.

Discussion

Our group has been investigating the role of the secreted protein Reelin in neural development.45,46,47 In humans, deficiency of the Reelin receptor VLDLR causes CAMRQ,48 a condition that closely resembles the phenotype resulting from ATP8A2 dysfunction.14,15,16,17,18 This similarity prompted us to examine the potential roles of neuronal flippases in brain development. We initially hypothesized that simultaneous deletion of ATP8A1 and ATP8A2 in mice would result in cerebellar malformation, thereby recapitulating the phenotypes observed in humans with ATP8A2 or VLDLR deficiency. However, ATP8A1/ATP8A2 DKO mice did not exhibit cerebellar malformations (Figures 2A–2H). Instead, they displayed unexpected phenotypes, which motivated further investigation and ultimately led to the following findings. First, the simultaneous loss of ATP8A1 and ATP8A2 does not induce major abnormalities during embryonic development. Second, ATP8A1 and ATP8A2 exert partially redundant functions in early postnatal development, as ATP8A1/ATP8A2 DKO mice show more severe growth retardation and neurological phenotypes than ATP8A2 KO mice. Third, ATP8A1/ATP8A2 DKO mice exhibit no gross structural abnormalities in the brain, suggesting that the neurological phenotypes arise from functional impairments rather than defects in morphogenesis. Fourth, ATP8A1 is predominantly localized to excitatory presynaptic terminals, whereas ATP8A2 is enriched at inhibitory postsynaptic sites. Fifth, loss of either ATP8A1 or ATP8A2 selectively increases the surface expression of GABAA receptors without significantly affecting AMPA receptors. Sixth, ATP8A2 facilitates GABAA receptor endocytosis, potentially through DNM2. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that proper regulation of phospholipid asymmetry by ATP8A1 and ATP8A2 is essential for the functional maturation of neuronal networks.

To elucidate the role of PS flippases in the central nervous system, we addressed two fundamental questions: which PS flippases are functionally expressed in neurons, and what phenotypic consequences result from their genetic ablation? The P4-ATPase family comprises 14 members, several of which catalyze PS translocation across membranes.28,30,49 In the adult mouse brain, it was reported that ATP8A1 and ATP8A2 account for ∼56% and ∼13%, respectively, of total P4-ATPase.13 Nevertheless, their specific roles in neurons have remained unresolved, largely because glial cells numerically predominate in the adult brain. Moreover, the developmental and region-specific functions of ATP8A1 and ATP8A2 in the brain have not been systematically defined, underscoring the need for direct genetic and functional analyses. We generated ATP8A1 and ATP8A2 KO mice and took advantage of the fact that TMEM30a is prone to degradation when not complexed with a P4-ATPase.23,31,50 This approach enabled us to estimate the relative contributions of ATP8A1 and ATP8A2 within the P4-ATPase family in the cerebral cortex, hippocampus, and cerebellum of neonatal mice (Figure 1). It should be noted that these values represent relative protein abundance rather than enzymatic activity.

A major finding from our study is the subcellular segregation of ATP8A1 and ATP8A2 into distinct synaptic compartments in cultured hippocampal neurons. The preferential localization of ATP8A2 at inhibitory post-synapses was unexpected and provides a mechanistic framework for understanding the selective vulnerability of inhibitory synapses to flippase deficiency.51 This differential localization further suggests that the lipid asymmetry is spatially compartmentalized at the synapse, thereby exerting tight control over distinct aspects of excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmission.

Functionally, we found that the loss of ATP8A1 or ATP8A2 selectively increased the surface expression of GABAA receptors without significantly affecting AMPA receptor trafficking in hippocampal neurons (Figure 4). This suggests that PS asymmetry maintained by ATP8A1 and ATP8A2 restrains the surface accumulation of GABAA receptors under physiological conditions. Notably, the effects on GABAA receptor trafficking were observed even in the absence of major changes in the overall lipid composition of the neonatal hippocampus (Figures 2M–2O), emphasizing that flippase-mediated lipid asymmetry, rather than global lipid abundance (which was observed in previous works on the flippase-deficient cells23,34), is crucial for receptor trafficking. We then found that ATP8A2 is involved in the endocytosis of GABAA receptors (Figure 5J) and that this event is accompanied by the decreased targeting of DNM2 to the vicinity of GABAA receptors (Figure 5R). The surface expression of GABAA receptors is regulated by a constant cycle of insertion and removal, with clathrin-mediated endocytosis serving as the primary pathway for receptor internalization.39 Within this process, DNMs act as the essential mechanical “scissors” that facilitate the transition of membrane proteins from the cell surface into the intracellular space.52,53 Together with the facts that DNM2 directly binds to PS (Figures 5K and 5L) and that ATP8A2 shows greater co-localization with extrasynaptic GABAA receptors than with synaptic ones (Figure 3Z), we speculate that the endocytosis of extrasynaptic GABAA receptors might be facilitated by the PS-bound DNM2 (Figure 6).

Interestingly, unlike the case of ATP8A2, the ATP8A1 deficiency had no effect on the endocytosis of GABAA receptors (Figure 5E). One possible scenario is that ATP8A1 might facilitate the membrane insertion of GABAA receptors into the plasma membrane. This model is supported by the observation that ATP8A1 strengthens glutamatergic synaptic transmission20,21 which is known to regulate the phosphorylation-dependent membrane insertion of GABAA receptors.54 It is also formally possible that ATP8A1 somehow affects the degradation and/or stabilization of GABAA receptors, although, to the best of our knowledge, such regulation is not reported for GABAA receptors. In sum, the exact mechanism by which ATP8A1 regulates the surface expression of GABAA receptors remains to be elucidated in further research.

Our findings provide important clarification of the roles of ATP8A1 and ATP8A2 during postnatal growth and brain development. While a previous report suggested early lethality of ATP8A1/ATP8A2 DKO mice,22 our ATP8A1/ATP8A2 DKO mice survived beyond birth and displayed progressive motor deficits. This discrepancy may reflect differences in genetic background or knockout strategies, but critically, it allowed us to uncover the physiological consequences of combined ATP8A1 and ATP8A2 deficiency. The more severe phenotypes observed in ATP8A1/ATP8A2 DKO mice compared with ATP8A2 KO mice highlight a significant degree of functional redundancy between ATP8A1 and ATP8A2 in vivo. Importantly, the pronounced reduction of glycosylated TMEM30a in the brain of ATP8A1/ATP8A2 DKO mice indicates that ATP8A1 and ATP8A2 together constitute the predominant neuronal P4-ATPase activity, particularly in the cortex and hippocampus. These findings underscore the indispensable and cooperative roles of ATP8A1 and ATP8A2 in maintaining neuronal function and strongly suggest that the disruption of their combined activity has far-reaching consequences for postnatal brain maturation. Electrophysiological analysis in postnatal ATP8A2 KO and ATP8A1/ATP8A2 DKO mice would be desirable, but is challenging because their severe neurological phenotypes make it difficult to identify the most relevant brain regions. Additionally, since ATP8A1 and ATP8A2 are also expressed in glial cells, whole-brain analyses are not straightforward. Addressing these issues will require cell type-specific and temporally controlled models, which are beyond the scope of this study.

A key question is where PS is exposed within neurons and to what extent ATP8A1 and ATP8A2 contribute to this process. To address this, we prepared primary hippocampal neurons from WT, ATP8A1 KO, ATP8A2 KO, and ATP8A1/ATP8A2 DKO mice, and assessed PS exposure using Annexin V, which specifically binds PS (Figure S5). PS exposure was detected only in neurons from ATP8A1/ATP8A2 DKO mice (Figure S5D), whereas little to no exposure was observed in neurons from the other genotypes (Figures S5A–S5C). These results indicate that loss of either ATP8A1 or ATP8A2 alone is largely insufficient to induce PS exposure at the cell surface, or that any such exposure is transient. Importantly, detectable PS on the outer leaflet is not necessarily required for localized enrichment of PS on the inner leaflet. Further studies will be needed to clarify the distribution of PS within the inner leaflet of neuronal membranes.

In conclusion, our results establish ATP8A1 and ATP8A2 as critical regulators of synaptic receptor trafficking via the maintenance of membrane lipid asymmetry. By elucidating their distinct subcellular localizations and functional specializations, this study advances our understanding of how neuronal membrane asymmetry shapes synaptic architecture and function. Future studies examining the dynamic regulation of flippase activity during synaptic plasticity and disease states will further illuminate the importance of membrane lipid asymmetry in the nervous system.

Limitations of the study

The surface expression and dynamics of the GABAA receptor were investigated in the primary-cultured neurons. Its importance in vivo remains to be investigated. The underlying basis for the severe symptoms and premature death of ATP8A1/ATP8A2 DKO mice has not been examined and remains unclear.

While our data support the model in which local PS asymmetry maintained by ATP8A2 facilitates DNM2 recruitment, it should be noted that the local PS enrichment at GABAA receptor-containing membranes was not directly visualized in this study.

Experimental model and study participant details

Mice

ICR, C57BL/6N, and BALB/c mice were obtained from Japan SLC. ATP8A1 Knockout (KO) mice and ATP8A2 Knockout (KO) mice were generated in the C57BL/6N background using CRISPR/Cas9 technology (see method details) and were subsequently backcrossed to ICR mice for at least 10 generations. For the ATP8A1 KO line, exons 7 and 13 of the ATP8A1 gene were deleted. The ATP8A2 KO line carried a 4-bp deletion plus a 1-bp mutation (Figure 1B), which was predicted to result in a non-functional ATP8A2 protein.

The animals were housed under a 12/12 h light/dark cycle with lights on between 6 A.M. and 6 P.M. with standard diets and water ad libitum. Both male and female mice were used in this study, and no significant sex-related differences were observed. All experimental protocols used in this study were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Nagoya City University (approval number: 23-001H03) and were performed in accordance with the Institutional Guidelines on Animal Experimentation of Nagoya City University.

For the growth curve analysis, pups were genotyped between P0 and P3. At P3, each litter was culled to four pups per cage and housed with its biological mother, who was heterozygous for the ATP8A1 or ATP8A2 mutation.

Method details

Establishing KO mice

We introduced gene mutations using CRISPR/Cas9 technology. Single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) were designed for each side and off-target analyses were performed using the CRISPick online software from the Broad Institute. The sgRNAs were transcribed in vitro using the Precision gRNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and tested according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The zygotes were collected from super-ovulated C57BL/6N female mice that had been mated with C57BL/6N males. sgRNAs/Cas9 ribonucleoprotein complexes were introduced into zygotes via a NEPA21 super electroporator (Nepa Gene). The electroporated zygotes were then cultured in KSOM/AA medium57 to reach the two-cell stage and then transferred into the oviducts of 0.5-day pseudopregnant ICR females. The obtained F0 generation by natural delivery or cesarean section was genotyped by PCR, and mutant mice were crossed with C57BL/6N mice to generate the F1 mice. For ATP8A1 KO mice, the exons 7 and 13 of ATP8A1 gene were deleted. For ATP8A2 KO mice, we had intended to delete exons 9 and 14, but none of the obtained mice had such mutation. The only mouse that was supposed not to express functional ATP8A2 protein was 4-bp deletion plus 1-bp mutation (Figure 1B). The sequences of each primer used for genome editing are as shown in Table S2. ATP8A1 KO and the ATP8A2 KO mice were backcrossed to ICR mice for at least 10 generations.

Genotyping

The tip of the tail or a piece of an ear was excised, placed in a solution containing 25 mM NaOH and 1 mM EDTA, and incubated at 98°C for 1 h. The solution was then neutralized with the equal amount of 40 mM Tris-HCl pH 5.5. An aliquot was used as a PCR template. Genotyping for ATP8A1 was performed using three primers (Table S2). The bands of 179 bp and 220 bp represents WT and ATP8A1 KO alleles, respectively. PCR for ATP8A2 KO mice was performed using three primers (Table S2). The bands of 124 bp and 141 bp represents WT and ATP8A2 KO alleles, respectively.

Production of monoclonal antibody against mouse ATP8A2

A His-tagged recombinant protein corresponding to residues 397–642 of mouse ATP8A2 was expressed in Escherichia coli and purified using Ni Sepharose 6 Fast Flow (Cytiva). Female BALB/c mice (6–8 weeks old) were immunized intraperitoneally with 100 μg of the purified peptide. The primary immunization was performed with Freund’s complete adjuvant (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical), followed by three booster injections at two-week intervals with Freund’s incomplete adjuvant (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical). For hybridoma generation, splenocytes were isolated from immunized mice and fused with mouse myeloma cells at a 5:1 ratio using polyethylene glycol. Fused cells were cultured in HAT medium for hybridoma selection. Supernatants were screened by ELISA and western blotting, and monoclonal antibodies were purified using Protein G Sepharose 4 Fast Flow (Cytiva). Epitope mapping was performed by ELISA with overlapping peptide fragments, resulting in the identification of two monoclonal antibodies recognizing distinct epitopes (5G2: epitope within residues 405–445; 8H2: epitope within residues 445–484). An equal mixture (1:1) of these antibodies was used for experiments.

Construction of expression plasmids

To generate the expression vector for HA-tagged mouse ATP8A2, the full-length coding sequence of mouse ATP8A2 was amplified by PCR from a cDNA library derived from mouse brain RNA. The amplified fragment was subcloned into the pCAGGS vector, and a hemagglutinin (HA) tag was then introduced at the site of N-terminus of ATP8A2 using a linker ligation strategy. The expression vector for Myc-tagged mouse TMEM30a was generated by subcloning the TMEM30a coding sequence from a pcDNA plasmid (a gift from Dr. T. Taguchi) into the pCAGGS vector. For bacterial expression, plasmids encoding pleckstrin homology (PH) domains were generated by cloning into the pET21 vector (Merck). The coding sequences for the PH domains of DNM1, DNM2, and DNM3 were amplified by PCR from a mouse brain cDNA library. Similarly, the sequences for the PH domains of evectin-2 (Evt2-PH-WT) and its mutant (Evt2-PH-KE) were amplified from pEF 2xPH-WT and pEF 2xPH-K20E (gifts from Dr. T. Taguchi), respectively. All constructs were verified by Sanger sequencing to confirm the correct open reading frame and sequence integrity.

Primary culture of mouse neurons

To compare WT and ATP8A1 KO hippocampal neurons, ATP8A1 +/- male and female mice were mated. To compare WT and ATP8A2 KO hippocampal neurons, ATP8A2 +/- male and female mice were mated. To compare ATP8A1 KO and DKO hippocampal neurons, ATP8A1 KO/ ATP8A2 +/- male and female mice were mated. After determining genotypes of embryonic or neonatal mice by PCR, hippocampal neurons were prepared and cultured as described previously.58 Briefly, hippocampi were collected and incubated with 0.25 % (w/v) Trypsin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37°C for 3 min. They were then incubated with 20 % (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Nichirei) in Neurobasal medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at room temperature (r.t.) for 1 min and 0.1 % deoxyribonuclease I (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical) in HBSS+MgSO4 at r.t. for 1 min. After being washed with Neurobasal medium, hippocampi were dispersed using a fire-polished Pasteur pipet in Neurobasal medium with 2 % (v/v) B27 Supplement (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 2 mM GlutaMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 1 % (v/v) penicillin-streptomycin solution (Nacalai Tesque) (NB+). The dissociated cells were then washed with Neurobasal medium, suspended in NB+, and the number of viable cells was counted using Trypan blue. The cells were then seeded onto 12 mm round glass in 24 well plates (Nippon Genetics) or 6 well plates (Nippon Genetics) coated with 0.01 % (v/v) poly-L-lysine (Sigma-Aldrich). Neurons were cultured in NB+ at 37°C in the presence of 5 % CO2. Seeding densities were as follows: 1.5 × 104 cells/cm2 for immunocytochemistry; 5 × 104 cells/cm2 for surface biotinylation assays.

For cerebellar neuron culture, the cerebellum from P0 mice was processed using the same protocol as for hippocampi, except that the cells were dispersed in Neurobasal medium with 10% (v/v) FBS and 1 % (v/v) penicillin-streptomycin solution, filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer, and seeded at a density of 2.2 × 105 cells/cm2.

Transfection

Primary cultured hippocampal neurons were transfected at 3 days in vitro (DIV) using the calcium phosphate method.59,60 Briefly, 250 mM CaCl2 was mixed in equal volume with 2×BES buffer (50 mM N,N-bis(2-hydroxyethyl)-2-aminoethanesulfonic acid (BES), 1.5 mM Na2HPO4, pH 7.1, 280 mM NaCl), and plasmid DNA (2.0 μg/60 μL solution) was added to this mixture. The resulting solution was applied to the cultures for 30–35 min at 37°C in 5% CO2. Neurons were subsequently washed with HEPES-buffered saline and returned to conditioned medium.

Western blotting

Western blotting was performed as previously described.61 Briefly, samples were separated by SDS-PAGE with Precision Plus Protein All Blue Prestained Protein Standards (Bio-Rad) as molecular weight markers. Proteins were then transferred to Immobilon-P PVDF membrane (Merck Millipore) using Trans-Blot SD Semi-Dry Electrophoretic Transfer Cell (Bio-Rad). To confirm the uniformity of transferred proteins, the blotted membranes were stained with a Ponceau S solution. The membrane was incubated with 3% skimmed milk then incubated with the primary antibody for 2 h at r.t., washed with Tris-buffered saline (TBS; 20 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl) containing 0.05% Tween-20, incubated with the horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody (1:4,000) for 1 h at r.t. The bands were then visualized with Immobilon Chemiluminescent HRP Substrate (Sigma-Aldrich). Images were captured using LAS4000-Mini (Fujifilm) and analyzed with Fiji/Image J software (NIH). The area of each blot was enclosed, and the total intensity of chemical luminosity was calculated using the following formula: area × (mean intensity - background near the band). The primary antibodies were used at the following dilution ratios: mouse anti-ATP8A2 (6.0 ng/μL), mouse anti-β-actin (1:8,000), mouse anti-GABAA receptor β3 (1:500), mouse anti-GluR1 (1:1,000), rabbit anti-ATP8A1 (1:2,000) and rabbit anti-TMEM30a (0.45 ng/μL).

Immunoprecipitation

Mouse brains were suspended in IP buffer (50 mM HEPES pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1 % CHAPS), sonicated using Sonifier 250 (Branson), and then centrifugated at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatants were collected, and protein concentration was measured using Protein Assay BCA Kit (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical). The supernatant was divided into two equal portions, and the antibody of interest and control IgG were added to each portion at the final concentration of 1 μg/mL. The mixtures were rotated at 4°C for 1 h. Protein-G Sepharose 4 Fast Flow (Cytiva) in IP buffer was then added, and the reaction was further continued for 2 h at 4°C on a rotator. The precipitated Protein-G Sepharose 4 Fast Flow was washed with IP buffer and then eluted with SDS-sampling buffer (final conc.: 62.5 mM Tris-HCl pH 6.8, 10 % (v/v) glycerol, 0.05 % (w/v) bromophenol blue, 2 % (w/v) SDS, 5 % (v/v) 2-mercaptoethanol).

Immunohistochemistry

Postnatal mice were transcardially perfused with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS. The brains were subsequently cryoprotected by sequential immersion in 20% and 30% sucrose in PBS, embedded in O.C.T. compound (Sakura Finetek), and rapidly frozen on dry ice. Tissue sections (14 μm thick) were prepared from the frozen brains using a CM1850 cryostat (Leica Biosystems). Tissue sections were rehydrated in PBS for 5 min, followed by antigen retrieval in 10 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) by autoclaving at 105 °C for 5 min. After cooling, sections were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min and blocked with 2% BSA in PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20 (PBS-T) for 30 min at r.t., and then incubated with the primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. The sections were washed with PBS-T and incubated with an Alexa Fluor–conjugated secondary antibody (1:500) for 1 h at r.t. Nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33342. After a final wash with PBS-T, the sections were mounted with Fluorescent Mounting Medium (Agilent Technologies). Fluorescence images were acquired using a BZ-X710 fluorescence microscope (Keyence).

Immunocytochemistry

Primary cultured neurons were fixed with 4% PFA for 5 min and permeabilized with PBS-T. The fixed cells were then blocked with 2% BSA in PBS-T for 30 min, followed by incubation with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After washing, the cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor–conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500) for 1 h at r.t. Nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33342. Following a final wash with PBS-T, the samples were mounted with Fluorescent Mounting Medium (Agilent Technologies). Fluorescence images were acquired using a BZ-X710 fluorescence microscope (Keyence) or an LSM800 confocal laser scanning microscope (Carl Zeiss). For synaptic density quantification, neurons were fixed at 14 DIV and immunostained as described above using antibodies against VGLUT1 and PSD95 for excitatory synapses, or VGAT and GABAA receptor β2/3 subunits for inhibitory synapses. Synaptic puncta were defined as regions showing co-localization of pre- and postsynaptic markers. Synaptic density was calculated as the number of double-positive puncta per μm of neurite length. Confocal fluorescence images were obtained using an LSM800. Fluorescence intensities and co-localization were quantified using Fiji. For the co-localization analysis of DNM2 and surface GABAA receptors, neurons were first blocked with 2% BSA in PBS for 30 min and then incubated with the antibody against GABAA receptor β2/3 subunits (clone BD17) for 90 min at r.t. to specifically label surface epitopes. The cells were subsequently permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min and incubated with an anti-DNM2 antibody overnight at 4 °C, followed by Alexa Fluor–conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500) for 1 h at r.t. Nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33342. After washes with PBS-T, the samples were mounted with Fluorescent Mounting Medium. Confocal fluorescence images were obtained using an LSM800 or LSM900 microscope (Carl Zeiss). Fluorescence intensities and co-localization were quantified using Fiji. The primary antibodies were used at the following dilution ratios: guinea pig anti-VGLUT1 (1:1,000), guinea pig anti-VGAT (1:500), mouse anti-GABAA receptor β2/3 subunits (clone BD17, 1:200), mouse anti-gephyrin (1:100), mouse anti-MAP2 (1:1,000), mouse anti-PSD95 (1:100), rabbit anti-ATP8A1 (1:1,000), rabbit anti-TMEM30a (1.5 ng/μL), rabbit anti-DNM2 (1:1,000), rabbit anti-GFAP (1:300), rabbit anti-Iba1 (1:1,000), and rat anti-HA (1:1,000). Colocalization between ATP8A1 or HA-ATP8A2 and various synaptic proteins was quantified by expressing the double-positive area as a fraction of the synaptic protein–positive area.

Biotinylation of cell-surface proteins

Surface biotinylation was performed essentially as described previously.10 Briefly, primary cultured hippocampal neurons at 12 DIV were washed with Wash buffer (20 mM HEPES pH 7.4, 1 mM CaCl2, 0.5 mM MgCl2, 120 mM NaCl), and incubated with 1 mg/ml EZ-link Sulfo-NHS-biotin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in wash buffer for 20 min on ice. Unreacted biotin was removed by washing with ice-cold PBS containing 100 mM glycine, followed by two washes with ice-cold PBS. Neurons were then lysed in Lysis buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 5 mM EDTA, 100 mM NaCl, 1 % TritonX-100, 0.1 % SDS) and the lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatants were collected and protein concentration was measured using Protein Assay BCA Kit, and a portion (10 %) was saved as input. The remaining supernatants were then mixed with NeutrAvidin Agarose Resin (Thermo Fisher Scientific), incubated at 4°C for 1 h, and centrifuged at 3,300 × g for 5 min at 4°C. The supernatants were collected as unbiotinylated fraction. The precipitated resin was washed with TBS, and then proteins were eluted by adding SDS-sampling buffer.

Lipidomics analysis

ATP8A1/ATP8A2 DHE and DKO mice were obtained by mating ATP8A1 KO/ATP8A2 heterozygote male mice with DHE female mice. The hippocampi were dissected out from P0 mice, placed in vials, and frozen with liquid nitrogen. To each vial, 1 mL of methanol and zirconia beads were added and crushed (4,000 rpm/90 sec). Each tissue homogenate was diluted by methanol to a tissue weight of 1 mg/mL. The LC-MS/MS technique and conditions were described previously.62 LC-MS/MS system was composed of ultraperformance liquid chromatography (Acquity UPLC; Waters) and an ESI tandem quadrupole mass spectrometer (Xevo TQ-XS; Waters). The injected samples were separated on a reverse-phase column with the flow rate of 0.3 ml/min at 45°C in a binary gradient system using acetonitrile/water (3:2, vol/vol) containing 10 mM ammonium formate as a mobile phase A and isopropanol/acetonitrile (9:1, vol/vol) containing 10 mM ammonium formate as a mobile phase B. The gradient condition was set as follows: initial condition (0 min), 5% B; 0–2 min, 5% B; 2–26.4 min linear gradient to 100% B; 26.4–36 min, 100% B; 36–36.1 min, linear gradient to 5% B; 36.1–40 min, 5% B. The capillary voltage, sampling cone voltage, and desolvation temperature were set to 2.5 kV, 30 V, and 400°C, respectively. The acyl-chain species of phospholipids were detected using the multiple reaction monitoring method, as reported previously.60 Raw data files (Data S1 and S2) were processed and analyzed using MassLynx software (Waters).

Endocytosis assay

Endocytosis of GABAA receptor was quantified by the method described previously.37,38 Briefly, cultured hippocampal neurons at 14–18 DIV were incubated with antibody against the extracellular domain of GABAA receptor β2/3 subunits (clone 62-3G1, 20 μg/ml) at 4°C for 1 h. After washing with ice-cold PBS, neurons were fixed with 4 % PFA in PBS for 10 min either immediately (to assess the surface expression of GABAA receptor) or after incubation in the NB+ medium at 37°C for 90 min (to assess the internalized GABAA receptor). The neurons were washed with PBS and treated with 2 % BSA in PBS for 30 min for blocking. The neurons were then incubated with 2% BSA in PBS containing 20 μg/mL Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-mouse IgG for 2 h at r.t. After washing with PBS, the membrane was permeabilized with 0.1 % Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min. The permeabilized neurons were then reacted with 2 % BSA in PBS containing 10 μg/mL Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated anti-mouse IgG for 1 h at r.t. After washing with PBS-T, the cells were mounted in Fluorescent Mounting Medium. Fluorescent images were captured using LSM800, and fluorescence intensities were quantified using Fiji. The fluorescence intensity of internalized receptors was quantified in regions excluding surface receptor signal. In each trial, the difference in mean fluorescence intensity of internalized receptors between the group in which endocytosis was promoted for 90 min and the group in which cells were fixed immediately was determined. This value was divided by the average fluorescence intensity of cell surface receptors as a measure of endocytosis efficiency.

Liposome cosedimentation assay

Liposome cosedimentation assay was performed essentially as described previously.43 Briefly, His-tagged PH domain proteins were expressed in Escherichia coli BL21 CodonPlus (Agilent Technologies). The expressed proteins were purified using Ni Sepharose 6 Fast Flow (Cytiva) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Liposomes (PE:PC:PS = 5:2:2 or 5:2:0) were prepared by standard lipid film hydration. Purified PH domain proteins were incubated with liposomes at r.t., followed by ultracentrifugation at 100,000 × g for 30 min at 20 °C using Optima MAX-XP (Beckman Coulter). The resulting pellet and purified PH domain peptide solution were analyzed by SDS–PAGE and Coomassie staining. The intensity of individual bands was then quantified with Fiji. The area of each band was enclosed, and the signal intensity of the target band was calculated using the following formula: area × (mean intensity - background intensity near the band). PS-binding capacity was expressed as the ratio of signal intensity in PS-containing versus PS-free liposomes.

qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from the cerebral cortex, hippocampus, and cerebellum of E18.5 mice using ISOSPIN Cell & Tissue RNA (Nippon Gene). The quality of the extracted RNA was checked by agarose gel electrophoresis. Total RNA (50 ng) was used to synthesize cDNA using Random Primers (Promega) and SuperScript III (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer's instructions in a 20-μl reaction.

PCR was performed using THUNDERBIRD Next SYBR qPCR Mix (Toyobo) on a StepOnePlus (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The cycling conditions were as follows: 95°C for 30 sec (one cycle), 95°C for 5 sec, and 61°C for 10 sec (40 cycles). The expression levels of all genes were normalized to the expression of β-actin and analyzed using the ΔΔCt method. The sequences of primers used are described in Table S2.

Annexin V staining

Primary hippocampal neurons (14 DIV) were washed with pre-warmed binding buffer (50 mM HEPES pH 7.4, 750 mM NaCl, 25 mM KCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 9 mM CaCl2) at 37 °C. To label cell-surface PS, live neurons were incubated with Annexin V-Biotin (1:10 in binding buffer) at 37 °C for 30 min. After washing with binding buffer, the neurons were fixed with 4% PFA in binding buffer for 10 min. The fixed neurons were then permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in binding buffer and blocked with 2% BSA in binding buffer with 0.05% Tween-20 for 30 min. The samples were then incubated with anti-GABAA receptor β2/3 subunits at r.t. for 2 h. After washing, the neurons were incubated with Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated anti-mouse IgG and Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated Streptavidin at r.t. for 1 h. Following a final wash with binding buffer, the samples were mounted with Fluorescent Mounting Medium. Fluorescence images were acquired using an LSM800.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Quantification and statistical analysis were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10. Data are presented as mean ± SEM. A one-sample two-tailed t-test was used to determine whether the mean significantly differed from a theoretical value of 1. A Welch's t-test (two-tailed) was used for comparisons between two groups when equality of variances was not assumed. One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test was used for comparisons involving three or more groups. To evaluate differences across multiple pairs of groups while controlling for the false discovery rate (FDR), multiple unpaired t-tests with Welch’s correction were performed, followed by the Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment (set at a desired FDR q = 5%). The number of independent experiments and replicates (n) is indicated in the figure legends. No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample size. Data distribution was assumed to be normal, but this was not formally tested. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
