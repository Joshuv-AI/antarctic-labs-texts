Suppression of LncRNA AC008406.3 sensitizes breast cancer cells to docetaxel via triggering cuproptosis
Authors: Chenguang Liu, Shiyang Liu, Zonghong Lu, Xinchen Cao, Xiaofei Tong, Ieong Sun, Jie Hao, Lin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundDocetaxel (DTX) is one of the commonly used chemotherapeutic agents for breast cancer. Cuproptosis, a newly defined form of cell death, significantly influences tumor progression. This study aims to identify cuproptosis-related long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) involved in regulating the sensitivity of breast cancer to DTX.MaterialsCuproptosis-related prognostic lncRNAs in breast cancer were identified through integrated co-expression, differential expression, and prognostic analyses based on the TCGA-BRCA cohort. The role of the identified lncRNA AC008406.3 in regulating cuproptosis in breast cancer cells was experimentally verified. The impact of DTX on the induction of cuproptosis was investigated, and rescue experiments were conducted to validate the involvement of AC008406.3 in modulating breast cancer sensitivity to DTX.ResultsAC008406.3 was identified as a potential cuproptosis-related prognostic lncRNA in breast cancer, exhibiting high expression in breast cancer samples, which was associated with unfavorable prognostic outcomes. Knockdown of AC008406.3 specifically induced cuproptosis in breast cancer cells, as evidenced by increased intracellular copper levels and downregulation of lipoylated proteins and Fe–S cluster proteins, thereby suppressing cell proliferation, invasion, and migration; conversely, AC008406.3 overexpression elicited opposite phenotypic changes. Notably, DTX induced the occurrence of cuproptosis in breast cancer, whereas AC008406.3 suppressed this process, thereby diminishing DTX efficacy. However, AC008406.3 knockdown synergistically enhanced the anti-tumor efficacy of DTX in inhibiting breast cancer growth.ConclusionAC008406.3 reduces the sensitivity of breast cancer to DTX by suppressing cuproptosis. Targeting AC008406.3 may represent a potential strategy to sensitize breast cancer cells to DTX.Keywords: AC008406.3, docetaxel, cuproptosis, breast cancer, lncRNA

Introduction

According to data released by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) of the World Health Organization in 2022, breast cancer is the second most common cancer globally,1 accounting for 11.6% of all cases, and ranks first among cancers in women, with an incidence rate of 23.8%. In terms of cancer mortality, breast cancer ranks fourth, accounting for 6.9%.2 Despite the relatively favorable prognosis of early-stage breast cancer, challenges remain in treating advanced and metastatic breast cancer. In clinical practice, aside from surgery and endocrinotherapy, chemotherapy is the most commonly used treatment for breast cancer. Docetaxel (DTX) is a first-line chemotherapeutic agent for breast cancer. Its cytotoxic mechanism is known to inhibit cell mitosis by promoting and stabilizing microtubule structures, thereby preventing the proliferation of cancer cells.3 Additionally, DTX can promote apoptosis by downregulating the anti-apoptotic oncoprotein Bcl-2.4 Research by Wei Huang et al. reported that the cytotoxicity of DTX can be enhanced in the presence of ferroptosis inducers.5 Accordingly, this suggests that the mechanisms by which DTX induces cell death are not singular. Notably, half of the patients fail to respond to DTX treatment, and those who initially show a response often develop resistance later.6 Therefore, uncovering the mechanisms of DTX insensitivity and enhancing its efficacy are critical for developing targeted therapies for breast cancer patients.

Copper, an essential trace element required by both normal and cancer cells, is involved in many essential physiological processes, including redox homeostasis and cellular pathway regulation.7 Copper metabolism imbalance may induce or exacerbate certain diseases, such as Wilson’s disease and malignancies. With regard to tumor progression, copper has been found to play a significant role in tumor cell proliferation,8 angiogenesis,9 and metastasis.10 Earlier studies have observed that copper overload can lead to cell death, but the underlying mechanisms remain unclear.11,12 In 2022, Peter et al. defined a novel form of copper-dependent cell death, distinct from previously known mechanisms, which they termed cuproptosis. Cuproptosis is triggered by the accumulation of excess copper ions, leading to the aggregation of lipoylated proteins and the destabilization of Fe–S cluster proteins, which causes proteotoxic stress and ultimately results in cell death.13,14 Elevated copper levels have been detected in the blood and tumor tissues of breast cancer patients.15 However, the copper ion metabolism status and susceptibility to cuproptosis in breast cancer remain unclear.

As research into the human genome progresses, its complexity becomes increasingly evident. Protein-coding RNA accounts for less than 2% of the entire genome, while non-coding regions occupy the vast majority of the genome.16 Long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) is an important component of the non-coding genome, defined as non-coding RNA longer than 200 nucleotides. LncRNAs often exhibit multifunctionality, performing a variety of roles within cells, including the regulation of gene expression and post-translational modifications.17 LncRNAs regulate cancer development by influencing genome damage through the activation or suppression of tumor-related factors,18,19 and by encoding biologically active peptides.20 However, the role of cuproptosis-related lncRNAs in breast cancer remains unclear.

Based on transcriptomic data from the TCGA database, we identified cuproptosis-related lncRNAs that impact breast cancer prognosis, with a particular focus on AC008406.3, which exhibited the largest absolute log fold change (logFC) in differential expression analysis between tumor and normal tissues. Further experiments demonstrated that AC008406.3 suppressed cuproptosis, thereby promoting breast cancer progression, characterized by enhanced tumor cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. We subsequently found that DTX effectively kills breast cancer cells by inducing cuproptosis, but this process was reversed by AC008406.3, suggesting that AC008406.3 reduces DTX efficacy in breast cancer. Overall, targeting AC008406.3 to activate cuproptosis may represent a promising strategy for enhancing breast cancer sensitivity to DTX.

Results

AC008406.3 is a potential cuproptosis-related lncRNA influencing breast cancer prognosis

Following genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 positive selection screening, 10 cuproptosis-related genes were identified by Peter and colleagues, including FDX1, LIAS, LIPT1, DLD, DLAT, PDHA1, PDHB, MTF1, GLS, and CDKN2A.12 Based on TCGA-BRCA data, Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to identify lncRNAs that are co-expressed with the above cuproptosis-related genes, with the correlation coefficient set at 0.2 (Figure S1A). By intersecting these with the differentially expressed lncRNAs between breast cancer and normal tissues, 701 lncRNAs were obtained. A total of 20 cuproptosis-related lncRNAs significantly associated with overall survival (OS) were further identified through Lasso-Cox regression analysis (Figure S1B–D). Among these, lncRNA AC008406.3 was selected as the candidate prognostic lncRNA due to its maximum absolute logFC value (Table S1) and p-value < 0.05 in the Cox proportional hazards regression model (Figure S1D). The cuproptosis-related genes associated with AC008406.3 include MTF1, LIPT1, and DLAT (Figure S1E). AC008406.3 is located on chromosome 5 (chr5:135,034,521 – 135,035,894, positive strand) and has a single transcript of 1374 bp (Figure S1F). AC008406.3 is highly expressed in breast cancer and impacts prognosis

Subsequently, analysis of RNA-seq data from 1039 breast cancer cases in the TCGA database revealed that AC008406.3 expression levels in tumor tissues were significantly higher than those in normal tissues (Figure 1B). Consistent results were also observed in clinical samples from the Tongji cohort, where AC008406.3 was highly expressed in breast cancer tissues (Figure 1C). The optimal cutoff value was utilized to categorize patients into high- and low-expression groups based on AC008406.3 expression levels. In the prognostic analysis, the high AC008406.3 expression group experienced a poor prognosis (Figure 1D). The expression level of AC008406.3 did not show significant differences across different molecular subtypes (Figure 1E). Subsequently, AC008406.3 expression was evaluated in three breast cancer cell lines, revealing the highest expression in BT-549, intermediate expression in MDA-MB-231, and the lowest expression in MCF-7 (Figure 1F). Considering the differential endogenous expression levels, the MDA-MB-231 and BT-549 cell lines were used to investigate the effects of AC008406.3 knockdown (Figure 1G), whereas the MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells were used to assess the impact of AC008406.3 overexpression (Figure S1G). To better understand the functional roles and underlying mechanisms of lncRNA AC008406.3, determining its subcellular localization is essential. RNA-FISH revealed that AC008406.3 was primarily located in the cytoplasm of MDA-MB-231, BT-549, and MCF-7 cells (Figure 1H and S1H).

Breast cancer is susceptible to elesclomol-induced cuproptosis

Previous evidence has shown elevated copper ion levels in both cancerous tissues and plasma samples of breast cancer patients,15 and copper overload has been confirmed to induce cuproptosis.12 However, the susceptibility of breast cancer cells to cuproptosis remains unclear. To verify whether the copper ionophore elesclomol (ES) could induce cuproptosis in breast cancer, the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values of ES in three breast cancer cell lines were first determined, all of which were around 50 nM (Figure 2A and S2A). ES treatment increased intracellular copper levels in breast cancer cells, and co‑treatment with CuCl₂ further amplified this effect in a dose‑dependent manner (Figure 2B). Tetrathiomolybdate (TTM), as a copper chelator, can chelate excess intracellular copper. In breast cancer cell lines, ES-induced cell death could only be rescued by TTM, but not by other cell death inhibitors. In contrast, ML162‑induced ferroptotic cell death was rescued by the antioxidant NAC and by the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer‑1 (Figure 2C and S2B). These findings suggest that breast cancer cells are vulnerable to ES-induced cuproptosis, which is distinct from other forms of cell death.

AC008406.3 specifically inhibits cuproptosis in breast cancer

After AC008406.3 knockdown, there was a significant increase in intracellular copper concentration in breast cancer cells, which was further enhanced following ES-Cu treatment (Figure 2D). Mechanistically, silencing AC008406.3 downregulated the expression of lipoylated proteins (Lip-DLAT, Lip-DLST) and Fe–S cluster-associated proteins (FDX1, LIAS), which are markers of cuproptosis, with these changes becoming more pronounced after ES-Cu treatment (Figure 2E). The AC008406.3 overexpression group exhibited effects opposite to those observed in the AC008406.3 knockdown group (Figure S2C–E). Regardless of whether AC008406.3 was overexpressed, the expression of key regulatory proteins involved in ferroptosis (GPX4, xCT) and apoptosis (Cleaved-Caspase-3, PARP1) remained unchanged (Figure 2E and S2D). The apoptosis rate detected by flow cytometry further confirmed that neither AC008406.3 nor the cuproptosis inducer (ES-Cu) influenced the apoptosis process (Figure 2F and S2F). The above findings suggest that AC008406.3 specifically inhibits cuproptosis in breast cancer cells rather than other cell death pathways.

AC008406.3 promotes breast cancer progression by inhibiting cuproptosis

In breast cancer cell lines, CCK-8 and colony formation assays revealed that both AC008406.3 knockdown groups exhibited slower cell proliferation compared to the control group (Figure 3A-B). On the contrary, the AC008406.3 overexpression group exerted a promotive effect on breast cancer cell proliferation (Figure S3A, B). In terms of breast cancer cell migration, similar results were observed in both Transwell and wound healing assays, where the AC008406.3 knockdown groups showed reduced cell migration compared to the control group (Figure 3C, D). Conversely, the overexpression group exhibited a promotive effect on breast cancer cell migration (Figure S3C, D). To simulate the extracellular matrix barrier, Matrigel-coated Transwell membranes were used to assess the invasive capability of the tumor cells. The experiment revealed that AC008406.3 knockdown significantly inhibited, whereas its overexpression significantly promoted, the invasive capacity of breast cancer cells (Figure 3E and S3E).

Further experiments were conducted to clarify whether AC008406.3 affects breast cancer cell proliferation through cuproptosis. CCK-8 assays and colony formation assays revealed that the reduction in cell proliferation capacity caused by AC008406.3 knockdown was reversed by the copper chelator TTM (Figure 4A, B). Conversely, the cuproptosis inducer ES-Cu reversed the proliferation‑promoting effect of AC008406.3 overexpression vector (Figure S4A, B).

Knockdown of AC008406.3 inhibits tumor growth in a breast cancer xenograft model

To establish a xenograft model, MDA-MB-231 cells were infected with an AC008406.3-knockdown lentivirus, and after validation of knockdown efficiency, the cells were injected subcutaneously into nude mice (Figure 5A). After 3 weeks of tumor formation and development, both the AC008406.3 knockdown groups exhibited slower tumor growth compared to the control group, as indicated by a smaller tumor size and volume, and lighter weight (Figure 5B–D). The IHC results showed that AC008406.3 knockdown significantly reduced the number of Ki-67-positive proliferating tumor cells, providing further evidence of the pro-tumorigenic effect of AC008406.3 in breast cancer (Figure 5E). Notably, AC008406.3 knockdown significantly suppressed the expression of the cuproptosis regulator FDX1, suggesting the occurrence of cuproptosis in the AC008406.3 knockdown groups (Figure 5F). In summary, knockdown of AC008406.3 enhances the susceptibility of breast cancer cells to cuproptosis, thereby significantly inhibiting xenograft tumor growth.

Docetaxel triggers cuproptosis in breast cancer

As the concentration of DTX increased, the expression levels of FDX1 gradually decreased. The addition of CuCl₂ resulted in a mild decrease in FDX1 expression, without reaching statistical significance (Figure 6A and S5A). Meanwhile, DTX dose-dependently downregulated FDX1, whereas the copper chelator TTM significantly increased FDX1 expression (Figure 6B and S5B). Subsequently, IHC analysis was conducted on matched clinical samples collected before and after the DTX-based neoadjuvant chemotherapy. The results indicated that FDX1 staining was weaker in post-treatment samples compared to pre-treatment samples in patients who received DTX-based neoadjuvant chemotherapy (Figure 6C), suggesting that DTX may induce cuproptosis, leading to FDX1 depletion. The chemotherapy efficacy of 44 patients who received a DTX-based neoadjuvant chemotherapy regimen was assessed postoperatively using the Miller‒Payne grading system.21 AC008406.3 high expression was more frequently observed in the G1–G3 (poor response) group, while low expression was more prevalent in the G4–G5 (good response) group (Figure 6D). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis showed that AC008406.3 had good predictive performance for chemotherapy response, with an AUC of 0.86 (Figure 6E). Additionally, DTX induced breast cancer cell death in a concentration-dependent manner, an effect that was further enhanced following AC008406.3 knockdown but reversed upon AC008406.3 overexpression (Figure 6F and S5C), indicating that AC008406.3 weakens the tumoricidal effect of DTX on breast cancer cells. Following DTX exposure, the intracellular copper levels were increased (Figure 7A and S5D), coinciding with a reduction in the expression of cuproptosis regulators Fe–S cluster-associated proteins and lipoylated proteins (Figure 7B, C and S5E, F). These findings confirm that DTX triggers cuproptosis in breast cancer.

AC008406.3 reduces DTX sensitivity in breast cancer via suppressing cuproptosis

The above results suggest that AC008406.3 reduces the cytotoxic effects of DTX. We then investigated whether this process was associated with cuproptosis. Under DTX treatment, AC008406.3 knockdown resulted in increased copper levels within breast cancer cells and a further reduction in the expression of cuproptosis regulators, thereby amplifying the cytotoxic effects of DTX. However, upon the addition of copper chelator TTM, intracellular copper levels decreased, leading to the restoration of the expression of cuproptosis regulators and a reduction in the cytotoxicity of DTX (Figure 7A–D). These findings were further confirmed in the overexpression group, which showed opposite trends (Figure S5D–G). Cells undergoing cuproptosis will exhibit characteristic alterations in mitochondrial morphology. TEM revealed that after 24-h exposure to ES-Cu and DTX, the mitochondria exhibited swelling, cristae loss, and vacuolization. However, when co-treated with the AC008406.3 overexpression vector, these morphological abnormalities were significantly alleviated (Figure 7E). This morphological evidence further supports the role of AC008406.3 in mitigating the cytotoxic effects of DTX. Furthermore, in vivo experiments demonstrated that tumors in the knockdown group showed a more pronounced reduction in size and weight following DTX treatment (Figure 8A–C). Subsequently, IHC analysis revealed weaker staining for both the proliferation marker Ki-67 and the cuproptosis regulator FDX1 in the knockdown groups (Figure 8D). Collectively, the combination of AC008406.3 knockdown and DTX exerts a robust anti-tumor effect on breast cancer via synergistically promoting cuproptosis.

Discussion

Breast cancer is one of the most pressing public health issues globally, with early diagnosis and timely treatment being particularly crucial. The development of cancer is a result of multiple factors working in concert,22 and the management of breast cancer has long entered an era of comprehensive, multi-targeted therapies. Unveiling cell death mechanisms has led to the discovery of novel therapeutic targets for cancer treatment.23 Copper is one of the essential metal elements in organisms, maintaining low concentrations and dynamic equilibrium under physiological conditions. As observed by Peter et al., the copper ionophore ES induced cuproptosis in cells by affecting mitochondrial metabolism.13 Given that breast cancer is troubled by enhanced mitochondrial metabolism, targeting cuproptosis may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for breast cancer treatment. Prior to the emergence of the cuproptosis concept, copper and copper-related factors were already known to be closely associated with cancer progression. The copper-binding protein ATOX1 was capable of mediating the migration of breast cancer cells.24 Copper depletion therapy has been applied in Wilson’s disease and neurodegenerative disorders,25,26 and it has emerged as a promising treatment approach in cancer therapy.27,28 Therefore, focusing on cuproptosis-related factors may provide novel insights into breast cancer treatment.

After correlation and differential analyses, the cuproptosis-related lncRNA AC008406.3 was selected for the present study. AC008406.3 was highly expressed in the breast cancer tissues and associated with poor prognosis. Although different molecular subtypes of breast cancer exhibit distinct tumor characteristics, we did not observe significant differences in AC008406.3 expression levels across these subtypes, suggesting that the mechanism of AC008406.3 may have a universal role in breast cancer. Therefore, we selected breast cancer cell lines representing different molecular subtypes for subsequent studies. The subcellular localization of lncRNAs is a critical aspect of their functional versatility and regulatory roles in cellular processes.17 LncRNAs in the cytoplasm can interact with proteins and other RNA molecules, forming complexes that regulate critical signaling pathways involved in cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion.29 In the present study, AC008406.3 was found to be localized in the cytoplasm, suggesting that it may function through post-transcriptional mechanisms, possibly via interactions with miRNAs or RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) to regulate cuproptosis. Similarly, in subsequent experiments, we observed that the dysregulation of AC008406.3 expression led to an imbalance in copper metabolism, subsequently affecting the proliferation, migration, and invasion capabilities of breast cancer cells. Specifically, AC008406.3 was found to prevent the occurrence of cuproptosis, as indicated by the reduced levels of copper, upregulation of the lipoylated proteins and Fe–S cluster-associated proteins, ultimately promoting breast cancer tumor growth. This study also found that AC008406.3 was associated with key cuproptosis-related genes, including the metal regulatory transcription factor (MTF1). MTF1, a metal-responsive transcription factor involved in metal homeostasis and stress responses, has been reported to suppress cuproptosis through promotion of ISCA2-mediated Fe–S cluster assembly.30 Future studies will focus on identifying miRNAs or RBPs that directly interact with AC008406.3 and evaluating whether AC008406.3 regulates MTF1 expression through these partners to modulate cuproptosis susceptibility in breast cancer cells.

Most breast cancer patients require chemotherapy, with DTX emerging as one of the most commonly used clinical agents. Not all patients benefit from DTX, and its toxic side effects remain a significant concern.31 Therefore, uncovering the mechanisms underlying DTX insensitivity and identifying strategies to enhance its efficacy are of critical importance. Generally, apoptosis has been regarded as the primary mechanism through which DTX induces cancer cell death.32 However, as research progresses, it has become evident that the therapeutic effects of DTX are also closely associated with multiple forms of cell death beyond apoptosis.33 A recent study by Wen et al. revealed that, in prostate cancer, cuproptosis can enhance the chemosensitivity to DTX by inhibiting autophagy.34 Clinically, DTX serves not only as adjuvant chemotherapy but also as neoadjuvant treatment before surgery in breast cancer patients.35 This study initially reveals that DTX is sufficient to trigger cuproptosis in breast cancer, as evidenced by the reduction of the cuproptosis regulator FDX1 in both patient and cellular samples following DTX chemotherapy, a process that is affected by copper levels. Thus, we hypothesize that promoting cuproptosis may serve as a potential therapeutic approach to improve DTX efficacy.

Given that AC008406.3 can influence breast cancer progression through cuproptosis, AC008406.3 may play a regulatory role in the process of DTX-induced cuproptosis. These results indicate that AC008406.3 reduces the anti-tumor efficacy of DTX by inhibiting cuproptosis. Our study opens new avenues for targeted therapies that exploit the cuproptosis-related agents in combination with traditional chemotherapeutic drugs. By manipulating AC008406.3 levels, it may be possible to sensitize breast cancer cells to DTX, ultimately improving patient outcomes. However, further investigations are warranted to elucidate the precise molecular mechanisms by which AC008406.3 influences copper metabolism and cell death pathways.

In summary, this study provides a novel perspective for advancing DTX chemotherapy in breast cancer. Elevated lncRNA AC008406.3 levels in breast cancer samples suppressed cuproptosis and were associated with poor prognosis. Combining AC008406.3 knockdown with DTX chemotherapy synergistically activated the cuproptosis pathway, thereby inhibiting breast cancer growth. Targeting AC008406.3 represents a viable strategy to enhance the therapeutic efficacy of DTX in breast cancer.

Materials and methods

Reagents and antibodies

The reagents’ information is listed as follows: Elesclomol (ES; MedChem Express HY-12040), ML162 (MedChem Express HY-100002), Tetrathiomolybdate (TTM; Sigma-Aldrich 323446), Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1; MedChem Express HY-100579), N-acetylcysteine (NAC; MedChem Express HY-B0215), Necrostatin-1 (Nec-1; MedChem Express HY-15760), Z-VAD-FMK (ZVF; MedChem Express HY-16658B), Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; MedChem Express HY-Y0320), DTX (MedChem Express HY-B0011), Anhydrous copper (II) chloride (CuCl2; Macklin 7447-39-4).

The antibodies’ information used for Western blotting and immunohistochemistry is listed as follows: HRP-conjugated GAPDH (Proteintech, HRP-60004, 1:5000 for WB), FDX1/ADX (ABclonal, A20895, 1:500 for IHC and 1:800 for WB), DLAT (ABclonal, A8814, 1:1000 for WB), LIAS (Proteintech, 11577-1-AP, 1:2000 for WB), Lipoic acid (Abcam ab58724, 1:2000 for WB), Cleaved-Caspase-3 (Huabio, ET1602-47, 1:1000 for WB), PARP1 (Proteintech, 13371-1-AP, 1:2000 for WB), GPX4 (Zenbio, 381958, 1:800 for WB), SLC7A11/xCT (ABclonal, A2413, 1:1000 for WB), Ki-67 (Servicebio, GB111499-100, 1:500 for IHC).

Human tissue sample collection

The human study was approved by the Human Ethics Committee of Tongji Hospital of Tongji Medical College of Huazhong University of Science and Technology (No. TJ-IRB202407108 and TJ-IRB202406063), in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki of 1975. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to enrollment. Human tissue specimens were collected from Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology. Paired breast cancer and adjacent normal tissues (NAT) were obtained from 10 female patients diagnosed with primary breast cancer between December 2023 and April 2024 to assess the differential expression of AC008406.3. In addition, paired breast tissue samples from 10 patients were collected before and after neoadjuvant chemotherapy between June 2022 and December 2023 to evaluate changes in the cuproptosis marker FDX1. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Patients diagnosed with primary breast cancer by pathology. (2) Female patients were treated with a neoadjuvant chemotherapy regimen that included DTX and completed the full course of chemotherapy. (3) Complete medical records and pathological results were obtained. (4) Patients who did not achieve pathological complete response (pCR) after neoadjuvant chemotherapy.36 Furthermore, breast cancer tissues from 44 patients prior to neoadjuvant chemotherapy were collected between June 2022 and December 2023 to examine the association between AC008406.3 expression levels and the chemotherapy response, applying the inclusion criteria (1)–(3) above.

Bioinformatics analysis

Transcriptome and clinical data were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://cancergenome.nih.gov/). A total of 1,168 cases were used to compare gene expression profiles between cancerous and normal tissues. After merging the transcriptome data with the clinical data, a total of 1,039 cases were used for survival analysis. The molecular subtype data were obtained from the TCGA clinical dataset on the UCSC Xena platform (https://xena.ucsc.edu/). After removing “blank” and “normal-like” entries from the PAM50 RNA data,37 a total of 822 breast cancer samples were identified. Following integration with transcriptomic data, 789 samples were included in the molecular subtype-related analysis.

Cell culture

MDA-MB-231 (Cat. No. SCSP-5043), 293T (Cat. No. SCSP-502), and BT-549 (Cat. No. SCSP-5397) cell lines were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Shanghai Institute of Biochemistry and Cell Biology, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). The MCF-7 (Cat. No. iCell-h129) cell line was purchased from iCell Bioscience Inc. (Shanghai, China). All cell lines were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination using PCR-based assays and were confirmed to be mycoplasma-free. The three cell lines, MDA-MB-231, 293T, and MCF-7, were cultured in DMEM high-glucose medium (Hyclone, USA; SH30243.01), while the BT-549 cell line was cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Hyclone, USA; SH30027.01). All media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA; 10082147) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (100×) (Biosharp, Anhui, China; BL505A). The MCF-7 cell line was additionally supplemented with 0.01 mg/mL insulin (HTBT, Hefei, China). All cell lines were verified through short tandem repeat profiling.

Plasmids construction, transfection, and lentiviral infection

The full-length sequence of the AC008406.3 gene was obtained from LNCipedia v5.2 (https://lncipedia.org/). To obtain the overexpression plasmid for this gene, the full-length cDNA of human AC008406.3 was ligated into the enzyme-digested pLV3-CMV-EGFP-Puro plasmid (Sangon, Shanghai, China). Single guide RNAs (sgRNAs) targeting human AC008406.3 were synthesized and incorporated into the LentiCRISPR v2-Puro plasmid (Tsingke, Beijing, China). The sequences of the sgRNAs are listed in Table S2. ExFect Transfection Reagent (Vazyme, Nanjing, China; T101) was utilized to introduce the obtained plasmid or empty vector plasmid into the breast cancer cells. Transfection was carried out for a duration of 48 h.

The synthesized plasmid, VSV-G plasmid, and GAG plasmid were co-transfected into 293 T cells using polyethylenimine linear (PEI; APExBIO, Houston, TX, USA; K1029). The medium was refreshed after 12 h, and the supernatants containing the virus were collected at 48- and 72-h post-transfection. The supernatants were subjected to ultracentrifugation, and the pellet was resuspended in complete medium. Following quality assessment, the viral solution and 6 µg/mL of polybrene (General, Anhui, China; TA003) were added to fresh medium to infect breast cancer cells. The stable transfected cell lines were selected using puromycin (Invitrogen, Grand Island, NY, USA; A1113803).

Cell viability

The Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Beyotime, Shanghai, China; C0039) was utilized for the detection of cell viability. Cells seeded in 96-well plates were subjected to appropriate treatments. The culture medium was replaced with a working solution containing complete medium and CCK-8 solution at a 9:1 ratio. The culture plate was incubated at 37 °C for 1 h, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Molecular Devices, Shanghai, China).

Cell proliferation, migration, and invasion assay

Cell proliferation was assessed using the CCK-8 and colony formation assays. For both assays, the cells were counted and seeded at equal densities. In the CCK-8 assay, cells were seeded in a 96-well plate. In the colony formation assay, cells were seeded in a 6-well plate. The culture medium was refreshed every 2–3 d. After 1–2 weeks, colony formation in different groups was assessed simultaneously following fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde (Servicebio, Wuhan, China; G1101) and staining with crystal violet (Servicebio, Wuhan, China; G1014).

Cell migration was assessed using the wound healing and Transwell assays. For the wound healing assay, a uniform-width scratch was made vertically in each well after cells reached confluence. Cell migration was observed and photographed. For the Transwell assay, cells suspended in serum-free medium were placed in the upper chamber, while the lower chamber contained complete medium. After 24 or 48 h, the underside of the upper chamber was stained with crystal violet to assess cell migration.

To assess cell invasion, the upper chamber was pre-coated with 60 µl of Matrigel (Corning, NY, USA; 354234), and the incubation time was extended to 48 or 72 h. The remaining procedures were conducted as in the Transwell migration assay. In the Transwell migration and invasion assays, three fields of view were randomly selected for cell counting to perform statistical analysis.

Measurement of intracellular copper

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates, and when the confluency reached approximately 70%, appropriate drug treatments were applied to the experimental and control groups and incubated for 24 h. Following the instructions provided in the Cell Copper (Cu2+) Colorimetric Assay Kit (Elabscience, Wuhan, China; E-BC-K775-M), the cells were lysed and subjected to low-speed centrifugation. The intracellular copper concentrations were then quantified using a microplate reader.

Flow cytometry

Cell apoptosis was measured using the Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (Meilunbio, Dalian, China; MA0220). Specifically, the cells were washed three times with pre-cooled binding buffer, then stained with Annexin V-FITC dye for 15 min, followed by PI dye for 5 min. Flow cytometry (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) analysis was conducted within 1 h.

Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

For sample preparation, tissue samples were cut into appropriate sizes and thoroughly homogenized in lysis buffer, while cells were directly lysed in lysis buffer after removing the culture medium. Total RNA was extracted from the tissue or cells according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Accurate Biology, Hunan, China; AG21017). RNA was then reverse transcribed into cDNA using a Reverse Transcription Kit (Accurate Biology, Hunan, China; AG11705). The reaction system, containing Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China; Q712) and primers, was subjected to a RT-qPCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) for amplification cycles. The expression levels of target genes were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences are listed in Table S3.

Western blotting

The cells were lysed using RIPA lysis buffer (Biosharp, Anhui, China; BL504A). Following centrifugation, the supernatant containing the protein sample was collected for immunoblot analysis. Total proteins were separated by SDS–PAGE gels and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany; IPFL00010). The membranes were incubated in blocking solution, followed by overnight incubation with the primary antibodies at 4 °C. The next day, the membranes were incubated with the secondary antibodies and washed three times with TBST solution. Band visualization was performed using the ChemiDoc XRS+ system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

The tissue samples were cut into appropriate sizes and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. The paraffin-embedded tissue was sectioned into 5 µm thick slices. The slices were subjected to deparaffinization and hydration, followed by antigen retrieval. After blocking with goat serum, the sections were incubated with primary and secondary antibodies. Staining was developed using DAB solution (Servicebio, Wuhan, China; G1212), and after counterstaining and mounting, the sections were observed under a microscope. Cytoplasmic immunohistochemical staining was scored based on two criteria, the staining intensity and percentage of positive cells, both of which were graded on a 4-point scale.38 The final score was obtained by multiplying the two individual scores. Nuclear immunohistochemical staining was scored by calculating the number of positive cells. Three random fields of view were selected for statistical analysis.

The preliminary steps for H&E staining were the same as those for IHC. The hydrated sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (Servicebio, Wuhan, China; G1005), which rendered the nuclei blue-purple and the cytoplasm pink. After mounting, the sections were imaged under a microscope.

In situ hybridization (ISH)

According to the instructions provided by the ISH kit (Boster, Wuhan, China; MK1030), the digoxigenin-labeled probe hybridization solution was diluted to a concentration of 2 μg/mL. After deparaffinization and nucleic acid denaturation, the tissue samples were hybridized with the probe solution. Following hybridization, a biotinylated anti-digoxigenin antibody was applied, and staining was performed using DAB. After nuclear counterstaining, the samples were mounted and observed under a microscope (Olympus, Japan). The probe sequence is shown in Table S4. The staining score was evaluated in the same manner as IHC. The median score of the 44 cases was used as the cutoff value for defining high and low expression of AC008406.3. Values above the cutoff were classified as high expression, while values below the cutoff were classified as low expression. Furthermore, ROC analysis was performed to evaluate the predictive performance of AC008406.3 expression for chemotherapy response.

RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization (RNA-FISH)

A recombinant plasmid containing the probe template sequence was constructed. After cloning and linearizing the probe template, in vitro transcription was performed to synthesize RNA probes (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) for in situ hybridization. The probe sequence is listed in Table S5. After preparing and fixing the cell coverslips, efficient and complete hybridization with the probe was performed. Following nuclear staining, the coverslips were mounted and observed under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Transmission electron microscope (TEM)

The cell samples were fixed using a fixation solution and dehydrated through a graded ethanol series. The samples were then infiltrated and embedded in 812 embedding resin. Ultrathin sections were prepared, mounted on copper grids, and stained. Mitochondrial morphology was observed using TEM (Hitachi, Japan).

Xenograft assay

The animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (No. TJH-202403014). This study was conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines and institutional animal care and use standards, and the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals. Twenty-four four-week-old female BALB/c nude mice with comparable body weights were purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. To minimize potential confounders, the animals were housed under identical conditions. The animals were randomly divided into three groups using a computer-generated randomization sequence (n = 8 per group): LV-sgNT, LV-sgAC-1, and LV-sgAC-2. Specifically, MDA-MB-231 cells (5 × 106 cells in 0.2 mL PBS) infected with either the sgNT lentivirus or two distinct AC008406.3 knockdown lentiviruses were subcutaneously injected into the fourth mammary fat pad of the nude mice. All implantation procedures were performed under inhalational anesthesia using isoflurane (3%–4% induction and 1%–2% maintenance). After 3 weeks of tumor formation, half of the mice in each group were randomly selected for tumor collection and euthanized by gradual-fill CO₂ inhalation (30%–70% chamber volume per minute), followed by cervical dislocation to ensure death. The remaining mice received intraperitoneal injections of DTX (10 mg/kg) every 3 d for a total of 6 treatments. Mouse numbers were selected based on a prior study with this model.39 Following the treatment course, the remaining mice were euthanized by CO₂ inhalation, and the tumors were harvested. No animals or data points were excluded from the analysis, as no unexpected health issues or technical problems occurred during the experiments. Investigators were aware of group allocation during the conduct of the experiment, but outcome assessment and data analysis were performed in a blinded manner. The tumor growth in mice was monitored every 3 d, and the body weight and tumor volume were recorded. All harvested tumor tissues were weighed and photographed, then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for IHC and HE analysis. The formula for tumor volume calculation was: volume = length × width2 × 1/2.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software, R 4.4.1 software, and Origin 2021 software. During the selection process of lncRNAs, differential analysis was performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, while Pearson correlation analysis was employed to assess the linear relationship between two variables. Prognosis-related lncRNAs were identified using Lasso and Cox proportional hazards regression models. All data were presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) from at least three independent replicates. The Student’s t-test was employed to compare differences between two groups. The Kruskal‒Wallis test was used to assess differences among molecular subtypes. When two independent variables were present, a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was utilized for the comparison. The Kaplan‒Meier method and log-rank test were employed for survival analysis. The R packages “limma”, “survminer”, “survival”, “ggpubr”, “pROC”, and “ggplot2” were utilized in the analysis and visualization processes. Chi-square tests were used to analyze the correlation of categorical variables. All p-values were two-sided, and a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
