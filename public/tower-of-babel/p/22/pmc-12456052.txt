Homozygous FAT1 frameshift mutation linked to glomerulotubular nephropathy with impaired cell adhesion and Rap1 signaling
Authors: Yang Fei, Zhouhui Jin, Li He, Meng Zhang, Qiong Jiao, Qiye Liu, Fujun Lin, Wei Wang, Niansong Wang, Aili Cao, Dongsheng Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGenetic mutations are closely linked to various renal diseases, revealing important molecular mechanisms that contribute to kidney dysfunction. Here, we reported a 35-year-old Chinese female diagnosed of glomerulotubular nephropathy with multiple extra-renal manifestations including ptosis, corneal dystrophy, macular degeneration, right foot syndactyly. Whole-exome sequencing identified a homozygous frameshift variant in FAT1 (NM_005245: c.7444_7445delGT, p.Val2482AsnfsTer16). Further analysis revealed that this mutation caused translation repression of FAT1. RNA sequencing showed dysregulation of cell adhesion and Rap1 signaling pathways, while immunofluorescence staining demonstrated disrupted β-catenin junctions and cytoskeletal abnormalities in patient-derived primary urinary epithelial cells. Pull-down assays indicated that the reduction in activated Rap1 levels was correlated with the observed cellular defects. These findings provide compelling evidence that this loss-of-function homozygous FAT1 variant is causally associated with nephropathy and congenital anomalies, likely through degradation of transcribed mRNA and impaired protein expression. The results emphasize the critical role of FAT1 in renal development and provide new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying these conditions.Keywords: Cell adhesion, congenital anomalies, FAT1, nephropathy, urinary epithelial cells

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) affects over 10% of adults worldwide and represents a significant public health burden [1]. Glomerular and tubular injuries, particularly primary forms, are common causes of CKD and end-stage renal disease (ESRD) [2]. Assessment of kidney function using eGFR, urine microscopy, and proteinuria quantification is critical for diagnosing and evaluation of glomerular disease. Current treatment aims to manage complications (edema, hypertension, proteinuria, hyperlipidemia, hypercoagulability, and thrombosis) and to target the underlying cause of glomerular disease [2]. Diagnosis and individualized therapy mainly rely on renal biopsy results.

Genetic factors can also affect the onset and progression of kidney diseases [3]. It has been increasingly recognizable that hereditary kidney diseases constitute a major subset of CKD. Advances in whole-exome sequencing (WES) have revolutionized the molecular diagnosis of hereditary kidney diseases in pediatric and adult populations, enhancing diagnostic accuracy and facilitating the discovery of novel genotype-phenotype correlations [4–6]. Identification of pathogenic variants in known and novel genes provides key insights into renal pathophysiology, particularly in monogenic diseases where specific mutations shape clinical manifestations and treatment responses [7–9].

Proteins implicated in hereditary nephropathies often exhibit diverse molecular functions and expression patterns, which can lead to pleiotropic effects across multiple organ systems [10]. This complexity is a hallmark of monogenic kidney disorders, where mutations in single genes may give rise to both renal and extrarenal manifestations [11]. For instance, syndromic nephropathies may affect the eyes, ears, or cardiovascular system, reflecting the wide functional scope of the implicated proteins [12,13]. Understanding their functions within and beyond the kidney is vital to unraveling disease mechanisms and developing targeted therapies.

FAT1 (FAT atypical cadherin 1) is the human ortholog of the Drosophila fat gene and is located on chromosome 4q35.2. It encodes a large mRNA transcript that translates into a 4,588-amino acid transmembrane protein composed of 34 cadherin repeats, five EGF-like domains, a laminin G-like domain, and a single transmembrane region. Fat1 is broadly expressed, with particularly high levels in the kidney, and localizes to intercellular junctions and the slit diaphragm of podocytes [14,15]. Importantly, FAT1 undergoes regulated proteolytic cleavage, releasing an intracellular domain that can translocate to the nucleus and modulate transcriptional activity [16].

Functionally, FAT1 plays critical roles in cell adhesion, planar cell polarity, and cytoskeletal organization, contributing to apical-basal polarity in epithelial cells [17–19]. It interacts with core components of the Wnt/β-catenin and Hippo signaling pathways, thereby influencing cell migration, polarity, and proliferation [16,20]. Both human and animal studies have shown that FAT1 deficiency leads to glomerular malformation and renal dysfunction, highlighting its essential role in kidney development and homeostasis [14,15]. Thus, it is critical to investigate the molecular and cellular consequences of its mutations.

Here, we report a patient from a consanguineous family presenting congenital malformations and glomerulotubular nephropathy caused by a homozygous pathogenic FAT1 variant. Through this case and mechanistic studies, we aim to deepen the understanding of FAT1-related disease and inform future therapeutic strategies targeting Fat1 dysfunction.

Methods

Whole-exome sequencing (WES) and Sanger sequencing

The study was approved by the ethics committee of Xinhua Hospital Affiliated to Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, Shanghai, China (XHEC-C-2024-185-2). Written informed consent was obtained from their parents and control subjects.

Genomic DNA was isolated from peripheral blood using the QIAamp DNA Midi Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Whole-exome capture was performed on both patients using the SureSelect Human All Exon 50 Mb Kit (Agilent, CA, USA). Paired-end sequencing was carried out on the Genome Analyzer IIx (Illumina, CA, USA), and reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg19) using Novoalign (Novocraft, 2010) [21].

Variant calling was conducted following Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) best practices, including base quality score recalibration (BQSR), indel realignment, and variant quality score recalibration (VQSR). Identified variants were functionally annotated with ANNOVAR, and quality control steps included filtering for read depth ≥10, variant quality score ≥30, and allele balance >0.3 [22–24]. To prioritize candidate variants, we excluded synonymous and common variants using public databases, including 1,000 Genomes Project, and the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD), as well as our in-house exome dataset derived from over 200 ethnically matched controls.

We focused on variants associated with renal conditions, including renal impairment, proteinuria, and abnormal kidney development, based on public databases like PubMed, OMIM, and HGMD. The FAT1 (NM_005245: c.7444_7445delGT, p.Val2482AsnfsTer16) variant was validated by Sanger sequencing using an ABI PRISM 3730xl Genetic Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, CA, USA). The primer sequences were: FAT1 forward 5′-AGTTGCAGTATTCCATTCTGTCTGG-3′ and reverse 5′-CACCTCCATCACCAGGGTATGTAGG-3′.

Ophthalmologic examination

The ophthalmologic examination included refraction, visual acuity assessment, funduscopy with dilated pupils, fundus photography, corneal topography, multifocal electroretinography, cycloplegic refraction, and an orthoptic evaluation.

Protein structure prediction

The three-dimensional structure of wild-type and mutant FAT1 was predicted by AlphaFold Server (https://alphafoldserver.com). The FAT1 (c.7444_7445delGT, p.Val2482AsnfsTer16) mutant harbors a two-nucleotide deletion (guanine at position 7444 and thymine at 7445) within the coding region of the FAT1 gene, causing a frameshift and altering the downstream open reading frame. As a result, the mutant protein is identical to the wild-type FAT1 up to amino acid residue 2481 (threonine). From residue 2482 onward, the amino acid sequence is completely altered, generating a novel C-terminal peptide (∼NWRQFAQSCFPSERI) that terminates prematurely due to a stop codon. All native C-terminal residues beyond position 2482 in the wild-type protein are thus absent in the mutant.

Based on this altered reading frame, the corresponding nucleotide sequence was translated to derive the mutant-specific peptide, which was appended to the N-terminal portion of wild-type FAT1 (residues 1–2481) to reconstruct the full-length mutant protein. The resulting chimeric sequence was submitted to the AlphaFold Server for three-dimensional structural prediction of the frameshifted FAT1 variant.

The pLDDT scores of the structure of wild-type and mutant are basically above 70. pLDDT is a per-residue confidence score estimate on a 0–100 scale where a higher value indicates higher confidence. In addition, the pTM score of FAT1(1–4181) is 0.39, and FAT1_mut is 0.5. The predicted template modeling (pTM) is derived from a measure called the template modeling (TM) score, which means the predicted accuracy of the entire structure [25,26]. Structural diagrams were prepared by PyMOL.

Isolation and culture of urine-derived primary renal epithelial cells

The procedure for isolating primary urinary epithelial cells was based on a previously established protocol [27,28]. Epithelial cells derived from the patient’s urine and four healthy controls were cultured. Briefly, urine samples were centrifuged at 400×g for 10 min at room temperature. The cell pellets were rinsed with PBS containing penicillin, streptomycin, and amphotericin B, then spun again at 200 g for 10 min. The pellets were resuspended in 2 mL of DMEM-F12 (Sigma-Aldrich, MA, USA), containing 10% fetal bovine serum, antibiotics, and renal epithelial cell growth supplements (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland). This suspension was seeded onto a gelatin-coated 12-well plate and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Colonies appeared in 4–5 days and were switched to a proliferation medium (a 1:1 mix of renal epithelial and mesenchymal cell media). Once cells reached 70–80% confluence, they were passaged using TrypsinLE (Gibco, MA, USA).

RNA-seq

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA purity and quantification were evaluated using the NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). RNA integrity was assessed using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Libraries were prepared and sequenced by OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The data have been deposited with the Gene Expression Omnibus and are accessible through accession number GSE282237.

Immunofluorescence staining of cells

Cultured cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS for 10 min, followed by washing with PBS and permeabilization/blocking in 0.5% BSA, 0.5% Tween, 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS. Cells were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibody. After PBS washes, cells were treated for 1 h at room temperature with Alexa 488-conjugated secondary antibodies, for F-actin staining, Phalloidin (ab176757, Abcam, Cambridge, UK). Following additional PBST washes, the cells were mounted using ProLongTM Gold Antifade Mountant with DAPI (P36931, Invitrogen, MA, USA). Primary antibodies included FAT1 (bs-4278R, Bioss, Beijing, China), β-catenin (ab32572, Abcam, Cambridge, UK). Imaging was performed using Olympus microscopes.

Immunohistochemistry

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue sections (4 μm) were incubated with 3% H2O2 for 10 min at room temperature to quench endogenous peroxidase activity. Antigen retrieval was performed in 0.01 M citrate buffer (pH 6.0) using a microwave for 10 min. The sections were blocked with 2.5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated overnight with the FAT1 antibody (bs-4278R, Bioss, Beijing, China) at 4 °C. After applying the secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature, antigen visualization was performed using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB), followed by counterstaining with hematoxylin.

Rap1 activation assay

Rap1-GTP levels were measured from lysates of patient- and healthy control-derived urine renal epithelial cells using a GST-RalGDS fusion protein, as previously described [29,30], following the manufacturer’s instructions (16120, ThermoFisher, MA, USA).

Western blotting

For Western blot analysis, cells were lysed with RIPA buffer (P0013B, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors (P1046, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). After centrifugation at 12,000×g, the supernatant was collected. Protein concentration was determined using the BCA assay kit (23225, ThermoFisher, MA, USA). Equal amounts of protein (40 µg per well) were separated by SDS–PAGE on 4–12% YoungPageTM gels (M01004, Genescript, Nanjing, China), transferred to PVDF membranes, and incubated with primary antibodies against FAT1 (30733-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), Rap1 (HA721335, Huabio, Hangzhou, China), and GAPDH (10494-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) for normalization. Membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies for 45 min at room temperature, washed, and scanned using the Bio-OI OI900 infrared scanner. The results were analyzed with ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA). Data are expressed as mean ± SD, and an unpaired t-test was used to compare the two groups. Prior to the t-test, an F test was performed to assess the equality of variances. A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical presentation

The patient was a 35-year-old Chinese woman who had been experiencing proteinuria for 5 years at the time of presentation. Notably, her parents are cousins (Figure 1A). At 18 years old, she underwent surgery for bilateral ptosis. She was diagnosed with acute pyelonephritis at ages 26 and 33, both episodes resolved by antibiotic treatment. The patient had no history of hypertension, diabetes, or a family history of renal disease. The patient’s baseline clinical characteristics were shown in Table 1. Laboratory evaluations indicated normal renal function, with microscopic hematuria (urine RBC 56/µL) and proteinuria (24-h protein excretion of 0.81 g, urine albumin-creatinine ratio (UACR) of 537.5 mg/g). All autoantibody tests were negative, and mild hypothyroidism was detected (FT4 10 pmol/L, TSH 4.73 mIU/L).

Considering her consanguineous family history, clinical features of glomerulotubular nephropathy and multisystemic involvements, we conducted WES on the patient and a novel homozygous frameshift variant (c.7444_7445delGT, p.Val2482AsnfsTer16) in exon 10 of the FAT1 gene was identified. Sanger sequencing confirmed the presence of the variant in heterozygous form in both the patient’s parents and the patient’s son, all clinically unaffected (Figure 1B). This variant was not found in public databases, including ESP, 1000 Genomes, ExAC, or ClinVar/HGMD.

Meanwhile, due to the persistent albuminuria and hematuria, a renal biopsy was performed afterwards. Light microscopy revealed hyalinization of one glomerulus with mild mesangial proliferation, with about 10% interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy. Immunofluorescence staining was negative (Figure 1C). Electron microscopy revealed 40%∼50% foot process effacement with slightly thinning of the glomerular basement membrane (GBM width between 150 and 250 nm) (Figure 1D).

In addition, we also noticed extra-renal abnormality in the patient. Dysmorphic facial features included arched eyebrows and an elongated philtrum (Figure 1E). Ocular examination showed myopia (Left: 0.5; Right: 0.6), normal optic axis, and corneal diameter (Figure 1E). Fundus photography identified macular degeneration and Orbscan showed corneal dystrophy in both eyes. Additional physical examination revealed right foot syndactyly (toes 2 and 3 on the right) (Figure 1F). Hearing tests revealed no loss of function.

The patient was then treated with a renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) inhibitor, Losartan, starting at 50 mg per day, which was increased to 100 mg per day under stable blood pressure. After 1 year of treatment, a partial remission in proteinuria was observed, with 24-h urinary protein decreasing from 0.81 g to 0.57 g. Subsequently, the SGLT2 inhibitor, Dapagliflozin (10 mg per day), was added, leading to a reduction in proteinuria (UACR of 222 mg/g) after 1 month. The UACR further decreased to 71 mg/g with combined use of both ARB and SGLT2 inhibitors over the following 2 months. Changes in proteinuria were shown in Supplementary Figure S1.

Structural impact of the FAT1 p.Val2482AsnfsTer16 variant

A comprehensive understanding of the structural alterations induced by mutation in FAT1 gene is vital for elucidating their functional implications. Figure 2A (top) presents the amino acid sequence of wild-type FAT1 from position 2480 onwards, while Figure 2A (bottom) depicts the sequence of the FAT1 p.Val2482AsnfsTer16 mutant starting at the same position. The FAT1 p.Val2482AsnfsTer16 variant results from the deletion of guanine (G) at position 7444 and thymine (T) at position 7445, leading to a frameshift mutation. The mutant retains the same sequence as the wild-type from the start codon (M1) to position T2481. However, from position 2482 onwards, the mutant sequence (∼NWRQFAQSCFPSERI) diverges significantly from the wild-type, resulting in premature translation termination. Consequently, the mutant lacks all amino acids from position 2482 onwards.

To further investigate the structural implications of these mutations, AlphaFold was employed to predict the three-dimensional conformation of the extracellular region of wild-type FAT1 (amino acids 1–4181), with the structure color-coded from blue to red to represent positions 1 to 4181 (Figure 2B). The predicted model indicates that the extracellular portion predominantly comprises 34 cadherin repeats and EGF-like domains. In the case of the FAT1 p.Val2482AsnfsTer16 mutant (amino acids 1–2498), the three-dimensional structure reveals that the mutant preserves only the first 23 cadherin repeats near the N-terminus (Figure 2C). The remaining 11 cadherin-like repeats, EGF-like domains, transmembrane region, and the entire intracellular segment are completely absent (Figure 2D).

Differential FAT1 expression in renal epithelial cells and kidney tissue

We next evaluated changes in FAT1 protein expression in patient-derived renal epithelial cells and kidney tissue. As shown in Figure 3A, FAT1 expression was significantly reduced in patient-derived samples compared to healthy controls. Quantitative analysis (Figure 3B) confirmed a marked downregulation of FAT1 protein levels. Immunohistochemistry on renal biopsy tissues (Figure 3C) corroborated decreased Fat1 expression in the patient’s kidney.

Immunofluorescence staining (Figure 3D) revealed that in healthy individuals, FAT1 is predominantly localized to the cell membrane of urine-derived renal epithelial cells, with some presence in the nucleus. In contrast, in the patient carrying the FAT1 variant, membrane-associated FAT1 expression was completely absent, with staining restricted solely to the nucleus. These findings demonstrate consistent FAT1 downregulation in patient renal epithelial cells and kidney tissue, underscoring FAT1’s critical role in disease.

Transcriptomic analysis reveals key pathways altered by FAT1 mutation

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms affected by the FAT1 mutation, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed on patient-derived and healthy control-derived renal epithelial cells, with three technical replicates per group. Differential expression analysis using DESeq2 identified genes with adjusted p-values (Q values) < 0.05 and fold changes > 2 or < 0.5 as significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs). The volcano plot (Figure 4A, B) shows 2,139 genes significantly downregulated and 2,474 genes upregulated in FAT1 mutant cells compared to controls.

(B) Bar chart illustrating the number of DEGs between the FAT1 mutant and control groups. (C) Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis highlighting pathways affected by DEGs in the FAT1 mutant versus control groups. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis revealed significant alterations in focal adhesion, ECM-receptor interaction, and cell adhesion molecule pathways (Figure 4C). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) further showed enrichment of focal adhesion, cell junction, adherens junction, and cadherin binding pathways, highlighting their importance in cell-cell adhesion and organ development (Figure 4D).

FAT1 mutation disrupts β-catenin localization and Rap1 activation in renal epithelial cells

Both endogenous and exogenous FAT1 were found to interact with β-catenin, functioning as a tumor suppressor by inhibiting β-catenin-mediated transcription [18]. Consequently, we analyzed alterations in β-catenin in urine-derived renal epithelial cells harboring FAT1 p.Val2482AsnfsTer16. In normal control cells, β-catenin localized to both the nucleus and the cell membrane, with prominent expression at cell–cell junctions (indicated by white arrows). In contrast, FAT1 mutant cells exhibited a loss of β-catenin at these junctions and significantly reduced nuclear expression (Figure 5A). At cell–cell junctions, E-cadherin/β-catenin complexes play a crucial role in sensing and transmitting mechanical forces by linking to F-actin [31]. Immunostaining revealed disrupted recruitment of β-catenin to actin filaments in FAT1 mutant cells (Figure 5B). Rap1, known for its rapid and transient activation in response to chemoattractant stimuli, contributes to establishing cell polarity by locally modulating the cytoskeleton [29]. Pull-down assays using GST-RalGDS fusion proteins on lysates from starved renal epithelial cells showed that control cells had significantly higher GTP-bound Rap1 levels than mutant cells (Figure 5C, D).

(B) Immunofluorescence staining of F-actin (red) and DAPI (blue) in control and FAT1 mutant cells. Scale bar: 20 μm. (C) Pull-down assays of GTP-bound Rap1 show significantly lower activation in mutant cells compared to controls. Discussion

Our study is the first to identify a new mutation in the FAT1 gene associated with a syndrome characterized by ptosis, corneal dystrophy, macular degeneration, foot syndactyly, and glomerulotubular nephropathy in Asian population. The novel homozygous frameshift variant (NM_005245.4: c.7444_7445delGT, p.Val2482AsnfsTer16) has not been previously reported.

We confirmed significantly reduced FAT1 expression in patient-derived renal epithelial cells and kidney tissue. The loss of membrane-localized Fat1 disrupted key signaling pathways, including focal adhesion and ECM-receptor interaction, which are essential for β-catenin localization and Rap1 activation—both critical for maintaining cell–cell junctions and cytoskeletal organization. These disruptions underscore FAT1’s role in renal pathophysiology, particularly in structural and signaling maintenance. Interestingly, though the patient shares common features with other FAT1 mutation cases, such as bilateral ptosis and webbed toes, the renal phenotype varies widely and the ocular examination also shows little difference [32,33].

FAT1 mutations have garnered significant attention in oncology, being associated with a diverse array of cancers, including head and neck cancers [34], T-cell lymphoma [35], oral squamous cell carcinoma [35], and colorectal cancer [36]. Emerging evidence also links FAT1 mutations to non-cancerous conditions, such as facioscapulohumeral dystrophy-like phenotypes [37,38] and bipolar affective disorder [39], emphasizing the gene’s broader biological significance.

In nephrology, FAT1 mutations have been implicated in diverse renal disorders. Gee et al. [40] identified FAT1 variants in individuals with steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome. Subsequent studies, including those by Shojaei et al. [40] and Lahrouchi et al. [32], demonstrated that FAT1 knockout models exhibit significant developmental defects mirroring patient phenotypes, further establishing FAT1 as a pleiotropic gene with implications in both glomerular and tubular abnormalities. Most recently, Fabretti et al. [41] reported four different FAT1 mutations in three families with variable renal presentations. Our patient exhibited only microscopic hematuria, mild proteinuria, and stable renal function. This varied phenotype in renal injury may be attributed to compensatory signaling pathways or the effect of a single-site mutation.

Animal models have shown that podocyte-specific Fat1 deletion leads to glomerular barrier dysfunction and loss of foot processes [40,42]. Similarly, tubular-specific FAT1 deletion impairs cell migration and disrupts small GTPases such as RAC1 and CDC42, affecting lumen formation [40]. Our RNA-seq data further revealed that dysregulation of cell adhesion–related pathways were likely ascribed to the impaired FAT1 protein synthesis or stability supported by the markedly reduced FAT protein levels in patient-derived cells and kidney tissue.

FAT1 localizes to the plasma membrane, particularly in filopodia, lamellipodia, and cell–cell junctions [43] involved in the interaction with β-catenin [44] and regulation of Wnt signaling [45]. In our study, mutant FAT1 lost its ability to anchor β-catenin to the membrane, resulting in reduced junctional localization and finally likely contributes to ocular abnormalities, consistent with the hypothesis proposed by Lahrouchi et al. [32] While our patient’s ocular phenotype differs from previously reported cases, the underlying pathogenesis may similarly stem from impaired cell adhesion due to FAT1 dysfunction.

The precise mechanisms underlying renal injury in FAT1-related disease remain incompletely understood. Our KEGG analysis suggests that FAT1 mutations impair the Rap1 signaling pathway. Rap1 regulates integrin and cadherin function at the membrane, promoting adhesion, tissue integrity, and cell communication [29,46]. Observation on the inactivated Rap1 in mutant cells further supported the central role for defective adhesion signaling in the disease phenotype.

Although diffuse GBM thinning was observed on biopsy, the patient did not meet the criteria for thin basement membrane nephropathy (TBMN), which is often associated with collagen IV gene variants [47,48], none of which were found in our patient. Emerging studies have also linked FAT1 mutation to other forms of nephropathy, including de novo membranous nephropathy associated with antibody-mediated rejection [49]. However, the therapy for those patients is still a dilemma. Our patient has shown a favorable response to renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) inhibitors and SGLT2 inhibitors, suggesting that these treatments may also benefit hereditary kidney diseases to some extent.

This study has several limitations. It only described a single case, limiting generalizability. Further studies involving larger cohorts are required to validate our findings and explore phenotype variability. Although we utilized RNA-seq and protein modeling, lack of in vivo models for this specific mutation resulted in the failure of a comprehensive understanding on the mechanism. Moreover, while early treatment response was favorable, the long-term efficacy and safety of RAAS and SGLT2 inhibitors in FAT1-related nephropathy remained to be determined.

Conclusion

Our study provides compelling evidence that the novel homozygous frameshift FAT1 variant (p.Val2482AsnfsTer16) is associated with glomerulotubular nephropathy and congenital anomalies, enhancing our understanding of FAT1-related disorders and suggesting potential therapeutic targets. Future research should focus on developing transgenic models to explore the functional consequences of specific FAT1 mutations and their role in genetic kidney diseases and other chronic kidney conditions.
