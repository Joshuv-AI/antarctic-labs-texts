Introduction: Duchenne muscular dystrophy and the respiratory system

Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) is a genetic condition caused by mutations of the gene located on the X-chromosome that codes for the protein dystrophin. As a result of this X linkage, most people diagnosed with DMD are male, although some female carriers may be “manifesting” and may also develop significant symptoms [1]. Dystrophin is a protein that is essential for muscle fibre integrity and repair, and without it, skeletal muscles are progressively damaged over time, resulting in fibrosis and fat replacement [2]. DMD is the most common and severe form of muscular dystrophy in children and its prevalence is 19.8 per 100 000 male births worldwide [3]. Research and development of therapies aimed at restoring dystrophin function or compensating for dysfunction are ongoing [4]. However, there is currently no curative treatment for DMD.

Skeletal muscles are the first muscles to be noticeably affected: children may be slow to stand and walk [5, 6], and may demonstrate the classic Gowers' sign when standing up, i.e. they walk their hands up their own legs to stand. This is due to weakness in their gluteal and quadriceps muscles. Patients experience progressive muscle weakness and wasting, leading to a gradual decline in motor function. Historically, children with DMD tended to stop walking in early adolescence [5], although the advent of corticosteroids has slowed the rate of decline in muscle function and prolonged ambulation by 2–4 years [7].

The impact of DMD on respiratory function tends to become more noticeable after the loss of ambulation in adolescence and adulthood [8]. This respiratory system decline is largely due to intercostal and diaphragmatic muscle weakness, alongside a weakness in cough strength. Inspiratory muscle weakness initially causes nocturnal hypoventilation and contributes to sleep disordered breathing [9]. Expiratory muscle weakness can cause poor cough and an inability to clear respiratory secretions. There is development of scoliosis associated with the loss of ambulation [10]. Lateral displacement and rotation of the vertebral bodies can alter the mechanics of ventilation and movement of the associated ribs during respiration. The abnormal shape of the thoracic cavity can put the respiratory muscles at a mechanical disadvantage, and the organs contained within the thorax can be displaced and compressed. Respiratory reserve is considerably reduced and deteriorates further as the scoliosis progresses [11].

Respiratory complications contribute significantly to morbidity and unplanned hospitalisations, and are the second leading cause of mortality in DMD after cardiac failure [12]. Respiratory complications include respiratory muscle fatigue, mucus plugging, atelectasis, pneumonia and respiratory failure. Treatment of these complications should be aggressive and proactive in approach: best practice guidelines would suggest employment of techniques to maintain lung compliance, avoid sleep disordered breathing and manage respiratory secretions before respiratory decompensation becomes established [8]. More recent guidelines recommend introduction of these techniques at an earlier stage (and so patients are often younger), with the introduction of noninvasive ventilation (NIV) and assisted coughing at lower levels of respiratory impairment than previously recommended in earlier guidelines [8]. Physiotherapists, as part of the multidisciplinary team, are involved in commencing these techniques.

Normal airway clearance: mucociliary clearance and cough

The airway mucosa consists of three layers: the cellular layer containing ciliated and epithelial cells, a serous fluid layer within which cilia beat freely, and a mucus gel layer. Mucus is secreted by secretory epithelial cells (goblet cells and serous cells) and submucosal glands. Inhaled particles, microbes and cellular debris are caught in the mucus and wafted towards the larynx by the rhythmic and coordinated beating of the cilia [13]. Mucus is then swallowed or expectorated by coughing.

A normal cough consists of four stages [14]. The first is the receptorial phase, where there is stimulation of the cough receptors, which send an impulse to the brain via the vagus nerve. The second stage is the inspiratory phase, where the glottis opens with rapid inhalation due to contraction of the diaphragm and other inspiratory muscles. The third stage is the compressive stage and is very brief: there is glottic closure as well as a strong contraction of the abdominal muscles and other expiratory muscles to allow intrathoracic pressure to build up and compress the alveoli and bronchioles. The fourth stage consists of sudden glottic opening and compression of the intrathoracic airways to create an explosive air flow. Exhalation continues with relaxation of the diaphragm.

Airway clearance and cough in neuromuscular disease

In patients with DMD, it is reasonable to assume they have a normal mucociliary transport system. In general, normal mucociliary transport is enhanced by normal tidal volume breathing: intrathoracic airways are compressed during normal exhalation, which creates a proximal airflow that assists in the mobilisation of secretions proximally towards the larynx. However, in DMD, weakness of the respiratory muscles may result in a reduced tidal volume and a reduction in functional residual capacity, which may result in increased atelectasis and thus trapping of mucus. Lower expiratory airflow in patients with neuromuscular weakness may result in reduced movement of secretions towards the larynx [15]. The use of NIV to maintain normal tidal breathing may also help prevent secretion retention.

Impaired cough in neuromuscular disease is caused by multiple factors. Inspiratory muscle weakness reduces the ability to generate large inspiratory volumes. Glottic closure may be compromised due to bulbar weakness, and this can limit the ability to generate increased intrathoracic pressure. The incidence of bulbar muscle weakness in patients with DMD is unknown, but progressive pharyngeal muscle weakness and prolonged steroid exposure can lead to development of symptoms of obstructive sleep apnoea [16], and in the late ambulatory stage of the disease, dysphagia is increasingly common [17]. Expiratory muscle weakness affects both the ability to create an increased intrathoracic pressure and the expiratory airflow velocity [18].

Assessment of cough

Assessment of an individual's cough is essential to determine which airway clearance technique may be required to prevent a build-up of respiratory secretions. Chatwin and Wakeman [19] describe an approach to assessing a patient's cough starting with questioning the individual on whether they can clear secretions during a chest infection. It has been accepted that peak cough flow (PCF) rates can drop during a respiratory infection [20, 21], so it is important to question patients on whether they can manage their secretions during a cold or chest infection. Observation of the patient coughing is also important, to monitor inspiratory and expiratory muscle activity along with listening to the quality and sound of the cough: does the cough sound effective? Finally, measurement of PCF can give a more objective view on the strength of the cough. Measuring PCF is noninvasive and can be completed at the bedside or in the clinic. A face mask is attached to a paediatric peak flow meter, and the patient is asked to take a deep breath in. The mask is placed firmly over the nose and mouth and the patient is instructed to cough as hard as they can. This may be repeated as needed, and the highest reading achieved is considered their PCF.

A PCF of 360–1200 L·min−1 is considered a normal cough strength [20]. Adults require a PCF >160 L·min−1 for an effective cough [22], as measured for readiness for extubation. Adults with neuromuscular disease who have an assisted PCF <270 L·min−1 when well are considered to be at risk of retaining bronchial secretions and respiratory failure in case of bronchial infections [20, 21]. This is because it is accepted that during times of respiratory infection, PCF rates may drop [20, 23]. It is therefore recommended that additional strategies for cough augmentation for managing secretions in the event of a chest infection be put in place when assisted PCF drops below 270 L·min−1 [17]. Most episodes of respiratory failure in people with neuromuscular disease are likely to be caused by ineffective coughing during chest infections [24, 25].

Maximal insufflation capacity and breathstacking

Whereas forced vital capacity (FVC) measures the volume of air forcefully exhaled after a deep breath in, maximal insufflation capacity (MIC) measures the maximum volume of air that can be held in the lungs using breathstacking and a closed glottis [26]. Normal breathing consists of varying tidal volumes with intermittent deep breaths and sighs and this variety of breath can help to maintain ribcage mobility and lung compliance. In neuromuscular disease, inspiratory and expiratory muscle weakness can lead to reduced tidal volume breathing with few or no sighs or deep breaths. This can result in reduced expansion of the lungs and chest wall, which may lead to stiffening of the ribcage and reduced chest wall compliance [24]. FVC and MIC are reduced, and this can affect the strength and effectiveness of a patient's cough.

MIC can be measured using a spirometer: the patient takes a deep breath in, then breathstacks via breathing exercises, a manual resuscitator or ventilator to the maximum volume held with a closed glottis, then exhales into the spirometer to measure the volume of air exhaled. Studies have demonstrated that MIC can increase in patients with neuromuscular disease with breathstacking exercises [27] and be maintained in the presence of a decreasing FVC [26]. The positive effect of maximising MIC on PCF [28] ensures MIC must be considered when employing techniques to improve cough effectiveness.

One method of increasing insufflation to allow patients reach their MIC is through “breathstacking” or “air stacking”. This can be achieved mechanically using positive pressure ventilators and intermittent positive pressure breathing devices, with set pressure or volume limits and a face mask or mouthpiece [25, 26, 29]. The patient is instructed to take breaths regularly with the device to maintain lung compliance, or to fill their lungs to capacity to reach MIC and then remove the interface and cough (with or without manual assistance). Patients can also achieve breathstacking independently with breathing exercises and glottic closure, or with the use of an external self-inflating manual resuscitator bag with a one-way valve if needed to prevent an air leak [25]. The patient takes or receives multiple inspiratory breaths without exhaling between breaths, until they reach MIC [25, 29]. The mask is then removed and the person releases the breath as forcibly as they can, i.e. attempts to cough. This can be further augmented by the addition of a manually assisted cough.

A study comparing cough augmentation using air stacking with a manual resuscitator and a mechanical ventilator demonstrated no difference in cough effectiveness as measured by MIC and PCF [30]. The authors acknowledged the relatively inexpensive cost of the manual resuscitator, which may improve access to this adjunct in patients with DMD. Maintenance of lung compliance with breathstacking is essential to increase the effectiveness of cough [31]. It is also worth noting that by increasing the inspiratory volume alone, expiratory muscle strength can be assisted by optimising the length–tension relationship of the muscles. The large inspiratory reserve volume also increases the elastic recoil of the lungs, thereby increasing expiratory pressures and flows and improving cough effectiveness [31]. An analogy between maintaining function in limbs through range of motion exercises in patients with muscular weakness, and using breathstacking exercises to maintain lung and chest wall mobility in these patients is made by Kang and Bach [26]. This “routine” part of physical therapy is often overlooked in terms of respiratory care, despite the effect poor chest wall mechanics have on PCF rates and, potentially, morbidity.

Manually assisted coughing

Manually assisted coughing involves an abdominal thrust manoeuvre to increase the intra-abdominal and intrathoracic pressure, and upward movement of the diaphragm, to assist with expiratory airflow [18, 25]. Hands and/or forearms are placed below the ribcage and as the patient coughs, the carer performs a forceful inward and upward movement to enhance the expiratory airflow. Before the emergence of mechanical insufflation-exsufflation (MI-E), manually assisted coughing was the most commonly used expiratory assistance technique for patients with respiratory muscle weakness. Manually assisted cough manoeuvres can be combined with other inspiratory assistance techniques such as breathstacking, and have been shown to significantly enhance PCF [24, 30]. Manually assisted cough can also be combined with MI-E to increase PCF rates [32, 33].

Mechanical insufflation-exsufflation

MI-E devices are used to mechanically improve cough strength by increasing both inspiratory and expiratory airflows and therefore increasing PCF. MI-E devices deliver a preset positive pressure airflow into the airways to achieve maximum lung inflation (insufflation) before rapidly shifting into a preset negative pressure (exsufflation), thereby simulating a cough with high expiratory flow rates [15, 25]. This airflow movement mobilises secretions up towards the mouth until they are high enough to be expectorated or suctioned from the mouth or nasopharyngeal airway. There are various devices available on the market but all work in a similar way: the device is connected via tubing to an oronasal mask, mouthpiece or catheter mount and applied to the patient. These devices can be used in patients with and without artificial airways. Patients are instructed to allow the device to fill their lungs and then to cough if needed, when the device sucks the air out.

There are certain contraindications and precautions associated with MI-E [25, 34]. Contraindications include bullous emphysema, surgical emphysema, undrained pneumothorax, known susceptibility to pneumothorax, pneumomediastinum, recent barotrauma, known cardiac instability and tracheo-oesophageal fistula. Precautions include a history of previous pneumothorax, recent lobectomy/pneumonectomy, cardiovascular insufficiency, acute abdominal distention, poor patient cooperation and a pulmonary air leak.

In terms of initiating MI-E, both protocolised and individualised approaches can be used. There are two schools of thought in terms of pressures. Earlier studies advised pressures of +40 to −40 cmH2O be used [3, 35], and indeed more recently Bach and Choi [36] encouraged higher settings such as insufflation of 50–60 cmH2O and exsufflation of −50 to −60 cmH2O, as these pressures are more closely associated with the original Cof-flator device (OEM Co., Hartford, CT, USA), which was initially introduced in the USA. Alternatively, a more individualised approach has been suggested [19], as greater exsufflation pressures than insufflation pressures, together with a shorter insufflation time than exsufflation time, have been associated with improved efficacy of MI-E [37]. PCF should continue to be measured, although it is noted that the PCF measured on the MI-E devices may not be accurate but can be used to establish a trend [25].

MI-E has been shown to be effective in avoiding hospitalisation and preventing respiratory failure during chest infections, and patients are advised to increase usage of it during “chest colds” [34]. The optimum treatment “dose” has not been established with MI-E. Bach et al. [20] have recommended a protocol regarding its use, relying on factors such as the presence of secretions and a reduction in oxygen saturation levels to <95%. However, other studies have found that while MI-E has been recommended by therapists for use twice a day, in reality patients are using it when there is the presence of increased secretion burden [19]. Education of patients and their caregivers is essential to instil routine daily use, knowing the effect MI-E can have in terms of chest wall mechanics as well as airway clearance.

Noninvasive ventilation

The use of NIV in conjunction with adequate airway clearance prolongs survival in patients with DMD [38, 39]. While it is beyond the scope of this review to explore the full role of NIV in this patient population, it is important to consider the impact NIV has on airway clearance. Nocturnal assisted ventilation should be introduced when patients develop symptoms of hypoventilation or sleep disordered breathing [40]. A non-exhaustive list of symptoms includes fatigue, shortness of breath, morning headaches, frequent nocturnal wakening or difficulty waking in the morning, difficulty concentrating, and frequent wakening with tachypnoea or tachycardia. As some patients may not report any symptoms despite having clinical signs of nocturnal hypoventilation, it is generally recommended to introduce nocturnal NIV when FVC drops below 50% predicted [8].

With declining lung function, patients can develop symptoms of hypoventilation despite using NIV nocturnally. They may start to extend their use of NIV into daytime hours up to 24 h·day−1. Along with devices with adequate battery life, a variety of interfaces may improve patient comfort and adherence with therapy. Oronasal masks, nasal masks and nasal pillows, as well as mouthpiece attachments, can allow patients to increase their use of NIV while continuing to participate in their daily lives and activities [41, 42].

One of the aims of NIV in this patient population is to improve ventilation through correction of hypoventilation [16]. This improved airflow through poorly ventilated airways and areas of microatelectasis can help to prevent small amounts of sputum retention by maximising the natural mucociliary escalator. This, along with airway clearance with assisted coughing, can help to prevent secretions accumulating.

Exercise and respiratory muscle training

There is no clear evidence for the routine use of specific respiratory muscle training in DMD [35, 43]. The effects of exercise on muscle degeneration in dystrophinopathies, although not fully understood, can include damage to the structural fragility of muscles, metabolic abnormalities contributing to ischaemia during exercise and reduced exercise capacity [8]. It is generally accepted that eccentric muscle activity and high intensity resistance training should be avoided [44]. However, submaximal aerobic exercise or activity has been recommended, especially early in the course of the disease, avoiding over-exertion and overwork and allowing adequate rest [8].

Conclusion

Respiratory dysfunction in patients with DMD is inevitable as the disease progresses, due to both inspiratory and expiratory muscle weakness, alongside structural changes such as scoliosis, becoming more pronounced after loss of ambulation. A full respiratory assessment should be completed regularly, with objective measurements of PCF and MIC taken, alongside a full subjective history of recent chest infections and the ability to manage respiratory secretions. This will help to identify problems and allow the early implementation of strategies to help slow, or at least ameliorate, the decline in respiratory muscle function. The role of physiotherapy is largely proactive and anticipatory in nature. Strategies to improve cough effectiveness, such as breathstacking, assisted coughing or MI-E, should be employed when assisted PCF falls below 270 L·min−1. NIV should be considered when there are signs of nocturnal hypoventilation or, in the absence of these, when FVC drops below 50% predicted.

Developing a positive relationship between the patient and the respiratory equipment (ventilators, cough assist devices, equipment for breathing exercises) is imperative, along with that between the patient and the multidisciplinary team, to address respiratory issues as they arise.
