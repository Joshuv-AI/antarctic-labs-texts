Lighting the path: a narrative review of non-molecular intraoperative lung imaging modalities
Authors: Tyler Lunow-Luke, Josephine E. Chang, Robert Painter, Carlin Lee, Ronak Naik, Johnathan Tran, Chris Campbell, Gordon T. Kennedy, Anthony J. Durkin, Hari B. Keshava
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectiveCurrent protocols lack validated non-destructive modalities to intraoperatively evaluate lung tissue without excision and histological processing, a critical step for confirming negative margins during cancer resections. Surgeons currently rely on preoperative radiologic imaging and intraoperative tactile feedback. Consequently, there is a persistent need to improve intraoperative identification and visualization of lung pathologies, including nodules, ground-glass opacities, and their margins relative to healthy parenchyma. Optical imaging modalities have emerged as promising tools for intraoperative lung visualization. While molecular imaging approaches have been extensively reviewed, non-molecular intraoperative optical modalities remain comparatively underrepresented. This narrative review summarizes recent clinical developments in non-molecular optical imaging of lung tissue and compares them with contrast-dependent approaches. Inclusion and exclusion criteria focused the scope on primary literature reporting intraoperative optical imaging of lung parenchyma or bronchial tissues between 2008 and 2025. Imaging modalities were evaluated based on their ability to visualize lung tissue and discriminate healthy from abnormal regions.MethodsA PubMed search (2008–2025) identified English-language studies on intraoperative optical imaging of lung parenchyma using relevant spectroscopy-related terms. Results were screened using predefined criteria and independently reviewed for reliability. Studies not focused on lung parenchyma were excluded.Key Content and FindingsMolecular imaging techniques included indocyanine green (ICG)-based imaging, with and without targeted probes, as well as confocal laser endomicroscopy (CLE). Exogenous contrast-dependent modalities demonstrate efficacy in lung surgery, enabling identification of nodules and intersegmental planes. However, these approaches are limited by contrast spillover into the operative field, dye-associated adverse reactions, narrow imaging time windows, and reliance on surrogate biomarkers that provide limited quantitative information. In contrast, non-molecular imaging modalities operate independently of exogenous agents and directly measure intrinsic optical properties of tissue. Their limitations include shallow tissue penetrance and challenges associated with analyzing large spectral data sets. Machine learning (ML) techniques may enhance both molecular and non-molecular imaging by improving image processing, enabling data integration, and supporting complementary multimodal imaging strategies.ConclusionsNon-molecular intraoperative imaging modalities are clinically feasible and safe. They enable differentiation of pathological and normal lung tissue while assessing physiologic properties without contrast agents, thereby avoiding spill-over and time constraints. These technologies offer to enhance intraoperative visualization beyond preoperative imaging and tactile assessment. With refinement and validation, non-molecular optical imaging may reduce dependence on histopathology, improve intraoperative decision-making, and enhance surgical outcomes.

Introduction

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer mortality in the United States, largely attributable to poor screening efforts and symptomatic presentation late in the disease progression (1). Cancer staging is primarily performed with pre-operative imaging including chest X-ray, computed tomography (CT), and positron emission tomography (PET) (2). Surgical resection remains the primary treatment option for early-stage non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), highlighting the importance of improving cancer detection and negative-margin confirmation. Pathological assessment of excised tissue is the current recommendation for evaluating abnormal lung tissue and resection margins per the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines (3).

During resection of lung masses and nodules, location and surgical margins can be difficult to assess. Surgeons utilize techniques for intraoperative localization in the lungs including referencing pre-operative imaging, palpation, fluorescent dyes, and molecular probes. However, current protocols lack validated non-destructive modalities to evaluate lung tissue intraoperatively and in real-time, to assess histology without excision. Technological advancements in light-based molecular and non-molecular imaging techniques have shown potential to aid in nodule and mass detection, ultimately aiding surgical decision-making (4-7).

In the field of optical imaging, “molecular imaging” techniques use exogenous molecular compounds with specific known optical signatures, such as indocyanine green (ICG) (8-10), γ-glutamyl hydroxymethyl rhodamine green (gGlu-HMRG) (11), and glutamine-alanine-2-methyl silicon rhodamine (QA-2MeSiR) (12) which allow visualization of a specific region of tissue. The optical marking compound can be linked to a molecular probe with properties specific to the physiology of target cellular tissue to increase the specificity of the image signal. Through this strategy, molecular imaging techniques can help identify lung nodules that express certain receptors or cellular products (5,13,14). However, this usually requires instillation of an optical compound, which has several limitations for intraoperative use, including obscuring the surgical field and requiring pre-treatment with the visualization compound (15).

In contrast, “non-molecular” optical imaging techniques directly visualize cellular structures without exogenous contrast dye by taking advantage of the inherent optical properties of the target tissue. Specific wavelengths of light with known biophysical interactions are emitted onto the target tissue, permitting various absorption, emission, and reflectance spectra to be measured. The measured spectra and optical properties vary by technique depending on the technology and properties of the tissue (16). While non-molecular modalities may still measure optical properties of molecules intrinsic to the target tissue, they do not use an exogenous targeted contrast agents for primary visualization. For example, Wang et al. estimated the extracellular matrix (ECM) structure of mammary adenocarcinoma by generating an optical harmonic frequency [second harmonic generation (SHG)] from collagen fibers in the ECM, demonstrating that structural properties of living tissues can be visualized without a probe (17). This approach has the potential to similarly aid with lung nodule detection, without the requirement of injecting any compounds.

Previous review papers (5,7) have summarized advances in molecular imaging techniques to visualize lung pathology, but a summary of non-molecular intraoperative techniques remains sparse in the literature. This review categorizes and evaluates recent developments in non-molecular lung imaging modalities and compares them to contrast-dependent imaging strategies, providing an assessment of current intraoperative imaging technological capabilities in thoracic surgery. We present this article in accordance with the Narrative Review reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2025-aw-2259/rc).

Methods

A comprehensive search of the PubMed database was reviewed for primary literature published electronically between 2008 and 2025 for developments in intraoperative lung imaging using optical light-based modalities. Specific search strategy included primary literature published in English language, with the key terms ‘lung’, ‘intraoperative’, ‘thoracic surgery’, ‘optical spectroscopy’, and ‘infrared spectroscopy’. Search terms were chosen by the authors for their prevalence and relevance to prior literature reporting intraoperative optical imaging of lung parenchyma. Seven permutations of the search terms were queried in PubMed to thoroughly review the published literature by two independent investigators (Table S1). Search results were assessed based on pre-established inclusion and exclusion criteria (Table 1) and evaluated for their reliability by independent review of three investigators. Papers primarily reporting optical imaging of non-parenchymal lung and thoracic tissues were excluded to focus the review on the landscape of current imaging strategies that are being investigated in lung parenchyma and associated surgical procedures. The findings of each study were summarized and categorized by visualization technique and primary tissue type (Table 2).

Results

The search strategy yielded a total of 1,795 search results, with 55 publications satisfying the inclusion and exclusion criteria. These were then categorized based on specific imaging strategies utilized (Table 2). Of the 55 publications that met the inclusion/exclusion criteria, 33 described exogenous contrast agent-dependent molecular lung imaging strategies. Twenty-two of the included papers were classified as non-molecular lung imaging modalities, which do not rely on exogenous contrast dyes for visualization.

Discussion

Contrast agent-dependent imaging strategies

ICG

The first development in this method was the use of ICG fluorescent dye (18,19). The use of fluorescent dyes such as ICG, fluorescein, methylene blue, 5-aminolevulinic acid (5-ALA) have been widely adopted in other surgical subspecialties such as colorectal surgery, esophageal surgery, emergency general surgery, and surgical oncology (20,21). However, this approach has only recently been explored in intraoperative lung imaging (22).

ICG was initially approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 1959 and has since been cleared for usage in various fluorescent imaging systems through the [510(k)] application (23,24). The initial FDA clearance of ICG included identification of lymphatic pathways for tumor resection, visualization of biliary vessels and blood flow. Imaging with ICG is possible using near-infrared (NIR) wavelengths, with a peak absorption of 800 to 810 nm and peak emission of 835 nm, which are safe in human tissues (23,25). An advantage of ICG and other NIR fluorophores over radiation-based imaging such as CT and traditional X-ray technology is the absence of high-energy radiation, which contributes to cumulative risk of developing malignancy. ICG has become an attractive method of intraoperative imaging because of its affinity for intravascular proteins, such as albumin, which helps it stay in circulation until it is metabolized through hepatobiliary processes (15). This property allows surgeons to visualize lumened structures which contrasts with the surrounding parenchymal structures, ultimately aiding in surgical decision making in a variety of contexts. ICG has also been demonstrated to be safe within the context of pulmonary surgeries in a pediatric population (26).

Gilmore et al. described the use of ICG in 2013 in their attempt to utilize ICG to identify tumor lymph node drainage in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) stage I/II patients (19). This dose escalation study demonstrated safety in an intraoperative thoracic surgery setting in addition to exploring a potential use of the imaging strategy. Subsequent developments of ICG-based lung imaging include mapping intersegmental lung borders during segmentectomy and visualizing tumor margins (27-29). All these applications take advantage of the same imaging principle of injecting ICG into vascular or lymphatic circulation. The next major development in ICG imaging departed from vascular-based strategies and investigated tagging parenchymal lung tissue with ICG contrast agent. In 2017, Hachey et al. injected ICG into lung nodules during biopsy to map the lymph node drainage from the lesion of interest (30). Diffusion of ICG to sentinel lymph nodes could be visualized by NIR fluorescence during video-assisted thoracic surgery (VATS) wedge resection. Building on this strategy, Sun et al. injected ICG into lung vasculature after VATS segmentectomy to visualize positive lung tumor margins, which appear as ICG “hotspots” due to increased vascular density (31). Pre-operative injection of ICG directly into lung nodules has also been explored by placing an ICG “tattoo” under CT-guided needle placement, allowing for precise ICG delivery to a known nodule location (32).

Inhaled-ICG has also been explored as a delivery mode to identify pulmonary nodules for resection (33). When nebulized, ICG preferentially distributes to non-pathologic lung parenchyma, allowing for visualization of tumor margins and intersegmental planes (34,35). The ability to visualize intersegmental planes has also demonstrated to be safe and useful in pediatric patients undergoing surgery for congenital pulmonary airway malformation (36-38).

While ICG offers a safe and relatively simple method of visualizing lung tissue, there are several disadvantages limiting its widespread adoption. ICG has the potential to spill out of its intended target and obscure anatomical features, complicating the surgeon’s view and potentially contributing to surgical error. Lung tumors also required different optimal ICG dosing depending on the tumor type (29). This is partially due to a specific imaging window before fluorescent intensity drops due to serum protein interactions and hepatic clearance. Efforts to identify the optimal ICG dose in various malignant lung nodules has helped overcome these operational barriers (39). The low reliability of ICG to distinguish between tissue types is another hurdle for clinical applications. This includes distinguishing tumor from normal lung parenchyma and discriminating between lung cancer types. The utility of ICG may also diminish with experienced surgeons, who are more consistently able to identify nodules and intersegmental planes without visual assistance (40).

Molecular probes

To address some of these disadvantages, various trials have explored increasing the specificity and stability of ICG (41). The most promising method for increasing specificity of lung imaging is to combine ICG with an molecular probe (42). Clinical trials have identified folate-receptor alpha (FRa) as a relatively specific lung-imaging molecular probe, which has been demonstrated to be overexpressed in lung adenocarcinoma compared to normal lung parenchyma (43,44). Linking ICG to a FRa molecular probe improves localization of fluorescence at the site of interest and reduces the risk of ICG spill over in the surgical field. Molecular imaging additionally allows for identification of lung adenocarcinoma in real-time during VATS and still allows for visualization of tumor margins (14,45-47). FRa overexpression more frequently occurs in lung adenocarcinoma, limiting its utility as a probe for other cancer types. However, ICG-linked FRa probes such as pafolacianine have demonstrated high rates of nodule localization in other FRa-positive lung nodules (46). Pafolacianine has also demonstrated utility in bronchoscopic identification of lung nodules in mouse and porcine models (48). In addition to FRa, other lung cancer specific targets have been investigated, including epithelial growth factor receptor (EGFR) (49), sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 (SGLT-2) (50), integrin αvβ3-targeted cRGD-ZW800-1 (51), bespoke nanocluster (NapA-AuNCs) (52), and an iodinated hydrogel (53). Beyond nodule localization, ICG-linked hydrogen peroxide probes (HCy-H2O2) have been reported to help detect lung injury which expresses higher levels of H2O2 (54).

Probe-based imaging addresses some of the disadvantages of ICG-only imaging but still requires preoperative treatment with a chemical agent. Successful imaging of molecular probes also depends on depth from the pleural surface and is less reliable for tumors further from the pleural surface. Okusanya et al. report that NIR imaging of ICG could identify pulmonary nodules up to 1.3 cm from the pleural surface (55). Compared to NIR-I (700–900 nm), NIR-II (1,000–1,700 nm) has demonstrated superior intensity and signal-to-background ratio (SBR) measurements of ICG in lung cancers, reducing some of the initial obstacles of ICG use in lung nodule detection (56). More recently, machine learning (ML) has been integrated with molecular imaging to further enhance tumor-to-background ratio (TBR) of ICG in lung nodules. Azari et al. report an ML algorithm which reliably identified lung cancers in real-time during thorascopic procedures, with rapid quantification of TBR (57). The emergence of artificial intelligence (AI) and ML as adjuncts to molecular imaging has the potential to improve real-time image processing and tissue classifications through faster processing of large data sets and identification of unique optical signatures.

Confocal laser endomicroscopy (CLE)

CLE has also been investigated as an exogenous contrast-dependent imaging modality in lung parenchyma. Using a fiberoptic laser, CLE can obtain microscopic images of tissue histology intraoperatively. This has primarily been explored in thoracic surgery during bronchoscopic procedures (58,59). Intraoperative staining with acriflavine allows for CLE visualization of bronchial epithelium and diagnosis of intraluminal lung cancers due to loss of homogeneity with more large dark cells (58). A similar technique using several staining dyes was able to identify histological changes in lung cancer tissues, including alveolar wall thickening, edema, and increased macrophage concentration (59). CLE has the potential to merge features of traditional histological assessment with intraoperative imaging, reducing barriers to clinical translation and relying on validated methods of pathological diagnosis. The ability to perform histological assessment in the operating room in vivo may save valuable time by streamlining operating room workflow, ultimately reducing healthcare associated costs. Yet this approach is still susceptible to the risks associated with using exogenous contrast agents (60). To resolve this, CLE imaging has also been trialed without contrast agents to visualize lung pathologies (60,61). Spectral data from the CLE laser allows for detection of elastin structure in alveoli to assess tissue architecture, and classification of normal and abnormal lung parenchyma (60). The microscopic field of view in CLE makes it less useful for visualizing entire lung nodules and surrounding margins. Further refinement of CLE, with and without exogenous dyes, has the potential to improve intraoperative diagnosis of lung pathologies and reduce reliance on traditional histological techniques.

Autofluorescence imaging

Clinical trials investigating autofluorescence imaging techniques preceded contrast agent-based techniques and are more varied in application (Figure 1). Autofluorescence imaging relies on the physical principle of certain endogenous biomolecules that fluoresce when illuminated with light that feeds into a corresponding characteristic optical absorption band, typically in the blue/violet (350–450 nm) wavelengths. Normal mucosal tissue will emit green light with the majority of autofluorescence from nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate [NAD(P)H] and flavins and weaker contributions from collagen, elastin, melanin, keratin, and porphyrins (62). Premalignant and malignant tissues emit significantly less green wavelengths and red-shifted autofluorescence (63,64). The first clinical applications of autofluorescence for lung imaging was bronchoscopic assessment of early neoplastic changes in asymptomatic smokers (64). Subsequent autofluorescent bronchoscopy (AFB) trials added red light reflectance to this approach, which provided a reference signal to compare against the intensity of green autofluorescence (63). Advances in AFB have allowed for higher sensitivity in detection of early mucosal lesions in patients with lung cancer (65). The general principle of using spectral imaging was expanded with narrow band imaging (NBI) to include imaging of mucosal blood vessels to detect lung cancer (66-68). NBI uses narrow blue and green wavelengths of light to excite hemoglobin within surface capillaries and deeper thicker blood vessels respectively (67,68). NBI has been used to measure tissue response to neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy, with decreased magenta intensity correlating to normalization of the bronchial epithelium (69).

Autofluorescence imaging techniques do not require preoperative injection of fluorescent markers and avoid the technical difficulties associated with contrast agent-dependent strategies. The relatively high light intensity required for autofluorescence makes it an inefficient process and contributes to faster photobleaching. Pulsed laser or light sources address this issue by providing high peak intensity with low average intensity. However, pulsed light sources are technically more complex and problematic compared to steady state light sources. Additionally, autofluorescence relies on an unobscured view of the target tissue, meaning blood and other dense intraoperative structures can interfere with measurements. Independence from contrast agents also reduces risks associated with intravenous injections including infection, hypersensitivity reactions, and drug interactions. Relying on emission, absorption, and reflectance spectra also provides quantitative properties of the actual tissue properties rather than proxy measurements of a contrast agent, ultimately improving sensitivity of disease detection (63,64,67,68).

The complexity of interpreting complex spectral data is a consequence of autofluorescence compared to contrast-dependent molecular imaging strategies. To better quantify lung autofluorescence, additional optical processing approaches have been integrated with autofluorescence, including Fluorescence Lifetime Imaging Microscopy (FLIM) and SHG imaging, which not only improve data processing, but may also enhance optical categorization of normal and cancerous lung tissues (70). Improved test sensitivity of autofluorescence imaging techniques is also accompanied by increased false-positive rates of disease detection, especially for lung cancer imaging (63). Edell et al. comment that the increased false-positive rate may be skewed by the ability to detect pre-malignant tissue with normal cell architecture, suggesting an ability to detect neoplastic lung tissue at an earlier stage of disease progression (63). Autofluorescence imaging may not be the optimal approach for all dysplastic lung tissue types due to inherent variations in the structural properties of the dysplastic tissue (63,67,68). Current studies demonstrate higher rates of reliability when imaging adenocarcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma of the lung (63,67,68). It is unknown if autofluorescence imaging improves long-term survival in lung cancer patients, prompting calls for a registry to be established in this field of study (63).

Spectral imaging

Spectral imaging techniques typically offer quantitative measurements of tissue parameters which are more specific than the surrogate measurement of light intensity in contrast agent-dependent molecular imaging strategies. This is achieved by measuring reflectance spectra from the target tissue and correlating the results with spectra of known biophysical compounds.

Diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (DRS)

Autofluorescence lung imaging techniques have provided the groundwork for more complex spectral imaging techniques that measure spectral properties of lung tissue beyond tissue components with autofluorescence properties. DRS uses two spectral ranges of 400–1,100 nm and 900–1,700 nm to detect the absorption and scatter spectra of lung tissues. These wavelengths allow for detection of the fractional volumes of blood and water, blood oxygen saturation, and reduced scattering coefficient of light (71). DRS is often used as quantitative spectroscopy without production of an image; however it also can be used as a direct visualization strategy. Spliethoff et al. demonstrated clinical feasibility of this technology in a transthoracic lung biopsy application, showing that DRS can differentiate normal lung parenchyma from tumor tissue. Results suggest that water fraction and scattering are the most useful DRS measurements for differentiating tumor tissue from normal lung parenchyma, which is consistent with prior studies (71). However, DRS is reported to have worse performance in differentiating lung cancer tissues from healthy parenchyma compared to autofluorescence (72). DRS can also be used during CT-guided lung biopsy to confirm needle placement and presence of tumor tissue at the sampling site, but further studies are needed to confirm DRS’ utility as a biopsy guidance tool. Other studies have investigated the potential of DRS to evaluate tissue architecture in lung, skin, breast, and kidney (73-76). A similar approach using NIR spectroscopy was also explored in the pediatric population, measuring oxygen saturation levels to distinguish neonatal pneumonia from transient tachypnea (77). More recently, DRS has been combined with autofluorescence to develop bimodal visualization of lung nodules. Costa et al. combined DRS with time-resolved autofluorescence to intraoperatively identify tumor margins, demonstrating feasibility of differentiating tumor, fibrotic, and normal lung parenchyma in real-time (78).

Hyperspectral imaging (HIS)

Detection of properties that differentiate healthy from pathologic tissue can be improved by imaging spectra at more wavelengths to find the maximum absorption and reflectance for any given sample. HSI takes advantage of this strategy by measuring spectra at several hundred different wavelengths. Software calculates the spectra from all wavelengths and interpolates an output report with maximum reflectance, absorption, and scatter wavelengths for the chosen parameters. HSI has been clinically applied to evaluate bronchus perfusion following pulmonary resection. Tissue oxygenation (StO2), near-infrared-perfusions (NIP) index, organ hemoglobin index (OHI), and tissue water index (TWI) measurements were computed from the spectral data set to evaluate bronchial perfusion and develop a predictive profile of postoperative bronchopleural fistulas (BPF). Ellebrecht et al. demonstrated the safety and feasibility of HSI to evaluate pulmonary perfusion in a thoracic surgery setting (79). While the broad spectral information in HSI is an advantage over more restricted optical approaches, it remains vulnerable to differences in tissue thickness, with a reduced ability to differentiate between tissue types in thin samples (2–10 µm) compared to thicker samples (500 µm) in simulated experiments (80). This further underscores the tradeoffs of light penetration, intensity, and signal consistency across optical imaging modalities.

Optical coherence tomography (OCT)

In addition to autofluorescence and spectral imaging modalities, OCT has emerged as a non-molecular intraoperative lung imaging technique. OCT measures backscattered or backreflected light to develop two-dimensional cross-sectional images of tissues, analogous to ultrasound technology (81). While well-studied in ophthalmic and retinal tissues, OCT has more recently been applied to detect pulmonary lesions (82,83). Hariri et al. applied OCT to ex vivo tumor samples to diagnose lung carcinomas. Despite reporting lower sensitivities and specificities compared to standard tissue biopsy and histology, OCT demonstrated early capability to identify lung carcinomas, although not as a replacement for tissue biopsy (82). Building on this work, Zhou et al. utilized OCT in transbronchial biopsy of peripheral lung nodules. Compared to endobronchial ultrasound (EBUS), OCT had a higher accuracy in characterizing the histopathology of lung nodules but had a lower diagnostic yield (83). OCT has also been explored as an endobronchial technique for assessing early idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (84). The ease of use, relatively simple data processing, and similarity to ultrasound reduce the translational burden of OCT in clinical settings. OCT differs from other spectral modalities due to a lack of quantitative data and reliance on user interpretation of the image, suggesting it is more useful in identifying tissue differences, such as lung nodule localization, than diagnosing or classifying different pathologies.

ML

A major obstacle in non-molecular imaging modalities is the demand for processing large complex data sets from the associated optical sensors. With the mainstream emergence of AI in consumer technologies since 2023, ML has become a potential solution to processing and interpreting large spectral data sets (85). Tian et al. applied a deep learning model to Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) imaging, reporting improved lung cancer classification compared to baseline software methods (86). The use of ML to process infrared spectral data demonstrates the potential to apply ML to other non-molecular imaging modalities and similarly improve tissue classification and signal recognition. Additionally, the enhanced processing and analytical capacity of ML may open the door to multimodal imaging techniques. To improve detection of lung adenocarcinoma, Xu et al. applied a deep learning model to a multimodal non-molecular imaging system including FTIR, UV-vis, and autofluorescence spectra with highly accurate detection and classification (87). Similarly, ML has been used in a multimodal system that combines molecular probe-based imaging to obtain UV-vis and fluorescence spectra, with non-molecular imaging strategies—FTIR and Raman spectroscopy—to detect early lung cancer (88). ML may be further utilized to explore other multimodal imaging systems, with improved intraoperative real-time image processing and lung tissue classifications, to overcome limitations of single-modality imaging and assist surgeon decision making. As advances in ML algorithms improve, intraoperative interpretation of spectral data will become faster and more detailed, providing surgeons with more specific information and reducing barriers to clinical adoption.

Limitations

This scoping review aims to summarize current non-molecular intraoperative lung imaging modalities being investigated in intraoperative settings on parenchymal lung tissues but may not capture the full spectrum of non-molecular techniques which are being developed. There are other promising light-based imaging modalities being investigated to detect and diagnose lung cancer, such as Raman spectroscopy, however these technological efforts are primarily applied to serum and liquid biopsy specimens (89). While biofluid-based methods of detecting lung cancer is an important research field in medicine, they have limited utility for intraoperative visualization in thoracic surgery. A yield of 55 publications from 1,795 search results in PubMed may indicate that the inclusion/exclusion criteria were too strict, however the authors intentionally focused the scope of the review to focus on intraoperative imaging modalities, that have the potential to ultimately aid decision making in thoracic surgery procedures. Additional databases can also be searched in the future to produce a more comprehensive review. The classification of included publications also represents an area of oversimplification as differences in application and technology exist within groupings, suggesting additional classifications may be needed.

Conclusions

Visualizing lung tissue with non-molecular imaging modalities demonstrates clinical feasibility and safety. Autofluorescence, reflectance-based spectral imaging, and OCT are not reliant on exogenous chemicals, eliminating the need for contrast-agent injection and dosing. This streamlines the clinical workflow and reduces physiological risks associated with injections of contrast agents. Non-molecular imaging modalities are also capable of quantifying the physical properties of tissue, rather than simply measuring the fluorescent intensity of a surrogate molecule. Early attempts have demonstrated the potential for non-molecular imaging modalities to develop light-based profiles of lung tissue, allowing physicians to identify pathogenic tissue from normal lung parenchyma. ML has demonstrated to be useful in processing and analyzing large spectral data sets and may be a pathway for complementary multimodal imaging techniques in the future. Additional studies are needed to develop reliable profiles of lung tissue pathologies, especially for lung cancer categories. Comparison studies between contrast-agent dependent and non-molecular imaging strategies will also be needed to evaluate relative strengths and weaknesses of these different imaging approaches. Future investigations should focus on validating the spectral signatures of normal lung tissue and lung-associated pathologies, especially lung cancers. Additional efforts should be made to miniaturize non-molecular imaging devices, so that they are compatible with thorascopic cameras used in minimally invasive thoracic procedures. Evidence demonstrating safety and reliability of these devices will also be needed to overcome regulatory hurdles. For contrast-based imaging techniques, dose safety and optimization studies will be needed to achieve regulatory clearance. With further development in the field of intraoperative lung imaging, it may be possible to reduce reliance on pathology lab assessments, provide real-time intraoperative visualization of lung disease, aid surgeon decision-making, and improve patient outcomes.
