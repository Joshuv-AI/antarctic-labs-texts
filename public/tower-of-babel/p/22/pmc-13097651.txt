Clinical characteristics in hospitalized children with infectious mononucleosis in Shanghai China: a single-center retrospective study
Authors: Xueqi Zhao, Qiuchen Wu, Jiajia Lv, Zhenwei Xia, Min Wu, Qun Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate clinical features and peripheral blood inflammatory markers in children with Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)-induced infectious mononucleosis (IM), analyze their correlation with disease severity, enable early identification of severe cases, and provide a theoretical basis for early clinical intervention.MethodsA retrospective analysis was performed on 156 hospitalized children with EBV-related IM (January 2019-December 2024). EBV nucleic acid was detected via real-time PCR. Patients were grouped by blood EBV-DNA load (positive/negative). Demographics, clinical manifestations, laboratory findings, and liver/spleen/lymph node imaging were collected and analyzed.ResultsAmong 156 pediatric IM patients (mean age 7 years), IM occurred predominantly in spring (28.20%) and autumn (28.21%), with children aged ≥ 7 years identified as the high-risk group. EBV-DNA positivity was higher in summer and winter. EBV-DNA positivity correlated with disease severity, as evidenced by longer hospitalization (9 vs. 7 days, P = 0.038), elevated inflammatory markers (LDH, ALT, AST), coagulation abnormalities (D-dimer; P < 0.05), and altered immune profiles (elevated CD3+CD8+ T cells and reduced NK cells; P < 0.05). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis identified CD3+ T cells (AUC = 0.664) and D-dimer (AUC = 0.692) as primary predictors biomarkers for IM, with CD8+ T cells, LDH, and ALT as supportive biomarkers. Biomarker combination boosted predictive accuracy (AUC = 0.713, 72.1% sensitivity, 62% specificity), demonstrating key synergistic diagnostic value in EBV pathogenesis.ConclusionEBV infection is prevalent among school-aged children in Shanghai, causing significant cellular and humoral immune dysfunction. Longitudinal monitoring of immunological parameters is crucial. EBV-DNA load is a valuable prognostic biomarker for severe IM; elevated inflammatory markers and immune dysregulation in EBV-DNA-positive children are critical for severity assessment.Keywords: Epstein-barr virus, Biomarkers, Children, Viral load, Retrospective study, Atypical-lymphocyte, Infectious mononucleosis, Immune abnormalities, Hepatomegaly

Introduction

Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), classified as human herpesvirus 4, is the most commonly infected human virus globally. Over 90% of individuals worldwide are infected with EBV, yet most infections are asymptomatic, especially in infants and children younger than 6 years old age. It exhibits high prevalence worldwide, with oral secretions being recognized as the main transmission route in children [1]. Primary EBV infection in children may lead to infectious mononucleosis (IM), an acute and serious condition marked by significant lymphocytosis [2], characterized by fever, lymphadenopathy, and pharyngitis [3]. The primary EBV infection in developed nations (e.g., Europe and the United States) is common in late adolescence, individuals in developing areas are exposed to the virus at a significantly younger age [4]. The capacity of EBV to switch between latent and lytic life cycles allows it to induce chronic infections, which can periodically relapse or reactivate [5]. Individuals with compromised immune systems are at a higher risk of developing symptoms upon viral reactivation [6]. The association between EBV infection and several cancers, including Hodgkin’s lymphoma, non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma, Burkitt’s lymphoma, and nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NC), which is well-established. Additionally, chronic or recurrent EBV infection is strongly associated with immune disorders, such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), systemic scleroderma (SSc), Sjögren’s syndrome (SS), rheumatoid arthritis (RA), connective tissue disease (CTD), and multiple sclerosis (MS) [7]. A study indicated that in China, EBV antibody seroprevalence increased rapidly from 1 to 6 years, reaching 82.5% by 6 years, and nearly all children had seroconverted by the age of 10 [8]. Yet, currently there is a lack of systematic analysis on the relationship between the immune status and changes in various biochemical indicators of IM patients and EBV viral load. Therefore, this study is based on the EBV infection related primary IM children admitted to our hospital as research objects, exploring the relationship between clinical characteristics, liver, spleen, lymph node enlargement state, serum inflammatory and biochemical indicators, variations in cellular and humoral immune levels, and viral load in EBV infection related primary IM children. Due to the incomplete development of children’s immune function, some patients present with atypical EB virus infection. Early diagnosis, timely and accurate assessment of the condition, and precise individualized treatment are more conducive to the recovery of the child.

Methods

Study subjects

156 patients under 15 years old with EBV-IM who were treated in the Department of Pediatrics Inpatient, Ruijin Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine from January 2019 to December 2024. The inclusion criteria of IM is as follows: (1) age 1–15 years old; (2) Clinically diagnosed cases that meet the diagnostic criteria for IM in the Experts consensus on diagnosis and treatment of Epstein-Barr virus infection-related diseases in children. Exclusion criteria: presence of serious underlying diseases (e.g., primary immunodeficiency, malignancy, and/or other systemic diseases), co-infections (e.g., CMV, adenovirus) and/or absence of complete medical records. Severe IM is defined by the occurrence of one or more of the following: (1) death; (2) Otorhinolaryngological (ENT) locoregional complications like as upper airway obstruction due to hyperplasia of oropharyngeal lymphoid tissues such as tonsils or adenoids, or phlegmon; (3) splenic complications (splenic hematoma or rupture); (4) pulmonary complications (interstitial lung disease); (5) cardiac complications (myocarditis, pericarditis); (6) hepati complications (ALT > 10 × upper limit of normal or symptomatic hepatitis with icterus/nausea); (7) hematological complications (macrophage activation syndrome, characterized by the combination of ferritin > 10,000 ng/mL, bicytopenia, cholestasis/cytolysis, elevated lactate dehydrogenase, elevated triglycerides, and reduced fibrinogen, or hemolytic anemia with hemoglobin < 8 g/dL, or thrombocytopenia < 50,000/mm³ with hemorrhagic manifestations, or neutropenia < 500/mm³); (8) neurological complications such as encephalitis, meningitis, acute polyradiculoneuritis, facial nerve palsy; (9) digestive complications (colitis); (10) and/or ophthalmologic complications (uveitis) [9, 10]. The detailed study design was demonstrated in Fig. 1. All patients received conventional ganciclovir treatment and were of Chinese ethnicity from East China [11, 12]. They were categorized into three age groups: A < 3 years, 3 ≤ A ≤ 6 years and A ≥ 7 years. Data collection was conducted using hospital information system: the gender, age, duration of disease, admission time, clinical manifestations, laboratory indicators, EBV-DNA load, etc. All clinical data were acquired within 24 h after admission, including course of hospital stays, degree of fever, fever duration, tonsillopharyngitis, lymphadenopathy, hepatomegaly, splenomegaly, white blood cell (WBC), neutrophil count (NEU), lymphocyte (LYM), blood platelet count (PLT), C-reactive protein (CRP), procalcitonin (PCT), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), lactic dehydrogenase (LDH), and coagulation indexes. Fever duration refers to the number of days when the body temperature exceeds 37.8 degrees Celsius at least once. Peripheral blood samples of approximately 2 mL were collected from hospitalized patients under sterile conditions and placed in EDTA tubes. These samples were then transported to the clinical laboratory for subsequent analysis. Following centrifugation, EBV-DNA was isolated from the peripheral blood plasma, and real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was conducted using the 7500 Real-Time PCR Instrument from applied biosystems by Thermo Fisher Scientific to identify and quantify EBV nucleic acid in each sample. For this research, the EBV Nucleic Acid Amplification (PCR) Fluorescent Quantitative Detection Kit (National Medical Device Approval No. 20173400176; Daan Gene Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China) was employed. The limit of detection (LOD) of this kit met the in-house laboratory quality control standards, reaching a sensitivity of 5.0 × 10² gene copies/mL. When validated against national reference standards (quantified in international units per milliliter, IU/mL), the kit detected a minimum concentration of 250 IU/mL; a kit-specific calibration factor was applied for unit conversion, with 1 gene copy/mL equivalent to 1.31 IU/mL (note that the kit’s native quantitative unit is gene copies/mL, whereas national reference standards are standardized to IU/mL). The EBV viral load assessment was carried out in accordance with the instructions provided in the commercial PCR detection kit, incorporating negative, critical, positive, and four quantitative standards in each assay. The DNA viral loads were determined by comparing the cycle threshold (Ct 35) values of the samples to the standard curve as outlined in the protocol. The percentage of CD3⁺, CD4⁺, CD8⁺, NK and CD19⁺ cells were detected by flow cytometry (Gold standard method, BD FACS Canto II). ALT and AST were detected by velocity method (Beckman AU5821). The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Affiliated Ruijin Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine [(2025) LRC No. (243)] with a waiver of informed consent, and this study was conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Study flow chart

Diagnostic criteria of EBV-related IM [2, 13]

The diagnostic criteria was defined as follows: (1) Patients exhibiting at least 3 of the characteristic symptoms: fever, cervical lymphadenopathy, hepatomegaly, splenomegaly, pharyngeal tonsillitis, or eyelid edema; (2) Peripheral blood analysis demonstrating either atypical lymphocytes comprising ≥ 10% of total lymphocytes and/or total lymphocyte count ≥ 5.0 × 10⁹/L; (3) Specific serological antibody profiles: Positive anti-viral capsid antigen (VCA)-IgM and anti-VCA-IgG antibodies, with negative anti-EBV nuclear antigen (EBNA)-IgG antibody; Negative anti-VCA-IgM antibody, but positive anti-VCA-IgG antibody with low avidity.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 20.0 was used to conduct statistical analysis on the collected and sorted data. A P value less than 0.05 indicates that the difference is statistically significant. A normality test was performed on all measurement data. The measurement data that conform to a normal distribution was expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). An independent samples T-test was used for the comparison between the two groups. The measurement data that do not conform to a normal distribution are expressed as [M(P25, P75)], and the Mann-Whitney U test in non-parametric tests was used for the comparison between the two groups. The counting data are expressed as frequency [n(%)], and the Chi-Square test was used for the comparison between the two groups. When conducting a correlation analysis, a normality test was carried out on the data of each group. For the measurement data that conform to a normal distribution, the Pearson correlation test was used for the correlation analysis, and for the measurement data that do not conform to a normal distribution, the Spearman bivariate correlation analysis was adopted. Meanwhile, the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was used to evaluate the predictive and diagnostic value of indicators for IM. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To account for multiple comparisons, we applied False Discovery Rate (FDR) (Benjamini-Hochberg) adjustments to all analyzed variables, and corrected p-values are reported.

Results

Patient characteristics

Among the 156 hospitalized patients included in this study, 79 were male and 77 were female, resulting in a male-to-female ratio of 1:1. The mean age was 7 years, ranging from 9 months to 15 years. The age distribution of the patients is illustrated in Fig. 1: 9.62% (15) of the 156 patients were A < 3 years, 28.21% (44) were 3 ≤ A ≤ 6 years, 62.17% (97) were A ≥ 7 years (Fig. 2).

Age distribution of the 156 patients with infectious mononucleosis associated with Epstein-Barr virus infection

As shown in Fig. 3A, the ≥ 7 year group (62.18%) formed the apex of the age distribution pyramid, flanked by 3–6 years (28.20%) at the mid-level. A statistically significant year-by-year increase was observed in the ≥ 7 year-old IM proportion: from 39.39% (13/33) in 2019 to 76.47% (26/34) in 2024, demonstrating a 37.08% absolute rise over 6 years (P = 0.0021), Concurrently, the 3–6-year-old group experienced a significant decline, dropping from 42.42% (14/33) in 2019 to 17.65% (6/34) in 2024 (P = 0.013) (Fig. 3B).

Constituent ratio variations of IM cases by age group and calendar year. Constituent ratio variations of IM cases by age group (A) and calendar year (B)

Among inpatient children, 87 (55.77%) were the EBV-DNA positive, with 26.28% (41) males and 29.49% (46) females. The average age was 7 years. From 2019 to 2024 (n = 156), IM case constituent ratios exhibited annual bimodal peaks in spring (Mar-May: 28.20%) and autumn (Sep-Nov: 28.21%), contrasting with persistent seasonal nadirs in summer (Jun-Aug: 20.51%) and winter (Dec-Feb: 23.08%), forming a stable ‘double-peak’ seasonal rhythm. Over the 6-year period, IM case constituent ratios first declined to a trough in 2020 (7.69%), then surged to an all-time high in 2024 (21.8%), demonstrating a V-shaped rebound (Fig. 4A, B).

In contrast to the spring-autumn incidence peak of IM (55.77% of 156 cases from 2019 to 2024), diagnostic samples showed an inverse seasonal pattern for EBV-DNA positivity. Summer-winter specimens had the highest positive rate (68.12% vs. 45.98% in spring-autumn, P = 0.0048), as shown in Table 1; Fig. 4C. An unusual interannual spike occurred in 2021, with EBV-DNA positivity surging to 67.86% (compared to 48.48% in the baseline year of 2019). The rate then declined to 50% by 2024, making 2021 the year with the highest positive rate in the six-year dataset (Fig. 4D).

Clinical features

Clinical manifestations

Clinical manifestations of IM in 156 children included cervical lymphadenopathy (85.44%, 135/156), fever (83.54%, 132/156), splenomegaly (67.09%, 106/156), tonsillopharyngitis (54.43%, 86/156), puffy eyelid (31.01%, 49/156), hepatomegaly (20.89%, 33/156), and rash (13.29%, 21/156). Cervical lymphadenopathy and fever were the most prevalent symptoms, consistent with EBV-related systemic inflammation (Table 2). Among pediatric IM patients, splenomegaly (77.1%, 74/97) was the most prevalent in the ≥ 7 year group (P = 0.044).

Hematological and biochemical profiles

Median hematological parameters in IM patients (n = 156) exceeded pediatric norms: WBC 11.01 × 10⁹/L (normal 4.3–11.3), lymphocytes 5.965 × 10⁹/L (1.8–6.3), reflecting typical reactive lymphocytosis. EBV-DNA median was 848 copies/mL (Table 2). An increase in EBV copy number indicates an increase in the replication activity of the virus in the body. Significant variations were observed in HGB, ALT, and CK-MB levels(P < 0.05). The ≥ 7 year group exhibited the highest median HGB (130 g/L, IQR 124–136.5) and the highest ALT (74 IU/L, 40–178), whereas CK-MB was lowest in this group (0.75 IU/L, 0.575–1)(Table 2).

Immunological parameters

Immunoglobulin levels (IgM, IgG, and IgA) and T-cell subsets (CD3+, CD3+CD4+, CD3+CD8+, CD4+/CD8+, NK, CD19+) were measured. In humoral immunity, the ≥ 7 year group showed the highest median IgA (2.18 g/L, 1.49–2.61, P = 0.016) and IgG (12.04 g/L,10.43–13.93, P = 0.000) among all age groups (< 3/3–6/≥ 7 years), with significant intergroup differences (P < 0.05). Regarding cellular immunity, the ≥ 7 year group had the lowest median percentages of CD3+CD4+ (16.95%, IQR 12.9–21.8%) and CD19+ (3.6%, IQR 2.65–6.6%), which were significantly lower than those in the other groups (all P < 0.05). Discussion

Impact of the pandemic on disease incidence dynamics

The study collected pediatric patients with IM in Shanghai, China over the past five years, systematically and comprehensively analyzed the clinical characteristics and relevant biochemical indicators in the blood of IM patients. Interestingly, this period encompasses the COVID-19 pandemic. We found a significant age shift in EBV-related IM cases from 2019 to 2024 (Fig. 3A). Contrary to prior findings [14, 15], school-aged children (≥ 7 years) emerged as the predominant group (76.47% in 2024 vs. 39.39% in 2019), with a mean age of 7 years. COVID-19 non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs) (2020–2021) disrupted early EBV exposure in preschoolers (3–6 years), creating an “immune gap” [16]. Post-pandemic school reopenings increased classroom density and social mixing, exposing immunologically naive ≥ 7 years old to EBV, which may have contributed to a 37.08% surge in symptomatic IM [17, 18]. Reduced early-childhood transmission (daycare/family) could be linked to incidence declining in 3–6 years old cases (42.42% to 17.65%), suggesting a potential association between delayed EBV exposure and the increased incidence of IM in older children [19–21] (Fig. 3A and B).

This study found higher IM incidence in Shanghai during spring/autumn school reopenings, aligning partially with Danish data [22], while EBV-DNA peaked paradoxically in summer/winter due to asymptomatic viremia [23, 24]. (Fig. 4A and C).

Over six years (2019–2024), pediatric IM incidence and EBV-DNA positivity exhibited contrasting trends linked to pandemic-driven societal and immunological shifts [25]. Pre-pandemic, IM accounted for 21.15% of pediatric cases (2019), plummeting to 7.69% in 2020 during lockdowns. Post-lockdown easing in 2021, IM rebounded to 17.95% due to NPIs against COVID-19 and its complications (long COVID-19) drastically curbed the transmission of EBV, stabilizing at 14.1% during Shanghai’s 2022 lockdown before it reached the peak incidence of 21.8% in 2024 within the past 6 years [16, 26](Fig. 4B). Meanwhile, EBV-DNA positivity followed an inverted U-shape: peaking at 67.86% in 2021 (acute primary infections amid immune debt) before declining to 50% in 2024, near pre-pandemic levels (48.48% in 2019) (Fig. 4D), reflecting pandemic immune gaps and latent reactivations. Shifts in diagnostic priorities during the COVID-19 pandemic may underrecognize persistent EBV viremia, characterized by measurable viral loads without symptoms [27–30]. Interestingly, following the re-liberalization of pandemic-related restrictions and the resumption of social activities, a marked rebound in infectious diseases was observed. These findings indicate the complex interplay of public health policies, viral epidemiology, and immune maturation, advocating integrated surveillance to address delayed immune training in post-pandemic populations [31, 32]. However, the lack of direct data on EBV transmission dynamics and adherence to pandemic measures limits our ability to infer causality, and we thus suggest that future prospective studies with serological surveillance of EBV exposure are needed to validate this hypothesis.

Clinical Insights

Clinical features

IM is known to be a benign lymphoproliferative condition and acute infectious disease caused by EBV, which presents the typical clinical syndrome including fever, tonsillopharyngitis, cervical lymphadenopathy, and a range of other manifestations [33]. As detailed in Table 2, In addition to cervical lymphadenopathy, fever, and tonsillopharyngitis were common symptoms in pediatric IM patients, there is also splenomegaly. We found splenomegaly (77.1%) was most prevalent in the ≥ 7 years old group. Clinical features of IM vary across different age groups: school-aged children (≥ 7 years old) exhibit immune activation-driven splenomegaly and prolonged fever, while younger children (< 3 years) exhibit asymptomatic manifestations [34].This divergence not only validates the classical “immune maturity-infection phenotype” theory [35] but also provides localized evidence for post-pandemic precision care of pediatric IM-especially in Shanghai’s biannual semester system, where splenomegaly screening for ≥ 7 years old returning to school should be prioritized to alert against severe disease trends (Table 2).

Host immunity and laboratory markers

The trend of humoral immunity response, such as IgA/IgG levels, increases with age, peak at 7 years. At the same time, EBV-infected B cells present antigens via MHC I, triggering CD8⁺ T cell differentiation and disrupting CD4⁺/CD8⁺ T cell balance. EBV restricts MHC II presentation and hijacks CD4⁺ T cell metabolism via CD28/IL-2 downregulation, promoting hypometabolism for persistence [36]. CD4+ T cell counts and CD4+/CD8+ ratios gradually decrease with age, reaching a nadir at 7 years. During the acute phase of infection, although CD4+ T cell exhibit a significant proliferative trend, studies have shown that EBV will impose restrictions on the presentation process of its antigens on MHC class II molecules. This restrictive mechanism leads to a significant decrease in the ability of CD4+ T cell to recognize infected cells. Meanwhile, EBV further weakens the activity of CD4+ T cell by reducing activation signals and proliferative signals, thus achieving immune evasion to a certain extent [37]. In addition, CD4+ T cell play a crucial regulatory role in the immune response and are capable of releasing a large number of cytokines. These cytokines potently drive the proliferation and differentiation of B lymphocytes, thereby leading to increased production of immunoglobulins IgA and IgG in the humoral immune response. This is consistent with the results of elevated plasma IgA and IgG levels during EBV infection indicated in our research. However, this process also results in a large consumption of CD4+ T cell during the course of viral infection [38]. A reduction in the number of CD4+ T cell impedes viral clearance, thereby prolonging EBV replication and inflammation.

The finding that EBV-DNA copy positive groups have lower NK cells and CD19⁺ cells compared to EBV-DNA copy negative ones is linked to infection stages, immune dynamics, and viral-host interactions (Table 3). EBV-DNA copy positive groups, in the acute replication phase, exhibit lower NK cell counts because EBV suppresses NK cells via evasion strategies such as MHC class I downregulation and induces NK cell exhaustion from prolonged activity. In contrast, EBV-DNA copy negative groups, often in resolution or latent phases, show elevated NK cells due to sustained immune surveillance against residual virus. Overall, the lower NK cell counts in EBV-DNA copy positive groups stem from viral immune evasion and NK exhaustion, in contrast to the sustained immune surveillance and B cell maturation in negative groups [37].

Meanwhile, EBV-DNA copy negative group may reflect low-level latency, triggering chronic immune activation to control reservoirs. EBV-DNA copy positive group, with active replication, disrupts these responses, lowering cell numbers. This finding highlights the need for monitoring post-infectious symptoms like fatigue, malaise in a subset of children, guiding prolonged follow-up beyond the acute phase.

The copy numbers of EBV-DNA and clinical manifestations

Patients with EBV-DNA positive group (60.3%, 94/156) had prolonged median hospitalization (9 days, IQR 6–13), with elevated LDH, ALT, AST, CD3+ T cell, CD3+CD8+ T cell and D-D. These results different from the study of Zhang et al. [39]. Ye et al. observed elevated NK cell proportions in IM [40]; however, our study indicates reduced NK cell counts (8.05% vs. 9.5%, P = 0.025), which are associated with impaired antiviral defense and consistent with the findings of Zhang et al. [39]. All the above findings suggest a correlation between the level of EBV copy number and the severity of the disease including hospital days, immune dysregulation, splenomegaly, and coagulopathy, highlighting EBV-DNA as a prognostic biomarker for severe IM. While our findings contrast with Odame et al.’s report which demonstrated that there is no significant correlation between EBV-DNA copies numbers and acute IM severity [41], our study align with Shi et al. [42]. Critically, our study demonstrated EBV-DNA positive group exhibited immune dysregulation, and inflammatory escalation, EBV-DNA load can serve as a predictive biomarker of disease severity. Monitoring changes in EBV-DNA load is helpful for evaluating disease progression and immune status.

The ROC curve highlights the percentage of CD3+ T cell and D-D as primary predictors of disease severity in the EBV-DNA positivity group. Our study indicate the percentage of CD3+ T cell (> 83.25%) have high sensitivity (88.2%) and the level of D-D (> 0.645 mg/L) moderate specificity (62%). The elevated CD3+ T cell likely reflects robust T-cell activation during acute EBV infection, while elevated D-D may signal EBV-associated coagulopathy or inflammatory cascades. Supportive biomarkers, including CD3+CD8+ T cell (> 48.4%), LDH (> 414.5 IU/L), and ALT (> 58 IU/L), showed moderate predictive utility (AUC 0.626–0.656), aligning with cytotoxic T-cell activity (CD8+), tissue injury (LDH), and hepatic injury (ALT). Notably, the high sensitivity of CD3+CD8+ T cell (86.8%) and LDH (67.6%) supports these indicators’ role in early prediction of the IM, though limited specificity (48–64%) reduces diagnostic precision. Combining these biomarkers improved predictive accuracy (AUC = 0.713, sensitivity = 72.1%, specificity = 62%), suggesting synergistic value in capturing multifaceted EBV pathogenesis-viremia, immune activation, and organ involvement. While CD3+ T cell and D-D may help differentiate acute EBV-driven disease (e.g., IM) from latent states, their modest AUCs (< 0.7) values are common for pediatric infection-related biomarkers due to the heterogeneity of disease presentations in this population, and that the biomarkers’ utility lies in their complementary value to routine clinical evaluations, emphasize the need for multi-parameter models in clinical practice [43].

In clinical practice, most children with EBV infection follow a self-limiting course. As shown in our study, ALT and LDH have moderate predictive value for initial evaluation of most cases. However, multi-parameter evaluation including EBV-DNA load and immune profiling is essential for risk stratification in severe or high-risk hospitalized patients. Although affordable and covered by medical insurance in China, universal screening is not recommended for uncomplicated cases. Instead, these tests should be used selectively in high-risk inpatients to guide monitoring and individualized management. Our findings support targeted use of EBV-DNA load and immune markers in high-risk children, not routine screening for all.

Conclusions

This study focuses on EBV infection and its associated immunological characteristics among school-aged children in Shanghai, yielding the following key conclusions: First, EBV infection is highly prevalent in this population and leads to significant dysfunction in both cellular and humoral immunity, underscoring the need for longitudinal monitoring of immunological parameters to guide clinical management. Second, EBV-DNA load is a reliable prognostic biomarker for severe IM; dynamic monitoring of EBV-DNA load changes is effective for assessing disease progression and patients’ immune status, and prolonged follow-up beyond the acute phase is critical for tracking long-term outcomes. Third, CD3+ T cell percentage and D-D levels have practical value in differentiating acute EBV-driven IM from latent EBV infection. ROC curve analysis further confirms these two indicators as primary predictors of disease severity in EBV-DNA-positive patients with a CD3+ T cell percentage > 83.25% achieving high sensitivity (88.2%) and a D-D level > 0.645 mg/L showing moderate specificity (62%). Integration of these biomarkers yielded a marked improvement in predictive accuracy (AUC = 0.713, sensitivity = 72.1%, specificity = 62%). Notably, this diagnostic indicator combination enables the prompt identification of high-risk children, which is critical for avoiding two common clinical pitfalls: overtreatment in patients with mild IM and delayed management in those with severe disease.

Limitations

Our study excluded immunocompromised subjects and confirmed no CMV/adenovirus co-infections, but unrecognized subclinical co-infections or rare immune deficiencies cannot be fully ruled out. As a longitudinal single-center retrospective study spanning the COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2023), it has limited sample size, representativeness and generalizability. To address the limitations and strengthen the translational value of our findings, we propose that future research should implement a multi-center and prospective cohort study with more comprehensive immunological profiling and pathogen screening.
