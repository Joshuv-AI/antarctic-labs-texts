circKIAA1462 impaires autophagy and promotes pyroptosis in retinal pigment epithelium via the miR-183-5p/HMGB1 axis in diabetic retinopathy
Authors: Lufei Wang, Jia’nan Xie, Longfei Yang, Jinsong Zhao, Xin Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiabetic retinopathy (DR) is one of the most prevalent complications of diabetes which could lead to vision impairment. Dysfunction of the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) is an early pathogenic event, where various mechanisms may contribute to the disease progression. circular RNAs (circRNAs) have been reported to be important regulators in diabetic complications, acting as miRNA sponge, participating in the regulation of gene transcription or coding short peptides.MethodsCircular RNA microarray and RNA-seq were used to identify differentially expressed circRNAs in ARPE-19 cells in normal and high glucose treatments. The expression of cKIAA1462, miR-183-5p, and high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) were determined using quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction and Western blot. In vitro experiments, such as flow cytometry, Western blot, and Electron microscope (TEM) were conducted. The binding interaction was confirmed using dual-luciferase reporter and overexpression/inhibition experiments. cKIAA1462 was knocked down via intravitreal lentiviral injection in diabetic mice, followed by expression level detection, functional analysis and histological assessments.ResultscKIAA1462 was significantly upregulated under high glucose conditions both in vitro and in vivo (in retinas of diabetic mice). It acted as a molecular sponge for miR-183-5p, increasing the expression of HMGB1. Elevated HMGB1 concurrently impaired autophagic flux (increased p62, decreased autophagosomes) and activated the NLRP3 inflammasome (upregulated NLRP3, ASC, caspase-1), promoting pyroptosis. Silencing cKIAA1462 in vivo restored autophagy, suppressed pyroptosis, improved retinal structure, and enhanced electroretinogram responses in diabetic mice.ConclusionThe cKIAA1462/miR-183-5p/HMGB1 axis plays a critical role in diabetic RPE injury by dual regulation of autophagy and pyroptosis. Targeting this pathway may offer a novel therapeutic strategy for early diabetic retinopathy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12967-026-08560-w.

Introduction

Diabetic retinopathy (DR) represents a prevalent microvascular complication of diabetes mellitus and serves as a leading cause of visual impairment among the working-age population globally [1]. Beyond its characteristic vascular pathology, dysfunction of the retinal neurovascular unit occurs in the early stages of the disease. The retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), a crucial component of the outer blood-retinal barrier, plays an indispensable role in maintaining retinal homeostasis [2]. Under persistent hyperglycemia, functional impairment of RPE cells is considered a key factor driving the progression of DR.

Recent studies suggest that processes such as autophagy and pyroptosis are significantly involved in diabetic RPE injury [3, 4]. These two pathways could interact in a complex manner, collectively influencing RPE cell fate. High mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), an important damage-associated molecular pattern molecule, has been suggested to participate in the regulation of both autophagy and inflammation in DR [5–7]. Clinical and basic research indicates upregulated expression of HMGB1 in the retinas of diabetic patients, potentially activating downstream inflammatory signaling [8]. Furthermore, HMGB1 has been shown to influence autophagic flux, although its precise regulatory mechanisms in diabetic RPE require further investigation [5].

With advances in non-coding RNA research, circular RNAs (circRNAs)—a class of RNA molecules with covalently closed circular structures—have demonstrated important regulatory functions in various diseases by modulating gene expression through acting as microRNA (miRNA) sponges [9]. Numerous microRNAs (miRNAs) show altered expression in DR and modulate critical biological pathways. They can modulate inflammation, influence angiogenesis and endothelial integrity primarily, regulate retinal cell survival and apoptosis [10].

The present study aimed to investigate the effects of circRNA/miRNA/mRNA regulatory axis existing in RPE cells under diabetic conditions. Through sequencing, we identified the upregulation of circular RNA cKIAA1462 under high glucose conditions and confirmed its altered expression in diabetic models. Further investigation suggested that cKIAA1462 may regulate HMGB1 expression by sequestering miR-183-5p, thereby modulating both autophagy and pyroptosis in RPE cells. These findings offered a new perspective for understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying diabetic retinopathy.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and transfection

ARPE-19 cells (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) were cultured in DMEM/F12 (HyClone, USA) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, GIBCO, USA) at 37 °C with 5% CO2. For DR modeling, cells were exposed to 50 mM glucose (Solarbio, China) (high glucose group, HG). Cells cultured under normal condition (5 mM glucose) were used as a control group, and under 50 mM mannitol (Solarbio, China) as osmotic control (OS group).

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was quantified via MTS assay (CellTiter 96® AQueous One Solution, Promega). After adding 20 µl reagent into each well, cells were incubated (37 °C/5% CO₂, 1 h). OD₄₉₀ was measured on a VarioSkan microplate reader (Thermo Scientific) using background-adjusted blanks. Triplicate wells per group were analyzed in three independent experiments, with results normalized to control (%).

Vectors construction and transfenction

To overexpress or suppress circKIAA1462 (cKIAA1462), the full-length of cKIAA1462 (Circ-oe) or verified sh-cKIAA1462 (SiCirc) was cloned into the pLC5-ciR vector (Invitrogen, USA) while empty pLC5-ciR as negative control (Vector). For intravitreal injection, designed si-cKIAA1462 was packaged into lentivirus (Cyagen, China). The hsa-miR-183-5p mimic, mimic control, miR-183-5p inhibitor, inhibitor control, human HMGB1 specific siRNA and negative siRNA, HMGB1 overexpression vector were purchased from GenePharma (China). The cells were transfected at approximately 80% confluence by Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Life Technologies, USA) or Lipofectamine 3000 (Life Technologies) according to the manufacturer’s protocols.

Flow cytometric analysis of apoptosis/necrosis

According to the manufacturer’s instructions, the cells were harvested, washed twice with PBS (PBS, 0.01 M, pH 7.4) and dual-stained with Alexa Fluor® 488 Annexin V and PI (Invitrogen Kit) in binding buffer. After 15-min RT incubation in dark at room temperature (RT), samples were analyzed on a Beckman Coulter EPICS XL-MCL flow cytometer within 30 min. Viable cells were first gated based on forward scatter (FSC) and side scatter (SSC) to exclude debris and cell aggregates. Early apoptotic cells were defined as Annexin V+/PI−, late apoptotic cells as Annexin V+/PI+, and necrotic cells as Annexin V−/PI+. At least 10,000 events per sample were acquired, and data analysis was performed using FlowJo software. All flow cytometry experiments were performed in triplicate and repeated three times independently.

Transfection of GFP-RFP‐LC3 adenoviruses

Cells were cultured and transfected with mGFP-RFP‐LC3 adenoviruses according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Hanbio, China). Cells were observed under an Olympus FV1200 confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM). As GFP is sensitive to acidic conditions, when autophagosomes fuse with lysosomes, GFP fluorescence is quenched and only red fluorescence can be detected. Yellow puncta (GFP+RFP+) and red puncta (GFP-RFP+), representing the autophagosomes and autolysosomes, respectively, were recorded by CLSM. For quantification, at least five randomly selected fields per sample were imaged, and the numbers of yellow puncta (GFP+RFP+) and red-only puncta (GFP−RFP+) were counted using ImageJ software. The analysis was performed blinded to the experimental groups. The ratio of red-only puncta to yellow puncta was calculated. Each experiment was repeated three times independently.

Animals and intravitreal injection

Wild-type C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Liaoning Changsheng Experimental Animal Center (Shenyang, China). DR was induced by intraperitoneal injection of streptozotocin (STZ) (Macklin, China). WT mice (8 weeks old, male) were fasted for 6 h before STZ injection. They received STZ (50 mg/kg in 10 mM citrate buffer, pH 4.5) injection for 5 consecutive days. Control mice were administered sodium citrate buffer. All mice were bred in an air-conditioned room with a 12-hour light-dark cycle. They were fed with standard laboratory chow and allowed free access to water. Seven days later, the blood from the tail vein was used for the measurement of glucose level. The mice with a blood glucose level of over 300 mg/dL were deemed diabetic. db/db mice, purchased from Gempharmatech (China), carry a point mutation in the leptin receptor that causes impaired leptin signaling, resulting in obesity, insulin resistance, and hyperglycemia.

The anesthetized mice were treated with topical anesthetic drops, and their pupils were then dilated. Under a microscope (Topcon, Japan), a 33-gauge disposable needle passed through the sclera near the equator into the vitreous cavity, avoiding the damage to the lens. Approximately 1.5 µL lentivirus of 1 × 109 TU/mL was subsequently injected into the vitreous cavity with direct observation of the needle above the optic nerve head. The lentivirus was injected 4 weeks after diabetes induction.

Western blot (WB) assay

Total protein of cells and retinas were extracted as previously described [11] and were quantified via BCA assay. Samples (30 µg/lane) were resolved on 10% SDS-PAGE gels and electrotransferred to 0.45 μm PVDF membranes (Millipore). After 1-h blocking in 5% non-fat milk/TBST, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, followed by 60-min incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (anti-rabbit or anti-mouse; 1:5,000, Millipore) at RT. Quantification of Western blots was processed using Image Pro Plus software (Media Cybernetics Inc., USA). The following antibodies were used in this study: anti- NLRP3 (Servicebio, China; 1:2000 dilution), anti- ASC (Servicebio, China; 1:2000 dilution), anti-Caspase-1 (Servicebio, China; 1:750 dilution), anti-IL-1β (Signalway Antibody, USA; 1:1000 dilution), anti-IL-18 (Signalway Antibody, USA; 1:500 dilution), anti-TNF-α (Signalway Antibody, USA; 1:2000 dilution), anti-HMGB1 (Abcam, UK; 1:1000 dilution), anti-LC3-II (Sigma, USA, 1:500 dilution), anti-p62 (Abcam, UK; 1:1000 dilution), anti-Beclin1 (Signalway Antibody, USA; 1:200 dilution) and anti-β-actin (CMCTAG, USA; 1:5000 dilution).

Quantitative real time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was isolated from the treated cells using Eastep Super total RNA extraction kit (Promega, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Total RNA of miRNA was polyadenylated and reverse transcribed with an All-in-One miRNA first-strand cDNA synthesis kit (TransGen Biotech, China). RNA was reverse transcribed into First-strand cDNA using First Strand cDNA synthesis kit (TransGen Biotech) and then amplified using FastStart Universal SYBR Green Master mix with LightCycler 480 (Roche Diagnostics) according to the instruction. U6 or GAPDH was served as an internal reference gene and the relative miRNA or mRNA expression was determined using the 2−ΔΔCt method. All primers used in this study were synthesized by and purchased from GeneCopoeia.

Circular RNA sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

ARPE-19 cells were cultured in the presence of 5.6 mM glucose (n = 3, NG) or 50 mM glucose (n = 3, HG) for 24 h, and then Total RNA was extracted from each sample. RNA purity and concentration were assessed using a NanoPhotometer® spectrophotometer (A260/A280 > 1.8 for all samples), and RNA integrity was evaluated using an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100. The RNA integrity numbers (RIN) ≥ 6.3 was allowed for sequencing.

Ribosomal RNA was depleted using an rRNA Removal Kit. Stranded RNA-seq libraries were prepared and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with paired-end 150 bp (PE150) reads, generating approximately 12 Gb of raw data per sample. Raw reads were processed to remove adapter sequences, reads with > 10% N, reads with > 50% of bases having a Phred quality score ≤ 20, and reads containing poly A/T/G/C. The quality of resulting clean data: average Q20 = 97.35%, average Q30 = 92.56%.

Clean reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg38) using HISAT2. Circular RNAs were identified and quantified using find_circ and CIRI with default parameters. Differentially expressed circRNAs were identified using edgeR with a fold change (FC) threshold of ≥ 1.5 and a P-value < 0.05. Given the exploratory screening design, no multiple testing correction was applied; candidate circRNAs were subsequently validated by qRT-PCR.

Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed on the predicted target genes of differentially expressed circRNAs using the clusterProfiler R package. Enriched terms with a corrected P-value < 0.05 (Benjamini–Hochberg) were considered statistically significant.

Dual-luciferase reporter gene system

To validate the direct binding between circKIAA1462 (cKIAA1462) and miR-183-5p, as well as the binding between miR-183-5p and the 3′UTR of Hmgb1 mRNA, dual-luciferase reporter assays were performed. Putative binding sites were predicted using the online StarBase software (http://starbase.sysu.edu.cn/).

For the circKIAA1462–miR-183-5p interaction, wild-type (Wt) and mutant (Mut) sequences of circKIAA1462 containing the predicted miR-183-5p binding region were synthesized and cloned into the psiCHECK-2 vector (Hanbio Biotechnology, China). For the miR-183-5p–Hmgb1 3′UTR interaction, the Wt and Mut 3′UTR regions of Hmgb1 mRNA were cloned into the same vector.

All reporter plasmids were co-transfected with hsa-miR-183-5p mimic or negative control miRNA (miR-NC) into 293T cells seeded in 96-well plates (50–70% confluence). Each well received 0.16 µg of plasmid and 5 pmol of miRNA mimic, with 0.3 µL of transfection reagent (Hanbio Biotechnology, 0.8 mg/mL). After 48 h, cells were lysed and luciferase activities were measured and the Dual-Glo™ Luciferase Assay System (Promega) was used. Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity for each sample. All experiments were performed in triplicate and repeated three times independently.

Transmission electron microscope (TEM)

Transmission electron microscopy (Hitachi HT7800, Tokyo, Japan) was employed to examine autophagosome-like vesicles in ARPE-19 cells and retina. Following fixation with 2.5% glutaraldehyde (overnight, 4 °C) and post-fixation with 1% osmium tetroxide (1 h, room temperature), samples underwent graded ethanol dehydration. Cells were embedded in epoxy resin, ultrathin-sectioned (70 nm), and stained with uranyl acetate/lead citrate prior to ultrastructural analysis. Autophagosome-like structures were identified by the presence of double-membrane vesicles containing cytoplasmic material or organelles. For cell samples, at least 50 cells per group were examined; for retinal sections, at least three different retinal sections per mouse and three mice per group were analyzed. Quantification of autophagosomes was performed by counting the number of autophagosome-like vesicles per cell (for ARPE-19 cells) or per 100 μm² of RPE layer (for retinal sections).

Immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry

ARPE-19 cells were seeded on cell slides and incubated overnight. Next, the cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100, and blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin (Beyotime, China). The cells were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibody. For the secondary antibody, Alexa Fluor 555-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (Abcam, 1:500) or FITC-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:200, Invitrogen) was used, and the mixture was incubated at room temperature for 1 h. 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Beyotime, China) was used to stain cell nucleus. After washing 3 times with PBS, the sections were sent for photographing under a fluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany).

As for the retinas, the sections were incubated with 1% BSA for 1 h to block nonspecific binding. Then, the sections were incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. After washing with PBS, the sections were incubated with a second antibody Alexa Fluor 555-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (1:500, Abcam) for 1 h at room temperature. The nucleus was counterstained with DAPI, and images of the retina were taken with an epifluorescence microscope (Olympus BX53, Japan).

RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH)

Cy3-labeled cKIAA1462 probes were designed and synthesized by Servicebio. FISH assays were carried out using an RNA FISH Kit (GenePharma) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. In brief, the fixed retina was blocked with 1× blocking solution for 30 min at 37 °C. Subsequently, the tissues were hybridized with the corresponding probes at 37 °C for 12 h in a humidified chamber. After the nucleus was stained with DAPI for 20 min, the retinas were inspected and imaged with the epifluorescence microscope (Olympus BX53, Japan).

Electroretinography (ERG)

ERG was recorded by using the RetiMINER System (AiErXi Medical Equipment Co., Ltd., Chongqing, China) as previous reported [12]. After overnight dark adaptation (> 12 h), mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (RWD Life Science, China). The pupils were fully dilated with 0.5% tropicamide and 0.5% phenylephrine hydrochloride (Xingqi, China), and the corneas were coated with 2% hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (Saikesaisi, China) to maintain moisture and conductivity. The mice were placed on a heating pad and the ground electrode was placed at the tail end, the reference electrode subcutaneously at the midline between the ears, and the gold ring electrode at the cornea. Scotopic ERG of a series of single white flashes of intensity ranging from 0.1 to 30 cd s/m2 was recorded. A-wave amplitudes were measured from the baseline to the negative peak, and b-wave amplitudes were calculated from the peak of the a-wave to the peak of the positive waveform. Each stimulus was recorded three times.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 21.0 software (SPSS, USA) was employed for statistical analysis. Data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test. For comparisons between two groups, the Student’s t-test was used when data met the assumptions of normality and equal variance; otherwise, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied. For comparisons among more than two groups, one‑way ANOVA was used if data met both normality and homogeneity of variance assumptions, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for pairwise multiple comparisons. When these assumptions were violated, the Kruskal–Wallis test was employed, followed by Dunn’s post hoc test with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. For high‑throughput RNA‑seq data, differentially expressed circRNAs were identified using edgeR with a fold change threshold of ≥ 1.5 and a nominal P‑value < 0.05. A two‑sided p‑value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 21.0 software (SPSS, USA).

Results

HMGB1 regulates cell autophagy and activates proptosis in RPE cells in high glucose (HG) condition

High-glucose affected adversely multiple facets of ARPE-19 cells, including but not limited to viability, apoptosis and proptosis. In this study. HG inhibited cell viability in a time-dependent manner compared with the NC group (Supplementary Fig. 1, S1). The Western Blot results showed that HG increased the expression levels of NLRP3, ASC, caspase-1 in ARPE-19 cells and retinas of diabetic mice (Fig. 1A-B), which indicated that high-glucose induced proptosis in ARPE-19 cell and retina. Several previous studies have demonstrated that autophagy, which is regulated by HMGB1, is also involved in DR development. WB analysis showed that the expression of HMGB1 and LC3B was significantly up-regulated under HG condition. However, the expression level of p62 was also increased which indicated that autophagic flux may be impaired by HG (Fig. 1C and D). TEM analysis revealed that high glucose (HG) reduced autophagosome formation and induced cellular swelling and vacuolization in ARPE-19 cells compared to the NC group (Fig. 2025E). These results indicated that HG suppressed autophagic flux and promoted pyroptosis in RPE cells.

Previous studies demonstrated that HMGB1 knockdown restores autophagic flux, attenuates inflammatory cytokine expression, and suppresses apoptosis in RPE cells during early diabetic retinopathy (DR) [5, 8]. Building on this evidence, the current study constructed and transfected HMGB1 overexpression and knockdown constructs (S2), and then revealed that HMGB1 overexpression in high glucose (HG)-exposed ARPE-19 cells significantly upregulates NLRP3 inflammasome components (NLRP3, ASC, caspase-1) and elevates LC3-II levels, while concomitant p62 accumulation indicates impaired autophagic flux (Fig. 1F). Conversely, HMGB1 knockdown under HG conditions downregulates NLRP3/ASC/caspase-1 expression and enhances LC3B-II conversion with reduced p62 deposition (Fig. 1G). These findings collectively suggested HMGB1 as a pivotal regulator coordinating pyroptosis and autophagic activity in diabetic RPE pathogenesis.

miR-183-5p activates autophagy and alleviates proptosis in the HG conditioned ARPE-19 cells by regulating HMGB1 expression

According to TargetScan, miRDB databases, and the multiMiR package, 293 miRNAs were predicted to be potentially binding to HMGB1, with consensus predictions from at least two databases. By analyzing three datasets (GSE199852, GSE74559, and GSE160308), we identified downregulated miRNAs in diabetic retinopathy vitreous and retina samples that were also among the predicted HMGB1-binding miRNAs. Specifically, 43 such miRNAs were found in GSE199852, 8 in GSE74559, and 7 in GSE160308. After removing duplicates, a total of 55 unique miRNAs remained. From these, we selected miRNAs that showed significant downregulation and had homologous counterparts in mice(S3). miR-183-5p was selected as it was found to target HMGB1 [13], and we validated decreased expression of miR-183-5p alongside with elevated expression of HMGB1 in the retinas of STZ-induced diabetic mouse models and db/db mouse models, as well as in high-glucose-treated RPE cells (Figs. 1C-D and 2A). Transfection of synthesized miR-183-5p inhibitor into RPE cells caused successful miR-183-5p knockdown (as revealed by results from qPCR), with the inhibitor group showing increased HMGB1 expression, while overexpression of miR-183-5p in cells resulted in reduced HMGB1 expression (Fig. 2B). Luciferase reporter assays suggested potential binding sites between miR-183-5p and HMGB1(Fig. 2C). Co-overexpression of miR-183-5p and HMGB1 demonstrated that miR-183-5p could still downregulate HMGB1 expression, indicating that miR-183-5p may bind HMGB1 and regulate HMGB1 expression (Fig. 2D).

Compared to the high-glucose group, the high-glucose + miR-183-5p inhibitor group exhibited further elevated HMGB1 levels. While the autophagy-related LC3II expression showed no significant change, p62 expression increased, indicating impaired autophagic flux. Additionally, pyroptosis-related markers (NLRP3, ASC, caspase-1) were upregulated (Fig. 2E). Under high-glucose conditions, administration of miR-183-5p mimics reversed the high expression of HMGB1 in RPE cells, slightly increased LC3II expression, decreased p62 expression, and reduced the levels of NLRP3/ASC/caspase-1 (Fig. 2F). Electron microscopy detection of autophagolysosomes revealed a reduction in autophagolysosomes in high-glucose cultured RPE cells; further suppression of miR-183-5p led to an even lower number of autophagolysosomes, while transfection with miR-183-5p mimic to overexpress miR-183-5p significantly increased the quantity of autophagolysosomes (Fig. 2G).

These findings suggest that downregulated miR-183-5p in diabetic retinas may influence RPE autophagy and pyroptosis by binding HMGB1 and regulating HMGB1 expression.

Identification of cKIAA1462 as a high glucose–regulated circRNA in RPE cells

To compare ARPE-19 cells cultured under high-glucose and standard conditions, RNA-seq was performed to identify differentially expressed RNAs, revealing 92 upregulated circRNAs (Fig. 3A). By RegRNA 3.0 and CircInteractome, 15 circRNAs were predicted to bind to miR-183-5p. ARPE-19 cells were divided into three groups—normal glucose, high glucose, and hyperosmotic—and the top five circRNAs with high binding scores (hsa_circ_0000348, hsa_circ_0001312, hsa_circ_0008875, hsa_circ_0018069 and hsa_circ_0019006 ) were quantified by qPCR to identify those with increased expression under high glucose but with unchanged expression under hyperosmotic conditions (Fig. 3B). Among them, hsa_circ_0018069 is derived from the third exon of human NM_020848, corresponding to the third exon of mouse NM_001081963 basing on homology, and it is considered suitable for determining the biological function using the mouse model. As the circ_0018069 originating from the host gene KIAA1462, it was named as circKIAA1462 (cKIAA1462). RNase R treatment assays showed that cKIAA1462 expression remained largely unchanged after RNase R digestion, while linear KIAA1462 expression was significantly downregulated (Fig. 3C), suggesting that cKIAA1462 is more stable than its linear counterpart. Furthermore, data indicated that cKIAA1462 expression was higher in the cytoplasm than in the nucleus (Fig. 3D), implying its predominant cytoplasmic localization. To further investigate whether diabetic stress affects cKIAA1462 expression in the mouse retina, retinas from STZ-induced and db/db diabetic mice were examined, revealing the upregulated expression ofcKIAA1462 (Fig. 3E).

cKIAA1462 regulates HMGB1 expression and RPE function by acting as a sponge for miR-183-5p in vitro

The study further investigated whether cKIAA1462 can regulate RPE cell function. The results showed that we successfully designed both cKIAA1462 overexpression and siRNA constructs, which did not affect KIAA1462 mRNA expression (Fig. S4). Western blot analysis revealed that cKIAA1462 overexpression led to decreased LC3 expression with increased p62 expression, whereas cKIAA1462 knockdown resulted in increased LC3 expression with decreased p62 expression. Autophagic flux assays demonstrated that cKIAA1462 overexpression enhanced autophagic flux (Fig. 4A-B). Regarding pyroptosis, cKIAA1462 overexpression up-regulated the expression of pyroptosis-related proteins NLRP3, ASC, and caspase-1. In contrast, cKIAA1462 knockdown produced the opposite effects on the expression of these proteins (Fig. 4C-D).

Since cKIAA1462 is primarily expressed in the cytoplasm of RPE cells, we hypothesized that cKIAA1462 regulates cellular functions at the post-transcriptional level by acting as miRNA sponge. The results of the dual-luciferase reporter assay showed that the miR-183-5p mimic significantly reduced the luciferase activity of the wild-type cKIAA1462 reporter in RPE-19 cells, but not that of the mutant (Fig. 4E). In addition, overexpression of cKIAA1462 decreased the expression levels of miR-183-5p and increased the expression of HMGB1 in ARPE-19 cells, whereas inhibition of cKIAA1462 resulted in elevated miR-183-5p expression and reduced HMGB1 expression (Fig. 4F). Co-transfection of cKIAA1462 siRNA and miR-183-5p inhibitor restored the increase of miR-183-5p and the reduce of HMGB1 expression; however, when cKIAA1462 was inhibited while HMGB1 was overexpressed, the elevated miR-183-5p expression remained unaffected, and HMGB1 expression was increased (Fig. 4G-H). We also found that transfection of miR-183-5p inhibitors or mimics did not alter the expression of cKIAA1462 and KIAA1462 mRNA in RPE cells (Fig. S5). These results indicated that cKIAA1462 acts solely as a sponge for miR-183-5p and influences miR-183-5p activity by sequestering it from its target genes.

We further investigated the impact of the cKIAA1462/miR-183/HMGB1 axis on cellular functions under high-glucose conditions. Apoptosis assays revealed that suppression of cKIAA1462 under high glucose reduced the apoptosis rate, whereas co-transfection of cKIAA1462 siRNA and miR-183-5p inhibitor increased apoptosis (Fig. 5A). In terms of pyroptosis, Western blot analysis showed that transfection with cKIAA1462 siRNA decreased the expression of pyroptosis-related factors (NLRP3, ASC, caspase-1) as well as the proinflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-18, and TNF-α, compared to the control group. However, co-transfection with miR-183-5p inhibitor, increased the expression of these proteins. Immunofluorescence staining of NLRP3, ASC, and caspase-1 showed a similar trend (Fig. 5B). Regarding autophagy, Western blot analysis indicated that transfection with cKIAA1462 siRNA led to increased expression of LC3 and Beclin-1, decreased expression of p62, and an elevated number of autophagosomes (as observed under electron microscopy). The mRFP-GFP-LC3 dual-fluorescence assay revealed a decrease in yellow/red puncta, suggesting the enhanced autophagic flux. However, co-transfection with cKIAA1462 siRNA and miR-183-5p inhibitor, reversed the increased levels of autophagy-related proteins and autophagosome formation induced by cKIAA1462 siRNA, and resulted in impaired autophagic flux (Fig. 5C-E). Furthermore, simultaneous overexpression of HMGB1 and suppression of cKIAA1462 was used to validate the regulatory role of the cKIAA1462/miR-183/HMGB1 axis. It was found that HMGB1 overexpression exacerbated the inhibitory effect of cKIAA1462 siRNA on pyroptosis and the activating effect on autophagy under high-glucose conditions (Fig. 5F-J). These results demonstrated that under high glucose, cKIAA1462 regulates HMGB1 expression by sponging miR-183, thereby influencing pyroptosis and autophagy.

cKIAA1462 regulates retinal pyroptosis, autophagy, and RPE function in vivo

We used an STZ-induced diabetic model to investigate the role of cKIAA1462 in retinal dysfunction. Intravitreal injection of si-cKIAA1462 (packaged in lentivirus) reduced the expression of cKIAA1462 but not cKIAA1462 mRNA (Fig. S6). One month after intravitreal injection, RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization (RNA-FISH) was performed to localize cKIAA1462. High expression of cKIAA1462 was observed in the retina of the diabetic model group, whereas its expression was scarcely detectable in the treated group (Fig. 6A). Concurrently, intravitreal delivery of cKIAA1462-silencing lentivirus significantly reduced cKIAA1462 RNA levels, increased mmu-miR-183-5p expression, and decreased HMGB1 protein expression in the retinas of diabetic mice (Fig. 6B). The expression of pyroptosis-related factors (NLRP3, ASC, caspase-1) as well as IL-1β, IL-18, and TNF-α was elevated in diabetic mice, while silencing cKIAA1462 reduced the expression of these proteins (Fig. 6C). Immunofluorescence staining revealed a consistent trend: cKIAA1462 knockdown decreased the expression of NLRP3, ASC, and caspase-1 across retinal layers, particularly in the RPE layer (Fig. 6D).

Meanwhile, analysis of retinal autophagy-related proteins showed the decreased expression of LC3 and Beclin1 along with the increased p62 in diabetic mice. In the injection group (cKIAA1462-knockdown), LC3 and Beclin1 expression was increased while p62 expression decreased (Fig. 6E). Retinal immunofluorescence staining confirmed that silencing cKIAA1462 reversed the diabetes-induced downregulation of LC3 and Beclin1 and upregulation of p62 (Fig. 6F). TEM examination of RPE cells revealed that compared to normal retinas, diabetic retinas contained fewer autophagosomes and phagocytosed photoreceptor outer segments. Inhibition of cKIAA1462 in diabetic retinas increased the number of autophagosomes and enhanced the phagocytic capacity of RPE cells, suggesting that cKIAA1462 may regulate both autophagy and phagocytic function in diabetic RPE (Fig. 6G). Retinal function was assessed by ERG, which showed that the amplitudes of a-wave and b-wave were reduced in diabetic mice. Treatment with cKIAA1462 silencing improved both a-wave and b-wave amplitudes (Fig. 6I).

Discussion

The RPE constitutes the outer blood-retinal barrier, and its dysfunction is a key early event in diabetic retinopathy (DR) [14]. While DR has long been considered primarily as a microvascular disorder, accumulating evidence indicates that dysregulated autophagy and chronic inflammation in the RPE contribute significantly to disease pathogenesis [15, 16]. However, the upstream molecular mechanisms that integrate these two pathological processes remain incompletely understood. In this study, we identified a glucose-responsive circular RNA, cKIAA1462, that is significantly upregulated in RPE cells under high-glucose conditions and in the retinas of STZ-induced and db/db diabetic mice. We further demonstrated that cKIAA1462 acts as a competitive endogenous RNA (ceRNA) by directly sponging miR-183-5p, leading to de-repression of HMGB1. Functional experiments revealed that this axis concurrently impairs autophagic flux and activates the NLRP3 inflammasome, thereby driving RPE injury. Importantly, intravitreal silencing of cKIAA1462 not only corrects these molecular abnormalities but also improves retinal morphology and function, as evidenced by the recovery of ERG a-wave and b-wave. These findings established the cKIAA1462/miR-183-5p/HMGB1 axis as a novel regulatory pathway that couples hyperglycemic stress to RPE dysfunction (Graphical Abstract).

The roles of circRNAs in DR are increasingly being uncovered, with studies showing that they can influence disease progression by modulating critical processes such as apoptosis and inflammation [17–19]. Our discovery that cKIAA1462 is upregulated specifically under hyperglycemic stress—and not under hyperosmotic control conditions—suggests that its induction is a genuine response to high glucose rather than an artifact of osmotic change. This specificity positions cKIAA1462 as a potential early sensor of metabolic stress in the RPE. Mechanistically, we provide direct evidence that cKIAA1462 is predominantly cytoplasmic and functions as a molecular sponge for miR-183-5p. Dual-luciferase reporter assays confirmed a direct interaction between cKIAA1462 and miR-183-5p, and rescue experiments showed that the effects of cKIAA1462 knockdown are reversed by co-inhibition of miR-183-5p. These findings support a linear hierarchical pathway in which cKIAA1462 acts upstream of miR-183-5p.

HMGB1, a well-established damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) molecule, is a pivotal driver of diabetic complications. Its pathogenic role is mediated through receptors such as TLR4 and RAGE, activating downstream inflammatory signaling cascades implicated in DR [8]. Previous work has shown that HMGB1 impairs autophagic flux in RPE cells during early DR via a cathepsin B-dependent pathway [5], and that HMGB1 activates the NLRP3 inflammasome [20]. Our data align with and extend these observations. We demonstrate that HMGB1 is a direct target of miR-183-5p, as validated by luciferase assays, and that HMGB1 overexpression under high glucose simultaneously upregulates NLRP3 inflammasome components (NLRP3, ASC, caspase-1) and impairs autophagic flux (increased p62 and elevated LC3-II). Conversely, HMGB1 knockdown restores autophagic flux and reduces pyroptosis. Thus, HMGB1 serves as a molecular nexus that integrates two key pathological processes—autophagy dysfunction and inflammasome activation—in the diabetic RPE.

Regarding the upstream regulation of HMGB1, our study identified a previously unrecognized ceRNA network. While the miR-183 family is known to contribute to retinal homeostasis [20], the specific role of miR-183-5p in DR has remained undefined. We found that miR-183-5p is downregulated in diabetic retinas and in high-glucose-treated RPE cells, and that its downregulation is necessary for HMGB1 upregulation. Transfection of miR-183-5p mimics restored autophagic flux and suppressed pyroptosis, whereas miR-183-5p inhibitors exacerbated the phenotype. These results positioned miR-183-5p as a protective factor whose loss under hyperglycemia contributes to RPE injury. The observation that cKIAA1462 overexpression decreases miR-183-5p levels, and that cKIAA1462 knockdown increases them, suggests that cKIAA1462 may sequester and reduce the effective pool of miR-183-5p. This model is further supported by the fact that miR-183-5p inhibitors reversed the protective effects of cKIAA1462 knockdown, and that HMGB1 overexpression abrogated these effects as well.

The functional consequences of this axis are substantial. In vitro, cKIAA1462 knockdown under high glucose reduced apoptosis and pyroptosis, enhanced autophagic flux, and increased autophagosome formation. In vivo, intravitreal delivery of si-cKIAA1462 in diabetic mice decreased retinal HMGB1, increased miR-183-5p, suppressed NLRP3 inflammasome activation, and restored autophagy-related protein expression. Importantly, these molecular improvements translated into structural and functional benefits: retinal thickness and morphology were significantly improved, and ERG a-wave and b-wave amplitudes—which reflect photoreceptor and bipolar cell function—were partially rescued. These data provided proof-of-concept that targeting cKIAA1462 could be a viable therapeutic strategy for early DR.

In conclusion, this study identified the cKIAA1462/miR-183-5p/HMGB1 axis as a novel regulatory pathway that couples hyperglycemic stress to impaired autophagy and NLRP3-mediated pyroptosis in RPE cells. Our findings highlighted cKIAA1462 as a potential therapeutic target and its retinal expression as a possible biomarker for early diabetic retinopathy. Interventions aimed at inhibiting cKIAA1462 or restoring miR-183-5p function may simultaneously ameliorate autophagic dysfunction and suppress deleterious inflammation, thereby preserving RPE integrity and retinal function.
