Identifying necessary conditions for medical students’ adoption of AI in the future practice: a survey study in Canada
Authors: Mickaël Ringeval, Louis Raymond, Guy Paré
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs artificial intelligence (AI) technologies become increasingly embedded in healthcare, the readiness of future physicians to adopt these tools is of growing concern. While prior studies have examined predictors of technology acceptance, less is known about the conditions that are essential, rather than merely influential, for medical students to form strong intentions to use AI-based health technologies (AIHTs) in clinical practice. This study addresses this gap by identifying both necessary and sufficient conditions for AI adoption intentions among medical students.MethodsDrawing on the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research and the COM-B model (Capabilities, Opportunities, Motivation–Behavior), the study surveyed 177 students at a Canadian medical school. Necessary Condition Analysis (NCA) and fuzzy-set Qualitative Comparative Analysis (fsQCA) were applied to explore how students’ familiarity with AIHTs, hands-on experimentation, perceived curricular importance, and beliefs about AI’s role in future medical tasks influence their adoption intentions.ResultsThe findings reveal that two conditions are necessary for strong AI adoption intentions: (1) a belief in the future relevance of AIHTs to medical tasks, and (2) a positive attitude toward the inclusion of AIHTs in the medical curriculum. The fsQCA further identifies two distinct “AI profiles” or configurations that are sufficient to foster strong adoption intentions, illustrating multiple pathways to readiness.ConclusionThese results highlight the importance of curricular design that not only builds technical familiarity but also fosters motivation and belief in AI’s clinical relevance. The study offers practical insights for medical educators aiming to prepare students for a digitally integrated healthcare environment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09432-z.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Medical education, Digital health, Curriculum design, Technology adoption, NCA, fsQCA

Introduction

The mindset of the younger generation is undergoing a profound transformation, ranging from altered communications patterns [1] to a growing prioritization of mental well-being, trends driven by digital transformation, rapid technology advancements, shifting societal values, and greater access to information [2]. This generation, often referred to as digital natives, exhibits a natural inclination toward adopting and adapting to technological solutions in their daily lives and professional environments [3]. They tend to value efficiency, innovation, and connectivity, which are hallmarks of modern technological ecosystems [4, 5]. Moreover, their expectations are shaped by global challenges such as economic shifts, climate change, and pandemics, which have collectively reinforced the importance of resilience, adaptability, and evidence-based approaches in decision-making [6].

Medical students, as part of this generation, are no exception. Their perceptions and intentions regarding digital health (dHealth) technologies are shaped by a combination of their evolving experiences, exposure, and changing professional landscapes. As the healthcare sector undergoes a digital transformation, fueled by advances in artificial intelligence (AI), telehealth, and mobile health technologies, the need for medical professionals to integrate these tools into their practice has become increasingly apparent [7]. However, the adoption of AI technologies in medical practice is contingent on the readiness and willingness of future physicians, which are strongly influenced by their educational experiences and individual mindsets [8].

The development of medical students’ perspectives on dHealth technologies, in general, and AI technologies in particular, is a dynamic process, shaped by various factors such as curriculum content, and hands-on exposure to digital tools [9–11]. For instance, structured experimentation with electronic medical records (EMR) and AI-based diagnostic tools can significantly enhance students’ confidence in using these technologies [12]. Additionally, external disruptions, such as the COVID-19 pandemic, have accelerated the adoption of dHealth technologies such as AI, providing a unique lens to examine how real-world events influence students’ views and intentions [13, 14].

AI technologies have emerged as critical tools in modern healthcare and are a driving force behind its digital transformation [15]. By leveraging machine learning (ML) algorithms and predictive analytics, AI can assist in early disease detection, personalized treatment planning, and automation of administrative tasks, ultimately reducing healthcare costs and improving patient outcomes. AI-driven tools are particularly impactful in optimizing healthcare resource allocation, especially for vulnerable populations such as the elderly and socially isolated [16, 17]. Furthermore, AI-powered applications enable real-time patient monitoring, advanced image analysis, and intelligent decision-support systems, capabilities that proved invaluable during global health crises like the COVID-19 pandemic [18, 19]. This technological momentum reinforces the need for AI literacy to become a core competency in healthcare and physicians are expected to have the ability to interpret AI-generated recommendations, ensure the ethical use of AI in clinical practice, and integrate AI tools seamlessly into patient care workflows.

However, despite the growing emphasis on AI integration in healthcare [20], a key gap remains in understanding the preconditions under which medical students develop a strong intention to adopt these technologies. Existing research has largely focused on factors that facilitate adoption [8, 21], yet it remains unclear which conditions are essential and non-compensable in shaping behavioral intentions. Prior studies have advanced knowledge on students’ perceptions and intentions regarding digital health and AI, including configurational analyses of AI adoption [8], the role of digital health competences and AI beliefs in evidence-based medicine [6], and the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on these perceptions [22]. While these studies provide important insights into determinants, configurations, and evolution of AI-related attitudes, they do not identify which conditions must be in place for strong adoption intentions to emerge.

This study builds on this stream of research while offering a distinct analytical perspective. Using the same survey dataset as part of a broader research program, prior work has examined (1) configurational pathways to AI adoption intentions using fuzzy-set Qualitative Comparative Analysis (fsQCA) [8], (2) the influence of digital health competences and AI beliefs on the practice of evidence-based medicine using structural equation modeling [6], and (3) changes in medical students’ perceptions and intentions in a pre- and post-COVID-19 context [23]. In contrast, the present study adopts a necessity-based perspective through Necessary Condition Analysis (NCA), complemented by fsQCA, to distinguish conditions that are required from those that are merely helpful. By introducing bottleneck analysis, we further identify the minimum levels that such conditions must reach to enable strong adoption intentions. This approach provides a more precise and actionable understanding of AI readiness, offering insights that cannot be derived from prior variance-based or configurational analyses alone.

This study therefore investigates medical students’ mindsets toward AI health technologies (AIHTs) by examining their behavioral intentions through two complementary causal lenses. First, using NCA, it identifies the essential factors that must be present for medical students to develop the intention to use AIHTs in their future medical practice. Unlike traditional variance-based approaches, NCA uncovers non-compensable conditions, providing a nuanced understanding of the determinants of behavioral intention [24]. The study thus addresses the following question: What are the necessary conditions for medical students to develop a strong intention to use AIHTs in their future practice?

Second, adopting a configurational perspective, this study examines how combinations of medical students’ capabilities, opportunities and motivations jointly influence their intentions. Using fsQCA, it identifies configurations of conditions that are sufficient to produce strong adoption [25]. This leads to a second research question: What combinations of conditions are sufficient for medical students to develop a strong intention to use AIHTs in their future practice?

Background

To better understand the adoption of AIHTs among medical students, this study draws on the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) and the Capabilities, Opportunities, Motivation, and Behavior (COM-B) model. The CFIR identifies individual characteristics as pivotal determinants in implementing evidence-based innovations, emphasizing the role of personal skills, motivations, and external conditions [26]. Complementing this, the COM-B model highlights how behavior is influenced by the interaction of capabilities (e.g., knowledge and experiential competences), opportunities (e.g., exposure to AI technologies), and motivation (e.g., belief in AI’s potential in healthcare) [27]. Figure 1 shows this integrated conceptual model, illustrating how both frameworks converge on shaping the intention to adopt AI.

Application of CFIR and COM-B frameworks to explain intention to adopt AI

Prior research has shown that experiential competences, such as hands-on engagement with dHealth tools, play a critical role in fostering positive attitudes and behavioral intentions toward AI adoption [21]. Experiential competences are particularly important for medical students, who must navigate increasingly complex digital systems while maintaining a patient-centered approach. These skills enable future physicians to apply AI tools effectively, such as predictive analytics and decision-support systems, in clinical settings [20, 28]. Despite this, evidence suggests that most medical students possess limited hands-on experience with AIHTs, highlighting a significant gap in current medical curricula [8, 23].

In addition to experiential competences, students’ perceptions of the role of AIHTs in future medical practice significantly shape their willingness to adopt these technologies. A positive perception of AI as a transformative tool can enhance students’ motivation to integrate AI into their workflows. For instance, students who view AI as augmenting clinical tasks, rather than replacing human decision-making, are more likely to embrace its use [29]. However, negative perceptions, often fueled by misconceptions or a lack of formal training, can hinder adoption efforts [20].

Moreover, these behavioral factors can be influenced by external disruptions, such as the COVID-19 pandemic. The pandemic served as a catalyst for the adoption of digital health technologies, accelerating familiarity with telehealth, mobile health apps, and AI-based tools. It also underscored the importance of integrating AIHTs into medical education to prepare students for a digitally driven healthcare environment [14, 30].

Based on the above theoretical and empirical background and following the “necessity causal perspective” that underlines this research [31], we developed a research model that relates medical students’ AI capabilities, opportunities and motivation to their behavioral intentions with regard to AI (Fig. 2).

From a more conventional causal perspective, that of “sufficiency”, the research model also posits that medical students’ AI adoption intentions will be influenced by their AI profile, that is, by the combination (or configuration) of their AI capabilities, opportunities and motivation [32, 33]. Thus, when associated to a strong intention to adopt AI, such AI profiles regroup causal conditions that are deemed “sufficient” to achieve the desired outcome [8].

Beyond these empirical studies, recent literature has also examined medical students’ attitudes toward AI and the integration of AI into medical curricula. Systematic reviews highlight a growing interest in AI among medical students, coupled with a perceived lack of formal training and preparedness (e.g., Ahsan, 2025). Empirical studies further show that exposure to AI-related content within the curriculum can positively influence students’ attitudes and perceived readiness to engage with these technologies (e.g. [34]), . In parallel, prior research in information systems and technology adoption has increasingly emphasized the value of necessity-based approaches, such as NCA, to identify conditions that must be present for desired outcomes to occur, complementing traditional variance-based methods. Together, these streams of research reinforce the need to better understand not only what influences AI adoption, but what is fundamentally required for it to occur.

Methods

Study context and population

This study draws on a dataset collected as part of a broader research program on medical students’ perceptions of digital health and AI. While this dataset has been used in prior studies addressing related questions [e.g., 6,8,21], the present analysis focuses specifically on identifying necessary conditions for AI adoption intentions. The study was conducted at a large Canadian medical school offering a five-year undergraduate medical curriculum. Although no formal AI training was integrated into the core curriculum, students were provided with opportunities to attend workshops and seminars on EMR systems, mobile technologies, AI applications, and ethical considerations related to dHealth technologies. The study targeted all 1,462 medical students enrolled at the institution, spanning all academic levels from preparatory year to final-year clerkship.

Data collection

Data was collected through an online survey administered in May 2023 (N = 177). The survey was distributed using the Qualtrics platform and promoted via institutional mailing lists and student association newsletters. Participation was voluntary, with no incentives provided. The study design and survey instrument were approved by the medical school’s ethics committee. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. The survey captured five key dimensions: demographic characteristics (e.g., age, gender, academic level); familiarity with and experimentation using AIHTs; perceptions of the importance of AIHTs education in the medical curriculum; beliefs about the role of AIHTs in future medical practice; and behavioral intentions to integrate AIHTs into future professional activities. Items were scored using five-point Likert scales (ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree”) and binary yes/no response formats. Note that AIHTs were considered in this study to include four types of technologies, that is, artificial intelligence-based, machine learning-based, big data-based and internet of things-based health technologies.

Questionnaire development and validation

No existing questionnaire was identified that adequately captures the variables of interest in this study. Consequently, a new instrument was developed and literately refined, drawing on validated measures used in related contexts (e.g., 8). The final questionnaire comprised 70 five-point Likert scale items and 8 dichotomous (yes/no) items measuring the intention to use AIHTs in future medical practice.

Unlike the other constructs, which were operationalized as reflective multi-item Likert scales, the outcome variable (intention to use AIHTs) was specified as an index composed of eight dichotomous items, each corresponding to a distinct clinical task (e.g., diagnosis, prognosis, image analysis). This operationalization follows a formative measurement logic, whereby each item captures a specific and non-interchangeable manifestation of intended AI use in practice. In this context, intentions are inherently task-dependent: medical students may express willingness to use AI for certain clinical activities but not for others. Aggregating these items into an index therefore provides a more behaviorally grounded and comprehensive representation of adoption intention across multiple domains of medical practice.

From a psychometric standpoint, index measures are not expected to exhibit high internal consistency, as their indicators are not interchangeable but collectively define the construct’s domain. Accordingly, the resulting index—ranging from 0 to 8—reflects the scope and diversity of intended AI use rather than the intensity of a single latent attitude. From a theoretical perspective, this approach is consistent with prior research emphasizing the task-specific nature of technology use intentions and their dependence on the fit between technologies and particular work activities [35].

To ensure content validity, the instrument was reviewed by a panel of 10 medical students who were not included in the main study sample. Details of the measurement items are provided in the Supplemental Material (Appendix A). Composite scores for the four reflective constructs (causal conditions) were computed by averaging item responses, whereas the index variable (outcome) was computed by summing the eight binary items (yes = 1, no = 0). No reverse coding was requested.

Data analyses

The survey data were first analyzed using descriptive statistics. Recommended correlational and factor-analytic statistical procedures were used to confirm the absence of multicollinearity and common method bias in the data [36, 37]. To address the first research question, NCA was applied to the sample. The NCA was conducted using Dul’s [31] “NCA” package on the R platform (version 3.3.1) to identify essential conditions for desired behavioral outcomes, offering insights into key factors shaping medical students’ intentions to use AIHTs. To address the second research question, fsQCA was applied. The fsQCA was conducted using Dusa’s [38] “QCA” package on the R platform (version 4.2.3).

Using both NCA and fsQCA offers a more complete understanding of what shapes medical students’ AI adoption intention. Following a causal logic of “necessity”, NCA helps us examine how individual factors such as the students’ AI capabilities, opportunities and motivation directly condition their intentions. In contrast, following a causal logic of “sufficiency”, QCA looks at how different combinations of factors work together to produce strong intentions [29]. This dual approach allows us to capture both the specific effects of single factors and the broader patterns that emerge when multiple influences interact.

Ethics approvals

This study was reviewed and approved by the [name of University to be inserted upon acceptance]’s Research Ethics Committee for Health Sciences and Society (approval number: CERSES-19-108-D). All participants provided informed consent electronically prior to beginning the survey. The consent form included a detailed description of the study’s objectives, procedures, potential risks and benefits, and emphasized the voluntary nature of participation, including the right to withdraw at any time without consequence.

Necessity analysis

The NCA method differs from - and thus complements - traditional causal methods (e.g., regression, structural equation modeling) and configurational methods (e.g., QCA) in that it is based on a causal logic of “necessity” rather than “sufficiency” [31]. Using this alternative view of causality, one seeks to identify the degree to which certain “conditions” (say, having a strong belief in the role of AIHTs in the future of medicine) are “necessary” (but not “sufficient”) to achieve a certain “outcome” (say, having a strong intention to use AIHTs in one’s future medical practice) [43]. Note here that a sample size of 50 to 100 is needed for NCA analyses to detect necessary conditions with an effect size greater than 0.30, that is, a ‘moderate’ to ‘large’ effect, and with a level of power greater than the 0.80 threshold [31].

The NCA method starts by drawing a scatterplot between each condition (X) and the outcome (Y). In our case, this step produced four such scatterplots, two of which are presented as examples in Fig. 3. Next, two “ceiling lines” are drawn in each scatterplot, i.e. the Ceiling Envelopment- Free Disposal Hull (CE-FDH) and the Ceiling Regression-Free Disposal Hull (CR-FDH), thus separating the “ceiling zone” (i.e. the empty space in the upper-left corner where a high degree of X is necessary for a high degree of Y) from the “scope” (i.e. the full space of actual and potential observations), and doing so with as much accuracy as possible. In this study, the CR-FDH was used as ceiling line because the conditions and outcome are continuous rather than discrete [24].

Example of the scatterplots drawn by the NCA

Given the chosen ceiling technique (CR-FDH) and as presented in Table 5, the NCA procedure then calculates, for each condition, the “effect size” and significance level of this effect (“p-value”) to determine if the condition is truly “necessary” to attain the desired outcome [44]. Ranging from 0 to 1, the effect size is calculated as the quotient of the ceiling zone over the scope, with an effect size less than 0.1 being “small”, between 0.1 and 0.3 being “medium”, between 0.3 and 0.5 being “large”, and greater than 0.5 being “very large” [24]. Moreover, for a condition to be considered necessary, its effect size must not only be greater than 0.1 but its p-value must also be less than 0.05, as the ceiling zone as observed may be due to random chance [45]. Returning to Table 3 with this last necessity criterion in mind, one finds that two out of the four conditions are indeed necessary for medical students to have a strong intention to use AIHTs in their future medical practice (thus confirming Hypotheses 3 and 4).

The first finding is that a strong belief in AIHTs’ role in the future of medicine is the most important necessary condition (NC) for students to eventually use these technologies in their medical practice. This would behove medical educators to instill this belief by developing their students’ knowledge of - and experience with - AIHTs. Another finding is that a high degree of importance accorded by medical students to AIHTs within their curriculum has emerged as a second NC. Indeed, medical educators must now answer this demand by redesigning the curriculum to make greater room for dHealth technologies in general, and AI-based technologies in particular, including not only generative AI but also machine learning and big data analytics as they actually and potentially apply to the practice of medicine.

The preceding results may be further nuanced, as the NCA method qualifies the necessity of a condition not only “in kind” (a high level of X is necessary to attain a high level of Y) but also “in degree” (a certain level of X is necessary to attain a certain level of Y). Returning to Fig. 3, this is done through the Y = aX +b regression equation where a is the ceiling line (CR-FDH) and b is the intercept. As presented in Table 6, a “bottleneck” table is thus produced, in which a specific level of the outcome is associated to a specific level of the four conditions. For instance, to attain a 60% level in the Intention to use AIHTs in future medical practice, the minimum level required of the Role of AIHTs in future medical tasks is 30.6%, whereas the minimum level required of the Importance of AIHTs in the medical curriculum is 10.6%.

Sufficiency analysis

To identify medical students’ AI profiles associated with varying levels of intention to use AIHTs in their future practice, we conducted a fuzzy-set Qualitative Comparative Analysis [46]. As a second-generation configurational method, fsQCA uses Boolean algebra to determine combinations of factors that can produce the same outcome, wherein each factor is deemed to be a fuzzy set [46]. By accounting for equifinality [25], fsQCA allows for multiple configurations of factors to yield similar outcomes, providing insight into how different combinations of AI capabilities, opportunities and motivation among medica students shape their intention to adopt AI. In this study, equifinality suggests that students with different AI profiles can exhibit similarly strong intentions to use AIHTs, depending on how individual conditions are configured within each profile. This method also accommodates causal asymmetry, meaning that AI profiles associated with strong intentions to adopt AI may differ from those linked with weak intentions [25]. Following fsQCA guidelines [47], we completed the calibration (Table 3), necessity analysis, and sufficiency analysis of the four configurational elements (Fig. 2), using the ‘QCA’ software package (v.4.2.3) in R [38].

With regard to calibration, each variable in fsQCA is considered as a “fuzzy set”, i.e. as having different degrees of membership into the set [46]. Hence, each of the five research variables must be calibrated into a fuzzy set by identifying three points of membership: full membership, full non-membership, and cross-over. Full membership signifies a “high” degree of intensity in any of the variables, full non-membership signifies a “low” degree of intensity, and cross-over is the point of maximum uncertainty with regards to a variable’s degree of intensity. Given that the nature of our measures causes the distribution of certain variables to be skewed, percentiles were used for data calibration as recommended [48]. The top quartile (75th percentile) and bottom quartile (25th percentile) values were used as cutoffs for full membership and non-membership, and the median (50th percentile) as the cross-over point (see Table 3). Moreover, 0.5 membership scores in the calibrated data, meaning cases neither in nor out, were handled in the fsQCA analysis by adding 0.001 to the score as recommended [25]. Note also that in deriving the configurational solution and on the basis of prior research [6, 8], the fsQCA analysis hypothesized a positive directional effect for each of the four configurational elements (i.e. the presence of a high-intensity element implies the presence of a high-intensity outcome).

In the sufficiency analysis, as the following fsQCA step, the analysis identifies configurations (AI profiles) that are “sufficient” to yield the desired outcome [46]. Employing Boolean algebra and counterfactual analysis, fsQCA reduces the truth table into three solution types: parsimonious, intermediate, and complex. Due to its complexity and limited interpretive value, the complex solution (which generates all possible configurations) is simplified into parsimonious and intermediate solutions. The intermediate solution, derived through counterfactual analysis of both complex and parsimonious solutions, identifies “core” conditions (those in the parsimonious solution) and “peripheral” conditions (those in the intermediate solution only) [46]. Core conditions exert strong influence and are essential to any configuration, while peripheral conditions have a weaker effect and may be substituted or omitted. Combining the parsimonious and intermediate solutions is recommended to pinpoint core and peripheral conditions in configurations [25].

AI profiles for weak intentions to use AIHTs in future medical practice

In addition to equifinality, the configurational approach taken here allows for causal asymmetry, i.e., the possibility that the causal conditions for the presence of the preferred outcome will differ from those for its absence [25]. As this approach allows for nonlinearity in causation, the same configurational element may have different causal roles within different configurations. In demonstrating causal asymmetry and as shown in Table 7, further results of the fsQCA analysis identify three AI profiles (configurations) associated with a weak intention on the part of medical students to use AIHTs in their future practice (WI1, WI2, WI3). Here, the lack of a strong belief in the role of AIHTs in future medical tasks” is the one core condition shared by all three weak-intention configurations. This last causal condition may be considered as necessarily “refraining” medical students from forming a strong intention to adopt AI, and thus as a reconfirmation of Hypothesis 4.

Discussion

This study provides new insights into the factors influencing medical students’ intention to integrate AIHTs into their future medical practice. By employing both NCA and fsQCA, this research identified key conditions that are indispensable for fostering strong behavioral intentions among medical students.

Our findings reveal that a strong belief in the role of AIHTs in the future of medicine consistently emerged as the most critical necessary condition. However, belief alone does not equate to a positive attitude or readiness for AI integration in future practice. Students may recognize AI’s potential yet feel unprepared to use it effectively. This highlights the need for structured AI literacy and competency-building within medical education. Educators should leverage this belief by incorporating AI-focused coursework covering clinical decision support, predictive analytics, and ethical considerations. Additionally, immersive training using augmented reality-based simulations could provide dynamic and adaptive environments where students can interact with AI tools, analyze their performance, and better understand their limitations. Such technologies allow medical trainees to experiment with AI in scenarios where it may fail, helping them develop a more nuanced appreciation of AI’s strengths and weaknesses while increasing their confidence in real-world applications.

Another key finding is the increased importance placed on the integration of AIHTs into medical curricula in the post-COVID-19 period, reflecting students’ growing awareness of AI’s relevance to their future practice. While familiarity with AIHTs alone is insufficient, structured interdisciplinary collaboration and real-world exposure can bridge the gap between theoretical knowledge and application. Medical schools should encourage collaborations with AI and data science experts, integrate AI-assisted decision-making into clinical rotations, and emphasize human-AI collaboration to ensure that future physicians can critically assess AI outputs rather than rely on them passively. Further, as AI reshapes medical specialties, career-oriented discussions and adaptability training should be prioritized to prepare students for the evolving healthcare landscape.

A final finding lies in the uncovering of two equifinal AI profiles, that is, two configurations of medical students’ AI capabilities, opportunities and motivation that are both associated with a strong intention on their part to use AIHTs in their future practice. For the most prevalent AI profile, the condition of students’ strong belief in the role of AI in their future medical tasks was sufficient, together with the condition that they have a positive attitude toward the importance of AI in the medical curriculum. For the second AI profile, a high level of familiarity with AI, a low level of experimentation with AI and a positive attitude towards the importance of AI in the medical curriculum were the three conditions sufficient for students to have a strong intention to adopt AI in their future medical practice.

An important and somewhat counterintuitive finding of this study is that familiarity with AIHTs, in combination with the absence of experimentation, can still be associated with strong adoption intentions (SI2 configuration). This result challenges the assumption, embedded in the COM-B framework, that capability and opportunity are necessarily required for behavioral intention. While prior research has emphasized the role of hands-on experience in fostering confidence and adoption [8, 21], our findings suggest that intention may develop even in the absence of direct experimentation, provided that students possess sufficient familiarity and hold strong beliefs about the relevance of AI in their future practice. One possible explanation is that familiarity may be acquired through indirect exposure (e.g., coursework, media, peer discussions), which can shape expectations without requiring direct use. This points to a distinction between experiential readiness and cognitive readiness, where the latter may be sufficient to trigger behavioral intention.

Contribution

All in all, both the NCA (necessity) and fsQCA (sufficiency) results suggest that familiarity with and hands-on experimentation using AIHTs alone are insufficient; they must be complemented by curriculum-wide initiatives that instill the perceived importance of AIHTs in clinical practice. Compared to prior research, this study offers a unique perspective by focusing on the necessary conditions for medical students’ behavioral intentions to adopt AIHTs, rather than variance-based predictors. While prior research emphasized experiential competences and exposure to dHealth tools as facilitators of adoption (e.g., 8,20), the present study highlights belief in the role of AIHTs and their integration into medical curricula as indispensable conditions. Similarly, the study extends insights from Park et al. [29], which explored discipline-specific perceptions of AI, by adopting a broader approach to general medical education.

This study also builds upon previous findings, in which the causal relationship between medical students’ perceptions and intentions with regards to dHealth technologies was assessed [22, 23]. While similarly underscoring the increasing familiarity with and interest in dHealth tools, this study uniquely employs NCA to identify thresholds that must be met for behavioral intention formation. Unlike previous studies which mostly used regression or structural equation modeling (SEM) approaches to explain variance in medical students’ attitudes and intentions towards AI (e.g. [6, 49]), , the present study seeks to uncover which conditions are absolutely essential for the intention to adopt AIHTs, offering a complementary yet distinct perspective. Drawing on the CFIR and COM-B frameworks, this article further contextualizes its findings within the broader theoretical landscape, demonstrating how motivation and belief systems interact with capabilities and opportunities to influence behavior.

The findings of this study highlight critical areas for curriculum reform and provide actionable opportunities for medical educators. First, given that belief in the role of AI in future medical practice emerges as a necessary condition, educational interventions should prioritize shaping students’ perceptions of AI’s relevance. This can be achieved through the integration of clinical case studies, real-world applications, and physician testimonials illustrating the value of AI in diagnosis, treatment, and patient management. Such approaches can also help address students’ concerns and misconceptions, ensuring that AI is viewed as a complement to, rather than a replacement for, human decision-making. Second, the perceived importance of AI within the medical curriculum also constitutes a necessary condition, suggesting that AI should be embedded as a core component of medical training rather than treated as an optional topic. Medical schools should consider introducing structured AI modules across different stages of the curriculum, covering both technical aspects (e.g., machine learning applications) and ethical considerations (e.g., data privacy and responsible use). Third, while structured exposure to AI tools through simulations, workshops, or internships can enhance familiarity and confidence, our findings challenge the assumption that hands-on experimentation is a prerequisite for developing strong adoption intentions. Early-stage integration may therefore rely on lower-resource approaches, such as lectures, simulations, or case-based discussions, to build familiarity and shape beliefs before scaling up experiential training. Finally, the identification of multiple sufficient configurations indicates that there is no single pathway to fostering AI adoption. Educational strategies should thus remain flexible and tailored to different student profiles, combining cognitive (knowledge), experiential (exposure), and motivational (beliefs) components in different ways.

Limitations and conclusion

While this study provides important contributions, it is not without limitations. First, there are the intrinsic limitations of the NCA and fsQCA methods in terms of their respective explanatory power (e.g. [24, 25]), . With regard to fsQCA in particular, results can be affected by researchers’ decisions regarding the calibration of variables and by their choices regarding frequency and consistency cutoffs [48]. In this respect, while we applied the recommended guidelines regarding calibration, frequency and consistency cutoffs [24, 50], we also conducted additional analyses with similar results (cf. Appendix B in the Supplemental Material), which provide evidence of the robustness of our configurational results [51].

The relatively low survey response rate (12%) may introduce selection bias, as students with a pre-existing interest in AIHTs might have been more likely to participate. Additionally, the study was conducted at a single Canadian medical school, which may limit the generalizability of findings to other educational contexts or countries. Future research could explore cross-institutional comparisons that examine the long-term impact of AI training on clinical practice.

While interest in AIHTs is growing, meaningful integration into the formal medical curriculum remains a challenge [52]. Relying on voluntary workshops is insufficient; structured exposure through required courses and clinical rotations is essential. This requires institutional support, including curriculum reform and policies that address medico-legal concerns, such as liability protections for AI use. Highlighting real-world successes can also help legitimize AIHTs and foster broader institutional acceptance. Without such support, student curiosity may fade, limiting the impact of digital transformation in medical education.
