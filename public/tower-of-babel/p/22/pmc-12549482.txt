Acne Conglobata: Understanding This Rare Form of Acne and Management Strategies
Authors: Angela Yen Moore, Kara K. Hurley
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Acne conglobata is a severe, often eruptive, form of acne included in the follicular occlusion tetrad along with hidradenitis suppurativa, pilonidal cysts, and dissecting cellulitis of the scalp. The pathogenesis is complex and likely multifactorial with contribution from both extrinsic and intrinsic factors. Studies have shown a slight male predominance. Acne conglobata is clinically characterized by large, purulent nodules and abscesses, coalescing into large masses or plaques. It is often recalcitrant to common acne therapies, making management difficult for clinicians. Isotretinoin is considered the first-line therapy but must be started at a conservative dosage as it can paradoxically worsen flares. Biologic agents, such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF) and interleukin (IL)-17 inhibitors, along with photodynamic therapy (PDT) and carbon dioxide (CO2) laser therapy have been reported as effective. Large prospective studies focusing on the management of acne conglobata are lacking. Acne conglobata often causes severe scarring, creating a large psychosocial burden for the patient and negatively impacting quality of life.

Introduction

Acne conglobata is a rare, severe form of nodulocystic acne that carries a significant impact on the patient’s quality of life. It is characterized by purulent nodules often coalescing into large plaques. Acne conglobata is considered a component of the follicular occlusion tetrad along with hidradenitis suppurativa, pilonidal cysts, and dissecting cellulitis of the scalp. This severe form of acne is often recalcitrant to common therapies, making management difficult. In this narrative review, we aim to highlight the epidemiology, pathogenesis, clinical features, and treatments for acne conglobata. Epidemiology and Pathogenesis

The prevalence of acne conglobata is difficult to assess given the rarity of the condition. A 1992 population-based study estimated the prevalence of acne conglobata in 1974 to be 832,000 for women and 1,319,000 for men ages 15 to 44 with a male to female ratio of 1.6:1 [1]. Genetic factors are believed to be implicated, supported by a case of familial acne conglobata across three generations [2]. Additionally, acne conglobata has been documented to have higher prevalence in patients with Klinefelter’s syndrome [3]. The Notch signaling pathway has been implicated in the pathogenesis of hidradenitis suppurativa (HS). Mutations lead to epidermal and follicular abnormalities in mouse skin that histopathologically resemble to those in human hidradenitis suppurativa [4]. Additionally, Notch regulates cell fate during epidermal differentiation and is associated with cyst formation [5]. The γ-secretase complex is an important component of the Notch signaling cascade. A recent study published two novel mutations in the NCSTN gene, a subunit of the γ-secretase complex, in a large family with hidradenitis suppurativa and acne conglobata [6].

Follicular occlusion is a crucial triggering mechanism in acne conglobata, hidradenitis suppurativa, pilonidal cysts, and dissecting cellulitis of the scalp, though each entity carries distinct clinical features, and exact pathogenesis is poorly understood [7]. It has been suggested that Cutibacterium acnes, a bacteria implicated in the pathogenesis of acne, increases expression of Toll-like receptors (TLRs) 2 and 4 which could play an essential role in acne-linked inflammation [8]. A genomic DNA analysis of 191 patients with acne vulgaris and 75 controls investigated the impact of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) of the TLR4 gene in acne. A stepwise multiple logistic regression analysis was done with specifically acne conglobata as a dependent variable. Independent variables included gender, family history of acnes, increases of disease intensity over time, intake of any therapy, skin isolation of C. acnes, and carriage of any TLR4 gene. It was found that being a carrier of any TLR4 gene SNP was linked with a protective role against acne conglobata. All other independent variables had no significant impact [9]. A case has been reported of Cutibacterium avidum cultured from a lesion of a patient with acne conglobata, who improved significantly on IV moxifloxacin and oral isotretinoin therapy [10]. C. avidum is largely commensal and part of the skin microbiota but has been shown to cause surgical site and other opportunistic infections [11]. The role of C. avidum and other bacteria in the pathogenesis acne conglobata could be an area of future study.

The role of the gut microbiome in the pathogenesis of acne has recently become a topic of interest. Various studies have demonstrated that patients with acne have distinct gut microbiota, though no studies specifically looking at acne conglobata were found [12–14]. It has been shown that changes in the abundance of a specific microbe can modify host gene expression, suggesting that manipulation of the microbiome could be therapeutically useful [15]. Nolan et. al demonstrated that treatment of acne with isotretinoin correlated with shifts in C. acnes strain composition within the follicular microbiome in patients who achieved 2-grade improvement in physician global assessment score [16].

Extrinsic factors can also contribute to the pathogenesis of acne conglobata. Anabolic-androgen steroids such as testosterone are commonly used to enhance physical performance and are known to cause acne. A review by Melnik et. al found that acne conglobata can be triggered by anabolic-androgen steroids even in the absence of a prior history of acne [17]. Vitamin D levels may also play a role. A prospective study in Egypt found significantly lower 25-hydroxycholecalciferol levels in of 90 patients with acne vulgaris compared to 60 age-sex matched controls. Additionally, there was a significant inverse relation between vitamin D level and severity of acne vulgaris before treatment, with serum levels significantly increasing after isotretinoin treatment [18]. Vitamin D2 has been reported to be therapeutic for acne conglobata, but larger studies are needed to establish a definitive relationship between severe acne and vitamin D [19].

Clinical Features

The hallmark of acne conglobata is severe, purulent inflammatory nodules and abscesses, which coalesce into large masses or plaques. Lesions can involve the face or trunk. Patients often have a history of mild to moderate acne with eruptive onset of severe lesions. Acne conglobata often causes extensive, disfiguring scarring which leads to a significant psychological burden for the patient. Systemic symptoms are not characteristic of acne conglobata. Acne fulminans is a separate entity defined by rapid onset of severe suppurative to ulceronecrotic lesions, often with hemorrhagic crust. Systemic symptoms such as fever, arthralgias, myalgias, hepatosplenomegaly, malaise, and osteolytic bone lesions are typically present, but are not mandatory [20]. Comorbid Conditions

Acne conglobata can occur as a manifestation of PAPA (pyogenic arthritis, pyoderma gangrenosum, and acne) syndrome, an autosomal dominant autoinflammatory condition. Some patients with PAPA syndrome carry mutations in the PSTPIP1 gene, which interacts with the pyrin protein and is integral for proper inflammatory signaling. Other features of PAPA syndrome include pyogenic arthritis and pyoderma gangrenosum. PSTPIP1 dysfunction is also implicated in PAPASH (pyogenic arthritis, pyoderma gangrenosum, acne, and suppurative hidradenitis) and PASH (pyoderma gangrenosum, acne, and suppurative hidradenitis) syndromes [21]. Necrotizing scleritis is another possible comorbidity of acne conglobata. Fremlin et al. report a case of necrotizing scleritis in a patient with acne conglobata which resolved with isotretinoin. Necrotizing scleritis is a severe, sight-threatening condition that requires early recognition and management by ophthalmology [22].

Treatments

Acne conglobata is often refractory to treatments for acne vulgaris such as topical tretinoin, topical antibiotics, and systemic antibiotics. There is an absence of large-scale studies evaluating the most efficacious treatment for acne conglobata. Orally administered isotretinoin is currently considered the first-line treatment, with lasting effects possible after treatment is completed [23]. Leyden et al. demonstrated Propionibacterium populations globally decreasing in the skin microbiome during treatment with isotretinoin but noted a certain Propionibacterium community emerge and remain after isotretinoin treatment. They suggested that isotretinoin creates a “population bottleneck” that selects for “healthy” Propionibacterium communities [24]. While isotretinoin is effective for the treatment of severe acne, it is well established that isotretinoin initiation can induce an acne fulminans-like flare. It is suggested that the risk can be mitigated by starting at doses of < 0.5 mg/kg for the first 3–4 weeks of therapy [25]. Guidelines for the treatment of acne fulminans suggest starting oral corticosteroids (0.5–1.0 mg/kg) for at least 2–4 weeks until hemorrhagic crusts or erosions resolve followed by a combination of corticosteroids and isotretinoin at 0.1 mg/kg for at least 4 weeks. Corticosteroids can slowly be tapered over 1–2 months pending clinical response [20]. Daoud et al. however, argue that isotretinoin should be avoided entirely in patients with acne conglobata and concomitant hidradenitis suppurativa due to worsening of acne [26].

Orally administered dapsone for the treatment of acne has fallen out of favor because of systemic side effects including hemolytic anemia, peripheral neuropathy, methemoglobinemia, and agranulocytosis. It is still employed as an effective treatment for numerous neutrophilic dermatoses with close laboratory monitoring. Topical dapsone gel has gained popularity as a second- or third-line agent. Orally administered dapsone has been reported to successfully treat acne fulminans both as monotherapy and in combination with corticosteroids, though literature evaluating dapsone for the treatment of acne conglobata is lacking [27–29].

Aminolevulinic acid photodynamic therapy (ALA-PDT) has recently been highlighted as a safe and effective off-label treatment for moderate to severe acne, though the mechanism is poorly understood. Liu et al. proposed that ALA-PDT augments the inflammatory response by cyclooxygenase-2 (COX2)-mediated M1 macrophage polarization [30]. In a prospective, open, parallel-arm study of 75 patients with facial acne conglobata, 35 patients received PDT with topical 5% ALA and red light every 10 days for a month. The control group received a Chinese herbal medicine mask plus red light. The treatment group had a statistically significant reduced number of pustules, nodules, and cysts after the first and the second PDT treatments. The treatment group had a cure rate of 87.50% (p < 0.01) compared to a cure rate of 62.86% in the control group [31].

Given the recalcitrant nature of acne conglobata, a combination of treatments may be required to achieve remission. Single case reports of successful combination treatment of acne conglobata with PDT in conjunction with surgical deroofing, carbon dioxide (CO2) laser, and adalimumab have been published [32, 33]. A case of clearance with CO2 laser in conjunction with topical tretinoin has also been reported [34]. It must be noted that the case reports lack statistical relevance, and larger studies are needed. CO2 laser may also be useful in improving the cosmetic appearance of scarring from prior inflammatory nodules.

Newer, more targeted biologics have been reported to successfully treat acne conglobata. Tumor necrosis factor (TNF) inhibitors are a well-established treatment for hidradenitis suppurativa which shares a similar pathophysiology to acne conglobata (i.e., follicular occlusion tetrad). Case reports have emerged suggesting TNF inhibitors as a promising therapy refractory acne conglobata, though large studies are lacking. The first reported case of adalimumab monotherapy was an 18-year-old man who failed therapy with doxycycline, oral isotretinoin monotherapy, and isotretinoin in combination with prednisolone followed by dapsone. The patient achieved clearance of clearance of nodular lesions after 12 weeks of therapy with sustained efficacy after 12 months of twice monthly maintenance dosing [35]. Another case reported clearance with adalimumab monotherapy in a 26-year-old man with painful, recurrent, inflammatory cysts on his upper back and neck refractory topical and systemic antibiotics, isotretinoin, dapsone, and trimethoprim and prednisolone combination [36]. A 2015 retrospective study of 11 patients with recalcitrant acne conglobata treated with TNF inhibitors found efficacy in 7 of 10 patients treated with adalimumab, with clinical response noted within the first 3 months. One of the seven cases became unresponsive after months of therapy and subsequently became unresponsive to etanercept and certolizumab. One case treated with infliximab did not improve [37]. It must be noted that TNF inhibitors have been reported to cause paradoxical acneiform eruptions in select cases, particularly when used for the treatment of inflammatory bowel disease [38]. Larger longitudinal studies are needed to validate efficacy and persistence of remission following cessation of therapy.

Secukinumab is an interleukin (IL)-17 inhibitor US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved for the dermatologic indications of plaque psoriasis and HS. Jiang et al. report a recent case of a 21-year-old man with acne conglobata refractory to orally administered minocycline, isotretinoin, and adalimumab that cleared with secukinumab. Complete resolution was noted after 8 weeks of therapy. The patient was maintained on the medication for 3 months after titration and remained clear for 5 months after discontinuation of the medication [39]. It should be noted that another IL-17 inhibitor, bimekizumab, has recently been approved by the FDA for the treatment of HS. Given the pathogenic link between HS and acne conglobata and the well-documented efficacy of IL-17 inhibitors for HS, larger studies investigating IL-17 inhibitors for acne conglobata are warranted.

Conclusion

Acne conglobata is rare condition that can lead to disfiguring scars with significant psychological impact to the patient. Prompt diagnosis and initiation of treatment are imperative for best outcomes. Acne conglobata is often persistent and recalcitrant to common therapies, with recent case reports describing new, effective therapies. Most of the literature on acne conglobata consists of case reports and case series. Larger studies investing epidemiology, pathogenesis, and treatment are needed to fully understand this complex, severe variant of acne.
