Five Grammar Terms to Improve Practice and Preservice Pedagogy in Speech, Language, and Hearing Sciences
Authors: Janna B. Oetting
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Purpose:The purpose of this article is to encourage the adoption of five grammar terms within the field of speech, language, and hearing sciences. The terms are needed to accurately describe and distinguish between the grammars of children with and without grammar weaknesses across the many dialects of English children learn as either a monolingual, multilectal, or multilingual speaker.Conclusion:Adopting the five grammar terms transforms how one provides clinical services to children across dialects of English and changes how one guides preservice students in speech, language, and hearing sciences to inclusive practices related to the assessment and treatment of grammar.

Fields must evolve as new findings are discovered. One important facilitator of change is the development and refinement of terms used within a field. As examples, classification accuracy along with sensitivity and specificity are terms introduced in the early 2000s that have helped transform how assessment tools are selected by clinicians and diagnostic methods are taught to preservice students (Dollaghan, 2007).

The purpose of this article is to encourage the adoption of five grammar terms to improve clinical practice and preservice pedagogy within the field of speech, language, and hearing sciences. The need for these terms has increased as the United States, our clients, and our preservice students have become more linguistically diverse and vocal about the field's professional lexicon (e.g., Brea-Spahn & Fabiano-Smith, 2023; Washington & Iruka, 2025).

Current Grammar Terms

Many of the existing terms within the field were developed to talk about standard varieties of English; in the United States, I refer to these dialects as General American English (GAE) to distinguish them from the English varieties spoken in other countries. However, GAE is only one of many English dialects spoken in the United States, and terms created for this dialect can be misnomers when applied to others. As an example, the term third-person singular –s is typically used to describe the morpheme that attaches to a verb when the subject is a third-person singular noun or pronoun (e.g., Dan hikes, she swims). This term works for GAE, but several other dialects of English, including African American English (AAE) and Southern White English (SWE), allow this morpheme to be produced with first-, second-, and third-person plural subjects (e.g., I brings, you tells, they comes). Thus, a more inclusive and accurate term for this morpheme across dialects of English is verbal –s (i.e., the –s morpheme that attaches to verbs).

Current Practice and Pedagogy

Current practice and pedagogy can be illustrated by a funnel with an invisible filter (see Figure 1). The top of the funnel represents the many English dialects children learn as either a monolingual, multilectal, or multilingual speaker. In the context of this article, monolingual English speakers learn and produce one dialect of English (e.g., GAE, AAE, SWE). Multilectal English speakers learn and produce two or more dialects of English (e.g., AAE and GAE, AAE and SWE, SWE and GAE). Multilingual English speakers learn and produce a dialect of English that is influenced by another language(s), often because they learned English either as an additional language or at the same time as one or more other languages (e.g., Spanish and English, Vietnamese and English). English speakers who are both multilectal and multilingual also exist as in the case of children who learn another language in addition to English and two or more dialects of one or more of the languages they know (e.g., Mexican Spanish and Caribbean Spanish for Spanish, AAE and GAE for English). Studies of speakers who are both multilectal and multilingual are needed, and fortunately, some work is beginning to appear in the literature (e.g., Berry, 2025; Gatlin-Nash et al., 2021; Lee-James & Washington, 2018; Privette & Fabiano, 2024; for an example in psychology, see Antoniou et al., 2016).

GAE funnel that limits scientific precision and perpetuates deficit views within practice and pedagogy. GAE = General American English.

The important point for this article is that many dialects of English represented at the top of the funnel make use of a wider range of grammatical forms and functions than found in GAE. The bottom of the funnel represents the field's use of terms filtered for GAE that are typically used in clinical practice and when teaching preservice students about child language development, disorders, assessment, and treatment. Use of GAE-centric terms in practice and when teaching restricts what clinicians and preservice students know, assess, and treat when serving children. When terms are not inclusive of all English dialects, they lack the scientific precision needed to fully understand every child's linguistic strengths and needs. Noninclusive terms also perpetuate deficit views of dialects that differ from GAE.

Terms to Adopt

The five recommended terms are the following: dialect general overt, dialect specific overt, syntactically rich zero, syntactically empty zero, and camouflaged. As will be discussed, these forms are typically not used in current practice or preservice pedagogy when child language development, disorders, assessment, and treatment are discussed. The five recommended terms were created from studies of children with and without developmental language disorder (DLD) within the context of rural and urban southern dialects of AAE and rural dialects of SWE. Like other dialects at the top of the funnel, AAE and SWE contain grammar forms and functions not found in GAE. Thus, new terms are needed to accurately describe and distinguish between the grammars of the children with and without DLD within these dialects. The five terms have also played a role in the development of dialect-informed, strategic scoring systems, and dialect discovery worksheets that can be used for assessment and treatment when a child presents with DLD or with any other condition that may lead to weak grammar abilities (Moland & Oetting, 2025; Oetting, 2024; Oetting et al., 2022; Oetting & Gregory-Martin, in press). Although designed for AAE and SWE, these terms work well for learners of all dialects of English, including GAE. These terms are also relevant for adults, although clinical practice and preservice pedagogy for adults are outside the scope of this article.

Dialect General Overt

Dialect general overt forms are perceived by listeners and appear in many, if not all, dialects of English, including AAE, GAE, and SWE. As such, when heard or read within a transcript, these forms are uninformative as to the dialect of the speaker. Given that they appear in many dialects of English, I also refer to them as English general overt forms. Examples include copula and auxiliary am, is, are, was, and were; regular past tense −ed on the verb “mowed”; irregular past tense on the verb “saw”; and verbal –s on the verb “mows.” Historically, these forms have been referred to as standard, mainstream, noncontrastive, and GAE—terms that carry meaning only when GAE or another standard dialect of English is referenced. As a replacement, the term dialect general overt allows one to talk about these forms as part of any dialect of English and without reference to a dialect a child may not speak.

Dialect Specific Overt

Dialect specific overt forms are perceived by listeners and appear in some, but not all, dialects of English. Given this, I also refer to these forms as English specific overt to indicate that they are specific to some, but not all, speakers of English. Examples include the multifunctional ain't, habitual be (e.g., he be running, she be so scary), preterite had (e.g., I fell and then she had called my dad), and several past tense (e.g., drunk, brung, fount, turnt, seen, ated) and past participle (e.g., broke, ate, dranken, tooken) forms. Historically, these forms have been combined with zero forms and referred to as nonstandard, nonmainstream, vernacular, and contrastive—terms that again carry meaning only when GAE or another standard dialect of English is referenced. As a replacement, the term dialect specific overt allows one to talk about children's use of these forms within the context of their respective dialects. The new term also helps highlight the overt nature of these forms, distinguishing them from zero forms.

Syntactically Rich Zero

Syntactically rich zero forms are phonetically silent because they have no audible pronunciation. Although not perceived by listeners, these zero forms serve grammatical functions and are sometimes referred to as covert markers or nonovert markers of grammar (Green, 2019). As an example from AAE, GAE, and SWE, the zero relativizer, which is denoted with the symbol Ø in the utterance The book Ø Matt read was good, is syntactically rich and functions as part of the utterance's subordinate clause even though it is not audible. Consider also the zero past tense form of the verb “hit” in the two utterances below.

Speaker A: I hitØ the ball.

Speaker B: Did you hit a homerun?

In the first utterance, Speaker A produces the verb “hit” with a zero marked past tense form, denoted as hitØ. Although not audible, Speaker B understands that Speaker A is talking about an act of hitting that occurred in the past, and the speaker follows with a question that marks the past tense on the auxiliary did (Guasti, 2017). In this exchange, a follow-up question without past tense marking as in Do you hit a homerun would have been ungrammatical and nonsensible. As shown, the zero marker on the verb hitØ serves a grammatical function even though it is not audible.

Whereas GAE has a small inventory of syntactically rich, phonetically silent zero forms, other English dialects have many more. In AAE and SWE, for example, syntactically rich, phonetically silent zero forms can be produced with several verbs in past tense (e.g., yesterday, he walkØ) and verbal –s (e.g., today she bikeØ) contexts and in some copula and auxiliary be (e.g., they Ø cold, you Ø studying) contexts. The frequencies of syntactically rich, phonetically silent zero forms vary by dialect, with AAE containing more of these zero forms than SWE and with AAE and SWE containing more of these zero forms than GAE. Depending on the dialect, frequencies of these zero forms also vary by linguistic context. Using AAE as an example, clauses supporting verbal –s morphemes contain higher frequencies of zero forms than clauses supporting past tense morphemes (e.g., Green, 2019). Also, syntactically rich, phonetically silent zero forms in AAE are more frequent in present tense copula and auxiliary be (i.e., is, are) clauses than in past tense copula and auxiliary be (i.e., was, were) clauses (e.g., Roy et al., 2013) and more frequent in second-person (i.e., are) clauses than in third-person singular (i.e., is) clauses (e.g., Roy et al., 2013; Wyatt, 1996). Although more research is needed, syntactically rich zero forms have been documented in studies of children aged 5 years and older (Oetting et al., 2022).

Syntactically Empty Zero

Syntactically empty zero forms are omissions that lack syntactic content. Across dialects of English, these forms are produced by typically developing toddlers when they are in the very early stages of grammar development. Consider the early utterances of Adam, Eve, and Sarah from Roger Brown's (1973) corpus. At 18 months, Eve produced grammatical omissions in the utterances it break (line 36) and Eve lunch (line 384); across all dialects of English, these utterances would be viewed as containing omissions if spoken by an 18-month-old. As children age and their linguistic systems become more complex, omissions decrease in frequency and are replaced by overt forms and syntactically rich zero forms that are produced at dialect-appropriate frequencies and within dialect-appropriate linguistic contexts.

Again, more studies of zero forms are needed, especially because after the age of 4 or 5 years, children with DLD produce more of these forms than their same dialect-speaking typically developing peers. This finding has been documented in AAE, GAE, and SWE (Oetting et al., 2019, 2021, 2022; see also Rice, 2003). The important point here is that zero forms can be syntactically rich or syntactically empty depending on a child's use of these forms, and both types of zero forms should be better understood and incorporated into clinical practice and preservice pedagogy.

Camouflaged Forms and Functions

Camouflaged forms serve multiple grammatical functions (Spears, 2015). As homomorphs, they are like homophones that reference content words (e.g., bank [of a river] vs. bank [to store money]). Across dialects of English, –ing is a camouflaged form because it serves as a marker of progressive aspect (e.g., he is biking) and used to form gerunds (e.g., biking is fun) and adjectives (e.g., The interesting book). In some English dialects, such as AAE, camouflaged forms serve dialect general and dialect specific functions, as in the case of had + verb, which can serve as a dialect general overt past participle (e.g., She had called them before they closed) and as a dialect specific overt preterite form for past tense (e.g., I fell and after that, she had called my dad). Zero forms are also inherently camouflaged, because as just mentioned, without an analysis of the frequencies and linguistic contexts of the zero forms, it is difficult to know if a child's zero forms are syntactically rich or empty. If you are not a native speaker of a child's dialect, you may interpret a camouflaged form or function incorrectly, using your dialect rather than the child's dialect to interpret grammaticality and meaning.

Conclusions

Adopting these five terms has changed how I talk to others about clinical practice and how I introduce preservice students to grammar and guide them to inclusive practices related to assessment and treatment (for examples of inclusive practices, see Byrd et al., 2019; Knight et al., 2021). These five terms generate discussions with clinicians and preservice students about how children acquire their grammar forms and functions, including the nuances of AAE, GAE, SWE, and other dialects, and more personal reflection about each of our idiolects relative to others in our families, friend groups, and communities. When teaching regular past tense across dialects of English, one can now talk about its various forms, which include, for all dialects, dialect general overt forms and, for some dialects, dialect general overt forms, dialect specific overt forms, and syntactically rich zero forms. Across dialects of English and for very young children who have yet to develop the necessary skills to produce syntactically rich clausal structures, one may also expect to hear syntactically empty zero forms.

As mentioned earlier and as represented at the top of the funnel in the figure, children learn many different dialects of English, and new grammar terms are needed to describe children's acquisition and use of these dialects with inclusivity and accuracy. Adoption of the five terms across dialects also adds consistency of terms in practice and when teaching. For example, in a study of Spanish–English bilingual children's productions of English past tense forms, Jacobson and Yu (2018) describe some of the productions as either double markings (e.g., dranked), overregularizations (e.g., ated), or stems (e.g., eatØ). Using the recommended terms, these same forms can now be referred to as dialect specific overt forms (dranked and ated) and zero forms (eatØ). In a study of auxiliary be forms by children classified as Gullah/Geechee-influenced AAE speakers, Berry and Oetting (2017) describe productions of də and been as nonmainstream. Using the recommended terms, these forms can now also be referred to as dialect specific overt forms. As shown by these two examples, adopting the same terms across dialects of English provides a common starting point that can then be followed with more in-depth conversations about any dialect of interest.

Adopt Early and Be Flexible

I recommend the five new terms be adopted early in foundational courses. Although undergraduate and graduate programs may cover linguistic diversity within dedicated chapters of a course or within an elective, when these terms are not introduced at the onset of the preservice students' training, we require them to unlearn terms for grammar that lack scientific precision and are not inclusive. I also recommend flexibility. As previously mentioned, the term dialect can be a negative trigger for some students, clients, and families. Thus, dialect general and dialect specific can also be referred to as English general and English specific, just as a dialect can also be referred to as a language, a variety, and a system.
