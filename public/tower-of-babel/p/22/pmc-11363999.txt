Cold atmospheric plasma enhances SLC7A11-mediated ferroptosis in non-small cell lung cancer by regulating PCAF mediated HOXB9 acetylation☆
Authors: Chenxing Zhang, Hongyang Liu, Xiaohu Li, Nan Xiao, Huanxiang Chen, Haoran Feng, Yang Li, Ying Yang, Ruike Zhang, Xiangzhuan Zhao, Yanmin Du, Lu Bai, Ruonan Ma, Junhu Wan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Lung cancer is a leading cause of cancer death worldwide, with high incidence and poor survival rates. Cold atmospheric plasma (CAP) technology has emerged as a promising therapeutic approach for cancer treatment, inducing oxidative stress in malignant tissues without causing thermal damage. However, the role of CAP in regulating lung cancer cell ferroptosis remains unclear. Here, we observed that CAP effectively suppressed the growth and migration abilities of lung cancer cells, with significantly increased ferroptotic cell death, lipid peroxidation, and decreased mitochondrial membrane potential. Mechanistically, CAP regulates SLC7A11-mediated cell ferroptosis by modulating HOXB9. SLC7A11, a potent ferroptosis suppressor, was markedly reduced by HOXB9 knockdown, while it was enhanced by overexpressing HOXB9. The luciferase and ChIP assays confirmed that HOXB9 can directly target SLC7A11 and regulate its gene transcription. Additionally, CAP enhanced the acetylation modification level of HOXB9 by promoting its interaction with acetyltransferase p300/CBP-associated factor (PCAF). Acetylated HOXB9 affects its protein ubiquitination modification level, which in turn affects its protein stability. Notably, the upregulation of SLC7A11 and HOXB9 mitigated the suppressive effects of CAP on ferroptosis status, cell proliferation, invasion, and migration in lung cancer cells. Furthermore, animal models have also confirmed that CAP can inhibit the progression of lung cancer in vivo. Overall, this study highlights the significance of the downregulation of the HOXB9/SLC7A11 axis by CAP treatment in inhibiting lung cancer, offering novel insights into the potential mechanisms and therapeutic strategies of CAP for lung cancer.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains one of the leading causes of death worldwide [1]. It can usually be divided into small cell lung cancer (SCLC) and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), and the majority cases of lung cancers (∼80%) are NSCLC [2]. Currently, surgical resection combined with chemotherapy and radiotherapy is the most common treatment for lung cancer [3,4]. Several recent advances in lung cancer management, including the use of targeted therapy and immunotherapy, as well as the identification of oncogenes, have revolutionized the treatment of lung cancer [5]. Despite the continuous improvement of treatment, the survival rate for lung cancer patients remains considerably low. With increasing drug resistance and recurrence rate, traditional treatment methods have certain limitations [6]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to find novel therapeutic targets or approaches to develop innovative treatment modalities for lung cancer.

Cold atmospheric plasma (CAP) is an ionized gas generated at ambient temperature, exhibiting a gas temperature proximate to room temperature and high reactivity [7]. Recent research has demonstrated the anti-cancer efficacy of CAP in a range of malignancies both in vivo and in vitro, including melanoma, breast cancer, ovarian cancer, and gastric cancer [[8], [9], [10], [11]]. In comparison to conventional anti-cancer modalities and pharmaceutical agents, CAP presents as a promising selective treatment option with significant potential for impeding tumor progression and related outcomes [12]. Notably, CAP has been shown to selectively target cancer cells in vitro while preserving normal cell integrity [13]. The potential anti-cancer properties of CAP may be attributed to certain reactive oxygen species (ROS) in its gaseous and liquid phases, such as atomic oxygen (O), hydroxyl (∙OH), ozone (O3), singlet oxygen (1O2), superoxide anion (∙O2−), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), nitrate (NO3−), and peroxynitrite (∙ONOO−) [14]. There are many mechanisms by which CAP can cure cancer, such as inducing apoptosis and autophagy, pyroptotic signaling pathway [15,16], etc. However, there is currently limited research on the functional effects of CAP on lung cancer cells, and the molecular mechanism of its role in lung cancer progression are also not clear.

Ferroptosis is a recently discovered non-apoptotic form of cell death induced by metabolic stress [17]. It is caused by cystine depletion and excessive production of lipid ROS, especially lipid hydroperoxide, in an iron-dependent manner. The glutathione (GSH) antioxidant system has been recognized as a major cell-protection system, preventing ferroptotic cell death [18]. Erastin is a synthetic small-molecule compound that inhibits the activity of cystine-glutamate antiporter (system Xc-) and causes GSH to be depleted [19]. The amino acid antiporter system Xc- is composed of two subunits: the heavy-chain subunit SLC3A2 and the light-chain subunit SLC7A11. In turn, they produce GSH, an endogenous antioxidant, through serial reactions after converting extracellular cystine to intracellular glutamate [20]. Like other forms of regulatory cell death, ferroptosis is strictly regulated in normal cells, and its disorder is related to a variety of pathological conditions and diseases. Excessive ferroptosis, for example, causes ischemia-reperfusion injury, kidney failure, and neurodegeneration [21], while insufficient ferroptosis has been linked to cancer progression [22]. Over the last few years, researchers have been researching mechanisms underlying ferroptosis susceptibility and resistance, particularly in the context of cancer. However, it remains largely undefined whether there is a clear association between CAP and ferroptosis in lung cancer cells.

Given that both CAP and ferroptosis are implicated in ROS pathways, it is hypothesized that the anti-cancer properties of CAP may be linked to ferroptosis in tumor cells. Here, a series of experimental evidence demonstrates that CAP treatment effectively suppresses the proliferation, migration, and invasion of NSCLC cell lines. Mechanistically, we explored the changes in molecules associated with ferroptosis in NSCLC cell lines treated with CAP and found that HOXB9/SLC7A11 axis plays an important role in the anti-cancer process of CAP. The discovery that CAP is a positive modulator of ferroptosis offers opportunities for facilitating CAP-based lung cancer therapies.

Results

Characterization of reactive species in CAP-treated cell culture medium

The experimental setup for CAP included a high-voltage electrode, a dielectric layer, a mesh electrode, and an oscilloscope as illustrated in Fig. 1A. The flow rate was controlled at 1.0 L/min by a flow controller. The CAP plasma was consistently generated within the mesh electrode, as shown in Fig. 1B. The oscilloscope was utilized to record the waveforms of discharge voltage and current, revealing a discharge power of 9.0 W as depicted in Fig. 1C. Optical emission spectroscopy (OES) was employed to identify the active species present in the CAP, specifically focusing on the main types of active species. The OES was utilized to observe wavelengths within the range of 250–500 nm. The results of the OES analysis indicated the production of nitrogen oxides, as shown in Fig. 1D. The culture dish, containing liquid medium, was then subjected to treatment with a CAP device. Firstly, the impact of CAP on the physicochemical characteristics of the liquid culture medium was assessed. The results revealed that CAP treatment led to an increase in temperature and conductivity. While there was no significant change in pH value, which may be related to the buffering ability of the cell culture medium, as depicted in Fig. 1E–G. Subsequent analysis revealed an increase in O3, ∙OH, NO3−, H2O2, ∙O2−, ∙ONOO−, and 1O2 levels following CAP treatment (Fig. 1H–N), consistent with OES data. CAP inhibits lung cancer cells proliferation, migration and invasion in vitro

In order to investigate the biological implications of CAP on NSCLC, we selected human NSCLC cell lines H1975 and H358 for subsequent functional analyses. The results of the CCK-8 experiment indicated that treatment with CAP significantly decreased the proliferation ability of lung cancer cells compared to the control group (Fig. 2A). Subsequently, to further investigate whether the inhibitory effect of CAP on the proliferation ability is time-dependent, H1975 and H358 cells were exposed to CAP for varying durations (0 s, 15 s, and 30 s) with clone formation and EdU experiments. The migratory and invasive capabilities of cancer cells play a crucial role in their metastatic spread to neighboring tissues and distant organs, ultimately contributing to poor outcomes for patients. Consequently, we employed transwell (Fig. 2D and E) and wound healing assays (Fig. 2F and G) to assess the impact of CAP treatment on the migratory and invasive potential of NSCLC cells. The results demonstrate a significant decrease in the migratory and invasive capacities of H1975 and H358 cells in the 15s and 30s CAP treatment groups compared to the control group. In addition, we analyzed the effects of CAP treatment on the cell cycle of H1975 and H358 cells through flow cytometry (Fig. 2H and I), and found that CAP treatment significantly arrested the cell cycle, which may be the reason for the decrease in their proliferation ability.

Upon treating normal lung epithelial cells BEAS-2B with CAP, it was observed that CAP treatment did not yield a significant impact on their migration and proliferation ability through wound healing assays (Fig. 2J), clone formation and EdU assays (Figs. S1A and B). Consistent with previous reports, our experimental results once again demonstrate the selective cytotoxicity of CAP against tumor cells, which may be associated with tumor cells showing higher ROS levels and greater sensitivity to ROS [23]. Additionally, the high expression of aquaporins in tumor cells makes them more susceptible to absorbing ROS produced by CAP, thereby increasing intracellular oxidative stress levels and ultimately leading to cell death. These findings suggest that CAP treatment effectively hinders the proliferation, migration, and invasion of NSCLC cells in vitro.

CAP induces ferroptosis in lung cancer cells

In order to clearly explain the potential molecular mechanism of CAP mediated regulation of tumor progression, we performed the RNA-sequencing on H358 cells treated with CAP for 30s to screen possible signaling pathways. We totally found 2552 up-regulated genes and 3064 down-regulated genes with CAP treatment in H358 cells. The volcano plot and heatmap show the significant differences after CAP treatment (Fig. 3A and D). Subsequently, these genes were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses. Based on the results of GO and KEGG analyses, these differential genes involved in multiple signaling pathways, including cell migration, cell cycle and cell junction (Fig. 3B and C), which were closely related to tumor occurrence, development, and metastasis. Interestingly, significant changes in ferroptosis-related genes have been noted among these genes. Given the emerging significance of ferroptosis as a potential therapeutic approach for lung cancer, we next focused on the role of CAP in regulating ferroptosis. Prior to CAP treatment, NSCLC cells were pre-treated with Erastin or RSL3 for 10 h. The CCK-8 assay revealed that CAP treatment augmented the suppression of cell viability induced by Erastin and RSL3 (Fig. 3F). Furthermore, analysis of ferroptosis-specific markers, including GSH, malondialdehyde (MDA), and lipid ROS levels, demonstrated a significant decrease in GSH and an increase in MDA and lipid ROS accumulation following CAP treatment (Fig. 3G–I). The administration of CAP treatment resulted in heightened vulnerability of NSCLC cells to ferroptosis. Besides, as indicated by JC-1 staining, a reduction in the mitochondrial membrane potential of cells was observed following CAP treatment (Fig. 3J). Collectively, these results suggest that CAP treatment has the potential to induce cell death by facilitating ferroptosis in NSCLC cells.

CAP promotes ferroptosis in lung cancer cells by inhibiting the expression of SLC7A11

In order to clarify the specific molecular mechanism of CAP regulating ferroptosis, we selected the ferroptosis regulatory genes with differential changes in sequencing results for RT-qPCR validation. Subsequent RT-qPCR analysis indicated changes in genes such as SLC7A11, TFRC, GPX4, and SLC3A2 following treatment with CAP, with SLC7A11 exhibiting the most pronounced difference (Fig. 4A). Consistent with the mRNA findings, Western blot analysis demonstrated a notable reduction in SLC7A11 protein level, with not much change in GPX4 protein levels (Fig. 4B). These results suggest that CAP treatment may induce ferroptosis in non-small cell lung cancer cells by downregulating the expression of SLC7A11. The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) lung cancer database was utilized for SLC7A11 analysis, revealing a significantly higher expression of SLC7A11 in tumor tissues compared to normal tissues (Fig. 4C). To further investigate whether CAP treatment inhibits cancer progression by regulating SLC7A11, SLC7A11 was overexpressed in NSCLC cells and subjected to CAP treatment (Fig. 4E). Colony formation, transwell, and wound healing assays were performed. The results revealed that the cells treated with both CAP and overexpressed SLC7A11 exhibited a higher number of colonies, increased cell migration, and accelerated wound healing compared to the group treated with CAP alone (Fig. 4F–H). These findings suggest that the overexpression of SLC7A11 mitigates the inhibitory effects of CAP on the proliferation and migration capabilities of NSCLC cells. Furthermore, levels of lipid ROS and MDA, as well as JC-1 staining, were evaluated to determine how CAP affects ferroptosis regulated by SLC7A11. The findings revealed that the overexpression of SLC7A11 significantly decreased the buildup of lipid ROS and lipid peroxidation, as well as restored the decline in mitochondrial membrane potential compared to solely undergoing CAP treatment (refer to Fig. 4I, J, and K). These results collectively suggest that the inhibition of SLC7A11 expression by CAP treatment may induce ferroptosis in NSCLC cells, consequently impeding their proliferation and migration capabilities.

HOXB9 directly targets the transcription of SLC7A11 and affects CAP regulation of SLC7A11 expression

SLC7A11, the twelve-pass transmembrane transporter protein, coupled to SLC3A2 by a disulfide bond, has been described as a potent ferroptosis suppressor. In order to identify the regulatory factors that affect the transcription of SLC7A11, we searched for databases predicting transcription factors. We found that many genes in the HOX family were predicted as transcription factors of SLC7A11 by combining Human TFDB, PROMO, and GTRD databases (Fig. 5A and Table S1). In order to further evaluate the potential role of 39 HOX genes in lung cancer, the clinical relevance of these gene expression in lung cancer was assessed in TCGA lung cancer database. We found a statistically significant increase in nine genes (HOXA1, HOXA13, HOXB7, HOXB9, HOXC6, HOXC8, HOXC9, HOXD11, and HOXD12) between lung cancer and adjacent tissues (Fig. 5B and C). After combining the expression HOX genes with survival data for lung cancer in TCGA, univariate cox regression analysis was carried out to identify genes related to prognosis. Interestingly, among the HOX genes related to the prognosis, the nine genes were dangerous with HR > 1 (Fig. 5D), and high expression was associated with a significantly poor prognosis in these nine genes (Figs. S2A–I). Our RNA-seq analysis demonstrated significant alterations in multiple HOX family genes following CAP treatment. Specifically, the heatmap visualization highlighted notable changes in HOXB9 expression, which exhibited the most pronounced decrease post-treatment (Fig. 5E). Subsequent validation through Western blot and RT-qPCR assays confirmed a significant reduction in both mRNA and protein levels of HOXB9 in NSCLC cells following CAP treatment, consistent with our RNA-seq findings (Fig. 5F and G). This suggests that HOXB9 may be a transcription factor for SLC7A11. In order to elucidate the transcriptional regulation of HOXB9 on SLC7A11, we utilized the JASPAR database for the prediction of the binding site of HOXB9 on the SLC7A11 promoter (Fig. 5H). Subsequently, SLC7A11-WT and SLC7A11-MUT luciferase reporter plasmids were constructed to confirm the binding of HOXB9 to SLC7A11 (Fig. 5I). The findings indicated a significant reduction in luciferase activity in the SLC7A11-WT group upon HOXB9 knockdown, with no notable difference observed in the SLC7A11-MUT group (Fig. 5J). Spearman correlation analysis was employed to assess the relationship between mRNA expression levels of HOXB9 and SLC7A11 in the TCGA lung cancer database. The results indicate a positive association between the levels of HOXB9 and SLC7A11 expression, as demonstrated in Fig. 5K. Particularly noteworthy is the observed decrease in SLC7A11 expression upon HOXB9 knockdown, and conversely, the increase in SLC7A11 expression following HOXB9 overexpression in H358 cells, as illustrated in Fig. 5L and M. For further confirmation, we performed ChIP-qPCR to detect the enrichment of HOXB9 on SLC7A11 promoter. The results showed that HOXB9 was indeed enriched at the promoter region of SLC7A11. Compared with the control group, overexpression of HOXB9 can enhance its binding ability on SLC7A11 promoter, while knocking down HOXB9 can reduce its binding ability of HOXB9 on SLC7A11 promoter. More importantly, we found CAP treatment resulted in a decrease in the binding ability of HOXB9 on SLC7A11 promoter, further indicating that CAP may inhibit SLC7A11 expression by inhibiting HOXB9 (Fig. 5N). These results suggest a potential regulatory role of HOXB9 as a transcription factor for SLC7A11 and further suggest that treatment with CAP may suppress SLC7A11 expression by inhibiting HOXB9.

HOXB9 is upregulated in lung cancer tissues and stimulates lung cancer cell proliferation and migration

To determine whether HOXB9 can affect cell proliferation, we separately knocked down and overexpressed HOXB9 in H358 cells. CCK-8 assays were performed to examine the effects of HOXB9 on lung cancer cell proliferation. We found that cell proliferation ability is repressed by HOXB9 knockdown and stimulated by HOXB9 overexpression in lung cancer cells (Fig. 6A). In addition, inhibiting HOXB9 suppressed the migratory capacities of lung cancer cells, while increasing HOXB9 enhanced the migration of lung cancer cells in the transwell migration assay (Fig. 6B and C). Based on these results, HOXB9 is thought to be involved in lung cancer cell proliferation and migration. As a potential transcription factor of SLC7A11, we investigated whether HOXB9 affects lung cancer cell ferroptosis. HOXB9 inhibition significantly increased erastin-induced ferroptotic cell death in H358 cells (Fig. 6D). Simultaneously, we monitored changes in GSH and ROS levels. ROS results indicated that HOXB9 knockdown upregulated ROS levels regardless of Erastin treatment. Surprisingly, we found that knocking down HOXB9 alone did not significantly alter cell viability and GSH levels but further exacerbated erastin-induced suppression of viability and GSH levels. Subsequent validation in H1975 cells revealed a mild suppression of viability and GSH levels upon HOXB9 knockdown (Figs. S3A–C). Besides, it is noteworthy that Fer-1 treatment almost completely reversed erastin-induced cell death, decreasing GSH and lipid ROS accumulation, both in HOXB9-knockdown and control cells (Fig. 6E–G). In summary, these data suggest that HOXB9 negatively regulates ferroptosis through modulation of SLC7A11 expression. To further investigate the clinical correlation between HOXB9 and lung cancer, we examined the expression of HOXB9 in clinical tissues of lung cancer. HOXB9 was found significantly overexpressed in TCGA lung cancer tissue compared with adjacent non-tumor tissues, as well as in the paired set (Fig. 6H and I). CAP inhibits HOXB9 expression by regulating PCAF mediated HOXB9 acetylation modification

According to the research we conducted previously, acetyltransferase p300/CBP-associated factor (PCAF) can interact with HOXB9 and mediate its acetylation modification. The level of HOXB9 acetylation can affect the growth and progression of cancer [[24], [25], [26]]. However, the expression level of PCAF was unaffected, despite knockdown or overexpression of HOXB9 (Fig. 7A). Moreover, knocking down or overexpressing PCAF also does not affect the expression level of HOXB9 (Fig. 7B). This indicates that there is no mutual regulation between HOXB9 and PCAF at the transcriptional level. CAP inhibits the progression of lung cancer by inducing ferroptosis in vivo

In order to further confirm CAP suppresses lung cancer progression by promoting ferroptosis, we performed additional validation studies using animal models. BALB/c nude mice were selected as the experimental subjects. Ferrostatin-1(Fer-1) is a potent and selective ferroptosis inhibitor that inhibits cell death by preventing damage to membrane lipids through a reductive mechanism. And it can inhibit erastin-induced ferroptosis. We added Fer-1 while using CAP treatment to measure if it can offset the ferroptosis caused by CAP. The mice were implanted with H358 cells and divided into three groups, each receiving their respective treatments every five days. The first group received PBS, the second group received CAP-treated PBS, and the third group received CAP-treated PBS along with Fer-1 (Fig. 9A). Tumor volume was measured every five days, and after a 30-day observation period, the tumors were harvested and weighed. It was found that the group treated with CAP exhibited a significant inhibition of tumor growth in comparison to the control group. The tumor size and weight in the group that treated with CAP and Fer-1 were found to be at a moderate level, suggesting that Fer-1 attenuated the anti-cancer efficacy of CAP (Fig. 9B–D). The outcomes of Western blot analysis of tumor proteins (Fig. 9E) and IHC staining (Fig. 9F) were consistent with previous findings, a significant reduction in both SLC7A11 and HOXB9 in CAP treatment group, while the changes in GPX4 are not significant. And Fer-1 could offset such effects that CAP down-regulated the expression of SLC7A11 and HOXB9. To further clarify the effect of CAP on ferroptosis, we detected the lipid peroxidation products 4-HNE and MDA by IHC staining. The results showed that the expression levels of 4-HNE and MDA were greatly increased after CAP treatment, while the group which treated with CAP along with Fer-1 has a medium expression level. This suggests that Fer-1 counteracted CAP-induced lipid peroxidation. To assess the safety of CAP in mice, the hearts, lungs, livers, spleens, and kidneys from each group of mice were extracted for H&E staining (Fig. 9G and H). The results proved that CAP is safe for mice. Discussion

Lung cancer accounts for a considerable portion of malignant neoplasms. Despite the availability of numerous conventional therapies for combating this disease, the overall prognosis remains unfavorable. Consequently, there is a pressing need to advocate for a paradigm shift in the treatment of lung cancer. The emergence of CAP in recent years has introduced novel approaches for lung cancer treatment. Currently, CAP is being explored for its potential in targeting lung cancer through the pyroptosis pathway [15], in combination with iron oxide-based magnetic nanoparticles [27], or in conjunction with paclitaxel-loaded core-shell magnetic nanoparticles [28]. Following treatment with CAP, there was a notable elevation in levels of ROS within the plasma-activated medium [29]. This study provides evidence supporting the inhibitory effects of CAP on the growth and progression of lung cancer via the induction of ferroptosis, as demonstrated in both in vivo and in vitro experiments. Furthermore, our findings suggest a potential mechanism by which CAP modulates the ferroptosis pathway in lung cancer cells through the HOXB9/SLC7A11 axis, with HOXB9 acting as a transcriptional regulator of SLC7A11 to enhance its expression (Fig. 9I).

As one of the HOX gene family member possessing homeobox, HOXB9 plays a crucial role in embryonic development and tumorigenesis [30,31]. HOXB9 is one of the most highly conserved transcription factors in the evolution of organisms. In adult organisms, the expression of HOXB9 is strictly regulated due to its crucial role during embryonic development. The expression of HOXB9 was upregulated in breast tumors [32] and endometrial cancer [33], promoting cancer progression and metastasis by upregulating factors associated with tumor fate. Nevertheless, the downregulation of HOXB9 has been associated with a poor survival prognosis in patients with colon adenocarcinoma [34], gastric cancer [35], and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma [36]. Thus, the role of HOXB9 appears to vary among different types of tumors, indicating its complex mechanisms and roles in tumorigenesis. However, there is very little research on HOXB9 in regulating lung cancer. HOXB9 has been reported upregulating EMT-related molecules in lung cancer cells and promoting cell migration. Our previous findings demonstrated that HOXB9 interacts with and is acetylated by acetyltransferase p300/CBP-associated factor (PCAF) in lung cancer cells. Besides, HOXB9 acetylation inhibited lung cancer progression by directly regulating JMJD6 gene expression [24]. However, the molecular regulatory mechanism for HOXB9 regulation in lung cancer remains largely unclear. In this study, we demonstrated a significant association between HOXB9 expression and lung cancer clinical stage. Specifically, elevated levels of HOXB9 expression are linked to a poorer prognosis in lung cancer patients. Additionally, our findings indicate that CAP amplifies the acetylation of HOXB9 by PCAF, leading to a downregulation of HOXB9 expression. Our study also found that acetylation of HOXB9 can cause an increase in its ubiquitination level and a decrease in protein stability. This indicates that acetylation of HOXB9 significantly promotes its protein degradation, which has not been previously discovered. This study represents the first investigation into the role of CAP in lung cancer through regulating the HOXB9 mediated signaling pathway.

Ferroptosis is a newly identified cell death process depend on iron and lipid peroxidation. In ferroptosis process, the signaling axis SLC7A11-GSH-GPX4 mediates a major surveillance system. GPX4 reduces lipid peroxides at the expense of glutathione, and utilizes GSH to detoxify lipid hydroperoxides into lipid alcohols. The cystine-glutamate antiporter system Xc- is involved in maintaining intracellular GSH levels. It consists of light chain subunits SLC7A11 and heavy chain subunits SLC3A2, which facilitates the exchange of extracellular cystine and intracellular glutamate through the plasma membrane. The SLC7A11 gene plays multiple roles in the regulation of many pathophysiological processes, including redox homeostasis, ferroptosis, and drug resistance. The accumulating evidence suggests that SLC7A11 plays a role in drug resistance therapy for various types of tumors including lung adenocarcinoma [37], gastric cancer [38], colorectal cancer [39], glioma [40], etc. The currently known transcription factors of SLC7A11 include HIF-1α [41], NRF2 [42], SP1 [43], NF-κB p65 [44], etc. Therefore, SLC7A11 may be a new cancer therapy target. However, it remains unknown whether HOXB9 functions in regulating ferroptosis process. Although there are currently no reported associations between HOX or HOXB9 and ferroptosis, our team's previous research has established HOXB9 as a transcription factor closely linked to acetylation. Acetylation is a significant post-translational modification influencing protein function, stability, and interactions. Known to impact various diseases such as cancer, neurological disorders, and cardiovascular conditions, acetylation plays a pivotal role in numerous cellular signaling pathways, including ferroptosis. For example, acetylation of various proteins like ACSL4, HSPB1, EIF4E and TP53 is involved in the regulation of ferroptosis [45]. Therefore, we hypothesize that HOXB9 may modulate ferroptosis through its regulatory effects on acetylation. Our research identifies HOXB9 as a novel transcription factor for SLC7A11, supported by ChIP and dual luciferase assays. Subsequent experiments further establish the critical role of the HOXB9/SLC7A11 signaling axis in regulating ferroptosis in lung cancer cells. These findings suggest that the HOXB9/SLC7A11 axis may mediate the impact of CAP on lung cancer progression through the mechanism of ferroptosis.

CAP, a promising anti-cancer therapy, demonstrates considerable potential for the treatment of lung cancer. In both in vivo and in vitro studies, CAP exhibits substantial inhibitory effects on lung cancer cells by inducing ferroptosis. Although our data support the potential of CAP as a novel approach to lung cancer treatment, further research is required to confirm its effectiveness, safety, and optimal utilization. Additionally, it is imperative to investigate the specific mechanisms and targets of CAP treatment for lung cancer in greater depth. Future investigations may also explore the possibility of combining CAP with other anti-cancer therapies, such as radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy, to potentially enhance efficacy and minimize adverse effects. In conclusion, our findings point to a potential therapeutic strategy for combating lung cancer, suggesting that CAP/HOXB9/SLC7A11 axis could serve as therapeutic target in future lung cancer treatments.

Experimental section

Cell culture, reagents and antibodies

Human NSCLC cell lines, H1975 and H358, and human normal lung epithelial cell line BEAS-2B were cultured at 37 °C with 5 % CO2. BEAS-2B was cultured in DMEM. H1975 and H358 were cultured in RPMI-1640. Both DMEM and RPMI-1640 were supplemented with 10 % (vol/vol) fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1 % penicillin/streptomycin. For ferroptosis related experiments, cells were treated with Erastin (Selleck, Cat#S7242) or RSL3 (Selleck, Cat#S8155) or Fer-1 (MCE, Cat#HY-100579). The antibodies used in this study were listed as follows: anti-SLC7A11 (Abways, Cat#CY7046), anti-GPX4 (Abways, Cat#CY6959), anti-β-actin (Proteintech, Cat#81115-1-RR), anti-HOXB9 (Abways, Cat#CY5467), anti-HA (Proteintech, Cat#81290-1-RR), anti-FLAG (Proteintech, Cat#66008-4-Ig), anti-acetylated-Lysine, (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat#9441), anti-Tubulin, (Proteintech, Cat#11224-1-AP). Sequences of shRNA used in this study were presented in Table S3.

Plasma device and CAP treatment

CAP in this study is a surface microdischarge (SMD) plasma. Air is the working gas. The chamber was flushed with working gas for 5 min to guarantee the discharge generated under the working gas. The flow rate was controlled at 1.0 L/min by a flow controller. Discharge current was measured by a current probe (Pearson Electronics, 2877) and discharge voltage was measured by a high-voltage probe (Tektronix, P6015A). The oscilloscope (Teledyne, WaveSurfer 3000) was utilized to record the waveforms of discharge voltage and current, the discharge power is 9.0W. 4 ml of cell culture medium was added to a 60 mm culture dish and treated with CAP for 15s, 30s, and 60s. Then, using CAP-treated medium for cell culture.

Measurements of ROS

The conductivity of CAP-treated cell culture medium was measured by an electric conductivity meter. The pH measurement was performed using a pH meter (Mettler Toledo). The O3 concentration in the chamber was measured by an ozone detector (NGP5-50-O3, Ningbo Gaopin Technology Co., Ltd., China). The optical emission spectra (OES) were detected by AvaSpec-ULS 4096CL-EVO (Avantes, Lafayette, CO, USA). For ∙OH, after adding terephthalic acid, measure the fluorescence intensity (excitation wavelength 310 nm and emission spectrum at 425 nm). NO3− concentration was detected by spectrophotometric method. After adding H2O2 detection reagents (Beyotime, China), measure the absorbance at 560 nm of medium. To detect ∙O2− generated by CAP, adding nitro blue tetrazolium (Solarbio, China) and measure the absorbance at 580 nm ∙ONOO− intensity was measured by the probe of 2, 7-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (H2DCFDA). TEMP was used to spin-trap 1O2.

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8), colony formation and EdU assays

For CCK-8 assays, 2000 cells were seeded per well in 96-well plates. 10 μl of the CCK-8 reagent (Beyotime, China) mixed with 90 μl of complete medium were added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 2h, then measure the absorbance at 450 nm. For colony formation assays, cells were cultured in six‐well plates at an amount of 1000 cells per well at 37 °C with 5 % CO2 for 10–14 days. Cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde and stained with crystal violet, then count the number of colonies. EdU assays were performed by EdU assay kit (Beyotime, China). Using 10 μm EdU to Incubate the cells for 2 h, then fixed with paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with Triton X-10. Incubating cells with Click Reaction Solution for half an hour and using Hoechst 33342 for nuclear staining. Cell images were captured using an Inverted Fluorescence Microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Transwell and wound healing assays

Transwell chambers (Corning, USA) were used for transwell assays. For migration assays, add 200 μl of serum-free medium containing 4 × 104 suspended cells to the upper chamber, and 500 μl of medium containing 20 % FBS to the lower chamber. After 16h incubation, fixed with paraformaldehyde and stained with crystal violet. The experimental method of invasion assays is the same as that of migration assays but with Matrigel (Yeasen, China) in the upper chamber. For wound healing assays, when cells that plated in 6-well plates reached 90 %, the 10 μl pipette tip was used to create artificial gaps. Images of 0h were recorded. After culturing in serum-free medium for 24 h, the gap at the same location was recorded again.

Flow cytometry

Flow cytometry was used to detect apoptosis, cell cycle and lipid ROS accumulation. They were analyzed by Annexin V-FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit (Beyotime, China), PI Cell Cycle and Apoptosis Analysis Kit (Beyotime, China) and Reactive Oxygen Species Assay Kit (Beyotime, China) separately. Followed the instructions to conduct these experiments, then make samples shielded from light and measured by flow cytometry timely.

RT-qPCR

The total RNA of cells was extracted by FastPure Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit V2 (Vazyme Biotech Co.,Ltd). The extracted RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using Hifair III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix for Qpcr (Yeasen, China). The RT-qPCR assays were conducted to measure mRNA expression using Hieff qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (YEASEN, China). Using GAPDH as the internal reference, the relative expression levels of mRNA were calculated using 2−ΔΔCT method.

Cell viability assay

4000 cells were seeded per well in 96-well plates. Treated with 10 μM Erastin or 4 μM RSL3 or 5 μM Fer-1 for 10 h and then treated with CAP. The subsequent experimental steps are the same as the CCK-8 assay used to detect proliferation ability mentioned above.

Western blot and co-immunoprecipitation

Cells and tissues were lysed using RIPA Lysis Buffer (Beyotime, China) and Protease inhibitor (Beyotime, China). BCA assay kit was used to measure the protein concentration. As for co-immunoprecipitation, the supernatant of protein lysis solution was mixed with Anti-DYKDDDDK (Flag) Affinity Gel (YEASEN, China) and incubated at 4 °C for 4 h. The precipitate obtained by centrifugation was washed three times with TBS. Add 2× loading buffer and boil the protein for 5 min, then follow the Western blot steps above for the experiment.

MDA assay

The lipid peroxidation levels were detected by Lipid Peroxidation MDA Assay Kit (Beyotime, China). Cell samples were lysed using RIPA lysis buffer and the supernatant was taken. Firstly, the protein concentration was measured using the BCA assay kit. Then, mixed MDA detection working solution with each protein sample and heated at 100 °C for 15 min. Finally, the protein sample was centrifuged and the supernatant was added to a 96 well plate for absorbance measurement at 532 nm.

Immunofluorescence

Cells were seeded in glass bottom cell culture dishes. Fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 15 min, 0.5 % Triton X-100 was transparent for 20 min, and immunofluorescence blocking solution was used to block cells, and incubated the primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. After washing off the unbound primary antibody, the fluorescent secondary antibody was incubated in dark for 1 h, and the cell nucleus was stained with Hoechst. The immunofluorescence images were captured under a Laser confocal microscope. (Zeiss LSM880, Germany).

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

The stable H358 cells were crosslinked with formaldehyde. After crosslinking, the cells were lysed and sonicated to obtain DNA fragments. Specific antibodies were added to the chromatin to bind to the target protein. Put in protein A and protein G to bind to the antibody-target protein-DNA. Performed reverse cross-linking at 65 °C for 10 h after washing off non-specific binding. Purify DNA and analyze it through RT-qPCR.

Immunohistochemistry

Paraffin coated tissues were baked in a 60 °C for 40 min, dewaxed in xylene. Antigen retention was performed in a sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for 15 min. Then, the tissues were incubated at room temperature with 3 % hydrogen peroxide for 10 min and blocked with 3 % bovine serum albumin for 30 min. Then, the tissue slices were incubated overnight with the primary antibodies at 4 °C. The tissues were incubated with horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 30 min. Then, hematoxylin was used for re-staining and 70 % alcohol was used for dehydration. Finally, images were captured using a microscope.

Nude mice xenograft model

Female BALB/c nude mice aged 4–5 weeks were purchased from GemPharmatech (Jiangsu, China) and housed in a pathogen-free environment. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Experimentation Ethics Committee of Zhengzhou University. A three-day adaptation period was given to the mice. 5 × 106 H358 cells were suspended in 100 μl PBS and injected subcutaneously into the mice. The volume of tumor was recorded every 5 days and three groups of mice were treated every 5 days: untreated PBS, CAP treated PBS, CAP treated PBS and Fer-1. After 30 days, the mice were executed and the tumor weight was recorded.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data underwent statistical analysis utilizing GraphPad Prism 8 and are reported as means ± standard deviations (SD). The differences in results were analyzed using independent t-tests or one way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Significant differences were shown by *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001.
