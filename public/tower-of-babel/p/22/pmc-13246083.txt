Discordance between remnant cholesterol and LDL-C associated with the risk of new-onset cardiometabolic multimorbidity: A prospective cohort study
Authors: Lei Rong, Hongdan Fan, Qinghua Fang, Xingyu Li, Jie Tan, Jing Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Remnant cholesterol (RC) and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) are widely recognized for their distinct roles in cardiovascular disease risk, yet their combined influence on cardiometabolic outcomes remains insufficiently studied. This study aimed to investigate the association of cumulative remnant cholesterol (CumRC) exposure and the discordance between RC and LDL-C with the incidence of cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM). All data for this study were obtained from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study. CumRC was calculated as the average of RC values measured during waves 1 and 3, divided by the total exposure duration. The discordance between RC and LDL-C was defined as the difference in percentile units (>15 units). Cox proportional hazards models, along with subgroup and sensitivity analyses, were used to evaluate the associations between CumRC, the discordance between RC and LDL-C, and CMM risk. A total of 5867 participants without CMM were recruited from China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study 2011 and followed up in 2018. Over a 7-year follow-up period, 435 participants developed CMM. Higher CumRC levels were associated with an increased risk of CMM. Comparing the extreme quartiles of CumRC, the hazard ratio (HR) for CMM was 1.77 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.31–2.40). After adjusting for traditional risk factors, each 10 mg/dL increase in CumRC was associated with a 3% higher risk of CMM (HR: 1.03, 95% CI = 1.01–1.04). In the discordance analysis, the discordantly high RC group showed a higher risk of CMM (HR: 1.31, 95% CI = 1.04–1.64) compared to those with higher LDL-C. This association held even among individuals with well-controlled LDL-C levels. The results remained robust across various sensitivity analyses. Elevated CumRC exposure is associated with an increased risk of CMM. Discordantly high RC, not discordantly high LDL-C, is significantly associated with CMM onset among Chinese adults.

1. Introduction

Cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM), defined as the coexistence of at least 2 cardiometabolic diseases – primarily diabetes, heart disease, and stroke – poses an increasing global health challenge, especially with the aging population.[1,2] The prevalence of CMM has risen significantly, and individuals with CMM have been shown to have more than 3 times the risk of all-cause mortality compared to those without cardiometabolic conditions.[3] This significantly impacts quality of life, life expectancy, and increases the burden of disease.[4] Early identification of high-risk individuals and effective control of risk factors are therefore of critical clinical importance.

Cholesterol metabolism plays a key role in cardiovascular diseases. Various treatments targeting low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) have been proven to reduce cardiovascular risk.[5,6] However, even among individuals achieving optimal LDL-C levels, a substantial residual cardiovascular risk remains.[7] In such cases, triglyceride-rich lipoproteins (TRLs) and their cholesterol-rich remnants have emerged as novel lipid intervention targets.[8-10] Remnant cholesterol (RC) refers to the cholesterol content within TRLs and their metabolic remnants, primarily intermediate-density lipoproteins (IDL) and very low-density lipoproteins (VLDL) in the fasting state, and chylomicron remnants in the non-fasting state.[11] These lipoprotein particles can penetrate the arterial wall and be absorbed by macrophages and smooth muscle cells, contributing to atherosclerosis.[12]

Due to the widespread use of statins, the introduction of proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 inhibitors, and the adjunctive use of other lipid-lowering agents such as ezetimibe, the effectiveness of lipid management has significantly improved. However, among some individuals achieving optimal LDL-C levels, the risk of acute coronary syndrome remains elevated, making the issue of residual cardiovascular risk increasingly prominent. Consequently, TRLs have gained increasing attention as a potential target for reducing residual risk. In addition, the safety of polypharmacy has also become a growing concern. Ardahanli noted that the concomitant use of high-intensity statins and ticagrelor in patients with acute coronary syndrome may be associated with an increased risk of rhabdomyolysis, highlighting the need for individualized risk-benefit assessment in high-risk patients.[13]

Previous studies have explored the associations between RC and cardiovascular disease, stroke, and diabetes.[14-16] Most of these studies have focused on either RC alone or compared the effects of LDL-C and RC independently on outcomes, without considering their interaction. Additionally, previous findings overlooked the dynamic changes in RC over time, which could influence outcomes. Notably, recent studies have begun to examine the impact of RC-LDL-C discordance on composite cardiovascular risk,[15] stroke,[17] and hypertension,[18] revealing the importance of such discrepancies in risk stratification for these conditions. However, the association between cumulative RC (CumRC), RC-LDL-C discordance, and CMM, particularly in the Chinese middle-aged and elderly population, remains unclear.

Therefore, we conducted a prospective cohort study based on the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) to investigate the association between CumRC and CMM risk. Additionally, we performed a discordance analysis to explore the potential impact of RC-LDL-C discordance on CMM risk.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design and population

This study utilized data from the CHARLS collected between 2011 and 2018. CHARLS is a nationwide survey representing middle-aged and older adults (aged ≥ 45 years) from 450 rural administrative villages and urban communities across 28 provinces in mainland China. The survey initially recruited 17,708 participants, who were followed up every 2 to 3 years (the second wave in 2013, the third in 2015, the fourth in 2018, and the fifth in 2020). The CHARLS study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Peking University, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. This study adhered to the strengthening the reporting of observational studies in epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines.

In this study, participants interviewed in the first wave were considered as the baseline, and outcomes were followed up from the second to the fourth wave (2013–2018). We excluded participants who were under 45 years old or had missing age data, those without available lipid data, and those with CMM at baseline. Additionally, we excluded participants with triglyceride levels of 400 mg/dL or higher due to concerns about the accuracy of LDL-C concentration calculation in individuals with extremely elevated triglyceride levels. Ultimately, 5867 eligible participants were included in the analysis. The detailed selection process is presented in Figure 1.

2.2. Laboratory methods

Fasting blood samples were collected during both the 2011 and 2015 surveys and then transported to a central laboratory in Beijing for standardized testing. Lipid profiles, including total cholesterol, triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and LDL-C, were measured using enzymatic colorimetric methods. All laboratories involved in the testing were standardized and certified, with the coefficient of variation for blood biomarker measurements kept below 5%. RC was calculated by subtracting HDL-C and LDL-C concentrations from the total cholesterol concentration.

2.3. Calculation of CumRC

CumRC was calculated as the average of RC values measured during the first and third waves, multiplied by the duration of total exposure. Specifically, the formula is as follows: (RC2011 + RC2015)/2 × Time (2011 − 2015). This method has been applied in several previously published studies.

2.4. Data collection

In the baseline survey of the first wave, trained interviewers collected information using standardized questionnaires. Data included demographic characteristics (age, gender, residence, educational level, and marital status), smoking status, current alcohol consumption, and self-reported physician-diagnosed diseases (hypertension, dyslipidemia, heart disease, stroke, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, and cancer). Smoking and alcohol consumption were self-reported and categorized as “yes” or “no.” Nighttime sleep duration was self-reported and classified as <7 hours, 7–9 hours, or >9 hours. Residential areas were categorized as rural or urban. Educational levels were classified as below primary, primary education, secondary education, and college or above. Marital status was classified as married or other. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing weight (in kilograms) by height squared (in meters squared). Systolic blood pressure and diastolic blood pressure were measured in a seated position using a mercury sphygmomanometer, and the average of 3 readings was calculated. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mm Hg, diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mm Hg, or a self-reported history of a hypertension diagnosis.

2.5. Discordance definition

We used cumulative distribution functions to standardize CumRC and cumulative LDL-C levels in the cohort into percentiles and calculated the difference between their percentiles. A difference >15 percentile units was defined as discordant. Based on previous studies,[17,19,20] the population was divided into 3 groups: discordant-low group (CumRC percentile minus cumulative LDL-C percentile < −15 percentile units); concordant group (−15 percentile units ≤ (CumRC percentile minus cumulative LDL-C percentile) < 15 percentile units); and discordant-high group (CumRC percentile minus cumulative LDL-C percentile > 15 percentile units). Percentiles were calculated based on the cumulative distribution of CumRC and cumulative LDL-C in the entire cohort. The difference between the 2 percentiles was then computed for each participant.

2.6. Assessment of outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was the first occurrence of a CMM event during waves 2 to 4, defined as the presence of more than 1 condition in an individual, such as stroke, cardiac events, and diabetes. Incident cases of stroke, cardiac events, or diabetes were assessed through the following questions: “Have you been diagnosed with diabetes or high blood sugar by a doctor?” “Have you been told by a doctor that you have been diagnosed with a heart attack, angina, coronary heart disease, heart failure, or other heart problems?” “Has a doctor ever told you that you have been diagnosed with a stroke?”

2.7. Statistical analyses

Baseline characteristics were analyzed for participants newly diagnosed with CMM and those not diagnosed with CMM. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of the data. Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation, while skewed continuous variables were presented as median (interquartile range). Categorical variables were expressed as percentages (%). Differences between groups for normally distributed variables were assessed using the unpaired t-test. The Mann–Whitney U test and Kruskal–Wallis H test were used to compare skewed variables between 2 and 3 groups, respectively. For categorical variables, the chi-square test was applied where appropriate.

Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate the association between CumRC and the risk of incident CMM, expressed as HR with 95% confidence intervals (CI). The follow-up period was calculated from the baseline date to the date of stroke, death, or the last follow-up interview, whichever occurred first. CumRC was analyzed both as a continuous variable and as a categorical variable (grouped by quartiles). The proportionality assumption was verified using the Schoenfeld residuals test, with no violations observed (P > .05). Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 was adjusted for age, gender, residence, education level, marital status, smoking status, current alcohol consumption, and nighttime sleep duration. Model 3 was further adjusted for BMI, serum creatinine, hypertension, and C-reactive protein. The same models were used to assess the association between the percentile discordance of RC and LDL-C with CMM incidence. Restricted cubic spline analysis was performed to explore potential nonlinear relationships and to describe the dose–response relationship between CumRC, discordance percentages, and CMM. In further subgroup analyses, we stratified participants by LDL-C levels, primarily using 2 thresholds: 100 mg/dL and the median value, to assess the association between CumRC, discordance groups, and CMM incidence across different LDL-C levels.

Additionally, we conducted several sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of the study results. First, we used multiple imputation methods to account for any missing data and repeated the primary analysis. Second, we employed a competing risk model with both death and loss to follow-up as potential outcomes to validate the relationship between CumRC, discordance groups, and the study outcomes, and calculated subdistribution HRs. Finally, we used a threshold of 10 percentile units to define the discordance groups for RC and LDL-C.

All 2-sided P < .05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

3.2. CumRC and risk of CMM

Table 2 presents the results of the association analysis between CumRC and incident CMM. In 3 progressively adjusted multivariable Cox regression models, CumRC showed a positive association with incident CMM. After adjusting for age, gender, education level, residence, smoking status, alcohol consumption, sleep duration, hypertension, creatinine, BMI, and C-reactive protein, the hazard ratio (HR) for CumRC in relation to incident CMM was 1.03 (95% CI = 1.01–1.04), indicating that each 10 mg/dL increase in CumRC was associated with a 3% increase in CMM risk. Compared to participants in the first quartile, those in the fourth quartile had an HR of 1.77 (95% CI: 1.31–2.40).

3.3. Association of discordant RC and LDL-C with the risk of CMM

Table 3 shows the positive association between the percentiles of discordance between CumRC and cumulative LDL-C and the risk of CMM. In the fully adjusted model, the HR for discordant percentiles was 1.03 (95% CI = 1.00–1.05). Using a 15% difference as a threshold, discordant percentiles were divided into 3 groups: discordantly low group, concordant group, and discordantly high group, each comprising approximately one-third of the study population. Compared to the discordantly low group (participants with relatively lower CumRC levels), the discordantly high group (participants with relatively higher CumRC levels) showed a significantly increased CMM risk (HR = 1.31, 95% CI = 1.04–1.64, fully adjusted model), whereas the increased CMM risk in the consistent group was not statistically significant (HR = 1.16, 95% CI = 0.91–1.48).

3.5. Sensitivity analysis

We analyzed the association between CumRC, discordance percentiles, and CMM risk among participants with optimal LDL-C levels (<100 mg/dL). As shown in Table 4, even when LDL-C levels were optimal, increased CumRC was still significantly associated with a higher risk of CMM (HR = 1.02, 95% CI = 1.00–1.04). Additionally, individuals in the highest quartile had a 77% higher risk of CMM compared to those in the lowest quartile (HR = 1.77, 95% CI = 1.31–1.40). In the discordance analysis, CMM risk among individuals with optimal LDL-C levels also showed a positive association with the percentage of discordance between CumRC and LDL-C (HR = 1.06, 95% CI = 1.01–1.12); compared to the low discordance group, the high discordance group had a significantly increased CMM risk (HR = 6.44, 95% CI = 1.58–26.3). When the LDL-C threshold was changed to the median (<114.4 mg/dL), the results remained consistent (Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content).

In the analysis using multiple imputation to estimate pooled data, we obtained results consistent with the initial analysis (Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content). The subdistribution hazard calculated in the fully adjusted competing risk model was also similar to the initial analysis. When defining discordance as >10 percentile units, the results further demonstrated a significantly increased CMM risk in the high RC discordance group (Table S3, Supplemental Digital Content). In additional sensitivity analyses, we further adjusted for lipid-lowering medication use and physical activity based on model 3, the associations between CumRC, RC-LDL-C discordance (Table S4, Supplemental Digital Content), and incident CMM remained materially unchanged (Table S5, Supplemental Digital Content). Overall, these sensitivity analysis results were consistent with our primary findings, underscoring the robustness and reliability of our results.

4. Discussion

In this prospective cohort study of middle-aged and elderly individuals in China, we identified, for the first time, that high CumRC exposure is a significant risk factor for new-onset CMM. Furthermore, among individuals with substantial differences between RC and LDL-C levels, those with relatively higher RC levels were significantly associated with an increased risk of CMM. Even among individuals with optimal LDL-C levels, high CumRC and discrepancies between RC and LDL-C remained risk factors for CMM. Early identification of RC risk, particularly at normal LDL-C levels, may aid in distinguishing high-risk individuals for CMM, thereby improving the effectiveness of preventive and intervention strategies.

The association between dyslipidemia, including abnormalities in LDL-C, HDL-C, and lipoprotein(a) metabolism, and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) is widely and consistently recognized.[21,22] Observational studies have also explored their correlation with CMM,[23] though the independent relationship of TRLs remains uncertain. In recent years, a growing body of research has focused on the link between RC and cardiovascular disease incidence. Studies on the Copenhagen population, for example, suggest that RC significantly improves the predictive accuracy of traditional cardiovascular risk models for myocardial infarction and ischemic heart disease,[24] while notably elevating ASCVD risk in diabetic populations.[25] In studies involving Chinese populations, elevated RC levels have been shown to correlate significantly with the progression of impaired fasting glucose,[26] cardiovascular event risk,[27] and stroke incidence.[28] Furthermore, a study of over 3,00,000 UK Biobank participants, employing both observational and genetic correlation methods, confirmed a causal link between RC and CMM.[29] Our findings align with previous research, demonstrating a strong association between RC and the increased risk of type 2 diabetes, ASCVD, stroke, and multiple cardiometabolic conditions. Additionally, we confirmed the association between CumRC and CMM risk even among individuals with optimal LDL-C levels. Notably, in individuals with elevated RC, apolipoprotein B (ApoB) levels are typically also increased. Hvid et al found that elevated RC leads to increased ApoB levels, which further raises the risk of ASCVD.[30] ApoB is present in various atherogenic lipoproteins, including VLDL and IDL, and these lipoprotein particles deposit cholesterol in the arterial walls, promoting the development of atherosclerosis and exacerbating cardiovascular disease risk.[31] Increasing evidence suggests that novel lipid markers, such as ApoB and non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (non-HDL-C), are superior indicators of atherogenic lipoprotein burden, particularly in the context of diabetes and other lipid abnormalities.[32] Non-HDL-C not only includes LDL-C but also VLDL, IDL, and RC, reflecting the total cholesterol content carried by these particles. These lipoprotein particles play a key role in the development of atherosclerosis. Therefore, non-HDL-C provides a more comprehensive assessment of atherosclerotic risk than LDL-C alone. This further supports our findings that, even with optimal LDL-C control, elevated RC – particularly the accumulation of TRLs – continues to pose a significant threat to ASCVD and other cardiometabolic diseases. Although current guidelines do not target RC directly, emerging therapies such as Apolipoprotein C-III and angiopoietin-like protein 3 inhibitors have shown potent RC-lowering effects in clinical trials, offering potential options for managing residual risk in patients with discordantly high RC.[32] These results highlight the need for more comprehensive lipid management strategies that go beyond LDL-C control and actively address the residual risks posed by RC, ApoB, and non-HDL-C.

Beyond atherosclerosis and metabolic dysfunction, elevated cumulative remnant cholesterol (CumRC) may also contribute to early subclinical myocardial impairment. A recent study showed that metabolic dysfunction-associated with steatotic liver disease was independently associated with impaired left ventricular global longitudinal strain, a marker of subclinical myocardial dysfunction, even in the absence of manifest cardiovascular disease.[33] This finding suggests that long-term metabolic disturbances, such as persistently elevated CumRC, may not only impair vascular function but also lead to myocardial lipotoxicity, low-grade inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and microvascular impairment, thereby contributing to early myocardial injury and increasing the risk of CMM. These mechanisms provide additional support for the association between CumRC and CMM risk observed in our study, emphasizing the broader cardiovascular impact of CumRC exposure, even in individuals with well-controlled LDL-C levels.

Additionally, it is worth noting that a limitation of previous studies is that they assessed RC at a single time point, lacking repeated RC measurements, which limits a comprehensive understanding of how changes in RC influence disease progression. A recent observational study involving young and middle-aged adults in the United States, however, calculated cumulative RC exposure based on at least 6 consecutive RC measurements and found an independent association between cumulative RC exposure and cardiovascular event risk.[34] This association has also been confirmed in studies involving Chinese populations.[35] Xiao et al further demonstrated a link between cumulative RC exposure and major adverse cardiovascular outcomes in individuals over 75 years old with atherosclerotic heart disease.[36] In our study, we applied a similar approach to prior studies,[35-37, calculating the continuous variable CumRC based on baseline and repeated RC measurements across follow-up periods. Our findings suggest that high CumRC exposure is associated with an increased risk of CMM, providing more direct evidence for the importance of monitoring and maintaining appropriate RC levels for CMM prevention. Notably, we found that RC-LDL-C discordance, primarily characterized by high RC and low LDL-C levels, also appears to be strongly associated with CMM. In the continuous analysis, each 10 mg/dL increase in CumRC was associated with a 3% higher risk of CMM (HR = 1.03), an effect size comparable to those reported for other emerging lipid markers in similar populations. Moreover, individuals with persistently elevated CumRC had a 77% higher relative risk of developing CMM compared with those with lower CumRC levels. This magnitude of effect is sufficient to help identify high-risk individuals who may benefit from closer monitoring or more intensive preventive interventions, even when LDL-C is well-controlled. For patients with high CumRC despite achieving optimal LDL-C targets, clinical consideration may be given to more frequent follow-up or earlier lifestyle interventions. These findings support the use of CumRC as an adjunctive tool for CMM risk assessment, particularly in populations with residual risk despite well-controlled LDL-C. Recently, the RC-LDL-C discordance has gained increasing attention, as studies have demonstrated its association with various diseases. Prospective studies have shown that even in individuals with optimal LDL-C levels, RC-LDL-C discordance is independently linked to stroke and atherosclerosis.[17,20,38] Observational studies further suggest that RC is more strongly associated with hypertension than LDL-C,[39] and research by Guo et al clarifies that individuals with high RC and low LDL-C discordance are at significantly increased risk of hypertension.[18] Beyond RC, other novel biomarkers such as the uric acid-to-HDL ratio have also been shown to be associated with hypertension risk.[40] These findings underscore the importance of RC-LDL-C discordance in relation to cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease, aligning with our results. Our study is the first to explore the longitudinal association between RC-LDL-C discordance and CMM incidence, and we found that high RC is associated with an increased CMM risk, even among individuals with optimal LDL-C levels.

Based on the above findings, we further explored the formation and metabolic mechanisms of RC to gain a deeper understanding of its role in CMM risk. TRLs are gradually hydrolyzed during metabolism, forming small, dense, cholesterol-rich residual particles.[9] Approximately one-third of non-fasting plasma cholesterol resides in these residual particles, which can readily enter the subendothelial layer through active transcytosis, forming foam cells and promoting the development of atherosclerosis.[41] Compared with LDL, these residual particles are not only larger and rich in apolipoproteins C-III and E, but they also exhibit a stronger affinity for proteoglycans, increasing their retention in the arterial wall and thereby exacerbating atherosclerosis.[12,42] In addition to its significant role in AS progression, RC also plays a crucial regulatory role in insulin metabolism, further influencing metabolic health. In the metabolism of TRLs, insulin acts as an essential regulator by promoting fat storage, inhibiting lipolysis, regulating the assembly and secretion of VLDL and chylomicrons, stimulating lipoprotein lipase activity, and suppressing apolipoprotein C-III expression.[43,44] However, in diabetic patients, insulin resistance, hyperinsulinemia, and hyperglycemia can lead to elevated RC levels.[16,45] Studies further suggest that the large size and high cholesterol content of RC particles may exert a toxic effect on pancreatic β-cells, reducing insulin production,[46,47] while also triggering chronic inflammation, lipotoxic deposition, and damage to insulin signaling, thereby worsening insulin resistance.[48] Based on these mechanisms, although the relationship between CumRC and CMM is not yet clearly causal, high CumRC can be recognized as an important marker for identifying high-risk CMM populations.

The study has some limitations, and the conclusions should be interpreted within this context. First, our RC and CumRC values were estimated through calculation rather than direct measurement. Additionally, the calculation of CumRC may incorporate data collected near or at the time of the outcome, potentially introducing bias. Nevertheless, calculated RC and residual cholesterol have been shown to correlate closely with directly measured concentrations. This method of calculating RC and assessing cumulative exposure has been widely applied in numerous studies.[34,35,37] Future studies with repeated lipid measurements at multiple time points are warranted to more accurately assess CumRC exposure and to validate our findings. Second, the CMM diagnoses in our study were based on self-reported physician diagnoses, which may introduce information and recall bias. Third, the study relies on observational data, which may lead to biased associations due to residual confounding, despite our efforts to account for as many relevant factors as possible. Therefore, causal relationships cannot be confirmed. Lastly, our study only includes middle-aged and elderly individuals in China, which may limit the generalizability of the findings.

5. Conclusions

This prospective cohort study of middle-aged and elderly individuals in China demonstrates that high CumRC levels and RC/LDL-C discordance are closely associated with an increased risk of CMM, even among individuals with optimally controlled LDL-C levels. These findings suggest that maintaining appropriate RC levels may be associated with lower CMM risk, an association that persists even among those with well-controlled LDL-C.
