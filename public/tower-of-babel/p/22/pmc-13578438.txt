Entrepreneurial success across novice, serial and portfolio entrepreneurs
Authors: Jarosław Ropęga, Agnieszka Lipińska-Grobelny
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The aim of the article is to examine the relations between the type of entrepreneur (novice, serial, portfolio) and entrepreneurial success understood as a process that includes opportunity identification, opportunity exploitation, and the firm’s performance. The study, based on Shane’s framework and the resource-based view extended to include psychological capital, was conducted among 326 micro and small entrepreneurs in Poland. The results showed that the type of entrepreneur does not differentiate the overall level of success; however, it does influence its specific dimensions. Serial entrepreneurs demonstrate greater effectiveness in exploiting opportunities, whereas portfolio entrepreneurs are more successful in identifying them. Psychological factors such as self-efficacy, autonomy, and optimism proved to be stronger explanatory variables of success than the demographic variables analysed, and their importance appeared to be more strongly associated with success among more experienced entrepreneurs. The study confirmed the multidimensional nature of entrepreneurial success, integrated the resource-based perspective with the concept of psychological capital, and highlighted the need for tailored developmental support for different types of entrepreneurs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-59807-x.

Introduction

For a considerable time, entrepreneurship has been a salient subject of scientific inquiry on account of its role in economic development, innovation, and job creation. Micro and small enterprises (SMEs) constitute more than 99% of firms in Europe and account for nearly two-thirds of total employment1. In Poland, SMEs generate approximately half of national GDP, underscoring the importance of individual entrepreneurial activity for economic performance2. Despite extensive research on firm performance and entrepreneurial outcomes3,4, substantial heterogeneity in entrepreneurial success remains insufficiently explained.

Recent studies increasingly conceptualize entrepreneurship as a dynamic process involving opportunity identification, development, and exploitation5–7. This perspective highlights temporal dynamics, learning processes, and contextual influences, while recognizing that entrepreneurial success is multidimensional and includes both objective performance indicators and subjective outcomes such as satisfaction and well-being8–10. However, empirical research rarely integrates a process-based view with systematic analysis of differences between entrepreneur types.

Evidence from habitual entrepreneurship research suggests that prior entrepreneurial experience shapes opportunity-related behaviors and decision-making patterns11,12. At the same time, psychological characteristics such as self-efficacy, proactivity, and resilience have been identified as important predictors of entrepreneurial outcomes13,14, whereas the role of demographic factors remains inconsistent and often indirect15. Nonetheless, integrated models simultaneously examining entrepreneurial process stages, entrepreneur type heterogeneity, and the combined influence of psychological and demographic factors remain limited.

This perspective is particularly relevant in contemporary business environments characterized by rapid innovation, digital transformation, and increasing uncertainty, where entrepreneurs are required to continuously adapt, learn, and reconfigure available resources in response to emerging opportunities.

This study aims to develop and empirically test an integrated model of entrepreneurial success in the SME context that combines a process-based and multidimensional perspective with an analysis of entrepreneur type differences and individual-level determinants. The research integrates the process perspective16, the resource-based view17, and the concept of psychological capital18 to provide a comprehensive examination of mechanisms associated with entrepreneurial success across varying levels of entrepreneurial experience.

Theoretical background and hypotheses development

Conceptualizing entrepreneurial success: a multidimensional and process-based perspective

The concept of entrepreneurial success has been the subject of debate in the literature for a considerable time now due to its multidimensional and subjective nature. As Wiklund et al.4 point out, success can mean economic growth, as well as owner satisfaction, value realization, or social impact. In the context of SMEs, where the entrepreneur often acts as both owner and manager, success is closely linked to their personal sense of fulfilment10. In such entities, the boundary between the success of the company and the success of the individual is particularly blurred, as the entrepreneur’s definition of success reflects their individual values, motivations, and a sense of purpose10,19. Therefore, success should be defined in a multidimensional way, taking into account financial, psychological, and social aspects4,20.

In parallel, contemporary entrepreneurship research has shifted from static outcome-oriented perspectives toward a process-based view in which success unfolds through a sequence of activities, including opportunity identification, opportunity exploitation, and the realization of economic outcomes6,7,16. This perspective emphasizes temporality, learning, and contextual embeddedness, suggesting that success results from interactions between individual characteristics, resources, and environmental conditions5. Within SMEs, where entrepreneurs are frequently both owners and managers, the interdependence between personal and organizational success is particularly strong. As a result, success should be operationalized as a dynamic construct reflecting cognitive, behavioural, and economic dimensions rather than a single performance outcome.

Building on Shane’s16 process model and Wach et al.’s9 multidimensional framework, this study conceptualizes entrepreneurial success across three interrelated dimensions. The cognitive dimension reflects the ability to identify and evaluate opportunities, the behavioural dimension captures the effective exploitation and implementation of these opportunities, and the economic dimension represents perceived firm performance. These dimensions are mutually reinforcing: effective opportunity recognition enhances strategic decision-making, successful exploitation generates tangible results, and positive outcomes strengthen future motivation and learning processes5,6. Such a framework aligns with empirical practices in SME research, where subjective performance assessments often serve as valid proxies for objective indicators due to data constraints21,22.

Individual resources and psychological determinants of entrepreneurial success

The resource-based view17,23 provides an additional theoretical foundation by emphasizing that competitive advantage arises from valuable, rare, and inimitable resources. In SMEs, the entrepreneur represents a central strategic resource encompassing knowledge, experience, skills, and social networks24. Opportunity identification relies primarily on cognitive resources such as prior knowledge and market awareness, whereas opportunity exploitation reflects behavioural capabilities related to decision-making, coordination, and resource mobilization. Economic outcomes, in turn, indicate the effectiveness of resource deployment and organizational adaptation.

Extending the resource-based perspective to the individual level, psychological perspectives highlight the role of individual differences in shaping entrepreneurial outcomes. Meta-analyses consistently show that psychological traits and capabilities, such as self-efficacy, resilience, proactivity, and optimism, are strong predictors of business performance and entrepreneurial persistence13,14,25. These characteristics are often conceptualized as psychological capital, comprising hope, efficacy, resilience, and optimism18. Psychological capital enhances motivation, learning from failure, and adaptive decision-making under uncertainty8,26. From a process perspective, such resources influence all stages of entrepreneurial activity, shaping opportunity recognition, strategic responses, and the ability to cope with setbacks.

From a resource-based perspective, psychological capital can be viewed as a valuable intangible resource, because it enhances opportunity recognition, persistence, adaptive decision-making, and the ability to cope with uncertainty. However, its fit with the Resource-Based View is not straightforward. Unlike many organizational resources, psychological capital is not necessarily rare and can be developed through learning, experience, and training. Nevertheless, its entrepreneur-specific configuration, accumulated through individual experiences and embedded in unique cognitive and behavioural patterns, may make it difficult to imitate. Thus, psychological capital may contribute to entrepreneurial success not as an isolated source of sustained competitive advantage, but as a dynamic resource, whose value depends on its interaction with entrepreneurial experience, knowledge, and social capital.

In contrast, demographic characteristics, including age, gender, and education, have shown inconsistent and often indirect relationships with entrepreneurial success15,27. Human capital theories suggest that education and prior experience facilitate knowledge acquisition and access to resources28, yet empirical studies indicate that demographic variables alone rarely explain substantial variance in entrepreneurial outcomes when psychological or behavioural factors are included29,30. Instead, demographic characteristics frequently operate as contextual factors influencing access to networks, capital, and opportunities rather than directly determining performance.

Entrepreneurial experience and development of hypotheses

Entrepreneurial experience represents a key source of heterogeneity among entrepreneurs. Habitual entrepreneurship research distinguishes novice, serial, and portfolio entrepreneurs, whose accumulated experience shapes cognitive processes, strategic decision-making, and opportunity-related behavior11,12. Habitual entrepreneurs typically possess broader networks, deeper industry knowledge, and more developed heuristics, which facilitate both opportunity identification and exploitation31,32. Entrepreneurial learning theory further suggests that repeated venture experience enhances reflective learning, emotional regulation, and adaptive responses to uncertainty33,34.

Opportunity identification relies on cognitive schemas and prior exposure to diverse markets35,36, while opportunity exploitation depends on behavioural capabilities, including resource mobilization and execution skills37. Empirical evidence suggests that experienced entrepreneurs often demonstrate higher productivity and more consistent outcomes, though contextual factors (e.g., industry conditions, environmental uncertainty) can moderate financial performance15,38.

The relative importance of individual resources shifts across the entrepreneurial lifecycle. For novices, demographic characteristics may shape access to initial opportunities, whereas for experienced entrepreneurs, psychological capabilities and accumulated knowledge become more decisive determinants of performance39,40. Psychological capital, comprising self-efficacy, resilience, optimism, and hope, develops over time, reinforcing adaptive learning and strategic flexibility13,29. Consequently, the interaction between entrepreneur type, psychological resources, and demographic characteristics represents a central mechanism in explaining entrepreneurial success, forming the core of our conceptual model.

From a process perspective, entrepreneurial success emerges not only from accumulated experience but from how entrepreneurs interpret feedback, update cognitive schemas, and apply learning to subsequent ventures6,16,33. Habitual entrepreneurs follow distinct experiential trajectories, associated with different patterns of strategic orientation, network utilization, and opportunity engagement11,12. Simultaneously, resource-based theory highlights that intangible resources, particularly psychological and cognitive capabilities, shape opportunity evaluation and exploitation under uncertainty17,23. Empirical evidence confirms that psychological resources such as self-efficacy and resilience consistently support adaptive behavior and venture outcomes, whereas demographic characteristics primarily serve as contextual enablers rather than direct determinants13,15,29.

Importantly, the effects of psychological capital are subject to boundary conditions. Psychological resources do not automatically lead to entrepreneurial success, as their effectiveness depends on access to opportunities, complementary resources, and supportive environments. In resource-constrained contexts, high levels of psychological capital may be insufficient to generate superior outcomes. Entrepreneurial success is therefore likely to result from the interaction of psychological resources, entrepreneurial experience, and contextual factors.

Despite these advances, limited research has examined how entrepreneur type and individual resources jointly influence multidimensional entrepreneurial success, including cognitive (opportunity identification), behavioural (opportunity exploitation), and economic (firm performance) outcomes. To address this gap, the following hypotheses are proposed, each directly informed by the central model and process-based perspective:

H1: The overall level of entrepreneurial success differs across entrepreneur types (novice, serial, portfolio).

H2: The level of entrepreneurial success in each dimension (cognitive, behavioural, economic) differs across entrepreneur types.

H3: Psychological factors are stronger explanatory variables of entrepreneurial success than demographic variables.

H4: The influence of psychological and demographic factors on entrepreneurial success varies across entrepreneur types.

H5: Demographic factors play a supportive but weaker role than psychological factors in explaining entrepreneurial success.

H6: The importance of psychological factors in explaining entrepreneurial success increases with entrepreneurial experience.

Methods

Sample and data collection

The study included 326 entrepreneurs (men and women) operating in the micro- and small-enterprise sector, categorized into three types of entrepreneurship: serial (n = 110), portfolio (n = 105), and novice (n = 111). Women constituted 45% of the sample, men 53%, and 2% reported a different gender or did not disclose it. Gender distribution varied across entrepreneurship types, with women predominating among novice entrepreneurs (52.3%) and men among serial (52.7%) and portfolio entrepreneurs (60%). Participants were aged 18 to 67 years, with the largest groups aged 35–44 years (33.4%), 45–54 years (26.1%), and 25–34 years (25.2%). Portfolio entrepreneurs were most commonly 45–54 years old (28.6%), whereas novice entrepreneurs were predominantly 25–34 years old (30.6%). Educational background varied: college degrees were held by 56.4% of serial entrepreneurs, 46.7% of portfolio entrepreneurs, and 65% of novices; secondary education by 26–44%; vocational education by 6–11%; and lower secondary education by ≤ 4%. Regarding business characteristics, most serial and portfolio entrepreneurs founded their firms before 2009 (29.1% and 28.6%, respectively) or between 2009 and 2016 (28.2% and 27.6%). A majority of participants operated in the service sector (62.3%), while manufacturing was more common among portfolio entrepreneurs (35.2%) than serial (12.7%) or novice entrepreneurs (16.2%). Micro- and small enterprises were equally represented (50% each), although serial and novice entrepreneurs more often managed micro-enterprises (61.3% and 55.5%), while portfolio entrepreneurs mainly managed small firms (67.6%).

Data were collected via an online survey using LimeSurvey between 20 June and 18 July 2024. The study protocol was approved by Research Ethics Committee of the University of Łódź (Resolution No. 6/KEBN-UL/IV/2023-24) and adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Inclusion criteria were: (1) operating a micro- or small-sized enterprise, (2) belonging to one of the defined entrepreneurship types (serial, portfolio, or novice), (3) business founded no later than 31 December 2021, (4) age ≥ 18 years, and (4) providing informed consent. Participants were recruited via direct contact and a research agency. Survey participation required checking a consent box, and participants could withdraw at any time.

Measures

A combination of a researcher-developed survey and five standardized psychological questionnaires were used to assess correlates of entrepreneurial success according to Shane’s process-multidimensional framework (2003). Survey items assessed demographics, business experience, parental entrepreneurship, social support, and entrepreneurial success.

Entrepreneurial success (13 items, α = 0.73), was operationalized across three dimensions: (1) opportunity identification (4 items, α = 0.79), measuring the ability to recognize market opportunities (modified from Kuckertz41), (2) opportunity exploitation (4 items, α = 0.60), assessing actions to implement identified opportunities, (3) firm performance (5 items, α = 0.84), measuring perceived financial performance over the past three years (revenue, profits, customers, competitive position, and market share). All items were rated on Likert-type scales. Although the reliability coefficient for the opportunity exploitation scale (α = 0.60) fell slightly below the conventional threshold of 0.70, it remains within an acceptable range for exploratory research, particularly given the limited number of items and the well-known sensitivity of Cronbach’s alpha to scale length. Moreover, the items demonstrated conceptual coherence, were derived from established operationalizations of opportunity-related behaviors, and were retained on the basis of their theoretical relevance. Nonetheless, the interpretation of the findings concerning this subscale should be undertaken with appropriate caution.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using SPSS v29. Differences between entrepreneurship types were examined with one-way ANOVA and Kruskal-Wallis tests. Linear regression analyses were performed to identify psychological, demographic, and social correlates of entrepreneurial success for each entrepreneurship type. Effect sizes (eta², R²) and significance levels are reported.

Research results

Differences in entrepreneurial success across entrepreneurship types

To examine whether entrepreneurship type was associated with differences in overall entrepreneurial success and its dimensions (opportunity identification, opportunity exploitation, and firm performance), one-way ANOVA and Kruskal-Wallis tests were performed (hypotheses 1–2). No significant differences were observed in overall entrepreneurial success among the three entrepreneurship types (novice, serial, and portfolio). Serial entrepreneurs had the highest mean success score (M = 43.12, SD = 7.09), followed by portfolio entrepreneurs (M = 42.81, SD = 7.42) and novice entrepreneurs (M = 41.71, SD = 6.88). One-way ANOVA indicated that these differences were not statistically significant (F(2, 323) = 1.19, p = 0.30; Supplementary Materials, Table S1).

For opportunity identification, ANOVA indicated a marginally significant trend, suggesting that more experienced entrepreneurs may exhibit a slightly higher ability to identify opportunities compared to novices (F(2, 323) = 2.67, p = 0.07, η² = 0.02). Portfolio (M = 19.2, SD = 4.73) and serial entrepreneurs (M = 19.1, SD = 4.35) scored descriptively higher than novices (M = 17.92, SD = 4.62); however, these differences should be interpreted with caution, as the p-value exceeded the conventional significance threshold and the effect size was very small. Thus, the observed pattern may reflect a weak tendency rather than a robust group difference (Supplementary Materials, Table S2).

Regarding opportunity exploitation, the Kruskal-Wallis test revealed significant differences between groups (H(2) = 9.17, p = 0.01; Supplementary Materials, Table S3). Serial entrepreneurs had the highest mean rank (MR = 175.23), indicating the greatest ability to transform identified opportunities into action, whereas portfolio entrepreneurs had the lowest (MR = 141.22). This pattern may suggest that portfolio entrepreneurs, despite identifying opportunities, exploit them less.

For firm performance, one-way ANOVA indicated no significant differences between entrepreneurship types (F(2, 323) = 0.06, p = 0.93; Supplementary Materials, Table S4). Mean scores were similar across groups: novice (M = 17.78, SD = 4.51), serial (M = 17.97, SD = 5.15), and portfolio entrepreneurs (M = 17.99, SD = 4.25), suggesting that subjective evaluation of financial performance was comparable among all entrepreneurship types.

Entrepreneurial success across types of entrepreneurship: psychological, demographic, and social factors

To examine how psychological, demographic, and social variables relate to entrepreneurial success across different types of entrepreneurship (novice, serial, and portfolio), linear regression analyses were conducted separately for each group (hypotheses 3–6). Given the subgroup structure of the analyses and the associated risk of model overfitting due to reduced sample sizes within groups, results should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Demographic and social variables were transformed as needed to ensure comparability across models.

For novice entrepreneurs, the regression model reached only trend-level significance (F(25, 85) = 1.53, p = 0.07), explaining 11% of the variance in entrepreneurial success (R²adj = 0.11; Table 1). Given that the overall model did not meet conventional levels of statistical significance, the results should be interpreted with considerable caution and treated as exploratory. Although several variables (optimism β = 0.31, extraversion β = 0.23, personal development β = 0.21, and education β = 0.19) showed statistically significant or near-significant associations within the model, their interpretation is limited by the marginal overall model fit and relatively low explanatory power. These findings may indicate potential tendencies rather than stable or robust determinants of entrepreneurial success (Table 1).

Discussion and conclusions

This study examined the relationship between entrepreneur type and entrepreneurial success, conceptualized as a multidimensional construct encompassing opportunity identification, opportunity exploitation, and firm performance. The findings provide partial support for the proposed integrated model, suggesting that entrepreneurial success is shaped not only by entrepreneurial experience reflected in entrepreneur type, but also by psychological resources and behavioural processes. Overall, the results indicate that entrepreneur type alone is insufficient to explain variation in entrepreneurial success across its dimensions.

The results provided limited support for hypotheses H1 and H2. Contrary to expectations, no significant differences were observed in overall entrepreneurial success across novice, serial, and portfolio entrepreneurs; thus, H1 was not supported. Although serial entrepreneurs achieved slightly higher mean scores, followed by portfolio and novice entrepreneurs, these differences were small and statistically non-significant. This suggests that entrepreneurial experience alone may not translate into higher overall success, but rather its effects likely depend on how effectively experience is converted into actionable knowledge and resources11,33. Similarly, no significant differences emerged in the economic dimension of entrepreneurial success, operationalized as subjective firm performance. The relatively uniform evaluations across groups indicate that novice, serial, and portfolio entrepreneurs perceive their financial and market outcomes in a comparable manner. Given the self-reported nature of this measure, these findings should be interpreted with caution and may reflect shared evaluative standards rather than objective performance differences.

Hypothesis H2 received partial support. The results indicate that entrepreneurial experience is related to opportunity-related processes, although its effects differ across dimensions. No statistically significant differences were found in opportunity identification; however, a weak trend suggested that more experienced entrepreneurs may exhibit slightly higher ability to recognize opportunities. Due to the small effect size, this pattern should be interpreted cautiously, although it is broadly consistent with the knowledge-based perspective48,49. In contrast, significant differences were observed in opportunity exploitation. Serial entrepreneurs demonstrated the highest levels of opportunity implementation, whereas portfolio entrepreneurs showed the lowest. This suggests that entrepreneur type may be more strongly associated with behavioural execution than with opportunity recognition12,50. One possible explanation is that portfolio entrepreneurs engage in more diversified and coordinating activities across multiple ventures, which may reduce their focus on the execution of individual opportunities, consistent with the social capital perspective51. However, this interpretation remains tentative. Overall, no differences were found in economic performance, supporting the view that SME success is multidimensional and not reducible to financial outcomes alone10,20.

The results provide partial support for hypothesis H3, suggesting that psychological factors may play a more prominent role in explaining entrepreneurial success than demographic characteristics. In line with hypothesis H4, the importance and configuration of these factors may vary across entrepreneur types. Novice entrepreneurs appear to rely more on general motivational traits such as optimism and extraversion, whereas more experienced entrepreneurs may benefit more from characteristics such as autonomy, self-efficacy, and decision-making-related capabilities13,29. The explanatory power of the regression models increased with entrepreneurial experience, from 11% among novices to 40% among portfolio entrepreneurs. This pattern may provide tentative support for hypothesis H6 and may indicate that psychological resources become more differentiated and potentially more relevant as entrepreneurial activity becomes more complex33,39. However, given the cross-sectional design, these findings cannot be interpreted in causal or developmental terms and should be considered exploratory.

Hypothesis H5 received partial support. Among demographic variables, education may contribute to entrepreneurial success in some contexts, possibly by enhancing access to knowledge and cognitive resources27. In contrast, other demographic factors such as age, gender, and family background did not emerge as significant determinants in the present models. Overall, demographic variables may function more as background or enabling conditions rather than direct explanatory variables of entrepreneurial success15,27. Taken together, the findings may be broadly consistent with a process-oriented perspective on entrepreneurial success. While experience may be associated with differences in opportunity-related activities, its effects likely depend on psychological and cognitive resources. Accordingly, entrepreneurial success may be understood as the result of an interplay between experience, individual capabilities, and contextual conditions16,18, although further research is needed to confirm these relationships.

Theoretical contribution

This study contributes to entrepreneurship research by empirically validating an integrated model linking entrepreneurial experience, individual resources, and multidimensional success outcomes. The findings indicate that entrepreneurial success emerges from the interaction between entrepreneur type and psychological resources rather than from experience alone.

First, the study supports a process-based and multidimensional view of entrepreneurial success9,16, showing that opportunity identification, opportunity exploitation, and firm performance are distinct yet interrelated dimensions. Second, the findings extend the resource-based view17,23 by demonstrating that psychological factors constitute key strategic resources at the individual level. Psychological variables showed stronger explanatory power than demographic characteristics, highlighting the central role of psychological capital in SMEs. Third, the results indicate that the explanatory patterns of psychological resources differ across entrepreneur types. Novice entrepreneurs rely more on general motivational traits, whereas experienced entrepreneurs benefit from more advanced cognitive and self-regulatory capabilities such as autonomy and self-efficacy. Finally, the study refines habitual entrepreneurship theory12 by showing that differences between entrepreneur types are reflected in how opportunities are identified and exploited rather than in overall performance. This supports a functional rather than hierarchical understanding of entrepreneurial experience and aligns with recent work linking personality and social capital in entrepreneurship55.

The findings may also be interpreted in light of recent research emphasizing the role of human resource management practices, innovation capabilities, and digital transformation in shaping entrepreneurial adaptation and organizational performance. These studies suggest that the effectiveness of individual entrepreneurial resources depends not only on personal characteristics but also on the entrepreneur’s ability to learn, adapt, and reconfigure resources in response to changing technological and organizational environments56–58. From this perspective, psychological capital may be viewed as one component of a broader set of capabilities that support entrepreneurial opportunity recognition and exploitation under conditions of increasing complexity and uncertainty.

Practical implications

The findings indicate that tailoring entrepreneurship support programs based solely on firm size or industry sector may be insufficient. Instead, support mechanisms should be primarily adapted to entrepreneurs’ prior experience and their psychological capability profiles.

For novice entrepreneurs, interventions should prioritize the development of foundational psychological resources, such as optimism, self-efficacy, and social competence, through short training modules and structured mentoring. These efforts should be complemented by guided opportunity evaluation exercises aimed at reducing hesitation and building decision-making confidence. For serial entrepreneurs, support should focus on enhancing execution efficiency. This includes improving prioritization skills, resource allocation across multiple projects, and accelerating decision-making processes. Coaching sessions and peer benchmarking groups may prove particularly beneficial in helping this group maintain focus and avoid overcommitment. For portfolio entrepreneurs, who simultaneously manage several ventures, interventions should emphasize network orchestration and multi-venture coordination. Facilitated peer networks, collaboration platforms, and tools supporting delegation and governance are recommended to address their specific challenges.

Across all entrepreneur types, systematic assessment and development of psychological competencies should be integrated into support programmes. Consequently, policy design should shift from demographic-based targeting toward a staged, capability-driven approach that aligns with the entrepreneurial experience level of the beneficiaries. In practical terms, this may involve incorporating brief assessments of psychological resources, such as self-efficacy, optimism, and autonomy, into entrepreneurship support programmes to identify developmental needs at different stages of entrepreneurial activity. Entrepreneurship incubators, accelerators, and public support agencies could then use these assessments to direct entrepreneurs toward tailored mentoring, training, or networking interventions that correspond to the psychological profiles associated with success in each entrepreneur group. Such an approach may improve the allocation of support resources and increase the effectiveness of entrepreneurship development initiatives.

Study limitations and future research directions

This study provides an empirical contribution by examining how entrepreneur types and psychological factors relate to multidimensional entrepreneurial success. At the same time, several limitations should be acknowledged, particularly those related to the cross-sectional design and causal inference. In line with Shane’s Model of Entrepreneurial Opportunity, which conceptualizes entrepreneurial outcomes as being influenced by individual-level psychological and cognitive characteristics, the present study assumes a theoretically grounded directional relationship from psychological resources to entrepreneurial success. However, despite this theoretical rationale, the cross-sectional nature of the data does not allow for definitive causal inference. Moreover, several methodological considerations should be kept in mind. Some scales demonstrated internal consistency below conventional benchmarks, which is not uncommon in exploratory research employing brief measures. The use of self-reports, while well-established in SME studies, may introduce perceptual biases and common method variance. The subgroup regression analyses were based on limited sample sizes, which may affect the robustness of the estimates. Therefore, these results should be viewed as exploratory, offering valuable insights that invite further investigation rather than providing definitive conclusions.

Despite these limitations, the study offers several important contributions. It integrates psychological and resource-based perspectives with entrepreneur typologies, providing a more comprehensive explanation of entrepreneurial success. By adopting a multidimensional approach, it captures not only economic outcomes but also cognitive and behavioural dimensions such as opportunity identification and exploitation. Moreover, by comparing novice, serial, and portfolio entrepreneurs, the study highlights the heterogeneous nature of entrepreneurship and challenges one-size-fits-all explanations of entrepreneurial success. The findings also suggest that the explanatory role of psychological resources may vary across entrepreneur types, supporting a more context-dependent understanding of entrepreneurial performance.

Future research would benefit from longitudinal designs that allow for stronger causal inference and the examination of reciprocal relationships. Cross-country comparative studies could further clarify the role of institutional context. It would also be valuable to strengthen the operationalization of psychological resources within a Resource-Based View framework, particularly regarding their inimitability and dynamic nature. Methodologically, future studies should aim to reduce potential overfitting through larger samples, more parsimonious models. Finally, future work could enhance practical implications by providing more specific, evidence-based recommendations tailored to different entrepreneur types, particularly in the context of innovation and digital transformation. Future research could also examine how psychological capital interacts with innovation-related capabilities, human resource management practices, and digital transformation processes to influence entrepreneurial success across different types of entrepreneurs.
