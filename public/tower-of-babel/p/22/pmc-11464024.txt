Cutaneous mucormycosis shortly after renal transplantation
Authors: John C Lam, Hannah K Gray
Published: 2024

A 66-year-old man with hypertensive nephrosclerosis and no history of diabetes underwent renal transplantation and was given antithymocyte globulin induction during surgery. One day later, he noted a raised, well circumscribed, mildly tender area of purpura. He reported that he had scraped his right forearm on a metal garbage can lid 1 week before the transplant. The transplant team was concerned about cellulitis and prescribed piperacillin–tazobactam; however, the pain intensified and the lesion worsened (Figure 1).

Lesion of the right forearm in a 66-year-old man, (A) 3 days after renal transplantation, showing superficial erosion, violaceous erythema, associated ecchymoses, and hemorrhagic crusting, and (B) 7 days after transplantation, showing an enlarging hemorrhagic ulcer with eschar, ecchymoses, and crusting.

Because of the worsening on empiric antibiotics, we biopsied the lesion; histopathology showed invasive fungal elements suggestive of Mucorales (Appendix 1, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.240881/tab-related-content). Limb-salvaging débridement of the right forearm found necrosis of the dermis, subcutis, and skeletal muscle. Polymerase chain reaction amplification confirmed Rhizopus microsporus, a species within the taxonomic order Mucorales. We prescribed a prolonged course of oral isavuconazole 372 mg daily and a 2-week course of liposomal amphotericin B at 5 mg/kg daily. Six weeks after starting antifungal therapy, the patient’s wound looked healthy, and we decided to continue isavuconazole for the time being.

Rhizopus species are ubiquitous environmental saprophytic fungi that can cause cutaneous mucormycosis through trauma. Risk factors include uncontrolled diabetes mellitus, hematological malignancy, stem cell transplantation, prolonged severe neutropenia, and trauma. The tropism of Mucorales for angioinvasion leads to rapidly worsening gangrene.1 Early diagnosis is critical, given the 10%–25% mortality rate associated with localized cutaneous mucormycosis and the greater than 90% mortality associated with disseminated disease. 2 Diagnosing cutaneous mucormycosis can be challenging because of its nonspecific presentation; clinicians should have a low threshold to biopsy nonhealing necrotic wounds for culture and histopathology, particularly in immunocompromised patients (Appendix 2, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.240881/tab-related-content). In our patient, we hypothesize that the depletion of T-lymphocytes caused by the use of antithymocyte globulin induction put him at risk of developing mucormycosis. Surgical débridement is important because antifungal medications are usually insufficient on their own.3
