A 2–year retrospective cohort study of pulp-related outcomes of permanent anterior teeth with uncomplicated crown fractures
Authors: Samudi Sathsara Kalubowila, Handapangoda Mudalige Kanisha Gimahani Handapangoda, Ihala Wanni Arachchilage Priyake Dheera Palipana, Ranathunga Mudiyanselage Sonali Dilini Kumari Ranathunga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUncomplicated crown fractures (UCFs) are the most common form of traumatic dental injury in permanent dentition. However, studies on long-term pulp outcome in South Asian populations are limited. This study aimed to analyse pulp survival of permanent anterior teeth with uncomplicated crown fractures in a Sri Lankan population.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted following STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines. Records of 257 teeth with uncomplicated crown fractures in patients attending a tertiary dental care centre in Sri Lanka between 2018 and 2023 were used. Pulp status was assessed using clinical and radiographic criteria over a 24–month period and categorised as pulp survival, pulp necrosis, or pulp canal obliteration. Cox regression, the log-rank test, and Kaplan–Meier survival analysis were employed to identify predictors of pulp necrosis.ResultsAt 24 months, pulp survival was observed in 92.6% of teeth, pulp necrosis in 4.7% of teeth, and pulp canal obliteration in 2.7%. Teeth with enamel-only fractures had 100% pulp survival within this sample, whereas enamel-dentine fractures showed reduced survival. Tooth substance loss greater than 40% significantly increased the risk of pulp necrosis (Hazard Ratio (HR) = 5.00, 95% CI: 2.17–11.54, p < 0.001). Teeth with concomitant luxation injuries were also significantly associated with reduced pulp survival (p = 0.007). Treatment delay and stage of root development were not significant predictors.ConclusionsUCFs generally demonstrate favourable pulp survival. However, concurrent luxation injuries and extensive tooth substance loss were associated with increased risk of pulp necrosis. Adherence to trauma management protocols and long-term monitoring are essential for optimal outcomes and for early detection of complications.

Introduction

Traumatic dental injuries are a common occurrence, affecting one-fourth of school-aged children worldwide, and constitute the second leading cause for tooth loss in populations under 19 years of age [1]. Furthermore, one-third of adults have experienced dental trauma to permanent dentition. While deciduous teeth often sustain luxation injuries, permanent teeth encounter more hard tissue injuries [1–3].

An uncomplicated crown fracture (UCF) is defined as a fracture of the dental crown causing loss of enamel or enamel and dentine, without pulp exposure. These account for one-third of all traumatic injuries in the permanent dentition [4]. Compared to teeth with complicated crown fractures (involving direct pulp exposure), teeth with uncomplicated crown fractures have a favourable pulpal prognosis, reported as 72.3% and 82.3%, respectively [5].

According to the published research, several factors influence pulp-related outcomes, including the presence of concomitant luxation injuries, the degree of root development, the interval between trauma and treatment, and the extent of the fracture [1, 2, 5–8]. Clinicians need to consider these factors when determining the most appropriate course of action for managing traumatic dental injuries. While pulp-related outcomes after uncomplicated crown fracture (UCF) have been investigated in several studies, the majority of these investigations have been conducted in Europe and East Asia. Few studies have used survival analysis techniques to assess the long-term pulp prognosis and related risk variables, and there is still a scarcity of evidence from South Asian populations [2]. Additionally, there are no studies on the impact of concurrent luxation injuries and fracture severity on pulp survival in this cohort. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to evaluate pulp survival of permanent anterior teeth with UCFs in patients treated at a major tertiary dental care centre in Sri Lanka.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective study was conducted in accordance with the STROBE guidelines using archived clinical and radiographic records from patients treated at the Restorative Dentistry Unit B, Institute of Oral Health, Sri Lanka, a tertiary referral centre, between January 2018 and December 2023. Ethical clearance was obtained from the Ethics Review Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, University of Colombo.

All trauma cases recorded during the study period were screened to identify eligible patients. Due to the retrospective nature of this study and limitations in record retrieval systems, the exact number of originally screened cases could not be determined and STROBE flow diagram could not be constructed. After applying inclusion and exclusion criteria, 153 patients who sustained uncomplicated crown fractures to 257 permanent anterior teeth were included for the final analysis. Teeth with incomplete clinical and radiological records, root fractures, other pre-existing pathologies (caries, periodontal diseases, developmental abnormalities), previous traumatic injuries or repeated episodes of traumatic injuries, or those initially managed elsewhere were excluded from the study. Only teeth that completed the 24-month clinical and radiographic review were included; teeth lost to follow-up or extracted before this period were excluded.

Selection bias may have occurred because only patients who completed 24–month follow-up were included. The use of retrospective records may have resulted in information bias. Furthermore, inclusion of multiple teeth from the same patient may possibly have resulted in clustering effects.

Data collection and measurement

During the initial visit, all the patients were clinically examined by a House Officer, and the diagnosis, including the presence of associated luxation injuries and the extent of tooth substance loss, was verified by a postgraduate trainee in Restorative Dentistry using a standardised dental trauma record form. According to the International Association of Dental Traumatology (IADT) guidelines, treatment, clinical and radiographic follow-up (1-, 3-, 6-, 12-, 18-, and 24-month) was provided by House Officers under the supervision of postgraduate trainees in Restorative Dentistry.

Teeth were classified into 2 main groups according to the presence or absence of concurrent luxation injury, and Andreasen’s classification was utilised to diagnose the type of luxation injury [9, 10]. The extent of the fracture was assessed using 2 methods according to the tissue involved and the percentage of tooth substance loss. Standard periapical radiographs were used to assess the degree of root formation (Table 1). The amount of tooth substance loss was recorded based on visual assessment of the clinical records and periapical radiographs and categorised into predefined groups as < 20%, 20–40% and > 40% according to standard clinical judgment.

For the current retrospective study, data were obtained independently by two trained postgraduate trainees in Restorative Dentistry using a predefined set of diagnostic criteria and a standardised data collection form. Clinical records and radiographs were reviewed independently, and variables requiring subjective assessment, including the percentage of tooth substance loss, were categorised after independent evaluation. Any inconsistencies in the categorisation were resolved through discussion. Although formal inter-rater reliability assessment was not performed, the use of predefined evaluation criteria, independent review, and consensus discussions helped to minimise subjective variation.

Treatment was provided according to IADT guidelines and included recontouring and polishing, reattachment of the fractured crown segment, or composite restorations, along with luxation injury management where necessary [9]. If the fracture line was in close proximity to the pulp, a protective calcium hydroxide (Calcicur; Voco, Cuxhaven, Germany) lining was placed before the reattachment or the composite restoration (SwissTech Composite, Coltene Whaledent, Altstätten, Switzerland used with the manufacturer’s adhesive system). Time elapsed after the trauma and the treatment was recorded as < 24 h, 24–72 h, and > 72 h.

The pulp-related outcomes were evaluated at each follow-up visit over a 24-month period and were classified into 3 groups according to the criteria utilized by previous studies: pulp survival, pulp necrosis, and pulp canal obliteration. Clinical and radiological findings were utilised for pulp status diagnosis [2, 11]. Both the electric pulp tester (EPT) and heated gutta percha (HGP) were used to assess the pulp sensibility (Table 2).

Data analysis was conducted using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), version 25 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Each tooth was taken as an independent observation. Some patients sustained trauma to more than one tooth, so independence of observations may not be entirely ensured. Data analysis was done using survival analysis. The risk factors associated with pulp survival prognosis were investigated using the Kaplan–Meier curves with log-rank tests. A multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression model was used to identify independent predictors of pulp necrosis. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Overall outcomes

A total of 257 teeth with uncomplicated crown fractures in 153 patients were included in the study. Among all patients, 101 (66.0%) were male, and 118 (77.1%) were younger than 19 years of age. 66 (45.0%) of patients reported within 24 h, and 57 (37.3%) of patients attended the clinic after 72 h. Composite resin restoration was the most common treatment (74.7%) of teeth, followed by reattachment of the fractured segment (11.3%) and recontouring without any restorative intervention (14.0%).

At 24 months, pulp survival was observed in 238 teeth (92.6%), pulp canal obliteration in 7 teeth (2.7%), and pulp necrosis in 12 teeth (4.7%). The distribution of pulp outcomes according to clinical variables is depicted in Table 3. Teeth with concomitant luxation injuries showed significantly lower pulp survival compared to teeth without luxation injuries (p = 0.007). Teeth with greater than 40% tooth substance loss exhibited significantly increased risk of pulp necrosis (HR = 5.00, 95% CI: 2.17–11.54, p < 0.001). In contrast, there was no significant association between the pulp survival and stage of root development (p = 0.175), tissue involved (p = 0.629) and time elapsed before treatment (p = 0.837) (Figs. 1, 2, 3 , 4 and 5).

Pulp-related outcome of teeth with pure uncomplicated crown fractures

Of 159 teeth with pure UCFs, 48 teeth (30.2%) had enamel fractures and 111 (69.8%) had dentine involvement. Overall pulp survival was 98.1% and 97.5% in 12-month and 24-month, respectively. 100% pulp survival was observed in teeth with enamel fractures. Three teeth that underwent pulp necrosis had dentine involvement with more than 40% tooth substance loss, closed apices, and treatment delays of 24–72 h. Only one tooth (0.62%) underwent pulp canal obliteration at the end of 24-month. In this subgroup, Kaplan–Meier analysis showed that the percentage of substance loss was significantly associated with pulp necrosis (p < 0.001), while fracture extent (p = 0.252), apical closure (p = 0.181), and treatment delay (p = 0.130) were not.

Pulp-related outcomes of teeth with uncomplicated crown fractures and concomitant luxation injuries

Ninety-eight teeth sustained both UCFs and luxation injuries. Of these, 33 teeth (33.7%) had enamel fractures and 65 (66.3%) had dentine involvement. Pulp survival was 86.7% at 12-month and 84.7% at 24 months. Six cases developed pulp canal obliteration, all in teeth with complete root formation and severe luxation injuries. Kaplan–Meier and Cox regression analyses confirmed that the percentage of tooth substance loss (HR = 6.18, 95% CI: 2.50–15.28, p < 0.001) and extent of the fracture into dentine (HR = 0.07, 95% CI: 0.01–0.68, p = 0.022) were independently associated with an increased risk of pulp necrosis. Type of luxation injury (HR = 1.89, 95% CI: 1.34–2.68, p < 0.001) was also significantly associated with increased risk of pulp necrosis. Teeth with concussion and subluxation had better pulp survival compared to teeth that suffered from intrusion, lateral luxation, and extrusion. (Table 4) Fracture extent, apical closure, and treatment delay were not statistically significant in this subgroup (Table 5).

Discussion

The overall pulp survival of the current study was 92.6% at 24 months, which is consistent with previous studies of 75% to 100% [2, 4, 5, 7, 12]. These findings are also consistent with a recent systematic review, which concluded that uncomplicated crown fractures usually have favourable clinical outcomes when managed according to established trauma protocols [12]. Enamel fractures had an exceptional prognosis, with 100% survival, while enamel–dentine fractures had a slightly higher risk of pulp necrosis. Tooth substance loss greater than 40% was independently associated with an increased risk of pulp necrosis, confirmed by both Kaplan–Meier and Cox regression analyses. These findings support earlier observations that emphasised the adverse effect of deeper dentine involvement by increasing dentinal permeability and facilitating the ingress of microorganisms and bacterial toxins toward the pulp. Additionally, disruption of the odontoblastic layer and dentinal tubules due to deeper fractures may impair the pulp’s defensive response and reparative potential [4, 5, 13]. The European Society of Endodontology (ESE) position statement issued in 2021 also underscores that dentine exposure leads to pulp permeability and therefore should be sealed promptly to preserve vitality [14, 15].

Clinically, the finding that teeth with greater tooth substance loss had a substantially higher risk of pulp-related complications may assist clinicians in identifying teeth that will benefit from prompt sealing of exposed dentine, conservative restoration, more frequent follow-up visits, and clear communication regarding the increased risk of pulp necrosis and the need for long-term monitoring.

Even though the degree of tooth substance loss was independently judged by two trained postgraduate trainees and disagreements were settled by consensus, inter-rater reliability assessment was not performed. Therefore, some degree of subjective variability in the categorisation of tooth substance loss cannot be completely excluded.

According to previous studies, teeth with concurrent luxation injuries had significantly lower pulp survival (84.7% at 24 months) than teeth without (97.5% at 24 months) [2, 11, 13]. Intrusion and lateral luxation were strongly associated with pulp necrosis and pulp canal obliteration, probably due to disruption of the blood supply. Furthermore, Wang et al. (2014) found that severe luxation injuries accompanied by crown fracture led to notably higher pulp necrosis risk, especially in completed roots. This supports our findings that luxation type is a significant predictor of outcome.

Contrary to some studies, stage of root development and treatment delay did not show a significant difference in the current study [2, 11, 14, 15]. The limited number of pulp necrosis events in our cohort could have masked a weaker relationship, particularly when overshadowed by tooth substance loss and the effect of luxation injuries.

The primary outcomes assessed “pulp survival, pulp necrosis, and pulp canal obliteration” are in line with the biological outcomes suggested for dental trauma research, despite the fact that this retrospective study was designed prior to the release of the International Association of Dental Traumatology Core Outcome Set. The comparability and therapeutic relevance of research in this area would be further strengthened by future prospective studies that include the entire Core Outcome Set [1, 9].

It is crucial to remember that pulp necrosis can appear years after the traumatic incident. Literature states that teeth with intrusion or lateral luxation manifest pulp necrosis later than milder periodontal injuries such as concussion, emphasizing increased risk over time [15–17]. Therefore, prolonged monitoring is advised even in cases of an initial favorable response. Strict adherence to IADT protocols, prompt sealing of dentine and conservative restoration are essential for optimising outcomes. Regular clinical and radiographic monitoring remains critical, especially for teeth that suffered concurrent luxation injuries.

The current study has several limitations that should be taken into account when interpreting the findings. First, its retrospective design relied on existing clinical records of patients who completed the planned 24-month follow-up, which may have introduced selection bias, potentially leading to an overestimation of pulp survival. Furthermore, variations in the completeness and quality of the clinical records may have influenced the accuracy of data collection despite the use of predefined eligibility criteria and a standardised data collection form.

Second, even though the unit of analysis was the individual tooth, some patients contributed more than one tooth to the study. As a result, the independence of observations was not entirely ensured, and clustering was not accounted for in the statistical analysis, potentially leading to an underestimation of standard errors and an overestimation of statistical significance. Therefore, the reported confidence intervals and p-values should be interpreted with caution. Analytical approaches that account for clustered observations, such as mixed-effects survival models or robust variance estimation, should be considered for future studies to provide more precise estimates of the associations.

Lastly, only 12 cases of pulp necrosis were detected during the 24-month follow-up period. Therefore, the multivariable Cox regression analysis was based on a limited number of outcome events relative to the number of explanatory variables included in the model. As reflected by the wide confidence intervals observed for some predictors, this may have reduced the stability of the regression model and the precision of the estimated hazard ratios. Additionally, the limited number of pulp necrosis events may have reduced the statistical power to identify other clinically relevant predictors. Therefore, the absence of statistically significant associations for other variables and reported hazard ratios should be interpreted cautiously.

To validate these findings, multicenter studies with larger populations and longer follow-up periods are recommended. Despite these limitations, this study contributes valuable evidence regarding pulp-related outcomes of uncomplicated crown fractures in a South Asian population.

Conclusion

UCFs frequently show a good pulpal prognosis. Although concurrent luxation injuries and tooth substance loss greater than 40% were associated with an increased risk of pulp necrosis, these findings should be interpreted in light of the limited number of outcome events and require confirmation in larger prospective studies. For optimal outcomes and to ensure early detection of adverse pulpal changes, careful monitoring and adherence to evidence-based trauma management protocols are essential.
