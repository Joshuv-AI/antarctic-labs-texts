Complications after flexor tendon surgery: A retrospective cohort study of 336 patients
Authors: Benedicte Heegaard, Rasmus Wejnold Troest
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe management of flexor tendon injuries in the hand is a well-known challenge worldwide, and postoperative complications have been reported with a pronounced incidence. However, little is known about possible associations with adverse events following surgery.PurposeThe aim of this study was to report outcomes and incidence of postoperative complications in patients undergoing surgery after traumatic flexor tendon injuries in the hand.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed medical records of 336 patients who had been surgically treated for flexor tendon injuries of the hand in two hospitals in the period of 2010–2020. We divided patients in groups and compared those with major complications (n = 56) to those without (n = 280). Major postoperative complications were defined as rerupture, adhesion leading to surgery and infection leading to revision and/or admission.ResultsIn total, 17 % experienced a major complication following surgery. We found that 7.7 % of patients experienced rerupture of the sutured tendon, 6.5 % of patients experienced adhesion leading to tenolysis and 2.4 % experienced infection leading to revision and/or admission. Traumas in zone II of the hand and lesions in both flexor digitorum superficialis and profundus were both separately associated with a greater risk of major complications (OR 2.132 and OR 2.317, respectively).ConclusionThe incidence of patients experiencing major complications following flexor tendon surgery is 17 %, and patients with traumas in zone II of the hand and multiple tendon lesions have a higher risk of experiencing a major postoperative complication

Introduction

Flexor tendons injury in the hand is a common trauma in all groups of age and is mostly prevalent in young male patients.1,2 Many surgeons have for decades mastered the skill of suturing the tendons with a variety of different suture methods and in later years a consensus of the recommended methods is starting to form.3 For a long time, it has been widely accepted that traumas in zone II of the hand are challenging to operate on and that patients with trauma in this region experience more adverse outcomes and a poorer function of the hand post-operative compared to patients with trauma in other zones.4,5 However, improvements in surgical techniques and the post-operative rehabilitation seem to improve the outcomes gradually.6 The ideal timing for surgery following trauma has also been a subject of discussion. It has been suggested that one should perform primary surgery on flexor tendon lesions within days to better the result and minimize the risk of complications,4,7 yet the maximum time from injury to surgery seems to create disagreement among surgeons.8 Other potential risk factors of an adverse outcome such as age, gender and smoking have been associated with adverse outcomes in various studies, but results are conflicting. Essentially, we need more information with larger patient cohorts to determine possible associations with adverse outcomes.

The aim of this study is to investigate the post-operative outcomes of patients with flexor tendon lesions and explore possible associations with major complications and decreased function.

Methods

This is a retrospective review of electronic medical records for patients surgically treated for flexor tendon lesions in the hand at either of two university hospitals in a major capital city in the period of 2010–2020. Following ethical approval, the records of patients diagnosed with code DS661, DS660 or DM663 (International Classifications of Diseases, 10th Revision) and/or the procedure code KNDL42A from the year 2010–2020 were extracted from the National Patient Registry. A minimum of two years of follow-up was required. A total of 679 patients were reviewed. This article only includes patients with lesions in flexor digitorum profundus (FDP), flexor digitorum superficialis (FDS) or flexor pollicis longus (FPL). We included patients who attended examinations following surgery and who had a final examination, in which they were medically concluded at the orthopedic department. In this study, we do not distinguish between the operating surgeons, however, all procedures were performed by trained hand surgeons or supervised by trained hand surgeons who have practiced as specialists for >5 years.

Patients were excluded from the study if they 1) had trauma in other tendons (flexor carpi ulnaris, flexor carpi radialis etc.), 2) they had no follow-up after surgery, 3) they had large and complex traumas, 4) they received a palmaris longus graft at the primary surgery, 5) they had spontaneous rupture of the tendon due to a previous fracture treated with a plate, or 6) they were not surgically treated for their flexor tendon rupture.

In total, we included 336 patients in this study. This paper has been conformed to the STROBE guidelines.

Patients and outcomes

The electronic medical records were searched for information such as patient gender, age at surgery, operating technique, zone of the injury, time from primary injury to surgery, time from surgery to last contact, number of contacts with surgeons, nurses and hand therapist, adverse outcomes post-operative and the function of the finger at the final visit. We were not able to include smoking-status nor the prevalence of diabetes among patients in our analysis, as this information was not included in most of the journals.

For primary analysis, we compared the above-mentioned variables in patients with major complications to patients without major complications.

Major complications following surgery were defined as either rerupture of the tendon, adhesions leading to tenolysis or infection leading to admission and/or revision.

The post-operative function of the finger is determined at the last visit using the Pulpa-Vola distance (PVD) in centimeters. A PVD of ≥2 cm leads to insurance coverage in the country of this study, which is why the outcome of our patients are divided according to end PVD at ≥ or <2 cm.

The zones of the hand were based on the Verdan classification.9

Operative technique and postoperative care

The technique of tendon repair as well as pulley venting has gradually been modified from 2010 to 2020 in the two hospitals, meaning that we have no exact dates of changes in the regimens.

All patients were operated on under ultrasound-guided infraclavicular brachial plexus nerve block performed by our department of anesthesia. The surgery was performed under tourniquet control and the patients were in the supine position. The wounds were cleaned with saline and the skin was then disinfected. Adequate elongation of the wound was performed as to investigate the extend of the injury. The affected tendon/tendons were retracted, and the ends of the tendons were trimmed.

Associated nerve injuries were treated as per protocol and sutured under loop-magnification or microscope magnification. Tendon repair technique was at the discretion of the surgeon and included >10 different methods with or without epitendinous sutures. In this study, we divided patients in three groups accordingly to the number of strands used: 1) two-strands, 2) four-strands or 3) six/eight-strands. Patients who underwent surgeries with markedly different techniques from the others were not included in the groups.

In the period of 2010–2020 patients have received heterogenous postoperative care. In the first years of the inclusion period, the rehabilitation was more conservative with immobilization and passive range of motion. In the later years it gradually evolved with the introduction of early active mobilization. Addressing the heterogeneity of the postoperative care during the inclusion period, we divided patients in groups having surgery between 2010–2015 and 2016–2020. From around 2015 and ongoingly almost all patients followed the same postoperative rehabilitation: Hands were postoperatively placed in a cast which was removed 3–5 days following surgery, and active range of flexion and extension within the limits of the splint was initiated simultaneously with our hand therapists. The molded splint was constructed at the first visit but was not shortened at two-weeks. After four weeks the splint is only used at night and in high-risk situations. At six weeks the splint is discontinued, and common activity of daily living (ADL) is gradually allowed. After twelve weeks full weight bearing in ADL is allowed.

Statistics

For our analysis we used IBM SPS Statistics version 28.01.0. We applied Student’s T-test for the analysis of continuous variables and Chi Square test for binary variables. Many of our continuous variables are not normally distributed and are therefore presented with a median value and a range from lowest to highest value. For non-normally distributed analysis we applied the Mann–Whitney U test.

For primary multivariate analysis, we compared patients with major complications to patients without major complications. For this we used logistic regression analysis adjusted for age, gender, damaged finger, nerve injury, zone, the year of surgery and time from injury to surgery.

For sensitivity analysis, we divided patients in groups having surgery ≥3 vs <3 days and ≥7 vs <7 days after the primary injury.

For our secondary multivariate regression analysis, we compared the number of strands used in surgery in relation to major complications. For this, we used logistic regression analysis, adjusted for age, gender, damaged finger, nerve injury, zone, and time from injury to surgery.

P-values of <0.05 were defined as statistically significant.

Postoperative complications

In total, 56 patients (17%) experienced a major complication following surgery whilst 280 patients did not. We found that 26 (7.7%) patients experienced rerupture of the sutured tendon, 22 (6.5%) patients experienced adhesion leading to tenolysis, 8 (2.4%) patients experienced infection leading to revision and/or admission. Risks of adverse outcomes

In crude comparison, more patients with major complications had injuries in zone II (64%) compared to patients without major complications (46%) (p=0.019). Additionally, more patients with major complications had lesions in both FDP and FDS (44%) compared to 27 % (p = 0.012). These results are listed in Table 4. The same association was found in adjusted logistic regression analysis, where patients with injuries in zone II and patients with injury to both FDP and FDS had higher risk of a major complication (OR 1.997 [1.061–3.759] and OR 2.613 [1.378–4.953], respectively). Age, gender, concomitant nerve injury, the year of surgery and the number of days from injury to primary repair were not associated with an increased risk of major complication. In multivariate analysis adjusted for the above-mentioned variables, we found no difference in major complications in patients sutured with four strands (ref) compared to six-eight strands (OR 1.296, [CI 0.636–2.641]). Patients sutured with four or six-eight strands had a higher risk of postoperative complication compared to patients sutured with two strands (OR 2.978, [CI 1.103–8.045] and 3.914, CI [1.3–11.774], respectively).

As expected, more patients experiencing a major complication had an end PVD ≥2 cm (25%) as compared to patients who did not experience a major complication (10%) (p = 0.006). Moreover, patients with a major complication had statistically significant more visits with surgeons, nurses, and hand therapists post-surgery (p<0.001). These results are listed in Table 4. We demonstrated no differences in frequencies of major complications when comparing each year between 2010 and 2020.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to investigate the postoperative outcomes of patients with flexor tendon lesions and explore possible associations with post-operative complications.

In this study we demonstrated that zone II injuries and lesions in both FDP and FDS were separately associated with a higher risk of postoperative major complications.

This study is limited by its retrospective design. We were not able to investigate certain possible associations with an adverse outcome such as smoking-status and diabetes-status, as these variables did not appear in most journals. Also, 53 patients were not included in this study due to no follow-up after surgery. Some of these patients were tourists and did not receive further hospital controls, as they would travel home after the surgery. Meanwhile, other patients simply did not show up to the controls. We are not able to conclude any outcomes for these patients, however, theoretically we believe that if they experienced postoperative complications, they would seek medical advice. If the patients lost to follow-up did not experience complications, the percentage of major complications would theoretically decrease in this cohort. Another important limitation is the missing distinguishment of the precise surgical methods and the post-operative rehabilitation regime, as these have changed gradually during the period of inclusion. This means that our patient group is highly heterogeneous when it comes to operation technique and rehabilitation program. Trying to address this issue, we compared the incidence of major complications in the years from 2010–2015 and 2016–2020, demonstrating no difference.

The rates of major complications in this study were 7.7 % for rerupture, 6.5 % for tenolysis and 2.4 % for serious infection.

A recent big database study by Demers et al10 with >10.000 patients from the US reported an incidence of tenolysis at 6 % in the period of 2010–2020. One other study by Bamal et al11 with only 68 patients presented a tenolysis rate of 3.5 %, while a larger study with >1300 patients by Svingen et al.12 from Sweden presented a rate at 5 %. Lastly, Reito et al.7 reported an incidence at 5.9 % of patients having surgery for adhesions in a cohort of 187 patients.

Rerupture rates reported in other studies also varies a great deal. One study by Duru et al13 reported a rate as low as 1.5 %, however this study was rather small with only 61 patients. Svingen et al.12 reported a rate at 5 % and Reito et al.7 reported a rate of 7 %. Lastly, the Bamal et al.11 study with 68 patients reported an incidence as high as 11.6 %. In summary, our rates of tenolysis and rerupture are very comparable to the studies with larger cohorts while it is rather different compared to the studies with small cohorts. This indeed emphasizes the great value of producing studies with larger cohorts. Surprisingly, not many studies have reported the incidence of serious infection following flexor tendon surgery. One study by Ranjan et al.14 reported an incidence of 2.2 % for revision surgery while another by Cooper et al.15 reported an incidence of 1.25 % among children. We reported a similar incidence of 2.4 % for serious infection.

For many years it has been a widely accepted that patients with zone II injuries experience more postoperative complications compared to other zones,5 however, newer reviews report that patients with zone II injuries are experiencing the same results as patients with injuries in other zones due to better surgical techniques.6 Nevertheless, our results still demonstrate that surgeries in zone II is challenging.

Furthermore, we demonstrated in this study that patients with both FDP and FDS lesions have a higher risk of experience a major complication compared to those with lesions in only one tendon in spite of adjustment of the zone of injury in the analysis, a finding supported by other previous studies.12,16,17 Whether or not one should repair both FDP and FDS seems to be determined by each surgeons’ preferences. As demonstrated by Shalimar et al.18 32 % of Malaysian surgeons would repair both tendons and 36 % would only repair FDP. Recently, Sadek19 demonstrated that patients sutured in both FDP and FDS had a greater power grip after surgery, however data on this topic is limited, and the decision of repairing FDS still varies greatly depending on intraoperative findings such as sliding of the sutured tendons.

The time elapsed from injury to surgery is a topic of great discussion. In recent reviews by Tang,3,20 it is described that the timing of surgery after injury does not create a major difference in outcome, however, if the time exceeds 3 or 4 weeks after the injury, this will often require a more extensive repair and perhaps antibiotics. Supporting this, Munz et al.21 revealed fairly good results in patients having surgery up to 5 weeks post injury. Contrary, Reito et all7 demonstrated in their study that patients having surgery between 3 and 7 days after the injury had a higher risk of major complications, and Reddy et al.22 reported that patients having surgery after 14 days experienced more major complications. We did not establish a correlation between the time from injury to surgery in patients who experienced major complication.

We compared the number of strands in the sutured tendons, demonstrating no difference between four- and six/eight-strand methods. Patients sutured with two strands had a lower risk of complications, but these patients had different injuries including jersey lesions in zone I, so the results should be interpreted with caution. These results do not imply that a lesion in zone II-V should be operated with two strands. Over time, it has been more widely accepted that surgeons should use methods with at least four strong core sutures, and preferably even six strands or more, depending on the zone of injury.3,6,20,23 Additionally, pulley venting is now a regular practice among many surgeons, but the outcome widely depends on the correct identification intraoperatively of the pulleys and understanding their anatomy.3,20

Not surprisingly, we found that patients experiencing major complications had more contacts with the hospitals and a prolonged rehabilitation of a median of 1 year.

One future possible option of optimizing flexor tendon repair could be the WALANT (Wide-awake local anesthesia no tourniquet) method,24 which theoretically would provide better intraoperative overview of the tendon’s function. However, we need more studies to support this.

In general, when comparing our study to prior literature, we have a relatively large sample size, and the complication rates are very much comparable to other larger studies. Prospective studies and smaller sample sizes generally seem to report decreased complications rates; however, given the nature of the studies, this can be expected.

Finally, the incidence of patients experiencing major complications following flexor tendon surgery is 17 %, and patients with traumas in zone II of the hand and multiple tendon lesions were associated with a higher risk of major postoperative complications.
