Multiplexing Two Types of High‐Order Topological Corner States in Kagome Lattice Photonic Crystals
Authors: Ning‐Jing Luo, Hang Chen, Meng‐Yu Li, Xin‐Tao He, Jian‐Wen Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTopological corner states with wavelength‐selective excitation and directional routing enable on‐chip wavelength‐dependent beam splitters (WDBSs) for multiplexing technology. In this work, we employ two types of topological corner states to implement WDBS in kagome lattice photonic crystals. A tight‐binding model incorporating both nearest‐neighbor and next‐nearest‐neighbor couplings is applied to describe the bulk and corner states in the second‐order topological photonic crystal. A 120‐degree‐turning interface is constructed that supports two types of corner states, where the type‐I corner state is even mode originating from nearest‐neighbor coupling and the type‐II state is odd mode originating from next‐nearest‐neighbor coupling. The spatial Fourier transformation of eigenstates indicates that the type‐I mode supports propagating waves along 30‐degree channel and is inhibited along the 90‐degree channel, and vice versa for type II. With different resonant wavelengths, the two types of corner states have direction‐wavelength locking mechanisms, enabling us to propose a 1 × 2 channel WDBS. Such WDBS design has achieved a low‐crosstalk multiplexing based on the directionality of corner states. This finding provides guidance for multiplexing different types of topological photonic states, which is conducive to promoting the design of on‐chip wavelength/mode‐division multiplexers with topological protection.

Introduction

Topological insulators have undergone extensive theoretical and experimental investigations, primarily driven by their unique edge states and exceptional transport characteristics [1, 2]. In recent years, the concept of higher‐order topological insulators has been proposed featuring lower‐dimensional boundary states, which go beyond the conventional bulk‐edge correspondence of traditional topological insulators (i.e., a d‐dimensional [dD] topological insulator possesses (d‐1)D edge states) [3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. For example, in the case of a second‐order 2D topological insulator, not only do 1D edge states propagating along the boundary exist, but also 0D corner states localized at the corners. In photonic systems, photonic crystals (PCs) have emerged as a promising platform for implementing topological concepts due to their tunable geometric structures in subwavelength units and highly controllable band dispersions [8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. Topological photonic crystals (TPCs) have been used to demonstrate many topological physics with non‐trivial phases, such as quantum Hall effect [9, 13], quantum spin Hall effect [14, 15, 16, 17], quantum valley Hall effect [18, 19, 20], and second‐order topology with nonzero 2D Zak phase [21, 22, 23, 24, 25]. One of the typical mechanisms to realize the second‐order non‐trivial topological phase is the 2D Su–Schrieffer–Heeger (SSH) model, when intra‐unit‐cell sites are connected via internal couplings and adjacent unit cells via external couplings [25, 26, 27, 28]. A topological phase transition occurs when internal and external coupling strengths are equal. The 2D photonic SSH model is more experimentally feasible: topological phase transitions can be achieved by regulating the distance between dielectric rods or air holes in the same and nearby unit cells through shrinking/expanding. The photonic second‐order corner state (SOCS) has been theoretically and experimentally verified in the square‐lattice topological PCs [25, 29, 30, 31]. Note that such a type of corner state with even symmetry is induced by short‐range interaction of nearest‐neighbor (NN) inter‐cell sites (labeled as type I). Experimental observations revealed that the type‐I corner states in PC slab exhibit a high‐Q factor, which directly verifies its potential for application as a nanocavity for intriguing light–matter interaction [32, 33, 34, 35, 36].

On‐chip optical beam splitter (BS) is one of the core components to develop multiplexing technology for integrated photonics. By independently modulating and parallel transmitting optical signals with different degrees of freedom (DoFs) of light, one can implement various types of DoF‐dependent BS (e.g., amplitude, wavelength, polarization, mode) to improve the capacity of optical information processing [37, 38, 39, 40] and also offers technical insights for metasurface research [41]. The introduction of topological properties of PCs into BS designing will be promising for realization of on‐chip multiplexing technology with topological robustness and compact size. For example, based on the first‐order TPCs with valley Hall effect, some topological BSs have been proposed for the prototype of nanodevices, including 3‐dB power splitting for quantum interference [42, 43, 44], polarization‐controllable splitting with large bandwidth [45, 46, 47] and wavelength‐dependent splitting for wavelength division multiplexing [48, 49, 50, 51]. In regard to second‐order TPC, another type of corner state (labeled as type II), dominantly induced by long‐range interaction of next‐nearest‐neighbor (NNN) inter‐cell site, has been observed in microwave kagome lattice PCs [52], and a series of works were subsequently carried out based on two types of SOCSs [53, 54, 55, 56, 57, 58]. Different from type I, the type‐II corner state manifests odd symmetry and different resonant wavelength, enabling it to manipulate two types of SOCSs simultaneously [57]. Through selectively controlling the wavelength and mode symmetry of incident waves, one can achieve a wavelength‐dependent beam splitter (WDBS) to multiplex these two types of SOCSs and can be conductive to develop the technology of topological wavelength division multiplexing. However, an intuitive understanding of the mechanism on controlling the selective excitation of corner states was previously lacking, which is limited for the practical design of topological WDBS based on the two types of SOCSs.

In this work, we realize WDBS in kagome lattice PCs by employing two types of SOCSs and revealing their direction‐wavelength locking mechanism, which provides significant guidance for the theoretical prediction of multiport topological routing. For one, the second‐order topological photonic crystals (SOTPCs) are designed by the lattice deformation of a kagome lattice PC based on a 2D SSH model. A tight‐binding model (TBM), which incorporates both NN and NNN couplings to account for long‐range interactions, is employed to characterize the bulk and corner states of SOTPCs in the kagome lattice. We construct a 120‐degree‐turning interface that supports dual corner states—exactly the two types of SOCSs, namely type I and type II. With respect to the spatial symmetry of these two states, type‐I and type‐II SOCSs correspond to an even and odd mode. This configuration can be validated through both TBM calculations and numerical simulations. Spatial Fourier transforms of these two types of SOCSs reveal a special directional selectivity that the type‐I case permits wave propagation along a 30‐degree channel while suppressing it along 90‐degrees, with the inverse behavior observed for type II. Combined with the feature of their distinct resonant wavelengths, these states exhibit direction‐wavelength locking, enabling the design of a 1 × 2 channel WDBS.

Theory of 2nd‐Order Topological Photonic Crystal

As shown in Figure 1a, the SOTPCs of our work are designed in kagome lattice. Rhombic‐profile holes (side length: L = 0.28a) are distributed on the silicon background to form a unit cell (lattice constant: a = 407 nm) composed of three air holes (i.e., cluster) arranged in a triangle lattice. Note that the effective permittivity of the dielectric background is ε eff = 8.12, determined from the TE0 mode index of a silicon slab with a thickness of 0.541a. This thickness corresponds to a 220‐nm‐thick silicon slab when the operating wavelength is approximately 1550 nm. In theory, such SOTPC can be investigated by means of the 2D SSH model which provides a clear picture for analyzing its intrinsic mechanism. Figure 1e gives the schematic of a 2D SSH model consisting of intra‐cell coupling ta, NN inter‐cell coupling tb and NNN inter‐cell coupling tc. Regarding an unperturbed kagome lattice, that is the inter‐cell and NN intra‐cell couplings are equivalent, the distance from the center of the unit cell to the center of rhombic holes is R1=3a/6 (inset [a1] of Figure 1). To obtain trivial and non‐trivial second‐order topological phases, one can detune the strengths of intra‐cell coupling and NN inter‐cell coupling (ta≠tb), corresponding to adjusting the intra‐cluster distance of three air holes in practice. By making the air holes shrink inward or expand outward, a PC (inset [a2] in Figure 1) with trivial phase can be obtained when R = R 2 = L2 and a PC (inset of [a3] in Figure 1) with topologically non‐trivial phase can also be obtained when R = R 3 = 3a3−L2. Figure 1b–d show the TE‐polarized bulk band structures of the corresponding shrunken PC (a2), the original PC (a1) and the expanded PC (a3). For the original PC (a1), the TE‐polarized band exhibits a degenerated Dirac point at K. When the topological phase transitions in kagome lattice PCs are induced by expanding or shrinking the air clusters (see the inset), the degenerated Dirac point is splitting and a bandgap is opened. In this case, the trivial PC (a2) and the non‐trivial PC (a3) have the same band structure with a 15.2% band gap from 0.243c/ato0.283c/a (c is the velocity of light in vacuum).

Therefore, this model is convenient to analyze the topological nature of kagome lattice PCs.

Localized Mode Based on Double Corner States

Different from the first‐order topology, higher‐order topological phases exhibit distinct characteristics beyond conventional bulk‐edge correspondence. For example, a 2D PC with a non‐trivial second‐order topological phase supports 0D corner states, that is SOCSs. In this section, we will discuss the topological properties of two types of SOCSs based on kagome lattice PCs, and analyze the frequency characteristics as L (the side length of the rhombic air holes) increases. As shown in Figure 2a, we designed a 120‐deg bend interface formed by trivial PC (a2) surrounding non‐trivial PC (a3) to obtain corner states. The non‐trivial PC (a3) is arranged as a triangular shape with a side length of 20a, and the outer region is terminated with the topologically trivial PC (a2). Due to the difference in topological phase [26, 27], there are several intrinsic states (isolated from bulk and edge states) that localize around the corner, that is, corner states. For confirmation, we obtained the eigenmodes spectrum of this 120‐deg bend interface via the finite‐element method (FEM) in Figure 2b. In simulation, the entire PC structure has a size of 85a × 253 a and is covered with a scattering boundary. Within the bandgap, there exist edge states and two separated eigenmodes. The red point represents type‐I corner state operating a lower frequency (around 0.263c/a), whereas the orange point represents the type‐II case featuring a higher frequency (around 0.264c/a). To gain deeper insights into the characteristics of the corner states, the evolution of eigenfrequencies is plotted as a function of side length L in Figure 2c, from a side length of 0.25a to 0.31a. As L gradually enlarges (i.e., the proportion of air in PCs increases accordingly), the eigenfrequencies of both type‐I and type‐II states increase. Particularly, the eigenfrequency curve of type I intersects with that of type II at L = 0.306a. The inset shows that the difference Δf (the frequency difference between the type‐II and the type‐I SOCSs) changes from a positive value to a negative value as L increasing. Note that type I manifests a faster increase in eigenfrequency than that of type II, because most of the electromagnetic energy of the type‐I corner state is localized in the air‐hole region of the corner.

To clarify the corner states in theory, a supercell TBM analogous to the 120‐deg bend interface is constructed. As schematically shown in Figure 2d, the corners are composed of isolated sites (marked by red dashed boxes) and dimers (marked by orange dashed boxes). The sites in the yellow dashed box represent the boundaries of the structure. For the TBM eigenmode spectrum of Figure 2e, we can observe two sets of eigenmodes (marked by red dots and orange dots) localized within the band gap and isolated from bulk and edge states, corresponding to the two types of SOCSs. The type‐II corner state is isolated from the upper bandedge of edge states (marked by yellow‐green dots). The eigenstate of type I has even‐symmetry distribution and its eigenstate energy mainly concentrates on isolated sites, whereas the eigenstate of type II (energy is dominant for dimers) has odd symmetry (see Appendix A and Figure 3). Next, we discuss the eigenenergy variation of SOCSs in TBM. An increase in L corresponds to a lower filling ratio of dielectric media within the unit cell for the PC structure, which will weaken the coupling between the nearest air holes and thereby reduce the intra‐cell coupling t a and NN coupling t b . Such an effect on intra‐cell coupling is dramatically stronger than that of NN coupling, resulting in an increase in the ratio t b /t a . On the other hand, as the electric‐field energy of the corner state is in favor of concentrating on the high‐dielectric region of PC (in order for lower frequency), the decrease of filling ratio leads to reducing the energy in the dielectric region and causing an increase in M0 (in order for higher operation frequency). Therefore, we consider the NNN interaction coefficient t c used in the calculations is fixed as t c = 0.4 with varying ratio t b /t a , and the on‐site energy term M0 is proportional to t a (M0∝ t a ). As shown in Figure 2f, the eigenenergy of both two types of SOCSs increase when increasing the ratio t b /t a . Particularly, the eigenenergy of type I exhibits a steeper increase compared to that of type II, in agreement with the evolution trends of simulation results (Figure 2c). The inconsistencies in Figure 2 are mainly derived from the much more complex condition of PC design than TBM. For one, to precisely describe the actual PC, it is essential to include higher‐order coupling terms beyond the NN and NNN couplings in TBM. For another, the TBM interface only considers one domain wall and the inter‐cell couplings are cut off at the edge and corner, whereas the PC interface should be constructed by a topologically non‐trivial PC and another trivial PC, thereby introducing additional couplings at the edge and corner. Nevertheless, we should emphasise that the corner states in TBM maintain two key features: the deterministic existence of two types of SOCSs and their spatial symmetry, providing intuitive guidance to understand the formation mechanism of these corner states. TBM can qualitatively and intuitively describe the dispersion relationship of PCs, and clearly demonstrate the evolution process of type‐II corner state splitting from edge states through parameter scanning.

Direction‐Wavelength Locking Mechanism

Further, we focus on the distributions of these two types of SOCSs in both the real space and momentum space, in order to reveal the direction‐wavelength locking mechanism. Figure 4a,e give the real‐part eigenfield patterns of H z at resonant wavelength (λ 1 = 1551 nm and λ 2 = 1546 nm, respectively), that is, type‐I and type‐II SOCSs. The type‐I state exhibits an even symmetry with respect to the yellow dashed line, whereas the type‐II state is an odd mode. To further explore the resonant modes in momentum space, we extract the H z eigenfields with various wave vectors by a 2D spatial Fourier transformation (FT). Figure 4b,f gives the 2D spatial FT spectrum of H z for type I and type II, respectively. In the FT spectrum, the wave vector components of the excited fields are predominantly distributed along the Brillouin zone boundary, with a notable concentration at the six K points. This phenomenon arises from the opening of the photonic bandgap at the degenerate K points, which strongly modulates the propagation and localization of the excited optical modes.

Eigenfield patterns of the two types of corner states in real space and those in Fourier space. (a) and (e) Real‐part Hz fields in real space for type I (λ1=1551nm) and type II (λ2=1546nm) states, respectively. Type‐I state has even symmetry with respect to the yellow dashed line, whereas type‐II state is odd mode. (b) and (f) 2D spatial Fourier transformation (FT) spectrum of Hz field for type I and type II, respectively. For type I, the field component along (d) 90‐deg direction is much stronger than that of (c) 30‐deg direction. On the contrary, type II presents a field component along (g) 30‐deg direction, whereas there is almost no component along (h) 90‐deg direction. That means the two types of corner states have specific directional selectivity and can be coupled to different directions, achieving spatial separation of the two states.

More importantly, the wavevector components become extremely weak in some specific directions of Γ−M, enabling us to manipulate the directional selectivity of excitation. For example, the type‐I SOCS has few components along the 30‐deg direction (blue arrow in Figure 4b) whereas type II has almost no component along 90‐deg direction (red arrow in Figure 4f). To visually demonstrate field characteristics, we also depict the distributions of wavevector components (normalized by the maximum component in k space) along these two directions. For Type I, the field component along the 90‐deg direction (Figure 4d) is much stronger than that of the 30‐deg direction (Figure 4c). On the contrary, Type II presents a peak at the M point of the 30‐deg direction (Figure 4g), whereas there is almost no component along the 90‐deg direction (Figure 4h). Due to the different resonant wavelengths of these two states, the directional selectivity is locked to wavelength, namely direction‐wavelength locking mechanism. Therefore, we can conclude that the two types of SOCSs have specific directional selectivity and can be coupled to different directions, achieving spatial separation of the two states.

Design of Wavelength‐Dependent Beam Splitter

Now we apply the directional selectivity of two types of SOCSs to design a wavelength‐dependent BS. Figure 5a shows the schematic of a 1 × 2 channel BS based on SOTPC bend interface, of which some air holes are filled with silicon to form three types of defect PC waveguides (see Appendix C and Figure 7). The footprint of designed BS is 105a × 283 a. In the structure, three different types of PC defect waveguides have been designed to achieve high‐efficiency coupling with three ports. The operation wavelength range of these defect modes covers the resonant wavelengths of two types of SOCSs (see Appendix B and Figure 6). The channel along 90‐deg direction (output to Port 2) supports corner mode for type I, whereas the channel along 30‐deg direction (output to Port 3) supports corner mode for type II. Line‐defect PC waveguides are introduced at all three ports with the aim of improving the coupling efficiency. In simulation, a line source is placed at the left waveguide, and a line detector (e.g. Port 2 and Port 3) is placed at the other two output waveguides to measure the transmitted power. The width of line source and line detector are the same as the width of strip waveguides. The incident waves that contain two types of SOCSs wavelengths propagate along the strip waveguide and defect waveguide, and then excite the two types of SOCSs when the coupling length between waveguide and corner is proper (see Appendix D and Figure 8 for detailed discussion of on the influence the coupling distance l 3 of insertion loss and bandwidth). Here, l 1 = 4.5a, l 2 = 2.25 × 2a3 and l 3 = 6.75 × 2a3. In the COMSOL simulation, the TEM mode approximation was employed to solve for these two dominant modes separately, thereby allowing the device transmittance to be calculated based on their superposition. This simplified approach accurately captures the dispersion and coupling characteristics of the waveguide while avoiding the high computational cost of a full 3D vectorial simulation, thus ensuring the reliability of the transmittance results. Figure 5b shows the simulated field distributions of the two excited wavelengths at 1556 and 1548 nm, respectively. The type‐Ι SOCSs (1556 nm) is almost guided into the defect waveguide (along Γ−M′ direction) and output from Port 2, whereas the type‐II case is mainly guided into the defect waveguide (along Γ−M direction) and output from Port 3. To check the wavelength‐division performance, we calculate the transmittance at Port 2 (T 21) and Port 3 (T 31) in Figure 5c. T 21 and T 31 denote the ratios of the output power in Port 2 and Port 3 to the incident power in Port 1. In the transmission spectrum of the output from Port 2 (red lines), there is a dominant transmission peak at 1556 nm corresponding to type I, whereas in the transmission spectrum of the output from Port 3 (blue lines), there is a transmission peak at 1548 nm corresponding to type II. Hence, such WDBS design with 1 × 2 channel enables us to multiplex the two types of SOCSs, and can be conducive to on‐chip WDM applications based on topological modes. And we can increase the port number of output channels by cascading different designs of SOCSs, showing potential applications for multi‐beam routers in multiplexing technology. Furthermore, we have discussed the tolerance of device performance (the wavelength difference of two type of SOCSs and the device crosstalk) on the side length L and the slab thickness h, which can better establish the robustness and practicality of the proposed device (see Appendix E and Figure 9).

Finally, we will discuss the variation of wavelength‐dependent splitting by changing angle θ of orientation between Port 3 and the x axis. Figure 5d shows the transmission spectra for θ = 20° and θ = 45° designs. Both Type‐I and Type‐II SOCSs are coupled to Port 3 that degrade the wavelength‐division performance of WDBS, although Port 2 can basically maintain selective couple of Type I. To compare the beam splitting more intuitively, we calculate the crosstalk (CK) that one of the important performance indicators for optical multiplexing devices. Here, the CK is defined as the transmittance ratio of the leakage wavelength to the signal wavelength at the target port, that is CK=10lgTleakage/Tsignal. For Port 3, we aim to selectively excite the type‐II corner state, and thus the signal (leakage) wavelength should be 1548 nm (1556 nm). Figure 5e gives the CK curve of Port 3 as a function of θ. We can observe the CK curve reaches a minimum value (i.e., −13.32 dB) at θ = 30°, in correspondence with the FT analysis in Figure 4. For Port 3, there is another crosstalk dip at θ = 40° (target to couple the type‐II corner state at 1548 nm), but the type‐I corner state at 1556 nm is coupled simultaneously and thus the CK for Port 2 degrades sharply. In a word, the wavelength‐division mechanism based on the selective excitation of corner states can be theoretically predicted, and thus the two output channels separate different wavelengths deterministically. It will benefit us to design WDBS with topological robustness on dimensional bias.

Conclusion

In summary, we have theoretically explored the eigenstates of SOTPCs in kagome lattice, and discussed the eigenfrequency variation of the two types of SOCSs by changing the size length of PC structures and coupling coefficients of TBM. The two types of SOCSs with different resonant wavelength manifest distinct directional selectivity, and thus we have successfully applied the direction‐wavelength locking property to implement a topological WDBS. The manipulation of wavelength DoFs in SOCSs will enrich the capability of light manipulation in the emerging field of topological nanophotonics. The proposal of topological WDBS provides a novel method to design on‐chip passive devices, particularly for the development of wavelength/mode‐division multiplexers with topological protection. Besides, benefiting from the high‐Q modes and resonant wavelength distinction of SOCSs, we can promote the topological features of SOCSs into light–matter interaction with dual‐wavelength effect, including exciton/phonon polariton and nanolasers.

Evolution of WDBS Transmission Spectra, Bandwidth and Q Factor With Coupling Distance l 3

In this section, the coupling distance l 3 was varied to investigate its impact on the output transmission spectra, bandwidth (BW), and qualify (Q) factor, with the goal of identifying the optimal l 3 for minimizing coupling loss and maximizing the overall performance of the device. As shown in Figure 8a–c, for Port 3, at l3=5.25×2a3, the transmission spectrum exhibits a resonance peak with a Q factor of 5570.4 and a BW of 0.278 nm. As l 3 increases to 6.25×2a3, the resonance peak intensity reaches its maximum, whereas the Q factor slightly decreases to 5491.3 and the BW narrows to 0.282 nm. This indicates that l3=6.25×2a3 provides the optimal coupling strength for maximizing the output power at Port 3. When l3 is further increased to 7.25×2a3, the Q factor rises to 8462.1 and the BW decreases to 0.183 nm, but the peak transmission intensity is reduced compared to the case at l3=6.25×2a3. At optimized l3, the transmittance of Port 3 is improved by 0.188, indicating the coupling loss is reduced to be 2.65 dB. This value is well below the 3 dB threshold for low‐loss on‐chip photonic integration.

Effect of Key Geometric Parameters on Device Performance

To guide the design of low crosstalk topological WDBS, we have discussed the evolution of crosstalk and wavelength difference (Δλ) with side length L and slab thickness h. Meanwhile, by fixing pores at the edge of the unit cell, the manufacturing error at distance R can be ignored. Figure 9 systematically characterizes the performance of topological corner states by plotting device crosstalk (CK, blue solid line with filled circles) and dual‐resonance wavelength difference (Δλ = λ II − λ I, red solid line with asterisks) versus two geometric parameters: L and h. In Figure 9a, as L increases, Δλ exhibits a clear monotonic growth, confirming that the spectral separation of the two corner modes can be predictably tuned. Specifically, when L ranges from 0.268a to 0.296a, |Δλ| exceeds 2 nm, thereby ensuring well resolved dual resonances. The crosstalk, however, displays a more complex non‐monotonic behavior, with a range of L (approximately 0.27a–0.282a) in which the crosstalk remains below −10 dB. As for h, the |Δλ| still exhibits a gradual increasing trend and remains clearly separable over h = 190–250 nm (Figure 9b). In summary, practical design windows are identified in the L spaces, where both sufficient wavelength separation and low crosstalk can be simultaneously achieved. Consequently, these results provide clear, actionable guidelines for fabricating robust topological WDBS with predictable performance and high signal integrity.
