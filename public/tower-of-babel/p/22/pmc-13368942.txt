Quantitative proteomics identifies GntR as a novel potential DIVA antigen for bovine brucellosis
Authors: Haoqing Lv, Xiaxia Liu, Yi Luo, Chunhui Ma, Lvfeng Yuan, Liyuan Liu, Wei Ran, Yinjuan Song, Suzi Zhang, Pengcheng Gao, Fuying Zheng, Yuefeng Chu, Jian Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBrucella is a zoonotic pathogen causing brucellosis. Current serological tests cannot differentiate infected from vaccinated animals (DIVA) due to antibody interference from live vaccines. This study used parallel accumulation-serial fragmentation (PASEF)-based quantitative proteomics to compare virulent S2308 and vaccine A19 strains of Brucella abortus. Among 2,131 identified proteins, 302 were differentially expressed, including 43 and 32 proteins exclusive to S2308 and A19, respectively. Bioinformatic analysis showed enrichment in metabolic and transport functions. The transcriptional regulator GntR family (GntR), with significantly high expression in S2308, was selected as a DIVA candidate antigen. Reverse transcription quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) and western blot confirmed significantly higher GntR transcription and expression in S2308 during logarithmic growth. Serological validation indicated stronger reactivity of GntR with sera from wild strain-infected cattle than from A19 vaccinated or negative cattle. An indirect enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (iELISA) based on GntR was established and showed high sensitivity and specificity, consistent with standard bovine brucellosis diagnostics. Dynamic monitoring in a mouse model revealed that iELISA enabled DIVA differentiation from 0 to 21 days post-infection. Thus, GntR is a novel potential DIVA antigen, providing a robust serological tool to distinguish B. abortus wild strain infection from A19 vaccination in cattle.Key points• PASEF-based quantitative proteomics to compare proteomic profiles of B. abortus S2308 and A19.• GntR transcription and expression are higher in Brucella S2308 than in A19 during logarithmic growth.• A novel potential DIVA antigen GntR was identified for bovine brucellosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00253-026-13872-4.

Introduction

Brucella is a Gram-negative facultative intracellular pathogen that causes brucellosis, a zoonotic disease that poses considerable threats to the livestock industry and public health (Holloway et al. 2025). Among the recognised Brucella species, Brucella melitensis, Brucella abortus, and Brucella suis are the most common causative agents of brucellosis in livestock and humans (Sagona et al. 2025).

Early diagnosis and large-scale vaccination of susceptible animals are the most effective strategies for brucellosis prevention and control (Moreno et al. 2022). Immunological clearance remains critical for controlling animal brucellosis. The live attenuated B. abortus A19/S19 vaccine has been widely used globally (Heidary et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2022), but it still has certain shortcomings. Specifically, it is difficult to distinguish serologically between antibodies produced in animals after vaccination and those induced by natural infection (Lalsiamthara and Lee 2017; Yang et al. 2021). This has severely disrupted the implementation of epidemiological investigation and eradication (Blasco et al. 2023).

Differentiating infected from vaccinated animals (DIVA) capability refers to the ability to distinguish vaccinated animals from those naturally infected with Brucella via serological detection. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop novel DIVA antigens and technologies (Krasteva et al. 2025). In recent years, comparative proteomics has become a powerful tool for the identification of bacterial virulence factors and diagnostic antigens. By comparing the proteome profiles of virulent strains and vaccine strains, it is possible to identify proteins that are specifically expressed in virulent strains but absent or downregulated in vaccine strains, which are ideal candidates for DIVA antigens (Faria et al. 2020; Pajuaba et al. 2012). Parallel accumulation-serial fragmentation (PASEF)-based quantitative proteomics, characterized by ultra-high sensitivity and throughput (Skowronek et al. 2025), has emerged as a robust approach for screening novel detection of pathogen proteins.

In this study, PASEF-based quantitative proteomics was performed to compare the proteomic profiles of the virulent B. abortus strain S2308 and the vaccine strain A19. A novel potential DIVA antigen, transcriptional regulator GntR family (GntR), was identified through quantitative proteomics, expression analysis in Brucella, and serological validation. Subsequently, a GntR-based indirect enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (iELISA) method was established and optimized, and its diagnostic performance and DIVA potential for bovine brucellosis were evaluated. This study provides a novel candidate DIVA antigen and a promising serological detection method for the prevention and control of bovine brucellosis.

Materials and methods

Animals, bacteria and infections

All animal experiments were approved and carried out following the standards and guidelines of the Gansu Animal Experiments Inspectorate and the Gansu Ethical Review Committee (License no. SYXK [GAN] 2014–003), as well as the Committee for the Ethics of Animal Experiments of the Lanzhou Veterinary Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences. The experimental mice used in the study were specific-pathogen-free (SPF) C57BL/6 J mice (6–8 weeks old), sourced from the Laboratory Animal Center of Lanzhou Veterinary Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences. The B. abortus strains employed in this study included the attenuated vaccine strain A19 (Cheng et al. 2021) and the international standard virulent strain S2308 (Suárez-Esquivel et al. 2016), both stored in the Animal Biosafety Level 3 Laboratory of Lanzhou Veterinary Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences. Yersinia enterocolitica O:9 (ATCC 55075), Escherichia coli O157:H7 (ATCC 43888), E. coli O78:H11 (H10407), Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC 6538), Listeria monocytogenes (ATCC 19115), Streptococcus pneumoniae (ATCC 6305), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (ATCC 10145), and Salmonella enterica (ATCC 9261) were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). All experiments with Brucella were performed in the biosafety level 3 (BSL-3) facilities in the Lanzhou Veterinary Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences approved by the Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs and China National Accreditation Service for Conformity Assessment.

Serum samples

Bovine serum samples, including B. abortus wild strain-infected sera, A19-vaccinated sera, and negative sera, were kindly provided by the China Institute of Veterinary Drug Control (Beijing, China). All serum samples were pre-characterized using the Rose Bengal plate agglutination test (RBT) and a commercial competitive ELISA (cELISA) kit for Brucella (Harbin National Engineering Biological Co., Ltd., Harbin, China, Cat# NHE46100). Sera of Y. enterocolitica O:9, E. coli O157:H7, E. coli O78:H11, S. aureus, L. monocytogenes, S. pneumoniae, P. aeruginosa and S. enterica were prepared and preserved in our laboratory. The standard strains of Y. enterocolitica O:9 and E. coli O157:H7, which share cross-reactive O-antigen LPS with Brucella and are the main interfering bacteria in brucellosis serodiagnosis, were used in the specificity assay (Bonfini et al. 2018). Both the polyclonal antibody against GntR protein and the monoclonal antibody against Omp16 protein were prepared and preserved by our laboratory.

PASEF-based quantitative proteomics analysis

B. abortus S2308 and A19 were cultured in tryptic soy broth (TSB, HopeBio, Qingdao, China, Cat# HB0382) at 37 °C with shaking at 180 rpm until the logarithmic growth phase (OD₆₀₀ = 0.6–0.8) (n = 3). Bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation at 4 °C (12,000 × g, 5 min) using a centrifuge (Eppendorf AG, Hamburg, Germany, Model 5425 R), washed three times with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Servicebio, Wuhan, China, Cat# G0002-2L), inactivated at 85 °C for 1 h in a water bath (Shanghai Yiheng Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China, Model HH-4). Total protein was extracted from inactivated bacterial pellets using lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. Protein concentration was determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and protein integrity was assessed by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) on 12% pre-cast gels, followed by Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining. After quality control, proteins were reduced with dithiothreitol (DTT), alkylated with iodoacetamide (IAA), and digested overnight at 37 °C with trypsin (Hualishi Tech, Beijing, China) at a 1:50 enzyme-to-substrate ratio. The resulting peptides were desalted and separated on a C18 column using an EASY-nLC 1200 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and analyzed by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) on a timsTOF Pro mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics, Billerica, MA, USA) operated in data-dependent acquisition mode with PASEF.

The raw mass spectrometry data was processed using MaxQuant (version 1.6.17.0, https://www.maxquant.org, Max Planck Institute of Biochemistry, Martinsried, Germany) (Tyanova et al. 2016), with the database search sequence file being uniprot-Brucella.abortus-235_2023_10_10.fasta, obtained from the UniProt Knowledgebase (https://www.uniprot.org, European Bioinformatics Institute (EMBL-EBI), Hinxton, UK; Swiss Institute of Bioinformatics (SIB), Geneva, Switzerland; Protein Information Resource (PIR), Washington, D.C., USA) (UniProt Consortium 2022). The search engine being Andromeda (Cox et al. 2011), label-free quantification (LFQ) analysis (Cox et al. 2014) was performed with carbamidomethylation set as a fixed modification and a false discovery rate (FDR) cutoff of 1%. This proteomic workflow was conducted with the assistance of OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Proteins with a fold change ≥ 2 or ≤ 0.5 and a p-value < 0.05 were considered as differentially expressed proteins (DEPs). Proteins upregulated were defined as highly differentially expressed (hDE) proteins (fold change ≥ 2 and p-value < 0.05), and proteins downregulated were defined as lowly differentially expressed (lDE) proteins (fold change ≤ 0.5 and p-value < 0.05). Among them, log2FoldChange = 15 and log2FoldChange = −15 both indicate exclusive expression. The raw and processed PASEF-based quantitative proteomic data generated in this study have been deposited in the Genome Sequence Archive (GSA) of the National Genomics Data Center (NGDC), China National Center for Bioinformation (CNCB) with the accession number: [OMIX013693].

Gene Ontology (GO) annotation was performed using the eggNog database (http://eggnog5.embl.de, European Molecular Biology Laboratory, Heidelberg, Germany) (Hernández-Plaza et al. 2022), covering three ontologies: biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the KEGG database (https://www.kegg.jp, Kanehisa Laboratories, Kyoto, Japan) (Kanehisa et al. 2024). Protein family, domain, and binding site predictions were performed using InterPro (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro, EMBL-EBI, Hinxton, UK) (Blum et al. 2024). Protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks were predicted and analyzed using the STRING database (https://string-db.org, SIB, Geneva, Switzerland) (Szklarczyk et al. 2023).

Analysis of antigenicity of DEPs

The physicochemical and structural characteristics of proteins expressed exclusively in S2308 were predicted using the DNASTAR software (version 7.1, Protean module; DNASTAR, Inc., Madison, WI, USA). Specifically, we analysed the selected DEPs from multiple perspectives, including hydrophilicity (Hydrophilicity Plot), α-helix amphipathic regions (Alpha Amphipathic Regions), β-sheet amphipathic regions (Beta Amphipathic Regions), Antigenic Index (Antigenic Index), and surface probability (Surface Probability Plot). Rather than relying solely on the mean value of each parameter across the full-length protein, we focused on local regions where the antigenic index consistently exceeded 1.0 and simultaneously exhibited positive hydrophilicity and surface probability > 1.0, as visualized by overlaying the three plots. Proteins containing at least one such coincident high-score region were considered bioinformatically favorable.

Expression and purification of candidate target proteins

Recombinant pCold I expression vectors (Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) encoding HlyD and GntR were constructed using standard molecular cloning techniques. The coding sequence of gene HlyD was amplified using forward primer 5′–GGAATTCCATATGATGGGCACACAATCGCT–3′and reverse primer 5′–ACGCGTCGACCTATTTCGCACCAAGATCGAC–3′. The coding sequence of GntR was amplified using forward primer 5′–GGAATTCCATATGTTGTCGGCTTACGAGTG–3′and reverse primer 5′–ACGCGTCGACTTATTTTCTTGCGTCATTGT–3′. The verified plasmids were transformed into competent E. coli BL21(DE3) cells (TsingKe Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China, Cat# TSC-C01) for protein expression. The recombinant protein was induced with 0.1 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) at 16 °C for 24 h. Bacterial cells were harvested and lysed by ultrasonication. The His-tagged recombinant proteins in the supernatant were purified using Ni⁺ resin (GenScript Biotech Corp., Piscataway, NJ, USA, Cat# L00250) with gradient elution in imidazole buffer. Purified proteins were analysed by SDS-PAGE and confirmed by western blot (WB) using rabbit anti-His-tag monoclonal antibody (ABclonal Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China, Cat# AE086, 1:10,000) and horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA, Cat# 31,460, 1:10,000).

Analysis and screening of candidate target proteins by iELISA

Purified recombinant proteins were diluted to 3 μg/mL with PBS, coated onto 96-well microplates (Corning Inc., Corning, NY, USA, Cat# 3590) at 100 μL per well, and incubated at 4 °C overnight. After three washes with PBST (0.1% Tween-20 in PBS), plates were blocked with 200 μL of 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Biosharp Life Sciences, Hefei, China, Cat# BS114-100 g) at 37 °C for 2 h. Following another wash cycle, 100 μL of serum samples (diluted 1:40 in PBS) were added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Plates were washed again and incubated with HRP-conjugated rabbit anti-bovine IgG (Biodragon Immunotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China, Cat# BF03018) at a 1:10,000 dilution for 1 h at 37 °C. After final washes, 100 μL of 3,3',5,5'-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate solution (Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China, Cat# PR1200) was added per well and developed in the dark for 10 min. The reaction was terminated with 50 μL stop solution (Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China, Cat# C1058), and absorbance at OD₄₅₀ was measured using a microplate reader.

Validation of candidate antigen by western blot

Purified recombinant GntR protein was diluted with PBS, mixed with 5 × SDS loading buffer (ABclonal Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China, Cat# RM00001), and boiled at 100 °C for 10 min. Proteins were separated by 12% SDS-PAGE and subsequently transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA, USA, Cat# ISEQ00010) using a wet transfer system. Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk (Applygen Technologies Inc., Beijing, China, Cat# P1622-500G) in PBST at 37 °C for 1 h, followed by three washes with PBST. Subsequently, the primary antibody was randomly selected (n = 5) from wild strain, A19-immunized, and negative serum for verification, with a dilution ratio of 1:1000. After three washes with PBST, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated rabbit anti-bovine IgG (1:10,000 dilution) for 1 h at room temperature. After three additional washes, the blots were developed using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate (ABclonal Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China, Cat# RM00021) and imaged using a gel imaging system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., Hercules, CA, USA).

Genomic sequence alignment of GntR

The full-length coding sequences (CDS) of GntR from S2308 and A19 were retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) GenBank database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genbank, National Center for Biotechnology Information, Bethesda, MD, USA) (Sayers et al. 2025). The accession number of S2308 genome was GCA_000054005.1, and the locus tag of GntR was BAB_RS30940, which was annotated as a GntR family transcriptional regulator. The accession number of A19 genome was GCA_003290345.1, and the locus tag of GntR was DR186_RS15845, which was also annotated as a GntR family transcriptional regulator. Nucleotide sequence alignment and homology analysis were performed using DNAMAN sequence analysis software (version 9.0, Lynnon Biosoft, Montreal, QC, Canada) to compare the sequence identity, base mutations, insertions, and deletions of GntR CDS between the two strains.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) of GntR

B. abortus S2308 and A19 were inoculated into TSB, and cultured at 37 °C with shaking at 180 rpm. The strains were continuously passaged for 3 generations to stabilize the growth state, then expanded to the logarithmic growth phase. Total RNA of B. abortus strains was extracted using the MolPure® Bacterial RNA Kit (YEASEN Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China, Cat# 19301ES50). The concentration and purity of total RNA were determined using a NanoDrop 2000 microspectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). RNA samples with an OD₂₆₀/OD₂₈₀ ratio of 1.8–2.0 were used for subsequent reverse transcription. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China). qPCR was performed using the AceQ Universal U + Probe Master Mix V2 (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China, Cat. No. Q513-02) on a CFX Opus 96 Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA, Cat. No. 12011319). Five biological replicates were set for each strain, and 3 technical replicates were set for each biological sample. The specific primers and TaqMan probes for the target gene GntR and the internal reference gene for lactate dehydrogenase (ldh) were designed using Oligo primer analysis software (version 7.0, Molecular Biology Insights, Inc., Cascade, CO, USA), with sequences as follows: GntR forward primer: 5′-GTTGCCTCCGCCGATCTCAC-3′; GntR reverse primer: 5′-CGCATCGCTCGCCCGTTC-3′; GntR TaqMan probe: 5′-Fam-CCGTCCAGCACCTCACGCACAG-BHQ1-3′; ldh forward primer: 5′-ATGGCAAGCCGACCACCGA-3′; ldh reverse primer: 5′-AGTCCAGCACTTCCGGTCT-3′; ldh TaqMan probe: 5′-Hex-CATGATGCCCGCCAGAAGCTCGAT-BHQ1-3′. The relative transcription level of GntR was calculated using the Ct method (2 (−ΔΔCt)) (Livak and Schmittgen 2002), with the ldh gene as the internal reference to normalize the loading difference, and B. abortus S2308 set as the reference control.

Protein level of GntR in B. abortus strains at logarithmic growth phase

B. abortus S2308 and A19 were harvested at logarithmic growth phase (OD₆₀₀ = 0.6–0.8). Bacterial pellets were washed three times with ice-cold PBS, resuspended in PBS to equal OD₆₀₀, then supplemented with 1 mg/mL lysozyme (Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China, Cat# L8120) and 1 mg/mL phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF; Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China, Cat# P8340). The suspension was ultrasonicated on ice, and the supernatant containing total soluble proteins was collected. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA assay kit (Jiangsu CoWin Biotech Co., Ltd., Taizhou, China, Cat# CW0014S). Equal amounts of protein were separated by 12% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in PBST at 37 °C for 1 h, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with mouse anti-GntR polyclonal antibody (1:1000) and mouse anti-Omp16 monoclonal antibody (1:1000, internal control). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (1:10,000) for 1 h at room temperature. Blots were developed with ECL substrate. GntR expression was quantified by densitometry using ImageJ (version 1.53e, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) and normalized to Omp16 (Zhi et al. 2020).

Establishment and optimization of GntR-based iELISA

The GntR protein was diluted to 4 μg/mL with PBS, then 100 μL per well was added and the plate was coated overnight at 4 °C. After washing three times with PBST, 200 μL of 5% BSA per well was added and the plate was incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Following another three washes with PBST, 100 μL of wild-strain Brucella positive serum per well (designated as P) was added, and an equal volume of Brucella A19 vaccine immune serum was added to the negative control wells (designated as N); the plate was then incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. After washing three times with PBST, horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labeled rabbit anti-bovine IgG secondary antibody was added to each well and the plate was incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Following three washes with PBST, 100 μL of TMB chromogenic solution per well was added and the plate was developed in the dark at 37 °C for 5 min. If the colour development was incomplete, the chromogenic time was appropriately extended. Subsequently, 50 μL of stop solution per well was added to terminate the reaction, and the OD₄₅₀ nm value was measured.

Furthermore, based on the P/N OD₄₅₀ values, optimization experiments were conducted on the established iELISA method. The following parameters were determined: the optimal coating concentration of GntR and the dilution ratio of HRP-labeled secondary antibody, the coating buffer and incubation conditions, the composition of the blocking buffer and incubation time, the serum dilution ratio, the incubation time of serum and secondary antibody, the composition of the secondary antibody diluent, and the chromogenic time of TMB substrate. All optimization experiments were performed in duplicate. The cut-off value of the assay was determined using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and Youden's index calculation with 142 serum samples (39 wild strain-infected, 103 A19-vaccinated). Specificity was evaluated using sera positive for 8 non-Brucella pathogens (listed in the Serum samples section). All specificity tests were performed with three independent biological replicates (n = 3). Analytical sensitivity was assessed using serial dilutions of confirmed positive serum samples with three replicates per dilution. Repeatability was examined using intra- and inter-assay precision tests with three replicates per sample. Concordance analysis was performed by comparison with the standard RBT and commercial cELISA kit using 115 independent serum samples (39 wild strain-positive and 76 non-immunized Brucella-negative sera).

Dynamic detection of GntR-specific antibodies in S2308 and A19 infected mouse

B. abortus S2308 and A19 were cultured in TSB medium to the logarithmic growth phase, harvested by centrifugation at 4 °C (12,000 × g, 5 min), washed three times with sterile PBS, and adjusted to a final concentration of 1 × 10⁶ colony-forming units (CFU)/mL. Mice in the infection group were intraperitoneally injected with 100 μL of S2308 suspension (1 × 10⁶ CFU/mouse, n = 4), and mice in the vaccination group were intraperitoneally injected with 100 μL of A19 suspension (1 × 10⁶ CFU/mouse, n = 4). Blood samples were collected from the retroorbital venous plexus at 7, 14, 21, 28, and 35 days post-infection. GntR-specific antibody levels in mouse sera were detected using the optimized GntR-based iELISA, following the standard protocol described above.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All tests were performed in accordance with biostatistical requirements. Statistical differences between treated and control groups were determined by analysis of variance using SPSS software (version 18.0, SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism (version 9.0, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA), wherein “ns” indicates no significant difference and *, **, and *** indicate statistically significant differences at p < 0.05, p < 0.01, and p < 0.001, respectively.

Results

Proteomic identification and functional analysis of DEPs in B. abortus S2308 and A19 strains

Using PASEF-based quantitative proteomics, we identified a total of 21,152 peptides and 2131 proteins from B. abortus S2308 and A19 strains (Fig. 1A). Three hundred and two DEPs were identified, including 201 hDE proteins (fold change ≥ 2 and p-value < 0.05) and 101 lDE proteins (fold change ≤ 0.5 and p-value < 0.05) (Fig. 1B). The full list of DEPs is provided in Supplemental Table S1. Among these DEPs, Forty-three proteins were detected exclusively in S2308 at the protein level at logarithmic growth phase in vitro, while Thirty-two proteins were detected exclusively in A19 (Fig. 1C, Supplemental Table S1). Principal component analysis (PCA) showed clear separation between the S2308 and A19 groups, with good intra-group repeatability (Fig. 1D). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed to investigate the functional pathways involving DEPs. These were categorized into Cellular Processes, Human Diseases, Genetic Information Processing, Environmental Information Processing, and Metabolism. Significantly enriched pathways for the DEPs included ABC transporters, Atrazine degradation, and Quorum sensing (Fig. 2C). Among the ABC transporters, the expression of numerous substrate- and ATP-binding proteins associated with specific substrates was upregulated. This included iron (III) transport system substrate-binding protein, osmoprotectant transport system substrate-binding protein, multiple sugar transport system ATP-binding protein, trehalose/maltose transport system substrate-binding protein, putative multiple sugar transport system permease protein, and erythritol transport system substrate-binding protein (Supplemental Fig. S2). Within Quorum sensing, proteins involved in acid tolerance, toxoflavin biosynthesis, and post-exponential growth phase modulation were upregulated (Supplemental Fig. S3). Further analysis showed that lDE proteins were primarily enriched in pathways such as Zeatin biosynthesis, Ribosome biogenesis in eukaryotes, and proteoglycans in cancer, whereas hDE proteins were predominantly enriched in ABC transporters and quorum sensing (Supplemental Fig. S1C, D). In summary, lDE proteins were mainly enriched in metabolic and cell cycle-related pathways, while hDE proteins were mainly enriched in substance transport, quorum sensing and amino acid degradation-related pathways. This result further clarified the functional division of different expression trend proteins.

InterPro enrichment analysis was used to predict protein families, domains, and binding sites for DEPs. The DEPs were significantly enriched in the periplasmic binding protein-like I and Rhodanese-like domain (Fig. 2D). The lDE proteins were primarily associated with Per-ARNT-Sim (PAS) fold-3 and the ferritin-like superfamily. Several domains with high enrichment significance were related to signal sensing, stress response and iron metabolism, suggesting that lDE proteins were mainly involved in environmental perception and metabolic regulation. hDE proteins were predominantly associated with periplasmic binding protein-like I and periplasmic binding protein. Besides, ABC transporter-like, ATP-binding domain also were significantly enriched (Supplemental Fig. S1E, F). These results were in line with the KEGG analysis.

Collectively, the above results demonstrate substantial proteomic divergence between the S2308 and A19 strains, which provides molecular insights into the distinct phenotypic traits of the two strains.

Antigenicity prediction and screening of candidate DIVA antigens

The physicochemical and structural characteristics of the proteins were predicted using DNASTAR software. To explore the functions and antigenicity of these DEPs, comprehensive analyses were performed from multiple perspectives, including hydrophilicity, α-helix amphipathic regions, β-sheet amphipathic regions, Antigenic Index, and surface probability. Proteins with strong hydrophilicity, high surface exposure, stable secondary structures favourable for binding, and strong antigenicity were selected. Systematic screening revealed two S2308-specific expressed proteins with high antigenicity and surface probability: HlyD family secretion protein (HlyD, Accession: Q577M4) and Transcriptional regulator, GntR family (GntR, Accession: Q576U7). The detailed analysis results for all DEPs are provided in Supplemental Table S2, S3.

Establishment, optimization and cut-off value determination of GntR-based iELISA

To preliminarily verify the clinical application potential of the GntR protein as a DIVA serological diagnostic target for B. abortus, we systematically optimized the reaction conditions of the GntR-based iELISA. Checkerboard titration identified the optimal coating concentration of GntR as 2.0 μg/mL, and the optimal dilution of HRP-conjugated rabbit anti-bovine IgG as 1:10,000 (Table 1). Comparative analysis of coating conditions revealed that PBS buffer exhibited superior performance compared to carbonate buffer solution (CBS) buffer, with optimal coating achieved at 37 °C for 1 h (Fig. 6A, B). Evaluation of eight different blocking buffers demonstrated that 1% skim milk with a 60-min blocking time yielded optimal results (Fig. 6C, D), the optimal dilution of serum was 1:100 (Fig. 6E). Further optimization established serum and secondary antibody incubation times of 30 and 45 min, respectively (Fig. 6F, G), PBS was identified as the optimal diluent for the secondary antibody (Fig. 6H). The TMB substrate reaction time was optimized to 5 min at 37 °C, resulting in the highest P/N ratio (Fig. 6I). Moreover, we dynamically monitored GntR-specific antibody levels at different post-infection days in B. abortus S2308 and A19-infected mouse models (Fig. 7E). The results showed that GntR-specific antibodies were rapidly induced in S2308-infected mice as early as 7 days, and antibody levels continued to rise until 28 days, reaching a peak at this time point. Discussion

Brucellosis, caused by Brucella spp., poses a severe threat to human and animal health and public safety (Jiao et al. 2025; Huang et al. 2025). Vaccination with the existing strains remains the cornerstone of successful bovine brucellosis prevention and control programs worldwide (Dorneles et al. 2015; Liu et al. 2025a, b). However, existing vaccines cannot distinguish between immunized animals and those infected with wild strain, which severely hinders the purification and eradication of the epidemic (Chaudhuri et al. 2021; Nandini et al. 2023). Therefore, the development of novel DIVA antigens and their supporting detection methods is of great practical significance. Consequently, there is growing interest in alternative antigens, such as outer membrane proteins (OMPs), inner membrane proteins, cytoplasmic proteins, and ribosomal proteins, which demonstrate considerable potential as tools for brucellosis diagnosis (Carvalho et al. 2016; Zhang et al. 2025). Thus, identification of strain-specific, highly immunogenic proteins is essential for developing targeted, specific diagnostic assays.

Emerging research includes the application of proteogenomics, transcriptomics, and metabolomics to elucidate Brucella biology and host–pathogen interactions (Carvalho et al. 2016). Proteogenomics, which integrates genomic and proteomic data, has identified novel virulence factors and post-translational modifications that regulate Brucella stress response and immune evasion. Given the crucial role of membrane proteins in pathogenesis, proteomics offers a powerful avenue for investigating the key pathogenic mechanisms. Proteomic studies have identified immunogenic proteins, such as Omp19, Omp25 and Omp28, as potential vaccine candidates (Yao et al. 2022; Liu et al. 2025a, b). Comparative proteomic profiling of different strains enables the identification of unique protein markers. This provides a prospective approach for distinguishing natural infections from vaccinations, thereby advancing diagnostic and control strategies (Ye et al. 2025).

In this study, we applied PASEF-based quantitative proteomics to compare the proteomes of the virulent B. abortus strain S2308 and the attenuated vaccine strain A19. A total of 2131 proteins were identified, including 302 differentially expressed proteins, comprising 43 proteins exclusively detected in S2308 and 32 proteins exclusively detected in A19 during the logarithmic growth phase. We identified GntR as a novel potential diagnostic antigen capable of DIVA. It exhibited strong immunoreactivity with Brucella wild strain-infected sera, and low reactivity with A19-vaccinated or negative sera. The transcription and expression levels of GntR were significantly higher in S2308 than in A19 during the logarithmic growth phase, while the sequence homology between the two strains was 100%, which may indicate the extremely complex transcriptional regulation pattern of transcription and uniqueness of the biological characteristics of Brucella (Viadas et al. 2010; Chain et al. 2005).

According to the functional enrichment analysis of differentially expressed proteins, GO annotation results showed that the differentially expressed proteins were significantly enriched in urea metabolic processes, guanosine biosynthetic processes, as well as the periplasmic space and outer membrane components. At the molecular function level, transmembrane transporter activity was predominant. The differential expression of urea metabolism-related proteins may be associated with the adaptive abilities of virulent and attenuated strains in different microenvironments (Sangari et al. 2010). Studies have found that urease-producing strains exhibit tolerance to in vitro strong acid environments in the presence of urea (Sangari et al. 2007), suggesting significant differences in basic metabolism and proliferation capacity between virulent and attenuated strains. The differential enrichment of periplasmic space and outer membrane proteins indicates that virulent and attenuated strains differ significantly in surface structure and host interaction, which is closely related to the virulence of Brucella (Uzureau et al. 2007; Valguarnera et al. 2018).

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that the significantly enriched pathways mainly included the ABC transporter system, atrazine degradation, and quorum sensing. The ABC transporter system and quorum sensing pathways were significantly enriched in highly expressed proteins. The ABC transporter system is involved in the transmembrane transport of various substrates, including amino acids, carbohydrates, ions, etc., and is crucial for bacteria to acquire essential nutrients in nutrient-limited intracellular environments (Roset et al. 2004). This finding in the present study is consistent with the results of a comparative proteomic study between virulent and attenuated strains of B. melitensis (Zhang et al. 2022). Quorum sensing has been verified as a key regulator of the virulence program in Brucella, enabling persistent infection (Caudill et al. 2025). The differential expression of these pathways may represent an important molecular basis for the virulence differences between virulent and attenuated strains. Pathways significantly enriched in lowly expressed proteins, such as folate biosynthesis and the cell cycle, may be associated with the attenuated characteristics of the A19 vaccine strain.

InterPro domain analysis further revealed that the differentially expressed proteins were significantly enriched in the periplasmic binding protein-like I domain, the rhodanese-like domain, and the ferritin-like superfamily. Periplasmic binding proteins are key components of the ABC transport system, primarily involved in substrate uptake and transport (Herrou et al. 2017), and the enrichment of their domains corroborates the enrichment results of the ABC transport pathway. The ferritin-like superfamily participates in iron homeostasis regulation, antioxidant stress, and metabolic adaptation (Almirón and Ugalde 2010). The differential expression of the above domains suggests significant differences in substance transport and metabolic adaptation capabilities between virulent and attenuated strains.

We established an indirect ELISA method based on GntR, which exhibited favourable diagnostic performance with high sensitivity and specificity, an AUC of 0.9925. Both the intra-assay and inter-assay coefficients of variation were below 10%. In terms of coincidence rate verification, due to lack of standards for current DIVA detection of B. abortus, this study selected wild strain-infected sera with clear backgrounds and negative sera for preliminary matching analysis. The total coincidence rate with conventional clinical RBT and cELISA was 99.1%, which further verified the potential of GntR as a serological diagnostic target. In addition, dynamic detection in mouse models showed that GntR-specific antibodies can be detected as early as 7 days after S2308 infection, and are significantly higher than those in the A19 immune group within 7–21 days, providing a clear time window for the early differential diagnosis of this antigen. Although no significant difference in antibody levels was observed between the two groups at 28–35 days post-infection, this may be related to the differences in host species, infection routes and immune response characteristics (Muñoz et al. 2008; Demars et al. 2019).

The indirect ELISA method based on the GntR protein preliminarily established in this study can serve as a technical reserve for DIVA diagnostic techniques. This method uses the GntR protein unique expressed to the virulent B. abortus strain S2308 as the coating antigen, and its detection target avoids cross-reactions with vaccine-immune serum (Foster et al. 2025). GntR-based iELISA shows potential for large-scale screening needs of grassroots animal disease detection (Bedir et al. 2025). However, it also has certain limitations. On the one hand, it has not yet included wild strain-infected sera from different geographical regions, different infection stages, and different cattle breeds, as well as immune sera at different time points after A19 vaccine immunization for systematic verification. On the other hand, the clinical applicability and broad-spectrum nature of this method still need to be further confirmed in larger-scale clinical samples. Further process optimization and system verification are still required before large-scale popularization and application at the grassroots level.

In summary, this study successfully screened GntR as a potential DIVA antigen using a comparative proteomics strategy, and preliminarily verified its application value in the serological differential diagnosis of brucellosis, providing new technical reserves for the prevention, control and purification of brucellosis.
