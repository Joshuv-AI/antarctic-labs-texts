Study on the cutting technology of chayote (Sechium edule) and the physiological mechanism of rooting regulation by exogenous hormones
Authors: Zhili Chen, Xin Guo, Shaobo Cheng, Xiaoting Zhou, Zhongqun He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground‘Tuershao’ is a new chayote (Sechium edule) variety whose tuberous roots and tender shoots are edible. These parts are rich in beneficial substances, including amino acids, proteins, phenolic compounds, flavonoids, cucurbitacin, and selenium, demonstrating their unique value. However, its ovary cannot develop into a mature fruit, and cutting techniques help to achieve the breeding of ‘Tuershao’.ResultsThis study found that using peat soil as the substrate and treating semi-hard branches with 1.0 g·L⁻¹ indole-3-butyric acid (IBA) achieved the highest rooting rate of 94.33%, with the maximum values in root length, root surface area, root volume, and root diameter. Additionally, exogenous hormone treatment led to greater fluctuations in the contents of soluble sugar, starch, and soluble protein in ‘Tuershao’. The activities of catalase (CAT), polyphenol oxidase (PPO), peroxidase (POD), and superoxide dismutase (SOD) were also enhanced. The contents of endogenous hormones such as auxin (IAA), cytokinin (CTK), and gibberellin A3 (GA3) increased, while abscisic acid (ABA) decreased.ConclusionsTreating semi-hard branches with 1.0 g·L⁻¹ IBA on peat soil shows the best effect in promoting rooting of ‘Tuershao’ cuttings. Exogenous IBA can promote nutrient metabolism, enhance antioxidant enzyme activities, and regulate endogenous hormone levels in ‘Tuershao’ cuttings, thereby further promoting rooting.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07455-0.Keywords: Chayote, Cuttings, Root system, Hormone

Introduction

Chayote (Sechium edule, 2n = 2x = 28) is a perennial herbaceous climbing plant in the Cucurbitaceae family, originating from Mexico and widely planted in Japan, India, and southwestern China [1]. Previous studies have shown that the starch content in chayote tubers is comparable to that in potatoes, indicating its potential as a substitute for starch-based crops [2, 3]. Moreover, tubers serve as a valuable additional source of high-quality fiber and carbohydrates [4]. Several recent studies have demonstrated that starch and pectin components in chayote tubers can be used to produce bioactive films in industry and have the potential to develop new food materials suitable for different processing conditions and application fields [5]. ‘Tuershao’, a chayote variety selected by our research team for many years, has larger tubers than other varieties, being rich in starch, sugar, proteins, and cellulose, thus possessing promising application prospects [6].

The rooting of plant cuttings is affected by aging effects, where the rooting efficiency of cuttings declines with the age of the mother plant and the cuttings themselves. This is due to severe lignification of cuttings, reduced cell differentiation ability, and difficulty in forming callus and inducing root primordia [7]. Additionally, the length, diameter, and type of cuttings influence cutting survival rates [8]. Commonly used cutting substrates include perlite, coconut coir, peat, river sand, etc. A good substrate not only supports plant cuttings but also provides breathability, water retention, and organic matter, creating suitable conditions for rooting [9–11]. During the adventitious root formation period of cuttings, plant growth regulators can promote endogenous hormone synthesis and regulate hormone balance in cuttings [12–14].

However, the mechanism by which exogenous hormones promote root formation remains partially unclear. Previous studies have shown that the rooting process of cuttings involves physiological activities in two stages—wound healing and root system formation—requiring substantial energy. Nutrients in plants, such as starch, soluble proteins, and soluble sugars, serve as the main energy sources, showing significant dynamic changes (increasing or decreasing) during rooting [14, 15]. Besides providing energy, the activities of antioxidant enzymes in cuttings, such as catalase (CAT), polyphenol oxidase (PPO), peroxidase (POD), and superoxide dismutase (SOD), are associated with the growth and development of adventitious roots. These enzymes scavenge excessive reactive oxygen species in cuttings to maintain redox balance [16, 17]. More importantly, endogenous hormones like auxin (IAA), cytokinin (CTK), and gibberellin A3 (GA3) can promote callus and adventitious root formation during cutting [17, 18]. ABA promotes the excessive decomposition and metabolism of stored nutrients to enhance the stress resistance of cuttings [17]. Common exogenous hormones can regulate endogenous hormone levels in cuttings at appropriate concentrations, promote cell division and differentiation, and thus improve cutting survival and rooting rates [12, 14]. These findings provide insights for optimizing ‘Tuershao’ cutting technology and exploring the physiological mechanism of exogenous hormones regulating its rooting.

This study investigates the effects of cutting types, substrate types, rooting agent types, and concentrations on the rooting rate and root system parameters of ‘Tuershao’ cuttings. Additionally, it examines the impacts of exogenous hormones on nutrients, antioxidant enzyme activities, and endogenous hormones during ‘Tuershao’ cutting rooting, accompanied by histological observations. These studies aim to optimize ‘Tuershao’ cutting technology and provide new insights into the physiological mechanism of exogenous hormones promoting cutting rooting.

Results

Effects of different cutting types on rooting of ‘Tuershao’ cuttings

The rooting rates of semi-hard and hard branches were 91.03% and 88.67%, respectively, significantly higher than the 37.12% of epicormic branches (Fig. S1A). In addition, compared with epicormic branches, the root length, root surface area, and root volume of semi-hard branches significantly increased by 94.12%, 78.47%, and 57.55% (Fig. S1B, C, and D), while the root length and root surface area of semi-hard branches were 16.49% and 11.29% higher than those of hard branches, respectively (Fig. S1B and C).

Effects of different substrate types on rooting of ‘Tuershao’ cuttings

The rooting rate of cuttings under perlite and peat soil treatment was significantly higher than that under river sand treatment. The rooting rate of cuttings under peat soil treatment was the highest, at 93.33%, while that under river sand treatment was the lowest, at 76.67% (Fig. S2A). In addition, compared with perlite treatment, the root length and root volume of cuttings under peat soil treatment increased significantly by 13.77% and 9.29%, respectively, while the root surface area and root volume of cuttings under river sand treatment decreased significantly by 8.92% and 12.61%, respectively (Fig. S2B, C, and D).

Effects of different hormone types on rooting of ‘Tuershao’ cuttings

Under the treatment of 1.0 g · L−1 IBA (indole-3-butanoic acid), 1.5 g · L−1 ABT1 (ABT1 rooting powder), or 1.0 g · L−1 RP (Rooting Powde), the cuttings showed a high rooting rate. Among them, under the treatment of 1.0 g · L−1 indole-3-butanoic acid (IBA), the rooting rate of the cuttings was the highest, reaching 94.33%, which was significantly higher than other treatments. Moreover, the root length, root surface area, and root volume of the cuttings were also the highest under this treatment (Fig. S3A, B, C, and D).

Analysis of orthogonal experiment results

In the orthogonal experiment, the rooting rates of cuttings under T1 and T6 treatments were higher, at 86.67% and 94.33%, respectively (Fig. 1A). In addition, the root length of the cuttings under T1 and T6 treatments was longer, at 44.15 cm and 48.11 cm, respectively. The root surface area of the cuttings under T1, T4, T6, and T8 treatments was larger (Fig. 1C). The root volume of the inserted strips under T4 and T6 treatments had a large value (Fig. 1D). However, there was no significant difference in the root diameter of the cuttings under these treatments (Fig. 1E).

Effects of exogenous hormone IBA on rooting of ‘Tuershao’ cuttings

The rooting rate of cuttings significantly increased to 92.22% under exogenous hormone treatment, while the survival rate of the control group was only 22.22%, representing a 3.15-fold difference between the two groups (Fig. 2A). During the rooting process of ‘Tuershao’ cuttings, the contents of soluble sugar, starch, and soluble protein generally showed a trend of decrease-increase-decrease (Fig. 2B, C and D). Notably, these indicators in the experimental group reached their peaks on day 10, serving as turning points in the trend, whereas the control group exhibited this pattern on day 15, lagging behind the experimental group by a period. On the other hand, the variation trends in the experimental group were more pronounced than those in the control group, and the contents of starch and soluble protein were consistently higher in the experimental group.

Survival rate and changes in nutrients. A: Rooting rate; B: Soluble sugar content; C: Starch content; D: Soluble protein content. Different letters indicate significant differences at the level of P < 0.05.

Dynamic changes in antioxidant enzyme and polyphenol oxidase activities during rooting of ‘Tuershao’ cuttings

During the rooting process of ‘Tuershao’ cuttings, the activities of catalase (CAT) and superoxide dismutase (SOD) both exhibited a trend of first increasing and then decreasing (Fig. 3A and C). Under exogenous hormone treatment, the SOD activity in the experimental group was generally higher than that in the control group. The activity of peroxidase (POD) showed a trend of increase-decrease-increase over time, and the POD activity under exogenous hormone treatment was significantly higher than that in the control group from day 10 to day 20 (Fig. 3B). The activity of polyphenol oxidase (PPO) presented a trend of decrease-increase-decrease over time, and the activities of CAT and PPO under exogenous hormone treatment were significantly higher than those in the control group from day 0 to day 10 (Fig. 3D). Notably, under exogenous hormone treatment, the activities of these enzymes all peaked on day 10, while in the control group, the activities of these enzymes generally peaked on day 15, except for POD, whose activity peaked on day 10 and reached a valley on day 15.

In the control group, from day 10 to day 20, the IAA content showed a downward trend (Fig. 4A), and the CTK content showed an upward trend (Fig. 4B). The contents of GA3 and ABA in the control group showed no obvious change trend throughout the process (Fig. 4C and D).

Observation of the external morphology of the rooting process of ‘Tuershao’ cuttings

Through external morphological observation (Fig. 5), the cuttings of ‘Tuershao’ belong to the complete callus rooting type, and the rooting process of the cuttings can be roughly divided into the callus formation stage (0–10 days), adventitious root formation stage (15th day), and adventitious root elongation stage (20th day). On the 0th day of cutting, the base of the cutting was emerald green, and no callus was formed. On the 5th day of cutting, a small amount of white callus formed at the protruding part below the stem node at the base of the stem segment, the wound at the end dried and turned yellow, and the wound gradually healed. Meanwhile, a small amount of callus also formed above the node. On the 10th day of cutting, a large amount of callus formed at the node, and the remaining part of the petiole also produced a large amount of callus. The callus gradually decreased from the node to the upper and lower ends, and the callus on the stem was more protruding than that on the 5th day. On the 15th day, adventitious roots first grew from the node. At this time, the roots were sparse, and the epidermis of the stem segment below the stem node cracked longitudinally. On the 20th day, a large number of adventitious roots emerged from the node and below the node. Most roots grew out from the crack, and a small number of roots grew out from the remaining part of the petiole.

External morphological changes of cuttings during the entire rooting process. Note: Left to right: 0–20 days; top shows overall branch growth, bottom shows partial branch growth. Anatomical observation of the rooting process of ‘Tuershao’ cuttings

Through the anatomical structure observation of the rooting process of ‘Tuershao’ cuttings, the cross-section of the stem segment on the 0th day of cutting is shown in Fig. 6A. The tissue structure from outside to inside is composed of epidermis, cortex, phloem, vascular bundle cambium, xylem and pith cavity. The outermost is the epidermis, and the outermost layer is a layer of cells closely arranged. Below the epidermis is the collenchyma of the cortex. There are several layers of closely arranged cells in the cortex, which are irregular polygons. They are a whole circle around the vascular bundle, which is sclerenchyma with a small intercellular space. Below the circular tube fibers are the parenchyma cells of the cortex with uneven arrangement and different sizes, which also present irregular polygons. Below the parenchyma cells is the vascular bundle, which is composed of primary phloem, primary xylem and fascicular cambium. The primary xylem is in the shape of a circular tube, the internal duct diameter is small, and the external duct diameter is relatively large. There are primary phloem inside and outside, which is a typical bicollateral vascular bundle structure. Between the external primary phloem and the primary xylem is the fascicular cambium, which can form xylem inward and phloem outward. After the phloem is the pith. Through detailed analysis and careful observation of the cross-section of the stem segment, it is found that there is no latent root primordium inside the cutting, so it is inferred that ‘Tuershao’ belongs to the induced rooting type, which needs the stimulation of external conditions to induce the root primordium.

As shown in Fig. 6B-F, on the 5th day of cutting, the parenchyma cells become active and have strong meristematic ability. The parenchyma cells quickly dedifferentiate and begin to divide into smaller parenchyma cells with irregular shapes, squeeze toward the sclerenchyma, and some of the parenchyma cells break through the sclerenchyma, continue to divide toward the epidermis, and gradually form white callus. On the 10th day of cutting, the parenchyma continues to dedifferentiate and divide, expand outward, and the volume gradually increases, pushing the epidermis outward to form irregular protrusions, and the white callus is visible to the naked eye outside. On the 15th day of cutting, the parenchyma cells continue to divide and expand outward, and the protrusion is larger than that on the 10th day. At the same time, it is observed that on the 15th day, under the continuous division of the parenchyma, some meristems with close arrangement and irregular rectangular shapes are formed. These tissue cells are small in volume and have obvious differences from the surrounding parenchyma, and gradually break through toward the cortex. The tissue composed of these cells is the adventitious root primordium differentiated from the callus. On the 20th day, the adventitious root primordium differentiated from the callus continues to divide and extend, and finally breaks through the epidermis and extends outward to form visible adventitious roots.

Discussion

The internal structures of cuttings vary among plant species, leading to different rooting performances [19, 20]. In this study, the upper epicormic branch, middle semi-hard branch, and lower hard branch of ‘Tuershao’ were used. Semi-hard branch and hard branch showed significantly higher rooting rates, root length, root surface area, root volume, and root diameter than the epicormic branch. This may be attributed to the stronger stress resistance of semi-hard and hard branches (reducing damage and infection) and their richer nutrient reserves and higher metabolic activity, providing sufficient energy for rooting [21–23]. Peat soil showed superior rooting effects, likely due to its optimal water retention and air permeability matching the external conditions required for ‘Tuershao’ rooting [24, 25].

Nutrients such as soluble sugar, soluble protein, and starch serve as the material basis and energy source for cutting rooting [26, 27]. Rooting is an energy-consuming process, with energy preferentially supplied to the base, accompanied by dynamic changes in these nutrients [28–30]. Exogenous hormones were reported to affect rooting by enhancing protein-nitrogen activity during root development in mangrove cuttings [31]. In this study, the content of soluble sugars, starch, and soluble proteins showed a trend of first increasing and then decreasing. It is worth noting that their inflection points all occurred on the 10th day. Combined with external morphological observation and slice observation of the rooting process of cuttings, this may be because root primordialization and epidermal breakthrough require a large amount of starch and soluble sugars to obtain energy, while soluble proteins act as energy substances to be consumed [32, 33]. Exogenous indole-3-butyric acid (IBA) accelerated these nutrient changes and advanced the inflection points, suggesting regulatory effects on nutrient metabolism [34, 35].

Antioxidant enzymes and polyphenol oxidase play a crucial role in rooting of cuttings, as they can clear reactive oxygen species to alleviate stress [36]. In this study, the activity of these enzymes also showed a trend of first increasing and then decreasing during the rooting process of cuttings, which was more pronounced under IBA treatment, with a peak appearing on the 10th day. This may be because primordium differentiation and epidermal breakthrough make cuttings more susceptible to stress, leading to a surge in the activity of these enzymes to cope with the increase in reactive oxygen species [37, 38]. In addition, IBA treatment significantly increased the peak activities of catalase (CAT), superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), and polyphenol oxidase (PPO), indicating that IBA enhanced antioxidant capacity, eliminated oxidative stress, and created a favorable internal environment for rooting [39].

The interaction between exogenous and endogenous hormones in the process of adventitious root formation is complex [40]. Auxin (IAA) accelerates nutrient transport and root primordia differentiation [41, 42], cytokinin (CTK) promotes thin-walled cell division to form callus and root primordia [43], and gibberellin A3 (GA3) promotes cell differentiation at low concentrations [44]. In this study, the levels of these endogenous hormones gradually increased during the rooting process of cuttings and remained at a high level during adventitious root growth (days 10–20). However, abscisic acid (ABA), as a plant stress hormone, showed little change in its content, which may indicate that ABA is not a key hormone for cuttings rooting [45]. In addition, IBA treatment increased the content of IAA, CTK, and GA3, while reducing the content of abscisic acid (ABA), promoting the rooting of cuttings in this way [46]. Studies have shown that exogenous hormone IBA can be converted into endogenous auxin (IAA) by enzymes in plants, directly increasing the content of IAA [47]. However, further research is needed on how the exogenous hormone IBA regulates the levels of endogenous hormones GA3, CTK, and ABA.

Multivariate statistical analysis is helpful for a better understanding of the results. PCA (Principal Component Analysis) can simplify multiple indicators into a few comprehensive ones, enabling direct observation of complex indices. In this study, PCA clearly revealed the specific patterns of cuttings at different rooting stages. PC1 is mainly composed of root system parameters, reflecting the overall characteristics of root growth, while soluble sugar and starch show negative loadings on PC1. This trade-off relationship reflects the material allocation strategy of plants during growth. Correlation analysis indicated that root system parameters were closely related to soluble sugar and starch contents, suggesting that soluble sugar and starch provide energy for cutting rooting [26]. Meanwhile, IAA, CTK, and ABA were all positively correlated with GA3 content, which may indicate that GA3 plays a dominant role in the regulation of endogenous hormones during cutting rooting [44].

Materials and methods

Test materials

All experiments were conducted from April to December 2023 in plastic greenhouses at the Chongzhou Base of Sichuan Agricultural University in Chengdu, Sichuan Province, China (103°38’31.67’’E, 30°33’16.59’’N), an area with a subtropical humid monsoon climate (annual average temperature 16.0°C, sunshine 1161.5 h, precipitation 1015.2 mm concentrated in May–September, frost-free period 283 days). The ‘Tuershao’ used in the experiment was cultivated by our team in the early stage. We selected healthy ‘Tuershao’ plants with mother plants aged 4 years and no pests or diseases. Starting from the end near the root, we cut the 2nd to 3rd, 5th to 6th, and 8th to 9th nodes from the stem vine with 10–12 nodes, which were used as hard branches, semi-hard branches, and tender branches, respectively. The cutting points are all located in the middle of the internodal segment. The upper end of the branch has a flat opening, and the lower end has a slanted opening. Keep 1–2 leaves from the upper section. For fully mature large leaves, cut off the edges of the leaves and keep half of the leaf area. Leave a leaf bud on each section and store it in water to keep it fresh. Rooting agents include IBA (indole-3-butanoic acid, Beijing Solaibao Technology Co., Ltd.), ABT1 (ABT1 rooting powder, Beijing Abbiti Biotechnology Co., Ltd.), and RP (Rooting Powde, Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). Dissolve the reagent in ultrapure water to obtain the corresponding rooting agent. The following experiments were conducted separately.

Effects of cutting types on rooting

Upper epicormic branch, middle semi-hard branch, and lower hard branch were treated with 1.0 g·L⁻¹ IBA for 10 s, then planted in peat soil (3 treatments, 30 cuttings each). Afterwards, pour a small amount of water every day to keep the substrate moist. Rooting rate, root length, surface area, volume, and diameter were measured after 20 days.

Effects of substrate types on rooting

Semi-hard branches were treated with 1.0 g·L⁻¹ IBA for 10 s, then planted in peat soil, perlite, or river sand (3 treatments, 30 cuttings each). Afterwards, pour a small amount of water every day to keep the substrate moist. Rooting parameters were measured after 20 days.

Effects of hormone types and concentrations on rooting

IBA, ABT1, and Rooting Powde were prepared at 0.5, 1.0, and 1.5 g·L⁻¹. Semi-hard branches were soaked for 10 s, planted in peat soil (9 treatments, 30 cuttings each). Afterwards, pour a small amount of water every day to keep the substrate moist. And rooting parameters were measured after 20 days.

Orthogonal experiment design

Treatments included epicormic branch/semi-hard branch/hard branch, 1.0 g·L⁻¹ IBA/1.5 g·L⁻¹ ABT1/1.0 g·L⁻¹ Rooting Powde, and peat/perlite/river sand (detailed in Tables 1 and 30 cuttings per combination). After treatment, pour a small amount of water every day to keep the substrate moist. Root length, diameter, surface area, and volume were measured after 20 days.

Mechanism study on rooting of ‘Tuershao’ cutting propagation

The semi-hard branches were soaked in 1.0 g·L⁻¹ IBA solution for 10 s and then cut into peat soil as the experimental group, while the semi-hard branches were soaked in clear water for 10 s and then cut into peat soil as the control group. Each treatment had 105 cuttings. Afterwards, pour a small amount of water every day to keep the substrate moist. The first sampling was carried out on the day of cutting, and subsequent samplings were conducted every 5 days, with a total of 5 samplings. Soluble sugar, starch, soluble protein, polyphenol oxidase (PPO), superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), peroxidase (POD), auxin (IAA), cytokinin (CTK), gibberellin A3 (GA₃), and abscisic acid (ABA) were determined, and the rooting rate was calculated on the 20th day.

Optical microscopy analysis

Select the branches treated with IBA in Sect. 2.6. Starting from the day of cutting, every 5 days, the stem segments during the rooting process of ‘Tuershao’ cuttings will be selected, sampled, and photographed. Cut a sample with a thickness of about 1 cm at the bottom of each branch and place it in FAA fixative solution for paraffin sectioning. Fix the sample with FAA fixative at 4 ° C for 24 h, and gradually dehydrate it with 85%, 95%, and 100% ethanol solutions for 2 h. 100% ethanol needs to be treated twice. After transparency with xylene, immerse in wax for embedding, slice with a slicer, and stain with pure green. This testing is entrusted to Pan Ruiyunzhi Technology (Zhengzhou) Co., Ltd [48].

Determination of rooting rate and root system parameters

After washing the treated plants with clean water, the root length was measured with a ruler, and the rooting rate was calculated with the standard of root length ≥ 1 cm. The root system was scanned using a root system scanner (MICROTEK ScanMaker i850), and the root length, root surface area, root volume, and root diameter were analyzed with Microtek ScanWizard Pro analysis software.

Determination of nutrient contents

0.5 g of fresh stem segments of ‘Tuershao’ cuttings were weighed respectively, and the soluble sugar content and starch were determined by the anthrone-sulfuric acid method [49]. 0.5 g of fresh stem segments of ‘Tuershao’ cuttings were weighed, and the soluble protein content was determined by the Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250 method [50].

Determination of antioxidant enzyme and polyphenol oxidase activities

0.3 g of fresh stem segments were weighed respectively, and kits were used to determine the activities of catalase (CAT), polyphenol oxidase (PPO), peroxidase (POD), and superoxide dismutase (SOD) [51].

Determination of endogenous hormones

0.3 g of fresh stem segments were weighed respectively, and the contents of endogenous hormones auxin (IAA), cytokinin (CTK), gibberellin (GA₃), and abscisic acid (ABA) were determined by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits [14].

Data analysis

Experimental data were statistically analyzed by analysis of variance (ANOVA) using IBM SPSS Statistics 23.0. At a significance level of P ≤ 0.05, the LSD (Least Significant Difference) multiple range test was used to determine the differences between treatments. Data were expressed as mean ± standard error (SE). Excel 2016 and Origin 2022 were used for mapping, respectively.
