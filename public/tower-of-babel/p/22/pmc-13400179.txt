Contribution of E190D and Q226H Mutations in HA to the Receptor‐Binding Profile of D1.1 Genotype H5N1 Viruses
Authors: Yang Fang, Lei Yang, Shumei Zou, Liqi Liu, Wenfei Zhu, Dayan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA 2024 human HPAI H5N1 case in British Columbia showed mixed viral populations containing HA‐190D (28%) and HA‐226H (35%) variants in tracheal aspirate sequencing. In this study, solid‐phase binding assay and molecular docking were used to evaluate the contribution of HA‐E190D/Q226H mutations to the viral receptor profiles. Our results showed that HA‐E190D marginally reduced sialic acid α2,3 receptors' affinity, while HA‐Q226H impaired both sialic acid α2,3 and α2,6 receptors' binding. These results demonstrate that neither mutation strengthens viral binding to human‐type receptors, indicating that such substitutions are unlikely to heighten the public health threat posed by the virus for now.Keywords: E190D, HPAI H5N1, public health threat, Q226H, receptor profile

Highly pathogenic avian influenza viruses (HPAIVs) pose a substantial threat to global animal health and the stability of agricultural economies. In recent years, the H5N1 subtype has notably expanded its host spectrum, enabling cross‐species transmission that transcends its traditional reservoirs of wild birds and domestic poultry to infect a range of mammalian species, such as humans [1, 2]. A key barrier to efficient human‐to‐human transmission of avian influenza virus (AIV) lies in the mismatch between the receptor‐binding specificities of their HA and the receptor profiles of human respiratory tissues. For AIVs to achieve sustained avian‐to‐human transmission, they typically acquire enhanced binding affinity for human‐type receptors [3, 4]. Consequently, continuous surveillance of the receptor‐binding properties of circulating viruses is indispensable for the early evaluation of cross‐species transmission risks [5, 6].

In November 2024, a case of severe illness caused by HPAIVs H5N1 infection was reported in British Columbia, Canada, involving a 13‐year‐old girl with preexisting mild asthma and obesity. Genetic sequencing of the H5 HA gene revealed a mixed viral population at two key codons: 190E (H3 numbering) with the D variant at 28% allele frequency and 226Q with the H variant accounting for 35% [7]. These variants may represent adaptive changes of the virus; however, it remains unclear whether such mutations can alter the viral receptor‐binding properties.

To evaluate the contribution of the HA‐E190D and Q226H mutations to the receptor‐binding profiles, we rescued a series of recombinant H5N1 vaccine strains with HA and NA gene from A/British Columbia/PHL‐2032/2024 (BC24) and six internal genes from A/Puerto Rico/8/1934 (H1N1) (Table S1). All performance involving the live viruses was conducted in BSL‐2 laboratory. Viral rescue was only successfully achieved for strains carrying the 226Q residue, while attempts to rescue viruses with 226H failed.

Receptor‐binding specificity of influenza viruses was characterized via solid‐phase direct binding assay using three pairs of α2,3‐linked and α2,6‐linked sialylglycopolymers. The result showed that avian influenza virus A/Anhui/1/2015 (H5N1) bound to sialic acid α2,3 receptors, and seasonal A/Hubei/075/2024 (H1N1) bound to sialic acid α2,6 receptors, as expected. Rescued bc24 viruses with either HA‐190D or HA‐190E bound exclusively to sialic acid α2,3 receptors, showing no affinity for sialic acid α2,6 receptors. Although the E190D mutation slightly reduced the viral binding affinity toward sialic acid α2,3 receptors, it maintained its original receptor‐binding specificity (Figure 1A).

To systematically evaluate the effects of 190E/226Q, 190E/226H, 190D/226Q, and 190D/226H mutations on receptor‐binding profiles, we conducted molecular docking analysis [8]. In the 226Q background, E190D failed to strengthen hydrogen bond interactions or markedly reduce binding energy to α2,3‐linked sialic acid receptors, thus causing no obvious shift in receptor‐binding preference, which was consistent with solid‐phase assay results. Although molecular docking suggested that E190D could optimize hydrogen bonding to facilitate binding to α2,6‐linked sialic acid receptors, this predicted affinity improvement was not confirmed by solid‐phase assays. In general, E190D only induced subtle structural changes that were insufficient to alter viral receptor usage preference [9]. Differently, in the 226H genetic background, E190D was predicted to increase hydrogen bond numbers and decrease binding energy for both α2,3‐ and α2,6‐linked sialic acid receptors, implying that this mutation could effectively elevate viral binding affinity to both receptor types. Generally, regardless of whether residue 190 was E or D, the Q226H mutation uniformly weakened the binding ability of influenza viruses to both α2,3‐ and α2,6‐linked sialic acid receptors (Figures 1B and S1 and Table S3).

Discussion

Host adaptation of influenza A viruses from animals to humans is mainly determined by the recognition of sialic acid receptors on host respiratory tracts mediated by HA protein [10]. Our results revealed that E190D and Q226H mutations in HA of BC24 virus could not shift viral binding preference toward human‐type α2,6‐linked sialic acid receptors, consistent with earlier relevant studies. Using recombinant HA proteins, Ni et al. and Kovács et al. confirmed that E190D and Q226H markedly reduced viral binding capacity to both α2,3‐ and α2,6‐linked sialic acid receptors, indicating impaired intrinsic HA‐glycan interaction [11, 12]. Using plaque‐purified viruses derived from the original clinical sample after passage in MDCK cells, Kandeil et al. found that D190 mutant viruses still exclusively bound to α2,3‐linked sialic acid receptors without any α2,6 receptor adaptation [13]. In summary, all available evidence proves that neither E190D nor Q226H can drive viral adaptation to human‐preferred α2,6‐linked receptors. Additionally, Q226H is easily lost during viral rescue and cell passage and is rarely detected in global H5N1 D1.1 surveillance strains (Table S2). These findings collectively indicate that the current E190D and Q226H mutations might not increase the zoonotic transmission potential and public health risk of prevailing H5N1 viruses at present.
