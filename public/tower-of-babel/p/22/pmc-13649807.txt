Hybrid Surgery for Urethral Diverticulum Penetrating the Bladder
Authors: Yugo Sawada, Yasuhide Kitagawa, Kojiro Nishio, Tokumasa Hayashi, Shino Tokiwa, Mika Nagae, Masayoshi Jimmy Nomura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction and HypothesisUrethral diverticula have various morphological patterns, and in complex cases, a transvaginal approach can be difficult. MethodsHere, we report a case in which a urethral diverticulum originating from the ventral (posterior) side of the urethra penetrated the bladder through the diverticular wall. A 54-year-old premenopausal woman was diagnosed with a urethral diverticulum on the ventral side of the urethra and a communicating branch penetrating from the diverticular wall to the bladder, triggered by recurrent cystitis. Surgical treatment consisted of transvaginal excision of the urethral diverticulum and laparoscopic open bladder surgery to remove as much of the fistula as possible and repair the bladder penetration.ResultsVoiding urethrography on the 7th day after surgery and magnetic resonance imaging 1 year after surgery revealed the disappearance of the fistula, and no recurrence of the urethral diverticulum was observed.ConclusionThis suggests that a hybrid approach combining the transvaginal and laparoscopic approaches might be useful for the surgery of complex urethral diverticula that affect the bladder.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00192-026-06581-zKeywords: Laparoscopic surgery, Urethral diverticulum, Vaginal surgery

Introduction

Urethral diverticula are prone to infection and inflammation and may lead to stone or tumor development, making surgical excision an appropriate treatment option. Diverticula vary in morphology, including those that surround the urethra, those that grow so large that they compress the bladder wall, and those that develop into a complex multilocular configuration [1]. Diverticula are typically removed via a transvaginal approach; however, complete removal through this method alone is expected to be technically difficult if the diverticulum extends close to the bladder. Furthermore, surgical treatment may be avoided because inadequate urethral or bladder injury repair increases the risk of complications such as urethrovaginal and vesicovaginal fistulas. Herein, we describe the hybrid surgery technique combining a transvaginal approach and laparoscopy to treat a rare urethral diverticulum that penetrated the bladder.

Materials and Methods

A 54-year-old woman (G3P3) presented with recurrent cystitis. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a urethral diverticulum with an 11 × 9 mm stone, with its opening 22 mm from the internal urethral orifice and stretching into the urethra on the posterior side, and a fistula tract from the diverticulum to the bladder. Voiding cystourethrography (VCUG) revealed a wedge-shaped perforation tract between the urethral diverticulum and the bladder wall; however, no contrast medium communication between the urethral diverticulum and the bladder was seen. Cystoscopy revealed a fistular tract opening in the midline of the interureteric ligament, and a purulent discharge seeping from the bladder opening owing to vaginal-wall compression, leading to a diagnosis of a urethral diverticulum penetrating the bladder.

Procedure

Transvaginal Approach

Under general anesthesia, the surgery was started in the lithotomy position. A 4-cm midline incision was made in the anterior vaginal wall 1.5 cm distal to the external urethral meatus. The urethral diverticulum was excised. The urethral opening of the urethral diverticulum was closed by repeated single ligations using 3–0 absorbable sutures. However, no bladder penetration tract was identified inside the urethral diverticular wall. A Martius flap was formed at the urethral mucosal closure, and the vaginal wall was closed. Although there is no video recording of this procedure, it was similar to a typical female ventral urethral diverticulotomy performed in our department and in general [2].

Laparoscopic Intraperitoneal Approach

The patient was placed in the lithotomy position. Four trocars were placed in a “diamond” shape: above the umbilicus; halfway between the umbilicus and the pubic symphysis; in the left iliac fossa; and in the right iliac fossa. The bladder was opened transabdominally through a midline incision, and the opening was widened using four support sutures, including two external sutures. The opening of the urethral diverticulum was identified within the bladder, and a traction suture was placed at the opening. While applying tension to the fistula tissue with traction sutures, a cylindrical excision was made with scissors, and as much fistula tissue as possible was removed to the level of the pubocervical fascia. The bladder-wall defect was divided into two layers (pubocervical fascia and bladder mucosa), and each layer was closed using repeated interrupted sutures. The open bladder was then divided into two layers (bladder mucosa and muscle layers) and closed to complete the surgery.

Results

The surgical time and the blood-loss volume were 320 min and 20 ml respectively. On the 7th postoperative day, a VCUG confirmed no bladder or urethral leakage, and the urethral catheter was removed. An MRI performed 1 year after surgery showed no urethral diverticulum recurrence. Furthermore, subjective symptoms such as pain during urination, frequent urination, urinary incontinence, and difficulty urinating were not observed.

Discussion

The paraurethral glands, which are thought to be the source of urethral diverticula, open in the distal one-third of the urethra and extend proximally parallel to the posterior urethral wall; therefore, most urethral diverticulum openings are located on the distal posterior urethral wall [3, 4]. The muscular structure around the urethra is composed of, from the inner layer, longitudinal smooth muscle, circular smooth muscle, external urethral sphincter, and outermost smooth-muscle layer, and it has been suggested that the muscular layer arrangement might differ between the proximal and distal urethra [5]. The shape of urethral diverticula may be horseshoe or circumferential, surrounding the urethra in addition to being unilocular, which may be influenced by the muscular structure around the urethra. Although this is a rare case of urethral diverticula penetrating the bladder, it is thought that the urethral diverticulum, which developed on the distal posterior wall of the urethra, formed perforating branches along the paraurethral glands to the bladder and penetrated it.

Transvaginal removal is the most effective treatment of symptomatic urethral diverticula. Postoperative complications of transvaginal urethral diverticula include recurrence (0–22%) and urethrovaginal fistula (0–8%), and the incidence varies depending on the size, shape, and location (proximal or distal) of the diverticulum [1]. There have been reports of the laparoscopic removal of urethral diverticula primarily located on the anterior side of the urethra [6–8]. In this case, where the urethral diverticulum had penetrated the bladder, resection and repair of not only the urethral diverticulum but also the bladder tissue was required. Therefore, surgery using the transvaginal approach alone would likely result in insufficient bladder fistula resection/repair unless the surgeon is highly skilled in transvaginal surgery, thereby increasing the risk of postoperative complications from the vesicovaginal fistula. Initially, we had considered removing the diverticulum and repairing the fistula to the bladder using transvaginal surgery alone, but we determined midway through that completing these procedures using transvaginal surgery alone would increase the risk of perioperative complications, so we switched to a combination of transvaginal approach and laparoscopic surgery, which was the method we had planned before the surgery. The urethral diverticulum and as much of the perforation as possible were removed via the transvaginal surgery, whereas bladder-tissue resection and repair were performed laparoscopically using the O'Conor technique, which has been reported to have a high success rate in vesicovaginal fistula surgery [9, 10]. PubMed, Google Scholar, and Web of Science databases were searched using the keywords “urethral diverticulum,” “bladder penetration,” “urethral diverticulum removal,” “vaginal surgery,” and “laparoscopic surgery” to determine whether similar cases or procedures had been reported. Only a few studies in the literature have reported on urethral diverticulum penetration into the bladder. In addition, there have been very few reports of urethral diverticula treated with hybrid surgery using both vaginal and laparoscopic approaches, as in the present case. In such cases, we believe that hybrid surgery, in which the bladder wall and bladder mucosa are approached laparoscopically rather than relying solely on transvaginal surgery, is effective.

Preoperative evaluation of special and complex urethral diverticula is desirable using multiple examination devices. MRI, VCUG, cystoscope, and ultrasound play complementary roles. MRI is useful for static evaluation of urethral diverticula, whereas VCUG allows evaluation of the urethral diverticula and the degree of patency of the fistula under dynamic conditions, such as during urination. Through cystoscopy, the peridiverticulum cystoscope was able to confirm not only the opening of the urethral diverticulum but also the positional relationship between the fistula and the ureteral orifice, as well as purulent discharge from the fistula. Ultrasound is an examination that can be performed even during surgery, and can non-invasively complement other examination information at any time.

The following are important points about this intraperitoneal surgical approach: insertion of a ureteral stent is recommended to avoid the risk of direct injury to the ureter or suture insertion during the removal of unnecessary tissue from the bladder and subsequent suturing of the defect. Furthermore, with a double-J stent, the tail of the immediate bladder stump is located within the bladder and interferes with intravesical manipulation; therefore, inserting a single-J stent and having the stump exit through the external urethral orifice is recommended. A suture was placed around the fistula, which was then dissected and resected while pulling the suture. Direct grasping and pulling of the fistula tissue using forceps can easily crush it. However, application of tension to the dissected tissue using traction sutures facilitates visualization and tissue dissection. After removing as much fistula tissue as possible, the bladder muscle and pubocervical fascia were closed using four single-knotted sutures. Tying each single-knot suture individually can make it difficult to identify the edges in the deeper layers. Therefore, each ligation was performed only after all stitches had been inserted and the layer edges were clearly identified. Secure closure of each layer can prevent postoperative complications such as vesicovaginal fistulas.

Conclusion

In this case, the surgical treatment of a urethral diverticulum penetrating the bladder, urethral diverticulum removal was performed transvaginally, and the intravesical fistula was resected and repaired laparoscopically, with a favorable postoperative course. The application of hybrid surgery as a surgical procedure for urethral diverticula with bladder lesions should also be considered.
