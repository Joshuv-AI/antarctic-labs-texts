Identification of Frailty Subtypes in African American Adults With and Without Type 2 Diabetes: A Latent Class Analysis From the Jackson Heart Study
Authors: Felicia R. Simpson, Mark A. Espeland, Alain G. Bertoni, Jai J. Legins, Torre S. Lloyd, Tanaiya A. Sumter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsTo identify frailty subtypes among African American adults with and without diabetes.MethodsThis analysis included data from 3979 African American men and women who enrolled in the Jackson Heart Study at baseline (Exam1: 2000–2004). To identify frailty subtypes, we selected 25 age‐related deficits in health and functional outcomes as indicator variables to use in multiple‐group latent class analysis (LCA), with diabetes status as the grouping variable. Components included markers of general health, physical function, social engagement, work, activities of daily living, behaviour/lifestyle, sleep, age‐related chronic diseases, pain, clinical biomarkers, and sensorineural abilities.ResultsThree frailty groups were identified for adults with and without diabetes: the ‘severely frail’ group (15.2% of those without diabetes; 22.4% of those with diabetes); the ‘moderately frail’ group (21.7% of those without diabetes; 13.1% of those with diabetes); and the ‘not frail’ group (63.1% of those without diabetes; 64.5% of those with diabetes). Adults in the ‘severely frail’ group had higher probabilities of poor health perception, limited mobility, high blood pressure, obesity, and claudication, and this was similar for those with and without diabetes. Among individuals in the ‘moderately frail’ group, higher probabilities of poor health perception, limited mobility, high blood pressure, obesity, and claudication were found among those with diabetes, whereas only sleep quality and obesity had higher probabilities among those without diabetes.ConclusionsThree heterogeneous frailty subgroups were identified among African American adults, with similarities and differences noted for those with and without diabetes with regard to high probabilities for certain deficits in health and functional outcomes. Further investigation is needed to evaluate if these subgroups predict health‐related outcomes.

Introduction

The prevalence of type 2 diabetes is projected to double globally by 2050 [1, 2] and disproportionately affects African American adults in the US who are twice as likely to develop type 2 diabetes as white adults [3, 4]. Type 2 diabetes is a metabolic disorder characterised by chronic hyperglycaemia [5, 6, 7] that accelerates biological ageing and frailty [8, 9, 10, 11] which serves as a marker of an individual's ‘ageing‐related health state’ [12]. The prevalence of frailty is higher in African American adults compared to white adults [13]. However, the prevalence and consequences of frailty among African American adults with diabetes are not well described. In the African American Health project, adults with diabetes had higher scores on frailty scales compared to those without diabetes, and higher frailty scores were associated with increased disability, functional limitations, and mortality [13]. Further, African American participants with diabetes in the African American Health Study were more likely to have Medicaid coverage, lower household incomes, poorer performance on animal naming tests, and worse self‐rated health [13].

The most widely used tools used to assess frailty include Fried's frailty syndrome approach [14, 15] and Rockwood's deficit accumulation model [16, 17, 18], which are both predictive of adverse health outcomes [18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26]. Deficit accumulation frailty indices are scored with all components weighted equally because each component is posited to provide information on the ageing process, although the individual components may differ in perceived severity, associated medical costs, and overall burden. How ageing is manifested by these components varies depending, at least in part, on exposures, genetics, and behaviour. Prior studies have used the data‐driven approach of latent class analysis (LCA) to identify subgroups of frailty [27, 28, 29, 30] that reflect the heterogenous nature of frailty phenotypes. LCA is a statistical procedure used to identify subgroups within a heterogenous population [31]. The subgroups are referred to as latent groups (or classes). The term ‘latent’ means unobserved [31]. LCA identifies subgroups within a population that cannot be measured directly but are inferred from patterns in observed data (indicator variables).

Deficit accumulation frailty indices have been used to identify frailty subtypes across many conditions, leading to personalised approaches to clinical care [32]. They have been incorporated into electronic health records to set treatment goals and anticipate overall health care utilization [33]. To date, however, few studies have investigated frailty subtypes among African‐American adults with and without diabetes, who may present with different risk factor profiles and health care needs than other groups [13]. Furthermore, frailty in African American adults with and without diabetes has been assessed using the FRAIL scale from the International Association of Nutrition and ageing, the Study of Osteoporotic Fractures (SOF) frailty scale, the Cardiovascular Health Study (CHS) frailty phenotype, and the frailty index (FI) using the African American Health study. The objective of the current analysis is to use LCA to identify clusters of individuals among those with and without diabetes who manifest the ageing process differently from others, as captured by reweighing the frailty index components. To our knowledge, our study is the first to examine frailty subtypes in African American adults with and without diabetes using LCA.

Methods

Data Source

For this analysis, we performed a secondary data analysis of the Jackson Heart Study (JHS). The study design and methods have been previously published [34]. The JHS is a large, community‐based, observational study that recruited 5306 African American adults from the Jackson, Mississippi geographical area (Hinds, Madison and Rankin counties) to investigate the causes of cardiovascular disease. Recruited participants were African American men and women aged 35–84 years at baseline, except in the nested family study that also enrolled family members aged ≥ 21 years. Participants completed in‐person exams (Exam 1: 2000–2004; Exam 2: 2005–2008; and Exam 3: 2009–2013) during which interviews were administered to assess lifestyle factors, psychosocial measures, socio‐demographics, and medical history, and physical examinations were done to assess blood pressure and anthropometrics. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review boards of the University of Mississippi Medical Center, Jackson State University and Tougaloo College.

We conducted a cross‐sectional study of the baseline Jackson Heart Study data. For the present analysis, the following exclusions were made: participants aged < 45 years at baseline (N = 1261); did not have data available at baseline for > 20% of the deficits evaluated (N = 28); and were missing diabetes status (N = 38). As a result, the final analytical cohort consisted of 3979 individuals.

Selection of Deficits in Health for the Latent Class Analysis

We selected 25 age‐related deficits in health and functional outcomes as indicator variables from exam 1 based on previously reported deficit accumulation models of frailty [16, 17], and investigated these among individuals with and without diabetes. Components included markers of general health, physical function, social engagement, work, activities of daily living, behaviour/lifestyle, sleep, age‐related chronic diseases, pain, clinical biomarkers, and sensorineural abilities. During the initial clinical exam, data was collected on medical history and physical examination, blood and urine analysis, and information on physical activity, stress, and socioeconomic factors. This data included self‐report responses and clinical measurements. Table 1 provides a summary of the components and their scoring. Additionally, the Simpson deficit accumulation frailty index from the Action for Health in Diabetes (Look AHEAD) Trial [35] was calculated using the 25 deficits and compared for those with and without diabetes to evaluate if both metrics are capturing the ageing processing similarly. Cutoffs for the 25 health deficits were based on those used in the Simpson deficit accumulation frailty index [35]. These thresholds are also consistent with those applied in previously developed frailty index constructions [16, 17].

The deficit indicator variables were investigated overall and by diabetes status. Diabetes was defined as current use of insulin or oral antidiabetic agent or self‐report of physician's diagnosis, fasting glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL, or haemoglobin A1c ≥ 6.5%.

Statistical Analysis

We used chi‐square tests or t‐tests to compare baseline characteristics by diabetes status. We described the total sample by presenting frequencies and percentages for categorical baseline characteristics, and means with standard deviations for continuous baseline characteristics, stratified by diabetes status. To examine whether the latent class model differs according to diabetes status, we performed multiple‐group LCA with diabetes status as the grouping variable. Multiple‐group LCA is an extension to the standard LCA that allows us to examine whether the latent class structure differs across a predefined group. LCA applies a statistical model to estimate the probability that each individual belongs to each latent (unobserved) class and then assigns individuals to the class with the highest probability. In multiple‐group LCA, individuals are classified using group‐specific posterior membership probabilities, which are estimated via maximum likelihood using the Expectation–Maximisation algorithm. The model estimates both class membership probabilities and item‐response probabilities within each group (diabetes status), and individuals are assigned to classes based on their group‐specific posterior probabilities. We iteratively fitted models comprising up to six classes and selected the model with the best statistical properties. For each class solution, we presented the Log‐Likelihood, Akaike Information Criteria, (AIC), Bayesian Information Criteria (BIC), and Entropy. Based on the number of latent classes identified, we assigned qualitative labels to describe each frailty subgroup based on the overall patterns of the item response probabilities by diabetes status. We also created a frailty index (FI) score based on the 25 indicators variables to determine if the metrics are capturing the ageing progress differently. The variables were recoded on a scale to 0–1 based on the levels of severity for each deficits. The FI score is derived by dividing the number of identified deficits by the total number of potential deficits assessed, resulting in a score between 0 and 1. Statistical and LCA Analysis, description of characteristics, and tests of group differences were performed using SAS software. A two‐tailed p‐value of ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To identify the optimal number of latent classes and the best‐fitting model, we began with a one‐class solution and sequentially increased the number of classes by one. Models with one through six classes were estimated and evaluated using multiple statistical fit indices. Model fit was assessed based on the log‐likelihood, Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and entropy. Consistent with recommendations in the latent class analysis literature, BIC is commonly emphasised as a key indicator of model fit. Across the estimated models, BIC decreased from the one‐class to the three‐class solution and then increased for models with four or more classes (Supporting Information S1). The lowest BIC value (−64925.02) was observed for the three‐class model, indicating the best balance between fit and model complexity. Although AIC continued to decrease through the six‐class model, this pattern is expected because AIC imposes a weaker penalty for additional parameters and therefore tends to favour more complex models. The log‐likelihood improved with each additional class, as anticipated; however, the magnitude of improvement diminished substantially after the three‐class solution (Supporting Information S2). Entropy for the three‐class model (0.70) suggested adequate classification precision and was comparable to, or better than, that of models with more classes.

Based on the combined evidence from BIC, entropy, likelihood‐based fit indices, and substantive interpretability, the three‐class model was selected as the optimal solution. In addition to meeting statistical criteria, this model yielded interpretable and distinct latent classes, whereas models with more classes showed only marginal improvements in fit and reduced conceptual clarity.

For indicators that diagnoses disease and potential functional consequences, we conducted cross‐tabulations of stroke and mobility within each class to assess the local independence assumption. Within each latent class, observed counts were approximately equal to expected counts, Pearson residuals were very small (< 2), and chi‐square tests showed no meaningful association between the two items. Overall, stroke and mobility did not demonstrate residual correlation within any class. The conditional probabilities of indicators in each class are summarised in Table 3 by diabetes status. Of the 2866 individuals without diabetes, 63% were in latent class 1, 15% in latent class 2, and 22% in latent class 3. Of the 1113 individuals with diabetes, 65% were in latent class 1, 22% in latent class 2, and 13% in latent class 3. Latent class 2 was designated as ‘severely frail’ and this group was characterised by having high probabilities for several indicator variables for both individuals with and without diabetes. Individuals without diabetes in the ‘severely frail’ class had higher probabilities for poor health perception and limited physical function and abilities compared to individuals with diabetes in this latent class. Further, individuals with diabetes in the ‘severely frail’ group had higher probabilities of high glucose levels and obesity compared to individuals without diabetes in this latent class. Latent class 3 was designated as ‘moderately frail’. Among individuals identified as ‘moderately frail’, the average of the item response probabilities were higher for individuals with diabetes compared to individuals without diabetes. Individuals with diabetes in the ‘moderately frail’ class had higher probabilities for poor health perception, sleep quality, low HDL cholesterol, high blood pressure, limited physical function and abilities. Members of latent class 1 had lower probabilities in the majority of indicators and was designated as ‘not frail’. Among individuals with diabetes in the ‘not frail’ class, there were higher probabilities for obesity, high glucose, and low HDL cholesterol. Measurement invariance was evaluated to determine whether the latent class structure was comparable across diabetes groups (diabetes vs. no diabetes). We conducted a likelihood ratio test by comparing the constrained model, which assumed equal item‐response probabilities across groups, with the sum of the group‐specific unconstrained models. The resulting test statistic was G2=0 with a corresponding p‐value of 1.00, indicating no evidence that the unconstrained model provided a better fit. These results support the conclusion that measurement invariance holds across diabetes groups, and that the latent classes can be interpreted equivalently for individuals with and without diabetes.

Discussion

Our data‐driven approach to classifying frailty among African American adults identified three heterogenous subgroups among those with and without diabetes. Individuals classified as ‘severely frail’ were characterised as having high probabilities for deficits in health and functional outcomes in both individuals with and without diabetes. In line with prior literature, we observed that individuals in the severely frail class exhibited more of the clinical characteristics and traditional risk factors of ageing compared to other frailty subgroups [16, 17, 21, 22, 24].

While previous studies have evaluated frailty in African Americans [13, 18, 19], the prevalence and consequences of frailty among African American adults with and without diabetes are not well described in the literature. Chode et al. [13] examined the association of diabetes with health outcomes and frailty and found that African Americans with diabetes had increased functional limitations, lower physical performance, and disability. The study further found that frailty appears to identify a higher‐risk subgroup among African American adults with diabetes, These findings are similar to the current findings using data from the JHS. When examining the latent class model for those with and without diabetes, we found similarities and differences in the item response probabilities. The sum of the class probabilities was higher in the ‘moderately frail’ latent class for individuals with diabetes compared to individuals without diabetes. It appears our approach to examining frailty identified clusters of African Americans who manifest the ageing process differently from others based on diabetes status. Our findings showed that poor health perception, high blood pressure, obesity, mobility impairment, and claudication were likely to co‐occur in African Americans with diabetes at moderate and severe stages of frailty. Public health and clinical practice might benefit from integrating frailty measures into diabetes care to better stratify risk, guide preventive strategies, and tailor interventions for African Americans. Incorporating more routine frailty screening among African Americans with diabetes could help identify the frailty class they most closely resemble, particularly given the co‐occurrence of multiple health deficits. Based on our findings, African Americans with diabetes may benefit from earlier or more intensive frailty prevention strategies, such as a multidomain lifestyle intervention that includes diet, physical activity, cognitive training, and vascular risk management to help slow or potentially reverse the progression of deficit accumulation frailty. Further results suggest that diabetes management should be individualised according to frailty class, and that these classes may serve as a useful framework for guiding resource allocation.

Previous studies have employed a data‐driven latent class analysis (LCA) approach to identify frailty subtypes using data from the I‐Lan Longitudinal Aging Study (ILAS), the Cardiovascular Health Study (CHS), and the Survey of Urban Community Cardiovascular Experiences, Engagement, and Disparities (SUCCEED) survey [36, 37]. These studies primarily relied on indicator variables derived from Fried's frailty phenotype to characterise frailty subtypes. However, there was little to no representation of African American participants in these cohorts. To date, the present study is the first to examine deficit accumulation frailty subtypes among African American adults with and without diabetes using variables based on the frailty index approach.

Further, chronic inflammation has been identified as a key contributor to the development of type 2 diabetes and its associated complications. Evidence indicates that individuals with type 2 diabetes exhibit elevated inflammatory markers, which contribute to oxidative stress, accelerated muscle loss, and endothelial dysfunction [38, 39, 40, 41]. Additionally, deficit accumulation frailty has been linked in the literature to increased levels of inflammatory cytokines [42, 43]. Together, these findings suggest that chronic inflammation may serve as a biological pathway through which metabolic and vascular diseases accelerate progression towards frailty. From a clinical perspective, this underscores the importance of targeting inflammation through lifestyle interventions, optimised disease management, and treatment of comorbidities to help prevent or slow the progression of frailty in African Americans.

This study has limitations. First, this study was conducted in a geographically localised population in Jackson, Mississippi, with participants recruited from a single site. As a result, the findings may not be generalisable to African Americans living in other regions of the United States, to other racial or ethnic groups, or to older or institutionalised populations. Second, we did not investigate whether these deficit accumulation frailty subtypes were associated with clinical events or mortality in our analyses. Further work needs to be done to test the predictive validity of the frailty latent classes to predict mortality and other clinical outcomes in African American adults with and without diabetes. Therefore, our findings require further validation before they can be translated into clinical practice. Lastly, the indicator variables included in the latent class analysis were selected based on prior frailty work and availability in JHS. Thus, other indicator variables that were not available in JHS should also be investigated in future work on frailty subtypes. We acknowledge that further validation in future studies is needed to clarify how diabetes may influence the manifestation of frailty in African American populations.

In summary, our study identified 3 frailty subtypes among African American adults with and without diabetes. These findings suggest that diabetes may alter frailty in African Americans, particularly at the moderate and severe frailty stages. Interventions to delay or prevent the progression of frailty in the early stages of frailty are needed, as frailty may affect the management and sequalae of diabetes in African Americans. The relationship between frailty and diabetes mellitus remains a complex topic [38, 39] and may be bidirectional [44].
