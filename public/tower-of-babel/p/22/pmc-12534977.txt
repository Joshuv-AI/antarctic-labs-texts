Methodological and applicability pitfalls of clinical prediction models for asthma diagnosis: a systematic review and critical appraisal of evidence
Authors: Pakpoom Wongyikul, Phichayut Phinyo, Pusit Seephueng, Krittai Tanasombatkul, Theerasuk Kawamatawong, Chamard Wongsa, Torpong Thongngarm
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

However, concerns about the transparency and usability of these models remain. Background

Asthma is characterised by recurring symptoms of airflow obstruction, including wheezing, shortness of breath, chest tightness, and coughing, which can vary significantly in intensity either spontaneously or in response to treatment [1, 2]. Its diagnosis typically relies on identifying airway hyperresponsiveness (AHR) or detecting objective signs of airflow obstruction with partial reversibility [3]. However, misdiagnosis of asthma—encompassing both overdiagnosis and underdiagnosis—remains a major clinical challenge. This issue arises from the misinterpretation of symptoms and lung function tests [3–5]. Asthma diagnosis is further complicated by symptom overlap with other conditions, such as allergic reactions, ischaemic heart disease, subglottic stenosis, pulmonary hypertension, or other obstructive lung diseases [5, 6]. Overdiagnosis may lead to unnecessary and potentially harmful treatment strategies, as well as increased healthcare expenditure. Underdiagnosis, on the other hand, can result in impaired quality of life, long-term deterioration of lung function, and serious clinical consequences [7].

Clinical prediction models (CPMs) combine two or more effective predictors (e.g., age or symptom features) to estimate an individual's probability or risk of having a specific disease [8]. These models are designed to enhance healthcare workflows by providing accurate diagnostic probabilities and practical guidance on available tests [9–12]. In the era of big data, machine learning (ML) has gained significant popularity for its impressive efficiency in handling high-dimensional predictors such as imaging, genetic sequencing, and lung function parameters [13]. Unlike traditional techniques, ML uses computationally intensive, data-driven methods that require fewer user-defined modelling decisions, making it particularly suited for developing complex models in modern healthcare [13, 14].

The challenges of identifying patients at high risk of asthma have driven the development of numerous prediction models, ranging from traditional regression-based approaches to advanced ML algorithms [15–17]. Despite this, few models have been adopted in clinical practice, primarily due to longstanding concerns about transparency, completeness of reporting, and overall usability [18]. Studies on supervised ML-based CPMs, in particular, often lack the methodological rigor seen in traditional regression-based models, further limiting their adoption [19]. The introduction of the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) guidelines and the Prediction Model Risk of Bias Assessment Tool (PROBAST) [20, 21] has placed greater emphasis on improving the quality of reporting and bias assessment in predictive model studies. Adherence to these guidelines, alongside the use of robust study designs and modelling strategies, can enhance the clinical applicability of these models and support their integration into routine practice.

This review aims to provide an overview of existing diagnostic models for asthma, focusing on the methodological design and applicability of studies using traditional regression-based CPMs, supervised ML-based CPMs, or both.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted and reported in compliance with the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) [22]. The study protocol was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42022357796).

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion criteria focused on studies that developed or validated multivariable prediction models. According to the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis Based on Artificial Intelligence (TRIPOD-AI) [23], these models were classified as either regression-based or supervised ML-based. Eligible studies met the following criteria: (1) included patients intended to be diagnosed with asthma or patients with non-specific respiratory symptoms (e.g., wheeze, shortness of breath, or cough, where asthma was suspected); (2) examined the diagnostic accuracy or performance of the multivariable prediction models; (3) included at least one multivariable prediction model; and (4) reported at least one diagnostic parameter of interest (e.g., accuracy, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), or likelihood ratio) or provided sufficient information on true positive, false positive, true negative, and false negative values.

We excluded: (1) studies in languages other than English; (2) studies with no abstract, inaccessible full text, or duplicate publications; and (3) studies with insufficient or irrelevant data on diagnostic parameters.

Searches

Standard biomedical databases were searched using a pre-defined strategy, covering their inception dates through September 2024. Due to unforeseen circumstances, the project was paused for one year. As a result, the literature search was updated to include studies up to September 2024, thereby extending the original coverage period registered in PROSPERO, which ended in September 2022. These databases included PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, EMBASE, and the Cochrane Controlled Trials Register. For each database, KT and PW incorporated appropriate controlled vocabulary terms (e.g., MeSH for PubMed and EMTREE for EMBASE) into the search syntax, as detailed in Additional File 1: Tables S1–S4. A search for grey literature or unpublished studies was performed using Google Scholar. Additionally, two authors (KT and PW) reviewed the reference lists of prior systematic reviews and meta-analyses on the same topic. Relevant studies identified through reference reviews but not captured in the electronic database searches were also included.

Study selection process

Duplicates from each database were removed using a citation manager (EndNote 20), followed by manual verification. Titles and abstracts were screened by two independent reviewers (KT and PW) using Rayyan, a web application for screening systematic review records [24], to identify potentially eligible studies. Full-text articles were retrieved and reviewed for eligibility by the same two reviewers (KT and PW). Any disagreements during the screening process were resolved through consultation with methodological (PP) and clinical experts (TK and TT).

Data extraction

The following information was independently extracted from each article by two researchers (PS and PW): study authorship, year of publication, study period, country, study design, inclusion and exclusion criteria, model algorithm, and study methodology, including details on participants, predictors, outcomes, and analysis domains. These domains were extracted based on the items and signalling questions from TRIPOD+AI and the Prediction Model Risk of Bias Assessment Tool (PROBAST) [21, 23]. Any discrepancies during data extraction were resolved through discussion with the principal investigators (PP, TT, and TK). If multiple methods were used within the same model algorithm, the modelling approach with the highest performance was selected.

Risk of bias (quality assessment)

The methodological quality of each prediction study included in this systematic review was evaluated using PROBAST, a tool for assessing the risk of bias (ROB) and applicability of diagnostic and prognostic prediction model studies [21]. Two authors (PS and PW) independently assessed the risk of bias. A study was categorised as low risk when all domains were rated as low risk, and as high risk when at least one domain was rated as high risk. A study was rated as having an unclear risk if an unclear risk of bias was noted in one or more domains, while all other domains were rated as low risk [21]. Any discrepancies during the quality assessment were resolved through discussion with the other authors (PP, TT, and TK). In this study, we accepted the use of standard definitions for asthma diagnosis [2, 25], including: (1) spirometry or pulmonary function tests (e.g., FEV1/FVC ratio, FEV1 reversibility of 12% and 200 mL or greater); (2) methacholine challenge to assess bronchial hyperresponsiveness; (3) exhaled nitric oxide levels; and (4) peak flow variability. Other diagnostic methods were rated down in the outcome domain.

Synthesis of results

To ensure a clear and consistent methodological comparison at the study level, we categorised studies as regression-based when only regression techniques were reported, and as supervised ML-based when any non-regression method was employed. Study characteristics, including sample size, patient characteristics within the study, asthma prevalence, utilised predictors, and study design, were described using percentages, medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), or means with standard deviations (SD), as appropriate. The reported number of events was combined with the reported number of candidate predictors to calculate the number of events per variable (EPV). Internal validation methods were categorised as split-sample, cross-validation, or bootstrapping. Supervised ML-based CPMs were grouped by their main model algorithms [14]: Decision Tree (DT), K-Nearest Neighbours (kNN), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Random Forests (RF), and deep learning, while all other model algorithms were grouped under "Others." The model predictive performance and diagnostic accuracy indices were collected and reported as AUC, sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy for each model algorithm. Due to substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity across studies, we did not perform a quantitative synthesis of model performance, as pooling the results would not have been meaningful.

Results

A total of 4,306 records were identified from four databases, along with nine additional records found through a manual search. After duplicates were removed and the records screened, 69 studies involving 169 CPMs and 312,522 patients were included in this systematic review (Fig. 1). The list of excluded studies and the reasons for their exclusion are provided in Additional File 1: Table S5.

Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) flow diagram of included and excluded studies

Of the included studies, 54 assessed the performance of supervised ML-based CPMs [16, 17, 26–76], while 15 evaluated regression-based CPMs [15, 77–90]. The overall risk of bias was high for both regression-based and supervised ML-based CPM studies (Fig. 2), while overall applicability concerns were categorised as 13.04% low, 10.14% unclear, and 76.81% high (Supplementary Figure S1) across both regression-based and supervised ML-based CPM studies. Study characteristics and data source

Supervised ML-based CPM (54 studies)

Fifty-three studies (98.2%) aimed to develop new CPMs, while only one study (1.8%) externally validated an existing model [48]. The majority of supervised ML-based prediction models were developed or validated using sample sizes of 100–499 patients (18 studies). Another 18 studies used sample sizes exceeding 500 patients, while 14 studies used fewer than 100 patients (Table 1). Four studies did not report sample size information. Twenty-nine studies retrieved data from secondary/tertiary care (53.7%), 9 studies from the general population, and 12 studies did not report the study setting. The median asthma case proportion was 46.8% (IQR: 37.4%–64.8%), although 11 studies did not report this proportion. The median proportion of female participants was 49.5% (IQR: 42.4%–61.0%), with 24 studies (44.4%) not reporting information on sex (Table 1).

Regression-based CPM (15 studies)

For studies on regression-based CPMs, 10 studies aimed to develop new CPMs, 3 studies validated existing CPMs [80, 83, 90], and 2 studies developed and validated CPMs [81, 82]. Nine studies (80.0%) had sample sizes greater than 500 patients, while six studies (40.0%) had sample sizes ranging from 100 to 499 patients (Table 1). Most of the studies used data from secondary/tertiary care (9 studies, 60%). However, 3 studies (20%) did not report the study setting. The median asthma case proportion was 39.9% (IQR: 16.3%–52.5%). The median proportion of female participants was 57.9% (IQR: 48.7%–60.0%) (Table 1).

Methodological design and Risk of bias

Participant domain

Overall, 28 out of 54 (51.9%) studies on supervised ML-based CPMs recruited patients using a case–control approach, in which the controls included healthy volunteers and individuals with other conditions, such as COPD. Data were collected prospectively in 15 studies (27.7%) and retrospectively in 36 studies (66.7%) (Table 1). The number of high, low, and unclear risk of bias studies were 43 (79.6%), 6 (11.1%), and 5 (9.3%), respectively (Fig. 3A).

Risk of bias in the included studies stratified by modelling approach. (A) Supervised machine learning-based studies, and (B) Regression-based studies

Of the total, 13 out of 15 (86.7%) studies on regression-based CPMs recruited patients using a population-based approach. Data were prospectively collected in 10 studies (66.7%) and retrospectively collected in 5 studies (33.3%) (Table 1). The number of high, low, and unclear risk of bias studies were 5 (33.3%), 9 (60.0%), and 1 (6.7%), respectively (Fig. 3B). Details on patient characteristics and recruitment in each study are provided in Additional File 1: Table S6.

Predictor domain

The median number of predictors used in studies on supervised ML-based CPMs was 15 (IQR: 7–23), with 18 studies (33.3%) not reporting the number of predictors. Of 54 studies, 40 (74.1%) used clinical predictors, 8 studies (14.8%) biochemical predictors, 15 (29.6%) used pulmonary function predictors, and 13 (24.1%) used genetic predictors. Clinical predictors frequently included extracted respiratory sounds (30%), symptoms (26%), and demographic data (22%) (Fig. 4A). In contrast, exhaled breath condensate parameters (6%)—such as volatile organic compounds and gas electronic nose sensory—and complete blood counts (6%) were the most common biochemical predictors (Fig. 4B). A total of 19% and 7% of studies used pulmonary function parameters and respiratory mechanics assessments as predictors, respectively (Fig. 4C). The number of high, low, and unclear risk of bias studies were 19 (35.2%), 31 (57.4%), and 4 (7.4%), respectively (Fig. 3A).

Distribution of predictors used in regression-based (orange) and supervised machine learning-based (yellow) clinical prediction models for asthma diagnosis. The proportion of predictors in the clinical, biomedical, pulmonary function, genetic category, are illustrated in Fig. 4 A, 4B, 4 C, and 4D, respectively

All studies on regression-based CPMs reported the number of predictors, with a median of 8 predictors per model (IQR: 5–12). Clinical predictors were used in 100% of the studies, while 2 studies (13.3%) used biochemical predictors, 7 studies (46.7%) used pulmonary function predictors, and 3 studies (20.0%) used genetic predictors (Table 1). For the risk of bias in this domain, the number of high and low risk of bias studies were 7 (46.7%) and 8 (53.3%), respectively (Fig. 3B). Additional File 1: Table S7 provides detailed information on the predictors.

Outcome domain

Among studies on supervised ML-based CPMs, 17 (31.5%) adhered to standard asthma diagnostic definitions, 8 (14.8%) used suboptimal methods, and 29 (53.7%) did not specify the diagnostic method. In comparison, 10 (66.7%) studies on regression-based CPMs adhered to standard definitions, while 5 (33.3%) used suboptimal methods.

Overall, in studies on supervised ML-based CPMs, the number of high, low, and unclear risk of bias studies was 18 (33.3%), 10 (18.5%), and 26 (48.2%), respectively. Meanwhile, studies on regression-based CPMs exhibited 9 (60.0%) studies with high risk of bias and 6 (40.0%) studies with low risk of bias (Fig. 3A, 3B). The asthma diagnostic definitions used in each study are summarized in Additional File 1: Table S6.

Analysis domain

The model algorithms used in supervised ML-based CPMs included DT in 17 studies, kNN in 16 studies, SVM in 24 studies, RF in 19 studies, deep learning techniques in 29 studies, and other algorithms (e.g., Gradient Boost, XGBoost, Naïve Bayes, Bagging, etc.) in 36 studies. Logistic regression, with or without penalization, was the only statistical algorithm used in studies on regression-based CPMs.

Regression-based CPMs had a higher sample size relative to candidate predictors, with a median EPV of 16.2 (IQR: 14.0–42.0), compared to supervised ML-based CPMs, which had a median EPV of 8.2 (IQR: 4.6–50.6). The most common method used to assess overfitting in supervised ML-based CPMs was cross-validation (33 studies, 61.1%), followed by split sampling (6 studies, 11.1%). In regression-based CPMs, 3 studies (17.7%) used split sampling, 2 studies (13.3%) used bootstrapping, and 1 study (6.7%) used cross-validation (Table 1).

Overall, 26 out of 69 studies (37.7%) did not report the methods used to handle missing data (Table 2), while 8 studies (11.6%) relied on complete case analysis. Predictor selection based on univariable analysis was reported in 2 studies (11.8%) using regression-based CPMs and in 4 studies (7.7%) using supervised ML-based CPMs. Furthermore, 63 out of 69 studies (91.3%) inadequately reported model performance measures (Table 2), particularly the confusion matrix, resulting in a high risk of bias in this domain across all studies (Figs. 3A, 3B).

Predictive performance

The most commonly reported performance metrics were AUC, overall accuracy, sensitivity (recall), and specificity. PPV and NPV were reported more frequently in regression-based studies, whereas overall accuracy was more commonly reported in supervised ML-based studies (Fig. 5). All supervised ML-based CPMs demonstrated higher performance compared to regression-based CPMs, with SVM achieving the best overall performance, showing values above 0.90 across these metrics, followed by deep learning techniques and kNN (Table 3). Discussion

This review evaluated key methodological components—participants, predictors, outcomes, and analysis—as well as the applicability of 69 published studies that developed or validated CPMs for diagnosing asthma. Both modelling approaches exhibited an overall high risk of bias, primarily driven by a high risk in the analysis domain. Underreporting of key items was significantly more common in studies employing supervised ML-based CPMs compared to regression-based CPMs, and these studies also raised greater concerns regarding reproducibility and applicability in this field. The findings of this review highlight the need for greater transparency and improved methodological quality in the development and validation of future CPMs, both from researchers generating the models and from reviewers assessing them prior to publication.

Participant domain

An ideal participant recruitment process should involve prospective, consecutive recruitment (population analogue)—where individuals at risk for the outcome are consecutively enrolled—to reflect the natural prevalence of a specific location [91]. Eligible patients should include those undergoing diagnostic assessment, presenting with signs or symptoms suggestive of asthma—ranging from minimal to severe—to reflect real-world clinical populations. However, the complexity of advanced computational approaches required to detect associations between predictors and outcomes may lead model developers to be concerned about class imbalance [92]. This concern makes case–control designs—with a more balanced representation of cases and controls—appear more attractive and efficient than consecutive recruitment. Nevertheless, case–control designs distort the proportion of cases relative to the true disease prevalence and introduce spectrum bias. Spectrum bias arises when a study fails to include a diverse patient population representing the full spectrum of disease severity [93], potentially resulting in artificially inflated performance metrics for CPMs [94]. In this review, 52% of studies on supervised ML-based CPMs employed a case–control design, often using healthy volunteers as controls. Consequently, the generalisability of findings from these studies was limited, as healthy individuals rarely present to the hospital in routine clinical practice.

Reporting the data source and study setting is also essential, as it enables readers to understand the characteristics of the at-risk population and assess the model’s transportability [23]. This review found that approximately 20% of studies across both modelling approaches did not report this important information.

Predictor domain

Pulmonary function tests, the gold standard for asthma assessment, may not be readily available in certain settings or countries [1, 3, 4]. In such contexts, CPMs are primarily used as screening tools to improve clinical workflow by identifying individuals who require pulmonary function testing. Predictors such as demographic information, symptoms, clinical signs, and biomarkers are often readily available and can be consistently assessed across participants when the model is applied. However, a substantial proportion of studies—28% of supervised ML-based CPMs and 47% of regression-based CPMs—incorporated predictors such as pulmonary function parameters, bronchial hyperresponsiveness, and assessments of respiratory mechanics. As these parameters form part of the diagnostic gold standard, their inclusion may lead to inflated performance metrics for CPMs [95]. Furthermore, these predictors may not be available at the point of care when the model is intended for use. Although accessible in certain contexts, the utility of such CPMs remains limited, as clinicians may opt to proceed directly to the gold standard test instead. Simpler CPMs that incorporate readily available predictors [10–12, 96] may be more appealing to healthcare professionals than complex, high-performing models that lack applicability in real-world clinical settings.

Outcome domain

Deficiencies in reporting the definition of asthma diagnosis were widespread in studies on supervised ML-based CPMs, affecting approximately 50% of the total studies, whereas such issues were rarely observed in regression-based CPM studies. This lack of clarity significantly raised concerns regarding the models' internal validity, reproducibility, and generalizability. Five studies on regression-based CPMs [81–83, 85, 86] and eight studies on supervised ML-based CPMs [30, 31, 35, 45, 48, 54, 67, 72] employed suboptimal definitions of asthma, including reliance on medical notes, specialist opinions, or the use of bronchodilator medications. These suboptimal definitions introduced outcome misclassification, commonly referred to as "imperfect gold standard bias" [95]. The definition of asthma influences its prevalence within a population [96], and inherently affects the predictive performance and probability estimates of a model.

Analysis domain

The most commonly used supervised machine learning methods were deep learning techniques, SVM, RF, and DT. The amount of training data required to produce a stable model generally depends on the complexity of the model's internal architecture and the dimensionality of the predictors [97]. Simulation studies have demonstrated that modern modelling techniques, such as SVM and RF, may require up to 10 times more events to achieve stability [98]. However, the median EPV in studies on supervised ML-based CPMs was only 8.2, and some of these studies did not specify the number of predictors used.

Additionally, split-sampling was frequently employed as a method for internal validation, while complete case analysis—excluding individuals with missing data on any predictors or outcomes—was used in certain studies. This approach further reduced the effective sample size for these models [18]. Reporting on missing data and the methods used to address these issues is a critical component of developing or validating CPMs [23, 99]. While certain supervised ML-based methods, such as random forests with surrogate splits, have built-in capabilities for handling missing data [100], most studies did not clarify whether or how such techniques were applied. Notably, half of the included studies, particularly those using supervised ML, failed to report any details about missing data [99].

Predictive performance

Regression-based methods, particularly logistic regression, were frequently used as benchmark models in several studies to compare against the predictive performance of supervised ML-based models. Despite the high performance observed in most supervised ML-based CPMs, several significant methodological flaws were identified more frequently than in traditional regression-based CPMs. These included spectrum bias arising from case–control designs, incorporation of gold standard predictors into the model, and the use of suboptimal definitions of asthma. Such limitations contribute to unreliable outcomes and reduce the applicability of these models [18, 94, 95]. PPV and NPV were rarely reported in supervised ML-based studies, possibly due to the use of case–control designs, which limit the reliability of these metrics as the study population does not reflect the true disease prevalence.

Strengths and limitations

In this review, we focused on both modelling approaches (regression-based and supervised ML-based) specifically used in asthma diagnosis. We comprehensively appraised and compared studies utilizing each modelling approach across key aspects of methodology and applicability. Severe issues with general conduct and reporting were identified, particularly in studies on supervised ML-based CPMs. With the growing use of CPMs in this field, our findings reinforce the need for academic institutions and CPM developers to comply with reporting and quality assessment guidelines from the outset [23, 101]. In addition, journal editors are encouraged to request the reporting checklist of key methodological items outlined in these guidelines to enhance the quality and practical utility of future CPMs. These improvements contribute to downstream benefits, including enhanced reproducibility and external validation, thereby promoting broader adoption of models across diverse settings—for example, the Alvarado score for acute appendicitis [102] or the Ottawa ankle rule for ankle injuries [103]. Once a well-established CPM exists, there is no justification for developing a new one without clearly demonstrated added value.

The core structure and risk of bias of each study were extracted and assessed primarily based on the items and signaling questions from PROBAST [21]. The tool was originally developed for studies on regression-based CPMs. However, the criteria for rating the risk of bias were modified for assessing both model approaches. Although most studies were conducted after the publication of these guidelines, only two regression-based CPM studies referenced the TRIPOD reporting guidelines [46, 81]. There were notable differences in terminology across studies. For example, terms such as "testing" were used to describe internal validation, "model validation" referred to model tuning, and features were inconsistently labelled as "predictors" or "variables." Additionally, several data preprocessing methods used in studies on supervised ML-based CPMs were poorly described, making them difficult to interpret. As a result, our bias assessment for this step may have incorrectly categorised certain studies as low risk. Our search strategy was restricted to English-language literature, and inconsistencies in terminology may have contributed to the underreporting of specific items during data extraction from studies in other languages. However, as this review includes studies published between 2001 and 2024, we believe the findings remain relevant to the current state of research. In our review, exclusion based on language was applied during the full-text screening phase, resulting in the removal of only one study [104].

Conclusions

This study highlights that many asthma diagnostic CPM studies often overlooked critical aspects, including appropriate analytical methods, the practicality and availability of predictors, clear outcome definitions, and integration into clinical workflows. Supervised ML-based CPMs, in particular, were frequently associated with underreporting and challenges in model applicability. Adhering to TRIPOD+AI guideline could significantly improve the quality and transportability of these models, facilitating their practical implementation.
