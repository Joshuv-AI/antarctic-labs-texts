Scalp hair and serum iodine as potential biomarkers for iodine intake and their association with the thyroid function
Authors: Yozen Fuse, Yo Kunii, Nobu Tsukada, Yoshiya Ito, Yoshimasa Shishiba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractContextThe association of iodine nutritional status with thyroid function in iodine-replete countries is controversial and more sensitive biomarkers than urinary iodine concentration is required.ObjectiveTo evaluate the use of scalp hair and serum iodine concentration as measure of iodine status through their association with thyroid indicesMethodsA nationwide survey was conducted between 2016 and 2023. Iodine concentration in serum (SIC), hair (HIC), urine (UIC) and estimated 24-hour urinary iodine excretion (eUIE), dietary iodine intake (DII) as well as serum TSH, FT4, and FT3 concentrations, thyroid antibody (ThAb) was measured in Japanese adults.ResultsThe median UIC in 2771 adults was 295.0 μg/L and within the WHO's adequacy range of iodine intake. The high iodine intake assessed by UIC, eUIE and SIC was associated with higher TSH and lower thyroid hormone levels. Urinary iodine concentration, eUIE and DII were higher in the group with a high TSH level. The subjects with positive ThAbs had significantly higher SIC, DII, TSH and slightly lower FT4 levels. The median HIC correlated positively but weakly with SIC, eUIE and DII, while SIC correlated modestly with eUIE, UIC and weakly with DII. The 5th percentile of the SIC reference (49.9 μg/L) coincided with the threshold value of median UIC (<100 μg/L) for iodine deficiency by WHO.ConclusionHigher iodine intake results in changes of TSH and thyroid hormones within their referenced range. Serum and hair iodine concentrations might be feasible indicators for a population's iodine intake and further research is needed to validate their availability.

Iodine is both a micronutrient and an essential trace element in humans that is required for the synthesis of the thyroid hormones. Deficient as well as excessive iodine intake may result in thyroid dysfunction [1-3]. At the population level the association of iodine intakes with the prevalence of thyroid disorders appears to be U-shaped [4]. Iodine deficiency has many adverse effects throughout the human life cycle, still remains in all regions worldwide, and affects populations at all stages of economic development [1, 5]. In the past several decades, there has been considerable progress in improving iodine nutrition globally through universal salt iodization (USI) [6-8]. On the other hand, excess iodine intakes may occur at the population level from the consumption of an iodine-rich diet, eg, over-iodized salt, drinking water, animal milk, certain seaweeds, and dietary supplements. In some susceptible individuals, the exposure to iodine-rich substances results in adverse thyroidal effects [2, 3]; however, the correlation of iodine intake to the thyroid function and risk of thyroid disorders is not fully understood especially in long-standing iodine-sufficient populations like the Japanese, who have become adapted to a high iodine intake over the centuries [9].

The biomarkers of iodine status that are commonly used are urinary iodine concentrations (UIC), thyroglobulin (Tg), thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), goiter rate, or thyroid volume [1, 5, 8]. Of these biomarkers, the thresholds for iodine sufficiency or excess are established only for a population's median UIC and Tg in school-aged children (SAC) [10]. To define a population's iodine status, the World Health Organization (WHO) recommends using the median value of spot UIC in SAC aged 6-12 years as a representative sample because of the vulnerability to iodine deficiency and the accessibility to children in a school; however, spot UIC should not be used to evaluate individual iodine status [1, 7, 8]. Adequate iodine intake corresponds to the median UIC (mUIC) values in the range of 100-299 μg/L for children and 150-249 μg/L for pregnant women. The mUIC ≥ 300 μg/L and ≥ 500 μg/L values indicate excessive iodine intake in children and pregnant women, respectively; however, the term “excessive” means in excess of the amount needed to prevent and control iodine deficiency [1, 7]. In addition, UIC thresholds for the severity of iodine deficiency or excessive iodine intake have not been defined in adults [8].

It is still under debate which parameter is more reliable to assess a population's iodine status [3]. Iodine in hair and serum are other candidates for biomarker of iodine status. Although the mechanisms by which the trace elements are incorporated into hair are not fully understood, endogenous and environmental chemicals are accumulated into hair during hair growth. Studies on hair iodine concentration (HIC) or serum iodine concentration (SIC) and their relationship with thyroid function or iodine nutritional status are limited. The purposes of the present study are firstly, to assess the iodine nutritional status by measuring scalp HIC, SIC, and UIC as well as dietary iodine intake (DII) in Japanese adults except pregnant and lactating women, secondary, to characterize their relationship to thyroid function, ie, serum TSH, free thyroxine (FT4), and free triiodothyronine (FT3) as well as thyroid antibody (ThAb) positivity and thyroid diseases, thirdly, if possible, to determine safety iodine intake thresholds for thyroid dysfunction and lastly, to evaluate the hair and serum iodine concentrations as a feasible biomarker of the iodine status in adults.

Materials and methods

Subjects

Between 2016 and 2023, the survey was conducted in 7 geographically separated areas across Japan, from Rishiri island, Hokkaido to Tanegashima island, Kyushu. Survey areas were selected on the basis of their iodine status evaluated by mUIC in our national study [11], namely, 2 areas with high iodine intake and 5 other areas with adequate iodine status. Apparently healthy adults except pregnant and lactating women without previous or present history of thyroid disease by self-report were recruited. The participants who reported taking an iodine-rich supplements or medication such as amiodarone and povidone-iodine mouthwashes and those after use of an iodinated contrast agent or using hair cosmetics containing Kombu extracts were excluded. In each region, the subjects were selected according to a single-stage cluster sampling design with at least 25 subjects per cluster from 20 regions.

Methods

A questionnaire about the sociodemographic variables and anthropometric information were self-reported by the individual participants. Habitual DII was evaluated by a semi-quantitative iodine-specific food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) for iodine developed and validated by us [12, 13] and given in μg iodine per day. The daily iodine intake from each food consumed over the past 1 month was calculated based on frequency of consumption and iodine content of the specific food using the Japanese Food Composition Table 2020 [14] or other database when insufficient information was available.

Sample preparation

The participants were provided with iodine-free polypropylene cups and test tubes. The casual urine samples were obtained by themselves at home at any time of the day or on the day of the survey, simultaneously with blood samples, and stored at −30 °C until they were analyzed for iodine and creatinine concentrations. Venous blood and scalp hair samples were collected by nurses. Otherwise, the participants were requested to collect approximately 1.0 g of scalp hair samples in their home according to the illustrated instructions on the standardized hair collection method in which the hair was cut from the occipital head region above the external occipital protuberance at 1 cm from the scalp as a hair lock [15, 16] and a sample cut from the posterior vertex region of the head. Close to the scalp is preferred as this region of the scalp is associated with the least variation in growth rates [15]. Scalp hair samples were not available in 4 of the 20 regions, ie, Fukushima, Tokyo, Fukui, and Tanegashima Island. Not all participants were able to provide all of the urine, blood, and hair samples.

Analytical methods

The serum TSH, FT4, and FT3 concentrations were determined by electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLIA) using ECLusys™ TSH (BRID: AB_3095311, https://www.antibodyregistry.org/AB_3095311), FT4 (BRID: AB_2924686, https://www.antibodyregistry.org/AB_2924686), and FT3 (BRID: AB_2925177, https://www.antibodyregistry.org/AB_2925177), Roche Diagnostics, K.K. Tokyo, Japan. The reference ranges for Japanese used in this study were 0.2-4.5 μIU/mL for TSH, 0.8-1.6 ng/dL for FT4 and 2.2-4.3 pg/mL for FT3. They were defined by Ito Hospital, Tokyo, Japan in 2002, using 128 healthy adults, 26 males and 102 females, aged from 20 to 60 years old without goiter and with negative results for both TgAb and TPOAb (Personal communication with Dr Hiroshi Yoshimura in Ito Hospital, Tokyo). Three serum ThAbs were measured by ECLIA using ECLusys Anti-TPO™ (BRID: AB_2916057, https://www.antibodyregistry.org/AB_2916057), ECLusys Anti-Tg™ (BRID: AB_2894922, https://www.antibodyregistry.org/AB_2894922), and ECLusys TRAb™ (BRID: AB_2801453, https://www.antibodyregistry.org/AB_2801453), Roche Diagnostics, K.K. Tokyo, Japan. The cutoff values were 2 IU/L for TRAb, 30 IU/mL for TPOAb by the manufacturer and 40 IU/mL for TgAb [17]. The ThAb values above the cutoff level were considered positive.

The scalp hair, serum, and casual urine samples stored in refrigerated cubicles at −40 °C were analyzed for iodine content by using inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry (ICP-MS), iCAP Q with CASX260, Thermo Fisher Scientific K.K., Tokyo at the Central Research Laboratory, Tsukuba, Kotobiken Medical Laboratories Inc. For quality control of urinary iodine measurement this laboratory has been enrolled in the Ensuring the Quality of Urinary Iodine Procedures program of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), Atlanta, USA [18]. All frozen samples were thawed spontaneously at room temperature. One hundred μL of serum and 50 μL of internal standard solution were diluted into 5 mL of 0.5% tetramethyl ammonium hydroxide solution (TMAH) and directly analyzed for iodine by ICP-MS. The limit of detection (LOD) and background equivalent concentration (BEC) of this assay were 0.002 and 0.061 ppb, respectively. The intra- and inter- assay coefficient of variation (CV) were 5.3-6.0% and 9.1-12.3%, respectively. Urine samples were wet digested with TMAH and measured for iodine by ICP-MS as described previously [11].

The urinary creatinine (Cr) concentration was measured by colorimetric enzymatic assay and the urinary iodine was expressed as a concentration in μg iodine per 1000 mL of urine (UIC, μg/L) or relative to creatinine excretion (UI/Cr ratio, UI/Cr, μg/gCr). World Health Organization guidelines for IDD status use “μg/L” for cutoff points of UIC in IDD surveillance, and 1μg/L = 0.00788 nmol/L for conversion to S.I. units. Since the 24-hour urinary iodine excretion (UIE), adjusted for age and gender, is regarded as an indicator for the iodine status of individuals [19-21] we used the estimated 24-hour UIE (eUIE) by performing calculations based on measurement of iodine and creatinine concentrations in spot urine and using predicted anthropometry-based 24-hour creatinine reference values for healthy adults. The equation used for Japanese adults is as follows: Estimated Cr excretion (mg/24 hours) = height (cm) × 7.39 + weight (kg) × 15.12−age (year) × 12.63-79.90 for males; height (cm) × 5.09 + weight (kg) × 8.58−age (year) × 4.72-74.50 for females [22].

Each proximal 3 cm of individual scalp hair sample which may contain iodine accumulated during the last 2 to 4 months was cut into 1-cm segments. Approximately 100 mg of hair sample was weighed using a precision balance, transferred to polypropylene tubes (DigiTUBE®, GL Sciences, Tokyo, Japan), stirred in 10 mL of ethyl ether/acetone solution (3:1ww) for 10 minutes, rinsed twice with pure water and dried at 85 °C for 1 hour. They were digested with 2 mL of 25% TMAH solution in a 15 mL DigiTUBE® at 90 °C for 1 hour, then by adding pure water up to 5 mL and maintaining the solution at 90 °C for another 2 hours, a clear homogenous, particulate-free liquid medium is produced yielding digested hair in 10% TMAH solution. After cooling to room temperature this solution is diluted 10 times and the iodine concentration is measured by using ICP-MS. The LOD and BEC of this assay were 0.003 and 0.082 ppb, respectively. The intra- and inter-assay CVs were 3.9-6.9% and 12.0-13.8%, respectively. Hair iodine concentration was expressed as ppm (1ppm = 1μg/g).

The reagents used were: diethyl ether, acetone, and TMAH of JIS (Japanese Industrial Standards) special grade, Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Ltd, Japan; certified reference material for iodine, Iodide standard for IC, potassium iodide (pure material) in high purity water, 997 ± 5 mg/L iodide, Sigma-Aldrich Production GmbH, Switzerland; calibration standard for ICP-MS as internal standard solution, tellurium (Te), 1000 μg/mL and indium (In), 1000 μg/mL, SCP Science, Canada; certified reference materials for quality control, QM-S-Q1817 (iodine: 53.5 ppb), QM-S-Q1908 (iodine: 105.4 ppb), QM-S-Q1825 (iodine: 54.5 ppb), QM-S-Q2007 (iodine: 93.6 ppb), and QM-S-Q2008 (iodine: 135 ppb) for serum and QM-H-Q1610 (iodine: 4.50 ppb), QM-H-Q1616 (iodine: 9.66 ppb), and QM-H-Q1707 (iodine: 10.90 ppb) for hair, Centre de toxicologie/INSPQ, Canada.

Statistics

The results were presented as mean, SD, median, range, or interquartile range (IQR). Serum TSH, FT4, and FT3 concentrations and the iodine concentrations in urine, serum, and hair were not distributed normally and their logarithmically transformed values were therefore used to normalize the distribution. The normality of the transformed data was tested using the Anderson-Darling test, the D'Agostino and Pearson test, the Shapiro–Wilk test, and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. The differences between paired data or groups were examined using the Kruskal–Wallis test or Dunn's multiple comparison test. The differences between 2 unmatched groups for normally and non-normally distributed data were tested using the unpaired t test and Mann–Whitney test, respectively. The significance of differences in prevalence between the groups was determined using Fisher's exact test. Simple linear regression analysis was used to test for correlations between thyroid parameters, serum, urinary, and hair iodine concentrations. A P-value <.05 was considered significant. Data processing and statistical analysis were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 from GraphPad Software Inc. (San Diego, CA, USA).

This study was conducted according to the guidelines laid down in the Declaration of Helsinki. Approval for the research plan was obtained from the ethics committees of the Japanese Red Cross Hokkaido College of Nursing (approval numbers and date: No. 170 on December 19, 2013). Written informed consent was obtained from each of the participants.

Results

Characteristics of the subjects

A total of 2771 subjects aged 18 to 90 years old, 1517 males and 1254 females, were included in the study. The mean (SD) of age, height, weight, and BMI were 45.8 (15.8) years, 163.6 (8.8) cm, 62.7 (13.2) kg, and 23.3 (3.9) kg/m2, respectively. The number of the subjects included in each of the 20 regional divisions was from 25 to 395 with the mean of 138.

Iodine nutritional status, thyroid function, and thyroid diseases

The overall mUIC was 295.0 μg/L and within the WHO's adequacy range (defined by mUIC between 100 and 299 μg/L) for adults and children. Prevalence of the subjects with UIC less than 50 μg/L was 2.3%. The mUIC in each region was highly variable ranging from 169.0 μg/L in Tanegashima island to 943.0 μg/L in Fukui city, Hokuriku region. The number of samples and the median values of UI/Cr, eUIE, DII, HIC, SIC, serum TSH, FT4, and FT3 were presented in Table 1. The overall frequency of ThAb positivity, defined as positivity of 1 or more of the 3 types of ThAbs, was 12.6% of 2603 subjects, and positive TgAb, TPOAb, or TRAb were detected in 9.6%, 7.0%, or 1.2% of total subjects, respectively. The prevalence of thyroid diseases in 2771 subjects was: 0.79% for thyrotoxicosis including Graves' disease (0.43%); 0.22% for overt hypothyroidism; 5.02% for subclinical hypothyroidism, and 0.69% for Hashimoto's thyroiditis (data not shown).

Hair iodine concentration

Scalp hair samples were provided from 926 subjects; however, the amount of hair samples was highly variable and only 778 samples were large enough for iodine measurement since most of participants cut their own hair samples at home by themselves. Hair iodine concentration was not normally distributed, skewed to the left and exhibited a wide range of 0.04-129.5 ppm with the median value of 0.865 ppm. The median HIC for males was significantly higher than for females (1.36 vs 0.64 ppm), especially for those under 30 and 50-69 years of age. The median HIC values appear to increase with age until before age 70 in both genders; however, there was no significant difference in HIC values in females aged 30 to 70 and males aged 40 to 70 years old. The median HIC in females less than 30 years old was lower than those of other age groups, while HIC in males less than 39 years old was lower than those aged 50-59 or 60-69 years old (Fig. 1, Table S1 [23]).

Serum iodine concentration

The median and observed range of SIC were 74.4 and 17.3 to1128 μg/L, respectively. The median SIC value gradually increased from 67.3 to 105.0 μg/L with advancing age. Serum iodine concentration was significantly but slightly higher in males than females and increased with age; however, the differences of gender-specific age-related SIC change were very small (mean:3.3 μg/L), In the male subjects of 40-49 and 60-69-years old the median SIC was slightly higher than that in female subjects (78.3 vs 72.3 and 92.9 vs 85.4 μg/L, respectively) (Table S4 [26]). Serum iodine concentration correlated modestly with UIC (Spearman r = 0.4742, n = 1872, P < .0001) (Fig. 5A) and eUIE (Spearman r = 0.5482, n = 1868, P < .0001) (Fig. 5B), weakly with HIC (Spearman r = 0.3470, n = 458, P < .0001), age (Spearman r = 0.267, n = 2134, P < .0001), and DII (Spearman r = 0.2659, n = 1743, P < .0001) (data not shown). On a regression curve of SIC against UIC (UIC = 18.92*SIC-856.2), 100 μg/L of UIC that is defined as the threshold value for iodine deficiency in population by WHO corresponded to 50.5 μg/L of SIC that was almost same as the lower reference limit of SIC (49.9 μg/L) (Fig. 5A). The upper threshold value for iodine excess (300 μg/L) corresponded to 61.1 μg/L of SIC. In contrast, the upper limit of SIC reference range (194 μg/L) corresponded to 2814 μg/L of UIC.

Correlation among the biomarkers for iodine status and their association with thyroid function

The correlation matrix among UIC, eUIE, DII, SIC, and HIC revealed that UIC does not correlate with HIC but moderately correlated with SIC. Urinary iodine concentration strongly correlates with eUIE, and eUIE was more strongly related to SIC, HIC, and DII than UIC. Dietary iodine intake weakly correlates with UIC, SIC, and HIC to a similar degree. (Table 3). The correlations of HIC or SIC with other variables have already been described in detail.

Association of iodine status with thyroid function and thyroid antibody positivity

Compared with the negative antibody group, the subjects in the positive antibody group had significantly higher median values of SIC (79.9 vs 73.8 μg/L, P = .0008), DII (570.0 vs 454.5 μg/day, P = .0153), TSH (1.86 vs 1.59 μIU/mL, P = .0001), and slightly lower FT4 (1.24 vs 1.26 ng/dL, P = .0268) (Table S7 [29]); however, there was no significant difference of the prevalence of ThAb positivity among 3 groups divided by the reference range of UIC, eUIE, DII, SIC, and HIC (Table S8 [30]). The association of ThAb positivity with thyroid function and iodine status was assessed by defining “isolated positivity” as a positive for only 1 of the 3 thyroid antibodies, “double positivity” as a positive TPOAb and TgAb, and “triple positivity” as a positive TPOAb, TgAb, and TRAb resulting in 5 subgroups. In the subjects with triple positivity the median TSH value was lowest (0.85 μIU/mL) and the FT4 and FT3 values were highest (1.6 ng/dL for FT4 and 4.21 pg/mL for FT3), while the median TSH value was highest (2.38 μIU/mL), and FT3 was lower (3.04 pg/mL) in the subjects with double positivity than in the other groups. There were no significant differences of median UIC, eUIE, DII, and SIC among the 5 groups although the median HIC in subjects with isolated positive TPOAb was higher than that in the subjects with triple positivity (1.26 vs 0.17 ppm, P = .0436) (Table S9 [31]).

Discussion

This nationwide survey confirmed adequate iodine intake in Japanese adults with geographically heterogeneous distribution that was consistent with the results of our previous survey in schoolchildren [11]. The mUIC value of 295 μg/L was higher than that in SAC aged 6 to 12 years (269 μg/L) [8] and close to the upper threshold value of UIC in children (300 μg/L) to prevent and control iodine deficiency. According to the Global Scorecard 2023 on iodine nutrition in the general population by the iodine global network [32], mUIC of Japan is ranked at 5th from the highest among 135 countries with the median UIC level between 100 and 299 μg/L. On the other hand, there were 13 countries with mUIC ≥ 300 μg/L in the world. The regional difference in mUIC observed in this adult study was similar to the pattern in children determined by our previous nationwide survey as an overall trend [11]. The iodine intake of a population is closely related to traditional local food and lifestyle habits. In Japan rice is the staple food, and the major source of iodine is edible brown seaweed (kelp) that is served in a large variety of ways under several different names, eg, Kombu (Laminariaceae), Hijiki, and Wakame [33]. Iodine intake in the Japanese population has decreased compared with values reported prior to the 1990s [9]. In recent years, the catch as well as consumption of Kombu has declined sharply nationwide and at the same time, the traditional Japanese diet is being replaced by a Westernized diet followed by a decreasing iodine intake [34]. Alternatively, processed foods containing iodine extract are often used in the current Japanese diet, and together with Kombu, may still play a major role in iodine nutrition. The exact reason for the higher iodine intake observed in the Japanese population is not fully understood; however, high iodine intake might be preventing iodine deficiency in Japanese population without iodized salt and further investigation is necessary.

Scalp HIC

Hair is a robust and stable tissue that is less susceptible to contamination. Compared with conventional samples, such as blood or urine, it offers the advantage of confirming long-term exposure to drugs, metals, and micronutrients over periods ranging from several weeks to several months, depending on the length of the collected hair. The scalp hair grows at an average rate of 1 cm each month [35] and endogenous and environmental chemicals are accumulated into hair during growth. As a result, a hair sample can provide a metabolite profile that reflects exposure over months. Several studies have previously utilized human hair as a stable specimen for identifying robust biomarkers of various disorders. Five sources of trace elements incorporated into human hair in scalp are the matrix (the lower soft part of the hair root which encloses some blood vessels), sebum, sweat, the epidermis, and exogenous sources by environmental exposure [36]. In addition, the elements not only can enter the cortex via the matrix but also via the hair root sheaths which enclose the matrix [37]. The mechanism of iodine uptake from the blood, chemical form of iodine stored in hair and their distribution has not been fully elucidated. The distribution of an endogenous element is not always homogeneously spread over a cross-section of the hair [37]. Recently, highly homogeneously distributed iodine longitudinally along the hair strands and also across the cross-section of hair from the outer surface layer to the core was observed by using laser ablation ICP-MS which can provide spatial distribution of trace elements in hair [38].

Limited studies on hair iodine have been reported from iodine-replete areas [32] including Japan (mUIC: 269 μg/L) [39], Croatia (mUIC: 248 μg/L) [16, 40], Morocco (mUIC: 117 μg/L) [41], and Kazakhstan (mUIC: 183 μg/L) [42], and from iodine-deficient areas (mUIC <100 μg/L) [32], Arkhangelsk, Russia [43], and the central European part of Russia [44]. In healthy children the median HIC (range) values were 0.98 (0.45-1.64) ppm [41] or 1.98 (1.044-2.764) [42], while in children with goiter they were lower than those of healthy children: 0.12 (0.04-0.90) ppm [41], 1.191 (0.884-1.673) ppm [42], or 0.15 ppm [43]. There was a significant negative relation of thyroid volume with HIC [41-43]. In healthy Croatian adults (n = 870) the median HIC was 0.5 ppm with a wide range of 0.01 to 114 ppm without gender difference, and the range between 0.57 and 0.74 ppm was proposed as adequate iodine status [16]. In another Croatian study with 137 adults there was neither gender dependent difference of HIC nor significant correlation between HIC and whole blood iodine concentration [40]. In the central European part of Russia, the mean ± SEM of HIC measured in adults by ICP-AES (atomic emission spectrometry) was 6.93 ± 1.05 ppm (range: 0.29-37.9 ppm) without age-related change and higher in males than females (11.8 vs 3.76 ppm, n = 80) [40]. In 1551 Japanese male adults aged 20-60 years residing in the Tokyo metropolitan distinct the mean HIC increased from 0.377 to 0.644 ppm with advanced age [39].

In our results the median HIC value was 0.865 ppm and higher than those of some other reports in adults [16, 39, 40], and its wide range of variation, gender difference, and age-related change were consistent with those of previous reports [39, 44]. Hair iodine concentration correlated positively but weakly with SIC, eUIE, and DII in both genders suggesting that hair iodine appears to be a possible biological indicator for assessing long-term iodine status.

There are still several methodological problems related with hair analysis, namely, sample collection, pretreatment of hair strand, and iodine assay methods. The pretreatment methods include cleaning, digestion with alkali solution or microwave-induced combustion. Although a variety of washing procedures to remove external contamination by cosmetic treatments (permanent waving, keratin smoothing, and bleaching) from hair strands have been proposed, but as yet no standardized procedures are available. Further works are required to resolve these problems in order to validate the cutoff values for iodine sufficiency or deficiency.

Serum iodine concentration

Although research on the use of serum or whole blood iodine as an iodine status biomarker is limited, several studies have been reported in healthy adults from China [45-48], France [49, 50], the USA [51], and Australia [52]. According to the large epidemiological surveys in Chinese populations, the median SIC, and mUIC values in adults were 57.3 and 123.6 µg/L (n = 894) [35], 58.3 and 137.9 µg/L (n = 1499) [46], 70.6 and 155.8 μg/L (n = 1320) [48], and 78.0 and 181.6 µg/L (n = 902) [45], respectively. In a French study on a relatively small number of adult subjects, the mean (SD) value of plasma iodine was 58 [11] μg/L with the mean 24-hour UI of 94 μg and was higher in women than in men [49]. In a Australia study, the mean (range) of whole blood iodine concentrations was 32.5 (20.1-82.7) μg/L in males, and 28.5 (19.5-44.6 μg/L) in females without difference by gender or age [52].

The factors possibly associated with SIC were reported to be gender [50], residential area [47], or regional altitude [46] and seafood intake [47], whereas SIC was not associated with age, gender [46, 47, 52], or exercise status [46] nor with BMI, blood pressure, iodized salt intake, and smoking [47]. Serum iodine concentration positively correlated with UIC in adults [46, 48, 51], and when UIC increased through salt fortification or increased iodine in the water supply, serum iodine remained more stable in comparison [45, 46]. A 3-day titration diet study using iodized salt has been reported from the United States by measuring 24 hours UIC, serum iodine, and Tg concentrations in 10 adults aged 19-56 years. The 24-hour UIC increased as iodized salt consumption increased, while SIC elevated from the mean of 62.1 to 66.8 µg/L when 3 times the recommended daily allowance of iodine in the United States was consumed during a 10-day period, indicating that serum iodine significantly increased only when iodine intake was excessive. The authors concluded that serum iodine may be more indicative of long-term iodine status rather than recent iodine intake and that more long-term studies such as over weeks or months instead of days may be needed to evaluate long-term SIC changes [51].

In this study the median SIC in Japanese adults was 74.4 μg/L, consistent with those reported in Chinese adults (57.3-78.0 μg/L) [45-48], although the iodine intake was higher than those in China (mUIC: 295.0 vs 123.6-181.6 µg/L). Serum iodine concentration increased with age and was significantly but slightly higher in males than females; however, the differences of age-related gender-specific SIC were small (6-7 μg/L). Serum iodine concentration correlated modestly with eUIE and UIC, and weakly with HIC. These results of previous reports including our present study suggest that serum iodine appears to most closely represent the bioavailable iodine for the thyroid gland and may be reflective of individual iodine status and serve as biomarkers of iodine status.

An association of SIC with thyroid function and thyroid disease has been reported in adults [46-51]. Serum iodine concentration positively correlated with serum FT4, total T4, and total T3 [47], while correlation of SIC with serum TSH was conflicting between 2 studies [46, 47]. Higher SIC was associated with hyperthyroidism [46-48] and overt hypothyroidism in males [48], while lower SIC was associated with hypothyroidism [46], exclusively in females [48]. In an early report on association of plasma iodine with a variety of thyroid disease, low iodine level (<40 μg/L) was associated with hypothyroidism, 80-250 μg/L of iodine with hyperthyroidism, and iodine > 250 μg/L indicated iodine overload [50].

The reference ranges of SIC were reported to be 48.6-122.0 µg/L (5-95%ile) in euthyroid adults [45] or 36.0-79.3 µg/L by using the latent abnormal values exclusion method in the Chinese study [47]. In our study the 95% reference range of SIC in 1708 euthyroid subjects with negative ThAbs was 49.9-194 μg/L with wider range than that in Chinese. Interestingly, the lower limit of the SIC value coincided with the threshold value of mUIC for iodine deficiency by WHO (<100 μg/L) suggesting serum iodine may be useful in the diagnosis of iodine deficiency. Further research on association of SIC with thyroid dysfunction is needed.

Association between iodine status and thyroid function

In the present study the relationship of biomarkers of iodine intake with thyroid function and thyroid antibody positivity was evaluated from both sides by dividing the participants into separate groups according to the reference ranges of their indices. High iodine intake assessed by UIC and eUIE was associated with higher serum TSH and lower FT4 and FT3 values, on the other hand, UIC, eUIE and DII values were higher in the group with a high TSH level. The subjects with SIC values above the 95%ile had higher TSH with lower thyroid hormone levels, inversely, in the subjects with TSH < 0.2 μIU/mL or FT4 > 1.6 ng/dL SIC values were higher than those in other groups. At present we have no additional data to explain this paradoxical relation between serum iodine level and TSH.

Serum TSH is the most sensitive parameter indicating early changes in thyroid function and well reflects the iodine status exclusively in newborn infants; however, TSH levels are relatively insensitive to iodine nutritional status in adults [5]. In a previously iodine-insufficient population, despite the adequate iodine status at present through a USI program, significant changes of serum TSH level and thyroid size have been observed [53-55]. A shift of TSH reference range toward the right during a period of 10 years has been reported in Germany [54]. A large epidemiological study conducted on the Chinese population indicated an increase in UIC significantly associated with a TSH increase in the 50 and 97.5%ile and a decrease in the 2.5%ile [55]. It is not certain that these observations may apply to the populations in long-standing iodine-replete areas without iodine fortification like Japan. In a study in Greenland, the prevalence of thyroid dysfunction and UIC was evaluated in 2 areas of Greenland with different dietary habits and different levels of iodine intake [56]. The traditional Inuit diet is based on iodine-rich marine foods [57]. The median eUIE in East Greenland Inuit, West Greenland Inuit and non-Inuit were 225, 169, and 58 μg/24-hour, respectively. A difference in serum TSH and FT4 concentrations was not seen among the 3 participant groups. The pattern of hyper- and hypothyroidism in Greenlandic Inuit with adequate iodine intake was comparable with that observed in populations with iodine deficiency. In addition, more than 80% of the subjects with thyroid dysfunction did not have thyroid disease. The authors speculated that the iodine-rich Arctic diet has provided Inuit with a long-term excessive iodine intake for centuries causing an adaptation to high iodine intakes and a need for a higher iodine intake to prevent iodine deficiency disorders. Although major iodine source is different the similarities between Japanese and Greenlandic Inuit with regard to iodine nutrition and thyroid function require further investigation.

The possible reasons for the differences in an association of iodine status with thyroid function and ThAb positivity between our results and those of others might be attributed to previous and present iodine intake, dietary habits, iodine fortification and ethnicity. Japan is a long-standing iodine-replete country and iodine intake in Japanese population has been higher than other countries without iodine fortification. In this study we evaluated iodine nutritional status with iodine concentration in urine, serum and hair samples and diet study although their usefulness for iodine intake assessment is not yet fully validated except UIC and diet study, while almost all studies assessed population's iodine nutritional status with UIC. In addition, iodine nutrition might be depending on differences in the tolerance to high iodine intakes, genetic background or environmental factors other than iodine. Future studies should employ a longitudinal design and systematically collect serum or hair iodine samples as well as dietary iodine data to better elucidate the dynamic interplay between changes in iodine intake and thyroid function in varying iodine nutritional states.

Association of iodine status with thyroid antibody positivity

In this study higher iodine intake assessed by SIC and DII with higher TSH and lower FT4 were observed in positive ThAb subjects; however, there was no significant difference in the prevalence of ThAb according to the change of UIC, eUIE, SIC, HIC, and DII. Our results were consistent partially with previous reports which results were conflicting. Serum iodine concentration was higher in TgAb positive subjects than in TPOAb positive subjects [46]; however, in another study lower SIC was associated with positive TgAb and TPOAb in females [47]. On the other hand, there was no significant difference of serum iodine level in subjects with or without late latent autoimmune thyroiditis [47]. A U-shaped relationship between iodine status by UIC and the prevalence of ThAb positivity has been reported in Chinese adults [58]. In another large Chinese epidemiological study, the prevalence of ThAb positivity has remained low after 2 decades of USI, and the prevalence of positive TPOAb decreased significantly with the increase in iodine intake, and suggesting an adequate iodine nutritional status (UIC: 100-299 μg/L) is safe for thyroid autoimmunity, mostly in areas of previously mild iodine deficiency [59]. It is well recognized the benefits of iodine nutrition optimization although increased iodine intake in iodine-deficient populations is associated with transient increase in the prevalence of thyroid autoimmunity and a slight increase in the prevalence of hypothyroidism, mostly subclinical and in young women [60].

Thresholds value of iodine intake indices for thyroid dysfunction or disease

In this study there was no clear dose-response relationship of iodine status indices with thyroid function. The median TSH, FT4, and FT3 values in the subjects >95%ile of UIC, SIC, HIC, and DII fluctuated within their reference ranges.

As far as we know, to define the threshold value of DII for thyroid dysfunction or disease, a total of 19 interventional studies has been reported between 1958 and 2012 from the United States, Japan, and other countries [61]. In 3 of 5 Japanese studies [62-64], “Tororo Kelp” containing 10-70 mg of iodine or 27 mg of lecithin-bound iodine was orally administered to healthy adults for 1-87 days. The serum TSH levels transiently exceeded the upper limit in 9 of 23 subjects in 1 study [64] and in another study transient changes in serum TSH, FT, FT3, T4, and T3 concentrations were observed within the normal range [62]. Lecithin-bound iodine administered for 28 days resulted in a transient 13.6% increase of thyroid volume [63]. In the reports from countries other than Japan, the number of subjects was relatively small (4-36 subjects), iodine loading sources included kelp supplements, erythrosine, water purification agents, inorganic iodine and iodide, and daily iodine loading doses varied from 0.1 to 1000 mg and exposure time ranged from once to 90 days. Increases in TSH concentration and decreases in thyroid hormone concentration have been seen with a minimum of 0.5 mg of iodine or less once or a maximum of 1000 mg/day for 11 weeks continuously; however, all of these changes were within normal limits and reversible, and no clear dose-response relationship was observed. In the latest report from China [65], 256 university students were loaded with 12 different doses of iodine (100-2000 μg/day) for 4 weeks. The mUIC increased from 439 to 2295 μg/L at 4 weeks; however, the changes in serum TSH, FT4, and FT3 were within their normal range. Thyroid volume became smaller (12.2-32.4%) in the group with higher iodine intake (>1.5 mg/day). Iodine intake above 500-2000 μg/day increased the percentage of TSH > 5 mIU/L. The authors recommended 800 μg/day of iodine as the safe UL for daily intake in the adult Chinese population. The population in this study basically consumes excessive iodine (mean: 351 μg/day) from the diet (mean: 93-117 μg/day) with iodized salt (258 μg/day), and their mUIC were 287-329 μg/L without iodine loading. The difference of study population between this report and our study is in iodine fortification although the mUIC value was similar. Therefore, this result in Chinese population is unlikely to be applicable to other populations with different iodine intake. It is difficult to define a safe zone or threshold of iodine intake from previous studies and the present our study especially in iodine-replete population that have never experienced iodine deficiency.

Correlation among the indices for iodine status by biomarkers or diet study

Urinary iodine excretion is a good marker of very recent DII, that is, for several hours or days; however, UIC in casual urine sample is highly variable in individuals. food frequency questionnaires are useful for gathering data on habitual intake of foods relevant to the whole diet or specific micronutrient. Dietary iodine intake assessed by FFQ evaluates iodine intake from foods consumed over the past 1 month or more. Several studies on the association between DII by FFQ and UIC were reported in adults [66-69] or children [70] using single spot urine samples [67, 68] or 24-hour urine samples [66, 69, 70] and the results were conflicting.

FFQ was useful to classify subjects into low and high iodine intake groups, but the level of iodine intake by the FFQ tends to be overestimated than 24-hour UIE [66]. Dietary iodine intake in some foods correlated with UIC in spot urine sample [67]. Dietary iodine intake by FFQ was not associated with 3-day UIC in spot sample (Spearman r = 0.094), but correlated with the UIC/creatinine ratio (Spearman r = 0.265) [68]. In contrast, FFQ was moderately correlated with UIC in μg/L (r = 0.34) but not with UIE in μg/day by two 24-hour urine collection [69]. Iodine intake by FFQ was significantly correlated with the habitual daily iodine intake, as calculated from 2-repeated 24-hour UIE [70]. Discrepancy between these study results might be due to the difference of FFQ, that is, general FFQ or iodine-specific FFQ and/or urine collection methods.

In the present study, according to Spearman correlational and regression analysis among indices for iodine status, DII weakly correlates with UIC, SIC, and HIC to a similar degree. Urinary iodine concentration moderately correlated with SIC (Spearman r = 0.4742) and strongly with eUIE (r = 0.8277), this is not a surprise since eUIE was derived from UIC by estimated 24-hour creatinine excretion. Therefore, eUIE was more strongly related to SIC (r = 0.5432), HIC(r = 0.2463) and DII (r = 0.3458) than UIC. There are no reports on association of DII by FFQ with other indices for iodine status than UIC. Further modification and improvement for accurate results of iodine-specific FFQ are necessary.

Strength and limitations

The strengths of our study are as follows. First, we assessed iodine nutritional status in an iodine-replete adult population using scalp hair, serum, and urine samples. Second, the association of iodine status with thyroid function was thoroughly evaluated from both sides. One was the effect of iodine status on serum TSH, thyroid hormones, prevalence of ThAb positivity, and thyroid dysfunction. Another was the change of indices of iodine status according to the reference range of TSH and thyroid hormone concentrations. Third, previous studies on HIC have not fully assessed the relationship between hair iodine and other parameters for iodine nutrition as well as thyroid function. Fourth, the biochemical analyses for iodine concentration in urine, serum, and hair were all performed in 1 highly standardized laboratory with good quality control and uniform procedures during the study period. The limitations are: First, we used a single spot urine sample to evaluate iodine status instead of 24-hour urine or repeated spot urine samples limiting further interpretation of the association with other indices of iodine status due to its high variability. Second, we did not measure serum Tg concentration. Serum Tg is elevated in iodine-deficient areas due to TSH hyperstimulation and thyroid hyperplasia and showed a clear U-shaped curve against iodine intakes. Tg is a useful biological indicator for monitoring iodine status and thyroid function [10]. Third, the number of participants and the male to female ratio was highly variable in each region. Some of the participants did not provide all of the urine, blood, and scalp hair samples requested. We assumed missing data to be missing at random; however, we did not perform our analysis using single imputation, multiple imputation or generalized linear mixed model. Thus, potential bias caused by missing data and gender difference cannot be ruled out. Fourth, we observed an association between iodine nutritional indices and thyroid function; however, the cross-sectional study design limited the causal inference.

In conclusion, this study established the reference range of serum iodine and hair iodine concentrations, and they might be potential biomarkers for iodine intake. Further research is needed to validate their availability through prospective cohort studies. In healthy adults residing an iodine-sufficient area high iodine intake was a possible cause of higher TSH and lower thyroid hormone within their reference ranges; however, it was difficult to define the threshold value of iodine nutritional indices for thyroid dysfunction in this study. Future research priorities in iodine nutrition should include the incidence and prevalence of thyroid disorders associated with iodine intake assessed by using more robust indices at both the population and individual levels.
