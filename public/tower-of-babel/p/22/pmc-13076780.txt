Human stem cell-derived neurons establish functional inhibitory–excitatory cortical circuits in a chimeric transplantation model
Authors: Cameron P J Hunt, Kimberly R Thek, Jennifer Durnall, Stuart J McDougall, Clare L Parish, Carlos W Gantner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExcitation/inhibition balance is crucial to homeostatic brain function and disrupted in several neurodevelopmental and neurodegenerative diseases. Cortical inhibitory neurons derived from the medial ganglionic eminence (MGE) provide input to glutamatergic projection neurons, an essential function in neural processing. While neural grafts for stroke improve motor function following engraftment, these grafts typically contain only excitatory neurons (and glia), and little effort has been directed at understanding the role of inhibitory neurons in this context. Here, we generate a channelrhodopsin line to optogenetically control grafted human pluripotent stem cell-derived MGE interneurons in the context of a mixed inhibitory–excitatory chimeric transplantation model. We demonstrate robust differentiation toward either dorsal cortical excitatory or ventral MGE inhibitory fates. Co-grafts of inhibitory and excitatory neurons demonstrate lineage-specific maturation—predominantly excitatory CTIP2 + layer 5 projection neurons or inhibitory Calretinin + and SST+ interneurons. Co-grafts integrate and densely innervate both the graft and host tissues. We observed juxtaposition of excitatory and inhibitory fibres and synapses. Optogenetic stimulation confirmed that grafted inhibitory neurons are functionally integrated with grafted excitatory neurons, inducing inhibitory post-synaptic potentials, which were blocked with gabazine. Together, these data provide evidence of functional inhibitory–excitatory circuit reconstruction following transplantation and provide a platform for advanced cell therapies and disease modelling.Keywords: Cortex, Medial ganglionic eminence, Interneurons, Transplantation, Human pluripotent stem cells, Excitation–inhibition balanceSubject terms: Neuroscience, Stem cells

Introduction

Transplantation of neural progenitors into the adult brain provides a promising method for both neuronal cell replacement in injury or disease, and disease modelling. Cell replacement therapies for Parkinson’s disease1–4, spinal cord injury5–8, epilepsy9–12, and stroke13–16 have shown that transplanted neurons integrate into the host brain, innervate target nuclei and restore function in both pre-clinical models and phase I/II trials. While graft-to-host connectivity has been amply demonstrated, less is known about graft-to-graft interaction. This is particularly important in the context of complex cell replacement therapies (for example intracortical transplants for stroke) where cell loss is non-specific and true cell replacement requires both inhibitory and excitatory components alongside glia.

The majority (~ 70%) of ischemic strokes impact the cortex17, with typical cell preparations for cell replacement therapies in stroke consisting of differentiated glutamatergic neurons with variable glial contribution13–16. However, the transplantation of predominantly glutamatergic neurons risks disrupting both host and graft excitation–inhibition (E/I) balance. E/I imbalance is a common feature of neurodevelopmental18–22 and neurodegenerative disorders23–27. Therefore, to maintain correct E/I balance, transplanted cortical neurons must synaptically integrate with local GABAergic interneurons, be co-transplanted with a source of interneurons or be pharmacodynamically modulated. Co-grafts of MGE-derived interneurons present an attractive, integrated method to maintain dynamic E/I balance. However, direct evidence of excitatory-inhibitory integration in human grafts is lacking.

In addition to cell replacement approaches, neural transplantation can be adopted to create advanced models of human neurological disorders. At present, in vitro cultured neurons or organoids represent—at best—late stages of foetal development28. Fused organoid models have emerged as an exciting method to investigate multi-lineage neuronal interaction in vitro, yet these models typically recapitulate foetal identities in vitro29,30. While these models capture developmental events accurately, to study neurodegenerative disorders, further maturation of hPSC-derived neural cells is often necessary. The transplantation of hPSC-derived neurons allows for long-term maturation and emergence of disease phenotypes not seen in vitro31,32. In one striking example, transplanted dopaminergic neurons display alpha-synuclein Lewy Body pathology in the Parkinson’s disease brain after long-term transplantation (> 10y)33–35. Cortical hyperexcitability has been demonstrated in ALS patients, precedes overt motor neuron degeneration36–38, and has been demonstrated in SOD1, C9ORF72 or TARDBP mutation carrying iPSC-derived neurons in vitro39,40. However, a recent study utilising a large pool of sporadic ALS patient iPSC-derived neurons demonstrated hyperexcitability without overt cell death, highlighting the need for long-term maturation of neurons41. In addition, these models have generally utilised excitatory neurons and do not recapitulate physiological E/I imbalance fully, albeit Lall et al., demonstrate downregulation of inhibitory signalling in mixed cultures41. Co-grafts of excitatory and inhibitory wildtype or patient-derived neurons, alongside long-term in vivo maturation, will facilitate the study of E/I imbalance and neurodegeneration at more protracted time points and with greater disease relevance.

To advance this goal, here we directly assess the ability of excitatory and inhibitory human neuronal populations to integrate within a chimeric graft. Using an optogenetic approach, we demonstrate that inhibitory, MGE-derived interneurons synapse and modulate excitatory, cortical-derived neurons in response to optogenetic activation. Evidence of direct inhibitory–excitatory connectivity presents an exciting opportunity to model complex disorders in vivo and provides fundamental proof-of-concept that region-specific neuronal subtypes can functionally integrate following transplantation. These results have implications for complex disease modelling and cell replacement therapies for stroke.

Results

Generation of NKX2-1ChR2−YFP hESC for optogenetic control of MGE-derived inhibitory interneurons

To directly demonstrate inhibitory input onto excitatory neurons we generated a human embryonic stem cell (hESC) line harbouring both green fluorescent protein (GFP) under the NKX2-1 promoter (NKX2-1-GFP42, for enrichment of MGE-derived inhibitory interneurons) and channelrhodopsin2 linked to yellow fluorescent protein (YFP), ChR2-YFP, (hereafter NKX2-1ChR2−YFP). Specifically, we utilised the GAPTrap system to knock in ChR2-YFP downstream of GAPDH, allowing for constitutive expression at an equimolar ratio to GAPDH (Fig. 1a). After puromycin purification and clone selection, NKX2-1ChR2−YFP cells displayed homogenous membrane YFP expression as expected, maintained pluripotency (Fig. 1a, b, OCT4 positivity) and were karyotypically normal (data not shown). Flow cytometry confirmed robust YFP expression in live cells (Fig. 1c). We next confirmed that the GAPTrap system allows for consistent expression of ChR2-YFP after differentiation, as silencing of transgenes has been demonstrated previously. YFP expression was evident in neural progenitor cultures directed toward dorsal (PAX6+, OTX2+) or ventral (FOXA2+) fates (Fig. 1d), confirming the utility of NKX2-1ChR2−YFP for downstream functional studies.

Differentiation of MGE-derived interneurons and dorsal cortical excitatory neurons for co-grafting

Forebrain progenitors can be directed from dorsal to ventral fates by the addition of Sonic Hedgehog (SHH) or SHH agonists, resulting in a shift from excitatory cortical to inhibitory cortical interneurons (Fig. 2a). To generate cortical inhibitory neurons, we differentiated NKX2.1ChR2−YFP hESC toward the MGE lineage using an established protocol43 (Fig. 2a). We observed robust NKX2.1-GFP expression by day 18 following SHH and Purmorphamine addition, which peaked at day 35 (93.4 ± 1.4% GFP+) (Fig. 2b). Ventral forebrain fate of the neural progenitors was further confirmed by expression of OLIG2 and ISL1 together with NKX2-1, indicative of MGE identity (Fig. 2c). MGE progenitors also expressed low levels of the caudal ganglionic eminence (CGE) marker COUPTFII (6.9 ± 2.0%; Fig. 2c). At day 35, NKX2-1-GFP+ cells were sorted by flow cytometry and replated in neural maturation media (Fig. 2b). Following further maturation to Day 55 in vitro, most cells retained NKX2-1-GFP expression (74.4% ± 2.8) and expressed GABA (58.9 ± 3.5%) and GAD67 (61.0 ± 0.3%) (Fig. 2d). Day 55 MGE-derived neurons expressed markers of interneuron subtypes including Calretinin (CR; 10.5 ± 6.1%), Somatostatin (SST; 6.7 ± 1.0%) or Parvalbumin (PV; 1.3 ± 1.3%), in line with ventral forebrain patterning and the extended timeframe for human interneuron generation (Fig. 2e–g). Almost all CR+, SST + or PV+ neurons co-expressed NKX2-1-GFP (Fig. 2g), while the addition of DAPT significantly reduced the proportion of putative late-born PV+ neurons without altering the proportion of interneurons expressing NKX2-1-GFP (Fig. 2f–g).

Alongside, we differentiated H9-mCherry hESC toward a dorsal, glutamatergic fate, following an established protocol44 with adaptations45 (Fig. 3a). At day 25, dorsal cortical progenitors generated characteristic neural rosettes expressing OTX2 and Vimentin, with clear apicobasal polarity as demonstrated by apical cell division (PH3 + localised towards the rosette lumen) and basal TBR2 + intermediate progenitors at the rosette periphery (Fig. 3b). >90% of cortical progenitors expressed the dorsal forebrain markers OTX2, FOXG1, PAX6 and SOX2 (Fig. 3c). After extended maturation in vitro (day 55), dorsally differentiated cells retained mCherry expression and generated mixed populations of both upper and lower laminar-fated cortical neurons, expressing BRN2, TBR1, CTIP2 and SATB2, albeit with a bias toward CTIP2 (Fig. 3d) as observed previously44,45. Comparative analysis of dorsal and ventral differentiations by RT-qPCR revealed clear upregulation of excitatory neuron genes PAX6, EMX1/2, TBR1 and VGLUT2 in dorsal cultures (Fig. 3e). In contrast, ventral MGE cultures upregulated LHX6 (Fig. 3e). Maturation in vitro was confirmed by upregulation of MAP2 in day 55 cultures as compared to day 35 for both dorsal and ventral fated cultures (Fig. 3e). Next, we combined NKX2-1ChR2−YFP ventral neural cells (after GFP sorting) with dorsal neural cells in vitro at a 1:1 ratio to confirm compatibility. Mixed neural cultures could be observed by contrasting GFP or mCherry fluorescence (Fig. 3f). Together, the NKX2-1ChR2−YFP line, coupled with region-specific neural differentiation provides a toolset to investigate whether distinct, inhibitory–excitatory synaptic connections are made following grafting in vivo, an essential component of neural function.

Long-term maturation of co-grafted inhibitory–excitatory grafts

Inhibitory cells make up approximately 20–25% of all neurons within the cortex. We have previously observed that cortical progenitors are more proliferative than MGE progenitors following transplantation and thus utilised a ratio of 3:1 day 35 GFP + MGE or cortical progenitors. Athymic mice were co-grafted with a total of 50,000 cells unilaterally into the mouse cortex and left for long-term maturation (Fig. 4a). After 10 months, the graft could be identified by GFP or mCherry labelling (adjacent sections of same graft, Fig. 4b, c). Co-grafts of NKX2-1ChR2−YFP and H9-mCherry neurons demonstrated restricted placement predominantly within the motor cortex, albeit with some deposits in the corpus callosum and dorsal striatum. The ratio of mCherry-negative: mCherry-positive cells within the graft was 4.4, indicating that the H9-mCherry cortical population did not proliferate as expected, yielding a final, non-physiological inhibitory: excitatory cell ratio (Fig. 4d). We next assessed the innervation pattern of NKX2-1ChR2−YFP or H9-mCherry neurons. Both populations demonstrated abundant fibre growth in the prefrontal cortex (PFC), as well as adjacent to the graft core in the motor cortex (MCtx). Striatal innervation was also present, although this is likely due to cells placed in the dorsal striatum (Fig. 4e). Within the graft (human nuclear antigen, HNA+), we observed both SST+ (0.87 ± 0.29%) and abundant Calretinin+ (27.4 ± 3.0%) inhibitory interneurons (Fig. 4f, g). A single Parvalbumin+ cell was observed in 3 grafts (Fig. 4f, arrowhead), consistent with a low yield of this cell type in vitro. H9-mCherry+ cortical neurons were predominantly CTIP2+, indicative of a layer 5, corticothalamic identity, and consistent with the abundance of CTIP2 + neurons in vitro (Fig. 4h, i). Rare, H9-mCherry+/BRN2 + neurons could also be seen (Fig. 4h-i), likely representing a small population of upper layer cortico-callosal neurons. Together, these data demonstrate that cortical or MGE progenitors develop into appropriate populations of inhibitory and excitatory neurons after co-grafting and long-term maturation in vivo.

Optogenetic demonstration of direct inhibitory input onto H9-mCherry excitatory neurons

To visualise graft-specific synapses we utilised a human-specific synaptophysin antibody. The graft core was densely positive for synaptophysin (Fig. 5a). Synaptophysin could also be seen distal to the graft in the ipsilateral prefrontal cortex and juxtaposed between H9-mCherry and NKX2-1ChR2−YFP fibres (Fig. 5b). To determine if NKX2-1ChR2−YFP cells can be activated via ChR2 and if they synapse at H9-mCherry cells we took whole cell recordings from both neuron classes in mixed grafts between 10.5 and 11 months post-transplantation (Fig. 5c). Dodt gradient contrast and fluorescent imaging enabled identification of NKX2-1ChR2−YFP inhibitory interneurons and H9-mCherry derived excitatory neurons within the graft (Fig. 5c). NKX2-1ChR2−YFP and H9-mCherry neurons exhibited similar resting membrane potentials (NKX2-1ChR2−YFP: − 59.1 ± 2.9 mV; H9-mCherry: − 60.5 ± 1.4 mV) (Fig. 5d). Both cell types received synaptic input with 80% of NKX2-1ChR2−YFP and 73% of H9-mCherry neurons exhibiting spontaneous excitatory post synaptic currents and 29% NKX2-1ChR2−YFP and 33% of H9-mCherry neurons exhibiting spontaneous inhibitory post synaptic currents (data not shown). No NKX2-1ChR2−YFP or H9-mCherry neurons tested exhibited spontaneous action potential firing at rest. To compare the input frequency relationship of each cohort we injected current at 10 pA increments to initiate action potentials. Most NKX2-1ChR2−YFP neurons accommodated with increasing input in contrast to H9-mCherry neurons that maintained a regular firing pattern (Fig. 5d), yet their respective input frequency curves were similar (two-way repeated measures ANOVA; p = 0.314).

To confirm ChR2 expression in NKX2-1ChR2−YFP neurons, we applied LED pulses up to 200 ms to evoke optical excitatory currents with an average amplitude of 23 ± 6 pA (Fig. 5e). We observed optically induced excitatory currents in half of those neurons similarly tested (8 of 16 neurons). In these same 8 ChR2 functioning cells, ChR2 LED activation evoked an average optical excitatory potential of 8 ± 2 mV and initiated action potentials in 3 of the NKX2-1ChR2−YFP neurons (those with the largest ChR2 evoked currents/potentials, Fig. 5e). To determine if NKX2-1ChR2−YFP neurons synapse at H9-mCherry neurons we recorded LED synced postsynaptic currents. No H9-mCherry neurons expressed optical excitatory post synaptic currents or potentials with LED pulses. In contrast, LED pulses evoked inhibitory post synaptic currents in 4; excitatory post synaptic currents in 3; and both inhibitory and excitatory post synaptic currents in 1 of 25 H9-mCherry neurons recorded (Fig. 5f). These LED evoked inhibitory post synaptic currents were blocked by the GABAA receptor antagonist, gabazine (GBZ, Fig. 5f). In 4 of 8 ChR2 expressing NKX2-1ChR2−YFP neurons, LED pulses also evoked inhibitory post synaptic currents (Fig. 5g). Together, these results confirm synaptic integration between MGE-derived inhibitory interneurons and excitatory cortical neurons after co-grafting.

Discussion

Grafting of neurons into the rodent brain presents an opportunity for long-term maturation of hPSC-derived cells within appropriate physiological conditions. To this end, we observed consistent electrophysiological features of mature or maturing neurons, including resting membrane potential and input frequency relationships. Of note, all patched cells exhibited electrophysiological characteristics of neurons, indicating a low level of glial differentiation in grafts. One limitation of this study was the varying level of ChR2-induced currents provoked by blue light stimulation in NKX2-1ChR2−YFP neurons. However, in ~ 20% of tested neurons, LED stimulation consistently induced action potentials and across a graft of 37,500 (total grafted NKX2-1ChR2−YFP cells) this still represents thousands of ChR2-active interneurons. Despite the relatively low number of ChR2 functional cells, we were able to clearly observe delayed post synaptic currents after LED stimulation when patching H9-mCherry cortical cells. Given, the wide range of action potential onset variability and induced post synaptic currents it is difficult to determine whether NKX2-1ChR2−YFP input was mono or polysynaptic. However, given that NKX2-1ChR2−YFP-derived neurons are predominantly inhibitory, evoked inhibitory post synaptic currents are likely the result of monosynaptic input to H9-mCherry neurons. In contrast, we propose that the observed excitatory post synaptic currents are likely explained by a small population of excitatory ChR2 + neurons arising from the MGE differentiation and not from post-inhibitory rebound and or indirect activation by heat or light. Taken together, our electrophysiological data show that NKX2-1ChR2−YFP neurons drive inhibitory synaptic input to H9-mCherry excitatory cortical neurons in our chimeric transplantation model. Future studies, utilising higher levels of constitutive ChR2, will improve our ability to disentangle the role of inhibitory signalling in graft function.

Our novel channelrhodopsin line (NKX2-1ChR2−YFP) allowed for simultaneous enrichment of NKX2-1 + progenitors for grafting and selective activation of interneurons using blue light to probe synaptic integration. However, it is important to note that many cortical interneurons downregulate NKX2-1 upon migration and maturation in the cortex during development46. Therefore, while we did observe limited NKX2-1+/Calretinin+ neurons within the graft, the majority of Calretinin + or SST+ interneurons were NKX2-1- and our targeting of GFP+ neurons may not fully reflect the inhibitory populations present in the graft derived from NKX2-1 + progenitors. However, given optogenetic stimulation was targeted at the whole graft, both GFP+/− interneurons will have been stimulated, giving confidence to our demonstration of direct inhibitory–excitatory interaction when recording from mCherry+ cortical neurons. Utilising distinct reporters or removing the need for NKX2-1-GFP with more efficient differentiation will enhance our ability to investigate the full complement of generated interneurons.

Additionally, MGE-derived interneuron grafting is often carried out in the neonate brain, which promotes migration and dispersal of grafted neurons47, recapitulating the migratory stream from MGE to cortex during development48–50. We observed some—albeit limited—migration of cells away from the graft core. We speculate that both co-grafting with a source of excitatory neurons and grafting into the adult brain may limit the level of migration. Future work investigating similar chimeric transplantation models in the neonate will elucidate the role of the host environment on migratory potential of transplanted progenitors. Another limitation is likely the distinct regional identity of MGE-derived progenitors generated in this study. While we observed significant Calretinin+ interneurons and some SST+ interneurons, we observed very few PV+ interneurons. MGE-subtype specificity has been shown to be region specific, with PV neurons predominantly generated in the ventral MGE and SST/Calretinin interneurons in the dorsal MGE31,51–53. Given both the in vitro and in vivo lack of PV+ neurons in our hands, refining this protocol may allow a more physiological generation of mixed parvalbumin/SST interneurons, better recapitulating the cortical milieu.

In sum, we demonstrate that grafted MGE-derived interneurons directly synapse and provide inhibitory input into co-grafted excitatory neurons. Our data provides proof-of-principle that chimeric transplantation approaches can yield functional E/I input and present a blueprint for advanced cell replacement therapies for stroke. Further, this model presents an excellent opportunity to study diseases of E/I imbalance by utilising patient iPSCs. It allows for long-term maturation in vivo and generation of neuronal networks above and beyond current in vitro models. We anticipate that this work will lead to further studies targeted at altering MGE-derived interneuron identity, migration in the host, achieving the correct balance of inhibitory (~ 20%) and excitatory (~ 80%) neurons, and the use of disease-relevant iPSC.

Methods

Ethics statement

All animal procedures adhered to the Australian National Health and Medical Research Council’s (NHMRC) Code of Practice for the Use of Animals in Research and were approved by the Florey Institute for Neuroscience and Mental Health animal ethics committee (#21–045). Data is reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Human ESC culture

The human embryonic stem cell reporter lines NKX2-1ChR2-YFP and H9-mCherry were maintained in mTeSR1 (StemCell Technologies) on Laminin-521 (BioLamina; 5 µg/ml) and passaged at 70–80% confluence with ReLeSR (StemCell Technologies). For neural differentiation, ESCs were collected with Accutase (StemCell Technologies) and seeded onto fresh Laminin-521 plates at 0.375 × 106/cm2 in mTeSR1 with 10µM Y27632 (Tocris Bioscience). MGE differentiation was carried out using an established protocol43. Briefly, media was switched to N2B27 containing 2µM XAV939 (Tocris Bioscience), 200nM LDN193189 (Stemgent) and 10µM SB431542 (R&D Systems). From day 10–18, media was switched to N2B27 with 100ng/ml SHH (R&D Systems) and 1µM Purmorphamine (Stemgent). NKX2-1-GFP+ progenitors were FACS purified at day 35 for transplantation or further culture. For cortical differentiation, 200nM LDN193189 (Stemgent) and 10µM SB431542 (R&D Systems) were added in N2B27. At day 10, cortical progenitors were passaged 1:2 and transferred to N2B27 with 20ng/ml FGF2 (R&D Systems) for 8 days. For extended culture of MGE-derived or cortical-derived neurons, 40ng/ml BDNF (R&D Systems), 40ng/ml GDNF (R&D Systems), 0.05mM dcAMP (Tocris Bioscience), 200nM ascorbic acid (Sigma-Aldrich) and 1 µg/ml laminin (Sigma-Aldrich) were added. For MGE subtyping experiments, cultures were additionally treated with 7 days of DAPT (10µM, Sigma-Aldrich).

Generation of NKX2-1ChR2-YFP

1 × 106 NKX2-1-GFP (HES3) ESC42 were electroporated with 2 µg of each GAPDH targeting TALEN and 6µg of unlinearised target plasmid containing ChR2-YFP (yellow fluorescent protein) using the GAPTrap system54. The NKX2-1-GFP line was a gift from Edouard Stanley and Andrew Elefanty. Electroporation protocol was 1050 V, 30ms pulse width, 2 pulses using a Neon Electroporation system (Thermo Fisher). Electroporated cells, alongside non-electroporated controls, were plated on fresh laminin-521 coated dishes in mTeSR1 without penicillin-streptomycin and with 10µM Y27632. One week after plating, selection was commenced with puromycin. Single clones were selected manually and cultured further. ChR2-YFP expression was validated with immunohistochemistry against ChR2.

Transplantation

Foxn1nu athymic nude mice were group housed on a 12-hour light-dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water. Surgeries were conducted under 2–5% isofluorane anaesthesia. Day 35 MGE cultures were dissociated with Accutase and labelled with 0.5 µg/ml 4′,6-Diamidino-2-Phenylindole (DAPI; Sigma-Aldrich). Live, NKX2-1-GFP+ progenitors were purified using a FACS Aria III (BD Biosciences) with a 100 μm nozzle at 20 PSI and mixed 3:1 with H9-mCherry cortical progenitors at a final density of 0.1 × 106 cells/µl. Mice (n = 10 female Foxn1nu athymic nude) received unilateral injections of 0.5 µl of cells into the sensorimotor cortex (0.5 mm anterior, 1.5 mm lateral of bregma, and 1.5 mm below the dura surface).

Immunohistochemistry and analysis

Cells were fixed for 10 min with ice-cold 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilised in 0.3% Triton-X for 30 min. Mice (n = 4) were transcardially perfused with pre-warmed Tyrode solution and fixed with 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde. Brains were transferred to Sucrose solution (20%) overnight and cut at 40 μm (12 series) on a freezing microtome (Leica). Primary antibodies were added overnight in 0.3% Triton-X and 5% normal donkey serum. Antibody binding was visualised via chromogenic conjugation of the diaminobenzidine chromagen (VectaStain) or directly using fluorescently conjugated secondary antibodies. Darkfield imaging was carried out on a Leica DM6000 upright microscope while a Leica Thunder epifluorescence was used to capture fluorescence images. For in vitro quantification two duplicate wells were analysed across at least three separate differentiations. For in vivo quantification, 4 mice were analysed. Statistical analysis was carried out with GraphPad Prism software.

RT-qPCR analysis

MGE or cortical-derived cells were collected with Accutase, resuspended in 40 µl RNAlater (Thermo Fisher) and stored at − 80 °C. RNA isolation was carried out with an ISOLATE-II Mini kit (Bioline). RNA was converted to cDNA using SuperScript VILO cDNA synthesis kit (Thermo Fisher). 10ng cDNA was amplified with primers and PowerUp SYBR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher) under the following conditions: 50 °C for 2 min, 95 °C for 2 min & 40 cycles of; 95 °C for 15 s & 60 °C for 1 min. Gene expression results were determined with the ΔΔCt method from at least 3 independent biological replicates.

Whole cell recordings

Mice (n = 6) that had mixed NKX2-1ChR2−YFP/H9-mCherry grafts were deeply anaesthetised with 5% isoflurane, decapitated and the brain removed, blocked and rapidly cooled in artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF, 2 °C, containing (in mM): NaCl, 125; KCl, 3; KH2PO4, 1.2; MgSO4, 1.2; NaHCO3, 25; dextrose, 10; CaCl2, 2; (300 mOsmol)). No a priori criteria or randomisation used to determine which mice were taken for electrophysiology or immunohistochemistry. Coronal 200 μm slices of the forebrain incorporating the graft were sectioned on a vibratome (VT1200s; Leica Microsystems) and continuously perfused in aCSF solution, bubbled with 95% O2, 5% CO2, at 32 °C. Recording pipettes (3.0–6.7 MΩ) contained internal solution containing (in mM): NaCl, 6; NaOH, 4; KOH, potassium gluconate, 130; EGTA, 11; CaCl2, 1; HEPES, 10; MgCl2, 1; 0.01% biocytin (pH 7.3, 290 mOsmol, ECl − 68.8 mV). Consequently, recordings at VH > − 60 mV resulted in outward Cl− currents. Pipettes were visually guided to either NKX2-1ChR2−YFP or H9-mCherry expressing cells using a fixed stage scope (Zeiss Examiner) and camera (Rolera EM-C2, Q-Imaging, Surrey, BC, Canada). Whole-cell recordings were made in current clamp mode (MultiClamp 700B and pClamp 10.4, Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA). Signals were sampled at 20 kHz and filtered at 10 kHz. For Fig. 4, voltage clamp traces are displayed with a 1 kHz lowpass Bessel filter applied. Liquid junction potentials were corrected (− 6.6 mV at 32 °C) post recording. Pulses of light (465 nm, 10 mW at fibre tip) from a light emitting diode (LED, Plexon, Dallas, TX) were delivered via fibre optic affixed to a micro-manipulator and positioned to illuminate the graft tissue (Fig. 4c) to activate ChR2. Gabazine (GBZ, aka SR-95531, 3 µM) was super perfused onto the slice in aCSF at 2.5 ml/min.
