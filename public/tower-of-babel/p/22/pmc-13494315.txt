Red Blood Cell Distribution Width-To-Albumin Ratio and 90-Day Mortality in Sepsis Patients with and Without Sepsis-Induced Coagulopathy: A Retrospective Observational Study Using the MIMIC-IV Database
Authors: Baochang Xie, Yu Cao, Yuanyuan Xiao, Diyang Zhu, Xiaoyu Guan, Guangming Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe red blood cell distribution width-to-albumin ratio (RAR) has emerged as a novel prognostic biomarker in critically ill patients. However, its association with mortality in sepsis patients, particularly in the context of sepsis-induced coagulopathy (SIC), remains unclear.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study analyzed adult sepsis patients from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) version 3.1 database. RAR was calculated from the first laboratory measurements within 24 hours of intensive care unit (ICU) admission. The primary outcome was 90-day all-cause mortality. Cox proportional hazards regression, subgroup analyses, and restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression were performed. Discriminative ability was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves.ResultsOf 26,854 patients meeting sepsis criteria, 17,499 were excluded due to missing RDW or albumin data. A total of 9,206 sepsis patients were included, with 5,649 (61.4%) meeting SIC criteria. The overall 90-day mortality was 38.1% (absolute rates: 23.9% in the lowest RAR quartile to 55.5% in the highest). In the fully adjusted model (age, sex, SOFA, Charlson Comorbidity Index, infection source, vasopressor use, mechanical ventilation, renal replacement therapy, and lactate), each unit increase in RAR was associated with a 9% increase in mortality risk (hazard ratio [HR]=1.09, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.08-1.10, P<0.001). Compared with the lowest quartile, patients in the highest RAR quartile had a significantly higher mortality risk (HR=2.07, 95% CI: 1.91-2.25). The association remained consistent across subgroups. RCS analysis revealed a significant non-linear exposure-response relationship (P for non-linearity <0.001). DeLong testing confirmed that RAR (AUC=0.648) had statistically significantly higher discriminative ability than SOFA score (AUC=0.613, P<0.001) and SIC score (AUC=0.593, P<0.001) for 90-day mortality, though all AUC values indicated moderate discrimination.ConclusionsElevated RAR was independently associated with increased 90-day mortality after comprehensive covariate adjustment (highest vs. lowest quartile: HR=2.07, 95% CI: 1.91-2.25), with modest but statistically significant incremental discriminative ability over SOFA and SIC scores. As a biomarker derived from routine blood tests, RAR may complement existing tools for early risk stratification in sepsis. These findings are hypothesis-generating and warrant prospective validation.

Introduction

Sepsis, defined as life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection, remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in ICUs worldwide. 1 Despite advances in early recognition, antimicrobial therapy, and supportive care, sepsis continues to affect approximately 49 million people annually, with an estimated 11 million sepsis-related deaths, accounting for nearly 20% of all global deaths. 2 The heterogeneous nature of sepsis, combined with its rapid progression and complex pathophysiology, underscores the critical need for reliable biomarkers that can facilitate early risk stratification and guide clinical decision-making.

SIC represents a common and serious complication of sepsis, characterized by systemic activation of coagulation, consumption of coagulation factors, and impaired anticoagulant pathways. 3 The International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis (ISTH) introduced the SIC scoring system in 2017 to identify patients with early-phase coagulation dysfunction before progression to overt disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). 4 SIC is present in approximately 24% of sepsis patients and up to 66% of those with septic shock, and is associated with significantly increased mortality.5,6 Early identification of patients at high risk for adverse outcomes in the setting of sepsis, with or without SIC, remains an important clinical challenge.

The red blood cell distribution width (RDW), a routine component of the complete blood count that measures the variability in red blood cell size, has emerged as a promising prognostic marker in various disease states. 7 Elevated RDW has been associated with increased mortality in cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, respiratory disorders, and critical illness.8-10 In the context of sepsis, several studies have demonstrated that higher RDW values at admission are independently associated with increased short-term and long-term mortality.11-13 The proposed mechanisms underlying this association include systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and impaired erythropoiesis, all of which are prominent features of sepsis pathophysiology. 14

Serum albumin, the most abundant plasma protein, serves multiple physiological functions including maintenance of oncotic pressure, transport of various biomolecules, and antioxidant defense. 15 Hypoalbuminemia is common in critically ill patients and has been consistently associated with adverse outcomes in sepsis. 16 The decrease in albumin during sepsis results from reduced hepatic synthesis, increased capillary leak, and enhanced catabolism as part of the acute phase response. 17 Given that both elevated RDW and decreased albumin reflect distinct but interrelated pathological processes in sepsis, combining these two parameters into a single ratio may provide enhanced prognostic information.

RAR has recently been proposed as a novel composite biomarker that integrates information from both the inflammatory and nutritional/metabolic domains. 18 RAR has been shown to be associated with poor prognosis in various conditions, including acute respiratory distress syndrome, acute pancreatitis, and coronary heart disease.19-21 Recent studies using earlier versions of the MIMIC database have suggested that RAR may be a valuable prognostic marker in sepsis patients.22,23 However, the association between RAR and mortality in the specific context of SIC has not been previously explored, and the potential incremental value of RAR over established severity scores such as the SOFA score and SIC score remains to be determined.

Therefore, the objectives of this study were to: (1) evaluate the association between RAR and 90-day mortality in sepsis patients; (2) assess the consistency of this association across subgroups defined by age, disease severity, and SIC status; (3) compare the discriminative ability of RAR with SOFA score and SIC score for mortality prediction; and (4) explore the exposure-response relationship between RAR and mortality using RCS analysis. We hypothesized that higher RAR values would be independently associated with increased mortality risk, and that RAR would provide prognostic information complementary to existing severity scores.

Methods

Study Design and Data Source

This retrospective cohort study utilized data from the MIMIC-IV (version 3.1) database, a publicly available critical care database comprising de-identified health records of patients admitted to the ICUs at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston, Massachusetts, between 2008 and 2022. The database contains comprehensive clinical information including demographics, vital signs, laboratory measurements, medications, and outcomes. Access to the database was approved after completion of the Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative program (Record ID: 74881359). As the study utilized de-identified data from a publicly available database, informed consent was waived, and the study was exempt from institutional review board approval. This study was reported following the STROBE guidelines for observational cohort studies (Supplementary File 1).

Study Population

Adult patients (≥18 years) admitted to the ICU with a diagnosis of sepsis were eligible for inclusion. Sepsis was defined according to the Sepsis-3 criteria as suspected or documented infection with an acute increase in SOFA score of ≥2 points. Infection was identified based on International Classification of Diseases (ICD-9 and ICD-10) diagnosis codes. The following exclusion criteria were applied: (1) ICU length of stay <24 hours; (2) missing data for RDW or albumin within the first 24 hours of ICU admission; and (3) repeated ICU admissions (only the first ICU admission was included for patients with multiple admissions).

Definition of Exposure and Outcomes

The primary exposure variable was RAR, calculated as RDW (%) divided by serum albumin (g/dL). Both RDW and albumin values were obtained from the first available laboratory measurements within 24 hours of ICU admission. When multiple measurements were available within the 24-hour window, the earliest (first) value was used to ensure consistency with admission-state assessment. The vast majority of first laboratory measurements in MIMIC-IV are obtained within the initial hours of ICU admission as part of routine admission workup, which limits but does not eliminate variability in measurement timing. RAR was analyzed both as a continuous variable and as a categorical variable based on quartiles (Q1: lowest, Q4: highest).

SIC was diagnosed according to the criteria proposed by the ISTH, which includes: (1) platelet count (score 0-2: ≥150, 100-149, <100 ×109/L); (2) international normalized ratio (INR) (score 0-2: ≤1.2, 1.2-1.4, >1.4); and (3) SOFA score (score 0-2: 0-1, 2, ≥3). Patients with a total SIC score ≥4 were classified as having SIC.

The primary outcome was 90-day all-cause mortality. The secondary outcome was 28-day all-cause mortality. Mortality data were obtained from hospital records and social security death index linkage available in the MIMIC-IV database.

Covariates

Baseline characteristics collected within the first 24 hours of ICU admission included: demographics (age, sex), severity scores (SOFA score, SIC score), laboratory parameters (RDW, albumin, platelet count, INR, total bilirubin, serum creatinine, serum lactate), comorbidity burden (Charlson Comorbidity Index calculated from ICD-9/10 codes using the Quan et al algorithm), infection source (classified as pulmonary, abdominal, urinary, bloodstream, skin/soft tissue, or other based on ICD codes), treatment variables (vasopressor use, mechanical ventilation, renal replacement therapy within 24-48 hours of ICU admission), and ICU length of stay. Continuous variables were expressed as median with interquartile range (IQR) or mean with standard deviation, as appropriate. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics were compared between groups using the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. The Jonckheere-Terpstra test was used to assess trends across RAR quartiles.

Kaplan-Meier survival curves were constructed to visualize survival probabilities, and the log-rank test was used to compare survival distributions between groups. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to evaluate the association between RAR and 90-day mortality. Three models were constructed: Model 1 (unadjusted), Model 2 (adjusted for age and sex), and Model 3 (adjusted for age, sex, SOFA score, Charlson Comorbidity Index, infection source, vasopressor use, mechanical ventilation, renal replacement therapy, and serum lactate). HRs with 95% CIs were calculated. RAR was also analyzed as a categorical variable (quartiles), with the lowest quartile (Q1) serving as the reference group.

Subgroup analyses were performed to assess the consistency of the association between RAR and mortality across predefined subgroups, including age (<65 vs. ≥65 years), SOFA score (<7 vs. ≥7), and SIC status (SIC vs. non-SIC). Interaction terms were included in the Cox models to test for effect modification, with P for interaction <0.05 considered statistically significant.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to test the robustness of the primary findings: (1) excluding patients with extreme RAR values (outside the 1st-99th percentile range); (2) restricting the analysis to SIC patients only; (3) restricting the analysis to non-SIC patients only; and (4) restricting the analysis to patients with ICU length of stay ≥48 hours.

RCS regression with 3 knots (placed at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles) was used to explore the potential non-linear relationship between RAR and 90-day mortality. The median RAR value was used as the reference point. The likelihood ratio test was used to assess non-linearity by comparing models with and without the spline terms.

ROC curve analysis was performed to evaluate the discriminative ability of RAR, SOFA score, and SIC score for predicting mortality. The AUC was calculated and compared between predictors. The optimal cutoff value for RAR was determined using the Youden index (sensitivity + specificity - 1).

All statistical analyses were performed using Python (version 3.10) with the following packages: pandas, numpy, lifelines, scikit-learn, and scipy. A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals with rank-transformed time. Variance inflation factors (VIF) were calculated to evaluate multicollinearity among covariates. Formal comparison of AUCs between predictors was performed using the DeLong test. Model calibration was evaluated using the Brier score and calibration plots across predicted probability deciles. Decision curve analysis was performed to assess net clinical benefit across a range of threshold probabilities. The incremental prognostic value of RAR when added to SOFA score was quantified using the combined model AUC and DeLong comparison. To address borderline violation of the proportional hazards assumption, time-stratified Cox models were fitted separately for early (0-30 days) and late (30-90 days) follow-up periods. Continuous Net Reclassification Improvement (NRI) and Integrated Discrimination Improvement (IDI) were calculated to quantify the incremental prognostic value of RAR when added to SOFA, following Pencina’s method. To address potential selection bias from missing RDW or albumin data, inverse probability weighting (IPW) was applied as a sensitivity analysis: a logistic regression model predicted the probability of inclusion based on age, sex, and SOFA score, and the inverse probability weights (truncated at the 99th percentile) were applied in a robust weighted Cox model. Predicted absolute mortality risks at specific RAR values were estimated from the multivariable Cox model at the median covariate profile. A clinically oriented three-tier risk stratification (RAR <5, 5 to <7, and ≥7) was also evaluated to facilitate clinical interpretation.

When stratified by RAR quartiles, patients in higher quartiles demonstrated progressively worse clinical profiles. When RAR was analyzed as a categorical variable using quartiles, compared with the lowest quartile (Q1, reference), patients in Q2 had a 23% increased risk of death (HR=1.23, 95% CI: 1.13-1.34, P<0.001), those in Q3 had a 39% increased risk (HR=1.39, 95% CI: 1.28-1.52, P<0.001), and those in the highest quartile (Q4) had a 107% increased risk (HR=2.07, 95% CI: 1.91-2.25, P<0.001) after comprehensive covariate adjustment. The proportional hazards assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residuals; while RAR showed borderline violation (P=0.044), time-stratified Cox models fitted separately for early (0-30 days) and late (30-90 days) follow-up yielded consistent hazard ratios (HR=1.08 and 1.09, respectively), confirming that the association was stable over time and that the borderline violation did not materially affect the conclusions. Variance inflation factors for all covariates were below 12, indicating acceptable multicollinearity.

Subgroup and Sensitivity Analyses

Subgroup analyses were conducted to assess the consistency of the association between RAR and 90-day mortality across different patient populations (Figure 4, Table 2). The prognostic value of RAR was consistent across all subgroups, with HRs ranging from 1.07 to 1.18 per unit increase. Significant interactions were observed for age (P for interaction <0.001), sex (P for interaction <0.001), SOFA score (P for interaction <0.001), and SIC status (P for interaction =0.010). The association between RAR and mortality was stronger in patients aged ≥65 years (HR=1.12, 95% CI: 1.10-1.13) compared to those <65 years (HR=1.08, 95% CI: 1.07-1.09), in patients with SOFA <7 (HR=1.18, 95% CI: 1.15-1.20) compared to those with SOFA ≥7 (HR=1.07, 95% CI: 1.06-1.08), and in non-SIC patients (HR=1.12, 95% CI: 1.11-1.14) compared to SIC patients (HR=1.08, 95% CI: 1.07-1.09).Figure 4.Forest plot of subgroup analysis for the association between RAR and 90-day mortality

Forest plot of subgroup analysis for the association between RAR and 90-day mortality

Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of our findings. After excluding patients with extreme RAR values (1st-99th percentile), the association remained significant (HR=1.17, 95% CI: 1.15-1.19, P<0.001). The results were also consistent when restricting the analysis to SIC patients only (HR=1.08, 95% CI: 1.07-1.09, P<0.001), non-SIC patients only (HR=1.12, 95% CI: 1.11-1.14, P<0.001), and patients with ICU length of stay ≥48 hours (HR=1.08, 95% CI: 1.08-1.09, P<0.001).

Non-Linear Relationship and Predictive Performance

RCS analysis revealed a significant non-linear relationship between RAR and 90-day mortality (P for non-linearity <0.001, Figure 5A). Using the median RAR value (5.45) as the reference, the HR increased progressively with higher RAR values. At RAR=7.5, the HR was approximately 1.3, and at RAR=9.2, it was approximately 1.4 relative to the reference.Figure 5.Non-linear relationship and predictive performance. (A) RCS curve showing exposure-response relationship. (B) ROC curves comparing RAR, SOFA, and SIC scores

Non-linear relationship and predictive performance. (A) RCS curve showing exposure-response relationship. (B) ROC curves comparing RAR, SOFA, and SIC scores

ROC curve analysis was performed to compare the discriminative ability of RAR, SOFA score, and SIC score for predicting 90-day mortality (Figure 5B). For 28-day mortality prediction, RAR demonstrated comparable performance to SOFA score (AUC: 0.639 vs. 0.641, DeLong P=0.845). For 90-day mortality prediction, RAR showed modestly but statistically significantly higher discriminative ability (AUC=0.648) compared to SOFA score (AUC=0.613, DeLong P<0.001) and SIC score (AUC=0.593, DeLong P<0.001). A combined RAR+SOFA model yielded an AUC of 0.674, significantly higher than SOFA alone (DeLong P<0.001). The Brier score for the multivariable model was 0.188, indicating acceptable calibration. The calibration slope was 1.00 (95% CI: 0.95-1.05), indicating excellent calibration (Figure 6A). Decision curve analysis demonstrated that the RAR-inclusive model provided greater net benefit than SOFA alone or treat-all and treat-none strategies across the clinically relevant threshold range of 10-40% (Figure 6B). The optimal cutoff value for RAR in predicting 90-day mortality was 6.19, with a sensitivity of 46.8% and specificity of 74.4%.Figure 6.Model calibration and decision curve analysis. To quantify the incremental prognostic value of RAR beyond SOFA score, we computed continuous Net Reclassification Improvement (NRI) and Integrated Discrimination Improvement (IDI). Adding RAR to a SOFA-only model yielded a continuous NRI of 0.36 (95% CI: 0.34-0.39, P<0.001) and an IDI of 0.046 (95% CI: 0.042-0.051, P<0.001), indicating significant improvement in risk reclassification.

To translate the per-unit hazard ratio into clinically interpretable absolute risks, we estimated predicted 90-day mortality at specific RAR values from the fully adjusted Cox model at the median covariate profile. Predicted mortality increased monotonically: 40.2% at RAR=4, 45.6% at RAR=6, 51.3% at RAR=8, and 57.3% at RAR=10—corresponding to absolute risk differences of approximately 5 to 6 percentage points per 2-unit RAR increment. A clinically oriented three-tier risk stratification (RAR <5, 5 to <7, ≥7) yielded observed 90-day mortality rates of 26.0%, 40.1%, and 57.6% respectively. After comprehensive adjustment, the intermediate-risk tier had HR=1.35 (95% CI: 1.26-1.44) and the high-risk tier HR=2.01 (95% CI: 1.87-2.16) versus the low-risk reference.

To address the borderline violation of the proportional hazards assumption (P=0.044) for RAR, time-stratified Cox models were fitted for early (0-30 days) and late (30-90 days) periods. The RAR effect was largely consistent across periods: HR=1.082 (95% CI: 1.073-1.092) for early mortality and HR=1.094 (95% CI: 1.082-1.107) for late mortality, supporting the robustness of the overall estimate.

To assess the influence of selection bias from missing data, an inverse probability weighting (IPW) sensitivity analysis was performed. After weighting included patients (n=9,206) by the inverse probability of inclusion derived from age, sex, and SOFA score, the weighted RAR HR was 1.10 (95% CI: 1.03-1.17, P<0.001), closely matching the unweighted primary estimate (HR=1.09) and indicating that selection bias is unlikely to materially affect our conclusions. Multiple imputation was not applied because the substantial proportion of missing data (65% of candidates) and the fact that RDW and albumin constitute the exposure of interest itself preclude reliable imputation under standard missing-at-random assumptions.

Discussion

In this large retrospective observational study of 9,206 sepsis patients from the MIMIC-IV database, we demonstrated that RAR functions as a robust composite severity marker that is independently associated with 90-day mortality after comprehensive adjustment for demographics, comorbidities, disease severity, and treatment factors. Rather than acting as an independent mechanistic driver of outcomes, RAR likely reflects the convergence of multiple downstream consequences of systemic illness—including inflammation-mediated erythropoietic dysfunction (elevated RDW) and capillary leak combined with reduced hepatic synthesis (decreased albumin). The key findings include: (1) higher RAR values were significantly associated with increased mortality, with patients in the highest quartile having a 107% increased risk compared to the lowest quartile (HR=2.07); (2) the association remained robust across subgroups and sensitivity analyses; (3) RAR demonstrated modest but statistically significant incremental discriminative ability for 90-day mortality compared to SOFA and SIC scores (DeLong P<0.001); and (4) a non-linear exposure-response relationship was observed.

Our findings are consistent with and extend previous studies examining the prognostic value of RAR in critically ill patients. Xu et al conducted a retrospective cohort study using the MIMIC-IV version 2.0 database and demonstrated that RAR was significantly associated with 28-day, 90-day, and in-hospital mortality in sepsis patients, with an AUC of 0.633 for predicting 28-day mortality. 22 Similarly, Li et al. 23 analyzed 3,969 sepsis patients from the MIMIC-III database and found that the RDW-to-albumin ratio was independently associated with 30- and 90-day mortality, with predictive value comparable to the lactate-to-albumin ratio. 24 Chen et al further validated these findings in sepsis patients meeting Sepsis-3 criteria, reporting that RAR had greater predictive value for mortality compared to RDW or albumin alone. 25 Our study, utilizing the latest MIMIC-IV version 3.1 database with a larger sample size, confirms these associations and provides additional insights into the non-linear relationship between RAR and mortality through RCS analysis.

The biological plausibility underlying the prognostic value of RAR in sepsis is supported by the pathophysiological roles of both RDW and albumin in critical illness. RDW reflects the heterogeneity in circulating red blood cell size and has emerged as a robust prognostic marker in various disease states, including cardiovascular disease, respiratory disorders, and critical illness.7,10 In the context of sepsis, elevated RDW is thought to result from multiple mechanisms. Systemic inflammation, characterized by elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6 and tumor necrosis factor-α, suppresses erythropoietin-induced erythrocyte maturation and proliferation, leading to the release of immature reticulocytes into the peripheral circulation.12,26 Oxidative stress, which is markedly increased in sepsis through the generation of reactive oxygen species by activated leukocytes, further reduces red blood cell survival and disrupts normal erythropoiesis. 27 Additionally, inflammation-induced alterations in iron metabolism contribute to ineffective erythropoiesis and increased RDW. 28

Serum albumin, the other component of RAR, is a multifunctional protein that plays critical roles in maintaining oncotic pressure, transporting various biomolecules, and providing antioxidant defense. 15 Hypoalbuminemia in sepsis results from multiple factors, including decreased hepatic synthesis due to the acute phase response, increased capillary permeability leading to extravasation, and enhanced catabolism. 17 Previous meta-analyses have established hypoalbuminemia as an independent predictor of mortality in critically ill patients, with a dose-dependent relationship between lower albumin levels and worse outcomes. 16 The combination of elevated RDW and decreased albumin in the RAR thus captures two distinct but interrelated pathological processes—inflammation-induced hematopoietic dysfunction and nutritional/metabolic derangement—providing a more comprehensive assessment of disease severity than either marker alone.

An important aspect of this study is the examination of RAR in the context of SIC. SIC, defined by the ISTH criteria, represents an early phase of coagulation dysfunction in sepsis that precedes overt DIC. 4 The SIC scoring system, which incorporates platelet count, prothrombin time/INR, and SOFA score, was developed to identify patients at high risk for adverse outcomes who may benefit from early anticoagulant therapy. 29 In our study, 61.4% of sepsis patients met the criteria for SIC, which is consistent with previous reports showing SIC prevalence of 22-66% depending on the patient population.5,6 Notably, while SIC patients had higher mortality rates, the prognostic value of RAR was significant in both SIC and non-SIC subgroups, suggesting that RAR provides complementary information to the SIC score. Interestingly, the association between RAR and mortality was stronger in non-SIC patients (HR=1.13) compared to SIC patients (HR=1.08), indicating that RAR may be particularly useful for risk stratification in patients who do not meet SIC criteria.

The subgroup analyses revealed significant interactions between RAR and age, sex, SOFA score, and SIC status. The interaction with sex was modest in magnitude (per-unit HR 1.14 in men versus 1.08 in women) and of uncertain clinical importance. The stronger association between RAR and mortality in elderly patients (≥65 years) is consistent with previous findings by Xu et al, who reported a significant interaction between age and RAR on mortality outcomes. 22 This may reflect the diminished physiological reserve and reduced capacity for homeostatic regulation in older patients. The interaction with SOFA score, showing a stronger RAR-mortality association in patients with SOFA <7, suggests that RAR may be particularly valuable in identifying high-risk patients among those with apparently less severe organ dysfunction. This finding has important clinical implications, as these patients might otherwise be underestimated in terms of their mortality risk based on traditional severity scores alone.

The differential association between RAR and mortality across SIC status warrants mechanistic consideration. The prognostic value of RAR was somewhat attenuated in patients with sepsis-induced coagulopathy compared with non-SIC patients (HR 1.08 versus 1.12 per unit increase; P for interaction = 0.010). A plausible explanation is that in SIC, mortality risk is dominated by activation of the coagulation cascade, microthrombosis, and consumptive coagulopathy—processes already captured by the SIC scoring components (platelet count, international normalized ratio, and SOFA score). In this context, the additional information conveyed by RAR, which reflects inflammation-driven erythropoietic dysfunction and hypoalbuminemia, is partially subsumed by the coagulation-centered severity already quantified, reducing its marginal contribution. In non-SIC patients, where overt coagulopathy is absent, RAR may capture a complementary dimension of systemic illness, yielding a modestly stronger association. This pattern is consistent with RAR functioning as a general rather than pathway-specific severity marker, whose relative prognostic weight depends on the dominant pathophysiological process within a given subgroup.

The ROC analysis demonstrated that RAR had modestly but statistically significantly higher discriminative ability for 90-day mortality (AUC=0.648) compared to both SOFA score (AUC=0.613) and SIC score (AUC=0.593), as confirmed by DeLong testing (P<0.001). The AUC difference between RAR and SOFA (0.035) was statistically significant but clinically modest, and all AUC values indicated only moderate discrimination, consistent with the inherent complexity of mortality prediction in sepsis.22-25 Importantly, a combined RAR+SOFA model improved the AUC to 0.674, suggesting incremental rather than replacement value. It should be noted that RAR is a nonspecific composite marker reflecting general illness severity, including inflammation, malnutrition, and hematopoietic dysfunction, rather than a sepsis-specific biomarker. The optimal cutoff value of 6.19 may serve as an exploratory threshold, though prospective validation is needed before clinical application.

Several factors may explain why RAR achieves only moderate discrimination (AUC ≈0.65) in our cohort. First, mortality in sepsis is determined by a heterogeneous combination of host factors, infection severity, organ failures, and treatment response—no single biomarker, however biologically informative, can fully capture this complexity. Second, RAR aggregates two related but partially redundant signals (inflammation/erythropoiesis via RDW; nutrition/capillary leak via albumin); information overlap with SOFA components limits incremental discrimination. Third, our cohort was already enriched for sicker patients (median SOFA=7), in whom prognostic markers tend to plateau due to ceiling effects. Compared with serum lactate, a widely used severity marker in sepsis, RAR demonstrated higher discriminative ability for 90-day mortality in our data (RAR AUC=0.648 vs lactate AUC=0.592), and a combined RAR+lactate model further improved discrimination (AUC=0.671), consistent with their capturing complementary pathophysiological domains. These findings suggest that RAR’s main clinical role is not to outperform existing markers in isolation but to provide cost-free, immediately available complementary information for risk stratification.

An important question is whether RAR conveys prognostic information beyond that of its individual components. In head-to-head comparisons, the discriminative ability of RAR for 90-day mortality (AUC=0.648) was comparable to that of RDW alone (AUC=0.647; DeLong P=0.81) and superior to that of albumin alone (AUC=0.605; DeLong P<0.001), indicating that the discrimination of the composite is driven predominantly by its RDW component. When RDW and albumin were entered as separate covariates, RAR retained an independent association with mortality (adjusted HR=1.03 per unit, 95% CI 1.02-1.05, P<0.001), although the effect was attenuated and collinearity among the three terms was appreciable (variance inflation factors 2.4-5.6). Furthermore, when added to the SOFA score, the composite and its separate components yielded similar discrimination (C-index 0.621 for SOFA+RAR versus 0.640 for SOFA+RDW+albumin). Taken together, these analyses indicate that RAR does not provide discriminative information beyond that available from RDW and albumin considered jointly; rather, its principal advantage is parsimony—condensing two routinely measured laboratory values into a single, easily computed index that is independently associated with outcome and performs comparably to RDW. RAR is therefore best positioned as a convenient composite severity indicator rather than as a marker that outperforms its constituents.

This study has several strengths. First, we utilized the large, high-quality MIMIC-IV version 3.1 database, ensuring adequate sample size and comprehensive clinical data. Second, we employed multiple analytical approaches, including multivariable Cox regression, subgroup analyses, sensitivity analyses, and RCS regression, to thoroughly examine the RAR-mortality association. Third, we assessed the prognostic value of RAR in the specific context of SIC, an area that has not been previously explored. Fourth, the comparison with established prognostic tools (SOFA and SIC scores) provides a meaningful context for evaluating the clinical utility of RAR.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the optimal RAR cutoff (6.19) and the three-tier risk thresholds were derived and evaluated within the same cohort without a held-out validation set; their performance is therefore likely optimistic and should be regarded as exploratory pending validation in independent data. Second, the study was conducted using data from a single tertiary care center in the United States, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to other healthcare settings or populations. Third, we only assessed RAR at a single time point (within 24 hours of ICU admission), and dynamic changes in RAR during the ICU stay may provide additional prognostic information. 30 Fourth, we did not have data on nutritional factors such as iron, folate, and vitamin B12 levels, which may influence RDW values. Fifth, the moderate discriminative ability of RAR suggests that it should be used in conjunction with other clinical and laboratory parameters rather than as a standalone prognostic tool. Future prospective multicenter studies are warranted to validate these findings and to explore whether RAR-guided interventions can improve patient outcomes.

Several considerations regarding the interpretation of the RAR-mortality association warrant emphasis. First, the cross-sectional measurement design cannot rule out reverse causation: more severely ill patients may develop abnormal RAR (through inflammation-driven RDW elevation and acute-phase albumin depletion) precisely because of their worsening clinical state, rather than RAR causally driving mortality. Second, although we adjusted for a comprehensive panel of covariates, residual confounding from unmeasured factors—including pre-ICU illness duration, baseline frailty, micronutrient status (iron, folate, vitamin B12), anemia and red-cell transfusion (which directly modify RDW), hepatic dysfunction, malignancy, and immunosuppression (which alter albumin synthesis and catabolism), fluid balance and resuscitation volume (which affect measured albumin concentration through dilution), inflammation burden (CRP, interleukin-6, procalcitonin), and pre-admission medication exposure—cannot be excluded. RAR should therefore be interpreted as a non-specific composite severity marker rather than as an independent mechanistic contributor to mortality.

Third, the selection bias resulting from missing RDW/albumin data (n=15,423 sepsis patients with available baseline data excluded for missing RDW or albumin) deserves explicit consideration. Comparison of included versus excluded patients (Supplementary Table S1) revealed that included patients had higher disease severity (median SOFA 7 vs 6), longer ICU stays (4.0 vs 3.2 days), greater SIC prevalence (61.4% vs 47.5%), and higher 90-day mortality (38.1% vs 27.4%). This pattern suggests that albumin measurement was preferentially obtained in patients judged clinically more complex—a non-random selection mechanism. The inverse probability weighting sensitivity analysis showed that this selection did not materially alter our estimates (weighted HR=1.10, comparable to unweighted HR=1.09), but findings should still be interpreted as applicable primarily to sepsis patients in whom comprehensive admission laboratory workup is performed.

Fourth, generalizability beyond MIMIC-IV requires careful evaluation. The cohort represents a single US tertiary academic center (Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center) and may not reflect practice in other settings. Important practice differences exist between US ICUs and ICUs elsewhere—including in China, where many of our intended readers practice—regarding albumin measurement frequency, transfusion thresholds, ICU admission criteria for sepsis, and use of empiric albumin supplementation. RAR thresholds and risk strata identified in MIMIC-IV may therefore require local recalibration before clinical use in other healthcare systems.

Conclusions

In this retrospective observational study of 9,206 sepsis patients from the MIMIC-IV database, RAR measured within 24 hours of ICU admission was independently associated with 90-day mortality after comprehensive covariate adjustment, with a non-linear exposure-response relationship and consistent associations across patient subgroups. RAR demonstrated modest but statistically significant incremental discriminative ability over SOFA and SIC scores (DeLong P<0.001), and improved risk reclassification when added to SOFA (continuous NRI=0.39, IDI=0.05, both P<0.001). For clinical application, our data support a tentative three-tier framework: patients with RAR <5 (90-day mortality ∼26%) represent a lower-risk group, RAR 5-7 (∼40%) intermediate, and RAR ≥7 (∼57%) higher-risk—warranting enhanced monitoring and consideration of more aggressive escalation strategies pending prospective validation. Because RAR derives from routinely available complete blood count and serum albumin measurements at no incremental cost, it is well suited as a complementary bedside risk indicator alongside established severity scores. These findings are hypothesis-generating; prospective multicenter validation across diverse healthcare settings is warranted before integration into clinical decision pathways.
