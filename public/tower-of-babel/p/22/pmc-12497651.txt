High-throughput synaptic connectivity mapping using in vivo two-photon holographic optogenetics and compressive sensing
Authors: I-Wen Chen, Chung Yuen Chan, Phillip Navarro, Vincent de Sars, Emiliano Ronzitti, Karim Oweiss, Dimitrii Tanese, Valentina Emiliani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCharacterizing synaptic connectivity in living neural circuits is key to understanding the interplay between network structure and function during behavior. However, the throughput of current in vivo synaptic mapping methods remains very limited. Here, we present a framework for increasing mapping throughput and speed that combines two-photon holographic optogenetic stimulation of presynaptic neurons, whole-cell recordings of postsynaptic responses and compressive sensing reconstruction of sparse connectivity. Under sequential single-cell stimulation, the method enables rapid probing of connectivity across up to 100 potential presynaptic cells within ~5 min in the visual cortex of anesthetized mice, identifying synaptic pairs along with their strength and spatial distribution. Furthermore, in sparsely connected populations, holographic multi-cell stimulation combined with a compressive sensing approach further improved sampling efficiency and recovered most connections found using the sequential approach, with up to a threefold reduction in the number of required measurements. Overall, these results highlight the potential for higher throughput in vivo circuit analysis and deeper insights into brain structure–function relationships.Subject terms: Neural circuits, Neurophysiology

Main

Mapping connectivity among neurons in awake animals is of the utmost importance for a comprehensive understanding of neural circuits mediating behavior1. In vivo investigations under normal physiological conditions are essential to avoid disruptions to circuit function when connections are severed during tissue slicing procedures postmortem2,3. Most importantly, doing so in a functionally defined cell population in vivo is critical to correlate network organization with the observed function and to decipher the influence of real physiological signals underlying motivation, attention and reward on the observed behavior.

Correlated activity in neuronal populations in fluorescence imaging or extracellular multiple single-unit recordings4,5 may provide coarse and indirect information about connectivity, but they are confounded by the possibility of common synaptic inputs driving this correlation. Ascertaining connectivity presence can only be achieved using causal induction of action potentials (APs) at the presynaptic site(s) and intracellular recording of the evoked response (current or voltage) at the postsynaptic site(s)6–11. Although this process is typically assayed during paired recording and stimulation using glass micropipettes, this approach makes it difficult to achieve and maintain multiple intracellular recordings for prolonged periods, thereby limiting the experimental throughput. As such, paired recording and stimulation have so far been limited to investigating connectivity among a handful of neurons (maximum of 12 neurons in vitro9) and have been characterized with low yield (~0.3 connections probed per mouse) in vivo11.

Optical approaches such as glutamate uncaging and optogenetics provide an alternative means to increase the number of probed connections. In the first case, presynaptic spiking is induced using single-photon12,13 or two-photon (2P)14–16 multi-point photolysis of caged glutamate around dendrites or soma. Under 2P excitation, glutamate uncaging enables AP induction at near cellular resolution and, consequently, probing connectivity over several hundred potential presynaptic cells15,16. However, glutamate uncaging, only possible ex vivo, lacks control over the timing and number of induced spikes as well as the diffusion of the caged compound, which can affect the spatial resolution and cell-type specificity of presynaptic activation. Alternatively, presynaptic activation can be induced in vivo17 and in vitro18,19 using single-photon optogenetic stimulation. Leveraging precise control of the channel kinetics and the light sensitivity of opsins, single-photon optogenetic stimulation has achieved optical control of the timing and number of induced presynaptic spikes20, but cellular resolution is achievable only at shallow depths and under sparse opsin expression, limiting the study of connectivity in vivo to a single presynaptic neuron17,21.

By contrast, 2P optogenetics enables both in vitro and in vivo AP generation at cellular resolution22 and provides a powerful tool to precisely control larger ensembles of potential presynaptic neurons. Specifically, 2P illumination methods using laser scanning23–26 or parallel light patterning27–35 ensure scattering-resistant actuation of opsins deep in brain tissue, which can reach cellular resolution when combined with soma-targeted opsins29,30,33. Moreover, 2P stimulation of opsins with high light sensitivity and fast channel kinetics ensures temporally precise AP generation of millisecond latency and sub-millisecond jitter in vitro30 and in vivo24,34, which is critical to correlate the timing of presynaptic AP induction with postsynaptic responses.

Initial proof-of-concept experiments in vitro have established optogenetic-based connectivity mapping29,30,36 that were followed by recent studies elucidating the synaptic organization in the mouse medial prefrontal cortex37, characterizing intralaminar and translaminar monosynaptic connections in the mouse visual cortex38,39 and investigating motor circuits in transparent zebrafish larvae in vivo40. Performing a similar study in the mammalian brain in vivo has some major challenges to overcome. For example, the presence of time-varying neuromodulatory activity, whole-cell patching instability and membrane potential fluctuations owing to spontaneous activity during ‘up’ and ‘down’ states must be considered. These factors, among others, largely affect the probability and temporal properties of both presynaptic APs and the ensuing postsynaptic responses, which are typically of small amplitudes (~1 mV or a few picoamperes (pA)). These limitations require averaging across repeated trials to reduce noise and require long recording durations that are not compatible with whole-cell patch-clamp, which makes routine in vivo large-scale mapping of synaptic connectivity out of reach.

Here, we present an experimental and computational strategy for in vivo high-throughput synaptic connectivity mapping in which optogenetics and temporally focused 2D and 3D holographic light-multiplexing31 are combined to control the spiking activity at single-cell resolution and with sub-millisecond precision. We further demonstrate the speed and throughput of the method by combining 2P optogenetic multi-cell stimulation with compressive sensing (CS)19,41–43, an approach that uses sparsity and incoherent sampling to find a solution to the unknown connectivity vector in an underdetermined linear system of equations44.

We first demonstrate that mapping can be achieved using rapid, sequential stimulation of single presynaptic neurons expressing the fast, light-sensitive, soma-restricted opsin ST-ChroME33 and whole-cell recording for postsynaptic readout. The approach allows for estimation of connectivity rates and synaptic properties of layer 2/3 (L2/3) intralaminar connectivity, probing up to 100 potential presynaptic neurons per experiment and identifying several connected pairs, their synaptic strength and spatial distribution in a few minutes. We further probed the same presynaptic populations using multi-cell targeting and CS reconstruction. In this case, numerical de-mixing of the resulting synaptic responses reduced the number of required measurements and recovered most connections (>80%) with a threefold reduction in the number of measurements needed when applied to a population with low (<4%) connectivity rates.

Overall, to our knowledge, these results represent the first in vivo demonstration of high-throughput synaptic connectivity mapping at cellular resolution in the mammalian brain.

Results

The optical system

Experiments were carried out in a custom-built optical system (Fig. 1a) that included a path for 2P galvanometric scan imaging and a path for 2P holographic stimulation (see Methods). The system used two-step phase modulation to generate 12 µm temporally focused spots via a static phase mask and multiplexed them with a liquid crystal spatial light modulator (SLM) (Fig. 1b)31. Combined with a 500 kHz high-power fiber laser (10–25 W at the source, ~1–2.5 W after the objective), the system enables generation of dozens of spots within a 350 × 350 × 400 µm3 field of view (FOV).

AP properties upon single-cell and multi-cell stimulation

To determine the stimulation conditions that enable reliable and temporally precise AP generation, we measured AP properties under 2P holographic illumination of single or multiple neurons expressing the fast, soma-restricted opsin ST-ChroME33 L2/3 of mouse V1. Using 2P-guided whole-cell or cell-attached recordings, we assessed spiking activity in opsin-positive neurons identified by red mRuby fluorescence and monitored electrophysiological properties (Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1a) to identify putative pyramidal cells (three out of 32 patched cells were identified as interneurons).

We characterized photoinduced spiking using three holographic patterns: ‘1 target’ (targeting the patched neuron only), ‘10 targets’ (targeting the surrounding neurons) and ‘10 + 1 targets’ (targeting patched plus surrounding neurons) (Fig. 2a). Similar to those used for connectivity mapping experiments (Fig. 2b), the ‘1 target’ and ‘10 + 1 targets’ patterns were used to characterize AP properties of target cells while the ‘10 targets’ pattern provided insights into the spatial selectivity of multi-spot stimulation.

Single-cell illumination (‘1 target’) was tested with power densities of 0.1–0.6 mW µm−2 and durations of 2–10 ms. Photoactivated APs within 20 ms of stimulation onset were achieved in 78.6% (33 out of 42) of neurons at a power of ≤0.3 mW µm−2. To maximize AP probability, we used a 10 ms illumination duration across different power ranges (<0.15, 0.15–0.3, >0.3 mW µm−2) and revealed an increase in AP probability, while AP latency, jitter and count showed no significant differences (Supplementary Fig. 1b and Supplementary Table 2). For connectivity experiments, we used a power density of 0.15–0.3 mW µm−2 and a duration of 10 ms, yielding an AP latency of 5.09 ± 0.38 ms, jitter of 0.99 ± 0.14 ms, AP count per pulse of 1.08 ± 0.11 and AP probability of 81.13 ± 5.34% (n = 29–37 cells). These parameters minimized presynaptic activation variability, enhancing the robustness of connectivity mapping.

Next, using the parameters defined above, we compared photostimulation under single-spot and multi-spot illumination. We observed induced APs with low latency and sub-ms jitter upon stimulation patterns of ‘1 target’ and ‘10 + 1 targets’, but only rarely (one out of 11 cells) upon stimulation of ‘10 targets’ (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Tables 3 and 4), indicating stimulation selectivity. Importantly, latency, jitter, AP count and AP probability did not vary significantly upon single-cell or multi-cell stimulation (Supplementary Table 3).

We characterized on-target and off-target activation under stimulation sequences with patterns (total of 262 patterns in three cells) containing a variable number of spots (F = 1, 8 and 15) that include (Extended Data Fig. 3b) or exclude (Supplementary Fig. 2) the patched cell. Using the same illumination parameters as in the connectivity experiments, we confirmed reliable photoactivation (>90% spiking probability), with <5 ms latency and <1 ms jitter, regardless of the number of spots (Extended Data Fig. 3b). Off-target activation was infrequent (0.8–3.7%) and comparable to spontaneous spiking (2.7%). Off-axis patterns (z = ±50 µm) had ~15% AP probability but showed lower reliability and longer latencies (Supplementary Fig. 2b).

Spatial selectivity upon single-cell and multi-cell stimulation

Next, we quantified the spatial selectivity of AP generation upon single-cell and multi-cell stimulation by measuring the photoinduced spiking probability while axially and laterally shifting the three stimulation patterns (‘1 target’, ‘10 targets’ or ‘10 + 1 targets’) (see Methods) using the stimulation parameters in the range identified in the previous section (0.2 mW µm−2 power density; 10 ms pulse duration).

Upon ‘1 target’ or ‘10 + 1 targets’ stimulation in which the patched cell was targeted, we calculated >80% AP probability at zero lateral or axial offsets, and this progressively decreased when laterally or axially shifting the illumination pattern (Fig. 2d,e). The Gaussian fits of the spatial dependencies of the AP probability were used to derive the physiological resolution, defined as the FWHMs of the curves (lateral ‘1 target’, 9.6 ± 2.8 µm, n = 4 cells; lateral ‘10 + 1 targets’, 14.9 ± 4.2 µm, n = 5 cells; axial ‘1 target’, 55.5 ± 9.3 µm, n = 10 cells; axial ‘10 + 1 targets’, 62.9 ± 16.2 µm, n = 8 cells) (Fig. 2e). AP latencies as well as off-axis subthreshold depolarization are also reported (Supplementary Fig. 3).

The FWHMs of the AP probability align with trends obtained from convolving the spot profiles with the cell body and considering a typical photocurrent’s power dependence (see Methods and Supplementary Fig. 4). Unlike the lateral ‘top hat’ profile, the Gaussian z-profile produces axial tails, widening physiological resolution. In addition, using a single power level to maximize spiking probability, even in weakly expressing cells, broadens resolution by approaching opsin saturation. This effect could be minimized by using a power level adjusted for each cell based on its expression level.

Similar to a previous publication37, we estimated the 3D spiking probability function (SPF) as a binary function with lateral and axial size corresponding to the respective AP probability FWHMs (Fig. 2e(ii,iv)).

SPF analysis for single-spot and multi-spot stimulation showed slight broadening in the latter, particularly in some cells (Supplementary Fig. 5), probably because of imperfect soma targeting of opsin expression. However, the dataset was insufficient to confirm statistical significance. We used the minimum and maximum measured SPF values as the lower and upper limits for photostimulation spatial selectivity, respectively.

Neurons within the ellipsoidal SPF were considered co-activated, while those outside were not. This characterization may overestimate off-target APs within the ellipsoid and underestimate those outside. However, lower light intensities at the periphery probably result in less reliable and delayed APs (Supplementary Figs. 2b and 3b), minimizing their impact on detecting postsynaptic currents.

To estimate the density of opsin-expressing neurons, we extracted cell coordinates from six representative FOVs (350 × 350 × 100 µm) and found densities ranging from 25,800 to 118,000 cells per mm³ (63.9 ± 12.1 × 10³ cells per mm³) (Fig. 2f(ii)). Using these positions, we estimated the off-target AP probability (POT) for single-cell and multi-cell stimulation by counting positive cells within the SPFs around each neuron. As expected, POT increased with cell density, reaching up to 40% at full density under multi-cell (F = 11) stimulation, although actual values were lower in our experiments. The average POT for single-cell stimulation across the six FOVs was 6.4 ± 1.8%, similar to previous slice experiments37,38 (Fig. 2f(ii) and Supplementary Table 5).

For sparse connections (<10%), this off-target activation would result in an overestimation of the connectivity rate by approximately 0.6% and, even in the denser sample (POT ~ 12%; Fig. 2f(ii)), it would not exceed 1.2% (Supplementary Fig. 6, see also refs. 37,38). For multi-cell stimulation, the slight broadening of the SPF, although minimal, leads to a larger POT (18.6 ± 5.3%) (Fig. 2f(ii)), which might contribute to false-positive (FP) connections identification, as discussed later.

Notably, potential misidentification of presynaptic cells owing to off-target activation can be ruled out by performing a few additional stimulations near the identified presynaptic neuron and monitoring the effect on the postsynaptic response, as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 7. Similar to reports in a previous publication36, variations in latency and response reliability can help to identify the true source of synaptic input.

In vivo connectivity mapping with 2P holographic stimulation

Two strategies were used for in vivo connectivity mapping in L2/3 (Extended Data Fig. 4a): sequential connectivity mapping, in which we sequentially activated single potential presynaptic cells one by one (Fig. 3); and parallel connectivity mapping, in which we sequentially activated sub-groups of multiple potential presynaptic neurons (Fig. 4). In the latter case, the contributions of each presynaptic cell to the postsynaptic responses were reconstructed by using CS algorithms (Extended Data Fig. 4a,b), as explained in the following paragraphs. In both cases, we performed 2P-guided voltage-clamp recording to measure the excitatory synaptic current in a postsynaptic pyramidal neuron of L2/3.

Recording from opsin-negative neurons prevented the generation of photocurrent from direct opsin activation (Supplementary Fig. 1c). Holding the membrane potential at −70 mV (the reversal potential of inhibitory conductance) focused our acquisition on connections between putative excitatory neurons and also prevented spikes in the patched cell, thereby avoiding confounding individual postsynaptic inputs.

We extracted the coordinates of the opsin-positive neurons (N) surrounding the patched cell to define a set of potential presynaptic cells. Accordingly, we designed a stimulation procedure consisting of a number of measurements (M), each corresponding to a stimulation with a distinct holographic light pattern illuminating F targets: for sequential connectivity mapping, M = N and F = 1; for parallel mapping, M ≤ N and F > 1 (typically with F = 5–10; Supplementary Table 6 and Extended Data Fig. 4a).

Sequential connectivity mapping

In all experiments, we used stimulation conditions (0.2–0.3 mW µm−2 intensity; 10 ms pulse duration) that, as discussed above, enabled AP generation with high reliability, millisecond latency and sub-millisecond jitter (Fig. 2c). We investigated 12 FOVs, corresponding to 12 postsynaptic neurons in 12 animals. Each FOV comprised 20–99 targeted putative presynaptic opsin-expressing cells (on average, N = 45.8 ± 5.6 per experiment), coplanar with the patched cell (corresponding to 91 ± 5% of the visible opsin-expressing cells) within a distance comprising between 18.2 and 341.0 µm (549 potential presynaptic cells). Precalculated holographic phase masks for different illumination patterns were sequentially displayed on the SLM, and each putative presynaptic cell was stimulated 24–60 times to average the postsynaptic currents and assess response rates. Repetitive high-frequency (a few Hz) stimulation was avoided to prevent potential short-term synaptic plasticity11. Of note, for individual stimulation, individual postsynaptic traces ranging from 0 to 34 (that is, 0–70% of the total repetitions) were discarded because of large fluctuations of spontaneous activity, resulting in 9–42 remaining repetitions (that is, 30–100% of the total repetitions). Postsynaptic currents were defined based on two main criteria: an average peak amplitude exceeding the 2–3 pA threshold, corresponding to the typical recording noise level (Extended Data Fig. 5a–c); and a statistical test on individual traces across repetitions, comparing current stimulation-triggered averages (STAs) within a specific time window (Extended Data Fig. 5b) before and after photostimulation. From 549 potential presynaptic neurons across 12 FOVs, we identified 41 connections (3.25 ± 2.6 connections per FOV, ranging from one to nine). Several representative examples of postsynaptic responses identified in planar or volumetric FOVs are presented in Fig. 3a, Extended Data Figs. 5 and 6 and Supplementary Figs. 8 and 9. Overall, the identified connection rate, estimated as the ratio between responding sites in a FOV over the total number of probed presynaptic cells, was 7.6 ± 1.5%, ranging from 1.9% to 15.4% across the FOVs.

From each identified connection, we then characterized the response rates (Fig. 3b) as well as the amplitudes and kinetics (peak latency, rise time, decay time constant and current width; Fig. 3c, Supplementary Figs. 8 and 9 and Supplementary Table 7). For the average current traces, the identified synaptic responses displayed peak amplitudes of a few pA (5.0 ± 0.6 pA; the strongest detected response was 24 pA), fast rise time (4.3 ± 0.3 ms) and short onset latency (5.8 ± 0.4 ms). The latter, close to the photoinduced presynaptic AP latency (3.94 ± 0.53 ms; Supplementary Table 3), indicated a short synaptic delay and supported the monosynaptic nature of the identified connections11. The magnitude of postsynaptic current responses was quantified from the STA between 10 and 15 ms after stimulation (a typical time interval for responses occurrence; Extended Data Fig. 5b). The STA amplitude distribution from connected and non-connected cells were found to be largely non-overlapping, and values from the latter group approached zero (Extended Data Fig. 5d), confirming the accuracy of our identification method and the reliability of STA as a response proxy.

Although averaging across time and trials may result in a loss of some current response shape information, using a short time window after stimulation favors short-latency responses and minimizes contributions from spontaneous, polysynaptic or off-target inputs that are less precisely time-locked to the stimulation.

The response rate of each connection, defined as the probability of detecting synaptic current across individual traces, was 0.70 ± 0.027 (range, 0.39–1.00). This value, combined with the high probability in the presynaptic AP generation over multiple stimulations (previously characterized as >90%; Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 3), provides a good estimate of the synaptic failure rate. A few representative examples of average and individual trial current responses, from connected and non-connected cells, are shown in Supplementary Fig. 10, corresponding to four patterns in the FOV (reported later in Fig. 4b(iii)). Notably, response rates from these connected cells did not show significant variations across repetitions (Supplementary Fig. 11), indicating the reliability of presynaptic stimulation and the absence of response nonstationarity.

Connection properties versus presynaptic and postsynaptic soma distance

Next, we analyzed the dependence of the connection rate, strength and response on the distance between presynaptic and postsynaptic cell somata. Inter-somatic lateral distance between postsynaptic and potential presynaptic cells ranged from 18.2 µm to 341.0 µm (506 potential presynaptic cells over 11 FOVs). Out of the 40 identified presynaptic cells, most (36 out of 40; 90%) were located within 200 µm of the patched neuron, and no connections were identified above a distance of 250 µm (Fig. 3d(i)). Inter-somatic distance seemed to affect the connection rate, which decreased with distance (Fig. 3d(ii,iii)), but had no major effect on either the connection strength or the response rate (Fig. 3d(iv)). Of note, within the probed regions (medial-caudal), a comparison of the angular distribution between probed and connected populations revealed no preferential directionality of the connection rate (Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, P = 0.84; Extended Data Fig. 7).

Parallel mapping with multi-cell stimulation and CS

To increase the throughput of connectivity mapping, we tested a parallel approach in which we activated sub-groups of putative presynaptic neurons simultaneously and applied CS to reconstruct connectivity from the evoked postsynaptic response41,42. CS produces conditions under which a system of equations can be solved with fewer measurements than unknowns. The basic CS framework requires sparsity of connectivity, incoherently sampled measurements and linear summation of inputs, although multiple nonlinear extensions to the basic CS framework exist whereby linearity assumptions can be relaxed45. The results from sequential connectivity mapping showed an average connectivity rate of ~7.6%, which decreased with inter-cell distance (Fig. 3d), satisfying the sparsity condition for the CS application. Incoherent sampling was achieved using multi-spot holographic patterns, stimulating distinct sub-groups of potential presynaptic neurons in each trial44. Although both linear46,47 and nonlinear summation48,49 of inputs may occur, verifying linearity in each experiment is important to ensure that the basic CS assumptions are met.

Parallel mapping: simulations, metrics and parameters

To design the patterned stimulation protocol, we simulated a model network of Izhikevich model neurons with a user-defined connectivity rate (k) and a realistic excitatory and inhibitory synaptic weight distribution42. We simultaneously stimulated F out of the N presynaptic cells in M different patterns and recorded the evoked postsynaptic current on each trial. We used peak postsynaptic current responses as features in a basis pursuit CS algorithm41,44 to infer the estimated connectivity and compared them to the known connectivity in the model (see Methods).

In brief, the CS algorithm minimizes 12||y−Ax||2+λ||x||1, where y represents evoked postsynaptic currents, A is the M × N measurement matrix (describing the stimulation patterns) and x denotes synaptic strengths. The first term measures reconstruction error, while the second term, rescaled by the regularization hyperparameter λ, enforces sparsity to prevent overfitting.

To mimic the experimental conditions, simulations were performed over two different population sizes (N = 30 and 100) and connectivity rates (6.7% and 10%) corresponding to a total average number of connected cells (K) equal to two and ten, respectively. Reconstruction yields true positive (TP), true negative (TN), FP and false negative (FN) classifications, and performance was assessed by precision (TP / (TP + FP), indicating the fidelity of reconstruction, recall (TP / (TP + FN), representing the ability to detect connections and accuracy ((TP + TN) / N), an overall ratio of correct detections) (Extended Data Fig. 8a).

Simulations showed up to 96% accuracy with more than twofold reduction in measurements compared to single-cell stimulation, with better performance at lower connectivity rates (k ≤ 10%) and higher F values, providing better coverage of the population (Extended Data Fig. 8b–e).

Improvements saturated for F higher than 20%, suggesting that the use of a high number of spots per pattern (and consequently achieving better coverage) might not be particularly beneficial. Indeed simultaneous stimulation of many neurons might either increase off-target activation (Fig. 2e,f), potentially induce polysynaptic inputs or amplify nonlinearities in synaptic integration. Therefore, we designed the experimental protocol using stimulation patterns with F = 4 to F = 13 stimulated cells, corresponding, on average, to 19 ± 7% of the presynaptic population N, where each cell is targeted by at least two patterns, yet still resulting in a threefold reduction in the number of measurements.

Postsynaptic activity under multi-cell stimulation

In the same 12 FOVs shown in Fig. 3b,c, and under analogous excitation conditions (power density, 0.2 mW µm−2; pulse duration, 10 ms), we performed patterned stimulation (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 12) using 25–50 patterns for each FOV. According to the model results described above, each of the patterns targeted on average F = 8 ± 2.5 spots per pattern (range, 4–13) out of the N putative presynaptic cells, ranging between 20 and 99 cells. Each multi-cell stimulation pattern was repeated 18–60 times, refreshing the SLM pattern either every pulse or every series of six pulses, and individual traces with large fluctuations of spontaneous activity (0–37 repetitions per stimulation pattern; that is, 0–69% of the total repetitions) were excluded, resulting in 6–43 repetitions per stimulation pattern that were further analyzed (see Methods).

Upon stimulation of multiple putative presynaptic cells, the postsynaptic cell displayed an excitatory current of short onset latency (Fig. 4a(ii),b(ii) and Supplementary Fig. 12) resembling the synaptic current responses to presynaptic spiking (Fig. 3a(ii)). The criteria for determining postsynaptic current responses upon stimulation of 210 out of 395 multi-cell patterns were identical to those under single-cell stimulation (see Methods).

To quantitatively examine how the postsynaptic neuron integrated multiple presynaptic inputs, we calculated the STA amplitudes 10–15 ms after stimulation onset from the averaged postsynaptic current traces for both single-cell and multi-cell stimulations (see Methods). For each multi-cell pattern stimulation, its STA amplitude was compared to the sum of those calculated from the averaged response traces obtained in single-cell stimulation trials (Fig. 4a(iv,v),b(iii,iv)).

The STA amplitudes corresponding to multi-cell stimulation were positively correlated with the sum of those corresponding to single-cell stimulation (average correlation coefficient on the 12 FOVs, 0.66 ± 0.05; range, 0.37–0.91; P = 0.045, 0.0064 and 0.0012 for three experiments and P < 0.001 for nine experiments) (Fig. 4c(i)). We extended the same analysis by splitting the 12 FOVs into two groups: those containing one connected cell, for which no summation was expected under the multi-cell stimulation (K = 1, four FOVs) (Fig. 4c(ii)), and those containing >1 connected cell (K > 1, eight FOVs) (Fig. 4c(iii)). In all FOVs, we measured a positive correlation (average correlation coefficient on the 12 FOVs, 0.68 ± 0.10; range, 0.45–0.91; P = 0.0012 for one experiment and P < 0.001 for three experiments in the first group; coefficient, 0.65 ± 0.06, range, 0.37–0.81; P = 0.045, 0.0064 for two experiments and P < 0.001 for six experiments in the second group).

The slopes of the linear fits of the STA upon multi-cell stimulation versus the sum of those upon single-cell stimulation were 0.59 ± 0.09, ranging from 0.23 to 1.28 (n = 12 experiments). By merging all the 12 FOVs, we obtained a linear fit with a slope of 0.49 (395 multi-cell patterns in the 12 experiments) (Fig. 4c(i)), while the average slopes across experiments were 0.50 ± 0.16 (range, 0.28–0.95) in the pool of experiments with one connected cell (Fig. 4c(ii)) and 0.64 ± 0.11 (range, 0.23–1.28) in the case of >1 connected cell (Fig. 4c(iii)). In general, we noticed a large variability across FOVs for the correlation coefficients and slopes of the linear fits. In particular, several FOVs showed linear fits with slopes <1, suggesting that STA amplitudes under multi-cell stimulation were smaller than the sum of those under single-cell stimulation.

In addition to the overall positive correlation between multi-cell responses and the summation of single-cell responses, this deviation from perfect linear summation is an essential factor with which to evaluate the performance of CS strategies, as discussed further below.

Reconstructing connectivity using CS

We next tested whether responses recorded under multi-cell stimulation can be demixed to identify connected cells using an adaptive basis pursuit CS algorithm (see Methods). In each FOV of N potential presynaptic cells, responses to M measurements of simultaneous stimulation of F cells (such as those in Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 12; M range, 25–50 or 0.3–1.25 × N; 12 FOVs) were reduced to scalar values representing the STA amplitudes of averaged postsynaptic current traces, which served as inputs to the reconstruction algorithm. A connection-defining threshold was established to determine the presence or absence of connections in the CS reconstruction (Fig. 5a,b and Supplementary Table 6; see Methods). The performance of the reconstruction was evaluated according to precision and recall (Extended Data Fig. 8a). For each FOV, we precalculated a number of multi-cell stimulation patterns M, varying between 25 and 50, corresponding to approximately 0.3–1.25 × N. We then performed CS reconstruction over the responses to m stimulation patterns (subsets of the entire number set of M patterns available), corresponding to different compression ratios (CRs, defined as N / m) (Fig. 5a(iii-iv),b(iii-iv); see Methods). This parameter, inverse of the subsampling (m / N), also indicates the relative speed gain of parallel over sequential connectivity mapping, independent of the number of probed cells in each FOV (Extended Data Fig. 4a). In practical application, the experimental time required is inversely proportional to the CR (Extended Data Fig. 9). For example, a CR of three can reduce the recording time of the 12 FOVs from 1–6 min to 0.3–2 min. We evaluated the effect of both measurement subsampling and connectivity rate on reconstruction performance. As expected, precision and recall increased with increasing m, or equivalently when decreasing the CR (Fig. 5a(iii-iv),b(iii-iv),c,d), underlining a trade-off between reconstruction accuracy and measurement reduction. Under CR = 2 (CR = 3), precision decreased to 0.42 ± 0.27 (0.38 ± 0.19, mean ± s.d.) and recall to 0.68 ± 0.29 (0.61 ± 0.26) (Supplementary Table 6), yet we were able to reconstruct more than half of the total connections at 17 out of 32 in 11 FOVs (22 out of 41 for CR = 3 in 12 FOVs).

Overall, better reconstruction was achieved for experiments with sparser connectivity, with a more pronounced impact on recall values (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Table 8). For example, after sorting the 12 FOVs according to the connectivity rates found in single-target experiments, we found that connectivity rates below 6.5% (3.5 ± 1.9%; six FOVs, mean ± s.d.) resulted in 100% recall (eight out of eight connections) at CR = 1.23 ± 0.17 (all available measurements). Even at CR = 3, recall remained high at 0.77 ± 0.25, while precision, although reduced, was 0.37 ± 0.24. For denser connectivity (11.6 ± 3.8% for the other six FOVs), both precision and recall decreased to 0.40 ± 0.16 and 0.45 ± 0.17, respectively, for CR = 3 (Supplementary Table 8). Generally, this dependence on connection sparsity agrees with the theoretical basis of CS and previous modeling studies41,42.

The results reported in Fig. 5 were obtained with a fixed regularization factor (λ) of 0.1 and a fixed threshold for defining the presence or absence of a connection. We also investigated the effect of lower or higher λ, preferring either recall or precision, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 15b–d). Similarly, the connection-defining threshold to identify connected cells represents an additional adjustable hyperparameter that can strike a good balance between increasing recall (for example, from ~0.65% to 0.85%), albeit at a higher FP rate (precision would reduce from ~0.4% to ~0.2%) (Supplementary Fig. 15e,f).

To investigate the role of nonlinear synaptic integration, we compared reconstruction results using the STAs from the responses to patterned stimulation with those obtained using the summation of responses to single-cell sequential stimulation (mimicking a perfect linear integration). We found that overall, recall performance in experimental data was not significantly different, while precision significantly decreased when patterned stimulation was used, in particular for FOVs showing highly sublinear summations (Extended Data Fig. 10a). Network modeling provided a similar trend (Extended Data Fig. 10b), effectively demonstrating that precision is the variable most significantly affected by nonlinear synaptic integration. This was primarily because of the increase in FPs, consistent with the idea that postsynaptic response attenuation, possibly owing to lateral inhibition from stimulated cells, could be taking place.

Discussion

We present an approach for high-throughput in vivo synaptic connectivity mapping, combining 2P optogenetics with spatiotemporally shaped parallel stimulation and CS. We achieved optical control of presynaptic activation at cellular resolution by using 2P excitation, with temporally focused holographic spots, of neuronal somata expressing the fast, efficient, soma-restricted opsin ST-ChroME33, while the induced postsynaptic responses were whole-cell recorded. We demonstrated two methods for high-throughput connectivity mapping. In the first method, the connections between a postsynaptic cell and N putative presynaptic cells, contained within a 350 × 350 × 100 µm volume, were probed through single-cell sequential activation of the N cells. In the second method, connections were probed in the same population by replacing single-cell stimulation with parallel stimulation of subsets of F putative presynaptic cells41. In this case, the contribution of each presynaptic cell to the individual postsynaptic response was demixed using a CS algorithm.

The combination of temporally focused holographic light shaping and soma-targeted opsins provides the necessary spatiotemporal precision needed for in vivo connectivity mapping. Specifically, confirming previous findings29,30,33,34, we have shown that soma illumination enables the generation of reliable APs with latencies of a few milliseconds and sub-millisecond temporal jittering, and that the temporal resolution and precision were also maintained during multi-target illumination. Given that monosynaptic responses typically occur within <5 ms after presynaptic AP firing8,10,11, this precise knowledge of the presynaptic spiking time is essential to discriminate true synaptic responses from spontaneous or polysynaptic events. Combining temporally focused holographic excitation with the soma-targeted opsin ST-ChroME, we have demonstrated similar spatial precision in photostimulation between single-cell and multi-cell stimulation, with lateral and axial spatial SPF ranging between 10–15 µm and 56–63 µm, respectively.

These values reflect a balance between using sufficient power to reliably trigger presynaptic spikes and maintaining spatial resolution. Future improvements, such as transgenic lines with more uniform opsin expression, could enable lower power use and better resolution. Off-target activation also depends on the density of opsin-expressing cells, as sparser expression can improve precision. Moreover, when driven by cell-type-specific promoters, this also enables cell-type-resolved connectivity mapping38, and CS reconstruction performance can be enhanced by incorporating cell-type information42. Finally, to further reduce the uncertainty caused by off-target excitation, strategies like shifted stimulation patterns (Supplementary Fig. 7 and ref. 36) or volumetric calcium imaging around the target cell40 could be used, although they may add complexity or increase the number of required measurements.

Sequential mapping: features and improvements

We have optimized an experimental pipeline that incorporates fast GPU-based computation of photostimulation holograms and precise fine-tuning of stimulation sequences, in which targets are switched with each photostimulation pulse for high signal-to-noise detection of postsynaptic currents. With this approach, we can probe 100 presynaptic sites in approximately 5 min, while minimizing potential opsin inactivation and possible plasticity effects.

Overall, this approach has achieved an experimental yield of 3.4 connections probed per mouse (41 connections across 12 mice), representing a tenfold increase compared to what has been achieved in published in vivo electrophysiological studies11. A larger number of connection pairs could be probed by shortening the interval between sequential stimulations (ts), which is limited by the SLM refresh time (~50 ms), the photostimulation duration and recording time. In our setup, ts was ~100 ms (Extended Data Fig. 4a); however, faster SLMs (for example, 300 Hz refresh rate)24 or rapid multi-hologram scanning methods50 combined with deconvolution algorithms43 could significantly reduce ts.

Single-cell sequential activation revealed ~8% connectivity rate out of >500 presynaptic neurons probed. This value needs to be compared with the values of 2–19% reported in the literature for in vitro studies of excitatory connections in L2/3 of mouse visual cortex, obtained either through multi-electrode recording or optogenetic approaches7,8,36,38. Higher connectivity rates would be expected in vivo because of the absence of connection losses caused by tissue slicing procedures2,3. However, several factors might have led to this underestimation. First, the use of the hSyn promoter could induce opsin expression in both excitatory and inhibitory neurons (~10% in this study), while voltage-clamp at −70 mV reveals excitatory inputs only, thereby lowering the estimated connectivity rate. Second, an imperfect spatial voltage-clamp could preferentially detect proximal synaptic inputs arriving at basal dendrites while reducing the amplitude and detectability of more distal ones along apical dendrites. Third, the noisier whole-cell recording in vivo compared to slice preparations and the stricter conditions we set to identify reliable postsynaptic responses could have precluded detecting weak connections (connection strength of <2–3 pA or those with high synaptic failure rates). Fourth, non-uniform opsin expression might have resulted in a fraction of failures to depolarize presynaptic cells (measured as ~20% of the opsin-positive cells).

Parallel mapping: performance, applicability and optimization

Using multi-cell patterned stimulation combined with CS, we have demonstrated that, depending on the CR, we can retrieve between 28 (CR = 1.4 ± 0.2) and 22 (CR = 3) out of the 41 total connections identified by sequential mapping (12 FOV, 12 mice). We found that the reconstruction performance was affected by the sparsity, with improved recall and decreased FN rates when connectivity rates were low (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Table 8). The accuracy of reconstruction was higher in FOVs with sparse connectivity (~3.5%), where even CR = 3 was sufficient to retrieve the connected cells with on average ~0.8 recall (~0.4 FNs per FOV) and ~0.4 precision (~3 FPs per FOV). For FOVs with higher connectivity rates (~11.5%), at CR = 2 and CR = 3, the approach missed ~2.5 connected cells (FNs) per FOV at a FP rate of ~10% (~4 FPs per FOV; Supplementary Table 8). To minimize FPs, a small fraction of connections can be verified by subsequent single-spot sequential stimulation, requiring typically ~10–15% additional measurements and minimal impact on the CR, which could also enable more accurate connection strength estimates.

Furthermore, fine-tuning the regularization parameter λ and the threshold values to identify connected cells could further guide the reconstruction toward denser or sparser solutions, reducing either FPs or FNs according to specific experimental needs (Supplementary Fig. 15).

The basic form of CS requires linear integration of inputs, but multiple effects can deviate from perfect summation of synaptic currents. As already discussed, these might include both passive dendritic properties (that is, reduced synaptic driving force51) and active ones (that is, activation of voltage-gated potassium channels46 or concomitant inhibitory inputs52).

Specifically, with respect to nonlinear synaptic integration, direct or indirect inhibitory inputs can influence the linear summation of excitatory inputs. Direct inhibitory inputs may result from on-target activation of opsin-positive interneurons, whose expression is driven by the hSyn promoter we used. Indirect inhibitory inputs may result from polysynaptic recruitment of inhibition53. For instance, AP firing in single or multiple target excitatory cells could exert disynaptic inhibition onto neighboring non-target excitatory cells by parvalbumin-positive interneurons54 or somatostatin-positive interneurons55,56. However, we believe that the level of recruited inhibition in our experiments did not significantly reduce presynaptic spiking, as AP probability for targeted neurons remained >90% even with simultaneous stimulation of up to 15 neurons (Extended Data Fig. 3B). On the other hand, inhibitory inputs leaking from imperfect voltage-clamp, particularly at distal dendrites, could have attenuated excitatory postsynaptic current recordings. This could explain the observed sublinear summation of synaptic currents (average slope, ~0.6), thereby reducing precision in parallel mapping (Extended Data Fig. 10), even in the absence of compression.

Related to postsynaptic integration, supralinear summation driven by NMDA receptor activation at clustered inputs on the same dendrite could have also had a role48,49, although this is unlikely given that no more than three to four connected cells were simultaneously stimulated. Lastly, instrumentation or technical reasons, such as variations in access resistance during in vivo patch-clamp, could have also contributed to instability and additional variability in the response amplitudes between sequential and parallel mapping (Supplementary Fig. 14).

Despite the occurrence of reduced response amplitude under multi-target stimulation, CS remained robust—particularly when binary discriminating between connected and non-connected cells—compared to reconstructing full response amplitudes, as global rescaling of the reconstructed amplitude has minimal impact on clustering as long as the amplitudes stay above the noise level. On the other hand, when artificially imposing linear summation (Extended Data Fig. 10), both numerical simulations and experiments suggest that performance improves. This indicates that improved understanding of the underlying factors, optimizing conditions for linear integration, or considering nonlinear forms of CS, could significantly enhance the method’s performance.

Theoretical work41 and simulations (Supplementary Fig. 13) indicate that synaptic failures—both presynaptic and postsynaptic—could constitute a large predictor of reconstruction error. As in sequential mapping, combining opsins with activity indicators can help confirm presynaptic activation, detect off-target effects and monitor spontaneous activity to adjust the stimulation matrix over time29,37,40. In particular, the use of voltage indicators on presynaptic cells could enable correction for spike timing jitter57, which is crucial for evaluating synaptic summation and for accurate reconstruction42.

Overall, although in vivo parallel mapping might need further refinement and increased accuracy, especially in dense networks, compared to sequential mapping or ex vivo implementation43, it holds great potential under certain conditions. It may be especially useful when a full connectivity map is not required, but targeted tracking and manipulation of a few connections is sufficient to study phenomena like short-term plasticity or synaptic integration. Moreover, its best performance achieved under sparse connectivity highlights its promise for identifying rare connections within populations of hundreds or thousands of presynaptic cells, which is a scale that is currently inaccessible or highly impractical with single-cell sequential mapping. In such scenarios, efficient and rapid mapping of large and weakly connected networks requires balancing the number and spatial distribution of F spots per pattern to cover the entire tissue volume while minimizing off-target stimulation. As previously shown42, network sparsity and synaptic reliability primarily determine performance, while increasing F improves reconstruction up to a point past which high network activity masks postsynaptic responses. In our experiments, given F ~ 8, ~8% connectivity and ~5 pA synaptic amplitude, postsynaptic spiking was unlikely, although the likelihood of such an event increases with denser networks or more excitable neurons.

In this work, stimulation parameters (F, M) and patterns were predefined before the experiment. Efficiency could be improved through adaptive sampling, by adjusting stimulation patterns in real time based on prior responses (for example, excluding non-responding spots). This would require on-the-fly response analysis, rapid SLM refresh rates (~3 ms) and optimized, fast hologram generation (for example, using deep learning-based methods)58.

Optimizing the number of patterns (M) and repetitions (R) is also an important experimental design goal. We used up to N patterns with multiple repetitions to improve SNR and study synaptic summation. However, modeling suggests that using more unique patterns with fewer repetitions—while keeping total trials (M × R) constant—can yield equal or better performance (Supplementary Fig. 17). Tailoring this balance to the expected synaptic amplitude and noise level could further enhance mapping speed and efficiency.

Both sequential and parallel mapping benefit from large stimulation volumes, enabling studies of distributed populations across spatially extended brain areas. Our system accesses a 350 × 350 × 400 µm3 volume with uniform spot sizes and intensity correction for deeper regions, potentially covering one or more cortical layers and allowing intralaminar connectivity studies. Lateral accessible regions could also be enlarged by improving optical design59 and SLMs. In parallel mapping, larger volumes would not only expand the accessible neuronal population but would also increase the average distance between presynaptic and postsynaptic cells, thereby reducing effective connectivity rates, which would be an advantage for CS.

In this work, postsynaptic responses were performed using electrophysiology. Recent progress in 2P voltage imaging allows in vivo measurement of spiking activity and subthreshold signals57,60. This suggests that the development of new indicators (for example, with enhanced and linear sensitivity curves) should allow combining presynaptic optogenetic stimulation with postsynaptic imaging of both evoked subthreshold and suprathreshold voltage responses, thus enabling all-optical connectivity mapping in chronic settings.

In summary, this study successfully demonstrated in vivo optogenetics-based mapping of synaptic connectivity in the mammalian brain, achieving results that were previously unattainable. Notably, sequential presynaptic photostimulation enabled rapid probing of numerous connected cells per minute of recording and facilitated the reconstruction of a detailed local map of excitatory connections within L2/3 of the mouse visual cortex. We investigated limits and conditions in the application of parallel mapping and CS, highlighting trade-offs between accuracy and degree of sparseness. We believe that with further refinements, this approach holds great promise for enhancing the throughput in probing connectivity, particularly for long-range mapping across spatially distinct and sparsely connected brain regions61.

Methods

Animals

All animal experiments were performed in accordance with Directive 2010/63/EU of the European Parliament and of the Council of 22 September 2010. The protocols (APAFIS#14267–201803261541580 v3) were approved by the authors’ institutional ethics committee for animal research (CEED5). Experiments were performed by using adult male C57BL/6J mice (Janvier Labs). Mice were kept at 22 °C ambient temperature and 50% humidity on a 12 h light, 12 h dark cycle. Food and water were provided ad libitum. Some experiments of AP properties upon single-cell photostimulation were performed on adult female or male mice of the transgenic line GP4.3 (Jackson Laboratory), which express the calcium indicators GCaMP6s62 (n = 4 cells for characterizing presynaptic activation by patch-clamp recordings in four mice). Viral injections for expressing opsins in cortical neurons were performed on 4-week-old mice. Holographic stimulation experiments measuring induced spiking activity or mapping synaptic connectivity were carried out on mice 3–12 weeks after injection.

Viral injection and surgical procedures

Stereotaxic injection of viral vectors was performed when the mice were anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of a ketamine–xylazine mixture (0.1 mg of ketamine plus 0.01 mg of xylazine per g body weight). During photostimulation experiments, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane; given that isoflurane can potentially reduce neuronal excitability and synaptic transmission63, anesthesia was maintained using a light dose during experiments (2% for induction, 0.5–1% during experiments). Viral vectors AAV9-hSyn-DIO-ChroME-Flag-ST-P2A-H2B-mRuby3-WPRE-SV40 in combination with AAV9-hSyn-Cre-WPRE-hGH were used to express ST-ChroME. Through a craniotomy over V1 (3.5 mm caudal from bregma, 2.5 mm lateral from midline), 0.8–1 µl of viral vectors were infused by a cannula 250 µm deep (L2/3 of V1) at a speed of 80–100 nl min−1. To perform acute photostimulation experiments in vivo, the mouse was head-fixed by attaching the skull to a small metal plate. A circular craniotomy of 2 mm diameter was made over V1, and the dura mater was removed. Agarose of 0.5–2% and a cover glass were applied on top of the craniotomy to dampen the tissue movement.

Optical system for 2P holographic stimulation

The custom-built optical system included two paths enabling 2P holographic stimulation and 2P scanning imaging (Fig. 1a).

The configuration for generating 2P volumetric holographic light patterns by using computer-generated holography with temporal focusing has been previously described31. The stimulation laser (fiber amplifier laser; 1,030 nm, 250 fs pulse-width, operated at 500 kHz, maximum power 10 or 25 W; Satsuma HP or HP3, Amplitude Systems)64 first passed through a static phase mask (Double Helix Optics) to generate a circular holographic light spot. The laser beam then passed through a Fourier lens (L5, f = 190 mm) and projected (L6–7, f = 400 mm and 400 mm) on a blazed grating (830 lines per mm) for temporal focusing65. The diffracted first-order beam was collimated by a lens (L8, f = 750 mm) and projected onto a liquid crystal SLM (Hamamatsu Photonics, X10468-07). The SLM was controlled by MATLAB (MathWorks) and the custom-designed software Wavefront IV (ref. 66), which implemented a GPU-accelerated Gerchberg–Saxton algorithm (adapted from previous work67) to calculate the phase profiles required to multiplex the holographic spot generated by the static phase mask68. The beam size was then adjusted (telescope L9,10, f = 500, 750 mm) to fill the pupil of the objective (Nikon W APO NIR of ×40, or Nikon CF175 LWD of ×16). A handmade zero-order block after the focus of L9 physically suppressed the zero-order diffraction of SLM. The triggering and power control of the stimulation laser were implemented by combining a custom-written MATLAB script, a National Instruments card, a digitizer (Digidata 1550A, Molecular Devices) and Clampex software (Molecular Devices).

For the 2P scanning imaging, also similar to previous work34, a Ti:Sapphire laser (Coherent, Chameleon Vision 2) was power-controlled by a liquid crystal variable-phase retarder (Meadowlark Optics, LRC-200-IR1) and a polarizer tube (Meadowlark Optics, BB-050-IR1). The beam was directed at a pair of galvanometric mirrors (3 mm aperture; Cambridge Technology, 6215H series) placed between two telescopes (L12, f = 75, 175 mm and L3,4, f = 50, 350 mm) and recombined with the stimulation lasers before the objective by a dichroic mirror. The emitted fluorescence signal from the sample, reflected by a long-pass dichroic, was filtered by an infrared light-blocking filter (Semrock, FF01-750sp) and split into two channels (red and green) by a dichroic mirror (Semrock, FF555-Di-3) and two emission filters (Semrock, FF02-617/73 and FF01-510/84) and then collected by two photomultiplier tubes (Hamamatsu Photonics, R3896 and R9110 SEL). Imaging was controlled by the software ScanImage (Vidrio Technologies).

Calibration of the optical system

To align the stimulation and imaging fields, we photo-bleached a 2P multi-spot pattern on a rhodamine layer and then imaged the bleached pattern. Misalignment was corrected by stretching, rotation and translation of the stimulation field. Axial offsets were adjusted by applying a phase-wrapped spherical lens function to the SLM.

To characterize spot inhomogeneity caused by the diffraction efficiency of the SLM, one or more (5, 10 or 15) holographic spots, within a 300 × 300 µm, 6 × 6 grid with small random offsets, were sent to a 1 mm thick fluorescence slide (FSK6, Thorlabs) and the fluorescence images were collected by the same objective lens and imaged by a CCD camera (Hamamatsu C8484-05G02). In case of one holographic spot per pattern, the diffraction efficiency of each of the coordinates was sampled in one light pattern, while in the case of multiple holographic spots per pattern, each of the coordinates was sampled in three to five different light patterns.

To compensate for the diffraction efficiency loss, we adapted a previously described method30,69. In brief, the total power was adjusted by the inverse of total diffraction efficiency, and the weights of different light spots within the same pattern—which determined the relative power ratio—were adjusted individually by the inverse of the diffraction efficiency of the respective spot. The diffraction efficiency profile and the overall power efficiency when generating one spot per pattern were slightly different from those measured when projecting multiple spots per pattern, and thus their compensations were carried out separately.

For the characterization of the optical axial profile and visualization of 3D spot distributions, a thin rhodamine poly(methyl methacrylate) layer, spin-coated on a glass slide (<1 µm in thickness), was imaged from below by using a substage objective and a CMOS camera (MQ013MG-ON, Ximea). One or more (5, 10 or 15) diffraction efficiency-compensated holographic spots were sent to the fluorescence sample through the upper microscope objective while z-stack images of each of the light patterns were acquired by using the inverted microscope and a substage camera and moving the upper objective with a piezoelectric motor (Physik Instruments). The axial FWHM of each spot was estimated by fitting the profile to a Lorentzian function.

To characterize the holographic spot generation in three dimensions over a large axial range (Extended Data Fig. 2b), we acquired z-stacks of holograms of 36 spots on a 2D plane (laterally spanning over 300 × 300 µm2) shifted axially within a range of ±200 µm. In case of using 3D holograms to target cells distributed in large axial extensions spanning over 1–2 scattering lengths, the loss of tissue scattering, which is proportional to ezLs, with Ls= scattering length (here 150 µm)57,70,71, becomes non-negligible. To demonstrate the capability to compensate for scattering loss for each of the spots according to its depth within each 3D hologram, we generated ten 3D holograms, with each hologram containing 15 spots on five planes within ±z = 100 µm, either with or without compensation on the intensity of each spot according to its depth (Extended Data Fig. 2c,d).

All data analysis of optical characterization was done in a custom-written script in Python using the SciPy and scikit-image packages.

2P-guided electrophysiological recording in vivo

Patch-clamp recordings in lightly anesthetized mice were obtained under the visual guidance of 2P scanning imaging performed at 920 nm. Positive pressure of >150 mbar was initially applied to the pipette interior filled with the internal solution when the patch pipette penetrated the brain surface and was positioned within or near the opsin-expression region in V1. The pipette pressure was reduced to <100 mbar when inside the brain and ~20 mbar when approaching the cell. Spiking activity was measured by using whole-cell or cell-attached recording in current-clamp configuration. The excitatory current was monitored in a voltage-clamp configuration at −70 mV.

Patch pipettes of 5–8 MΩ tip resistance were fabricated from borosilicate glass by using a microelectrode puller (Sutter Instruments). The initial access resistance was 18.2 ± 0.5 MΩ for current-clamp recordings (n = 21; opsin-positive and opsin-negative cells in Supplementary Table 1) and 27.8 ± 3.1 MΩ for voltage-clamp recordings (n = 9; opsin-negative cells in Supplementary Table 1).

The internal solution of the patch pipette contained 135 mM K-gluconate, 4 mM KCl, 10 mM HEPES, 10 mM Na2-phosphocreatine, 4 mM Mg-ATP, 0.3 mM Na2-GTP and 25–50 µM Alexa Fluor 488 for pipette visualization. The external solution, which was applied on top of the craniotomy, contained 145 mM NaCl, 5.4 mM KCl, 10 mM HEPES, 1 mM MgCl2 and 1.8 mM CaCl2. Membrane potential recordings obtained by using whole-cell current-clamp were corrected for liquid junction potential (11.9 ± 0.2 mV, five measurements) offline. Voltage or current recordings were acquired by using a Multiclamp 700B amplifier and a Digidata 1550A digitizer controlled by pCLAMP10 software (Molecular Devices). Electrophysiology data were filtered at 6 kHz and digitized at 20 kHz.

In all experiments, we avoided photostimulating cells in close proximity to the patch pipette, usually inserted from the right side of the FOV, to prevent potential disruption of connections or distortion in light delivery. This explains the low number of probed cells in the right quadrant in Fig. 3d(i) and Extended Data Fig. 7.

Quantification of intrinsic electrophysiological properties

Intrinsic electrophysiological properties were accessed by injecting step current pulses from −150 pA or −250 pA with an increment of 50 pA for 150 or 500 ms in current-clamp configuration. The average membrane potential was estimated as the mean over 20–100 ms before current injection. The resting membrane potential was measured as the down-state membrane potential by selecting traces that were more hyperpolarized than −50 mV before correcting for the liquid junction potential. Input resistance (Rin) was calculated as the slope of the current–voltage curve under current-pulse injection of −150-0 pA or −250-0 pA during the down state72. The membrane time constant (τm) was estimated by fitting an exponential decay for 10–95% of the peak hyperpolarization during current injection of −50 or −100 pA. Membrane capacitance (Cm) was calculated as Cm = τm/Rin. Rheobase was determined as the smallest current injection that evoked an AP. AP properties were measured according to the first AP evoked at the rheobase. The AP threshold was measured as the voltage at which the slope exceeded 50 mV ms−1. AP amplitude was measured as the voltage between the AP threshold and AP peak, and AP half-width as the FWHM amplitude. Spontaneous and evoked firing rates were assessed, respectively, as either around (100–530 ms before and 200–900 ms after) or during current injection (150 or 500 ms, 100 pA more than rheobase).

The electrophysiological properties of ST-ChroME-negative and ST-ChroME-positive cells (Supplementary Fig. 1a) were obtained by comparing subsets of pyramidal neurons with similar series resistance (in the range of 16–20 MΩ) during whole-cell recordings.

In vivo photostimulation experiment for characterizing presynaptic activation

Single-cell or multi-cell stimulation using 2P holographic light shaping with temporal focusing was used in the characterization of induced AP properties (Fig. 2a–c) and the spatial selectivity of AP generation (Fig. 2d–f). Spiking activity was measured by performing whole-cell current-clamp or cell-attached recordings from opsin-expressing neurons in V1 of anesthetized mice. The soma coordinates of the patched cell and the surrounding opsin-expressing cells were extracted from 2P scanning imaging and registered manually during the experiment. Notably, among opsin-expressing cells, we did not observe any correlation between responsiveness to photostimulation and opsin fluorescence intensity. The phase profiles for patterns targeting the patched and/or surrounding cell soma were GPU-calculated (~200 ms per pattern) and addressed to the SLM. According to the diffraction efficiency profile (Fig. 1c,d), the stimulation powers required for delivering light patterns targeting the soma in different parts of the FOV with a specified power density (between 0.1 and 0.5 mW µm−2) were calculated. The spiking activity was measured upon brief illumination (between 2 and 10 ms) of a holographic light pattern with an inter-stimulus interval (ISI) of ≥2 s. For measuring the lateral selectivity of AP generation, the single or multi-target patterns were delivered at lateral offsets of 0, 10, 20, 30, 50, 70 or 90 µm relative to the patched cell soma by horizontally moving the recording stage where the mouse and the patch recording were situated. For measuring the axial selectivity, patterns were delivered at axial offsets ranging from 0 to ±100 µm above or below the patched cell soma by vertically moving the microscope objective. Experiments were performed either in cell-attached (8 out of 11) or in whole-cell (3 out of 11) configuration, and no significant difference was observed between the two modalities. Whole-cell recording data were used to obtain the values shown in Supplementary Fig. 3 by extracting the depolarization induced by off-axis stimulation that did not result in any spikes.

Spiking probability FWHMs (Fig. 2e) were obtained from global Gaussian fits of the merged set of all AP probability values at each axial offset for each cell, and the FWHM error was defined as the 0.95 confidence interval for the width parameter of the fit curve. The observed overlap of the resulting error range under (‘1 target’) and (‘10 + 1 target’) stimulation suggests no significant difference between the two stimulation patterns. Similarly, no significant differences were observed in the FWHMs obtained from fits of AP probability for individual cells independently (Supplementary Fig. 5b), although the latter, performed on a limited set of points, were less reliable.

Experiments depicted in Extended Data Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 2 reproduced stimulation protocols mimicking the stimulation sequences used for synaptic mapping (see section below) while recording from opsin-positive cells. Stimulation sequences of patterns with different numbers of spots, F, either targeting or not targeting the patched cells, were delivered. For the three experiments mentioned above, 85, 87 and 90 patterns (divided between F = 1, 8 and 15) were used, and each stimulation pattern was repeated 15, 30 and 10 times, respectively. For the experiments depicted in Supplementary Fig. 7, similar sequences of single-spot stimulation were delivered on presynaptic connected cells while monitoring an opsin-negative neuron, and the full set of patterns was then shifted laterally and axially to record variation of postsynaptic responses.

Estimation of off-target activation upon 2P holographic stimulation

We extracted the x–y–z coordinates of all opsin-expressing neurons in six representative FOVs by applying automatic cell recognition in Cellpose73 on 2P z-stack red fluorescence images. An additional manual correction allowed us to add and remove misidentified regions of interest and assured proper identification of all neurons. In each of the FOVs, cells within ±8 µm from the z = 0 central plane were selected as potential target cells (Fig. 2f(i), red dots) for estimating POT.

We then defined an SPF as a binary ellipsoid with the lateral and axial size corresponding to the AP FWHM extracted from the AP spatial probability reported in Fig. 2e. If a neighboring opsin-expressing cell was within the ellipsoid of a target cell, it was considered to be co-activated and contributed to off-target spikes.

The SPF, centered on each of the potential target cells, allowed us to estimate whether any neighboring cell was close enough to undergo unwanted photoactivation. The POT was then estimated by summing the total off-target activations and dividing by the number of targeted cells. The estimated POT shown in Fig. 2f was further corrected by multiplying the AP probability at zero spatial offset (85%) and the stimulability (~80%), which was the previously estimated fraction of opsin-expressing cells responding to photostimulation by spiking.

The cell density reported in Fig. 2f was calculated by dividing the number of neurons by the volume of the convex hull that contains all neurons. This approach, with respect to a normalization to the total stack volume, allowed us to better estimate cell density under highly localized opsin expression, in which expressing neurons are clustered and some portions of the stack do not present any opsin-expressing cells.

To show the dependence of POT on opsin-expressing cell density in Fig. 2f(ii), in addition to the six representative experimental FOVs, we simulated 50 FOVs, each with a size of 300 × 300 × 300 µm3, with neurons considered as non-overlapping 12 µm diameter spheres with varying cell densities, up to full neuron density (164,000 cells per mm3)74. We simulated possible distributions of these spheres using the Poisson disk sampling function in the quasi-Monte Carlo submodule in the SciPy Python library. To avoid forming clusters when simulating low cell densities caused by the Poisson disk sampling algorithm, we loosened the attribute of ‘radius_factor’ up to 15 (the default value is two), depending on the simulated density, to allow cells to cover the whole simulated FOV space. Once the distributions of simulated cell coordinates are generated, the rest of the processing follows the same procedure as the six representative experimental FOVs.

To obtain the numerical estimation of the effect of light power on the broadening of the photostimulation spatial profiles shown in Supplementary Fig. 4, we started from the normalized 2P fluorescent profile curve of the holographic spot, lateral Fx and axial Fz, reported in Fig. 1b(ii). Its square root, multiplied by different P values—surface power density values at the central position (either z = 0 for axial or averaged around x = 0 for lateral)—represent the effective laser light density profiles px,z=PFx,z. The effect of the power saturation of the opsin responses to different power was then estimated by composing these profiles with a typical 2P response saturation curve of expression R(px,z)=1−exp(−px,z2log10P902), with P90 representing the light density necessary to achieve 90% of the maximal response (resulting in the solid profiles in Supplementary Fig. 4b,c). Additionally, to take into account the size of the cell soma, we performed a convolution of R(px,z) with a simplified top hat shape of 15 µm width (resulting in the dashed profiles in Supplementary Fig. 4b,c).

Estimating the probability of generating a polysynaptic signal

In designing the parallel connectivity experiment, we roughly estimated the probability that the photostimulation of F neurons would result in the generation of potential postsynaptic spikes in the network, which could subsequently lead to a bisynaptic signal in the monitored patched neurons. In brief, consider a 500 µm-sided cube of tissue, corresponding to an ensemble of approximately 20,000 excitatory neurons, Ncells (cell density, 160k neurons per mm³, as in previous work74 and as shown in Fig. 2f(i)). The average connectivity rate is defined as k. We then define Nin as the required number of simultaneous synaptic inputs needed to induce a spike in a neuron. Therefore, in a highly simplified approach, we consider that a pattern will generate a postsynaptic spike in a given neuron if at least Nin out of the F photostimulated cells are connected to that neuron, with a probability given by Ppost=∑i=NinFFi∙ki∙(1−k)F−i. The total number of postsynaptic spikes in the network is therefore Ppost∙Ncells, and the probability that one of these will induce a bisynaptic signal is Pbisyn=Ppost∙Ncells∙k. For simplicity, we consider k = 7% (analogous to the rate observed in the planar sequential connectivity experiment; Fig. 3c) and assume it is homogeneous over the full volume. Considering the rheobase of putative pyramidal neurons in our experiment (~250 pA; Supplementary Table 1) and the typical amplitude of observed synaptic inputs (averaging 5 pA, with a maximum of 25 pA; Fig. 3c), we expect Nin to be relatively large, approaching ten. Nonetheless, even under conservative estimates, assuming Nin = 6, and with F = 10, Pbisyn remains below 1%, making it negligible in the parallel connectivity mapping protocol. Moreover, we expect the bisynaptic response to be less reliable and exhibit longer jitter, making it negligible when computing the average spike-triggered response across multiple repetitions.

However, we note that this probability is significantly influenced by the parameter Nin. In the presence of highly excitable neurons or stronger and denser synaptic connections, polysynaptic signals could have a more significant role in parallel connectivity mapping.

In vivo photostimulation experiment for connectivity mapping

Single-cell or multi-cell stimulation was performed by using 2P holographic light shaping with temporal focusing. In sequential connectivity mapping, single-spot illumination was applied to sequentially activate single potential presynaptic cells (Fig. 3). In parallel connectivity mapping, multi-spot illumination was used for activating sets of potential presynaptic cells and reconstructing individual synaptic responses using CS (Figs. 4 and 5 and Supplementary Table 6).

Excitatory postsynaptic currents were measured by performing 2P-guided voltage-clamp recordings at −70 mV from the cell soma of opsin-negative neurons in anesthetized mouse V1. Opsin-negative neurons were identified by the absence of the red fluorescence reporter mRuby and a null photoinduced current upon soma illumination (Supplementary Fig. 1c). The soma coordinates of the patched cell and the surrounding potential presynaptic cells expressing the somatic opsin ST-ChroME were manually identified during the experiment. Cells selected for photostimulation were mainly coplanar with the patched cell; this choice was driven by the often localized opsin expression (not highly extended in z) and the limited recording time available for performing both sequential and parallel connectivity mapping. Across the 12 FOVs, 91 ± 5% of the visible opsin-expressing cells in the 2D FOV were probed. For probing connections from N potential presynaptic cells, the measurement matrices specifying the F cells to be stimulated in each of the M measurements were defined. In sequential connectivity mapping (Fig. 3), we designed measurement matrices of F = 1 and M = N (diagonal matrix); in parallel connectivity mapping (Figs. 4 and 5), we designed measurement matrices of F > 1 and M ≤ N (except FOV number 5 in Supplementary Table 6, where M > N). The M phase profiles corresponding to the M light patterns, which targeted single or multiple potential presynaptic cell somata, were rapidly calculated by GPU (~200 ms per pattern) and the stimulation powers required for delivering the light patterns of a specified power density (0.2 or 0.3 mW µm−2) were calculated, taking into account the diffraction efficiency profile and additional losses owing to multi-spot generation (Fig. 1c,d). To induce millisecond APs in single or multiple potential presynaptic cells, light-pattern illumination that targeted individual potential presynaptic cell soma, of 10 ms duration and a specified power density of 0.2 or 0.3 mW µm−2 was delivered. Single-spot photostimulation patterns were repeated 24–60 times, while multi-spot stimulations were repeated 18–60 times. Laser stimulations were delivered at the same rate (stimulation rate at 7 or 10 Hz) in both single (F = 1) and multi-cell (F > 1) stimulations. Illumination patterns were regularly refreshed between each stimulation to avoid repetitive stimulation of the same potential presynaptic with a short inter-stimulus interval (ISI). Specifically, the design of the stimulation sequences and the corresponding ISI aims to avoid stimulation at too high a frequency, with the latter being maintained below the threshold for inducing plasticity11,75 in the network being probed. Although the actual ISI for each cell in a given FOV depends on the corresponding measurement matrix, the average ISI across all cells within a FOV can be estimated as N / (F × stimulation rate), where the stimulation rate represents the frequency at which stimulation patterns were refreshed during the sequence (typically at 7 or 10 Hz). The shortened ISI in parallel mapping is caused by the increase in F. Specifically, average ISI for sequential mapping is ISI(F = 1) = N/stimulation rate, while for parallel mapping is ISI(F > 1) = N / (F × stimulation rate), on average F-fold higher (see Extended Data Fig. 4).

During the photostimulation protocols, we verified the sample movements between repetitions of stimulations by repeating the acquisition of the 2P images, and, if movements were detected, we re-adjusted the preparation position with motor and piezo manual translations. The proper targeting of cells was also suggested by observation of bleaching of the mRuby fluorophore limited to the targeted neurons.

In sequential connectivity mapping, presynaptic cells were directly identified by detecting postsynaptic current responses time-locked to stimulation. In parallel connectivity mapping, connections were retrieved after applying the CS algorithm to the postsynaptic current upon multi-cell stimulation (as better described below).

Analysis of light-induced AP and photocurrent

Voltage traces were sampled at 20 kHz, filtered for noise at 50 Hz and 150 Hz. The latency of light-induced AP was calculated as the timespan between the illumination onset and the AP peak. The average AP latency was calculated as the mean of AP latencies across three to eight repetitions, and AP jitter was defined as the standard deviation of the AP latencies. The AP count was computed as the number of APs induced within 20 ms after illumination onset. The AP probability was computed as the probability of inducing ≥1 AP count. Current traces were sampled at 20 kHz, some filtered for noise at 50 Hz and 150 Hz. The peak current was calculated as the peak of the photocurrent within 30 ms after illumination onset. The charge (Supplementary Fig. 1c) was calculated as the product of current and time within 30 ms after illumination.

Analysis of postsynaptic currents

Postsynaptic current traces were sampled at 20 kHz, filtered for noise at 50 Hz and 150 Hz and downsampled to 5 kHz.

Across repetitions of individual postsynaptic currents, traces with large fluctuations—that is, whose amplitudes were >2× the 20th percentile of all fluctuation amplitudes between −20 and 80 ms relative to the stimulation onset—were first discarded.

Information about the synaptic responses was obtained from the remaining trials, analyzing either single repetition traces or average traces across repetitions. Specifically, to identify and evaluate the magnitude of synaptic current responses, we define the quantity of STA as the mean value of a current trace, averaged over a specific time window. STA is typically computed over an interval of a few ms before and after presynaptic photostimulation. Focusing primarily on excitatory currents of negative sign, STAs are often reported, for simplicity, with the sign inverted (positive values corresponding to inward currents), and are defined as STA amplitudes. Negative STA amplitudes (equivalent to outward currents), possibly from recording noise, spontaneous activity or remaining inhibitory inputs, were offset to zero.

The individual or average postsynaptic current trace was first baseline-subtracted, with a baseline corresponding to the average current in the interval −5 to 0 ms before photostimulation onset; then, we extracted the peak current as the maximum current value within 3–25 ms after photostimulation onset.

To declare a synaptic response in a postsynaptic current upon single-cell or multi-cell presynaptic stimulation, we combined two criteria: (1) we performed a one-sided paired t-test (requiring P < 0.05) between the values of the STA of single traces computed in the time interval right before (−5 to 0 ms) and after (5–20 ms) stimulation; and (2) we set a minimum threshold of 2–3 pA to the peak current of average trace (variable according to the noise of the whole-cell recording; Extended Data Fig. 5b,c). A target cell was declared as a presynaptic cell when a postsynaptic current response was identified in sequential connectivity mapping under single-cell presynaptic stimulation. The noise levels of the individual traces for each measurement, as well as the noise levels of the traces averaged over repetitions for each measurement, were assessed by computing the standard deviation of the current traces within a time interval of −5 to 0 ms before photostimulation onset (Extended Data Fig. 5a–c).

The connection rate was calculated for each experiment as the number of presynaptic cells, which were identified in sequential connectivity mapping, divided by the number of all potential presynaptic cells in a FOV.

For individual postsynaptic current traces that were declared as responses, we defined the response onset values, computed for individual traces, as the STA of the baseline current between −5 and 0 ms (from stimulation onset) minus 2 s.d. of the baseline current values in the same interval. Individual traces of no responses were identified as those whose STA between 10 and 15 ms did not reach their onsets. Visual inspection was then applied to validate responses or failures in individual traces. The response rate of a connection was determined as the ratio between the repetition number of no-failure traces and the number of non-discarded repetitions.

For average postsynaptic current traces, response onset values were computed in the same way as those for individual traces, and latencies were defined as the time at which the average current reached its response onset (restricted to the interval of 0–15 ms from the stimulation onset). Peaks were defined as the local minima of the average traces within 5 to 25 ms from the stimulation onset, and peak latencies and amplitudes were extracted as the time of the peaks from stimulation onset and as the differences between response onsets and peak values, respectively. Rise times were calculated as the time differences between 20% and 80% peak amplitudes, and offsets as the time reaching the values of onsets after the response peak. Half-widths were calculated as the timespan between the rising and decaying phases of the responses reaching 50% peak amplitudes and decay time by fitting exponentials from peaks to offsets by using the least-squares method and with an upper bound of 100 ms.

Of note, in agreement with previous reports, few connections of large amplitudes and high response rates were identified (as in Supplementary Fig. 9)6,11,76; such rare, strong and reliable connections may exert a dominant effect in network information processing77,78. The occasional occurrence of double peaks in postsynaptic current responses (Supplementary Fig. 9) may indicate either activation of two synapses or the occurrence of two presynaptic spikes of the target cell. Alternatively, it may be attributed to the unintended off-target activation of an additional presynaptic neuron, together with the target one.

To compare the current responses upon multi-cell stimulation versus the sum of those upon single-cell stimulation (Fig. 4), and to reconstruct synaptic weights by combining CS and responses upon multi-cell stimulation (Fig. 5 and Extended Data Fig. 4), STA amplitudes of the averaged current traces after pre-processing (filtering, down-sampling, discarding traces of large fluctuation and baseline subtraction) were computed. Of note, STA amplitudes corresponding to excitatory currents are reported as positive values, and as we focused on the excitatory synaptic inputs, negative values of STA amplitudes (corresponding to outward currents) were offset to zero. The choice of the time interval for computing the STA, here 10–15 ms after stimulation onset, was related to the previous characterization of peak latency under single-cell stimulation (Fig. 3c), according to which most of the current response peaks were included in this time interval. Nevertheless, the analysis performed with other time intervals (that is, 5–20 ms after stimulation onset) did not significantly affect the results of input summation (results not shown).

Effect of off-target activation on estimated connection rate

The prediction of the effect of off-target stimulation on the estimated connectivity rate (Supplementary Fig. 6) was based on the following expression for the probability of detection of a synaptic response under single-spot stimulation: Pexp=1−(1−k)1+POT where k is the true connectivity rate and POT is the probability of inducing off-target APs. The expression, as shown in previous work38, shows how, with POT>0, Pexp>k, resulting in a potential overestimation of the connectivity rate. In Supplementary Fig. 6a, the experimentally estimated connectivity rate and its absolute Pexp−k and relative (Pexp−k)k error are plotted as a function of k and POT. For low values of off-target probability POT and k, the expression can be approximated by: Pexp≈k−1−k∙log1−k∙POT≈k+c∙POT, with c>1. In Supplementary Fig. 6b, this expression is plotted, showing how, for low POT, the overestimation in connectivity rate is directly proportional to the off-target probability.

Experimental CS reconstruction of connectivity

CS aims to find solutions to an underdetermined problem under incoherent probing of a system that is sparse and linear44,79,80.

In parallel connectivity mapping, to probe the connectivity to the postsynaptic cell from N potential presynaptic cells (xN×1), M measurements of postsynaptic currents, upon stimulating subsets of multiple potential presynaptic cells according to the measurement matrix (AM×N), were recorded41. Incoherent probing was achieved by defining a stimulation matrix in which each row contains F non-zero elements corresponding to the targeted neurons in a photostimulation pattern, where the N cells were distributed randomly over the M stimulation patterns, with the only constraint of being sampled as homogeneously as possible (each cell stimulated a similar number of times).

As the feature input of the CS reconstruction, we considered the STA amplitudes of the average current traces induced by each multi-cell stimulation (calculated in the interval between 10 and 15 ms after stimulation onset). These scalar values defined the measurement vector yMx1. The goal of CS reconstruction was therefore to solve for x in yM×1=AM×NxN×1. To achieve this goal, we applied the basis pursuit solution to minimize over x the loss function: 12∥y−Ax∥2+λ∥x∥1, where λ is a regularization hyperparameter that influences the sparsity of the solution and prevents overfitting. This was implemented by using a CVX MATLAB package81,82 for convex optimization models, similar to previous work42. In the present study, λ = 0.1 was applied for connectivity reconstruction. The effect of λ on the performance of experimental data was explored in Supplementary Fig. 15b–d. The N output values of the reconstruction algorithm correspond to the STA amplitudes of the postsynaptic currents upon single-cell stimulation of N potential presynaptic cells, which represent the synaptic strength of connections.

To define connected or non-connected cells from the reconstruction output, k-means clustering for two clusters was applied to the reconstructed connectivity vector x, containing the reconstructed STA amplitudes. One cluster defined retrieved connections and the other defined non-connected cells (as shown in Fig. 5a(ii,iii),b(ii,iii)). We introduced a connection-defining threshold as the middle value between the centroids of the two clusters. This threshold is adaptive to the distribution of amplitudes of the reconstructed responses; therefore, it can vary with the CR. For instance, for a low number of measurements, the two populations (connected and non-connected cells) might not be well separated after CS reconstruction, leading to a low threshold from the k-means clustering. With an increasing number of measurements and improved coverage, the population of connected cells might become more distinct from the non-connected ones, causing the k-means clustering threshold to increase (as in Fig. 5a(iii),b(iii)). Note that for some FOVs at low subsampling numbers, coverage could be less than 1 (Fig. 5a(iii),(iv),b(iii),(iv),c(i)).

The performance of CS in parallel connectivity mapping was evaluated by comparing the reconstruction result with the ground-truth connectivity determined in sequential connectivity mapping. More precisely, TPs, TNs, FPs and FNs were determined by verifying whether reconstructed connected or non-connected cells corresponded to the identification obtained under single-cell sequential mapping (discussed and reported in Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 6). Precision was computed as TP / (TP + FP), indicating the relevance of all reconstructed connections. Recall was computed as TP / (TP + FN), specifying the retrieval of all reconstructed connections. Receiver operating characteristic curves were computed to visualize the performance of the CS reconstruction (Supplementary Fig. 15a) in terms of TP rate (y axis) and FP rate (x axis) at all classification thresholds. The area under the curve of the receiver operating characteristic curve was then computed as one of the evaluation metrics.

To examine how different hyperparameters—including the number of measurements m, the regularization parameter λ and the connection-defining threshold—affected the performance of CS reconstruction of network connectivity, reconstruction was performed under a grid search of these parameters (Supplementary Fig. 15b–f). Subsampling measurement number m was smaller than or equal to the total measurement number. The corresponding CR, which indicates the speed gain, was computed by CR = N / m. For each subsampling value (excluding m = M), different subsets of m measurements out of the total number of measurements were randomly chosen, and the number of subsets was equal to M to balance sampling across all potential presynaptic neurons, meaning that in each subset, each potential presynaptic neuron was sampled by a similar number of measurements. Means and s.e.m. of the reconstruction responses and performances for each of the subsampling values were obtained by averaging the values obtained across these subsets of measurements.

Simulation of CS connectivity reconstruction

Similar to previous work42, we considered an observed network of either N = 30 Izhikevich model neurons83 with an average of K® = 2 connections (~6.7% connectivity) or N = 100 neurons with an average of K® = 10 connections (10% connectivity) within a 1,000 neuron network. The Izhikevich model was chosen for its good balance between biological plausibility, diversity of firing characteristics and computational simplicity. A ratio of 80% excitatory positive postsynaptic weight and 20% inhibitory negative postsynaptic weight was chosen to mimic the mammalian cortex. Neurons were connected with uniform random probability. To produce the desired level of spontaneous firing, each neuron was independently excited with different currents sampled from a zero-mean Gaussian distribution. The variance of the current was tuned to evoke a firing rate of 0.2 Hz per neuron, averaged across the network. These parameters were chosen based on reported spontaneous activity in L2/3 V1 in awake mice84,85. We generated an M × N binary random stimulation matrix, A, in which each element took the value of ‘1’ with probability F / N and ‘0’ otherwise. For each measurement, a row was randomly selected without replacement from matrix A. Neurons corresponding to the indices in matrix A were forced to fire, and the induced postsynaptic current on one specific neuron was monitored. Specifically, peak current in the time interval (0–5 ms) after the synchronized stimulation-evoked presynaptic spikes defined the features vector y, input for basis pursuit reconstruction. Values of the resulting vector x that were at least as strong as 2% of the maximum possible weight in each network configuration were considered connections and used to evaluate precision, recall and accuracy metrics. Results reported in Extended Data Fig. 8 were obtained by varying over different values of M and with F ranging between 5% and 30% of N, and each condition was simulated over 500 different network configurations. In all the reported simulations (except for Extended Data Fig. 10b), potential nonlinearities in the summation of presynaptic inputs are not taken into account. In the simulation shown in Extended Data Fig. 10b, the sublinearities in the summation of synaptic response are obtained by considering the integrated responses amplitudes, R, when multiple inputs are present, as R = r1 + p* (r2 + r3 +..), where p is the sublinearity coefficient indicated in the figure, and ri is the amplitude of the individual input (with r1 having the largest magnitude). Off-target stimulation was implemented as a chance, proportional to the number of observed cells, N, that a cell will cause another specific cell to fire when it is stimulated. The mapping from observed cells to off-target cells was randomized for each simulation.

Statistical analysis

Statistical tests were carried out using MATLAB software. Data comparisons between experimental conditions (for example, optical properties of holographic light patterns of different spot numbers, AP properties upon stimulation of different holographic light patterns) were performed by using ANOVA in combination with the Tukey’s multiple comparisons method, paired t-test (for example, STA of individual postsynaptic current traces before and after presynaptic photostimulation), Wilcoxon signed-rank test (for example, POT upon stimulation of ‘1 target’ and ’10 + 1 targets’) or two-sample t-test (for example, electrophysiological properties of opsin-positive versus opsin-negative neurons). The number of experiments is reported either in text or in the figure legends. Data are presented as means ± s.e.m. unless otherwise indicated. Error bars represent s.e.m. unless otherwise indicated. No specific statistical methods were used to predetermine sample size; however, the number of experiments was chosen to account for variability across cells (for example, differences in excitability, opsin expression), animals and batches of injected mice. The number of mapping experiments (performed on 12 cells from 12 mice) aimed to provide a representative assessment of the method’s performance across FOVs with varying numbers of putative presynaptic and connected cells. The number of repetitions for each holographic photostimulation pattern was selected to average out potential stimulation failures, synaptic failures, noise and spontaneous activity. Some electrophysiological recordings were excluded if cells showed instability in holding potential, and individual current traces were discarded if they exhibited large spontaneous current fluctuations within the time windows of interest. Opsin-positive or opsin-negative cells for patching were randomly selected from red-fluorescent and non-fluorescent cells, respectively. Similarly, putative presynaptic neurons for photostimulation were randomly chosen from opsin-expressing cells in the FOV, and stimulation patterns were randomly shuffled. The identification of connected cells, both in sequential and parallel mapping, was performed blindly according to the fixed criteria described above.
