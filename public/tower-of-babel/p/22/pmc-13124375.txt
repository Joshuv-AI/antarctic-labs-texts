Trends and mortality due to acute respiratory distress syndrome in the United States (1999–2023): A 25-year nationwide study
Authors: Waleed Tariq, Neha Waseem, Sohaib Aftab Ahmad Chaudhry, Sheza Athar, Soha Waseem, Ehsanullah Alokozay, Aafeen Mujeeb, Awais Tariq, Mazhar Ali Shah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) remains a significant cause of morbidity and mortality in the United States. Although advances in critical care have improved outcomes, the Coronavirus Disease of 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic substantially altered ARDS epidemiology, necessitating updated analyses of national mortality trends. To examine 25-year trends (1999–2023) in ARDS-related mortality across demographic, geographic, and urbanization categories, and to evaluate the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on these patterns. We conducted a population-based descriptive study using CDC WONDER multiple cause-of-death data. ARDS-related deaths were identified by ICD-10 code J80. Crude and age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMRs) per 100,000 population were calculated. Temporal changes were analyzed using Joinpoint regression to estimate annual percent change (APC) and average annual percent change (AAPC) with 95% confidence intervals. Mortality rates were stratified by sex, age, race/ethnicity, census region, state, and urban–rural classification. Between 1999 and 2023, 364,924 ARDS-related deaths were recorded. National AAMRs declined steadily from 1999 to 2018 (APC −2.63; 95% confidence interval (CI): −4.74–−1.04), surged sharply during 2018 to 2021 (APC 85.78; 95% CI: 53.65–109.16), and decreased between 2021 and 2023 (APC −59.59; 95% CI: −68.58–−47.89), resulting in an overall AAPC of −1.90 (95% CI: −3.80–0.19). Males consistently exhibited higher mortality rates than females. Individuals aged ≥ 75 years had the highest AAMRs. Non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic/Latino, and American Indian/Alaska Native populations experienced disproportionately elevated mortality. Rural areas and the South and Midwest regions showed greater increases during the pandemic. California, Texas, Florida, and New York reported the highest cumulative death counts. ARDS mortality in the United States declined for nearly 2 decades but rose sharply during the COVID-19 pandemic, highlighting persistent disparities by age, sex, race, geography, and urbanization. Targeted interventions and equitable critical care access are essential to reduce future ARDS-related deaths. Detailed Visual Abstract is illustrated in the Central Illustration.

1. Introduction

Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is a critical condition characterized by acute-onset hypoxemia, diffuse pulmonary infiltrates, and the absence of cardiac failure as a primary cause. Clinically defined by the Berlin Definition, ARDS is categorized by the PaO2/FiO2 ratio into mild (200–300 mm Hg), moderate (100–200 mm Hg), or severe (<100 mm Hg).[1] Pneumonia, sepsis, trauma, and aspiration are common causes, with the Coronavirus Disease of 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic greatly impacting the global burden.[2] Despite advancements in critical care, ARDS continues to be linked with high morbidity and mortality, placing a significant strain on healthcare systems worldwide. The global incidence of ARDS varies due to differences in healthcare infrastructures, diagnostic criteria, and population characteristics. The LUNG SAFE trial noted an incidence of 10.4% among intensive care unit (ICU) admissions and 23.4% among mechanically ventilated patients.[2] Severity correlates with mortality, varying from 27% in mild cases to 45% in severe cases. (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22797452/). Senior individuals and patients with conditions like sepsis, pneumonia, or trauma experience an increased risk. Annually, ARDS affects approximately 10 to 86 individuals per 100,000 of the global population, with prevalence rising during the COVID-19 pandemic, which led to a surge in ARDS cases and fatalities, especially among older adults and those with comorbidities.[2,3]

In the United States (U.S.), ARDS constitutes 10% of ICU admissions and 23% of patients on mechanical ventilation.[2] Mortality rates declined from 5.01 to 2.82 per 100,000 annually between 1999 and 2013.[4] due to improvements in critical care, including lung-protective ventilation,[5] prone positioning,[6] and advanced haemodynamic monitoring. However, mortality remains high, with severe ARDS cases showing rates between 35 to 45% depending on severity.[3] The impact of ARDS is unevenly distributed and shaped by factors including age, comorbidities, and disparities in healthcare access.[7] The COVID-19 pandemic has had a profound impact on ARDS trends.[8] Approximately 33% of hospitalized COVID-19 patients develop ARDS, with severe cases exhibiting mortality rates surpassing 50%, particularly among older adults and individuals with preexisting medical conditions.[9] The pandemic highlighted disparities in ARDS outcomes, emphasizing the significance of healthcare access and comorbidities.

This comprehensive analysis of U.S. data from the centers for disease control (CDC) Wonder Database (1999–2020) reveals temporal changes in ARDS mortality influenced by demographic and geographic factors. It emphasizes the critical role of COVID-19 in reshaping ARDS’s public health impact. A further understanding of these trends is crucial for reducing ARDS-related morbidity and mortality.

2. Methodology

2.1. Study setting and population

This descriptive study analyzed ARDS-related mortality using death certificate data from the CDC WONDER database between 1999 and 2023. Mortality records were identified by the ICD-10 code J80, which corresponds to adult respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). This ICD-10 code has previously been used to identify strokes in administrative settings databases.[4] All U.S. deaths during this period were eligible, excluding those with missing age data. The database includes comprehensive mortality data across all 50 states and the District of Columbia and has been widely used for research on respiratory diseases. Deaths attributed to ARDS were identified from the Multiple Cause-of-Death Public Use record. The data was extracted in 2 subgroups to distinguish COVID-19-related ARDS from Classical ARDS. COVID-19 ARDS was identified in death certificates that listed both U07.1 (COVID-19) and J80 (ARDS) as underlying or contributing cause of death. Classical ARDS was identified as death certificates that listed ARDS (J80) alone as the underlying or contributing cause of death, but U07.1 (COVID-19) was absent. The study adhered to the strengthening the reporting of observational studies in epidemiology reporting guidelines, ensuring methodological rigor, and was exempt from the local institutional review board’s approval.[10]

2.2. Data abstraction

A thorough data abstraction was conducted, extracting information on demographics, urban-rural classification, region, state, year, and place of death. Demographics included sex, age, and race/ethnicity, categorized as non-Hispanic (NH) White, NH Black or African American, Hispanic/Latino, NH American Indian/Alaskan Native, and other. Age groups ranged from under 1 year to 85+ years. Based on data reported on death certificates, this information has been used previously for analyses of the CDC Wonder database.[11] Urban-rural classification adhered to the scheme established by the National Center for Health Statistics, distinguishing urban counties (which include large metropolitan areas with populations over 1 million and medium/small metros with populations between 50,000 and 999,999) from rural counties (those with populations under 50,000), as per the 2013 U.S. Census Classification.[12] According to U.S. Census Bureau definitions, regions were categorized as Northeast, Midwest, South, and West.

2.3. Statistical analysis

National ARDS mortality trends were analyzed by calculating crude mortality rates and age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMRs) per 100,000 individuals, stratified by year, demographics, geography, and urban-rural classification, with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). crude mortality rates was determined by dividing the number of ARDS-related deaths by the total population annually, while AAMR adjusted mortality data to the 2000 U.S. census population. This standardization process facilitates a more precise comparison of mortality rates across various populations and periods.[13] To ascertain the annual percent change (APC) with a 95% CI in AAMR, the Joinpoint Regression program (5.2.0.0) was utilized.[14–16] This method identifies significant changes in AAMR over time by fitting a log-linear regression model to the time-varying changes. APC was deemed to increase or decrease if the slope depicting the shift in mortality significantly differed from zero in a two-tailed t-test. P values of <.05 were regarded as statistically significant.

3. Results

From 1999 to 2023, a total of 364,924 deaths due to ARDS occurred in the US among individuals aged from under 1 year to over 85 years. The data were categorized by year, gender, race, urbanization, census region, ten-year age group, place of death, and state.

3.1. ARDS-related AAMR stratified by year

Out of the total number of deaths that occurred due to ARDS: 144,179 deaths occurred during the pandemic period from 2019 to 2023, listed ARDS alone as the underlying or contributing cause of death. Additionally, 107,027 deaths listed both ARDS and COVID-19 as the underlying or contributing cause of death, indicating that the post-2018 surge was likely due to COVID-19-related ARDS. From 1999 to 2018, the overall AAMR for ARDS declined at an APC of −2.63 (95% CI: −4.74–−1.04). However, between 2018 and 2021, it increased sharply to 85.78 (95% CI: 53.65–109.16), then dropped to −59.59 (95% CI: −68.58–−47.89) from 2021 to 2023. Over the entire period from 1999 to 2023, the average annual percent change (AAPC) was −1.90 (95% CI: −3.80–0.19). 3.2. ARDS-related AAMR stratified by gender

Among females, the AAMR decreased from 1999 to 2018 at an APC of −2.72 (95% CI: −4.69–−1.25), then rose sharply from 2018 to 2021 at 75.82 (95% CI: 47.39–95.46), before falling again from 2021 to 2023 at −55.56 (95% CI: −65.53–−44.87). Over the entire period (1999–2023), the AAPC was −1.87 (95% CI: −3.76–−0.12). In males, the rate declined by −3.66 (95% CI: −7.01–−1.13) from 1999 to 2017, increased to 64.77 (95% CI: 42.34–111.75) from 2017 to 2021, and then decreased by −60.24 (95% CI: −72.74–−48.02) between 2021 and 2023, resulting in an overall AAPC of −2.14 (95% CI: −4.89–0.11). 3.3. ARDS-related AAMR stratified by 10-year age groups

From 1999 to 2001, the rate among children under 1 year declined with an average percentage change (APC) of −17.85 (95% CI: −33.28–15.92), then increased from 2001 to 2006 by 29.15 (95% CI: −8.52–38.77), before dropping by −12.26 (95% CI: −19.89–−1.82) between 2006 and 2010. It remained relatively steady at −0.91 (95% CI: −2.89–3.49) from 2010 to 2023, with an average annual percentage change (AAPC) of 1.02 (95% CI: −0.23–2.77). For those aged 1 to 4, the rate fell from 1999 to 2013 by −2.92 (95% CI: −8.42–−1.62), then rose modestly from 2013 to 2023 by 1.95 (95% CI: −0.50–11.07), resulting in an AAPC of −0.92 (95% CI: −1.91–−0.06). Among those aged 5 to 14, a decline of −2.62 (95% CI: −10.91–−0.40) from 1999 to 2012 was followed by an increase of 2.70 (95% CI: 0.08–19.59) through to 2023, culminating in an AAPC of −0.22 (95% CI: −1.63–1.20).

Among those aged 15 to 24, the rate decreased by −3.58 (95% CI: −5.38–−2.41) from 1999 to 2018, surged by 36.81 (95% CI: 16.44–51.25) between 2018 and 2021, then dropped to −34.70 (95% CI: −49.05–−14.73) from 2021 to 2023, yielding an AAPC of −2.49 (95% CI: −4.29–−0.84). In the 25 to 34 age group, the rate declined by −1.89 (95% CI: −4.31–−0.09) from 1999 to 2018, rose sharply by 64.20 (95% CI: 38.82–82.45) through 2021, then declined again by −50.44 (95% CI: −64.65–−37.14) between 2021 and 2023, with an AAPC of −1.15 (95% CI: −3.72–0.48). Individuals aged 35 to 44 showed a decline of −2.03 (95% CI: −4.23–−0.46) up to 2018, followed by a leap of 81.45 (95% CI: 53.35–101.60) from 2018 to 2021, then a subsequent drop of −56.29 (95% CI: −67.68–−47.22) through 2023, resulting in an AAPC of −1.07 (95% CI: −3.37–0.39). In the 45 to 54 age group, a decline of −1.65 (95% CI: −3.70–− 0.05) from 1999 to 2018 was followed by a rise of 88.97 (95% CI: 59.94–107.88) from 2018 to 2021, then a fall of −62.06 (95% CI: −72.06–−54.02) from 2021 to 2023, resulting in an overall AAPC of −1.43 (95% CI: −3.71–−0.01). The 55 to 64 cohort displayed a similar pattern: a decrease of −1.46 (95% CI: −3.48–0.26) up to 2018, an increase of 86.90 (95% CI: 58.45–105.29) from 2018 to 2021, followed by a decline of −60.43 (95% CI: −69.38–−52.25) from 2021 to 2023, yielding an AAPC of −1.07 (95% CI: −3.04–0.56). Among individuals aged 65 to 74, rates fell by −2.84 (95% CI: −5.18–−1.05) from 1999 to 2018, then climbed to 87.63 (95% CI: 54.47–113.34) through 2021, and declined again by −60.55 (95% CI: −69.62–−48.45) by 2023, leading to an AAPC of −2.14 (95% CI: −4.12–0.14). In the 75 to 84 group, the rate decreased by −5.45 (95% CI: −9.56–−2.96) from 1999 to 2017, rebounded to 92.54 (95% CI: 41.27–125.43) through 2020, then declined to −35.46 (95% CI: −51.27–−23.06) through 2023, giving an AAPC of −1.48 (95% CI: −4.26–0.35). Finally, among those aged 85 and older, the rate dropped by −5.90 (95% CI: −10.43–−2.83) from 1999 to 2017, surged to 115.08 (95% CI: 61.32–155.59) between 2017 and 2020, then fell again by −42.66 (95% CI: −57.95–−29.59) through 2023, leading to an AAPC of −1.92 (95% CI: −5.21–0.22). 3.4. ARDS-related AAMR stratified by race/ethnicity

In American Indian or Alaska Native populations, the APC was −1.01 (95% CI: −4.25–1.59) from 1999 to 2018, surging to 97.99 (95% CI: 58.35–131.01) from 2018 to 2021, then falling to −66.07 (95% CI: −75.74–−52.54) from 2021 to 2023, resulting in an AAPC of −1.26 (95% CI: −3.85–1.75). In Black or African American populations, the APC was − 4.82 (95% CI: −9.64–−1.69) from 1999 to 2017, jumping to 110.43 (95% CI: 52.92–150.97) between 2017 and 2020, then changing to −39.80 (95% CI: −55.80–−26.68) from 2020 to 2023, yielding an AAPC of −0.74 (95% CI: −3.90–1.64). Among White individuals, the APC was −2.85 (95% CI: −4.42–−1.66) from 1999 to 2018, increasing to 74.47 (95% CI: 50.94–89.31) from 2018 to 2021, then shifting to −53.64 (95% CI: −63.05–−45.55) between 2021 and 2023, resulting in an AAPC of −1.72 (95% CI: −3.41–−0.58). In Hispanic or Latino populations, the APC was −5.21 (95% CI: −12.06–−0.54) from 1999 to 2017, rising to 141.65 (95% CI: 67.54–205.88) from 2017 to 2020, then decreasing to −42.01 (95% CI: −58.30–−27.43) between 2020 and 2023, with an AAPC of 0.21 (95% CI: −3.27–3.88). For the Other category, the rate declined by −4.64 (95% CI: −9.89–−1.13) between 1999 and 2017, increased to 86.38 (95% CI: 35.91–120.75) from 2017 to 2020, then decreased again to −37.93 (95% CI: −53.02–−26.11) from 2020 to 2023, resulting in an AAPC of −1.73 (95% CI: −4.45–0.82). 3.5. ARDS-related AAMR stratified by urbanization status

In urban areas, the APC was −3.55 (95% CI: −5.84–−1.70) from 1999 to 2018 before increasing to 130.43 (95% CI: 81.26–170.19) between 2018 and 2020, resulting in an AAPC of 4.79 (95% CI: 2.99–6.36). 4. Discusion

Drawing on national mortality records from 1999 to 2023, we analyzed trends in ARDS-related deaths across the United States, identifying a total of 364,924 deaths across all age groups, from infants (<1 year) to older adults (85+ years). During this 25-year period, ARDS-related AAMR declined steadily from 1999 to 2018 (APC: −2.63; 95% CI: −4.74–−1.04), rose sharply during the COVID-19 pandemic between 2018 and 2021 (APC: 85.78; 95% CI: 53.65–109.16), and then declined again from 2021 to 2023 (APC: −59.59; 95% CI: −68.58–−47.89), yielding an overall AAPC of −.90 (95% CI: −3.80–0.19). Stratified analyses revealed that males consistently exhibited higher AAMRs than females across most years, though both sexes experienced similar temporal trends. Racial disparities were pronounced: NH Black, Hispanic/Latino, and American Indian or Alaska Native individuals showed disproportionately higher mortality rates compared to White populations. Age-specific patterns indicated the highest death rates among older adults, particularly those aged 75 and above. Geographically, states with large populations such as California, Texas, Florida, and New York reported the highest total ARDS deaths, while rural and non-metropolitan areas experienced consistently higher AAMRs than urban centers. Regionally, the South and Midwest showed more pronounced increases during the pandemic years. These findings underscore significant disparities in ARDS-related mortality across sex, race, age, geographic location, and urbanization status.

The epidemiological trends observed in ARDS-related mortality over the past 2 decades are attributed to the underlying biological mechanisms of the syndrome. ARDS is not a primary disease but rather a final common manifestation of acute lung injury, triggered by a variety of underlying conditions ranging from pulmonary insults such as pneumonia or aspiration, to extra-pulmonary causes like sepsis or trauma.[17] Central to its pathogenesis is diffuse alveolar damage, characterized by injury to both alveolar epithelium and capillary endothelium. This damage leads to the leakage of protein-rich fluid into alveolar spaces, impaired gas exchange, and triggering hypoxemia.[18] A core component of ARDS pathophysiology is the dysregulated immune response. Pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 play a pivotal role by increasing vascular permeability, promoting neutrophil recruitment, and causing widespread alveolar inflammation.[19] These cytokine surges, often described as “cytokine storms,” are a hallmark of ARDS pathobiology and contribute significantly to the diffuse alveolar damage and systemic inflammation observed in severe cases.[20] In addition, oxidative stress contributes to endothelial injury and further perpetuates the inflammatory cascade, exacerbating pulmonary edema and reducing lung compliance.[21] Understanding these molecular drivers is crucial when interpreting mortality patterns over time, especially during periods of heightened infection-related hospitalizations. Taken together, these mechanisms demonstrate how ARDS arises from a convergence of immune dysfunction and barrier disruption, all of which have real-world implications for mortality trends as seen in our analysis.

Between 1999 and 2023, AAMR for ARDS in the United States exhibited 3 distinct phases: a sustained decline from 1999 to 2018 (APC: −2.63), a sharp surge from 2018 to 2021 (APC: +85.78), and a subsequent decline from 2021 to 2023 (APC: −59.59). The initial decrease in AAMR from 1999 to 2018 likely reflects major advancements in critical care medicine, particularly the widespread adoption of lung-protective ventilation strategies, low tidal volume ventilation, and improved sepsis management.[2] These interventions, standardized following the ARDS net trial in 2000, were later adopted globally and have consistently demonstrated reductions in ARDS-related mortality in both clinical trials and large-scale epidemiologic studies.[22]

The sharp rise in mortality between 2018 and 2021 corresponds to the emergence of the COVID-19 pandemic, which led to a dramatic increase in ARDS cases. SARS-CoV-2 triggers a potent systemic inflammatory response and extensive pulmonary endothelial injury, both of which are core pathophysiological drivers of ARDS.[23] During this period, over 70% of ICU-admitted COVID-19 patients developed ARDS, with reported mortality rates ranging from 35% to over 60%, depending on regional healthcare strain and access to ventilator support.[24] The sheer volume of critically ill patients overwhelmed ICU resources, especially in under-resourced and rural hospitals, resulting in delays in care and revealing significant gaps in staffing, infrastructure, and supply chains.[25]

Following 2021, mortality rates began to decline once again, likely due to multiple concurrent improvements: the rollout of COVID-19 vaccines, enhanced early recognition and triaging of respiratory failure, refined ventilator protocols specific to COVID-ARDS, and expansion of ICU capacity in many regions.[26] Despite this improvement, AAMRs have not returned to pre-pandemic lows, possibly due to residual sequelae such as post-ARDS fibrosis, delayed admissions, and widespread healthcare burnout. These temporal patterns highlight not only the evolving nature of ARDS care over the past 2 decades but also the vulnerability of critical care systems during large-scale public health crises.

Our results revealed notable differences in ARDS mortality patterns between males and females, suggesting that sex-based biological and behavioral factors significantly shape disease outcomes. While both genders experienced mortality fluctuations over the 1999 to 2023 period, men consistently had higher ARDS-related death rates, particularly during the 2018 to 2021 spike. A key factor contributing to this disparity is the immunoprotective role of estrogen in females. Estrogen enhances alveolar epithelial barrier function, stabilizes endothelium, and suppresses NF-κB–mediated cytokine release, reducing neutrophil infiltration and promoting resolution of lung injury.[26–29] These protective mechanisms are consistent with lower ARDS mortality in women, especially premenopausal. By contrast, testosterone may impair immune resolution; low serum testosterone is linked to endothelial dysfunction and exaggerated inflammatory responses,[30] and androgen receptor activation has been associated with enhanced pro-inflammatory signaling in lung immune cells.[31] However, some contexts show mitigating effects.[32] Beyond hormones, gene–sex interactions are also relevant: the epidermal growth factor 61A > G polymorphism has been associated with higher ARDS risk, with stronger effects reported in males.[33] Collectively, these pathophysiologic modifiers explain why men, who lack estrogen’s protective buffering and may carry risk polymorphisms, exhibit persistently higher ARDS mortality.[33,34]

Behavioral patterns also play a significant role. Men are more likely to engage in health-risk behaviors such as smoking and delayed medical help-seeking, which increase both the incidence and severity of ARDS presentations.[35] Moreover, comorbidities like hypertension, obesity, and cardiovascular disease; conditions more prevalent among men which amplify the risk of adverse ARDS outcomes, particularly when superimposed with respiratory infections like COVID-19.[36] Gender-specific differences in the delivery of critical care may also contribute: studies suggest men are more frequently subjected to aggressive interventions like mechanical ventilation, which, while necessary, can increase the risk of ventilator-associated complications and prolonged ICU stays.[37] Understanding these intertwined biological and systemic factors is crucial for tailoring sex-specific prevention and treatment strategies for ARDS.

Racial and ethnic disparities significantly influence ARDS mortality in the United States. NH Black individuals consistently experience the highest ARDS-related mortality, which can be linked to both biological and systemic factors. At the genetic level, variants in the MYLK gene alter endothelial cytoskeletal regulation, increasing vascular permeability and worsening alveolar flooding, thereby amplifying ARDS severity in this population.[38] Black individuals also exhibit higher prevalence of TNF-α and IL-6 promoter polymorphisms, which drive exaggerated pro-inflammatory cytokine release and promote the hyper-inflammatory ARDS sub-phenotype, correlating with poor outcomes.[39,40] These biological vulnerabilities act synergistically with elevated rates of comorbidities such as hypertension and diabetes, which worsen endothelial dysfunction and impair pulmonary repair.[37,41]

Hispanic and Latino populations also demonstrate elevated mortality risks, driven by barriers such as language discordance, underinsurance, and delayed presentation to care. Biologically, certain allelic distributions – such as polymorphisms in ACE and IL-10, may influence renin–angiotensin signaling and cytokine balance, predisposing individuals to dysregulated inflammatory responses.[42,43] Social determinants further compound these risks, as many work in essential occupations with heightened exposure to respiratory pathogens and reduced ability to seek timely medical care.[43]

Among American Indian and Alaska Native individuals, the highest rates of ARDS-related mortality have been reported in rural and reservation-based communities, where healthcare infrastructure is often sparse. This population faces disproportionately high rates of chronic lung injury risk factors, including smoking, asthma, and recurrent respiratory infections, which prime the airway for epithelial barrier dysfunction and maladaptive immune responses.[43] When overlaid with limited ICU availability and delayed intervention, these biological stressors culminate in worse ARDS outcomes.

Although Asian populations are less frequently studied in ARDS literature, emerging data suggest that polymorphisms in genes such as ACE, TNF-α, and SFTPB (surfactant protein B) may influence susceptibility to alveolar inflammation and surfactant dysfunction, impairing host defense and gas exchange.[41,44] Lower rates of preventive care and ICU utilization may also contribute to suboptimal treatment outcomes in Asian populations.[45]

Notable geographical and regional disparities were seen in the analysis. Our study has consistently shown that certain regions, particularly the Southern states, experience higher AAMRs for ARDS compared to other areas. For instance, in 2018, the South reported the highest ARDS-related AAMR at 3.3 per 100,000 people, while other regions exhibited lower rates.[46] These differences may partly reflect the warmer climate in the South, which affects seasonal infection patterns, while colder northern areas, such as New York, experience winter surges in ARDS-related mortality due to an increased incidence of respiratory infections.[47] Divergences are further pronounced when examining state-level data. Alabama, for example, had the highest annual average ARDS mortality rate at 5.47 per 100,000, whereas New York reported the lowest at 2.55 per 100,000.[47] Such variations may be attributed to differences in healthcare infrastructure, prevalence of comorbid conditions, and socioeconomic factors.

Urbanization status also plays a crucial role in ARDS mortality. Non-metropolitan areas, including micropolitan and rural regions, have been associated with higher ARDS-related mortality rates compared to large metropolitan areas. This trend underscores the challenges faced by rural communities, such as limited access to specialized care, fewer critical care facilities, and delayed diagnoses.[48] Populations in these regions often face a combination of geographic and economic barriers, including limited transportation options and low health literacy, which delay access to appropriate care.[49] Systemic issues such as healthcare deserts regions lacking adequate medical infrastructure exacerbate these challenges, particularly for low-income and minority communities.[50]

Alleviating disparities in ARDS-related mortality requires an integrated strategy that spans clinical care, health systems strengthening, and targeted policy reform. First, universal adherence to evidence-based ARDS management strategies – including low tidal volume ventilation, neuromuscular blockade in early severe ARDS, and timely initiation of prone positioning – must be emphasized and routinely audited across all clinical settings.[51] While these strategies are supported by strong evidence, implementation gaps remain wide, particularly in resource-limited institutions and community hospitals, where compliance with ARDS protocols has been shown to be suboptimal.[52]

Expanding tele-critical care infrastructure can serve as a crucial intervention to address geographical inequities in ARDS care. Tele-ICU models have demonstrated improved adherence to clinical best practices, more timely recognition of ARDS, and even reduced mortality in hospitals serving rural and underserved populations.[53] These models allow for remote intensivist oversight and real-time guidance, thereby bridging the expertise gap between urban and rural facilities. Additionally, early identification of high-risk individuals must be prioritized through improved clinical risk stratification tools. Incorporating validated prediction scores such as the lung injury prediction score in electronic health records, coupled with clinical decision support systems, could help clinicians identify patients at imminent risk of ARDS and initiate preventive strategies before critical deterioration occurs.[54] Artificial intelligence-based models have also shown promising results in predicting ARDS onset 24 to 48 hours in advance, offering a valuable window for intervention.[55]

From a systematic perspective, reducing ARDS mortality disparities also necessitates more inclusive clinical research. Minority populations are vastly underrepresented in clinical trials for critical care interventions, which limits generalizability and may inadvertently widen outcome gaps.[56] Increasing the enrollment of Black, Hispanic, Native American, and rural populations in ARDS-related trials is vital to ensure equitable efficacy of therapies across demographic groups.

Looking forward, public health efforts must focus on reducing structural barriers to timely acute care, including insurance coverage gaps, delayed emergency response in rural zones, and regional ICU capacity limitations. Integration of genetic and molecular evidence into prevention strategies will prove beneficial to close preventable ARDS gaps. Mendelian randomization has identified IL-13 as a causal driver of ARDS, suggesting that targeted therapies or risk stratification based on IL-13 activity could help intervene before respiratory failure develops.[57] Causal links between interleukin receptors such as IL-22 and IL-23R and ARDS provide further opportunities to build biomarker panels that guide early triage and precision immunomodulation.[58] Beyond inflammatory pathways, serum mannose has been shown to be protective, raising the possibility of simple, cost-effective metabolic interventions for at-risk patients.[59] Likewise, thyroid dysfunction has been implicated as a causal risk factor, making routine thyroid assessment and correction in critically ill populations a feasible step toward prevention.[60] Embedding these molecular insights into critical care; especially in underserved regions – could shift ARDS management from late rescue to equitable, proactive prevention. Investments in critical care workforce development, particularly in underserved areas, and continued federal support for rural hospital infrastructure are also essential. Without these systemic changes, innovations in ARDS therapy will continue to benefit some populations more than others, perpetuating preventable disparities in survival.

4.1. Limitations

While our study provides valuable insights into ARDS mortality trends over more than 2 decades, it is not without limitations. The retrospective nature of the analysis restricts control over data quality, and reliance on death certificate data may result in inaccuracies or underreporting of ARDS-related deaths. Shifts in ICD coding practices over time may also impact the reliability of trend analyses. Furthermore, the lack of detailed clinical data, such as comorbid conditions (sepsis, trauma or pneumonia) limits the depth of our findings. Moreover, ARDS diagnoses could not be corroborated using imaging or laboratory criteria due to unavailability at the CDC Wonder website. The absence of Unaccounted factors, such as social determinants of health and disparities in healthcare access, may also influence the observed trends, but could not be explored due to dataset limitations.

5. Conclusion

ARDS-related mortality in the United States declined steadily for nearly 2 decades but rose sharply during the COVID-19 pandemic before partially decreasing by 2023. Mortality remains highest among older adults, males, and racial and ethnic minorities, with notable geographic disparities affecting rural areas and Southern and Midwestern states. The pandemic amplified existing inequities in healthcare access and critical care capacity. Addressing these disparities requires targeted interventions, improved critical care resources, and equitable implementation of evidence-based ARDS management strategies. Ongoing national surveillance is essential to track trends, guide resource allocation, and reduce preventable ARDS-related deaths.
