The mediating role of resilience in the association between trauma type and CPTSD among Swiss older adults
Authors: Myriam V Thoma, Enya Redican, Mark Shevlin, Nathanael Adank, Andreas Maercker, Shauna L Rohner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjective: Limited research exists on the potentially differential associations of interpersonal and non-interpersonal trauma with symptoms of posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and disturbances in self-organization (DSO) in older adults. This study investigated both linear and curvilinear associations between trauma exposure (i.e. interpersonal versus non-interpersonal) and PTSD/DSO symptoms. It further tested whether psychological resilience mediates these relationships.Method: A total of N = 1,526 Swiss older adults (Mage = 72 years, SD = 6.20, range = 65–95 years) completed self-report measures, assessing interpersonal and non-interpersonal trauma exposure with the Life Events Checklist for DSM-5, PTSD and DSO symptoms with the International Trauma Questionnaire, and psychological resilience with the Brief Resilience Scale.Results: Interpersonal trauma (44.9%) was less prevalent compared to non-interpersonal trauma exposure (80.9%). Both trauma types were positively associated with PTSD (interpersonal: β = 0.15, p < .001; non-interpersonal: β = 0.10, p < .001) and DSO (interpersonal: β = 0.12, p < .001; non-interpersonal: β = 0.06, p = .012) symptoms, and negatively associated with resilience (interpersonal: β = −0.06, p = .030; non-interpersonal: β = −0.07, p = .032). A curvilinear association was found between non-interpersonal trauma exposure and DSO symptoms (β = −0.05, p = .030). Resilience partially mediated the relationship between both trauma types and PTSD (interpersonal: β = 0.02, p = .031; non-interpersonal: β = 0.02, p = .037) and DSO (interpersonal: β = 0.03, p = .031; non-interpersonal: β = 0.02, p = .033) symptoms. Female gender was significantly associated with lower resilience (β = −0.14, p < .001).Conclusion: The findings underscore the enduring psychological impact of both interpersonal and non-interpersonal trauma in later life. Resilience, as measured with a brief assessment, may serve as a protective factor against PTSD/DSO symptoms, highlighting its potential role in clinical interventions aimed at trauma-exposed older adults.KEYWORDS: PTSD, DSO, older adults, trauma exposure, resilience, interpersonal trauma, non-interpersonal trauma, mediation

Introduction

While the global burden of trauma on mental health is increasingly acknowledged (World Health Organization [WHO], 2024), its effects in later life remain insufficiently studied (Pless Kaiser et al., 2019). Trauma has widely established negative impacts of trauma on mental health (e.g. McKay et al., 2021; Sowder et al., 2018), and potentially traumatic events (PTEs) are highly prevalent among older adults, with lifetime exposure rates ranging from 53% to 90% (Benjet et al., 2016; Creamer & Parslow, 2008; Ogle et al., 2013; Thoma et al., 2025). Therefore, gaining a clearer understanding of how trauma exposure relates to psychological outcomes in later life represents an urgent public health priority. While existing evidence suggests that interpersonal trauma may have more severe psychological consequences than non-interpersonal trauma (Kilpatrick et al., 2013; Thomas et al., 2021), it is not yet clear whether these distinctions hold true in older age. Within this context, psychological resilience is increasingly recognised as a key protective factor that may buffer against the adverse effects of trauma (Bonanno, 2004; Bonanno et al., 2011; Liu et al., 2017; Masten, 2001; Southwick et al., 2014). However, empirical studies that simultaneously examine both the differential effects of trauma types and the moderating role of resilience in older adults remain scarce.

Exposure to trauma can lead to the development of stress-related disorders, most notably post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and complex PTSD (CPTSD). With the 11th revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11), the diagnostic criteria for PTSD were revised, and CPTSD was introduced as a distinct trauma-related diagnosis (WHO, 2019). According to ICD-11, PTSD is defined by three symptom clusters: Re-experiencing (Re), avoidance (Av), and a heightened sense of current threat (Th). ICD-11 CPTSD includes these core PTSD symptoms, in addition to disturbances in self-organisation (DSO), characterised by affective dysregulation (AD), a negative self-concept (NSC), and difficulties in relationships (DR). Although the clinical presentation of these mental health disorders may vary with age, the diagnostic criteria apply across all age groups (WHO, 2019). Prevalence rates for ICD-11 PTSD and CPTSD among older adults (≥60 years) differ markedly across countries, with PTSD estimates ranging from 1.1% to 4.8% and CPTSD rates from 0.2% to 8.2% (Creamer & Parslow, 2008; Maercker et al., 2018; McGinty et al., 2021). Cross-country differences in the prevalence of PTSD and CPTSD are well documented across various age groups, including older adults, and may reflect not only true variation in trauma exposure and vulnerability but also the presence of subthreshold PTSD and potential limitations of measurement instruments, particularly in older populations. This underscores the need to better understand risk and protective factors that shape trauma-related outcomes in later life.

Existing evidence supports a dose–response relationship between trauma exposure and risk of PTSD, whereby greater trauma exposure (e.g. the number, frequency, or variety of events) is associated with increased PTSD risk and symptom severity (Briere et al., 2016; Johnson & Thompson, 2008; Kolassa et al., 2010a; Wilker et al., 2015). Studies indicate that interpersonal trauma, especially during childhood (e.g. sexual or physical abuse), is associated with poorer mental health outcomes, including more complex symptomatology and a higher prevalence and severity of PTSD across both child and adult clinical populations (Cloitre et al., 2009; Kilpatrick et al., 2013; Rácz et al., 2023; Steine et al., 2017; Thomas et al., 2021). While dose–response relationships between trauma exposure and mental health outcomes have been well documented in younger cohorts, studies examining this relationship in later life remain scarce (for a notable exception, see Ogle et al., 2014). Given the cumulative nature of trauma across the lifespan and the demographic shift toward aging societies, further research is warranted to examine dose–response relationships in older cohorts, particularly in distinguishing the effects of interpersonal versus non-interpersonal trauma. Whether a privileged relationship exists between interpersonal trauma and CPTSD remains an open question. Clinical knowledge suggests such a link; however, some epidemiological studies indicate that CPTSD can also be associated with non-interpersonal trauma, particularly when such events occur alongside interpersonal or repeated trauma exposures (e.g. Hyland et al., 2017; Kairyte et al., 2022; Maercker et al., 2018). Clarifying the relative contribution of interpersonal versus non-interpersonal trauma to the development of PTSD and CPTSD in older adults therefore represents an important next step in advancing our understanding of trauma-related psychopathology in later life.

Furthermore, the assumption of a simple linear dose–response relationship has been increasingly challenged, as it overlooks individual differences in vulnerability, adaptation processes, and resilience that shape how trauma exposure translates into psychological outcomes (Kaysen et al., 2009; McNally, 2003). For instance, rather than following a simple pattern in which greater trauma exposure consistently leads to worse outcomes, evidence suggests that the relationship between trauma and outcomes is shaped by a range of risk factors. These include demographic characteristics (e.g. gender, age at trauma), cognitive and psychological variables (e.g. intelligence, neuroticism, subjective appraisal), biological factors (e.g. specific genotypes), comorbid mental health problems, and environmental influences such as family instability or low social support (Brewin et al., 2000; Kaysen et al., 2009; Kolassa et al., 2010b; McNally, 2003; Ogle et al., 2013; Rusmir et al., 2024). Beyond these person- and context-related moderators, theoretical models such as the Steeling Effect (Liu, 2015; Rutter, 1987, 2006, 2012) propose that exposure to moderate stress or adversity may, under certain conditions, foster adaptive coping and resilience. This implies a non-linear relationship between stress and health, suggesting that individuals with moderate stress exposure may exhibit more favourable health outcomes compared to those with either very low or very high exposure (Liu, 2015). Support for this comes from both animal and human research (for a review, see Höltge et al., 2018), including studies conducted with older adult samples (e.g. Höltge et al., 2019). These insights challenge the assumption of a strictly linear dose–response pattern and emphasise the need to explore more complex trajectories. The present study builds on this perspective by examining both linear and curvilinear associations between cumulative lifetime trauma exposure and PTSD/CPTSD symptomatology in older adults.

Resilience may act as a mediator between trauma exposure and mental health outcomes in later life. While the definition, conceptual understanding, and empirical operationalisation of resilience can vary widely, it can broadly be described as the ability to adapt positively in the face of adversity (Southwick et al., 2014). There are both brief and comprehensive approaches to measuring resilience, yet it remains unclear which measurements are the most appropriate. A recent study by Li et al. (2023) examined the mediating and moderating role of psychological resilience in the association between childhood trauma and depressive symptoms among N = 1,091 community-dwelling older adults in China. The results showed that resilience significantly mediated the relationship between childhood trauma severity and later-life depression, while no moderating effects were observed. These findings highlight the importance of considering psychological resilience as a potential mediating mechanism when investigating the link between trauma exposure and mental health outcomes in older adults (Li et al., 2023). To the best of the authors’ knowledge, no existing study has examined the mediating role of psychological resilience in the relationship between lifetime trauma exposure and symptoms of PTSD and DSO in older adults.

While our previous work with this dataset examined prevalence rates, symptom structure, and risk factors of ICD-11 PTSD and CPTSD (Thoma et al., 2025), as well as adjustment disorder and its position on the stress-response continuum (Redican et al., 2025), these studies did not address potential differences between interpersonal and non-interpersonal trauma, nor did they test resilience as a mediating mechanism. The present study therefore extends this line of research by differentiating trauma types and investigating whether psychological resilience mediates the (linear and curvilinear) associations between cumulative trauma exposure and trauma-related symptoms in older adults. We hypothesised that greater trauma exposure, particularly of an interpersonal nature, would be associated with higher symptom severity, and that resilience would mediate this relationship, such that higher trauma exposure would be linked to lower resilience, which in turn would predict greater PTSD and DSO symptoms.

Methods

Participants and procedure

This study used data from the Swiss Silver Stress and Resilience Study, which assessed ICD-11 stress-related disorders and resilience among German-speaking adults aged 65 or older in Switzerland. Participants were recruited between November 2023 and June 2024 via flyers distributed in public spaces and organisations, study advertisements, and articles in newsletters. Inclusion criteria were being aged 65 years or older, resident in Switzerland, and fluent (Swiss) German. Full procedural details are outlined in prior research (see Thoma et al., 2025). Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Arts and Social Sciences at the University of Zurich, Switzerland (ID: 23.10.21).

The final sample comprised 1,526 participants. The mean age was 72.34 years (SD = 6.20, range = 65–95 years), more than two-thirds (72%, n = 1,098) were female, 53.4% (n = 815) were in a committed relationship, and 90.0% (n = 1,374) had obtained a post-secondary education.

Measures

Trauma exposure

The Life Events Checklist for DSM-5 (LEC-5; Weathers et al., 2013) is a self-report measure used to assess trauma exposure. It assesses exposure to 16 potentially traumatic events that are interpersonal, including sexual assault, combat or exposure to a warzone, and assault with a weapon, or accidental (e.g. serious traffic accidents or natural disasters; Maercker & Augsburger, 2022). Participants indicated whether each event ‘Happened to me’, ‘Witnessed it’, ‘Learned about it’, ‘Part of my job’, ‘Not Sure’, or ‘Doesn’t apply’ across their entire life. For this study, the event was coded as experienced if participants indicated the responses ‘Happened to me’ or ‘Witnessed it’. In keeping with previous research (Ehring & Quack, 2010; Karatzias et al., 2019), sum scores were calculated for non-interpersonal trauma (natural disaster, fire or explosion, transportation accident, serious accident at work or home or during recreational activity, exposure to toxic substance, life-threatening illness or injury, severe human suffering, sudden and violent death, and sudden and unexpected death of someone close to you) and interpersonal trauma (physical assault, assault with a weapon, sexual assault, other unwanted or uncomfortable sexual experiences, combat or exposure to a war-zone, captivity, and serious injury and/or harm and/or death you caused to someone else). Higher scores indicated greater exposure to interpersonal / non-interpersonal trauma.

ICD-11 PTSD and CPTSD

The International Trauma Questionnaire (ITQ; Cloitre et al., 2018) is a self-report measure of ICD-11 PTSD and CPTSD. It is comprised of six items measuring PTSD symptoms (Re, Av, Th) and six items measuring DSO symptoms (AD, NSC, DR). Three items also assess functional impairment associated with the PTSD and DSO symptoms. Participants indicated how bothered they have been by each of the PTSD and DSO symptoms in the last month using a five-point Likert scale ranging from ‘Not at all’ (0) to ‘Extremely’ (4). Severity scores were generated by summing the items, with higher scores indicating greater symptom severity. The German adaptation of the ITQ has undergone prior validation (e.g. Lueger-Schuster et al., 2015, 2018) and has been used in studies involving older adults (Maercker et al., 2022; Thoma et al., 2025). Cronbach’s alpha for the PTSD (α = .85) and DSO (α = .83) subscales were excellent in the current study.

Resilience

The German version of the Brief Resilience Scale (BRS; Smith et al., 2008; Chmitorz et al., 2018) is a self-report measure used to assess psychological resilience. It is comprised of six items, three positively worded and three negatively worded. Participants indicated the extent to which they agreed with each of the statements using a five-point Likert scale ranging from ‘Strongly disagree’ (1) to ‘Strongly agree’ (5). Items were summed for a total resilience score, with higher scores indicating greater resilience. The German BRS has shown solid psychometric properties in population-based validations, including older adults (Chmitorz et al., 2018; Kunzler et al., 2018; Rösner et al., 2024). Cronbach’s alpha for the scale (α = .83) was excellent in the current study.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were first generated for interpersonal trauma, non-interpersonal trauma, total resilience, PTSD, and DSO scores using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 29. All remaining analyses were conducted in Mplus 8.9 (Muthén & Muthén, 2017) using robust maximum likelihood (MLR) estimation. Specifically, four regression models were specified and tested sequentially to determine whether a curvilinear association existed between interpersonal and non-interpersonal trauma and both PTSD and DSO total scores (i.e. hypothesis 1), and whether resilience significantly mediated these relationships (i.e. hypothesis 2). All models were adjusted for age and gender. The predictor variables (interpersonal and non-interpersonal trauma, including both linear and quadratic terms) and outcome variables (PTSD and DSO scores) were mean-centred. Mean centreing helps reduce micro-multicollinearity (i.e. excessive correlations) among variables (Iacobucci et al., 2015).

Hypothesis 1 was tested across Models 1 to 3. Model 1, the ‘null model’, estimated only the intercepts for PTSD and DSO symptoms. Model 2 (see Figure 1A), the ‘linear direct effects model’, examined the linear effects of interpersonal and non-interpersonal trauma on PTSD and DSO symptoms. Model 3 (see Figure 1B), the ‘linear and quadratic direct effects model’, examined both the linear and quadratic effects of interpersonal and non-interpersonal trauma on PTSD and DSO symptoms. To evaluate model fit, several fit statistics were used: Log-likelihood, with higher values indicating better fit; as well as the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC; Sclove, 1987), sample size-adjusted BIC (ssaBIC; Sclove, 1987), and Akaike Information Criterion (AIC; Akaike, 1987), with lower values on these indices indicating better fit. The model with the lowest BIC was considered the best-fitting model, with differences ≥ 10 providing strong evidence in favour of the model with the lower BIC (Raftery, 1995).

Hypothesis 2 was tested in Model 4. Model 4, the ‘direct and indirect effects model’ (see Figure 1C), examined both the direct effects of interpersonal and non-interpersonal trauma on PTSD and DSO symptoms, as well as the indirect effects via resilience. Any non-significant quadratic associations identified in Model 3 were excluded from Model 4. The adequacy of this model was examined using the aforementioned model fit criteria. To ensure comparability of BIC values across models (with psychological resilience included in Model 4 but not in all others), regression pathways involving the resilience variable were constrained to zero for Models 1 to 3.

Results

Descriptive statistics

Regarding interpersonal trauma, the most common types were other unwanted or uncomfortable sexual experience (29.0%; n = 443), physical assault (18.9%; n = 288), and sexual assault (14.2%; n = 216). The mean number reported by the sample was 0.73 (SD = 1.00, median = 0, range = 0–7). More than half of the sample (55.1%, n = 841) reported no interpersonal traumas, 26.2% (n = 400) reported one, 12.3% (n = 188) reported two, 4.2% (n = 64) reported three, and 2.2% (n = 33) reported four or more. There were no significant gender differences in mean interpersonal trauma scores for females (M = 1.63, SD = 0.85) and males (M = 1.57, SD = 1.03; t(683) = −0.62, p = .533). Regarding non-interpersonal trauma, the most common types were transportation accident (34.0%; n = 519), severe human suffering (43.6%; n = 665) and sudden violent death (15.9%; n = 242). The mean number reported by the sample was 2.14 (SD = 1.67, median = 2.00, range = 0–9). Almost one-in-five participants (19.1%, n = 291) reported no non-interpersonal traumas, 20.4% (n = 312) reported one, 23.1% (n = 352) reported two, 17.4% (n = 266) reported three, and 20.1% (n = 305) reported four or more. There was a significant gender difference in mean non-interpersonal trauma scores for females (M = 2.53, SD = 1.72) and males (M = 3.12, SD = 1.82; t(683) = 3.54, p < .001, d = .34).

Levels of PTSD, DSO, and resilience

The mean PTSD score for the sample was 4.99 (SD = 4.78, median = 3.50, range = 0–24), and there was a significant gender difference in mean PTSD scores for females (M = 5.32, SD = 4.88) and males (M = 4.14, SD = 4.40; t(839.14) = −4.51, p < .001, d = .34). The mean DSO score for the sample was 10.09 (SD = 3.92, median = 9.00, range = 0–24), and there was a significant gender difference in mean DSO scores for females (M = 10.23, SD = 3.99) and males (M = 9.73, SD = 3.72; t(1501) = −2.22, p = .023, d = .13). Finally, the mean resilience score was 21.21 (SD = 4.86, median = 21.50, range = 6–30), and there was a significant gender difference in mean resilience scores for females (M = 20.75, SD = 4.81) and males (M = 22.36, SD = 4.81; t(1507) = 5.84, p < .001, d = .34).

Moderate correlations were shown between resilience and total scores of PTSD (r = −.40, p < .001) and DSO (r = −.48, p < .001).

Testing the linear, dose–response versus curvilinear assumptions (Hypothesis 1)

The fit statistics for Models 1 to 3 are reported in Table 2. Findings demonstrated that the linear direct effects model (Model 2) provided a significantly better fit than the null model (Model 1) (▴BIC = 1682.708). Significant positive associations were shown between interpersonal trauma and both PTSD (β = 0.17, p < .001) and DSO (β = 0.14, p < .001) scores. Similarly, non-interpersonal trauma was significantly associated with PTSD (β = 0.13, p < .001) and DSO (β = 0.07, p = .008) scores. Older age (β = 0.13, p = .05) and female gender (β = 0.09, p < .001) were significantly associated with PTSD but not DSO scores. Model 2 explained 7.0% of the variance in PTSD scores and 3.5% of the variance in DSO scores.

Testing the mediating effect of psychological resilience (Hypothesis 2)

The fit statistics for the mediation model (Model 4) are provided in Table 2. Findings from Model 3 informed the development of Model 4, in which non-significant quadratic terms were removed. As shown in Table 3, this model explained 19.8% of the variance in PTSD, 24.9% in DSO, and 3.0% in psychological resilience, with direct and indirect standardised regression coefficients also reported. Overall, both trauma types were positively and significantly associated with PTSD (interpersonal: β = 0.15, p < .001; non-interpersonal: β = 0.10, p < .001) and DSO (interpersonal: β = 0.12, p < .001; non-interpersonal: β = 0.06, p = .012) symptoms. However, the relationship between non-interpersonal trauma exposure and DSO symptoms followed a quadratic pattern (β = −0.05, p = .030). Additionally, both trauma types were negatively and significantly associated with psychological resilience (interpersonal: β = −0.06, p = .030; non-interpersonal: β = −0.06, p = .032), which, in turn, was negatively and significantly associated with PTSD and DSO symptoms. Regarding indirect effects, both trauma types were indirectly associated with PTSD (interpersonal: β = 0.02, p = .031; non-interpersonal: β = 0.02, p = .037) and DSO symptoms (interpersonal: β = 0.03, p = .031; non-interpersonal: β = 0.03, p = .033) via psychological resilience. Finally, among the model’s covariates, only female gender was significantly associated with resilience scores (β = −0.14, p < .001).

Discussion

The first aim of this study was to investigate whether there is a linear and/or curvilinear relationship between different types of trauma exposure (i.e. interpersonal and non-interpersonal) and symptoms of PTSD and CPTSD. The second aim was to examine whether resilience mediates the relationship between trauma exposure and symptom severity. The results largely supported the proposed hypotheses: Interpersonal trauma showed a significant linear association with both PTSD and DSO symptoms, while non-interpersonal trauma showed a significant linear association with PTSD symptoms and a curvilinear association with DSO symptoms. Furthermore, resilience significantly mediated the relationship between trauma exposure and CPTSD symptoms, indicating that greater trauma exposure was linked to lower resilience, which in turn predicted increased symptom severity.

A substantial proportion of older adults in the sample reported exposure to interpersonal (44.9%) and non-interpersonal (80.9%) trauma. While 6.4% experienced three or more interpersonal traumas, this number was considerably higher for non-interpersonal events (37.5%). Men reported significantly more non-interpersonal trauma than women, while no gender differences were found for interpersonal trauma. These findings partly align with previous international data indicating that lifetime exposure to potentially traumatic events is highly prevalent among older adults (Benjet et al., 2016; Ogle et al., 2013), and that gender differences exist in the exposure to different trauma types (Olff, 2017). The lack of a significant gender difference in exposure to interpersonal trauma contrasts with existing research showing that women are more likely to report such experiences, particularly sexual assault and childhood abuse (e.g. Tolin & Foa, 2008; Maercker et al., 2018). We can only speculate about the reasons for this discrepancy. One possibility is that the older age of our sample is linked to age- or cohort-related effects that influence both reporting behaviour and the prevalence of trauma across genders. Methodologically, it may also reflect self-selection processes, with female or male interpersonal trauma survivors being more or less likely to participate in the study. Finally, differences in the operationalization and measurement of interpersonal trauma across studies may have contributed to the divergent findings. Future research should further investigate how sample characteristics and methodological approaches shape the observed associations between gender and interpersonal trauma exposure and reporting in older populations.

Regarding the first hypothesis, the findings showed that greater exposure to both interpersonal and non-interpersonal trauma were linked to elevated PTSD and DSO symptoms, with stronger effects observed for interpersonal trauma. The observed dose–response relationship between the number of traumatic experiences and PTSD symptoms in older adults contributes to the existing evidence suggesting that cumulative trauma exposure plays a key role in the development and persistence of PTSD (Johnson & Thompson, 2008; Kolassa et al., 2010b; Ogle et al., 2014; Rácz et al., 2023; Steine et al., 2017). The current findings extend previous research by demonstrating that greater exposure to interpersonal trauma is also associated with increased DSO symptoms in older adults. This is in line with existing conceptualisations of CPTSD, which emphasise the impact of prolonged, repeated, and interpersonal forms of trauma (Brewin, 2020; Maercker et al., 2022; WHO, 2019). Notably, the associations between trauma exposure and both PTSD and DSO were relatively weak in magnitude in the present study. Although the reasons for this are not entirely clear, it may reflect the low levels of symptoms in the present sample (for a full explanation, see Thoma et al., 2025), which could have reduced the capacity to detect stronger correlations.

A notable curvilinear effect was observed for non-interpersonal trauma in predicting DSO symptoms, suggesting that symptom severity increased at low to moderate levels of trauma exposure but decreased again at higher exposure levels. This inverted U-shaped association contrasts with the Steeling Effect theory (Liu, 2015; Rutter, 1987, 2006, 2012), which suggests that moderate levels of adversity are linked to more favourable health outcomes. The pattern identified in the current study may reflect a saturation effect or (mal-) adaptive processes, such as desensitisation or emotional numbing (e.g. McNally, 2003). Alternatively, individuals with higher trauma exposure may have developed coping strategies that mitigate further symptom escalation, or retrospective self-report biases may affect how cumulative trauma is perceived and reported. Nevertheless, the differential patterns (i.e. linear for interpersonal trauma and curvilinear for non-interpersonal trauma) underscores the importance of distinguishing between the impact of different trauma types (Cloitre et al., 2009; Kilpatrick et al., 2013; Rácz et al., 2023; Steine et al., 2017; Thomas et al., 2021). While non-interpersonal trauma can exert significant and lasting stress responses, it may not erode core beliefs about the self and others to the same extent as interpersonal trauma (Thomas et al., 2021). This interpretation is supported by our finding that the most common non-interpersonal events were severe yet impersonal in nature. These experiences may be less likely to affect self-identity or relationships, especially as older adults may have become more accustomed to such stressors over time (Cerino et al., 2024). Future research should aim to further disentangle the unique psychological mechanisms associated with trauma type and quantity.

In support of the second hypothesis, resilience functioned as a partial mediator of the relationship between trauma exposure and symptom severity. Specifically, greater exposure to both interpersonal and non-interpersonal trauma was significantly associated with lower resilience, which in turn was linked to higher PTSD and DSO symptoms. These indirect effects support the assumption that resilience serves as a protective factor that buffers the psychological impact of trauma (e.g. Bonanno, 2004; Southwick et al., 2014). The findings are consistent with previous research demonstrating that individuals with greater resilience resources show fewer trauma-related symptoms across the lifespan (Agaibi & Wilson, 2005; Bonanno & Mancini, 2012).

Consistent with the well-established sex differences in PTSD (e.g. Cloitre et al., 2019; McGinty et al., 2021), findings demonstrated that female sex was associated with higher PTSD scores in models testing linear and curvilinear effects. Different factors have been proposed to explain higher rates of PTSD among females including differences in trauma type, gender roles, genetic predispositions, and hormonal influences (Christiansen & Berke, 2020; Tolin & Foa, 2008). Notably, although females reported higher levels of DSO symptoms, female gender was not significantly associated with DSO symptoms after accounting for age and trauma variables. This aligns with prior studies showing inconsistent evidence for gender differences in CPTSD (McGinty et al., 2021). When psychological resilience was included as a mediator, the association between gender and PTSD became non-significant, suggesting that lower psychological resilience among females may partly explain observed gender differences in PTSD. Research pertaining to gender differences in psychological resilience is sparse, however, existing studies have indicated that older women tend to have lower resilience compared to males (e.g. da Silva-Sauer et al., 2020; Sardella et al., 2022). This may be linked to factors such as greater exposure to negative age-related outcomes, poorer mental health, and greater negative affectivity among older females (Sardella et al., 2022). Future research will be required to explore the potential role of psychological resilience in explaining gender effects in trauma symptoms among older adults. The study findings have several clinical and practical implications. The differential associations between interpersonal and non-interpersonal trauma with PTSD and DSO symptom clusters highlight the need for nuanced and comprehensive assessments of trauma history in clinical settings. The observed curvilinear relationship between non-interpersonal trauma and DSO symptoms suggests that the psychological consequences of cumulative trauma may not always follow a linear trajectory. Greater trauma exposure does not inevitably result in greater symptom severity, particularly in the case of non-interpersonal trauma. Clinicians should recognise that individuals actively engage with their experiences and may develop both adaptive and maladaptive coping strategies that shape symptom outcomes. Psychological resilience plays a key role in this process and should be a central focus of trauma-informed interventions, including those targeting older adults. Psychoeducation, cognitive–behavioural techniques, or supportive measures may help enhance resilience and buffer the long-term effects of trauma exposure in this population.

Several limitations of the current study should be acknowledged: (i) The cross-sectional design precludes any causal inferences regarding the relationships between trauma exposure, resilience, and PTSD/DSO symptomatology. Longitudinal studies are needed to fully capture the temporal dynamics of these associations. (ii) The reliance on self-report measures for the retrospective assessment of trauma exposure introduces potential recall bias, which may affect the accuracy of the data. Moreover, the instrument used to assess trauma exposure (LEC-5; Weathers et al., 2013) did not capture the frequency, timing, or severity of traumatic events, which are likely to be important for understanding long-term effects. While our study included a comprehensive assessment of lifetime trauma exposure, emotional neglect, which is a form of childhood adversity closely linked to CPTSD development, was not assessed. Future research should examine the role of emotional neglect both as a potential mediator of trauma-related symptoms and as a factor that may contribute to resilience in its absence. (iii) The sample was recruited using a non-probability sampling method and predominantly comprised of older (female) adults with relatively high levels of education and financial stability. As such, the findings may not be fully generalisable to the broader Swiss German-speaking older population, particularly those in the oldest age brackets or from more socioeconomically diverse backgrounds (Federal Statistical Office, 2024). Finally, our sample was self-selected, predominantly female, highly educated, and relatively young in mean age (72 years). These characteristics suggest a subgroup of older adults with comparatively higher vitality, cognitive functioning, and literacy, as well as gender- and education-related resilience factors which likely restricts the generalizability of the findings to more resilient and resourceful survivors rather than the broader population of older adults with similar adverse life histories.

Future research directions

Building on the current study, several avenues warrant further exploration. The observed curvilinear association between non-interpersonal trauma and DSO symptoms should be replicated and extended in future studies. For instance, advanced analytic techniques, such as latent class or growth mixture modelling, may offer valuable insights into heterogeneous trauma response patterns in older adults. Regarding the measurement of resilience, the current study applied the BRS, which captures an individual’s perceived ability to recover from adversity (Smith et al., 2008). Future research could benefit from a more comprehensive assessment that includes individual, socio-ecological, and contextual aspects of resilience, as well as a life-course perspective (Southwick et al., 2014; Ungar, 2012; Windle, 2011). In addition, structural brain changes at various life stages (e.g. early development, later life) can contribute to sensitive periods of heightened vulnerability to stressors (Lupien et al., 2009). While this study focused on cumulative lifetime trauma, future research should investigate specific traumatic experiences at distinct life stages (e.g. early, middle, late life) to better delineate developmental differences in stress and coping processes. Moreover, varied stressors have been linked to impairment or improvement in cognitive domains such as learning, memory, and verbal fluency (Comijs et al., 2011; Künzi et al., 2023). Incorporating neuropsychological assessments may elucidate the role of cognitive functioning in shaping symptomatology and resilience in trauma-exposed older adults. Furthermore, future studies should examine a broader range of potential mediators of PTSD and DSO symptoms in older adults. For instance, in a clinical sample of older adults (> 64 years), early maladaptive schemas have been shown to mediate the association between childhood trauma and CPTSD severity (Vasilopoulou et al., 2020) and may therefore represent a useful target for future investigations. Resilience accounted for only a small proportion of the variance in the overall model, particularly in comparison to the variance explained in PTSD and DSO outcomes. One possible reason may be the measurement of resilience in the current study: although the Brief Resilience Scale (Smith et al., 2008) minimizes participant burden, it may not fully capture the complexity of the resilience construct (e.g. Bonanno, 2004; Bonanno et al., 2011; Liu et al., 2017; Masten, 2001; Southwick et al., 2014). It is therefore likely that additional factors contribute to individual differences in psychological resilience. Future research should examine a broader range of predictors and mediators, such as social support, coping strategies, emotion regulation, and external resources (Liu et al., 2017), as well as psychological attributes (e.g. self-efficacy, optimism, positive self-image) and relevant sociodemographic characteristics (e.g. socio-economic status). Testing these factors within more comprehensive frameworks, for example through multiple mediation or moderated mediation models, could provide a more nuanced understanding of the mechanisms underlying resilience in older trauma-exposed populations.

Conclusion

This study set out to examine the associations between different types of trauma exposure, resilience, and PTSD and DSO symptoms in a sample of older adults in Switzerland. Findings revealed that both interpersonal and non-interpersonal trauma were significantly associated with PTSD and DSO symptom severity. The observed curvilinear association between non-interpersonal trauma and DSO symptoms hints at a more complex relationship between non-interpersonal trauma and symptoms of self-regulation. Psychological resilience was found to play a protective role between trauma exposure and CPTSD symptoms, even in older adults. Collectively, these results underscore the importance of (a) distinguishing between different trauma types; (b) considering the potential non-linear effects of trauma exposure; and (c) targeting psychological resilience in interventions for older adults. In sum, these findings contribute to a more nuanced understanding of trauma-related psychopathology and resilience in later life.
