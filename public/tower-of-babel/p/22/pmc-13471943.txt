Acute therapy with hemopexin (CSL889) resolves LPS-induced lung vaso-occlusion in sickle mice∗
Authors: Rikesh K. Dubey, Ravi Vats, Thomas Gentinetta, Tomasz W. Kaminski, Manikanta, Omika Katoch, Tirthadipa Pradhan-Sundd, Jesus Tejero, Brandon Greene, Gregory J. Kato, Paolo Rossato, Gerald Hoebarth, Prithu Sundd
Published: 2026

TO THE EDITOR:

Sickle cell disease (SCD) is a monogenic hemolytic disorder affecting ∼8 million people worldwide.1,2 Acute chest syndrome, a type of acute lung injury is a leading cause of mortality in SCD1 and involves lung vascular occlusion (pulmonary vaso-occlusion [PVO]) by neutrophil-platelet-erythrocyte aggregates.3, 4, 5, 6, 7 Hemoglobin and the downstream oxidation product heme released following intravascular hemolysis are potent damage-associated molecular pattern molecules that promote the activation of leukocytes, platelets, and endothelial cells to trigger vaso-occlusion,8,9 suggesting that the scavenging of heme or hemoglobin by replenishing plasma levels of hemopexin or haptoglobin, respectively, can be a potential therapy to prevent acute chest syndrome.10 CSL889,10 a human plasma–derived hemopexin, is currently being investigated in a phase 2/3 clinical trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT06699849) as an acute rescue therapy for patients with SCD hospitalized with active vaso-occlusive crisis.11 Here, we have used intravital (in vivo) lung imaging3 in knockin humanized SCD mice3,12 to assess whether therapeutic (after crisis) treatment with CSL889 can resolve lung vaso-occlusion.

Based on the prior studies, Townes SS and AS mice (male and female ∼12-16 weeks old) were used as SCD and control mice, respectively.3,13, 14, 15 IV challenge with hemoglobin or heme are widely used experimental models of vaso-occlusive crisis in SCD mice13, 14, 15; however, the goal of this study was to assess the effect of scavenging endogenous heme by CSL889 (hemopexin) on the development of lung vaso-occlusion without any interference of the exogenously administered hemin or hemoglobin. Therefore, we used a previously validated model of vaso-occlusive crisis triggered by IV challenge with a nanogram dose of lipopolysaccharide (LPS; from Escherichia coli 0111:B4), which was shown in prior studies to promote lung vaso-occlusion by neutrophil-platelet-erythrocyte aggregates in SCD but not in control mice.3,12,16 SCD mice were IV challenged with 0.1 μg/kg of LPS followed by a therapeutic (IV) treatment with either 60 or 160 mg/kg of CSL889 (based on the low- and high-dose arms tested in the ongoing clinical trial) or same volume of vehicle (saline) 30 minutes later, and lung vaso-occlusion was assessed 3 hours later using quantitative fluorescence intravital lung microscopy (qFILM) approach (Figure 1A) as described elsewhere.3,12,15,17 Given that IV challenge with 0.1 μg/kg of LPS does not trigger lung vaso-occlusion in control mice,3,12,16 the effect of CSL889 therapy was not assessed in control mice. Briefly, SCD mice were anesthetized and ventilated, and the lung was visualized using multiphoton excitation fluorescence microscopy.3,12,15 Identical to prior findings, neutrophil-platelet aggregates were found to occlude pulmonary arteriole bottlenecks (the junction of pulmonary arterioles with pulmonary capillaries) in SCD mice IV challenged with LPS + saline; however, such aggregates were absent and the blood flow was unobstructed in the lung microcirculation of SCD mice IV challenged with LPS + CSL889 (Figure 1B; supplemental Videos 1 and 2). Vaso-occlusive crisis in SCD mice has been shown to be associated with acute hemolysis, leading to the release of cell-free hemoglobin and subsequently cell-free heme.13,14 Indeed, IV challenge with 0.1 μg/kg of LPS led to a significant increase in the concentration of cell-free heme in the plasma of SCD mice (supplemental Figure 1). Therefore, we next assessed whether the therapeutic inhibition of lung vaso-occlusion by CSL889 (hemopexin) is secondary to the scavenging of endogenous cell-free heme released during vaso-occlusive crisis.10,13,14 SCD or control mice were IV challenged with saline or 0.1 μg/kg of LPS ± 160 mg/kg of CSL889, and peripheral blood was collected 2 hours later in EDTA and processed to generate platelet-free plasma (Figure 1F). Concentrations of heme-free and heme-bound CSL889 in the plasma were determined by UV-visible spectroscopy and spectral deconvolution as described elsewhere with minor modifications.18,19 Although endogenous mouse hemopexin cannot be distinguished from CSL889 (human exogenous hemopexin) based on the spectra, plasma hemopexin is known to be depleted in SCD but abundant in control mice,13 suggesting that any endogenous heme released during vaso-occlusive crisis (IV LPS challenge) would be available to bind to therapeutically administered CSL889 in SCD but not in control mice. Based on this, any increase in hemopexin concentration in SCD mice plasma following CSL889 treatment was expected to be reflective of exogenous CSL889. Indeed, the concentration of hemopexin-heme complex was elevated by >6-fold in SCD mice treated with LPS + CSL889 compared with LPS only (Figure 1G), but only by ∼1.5-fold in control mice treated with LPS + CSL889 compared with LPS only (Figure 1H). Contrary to heme-bound hemopexin, the plasma concentration of heme-free hemopexin was significantly elevated in CSL889-treated control mice (Figure 1J) but not CSL889-treated SCD mice (Figure 1I), suggestive of CSL889 consumption for heme scavenging in SCD but not in control mice. Heme-bound and heme-free hemopexin concentrations were added to estimate the total hemopexin concentration in the plasma, which was significantly elevated in both SCD and control mice treated with LPS + CSL889 compared with LPS alone (Figure 1K-L). Hemopexin clearance is known to be functionally heme dependent20 and accelerated in the SCD mice,10 thereby explaining the limited increase in the total plasma hemopexin observed in saline-treated SCD mice after CSL889 infusion (Figure 1K).

Cell-free hemoglobin released during intravascular hemolysis is scavenged by plasma haptoglobin; however, SCD is associated with the chronic depletion of haptoglobin as well.1,8,9 Therefore, we next used lung intravital microscopy (qFILM) to assess whether the therapeutic treatment with a human plasma–derived haptoglobin (CSL888) can also prevent the development of acute chest syndrome in SCD mice (Figure 2A). Indeed, therapeutic treatment with CSL888 also led to the resolution of IV LPS–triggered PVO by neutrophil-platelet aggregates in SCD mice (representative qFILM FOVs are shown in Figure 2B). Neutrophil-platelet aggregates were absent, and the blood was observed to flow unobstructed in the lung vasculature of SCD mice IV administered CSL888 after IV LPS challenge (supplemental Video 3). Quantitative analysis of the qFILM data revealed that CSL888 also resolved PVO in a dose-dependent manner, and the maximum inhibition was observed at the higher dose of 90 mg/kg. Anti–P-selectin antibody is the only US Food and Drug Administration-approved targeted therapy, which was previously shown to reduce hospitalization of patients with SCD21 and lung vaso-occlusion in SCD mice12 by ∼50%. Recently, we generated a SCD-Selp−/− mice, which was also found to be partially protected from the development of PVO.22 Subsequently, SCD-Selp−/− mice (n = 10 per group) were IV challenged with 0.1 μg/kg of LPS followed by therapeutic treatment with 60 mg/kg of CSL889, and PVO was assessed 3 hours later using qFILM (Figure 2F). Remarkably, therapeutic treatment with 60 mg/kg of CSL889 led to further reduction in PVO in SCD-Selp−/− mice. The number of PVOs per FOV (Figure 2G), number of large PVOs (size >1000 μm2) per FOV (Figure 2H), and percent FOVs with PVOs (Figure 2I) were significantly reduced by approximately twofold in SCD-Selp−/− mice therapeutically treated with CSL889 compared with SCD-Selp−/− mice treated with saline after IV LPS challenge. Collectively, these findings suggest that therapeutic treatment with human plasma–derived hemopexin (CSL889) resolves PVO and prevents the development of acute chest syndrome in SCD mice by scavenging cell-free heme. Unlike the US Food and Drug Administration-approved P-selectin antibody therapy, which is administered prophylactically, CSL889 not only ameliorates lung vaso-occlusion in the acute setting (after onset of crisis), but it also inhibits pathological mechanisms that are not affected by P-selectin inhibition. Eventually, the ongoing clinical trial will determine whether the acute therapy with CSL889 can reduce hospitalization and development of acute chest syndrome in patients with SCD admitted to the emergency department with vaso-occlusive crisis. Importantly, the ongoing clinical trial permits CSL889 dosing up to ∼72 hours after vaso-occlusive crisis, but in our preclinical model, CSL889 was administered to SCD mice shortly (∼30 minutes) after crisis induction. Therefore, further studies are warranted in the future to define the latest time window for effective therapeutic intervention with CSL889.
