Ec_Ou_ZC1, a new Tunavirus, reveals Cor superinfection exclusion and differential Avs susceptibility across Drexlerviridae subfamilies
Authors: Quanrui Zhang, Yingying Liu, Jiaoxia Qin, Jixia Wei, Fan Yang, Tao Zhang, Qunxin She, Chen Zhang, Min Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The global rise of antibiotic resistance has rekindled interest in bacteriophages (phages) as alternative therapeutics. Here, we isolated and characterized a novel Escherichia coli phage, Ec_Ou_ZC1, from the family Drexlerviridae, genus Tunavirus, sharing 94.72% genomic identity with Escherichia phage phiD2-2. Ec_Ou_ZC1 encodes an orphan methyltransferase and a Cor superinfection exclusion (SIE) protein. Heterologous expression of Cor blocked all tested FhuA-dependent Drexlerviridae phages (Ec_Ou_ZC1, T1, Bas03, Bas12, Bas13) but not T4 or T7, confirming family-specific SIE through FhuA receptor inactivation. Plaque assays against four antiviral STAND (Avs) systems revealed differential susceptibility within Drexlerviridae: Bas03 (Braunvirinae) was sensitive to SeAvs3, while Ec_Ou_ZC1, Bas05, Bas12, Bas13, and T1 (Tempevirinae and Tunavirinae) resisted all four systems, suggesting that Avs resistance depends on specific genomic features. Species-level phylogenetic analyses using concatenated single-copy orthologs robustly resolved evolutionary relationships within Tunavirus, separating Escherichia- and Shigella-infecting phages and providing a framework for predicting host range of new isolates.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12866-026-05347-3.

Introduction

Bacteriophages, or phages, are the most abundant biological entities on Earth, with an estimated ~ 10³¹ particles globally, and they exert profound influences on microbial community structure, bacterial evolution, and ecological interactions [1, 2]. The global escalation of multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacterial pathogens has compromised the efficacy of conventional antibiotics, driving renewed interest in phage therapy as both a complementary treatment to enhance antibiotic efficacy and as an alternative when antibiotics fail [3, 4]. The natural abundance of phages and their co-evolutionary dynamics with bacterial hosts position them as promising antibacterial agents against pathogenic bacteria [1, 5, 6].

Recent advances in phage genomics have led to a major revision of phage taxonomy by the International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV), replacing traditional morphology-based classification with a genome-based framework in which species and genus demarcation relies on nucleotide sequence similarity, while family-level classification is based on core gene sets and phylogenetic analysis [7, 8]. Within the class Caudoviricetes, the genus Tunavirus (T1-like phages in the family Drexlerviridae) infects members of the Enterobacteriaceae, particularly Escherichia coli, with some members also infecting Shigella species [9–11]. Tunavirus phages typically possess linear double-stranded DNA genomes ranging from 45 to 50 kb that encode approximately 80 genes [7, 12]. These genes are organized into functional modules for replication, morphogenesis, lysis, and host interaction, along with accessory regions that vary among isolates despite conserved morphology [7]. Although phages, such as Escherichia phage T1 and Shigella phage SH6, have been extensively studied, phylogenetic analyses based on single-copy orthologous genes (SCGs) [12–14], primarily terminases, portal proteins, capsid proteins, or their concatenations, have yielded inconsistent evolutionary relationships. Furthermore, polyvalent phages, such as Shigella phage SH6, which infect both Escherichia and Shigella species, complicate phylogenetic inference within Tunavirus when relying on limited marker genes. This challenge is further compounded when attempting to predict host range for novel isolates, particularly when experimental access to diverse pathogenic strains is limited.

Here, we report the isolation and genomic characterization of a novel Tunavirus, designated Ec_Ou_ZC1, isolated from E. coli. We employed phylogenetic analysis based on the congruence of multiple single-copy core gene phylogenies to construct a robust species-level phylogeny, achieving greater resolution of evolutionary relationships within Tunavirus than single-gene approaches provide. Notably, Ec_Ou_ZC1 consistently clustered most closely with Escherichia phage phiD2-2. Additionally, Ec_Ou_ZC1 encodes a Cor protein that mediates superinfection exclusion (SIE), blocking secondary infection by other Drexlerviridae phages through FhuA receptor inactivation. We experimentally validated the family-specific SIE activity of Cor by heterologous expression. We also assessed the susceptibility of Ec_Ou_ZC1 and related Drexlerviridae phages to antiviral STAND (Avs) immune proteins and found differential susceptibility within the family: while Bas03 was sensitive to SeAvs3, Ec_Ou_ZC1 and four other members resisted all four Avs systems, suggesting that specific genomic features underlie Avs resistance in certain Drexlerviridae phages. Together, these findings refine our understanding of Tunavirus evolutionary relationships and functional repertoires, underscoring their ecological, evolutionary, and therapeutic potential for combating antibiotic-resistant Enterobacteriaceae.

Materials and methods

Phage isolation, purification, and amplification

LB broth and LB agar (1.5%) were used for bacterial growth and phage propagation. Soft agar overlays contained 0.5% LB agar. SM buffer (100 mM NaCl, 8 mM MgSO₄, 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5) was used for phage dilution and storage. Phages were isolated from the river near hospital (Latitude: 27.948907°N, Longitude: 120.800999°E) in Wenzhou, Zhejiang province, China. Samples (10 mL) were centrifuged at 5,000 × g for 10 min, and the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane to remove bacterial cells. The filtrates were serially diluted (10-fold) using the saline magnesium (SM) buffer and mixed with E. coli BL21 (DE3), which lacks restriction-modification systems (ΔhsdSB) [15, 16]. We note that BL21(DE3) carries the defective λDE3 prophage, which provides T7 RNA polymerase but lacks phage structural and lysis genes and is therefore unlikely to interfere with lytic phage propagation. After the plates were incubated overnight at 37 °C, individual plaques were picked and resuspended in 100 µL of SM buffer for 1 h at room temperature. Following centrifugation at 5,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, 10 µL of phage suspension from different dilutions were mixed with 100 µL of log-phase host culture and overlaid with 6 mL of molten soft agar (0.5% LB agar) onto solid LB agar (1.5% LB agar) plates. After incubation at 37 °C until plaques had confluent lysis, the soft agar layer was scraped off, transferred into 50 mL centrifuge tubes, and homogenized with 15 mL of SM buffer. The suspension was shaken at 50 rpm at 4 °C for 4 h, centrifuged at 5,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C, and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane. The filtration was further concentrated using a 100 kDa ultrafiltration device at 3,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was subjected to repeated dilution and re-plating for five successive rounds to ensure clonal purity described in Fig. 1A. The resulting high-titer phage suspension was aliquoted and preserved in LB medium supplemented with 25% glycerol (v/v) at -80 °C for future use. Phage titers were determined using the double-layer agar method and expressed as plaque-forming units (PFU/mL). For morphological characterization, concentrated phage preparations were subjected to negative staining and examined using transmission electron microscopy (TEM).

Transmission electron microscopy of phages

Concentrated phage suspensions were diluted for morphological observation. 5 µL of diluted phage were applied onto a 200-mesh carbon-coated copper grid and allowed to adsorb for 5–6 min at room temperature. Excess liquid was carefully removed with filter paper, and the grid was subsequently stained with 5 µL of 2% phosphotungstic acid (~ pH = 7.0) for 3 min. After blotting off the excess stain, the grids were air-dried and examined using a transmission electron microscope operated at 80 kV. Multiple fields of view were imaged to record virion morphology, and the dimensions of phage structural components were measured using ImageJ software.

Stability of phages

Thermal stability

To evaluate thermal stability, 1 mL of phage suspension (1 × 107 PFU/mL) was incubated at different temperatures (4 °C, 25 °C, 37 °C, 50 °C, 60 °C and 70 °C) for 1 h in a water bath. After incubation, the residual phage titers were determined using the double-layer agar method, and survival curves were plotted to assess the effect of temperature on phage infectivity.

pH stability

To assess pH stability, SM buffer solutions were adjusted to pH 3, 5, 7, 9, 11 and 12. A 10 µL aliquot of phage stock (1 × 109 PFU/mL) was added to 990 µL of SM buffer at each pH condition and incubated at 4 °C for 1 h. After incubation, phage titers were determined using the double-layer agar method. The survival curves were plotted to evaluate the effect of different pH conditions on phage viability.

Determination of optimal Multiplicity of Infection (MOI) and one-step growth curve

Determination of optimal MOI

The optimal multiplicity of infection (MOI) was determined by mixing phage suspensions with exponentially growing E. coli BL21 (OD600 ≈ 0.5, approximately 4 × 10⁸ CFU/mL) at different ratios. Serially diluted phage suspensions were mixed with equal volumes (100 µL) of host culture to achieve MOIs of 100, 10, 1, 0.1, 0.01, 0.001, 0.0001, and 0.00001, respectively. The mixtures were incubated at 37 °C with shaking (180 rpm) for 16 h. Bacterial growth was monitored by measuring OD600 at 10 min intervals, and the phage titers at the end of incubation were determined using the double-layer agar method. The MOI that yielded the highest final phage titer was defined as the optimal infection ratio.

One-step growth curve

The one-step growth experiment was performed as previously described with modifications [17–19]. Exponentially growing E. coli BL21 (DE3) was prepared in LB at 37 °C with shaking (220 rpm) until OD₆₀₀ ≈ 0.5 (4 × 10⁸ CFU/mL). Phage Ec_Ou_ZC1 was added to 1 mL of culture at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.01 (final phage concentration = 4 × 10⁶ PFU/mL) and incubated at 37 °C without shaking for 5 min to allow adsorption. At t = 4 min, a 100-µL aliquot was withdrawn, centrifuged (12,000 × g, 1 min, 4 °C), and the supernatant titred by the double-layer agar method to quantify residual free phages and confirm adsorption efficiency (mean adsorption efficiency across replicates = 90.1% ± 0.4%). At t = 5 min, 100 µL of the adsorption mixture was transferred to 9.9 mL pre-warmed LB (37 °C), mixed by gentle inversion, and 1.0 mL of this intermediate was further diluted into 9.0 mL pre-warmed LB (final post-adsorption dilution 1:1,000); the diluted culture was incubated at 37 °C with shaking (220 rpm). The number of productively infected cells per mL (Ni) was determined empirically by the chloroform-based infective centres assay: at t = 8 and t = 10 min post-infection (within the eclipse phase), parallel 1.0-mL samples were collected into (a) an empty 1.5-mL tube to measure the pre-chloroform titre (free phages plus intracellular infective centres) and (b) a 1.5-mL tube containing 50 µL chloroform (vortexed 10 s) to measure the post-chloroform titre (free phages only, as chloroform lyses cells still in the eclipse phase before mature virions accumulate). Ni was calculated as the difference between the mean pre- and mean post-chloroform titres at t = 8 and t = 10 min. Subsequent samples were collected at t = 15, 20, 25, 30, 40, and 50 min (without chloroform) and at t = 60, 75, and 90 min (with chloroform treatment as above). All samples were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 1 min at 4 °C; chloroform-containing samples were centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 2 min at 4 °C for phase separation. Aqueous-phase supernatants were titred by the double-layer agar method. The average burst size was calculated as the mean post-chloroform plateau titre (t = 60, 75, 90 min) divided by Ni. Three independent biological replicates were performed, each with three technical replicates, on separate days using freshly prepared host cultures. Data are presented as mean ± SD across biological replicates.

Plaque assays

Plaque assays were performed as previously described [20–22]. Recombinant plasmids carrying EcAvs2 (Escherichia coli NCTC9087, Avs2), SeAvs3 (Salmonella enterica NCTC13175, Avs3), KpAvs4 (Klebsiella pneumoniae UNMC_8078, Avs4), EcAvs5 (Escherichia coli NCTC13384, Avs5) were synthesized and cloned by GenScript. All the plasmids were transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3), with the empty vector pACYC-Duet used as a control (chloramphenicol, T7 promoter). A single colony from each transformation was inoculated into LB broth and cultured overnight at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm. The overnight cultures were then diluted 1:100 into 3 mL of fresh LB medium supplemented with 35 µg/mL chloramphenicol and 25 µM IPTG and grown at 37 °C with shaking until reaching an OD600 of 0.4. Aliquots of 400 µL of bacterial culture were mixed with 6 mL of 0.5% LB soft agar containing 25 µM IPTG, and the mixtures were poured onto 1.5% LB agar plates. After solidification, 2.2 µL of 10-fold serial dilutions of phage lysates were spotted onto the surface. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 4 h before imaging.

Genomic DNA extraction, high-throughput sequencing, and bioinformatics analysis

The phage genome DNA was extracted using phenol chloroform with some optimizations [23]. Raw paired-end short reads were quality-controlled and adapter-trimmed using Trim Galore (v0.6.10) with default parameters. A total of 23,342,418 paired-end reads (11,594,321 read pairs) were generated, of which 87.9% were mapped to the assembled genome, with 85.3% properly paired, yielding an average sequencing depth of approximately 60,534× and 100% genome coverage at ≥ 100× depth. The processed clean reads were assembled using SPAdes (v4.0.0) in “metaviral” assembly mode, which incorporates viral-specific optimizations including high-coverage cycle detection for circular genome identification and specialized repeat-resolving for terminal repeats, to generate the complete circular genome. The assembled genome was annotated using pharokka (v1.7.5). For coverage analysis and visualization, a sequential bioinformatics pipeline was employed. Raw reads were mapped to the assembled genome using minimap2 (v2.30-r1287), followed by indexing and processing with SAMtools (v1.22.1). Coverage statistics were computed and converted to bedgraph format using BEDtools (v2.31.1). The final genome visualization integrating coverage data was generated using lovis4u (v0.1.2) [24].

Taxonomic classification and comparative genomic analysis

The assembled circular Ec_Ou_ZC1 phage genome was subjected to taxonomic classification using tax_myPHAGE (v0.3.4) according to the latest ICTV taxonomy (VMR_MSL40) [25]. The coliphage Ec_Ou_ZC1 was classified as a novel species within the genus Tunavirus. Subsequently, sixty-three complete genomes of Tunavirus members (taxid: 187287) were downloaded from the NCBI Virus database (accessed August 2, 2025). Eighteen Tunavirus genomes identified as identical duplicates were detected using “vclust deduplicate” (v1.3.1) and removed, resulting in forty-five unique Tunavirus members. Including Ec_Ou_ZC1, these forty-six Tunavirus genomes were aligned using “vclust align” (v1.3.1) with default parameters, and the results were visualized using ComplexHeatmap (v2.13.1). The three Tunavirus members with the highest similarity to Ec_Ou_ZC1 were selected for detailed genomic comparison using blastn2dotplots (v1.1.2) to analyze genomic variations in regions with < 95% sequence identity. Whole-genome alignments of these three closely related members were visualized using lovis4u (v0.1.2) with default settings. Structurally variable regions exhibiting < 95% sequence identity were annotated and compared at the proteome level using lovis4u (v0.1.2). Defense- and anti-defense-related genes were identified using DefenseFinder and PADLOC, both deployed by default within the lovis4u pipeline. Additionally, the structure of the HNH endonuclease from Ec_Ou_ZC1 was predicted using Swiss-Model and visualized with ChimeraX (v1.10.1).

Whole-genome orthologs-based phylogenetic tree inference

Six viruses were used solely as outgroup members in the initial phylogenetic analysis due to their lower sequence identity (< 35%) and coverage (< 50%). Subsequently, only the forty Tunavirus members, including Ec_Ou_ZC1, were grouped together for further phylogenetic analysis. All phage genomes were annotated using Pharokka (v1.7.5) as described above, and their evolutionary relationships were subsequently resolved using OrthoFinder (v3.1.0) [26, 27]. The phylogenetic reconstruction pipeline proceeded as follows: OrthoFinder was used to process proteome sequences in “all-vs-all” mode to identify orthogroups, and from which single-copy orthologs were selected as reliable phylogenetic marker genes. Each single-copy ortholog was individually aligned, and all alignments were concatenated into a supermatrix using the FAMSA method [28]. Gene trees were inferred from individual ortholog alignments, and the species-level tree was computed using ASTRAL-Pro [29]. The final phylogenetic tree was constructed using FastTree with a maximum likelihood (ML) approach [30].

Results

Isolation and morphological characterization of phage Ec_Ou_ZC1

Phage Ec_Ou_ZC1 was isolated from a surface water sample collected from the river near the hospital in Wenzhou detailed in materials and methods, using Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) as the isolation strain (Fig. 1A). The phage was purified by repeated single-plaque isolation on LB agar plates seeded with BL21 (DE3), where it produced clear and well-defined plaques (Fig. 1B). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) with negative staining was performed to examine its morphology. TEM analysis revealed that Ec_Ou_ZC1 has an icosahedral capsid with an average diameter of 60 nm and a long, non-contractile tail measuring approximately 150 nm in length consistent with siphophage morphology (Fig. 1C). The virions were dispersed and exhibited intact head–tail structures. These structural features are consistent with the morphological characteristics of Escherichia phages belonging to Drexlerviridae family, Tunavirus genus, suggesting that Ec_Ou_ZC1 is a tailed double-stranded DNA phage within this taxonomic group.

Bacteriolytic activity, one-step growth, thermal and pH stability of phage Ec_Ou_ZC1

We at first tested whether phage Ec_Ou_ZC1 could inhibit the dynamic growth of E. coli. As shown in Fig. 2A, we observed that the growth of E. coli following treatment with phage Ec_Ou_ZC1 at different MOIs from 0.00001 to 100 respectively was inhibited compared to counterpart without additional phage (MOI = 0) (Fig. 2A). Notably, even at extremely low MOIs, the phage still suppressed bacterial proliferation, highlighting its strong lytic capability. One-step growth curve analysis revealed that Ec_Ou_ZC1 had a latent period of approximately 20 min, followed by a rapid rise phase, and reached a plateau around 60 min post-infection (Fig. 2B). The average burst size was 204.5 ± 20.2 PFU per infected cell, calculated as the empirically determined plateau titre (mean of t = 60, 75, and 90 min post-chloroform samples; 7.40 ± 0.35 × 10⁵ PFU/mL) divided by the empirically determined number of initially infected cells in the diluted assay tube (3.64 ± 0.29 × 10³ cells/mL, measured by chloroform-based infective centres assay at t = 8 and t = 10 min post-infection). To better assess the potential of phage Ec_Ou_ZC1 for environmental applications, we evaluated its stability under different temperature and pH conditions. Ec_Ou_ZC1 was incubated at a range of temperatures for a defined duration, after which residual phage activity was determined. The results indicated that Ec_Ou_ZC1 remained highly stable at temperatures ranging from 4 to 50 °C, showing only a slight reduction in infectivity. However, the phage activity was markedly decreased at 60 °C and was completely lost at 70 °C (Fig. 2C). Regarding pH tolerance, Ec_Ou_ZC1 exhibited a broad stability across pH 5–11, whereas its infectivity dropped significantly at pH 3 and pH 12, where the phage was completely inactivated (Fig. 2D). These results suggest that Ec_Ou_ZC1 has excellent stability under a wide range of environmental conditions, making it a promising candidate for practical applications.

Genomic analysis of phage Ec_Ou_ZC1

The Ec_Ou_ZC1 phage genome consists of 48,932 bp with an average GC content of 45.35% comparable to that of the E. coli BL21 (DE3) host genome (approximately 50.8%) (Fig. 3). Genome annotation identified seventy-eight open reading frames (ORFs) within the Ec_Ou_ZC1 genome. Notably, the genome lacks antibiotic resistance genes, tRNA genes, and virulence factors, indicating minimal potential for mediating horizontal gene transfer of antibiotic resistance determinants. The predicted functional genes were classified into eleven modules based on their putative functions [31], including head and packaging, lysis, tail and connector, and nucleotide and auxiliary metabolism. The lysis module includes genes encoding an endolysin, a holin, and spanin components, confirming the lytic capability of Ec_Ou_ZC1. The Ec_Ou_ZC1 genome encodes several notable accessory elements. An orphan methyltransferase (MTase) gene was identified. Although PADLOC annotates this gene as a defense element, consistent with its homology to MTases in bacterial restriction-modification systems, phage-encoded orphan MTases are known to protect the phage genome against host restriction endonucleases through DNA methylation [32], effectively functioning as a defense evasion mechanism. Additionally, a cor gene was detected, encoding a superinfection exclusion (SIE) protein. Cor proteins are well characterized in phage T1 and closely related Tunavirus members, where they function by inactivating the FhuA outer membrane receptor, thereby preventing secondary infection by other FhuA-dependent phages [33, 34]. The genome also contains an intron-encoded HNH endonuclease within a structurally variable genomic region. Related HNH endonucleases have been characterized as nickases targeting co-infecting phage genomes [35, 36], though functional validation is required to confirm this activity in Ec_Ou_ZC1.

Whole-genome sequence alignments and genomic variation region comparisons

Based on the taxonomic classification results, Ec_Ou_ZC1 was identified as a new species within the genus Tunavirus. To characterize its genomic relationships, whole-genome pairwise sequence alignments were performed between Ec_Ou_ZC1 and the forty-five non-identical virus genomes (Table S1). The comparative analysis revealed that Ec_Ou_ZC1 exhibited the highest sequence identity (94.72%) with coliphage phiD2-2 (OR861627.1), followed by coliphage FCWL1 (94.66%, PP558327.2) and vB_EcoS_SH2 (92.49%, NC_047828.1). Notably, six phages demonstrated low genomic similarity to Ec_Ou_ZC1 and other thirty-nine phages, with pairwise identities below 35% and genome coverage below 50% as shown in Fig. 4A. To further investigate the genomic differences among the most closely related viruses, structural genomic comparisons were conducted between Ec_Ou_ZC1 and the three highest-identity phages (Fig. 4B). To enable direct comparison, all four genomes were reoriented to the terminase large subunit (TerL) as a common start position. Pairwise dotplot comparisons and tBLASTx-based genome alignments revealed high overall collinearity among the four genomes (Figs. 4B, S2B). A localized region of sequence divergence was identified, characterized by reduced nucleotide identity (90–95%) but conserved gene order. Additional pairwise comparisons among the three reference phages revealed that phiD2-2 and FCWL1 shared the highest whole-genome identity (99.96%), while phiD2-2 and vB_EcoS_SH2 exhibited 97.39% identity.

Comparative genomic analysis of variable regions

To comprehensively characterize the genomic differences within the variable regions, comparative genomic analysis was performed among Ec_Ou_ZC1 and its three closest relatives. The analysis revealed that these four genomes are largely collinear, with localized sequence variation within the variable region (Fig. 5A). At the proteomic level, coliphage vB_EcoS_SH2 showed the highest similarity to Ec_Ou_ZC1, with the latter containing four non-conserved proteins within the variable region, consistent with the genomic alignment results presented in Fig. 4B. Detailed proteomic comparison revealed a notable deletion of an RNA-binding protein and insertion of an intron-encoded HNH homing endonuclease between the polynucleotide kinase and deoxynucleoside monophosphate kinase genes. Structural superimposition of the HNH homing endonuclease with I-HmuI (PDB: 1U3E) using SWISS-MODEL revealed conservation of three catalytic residues (H79, N91, and N100), suggesting potential nickase activity (Fig. 5B). An independent template-free AlphaFold2 prediction (ColabFold v1.6.1; pLDDT = 92.1, pTM = 0.802) yielded a model in strong agreement with the SWISS-MODEL homology model (RMSD 0.92 Å over 47 aligned Cα atoms; Figure S1), corroborating the predicted fold and catalytic site architecture.

Evolutionary position of Ec_Ou_ZC1 and phylogenetic relationships among Tunavirus members

Based on the low genomic identity and coverage observed in the whole-genome alignments (Fig. 4A), six phages were excluded from further analysis. The remaining thirty-nine Tunavirus members plus Ec_Ou_ZC1 were used to reconstruct evolutionary relationships. Thirty-two single-copy genes (SCGs), identified as orthologs, were employed for phylogenetic inference. The phylogenetic analysis confirmed that Ec_Ou_ZC1 is most closely related to phiD2-2, followed by FCWL1 and vB_EcoS_SH2, consistent with the genomic alignment results (Fig. 4A). The ortholog-based phylogenetic approach effectively distinguished phages according to their host specificity, separating Escherichia-infecting from Shigella-infecting phages (Fig. 6A). Notably, three E. coli phages (vB_EcoS_SA32RD, vB_EcoS-DELF2, and AV106) clustered more closely with Shigella phages than with other Escherichia phages, despite their reported host range being limited to E. coli strains [37]. Stepwise phylogenetic analyses were performed separately for each host group, revealing increased orthologous SCG numbers: forty-seven for Escherichia phages and thirty-nine for Shigella phages. This difference suggests greater genomic divergence among Shigella phages compared to Escherichia phages (Fig. 6B and C). These phylogenetic relationships were independently confirmed by ViPTree analysis [38] based on genome-wide tBLASTx similarities (Figure S2). The refined phylogenetic analysis provided higher resolution of evolutionary relationships within both Escherichia and Shigella phage groups, with many species maintaining consistent phylogenetic positions within their respective subgroups, including our isolate Ec_Ou_ZC1. The analysis effectively clarified evolutionary distinctions between Escherichia and Shigella phages, with one notable exception: Shigella phage SH6, which clustered with Escherichia phages (Fig. 6C). This phylogenetic positioning of SH6 corresponds with its characterized polyvalent host range, capable of infecting both E. coli and S. flexneri strains, with apparent preference for E. coli [10].

Differential susceptibility of Drexlerviridae phages to Avs immunity and Cor-mediated superinfection exclusion

Avs STAND immune proteins are widely distributed among Enterobacteriaceae and trigger abortive infection upon recognizing conserved phage structural proteins, including terminases and portal proteins [21, 22]. Since Ec_Ou_ZC1 encodes intact terminase and portal genes, we assessed whether Avs-mediated immunity could target this phage and related Drexlerviridae members. We tested eleven phages from Drexlerviridae and other families against four Avs systems (EcAvs2, SeAvs3, KpAvs4, and EcAvs5) (Fig. 7A). A subset of seven phages — five Drexlerviridae members (Ec_Ou_ZC1, T1, Bas03, Bas12, Bas13) plus T4 and T7 as non-Drexlerviridae controls — were additionally tested against the Cor superinfection exclusion protein encoded by Ec_Ou_ZC1. The Drexlerviridae panel included representatives from Braunvirinae (Bas03), Tempevirinae (Bas05), and Tunavirinae (Bas12, Bas13, T1, Ec_Ou_ZC1) [39, 40]. All four Avs systems were functional, as confirmed by positive controls: T3 and T7 were sensitive to EcAvs2, Bas03 to SeAvs3, Bas21 to KpAvs4, and T4 and T6 to EcAvs5 (Fig. 7B, Figure S4A). Ec_Ou_ZC1, Bas05, Bas12, Bas13, and T1 were resistant to all four Avs systems. Notably, Bas03 was the sole Drexlerviridae member sensitive to one Avs system (SeAvs3), demonstrating that Avs-mediated immunity can target Drexlerviridae phages. Heterologous expression of Cor-SIE – whose sequence is conserved among Tunavirus members (Figure S4B) – abolished infection by all tested Drexlerviridae phages – Ec_Ou_ZC1, Bas03, Bas12, Bas13, and T1 – but had no effect on T7 or T4 (Fig. 7B, Figure S4C). Since Cor functions by inactivating FhuA [33, 34, 41], these results functionally confirm FhuA dependence for all five blocked phages. We note that receptor usage is not uniform across Drexlerviridae; Maffei et al. (2021) identified diverse terminal receptors including BtuB, YncD, TolC, and LptD among other family members [39].

Discussion

We characterized Ec_Ou_ZC1, a novel lytic Tunavirus phage closely related to phiD2-2, FCWL1, and vB_EcoS_SH2. Heterologous expression confirmed Cor as a family-specific superinfection exclusion factor targeting FhuA-dependent Drexlerviridae phages. Expanded Avs testing revealed differential susceptibility within Drexlerviridae, with subfamily-level correlation. Stepwise phylogenetic analyses robustly resolved Tunavirus evolutionary relationships, providing a framework for host range prediction.

Stepwise phylogenetic resolution of Tunavirus

Previous studies using four concatenated core genes could distinguish Tunavirus from related genera [12], but relationships within the genus remained ambiguous. An initial analysis using eight SCGs separated Escherichia- from Shigella-infecting phages, though three Escherichia phages clustered with Shigella phages (Figure S3). Excluding six low-similarity outgroup members, our stepwise approach using thirty-two SCGs clearly resolved this separation (Fig. 6A). Our pipeline incorporates ASTRAL-Pro, which infers the species tree from individual gene trees while accounting for gene tree discordance, providing robustness against misleading signal from recombination. Three outlier Escherichia phages (vB_EcoS_DELF2, AV106, vB_EcoS_SA32RD) consistently clustering within the Shigella clade, suggesting widened host ranges. Subgroup phylogenies with increased SCG numbers (forty-seven and thirty-nine for Escherichia and Shigella phages, respectively) further refined these relationships (Fig. 6B and C). The polyvalent phage SH6, capable of infecting both genera [10], occupied a boundary position consistent with its dual host range. This stepwise framework increases confidence in host range prediction for newly isolated phages, complementing computational tools such as PHERI and HOSTPHINDER that integrate host-side determinants [42, 43]. Ec_Ou_ZC1 consistently occupied a stable position within the E. coli-infecting lineage [39], though the recovery of bacterial growth at approximately 8–9 h post-infection (Fig. 2A) suggests that Ec_Ou_ZC1 would be most effective as a component of a phage cocktail.

Cor-mediated superinfection exclusion

Heterologous expression of the Ec_Ou_ZC1 Cor protein confirmed its function as a family-specific SIE factor: Cor blocked all tested Drexlerviridae phages (T1, Bas03, Bas12, Bas13, and Ec_Ou_ZC1) but not T4 or T7, consistent with Cor-mediated FhuA receptor inactivation [29, 30]. As illustrated in Fig. 8, Cor is produced during primary infection and inserted into the outer membrane, where it physically occludes FhuA and prevents receptor engagement by competing phages – a mechanism operating entirely at the cell surface, prior to phage DNA injection, and therefore independent from intracellular immune systems such as Avs. These data provide independent functional confirmation of FhuA dependence for Bas03, Bas12, Bas13, and T1 beyond T1’s previously characterized receptor usage [9]. This receptor-blocking strategy carries an inherent ecological trade-off: while it protects the producing phage’s progeny against superinfection by competing FhuA-dependent phages within the same infected cell, it simultaneously renders the host refractory to the producing phage itself in subsequent rounds — a cost likely offset in dense, phage-rich environments where superinfection pressure is high. The conservation of Cor homologs across more than half of reported Tunavirus members [36] suggests this receptor-targeting SIE strategy is a widespread and ecologically stable solution within the genus. Additionally, Ec_Ou_ZC1 encodes an HNH endonuclease with conserved catalytic residues (H79, N91, N100) homologous to the I-HmuI nickase [35, 36], suggesting a potential nickase role in inter-phage competition, though in vitro activity assays are required to confirm this function.

Differential Avs resistance within Drexlerviridae

Avs resistance and Cor-mediated SIE represent two mechanistically independent patterns of phage–host interaction operating at distinct cellular compartments (Fig. 8). While Cor acts uniformly at the outer membrane against all FhuA-dependent phages regardless of subfamily, Avs resistance varies sharply across Drexlerviridae subfamilies: Bas03 (Braunvirinae) was sensitive to SeAvs3, whereas all tested Tempevirinae and Tunavirinae members resisted all four Avs systems. This differential susceptibility is not attributable to non-functional Avs systems or general Drexlerviridae non-recognition: each system was confirmed active against at least one control phage under identical conditions, and Bas03’s sensitivity directly demonstrates that Avs-mediated immunity can target phages within this family. The resistance of Ec_Ou_ZC1 and related members therefore reflects specific genomic features distinguishing Tunavirinae and Tempevirinae from Braunvirinae, a phylogenetic correlation consistent with prior observations that defense resistance tracks Drexlerviridae subfamily affiliation [39]. To assess whether Avs systems could, in principle, target the resistant phages, we compared the sequences of the two canonical Avs recognition targets — the terminase large subunit (TerL) and portal protein — across all tested Drexlerviridae phages (Table S2). Sequence identity across the family is heterogeneous: Bas03 (Braunvirinae) shares only 56.3% amino acid identity (99% query coverage) for TerL and 57.4% identity (96% query coverage) for portal protein with Ec_Ou_ZC1, whereas within Tunavirinae and Tempevirinae, identity ranges from 65.0 to 99.2% for TerL and 65.0–98.1% for portal. This pattern of divergence raises a plausible alternative explanation for the differential Avs susceptibility we observe: the Avs systems tested here may simply fail to recognise the more divergent TerL/portal proteins of Tunavirinae and Tempevirinae members, whereas the comparatively closer relationship among the Avs-targets of Bas03 and its known Salmonella-phage triggers may allow productive recognition. Under this interpretation, differential susceptibility need not invoke an anti-defence factor at all. We cannot, in the absence of experimental proof, distinguish this passive evasion model from active suppression of Avs signalling by a phage-encoded factor such as the oMTase (Table S3). Furthermore, a survey of oMTase distribution across all tested Drexlerviridae phages revealed that homologs are present in all five Avs-resistant members (Ec_Ou_ZC1, T1, Bas13, Bas12, and Bas05; 64.2–99.2% amino acid identity to the Ec_Ou_ZC1 oMTase) but absent from Bas03, the sole Avs-sensitive member (Table S3). The absence in Bas03 was confirmed by tBLASTn against the complete genome at the nucleotide level (no hit at E < 10⁻⁵). The co-occurrence of the oMTase with the resistance phenotype is consistent with a protective role, although this correlation does not establish causation. Notably, the oMTase could be co-inherited with a divergent TerL/portal lineage as part of a shared genomic block without itself contributing causally to Avs evasion, so the correlation alone does not adjudicate between active and passive resistance mechanisms. If active suppression is involved, the oMTase (Fig. 8) is the most tractable candidate, given its homology to bacterial restriction-modification MTases [32], though no direct functional evidence currently supports this role. Gene deletion or heterologous expression experiments targeting the oMTase represent the logical next step to resolve this ambiguity.
