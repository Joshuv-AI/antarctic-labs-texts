Human uncultured adipose-derived stromal vascular fraction shows therapeutic potential against osteoarthritis in immunodeficient rats via direct effects of transplanted M2 macrophages
Authors: Yuma Onoi, Tomoyuki Matsumoto, Kensuke Anjiki, Shinya Hayashi, Naoki Nakano, Yuichi Kuroda, Masanori Tsubosaka, Tomoyuki Kamenaga, Kemmei Ikuta, Shotaro Tachibana, Yoshihito Suda, Kensuke Wada, Takuma Maeda, Akira Saitoh, Takafumi Hiranaka, Satoshi Sobajima, Hideki Iwaguro, Takehiko Matsushita, Ryosuke Kuroda
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe uncultured adipose-derived stromal vascular fraction (SVF), consisting of adipose-derived stromal cells (ADSCs), M2 macrophages (M2Φ) and others, has shown therapeutic potential against osteoarthritis (OA), however, the mechanisms underlying its therapeutic effects remain unclear. Therefore, this study investigated the effects of the SVF on OA in a human-immunodeficient rat xenotransplantation model.MethodsOA model was induced in the knees of female immunodeficient rats by destabilization of the medial meniscus. Immediately after the surgery, human SVF (1 × 105), ADSCs (1 × 104), or phosphate buffered saline as a control group were transplanted into the knees. At 4 and 8 weeks postoperatively, OA progression and synovitis were analyzed by macroscopic and histological analyses, and the expression of collagen II, SOX9, MMP-13, ADAMTS-5, F4/80, CD86 (M1), CD163 (M2), and human nuclear antigen (hNA) were evaluated immunohistochemically. In vitro, flow cytometry was performed to collect CD163-positive cells as M2Φ from the SVF. Chondrocyte pellets (1 × 105) were co-cultured with SVF (1 × 105), M2Φ (1 × 104), and ADSCs (1 × 104) or alone as a control group, and the pellet size was compared. TGF-β, IL-10 and MMP-13 concentrations in the medium were evaluated using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay.ResultsIn comparison with the control and ADSC groups, the SVF group showed significantly slower OA progression and less synovitis with higher expression of collagen II and SOX9, lower expression of MMP-13 and ADAMTS-5, and lower F4/80 and M1/M2 ratio in the synovium. Only the SVF group showed partial expression of hNA-, CD163-, and F4/80-positive cells in the rat synovium. In vitro, the SVF, M2Φ, ADSC and control groups, in that order, showed larger pellet sizes, higher TGF-β and IL-10, and lower MMP-13 concentrations.ConclusionsThe M2Φ in the transplanted SVF directly affected recipient tissue, enhancing the secretion of growth factors and chondrocyte-protecting cytokines, and partially improving chondrocytes and joint homeostasis. These findings indicate that the SVF is as an effective option for regenerative therapy for OA, with mechanisms different from those of ADSCs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-024-03946-3.Keywords: Stromal vascular fraction, M2 macrophages, Adipose-derived stromal cells, Osteoarthritis, Synovitis, Xenotransplantation, Rat model

Background

Regenerative therapy for osteoarthritis (OA) using stem cells harvested from adipose tissue has recently attracted considerable attention [1]. Only 0.0001–0.01% of bone marrow-derived mesenchymal cells (BMCs) show stem cell features, whereas approximately 5% of adipose tissue-derived nucleated cells show the characteristic phenotype of stem cells [2]. Adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs) are obtained by aspiration and enzymatic processing of adipose tissue, allowing easy, less invasive, and larger-scale collection of stem cells in comparison with BMCs. Moreover, ADSCs are comparable to BMCs in terms of their therapeutic effects on diabetic ulcers and nerve injury in animal models as well as cell differentiation in adipogenic, osteogenic, chondrogenic, and myogenic lineages [2–5]. Therefore, regenerative therapy using ADSCs is gaining popularity as an adjunctive therapy with promising results. However, the process of stem cell isolation and culture for preparation of ADSCs is an expensive, time-consuming step, preventing the widespread use of ADSCs in clinical practice.

The stromal vascular fraction (SVF), which is obtained by mechanical or enzymatic digestion of adipose tissue, is composed of an uncultured heterogeneous mixture of cells, including ADSCs, macrophages, pericytes, fibroblasts, blood cells, and vessel-forming cells such as endothelial cells, smooth muscle cells, and their progenitors [6]. The SVF can be prepared within 3–4 h of adipose tissue collection followed by cell isolation and scaffold cell seeding, making it superior to other stem cell sources in that SVF can be used for regenerative therapy more easily and quickly [7]. The SVF has been reported to be as effective as ADSCs in treating chronic lower-limb ulcers, ischemic cardiomyopathy, fat grafting, limb ischemia, and nerve regeneration [8–11]. Moreover, intra-articular injections of SVF can improve pain and joint function in OA, showing therapeutic effects comparable to those of ADSCs [12–15]. However, the proportion of ADSCs in the SVF is only approximately 1–16% [16], and the mechanisms underlying the effects of the SVF on chondrocytes remain unclear. The therapeutic effects of the SVF can be attributed to heterogeneous cells, and the mechanisms underlying the paracrine effects of SVF require elucidation.

Macrophages can be polarized into pro-inflammatory (M1) and anti-inflammatory (M2) phenotypes. M2 macrophages (M2Φ) induce the production of chondrogenic cytokines, such as transforming growth factor (TGF)-β and interleukin (IL)-10, which play important roles in wound healing [17, 18]. TGF-β plays a critical role in both cartilage homeostasis and repair by inhibiting the production of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), while IL-10 shows chondroprotective effects by downregulating the expression of inflammatory cytokines and inducing chondrocyte apoptosis [19–21]. Moreover, articular chondrocytes secrete TGF-β and IL-10, and the expression of their respective receptor chains has been demonstrated [22, 23]. Macrophages constitute approximately 20% of the SVF [24], and the majority of macrophages in the SVF have the M2 phenotype [25, 26]. Therefore, M2Φ are expected to play an important role in the therapeutic effects of the SVF on chondrocytes. In contrast, ADSCs polarize macrophages to the M2 phenotype via the prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) pathway in an inflammatory environment [27, 28]. Therefore, the paracrine effect of the SVF on OA may be influenced by ADSCs that polarize recipient macrophages to the M2 phenotype. Accordingly, the extent to which the donor M2Φ in the SVF influence its paracrine effects is unclear. Data obtained from previous studies are insufficient to assert that M2Φ in the SVF can function directly in the treatment of OA [29, 30].

Therefore, this study aimed to clarify the mechanism of action of the SVF by evaluating the role of M2Φ in the treatment of OA using a human–immunodeficient rat xenotransplantation animal model. We investigated the therapeutic effect of the heterogeneous cells in the SVF by comparing the findings for SVF and ADSC treatment groups with the same amount of stem cells. We hypothesized that treatment with the SVF would suppress OA by promoting cartilage anabolism and inhibiting catabolism through the synergistic effects of the heterogeneous M2Φ and ADSCs in the SVF.

Methods

In vivo experiments

Preparation of the SVF and ADSCs

The SVF was extracted from 24 patients (5 males and 19 females; mean age, 61.5 ± 5.3 years; body mass index, 23.8 ± 3.3 kg/m2) who underwent intra-articular SVF injection into the knee joint for the treatment of OA. The Celution® 800/CRS system (Cytori Therapeutics Inc.) was used to extract the SVF from the abdominal or breech subcutaneous fat of the patients. All patients underwent liposuction under general anesthesia to obtain 120–350 mL of adipose tissue. The extracted tissue was then processed using the Celution® 800/CRS System as described previously [31]. Briefly, the subcutaneous adipose tissue was removed, minced, and incubated with Celase® GMP, a mixture of highly purified collagenase, to digest the tissue. After digestion, the SVF was concentrated through centrifugation at 1000 rpm for 5 min and washed to remove the Celase® reagent. Thereafter, the SVF was prepared by extracting the target cells from the system and suspending it in 100 μL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Adherent stem cells in the SVF were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and passaged when confluence was reached. As in previous studies [2, 32], cells from the fourth passage were used as ADSCs in this study. Preliminary experiments showed that these cells exhibit mesenchymal stem cells-specific CD markers, as confirmed by flow cytometry: positive for CD73, CD90, and CD105, and negative for CD34 and CD45 (Supplemental figure).

The SVF containing 1 × 105 cells and 1 × 104 ADSCs was prepared under sterile conditions in 1 mL of rabbit serum albumin (RSA, 4%; Sigma–Aldrich). The rats were divided into three groups and received injections of the SVF containing 1 × 105 cells (SVF group), 1 × 104 ADSCs (ADSC group), or 100 μL of PBS (control group). Based on previous reports [32], in this study, the SVF/ADSC ratio was set at 10%, and the SVF and ADSC groups included equivalent amounts of ADSCs to determine the therapeutic potential of the heterogeneous cells in SVF.

Establishment of the rat OA model

Female athymic nude rats (F344/N Jcl rnu/rnu; age, 9 weeks; body weight, 140–160 g) were obtained from CLEA Japan (Tokyo, Japan). All animal procedures were approved by the local Ethics Committee (Permission No.: P210509) and conducted in accordance with the Japanese Physiological Society Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Anesthesia was induced by intraperitoneal administration of a mixture of ketamine hydrochloride (60 mg/kg) and xylazine hydrochloride (10 mg/kg). Destabilization of the medial meniscus (DMM) was induced in the right knee joint by transecting the anterior attachment of the medial meniscotibial ligament, as described previously [33, 34]. After closing the joint, the rats were immediately administered local transplantations of human SVF, ADSCs, or PBS. All rats were weight-bearing after recovering from anesthesia. The rats were euthanized with an overdose of ketamine and xylazine at 4 and 8 weeks after DMM to collect knee joint specimens for macroscopic and histological analyses. Two rats were excluded due to infection within a week of surgery. No rats died during the process. Six or five rats were analyzed in each of the three groups (SVF, ADSC, and control groups) at each time point; thus, the total number of rats analyzed was 96 (Fig. 1).

A schematic diagram illustrating the study design, the time course of the study, and the number of rats at each stage. SVF, stromal vascular fraction; ADSC, adipose-derived stromal cell; PBS, phosphate-buffered saline; DMM, destabilization of the medial meniscus

Macroscopic analysis

Macroscopic images were acquired using a Nikon D3000 camera (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). Rat cartilage lesions were classified according to the Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI) OA Cartilage Histopathological Assessment System and scored as reported in previous studies [35] (n = 6).

Histological analysis

For histological analysis, knee joint specimens from the three groups of rats were placed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin (n = 5). The specimens were decalcified using 4% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid at 20–25 °C for 3 weeks, embedded with paraffin, and cut into thin sagittal sections of approximately 6 μm. The general morphology and proteoglycan content of the cartilage tissue were assessed using safranin-O/fast green (Sigma–Aldrich) staining. Hematoxylin and eosin staining was performed to evaluate synovitis. Histological sections were visualized under a BZ-X700 microscope (Keyence, Osaka, Japan), and quantified according to the OA Cartilage Histopathologic Assessment System of the OARSI [36]. Synovitis in the synovium was scored using the modified OARSI score [37].

Immunohistochemical analyses

Immunohistochemical examinations of the three groups were performed after deparaffinization of the sections (n = 5). To block endogenous peroxidase activity, sections were incubated with 3% hydrogen peroxide (Wako Pure Chemicals, Osaka, Japan) for 30 min. Knee cartilage was evaluated using rabbit polyclonal antibodies against collagen type II (Collagen II) (1:50; Abcam, Tokyo, Japan), SRY box (SOX)9 (1:200; Abcam), MMP-13 (1:200; Abcam) and A disintegrin and metalloproteinase with thrombospondin motifs 5 (ADAMTS-5) (1:200; GeneTex, Irvine, CA, USA). The knee synovium was evaluated using F4/80 (a macrophage marker) (1:100; Biorbyt, Cambridge, UK), CD86 (an M1 macrophage marker) (1:200; Bioss, Woburn, MA, USA), and CD163 (an M2 macrophage marker) (1:200; Bioss). All antibody dilutions were prepared in PBS; after overnight incubation with the primary antibodies at 4 °C, the sections were stained with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G antibody (N-Histofine Simple Stain MAXPO; Nichirei Bioscience, Tokyo, Japan) and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. Signals were detected using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (Histofine Simple Stain DAB solution; Nichirei Bioscience), which generated a brown reaction product.

Semi-quantitative immunohistochemical analysis was performed using microscopic assessment (×100 magnification) of the sagittal section with the most severe OA lesion of the medial tibial plateau, focusing on six areas: the anterior, central, and posterior regions in the superficial and deep zones of the cartilage tissue. For complete evaluation of protein expression, a semi-quantitative method of assigning immunohistochemical values as a percentage of positive areas (collagen II) or positive cells (SOX9, MMP-13, and ADAMTS-5) was employed, with a maximum value of 100%. The expression of F4/80 as an immune and inflammatory cell marker was evaluated by using a previously reported scoring system for immunohistochemistry [38]. The M1/M2 (CD86/163) ratio was used to evaluate macrophage counts in the synovium [39]. The average of the gray values normalized to the number of nuclei was estimated to determine the expression levels, as described in a previous study [40].

Immunofluorescence staining

To evaluate the anti-inflammatory effects of the transplanted cells, rat synovial tissue was immunofluorescently stained for F4/80, CD163, and human nuclear antigen (hNA) (n = 5). The sections were incubated with 3% hydrogen peroxide (Wako Pure Chemicals) for 30 min to block endogenous peroxidase activity. The samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C with a conjugated primary antibody against F4/80 (1:100; orb466169-FITC, Biorbyt), conjugated primary antibody against CD163 (1:200; BS-2527R-A594, Bioss), and primary anti-mouse antibody against hNA (1:500; ab191181, Abcam). Subsequently, the sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated alpaca anti-mouse (1:500; SA5-10332, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for 2 h at room temperature to detect hNA. Finally, the nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 5 min, and fluorescence signals were captured using fluorescence microscopy. The percentages of CD163-positive and hNA-positive cells among the F4/80-positive cells were evaluated.

In Vitro experiments

Flow cytometry and sorting of M2Φ

The phenotype of the M2Φ in the SVF was determined by detecting cell markers using flow cytometry. The fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) mouse anti-human CD163 antibody (333,617, BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) was used for cellular staining. Fresh SVF cells were washed twice with PBS and incubated with antibodies in the dark for 20 min at room temperature. Fluorescence intensity was measured using a BD FACS Aria III cell sorter (BD Biosciences), after excluding doublets and dead cells (n = 5). Cells retaining FITC fluorescence were defined as M2Φ and used in the experiment immediately after sorting.

Pellet co-culture

Primary chondrocytes were isolated and cultured from the knee articular cartilage tissue harvested from a patient with OA during total knee arthroplasty. Surgeries were performed under sterile conditions and general anesthesia. Thinly sliced cartilage tissue was stripped, diced, and digested in DMEM containing 0.2% collagenase D for 3 h. A nylon filter (70 mm) was used to remove the undigested cartilage. Subsequently, the pellets were collected at the bottom of a 15-mL tube by centrifuging aliquots of chondrocytes at 1200 rpm for 5 min at 25 °C. The chondrocyte pellets (1 × 105 cells) were converted into a chondrogenic medium by adding DMEM with 15% FBS (Sigma–Aldrich), 100 U/mL penicillin (Sigma–Aldrich), 100 mg/mL streptomycin solution (Sigma–Aldrich), 0.1 mM dexamethasone (Sigma–Aldrich), 50 mM ascorbate-2-phosphate (Sigma–Aldrich), 0.4 mM proline (Sigma–Aldrich), 1% insulin–transferrin–sodium selenite (Sigma–Aldrich), and 500 ng/mL bone morphogenetic protein-6 (Sigma–Aldrich). The pellets and the SVF (1 × 105 cells), ADSCs (1 × 104 cells), or M2Φ (1 × 104 cells) were separated using sterilized culture plate inserts (Millicell; Millipore). Control groups were established with no cells in the membrane plates and only OA chondrocytes in 15-mL tubes. To confirm the therapeutic effect of the heterogeneous cells in the SVF, in vitro experiments were performed, and the findings were compared with those obtained using the same number of ADSCs or M2Φ as in the SVF. Pellets were incubated at 37 °C under 5% CO2, the medium was changed every 2–3 d (n = 5). After 3 weeks, the pellet sizes were evaluated and stained using safranin-O.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

After 48 h of pellet co-culture, the supernatants from the first exchange were collected. The concentrations of the growth factors and cytokines released in the control, ADSC, M2Φ, and SVF groups were determined using the manufacturer’s protocol for sandwich enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (TGF-β: DB100C, IL-10: D1000B, MMP-13: DY511, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA). IL-10 was five-fold concentrated using Amicon Ultra® centrifugal filters (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) before measurement (n = 5). The results were normalized to the amount of DNA in the control group.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software (version 16.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). The chi-square test or one-way analysis of variance followed by a posteriori Tukey’s test was performed to compare the results between the groups. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

In vivo experiments

SVF inhibited OA progression more effectively than ADSCs

In the macroscopic analysis, the control group demonstrated progression of OA and severe progression of cartilage erosion at 4 and 8 weeks after DMM, respectively. The ADSC group showed moderate progression of macroscopic cartilage fibrillation at 4 and 8 weeks after DMM, with lower OARSI scores than in the control group. The SVF group showed milder progression of cartilage degeneration than the ADSC group, with lower OARSI scores at each endpoint (Fig. 2A, B).

In the histological evaluation, the articular cartilage at 4 and 8 weeks after DMM showed similar trends as in the macroscopic evaluation. Histological findings in the control group showed progressive OA changes, including loss of proteoglycans and chondrocyte density in the superficial and intermediate layers of the cartilage. The ADSC group also showed progression of OA changes, including loss of proteoglycans and chondrocyte density in the midzone; however, OA progression was milder in the ADSC group than in the control group, with significantly lower OARSI scores at each endpoint in comparison with the control group. Importantly, the SVF group showed prevention of OA development with retention of the cartilage matrix, and significantly lower OARSI scores in comparison with those in the ADSC group (Fig. 2C–E). The control group exhibited synovitis with lymphocytic infiltration and multilayering of the synovium. In contrast, the ADSC group showed synovitis with mild multilayering, and the modified OARSI scores for synovitis at 4 and 8 weeks after DMM were significantly lower in the ADSC group than in the control group. Importantly, the SVF group showed only mild synovitis, and the modified OARSI scores at each endpoint were significantly lower in the SVF group than in the ADSC group (Fig. 2F, G).

SVF promoted cartilage anabolism and inhibited cartilage catabolism

Immunohistochemical staining of collagen II and SOX9 at 4 and 8 weeks after DMM showed that the SVF and ADSC treatments promoted the expression of large amounts of collagen II and SOX9 in cartilage tissues in comparison with the control group. Additionally, remarkably larger positive areas and cells were detected in the SVF group than in the ADSC group, respectively (Fig. 3A–D). In contrast, MMP-13 and ADAMTS-5 expression at 4 and 8 weeks after DMM were markedly lower in the SVF and ADSC groups than in the control group. In addition, a considerably lower percentage of positive cells were detected in the SVF group than in the ADSC group (Fig. 3E–H).

SVF promotes anti-inflammatory effects in the synovium

Immunohistochemical findings in synovial tissues showed that the F4/80 expression levels at 4 and 8 weeks after DMM were considerably higher in the control group than in the SVF and ADSC groups. The F4/80 scores in the control group were significantly higher than those in the SVF and ADSC groups, indicating increased macrophage infiltration. Moreover, the F4/80 score in the SVF group was significantly lower than that in the ADSC group (Fig. 4A, B). The CD86/CD163 expression ratio (M1/M2 ratio) was considerably lower in the SVF and ADSC groups than in the control group at 4 and 8 weeks after DMM, indicating increased infiltration of M2Φ in the SVF and ADSC groups. Furthermore, the M1/M2 ratio was significantly lower in the SVF group than in the ADSC group (Fig. 4C–E).

M2Φ in the SVF acted directly to suppress synovitis

Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that macrophages in the synovial tissue of rats at 4 weeks after DMM were labeled with F4/80. The proportion of CD163-positive cells among F4/80-positive cells was the highest in the SVF group and was significantly higher than that in the ADSC and control groups; the proportion of these cells in the ADSC group was significantly higher than that in the control group. Moreover, most of the F4/80- and CD163-positive cells in the SVF group expressed hNA, whereas only a few cells in the ADSC group and no cells in the control group were labeled with hNA (Fig. 5A, B).

In vitro experiments

Flow cytometry and sorting of M2Φ

Flow cytometry was performed to detect the percentage of CD163-positive cells in the SVF. The percentage of CD163-positive cells, which were defined as M2Φ, was approximately 13.4% ± 7.3% (Fig. 6A).

Pellet co-culture

Pellet co-culture was performed to evaluate the proliferation of chondrocytes cultured in the presence of SVF, M2Φ, and ADSC paracrine factors (Fig. 6B). All pellets stained positive for safranin-O and showed a vitreous extracellular matrix containing round chondrocytes. The pellet size of chondrocytes grown by co-culture was the largest in the SVF group, followed by the M2Φ, ADSC, and control groups, with significant differences among the groups (Fig. 6C, D).

ELISA in the co-culture medium

TGF-β and IL-10 levels in the co-culture medium were the highest in the SVF group, followed by the M2Φ group, the ADSC group, and the control group. In contrast, MMP-13 expression was the lowest in the SVF group, followed by the M2Φ, ADSC, and control groups (Fig. 6E).

Discussion

The most important finding of this study was that the M2Φ in the transplanted SVF could act directly on intra-articular tissues to secrete TGF-β and IL-10, which may have contributed to the therapeutic effects of the SVF on OA. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate a possible direct effect of the M2Φ within the SVF on OA treatment. These findings suggest that SVF transplantation may be an effective option for regenerative therapy in cases of OA.

In this study, to evaluate the differences in therapeutic mechanisms between the SVF and ADSCs, macroscopic, histological, and immunohistochemical analyses were performed in the in vivo experiments via the xenotransplantation of human SVF and ADSCs into immunocompromised rat OA models. Macroscopic and histological analyses revealed that the SVF group showed superior suppression of OA progression and synovitis than the ADSC group. Immunohistochemical staining showed that collagen II and SOX9 expression was higher in the SVF group than in the ADSC group; in contrast, MMP-13 and ADAMTS-5 expression was lower in the SVF group than in the ADSC group. SOX9, a chondrogenic transcription factor, plays an important role in chondrogenesis, particularly by activating collagen II coexpression [41, 42]. MMP-13 and ADAMTS-5 affect the degradation of collagen II and aggrecan, respectively, causing degenerative changes in cartilage, and have been used as indicators of therapeutic efficacy in OA models [43, 44]. In summary, the in vivo experiments revealed that increased anabolic effects and decreased catabolic effects on OA cartilage were more effective in the SVF group than in the ADSC group.

Next, macrophages in the synovium were investigated in the in vivo experiments to understand the mechanisms underlying the OA-suppressing effects of the SVF and ADSCs. Previous studies have indicated that synovial macrophages play an important role in the pathogenesis of OA [45–47]. M1 macrophages in the synovium exacerbate experimental OA, while M2Φ reduce its progression, and in particular, an increased M1/M2 ratio is associated with increased expression of inflammatory cytokines, resulting in OA [48, 49]. Previous studies have reported that ADSCs exert anti-inflammatory effects by polarizing macrophages to the M2 phenotype via the PGE2 pathway [27, 28]. Another report showed that intra-articular injection of the SVF increased the number of M2Φ in the synovium of a mouse OA model [29]. In this study, the SVF group showed markedly superior suppression of synovitis and a decreased M1/M2 macrophage ratio than the ADSC group. Thus, the SVF group, which contained the same number of stem cells as the ADSC group, showed stronger polarization toward M2Φ than the ADSC group, suggesting that the immunomodulatory effect of the SVF was partly influenced by heterogeneous cells other than ADSCs. In addition, immunofluorescence staining revealed hNA-positive nuclei in F4/80- and CD163-positive cells in rat synovial tissue in the SVF group. However, in the ADSC group, very few hNA-positive nuclei were found in the F4/80- and CD163-positive cells in the rat synovium. Thus, human-derived M2Φ in the transplanted SVF can directly affect the synovial tissues of recipient rats, and the paracrine effect of the SVF on OA may be due to the synergistic effects of ADSCs and M2Φ in the SVF acting directly and contributing to the maintenance of the synovial membrane and articular cartilage homeostasis.

Furthermore, the in vitro experiments were performed to confirm these in vivo findings. To assess the effect of M2Φ in the SVF, flow cytometry was performed to detect the percentage of CD163-positive cells in the SVF and sort M2Φ. Previous studies have reported that macrophages constitute approximately 20% of the SVF [24]. Of the macrophages in the SVF, approximately 70% were positive for M2 anti-inflammatory markers, supporting our results [25, 26]. M2Φ secrete TGF-β and IL-10, induce collagen II expression in chondrocytes, and decrease MMP-13 expression [50]. Previous studies have reported that co-culture of the SVF or ADSCs with isolated pellets produces notably larger pellets in the SVF group than in the ADSC group [30, 32]. Our results support these findings: moreover, the M2Φ group showed a larger pellet size than the ADSC group. In ELISAs, the levels of both TGF-β and IL-10 were highest in the SVF group, followed by the M2Φ, ADSC, and control groups. In contrast, the levels of MMP-13 were the lowest in the SVF group, followed by the M2Φ, ADSC, and control groups. Therefore, M2Φ were more effective than ADSCs in promoting the secretion of anti-inflammatory cytokines such as TGF-β and IL-10, inducing collagen II expression, enhancing anabolic effects on chondrocytes, and decreasing the expression of catabolic markers such as MMP-13. In summary, the paracrine effect of the SVF was influenced more by M2Φ than ADSCs, and the synergistic effect of M2Φ and ADSCs markedly increased anabolism and decreased the catabolic effect on chondrocytes.

Our study had several limitations. First, we administered fixed doses of the SVF and ADSCs via a single injection. However, the optimal dose in a rat OA model has not yet been determined. Second, we used a rat model of this experiment. Because rats are quadrupeds, the established OA model may not necessarily translate to a human model owing to differences in postural and gait dynamics between rats and humans. Third, the normal lifespan of a rat is approximately 2 years; however, the rats in this study were only 9 weeks old. Therefore, they may not correspond to changes in OA in middle-aged humans. Fourth, the progression of OA in the rat DMM model is relatively rapid compared to the generally slower and more variable progression of OA in humans. This difference in progression rate may affect the application of our findings to the OA treatment in human. Fifth, we used immunocompromised rats as a model because they are commonly used in similar studies on xenotransplantation to prevent the rejection of transplanted human tissues and cells [51–53]. However, several studies have reported that specific immune response signals inhibit OA progression [54, 55]. Thus, immunodeficiency may interfere with the therapeutic effects of the SVF. In addition, histological and immunohistochemical evaluations were performed using semi-quantitative methods. Therefore, more objective and accurate quantitative methods should be considered in future studies. Moreover, the M2Φ group was not included in the in vivo experiments of this study. The advantage of M2Φ over ADSCs in the in vitro experiments may be limited to the short term. To demonstrate the long-term advantage of M2Φ, longer follow-up in in vivo experiments is desirable. Finally, this study focused on M2Φ and ADSCs to investigate the therapeutic effect of SVF, however, other cells in the SVF, such as pericytes, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells were not examined. Previous reports indicate that these cells could affect the treatment of OA [56–58]. Therefore, the potential of these cells should be considered when using SVF in the future.

Conclusions

In comparison to the ADSC treatment with the same number of stromal cells, the SVF treatment was superior in promoting anabolic effects and inhibiting catabolic effects in chondrocytes. M2Φ in the SVF contributed to the therapeutic effects of chondrocytes and joint homeostasis by promoting the secretion of growth factors and chondrocyte-protecting cytokines. The paracrine effect of the SVF on OA could be influenced not only ADSCs but also M2Φ, suggesting that transplanted M2Φ may partially but directly affect the recipient tissue. Thus, the therapeutic mechanisms of the SVF may differ from those of ADSCs, offering several advantages over ADSCs and making the SVF an effective therapeutic option for regenerative therapy.
