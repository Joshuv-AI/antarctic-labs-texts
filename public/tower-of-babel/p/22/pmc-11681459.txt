Cardiovascular Outcomes Associated With Isolated Systolic or Diastolic Hypertension According to the 2017 AHA/ACC Guideline in Adult Cancer Survivors
Authors: Hyeyun Jung, Edvige Di Giovanna, Young‐Hak Kim, Tae Joon Jun, Kyuwoong Kim
Published: 2024

In 2022 the GLOBOCAN (Global Cancer Observatory) reported 20 million new cancer cases worldwide, marking a nearly 40% increase from 2012. 1 Advancements in cancer treatment and screening technologies are expected to contribute to the growing number of cancer survivors. 2 These cancer survivors are at higher risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD), partially attributable to treatment‐related cardiotoxicity, cancer‐associated inflammation, and shared risk factors such as hypertension. 3 Notably, isolated systolic or diastolic hypertension are increasingly recognized as independent risk factors for CVD, highlighting the importance of considering both systolic and diastolic blood pressure (BP) for specified CVD risk assessment. 4 Therefore, effective CVD prevention strategies tailored to individualize and optimize risk stratification are necessary for adult cancer survivors. However, CVD risk associated with the 2017 American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology (AHA/ACC) BP guideline for isolated systolic or diastolic hypertension remains understudied in a broad spectrum of adult cancer survivors.

No additional data are available to the public as the National Health Insurance Service strictly limits access to the database to authorized personnel only. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at the National Cancer Center (Republic of Korea) (Institutional Review Board no.: NCC‐2020‐0341) and followed the reporting guidelines outlined in STROBE. The requirement for obtaining informed consent from the participants was waived because of the anonymization of the data. After excluding individuals with prior CVD or previous use of antihypertensive drugs, we identified adults (aged ≥20 years) with a cancer diagnosis history (International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision [ICD‐10]: C00–C97) who survived >1 year without further cancer treatment records from the National Health Insurance Service database in the Republic of Korea. Using BP data of adult cancer survivors following a minimum of 2 minutes of rest in a seated position measured from January 1, 2009 to December 31, 2010 as a part of national health screening program, we categorized the patients into BP groups as having normal BP (untreated systolic BP [SBP] <120/diastolic BP [DBP] <80 mm Hg); elevated BP (SBP 120–129/DBP <80 mm Hg); stage 1 IDH (SBP <130/DBP 80–89 mm Hg); stage 1 isolated systolic hypertension (SBP 130–139/DBP <80 mm Hg); stage 1 systolic and diastolic hypertension (SDH) (SBP 130–139/DBP 80–89 mm Hg); and stage 2 IDH (SBP <140 mm Hg and DBP ≥90 mm Hg); stage 2 isolated systolic hypertension (SBP ≥140 mm Hg and DBP <90 mm Hg); and stage 2 SDH (SBP ≥140 mm Hg and DBP ≥90 mm Hg). We estimated the risk of composite cardiovascular events (ICD‐10: I20–I25, I50, and I60–I69), coronary heart disease (I20–I25), ischemic stroke (I63), and heart failure (I50) according to isolated or diastolic hypertension with Cox proportional hazards model adjusted for age, sex, insurance premium (proxy for income status), residential area, family history of heart disease, cigarette smoking, alcohol consumption, physical activity, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, alcoholic liver disease, liver cirrhosis, chronic kidney disease, peripheral artery disease, time since initial cancer diagnosis, type of primary cancer site treating death as competing risk (Fine and Gray model). To account for potential nonlinear relationships, we employed restricted cubic splines for age and explored interactions between key covariates that may modify the effects of blood pressure on cardiovascular outcomes. We used SAS 9.4 for all statistical analyses and considered a 2‐tailed P‐value <0.05 as statistically significant.

Among 173 951 adult cancer survivors (mean age, 58.4 [SD: 1.95], 44.9% men), thyroid cancer demonstrated the highest prevalence at 31.4%, followed by gastric (16.4%), colorectal (13.4%), breast (10.2%), lung (4.5%), prostate (4.5%), liver (4.1%), and cervical (2.1%). During 10 years of follow‐up, 9448 composite CVD events, 3857 coronary heart diseases, 3036 ischemic strokes, and 1066 heart failures occurred. Compared with those with normal BP, hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs for composite CVD events in those with elevated BP, stage 1 isolated diastolic hypertension (IDH), stage 1 isolated systolic hypertension, stage 1 SDH, stage 2 IDH, stage 2 isolated systolic hypertension, stage 2 SDH were 1.18 (1.10–1.27), 1.20 (1.12–1.29), 1.39 (1.29–1.51), 1.33 (1.24–1.42), 1.43 (1.27–1.61), 1.50 (1.39–1.62), and 1.71 (1.58–1.84), respectively (P trend<0.01) (Table). Multivariable‐adjusted analyses consistently yielded point estimates further from the null compared with age‐ and sex‐adjusted models, reflecting the impact of additional covariates on the observed associations. Survivors of liver cancer with stage 2 SDH exhibited the highest excess risk for composite CVD outcomes (HR=2.12 [95% CI, 1.47–3.05]), followed by survivors of colorectal cancer (HR=1.78 [95% CI, 1.30–2.44]), breast cancer (HR=1.66 [95% CI, 1.19–2.31]), gastric cancer (HR=1.55 [95% CI, 1.31–1.84]), prostate cancer (HR=1.54 [95% CI, 1.20–1.97]), lung cancer (HR=1.48 [95% CI, 1.07–2.03]), and thyroid cancer (HR=1.45 [95% CI, 1.21–1.74]). We found statistically nonsignificant evidence for this association among survivors of cervical cancer (HR=1.34 [95% CI, 0.69–2.63]). In addition, the use of spline representations for age and interactions among covariates revealed more complex relationships between blood pressure categories and cardiovascular outcomes, providing insights into how these factors may influence CVD risk in adult cancer survivors.

In a nationwide study of adult cancer survivors, elevated BP, stage 1 isolated systolic hypertension, IDH, and SDH and stage 2 IDH, stage 2 isolated systolic hypertension, and stage 2 SDH according to the 2017 AHA/ACC BP guideline subcategorization were associated with an increased risk of composite CVD, coronary heart disease, ischemic stroke, and heart failure. The previous observational study investigating association between 2017 AHA/ACC BP guideline categorization with CVD has been limited by only selecting patients with a history of breast, colorectal, or stomach cancer without the further subcategorization of isolated systolic or diastolic hypertension rather than including long‐term cancer survivors. 5 Our findings extend previous data by providing more precise risk stratification with isolated systolic or diastolic BP subcategorization according to the 2017 AHA/ACC guideline from nearly 170 000 survivors of all cancer sites. Overall, our findings provide additional clinical insights for a more comprehensive understanding of the association between isolated BP and CVD risk in adult cancer survivors, highlighting heterogeneity in excess CVD risk across certain cancer subtypes as well as the need for more personalized BP treatment strategies for CVD risk reduction in this vulnerable population. This study is limited by the absence of data on cancer staging for the primary cancer sites such as Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) summary staging. Also, information on specific cardiotoxic treatments, such as anthracyclines, trastuzumab as well as hypertension‐inducing antineoplastic agents like vascular endothelial growth factor inhibitors (eg, bevacizumab), could not be identified and incorporated into the analyses. Thus, future studies are warranted to investigate the potential interactions of initial cancer stage diagnosis, of specific cardiotoxic treatments, and the observed variations in excess risk across different CVD events by the BP subcategories (eg, stage 1 isolated systolic hypertension versus stage 2 IDH) among adult cancer survivors. Despite this limitation, our findings highlight the need for optimal BP subcategorization and management strategies for CVD prevention in adult cancer survivors.
