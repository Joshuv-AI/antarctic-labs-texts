Predictive role of surgical Apgar score for postoperative complications and survival in patients receiving esophagectomy: a meta-analysis
Authors: Tie Yang, Wei Zheng, Tianli Zuo, Chao Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe association between surgical Apgar score (SAS) and clinical outcomes among patients undergoing esophagectomy remains uncertain now. This study aimed to further identify the predictive role of SAS for postoperative complications and survival in patients receiving esophagectomy.MethodPubMed, EMbase and Web of Science databases were searched up to March 27, 2025. Primary endpoints included the postoperative overall complication (OC) and overall survival (OS) and secondary endpoints consists of specific complications and disease-free survival (DFS). Odds ratios (ORs) and hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were combined to assess the predictive value of SAS for complications and survival, respectively.ResultsTen studies with 2453 patients were included. Pooled results demonstrated that SAS was significantly related to the risk of overall complications (OR = 0.43, 95% CI: 0.33–0.57, P<0.001), but not associated with OS (HR = 0.84, P = 0.246). Furthermore, a low SAS predicted increased risk of anastomotic leakage (OR = 0.44, P = 0.004), chylothorax (OR = 0.33, P = 0.020), infection (OR = 0.43, P<0.001), 30-day mortality (OR = 0.40, P = 0.011), pneumonia (OR = 0.61, P = 0.037), reintubation (OR = 0.29, P = 0.003), respiratory complication (OR = 0.49, P = 0.014), gastrointestinal complication (OR = 0.57, P = 0.035), length of stay (OR = 0.60, P = 0.038), pulmonary complication (OR = 0.46, P = 0.003). However, SAS was not associated with the risk of other complications such as the cardiac complication (P = 0.215) and sepsis (P = 0.087) and DFS (P = 0.341) in esophageal cancer patients.ConclusionSAS may serve as novel prognostic indicator for postoperative complications in patients receiving esophagectomy and low SAS predicts increased risk for complications. However, more studies are needed to further verify above findings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13019-026-03906-3.Keywords: Surgical Apgar score, complication, survival, esophagectomy, meta-analysis

Introduction

Esophageal cancer is one of the most prevalent malignant tumors of the digestive tract worldwide [1]. Surgical resection remains the primary treatment modality for locally advanced esophageal cancer, particularly for esophageal squamous cell carcinoma and certain early-stage adenocarcinomas [2]. Esophagectomy is a crucial therapeutic approach for patients without distant metastasis, good overall health, and sufficient surgical tolerance. Common surgical techniques include transthoracic esophagectomy (TTE) and transhiatal esophagectomy (THE), with TTE being preferred for locally advanced cases due to extensive lymph node dissection [2]. However, esophagectomy is a high-risk procedure with a substantial incidence of postoperative complications, including pulmonary infections, anastomotic leakage, cardiovascular complications, and postoperative hemorrhage [3–5]. These complications not only affect short-term recovery but may also have adverse effects on long-term survival. Therefore, accurately predicting postoperative complication risks is crucial for improving perioperative management and optimizing patient outcomes.

Currently, the prediction of postoperative complications and survival following esophagectomy primarily relies on preoperative indicators such as patient age, body mass index (BMI), comorbidities, nutritional status (e.g., prealbumin levels), and tumor staging [5]. However, solely depending on preoperative indicators may not fully reflect the patient’s physiological state and perioperative risks. In recent years, the surgical Apgar score (SAS), a simple and objective intraoperative assessment tool, has been widely used to predict short-term outcomes in surgical patients [6]. SAS is calculated based on three intraoperative variables: lowest heart rate, lowest mean arterial pressure, and estimated blood loss. Initially developed for evaluating postoperative prognosis in abdominal surgery, SAS has demonstrated that lower scores correlate with a higher risk of postoperative complications and mortality. Studies have shown that SAS has predictive value in various surgical procedures, including cardiac, gastrointestinal, and hepatobiliary surgeries [7, 8]. However, its application in esophagectomy remains limited, and its predictive value for short-term complications and long-term survival following esophagectomy remains unclear.

Therefore, this study aims to investigate the clinical value of SAS in predicting short-term complications and long-term survival after esophagectomy, providing a new reference for perioperative management.

Materials and methods

This study was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines (2020) [9].

Literature search

PubMed, EMbase and Web of Science databases were searched up to March 27, 2025 with following terms: surgical Apgar score, SAS, esophagectomy and oesophagectomy. Detailed search strategy in the PubMed was shown in Supplementary file 1. Meanwhile, MeSH terms and free texts were also applied. And references in included studies were screened.

Inclusion criteria

Studies met following criteria were included: (a) patients received the esophagectomy due to the esophageal cancer or other diseases; (b) the SAS were assessed based on the estimated blood loss, lowest heart rate and lowest mean arterial blood pressure; (c) the association of SAS with postoperative complications or survival was explored with reported odds ratios (ORs) or hazard ratios (HRs) or sufficient data for calculation; (d) full texts were available and articles were published in English.

Exclusion criteria

Studies met following criteria were excluded: (a) studies with insufficient or duplicated data; (b) letters, editorials, reviews, case reports, animal trials or meeting abstracts.

Data collection

Following data were collected from included studies: the first author, year, country, sample size, age, type of disease, history of neoadjuvant therapy, type of SAS, cutoff value of SAS, endpoint, OR, HR and 95% confidence interval (CI).

In our meta-analysis, primary outcomes were defined as the postoperative overall complication and overall survival. Secondary outcomes included the anastomotic leakage, chylothorax, infection, 30-day mortality, pneumonia, reintubation, respiratory complication, cardiac complication, recurrent nerve paralysis, sepsis, gastrointestinal complication, length of stay, pulmonary complication, in hospital mortality, prolonged ventilation, septic shock and disease-free survival.

Quality evaluation

The methodological quality was assessed by the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) score and studies with NOS score ≥ 6 were defined as high-quality studies [10].

The literature search, selection, data collection and quality assessment were all performed by two authors independently.

Statistical analysis

All analysis was conducted by STATA version 17.0 software. Heterogeneity between studies was calculated by Q test and I2 statistics. When significant heterogeneity was detected representing as I2 > 50% and/or P < 0.1, the random effects model was applied; or the fixed effects model was applied [11]. However, for endpoints with less than five included studies, the random effects model was directly applied due to the limitation of Q test and I2 statistics in such situation [12]. ORs and HRs with 95% CIs were combined to evaluate the relationship of SAS with risk of complication and survival, respectively. Subgroup analysis for overall complication by the type of SAS was performed. If HRs with 95% CIs were not reported, then they would be calculated from the Kaplan-Meier survivals based on the method described by Tierney et al. [13]. Sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the stability of pooled results. Begg’s funnel plot and Egger’s test were performed to detect publication bias, and obvious publication bias was defined as P < 0.05 [14, 15]. When significant publication bias was detected, then the trim-and-fill method was applied [16].

Notably, due to the similarity between SAS, eSAS and modified eSAS, we regarded them as the same during the statistical analysis.

Meta-analysis results for primary outcomes

Ten included studies explored the predictive role of SAS for postoperative overall complication among patients receiving esophagectomy. Pooled results demonstrated that a SAS was significantly related to increased risk of postoperative complications (OR = 0.43, 95% CI: 0.33–0.57, P<0.001; I2 = 68.0%, P = 0.001) (Fig. 2A) and subgroup analysis by the type of SAS manifested similar results (eSAS: OR = 0.52, 95% CI: 0.36–0.74, P<0.001; SAS: OR = 0.43, 95% CI: 0.23–0.80, P = 0.008; modified eSAS: OR = 0.47, 95% CI: 0.25–0.87, P = 0.016) (Fig. 2B). However, SAS was not associated with the overall survival (HR = 0.84, 95% CI: 0.62–1.13, P = 0.246; I2 = 0.0%, P = 0.533) (Fig. 3). (Table 2)

Meta-analysis results for secondary outcomes

For individual complications, pooled results manifested that SAS was related to postoperative anastomotic leakage (OR = 0.44, P = 0.004) (Supplementary Fig. 1A), chylothorax (OR = 0.33, P = 0.020) (Supplementary Fig. 1B), infection (OR = 0.43, P<0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 1C), 30-day mortality (OR = 0.40, P = 0.011) (Supplementary Fig. 1D), pneumonia (OR = 0.61, P = 0.037) (Supplementary Fig. 1E), reintubation (OR = 0.29, P = 0.003) (Supplementary Fig. 1F), respiratory complication (OR = 0.49, P = 0.014) (Supplementary Fig. 1G), gastrointestinal complication (OR = 0.57, P = 0.035), length of stay (OR = 0.60, P = 0.038) and pulmonary complication (OR = 0.46, P = 0.003). However, SAS was not associated with the risk of cardiac complication (P = 0.215) (Supplementary Fig. 2A), recurrent nerve paralysis (P = 0.907) (Supplementary Fig. 2B), sepsis (P = 0.087) (Supplementary Fig. 2C), in hospital mortality (P = 0.472), prolonged ventilation (P = 0.089) or septic shock (P = 0.060). Meanwhile, it was revealed that SAS was not associated with disease-free survival (P = 0.341) in esophageal cancer patients (Table 2).

Sensitivity analysis

According to the sensitivity analysis, the pooled results for overall complications were stable and reliable and none of included studies caused an impact on the overall results (Fig. 4).

Discussion

This study explored the predictive role of SAS for postoperative complications and long-term survival in patients undergoing esophagectomy. According to our findings, a low SAS was significantly related to increased risk of postoperative overall complications including some specific complications such as the anastomotic leakage and chylothorax, but SAS could not serve as a predictor for long-term survival. Therefore, SAS may contribute to the risk evaluation and management for postoperative complications after esophagectomy based on current evidence.

The SAS, based on the lowest heart rate, lowest mean arterial pressure and estimated blood loss, is closely associated with postoperative complications after esophagectomy, including anastomotic leakage, chylothorax, infection, pneumonia, reintubation, and respiratory complications. Intraoperative bradycardia may indicate hemodynamic instability and impaired sympathetic response, leading to poor adaptation to postoperative stress and an increased risk of infections and pulmonary complications [27]. A low mean arterial pressure during surgery can result in inadequate tissue perfusion and hypoxia, which are major risk factors for anastomotic leakage and pulmonary complications. Additionally, excessive blood loss can cause hypovolemic shock, impair coagulation function, and increase the risk of infections and pneumonia due to transfusion-related immunomodulation (TRIM) [7, 28]. Collectively, these factors reflect intraoperative physiological stability, oxygen supply, and coagulation status, which are critical determinants of postoperative recovery. As a simple and objective tool, SAS provides a quantifiable measure of intraoperative stress and may guide perioperative strategies to improve patient outcomes after esophagectomy [6].

Beyond predicting postoperative complications, SAS may have broader clinical applications in esophagectomy patients. It can be used for postoperative monitoring and risk stratification, guiding the intensity of post-surgical care and early interventions for high-risk patients. Moreover, SAS can aid in enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) protocols by identifying candidates for accelerated rehabilitation while ensuring cautious management for those at higher risk. Emerging evidence also suggests that SAS may be associated with long-term survival, as perioperative instability often translates to poorer oncological outcomes. Furthermore, although the SAS is calculated intraoperatively, it can contribute to perioperative risk stratification and postoperative management rather than directly guiding preoperative decision-making for the same procedure. A low SAS reflects intraoperative physiological instability and surgical stress, which may help clinicians promptly identify high-risk patients who could benefit from intensified postoperative monitoring and early supportive interventions. In addition, when viewed at the population level, accumulated SAS data may inform future preoperative optimization strategies by highlighting patient subgroups that are more likely to experience adverse intraoperative conditions. Such insights may support the design of targeted prehabilitation programs, including nutritional support and cardiopulmonary rehabilitation, prior to esophagectomy. From a research perspective, SAS serves as a standardized intraoperative metric that facilitates the evaluation of surgical techniques, anesthesia strategies, and perioperative management protocols, thereby contributing to improved evidence-based practice [29].

Although our meta-analysis manifested that SAS predicted the risk of most specific postoperative complications, the number of included studies for some complications such as the gastrointestinal complication, pulmonary complication and septic shock were too small. This issue also exists in the analysis of overall survival, involving only three studies. Therefore, more studies are still needed to further identify the association between SAS and overall survival and above individual complications among patients receiving esophagectomy. Besides, due to the similarity of SAS, eSAS and modified eSAS, we regarded them as the same during the statistical analysis, although we performed the subgroup analysis by the type of SAS. However, minor differences might exist between them (Supplementary Tables 1–3). Further research may compare their prognostic value in esophagectomy patients. Furthermore, although postoperative complications have been reported to adversely affect long-term survival in several studies, the lack of a significant association between SAS and overall survival in our meta-analysis may be explained by several factors. First, SAS primarily reflects intraoperative physiological stress and immediate surgical risk, which are more directly linked to short-term postoperative complications than to long-term oncologic outcomes that are largely driven by tumor biology, pathological stage, and adjuvant therapies. Second, many postoperative complications are transient and potentially reversible, and their negative impact on survival may be mitigated by timely management, enhanced recovery protocols, and subsequent adjuvant treatment. Third, heterogeneity in follow-up duration, postoperative management, and adjuvant therapy across included studies may dilute the long-term prognostic effect of SAS on overall survival. Therefore, while SAS is a valuable predictor of postoperative morbidity, its ability to predict long-term survival after esophagectomy may be limited.

There are some limitations which should be noted. First, the overall sample size and number of included studies were relatively small. Secondly, due to the lack of original data, it is unable to perform subgroup analysis based on some confounding factors such as the neoadjuvant therapy and age. Thirdly, most studies are retrospective cohort studies, which might cause some bias. Fourthly, the significant heterogeneity for the overall complication was observed. However, the type of SAS did not seem to be the source of heterogeneity and we were unable to perform more subgroup analysis by other parameters.

Conclusion

SAS may serve as novel predictor for postoperative complications in patients receiving esophagectomy and low SAS predicts increased risk for complications. However, more high-quality studies are needed to further verify our findings.
