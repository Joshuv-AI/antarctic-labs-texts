Commentary: Improving the vaccination experience for children and adults
Authors: Cynthia M Rand, Peter G Szilagyi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTApproximately two out of three children and one out of four adults experience significant anxiety related to vaccination, which can result in vaccine avoidance. Promoting patient autonomy by empowering individuals to choose coping strategies that work best for them can enhance the overall vaccination experience. The CARD system is an innovative, patient-centered approach that improves the vaccination experience for both patients and healthcare providers.KEYWORDS: Vaccination, fear, needle, health services research

In this supplement to Human Vaccines and Immunotherapeutics, the authors present a collection of studies aimed at improving the vaccination experience for children and adults. Fear and anxiety related to vaccination are common, with nearly two-thirds of children and one-quarter of parents reporting needle-related fear. Concerns about pain, and fear and anxiety from needles leads to lack of vaccination in as high as 7–8% of the population,1 but relatively few studies have systematically addressed this issue. Until now.

The CARD (Comfort Ask Relax Distract) system, developed by Taddio et al., has demonstrated effectiveness in reducing negative symptoms such as pain and fear, while enhancing the overall vaccination experience. CARD has been successfully implemented in diverse settings, including school-based programs,2 community pharmacies,3 and long-term care facilities,4 with both child and adult participants. The system emphasizes patient autonomy, allowing individuals to plan their vaccination experience and select coping strategies that work best for them. The system trains vaccination providers how to modify their practices and environments to mitigate vaccine pain and fear of vaccination. This supplement builds on prior research by evaluating the implementation of CARD in schools, pharmacies, and university-based pop-up clinics, without the support typically available in research settings. Across these studies, CARD improved vaccination experiences and in one case, appeared to increase vaccination rates. Collectively, these findings highlight the potential for broader adoption of CARD to enhance vaccination outcomes.

A key feature of CARD is its dual focus on patient and provider participation. Patients use the CARD acronym to select strategies that improve their vaccination experience, while providers support them using the 4E model: Engagement, Education, Environment, and Evaluation. These interventions promote patient-centered care, support individual choice, and align with trauma-informed practices.5,6 To facilitate dissemination, the authors developed an online training module that teaches the CARD framework for patients and the 4E model for providers. The first article in this supplement details the iterative development of this training module, informed by user feedback, and guided by the Universal Design for Learning framework.7 Evaluation of the training demonstrated excellent knowledge acquisition and high provider confidence in implementing CARD. Importantly, the authors note that role-playing or coaching may be needed alongside the online course to achieve competency. This is a common issue in training programs – truly internalizing new procedures or practices requires consistent practice.8 Future steps for training on CARD include assessing the course’s impact on vaccination rates and implementation outcomes across diverse settings and learners.

The second manuscript examines the implementation of CARD in a 7th grade school setting to increase vaccination rates for human papillomavirus (HPV), hepatitis B, and meningococcal conjugate-ACWY vaccines.9 The authors used a clever variation of a pre-post study design: schools with low baseline vaccination rates were allocated to receive the CARD intervention, while schools with higher rates received usual care. They then conducted a difference-in-difference analysis to assess change in vaccination rates between the post period (2023–2024) versus the pre period (2022–2023). This is the first study to assess CARD’s impact on population-level vaccination uptake. One-third of students reported that knowing about CARD helped them agree to vaccination, while two-thirds said CARD improved their experience. Importantly, nurses noted that using CARD techniques did not increase the time required to deliver vaccinations. The largest improvement in vaccination rates was observed for the HPV vaccine, potentially due to its lower baseline rates or greater associated pain. However, this pragmatic study was not a randomized controlled trial (RCT), and this study design introduces potential confounders, as schools with lower baseline rates may have more room for improvement but also may be comprised of students who face greater barriers to care. A true RCT randomizing schools or primary care practices could provide more definitive evidence of CARD’s impact on vaccination rates.

The next two papers focus on the effectiveness10 and implementation11 outcomes of a hybrid effectiveness RCT evaluating CARD in community pharmacies. Participants at intervention pharmacies reported a better vaccination experience compared to their previous vaccinations, with those experiencing greater baseline needle fear benefiting the most. In the implementation study, vaccinators expressed high satisfaction with patient interactions and noted that the intervention did not require additional time. Given the growing number of vaccinations delivered in pharmacies compared to physician offices – particularly for adults – since the COVID-19 pandemic,12 these findings on CARD implementation in pharmacies are particularly noteworthy. Positive experiences may encourage individuals to return for future vaccinations.

The final article explores the use of CARD in university-based pop-up clinics for COVID-19 and influenza vaccinations.13 Pop-up clinics often present unique challenges, such as limited privacy and the presence of onlookers. Intervention clinics addressed these issues by increasing privacy and reducing staff to minimize stress. Pharmacy students reported that CARD facilitated their workflow and that they demonstrated high fidelity in its implementation. The study found that CARD improved the vaccination experience and reduced fear and pain among university students. This study was also not an RCT but included a before and after and cohort study. Integrating CARD training into pharmacist education may encourage future use of these techniques.

Although it is reassuring that CARD did not seem to require more time for the vaccination process, sustaining a CARD intervention does involves some minimal ongoing costs, such as restocking distraction tools and topical anesthetics, as well as onboarding and training new staff. These costs could be offset by increased vaccination rates, which would enhance public health outcomes.

The CARD system is an innovative, patient-centered approach that has demonstrated effectiveness and adaptability across diverse settings and provider types. The studies presented in this supplement offer a robust foundation for broader implementation of CARD, highlighting its potential to reduce vaccination-related fear and anxiety while likely improving vaccination uptake to prevent infectious diseases.

Together, these studies present an exciting opportunity to improve the vaccination experience for both patients and healthcare providers. The studies highlight our ability to improve patient-reported outcomes and experiences, thereby enhancing the impact and value of preventive care. The studies also highlight the important role of relational care as the foundation for healthcare. As Dr. Francis Weld Peabody once said: “the secret of the care of the patient is in caring for the patient.”14

Biographies
