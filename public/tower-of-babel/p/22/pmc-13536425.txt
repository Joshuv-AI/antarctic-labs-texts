Postoperative conductive hearing loss after full-arch-implantation surgery under general anesthesia in an oral submucous fibrosis patient: A case report
Authors: Hao-Chen Wang, Fu-Chuan Tsai, Chia-Che Wu, Chih-Yuan Fang
Published: 2026

Oral submucous fibrosis (OSF) is a chronic progressive disease that is highly prevalent in Southeast Asia, where it is strongly linked to betel-nut chewing.1 The underlying pathology involves excessive accumulation of collagen within the submucosal layer of the oral cavity, leading to the development of severe trismus, poor oral hygiene, excessive dental decay and, ultimately, the loss of teeth. When a patient requires full-mouth rehabilitation, the restricted mouth opening makes traditional dental procedures difficult to perform while the patient is awake. In many cases, surgical interventions, such as the placement of dental implants, must be conducted under general anesthesia. Clinicians typically focus on restoring oral function and monitoring for malignant transformation. Associated auditory complications, particularly those involving Eustachian tube dysfunction, are typically overlooked. This article presented a case of Eustachiantube-dysfunction-related hearing impairment in a patient with OSF following full-arch implant surgery.

A 58-year-old man with a 30-year history of betel-nut use and clinically advanced OSF was referred to our outpatient clinic. The patient had a maximum mouth opening of 23 mm and nonrestorable dentition and requested full-mouth dental implant rehabilitation (Fig. 1A). The patient initially received mandibular-implant-supported rehabilitation with immediate function under local anesthesia. One year later, the patient received maxillary arch implant surgery under general anesthesia (Fig. 1B and C). During postoperative follow-up, the patient reported a sudden reduction in hearing in the right ear lasting for 2 weeks. The patient was referred to the otolaryngology department for physical and comprehensive audiological evaluations. Clinical examination and audiogram findings suggested Eustachian tube dysfunction with middle-ear effusion (Fig. 1D). The patient subsequently underwent microscope-assisted myringotomy under local anesthesia. The patient reported satisfaction with his hearing recovery 1 week after the procedure, and audiometry at the 6-month follow-up confirmed the resolution of the Eustachian tube dysfunction and improvement in hearing thresholds (Fig. 1E).

Transient middle-ear pressure alterations and effusion following general anesthesia are well-documented.2–4 Investigations have shown that both nitrous oxide and Sevoflurane can induce considerably changes in middle-ear pressure and contribute to Eustachian tube dysfunction during or shortly after surgery. Positive pressure mask ventilation during anesthetic induction can also alter middle-ear pressure; therefore, preoperative evaluation of the Eustachian tube function might be essential. A review of 18 studies confirmed a significant correlation between OSF severity and hearing loss, with auditory symptoms reported in up to 76.7% of patients.5 Permanent conductive hearing loss is prevalent in patients with advanced stages of OSF. The underlying mechanism involves fibrotic alterations in the soft tissues surrounding the Eustachian tube that disrupt the tube’s functional integrity. Interference with middle-ear ventilation and drainage due to disrupted functional integrity can result in hearing impairment.

As noted, the patient had clinically advanced OSF (Khanna and Andrade classification stage III), which predisposed him to Eustachian tube dysfunction. Consequently, his Eustachian tube could not effectively compensate for the middle-ear pressure changes during general anesthesia, leading to a transient hearing impairment. Surgeons and anesthesiologists may consider preoperative screening and early postoperative otologic evaluation for patients with OSF scheduled to receive surgery under general anesthesia.
