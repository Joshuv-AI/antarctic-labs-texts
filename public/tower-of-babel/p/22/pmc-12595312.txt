Introduction

Paragangliomas are rare neuroendocrine tumors arising from paraganglionic tissue of the autonomic nervous system. These tumors are most commonly located in the carotid body, jugulotympanic region, or adrenal medulla (pheochromocytomas) [1]. However, intracranial paragangliomas, particularly those in the sellar and parasellar regions, are exceedingly rare, with fewer than 50 cases reported worldwide [2,3]. The sellar region, typically occupied by the pituitary gland, is an unusual site for paragangliomas due to the absence of native paraganglionic tissue, suggesting ectopic or developmental origins [4].

The diagnostic challenge of sellar-parasellar paragangliomas stems from their radiological similarity to more common entities such as pituitary macroadenomas and meningiomas [5]. On magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), paragangliomas often present with a characteristic “salt and pepper” appearance due to their vascularity, but this feature is not pathognomonic [6]. Clinically, these tumors may cause visual disturbances, headaches, and pituitary dysfunction, mimicking pituitary adenomas. However, unlike pheochromocytomas, sellar-parasellar paragangliomas rarely exhibit catecholamine hypersecretion [7].

Surgical resection is the cornerstone of treatment, but the hypervascular nature of these tumors and their proximity to critical neurovascular structures, such as the internal carotid artery (ICA) and optic chiasm, often preclude complete resection [8]. Adjuvant therapies, including stereotactic radiotherapy, are frequently employed for residual or recurrent disease [9]. This report presents a rare case of sellar-parasellar paraganglioma in a 25-year-old female, misdiagnosed initially as a pituitary macroadenoma, and discusses the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges encountered and the work has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria [10].

Case presentation

A 25-year-old female presented to our neurosurgery clinic in March 2025 with a four-month history of progressive headaches and visual disturbances. She reported blurred vision and difficulty reading, which had worsened over time. Additionally, she had been in lactational amenorrhea for 18 months following childbirth. There was no history of galactorrhea, acromegalic features, or symptoms suggestive of catecholamine excess, such as palpitations, hypertension, or diaphoresis. Her medical history was unremarkable, with no known genetic syndromes or family history of neuroendocrine tumors.

On examination, the patient exhibited bitemporal hemianopia confirmed by perimetry, indicating optic chiasm compression. She also reported diplopia when looking to the left, suggestive of cranial nerve involvement. Neurological examination revealed no motor or sensory deficits, and vital signs were within normal limits. There were no clinical signs of pituitary dysfunction, such as hypothyroidism or adrenal insufficiency, nor features of catecholamine excess.

A comprehensive pituitary hormonal profile was performed to evaluate for pituitary dysfunction. Results showed normal levels of thyroid-stimulating hormone, cortisol, growth hormone, and follicle-stimulating hormone. Mild hyperprolactinemia was noted, likely due to stalk compression rather than a prolactin-secreting adenoma.

Imaging

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain provided a comprehensive evaluation of the lesion, revealing a lobulated, extra-axial sellar-suprasellar mass with precise dimensions of approximately 6.0 cm (transverse diameter) x 3.5 cm (anteroposterior) x 3.5 cm (craniocaudal). The mass exhibited a significant extension into the left temporal fossa and demonstrated a pronounced bulge toward the right, exerting considerable stretching and displacement of the surrounding parasellar structures (Fig. 1, Fig. 2, Fig. 3, Fig. 4, Fig. 5). On T1-weighted imaging, the lesion appeared hypointense, reflecting its tissue composition, while T2-weighted imaging revealed a hyperintense signal punctuated by the classic “salt and pepper” appearance, a hallmark of high vascularity due to interspersed flow voids (Fig. 4, Fig. 5) [6]. Post-contrast T1-weighted images demonstrated strong, homogeneous enhancement throughout the mass, underscoring its hypervascular nature and aiding in the delineation of its boundaries (Fig. 1, Fig. 2, Fig. 3). The tumor exerted significant mass effect, compressing and displacing the pituitary stalk, optic chiasm, and optic tracts superiorly, as clearly depicted across multiple imaging planes (Fig. 1, Fig. 3, Fig. 4, Fig. 5). Additionally, the mass showed invasive characteristics, with clear evidence of left cavernous sinus invasion and encasement of the left internal carotid artery (ICA), a critical finding observed in both coronal and axial views (Fig. 2, Fig. 3, Fig. 4, Fig. 5). No posterior extension or associated hydrocephalus was noted, suggesting the lesion's confinement to the anterior and middle cranial fossa regions. The clinical and radiological findings prompted a thorough consideration of several differential diagnoses. Pituitary macroadenoma was initially favored, given its status as the most prevalent sellar mass and its frequent clinical presentation with visual disturbances and hormonal dysfunction, such as hyperprolactinemia potentially resulting from pituitary stalk compression (Fig. 1, Fig. 3). Meningioma was also considered a plausible diagnosis due to the lesion's extra-axial location and its homogeneous contrast enhancement, a typical feature of sellar-parasellar meningiomas (Fig. 1, Fig. 2, Fig. 3). Paraganglioma, though rare in this anatomical region, was included in the differential due to the distinctive hypervascularity and the characteristic “salt and pepper” appearance on T2-weighted MRI, which is suggestive of prominent vascular flow voids and supported by the histopathological correlation (Fig. 4, Fig. 5, Fig. 6, Fig. 7). Management

The patient's primary complaint was visual disturbance, necessitating surgical intervention to debulk the tumor and decompress the optic chiasm. In April 2025, an endonasal transsphenoidal approach was attempted. The nasal phase proceeded uneventfully, but upon dural incision, significant, uncontrollable bleeding was encountered, likely due to the tumor's high vascularity. After approximately one hour of attempting hemostasis, the procedure was aborted, and a biopsy sample was obtained. Histopathology from this sample was inconclusive, as it did not provide representative tissue.

Given the failure of the transsphenoidal approach, a transcranial approach was planned. The transsphenoidal approach was initially chosen due to its minimally invasive nature and common use for sellar masses, aiming to decompress the optic chiasm effectively. However, the unexpected hypervascularity led to uncontrollable bleeding, necessitating its abandonment. The transcranial approach was subsequently selected for its ability to provide better access to the tumor's extensive lateral and cavernous sinus involvement, allowing for safer resection despite the tumor's adherence to the ICA.

In May 2025, a frontospheno-temporal skin incision was made, followed by a left supraorbital (LSO) craniotomy. Intraoperatively, the tumor was found to be highly vascular and firm, with adherence to the cavernous ICA. Near-total tumor resection was achieved, though complete excision was not possible due to the tumor's vascularity and ICA encasement (Fig. 6). Four units of blood transfusion were required intraoperatively to manage blood loss.

Post-operatively, the patient's visual symptoms improved partially, with resolution of diplopia and partial recovery of visual fields. However, she developed grade IV ptosis of the left eye, likely due to cranial nerve III involvement during surgery. Follow-up imaging confirmed near-total resection with a small residual tumor in the cavernous sinus (Fig. 6). The patient was followed up 6 weeks after the second surgery, during which she had mild ptosis in her left eye. Stereotactic radiotherapy was planned for the residual disease to prevent recurrence. The patient was discharged with instructions for regular follow-up and monitoring for pituitary dysfunction.

Histopathological findings

Histopathological examination of the resected tumor revealed a neoplasm composed of round to oval cells arranged in nests (zellballen pattern), traversed by a delicate vascular network. Immunohistochemistry was positive for chromogranin A, a marker of neuroendocrine tumors, confirming the diagnosis of paraganglioma. There was minimal cellular atypia or mitotic activity, suggesting a benign tumor, though long-term follow-up is required to monitor for recurrence (Fig. 7).

Discussion

Sellar and parasellar paragangliomas are extraordinarily rare, with fewer than 50 cases reported in the literature [2,3,5]. This tumor's rarity underscores the importance of documenting such cases to enhance understanding of its epidemiology and management. Paragangliomas arise from chromaffin cells of the autonomic nervous system, typically found in the adrenal medulla or extra-adrenal paraganglia [1]. The presence of paragangliomas in the sellar region, where paraganglionic tissue is not normally found, suggests ectopic or developmental rests as a possible origin [4]. Genetic associations, such as mutations in succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) genes, have been reported in some paragangliomas, but no genetic testing was performed in this case due to resource limitations.

The clinical presentation of sellar-parasellar paragangliomas typically includes visual disturbances, headaches, and pituitary dysfunction, as seen in our patient [7]. Bitemporal hemianopia and diplopia result from compression of the optic chiasm and cranial nerves, respectively. Unlike pheochromocytomas, intracranial paragangliomas rarely secrete catecholamines, which explains the absence of hypertensive crises or other catecholamine-related symptoms in this case [7]. The mild hyperprolactinemia observed is likely secondary to pituitary stalk compression, a common feature in large sellar masses [11].

The radiological similarity of paragangliomas to pituitary macroadenomas and meningiomas poses a significant diagnostic challenge [5]. The “salt and pepper” appearance on T2-weighted MRI, caused by flow voids from prominent vascularity, is a hallmark of paragangliomas but is not specific [6]. Other imaging modalities, such as 68Ga-DOTATATE PET/CT, may aid in diagnosis by detecting somatostatin receptor expression, but these were unavailable in our setting [12]. Histopathology and immunohistochemistry, particularly staining for chromogranin A and synaptophysin, are critical for definitive diagnosis [13].

Surgical resection is the primary treatment for sellar-parasellar paragangliomas, but their hypervascularity and adherence to critical structures often limit complete resection [8]. The initial transsphenoidal approach in this case was abandoned due to excessive bleeding, a common challenge reported in the literature [3,9]. With the significant bleeding encountered during the first surgery, a vascular study was not performed, as it was thought to originate from the cavernous sinus, and preoperative embolization was not pursued due to logistical constraints. The subsequent transcranial approach allowed for near-total resection, though residual tumor remained due to ICA encasement. Preoperative embolization may reduce intraoperative bleeding, but this was not feasible in our case due to logistical constraints [14]. Adjuvant stereotactic radiotherapy is recommended for residual or recurrent tumors, as it offers excellent local control with minimal morbidity [9]. Long-term follow-up is essential, as paragangliomas may recur years after initial treatment [15].

Our case aligns with previous reports of sellar-parasellar paragangliomas, which describe similar clinical presentations, radiological findings, and surgical challenges [2,3,5]. The failure of the transsphenoidal approach due to hypervascularity is consistent with findings by Vasoya et al. [3], who noted significant intraoperative bleeding in two cases. The “salt and pepper” MRI appearance, seen in our patient, is a recurring feature in the literature [6,7]. Unlike some reported cases associated with genetic syndromes like von Hippel-Lindau [2], our patient had no such history, highlighting the sporadic nature of most sellar paragangliomas. To provide context, a comprehensive literature review conducted by Vasoya et al. has excellently summarized the previous 31 reported cases of sellar-parasellar paragangliomas, detailing their clinical presentations, surgical planning, and follow-up periods [3].

Conclusion

Sellar and parasellar paragangliomas are rare neuroendocrine tumors that mimic pituitary macroadenomas, posing significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. Their hypervascular nature and proximity to critical neurovascular structures complicate surgical resection, often necessitating a multidisciplinary approach involving microsurgery and adjuvant radiotherapy. High clinical suspicion, advanced imaging, and histopathological confirmation are crucial for accurate diagnosis. This case underscores the importance of documenting such rare tumors to improve understanding and management strategies. Long-term follow-up is essential to monitor for recurrence and optimize patient outcomes.
