Rare Early Coexistence of Classic Urothelial and Small Cell Carcinoma of the Urinary Bladder
Authors: Mohannad N. AbuHaweeleh, Amro Elfaieg, Abdelkareem Alhyari, Nada Abuhayeh, Ibrahim A. Khalil, Nabil Mahmood, Mohammed Akhtar, Khalid Al-Rumaihi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionSmall cell carcinoma of the bladder (SCCB) is a rare aggressive cancer, representing less than 1% of bladder cancers. Similar to small cell lung cancer (SCLC), it progresses rapidly, metastasizes early, and has a poor prognosis, complicating treatment. Unlike more common bladder cancers, SCCB often resists localized treatments and responds better to chemotherapy. However, its management generally relies on protocols for SCLC due to limited specific research.Case PresentationA 47‐year‐old male from Bangladesh presented with intermittent painless hematuria, weak urinary stream, and incomplete voiding for 2 months. His medical history includes diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, hypothyroidism, adrenal adenoma, and coronary artery disease. His physical examination was unremarkable. His blood work up showed hypochromic microcytic anemia and elevated creatinine. Urine cytology revealed atypical degenerated urothelial cells, polymorphonuclear leukocytes, and red blood cells. He underwent flexible cystoscopy, which showed a nodular tumor with a wide base at the left lateral bladder wall measuring less than 3 cm. He had a CT scan, which revealed irregular thickening in the left posterolateral bladder wall, suggesting muscle‐invasive carcinoma extending into the perivesical fat. He had an MRI scan which confirmed a 4 x 4.5 − cm mass with extravesical involvement. Histopathology examination from his transurethral resection of bladder tumor specimens showed mixed carcinoma, comprising both classic invasive urothelial carcinoma (UC) and SCCB. The small cell component exhibited scant cytoplasm, high nuclear‐to‐cytoplasmic ratio, hyperchromatic nuclei, and numerous mitotic figures, with synaptophysin and weak chromogranin positivity; UC cells were GATA‐3 positive. Surgical resection removed most of the tumor. The patient tolerated the procedure well, with no complications. After four chemotherapy cycles, he had a follow‐up PET CT, which showed significant tumor regression.ConclusionThis case highlights the unusual early coexistence of SCCB with classic UC in the bladder, emphasizing the importance of comprehensive histopathological and immunohistochemical evaluation for accurate diagnosis and treatment planning. Further research on SCCB could improve diagnosis, treatment strategies, and identification of molecular targets, potentially enhancing survival rates and patient quality of life.

1. Introduction

Small cell carcinoma of the bladder (SCCB) is a rare neuroendocrine carcinoma that constitutes less than 1% of all bladder cancers [1]. SCCB affects predominantly older males three times more than females, with a peak incidence in those aged 60–70 years, and a strong correlation with tobacco smoking [2]. Approximately 70% of SCCB cases are diagnosed at an advanced stage, with metastasis to regional lymph nodes or distant organs, including the lungs, liver, bones, and brain [3]. In rare instances, SCCB may present with paraneoplastic syndromes, such as ectopic adrenocorticotropic hormone production, leading to Cushing syndrome, or hypercalcemia due to parathyroid hormone–related protein secretion [1]. The combination of a high rate of local and distant metastasis and the likelihood of advanced‐stage diagnosis contributes to SCCB′s overall poor prognosis, with 5‐year survival rates as low as 10%–40% [1].

The clinical presentation of SCCB tends to be nonspecific, often mimicking more common bladder cancers like urothelial carcinoma (UC). Gross hematuria, reported in up to 88% of cases [1], is the most common symptom [4], often accompanied by irritative urinary symptoms such as dysuria, frequency, and urgency [1, 5]. These symptoms are also characteristic of UC, complicating the initial diagnosis based solely on clinical presentation [6]. Hereby, we present a case of a middle‐aged man with multiple comorbidities who presented with urinary symptoms and was later diagnosed with concurrent SCCB and UC at an early stage.

2. Case Presentation

A 47‐year‐old male from Bangladesh was referred from medicine to urology due to complaints of intermittent painless hematuria for the past 2 months. The hematuria was associated with a weak urinary stream and a sensation of incomplete voiding. He denied dysuria, fever, flank pain, or recent trauma.

The patient is a known case of diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, hypothyroidism, adrenal adenoma, and coronary artery disease. His medications include atorvastatin, bisoprolol, clopidogrel, furosemide, glyceryl trinitrate, insulin glargine, isosorbide dinitrate, levothyroxine, linagliptin, lisinopril, and pantoprazole. On physical examination, his vital signs were unremarkable, and the abdominal examination did not reveal any abnormalities.

An ultrasound of the urinary tract, done a year ago, identified a 3.1‐mm cyst in the midpole of the left kidney, with the urinary bladder appearing grossly normal. His laboratory investigations showed a decreased red blood cell count of 4.2x106/μ L (normal: 4.5–5.5x106/μ L), hemoglobin of 11.4 g/dL (normal: 13–17 g/dL), and a hematocrit of 35.2% (normal: 40%–50%). Other abnormalities included a low mean corpuscular hemoglobin of 23.6 pg (normal: 27–32 pg), a low mean platelet volume of 9.4 fL (normal: 9.7–11.1 fL), and an elevated absolute neutrophil count of 19.7x103/μ L (normal: 2–7x103/μ L). His blood chemistry indicated elevated creatinine levels at 153 μmol/L (normal: 62–106 μmol/L).

His urine cytology was taken and revealed atypical but degenerated urothelial cells, polymorphonuclear leukocytes, and red blood cells. A flexible cystoscopy was then done and showed a nodular tumor with a wide base on the left lateral bladder wall, measuring less than 3 cm. The left ureteric orifice was not visualized, whereas the right orifice appeared normal. Abdominopelvic MRI without contrast was conducted and identified a urinary bladder mass measuring 4x4.5 cm, arising from the left lateral wall posteriorly and inferiorly. The tumor exhibited intermediate T2 signal intensity, restricted diffusion, and extravesical extension into perivesical fat (Figure 1). Suspicious lymphadenopathy was noted, including a 4‐mm left obturator lymph node and several indeterminate small left iliac nodes. Additionally, linear T2 hypointensities were seen in the peripheral zone of the prostate.

Axial T2W MRI of the pelvis demonstrates a mass arising from the left lateral wall of the urinary bladder (asterisk) with extravesical extension to the left pelvic side wall (arrow).

He had CT of the thorax which showed bilateral upper lobe lung lesions with spiculated margins, the largest measuring 30 mm, along with nodular, reticular, and ground glass opacities suggestive of granulomatous infection or, less likely, primary pulmonary neoplasm (Figure 2). A confirmed diagnosis of nodular pulmonary tuberculosis was made in the context of the patient′s recently diagnosed bladder carcinoma. CT abdomen was ordered and demonstrated focal gallbladder wall thickening (15 mm), raising suspicion of neoplasm, and irregular thickening of the left posterolateral bladder wall consistent with muscle‐invasive bladder carcinoma, with no invasion of pelvic organs. Additionally, the lower left ureter appeared mildly dilated, and benign bilateral renal cysts were noted.

Axial contrast CT of the chest in lung window demonstrates a cavitating spiculated abnormality in the right upper lobe (arrowhead). Smaller air space opacities were seen elsewhere in both lungs (long arrow demonstrating one in the left upper lobe).

A PET CT was performed which showed intense uptake in the left lateral and posterolateral bladder wall, consistent with malignancy. Hypermetabolic activity was also noted in a 1.0‐cm left external iliac lymph node, whereas bilateral lung nodules demonstrated increased uptake suggestive of granulomatous infection (Figure 3). No abnormal metabolic activity was detected in the brain, osseous skeleton, or abdominal viscera. Lung biopsy was taken and confirmed necrotizing granulomatous inflammation, ruling out malignancy. The patient underwent four cycles of chemotherapy of carboplatin/etoposide. He had follow‐up PET CT postchemotherapy, which showed regression of the bladder tumor and no evidence of hypermetabolic nodularity in the bladder wall. Previously noted intensely hypermetabolic lymph nodes were no longer visible, indicating likely treatment response (Figure 3).

(A) Coronal FDG PET CT before chemotherapy demonstrates hypermetabolic left urinary bladder wall mass (long white arrow) and an avid left obturator lymph node (short white arrow). In addition, the cavitating lung lesion in the right upper lobe (long red arrow), as well as the other lung nodules, are FDG avid (short red arrow). (B) Postchemotherapy, coronal FDG PET CT demonstrates significant reduction in size and activity of the urinary bladder mass (long white arrow) as well as the right lung lesion (long red arrow). The FDG avid left obturator lymph nodes and other smaller lung nodules seen in the pretreatment FDG PET CT study have resolved.

A transurethral resection of the bladder tumor (TURBT) was performed, successfully resecting most of the tumor. Histopathology examination of his TURBT specimen revealed an infiltrative lesion characterized by the usual morphology of classic invasive UC. Geographic necrosis and crush artifacts were also present (Figure 4). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) showed strong synaptophysin positivity and weak chromogranin positivity in the small cells, with negative staining for GATA‐3 and NKX3.1. In contrast, classic invasive UC components were positive for GATA‐3 and negative for synaptophysin, chromogranin, and NKX3.1 (Figure 5). The combined morphologic and immunohistochemical findings supported the diagnosis of mixed classic UC and small cell carcinoma of the urinary bladder.

Postoperatively, the patient tolerated the procedure well, with no new complaints or abnormalities detected in laboratory investigations. The patient has been scheduled for a follow‐up PET‐CT scan, 3 months after the initial PET‐CT, and it will be discussed in upcoming multidisciplinary team (MDT) meetings for further management planning.

3. Discussion

We report a case of a middle‐aged male with multiple comorbidities who initially presented with urinary symptoms and was subsequently diagnosed with coexisting SCCB and UC at an unusually early stage. The coexistence of classic UC and small cell carcinoma of the urinary bladder is rare, and most reported cases describe small cell carcinoma as a dominant component or arising after prior treatment for UC [7–9]. In contrast, our case demonstrated early synchronous coexistence at initial diagnosis, without prior therapy, suggesting early divergent differentiation from a common progenitor cell. Unlike many reported cases that present at an advanced stage with aggressive clinical behavior, early detection in our patient enabled prompt recognition of both components, which is critical given that even a focal small cell component significantly worsens prognosis and alters treatment strategy [9, 10]. This case underscores the importance of thorough histopathological evaluation, including IHC, in newly diagnosed bladder tumors to avoid underdiagnosis of the neuroendocrine component and ensure appropriate multimodal management [8, 11].

Several theories have attempted to explain SCCB origin, with suggestions that it arises from multipotent stem cells within the bladder mucosa or from neuroendocrine differentiation of pre‐existing UC cells [1]. Rarely, UC may transform into SCCB during disease progression. This transformation is often driven by genetic and epigenetic changes, particularly under selective pressures from therapies like androgen deprivation, chemotherapy, or radiotherapy. Fuji et al. showed that microRNAs, especially involving miR‐145, are involved in the process [12]. UC cells can acquire stem cell–like properties, leading to transdifferentiation into neuroendocrine subtypes, such as SCCB. This cellular plasticity allows UC cells to transform and adopt more aggressive characteristics associated with SCCB. Molecular analyses reveal that UC‐to‐SCCB transformation is associated with high rates of RB1 and TP53 inactivation, which contribute to unchecked cell proliferation and tumor progression [13, 14]. Upon transformation, UC cells lose their typical urothelial morphology and acquire features consistent with small cell carcinoma, including increased chromatin granularity, scant cytoplasm, and high mitotic rates [8, 15]. This transformation makes SCCB more aggressive, causing rapid progression, early metastasis, and greater chemotherapy sensitivity. Clinically, UC to SCCB conversion alters prognosis and treatment, requiring aggressive systemic therapy targeting neuroendocrine features, as UC treatments may be ineffective [2].

Up to 40% of SCCB cases are reported to coexist with UC, often in the same tumor mass [1, 4]. This coexistence supports the theory that SCCB and UC may share a common clonal origin, likely from multipotent stem cells capable of differentiating along both urothelial and neuroendocrine pathways [1, 2, 16]. Histopathologically, SCCB and UC can be distinguished by their expression of neuroendocrine and urothelial markers, respectively, as seen in our case. Although SCCB shows positivity for neuroendocrine markers like chromogranin and synaptophysin, UC often stains positive for uroplakin and GATA‐3 [4]. Uroplakin is the distinguishing factor as it is negative in 100% of cases of SCCB [6]. The coexistence of UC and SCCB within the same tumor or patient complicates the clinical course and influences treatment planning. Patients with mixed SCCB‐UC tumors often exhibit a clinical course similar to pure SCCB, characterized by early metastasis and a poor prognosis. Given the aggressive nature of SCCB, even when coexisting with UC, these cases are managed as pure SCCB [4, 8], with an emphasis on systemic therapies and neoadjuvant chemotherapy over local treatments like radical cystectomy alone [2, 8]. The National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) and European Association of Urology (EAU) guidelines suggest that either concurrent chemoradiotherapy or platinum‐based neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by local consolidation can be used for localized disease. For tumors with small cell histology, regardless of muscle‐invasive status, treatment involves either chemoradiation or radical cystectomy [1, 15]. Studies suggest that mixed histology cases may present slightly better outcomes when confined to the bladder, but once metastatic, the prognosis remains poor [4, 8].

Noted for its aggressive growth and high metastatic potential, SCCB commonly resembles SCLC both histologically and genetically [1, 8, 15]. SCCB is frequently linked to key genetic alterations, particularly in TP53, RB1, and TERT, which are involved in tumor suppression and telomerase regulation [1]. TP53 and RB1 mutations, frequently observed in both SCCB and SCLC, contribute to uncontrolled cell growth and resistance to apoptosis. A recent next‐generation sequencing study revealed that SCCB also shows a high rate of mutational burden, indicating genomic instability that accelerates tumor progression [17]. Additional genes similar to SCLC, including delta like protein 3 (DLL3), a member of the Notch signaling pathway, have been implicated in SCCB′s neuroendocrine differentiation, often predicting poor outcomes [1]. The loss of heterozygosity and specific alterations in tumor‐suppressor genes underscore SCCB′s unique biological profile, yet more molecular research is needed to fully elucidate its pathogenesis and identify specific therapeutic targets [4].

Management strategies for SCCB adapt protocols from SCLC, with systemic chemotherapy forming the cornerstone of treatment [1, 8]. Platinum‐based chemotherapy, particularly with cisplatin or carboplatin, is commonly administered [4] alongside etoposide or other agents [1, 8, 15]. Alternatively, ifosfamide/doxorubicin in alteration with etoposide/cisplatin can be used [1]. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by radical cystectomy can significantly improve survival, with 5‐year survival rates in retrospective studies reaching 78% compared with 36% with cystectomy alone [9]. A recent study treated SCCB using two cycles neoadjuvant chemotherapy, including cisplatin and etoposide combination, followed by definitive chemoradiation [18]. Even in mixed SCCB‐UC cases, neoadjuvant chemotherapy [8, 19], especially MVAC regimen (methotrexate, vinblastine, adriamycin, and cisplatin) [2] has proven beneficial [19], thereby emphasizing the importance of targeting both components with systemic agents. For patients with metastatic disease, systemic chemotherapy is the primary approach. Platinum‐etoposide regimens remain standard, but newer treatments, particularly immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), such as atezolizumab [20] and durvalumab, are showing promise in SCCB [1], especially in treatment‐resistant cases. The IMvigor 210 trial highlights that neuroendocrine bladder tumors expressing high levels of neuronal antigens may benefit from ICIs, particularly in cases of advanced or metastatic SCCB [1, 13]. The PURE‐01 trial further supports the role of pembrolizumab in SCCB management, showing a complete pathological response rate of 37% in mixed variant histology bladder cancers, including SCCB [21].

The rarity of SCCB poses challenges in establishing standardized treatment protocols and conducting large‐scale prospective studies. The limited data necessitate treatment extrapolations from other cancers, such as SCLC, which may not entirely address SCCB′s unique biology. Despite these limitations, the aggressive nature of SCCB and its distinct molecular characteristics underscore the need for a tailored approach, integrating systemic chemotherapy, surgical resection, and emerging immunotherapies. Further research into SCCB′s genetic profile may uncover specific vulnerabilities, potentially allowing for novel targeted therapies. With the prevalence of DLL3 expression in SCCB, targeted agents such as Tarlatamab, a DLL3‐targeted antibody‐drug conjugate studied in SCLC [22], could represent a promising option if further validated in SCCB [1]. Additionally, the high prevalence of RB1 inactivation in SCCB suggests that therapies designed to restore or compensate for RB1 function may hold therapeutic value [4].

4. Conclusion

SCCB represents a highly aggressive and challenging malignancy that demands a multimodal, personalized treatment approach. Although progress has been made in understanding its pathogenesis and identifying potential treatment options, much remains to be learned about this rare cancer. Continued efforts to elucidate its genetic landscape and to test novel therapies will be essential in improving outcomes for SCCB patients, particularly those with advanced disease or transformation from UC.
