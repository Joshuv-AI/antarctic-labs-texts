Approaching Gold‐Standard Sensitivity in a Portable and Versatile Gold Nanoprisms Thermoplasmonic Platform for Lateral Flow Detection of Biomolecules
Authors: Carlos Cuestas Ayllón, Alba Martín Barreiro, África García Barrientos, María Moros, Valeria Grazú Bonavía, Jesús Martínez de la Fuente
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPlasmonic nanoparticles exhibit unique optical and thermal properties that make them ideal for next‐generation diagnostic technologies. Among them, gold nanoprisms (AuNPrs) are especially powerful due to their anisotropic geometry, tunable surface plasmon resonance in the near‐infrared (NIR) region, and high photothermal conversion efficiency. These features transform them from passive labels into active nanotransducers capable of amplifying diagnostic signals. Building on these advantages, we developed two AuNPr‐based sensing platforms: Thermo Lateral Flow Immunoassay (ThermoLFIA) and Thermo Oligonucleotide‐based Lateral Flow Assay (ThermoOLFA). By coupling AuNPrs with thermoresponsive substrates, both systems use localized heat generation to boost analytical sensitivity, enabling visual enhancement far beyond that of conventional gold spheres. ThermoLFIA demonstrated clinically relevant detection of gastrointestinal cancer biomarkers (CEA, CA19‐9, VEGF), outperforming standard ELISA tests. In parallel, functionalization of AuNPrs with DNA probes enabled ThermoOLFA to detect SARS‐CoV‐2 RNA with sensitivity comparable to RT‐qPCR, but without amplification steps and within 25 min. This proof‐of‐concept confirms that plasmonic nanoprisms can drive rapid, portable, and ultrasensitive diagnostics, opening a path toward point‐of‐care platforms with strong clinical and translational potential.

Introduction

Despite substantial advances in diagnostic technology, many cancers and infectious diseases still lack screening tools that are both affordable and sensitive enough for early‐stage detection. This lack means that diagnoses often occur only after the disease has progressed, when treatment options are fewer and prognoses poorer. What is urgently needed are diagnostic platforms that combine high sensitivity and specificity, minimizing false negatives at the critical early phase, with quantitative readouts suitable for monitoring therapy response and disease evolution. The COVID‐19 pandemic clearly illustrated this imperative, and in fact, the World Health Organization (WHO) stressed that rapid, decentralized testing followed by isolation and contact tracing is essential to break transmission chains and safeguard healthcare workers [1]. In general, reference methods such as ELISA and RT‐qPCR remain constrained by expensive equipment, specialized personnel, and hours‐long workflows, limiting their scalability. These limitations have intensified the search for portable, low‐cost assays that can deliver laboratory‐grade performance without sacrificing speed or simplicity. This is the reason why the development of portable, cost‐effective diagnostic systems with high clinical performance is therefore essential, not only to enable early and precise detection, but also to support ongoing clinical decision‐making and reduce the burden on centralized healthcare systems [2]. New diagnostic platforms, such as those integrating nanomaterials into microfluidic systems, have shown enhanced analytical performance and improved detection limits, while also enabling automation and simplifing the detection process for end‐users without specialized training. A prime example is the integration of Mirkin's bio‐barcode assay [3] with microfluidics, a technology that has significantly advanced the development of ultrasensitive and user‐friendly diagnostic platforms which is today commercialized by DiaSorin through platforms that simplify multiple biomarker detection [4].

These technological advancements align with the recent resurgence of point‐of‐care (POC) testing, which highlighted the value of diagnostic tools that are easy to use, rapid, cost‐effective, and operable outside of clinical settings or centralized laboratory infrastructure [5, 6]. In this context, lateral flow assays (LFAs) emerged as the predominant POC format, demonstrating the feasibility of decentralized testing on a massive scale. However, despite their global deployment and ease of use, conventional LFAs are limited by relatively low sensitivity and typically yield only qualitative results [7, 8]. To overcome these limitations, recent strategies have explored the integration of plasmonic gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) as dual optical and thermal reporters [9, 10]. Upon excitation at their localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) frequency, these nanoparticles can concentrate electromagnetic energy at the nanoscale, which is efficiently converted into localized heat. This photothermal effect allows AuNPs to act as thermally active labels, enhancing signal intensity in LFA platforms and enabling novel transduction mechanisms beyond conventional colorimetric readouts. Traditionally, the thermal signal generated by plasmonic nanoparticles has been captured using infrared thermal cameras to enable indirect analyte quantification [9, 11, 12]. However, by employing gold nanoprisms (AuNPrs), a subclass of gold nanoparticles with anisotropic geometry and strong near‐infrared (NIR) absorption, we have already demonstrated that this step can be bypassed. Thanks to their high photothermal conversion efficiency, AuNPrs can generate sufficient localized heating upon laser excitation to produce a visible imprint on thermosensitive paper at the test line. This feature offers strong potential for full integration, enabling both qualitative and quantitative detection in resource‐limited settings. Previous studies from our group highlighted the potential of these thermal labels for ultrasensitive biosensing, as demonstrated through two proof‐of‐concept approaches: a dot blot assay for protein detection [13], and a lateral flow assay enabling the selection of oligonucleotide probes as biolabels for nucleic acid detection [14, 15, 16].

While the dot blot assay demonstrated the high sensitivity of thermoplasmonic labels for protein immuno‐detection, its application remains limited to laboratory settings and is not suitable for clinical translation. To overcome this limitation, we developed here a thermal lateral flow immunoassay (ThermoLFIA), based on antibody‐mediated immunodetection, optimized for the rapid and sensitive identification of tumor markers relevant to gastrointestinal (GI) cancers, including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), carbohydrate antigen 19‐9 (CA19‐9), and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF). GI cancers are heterogeneous and often require multimarker evaluation; therefore, CEA and CA19‐9 were selected as clinically established markers for tumor burden and disease monitoring, while VEGF provides complementary information on tumor angiogenesis and progression, enabling direct benchmarking of the proposed platform.

Although evaluated individually in this study, their combined selection highlights the versatility of the system for future multi‐analyte diagnostic panels. Early detection of GI cancers remains a significant challenge due to the lack of affordable and scalable screening methods suitable for large‐scale screening campaigns [17, 18]. This approach yielded limits of detection (LoDs) as low as 2 ng mL− 1 for CEA, 5 ng mL− 1 for CA19‐9, and 0.3 ng mL− 1 for VEGF, supporting measurement in clinically relevant concentration ranges commonly targeted by commercial immunoassays [19, 20, 21]. Additionally, by integrating thermoplasmonic labels and a library of oligonucleotide probes into a lateral‐flow platform, we previously established an efficient biomarker‐selection workflow. Iterative screening showed that antiparallel triplex‐forming oligonucleotides (Polypurine Reverse‐Hoogsteen Hairpins, PPRH probes) are the most effective architecture for targeting SARS‐CoV‐2 RNA [14, 15, 16], providing a strong foundation for future amplification‐free assays on a user‐friendly POC format. We report now the engineering, optimization and clinical validation of a thermoplasmonic oligonucleotide lateral‐flow assay (ThermoOLFA).

The resulting device detects SARS‐CoV‐2 RNA directly from clinical samples in less than 25 min, without requiring reverse transcription or any amplification, yet attains RT‐qPCR‐equivalent sensitivity (Ct ≈ 35). Such rapid, high‐performance testing could be pivotal for mass screening of asymptomatic individuals carrying the virus, enabling faster outbreak containment and smarter public‐health triage.

Although validated here for SARS‐CoV‐2, the same platform can be reconfigured for virtually any pathogen, offering a flexible platform for broad infectious‐disease diagnostics. Together, these results underscore the transformative potential of plasmonic thermosensing in next‐generation diagnostics. By coupling the nanoscale control of photothermal nanostructures with the ease‐of‐use of lateral flow systems, these platforms bridge the gap between laboratory‐grade sensitivity and field applicability. As healthcare systems worldwide shift toward decentralization and early intervention, plasmonic nanoparticles offer a pathway to develop rapid, low‐cost, and highly sensitive assays that can be deployed in clinics, homes, or low‐resource settings without compromising key performance attributes such as sensitivity, specificity, and precision. To contextualize the novelty of this work, it is important to note that while individual components of the thermoplasmonic platform, such as gold nanoprisms synthesis, biomolecular detection strategies, and thermoplasmonic readout, have been previously reported by our group, these contributions were largely addressed separately and limited to proof‐of‐concept demonstrations. In contrast, the present study integrates these elements into a unified, end‐to‐end diagnostic system, including controlled nanoparticle synthesis, fully implemented lateral flow formats, portable readout, and validation in clinical samples. This integration represents a key advancement toward a scalable, industrially compatible, and point‐of‐care–ready platform with demonstrated clinical relevance.

Results and Discussion

Principle of the Thermal Lateral Flow methodology

Both platforms, ThermoLFIA and ThermoOLFA; utilize a unified thermal readout mechanism based on the photothermal properties of plasmonic AuNPrs [22], applied to two distinct molecular recognition formats: (1) immunoassay‐based protein detection and (2) nucleic acid hybridization for viral RNA detection. Both approaches share the same lateral flow architecture (Figure 1a) and thermal transduction mechanism (Figure 1b), enabling a versatile, low‐cost, and field‐deployable platform suitable for early diagnosis of both chronic (e.g., cancer) and infectious diseases (e.g., respiratory viral infections). In the first strategy, ThermoLFIA adopts a classical sandwich format for the detection of protein biomarkers such as CEA, CA 19‐9, and VEGF (Figure 1c). Detection antibodies are conjugated to AuNPrs via biotin‐streptavidin interaction, bind their target analyte upon sample application, and migrate along the nitrocellulose membrane.

(a) Schematic representation of the lateral flow strip architecture employed in both thermoplasmonic immunoassays for protein biomarker detection and oligonucleotide‐based assays for amplification‐free nucleic acid analysis. Each strip consists of a sample pad, a nitrocellulose membrane with designated test (TL) and control (CL) lines, and an absorbent pad, all assembled on a PVC backing card. A thermosensitive paper is positioned on the backside of the backing card, aligned with the region of the nitrocellulose membrane containing the test and control lines, enabling thermal signal readout upon laser irradiation. To enhance system reproducibility, particularly for ThermoOLFA assays, a rigid cassette housing was used to ensure consistent alignment, handling, and stable positioning of the strip and thermosensitive material during irradiation. (b) Workflow of the ThermoLFA biosensor. Following sample application and completion of lateral flow migration, the front side of the strip is irradiated at the test line using a 1064 nm NIR laser. The generated heat is transferred to the thermosensitive material on the backside, producing a visible color change. The thermal signal is then recorded using a portable optical reader or camera and quantified through digital image analysis of the color intensity. (c) Assay configuration for protein detection (e.g., gastric cancer biomarkers). Plasmonic gold nanoprisms functionalized with detection antibodies (Au@NPr@Ab detection) bind the target analyte, forming a sandwich immunocomplex with a capture antibody immobilized at the test line. A secondary antibody is used at the control line to confirm nanoparticle presence. (d) Assay configuration for SARS‐CoV‐2 RNA detection. In this hybridization‐based format, the viral RNA bridges a biotinylated capture probe and a complementary nanoparticle‐bound sequence (Au@NPr@PPRH). This complex interacts with streptavidin at the test line, enabling specific, amplification‐free detection of the genetic target. Differences in color schemes between panels (c) and (d) were intentionally introduced to distinguish between the protein‐ and nucleic acid‐based assay formats. Variations in the graphical representation of AuNPrs also reflect differences in their effective size and functionalization in each system.

The complex is captured at the test line (TL) by immobilized antibodies specific to the target. The control line (CL) contains a secondary antibody that recognizes the antibody bound to the nanoparticle, ensuring a positive signal regardless of analyte presence and serving as an internal validation of the assay. In the second strategy, ThermoOLFA, synthetic DNA probes are used to detect nucleic acid targets (Figure 1d). For detection of SARS‐CoV‐2 RNA, a sandwich‐type hybridization scheme is employed in which two sequence‐specific oligonucleotide probes, the detection probe and the capture probe, bind cooperatively to adjacent regions of the target RNA. The detection probe should be composed by 20 nucleotides long, with a GC content of 40%–55%, and was covalently attached to AuNPrs using a terminal amino group. This probe design was based on polypurine reverse Hoogsteen hairpins (PPRHs), previously described as TENADA detection probes [14].

The capture probe, designed to hybridize to an upstream sequence spaced by at least three nucleotides from the detection site, carries a biotin moiety at its 3’ end, which enables the hybrid complex to be immobilized at the test line via strong biotin‐streptavidin interaction. Streptavidin is pre‐deposited on the nitrocellulose membrane (Figure 1d), ensuring that only properly hybridized complexes are retained, thereby ensuring both high specificity and efficient lateral flow‐based signal capture. The control line was prepared by depositing functionalized BSA conjugated to a complementary probe that specifically recognizes the detection probe attached to the AuNPRs. This ensures a positive signal at the control line regardless of analyte presence, thus validating the test. Both formats rely on the same detection principle: laser irradiation of the test line induces localized heat generation via the localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) of accumulated AuNPrs. A thermosensitive paper integrated on the back side of the test strip, aligned with the test line through a rear viewing window, transduces this thermal signal by changing color from white to gray‐black.

This thermal response is directly proportional to analyte concentration, enabling semi‐quantitative (yes/no) detection but also quantification via basic image analysis tools (Figure 1b). When analyte levels are high, a visible colorimetric signal appears on the front side of the nitrocellulose membrane due to the intrinsic green color of AuNPrs, and laser irradiation is unnecessary. However, in the absence of visible signal, or when the response is faint and falls within a “negative” threshold, laser irradiation reveals a thermal imprint on the rear thermosensitive paper, thereby improving detection sensitivity for low‐concentration analytes. While the assay requires functionalized bioconjugates for target recognition, it operates as a single‐step, wash‐free process, with no need for signal amplification or additional development steps.

This preserves the standard lateral flow workflow while incorporating a low‐cost, camera‐free thermal readout system, enhancing both sensitivity and usability in point‐of‐care or resource‐limited settings [23]. Despite the above‐mentioned considerations, when a visible color signal was observed at high analyte concentrations, laser irradiation was still systematically applied during the development and optimization of the system to ensure consistency and enable quantitative readout. In the intended point‐of‐care workflow, particularly in a future near‐market prototype designed for routine use and manufacturability, the assay will operate in a single standardized mode in which irradiation is always performed, eliminating operator‐dependent decisions based on visual inspection. The colorimetric signal should therefore be considered complementary rather than a primary readout, ensuring that low‐positive samples are not missed while maintaining a simple and robust operational protocol.

Principle of the Thermal Lateral Flow methodology

AuNPrs were successfully synthesized using a slightly modified seedless method, carried out under static conditions (Figure 2a top), without agitation, at room temperature, yielding AuNPrs with a localized surface‐plasmon resonance band around 1200 nm (Figure 2b) [24]. Scaled‐up synthesis was developed by using a peristaltic pump to add reagents at 19°C under gentle stirring, ensuring uniform mixing. By adjusting the HAuCl4/Na2S2O3 ratio, the LSPR band was shifted to approximately 1100 nm, maximizing heat generation under NIR irradiation (Figure 2a bottom). AuNPrs synthesized under static conditions were used for ThermoLFIA assays, while the scaled‐up batch for ThermoOLFA assays. In both cases, the resulting colloidal suspension consisted predominantly of triangular AuNPrs, along with a fraction of undesired spherical nanoparticles.

Gel electrophoresis confirms successful attachment, as evidenced by a reduction in the free oligonucleotide band after functionalization. Assuming the initial band corresponds to 100% free oligonucleotide, a semi‐quantitative analysis suggests that approximately 65% of the probe is consumed during functionalization, consistent with partial conjugation to the nanoparticle surface.

An initial purification step using ultracentrifugation effectively reduced the presence of the spherical fraction, enriching the suspension in anisotropic, photothermally active AuNPrs (Figure 2a). These spherical by‐products were identified by their distinct LSPR peak around 540 nm, which served as a spectral marker to guide their removal. The partially purified AuNPrs were then PEGylated using HS‐PEG‐COOH (5000 Da) to improve colloidal stability and introduce functional carboxyl groups for subsequent biofunctionalization. A second purification step, performed via agarose gel electrophoresis following PEGylation, yielded highly purified nanoprisms (Figure 2a). As mentioned, the two synthesis approaches, manual and scaled‐up semiautomated, resulted in slightly different LSPR maxima.

However, both were sufficiently close to the laser emission, which is centered at 1064 nm in our irradiation system, enabling efficient remote activation of the AuNPrs as thermal labels. Since the thermal readout is governed by the photothermal response at the excitation wavelength, we verified that AuNPr batches obtained from both syntheses exhibit comparable heating behavior under 1064 nm irradiation at the working nanoparticle loading, supporting consistent excitation efficiency and quantitative thermal readout across batches. Batch‐to‐batch reproducibility was routinely monitored by UV‐Vis‐NIR spectroscopy, showing only modest variations in the NIR plasmonic profiles across independent syntheses. Separately, colloidal stability upon storage at 4°C was assessed by tracking the absorbance at 1064 nm over time, which showed minimal drift (Figure S1.1). In practical terms, the limited batch‐to‐batch variability observed at the excitation wavelength used in this work (1064 nm) is not expected to require major changes in the laser‐developing conditions for thermal readout. The assay can typically be operated within the same preset irradiation window (power and exposure time), with routine verification.

Comparable behavior was also observed across different strip preparations and probe formats built on the same AuNPr photothermal label backbone (e.g., SARS‐CoV‐2 and influenza‐related probes), suggesting that the dispersion in thermal signals is mainly influenced by typical strip and sample variability rather than systematic nanoparticle‐batch effects (Figure S1.2). Successful functionalization was evidenced by a shift to negative ζ‐potential values. Moreover, SEM analysis verified the triangular morphology and uniform side dimensions of the AuNPrs (Figure 2b,c). Quantification of nanoparticle concentration was performed using UV–vis spectroscopy and ICP‐AES, based on an experimentally determined extinction coefficient. The PEGylated AuNPrs were subsequently biofunctionalized with the appropriate molecular recognition element depending on the detection strategy: either specific antibodies (for ThermoLFIA) or a DNA probe (for ThermoOLFA).

Antibody Biofunctionalization of Gold Nanoprisms for ThermoLFIA

A modular and straightforward functionalization strategy was developed to enable flexible conjugation of biotinylated detection antibodies to AuNPrs, suitable for use in ThermoLFIA applications. The approach relies on covalent immobilization of streptavidin onto carboxyl‐terminated PEGylated surfaces, followed by selective binding of biotinylated antibodies, enabling rapid customization of AuNPrs for different target analytes.

To ensure colloidal stability and introduce reactive groups, AuNPrs were first modified with HS‐PEG‐COOH using a AuNPrs:PEG mass ratio of 1:1. PEG chains not only stabilize the nanoparticles sterically but also provide carboxylic acid groups for subsequent bioconjugation. The carboxyl groups were activated with EDC/sulfo‐NHS chemistry and incubated with streptavidin to enable covalent attachment (Figure 2d).

Since streptavidin contains four high‐affinity biotin‐binding sites, a specific orientation of immobilization is not necessary to preserve its binding capacity. Random coupling via amine groups is sufficient to retain functionality, simplifying the process compared to other biomolecules that require site‐directed conjugation [25]. To prevent non‐specific interactions that compromise specificity in detection, the remaining activated groups were blocked using 2.5% BSA. The functionalized AuNPrs (AuNPrs@streptavidin) were subsequently incubated with biotinylated antibodies specific for the target analytes (anti‐CEA, anti‐CA19.9, or anti‐VEGF), allowing for modular and specific probe preparation. UV‐Vis‐NIR spectra recorded after antibody conjugation remained comparable to those of the parent AuNPrs@streptavidin, indicating that the LSPR band was preserved and that the photothermal transduction properties of the labels were not significantly altered (Figure S1.3). The binding efficiency of streptavidin to PEGylated AuNPrs was evaluated using iodine‐125 (125I) radiolabeled streptavidin. Different streptavidin amounts (8, 10, and 15 µg mL−1) were incubated with the nanoparticles, and the bound protein was quantified by measuring gamma emission from the nanoparticle fraction. This was corroborated indirectly by colorimetric quantification of unbound protein in the supernatant using Bradford assay. The highest amount of bound streptavidin was achieved when 10 µg mL−1 protein was added, obtaining a binding yield of 56%. This corresponds to around 1626 streptavidin molecules per AuNPr. This value is consistent with the theoretical maximum number of streptavidin molecules per AuNPr. Considering a AuNPr as a triangular prism (270 × 234 × 9 nm, dimensions obtained by SEM, Figure 2b), the total surface area is around ∼70 400 nm2. Based on streptavidin structural dimensions and assuming a binding orientation maximizing its contact area, each streptavidin molecule was estimated to occupy about 31.5 nm2.

This projection leads to a maximum capacity of approximately 2235 streptavidin molecules per nanoparticle, fitting quite well with the obtained results. Although a different AuNPrs:PEG ratio (1:5) was also tested, the number of streptavidin molecules bound per nanoparticle was comparable to that observed with the 1:1 ratio. Based on these findings, the 1:1 AuNPrs:PEG ratio and 10 µg mL−1 streptavidin concentration were selected as optimal for streptavidin functionalization. As with streptavidin, the amount of biotinylated antibody bound vs. that offered was analyzed based on the specific interaction between the biotinylated antibody and streptavidin immobilized on the prism surface. Given the structural similarity among antibodies, biotinylated anti‐CEA was used as a model for this study. The amount of antibody bound to the nanoparticles (AuNPrs@streptavidin) was quantified using radioactivity‐based measurements at increasing concentrations of biotinylated anti‐CEA antibody added to the system (2, 6, and 12 µg mL−1). The results showed a decrease in binding efficiency with increasing antibody concentration: 80% at 2 µg mL−1, 70% at 6 µg mL−1, and approximately 50% at 12 µg mL−1. At the highest concentration tested (12 µg mL−1), the maximum amount of bound antibody was observed, reaching 6.4 µg mL−1, which corresponds to approximately 360 antibody molecules per prism. To estimate surface coverage, each antibody was approximated as a rectangle of 12 nm in height (standard IgG height) and 8.5 nm in width, based on the antibody structure from PDB ID: 1clo. This gives an approximate area of 85 nm2 per molecule. Considering the total available surface area on the streptavidin‐coated AuNPrs, a maximum of ∼533 antibodies could theoretically be accommodated per prism.

Therefore, the quantified binding corresponds to approximately 68% surface coverage, indicating that the nanoparticle surface is near saturation under these conditions. Based on these results, 12 µg mL−1 was selected as the optimal antibody concentration for nanoparticle functionalization.

The same procedure was applied to the additional commercial biotinylated antibodies selected for detection of other target analytes, resulting in approximately 5 µg mL−1 bound for both anti‐CA19.9 and anti‐VEGF antibodies. This corresponds to around ∼282 molecules per nanoparticle, fewer than those observed for anti‐CEA. These differences in total antibody binding likely reflect variations in the degree of biotinylating among the antibodies. After functionalization, the bioconjugates were stored in borate buffer at pH 8.0, which was identified as the buffer condition that provided the greatest colloidal stability for prolonged storage.

Biofunctionalization With DNA Probes for ThermoOLFA

As in the previous case, the AuNPrs were modified with PEG to enhance their stability and ensure successful subsequent functionalization. Although a 1:1 AuNPrs:PEG ratio was sufficient to ensure colloidal stability in static synthesis, a higher PEG excess was required during DNA probe functionalization. Since no BSA blocking step was included and DNA probes are too small to effectively shield the nanoparticle surface, additional PEG (1:2 AuNPrs:PEG ratio) was added during PEGylation to passivate the surface in advance. This helped reduce non‐specific interactions and maintain assay specificity. Then, AuNPrs were coupled to amine‐terminated PPRH detection probe through standard EDC/sulfo‐NHS chemistry (Figure 2e). For AuNPrs functionalization with oligonucleotides, different concentrations of 2.75, 5.5, 11 and 22 µg mL−1 of oligonucleotides were added to a AuNPrs solution (0.5 mg mL−1). Conjugation efficiency was evaluated by agarose gel electrophoresis with GelRed staining (Figure 2e).

The decrease in fluorescence intensity of the free oligonucleotide band in the supernatant after functionalization indicated partial binding, with residual unbound probe remaining in solution.

Maximum surface coverage was achieved by adding 22 µg mL−1 (corresponding to 1100 oligonucleotides per AuNPr), yielding 65% functionalization, approximately 715 oligonucleotides per AuNPr. Considering a PEG5000‐thiol footprint of 0.35 nm2, each AuNPr can accommodate roughly 83000 PEG molecules; however, only about 0.86% of these sites are functionalized with oligonucleotides. This limited functionalization is attributed to a combination of factors: low primary amine reactivity under the reaction conditions, hydrolysis of the activated groups over time, and electrostatic repulsion between the negatively charged nanoprisms and oligonucleotide probes. Although ionic strength was increased to reduce electrostatic repulsion by neutralizing these negative charges, this approach was limited by the need to maintain colloidal stability, which relies on electrostatic repulsion. Biofunctionalized AuNPrs retained a ζ‐potential of approximately −24.0 ± 0.8 mV, indicating colloidal stability and a negatively charged surface, while the UV–vis spectrum exhibited a sharp LSPR peak centered at ≈1100 nm, confirming the desired optical tuning (Figure 2c). AuNPrs photothermal‐conversion efficiency (PCE), in both native and biofunctionalized forms, was also measured using a previously reported procedure (Table S3.1 and Figure S3.1) [26, 27]. Real‐time temperature data recorded under near‐infrared (NIR) irradiation were plotted as ΔT versus time. Even at a nanoparticle concentration of 0.1 mg mL−1, the global temperature of the dispersion rose swiftly and plateaued at approximately 55°C. Crucially, biofunctionalization did not alter the heating profile, demonstrating that surface modification preserves their photothermal performance.

ThermoLFIA for the Detection of Gastrointestinal Cancer Markers

The optimal performance of the AuNPrs‐based ThermoLFIA depends on a finely tuned set of variables, many of which also apply to conventional lateral‐flow immunoassays. First, selecting the right membrane and pad materials is essential to ensure smooth and consistent capillary flow. Second, high‐quality detection labels with excellent sensitivity and specificity are key for enhancing analytical performance.

Reagent concentrations, including capture and detection probes, nanoparticles, and blocking agents, must be carefully balanced to maximize signal while minimizing background. Additionally, parameters such as sample volume, incubation time, and buffer composition require precise optimization to ensure consistent and reproducible readouts. Reliable biomolecule immobilization and robust nanoparticle‐conjugation chemistry further reinforce assay accuracy. Finally, clearly defined stability and storage conditions are crucial to maintaining long‐term reliability and ease of use. The different optimization steps are described individually in the following sections; however, for clarity, a comprehensive summary of all evaluated parameters and selection criteria is provided in Table S2.7, allowing an integrated overview of the system. Prior to selecting the final components for the LFIA, a range of raw materials were tested to evaluate their suitability for the intended application (Table S2.1). The main objective was to optimize the ThermoLFIA strip design by ensuring consistent and controlled flow of both the analyte and the bioconjugate through the nitrocellulose membrane, while maintaining high sensitivity and overall assay performance. In this LFIA configuration, antibody‐functionalized AuNPrs were aerosol‐deposited onto the conjugate pad, confirming that anisotropic nanostructures can be incorporated into standard strip‐manufacturing workflows.

To optimize this process, a range of pre‐treatment conditions for the conjugate pad were evaluated, including buffer systems, blocking agents, and surfactants, with the aim of enhancing the stability and shelf‐life of the biofunctionalized AuNPrs once deposited, while ensuring their reproducible release from the pad upon sample application and consistent performance of the assay. Deposition consistency was verified by image‐based analysis of the developed test line, quantifying grayscale/RGB intensity under constant acquisition conditions. Among the buffers tested, borate buffer supplemented with sucrose, BSA, Tween‐20, and PVP was selected for its ability to prevent false positives, ensure reproducible nanoparticle release and accumulation at the test and control lines, and maintain performance over time (Table S2.2 and Figures S2.1 and S2.2).

In parallel, four nitrocellulose membranes were evaluated to determine the most suitable option for optimal signal development. Although FF80HP has a larger pore size than FF120HP, the measured flow times for both membranes were relatively similar (1 min 23 s for FF80HP and 1 min 00 s for FF120HP), indicating that factors beyond pore size such as membrane thickness or surface treatments may influence fluid dynamics. In contrast, the plus versions showed a clear distinction, with FF120HPplus (smaller pore size) exhibiting a significantly slower flow time (4 min 28 s) compared to FF80HPplus (1 min 30 s). Based on these results, FF80HP was selected as the preferred membrane, as its slightly slower flow allowed for improved interaction time at the test line, thereby enhancing assay sensitivity (Table S2.3 and Figure S2.3). To further validate this choice, ignition threshold tests were conducted by irradiating membranes loaded with varying concentrations of AuNPrs at different NIR laser powers. FF80HP displayed the most pronounced and consistent thermal response, confirming its superior performance for photothermal signal generation in the ThermoLFIA system (Figure S2.4).

Thermosensitive paper was placed on the back side of the membrane to enhance thermal signal amplification. The detection antibodies were pre‐conjugated to plasmonic nanoparticles, as previously described, prior to their integration into the lateral flow assay. Meanwhile, the capture antibodies, specific to the test and control lines, were immobilized onto the nitrocellulose membrane during the strip fabrication process. Once the assay was completed, the test line area was irradiated with a 1064 nm NIR laser. The accumulated AuNPrs converted light into heat, generating a localized temperature increase proportional to analyte concentration. This thermal signal produced a distinct contrast change on the thermosensitive paper attached to the back of the test strip, enabling quantitative readout. At high analyte concentrations, the test line was clearly visible to the naked eye, making laser irradiation optional. However, for low analyte levels, where the colorimetric signal was faint or absent, NIR laser irradiation produced a distinct thermal imprint on the thermosensitive paper, enhancing detection sensitivity. Together, these two modes, direct visual colorimetric readout and NIR laser‐induced thermal readout, extend the assay's dynamic range beyond the limits of naked‐eye detection. Laser parameters such as irradiation power, time, and distance to the strip were optimized using a custom benchtop NIR setup (Figure 3a and Figure S2.6a), which features a broad power range (0–3 W) and includes a 3D‐printed holder accommodating multiple strips simultaneously, streamlining the process.

Strips were placed directly into the holder after chromatographic development, without an external cassette (Figure S2.6b). Thermal signal quantification was performed via image analysis of the thermosensitive paper. The intensity and size of the thermal spot were directly proportional to the heat generated, and thus to analyte concentration. Signal intensity was measured on a grayscale scale where 255 corresponds to pure white and 0 to pure black.

These values were plotted against antigen concentrations to construct calibration curves for each biomarker. Depending on the analyte and dynamic range, the data were fitted using appropriate regression models (see Experimental Section and Supporting Information). For CEA, although a non‐linear model provided a slightly improved global fit, a linear regression was selected within the working concentration range to ensure consistent and physically meaningful estimation of analytical parameters, particularly near the lower concentration limit. The resulting calibration models showed high correlation coefficients (R2 > 0.9), confirming the robustness of the quantification approach (Figure 3b–d and Figures S2.7 and S2.8 and Tables S2.4 and S2.5). The results obtained for each gastrointestinal cancer biomarker using spiked samples are presented below, with irradiation conditions individually adapted for each analyte to balance sensitivity and avoid signal saturation. Depending on test line visibility, either direct visual inspection or NIR‐induced thermal readout quantification was applied. The optimized ThermoLFIA sensor demonstrated strong analytical performance with the selected biomarkers, achieving detection limits aligned with clinically relevant thresholds. Detailed results on sensitivity, dynamic range, and limits of detection for each target analyte are presented below. For CEA detection, irradiation was performed at 1500 mW with two different exposure times: 4 min for the 0–10 ng mL−1 range and 2 min for 25–100 ng mL−1.

Although concentrations above 25 ng mL−1 produced a test line clearly visible to the naked eye, making laser irradiation unnecessary, NIR laser exposure was still applied at these levels to assess two key aspects: (1) whether very high analyte concentrations could saturate the thermal readout, and (2) whether adjusting the laser power could modulate signal intensity on the thermosensitive paper.

These experiments demonstrate the tunability of the thermal readout under saturating conditions and confirm that signal decay remains quantifiable within the assay's dynamic range. Additionally, at high concentrations, irradiation caused visible burning on the nitrocellulose membrane, producing a mark directly on the strip itself, unlike standard cases where the signal appears only on the thermosensitive paper. In addition to the thermal readout, we also evaluated the conventional visual modality (naked‐eye detection and image‐based test‐line quantification), with full calibration curves and LoD/LoQ analysis reported in the Figure S1.7 and Table S1.4. Overall, the determination of colorimetric modality yielded less robust quantification than the thermal readout, as test‐line signals were comparatively faint and diffuse at low analyte concentrations. The system achieved a visual detection limit for CEA of approximately 1 ng mL−1 and a calculated limit of detection (LoD) of 2 ng mL−1, both comfortably below the 5 ng mL−1 diagnostic cut‐off for active disease (Figure 3b and Table S1.4). For context, the gold nanopinecone‐enhanced LFA reported by Fox et al. [28] represents a significant advancement in analytical sensitivity, achieving ultralow limits of detection for CEA in the pg mL− 1 range. In the context of clinical decision‐making, however, CEA thresholds are typically within the ng mL− 1 range (3–7 ng mL− 1) [19, 20, 21]. In this context, the present work deliberately focuses on achieving reliable detection within clinically relevant ranges rather than pushing the absolute sensitivity limits. While the approach by Fox et al. relies on hierarchical, self‐assembled nanoparticle morphologies and includes multiple purification steps that may complicate process standardization, our strategy emphasizes reproducibility, simplified workflows, and compatibility with scalable manufacturing, while maintaining clinically meaningful performance.

While the simplified AuNP‐based colorimetric LFA by Tsogka et al. [29] achieves a comparable sensitivity to our system, with a visual LoD of 0.63 ng mL−1 within the same clinically relevant range, its applicability is limited to a narrow range of biomolecules. In contrast, our platform demonstrates superior versatility for the analysis of diverse analyte type. For CA19‐9, our sensor provided a visual LoD of 1 U mL−1 (equivalent to 1 ng mL−1) and a calculated LoD of 5 U mL−1 (5 ng mL−1), both well below the diagnostic threshold of 37 U mL−1. This performance compares favorably to the MWCNT‐based LFA reported by Huang et al. [30], which had a detection limit of 30 U mL−1 in whole blood. While the latter benefits from compatibility with unprocessed blood samples, its sensitivity is close to the clinical cut‐off, potentially limiting early detection. In contrast, our ThermoLFIA platform achieves lower detection thresholds while maintaining visual readability and ambient‐stable reagents, emphasizing its practicality for early‐stage diagnostics (Figure 3c and Table S2.5). In the case of VEGF detection, our ThermoLFIA achieved a visual LoD of 0.1 ng mL−1 and an RGB‐based LoD of approximately 0.3 ng mL−1, closely matching the AuNP‐based immunoassay developed by Fan et al. [31], which also reported a detection limit of 0.1 ng mL−1 for VEGF. Importantly, our method maintains this sensitivity using simple thermosensitive paper and short, low‐power irradiation, without requiring precision optics or specialized surface chemistries as required in Fan's method. The achieved LoD falls within the diagnostic range for VEGF‐related pathologies, confirming the suitability of our approach for tumor angiogenesis monitoring (Figure 3d and Table S2.5). To further assess the sensor's performance, we introduced a response efficiency metric, defined as a normalized sensitivity parameter that describes the relative change in signal per unit of analyte concentration within the working dynamic range (see Experimental Section).

Because it is expressed as a percentage variation relative to the baseline signal, this metric provides a scale‐independent descriptor of how effectively the sensor translates concentration differences into measurable signal changes. Using this normalized sensitivity metric, the biosensor exhibited response efficiencies of approximately 5% per ng mL− 1 for CEA, 2% per ng mL− 1 for CA 19‐9, and 24% per ng mL− 1 for VEGF, indicating moderate to high responsiveness within the explored concentration intervals, with VEGF showing a particularly steep relative signal increase at low concentrations. Comparison with already commercialized ELISA platforms (Table 1) is provided to contextualize the relative normalized sensitivity of the systems within established diagnostic technologies. Because the parameter is expressed as a percentage change relative to baseline signal, it offers a scale‐independent descriptor of how efficiently concentration changes are translated into measurable signal variation. This enables qualitative comparison of concentration‐to‐signal transduction efficiency across different commercially implemented analytical formats, irrespective of the specific readout modality used. Unlike traditional ELISA assays, which require laboratory equipment and trained personnel, our biosensor is simple, fast, and suitable for point‐of‐care use. These findings highlight the sensor's ability to deliver clinically relevant LoD and LoQ values along with a robust and interpretable signal across its working range, an essential feature for quantitative, user‐friendly diagnostics in point‐of‐care settings.

As part of this study, the effect of the human plasma matrix, a conventional biological fluid for this type of analysis, on assay performance was assessed (Figures S2.9, S2.10 and S2.11). The elevated viscosity and presence of abundant plasma proteins can lead to nonspecific adsorption phenomena and promote the formation of a nanoparticle‐protein corona. This corona may hinder the binding of the functionalized nanoparticles to their specific targets while the increased viscosity could compromise the overall capillary flow through the nitrocellulose membrane.

To evaluate plasma effects, samples containing different percentages of commercial human plasma diluted in PBS (1×) were spiked with the analyte CA19‐9. PBS alone was used as a control. Results showed that increasing plasma content progressively decreased the intensity of both test and control lines. Comparable signal intensity to the PBS control was only achieved at a 10% plasma dilution. However, even at this dilution level, visible aggregation occurred at the interface between the conjugate/sample pad and the nitrocellulose membrane, representing a significant limitation for assay performance. To address this issue, pad pretreatments and the sample buffer were re‐optimized by introducing surfactants, enabling reliable operation with 50% plasma in 10% (v/v) Tween‐20 (Figure S.10). This improved configuration preserved the analytical performance, including detection and quantification limits, previously established in PBS, confirming the method's robustness and applicability in clinically relevant matrices. Using CEA, the assay achieved a LoD of 2.34 ng mL−1 compared to 2.09 ng mL−1 in PBS (Figures S2.11 and S2.12). It should be noted that although the assay maintains a low LoD in diluted plasma, the equivalent LoD in the original, undiluted plasma sample increases proportionally to the dilution factor. Nevertheless, it still falls within the clinical threshold.

ThermoOLFA for the Detection of SARS‐CoV‐2

As previously mentioned for ThermoLFIA, optimal performance of the ThermoOLFA depends on several key parameters, including nitrocellulose membrane, pad selection, capillary flow efficiency, pre‐treatment conditions and hybridization efficiency.

Hybridization efficiency was optimized by systematically adjusting the sample volume and running buffer composition. Importantly, unlike both traditional LFIA formats and the ThermoLFIA we developed, which rely on pre‐deposition of the bioconjugate on the sample pad, our current method involves taking an aliquot of the swab‐collected sample and preincubating it with the bioconjugate in a specifically formulated buffer, prior to applying the mixture to the test strip. While this approach was less common prior to the pandemic, it has become easily implementable, as users are now familiar with sample handling procedures in self‐administered rapid diagnostic tests.

Buffer and sample volumes were optimized by screening formulations that account for the strong dependence of PPRH hybridization on ionic strength and pH, selecting PBS 10× with 0.5% BSA and 1% Tween‐20 as the incubation/running buffer and 25 µL as the optimal sample volume (Figure S3.2). Thermal papers were also screened by determining their color‐change temperature on a hot plate (Figure S3.3). Finally, based on prior membrane evaluations, FF80HP was selected because it enables thermal detection at lower temperatures, supporting high sensitivity with fewer AuNPrs at the test line.

Streptavidin was deposited in the test line and BSA bioconjugate carrying the DNA probe complementary to the PPRH, in the control line. The amount of AuNPrs bioconjugate added to the strip after incubation with the sample, along with the concentration of biotinylated capture‐probe, and the amount of detection probe coupled to the nanoparticles, were optimized to yield the final test‐line configuration.

Pre‐incubation time emerged as a critical parameter when working with samples prepared in spiked buffer, where skipping this step nearly abolished sandwich complex formation at the test line.

However, when real samples collected in viral transport medium (UTM) were tested, pre‐incubation dependence was effectively eliminated, likely due to the salts and proteins present in UTM facilitating rapid complex assembly. To evaluate the analytical performance of the ThermoOLFA, the limit of detection (LoD) was assessed using spiked samples ranging from 0 to 5000 pM of synthetic target probe, prepared in either incubation/running buffer or universal transport medium (UTM, Biocomma). As an example, we include an image showing the visual limit of detection (LoD) by eye and after laser irradiation for a concentration curve of the target probe prepared by spiking the incubation/running buffer in Figure S3.4. The optimization of the laser irradiation conditions and the amount of AuNPrs to be used was carried out, using the negative spiked control (0 pM) and the highest sample concentration (5000 pM). This titration step identified the optimal nanoparticle loading that ensured true‐negative results while maintaining strong signal intensity for positive samples. Using this optimized concentration, blank membranes were then irradiated under varying power levels and exposure durations to complete the limit of detection (LoD) evaluation. Irradiation parameters were first optimized to determine the power setting that minimized background signal within an exposure window of ≤90 s

ThermoOLFA performance depends on strip materials and hybridization efficiency; therefore, key parameters (pads/membrane, running/incubation buffer, and sample volume) were optimized (Figures S3.2 and S3.3). Unlike conventional LFIA formats, the method preincubates an aliquot of the sample with the AuNPr bioconjugate in a tailored buffer prior to strip application, improving sandwich complex formation and assay robustness.

The final conditions selected were PBS 10× with 0.5% BSA and 1% Tween‐20, using 25 µL sample volume (Figure S3.2). Laser irradiation and AuNPr loading were then adjusted to minimize background while maintaining strong positive signals within ≤90 s, yielding optimal settings of 10 µg AuNPrs, 60 s exposure, and 1.2 W (Figure S3.4). As previously detailed, a focused optimization of the ThermoOLFA system was performed, evaluating buffer composition, sample preparation, probe and nanoparticle loading, laser parameters, and alternative thermosensitive papers, including formats easier to integrate (e.g., adhesive‐type). These variations did not result in significant improvements, and these results were not included in the main manuscript, in contrast to the more impactful optimization performed for ThermoLFIA.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) with backscattered‐electron (BSE) imaging revealed nanoparticle accumulation at the test line only when the target analyte was present (Figure 4b,c). In its absence, the functionalized nanoparticles did not bind to the test line (Figure 4a). SEM imaging was used as a qualitative visualization tool to confirm nanoparticle accumulation at the test line. Quantitative assessment of assay performance was instead based on the thermoplasmonic signal, which provides a more robust and reproducible measure of analyte detection. The LoD and LoQ were established using a commercially available lateral‐flow reader that quantifies the grey spots, thermal imprints generated on the thermosensitive paper placed behind the test strip, following laser irradiation of the test line placed on the nitrocellulose membrane. Plotting grayscale intensity vs. analyte concentration produced calibration curves with excellent linearity (R2 > 0.90), Figure S3.5 and Table S3.2. Under buffer conditions, the system achieved a LoD of 1.32 pM and a LoQ of 2.67 pM, with a remarkably high correlation coefficient (R2) of 0.99994, indicating excellent linearity (Table S3.2).

The sensor also exhibited a response efficiency value of 0.02% and, demonstrating strong sensitivity and accurate signal conversion under ideal conditions (spiked buffered samples). In contrast, when evaluated using samples without complex media such as universal transport medium (UTM), the standard medium for patient swab collection, the LoD improved to 0.7 pM. This LoD, obtained with 25 µL of sample, corresponds to approximately 1.0 × 107 viral RNA copies (Table S2.3). This estimate is based on Avogadro's constant: 1 pM in 25 µL equals ∼1.05 × 107 molecules, while 0.7 pM corresponds to ∼1.05 × 107 molecules. The response efficiency increased to 1.01%, indicating greater signal variability in the presence of matrix components. Both the improved LoD and the higher response efficiency observed with UTM‐prepared samples suggest that components of this medium promote hybridization between the target RNA and the AuNPr conjugates employed for thermal detection.

Considering several experimental parameters including sample pre‐incubation, reagent addition, incubation conditions, buffer composition, and bioconjugate preparation prior to strip application and assuming homogeneous hybridization, each AuNPr is functionalized with an average of ∼700 DNA‐oligonucleotide probes. In theory, this would allow up to 700 RNA molecules to hybridize per nanoparticle. In practice, steric hindrance and multivalent binding reduce this number: if only 20%–40% of probes are accessible, each AuNPr is likely to capture ∼140‐280 RNA molecules, and if individual RNA strands occupy multiple probes, the effective number of distinct RNA molecules per nanoparticle would still be on the order of tens. This multivalency markedly enhances the probability of RNA capture and its retention at the test line. Moreover, our thermal‐contrast readout can detect AuNP mass below the visual threshold, enabling quantitative detection of target molecules in conditions where conventional visual LFA readout would fail.

Collectively, these factors explain why samples with Ct values around 35 can still generate sufficient signal to surpass the molecule count implied by the 0.7‐1 pM LoD.

To confirm the assay's specificity, different oligonucleotide probes targeting influenza A/B and E. coli, at concentrations exceeding those used for the SARS‐CoV‐2 probe (up to 10 nM), were tested. Under these conditions the biosensor produced no detectable test‐line signal, indicating complete absence of cross‐reactivity and, therefore, no false‐positive results (Figure S3.6).

Currently, no commercial lateral flow assays exist for the amplification‐free detection of SARS‐CoV‐2. genetic material. Existing LFAs target viral proteins (e.g., nucleocapsid) or antibodies generated by the infection and typically offer LoDs in the range of 1–10 ng mL−1. Since the onset of the COVID‐19 pandemic, substantial progress has been made in the development of LFA platforms for nucleic acid detection, particularly through the integration of nanomaterials and molecular amplification technologies. In 2020, Wang et al. [32] reported an amplification‐free lateral flow format for the fluorescence detection of SARS‐CoV‐2 RNA based on DNA–RNA hybrid capture using an S9.6 monoclonal antibody and fluorescent nanoparticles and demonstrated high clinical performance in a hospital evaluation. In 2021, Osborn et al. introduced a CRISPR/Cas9‐based LFA capable of high‐specificity DNA detection, achieving LoD between 10–100 fM. However, the system relied on pre‐amplification of target DNA, which limits its usability for POC applications [33]. In 2022, Naikoo et al. reviewed a broad range of nanomaterial‐enhanced biosensors, including systems using gold, carbon, and quantum dots, that improved detection sensitivity for nucleic acids and antigens. Nonetheless, many of these required complex workflows or amplification steps and lacked full integration into user‐friendly LFA formats [34].

Further advancements include the work of Yu et al. in 2024, who developed a Fe3O4@Au heterodimer nanoparticle‐based LFA for the multiplex detection of influenza virus nucleic acids. This platform combined magnetic pre‐concentration and optical detection to reach a LoD of 100 fM using pre‐amplified targets [35]. A notable leap occurred in 2025 when Lee et al. introduced a plasmonic scattering‐based LFA using anisotropic gold nanostructures that achieved a LoD of 15 fM without the need for nucleic acid amplification, showcasing one of the most sensitive label‐free platforms to date [36].

This was achieved by transparentizing the nitrocellulose membrane and placing a light‐absorbing backing card beneath it, enhancing signal detection. This approach also requires the use of relatively large (∼100 nm) gold nanostructures, whose synthesis is more complex and challenging to reproduce consistently. Despite these innovations, most commercial and published systems for viral RNA detection still rely heavily on nucleic acid amplification, including RT‐LAMP, RPA, and NASBA. Platforms such as INSIGHT offer population‐scale capabilities by coupling isothermal amplification with lateral flow and sequencing, but still face challenges in terms of reagent dependency, equipment needs, and workflow complexity [37, 38, 39]. Even the most advanced duplexed RT‐RPA systems require intricate process controls, which may complicate deployment in resource‐limited environments [40]. All these results indicate that lateral flow tests developed so far for genetic material detection require a pre‐amplification step. Nevertheless, their detection limits remain relatively high compared to our system. In this context, our ThermalOLFA system presents a transformative advancement in molecular diagnostics. It enables the direct detection of unamplified viral RNA using a double‐tag hybridization mechanism transduced via gold nanoparticle‐mediated thermal signals. Unlike most current systems that depend on PCR or isothermal amplification, ThermalOLFA achieves LoD values comparable to RT‐qPCR but without the biochemical or operational complexity.

Importantly, it does not require enzymes, temperature cycling, or specialized personnel, making it truly suited for decentralized POC deployment. To our knowledge, there is currently no other commercially available or published LFA that combines amplification‐free detection, higher analytical sensitivity as has been summarized previously, and quantitative output in a single, portable system.

The sample set was retrospectively selected to include a representative range of viral loads, rather than as a strictly consecutive prospective cohort, which may introduce some degree of selection bias and may not fully reflect real‐world prevalence distributions. This methodology has been demonstrated as an effective point‐of‐care tool for detecting SARS‐CoV‐2 and other viruses without requiring nucleic acid pre‐amplification.

It provides a practical alternative to recent technologies such as CRISPR‐Cas13a, recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA), loop‐mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP), and LAMP sequencing (LAMP‐Seq) [46, 47, 48, 49]. The ThermoOLFA platform employs PPRH probes, which impose a requirement for purine‐rich target sequences. Although this constitutes a design constraint, multiple suitable regions were identified within the SARS‐CoV‐2 genome, allowing the development of several probe designs. Furthermore, compatible regions have also been found in other genomes under investigation, supporting the broader applicability of the approach despite this limitation (Table 5).

Conclusions

We have developed and successfully implemented a point‐of‐care (POC) biosensing platform that combines the simplicity and accessibility of traditional lateral flow systems with the unique capabilities of plasmonic nanoparticles, whose thermal transduction properties constitute the key enabling element. These nanostructures, synthesized with high reproducibility and rigorously characterized both physiochemically and optically, efficiently absorb near‐infrared (NIR) light and convert it into localized heat. This property allows them to function as sensitive thermal labels, enabling enhanced signal generation at the test line. This biosensor was conceived as a proof of concept platform, in which the unique property of the nanoparticles was applied to two distinct systems: ThermoLFIA for detecting protein biomarkers associated with gastrointestinal cancer, and ThermoOLFA for detecting genetic material from pathogens such as SARS‐CoV‐2. These examples illustrate the versatility of the approach, which can be readily adapted to other relevant targets. The gold nanoprisms used as thermal labels were initially synthesized manually and later scaled up through partial protocol automation, enabling reproducible production at larger volumes. Their synthesis was followed by detailed physicochemical and optical characterization, and subsequent biofunctionalization was performed with good reproducibility.

By coupling the lateral flow strip with highly sensitive thermosensitive paper, capable of producing clear and defined color changes in response to modest temperatures increases, we introduce an additional dimension of signal amplification. In this configuration, test lines that may remain undetectable under conventional visual inspection due to low analyte concentrations become thermally revealed upon NIR irradiation. To address potential safety concerns associated with irradiation, we further designed a compact and cost‐effective prototype device (Figure 6), that ensures controlled and secure NIR exposure of the test strip through protective housing and integrated optics. This device, designed in accordance with laboratory safety considerations, represents a step toward translating the platform beyond research environments into healthcare settings. Furthermore, the integration of lateral flow cassettes compatible with a compact, user‐friendly optical reader has significantly improved reproducibility, portability, and ease of use. In this system, the thermal signal is generated through controlled NIR laser irradiation of the test strip, while the optical reader enables independent and quantitative assessment of the thermal response. These combined features contribute to improved analytical performance, simplified readout, and increased robustness of the sensing platform.

Overall, the thermoplasmonic readout enables reliable detection of clinically relevant concentrations of the tested cancer biomarkers within a rapid, low‐cost lateral‐flow format. Importantly, the same platform can be extended to amplification‐free viral RNA detection in clinical SARS‐CoV‐2 samples, including low‐viral‐load cases (Ct > 35), while maintaining a simpler workflow compared to conventional laboratory‐based immunoassays or nucleic acid amplification methods (e.g., PCR‐based methods).

The evolution from ThermoLFIA to ThermoOLFA highlights the potential of this approach to support different biorecognition strategies within a common platform. In this context, the system demonstrates versatility across distinct clinical applications. For gastrointestinal cancer biomarkers, it enabled visual detection of CEA, CA 19‐9, and VEGF at clinically relevant concentrations using a rapid and accessible format. In parallel, by integrating PPRH probes and a sandwich hybridization strategy, the system was adapted for viral RNA detection, without requiring reverse transcription or amplification steps, achieving clinically relevant performance in patient samples. The platform demonstrated performance comparable to RT‐qPCR in clinical validation, including 100% sensitivity in low‐viral‐load SARS‐CoV‐2 samples (Ct > 32), addressing a critical gap in existing rapid diagnostics and enabling more effective screening strategies. What sets these platforms apart is their inherent modularity and adaptability. By combining thermosensitive paper‐based readouts, tunable gold nanoprisms, and customizable biorecognition elements, the system can be adapted to a broad range of targets, from proteins and nucleic acids to small molecules and pathogens. While further work is required to fully translate this approach into a deployable diagnostic tool, including optimization of reagent stability, device integration, large‐scale validation, and regulatory approval, the results presented here establish a robust foundation for future development.

In this context, the platform represents a step forward toward the integration of nanoplasmonic sensing concepts into practical diagnostic formats, with potential applicability in point‐of‐care and decentralized testing scenarios. Its modular design enables fast assay development for emerging threats, chronic conditions, or personalized diagnostics. Integrating nanotechnology, a smart molecular detection design, and thermal physics, the platform delivers high analytical performance in a format that is accessible, scalable, and suited for global deployment.

As such, it represents a significant step forward in the evolution of point‐of‐care diagnostics and public health monitoring.

Synthesis, Purification, Biofunctionalization, and Characterization of AuNPrs for ThermoLFIA

Synthesis of AuNPrs

ThermoLFIA AuNPrs were synthesized based on a previously reported protocol involving sequential additions of sodium thiosulfate in the presence of iodide [19, 20, 21].

Before use, all glassware was washed with aqua regia and rinsed with Milli‐Q water. In a typical synthesis, 120 mL of 0.5 mm Na2S2O3 containing 10 µL of 0.1 M KI was slowly added to 100 mL of 2 mm aqueous HAuCl4 at room temperature without stirring. After 4 min of reaction, a second identical aliquot of Na2S2O3/KI solution (120 mL) was added, followed by another 4‐min incubation under the same conditions. Finally, 100 mL of 0.5 mm Na2S2O3 (without KI) was added, and the mixture was left to react undisturbed for 1 h at room temperature.

Surface Modification (PEGylation)

The resulting reaction mixture containing the AuNPrs were functionalized with HS‐PEG5000‐COOH (molecular weight 5000 g/mol) at a mass ratio of AuNPrs:PEG of 1:1 or 1:5. Sodium borohydride (NaBH4) was added at a 1:1 molar ratio relative to PEG. The pH of the solution was adjusted to 12 using 2 M NaOH, and the mixture was subjected to ultrasonication at 60°C for 1 h to promote PEG binding. Excess reagents were removed by centrifugation at 5000 × g for 15 min, repeated over three cycles.

Purification by Agarose Gel Electrophoresis

Further removal of spherical and pseudospherical AuNPs was performed by agarose gel electrophoresis. Large‐scale purification was carried out using 12‐well agarose gels at a final agarose concentration of 2.5% (w/v) in 0.5× TBE buffer.

For each run, 10 mL of the washed PEGylated‐AuNPrs were mixed with 1.8 mL of 25% glycerol in 0.5× TBE, loaded into the wells (maximum capacity ∼1800 µL per well), and subjected to electrophoresis at 140 V for 30 min.

During electrophoresis, spherical and small anisotropic AuNPs migrated into the agarose matrix, while the larger AuNPrs remained in the wells. After the run, the retained AuNPrs were recovered directly from the wells. The samples were washed twice with Milli‐Q water to remove residual TBE, then resuspended and stored in Milli‐Q water at 4°C.

Biofunctionalization With Streptavidin

The two types of AuNPrs, functionalized with different PEG ratios (AuNPrs:PEG 1:1 and 1:5 w/w), were subjected to the same conjugation protocol. For each reaction, 500 µL of AuNPrs at 0.5 mg mL−1 were incubated in 2 mL Protein LowBind Eppendorf tubes (Hamburg, Germany) with 500 µL of an activation buffer containing 3.5 mm sulfo‐NHS and 1.5 mm EDC in 10 mm MES buffer (pH 6.0). The mixture was incubated at 37°C with gentle agitation for 30 min. To remove excess crosslinkers, the solution was centrifuged twice at 9000 × g for 9 min at 4°C, each time discarding the supernatant and resuspending the pellet in 500 µL of fresh MES buffer (10 mm, pH 6.0). This step was carried out quickly and at low temperature to minimize hydrolysis of the reactive intermediate. Next, 500 µL of streptavidin solutions at varying concentrations (8, 10, and 15 µg mL−1 in MES buffer, 10 mm, pH 6.0) were added to the activated AuNPrs. The mixtures were incubated at 37°C for 1 h. Streptavidin stock solutions were previously quantified by measuring absorbance at 280 nm using an extinction coefficient of 41.33 M− 1·cm− 1. After the conjugation step, remaining activated carboxyl groups were blocked by adding 500 µL of 2.5% BSA in MES buffer (pH 6.0) and incubating at 37°C for 2 h. The mixture was then centrifuged twice at 4500 × g for 9 min to remove unbound BSA.

Biofunctionalization With Biotinylated Antibodies

Following the selection of the optimal streptavidin concentration for surface functionalization, AuNPrs@streptavidin conjugates were prepared under those optimized conditions and used for antibody coupling. To each conjugate, 500 µL of biotinylated detection antibody at various concentrations (2, 6, or 12 µg mL−1) in 10 mm MES buffer, pH 6.0 were added. The mixtures were incubated at 37°C for 1.5 h with gentle agitation to allow specific biotin‐streptavidin binding. Unbound antibody was removed by two washing steps at 4500 × g for 9 min using 50 mm boric acid buffer (pH 8.0). The final antibody‐functionalized AuNPrs were resuspended in the same buffer and stored at 4°C until use.

Radiolabeling of Proteins and Quantification of AuNPr Conjugation Efficiency

For radiolabeling, 125 µg of streptavidin or 56 µg of biotinylated detection antibody were incubated with 50 µL of radioactive isotope K1 2 5I (1 mCi mL−1) and 0.15 mg of chloramine‐T as the oxidizing agent in a total volume of 150 µL of 10 mm MES buffer (pH 6.0) for 15 s at room temperature. The reaction was quenched by adding 150 µL of 10 mm MES buffer (pH 6.0) containing 62 µg of tyrosine and 1.5 mg of potassium iodide. The radiolabeled proteins were purified using PD MiniTrap G‐10 columns, and their concentration was determined using the Pierce Coomassie Plus Assay (Bradford), calibrated with known concentrations of streptavidin or the biotinylated antibody. Radiolabeled proteins were used for conjugation to AuNPrs (streptavidin and biotinylated antibody, respectively) following the biofunctionalizacion protocol just described. After conjugation and centrifugation, gamma emission from both the AuNPr conjugates and supernatants was measured for 60 s using a Compugamma Wallac 1282 universal gamma counter (LKB). All measurements were performed in triplicate.

Physicochemical Characterization of AuNPrs

The final concentration of the purified AuNPRs was determined by inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy. Their LSPR spectrum and absorbance at 1050 nm were recorded using UV–vis spectroscopy. Gold concentration was also estimated using the Lambert‐Beer law, applying an extinction coefficient of ε1050 = 35.86 mL·mg− 1·cm− 1 for purified samples. Nanoparticle morphology and size were evaluated by field emission scanning electron microscopy equipped with an EDX detector, operating between 0 and 30 keV. The zeta potential (ζ‐potential) of AuNPrs was measured to confirm successful PEGylation and assess changes in surface charge after functionalization. Samples were diluted in Milli‐Q water to a final gold concentration of 0.01 mg mL−1, and measurements were conducted at 25°C in triplicate.

Synthesis, Purification, Biofunctionalization, and Characterization of AuNPrs for ThermoOLFA

Synthesis of AuNPrs

ThermoOLFA AuNPrs have been prepared following a methodology slightly modified from ThermoLFIA AuNPrs. Before use, all glassware was washed with aqua regia and rinsed with Milli‐Q water. In a typical synthesis, 140 mL of 0.5 mm Na2S2O3 aqueous solution containing 10 µL of 0.1 M KI was slowly added to 200 mL of 2 mm HAuCl4‐H2O solution over 30 s. After 4 min, a second identical aliquot of Na2S2O3/KI was added, followed by another 4‐min incubation under the same conditions. Finally, 60 mL of Na2S2O3 0.5 mm (without KI) was slowly added to the mixture, and the mixture was left to react for 1 h under magnetic stirring at 19°C.

Surface Modification (PEGylation)

The resulting reaction mixture containing AuNPrs were functionalized with HS‐PEG5000‐COOH (molecular weight 5000 g/mol) at a mass ratio of AuNPrs:PEG of 1:2 (in mg). HS‐PEG5000‐COOH was diluted in 1 mL Milli‐Q water and NaBH4 (10 mg mL−1) was added at a 1:1 molar ratio relative to PEG quantity. The pH of the solution was adjusted to 12 using NaOH (2 M), and the mixture was sonicated at 60°C for 30 min. The solution was incubated overnight at room temperature under gentle agitation to promote PEG binding. The resultant mix of gold nanoprisms and spheres was centrifuged at 5500 × g for 15 min at room temperature to remove unreacted reagents and unwanted by‐products. While the supernatant was discarded, the precipitate was resuspended in the same volume of water and two further washing steps were performed with Milli‐Q water using the same conditions.

Purification by Agarose Gel Electrophoresis

Further removal of spherical and pseudospherical AuNPs was performed by agarose gel electrophoresis. An aqueous dispersion containing PEG‐derivatized AuNPrs and gold nanospheres (2 mL, 1.5 mg mL−1) was mixed with loading buffer (0.5× TBE, 5% glycerol) and loaded into the wells of a 2.5% agarose gel placed in an electrophoresis cuvette filled with 0.5× TBE buffer. Electrophoresis was performed at 120 V for 40 min. During electrophoresis, spherical and small anisotropic AuNPs migrated into the agarose matrix, while the larger AuNPrs remained in the wells. After the run, the retained AuNPrs were recovered directly from the wells. The samples were washed twice with Milli‐Q water to remove residual TBE, then resuspended and stored in Milli‐Q water at 4°C.

Biofunctionalization With PPRH Probe

Afterward, PEG‐derivatized AuNPrs were functionalized using the amine‐modified PPRH probe. Briefly, 0.25 mg of PEG‐derivatized AuNPrs were incubated with EDC 3 mm and Sulfo‐NHS 7 mm in 0.5 mL of filtered 10 mm MES buffer pH 6 for 30 min at 37°C. After that the activated AuNPrs were centrifuged 9 min at 6500 × g. After removal of the supernatant, the AuNPrs were dispersed with 0.68 nmol of amino‐modified probe diluted in 0.5 mL of filtered 50 mm MES buffer pH 6 and incubated for 1.5 h at 37°C. Then 0.5 mL of alpha‐methoxy‐omega‐amino poly‐ethylene glycol (MeO‐PEG‐NH2) (750 Da), in filtered 50 mm MES buffer pH 6 were added to the AuNPrs solution for 2 h at 37°C added to derivatize the remaining activated carboxylic groups. Finally, biofunctionalized AuNPrs were washed out of ligand excess by centrifugation three times for 9 min at 6500 × g, and then pellets were resuspended in filtered 10 mm Hepes buffer pH 7.2 containing 0.1% tween20 and 0.1% BSA.

Physicochemical Characterization of AuNPrs

Same characterization set of techniques as for ThermoLFIA AuNPrs was used. For the functionalization of AuNPrs with oligonucleotides, agarose gel electrophoresis was used to evaluate the efficiency of oligonucleotide attachment. A 0.5% agarose gel was prepared by dissolving 0.5 g of agarose in 50 mL of 0.5× TBE buffer, heating the solution in a microwave, and filtering it through a 5 µm syringe filter. The gel solution was then mixed with 4 µL of GelRed nucleic acid stain and poured into an electrophoresis chamber. The supernatants collected before and after oligonucleotide conjugation were analyzed. Samples were centrifuged to remove residual AuNPrs, and the resulting supernatants were mixed with water and 6× DNA loading dye. A volume of 10 µL from each sample was loaded into the gel wells.

Electrophoresis was run at 100 V for 35 min, and the gel was visualized using a ChemiDoc imaging system (Bio‐Rad). Differences in band intensity were used to evaluate oligonucleotide binding efficiency.

Evaluation of the ThermoLFIA and ThermoOLFA Strips

ThermoLFIA System

To evaluate the ThermoLFIA strips, each carcinogenic biomarker (CEA, CA19.9, and VEGF) was diluted to the desired concentration in PBS 1× pH 7.4. A 100 µL volume of the prepared solution was applied onto the sample/conjugate pad, allowing the liquid to migrate by capillary action. Thermal analysis was conducted by irradiating the test line region using a 1064 nm NIR laser (laser power 1.5 W, time exposure 2 or 4 min). Before irradiation, the strips were dried either at 37°C for 15 min or at room temperature for 45 min to ensure consistent conditions.

ThermoOLFA System

Functionalized AuNPrs were premixed with the sample and other biotinylated oligonucleotide prior to being added to the sample pad. For LoD calculation, 25 µL of a spiked sample containing increasing concentrations of the DNA target (ranging from 0 to 5000 pM) was mixed with 10 µg of biofunctionalized AuNPrs, 0.0638 nmol (26.6 µL) of biotinylated reporter probe, and 23.4 µL of running buffer. The mixture was incubated for 15 min at room temperature and subsequently, 100 µL was applied to the sample pad. Within 5 min of sample application, the test (TL) and control (CL) lines on the nitrocellulose membrane developed visibly in high concentration positive cases, while no test line appeared for low concentration positive and negative samples. After a 15‐min chromatography step, the strips were dried at 37°C for 15 min and developed using a 1064 nm NIR laser (Laser power 1.2 W and time exposure 60 s).

For real sample testing, 25 µL of clinical SARS‐CoV‐2 sample (either positive or negative, in inactivated UTM, resulting in an effective four‐fold dilution of the sample) was mixed with 10 µg of AuNPrs functionalized with the amine‐modified detection PPRH probe, 0.0638 nmol of biotinylated reporter probe and 23.4 µL of PBS 10 X pH7.4 0.5% BSA 1% Tween 20.

A total of 100 µL of the mixture was loaded into the strip cassette. After a 15‐min lateral flow development run, the strip was directly irradiated with the NIR laser (1064 nm, 300 mW, 1 min). Positive samples produced a visible dark brown spot on the thermosensitive paper, while negative samples did not yield any thermal signal.

Statistical Analysis of the Results Obtained With ThermoLFIA and ThermoOLFA Biosensors

To evaluate the analytical performance of the gastric cancer biomarkers, raw signals were extracted using ImageJ and summarized as mean ± SD for each nominal concentration. Unless otherwise specified, each concentration level was measured in duplicate (n = 2 technical replicates). No inter‐group hypothesis testing (e.g., t‐tests or ANOVA) was performed, as the study focused on calibration and analytical performance. Calibration curves were obtained by linear regression for CEA and by a Hill (sigmoidal) model for CA19‐9 and VEGF. In the case of CEA, the response followed an exponential‐like trend across the full concentration range and provided a slightly improved global fit. However, for analytical parameter extraction, a linear regression was applied over the low‐concentration interval (0–10 ng mL− 1), where the response was approximately linear. This approach was selected to ensure consistent and IUPAC‐compliant estimation of the limit of detection (LOD) and limit of quantification (LOQ), avoiding artefacts that can arise when extrapolating non‐linear models near the blank level.

Clinical validation data were analyzed using OriginPro 2024 (OriginLab Corporation). A total of 104 upper‐respiratory specimens were evaluated in parallel using the ThermoOLFA platform and standard RT‐qPCR, which served as the reference method. Each sample was classified as “Positive” or “Negative” by both assays, and the results were used to compute diagnostic performance metrics. Binary outcomes were organized into a confusion matrix comprising true positives (TP), false negatives (FN), false positives (FP), and true negatives (TN). The following parameters were calculated according to standard definitions: Sensitivity=TPTP+FN Specificity=TNTN+FP Accuracy=TP+TNN PositivePredictiveValuePPV=TPTP+FP NegativePredictiveValueNPV=TNTN+FN

Cycle‐threshold (Ct) values of PCR‐positive samples (n = 55) ranged from 14.14 to 35.39, with a mean of 27.4 ± 6.0. For ThermoOLFA‐positive samples (n = 54), descriptive statistics (mean, median, SD, minimum, maximum, skewness, and kurtosis) were generated using the Statistics → Descriptive Statistics → Statistics on Columns function in OriginPro. The Shapiro‐Wilk test (W = 0.9275, p = 0.0029; α = 0.05) indicated a significant deviation from normality due to a slight skew toward higher Ct values, corresponding to low viral loads.

To further assess the clinical sensitivity across viral‐load ranges, a one‐sample t‐test (t(53) = −5.6934, p = 5.5 × 10− 7) was conducted to determine whether the mean Ct differed from 32, the approximate threshold between moderate and low viral loads. The result confirmed that the mean Ct of ThermoOLFA‐positive samples was significantly below 32, while all samples with Ct > 32 were nonetheless correctly identified as positive.

PCR‐positive specimens were subsequently stratified into three Ct‐based categories: ≤25 (high viral load), 26–32 (moderate), and >32 (low). The Ct distributions and detection rates for each category were visualized using histograms and bar charts generated in OriginPro with custom bin edges [10, 15, 22, 27, 32, 37, 42].

All descriptive and inferential analyses, plots, and statistical evaluations were performed in OriginPro 2024. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, and results are presented as mean ± SD unless otherwise stated.

Diagnostic Performance of ThermoOLFA

The diagnostic performance of the ThermoOLFA sensor was assessed using 104 upper‐respiratory clinical specimens tested in parallel by RT‐qPCR.

Sensor outputs (positive/negative) were compared against RT‐qPCR Ct values to build a confusion matrix and derive sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and overall accuracy. For the 54 sensor‐positive samples, descriptive statistics of Ct values (mean, median, standard deviation, skewness and kurtosis) were calculated, and the Shapiro–Wilk test (W = 0.9275, p = 0.0029) confirmed non‐normality, as already mentioned and expected for clinical datasets. A one‐sample t‐test against a reference Ct of 32 cycles (t (53) = −5.6934, p = 5.54 × 10− 7) demonstrated a significantly lower mean Ct, indicating high sensitivity at low viral loads. Stratified sensitivity analyses were also performed for Ct ranges ≤25, 25–32 and >32. The individual Ct values for all samples, are provided in the Table S3.3. In addition, the relationship between Ct values and thermal signal intensity was explored. Although a general trend of decreasing signal with increasing Ct was observed, no strict one‐to‐one correlation was found. This is consistent with the differences in detection principles (RT‐qPCR amplification vs. direct thermoplasmonic detection), as well as potential variability introduced during sample collection, storage, and processing (e.g., partial RNA degradation or matrix effects). All statistical analyses and data visualizations (histograms and bar charts) were carried out in OriginPro 2024 (OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA).
