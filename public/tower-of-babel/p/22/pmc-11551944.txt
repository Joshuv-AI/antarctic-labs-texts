Successful Mitral Valve Replacement in a 13-Year-Old With Active Carditis and Acute Severe Mitral Regurgitation
Authors: Naimisha Yenduri, Pranoti Toshniwal, Navaneetha Sasikumar, Raman Krishna Kumar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 13-year-old girl from the rural northeastern part of India presented with progressively worsening dyspnea on exertion. She was diagnosed with active carditis, severe acute mitral regurgitation, and heart failure. Intensive medical management was ineffective in controlling her heart failure. Notwithstanding active carditis, the patient underwent successful mitral valve replacement with an excellent outcome.Key Words: acute heart failure, mitral valve, rheumatic heart disease, valve repair, valve replacement

History of Presentation

A 13-year-old girl, hailing from the rural northeastern part of India, who was previously asymptomatic, presented with progressively worsening dyspnea on exertion; she had NYHA functional class II symptoms, which symptoms rapidly worsened to NYHA functional class III with the onset of orthopnea over 2 months’ duration. There was a history of high-grade, intermittent fever 3 months before the onset of symptoms, which lasted for 3 weeks and was not associated with any joint pain, swelling, rash, or sore throat. There was no other history of significant medical or surgical illness.

On admission, the patient was afebrile. She had a heart rate of 120 beats/min, bounding pulses, respiratory rate of 40 breaths/min, a raised jugular venous pressure, and a pulse oximetry saturation of 94% on oxygen. On cardiovascular examination, S1 was soft, S2 was normally split, and S3 gallop was present; there was pan systolic murmur at the apex, radiating to the axilla, and mid-diastolic murmur at the apex. Fine basal rales were present in the chest. Liver was palpable 4 cm under the right subcostal margin.

Differential Diagnosis

Acute rheumatic fever and infective endocarditis were considered differential diagnoses.

Investigations and Management

At the first outpatient clinic presentation, results of the patient’s laboratory investigations showed normal acute inflammatory markers. Anti-streptolysin O (ASO) titers were found to be elevated. Blood cultures showed no growth. Echocardiography revealed severe mitral regurgitation (MR) with thickened leaflets and flail anterior mitral leaflet (AML) due to chordal rupture (A2 > A3 >A1), with a significantly dilated left ventricle (Table 1) but preserved function (Figures 1 and 2).

The patient was started on intensive decongestants (furosemide infusion followed by intermittent boluses and then changed to oral furosemide) and heart failure medications (enalapril, spironolactone, and digoxin), on which she initially improved with the resolution of respiratory distress. She fulfilled only one major criterion (carditis) from the modified Jones criteria at this point. Given the patient’s characteristic history and presentation, with classic mitral valve involvement and elevated ASO titers, she received one dose of benzathine penicillin injection 1.2 million units given intramuscularly.

The plan was for an early mitral valve repair. She presented to the emergency department and was admitted 2 days later with high-grade fever and worsening breathing difficulty, again with no joint symptoms. On physical examination, she was tachycardic with a heart rate of 142 beats/min with bounding pulses, tachypneic with a respiratory rate of 54 breaths/min with intercostal retractions, and a drop in oxygen saturation to 88%. Auscultation revealed bilateral rales throughout the lung fields. She had an elevated jugular venous pressure, marked tender hepatomegaly, and bilateral pedal edema. No other localizing features were identified. Laboratory investigations revealed markedly elevated inflammatory markers (erythrocyte sedimentation rate and C-reactive protein) (Figures 3 and 4). Total counts were essentially in the same range with neutrophilic predominance. Echocardiography showed the same features as mentioned previously; no vegetation was noted. Chest radiograph findings were consistent with acute pulmonary edema (Figure 5). During this admission, the patient fulfilled one major criterion (carditis) and two minor criteria (fever and elevated inflammatory markers), with supportive evidence of recent streptococcal infection (raised ASO titers), of the modified Jones criteria 2015 update. She was diagnosed as having rheumatic fever with severe carditis.

The patient was started on pulse therapy with steroids, which was given for 5 days, then tapered to oral steroids at 2 mg/kg/day for a total of 14 days. In the subsequent hospital course, she continued to have tachycardia and a raised jugular venous pressure, with persistent tachypnea requiring noninvasive ventilatory support. Antibiotics were discontinued after repeated negative culture findings. Her renal function was rapidly declining, despite meticulous fluid management; which was attributed to cardiorenal syndrome and drug-induced insult. Her hemodynamics were dictated by mechanical issues of ruptured chordae and flail AML rather than the inflamed leaflets. Despite all measures, heart failure did not show any improvement. The patient was restricted to bed, and treatment was continued with diuretics (furosemide infusion, spironolactone, and metolazone), milrinone infusion, and steroids.

Considering the patient’s deteriorating clinical state due to severe, rapidly progressive, and refractory heart failure resulting from acute severe MR with flail AML, surgery was considered. The decision to operate in the face of advanced heart failure with renal insufficiency with active carditis was a challenging one, made after a thorough multi-disciplinary discussion with the cardiovascular surgeons and cardiac anesthesia team. Intraoperative findings revealed severe slimy vascular intrapericardial adhesions occupying the entire pericardial cavity, severe cardiomegaly, mild left atrial enlargement, severe MR with severe valvar disease- torn diseased, and flail AML (Video 1). The mitral valve leaflet tissue was too friable to permit a durable valve repair. She underwent mitral valve replacement with a 27-29 mm ON-X mitral prosthetic valve (ON-X Life Technologies).

In the postoperative period, the patient was continued on mechanical ventilatory support and gradually weaned off after 5 days to noninvasive ventilation. Echocardiography showed a well-functioning prosthetic mitral valve with no valvular/paravalvular leak and moderate left ventricular dysfunction that gradually improved over the next few days. Renal function initially deteriorated. Sustained low efficiency dialysis/ ultrafiltration was planned for in case of further deterioration. However, renal function gradually improved with conservative management. On postoperative day 4, the patient had a seizure, possibly attributed to uremic encephalopathy. Neurologic imaging could not be performed due to financial constraints. She was started on fosphenytoin, it was later changed to oral phenytoin; there were no further episodes of seizures. Steroids were tapered by 5 mg every week. Anticoagulation was started with warfarin for the On-X Mitral Prosthesis and titrated according to the international normalized ratio.

The other postoperative challenge faced in this case was nutritional rehabilitation. The patient’s prolonged hospital and ICU stay with a constant catabolic state led to a state of cachexia with profound emaciation and loss of muscle mass. She was started on a high-calorie and high-protein diet, multivitamin and mineral supplements were added as well. She was intolerant to the high osmolarity of the diet and developed loose stools, necessitating further dietary modifications. Injectable penicillin could no longer be given as prophylaxis for rheumatic heart disease (RHD) due to low muscle mass, and she was started on oral erythromycin given twice a day. She developed a bed sore, which was addressed with daily dressing and wound care. The uneducated background of the patient’s parents also made it very important for multiple counseling sessions about the condition and the need for long-term follow-up of the patient in liaison with local health services.

Outcome and Follow-up

Thirty days later, the patient was discharged with significant improvement in her cardiac status and ventricular function. She remains well and active 1 year after the surgery.

Discussion

Indolent carditis has been well described in high endemic regions. The patient may present with features of CHF, murmur, or cardiomegaly without any evidence of active carditis. A previous study from India, which included 126 children with acute rheumatic carditis (mostly from lower socioeconomic status), found that worsening of NYHA functional class (dyspnea) was the most common presenting feature seen in 48% of patients; raised erythrocyte sedimentation rate and elevated ASO were present in only 37% of children.1 They have found that the Jones criteria were most often not satisfied by patients being treated for acute rheumatic carditis. A recent unexplained worsening of dyspnea, young age, characteristic echocardiographic features, and significant MR, as was the case in the current child, should arouse the suspicion of rheumatic etiology and indolent carditis.

Decompensation in this patient was initially attributed to inflammatory pathology rather than mechanical factors causing valvular insufficiency.2 However, the principal cause of severe MR and cardiac failure in our case seems to be the mechanical factors resulting from ruptured chordae and flail AML, rather than the rheumatic inflammatory process. The former dictated the unusual course whereby the response to anti-inflammatory therapy was inadequate to improve the clinical status. Marcus et al2 attempted to elucidate the mechanism of severe MR in 73 patients with active rheumatic carditis; they found that the echocardiographic features correlated with surgical findings, with mitral valve prolapse seen in 94%, annular dilatation in 96%, and chordal elongation in 70%. The pathogenesis of MR in acute rheumatic fever starts with marked mitral annular dilatation, which predisposes some patients to chordal elongation and rupture. Heart failure resulting from or associated with active rheumatic carditis can generally be managed medically. In some situations, like the one we encountered, surgical management of the valve lesion may be necessary as a life-saving measure.3 Harris et al4 followed up 28 children with rheumatic mitral insufficiency; there were 8 fatalities, with deaths occurring between 11.5 and 15.5 years of age. Survival outcomes were better with mitral valve surgery, with operative mortality of only 1% in a study involving 106 children from India.5

The choice between a mechanical or bioprosthetic valve remains controversial. This was addressed in a study by Park et al,6 which included 24,347 patients who underwent aortic or mitral valve replacement; the authors compared the long-term outcomes associated with mechanical and bioprostheses according to the recipient’s age. They found that mechanical prosthesis was associated with a survival benefit over bioprosthesis, and the benefit was maintained in patients up to age 65 years for replacements in the aortic position and age 70 years for replacements in the mitral position. However, the pros and cons of mitral valve replacement with a bioprosthetic valve vs mechanical valve in the current 13-year-old patient was extensively discussed. Access to tertiary care for a redo open heart surgery, which is inevitable in the case of a bioprosthetic valve, was beyond the family’s socioeconomic and geographic background. Considering the accelerated rate of bioprosthetic valve degeneration in children, the odds that it would last till she conceives, likely a decade later, were also considered low.

Conclusions

Children with severe MR from active carditis and mechanical factors like chordal rupture can have an extremely poor prognosis even with aggressive medical management. Surgical correction of valvular insufficiency may be required, even in the presence of active carditis before multiorgan failure and death from CCF occurs. The decision to replace the mitral valve in our patient was dictated by the anatomy, considering the friable tissue and the high likelihood of failure of the repair in the immediate postoperative period itself. This case highlights the need for a high index of suspicion for indolent carditis in acute presentations and consideration of early mitral valve surgery in active rheumatic carditis with heart failure refractory to medical management.
