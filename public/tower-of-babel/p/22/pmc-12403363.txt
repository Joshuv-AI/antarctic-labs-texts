Economic status of Tigrayan women in the post-war period: status changes and challenges in 2024
Authors: Tslalmuz Haileslasie Desta, Mengistu Welday Gebremichael, Alemseged Gerezgiher Hailu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWar, derived from Old English “Werra,” signifies chaos, destruction, and antagonism. Origins of war are debated, with some arguing it is an inherent part of human nature and others a recent invention due to increased complexity in societies. War has profound consequences for individuals and communities, including emotional trauma, displacement, and economic disruption. Women in war zones often experience the devastating consequence of weapons and militarization on their families. Conflict in Africa disproportionately affects women, reversing developmental gains and deepening inequalities. The war significantly reduced women assets like beds, tables, and electronics, causing significant economic hardship and material losses. Additionally, solar units, generators, ironing presses, and oxen were significantly reduced, indicating the loss of essential agricultural assets. The majority of household heads were male (63.6%), with 39.7% being illiterate. Housing conditions remained stable before and after the war, with 78.8% having access to a bed. Food insecurity was low before the war, but worsened during the war. Post-war, 55.2% still faced food insecurity and 57.1% struggled to access healthy food. The losses highlight the severe economic and social disruption caused by the war, with women living conditions deteriorating as a result of displacement and destruction. Introduction

War, derived from Old English “Werra,” signifies chaos, destruction, and antagonism, encompassing mental, physical, social, and other forces, often referring to violent conflicts between nations [1].

Otterbein (2004) suggests warfare originated from two distinct paths: hunting large game and agricultural origins [2]. Hunting evolved as populations grew, leading to sophisticated weapons and warfare. Agriculture, focused on plant cultivation, did not engage until centralized political systems and city-states emerged, suggesting warfare was a consequence of complex political structures [3].

The origins of war are debated among scholars, with some arguing it is an inherent part of human nature and others arguing it is a recent invention due to increased complexity in human societies. The conflicting evidence highlights the complexity of understanding war’s evolution.

War has profound consequences for individuals and communities, including emotional trauma, displacement, and economic disruption. Women in war zones often experience the devastating consequence of weapons and militarization on their families. Conflict also leads to property rights, education, and infrastructure destruction, with some families selling children for survival.

War in Tigray has intensified women’s suffering, with numerous killed, raped, and displaced, and documented by human rights organizations like Human Rights Watch and Amnesty International. The region’s communication and journalist access issues hinder verification of survivors’ accounts. As more journalists gain access, disturbing stories of sexual violence emerge, with medical professionals reporting increased cases of women being drugged, captive, and subjected to gang rape.

This research project investigates the consequence of war on women’s economic, social, and political status in Tigray, Ethiopia. Using a cross-sectional survey and mixed data analysis, it aims to enhance understanding, address knowledge gaps, and provide insights.

The Tigray war has significantly affected women’s lives, exacerbating existing inequalities and creating new vulnerabilities, but there is a critical gap in understanding its specific effects.

Studies consistently demonstrate the heightened risks of violence against women during conflict, including sexual violence, which is often used as a weapon of war [4–7]. Moreover, this includes the deliberate spread of HIV as a secondary weapon [8].

War-induced disruptions in healthcare, education, and economic systems disproportionately affect women and children, with pre-existing stigmas and traditional laws complicating access to resources and political participation [9].

Numerous human rights abuses, including indiscriminate bombing of residential areas and acts of sexual violence, have been documented during the Tigray conflict, with Tigrayan women being disproportionately affected [10, 11].

This study explores the consequences of the recent conflict in Tigray, Ethiopia, on women’s economic, social, and political status. The war, which began in November 2020 and lasted until a ceasefire agreement was signed in November 2022, severely affected all aspects of life in the region. By situating the study in the post-war period, it aims to inform policy interventions that support women’s recovery and empowerment, addressing their unique needs and vulnerabilities in the aftermath of the conflict.

This study specifically focuses on the Tigray region of Northern Ethiopia, which endured the most sustained and severe impact of the armed conflict between the federal government and regional forces. Although conflict and instability also affected neighboring regions such as Amhara and Wollo, the scope of this study was intentionally limited to Tigray in order to provide an in-depth analysis of women’s post-war economic, social, and political status in one of the most heavily affected areas. While health was not stated as a primary objective, several consequences assessed—such as food insecurity, economic deprivation, and access to basic services—are recognized within global public health frameworks as indirect health impacts of conflict.

Methods

Study area and period

The study was conducted in the Tigray Region, the northernmost regional state of Ethiopia, covering 50,079 km2 with Mekelle as its capital. Tigray is bordered by Eritrea, Sudan, the Afar Region, and the Amhara Region, and is home to the Tigrigna, Irob, and Kunama people. The projected population for 2023 was 5,838,000, with 67.8% living in rural areas, reflecting rapid urbanization. The region is administratively divided into seven zones, including Mekelle (special zone), and underwent decentralization reforms between 2018 and 2020, increasing rural Woredas from 35 to 60 and urban Woredas from 12 to 35 [12]. This restructuring aimed to enhance service accessibility as smaller towns expanded their economic and administrative functions. The working language is Tigrigna, though Amharic is widely spoken in urban areas.

This study adopted the pragmatist research philosophy, integrating both quantitative and qualitative methods to examine the war’s consequence on Tigrayan women. Pragmatism valued practical solutions, allowing flexibility in combining positivist and interpretivist approaches based on the research question [13]. Unlike rigid philosophies, it enabled dynamic data analysis, ensuring methodological rigor and meaningful findings. This approach strengthened trustworthiness and enhanced the study’s ability to address real-world problems effectively.

The data collection was conducted in the aftermath of the conflict (August 30 to September 30, 2024), during the tail end of the rainy season, which coincides with the annual ‘hungry season’—a time of food scarcity prior to harvest. Additionally, disrupted road infrastructure significantly constrained the movement of goods and services, which further compounded economic challenges.

Study design

A cross-sectional survey was employed.

Data collection and sources

This study employed a mixed methods approach, which involves collecting and analyzing both qualitative and quantitative data. This approach is advantageous as it allows for a more comprehensive understanding of the research problem by utilizing different data collection and analysis methods. By combining these methods, the study aims to gain a deeper and more understanding of the research topic.

Data collection techniques

The study utilized structured questionnaires, random and nonrandom sampling methods, and qualitative data from key informants interviews, in depth interview and focus group discussions.

In-depth interviews were conducted with thirty-six and three key informants from the Tigray Women Affairs Bureau and Tigray Government Administration to gain insights into the consequence of war on women. Focus group discussions with 63 participants from various associations and sectors across nine Woredas were also held. Supplementary data, including site visit notes, memos, and photos, were collected to ensure the accuracy and reliability of the findings through triangulation.

Source population

The study's general population consists of rural and urban women’s women affected by the war in the nine selected Woredas between 2020 and 2023, as well as sectors operating in the area related to women’s activities.

Sampling technique and sample size

This study employed both probability and non-probability sampling methods. Lottery sampling was used as a probability technique, while purposive sampling was applied as a non-probability method. A multi-stage sampling procedure was implemented to enhance the representativeness of the sample and improve the generalizability of the findings.

The sample size was determined using Cochran’s formula (1977) to ensure a representative proportion of the population. Assuming maximum variability (p = 0.5), a 95% confidence level, and a ± 5% margin of error, the initial sample size was calculated as 385. To account for potential non-responses and other contingencies, an additional 10% was added, increasing the sample size to 424. Considering a design effect (DF) of 2, the final adjusted sample size was 848. The sample size for each Tabia was then allocated proportionally based on the number of women in each Tabia, using an appropriate allocation formula (Fig. 1).

Data processing

The collected data underwent several processing steps: quality checks were conducted in real-time during the survey, followed by data cleaning to correct errors and inconsistencies. Statistical techniques were applied during data entry to minimize errors, and statistical software was used for assistance. Once cleaned and entered, the data was analyzed using SPSS to perform statistical tests and generate descriptive statistics to address the research questions. These procedures ensured the data’s accuracy, reliability, and readiness for analysis.

Data analysis

After data collection, the data was cleaned, organized, and analyzed using a concurrent mixed approach. Quantitative analysis identified patterns through statistical methods, while qualitative analysis focused on interpreting text from interviews and focus groups. This combined approach provided a comprehensive understanding of the research topic.

Pilot test

A pilot test with 85 respondents was conducted to identify issues in the questionnaire, refine it, and improve its clarity, ensuring better data quality for the final survey.

Operational definition

Post-war period

Refers to the time following the cessation of active hostilities between the federal government of Ethiopia and the Tigray regional forces, marked by the signing of the Pretoria Peace Agreement in November 2022. Specifically, for the purposes of this research, the post-war period is defined as the timeframe from January 2023 onward, during which relative peace was restored and partial humanitarian access resumed.

Ethical considerations

The researcher followed ethical principles by obtaining permission from Mekelle University’s Ethical Review Board, securing written informed consent from participants, ensuring confidentiality of collected data, and treating participants with respect. Participants were informed about the study’s purpose, their rights, and the voluntary nature of participation, with assurances that their responses would remain confidential. and well-being of the participants and to ensure that the study is conducted in an ethical and responsible manner. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Result

The results presented here are based on data collected from 848 study participants, achieving a 100% response rate, providing a comprehensive overview of the key factors under investigation in the study.

The gender distribution of heads shows that males constitute the majority, making up 63.6%, while females account for 36.4%. Regarding educational status, a significant portion of women heads are illiterate (39.7%), followed by those with a primary school education (29.7%) and secondary school education (20.8%). Only a small fraction has attained a college education or higher (9.8%), highlighting varying levels of formal education within the population.

In terms of occupation the women were predominantly housewives (31.4%), followed by farmers (21.1%) and those engaged in self-employment or business (16.6%). Other roles include daily laborers (14.5%), government employees (8.7%), and private employees (4.4%), while students (2.6%) and the unemployed (0.7%) represent smaller groups. Furthermore, urban residency is more common, with 61.4% of women heads living in urban areas compared to 38.6% in rural settings.

The data provided was checked for population distribution and found to be normally distributed. The mean age of participants was 37.9 ± 9.9 years, indicating a generally middle-aged population with some variation in age. Women’s age at first marriage averaged 19 ± 3.2 years, while their spouses were older, with an average age of 25.5 ± 4.5 years at first marriage. This suggests a notable age gap between women and their spouses at the time of marriage.

On average, women had lived in the area for 19.7 ± 22 years, reflecting a mix of long-term residents and recent arrivals. The average women family size was 4.8 ± 1.5 members, with a small number of infants under one year (0.37 ± 0.5) per women. Most women members were aged 1–64 years, with an average of 4.4 ± 1.5 individuals in this age range, indicating a predominance of working-age or dependent members within women (Table 1).

Before the war, food insecurity was relatively low, with most individuals (94.7%) not worried about having enough food due to financial constraints. A small proportion (5.3%) expressed concerns about food scarcity. Similarly, before the war, the majority (87.3%) had access to healthy and nutritious food, while only 12.7% faced challenges in this regard. Most individuals also ate a variety of foods, with 93.9% not restricted to a limited range of foods due to resource limitations, and only 2.7% skipped meals for the same reason.

During the war, food insecurity dramatically increased, with 91.5% of individuals worried about not having enough food due to a lack of resources, and a significant shift occurred in eating habits. Most people (89.2%) reported eating fewer types of food because of resource scarcity, and 82.7% had to skip meals due to insufficient funds or resources. These statistics highlight the severe consequence the war had on food availability and security, with a notable increase in deprivation. The situation persisted after the war, as a significant proportion (55.2%) still worried about food availability, and many (57.1%) continued to struggle to access healthy food (Table 3).

Before the war, the primary sources of income for individuals were diverse, with the largest group relying on farming or agricultural production (32.4%) and informal employment (29.2%). Smaller groups earned income through formal employment (9.8%), small businesses (22.4%), and remittances from family members abroad (1.7%). However, since the war, the income situation worsened, as 64.2% of individuals reported a decrease in income, while only 25% experienced an increase. The economic situation was further exacerbated by a significant drop in savings and access to credit, as 81.4% of individuals reported having no personal savings or bank accounts after the war, compared to 52.2% before the war. Moreover, 97.6% of individuals reported having no access to credit or loans after the war, highlighting the severe financial limitations of post-conflict Tigray. Reasons for decrease of income includes loss of employment or work opportunities, agricultural income, lack of access to markets or customers and displacement.

In addition to financial hardships, many women faced physical and property damages. A significant portion of families (40.8%) reported property damage, and 24.2% of individuals experienced damage to their animals. Damage to crops also affected 13.4% of respondents, further undermining food security and income sources. The war also had a human cost, with 15.3% of respondents facing illness, injury, or death of a family member, though sexual violence was rare, with only 0.2% of individuals reporting such incidents. Despite these hardships, 41.2% of individuals did receive some form of support since the war ended, offering a slight relief to the affected populations. Types of supports obtained includes financial support (e.g., cash transfers, grants), loans (e.g., microloans, personal loans), food items and non-food items (Table 5) (Fig. 2).

Paired t-test

The paired t-test results show statistically significant changes in various women assets before and after the war. The number of beds before and after the war exhibited a significant reduction with a mean difference of 0.537 (95% CI: 0.305–0.405), t-value of 13.885, and p-value of 0.000, reflecting the severe consequence of the war on women’s resources. Similarly, the number of tables/chairs showed a significant decline with a mean difference of 0.585 (95% CI: 1.367–1.806), t-value of 14.185, and p-value of 0.000, indicating a loss of essential women items.

The number of refrigerators before and after the war also experienced a notable decrease, with a mean difference of 0.407 (95% CI: 0.099–0.187), t-value of 6.377, and p-value of 0.000. This reflects the significant economic hardship and material losses faced by women during the conflict. Similarly, the number of sofas decreased significantly with a mean difference of 0.331 (95% CI: 0.261–0.683), t-value of 4.413, and p-value of 0.000, underscoring the broader economic challenges.

The number of televisions before and after the war showed a significant reduction, with a mean difference of 0.435 (95% CI: 0.127–0.201), t-value of 8.787, and p-value of 0.000, highlighting the loss of important women entertainment resources. Likewise, radios/tapes experienced a substantial decrease with a mean difference of 0.983 (95% CI: 0.501–0.685), t-value of 12.710, and p-value of 0.000, further illustrating the consequence on communication and media access during the war.

Cell phones also saw a significant reduction, with a mean difference of 0.554 (95% CI: 0.370–0.485), t-value of 14.707, and p-value of 0.000, reflecting the loss of critical communication tools. The number of cars declined significantly with a mean difference of 0.606 (95% CI: 0.045–0.705), t-value of 2.423, and p-value of 0.029, indicating the consequence of the war on personal transportation.

The number of solar units before and after the war also saw a significant reduction with a mean difference of 0.934 (95% CI: 0.766–1.234), t-value of 8.509, and p-value of 0.000, illustrating the disruption of energy resources. In contrast, the number of generators showed no statistically significant change, with a p-value of 0.347, indicating no substantial negative consequence of the war on this asset.

The number of ironing presses showed a borderline significant reduction with a mean difference of 0.294 (95% CI: −0.004 to 0.231), t-value of 1.949, and p-value of 0.058, suggesting a small decline in this women asset. The number of oxen, however, experienced a significant decrease with a mean difference of 0.677 (95% CI: 0.462–0.668), t-value of 10.812, and p-value of 0.000, reflecting the loss of essential agricultural assets.

Cattle also faced a significant reduction, with a mean difference of 0.590 (95% CI: 0.515–0.783), t-value of 9.550, and p-value of 0.000, highlighting the loss of livestock vital to agricultural and economic activities. Similarly, the number of sheep decreased significantly with a mean difference of 0.865 (95% CI: 3.179–4.790), t-value of 9.784, and p-value of 0.000, further illustrating the negative effects of the war on livestock ownership.

Goats experienced a significant reduction with a mean difference of 0.538 (95% CI: 1.970–3.481), t-value of 7.118, and p-value of 0.000, underscoring the war’s detrimental effect on livestock holdings. The number of camels showed no significant change, with a p-value of 0.083, suggesting a smaller or less consequence effect on this asset.

Donkeys, however, showed a significant decline with a mean difference of 0.651 (95% CI: 0.319–0.539), t-value of 7.700, and p-value of 0.000, reflecting the broader losses in women resources. Finally, the number of chickens saw a significant increase after the war, with a mean difference of 0.954 (95% CI: 5.576–7.188), t-value of 15.589, and p-value of 0.000, possibly indicating changes in agricultural practices or shifts in livestock management during or after the conflict.

The Total Livestock Unit (TLU) data before and after the war were analyzed for a sample of participants. For the TLU before the war, the range was 8.29, with values ranging from a minimum of 2.71 to a maximum of 11.00. The mean TLU before the war was 5.6353, with a standard deviation of 1.77015, indicating moderate variability in the sample. The skewness value for TLU before the war was 0.596, suggesting a slight positive skew in the distribution, with values slightly concentrated around the lower end.

For the TLU after the war, the range was 8.28, with values ranging from a minimum of 0.56 to a maximum of 8.84. The mean TLU after the war was 3.6213, with a standard deviation of 2.11572, showing higher variability in the data compared to TLU before the war. The skewness for TLU after the war was 0.980, indicating a stronger positive skew, meaning the distribution was more concentrated toward the lower end of the range. This suggests that after the war, the TLU values were more clustered around the lower range compared to before the war (Table 6).

Discussion

This study was aimed to assess economic Status of Tigrayan Women in the Post-War Period: Status Changes and Challenges The study shows a significant change in Total Livestock Unit (TLU) values before and after the war, with a mean of 5.6 before the war and 3.6 after. This suggests a wider spread in livestock ownership, possibly due to conflict-related losses. The higher variability after the war may also indicate uneven impact.

Historically, the economic foundation of Tigray has depended significantly on the transshipment of food and livestock (particularly oxen) from the southern highlands toward export hubs such as Massawa and Assab. This strategy, rather than reliance on local food production, constituted the core of household viability and regional profit. The war has profoundly disrupted this economic model by severing trade routes, destroying infrastructure, and displacing communities. This disruption is reflected in the heightened poverty levels and reduced food security observed in the current study. Additionally, the conflict altered gender roles in critical ways. With many men engaged in conflict-related duties, women assumed expanded roles in agriculture, food processing, and informal trade. These shifts have increased the burden on women while simultaneously making them pivotal actors in community survival and recovery. The psychological trauma and economic shocks also continue to shape women’s capacity to resume normal livelihoods, and this study captures an important snapshot of that transitional period.

According to this study, there has been a notable decline in the number of beds, tables and chairs, and sofas, which suggests that fundamental family comfort items have been lost, most likely as a result of home destruction and evacuation. In areas afflicted by conflict, such as South Sudan and the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), comparable losses were noted. Conflict-displaced women in South Sudan, for example, reported a sharp decline in the quantity of furniture and other home goods (UNHCR, 2018). Home destruction and a shortage of resources to replace necessities are commonplace in post-conflict environments. Beds, tables, and couches are among the women items that have been lost due to forced displacement, infrastructure devastation, and the priority of survival needs over material possessions. The comparison with South Sudan underscores a common trend of material losses in conflict zones.

Since these items are frequently among the first to be lost during a conflict, significant reductions in refrigerators (mean difference of 0.407), televisions (mean difference of 0.435), and radios/tapes (mean difference of 0.983) illustrate the larger economic hardship. Similar results were noted in areas of violence in Liberia and Sierra Leone, where civil wars frequently resulted in the looting or destruction of civilian property, including televisions and refrigerators [14]. These things are frequently viewed as luxury goods that can be purchased during emergencies. These objects’ disappearance can be attributed to the disruption of supply lines and local economies, as well as the preference for necessities like food, water, and shelter above material possessions. Additionally, the displaced populations’ incapacity to substitute these goods in the post-conflict is a common pattern in African war-affected areas.

The decline in cell phone usage (mean difference of 0.554) is indicative of the loss of avenues for information and communication, which are essential during times of conflict. Comparable losses were observed in nations such as Mali and the Central African Republic (CAR), where displaced families were deprived of mobile devices and telecommunication infrastructure due to widespread communication breakdowns caused by violence and instability [15]. Cell phones and other communication equipment are frequently among the first things lost or left behind during a war because of looting, displacement, or destruction. The loss of these gadgets further isolates communities in war-torn locations, making it harder for people to communicate or obtain essential information about resources or safety.

Donkeys (mean difference of 0.651), goats (mean difference of 0.538), sheep (mean difference of 0.865), cattle (mean difference of 0.590), and oxen (mean difference of 0.677) decreased significantly. In many rural communities, livestock are essential to food security and the economy. As demonstrated by the 2011 famine in Somalia, where the loss of livestock as a result of violence, drought, and illness caused acute food insecurity and economic collapse, livestock loss in conflict zones is a regular trend [16]. During the civil conflict in the Democratic Republic of Congo, cattle and goats were among the most plundered and destroyed assets. Livestock provides food security and a source of income. They are frequently murdered, taken, or left behind in areas devastated by war because of displacement, reducing the community’s ability to recover economically. This trend is not only seen in Tigray but also across other African countries facing conflict, where livestock are heavily affected by violence and displacement.

The number of solar units (mean difference of 0.934) and cars (mean difference of 0.606) decreased significantly, suggesting that the conflict had a negative influence on both renewable and personal energy sources. During the Lord’s Resistance Army insurgency, cars and solar equipment were frequently plundered or destroyed in areas like Northern Uganda, where armed groups targeted residents and infrastructure [17]. In rural areas, where off-grid energy alternatives are essential, the loss of solar equipment is particularly noticeable. While the loss of solar units highlights the vulnerability of renewable energy sources during conflict, the loss of cars represents the breakdown of mobility and access to markets. Armed groups frequently view these assets as valuable or expendable targets for looting or destruction.

The number of ironing presses (p-value = 0.058) and generators (p-value = 0.347) did not vary significantly, indicating that some women either kept or were less likely to target these assets during the war. According to Buchanan (2014), similar results were noted in the Central African Republic (CAR), where basic women objects such as generators were occasionally preserved or kept because of their crucial function in supplying energy in the event of unstable power infrastructure. Ironing presses and generators might have been less important than other home things like food preparation tools or necessary furnishings, which is why they might not have been as frequently looted or destroyed in battle. Furthermore, the fact that some rural communities still use generators emphasizes how crucial they are to preserving basic living conditions despite the conflict.

After the war, some women have turned to small-scale poultry farming as a coping strategy, as seen by the unexpected increase in the number of chickens (mean difference of 0.954). Similar patterns have been observed in war-torn places such as northern Nigeria, where people, particularly those living in rural areas, have stepped up their poultry production as a way to regain food security after Boko Haram raids destroyed their cattle [18, 19]. Smaller-scale, more resilient agricultural production methods, such chicken farming, which needs less space and resources than larger cattle farming, are frequently adopted in post-conflict environments. This change may be a survival tactic used to replenish family supplies following massive animal losses.

The timing of the survey aligns with a critical period marked by seasonal food insecurity and restricted market access due to road disruptions. These concurrent factors may have influenced the reported economic difficulties, as women faced limited opportunities for income generation, trade, and access to basic commodities.

Limitation of the study

This study is limited by potential recall bias, the unavailability of comprehensive economic data due to the post-war context, and the exclusion of displaced or inaccessible populations, which may affect the generalizability of the findings.

The study was conducted in accessible areas of post-war Tigray, which are predominantly inhabited by Ethiopian Orthodox Christians. As a result, the sample may not fully represent the religious diversity of the entire region, including Muslim and Afar populations residing in the eastern escarpment. Restricted access due to insecurity and damaged infrastructure limited our ability to reach those areas.

While security and logistical challenges existed, the study was able to include several hard-to-reach and conflict-affected rural areas, including Bora Chelena.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the findings of this study reveal significant losses in women assets in Tigray due to the war, including reductions in essential items like furniture, appliances, communication devices, and livestock. These losses highlight the severe economic and social disruption caused by the conflict, with women living conditions deteriorating as a result of displacement and destruction. Despite the widespread asset loss, the increase in the number of chickens suggests a shift towards small-scale agriculture as a coping strategy. Overall, the study emphasizes the long-term consequences of war on women economies, underscoring the challenges of post-conflict recovery and the need for targeted interventions to restore livelihoods and rebuild assets in affected communities.

Beyond the economic vulnerabilities identified, this study underscores significant public health implications. In the aftermath of the conflict, women’s access to healthcare facilities has been severely limited due to infrastructural destruction, displacement, and insecurity. Additionally, the disruption of food supply chains and inflation has led to a shift in local diets—from traditional staples like teff and meat—to more affordable legumes and non-traditional foods. These dietary shifts have potential long-term consequences for nutritional health and food security in the region, particularly among women and children.

Recommendations

Targeted humanitarian aid should focus on restoring essential women assets and livestock to support economic stability and food security in war-affected communities. Small-scale agriculture, particularly poultry farming, can serve as a sustainable coping mechanism, supported through resource provision and training in resilient agricultural practices. Economic recovery initiatives, including microfinance, vocational training, and job creation programs, are essential for rebuilding livelihoods, especially in rural areas. Additionally, investing in infrastructure, such as homes, schools, and community centers, alongside community-based psychological support programs, can help restore stability and address long-term social and mental health consequences.
