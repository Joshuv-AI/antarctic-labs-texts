Diabetic striatopathy: Hyperglycemic chorea/ballism successfully treated with L‐dopa
Authors: Ryo Tsukaguchi, Masashi Hasebe, Kimitaka Shibue, Akihiro Hamasaki
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDiabetic striatopathy, a rare hyperglycemia complication, is characterized by chorea/ballism and striatal anomalies on neuroimaging, usually managed with glycemic control and haloperidol. However, practical strategies for haloperidol‐resistant cases are scarce. We describe a 76‐year‐old Japanese woman with diabetic striatopathy who initially presented with polydipsia, polyuria, and lower‐extremity weakness. Despite pronounced hyperglycemia (725 mg/dL), her blood glucose levels were reduced through saline infusion and intravenous insulin. Subsequently, she developed whole‐body ballism concomitant with striatal hyperintensity on T1‐weighted magnetic resonance imaging, which initially responded to haloperidol. Upon discontinuation of haloperidol, her symptoms relapsed and did not improve with the reintroduction of haloperidol. Dopamine transporter single photon emission computed tomography revealed diminished bilateral striatal uptake, suggesting presynaptic dopaminergic dysfunction. This finding prompted the initiation of L‐dopa, which significantly improved her symptoms. This case underlines the need to consider presynaptic dopaminergic dysfunction in diabetic striatopathy patients unresponsive to standard treatments, highlighting the effectiveness of L‐dopa in such scenarios.Keywords: Chorea, Diabetic striatopathy, L‐dopa

INTRODUCTION

Diabetic striatopathy is a rare complication associated with hyperglycemia, characterized by chorea/ballism and/or striatal anomalies such as hyperdensity on computed tomography (CT) or hyperintensity on T1‐weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) 1 . In most cases, glycemic control alone proves inadequate to improve chorea/ballism, thereby compelling the recourse to pharmacological interventions 2 . Haloperidol, commonly used due to its efficacy, is the treatment of choice; however, for those patients exhibiting resistance, there remains a conspicuous absence of well‐defined therapeutic approaches. The pathophysiology of diabetic striatopathy is not well understood, which further complicates the development of effective treatments.

Herein, we describe a patient diagnosed with diabetic striatopathy, who, while demonstrating resistance to haloperidol, was successfully treated with L‐dopa. Our case highlights the importance of considering diabetic striatopathy in patients presenting with hyperglycemia and neurological symptoms. Furthermore, this case provides insight into the possible role of presynaptic dopaminergic dysfunction in the disease's pathogenesis and underscores the effectiveness of L‐dopa for diabetic striatopathy 3 , 4 .

CASE REPORT

A 76‐year‐old Japanese woman (height 152.6 cm, weight 62.5 kg, body mass index 26.8 kg/m2) was admitted with polydipsia and polyuria, accompanied by difficulty walking due to lower‐extremity weakness 3 days prior. She was diagnosed with type 2 diabetes 4 years ago but received no medication and subsequently discontinued outpatient visits on her own accord. Physical examination revealed left‐dominant bilateral lower‐extremity weakness (4+/5 on the right and 4−/5 on the left). Laboratory tests showed high blood glucose levels (725 mg/dL) and glycated hemoglobin (over 16.6%), albeit without ketoacidosis. After receiving saline infusion and intravenous insulin, her blood glucose level had decreased to 341 mg/dL by the following day. However, she developed whole‐body ballism, characterized by sudden, large‐amplitude involuntary movements (Video S1). Neuroimaging revealed striatal abnormalities – hyperdensity on CT (Figure S1) and hyperintensity on T1‐weighted MRI scans (Figure 1a) of both the bilateral putamen and caudate nucleus – indicative of diabetic striatopathy 1 , with no evidence of stroke detected; these bilateral anomalies were speculated to cause bilateral lower‐extremity weakness, consistent with findings reported in previous studies 5 , 6 . Treatment with haloperidol, at a dose of 2 mg/day, alleviated the ballism and weakness and was tapered off at discharge, though with persistent putamen anomalies on neuroimaging (Figure 1b).

Striatal abnormalities observed through magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). (a) Hyperintensity on T1‐weighted MRI of bilateral putamen (arrows) and caudate nucleus (arrowheads). (b) Enhanced hyperintensity of the putamen (arrows) on T1‐weighted MRI before discharge from the initial admission despite improved ballism. (c) More augmented hyperintensity (arrows) on T1‐weighted MRI on readmission due to a relapse of symptoms.

Despite achieving stable glucose control with multiple daily insulin injections, bilateral lower‐extremity chorea, characterized by rapid, irregular involuntary movements, developed 8 days after discharge, accompanied by exacerbated striatal hyperintensity on T1‐weighted MRI (Figure 1c). Upon readmission, haloperidol treatment was restarted and increased to 20 mg/day; however, this failed to improve her chorea. Dopamine transporter single photon emission computed tomography (DAT‐SPECT) revealed diminished bilateral striatal uptake (Figure 2), and a positive response to an L‐dopa challenge test prompted the initiation of L‐dopa treatment. L‐dopa was incrementally increased to 450 mg/day, and haloperidol reduced to 3 mg/day, significantly improving her chorea. It is noteworthy that her blood glucose levels remained stable throughout her second hospitalization, allowing the transition from insulin to oral monotherapy at discharge.

DISCUSSION

In the present case, we characterized diabetic striatopathy, manifested through hyperglycemia, ballism/chorea, and striking striatal anomalies on CT and T1‐weighted MRI. Initially, during her first hospitalization, the patient found relief from ballism through glycemic control and the administration of haloperidol; however, during her second hospitalization, which was marked by a deterioration of her chorea, this therapeutic approach proved ineffective. This failure, coupled with findings from DAT‐SPECT imaging that suggested presynaptic dopaminergic dysfunction, necessitated a transition to L‐dopa treatment; this strategic shift markedly improved her symptoms. The current case highlights the potential of L‐dopa as a pivotal treatment option in complex scenarios of diabetic striatopathy where conventional treatments falter.

Diabetic striatopathy is a rare manifestation in patients with hyperglycemia. It is characterized by chorea/ballism and/or striatal anomalies such as hyperdensity on CT or hyperintensity on T1‐weighted MRI 7 . Two hypotheses have been proposed to explain the mechanisms: the metabolic and vascular theories, implicating depletion of gamma‐aminobutyric acid and acetylcholine, and hypoperfusion in the basal ganglia, respectively 8 , 9 , 10 . Microhemorrhages have also been considered; however, the definitive mechanism remains unclear. Notably, the neurological damage caused by diabetic striatopathy may be irreversible, which could have contributed to the recurrence of symptoms in this case 5 . While it predominantly affects Asian women with type 2 diabetes, as in the present case, bilateral chorea/ballism is uncommon, occurring in only 9.7% of patients, with the majority unilateral 2 . In most cases, glycemic control alone is insufficient to resolve chorea/ballism, requiring medication. Although haloperidol stands as a conventional therapy with proven efficacy, alternative medications such as clonazepam, tetrabenazine, or tiapride may be considered for patients resistant to haloperidol treatment. Notably, L‐dopa can be particularly effective in instances of diminished striatal accumulation on DAT‐SPECT, indicating presynaptic dopaminergic dysfunction 3 , 4 , 11 , 12 , 13 . The present case is particularly significant for illustrating the possibility of recurrence after remission through glycemic control and haloperidol, eventually leading to haloperidol resistance. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of diabetic striatopathy, demonstrating a change in haloperidol efficacy during follow‐up. This case also highlights the L‐dopa effectiveness for diabetic striatopathy, which is seldom reported 3 , 4 . In addition, this case suggests the need for additional exploration into the disease mechanisms, specifically the possible involvement of presynaptic dopaminergic dysfunction.

The median resolution times for CT and MRI abnormalities extend beyond the symptomatic resolution of chorea/ballism, typically 60 and 180 days versus 14 days, respectively 2 . This disparity between clinical improvement and neuroimaging resolution was also evident in the present case. Such discrepancy underscores the importance of clinicians not solely relying on neuroimaging but also considering symptomatic resolution as a crucial indicator of recovery. While neuroimaging is undeniably invaluable for diagnosing diabetic striatopathy, it should be complemented by careful observation of symptom improvement to gauge the full extent of patient recovery.

Currently, diabetic striatopathy is considered to be underdiagnosed. Among hospitalized patients with type 2 diabetes and inadequate glycemic control, approximately 0.57% are reported to have diabetic striatopathy, a figure that increases to 1.2% among those with undiagnosed neurological symptoms 14 . Therefore, clinicians should consider diabetic striatopathy in patients presenting with hyperglycemia and neurological symptoms. Our case highlights the vital role of neuroimaging in diagnosis and treatment, emphasizing the necessity for comprehensive evaluation in suspected cases.
