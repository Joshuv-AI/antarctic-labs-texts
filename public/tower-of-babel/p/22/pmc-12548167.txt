COVID-19 infection and cancer regression: a review of current evidence, potential mechanisms, and clinical perspectives on a Paradoxical phenomenon
Authors: Ikponmwosa Jude Ogieuhi, Victor Oluwatomiwa Ajekiigbe, Chinonyelum Emmanuel Agbo, Chidera Stanley Anthony, Adegbesan Abiodun Christopher, Jennifer Chinaecherem Onyehalu, Mercy Chisom Agu, Sylvia Mmesomachi Mbaji, Adewunmi Akingbola, Bisharat Ahmed, Owa Ogieuhi, Olufemi Akinmeji
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Since its emergence, the coronavirus (SARS-CoV-2) outbreak has been a pandemic responsible for about 7 million deaths worldwide. Numerous studies have been conducted to determine the virus’s multiorgan system involvement, particularly its relation to cancer biology. Spontaneous regression of cancer has been observed in some patients with the coronavirus, which may be attributed to the virus’s ability to trigger specific immune responses that can be oncolytic and help reduce and eliminate oncogenic cells. This study aims to explore the paradoxical effects of COVID-19 in inducing cancer regression. The paradoxical effect of SARS-CoV-2 infection has been attributed to the possibility of a heightened immune activation possibly triggered by the virus, and some of these include increased levels of cytokines such as interferon and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), as well as the activation of T cells and natural killer (NK) cells. COVID-19-induced cancer regression presents new perspectives on the relationship between viral infections and the immune system’s antitumor capabilities. This would help foster future research investigating specific immune pathways activated during SARS-CoV-2 and discover how these can be therapeutically harnessed to aid cancer regression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13027-025-00704-9.

Introduction

COVID-19 is a viral infectious disease caused by SARS-CoV-2. It first emerged in Wuhan, China, and was identified as the agent responsible for a series of atypical respiratory illnesses [1]. It rapidly became a pandemic and was officially declared on March 11, 2020. According to the World Health Organization [2], by November 2024, COVID-19 had resulted in approximately 777 million confirmed cases in 204 countries and 7 million confirmed deaths, with the spread, severity, and outcomes differing based on factors such as sex, co-morbidities, immune system functions, and ethnicity [1, 3]. Since its appearance, COVID-19 has significantly impacted the physical, financial, and social well-being of communities worldwide. It has also led to unprecedented hazards to mental health globally. Studies have been carried out throughout the progression of the pandemic to investigate the multiorgan system involvement of the virus. One particularly intriguing area of research is the interaction between COVID-19-induced immune dysregulation and cancer biology.

Currently, according to data reported by the WHO, cancer is the second leading cause of death throughout the world [4]. It is a disease that starts with genetic and epigenetic mutations that occur in specific cells, leading to uncontrolled proliferation and evasion of programmed cell death (apoptosis). The biological processes involved in carcinogenesis and abnormal cell growth are numerous, and treatment usually requires highly targeted therapies such as chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or immunotherapy. However, spontaneous regression (SR) of cancer occurs occasionally with the partial or complete disappearance of the tumor in the absence of any treatment or the presence of therapy which is considered inadequate to exert a significant influence on neoplastic disease [5]. SR is a rare phenomenon observable in all types of tumors but is more often seen in renal cell cancer, neuroblastoma, GCTs, and malignant melanoma [6]. Traditional mechanisms involved in SR have been studied, and it is usually mediated by apoptosis, anti-angiogenesis, immune system surveillance, terminal differentiation, cellular senescence, or genomic crisis resulting from telomere exhaustion [7]. SR has also been linked to infections, including acute febrile illnesses, suggesting a noteworthy interplay between infections and antitumor immunity [8, 9]. Most studies have tried to understand how the immune responses triggered by the infections interact with the traditional cancer regression mechanisms. One study reported two cases of cancer regression—one in acute myeloid leukemia (AML) and one in metastatic melanoma, observed shortly after acute, febrile infections [8]. The study revealed that acute infections may enhance clinically relevant antitumoral immune responses, especially when accompanied by high fever. Another study provided information on tested methods of inducing SR of cancer, including applying Coley’s toxin (heat-killed Streptococcus pyogenes and Serratia marcescens) [9]. The study reported that the mechanism of the therapeutic effect of Coley’s toxin was the increased expression of tumor necrosis factor (TNF), interleukins (ILs), and interferons (IFNs) in the patient’s body. Rare cases of temporary or prolonged clinical regression from cancers after SARS-CoV-2 infection have also been reported [10]. This introduces a rather unexpected twist, as during the pandemic, the risk of severe outcomes was greater in patients with co-morbidities, including cancer, especially since the virus was primarily associated with suppressing immune defences. The mechanism responsible for this paradoxical effect could hypothetically involve unexpected immune responses, oncolytic activity of the virus, or both [10]. This study explores the paradoxical effects of COVID-19 in inducing cancer regression and its implications.

Methodology

Literature search strategy

To conduct this review, an extensive literature search was conducted to identify the relationship between COVID-19 infection and cancer regression. Various databases were scrutinized to find relevant articles from their inception. We utilized the search engines of Google Scholar, PubMed, Cochrane, Web of Science, Embase, and DOAJ. We used keywords and a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms such as “COVID-19”, “Cancer”, “Tumor”, and “Cancer Regression”, and combined them with the appropriate Boolean operators (AND, OR). All articles relevant to the scope of this review were identified through cross-referencing citations from the articles selected and thoroughly reviewed.

Mechanisms of cancer regression

T-cell cross-reactivity

SARS-CoV-2 infection produces a strong adaptive immune response primarily involving T-cells [11]. The CD8 + cytotoxic T-cells are implicated in destroying the infected cells [12]. T-cell cross-reactivity occurs when a T-cell responds to structurally similar antigens in addition to the antigen that activates them. The cytotoxic T-cells specifically produced against SARS-CoV-2 infection also target cancer cells through this mechanism [13]. Tumor-associated antigens mimic the SARS-CoV-2 spike proteins, nucleocapsid, and membrane proteins [14, 15]. This leads to an unintended cytotoxic response. T-cell response, though initially directed to the virus, targets the tumor through shared epitopes [13].

A study on a 61-year-old woman diagnosed with a T2N0MX myoepithelial carcinoma of the left parotid showed a decrease in the size of the pulmonary nodules only after receiving the second dose of the COVID-19 vaccine [16]. Notably, there were higher fractions of CD4 + T-cells, CD8 + T-cells, natural killer cells, B cells, granzyme, B + cells, and dendritic cells post-vaccination compared to their fractions before vaccination. This suggests a cross-reactivity of immune response on the COVID-19 vaccine and the existing tumor [17] and overwhelming production of pro-inflammatory cytokines [10].

Innate immunity and NK cell activation

Natural killer (NK) cells are very important cells of the innate immune system, and they play a primary role in locating and eliminating abnormal cells [18]. Abnormal cells include those infected by viruses and those that turn cancerous. While the adaptive immune cells must be sensitized before they act, the NK cells act rapidly without prior sensitization [19]. They induce apoptosis in target cells by releasing perforin and granzyme [14]. Upon SARS-CoV-2 infection, NK cells are activated by producing pro-inflammatory cytokines, including Interleukin 12 (IL-12), IL-15, and Interferon-gamma (IFN-γ). While IL-12 and IL-15 enhance the proliferation, survival, and cytotoxic function of NK cells, IFN-γ increases the immune response against cancer cells [18]. Studies have shown that direct oncolytic action results from the activation of the NK cell through the cytokines released in response to SARS-CoV-2 infection [20]. Studies show that the activated cells contribute to cancer remission during SARS-CoV-2 infection through their targeted release of cytotoxic granules [19]. More so, certain tumors enhance their susceptibility to NK cell-mediated destruction by exhibiting stress-induced antigens [21] under inflammatory conditions associated with SARS-CoV-2 infection.

Oncolytic potential of SARS-CoV-2

Oncolytic viruses are capable of selectively infecting and destroying cancer cells while sparing normal cells [14]. Studies show that SARS-CoV-2 might be an oncolytic viral infection [17, 22]. This potential arises from hypothesized receptor utilization mechanisms, where overexpression of SARS-CoV-2 virus entry receptors (ACE2 and NRP1) in papillary renal cell carcinoma makes the tumor cells susceptible to viral infection and replication [23]. In addition, the virus may induce a systemic immune response, indirectly leading to tumor regression [24]. Hence, tumor remission could be achieved using oncolytic viruses instead of lymphodepleting high-dose chemotherapy [25].

A case report by Maryam Barkhordar et al. [26] showed an immune remission of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) in the absence of any disease-modifying therapy while infected and being treated for COVID-19 for a period of 2 months. Remission could not be linked to medications used to manage the viral infection. A profound reason for the unaccounted remission would be that SARS-CoV-2 induced intense immune responses [19], destroying the leukemic cells. Another study reported the transient remission of refractory NK/T-cell lymphoma in a patient co-infected with COVID-19, who only had a relapse after the resolution of COVID-19 [25]. In a case report of a Chinese man with lung cancer, a significant remission was observed only after he was diagnosed with SARS-CoV-2 with no change in cancer therapy [22]. Although there is no proof of the underlying mechanism through which these remissions occur, the intense induction of the innate immunity of SARS-CoV-2 infection is suspected. Also, Ottaiano and his colleagues reported three metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC) cases where stabilization with no change in tumor sizes was observed after first-line chemotherapy. Only after being infected by SARS-CoV-2 was regression observed [27].

Role of inducible nonclassical monocytes (I-NCMs) in cancer regression

Inducible CCR2 + nonclassical monocytes (I-NCMs) are a type of nonclassical monocyte (NCMs). It is implicated in regulating inflammation, immune surveillance, and anti-tumor responses. They secrete cytokines and partake in antigen presentation and the recruitment of immune cells. Studies have shown that direct activation of I-NCMs by SARS-CoV-2 is possible [28]. The SARS-CoV-2 RNA engages the Toll-like receptors (TLR3 and TLR4) [10], transforming the common monocytes into I-NCMs. The upregulation of CCR2 + receptors enables I-NCMs to infiltrate intra- and extravascular cancer sites, unlike the classical monocytes [29]. A study has shown that NK cells recruited by I-NCMs through the secretion of CCL6 increase tumor remission [29]. The study also reported that the regression of metastasis by I-NCMs is independent of T and B lymphocytes (see Fig. 1).

Implications of COVID-19-induced cancer regression for cancer therapy

A paradigm shift in understanding cancer-immune interactions

Although a dual diagnosis of cancer and COVID-19 may initially appear catastrophic, emerging evidence suggests a paradoxical benefit: the potential for viral infection to stimulate antitumor immunity. Reports of tumor regression coinciding with SARS-CoV-2 infection highlight the possibility that the immune system, when sufficiently activated, can mount effective responses against malignancies. This shifts the narrative from viewing COVID-19 solely as a complicating factor in cancer care to recognizing it as a unique opportunity to study the intersection of infection, immunity, and tumor biology.

Immune activation as a driver of tumor regression

SARS-CoV-2 infection is known to elicit profound systemic immune responses [37]. In patients with malignancies, this immune activation has been associated with tumor shrinkage or eradication [38]. Mechanistically, viral infection stimulates both innate and adaptive immune arms, with heightened production of cytokines such as interferon and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), along with robust activation of T cells and natural killer (NK) cells [39]. While these responses are primarily directed toward viral control, they may also exert cytotoxic effects on cancer cells, inadvertently promoting tumor regression [40].

Translational opportunities for cancer immunotherapy

These observations emphasize the therapeutic potential of mimicking virus-induced immune activation in oncology. COVID-19-associated tumor regression highlights the delicate relationship between viral infections and antitumor immunity, providing a model for novel immunotherapeutic strategies. Future research should aim to identify the specific immune pathways activated during SARS-CoV-2 infection and explore how they can be harnessed in a controlled and safe manner. In addition, combining established immunotherapies with novel agents that replicate viral immune stimulation may amplify clinical benefits and improve patient outcomes.

Opportunities for cancer immunotherapy based on COVID-19-induced regression

Enhanced T cell activation: SARS-CoV-2 infection is associated with strong activation of cytotoxic T cells, which are important in antitumor immunity [41]. Viral stimulation may serve as a model for improving immunotherapies that enhance T cell activity, such as oncolytic viruses or engineered viral vectors. Similar immune activation, including stimulation of cytotoxic T cells and NK cells, may be mimicked in cancer immunotherapy. Furthermore, understanding the patient-specific responses to viral infections is pivotal to the implications of COVID-19-induced cancer regression for cancer therapy. This is important because one of the most impactful insights from the COVID-19 cancer regression is the role of individual variability in immune responses. Considering that multiple factors such as genetics, pre-existing conditions, tumor characteristics, and microbiome composition influence the response of individual patients to both viral infections and cancer therapies [46, 47], therefore, understanding these various factors that influence patient response to viral infections and also cancer therapies may enable the more precise application of COVID-19-induced cancer regression for cancer immunotherapy. Challenges and ethical considerations

Risk of COVID-19 in cancer patients

Cancer patients, due to their weakened immune systems, are at greater risk of contracting COVID-19 [55, 56]. Studies have shown that cancer patients with SARS-CoV-2 infection often experience more severe outcomes [57]. For example, data from nine hospitals reported that these patients face a high likelihood of severe complications [58].

Evidence further suggests that COVID-19 increases the chance of complications and mortality in cancer patients [55, 56, 59]. Those who received antitumor therapy within 14 days before COVID-19 diagnosis were found to have a higher risk of severe illness [60, 61]. Similar studies also reported poor prognosis in cancer patients with COVID-19 [59, 62, 63]. This risk is compounded by other health conditions, such as heart disease and chronic lung disorders, which are linked to higher COVID-19-related deaths [64, 65].

Treatment-related vulnerability

Many cancer treatments, including chemotherapy, can cause prolonged myelosuppression, impaired immunity, and greater susceptibility to infections [66–68]. As a result, cancer patients are more likely to develop serious complications, require ICU admission, or face higher mortality [69–71]. The risk is particularly high in patients with hematologic malignancies, lung cancer, or metastatic disease [76]. Excessive cytokine release in severe COVID-19 can also worsen outcomes in cancer patients [50, 72].

Thrombotic risks and poor outcomes

Cancer is also associated with a prothrombotic state, which further worsens COVID-19 outcomes. The risk of venous thromboembolism is much higher in cancer patients, and several studies confirm that coagulation abnormalities play an important role in predicting prognosis in COVID-19 [69–71]. Research consistently shows that cancer patients with SARS-CoV-2 infection have higher mortality rates than non-cancer patients [72–77]. Gender differences have also been observed, such as higher mortality among men in the Italian cohort [78].

Disruption of cancer care

Beyond direct health risks, the pandemic has disrupted cancer care worldwide. Many patients experienced delays in treatment, permanent discontinuation of therapy, or missed diagnostic procedures [79]. These disruptions have further compromised outcomes for cancer patients during the COVID-19 pandemic [78].

Ethical concerns of viral therapies

The novel idea of using a virus to treat cancer raises important ethical challenges that need to be tackled. The foremost concern is patient safety. Intentionally exposing patients to a deadly virus has an increased risk of severe illness or death, particularly for individuals with weakened immune systems. Despite a continuous improvement in the management of the virus’s mortality in cancer patients, many questions regarding its impact on cancer remain unresolved [10].

Informed decision-making is essential. Patients and their caretakers must be informed about the risk factors related to the severity of COVID-19. To make well-informed patient care and treatment decisions, healthcare practitioners must stay current with COVID-19 research. Effective communication among healthcare professionals, patients, and caretakers is crucial to redesigning healthcare and balancing the benefits of care against the cost of social contact [64].

Viral therapies have the potential to make cancer patients feel like test subjects for experimental treatments, which may potentially lead to distrust in healthcare providers. Also, hospitals and healthcare personnel are stressed due to COVID-19’s extremely contagious nature [80]. Delays in treatment access and fear of infection discourage patients from seeking timely follow-ups. This uncertainty, stress, and social isolation have a major impact on the psychological and physical health of cancer patients [81–83].

Research on breast cancer patients has also revealed that anxiety connected to COVID-19 influences patients’ decision-making [84]. Cancer patients with COVID-19 are particularly vulnerable and need immediate, specialized care since their case-fatality rate is significantly higher than that of the general population [59].

Standardized treatment strategies for COVID-19 patients, particularly those with severe cases, must be developed globally to address poor outcomes. There is an urgent need for a systemic understanding of the clinical characteristics of COVID-19 and cancer patients. This will make preventing SARS-CoV-2 infection in these susceptible patients easier, tracking accurate disease progression indicators, and delivering efficient treatments [58].

Need for robust evidence

For COVID-19-induced immunotherapy to be regarded as a potential cancer treatment, strong and trustworthy scientific evidence is required. Its efficacy cannot be verified by isolated instances of cancer regression in COVID-19 patients. Research is urgently needed to determine whether oncolytic SARS-CoV-2 can selectively target cancer cells [85] and to direct the deployment of efficient preventive and control strategies for vulnerable populations [58].

In-depth research is required to explain observed tumor regression cases [86]. The fundamental causes and mechanisms are still unknown, despite some studies suggesting a decrease in tumors after SARS-CoV-2 infection [22]. Strong and trustworthy scientific evidence is necessary for viral-based immunotherapy to be regarded as a potential cancer treatment. Its efficacy cannot be confirmed by isolated instances of cancer regression in COVID-19 patients. The potential of oncolytic SARS-CoV-2 to target cancer cells precisely [85] and to direct the deployment of efficient prevention and control strategies for vulnerable populations requires immediate investigation [50]. More research is required to explain the reported tumor regression [86]. Although some research indicates a decrease in tumor size after infection with SARS-CoV-2, the underlying causes and mechanisms are not yet understood [22]. More investigation is required to improve the development of oncolytic virus therapeutics and clarify the anticancer mechanisms of SARS-CoV-2 [87].

A better understanding of COVID-19’s immunogenic influence and how it affects cancer pathways would optimize treatment approaches for cancer patients [10]. The current approach to managing COVID-19 in cancer patients, especially those with severe cases, should concentrate on treating comorbidities, supportive care, immunological dysfunction, and inflammatory reactions [58]. Additional precautions should also be implemented to shield individuals from the SARS-CoV-2 virus, especially cancer survivors with weakened immune systems or underlying medical disorders [88].

It is always necessary to balance the possible advantages of COVID-19 against its hazards. To lessen the negative consequences of the COVID-19 pandemic on this susceptible group, hospitals must improve their management strategies and advise cancer patients to take particular caution. It is strongly advised that cancer patients with COVID-19 follow different treatment strategies, receive intense treatment, delay chemotherapy or surgery, and ensure more thorough personal protection [64].

Continuous monitoring of other viruses is essential since SARS-CoV-2 is a new virus that can cause illnesses across species. These initiatives would improve the management of cancer patients during pandemics and increase readiness for future epidemics [64].

Future directions

Translational research is essential for studying tumor regression in patients infected with COVID-19. This includes randomized controlled clinical trials to verify the oncolytic potential of SARS-CoV-2 and retrospective studies that would assess cancer outcomes in COVID-19 patients. Laboratory models such as organoids or tumor xenografts should be designed to understand mechanisms like viral replication in tumor cells and immune system modulation [89]. For example, studying the interaction between SARS-CoV-2’s spike protein receptor-binding domain (RBD) and cancer-specific ACE2 receptors is crucial for uncovering potential treatment strategies and understanding COVID-19’s effects on cancer patients to find therapeutic targets [90]. In addition to this, future research should focus on the creation of genetically engineered viral platforms to target receptors for cancer treatment, such as ACE2 and neuropilin-1 (NRP-1), on specific cancer cells that are used as biomarkers for oncolytic viral therapies [14, 25, 91].

Recent studies suggest that immune activation and cytokine regulation of SARS-CoV-2 induce cancer cell apoptosis. The virus can reduce NK/T-cell lymphoma progression or metastatic colorectal cancer through this immune-mediated pathway [25]. Molecular mimicry, where viral proteins like the spike protein resemble tumor antigens, provokes an immune response against cancer cells. Research should focus on how SARS-CoV-2 may contribute to cancer regression and work in synergy with oncolytic viruses such as vesicular stomatitis virus [92–94]. This exploration could lead to combination therapies that enhance the effectiveness of oncolytic agents [13]. Furthermore, more immune modulations in balancing inflammatory response could benefit tumor regression, such as COVID-19 immune exhaustion, marked by lymphocyte depletion, which has also delayed tumor progression [25].

COVID-19 highlighted the potential of acute viral infections as anti-cancer agents. Beyond SARS-CoV-2, studies should evaluate other viral pathogens, such as influenza or adenoviruses, for their capacity to induce similar oncolytic effects [95, 96]. These investigations could help identify characteristic viral features that promote tumor regression. Based on these findings, a high standard of viral-based immunotherapies could be developed.

Conclusion

While the devastating impact of COVID-19 on cancer patients due to their immunosuppressed states has been well-documented, rare cases of spontaneous cancer regression associated with SARS-CoV-2 infection reveal a fascinating and paradoxical phenomenon. These observations suggest that the virus may trigger specific immune responses, such as heightened T-cell activation, natural killer (NK) cell-mediated cytotoxicity, and shifts in the tumor microenvironment, which collectively promote anti-tumor activity. This raises the possibility of leveraging such immune pathways in developing novel therapeutic strategies. However, these cases remain sporadic, with variability in patient responses and limited understanding of the underlying mechanisms, emphasizing the need for further investigation into how viral infections might modulate tumor growth or regression in cancer patients. Since this manuscript bases its argument mainly on isolated case reports, the conclusion should be interpreted with caution pending findings from large cohort studies and randomized controlled trials.

Future research must focus on understanding the precise immunological pathways and molecular mechanisms that link SARS-CoV-2 infection with tumor regression. A deeper understanding of these phenomena could potentially unlock innovative approaches in cancer treatment, such as viral-based immunotherapies designed to mimic beneficial immune activation without the associated risks of a viral infection. Additionally, these findings invite a broader discussion on the role of infectious agents in modulating cancer progression and the potential for controlled immunological interventions. While these insights offer hope, they must be balanced with caution due to the complexities of translating such discoveries into safe and effective treatments. Researchers may pave the way for groundbreaking therapies that harness the immune system’s innate power to combat cancer, providing new avenues of hope for patients with aggressive or refractory malignancies simply by advancing this field.
