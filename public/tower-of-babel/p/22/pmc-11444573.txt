Pityriasis rubra pilaris (PRP) is a rare inflammatory papulosquamous disorder1. It manifests in six clinical subtypes affecting both pediatric and adult populations2. Diagnosis involves a comprehensive cutaneous examination and histopathological analysis. Around one-fifth of patients exhibit tissue and/or peripheral eosinophilia. The patients with tissue and/or peripheral eosinophilia are usually older at presentation and more likely to have multisite disease compared to those without eosinophilia3. We report a case of a young female diagnosed to have PRP through histopathological examination with both tissue and peripheral eosinophilia, which is a rare combination in young patients. This is reported in line with the Surgical CAse REport (SCARE) 2023 criteria4.

Case report

A 14-year-old female presented with multiple itchy scaly lesions on her hands and legs. These lesions began as vesicles nine days after birth, which then ruptured and coalesced to form the plaques according to her mother. She also reported persistent hair loss since birth. The patient had an insignificant past history and there no family history of similar illnesses. She was treated with moisturizers and oral antihistamines in a local hospital multiple times in the past with no resolution. There was no history of any allergies.

On examination, there were multiple hypopigmented, hyperkeratotic erythematous plaques on the dorsal aspect of both hands and the extensor aspect of both legs, with intervening areas of normal skin. Short, fragile hair were noted in the areas of hair loss on the scalp (Figs. 1 and 2). Systemic examination including general physical examination, chest, cardiovascular, abdominal, and neurological examination revealed no abnormalities.

Histopathological examination revealed an acanthotic epidermis with elongated rete ridges and a thick suprapapillary plate. There were multiple foci of parakeratosis and orthokeratosis with focal hypergranulosis. No spongiosis was noted. The upper dermis showed perivascular lymphocytic infiltration with eosinophils.

Blood examinations revealed eosinophilia and anemia (Table 1). Renal and liver function tests, lipid profile, abdominal ultrasound, chest radiography, urine and stool routine microscopic examination and culture, serum IgE, and serology for HIV, Hepatitis B, Hepatitis C, and Syphilis revealed no abnormalities. Further investigations could not be done due to financial reasons.

Based on the morphology of the lesions and histopathological examination, she was diagnosed with pityriasis rubra pilaris. Treatment included 20 mg of isotretinoin per day, betamethasone ointment application each alternate day, and a moisturizer for 2 months. After 2 months, there was an improvement in the lesions and hair growth in the areas of hair loss. Isotretinoin was discontinued, and she was advised to continue using the moisturizer. The patient was asked to follow-up after 2 months but was lost to follow-up.

Discussion

Pityriasis rubra pilaris (PRP) is a rare inflammatory papulosquamous disorder1. It manifests in six clinical subtypes affecting both pediatric and adult populations2. Despite variations in presentation, common features across all subtypes include palmoplantar keratoderma and follicular papules coalescing into distinct plaques characterized by a reddish-orange hue and nonadherent flaking scales1. Generalized forms often exhibit areas of unaffected skin, referred to as ‘islands of sparing’2.

Its global prevalence varies significantly, from 1 in 500 new dermatology patients in Kenya to 1 in 50 000 in India5. The male-to-female prevalence ratio is approximately equal, though some studies suggest a slight male predominance5. There have been no reported cases from Nepal to our knowledge. PRP onset can occur at any age, with bimodal peaks during the first-to-second and fifth-to-sixth decades of life6.

The etiology remains largely unidentified. PRP-like eruptions have been linked to certain medications, particularly ponatinib and other kinase inhibitors7. It is known to have inconsistent temporal associations with viral infections, vaccinations, injuries, and other illnesses5. Inherited PRP, typically presenting before age 2, is associated with gain-of-function mutations in CARD148. These genetic variations are also found in familial psoriasis suggesting a pathophysiological overlap with psoriasis9.

It presents in a broad clinical spectrum, ranging from mild, localized disease confined to the extensor extremities to diffuse erythroderma, accompanied by intense pruritus, skin tightness, burning, stinging, and pain. While PRP can be self-limiting and asymptomatic in some cases, chronic symptomatic cases may need treatment5. Its clinical spectrum was initially categorized into five subtypes by Griffiths, based on onset (childhood vs. adult), lesion characteristics, and distribution10. These subtypes were later expanded to six, including an HIV-associated variant11,12. CARD14-associated papulosquamous eruption (CAPE) has been proposed as a distinct entity for early-onset PRP linked to CARD14 mutations13. Our patient falls into the CAPE subtype given her age of onset and clinical presentation. The subtypes include classical adult-onset (Type I), atypical adult-onset (Type II), classical juvenile-onset (Type III), circumscribed juvenile-onset (Type IV), atypical juvenile-onset (Type V), and HIV-associated (Type VI). The cardinal features in all subtypes include palmoplantar hyperkeratosis and hyperkeratotic follicular papules coalescing into well-demarcated red-orange plaques. Limited variants are mildly pruritic, while widespread PRP is associated with severe pruritus and pain diminishing the quality of life14,15.

Type I PRP, affecting 55% of patients, is the most common adult subtype10. It typically begins with fine scaling and erythema on the scalp, progressing to a cephalocaudal spread of papules and plaques with ‘islands of sparing’. Type II PRP, seen in 5% of patients, is characterized by more eczematous plaques, ichthyosiform scaling, coarse palmoplantar keratoderma, and pronounced alopecia10. Type III PRP, comprising 10% of cases, presents similarly to Type I but with a juvenile-onset and less frequent erythroderma16–18. Type IV PRP, the most common juvenile subtype, is focal with sharply demarcated follicular papules on erythematous bases, primarily affecting prepubertal children16–18. Type V PRP is a chronic generalized juvenile variant, often associated with gene variations13, while Type VI PRP is linked to HIV11,12. CAPE presents in early childhood with autosomal dominant inheritance of CARD14 mutations13. It may resemble classical psoriasis, PRP, or a hybrid, often with erythema of the cheeks, chin, and ears, and occasionally scleroderma-like changes of the hands5.

Diagnosis involves a comprehensive cutaneous examination and histopathological analysis. Screening for HIV is recommended due to the rare association.

Histologically, it is characterized by irregular psoriasiform acanthosis of the epidermis and a distinctive ‘checkerboard’ pattern of parakeratosis. Follicular plugging with shoulder parakeratosis is also common. The presence of thick suprapapillary plates and the absence of neutrophils in the stratum corneum help distinguish it from psoriasis3,5. Additionally, a normal or thickened granular layer under areas of parakeratosis, along with a variable mild superficial perivascular lymphohistiocytic infiltrate, occasional mild epidermal spongiosis, eosinophils, and focal acantholytic dyskeratosis have been noted3,5. Around 19.7% of patients exhibit tissue and/or peripheral eosinophilia. These patients were older at presentation and more likely to have multisite disease compared to those without eosinophilia3. This case of a young female exhibiting both tissue and peripheral eosinophilia is noteworthy.

The management is challenging. Symptomatic relief is achieved with emollients and mid-to-high potency topical corticosteroids. Keratolytic agents may benefit thick palmoplantar keratoderma. Systemic therapy, including high-dose isotretinoin, IL17 inhibitors (ixekizumab, secukinumab), and biologics targeting the IL23-IL17 axis are used for moderate-to-severe cases, with isotretinoin being the preferred treatment19.

Conclusion

This case contributes to the understanding of the rare dermatosis, pityriasis rubra pilaris, particularly its association with eosinophilia in a young patient. This unusual finding underscores the necessity for further research and evaluation to enhance understanding of its pathophysiology.
