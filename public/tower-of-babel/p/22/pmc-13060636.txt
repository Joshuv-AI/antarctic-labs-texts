Evaluating Perceived Value of Pharmacogenomic Testing After a Personalized Therapeutics Clinic Consultation
Authors: Thomas Chen, Amanda S. Andriessen, Matthew Jack, David George, Larry K. House, Clarence Chan, Kiang‐Teck J. Yeo, Mark J. Ratain, Peter H. O'Donnell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPharmacogenomics (PGx) offers patients personalized treatment by guiding medication selection and dosing based on their genetic profiles. However, widespread clinical implementation remains limited, in part due to patient and provider concerns about cost and willingness to pay (WTP). This study evaluated whether an outpatient clinical consultation that educates patients on their PGx results influences their WTP for PGx testing. As part of the PROTECT‐Rx trial at the University of Chicago, high‐risk patients received free PGx testing and a virtual consultation at the Personalized Therapeutics Clinic (PTC). Patients completed pre‐ and post‐visit surveys assessing PGx knowledge and WTP. Of 129 patients who had completed a PTC visit as of 1/2025, 76 completed both the PTC visit and surveys. WTP for PGx testing increased significantly from 68% pre‐visit to 83% post‐visit (p = 0.029). After controlling for social vulnerability, patients who had at least one cautionary or high‐risk PGx result were nearly 3 times more likely to report higher willingness‐to‐pay (OR 2.76, p = 0.037). A single PGx consultation with review of personal results significantly increases patients' WTP for testing, particularly among those with actionable PGx results. Understanding individual risk and the relevance of PGx enhances perceived value, suggesting that patient education is an important component in the broader adoption of pharmacogenomic testing.

Introduction

Pharmacogenomics (PGx) enables providers to make informed decisions about drug selection, adverse events, and dosage [1]. Despite its potential benefits, the integration of PGx testing into routine clinical practice faces a variety of challenges: limited PGx knowledge by patients and providers, slow result turnaround time, and calls for demonstrable utility/effectiveness [2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. In addition, one of the major challenges for both patients and providers is the cost of testing, including uncertainty around testing will be reimbursed by insurers [6, 7, 8].

An important factor to consider is how stakeholders perceive the value of PGx testing, including patients' willingness‐to‐pay (WTP). In a healthcare environment where payors wrestle with questions of medical necessity and clinical utility, where providers wonder if test orders will be reimbursed, and where patients may choose to seek direct‐to‐consumer PGx testing, WTP for PGx is a central factor determining adoption. Few studies have explored patients' WTP for PGx. A small cross‐sectional, web‐based survey of patients receiving prescriptions from a community pharmacy in Tennessee found 81% were interested in PGx testing, and, if the entire cost of the service was covered by insurance, 89% said they would use the service. However, patients were not willing‐to‐pay more than $120 [9]. Another study asked patients their WTP for testing for antidepressants. WTP for avoidance of one antidepressant switch was about $223, whereas WTP for reducing dosage‐adjustments by 1 month was $88 [10]. Another study in older patients showed 90% of patients were willing to provide a saliva sample, but only 47% were willing to pay for testing [11]. Lastly, data was collected on 808 adult attendees at the 2021 Minnesota State Fair showed 84% were comfortable with PGx testing and about 64% of respondents were willing to incur out‐of‐pocket cost for PGx testing: 38.9% were willing to pay less than $50, 18.2% between $50–149, and only 6.7% were willing to pay more than $150 [12].

Since PGx tests are not always covered by insurances, this can result in significant cost to the patient. A patient's willingness to bear these costs may depend on several factors: understanding of PGx, perceived benefits, and cost. At the University of Chicago (UC), we established a Personalized Therapeutics Clinic (PTC) to evaluate drug‐gene interactions (DGIs) and drug–drug interactions (DDIs) for patients interested in personalized medicine. This study examined whether a PTC consultation influenced patients' WTP for PGx testing. We hypothesized that if patients were given an opportunity to review their own PGx results during a clinic visit and better understand the potential relevance of those results to optimizing treatments and preventing adverse drug events, they would then express such perceived value as a higher WTP for testing.

Materials and Methods

Participants and Study Design

Participants were enrolled in the PROTECT‐Rx trial at the UC, which was approved by the IRB (clinicaltrials.gov #NCT05166694). Enrolled patients who are identified as being high risk for medication events are offered a one‐time virtual visit at the PTC. All patients were receiving routine care from a primary care or subspecialty physician at UC. Adults were eligible if they were prescribed ≥ 5 medications and taking ≥ 1 significant inducer/inhibitor, or were taking ≥ 1 medication with potentially actionable PGx. Recruitment occurred through electronic medical records (EMR) auto‐filtered lists, provider referrals, or flyers. A dedicated research coordinator approached eligible patients using a process of informed consent. PGx testing was provided at no cost within the trial, but PTC consultations were billed as standard virtual outpatient visits, with patients responsible for co‐pays.

Participants were genotyped in a Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments‐certified laboratory across a custom comprehensive panel of germline PGx markers, as described previously [13]. At the PTC visit, participants met with a clinical pharmacologist physician, and in most cases, an accompanying fellow from our Clinical Pharmacology and Pharmacogenomics fellowship program. Clinicians assessed DDIs and DGIs and provided formal recommendations to both the patients and their treating providers. Consultations were documented in the EMR.

Outcome Measures

After patients were enrolled in the study, participants completed a baseline survey before PGx testing. Patients did not have access to their PGx information until it was disclosed during the PTC visit. Afterwards, patients received a second (post‐PTC) survey, which consisted of the same questions. The primary outcome was to measure patients' perceived value, using two questions with scaled, categorical choices that asked patients their hypothetical WTP for PGx testing. “Our survey included the question: Are you willing to pay for a pharmacogenomics test out‐of‐pocket to find the best medications for you?” Response options were not willing to pay out‐out‐pocket, up to $100, more than $100 but less than $300, more than $300 but less than $500, and more than $500 (supplemental material). As the primary covariate of this outcome, each patient's individual PGx risk was categorized based on their prescribed medications at the start of the PTC visit using the classification system of our institutional Genomic Prescribing System (GPS), an EMR‐integrated, results portal that delivers a clinical interpretation of PGx information for each medication using traffic lights: genomically favorable (green‐light), genomically cautionary (yellow‐light), or genomically contraindicated (red‐light) [13].

Statistical Analyses

The primary analysis was to determine the influence of a PTC visit on patients' WTP for PGx testing. Each participant's survey answers before and after the visit were paired in a Wilcoxon Signed Rank Test analysis. We evaluated patient factors (gender, self‐reported race/ethnicity, education, insurance status, social vulnerability, number of medications, and number of cautionary/high risk medications) as potential mediators of WTP. Social vulnerability was quantified by the social vulnerability metric (SVM), assigned based on the patient's zip code, and organized into 2 quantiles for relative comparison [14]. Odds‐ratios (ORs) were calculated with a simple or multiple ordinal logistic regression model, as appropriate, where the outcome variable of WTP pre‐ or post‐visit was treated as an ordered categorical response. The final model was further confirmed with a likelihood ratio test. P‐values < 0.05 were considered significant. All analyses were performed using STATA, version 18.0.

For the primary analysis of WTP, PTC consultation showed a meaningful and statistically significant increase in WTP. Before the PTC visit, 68% of patients expressed WTP for PGx testing, and WTP increased to 83% after the visit (p = 0.029) (Figure 1).

Patients' willingness to pay (WTP) for pharmacogenomic testing before and after receiving their own PGx results within the context of a Personalized Therapeutics Clinic (PTC) visit. Patients' answers pre‐ and post‐PTC were matched. There was a meaningful, and statistically significant increase in patients' WTP following the PTC visit (68% potential WTP pre‐visit compared to 83% WTP post‐visit, p = 0.0288).

WTP for PGx was correlated with social vulnerability and social constructs among the participants within our study. Patients with SVM scores below the median, indicating lower social vulnerability, were significantly more likely to report higher WTP following the visit than those with higher social vulnerability, with an OR of 3.1 (95% CI = 1.3–7.3, p = 0.009), even after accounting for the presence of patient‐level cautionary/contraindicated PGx results. Separately, even when controlling PGx risk and SVM, self‐reported White patients were almost 4 times more likely to rate a higher WTP for PGx testing post‐visit than self‐reported Black patients (OR = 3.9, 95% CI = 1.01–15, p = 0.048). We found no statistically significant difference between gender, education, insurance status, and number of medications.

Finally, even after controlling for social vulnerability, patients who were prescribed at least one medication with a yellow or red‐light result (n = 56) were almost 3 times (OR 2.8, 95% CI = 1.06–7.19, p = 0.037) more likely to report a higher WTP group in comparison to those with no cautionary/high risk medications (n = 20) (Figure 2).

Patients taking medications that were shown to have cautionary (yellow light) or contraindicated/high risk (red light) PGx results were more likely to indicate a higher willingness to pay (WTP) for pharmacogenomic (PGx) testing. Even after controlling for social vulnerability, patients who were prescribed a cautionary/high‐risk PGx medication (n = 56) were 2.8 times more likely to report a higher WTP for PGx testing after receiving their PGx results during a PTC visit than their counterparts with no PGx risk medications (n = 20) (p = 0.037).

Discussion

This is the first published research, to our knowledge, that explores the impact of a PGx clinic with dedicated direct‐to‐patient disclosure of PGx results on patients' WTP for PGx testing. We found a significant increase in WTP following PTC consultation, highlighting the perceived value of PGx counseling. We also found that WTP was correlated with social vulnerability and self‐reported race. Notably, even after controlling for social vulnerability, we found that patients taking medications with cautionary/high risk PGx result associations were nearly three times more likely to express higher out‐of‐pocket WTP for PGx testing, suggesting that efforts to increase self‐understanding of individual PGx risk is an important component in measuring the true value of broader PGx testing. These findings could accelerate adoption of PGx in routine practice and may support policy changes around reimbursement and clinical implementation.

We examined the influence of social factors on patients' perceptions of PGx testing and WTP. Race also correlated with hypothetical WTP. These findings may represent the identification of current unmet needs within PGx evidence generation and might inform future educational efforts and public health resources that aim to support PGx adoption. Perhaps not surprisingly, individuals identified as the least socially vulnerable demonstrated higher WTP following their PTC visit. This finding is similar with the RIGHT Protocol Study, which showed that increasing financial strain was strongly associated with unwillingness to incur any out‐of‐pocket costs for pharmacogenomic testing. In that study, which only assessed CYP2D6 PGx information, 87% of participants were unwilling to pay more than $250, and 58% indicated that $100 was their maximum [15].

It stands to reason, as we found, that patients are generally more willing‐to‐pay for PGx when they are shown tangible and perceptible value regarding the treatments that they are currently taking and future possible medications based on disease states, such as the possibility of avoiding adverse effects or achieving better therapeutic outcomes. A second, perhaps obvious factor, is whether the patient will bear the cost of testing, or whether an insurer will. In one previous study, Gibson et al. evaluated the WTP of individuals in three different insurance scenarios: not covered, fully covered, and partially covered. As expected, when fully covered, the large majority of the sample (89%) would use a PGx testing service; when partially covered, the majority was only likely to pay less than $39; and when not covered, 23% would not want to use the service [8]. In another study performed within an antithrombosis clinic in Chicago, 44% of patients with high interest in a PGx test were willing to pay $20 or more [16]. In comparison, our study showed higher pre‐test WTP of 68%, perhaps reflective of the fact that all respondents had already agreed to enroll in a personalized medicine study, and perhaps also indicative of the relatively high baseline educational level of our study participants (approximately half held either a Master's or Doctoral degree in our cohort).

Commercial PGx testing in the United States without insurance typically ranges from $199 to $500, with many tests priced in the $300 range. These tests are generally pursued by interested patients, either independently or through provider referral. In our study, WTP increased from 68% to 83% following a PTC consultation, after patients were educated on and experienced their own PGx results. Notably, WTP was higher among patients who received yellow‐ or red‐light medication results. Following the PTC visit, 60% of patients were willing to pay up to $300, and approximately 50% were willing‐to‐pay more than $100, aligning with the current commercial pricing of PGx testing.

This study had several limitations. First, there were two attending clinicians staffing the PTC consultations on separate days, each of whom may bring different perspectives and approaches to patient care and education that may impact the consistency of results to patients. We strived to maintain consistency in the PGx information presented to patients, and the EMR‐embedded results system that was used to convey results provided this constancy across visits. To further promote uniformity, we relied on a standardized clinic template and outside resources, such as ClinPGx and FDA drug labels when interpreting and conveying PGx information. Another limitation is the small sample size as this study was conducted within an ongoing clinical trial at a single center, and the analysis reflects early experience with a real‐world PGx clinic model. Despite the sample size, we observed statistically significant changes in WTP and consistent associations with PGx risk and social vulnerability. The study was conducted at a single academic university medical center in an urban setting, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other healthcare settings. WTP was assessed using self‐reported survey data, which may be subject to hypothetical bias, especially since participants were provided actual testing free of charge. Lastly, there is potential selection bias, as individuals who are willing to undergo testing and be seen in the PTC may already be more interested in participating and, therefore, more willing to pay a higher cost.

Although PGx testing continues to gain clinical adoption being supported by growing evidence of clinical utility and cost‐effectiveness, insurance coverage remains suboptimal and cost continues to be a major factor influencing both providers and patients [7]. Understanding patients' perceptions of PGx is important, as they are the ultimate stakeholders and beneficiaries of personalized medicine. This study demonstrated that a single clinic consultation, through which patients were educated about and directly disclosed their personal PGx results, significantly increased patients' WTP for PGx testing, especially among patients taking cautionary/high risk PGx medications. Future work should continue to carefully measure the potential value of PGx in its many forms, as such findings may underpin important policy decisions around reimbursement and clinical implementation of PGx as a value‐add within specific health care delivery settings.
