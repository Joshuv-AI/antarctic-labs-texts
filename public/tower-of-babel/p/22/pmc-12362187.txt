Cranial bypass for occlusive carotid dissection in osteogenesis imperfecta: illustrative case
Authors: Eric A. Grin, Jacob Baranoski, Caleb Rutledge, Daniel D. Wiggan, Charlotte Chung, Eytan Raz, Vera Sharashidze, Maksim Shapiro, Howard A. Riina, Cen Zhang, Erez Nossek
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDOsteogenesis imperfecta (OI) is a connective tissue disorder characterized by fragile bones and vascular fragility, increasing the risk of vessel dissection and potentially complicating endovascular intervention. The authors present the first case of cranial bypass in a patient with OI.OBSERVATIONSA 38-year-old male with OI type I presented with a symptomatic left internal carotid artery (ICA) occlusive dissection managed with endovascular revascularization and stenting. Follow-up surveillance imaging identified an incidental right ICA dissection, also treated with stenting. Four years later, the patient experienced new right hemispheric symptoms. He was found to have progressive right ICA dissection on best medical management. Following an unsuccessful restenting attempt, he underwent a successful double-barrel superficial temporal artery–to–middle cerebral artery (STA-MCA) bypass to restore cerebral perfusion with no perioperative complications. Six-month follow-up DSA confirmed a patent bypass with robust flow, and the patient remained asymptomatic 1 year postoperatively.LESSONSSTA-MCA bypass can serve as a viable and effective revascularization option in patients with OI, whose disease predisposes them to vascular dissection. In these high-risk patients, cranial bypass is a safe method for effective flow augmentation to hypoperfused brain regions when endovascular interventions fail. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE25378

Osteogenesis imperfecta (OI) is a connective tissue disorder caused by abnormalities in type I collagen, leading to the formation of brittle bones.1 While primarily characterized by increased susceptibility to fractures, OI has also been associated with intracranial aneurysms and dissection.2–5 This is because type I collagen is thought to play a role in the structural integrity and elasticity of the vascular wall.2

Endovascular approaches, like flow diversion, can achieve primary vessel reconstruction, a potential advantage in connective tissue disorders.6,7 However, in OI, the underlying vascular fragility and dissection risk may complicate endovascular interventions. We present a case of internal carotid artery (ICA) dissection in a patient with OI type I, initially managed with endovascular stenting but complicated by progressive dissection and occlusion. This ultimately required an extracranial-to-intracranial superficial temporal artery–to–middle cerebral artery (STA-MCA) bypass to augment cerebral flow. We report the first cranial bypass in this high-risk patient population, highlighting this procedure’s potential for critical revascularization when conventional endovascular strategies are infeasible or unsuccessful.

Illustrative Case

Initial Dissections

A 38-year-old male with a history of OI type I, hypertension, and multiple surgeries earlier in life for fracture reductions and tendon repairs presented to an outside institution with acute right-sided weakness secondary to left ICA dissection. MRI showed left MCA infarcts. He underwent endovascular treatment with three Neuroform Atlas stents (Stryker) and was discharged on dual antiplatelet treatment (DAPT) with aspirin and ticagrelor. His left hemispheric symptoms resolved completely.

Three months later, follow-up DSA demonstrated patent left ICA stents and an incidental right ICA dissection not seen on initial imaging. Given concern that this occurred with concurrent DAPT, the patient underwent uncomplicated, elective right-sided treatment with an Enterprise stent (Cordis Neurovascular). He received annual follow-up vessel imaging and was transitioned to aspirin monotherapy after 1 year.

Four years later, the patient experienced acute-onset left-sided numbness. He presented to another institution, where CTA revealed right in-stent thrombosis. He was given tenecteplase and restarted on aspirin and clopidogrel. Persistent, intermittent left-sided numbness led to a switch to ticagrelor and then to apixaban.

Failed Endovascular Intervention

Two weeks after this transition, he began again experiencing frequent left-sided weakness, paresthesia, headaches, right ocular pain, and mental slowness. DSA at another institution confirmed right ICA stent restenosis. An endovascular attempt to deploy another stent was unsuccessful, and the guidewire tip broke off on exit, leading to progression of the right ICA dissection distal to the stent with complete ICA occlusion. The team concluded the intervention after demonstrating good cross-filling from the left ICA into the right ACA and MCA branches. MRI showed no new infarcts.

Experiencing the same persistent symptoms, the patient presented to our institution to discuss treatment options. During his evaluation, he had a heavy bout of sneezing immediately followed by a transient episode of complete left-sided weakness. MRI showed new subacute infarcts in the right watershed territory (Fig. 1A and B). DSA demonstrated no antegrade flow into the right ICA (Fig. 1C– F); CT perfusion imaging confirmed significant right hemispheric hypoperfusion (Fig. 1G and H). Given the failure of prior endovascular treatment, lack of right antegrade ICA flow, and symptomatic hypoperfusion with watershed strokes, a right-sided direct double-barrel STA-MCA anastomosis was planned.

One week later, he underwent right-sided combined direct double-barrel bypass and indirect encephaloduroangiosis. A double-barrel bypass was chosen to augment flow to both temporal and frontal lobes. Indirect encephaloduroarteriosynangiosis (EDAS) was performed to provide redundancy with dual anastomoses given the genetically predisposed fragile vasculature. The direct bypass allows for immediate flow augmentation in the setting of significant hypoperfusion, whereas the indirect bypass provides gradual, supplemental flow.

Surgery

Intraoperatively, the frontal and parietal STA branches were mapped with a handheld Doppler and dissected (Fig. 2A and B). The distal parietal STA branch was clipped and transected after confirming a cut flow of 152 mL/min. The distal frontal STA branch was clipped with a cut flow of 80 mL/min. The STA was flushed with heparinized saline. A right frontotemporal craniotomy was performed, and the dura was opened in a cruciate fashion. The targeted cortical MCA frontal and temporal branches were dissected and prepared for anastomosis (Fig. 2C). The frontal and parietal STA branches were prepared for anastomosis with meticulous dissection and fish-mouthing while ensuring sufficient length to reach both temporal and parietal M4 anastomotic sites, respectively, without tension. Temporary microclips were applied to trap the recipient segments. Anastomoses were performed using 10-0 nylon sutures (Ethicon). Two anchor stitches were placed at the heel and toe followed by running sutures for completion (Fig. 2D and E). Once backbleeding was performed, the anastomoses were concluded, and the clips were removed. Patency was confirmed with an ultrasonic transit-time flowmeter, with flow through the proximal donor STA measured at 51 mL/min. Intraoperative angiography demonstrated robust flow through the anastomosis (Fig. 3A). For the indirect bypass, the dural leaflets were flipped to promote EDAS. Titanium plates and screws were used to fix the ClearFit (Longeviti Neuro Solutions) sonolucent cranioplasty in position. The temporalis muscle was re-approximated and secured with 0 Vicryl sutures. The skin was closed in layers with 3-0 Vicryl and staples. There were no intraoperative or perioperative complications, and the patient was transferred to the ICU in stable condition.

Follow-Up

Two weeks postoperatively, trans-sonolucent cranioplasty ultrasound (TCUS) demonstrated robust flow across the two anastomoses (Fig. 3B and C). The patient’s symptoms were significantly improved, his cognitive slowing had resolved, and he returned to work. Three-month follow-up MRI showed no new strokes, and he was maintained on aspirin 325 mg daily.

Six-month follow-up DSA showed robust flow into the right STA frontal and parietal branches and through the frontal and temporal M4 anastomoses with good proximal and distal flow (Fig. 4A– D). An increased caliber of both STA branches was noted. While the wire fragments from the prior failed endovascular treatment remained in place, no plans to remove them were made due to substantial procedural risk and the lack of any symptoms. The patient remained asymptomatic and was doing well at the most recent clinical follow-up 1 year postoperatively. Discussion

Observations

This report presents the first known case of extracranial-intracranial STA-MCA bypass in a patient with OI, adding to the literature supporting OI as a risk factor for cerebrovascular complications (Table 1). OI has been associated with an increased risk of intracranial aneurysm formation, rupture, and multiplicity.2 Several studies have also reported spontaneous carotid, vertebral, and basilar artery dissections in patients with OI, which are often bilateral.8–11 Dissections can frequently be managed with antithrombotic therapy or, as was attempted in this case, endovascular intervention. However, there have been several instances in which antithrombotic therapy has failed to resolve vessel occlusion secondary to dissection, and patients have been left with significant neurological Endovascular approaches may be favored over open surgery in patients with OI due to the patients’ bone fragility and complications such as chronic subdural fluid collection, communicating hydrocephalus, and intracranial hematoma formation.1 However, fractures in patients with OI type I are less frequent after puberty, as ossification is complete; adults with OI type I may even achieve normal bone density.5

While open surgery poses important risks, including slower wound healing in patients with OI,2 endovascular approaches may also carry significant complications, particularly in patients with underlying vessel fragility. In this case, endovascular therapy failed to resolve the ICA dissection and resulted in complete ICA occlusion.

In this case, STA-MCA bypass was successfully used to augment cerebral perfusion without perioperative complications. From a technical perspective, use of running sutures during STA-MCA anastomosis can decrease operative time without compromising graft patency or maturation.12,13 Additionally, use of a sonolucent cranioplasty permits postoperative graft monitoring with TCUS, potentially reducing reliance on invasive follow-up vessel imaging.14

Limitations

This report is primarily limited by its retrospective, single-patient design, which restricts generalizability. Further research is needed to better characterize the natural history and optimal management of cerebrovascular disease in this patient population.

Lessons

This case demonstrates that direct extracranial-to-intracranial bypass can be a viable, safe, and effective revascularization strategy in patients with OI who develop cerebrovascular pathology. Despite concerns regarding bone fragility and impaired wound healing in this population,1 direct bypass surgery was performed safely and without complications. Thus, in carefully selected adult patients, particularly those with OI type I who have reached skeletal maturity and may have near-normal bone density, surgical approaches may be well tolerated.5

Patients with OI are predisposed to spontaneous arterial dissections, which may progress despite medical therapy or endovascular treatment.10,11 In such cases, extracranial-intracranial bypass offers an open surgical alternative. A combined direct (STA-MCA) and indirect (EDAS) bypass approach may be especially advantageous, offering both immediate and long-term perfusion augmentation while accounting for potential anastomotic fragility and ensuring redundancy.

Although this is the first reported use of cranial bypass in OI, prior case reports and small case series have documented successful coronary artery bypass in this population.15,16 However, these operations, often involving concomitant valve surgery, are high-risk. Cardiovascular surgery has been reported to carry a high mortality rate in patients with OI, largely due to tissue friability and perioperative bleeding.15,16 A meticulous surgical technique is essential.

More broadly, the present case expands the therapeutic options for patients with OI-related cerebrovascular disease secondary to their genetically fragile vasculature. When endovascular strategies fail or are contraindicated, surgical revascularization may offer a safe, definitive, and durable option in appropriately selected patients.
