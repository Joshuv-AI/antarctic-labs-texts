Polyclonal Vβ21.3 expansion in multisystem inflammatory syndrome in children despite SARS-CoV-2 vaccination
Authors: Stejara Netea, Liliane Khoryati, Sietse Nagelkerke, Sarah Benezech, Jim Keijser, Mariken Gruppen, Giske Biesbroek, Roel Lubbers, Naomi Ketharanathan, Emilie Buddingh, Nikki Schoenmaker, Arianne Brandsma, Theo Rispens, Irene Kuipers, Alexandre Belot, Taco Kuijpers
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMultisystem inflammatory syndrome in children (MIS-C) is a severe SARS-CoV-2-associated condition that shares clinical features with Kawasaki disease (KD), characterised by a distinct polyclonal expansion of Vβ21.3+ T cells. We report five patients diagnosed with breakthrough MIS-C despite COVID-19 immunisation, all within a limited time period at the beginning of the Omicron wave, to assess whether breakthrough MIS-C cases share the same TCR Vβ21.3 skewing seen in non-vaccinated MIS-C cases. We retrospectively reviewed five MIS-C patients hospitalised between December 2021 and April 2022 despite previous immunisation against SARS-CoV-2 (BNT162b2, an mRNA vaccine against S-protein). Immunophenotyping, including TCR Vβ subset distribution, was performed in four patients.Patients (100% male, 12.2–17.2 years) had a natural breakthrough SARS-CoV-2 infection following prior immunisation (between August 2021 and February 2022). Recent infection was proven by positive SARS-CoV-2 PCR and/or IgG antibodies against the nucleocapsid protein. Blood samples of four patients were available. All presented with Vβ21.3+ T cell expansion, similar to MIS-C patients and in contrast to vaccinated historical KD patients (n=10). The two patients with the earliest sampling post-illness displayed frequencies of Vβ21.3+ T cells exceeding the reference mean value+10×SD. These Vβ21.3+ T cells showed increased surface expression of activation (HLA-DR, CD38) and exhaustion (PD-1, TIM-3) markers.In conclusion, breakthrough MIS-C patients presented with features consistent with unvaccinated MIS-C patients, including the hallmark Vβ21.3+ T cell expansion, indicating that prior immunisation with an mRNA vaccine targeting the Wuhan strain did not fully protect against MIS-C at the wave of a novel emerging variant early 2022. Introduction

Multisystem inflammatory syndrome in children (MIS-C) is a SARS-CoV-2 associated condition sharing clinical features with Kawasaki disease (KD) and toxic shock syndrome but characterised by polyclonal expansion of activated Vβ21.3+ T cells occurring 4 weeks after the infection.15 The introduction of vaccination against SARS-CoV-2 has significantly diminished the chance of developing MIS-C6 and only a few cases of MIS-C despite vaccination have been reported, all mild and not requiring intensive care unit (ICU) admission for respiratory or cardiac support.7 8 Here, we report five patients who developed MIS-C despite prior SARS-CoV-2 immunisation, a phenomenon which we define as ‘breakthrough MIS-C’. Four of the patients required ICU admission. Since these severe disease presentations could provide insight into the pathophysiology of MIS-C and closely related hyperinflammatory syndromes, such as KD, we investigated these breakthrough MIS-C cases in detail for their TCR Vβ T cell expansion and antibody reactivity.

Case presentation

Within a time window of 5 months, between December 2021 and April 2022, five patients (all male, 12.2–17.2 years old) were hospitalised and diagnosed with MIS-C after previous immunisation against SARS-CoV-2 (figure 1). Although we did not have viral specimens available for genomic sequencing, the timeframe in which these patients were admitted was very suggestive of triggering of MIS-C by the late delta (patient 1, P1) variant and first Omicron wave (P2–P5).9 All patients adhered to the international WHO criteria for MIS-C (online supplemental table E1).10 Two (P2, P3) and three patients also met the diagnostic criteria for complete and incomplete KD, respectively (online supplemental table E2).11 All patients were treated with intravenous immunoglobulins (IVIG, 2 g/kg in 8–12 hours), aspirin (30–50 mg/kg/day, reduction to 3–5 mg/kg/day once fever had subsided for 48 hours) and methylprednisolone (30 mg/kg/day, maximum 1 g/day, 3 days) within 10 days from onset. Three patients were admitted to the ICU because of circulatory insufficiency requiring inotropic support (P1, P2 and P4); one patient (P3) was admitted to the ICU due to hypotension but recovered without inotropic support (online supplemental table E2). One patient was not admitted to the ICU (P5). All patients except for P3 showed a markedly reduced cardiac ejection fraction which rapidly normalised, a feature very typical for MIS-C but not seen in KD. None of the patients developed coronary artery aneurysms, a complication that is more typical of KD.11 A short case description of all patients is provided in the online repository (online supplemental data E1).

Prior to their MIS-C episode, all patients had received two doses of messenger RNA (mRNA) vaccines (BNT162b2) based on mRNA encoding the spike protein of the original Wuhan strain, apart from one patient (P4) who was only vaccinated once. P5 was vaccinated two times, but developed SARS-CoV-2 infection only 3 days after the second vaccination and was likely not fully protected against SARS-CoV-2 yet (figure 1).

All patients had antibodies against the nucleocapsid (anti-N) as well as the spike protein (anti-S) in pre-IVIG serum samples, indicating recent SARS-CoV-2 infection prior to the admission for MIS-C (online supplemental figure E1). The five breakthrough MIS-C patients had higher anti-N antibody levels compared with healthy controls with previous COVID-19 infection and vaccination <7 days prior to sampling (n=7) and patients with breakthrough COVID-19 infection despite vaccination (n=3), but this difference was not significant.

For four of the five patients, EDTA plasma samples were available, of which the earliest ones were selected (P1: 1.4 months post-IVIG; P2: none; P3: 1 day post-IVIG; P4: pre-IVIG; P5: 1 day post-IVIG). Concentrations of various proteins (ie, granulocyte colony-stimulating factor, S100A8, soluble interleukin (IL) 2 receptor alpha (CD25), fractalkine, chemokine ligand 2, C-X-C Motif Chemokine Ligand 9 (CXCL9), C-X-C Motif Chemokine Ligand 10 (CXCL10), interferon gamma, IL-17A, IL-18, IL-6, soluble suppression of tumourigenicity 2, granzyme B, tumour necrosis factor alpha, tumour necrosis factor-related apoptosis-inducing ligand, vascular endothelial growth factor A, N-terminal pro-atrial natriuretic peptide, angiopoietin-2) were measured by Luminex and compared with levels in the historical KD patients (n=10), healthy paediatric controls (n=3) and non-vaccinated MIS-C patients previously measured.12 Increased values of certain inflammatory proteins (eg, S100A8, CXCL9, IL-18, CD25, ST-2, angiopoietin-2, ANP) were seen in the breakthrough MIS-C patients, comparable to MIS-C (online supplemental table E3).12

Next, immunophenotyping was performed in the same four patients, using flow cytometry to assess the distribution of Vβ subunits in T cells, and compared with the distribution seen in historical KD patients vaccinated against SARS-CoV-2 in the same Q4 of 2021 (n=10). Vβ expansion was defined as a frequency of Vβ T cells that exceeded the reference mean value plus two times the SD in healthy children according to the corresponding age range, as previously described.5 The breakthrough MIS-C patients presented with Vβ21.3+ T cell expansion and—to a lesser extent—some other Vβ subsets (ie, Vβ5.1, Vβ3.6, Vβ17, Vβ18, Vβ20) varying per patient, similar to MIS-C patients1 (figure 2). The most noticeable coincident Vβ expansion was observed for P5, in whom Vβ17 expansion was observed as well.

The T cell expansions observed in breakthrough MIS-C patients contrasted with the observations in vaccinated historical KD patients (n=10) (figure 2). The two patients with the earliest sampling post-onset of MIS-C (<8 days; P1 and P5) displayed the highest frequency of Vβ21.3+ T cells exceeding the reference mean value plus ten times the SD, which is compatible with the transient nature of their presence in blood.1 No clinical associations were found between the frequency of Vβ21.3+ T cells and clinical or biochemical markers, including C reactive protein level, ICU admission or inotropic support.

In all patients, Vβ21.3+ T cells had increased expression of T cell activation markers (HLA-DR, CD38) compared with Vβ21.3− T cells and KD controls (n=10) (figure 3). In P1, P3 and P5 activated Vβ21.3+ T cells (HLA-DR+, CD38+) had increased expression of exhaustion markers (PD-1, TIM-3) (online supplemental figure E2). P4 was excluded from the latter analyses because there were too few activated Vβ21.3+ T cells for interpretation.

Given the rarity of breakthrough MIS-C, we conducted classical HLA class I typing to explore the potential role of genetic susceptibility. However, no associations with specific HLA alleles were identified (online supplemental table E4).

Discussion

These findings in breakthrough MIS-C patients show clinical and biological features consistent with unvaccinated MIS-C patients, including a temporary decrease in ejection fraction, need for inotropic support and the hallmark of Vβ21.3+ T cell expansion.

All five patients presented within a short timeframe of 5 months, coinciding with the first wave of Omicron BA.1 in 4/5 patients. After April 2022, neither additional breakthrough MIS-C patients were identified nor any unvaccinated MIS-C cases were admitted altogether, confirming that MIS-C had disappeared.13 The disappearance may relate partly to the rise in prior SARS-CoV-2 infection in the general population and COVID-19 vaccination status. More importantly, it is a strong argument for the decreased abilities of the subsequent Omicron variants to induce a hyperinflammatory response,14 since otherwise, breakthrough MIS-C cases as well as MIS-C in unvaccinated children should have continued to occur.

A few reports have described anti-N positive patients with clinical MIS-C after vaccination,71517 without cellular confirmation of Vβ typing. Additionally, studies have reported cases of MIS-C following vaccination against SARS-CoV-2, suggesting the vaccination itself to be the trigger for hyperinflammation (designated as MIS-V).1720 However, natural concomitant SARS-CoV-2 infection could not be definitively excluded in some of the reported patients.151720 Nevertheless, MIS-C was also described in patients negative for anti-N within a maximum of 4 weeks after one17 18 or two17 19 20 vaccination doses. Considering the short time frame in which the presented breakthrough MIS-C cases occurred during broad implementation of SARS-CoV-2 RNA vaccination in this age group, we believe these cases developed the ultrarare MIS-C despite and not due to previous vaccination. In particular, P1–P4 had been vaccinated several months before their MIS-C episode, without any health issues following the vaccination. This indicates that a direct effect of the vaccination is highly unlikely in our series.

Finally, the clinical presentation of MIS-C partially overlaps with other hyperinflammatory syndromes such as toxic shock syndrome.21 The latter is caused by an exacerbated cytotoxic adaptive immune response triggered on the binding of bacterial superantigens in a non-specific manner to T cell receptors and major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules.22 Such immune triggering in an MHC-unrestricted manner causes massive polyclonal T cell activation and cytokine release. Previous computational models have suggested structure and sequence homology of the SARS-CoV-2 spike glycoprotein with the bacterial superantigen staphylococcal enterotoxin B that holds a high-affinity motif for binding T cell receptors as an explanation for the cytokine storm that leads to multiorgan dysfunction in MIS-C.23

In addition to the expanded Vβ21.3+ T cells, some of the breakthrough MIS-C patients had expansions of other Vβ subsets as well. This was most notably seen in P5, who showed Vβ17 expansion as well. While a toxin-related immune reaction—such as that induced by staphylococcal enterotoxin B, which typically drives Vβ17 expansion24—cannot be definitively excluded, similar expansions of different Vβ subsets have been reported by others1 25 and are not consistently observed in all (breakthrough) MIS-C patients. Therefore, they are likely non-specific, in contrast to the Vβ21.3+ signature typical for MIS-C, or Vβ2+ expansion typically seen in toxic shock syndrome, which we did not observe in any of our breakthrough MIS-C patients (figure 2).

The clinical cases presented in the current study indicate that potentially life-threatening breakthrough MIS-C in SARS-CoV-2 vaccinated patients seems unlikely to be related to in vivo superantigenic properties of the spike protein. We did not find any association with classical HLA alleles, consistent with our previous study.1 Although an incomplete seroconversion following prior vaccination may be related to the MIS-C presentation as the consequence of a mild breakthrough infection, the anti-SARS-CoV-2 antibody levels during presentation are not suggestive of these lines of thinking. Other as yet elusive components and virus-related mechanisms to trigger the selective Vβ T cell expansion in MIS-C should be considered.
