Introduction

Acquired chronic radial head (RH) dislocations continue to present a significant challenge for paediatric orthopaedic surgeons. Typically, children have previously undergone multiple operations prior to referral. In addition to RH dislocation, there is often concomitant complex deformity, such as coexisting bony deformity, length discrepancy, and RH dysplasia, which often preclude acute correction with internal fixation. We report our 20-year experience of upper limb (UL) circular hexapod frames with ulnar osteotomy for gradual indirect RH reduction.

Hexapod constructs confer a number of benefits, including gradual multiplanar correction in six axes, permitting lengthening and angular manipulation of the ulna with indirect retensioning of the interosseous membrane and ultimately improved RH stability. It also provides dynamic, real-time assessment and continuous programme adjustment to maximise RH congruence. Other benefits compared with existing external fixator techniques include the use of a lower-profile two-ring construct with an open antecubital fossa to maintain elbow and wrist motion. This, in turn, prevents stiffness and allows the child to maintain functional use of the arm during treatment. To the authors knowledge, there are no technical reports or published results describing the use of a two-ring hexapod frame for correction of chronic RH dislocation in children or adults.

This study aims to describe the senior author's preferred surgical technique, which has been used for the last 20 years in our paediatric tertiary institution with a specialist interest in limb reconstruction.

Aims

The primary aim was to assess the success rate in maintenance of RH reduction. Secondary aims were the degree of correction achieved, child and parent satisfaction, range of movement, return to theatre, and unexpected complications.

Author's Preferred Technique

Preoperative Planning

Prior to surgery radiographs of the elbow and full-length forearm are taken of both the affected and unaffected side (for anatomical comparison) using scalar ball for measurements. Three-dimensional imaging, such as computed tomography scan, is not normally necessary, unless there is concern that there will be a block to reduction, e.g., multiple hereditary exostosis (MHE) with radial neck exostosis. Formal deformity assessment, ulnar osteotomy site, and external fixator planning with Taylor spatial frame (TSF) or any hexapod is recommended. The author's preferred technique is the use of proximal ring referencing with a virtual hinge. Typical principles and stages of correction include: (1) ulnar lengthening with over-distraction of the RH to allow clearance of the capitellum; (2) predominant angulation and very rarely minimal translation, i.e., to line up RH with capitellum; and (3) docking of RH without articular compression. The frame is locked once the RH is congruent and the radiocapitellar joint space is equal to the radiohumeral joint space.

Procedure

There are a number of important anaesthetic considerations. Typically, a general anaesthesia is performed without muscle relaxants. No regional blocks are performed; this permits visualisation of any muscle twitching during insertion of wires and pins. A nerve block, if required, is done at the end of procedure.

Following sterile preparation, the arm is free draped with no tourniquet and a radiolucent arm board is used. An examination under anaesthesia (EUA) is performed, and elbow range of motion is documented.

Proximal fixation (ulna only) is undertaken first. Fixation involves placement of a coronoid 4.5-mm hydroxyapatite-coated half pin (Fig. 1), mounted on a 105-mm 5/8th ring. Further fixation is with a 1.5-mm or 1.8-mm coronal plane reference wire (ulna only) from medial to lateral, and wires are tensioned 90–100 mm Hg and an additional 3-mm half pin using oscillating cordless drill to reduce the chance of ulnar nerve entanglement.

Coronoid 4.5-mm pin

Distal fixation (radius and ulna) requires a trans-radioulnar wire to “lock” the distal segment across four cortices. Care is taken to oscillate through soft tissues and interosseous membrane. The radius is manipulated, and the wire is seated on bone before passing through third and fourth cortex to ensure good hold. It is preferable to go through the ulna first, being a narrower bone distally. Rotate to check bones locked and move as single unit and err on the side of slight supination. This wire is critical to allow the radius to be pulled distally as the ulna lengthens. Watch the hand for any movement of fingers during wire insertion. Mount an appropriately sized 80–100 mm ring ensuring the arm is central and no soft tissue impingement. Secure with an additional 1.5-mm fine wire and 3-mm half pin from the distal radius system of Smith and Nephew (ulna only).

Complete the frame by attaching the TSF struts. Record strut number and length.

The struts can then be temporarily detached proximally to allow access for osteotomy. Perform a low-energy osteotomy using 2.5-mm drill, saline to cool, and small osteotome as per preoperative planning. The osteotomy should be close to the proximal pin site (Fig. 2). Ensure to check osteotomy is complete on image intensifier with translation in two planes and clinically with the feeling of osteotomy surfaces grating but gently.

Proximal ulna osteotomy close to proximal ring pin to maximise correction and tension on the interosseous membrane

Finally, reattach struts and correlate with previously recorded numbers. The forearm is positioned in slight supination. Tenodesis effect is checked to ensure there is no tethering of soft tissues. The arm is then meticulously cleaned and pin-site dressings are applied.

Postoperatively there should be clear documentation of peripheral nerve assessment. Physiotherapy is started immediately to maintain elbow and wrist flexion/extension. Prior to discharge, true anteroposterior and lateral TSF protocol radiographs with frame markers and scalar ball are obtained. Frame and deformity data parameters are double checked and inputted into TSF hexapod (or equivalent) software. Typically, the latency period is 5 days after which lengthening will begin at a rate of 0.75 mm/day.

The sequence is to distract the ulnar osteotomy adequately distal to the capitellum followed by changing the apex of the ulnar osteotomy depending on whether anterior or posterior by gradual angulation and maintaining a bit of length on the concave side until the radius is redirected towards the capitellum confirmed clinically by palpation and corroborated with fluoroscopic images that the RH is aligned in all planes. Once this is achieved, the ulnar osteotomy regenerate is compressed enough to make sure that the capitellum and the RH have a gap a bit bigger than the ulnar and trochlear joint space.

When the regenerate is uniform, the TSF struts are exchanged to Ilizarov rods with triple hinges so that the patient can then load the palm of the hand to stimulate bone formation and the rods are gradually dynamised. Translation of the fragments is avoided, as it may not tension the interosseous membrane (Figs 5 and 6).

Frame Removal

When radio-capitellar joint reduced and osteotomy site has radiographic evidence of union. Typically, this is completed under general anaesthetic with application of above elbow cast brace for further 4 weeks to protect the regenerate.

Materials and Methods

Patients were identified from a prospective limb-reconstruction database in a single-tertiary paediatric centre. All children treated with a circular/hexapod frame for chronic RH dislocations between 2000 and 2021 were eligible for inclusion.

Basic patient demographics, clinical range of motion, and radiographic parameters, including carrying angle, proximal ulna dorsal angulation (PUDA), proximal ulna varus angulation (PUVA), radiocapitellar congruence, change in ulnar length, variance at wrist, and bony union, were recorded. Return to theatre and unexpected complications were also recorded.

Results

A cohort of 127 UL frames were identified. Of these, 34 chronic RH dislocations (15 anterior and 17 posterior) were eligible for inclusion. The mean age at time of surgery was 10.6 years (5–17 years). Typical underlying pathologies included post-traumatic chronic Monteggia (17), MHE (11), nail-patella syndrome (2), Ollier's disease (2), osteogenesis imperfecta (1), and rickets (1).

At final follow-up of 26 cases (76%) had maintenance of congruent RH reduction. Only three cases (8%) re-dislocated; one re-dislocated secondary to new injury, and the remaining two both had MHE with ongoing growth disturbance between the ulna and radius and tethering within the forearm. Five children (15%) had some degree of minor persistent incongruency.

Mean follow-up duration was 4.2 years (9 months to 11.5 years). Mean radiographic correction achieved in coronal plane was 9.5°, sagittal plane 7.3°, and humeral forearm angle was 12°. Mean final gain in ulnar length was 7.25 mm, and for congenital deformities there was a final variance of −7 mm. All cases achieved bony union. Two children (6%) required secondary bone grafting. Mean frame duration was 166 days. Total mean range of motion at final follow-up was 64° (0–85) supination, 54° (0–90) pronation, with an extension–flexion arc of 2° (0–10) to 138° (120–145).

Due to the retrospective nature of the study, preoperative ranges were not available for comparison in all patients. A total of 15 patients had a complete data set of pre- and post-operative range of motion for comparison (Table 1).

The senior author's surgical technique has evolved over time. Prior to 2007, all ULs frames undertaken were classical Ilizarov fixators with traditional hinges, which had to be changed in clinic for the plane of correction. Following this, there was a gradual transition to using a hexapod construct (TSF™). The main reason for this change in practice was ease of use for both surgeons and families. The hexapod system allows easier correction in multiple planes with less frame adjustments for children and families, requires less frequent attendances, provides easy to understand prescriptions, coloured struts, and the ease of turns empowers children to become actively involved with their own frame correction. Since 2012, all UL frames have been exclusively hexapod constructs.

Discussion

Chronic RH dislocation is an extremely challenging problem to treat surgically. We have demonstrated a safe and effective technique that does not routinely require any open reduction of the RH or reconstruction of the annular ligament even in longstanding chronic cases.

The principles of this method lie in the relationship between the bony “ring” of the forearm and the interosseous membrane. Typically, patients have a deformity affecting the ulna. However, the true deformity may be very complex and three-dimensional. In simplistic terms, in anterior dislocations many cases demonstrate an increased apex anterior deformity (particularly post-traumatic cases), the interosseous membrane becomes relatively slack and the RH is dislocated anteriorly.1 In the average child, the normal PUDA is 5.7° posterior, although this can be compared with the contralateral elbow to account for normal variation.2 Through restoration or over-correction of the normal bony anatomy with an apex posterior proximal ulna, tension can be restored to the interosseous membrane which can be used indirectly to drag, reduce, and help maintain RH stability.

Using these principles, we have demonstrated that most patients undergoing frame correction achieve complete correction or minor subluxation. Only two cases re-dislocated gradually in the medium term, and previous assessment of a smaller cohort demonstrated that mild residual subluxation is very well tolerated clinically.3 In many cases, this subluxation was due to altered RH shape due to dysplasia rather than true subluxation

To the authors knowledge, this is the first study to describe the role of a two-ring hexapod construct with percutaneous ulnar osteotomy for indirect RH reduction, without the routine need for open reduction or annular ligament reconstruction in a chronic dislocation.4 It is also both the largest series with the longest follow- up currently reported in the literature.

The current published literature regarding the use of external fixator in RH dislocation is very limited and is almost exclusively very small number case series reporting the results of various external fixation constructs, including monolateral fixators, Ilizarov minifixator, and multiring traditional circular Ilizarov.3,5–7 However, these studies generally reported positive patient satisfaction, improved clinical range, maintenance of RH reduction, and absence of significant complications at follow-up, which are confirmed by the findings of this study.

Whilst in the vast majority of studies radial dysplasia is listed as an absolute contraindication to surgery, we have demonstrated the potential for successful surgery in select patients.8 A pragmatic approach to radial dysplasia is used in our institution, provided there is some degree of concavity to the RH and potential for at least a partially congruent joint consideration was given to reduction. In these cases, families and children are counselled appropriately in the preoperative period regarding the prognosis, risk of incomplete remodelling or persistent minor subluxation. We believe that, particularly in the younger age children, there is still potential for remodelling of the RH and that the capitellar shape may improve as further growth appears. We also have demonstrated in a previous smaller cohort that minor subluxation of the RH from persistent dysplasia is clinically well tolerated.

It has been observed by the senior author that in those who present late and who have not had a surgical reduction development of elbow and wrist pain is common, particularly if the dominant side is affected. If left untreated, progressive elbow valgus and prominence of RH are common. Gradual stretching and tension on posterior interosseous nerve can also act as a secondary source of pain generation. It is the authors' opinion that the concept of recreating anatomy is beneficial in the long term and is likely to make future reconstructive procedures easier should they be needed.

Conclusion and Clinical Significance

This study is an important source of information for preoperative counselling and prognosis. The majority of reported studies focus predominantly on post-traumatic chronic Monteggia injuries. This study has demonstrated the viability of external fixation and indirect reduction for a wide range of pathologies, including skeletal dysplasias. It does, however, highlight important prognostic information regarding the risk of recurrence. Whilst the vast majority of children do well regardless of the underlying pathology, children who have both significant growth remaining and a primary underlying condition that causes continued growth disturbance within the forearm such as MHE are at higher risk of recurrence. The children with the best prognosis are those with post-traumatic dislocation and limited RH dysplasia.

In conclusion, we believe that hexapod frame-assisted reduction for chronic RH dislocation in children is a safe and effective tool for a complex and difficult problem. The Sheffield Protocol provides a safe, dynamic, and methodological solution to an otherwise challenging pathology.
