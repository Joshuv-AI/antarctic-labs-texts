ABSTRACT
AbstractPatients with atypical variants of Alzheimer's disease (AD) often present at a younger age with predominantly non‐amnestic impairments and a more aggressive disease course. Historically, individuals with atypical presentations have not been included in large‐scale clinical trials, which typically focus on late‐onset, sporadic amnestic‐predominant AD. Consequently, treatment options and research efforts specific to atypical AD remain limited. The emergence of amyloid‐targeting therapies that slow disease progression underscores these challenges, as evidence supporting their efficacy in early‐onset amnestic and non‐amnestic AD variants is scarce. This perspective article argues that atypical AD represents an excellent disease model for clinical trials and proposes strategies to address critical gaps in clinical trial design for this population. Key considerations include optimizing participant selection approaches, establishing syndrome‐specific or surrogate biological and clinical endpoints, and fostering advocacy to enhance early and accurate diagnosis, equitable representation, and outcomes for these populations.

INTRODUCTION

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is increasingly recognized as a biologically and clinically heterogeneous condition. 1 , 2 , 3 While the “typical” presentation is characterized by a gradually progressive amnestic syndrome, 4 AD can also present in a variety of “atypical” syndromes. 1 We use “atypical AD” as an umbrella term encompassing predominantly non‐amnestic presentations of AD. However, atypical AD should not be considered a homogeneous diagnostic entity, as substantial heterogeneity exists across its clinical variants, which include dysexecutive AD (dAD), 5 logopenic variant primary progressive aphasia (lvPPA), 6 posterior cortical atrophy (PCA), 7 behavioral variant AD (bvAD), 8 and corticobasal syndrome due to AD (CBS‐AD) 9 (Table 1). Atypical variants are enriched in early‐onset cases (first symptoms <65 year old) 1 , 10 as they account for up to one‐third of early‐onset all‐cause dementia cases, with a reported prevalence of 15 to 65 per 100,000 in individuals aged 45 to 65. 11 , 12 However, they can occur at any age. Importantly, atypical AD variants exhibit distinct clinical profiles and syndrome‐specific patterns of tau deposition and neurodegeneration, rather than simply representing younger presentations of late‐onset amnestic AD. 3 As such, therapeutic mainstays must be adapted to better address the clinical needs of these patients.

Several terminology distinctions should be made explicit from the outset. While atypical AD is enriched among individuals with early‐onset AD (EOAD), these terms are not interchangeable. Atypical AD refers to non‐amnestic, syndromic presentations of AD and can occur across the age spectrum, whereas EOAD is defined solely by age at symptom onset (typically <65 years), regardless of clinical phenotype. It should also be noted that atypical AD is, in the vast majority of cases, sporadic. As such, it is not synonymous with dominantly inherited AD (DIAD), an autosomal‐dominant form of AD that accounts for about 10% of EOAD cases. 3 This article does not focus on DIAD, which, unlike atypical AD, already benefits from an established clinical trial infrastructure.

Patients with atypical AD face unique challenges. The specific impairments associated with these variants have direct consequences for functional independence. For example, predominant aphasic features in language AD may impair communication in social or occupational settings, while visuospatial deficits in PCA can preclude driving or reading early in the disease course. As such, these variants often affect individuals during periods of high occupational and family responsibility and are associated with a disproportionate socioeconomic burden compared to late‐onset amnestic AD. 13 In addition, the combination of non‐amnestic clinical presentations and frequent younger age often leads clinicians to overlook a neurodegenerative cause (e.g., favoring primary psychiatric/mood, primary sensory etiologies, hormonal changes), resulting in frequent misdiagnoses and/or delayed diagnosis. 3 , 14 , 15 , 16 Patients with atypical AD also frequently experience a more aggressive rate of cognitive decline compared to those with late‐onset, memory‐dominant AD. 17 , 18 , 19 As a result, patients may have more advanced symptoms at the time of diagnosis, hampering timely intervention and limiting opportunities for research participation.

From a biological standpoint, all AD variants share core neuropathological features, including amyloid beta (Aβ) plaques and neurofibrillary tau tangles. However, these variants exhibit marked clinical heterogeneity, which is thought to arise from differential vulnerability and degeneration of large‐scale brain networks. 1 , 20 This distinction has important implications for clinical trial design. While biomarker confirmation of AD pathology is necessary to ensure etiological specificity, it is not sufficient to account for the heterogeneity in clinical presentation across atypical variants. Patients may differ substantially in their predominant cognitive domains (e.g., language, visuospatial, executive, or behavioral), rates of progression, and functional impact, all of which can influence both eligibility criteria and the sensitivity of outcome measures. In addition, atypical AD variants are often associated with a higher tau burden, especially at younger ages of onset, 1 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 and follow clinical trajectories that are more directly driven by AD pathology rather than by co‐pathologies such as TAR DNA‐binding protein 43 (TDP‐43) proteinopathy or cerebrovascular disease. 25 , 26 As such, these presentations have been proposed to represent relatively “purer” manifestations of AD compared to late‐onset amnestic forms. 27

The heterogeneous nature of AD, exemplified in atypical forms of the disease, has posed major challenges for early diagnosis, research inclusion, and the development of targeted treatments or interventions. This systematic underrepresentation 28 has been recognized as a gap for patients with debilitating conditions who sorely need access to treatment options and research opportunities. 13 Indeed, most recent large‐scale Phase III randomized clinical trials (RCTs) examining the efficacy of disease‐modifying agents have heavily relied on memory‐based inclusion criteria, excluded lower age ranges, and neuroimaging outcomes (i.e., magnetic resonance imaging [MRI] and tau positron emission tomography [PET]) that emphasize temporal lobe involvement (Table 2). 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 This gap has become even more apparent with the recent regulatory approval of lecanemab and donanemab across multiple countries, which are Aβ‐targeting monoclonal antibody (mAb) therapies that have been shown to moderately slow clinical decline in early symptomatic AD. 29 , 30 These trials primarily enrolled amyloid‐positive individuals with amnestic mild cognitive impairment (MCI) or mild amnestic dementia, reflecting the typical, memory‐dominant AD phenotype. As such, while current appropriate use recommendations allow treatment in atypical AD variants, uncertainty remains regarding efficacy in patients presenting with diverse predominant symptoms affecting language, visuospatial processing, executive function, behavior, or motor function. In fact, the donanemab trials reported a trend toward lower efficacy in individuals with a high tau PET burden. 30 While these associations were found in an older population, high neocortical tau is characteristic of atypical AD, especially at younger ages. Complementing these results, recent neuropathologic evidence from aducanumab‐treated individuals demonstrated superficial cortical Aβ clearance, but no tau clearance. 34 These findings highlight the urgent need for more inclusive research and treatment strategies that account for AD heterogeneity.

The overarching aim of this perspective article is to provide a framework to adapt the clinical trial landscape to atypical variants of AD, with a focus on disease‐modifying therapies. We will first discuss the unique challenges and opportunities that atypical AD presents for the design of inclusive clinical trials. We will also review recent clinical, biological, and technological advances that can be leveraged to optimize trial design. We outline ongoing efforts, including longitudinal studies tracking cognitive and neurodegenerative trajectories in atypical AD, which may serve as platforms for trial‐ready cohorts. Finally, we delineate a series of recommendations for trial design that include or are specifically dedicated to atypical AD and underscore the importance of systemic advocacy across education, healthcare, community engagement, and research to enhance awareness, diagnostic accuracy, and referral pathways for research participation.

This perspective article builds on prior discussions and consensus among a multidisciplinary group of clinicians, researchers, and trialists, convened during a dedicated session at Alzheimer's Association International Conference (Toronto, July 29, 2025), and represents a joint effort between the Alzheimer's Association International Society to Advance Alzheimer's Research and Treatment Atypical AD Professional Interest Area (PIA) and the Clinical Trials Advancement Methods (CTAM) PIA.

CHALLENGES AND OPPORTUNITIES IN ATYPICAL AD

Several challenges have contributed to the underrepresentation of atypical AD in large‐scale trials of disease‐modifying agents. A central barrier is clinical heterogeneity across atypical variants. In contrast to late‐onset, memory‐dominant AD where memory measures are consistently used as diagnostic measures or primary outcomes, distinct phenotypes in atypical AD complicate the development and selection of outcome measures that capture the full spectrum of impairments. This limitation constrains both disease staging and the definition of clinically meaningful change. For example, the Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) 35 scale may not adequately capture impairment in patients whose primary deficits involve language, visuospatial processing, or executive functions. 36 Such heterogeneity also raises practical questions for trial design: Should atypical AD be integrated into conventional AD trials with the addition of outcome measures capturing non‐memory domains or studied separately with phenotype‐specific outcome measures? While reducing heterogeneity may improve interpretability, these design choices, combined with the lower prevalence of atypical AD relative to late‐onset amnestic AD, may limit sample sizes and complicate trial logistics. 37

Biological heterogeneity further raises considerations for trial design. Neuroimaging markers used to determine tau positivity or track treatment effects do not reflect the significant variability in patterns of tau deposition across AD phenotypes. 38 , 39 , 40 , 41 In atypical AD, tau deposition and atrophy follow syndrome‐specific patterns that often spare medial temporal regions. 24 , 42 , 43 Distinct trajectories have been described, including parieto‐frontal involvement in dAD, 5 , 20 , 44 , 45 , 46 left temporo‐parietal areas in lvPPA, 42 , 47 , 48 , 49 occipito‐parietal cortices in PCA, 7 , 16 , 50 , 51 anterior‐predominant areas in bvAD, 8 , 44 and asymmetric patterns of cortical involvement in CBS‐AD. 52 , 53 Consequently, regions of interest (ROIs) that focus on temporal lobe regions commonly used in tau PET studies may underestimate disease burden in atypical cases and be suboptimal for measuring therapeutic effects. 54 , 55 As with clinical outcomes, this calls attention to a trade‐off between practicality and statistical power (phenotype‐agnostic ROIs) versus precision (phenotype‐specific ROIs). In addition, some atypical variants often harbor higher amyloid and tau burden late‐onset, memory‐dominant AD. 21 , 55 , 56 , 57 , 58 Given the suspected reduced efficacy of current mAbs in patients with high tau burden, 30 should atypical AD be primarily approached with alternative therapeutic strategies – for example, tau‐targeting agents or co‐therapies addressing both amyloid and tau?

Despite these challenges, atypical AD also presents unique opportunities and may serve as an excellent model for trial design. 27 Compared with late‐onset amnestic AD, atypical variants decline more rapidly, 17 , 26 , 56 , 59 making cognitive declines more readily observable over traditional timelines for intervention trial. 60 Figure 1 illustrates more rapid rates of deterioration on the CDR scale in atypical AD phenotypes compared to late‐onset amnestic AD, based on a cohort combining datasets from Mayo Clinic Rochester and the Longitudinal Early‐Onset Alzheimer's Disease Study (LEADS). 61 However, we interpret these findings cautiously, as the CDR may underestimate the severity and progression of non‐amnestic symptoms. We hypothesize that measures with greater specificity to language, visuospatial, executive, behavioral, or motor domains would be even more sensitive to change in these populations, underscoring the need for their development and validation. This would corroborate studies finding shorter survival from symptom onset to death in atypical AD compared to typical AD, 5 , 62 a finding that is less dependent on the limitation of cognitive outcomes. Moreover, despite heterogeneity, substantial overlap exists across atypical presentations. 16 , 27 For example, most lvPPA patients exhibit deficits outside the language domain, 47 , 63 and dAD patients frequently show episodic memory impairment. 44 , 45 , 64 Neuroimaging likewise demonstrates relative overlap in tau deposition, atrophy, and functional alterations. 21 , 23 , 42 , 65 , 66 , 67 These findings suggest that certain cognitive and neuroimaging measures may be broadly applicable across atypical AD.

Importantly, the clinical trajectories of atypical variants more directly reflect AD pathology as the primary driver of symptoms, with less influence from co‐pathologies. 25 , 26 Neuroimaging studies support this, showing tau patterns tightly linked to atrophy and syndromic variability. 68 , 69 , 70 , 71 , 72 In contrast, late‐onset amnestic AD frequently reflects mixed pathology, 73 with vascular, α‐synuclein, and TDP‐43 co‐pathologies becoming increasingly prevalent with age and independently contributing to clinical symptoms. 74 , 75 , 76 , 77 , 78 Atypical variants of AD, particularly those with earlier onset, may be less influenced by such co‐pathologies and instead more directly reflect AD‐specific neurodegenerative processes. On this basis, one could hypothesize that therapies targeting amyloid and tau pathology could exert differential or potentially greater clinical effects in atypical AD, particularly if applied early in the disease course. However, this hypothesis remains untested, as atypical variants have been largely underrepresented or excluded from clinical trials of disease‐modifying therapies. As such, any suggestion of differential treatment response should be interpreted with caution, and there is a clear need for dedicated studies to evaluate the efficacy of anti‐amyloid and other disease‐modifying interventions in these populations.

Addressing these challenges requires integrating advances in the clinical and biological characterization of atypical AD. Prior work delineated syndrome‐specific impairments across variants, including simultagnosia and oculomotor apraxia in PCA, 7 , 79 word‐finding pauses and impaired sentence repetition in language variant of AD, 80 , 81 executive dysfunction in dAD, 5 , 45 and asymmetric parkinsonism and apraxia in CBS‐AD. 82 In addition, considerable heterogeneity exists within atypical AD variants. For instance, four distinct dAD subtypes have been identified, with differences in age at symptom onset and cognitive profile 45 ; PCA can present asymmetrically or along ventral/dorsal axes, influencing clinical features 7 , 50 ; and language variant of AD encompasses a broad spectrum of syndromic presentations with differing atrophy patterns and clinical profiles. These most commonly include lvPPA 6 , 83 but also cases that do not neatly fit established PPA classifications, 84 as well as rarer presentations such as agrammatic PPA 80 and primary progressive apraxia of speech. 85 These distinct profiles may provide a foundation for the development of syndrome‐specific outcome measures in clinical trials.

The organization of the LEADS 86 has been a foundational accomplishment for such advancements. LEADS is an international, multi‐site, longitudinal, observational study of sporadic EOAD, enrolling patients who present with a spectrum of amnestic and non‐amnestic AD phenotypes, do not harbor a known pathogenic mutation associated with dominantly inherited AD, and are under age 65 at enrollment. For instance, a recent study from LEADS created a standardized regression‐based indicator derived from a common neuropsychological battery, which demonstrated reliable measurement of decline across EOAD regardless of phenotype. 60 Parallel progress has been made in neuroimaging. A multi‐cohort study including LEADS data validated a MRI‐based meta‐ROI that strongly correlated with cognitive impairment in EOAD, 87 with specificity to AD pathology. A recent study highlighted the benefits of individualized tau PET ROIs compared to traditional meta‐ROI in tracking longitudinal tau‐PET, a methodology that could show promise in atypical AD. 88 Such measures show early promise for tracking therapeutic effects on large‐scale physiology across AD phenotypes in clinical trials.

Novel technologies may also show promise in the development of tools that can accurately capture and track fine‐grained impairments across AD phenotypes and, therefore, advance trial design in atypical AD. Digital cognitive tests have emerged as scalable, cost‐effective tools for monitoring cognition, in AD and other neurodegenerative syndromes. 89 , 90 , 91 , 92 , 93 , 94 In atypical AD, early applications include speech and language analysis in PPA variants to characterize and track speech disorders. 95 Furthermore, low‐cost and scalable artificial intelligence (AI)‐powered computational linguistic approaches have proven highly useful in identifying subtle markers of cognitive decline, with high diagnostic accuracy in PPA and PCA. 96 , 97 Other tools based on visual psychophysics have been shown to distinguish PCA from common ophthalmic conditions and may support the diagnosis and monitoring of visuoperceptual deficits. 98 Importantly, digital testing platforms are increasingly compatible with remote and unsupervised administration and have demonstrated strong feasibility and high correspondence with traditional neuropsychological measures in AD clinical trials. 91 , 99 These advances enable the integration of telemedicine and decentralized trial models, including remote cognitive assessments, virtual visits, and hybrid study designs. Such approaches may be particularly relevant for atypical AD populations, given their younger age at onset, ongoing occupational and family responsibilities, geographic dispersion, and the relative rarity of these syndromes. By reducing travel demands and site burden, telehealth‐enabled strategies can broaden access to clinical trials, enhance participant diversity, and support more frequent longitudinal monitoring with improved retention.

Further, advances in AI‐based neuroimaging also hold promise. 100 , 101 , 102 For example, machine learning‐based fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) PET tools have demonstrated high accuracy in classifying AD phenotypes, including atypical variants. 100 Such approaches may outperform traditional ROI‐based methods for diagnosis, phenotyping, and monitoring therapeutic response at the individual level and may facilitate multi‐site enrollment by improving cross‐site consistency in phenotyping.

In sum, atypical AD as a syndromic spectrum poses undeniable challenges for trial design, largely due to its clinical and biological heterogeneity. Yet its unique features may more accurately reflect the full phenotypic expression of AD and position it as an excellent model for trial design. Embracing this heterogeneity, rather than excluding it, offers the opportunity to create cognitive and neuroimaging tools that can be applied across the broader phenotypic spectrum of AD, advance disease understanding, and ultimately enable more inclusive and generalizable clinical trials.

CONSIDERATIONS FOR CLINICAL TRIAL DESIGN

The importance of better representing atypical AD variants in therapeutic research is increasingly recognized; however, effective strategies to achieve this goal remain challenging. Simply incorporating atypical cases into conventional frameworks risks reducing statistical power and obscuring variant‐specific responses, especially in smaller trials. Ultimately, this will require a deliberate framework that aligns biological precision with clinical diversity. Building on expert consensus, we outline below a set of practical recommendations to consider in guiding the design of more inclusive clinical trials across the AD phenotypic spectrum.

Broaden inclusion criteria and lower age thresholds

Atypical variants of AD are disproportionately represented among EOAD cases, yet most disease‐modifying therapy trials impose lower age limits that exclude many individuals living with AD. 103 Sporadic EOAD can present as early as the fourth decade of life. 5 Broadening eligibility criteria to include younger participants with biomarker‐confirmed AD regardless of clinical phenotype or conducting trials for individuals with atypical variants of AD would better capture the biological and clinical diversity of the disease.

Build trial‐ready cohorts

We define a trial‐ready observational cohort as one that integrates longitudinal clinical and biomarker characterization, validated and sensitive outcome measures, and an operational framework that enables efficient identification, screening, and enrollment of eligible participants into interventional studies. 104 Concrete steps toward establishing such cohorts in atypical AD include (i) the adoption of common diagnostic frameworks and standardized data elements across sites; (ii) harmonization of clinical assessments, including phenotype‐specific batteries tailored to non‐amnestic presentations; and (iii) systematic integration of AD biomarkers using validated and comparable thresholds to ensure biological confirmation and facilitate stratification. In addition, multi‐site coordination with centralized or interoperable data platforms should be prioritized to enable data sharing and cross‐cohort comparability. From an operational and regulatory perspective, ethics frameworks and Institutional Review Board processes should be structured to support rapid trial activation (e.g., through pre‐approved protocols or reliance agreements), thereby minimizing delays and enabling recruitment at earlier disease stages. LEADS represents a prominent example of a trial‐ready cohort in atypical AD and may serve as a template for future initiatives. Additional international efforts, including the Posterior Cortical Atrophy Asian Consortium, BeyeOMARKER, and the International LEADS (iLEADS), may further enhance representativeness and scalability.

Tailor outcomes to syndrome‐specific profiles

Current trial outcomes often emphasize memory performance, limiting sensitivity to changes in language, visuospatial, executive, or behavioral domains that define atypical AD variants. The CDR‐SB is a prime example of this, as it emphasizes memory function. This imbalance risks underestimating treatment effects in non‐amnestic phenotypes. In addition, each phenotype of AD merely represents an extreme end of a syndromic spectrum, where most patients often show impairments across several cognitive domains in addition to their predominant domain of impairment. To address this, outcome selection should be aligned with the trial design and target population and optimally extend beyond a single cognitive or behavioral domain. In large, inclusive trials encompassing the full AD spectrum, primary endpoints could rely on global or composite measures that are applicable across phenotypes, 105 , 106 ensuring comparability and statistical power. In contrast, trials exclusively dedicated to atypical AD phenotypes or multi‐arm designs stratified by clinical presentation should incorporate syndrome‐relevant primary outcomes that best capture the domains most affected if sufficiently powered (e.g., language measures for lvPPA, visuospatial tasks for PCA).

An important caveat is that apparent deficits outside the primary domain may partly reflect task demands rather than independent dysfunction, particularly in aphasic presentations where many neuropsychological measures rely heavily on language for instructions, comprehension, stimulus processing, or response demands and may therefore overestimate impairment across non‐language domains. 107 In addition, trials must also take into account that atypical AD variants may differ in several respects, including but not limited to rates of progression, 5 , 62 insight/anosognosia (e.g., greater impairment in bvAD relative to other variants), and neuropsychiatric symptom burden. 44 , 108 These differences may influence trial design, treatment adherence, and the suitability of patient‐reported endpoints.

Secondary endpoints should complement these measures by assessing functional independence (via informant report) and neuroimaging biomarkers, providing additional insights into disease progression and biological treatment effects. Outcome selection should also consider ecologically valid measures, including communication participation and other real‐world indicators of day‐to‐day functioning, that may better capture lived impairment than standard cognitive testing alone. In addition, stakeholder‐informed and community‐engaged approaches may help ensure that trial endpoints reflect relevant changes for patients and caregivers. Such a framework, combining harmonized primary outcomes with phenotype‐specific, functionally relevant, and multi‐modal secondary measures, would enhance both the sensitivity and clinical relevance of therapeutic trials, ensuring that efficacy signals are not obscured by outcome measures misaligned with the underlying phenotype.

Outcome selection naturally raises the question of defining a minimum clinically important difference. However, there is currently no consensus on what such a difference should be in AD trials, and proposed methods remain debated. 109 Moreover, the definition of a meaningful difference is dependent on the type of trial infrastructure. For instance, a Phase II trial investigating safety and feasibility should operate by different standards than Phase III trials assessing clinical efficacy. For the latter case, we provisionally propose that hybrid approaches integrating cognitive, functional, and informant‐based assessments are likely to provide the most robust estimate of clinically meaningful change and that observed effect sizes should, at minimum, be comparable to those reported in successful trials in typical amnestic AD.

Adapting trial design

Beyond participant inclusion and outcome measurement, innovation in trial design is essential for integrating atypical AD populations into therapeutic research. Traditional sporadic AD RCTs often have inclusion/exclusion criteria that do not accommodate the clinical and biological diversity of AD. Alternative and complementary trial architectures may better address this heterogeneity and facilitate the development of mechanism‐based therapies.

As a practical guiding principle, atypical AD participants may be included in broader AD trials when the therapy targets a mechanism shared across phenotypes, a common endpoint can capture significant change, and subgroup analyses are pre‐specified. In contrast, dedicated atypical AD or phenotype‐specific studies are more appropriate when syndrome‐specific outcomes are needed or when clinical or biological features suggest treatment response or progression may differ from typical amnestic AD. For rarer phenotypes, basket or platform approaches may offer a practical middle ground.

Several precedent‐setting efforts across neurodegeneration suggest that these approaches are operationally plausible. In autosomal‐dominant AD, the Dominantly Inherited Alzheimer Network Trials Unit showed that shared platform infrastructures can be implemented in biologically defined populations, 110 while the European Prevention of Alzheimer's Dementia project was developed as an adaptive proof‐of‐concept platform for secondary prevention. 111 More recently, Boxer and colleagues extended this logic to tau‐focused and related neurodegenerative indications through the Alzheimer's Tau Platform (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT06957418), the Progressive Supranuclear Palsy Clinical Trial Platform (NCT07173803), and platform‐design work in microtubule‐associated protein tau mutation carriers. 92 Outside AD, the HEALEY ALS Platform Trial further illustrates that multi‐regimen platform trials can operate at scale in neurodegenerative disease. 112 , 113 Although none of these directly solves the challenges of atypical AD, they provide a useful framework for how trial infrastructure might evolve. Phase II proof of concept and feasibility studies

Phase II studies provide a critical opportunity to pilot and validate novel biomarkers and outcome measures before large‐scale implementation. These smaller, more flexible trials can evaluate the sensitivity of imaging markers (e.g., tau PET, FDG PET, or MRI connectivity measures), test digital or cognitive composites tailored to specific variants, and assess feasibility across sites. Atypical AD may be particularly suited for these trials given the more rapid change in clinical and biomarker outcomes compared to late‐onset amnestic AD. Insights gained from such studies can inform the design of subsequent basket or phenotype‐specific Phase III trials, enhancing both methodological and clinical relevance.

Phenotype‐specific trials

Dedicated phenotype‐specific RCTs are designed to evaluate treatment efficacy within a distinct clinical variant of AD, for instance, in participants with PCA only. These trials enable more precise interpretation of therapeutic effects in relation to the cognitive and network‐level pathophysiology characteristic of the targeted phenotype. Outcome measures can be selected to align closely with the predominant cognitive or behavioral impairments, providing greater sensitivity to clinically meaningful change. Nevertheless, given the substantial overlap in symptom profiles across atypical AD variants, the inclusion of cross‐domain, mood/neuropsychiatric, and/or daily function measures remains valuable to capture broader impairments. In addition to clarifying efficacy, phenotype‐specific trials can generate data on safety, tolerability, and progression trajectories unique to each variant, informing both regulatory guidance and clinical translation. The primary limitations of these designs lie in the smaller sample sizes inherent in the lower prevalence of atypical AD, and even rarer individual variants, as well as reduced generalizability to the broader AD phenotypic spectrum. These challenges emphasize the importance of multi‐site collaborations, harmonized protocols, and trial‐ready cohorts to ensure feasibility and statistical robustness.

Multi‐arm/platform trials

In contrast, multi‐arm trials evaluate several interventions simultaneously within the same protocol and can stratify participants by phenotype, biomarker status, or both. This structure facilitates direct comparison of treatment responses across different atypical variants, while minimizing redundant control arms. Adaptive multi‐arm designs can also reallocate participants to more promising interventions as efficacy signals emerge. These trials are particularly suited for testing multiple disease‐modifying strategies targeting common mechanisms (e.g., amyloid or tau aggregation) across AD phenotypes. However, multi‐arm trials also entail methodological and statistical complexity due to potential treatment–arm interactions and the need for harmonized endpoints.

Basket trials

Basket trials allow diverse clinical phenotypes to be enrolled under a shared biological infrastructure defined by amyloid and tau positivity. 114 Within a single master protocol, such designs can (1) apply common biomarker‐based inclusion criteria while permitting syndrome‐specific secondary outcomes, (2) use adaptive randomization to optimize cohort allocation based on interim efficacy signals, and (3) incorporate platform flexibility, allowing the addition of new interventions or atypical subgroups without restarting the trial. However, basket designs also carry limitations: rare, atypical cohorts may remain underpowered, biological heterogeneity across phenotypes can complicate signal interpretation, and operational and regulatory complexities may challenge implementation. Anchoring enrollment in biology rather than phenotype thus requires careful attention to these constraints but ultimately balances efficiency and inclusivity in line with the shift toward mechanism‐based therapeutics.

Emulated trials

Emulated trials are based on observational studies and are designed to approximate the structure and rigor of RCTs using real‐world data sources such as electronic health records, registries, and longitudinal cohorts. 115 This approach is particularly valuable when conducting a randomized experiment is impractical or unethical. For example, re‐conducting trials to evaluate the efficacy of already approved mAbs in atypical AD would be highly challenging, given the ethical and logistical constraints of withholding an approved therapy. In such cases, emulated trials leveraging observational data from atypical AD patients treated with mAbs compared to historical, untreated controls may help in assessing treatment efficacy in this population. Emulated trials offer several advantages: They are grounded in causal inference principles, can better reflect “real‐world” populations, are cost‐efficient, and can fill critical gaps left by traditional RCTs. However, they remain susceptible to biases stemming from non‐randomized designs, selection effects, and missing or noisy data. It is also important to bear in mind that emulated trials are typically not used for drug approval by regulatory agencies; rather, they are used to fill gaps in RCTs following drug approval, for instance, in a subset of a clinical population that was underrepresented or excluded in the original RCT.

However, it is important to keep in mind that these approaches entail specific challenges. 116 , 117 Shared placebo groups may introduce bias if progression rates differ between phenotypes, harmonizing endpoints across domains requires careful pre‐specification, and smaller subgroup sizes necessitate multi‐center collaboration. Leveraging trial‐ready cohorts will be essential to accelerate recruitment and ensure the representativeness of atypical AD populations. Regulatory frameworks must also evolve to accommodate flexible structures, adaptive analyses, and varied progression trajectories inherent in these designs.

Across all of these approaches, feasibility and regulatory fit remain central considerations. Some atypical AD phenotypes may have insufficient prevalence to support adequately powered, stand‐alone Phase III trials, making multi‐center recruitment, shared infrastructures, and biomarker‐enriched basket or platform designs particularly relevant. At the same time, these approaches are not regulatory shortcuts; their value depends on rigorous pre‐specification of eligibility criteria, endpoints, subgroup analyses, and control strategies, as well as evidence that the selected outcomes are clinically relevant in the intended population. For very rare phenotypes, external‐control approaches may help fill post‐approval evidence gaps, but they are more likely to provide supportive than confirmatory evidence.

AWARENESS AND ADVOCACY

Lastly, ensuring equitable inclusion of atypical AD in clinical research requires action beyond trial design, extending to policy, education, and intentional community engagement. A coordinated, multidisciplinary approach is essential, bringing together clinicians, researchers, regulators, industry partners, and patient advocacy groups to define priorities and implement practical solutions. 118 , 119 , 120 Key areas of focus include the following: Education and awareness. Equip clinicians to recognize atypical presentations and their distinct diagnostic profiles. Integrate these concepts into medical education, specialty training, and continuing professional development to reduce diagnostic delays and misclassification. Diagnostic pathways. Guarantee equitable access to biomarker testing and advanced neuroimaging for early and accurate identification of atypical and early‐onset cases, regardless of geography or socioeconomic status. Research collaborations. Build harmonized, multi‐center infrastructures and registries (e.g., LEADS) to enable rapid recruitment for trial‐ready cohorts and data interoperability across cohorts. Community and patient engagement. Actively involve patients and caregivers in research planning, trial design, and dissemination to ensure that research questions and outcomes reflect their lived experience and priorities. Policy and regulatory alignment. Advocate for frameworks and funding mechanisms that explicitly support research on atypical AD and EOAD, fostering cross‐phenotype collaboration and innovation.

Systemic advocacy is not only a strategic necessity but also an ethical imperative. By coordinating efforts across education, healthcare, research, and policy, the field can enhance awareness, improve diagnostic accuracy, streamline referral pathways, and ensure that individuals with atypical AD have equitable access to participation in clinical trials and the full spectrum of therapeutic innovations.

CONCLUSIONS

Advancing therapeutic research in atypical AD carries both scientific merits and ethical responsibility. Building on the momentum of recent therapeutic breakthroughs, the field must broaden the scope of RCTs to encompass the full phenotypic spectrum of AD. Although this expansion presents substantial challenges, it also offers critical opportunities to advance our disease understanding and to serve underrepresented patient populations with unique needs. Incorporating atypical variants of AD into mainstream clinical trials is crucial to ensure generalizable and equitable access to emerging therapies that is truly representative evidence for diverse populations. This perspective outlines an initial framework for developing innovative, rigorous, and inclusive RCTs tailored to atypical AD.
