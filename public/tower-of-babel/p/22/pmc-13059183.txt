Spatiotemporal lag effects of surface water quality on liver cirrhosis mortality risk in China: a nine-year time-series study of environmental driving mechanism
Authors: Qiuping Chen, Quan Sun, Mingyi Shao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSurface water is a major source of domestic water supply in China, yet its quality remains uneven despite recent improvements. Chronic exposure to surface-water pollution has been implicated in liver injury, but evidence linking water quality to cirrhosis mortality—particularly accounting for delayed and non-linear effects—remains limited. We investigated the spatiotemporal associations between surface water quality and cirrhosis mortality in China.MethodsWe conducted a nationwide ecological study integrating surface water monitoring data and cirrhosis mortality data from 2013 to 2021. Distributed lag non-linear models (DLNMs) were applied to assess non-linear and lagged associations (0-2 years) between overall water-quality classes and key pollution indicators, including total phosphorus (TP), chemical oxygen demand, dichromate method (CODCr), and five-day biochemical oxygen demand (BOD5). Analyses were stratified by sex and urban-rural residence.ResultsSurface water quality was associated with cirrhosis mortality in a non-linear, delayed, and population-specific manner. Moderate and poor water quality exhibited distinct temporal risk patterns, with sustained exposure to moderate pollution associated with a higher cumulative mortality risk. TP and CODCr showed clear non-linear associations with mortality that intensified at longer lags, whereas BOD5 displayed a U-shaped relationship with relative risks remaining below unity. Stronger and more persistent effects were observed among males and rural residents.ConclusionsOur findings indicate that long-term exposure to moderate surface-water pollution and persistent nutrient and organic contamination contribute to cirrhosis mortality in China. Water-quality management strategies should account for delayed health effects and population vulnerability, with targeted interventions prioritising sustained pollution reduction and protection of high-risk groups.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-26792-9.

Introduction

Cirrhosis is the terminal stage of chronic liver disease and a major cause of global mortality. Progressive hepatic fibrosis leads to irreversible loss of liver function and severe complications, including portal hypertension, ascites, variceal bleeding, and hepatic encephalopathy [1, 2]. Chronic liver disease causes approximately 2 million deaths annually worldwide, with cirrhosis responsible for most cases [3]. According to the GBD 2021, an estimated 58.4 million individuals were living with cirrhosis globally in 2021, accounting for 1.43 million deaths. Although age-standardised rates have declined, China’s absolute burden has continued to rise and is projected to remain high because of population ageing and growth, underscoring the need for strengthened prevention strategies in high-burden regions [4].

Surface water pollution has emerged as an important environmental determinant of chronic disease. Deterioration in water quality threatens drinking-water safety and may contribute to long-term health risks through sustained low-level exposure. The World Health Organization estimates that more than 2 billion people lack access to safely managed drinking water [5, 6]. In China, surface water quality is classified into six categories (Class I to inferior Class V) [7]. Although national water quality has improved following implementation of the Water Pollution Prevention and Control Action Plan, exceedances of chemical oxygen demand (CODCr), five-day biochemical oxygen demand (BOD5), and total phosphorus (TP) remain common, mainly driven by agricultural and industrial pollution sources [8–12].

Surface water is the primary source of domestic water supply in China, and variations in source-water quality may not be fully offset by treatment processes, particularly during periods of intensified hydrological exchange such as the rainy season [13]. In rural areas, shallow groundwater and well water are hydraulically connected to surface water and therefore more vulnerable to contamination [14]. Hepatotoxic pollutants, including cyanobacterial toxins and emerging organic contaminants, have been detected in surface and groundwater and have been associated with liver injury and increased mortality risk in previous studies [15–17].

Accumulating evidence suggests that water quality deterioration is associated with liver injury and cirrhosis-related outcomes [18]. CODCr and BOD5 reflect organic pollution and oxygen-consuming substances linked to industrial effluents, pesticides, and microbial toxins that may contribute to hepatic oxidative stress and inflammation. Total phosphorus serves as a proxy for eutrophication, under which cyanobacterial toxins such as microcystins—well-established hepatotoxins—are more likely to occur and have been associated with liver injury and fibrosis [19–21]. These health effects are often non-linear and delayed, limiting the applicability of conventional linear models [22].

To address this gap, we integrated nationwide surface-water monitoring data with cirrhosis mortality records in China and applied distributed lag non-linear models (DLNMs) to characterise lagged and non-linear associations between water quality indicators and cirrhosis mortality at the population level. This study aims to inform environmental health risk assessment and prevention strategies rather than to establish individual-level causal mechanisms.

Methods

We sequentially conducted the following procedures: data collection and processing, correlation analysis and variable dependency assessment, multicollinearity diagnostics, construction of DLNMs, followed by subgroup and heterogeneity analysis (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Research framework diagram

Data sources

This nationwide ecological study integrated surface water quality data and cirrhosis mortality data in China from 2013 to 2021. Monthly surface water quality data were obtained from the National Surface Water Quality Monthly Report released by the China Environmental Monitoring Station [23]. These data included the proportions of moderate and poor water quality and the exceedance rates of CODCr, BOD5, and TP, calculated as the number of monitoring sections exceeding national standards divided by the total number of sections in each month.

Graphical data were digitised using GetData Graph Digitizer through coordinate calibration and automated extraction, with manual point selection applied when automated recognition was insufficient. Parallel manual extraction was conducted as an auxiliary validation to reduce uncertainty related to digitisation accuracy, and inter-rater reliability was quantified using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) to assess the consistency of extracted values [24]. All ICC values were ≥0.925, exceeding the predefined acceptability threshold of 0.90 and indicating satisfactory reliability of the digitisation process. Digitisation was applied only to aggregated summary indicators reported graphically at the national level, rather than to individual-level or site-specific measurements (Additional file 1: Table S1).

Cirrhosis mortality and prevalence data were obtained from a nationwide population health surveillance project conducted by the China National Population Health Science Data Center and were stratified at the national level by urban-rural residence and sex [25].

To control for socioeconomic confounding related to healthcare access and disease management, four indicators were collected from official statistical yearbooks: per capita gross domestic product, annual healthcare expenditure, education level (China Statistical Yearbook 2024), and the number of hospital beds per 1,000 population (China Health Statistics Yearbook 2024) [26, 27]. All socioeconomic variables were measured annually, with no missing data.

Data preprocessing

Surface water quality indicators exhibited pronounced seasonal variation consistent with precipitation patterns (Fig. 2). Missing observations in 2017 and several additional months were addressed using a hierarchical imputation strategy [28–30]. Seasonal-trend decomposition using Loess was first applied via the na_seadec() function in the imputeTS package [31], followed by linear interpolation for residual gaps (Formula 1). Imputation uncertainty was assessed using 10,000 Monte Carlo simulations, with the simulation mean adopted as the final estimate [32]. Monte Carlo simulations were used to propagate uncertainty from the imputation process under the assumption of random missingness, and the simulation mean was adopted to obtain a stable point estimate for subsequent analyses.Fig. 2Monthly trends of surface water quality in China

As cirrhosis mortality data were available annually, monthly surface water quality measurements were aggregated to annual means to construct an exposure metric reflecting long-term, cumulative population-level conditions, rather than short-term episodic fluctuations. This approach was adopted to align the temporal scale of exposure with the chronic nature of cirrhosis development and mortality, and to ensure consistency with the annually reported outcome data. Accordingly, the analysis was explicitly designed to estimate long-term associations at the population level, rather than acute or short-term effects. This aggregation reduced short-term meteorological noise, aligned exposure and outcome time scales, and facilitated assessment of cumulative and lagged effects, thereby supporting robust estimation of long-term associations between surface water quality and cirrhosis mortality [33, 34]. Correlation analysis and variable dependency assessment

As water quality indicators and cirrhosis mortality rates did not follow normal distributions (Shapiro-Wilk test, P < 0.05), Spearman correlation analysis was used to assess associations among water quality categories and the exceedance rates of CODCr, BOD5, and TP [35].

Correlation analysis was primarily conducted to identify interrelationships among exposure variables and to inform subsequent model specification by detecting potential redundancy and collinearity. These analyses were not intended to infer causal pathways but to support a parsimonious and statistically stable modeling strategy for the DLNMs.

Exploratory dependency analyses were additionally performed to further characterise relationships among selected water quality indicators. These analyses were used only as supplementary diagnostics to support decisions regarding variable inclusion and were not designed to evaluate mediation mechanisms or biological pathways linking water quality to cirrhosis mortality [36].

Multicollinearity diagnostics

Both water quality indicators and socioeconomic variables were considered as candidate predictors in the DLNM models. Multicollinearity among water quality indicators and among socioeconomic covariates was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs) and condition numbers [37, 38].

These diagnostics were conducted to evaluate potential redundancy and dependency among predictors and to inform subsequent model specification. Variables were retained or modeled according to predefined multicollinearity criteria to ensure numerical stability and interpretability of parameter estimates within an ecological time-series framework.

This strategy aimed to balance adequate control of confounding with the avoidance of unstable or over-parameterized models, rather than to establish etiological relationships among predictors.

Distributed lag non-linear model

DLNMs were employed to quantify non-linear exposure-response relationships and delayed effects of surface water quality indicators on cirrhosis mortality [39]. DLNMs incorporate cross-basis functions to jointly model exposure intensity and lag structure, allowing simultaneous estimation of exposure-response and lag-response associations [40, 41].

Model specification was guided by the results of correlation analysis and multicollinearity diagnostics conducted among water quality indicators and socioeconomic covariates. These preliminary analyses were used to evaluate potential dependency and redundancy among predictors and to inform the subsequent design of DLNM structures, with the aim of ensuring numerical stability and interpretability of parameter estimates. Exploratory dependency analyses were treated solely as supplementary diagnostics and were not intended to define mechanistic pathways linking water quality indicators to cirrhosis mortality.

Age-standardised cirrhosis mortality rates served as the outcome variable and were analysed at the national level and stratified by sex and urban-rural residence. Socioeconomic covariates that satisfied predefined multicollinearity criteria were included for confounding adjustment.

All DLNMs were implemented using the dlnm package in R [42]. Considering the temporal resolution of annual data and prior evidence, lag periods of 1–3 years were evaluated, and the optimal maximum lag was selected based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) [43]. Exposure-response and lag-response relationships were parameterised using natural cubic splines, with degrees of freedom defined as the maximum lag minus one to ensure sufficient model flexibility [44]. Given differences in water source use and exposure patterns across populations, analyses were stratified by gender and urban-rural residence. Given the limited number of annual observations within each stratum, subgroup analyses were explicitly framed as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Post-hoc power analyses and false discovery rate correction were performed to aid interpretation and to minimise the risk of overinterpretation, rather than to claim definitive subgroup effects, for each subgroup-lag-exposure combination based on observed effect sizes, variance estimates, and a two-sided α of 0.05. To address multiple subgroup comparisons across sex, residence, and lag structures, false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. These analyses were conducted to aid interpretation and assess the robustness of subgroup findings, rather than to redefine statistical significance thresholds [46]. All analyses and visualisations were performed in R using the tidyverse, forestplot, and ggplot2 packages.

Despite the concurrent improvements in overall surface-water quality and declines in cirrhosis mortality, the persistent increase in exceedance rates of key pollutants, particularly CODCr and TP, suggests potential discrepancies between composite water-quality classifications and specific pollutant burdens. This observation motivates further investigation into whether improvements in overall water-quality status translate into reductions in cirrhosis mortality and whether rising pollutant exceedances exert delayed or cumulative adverse effects. These hypotheses are formally examined using DLNMs to quantify the dynamic associations between surface-water quality indicators and cirrhosis mortality.

Correlation analysis

Spearman correlation analysis revealed several statistically significant associations among surface water quality categories and pollutant indicators (Fig. 4). Priority-quality water was positively correlated with moderate-quality water (r = 0.734, p < 0.01) and strongly negatively correlated with poor-quality water (r = −0.881, p < 0.001). Moderate- and poor-quality water proportions were also highly inversely correlated (r = −0.937, p < 0.001). Among pollutant indicators, CODCr exhibited a very strong positive correlation with TP (r = 0.937, p < 0.001), while BOD5 showed a moderate correlation with CODCr (r = 0.490), although this association did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.106). These findings indicate substantial overlap in information captured by several water quality indicators and suggest a high potential for collinearity if included simultaneously in regression models.Fig. 4Spearman correlation analysis of surface water quality indicators

Spearman correlation analysis of surface water quality indicators

Exploratory dependency analyses, including mediation and moderation models, did not provide evidence of significant indirect or interaction effects among the examined indicators (Additional file 3: Table S2; Additional file 4: Table S3). In mediation analyses, average causal mediation effects were not statistically significant across the tested pathways, and moderation analyses showed no significant interaction terms between water quality categories and pollutant indicators (all p > 0.05). These results do not support complex indirect or context-dependent dependency structures among the selected exposure variables.

Overall, the correlation and dependency analyses were used as diagnostic tools to characterise interrelationships among water quality indicators and to assess the risk of multicollinearity. The observed strong correlations informed subsequent DLNM model specification by guiding decisions on variable inclusion and model structure, with the aim of ensuring stable and interpretable estimation of lagged exposure-response associations with cirrhosis mortality.

Multicollinearity diagnostics were conducted for both surface water quality indicators and socioeconomic covariates to inform subsequent DLNM model specification. VIFs and pairwise correlations were calculated to evaluate redundancy and dependency among candidate predictors.

Gender and urban–rural stratification further revealed differential vulnerability. Male patients exhibited persistently higher lagged risks than females, consistent with gender-specific exposure patterns and metabolic susceptibility [48]. Rural populations experienced a pronounced excess risk at the 2-year lag, suggesting that exposure variability in decentralized water systems may translate into delayed but amplified health consequences.

Inferior-quality water and cirrhosis mortality

The association between inferior water quality and cirrhosis mortality showed strong temporal dependence. At lag 0 and lag 1, increasing proportions of inferior water were paradoxically associated with RRs below unity across all groups. However, this apparent short-term “protective” pattern reversed at lag 2, where mortality risk increased sharply and peaked at approximately 28% inferior-water proportion (Fig. 6B0-B2).

This lag-dependent reversal likely reflects the long latency of cirrhosis progression. Acute exposure to severely polluted water may trigger rapid behavioural avoidance, emergency water-source substitution, or cause deaths to be attributed to acute toxic events rather than cirrhosis. In contrast, prolonged exposure facilitates bioaccumulation of hepatotoxic substances through indirect pathways, ultimately accelerating fibrosis progression and increasing cirrhosis-related mortality. The persistence of gender and urban-rural disparities across lag periods further underscores the role of structural exposure differences rather than short-term behavioural responses alone.

TP exceedance and cirrhosis mortality

TP exceedance rates demonstrated a consistent inverted U-shaped relationship with cirrhosis mortality across all lag periods. Mortality risk increased rapidly with TP exceedance, peaking at approximately 26%, and declined toward the null as exceedance exceeded 35% (Fig. 6C0-C2). This pattern suggests that moderate TP pollution may represent the most hazardous exposure range, potentially due to eutrophication-related toxin release before large-scale intervention or exposure avoidance occurs [49].

Subgroup analyses revealed greater lag-related risk amplification among females, whereas urban–rural differences narrowed with increasing lag. These findings imply that gender-specific metabolic responses may modulate delayed toxicity, while improvements in urban water management may mitigate long-term exposure accumulation.

CODCr exceedance and cirrhosis mortality

CODCr exceedance exhibited a pronounced inverted U-shaped association with cirrhosis mortality, with peak risk occurring at approximately 28% exceedance. Importantly, the exposure level at which RR returned toward unity decreased progressively with longer lag durations, indicating that prolonged exposure lowers the threshold at which adverse effects manifest (Figs. 6D0-D2).

This pattern is consistent with the accumulation of refractory organic pollutants and sediment-associated compounds, which may sustain hepatic inflammatory stress even as bioavailable concentrations fluctuate [50]. Subgroup analyses showed consistently higher risks among females and an attenuation of urban-rural disparities with longer lags, suggesting that chronic exposure gradually overrides short-term infrastructural protections.

BOD5 exceedance and cirrhosis mortality

In contrast to other indicators, BOD5 exceedance exhibited a U-shaped exposure-response relationship, with relative risks remaining below unity across the observed range and lag periods. To formally assess whether this pattern reflected a genuine non-linearity rather than model artefact, we applied the established three-step test for U-shaped relationships proposed by Lind and Mehlum [51]. Both the linear and quadratic terms were statistically significant, the estimated turning point lay well within the observed exposure range, and slope tests confirmed a significantly negative association at lower BOD5 levels and a positive association at higher levels. Model diagnostics further indicated no substantial residual bias or heteroscedasticity, supporting the robustness of this non-linear pattern.

From a mechanistic perspective, BOD5 primarily reflects biodegradable organic matter and thus may act less as a direct hepatotoxic agent than as a contextual indicator of organic pollution dynamics [52]. Lower BOD5 levels may correspond to water systems with greater self-purification capacity and reduced persistence of more toxic co-pollutants, whereas higher BOD5 levels may signal increasing organic loading and co-exposure to oxidative stress-inducing compounds. Such a biphasic response is consistent with the toxicological concept of hormesis, in which low-level exposures are associated with lower risk while higher levels become detrimental [53]. While this interpretation warrants validation in individual-level studies, the combined statistical evidence and biological plausibility suggest that the observed U-shaped association for BOD5 is unlikely to be a spurious modelling artefact.

Discussion

In this nationwide ecological study, we applied DLNMs to characterise the spatiotemporal associations between surface-water quality and cirrhosis mortality in China. By jointly examining overall water-quality classes and key pollution indicators (TP, CODCr and BOD5), we demonstrate that the health effects of surface-water pollution are non-linear, delayed, and heterogeneous across population subgroups. These features are consistent with prior environmental epidemiological evidence showing that chronic liver outcomes are particularly sensitive to long-term, low-to-moderate environmental exposures rather than short-term extremes [54]. Our findings extend this literature by explicitly quantifying lag structures and identifying exposure ranges associated with the greatest cumulative mortality risk. Correlation and dependency analyses were used to inform model specification rather than to infer mechanistic pathways, thereby supporting a parsimonious and statistically stable modeling strategy.

A central observation is that moderate- and poor-quality water exhibit distinct temporal risk patterns. The inverted U-shaped relationship between the proportion of moderate-quality water and cirrhosis mortality suggests that intermediate levels of pollution may be associated with higher long-term disease burden than either excellent or severely degraded water conditions. This pattern may reflect a situation in which moderate pollution levels are insufficient to trigger behavioral avoidance or regulatory intervention, while remaining high enough to contribute to cumulative hepatic stress. Similar non-linear exposure–response relationships have been reported for other environmental stressors and chronic diseases, where sustained moderate exposure is linked to persistent oxidative stress and inflammatory responses [55]. In contrast, inferior water quality displayed a lag-dependent pattern, with weaker associations at short lags followed by increased risk at longer lags. This delayed effect is compatible with the slow and progressive natural history of cirrhosis and may indicate that short-term associations are masked by reporting dynamics or adaptive responses, while cumulative hepatotoxic effects become evident over time [56].

Indicator-specific analyses further highlight the importance of distinguishing among different dimensions of water pollution. Both TP and CODCr showed inverted U-shaped associations with cirrhosis mortality, with lag-sensitive inflection points shifting toward lower exposure levels as lag length increased. These findings are biologically plausible given existing evidence that chronic exposure to nutrient enrichment and refractory organic pollutants can contribute to liver injury through pathways previously implicated in inflammation, metabolic disturbance, and oxidative stress [55, 56]. In contrast, BOD5 exhibited a U-shaped association with mortality risk that remained below the null threshold, suggesting that BOD5 may primarily reflect biodegradable organic matter and co-vary with reductions in more toxic or persistent pollutants [52, 53]. Similar discrepancies between conventional water-quality indicators and health risk have been noted in previous environmental health studies, underscoring the need for indicator-specific interpretation rather than uniform regulatory weighting [57].

Consistent effect modification by gender and urban–rural residence further strengthens the public health relevance of these findings. Higher and more persistent risks among males and rural residents may reflect differences in occupational exposure, decentralised water-supply systems, and reduced buffering capacity against sustained pollution, as reported in previous studies of environmental liver disease risk [54, 57]. Although urban populations benefit from centralized treatment infrastructure, heterogeneous household-level water treatment and aging distribution systems may still permit meaningful exposure within certain pollution ranges. Together, these results suggest that uniform water-quality standards may insufficiently protect vulnerable subpopulations and that targeted mitigation strategies are warranted.

From a policy perspective, our results suggest several actionable implications. First, surface-water management strategies should prioritise long-term reductions in moderate pollution levels, which appear to be associated with the greatest cumulative health burden. Second, regulatory evaluations should explicitly account for delayed health effects, as short-term improvements in water quality may not immediately translate into reductions in cirrhosis mortality [55]. Notably, the observed lagged associations of 1–2 years indicate a potential window of opportunity for preventive intervention. Monitoring deterioration in water-quality indicators such as TP and CODCr could serve as an early warning signal for future increases in cirrhosis mortality. These findings support the development of surveillance systems integrating environmental monitoring with health-risk prediction, enabling timely policy actions such as pollution control and targeted screening in high-risk communities. Third, TP and CODCr emerge as priority indicators for protecting liver health, whereas BOD5 should be interpreted with caution as a direct proxy for cirrhosis risk. Finally, strengthening rural water-supply stability and upgrading urban distribution infrastructure may yield disproportionate benefits for high-risk populations [57].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, individual-level behaviors, clinical histories, and major liver disease risk factors were unavailable, limiting causal inference and raising the possibility of residual confounding. Future studies should address this limitation by constructing individual-level cohorts or applying causal inference approaches such as instrumental variable analysis. Second, the completeness of historical water-quality monitoring data was constrained by public data availability, necessitating imputation to ensure temporal continuity. This challenge is common in retrospective environmental studies, and future work should integrate multi-source data, including remote sensing products and cross-basin analogue methods, to improve exposure reconstruction. Third, although graphical data extraction was conducted using automated digitisation supplemented by manual verification and independent re-extraction, secondary data extraction inevitably entails limitations in precision compared with original records. Fourth, lag specification was constrained by the nine-year study period and annual temporal resolution, precluding assessment of longer-term effects that may be more closely aligned with the natural history of cirrhosis. Fifth, the limited time span reduced statistical power for subgroup analyses; these results should therefore be interpreted as exploratory signals of heterogeneity rather than confirmatory evidence. Finally, as an ecological observational study, our analysis establishes statistical associations rather than causal mechanisms, and ecological fallacy cannot be excluded [54]. Biological pathways linking pollutant exposure to liver injury and fibrosis progression could not be directly examined in this design. Future research should prioritise longitudinal individual-level cohorts integrating environmental exposure, clinical biomarkers, and health outcomes to more directly evaluate these relationships [56].

Concussions

This nationwide ecological study demonstrates that surface-water quality is associated with cirrhosis mortality in China in a non-linear and lagged manner. Moderate and poor water quality, as well as sustained elevations in TP and CODCr were linked to increased long-term mortality risk, with stronger effects observed among males and rural residents. These findings suggest that chronic exposure to moderate water pollution may contribute substantially to the population burden of liver disease. Public health strategies should prioritise long-term water-quality improvement and targeted protection of vulnerable populations to reduce cirrhosis-related mortality.
