Silent Inferolateral Postinfarction Left Ventricular Outpouching With Thrombus
Authors: José Martín Alanís-Naranjo, Sergio Alfonso Patrón-Chi, Pedro Sánchez-Tapia, Oscar Arturo Lozano-Cruz, Félix Damas-de los Santos, Miguel Ángel Cruz-Marmolejo, Daniel Campuzano-González, Gabriela Meléndez-Ramírez, Aloha Meave-González
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVentricular aneurysms (VAs) are rare complications of infarction. Inferolateral aneurysms with thrombus are uncommon and are often difficult to differentiate from pseudoaneurysms.Case SummaryA 60-year-old man with diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia was evaluated after incidental electrocardiographic evidence of inferior infarction and echocardiographic suspicion of pseudoaneurysm. Cardiac magnetic resonance revealed a large inferolateral VA with transmural fibrosis, preserved myocardial continuity, and an intracavitary thrombus. Coronary angiography showed diffuse right coronary disease with chronic occlusion of the posterolateral branch. Conservative management was chosen given transmural scar, absence of viability, unfavorable anatomy, and hemodynamic stability. Medical therapy included guideline-directed heart failure treatment, statin, and anticoagulation.DiscussionInferolateral VAs with thrombus are rare postinfarction findings. Cardiac magnetic resonance provides anatomic delineation, thrombus detection, and distinction from pseudoaneurysm.Take-Home MessagesVAs with intracavitary thrombus are rare and often clinically silent. Cardiac magnetic resonance is the gold standard for differentiating true aneurysms from pseudoaneurysms and for detecting thrombus. Early identification, even in asymptomatic patients, is critical to guide therapy and reduce systemic embolic risk.

On admission, the patient's physical examination and vital signs were unremarkable: blood pressure 100/74 mm Hg, heart rate 67 beats/min, respiratory rate 14 breaths/min, and oxygen saturation 93% on ambient air.

Past Medical History

The patient's medical history included longstanding type 2 diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia, all of which were under treatment and control. The patient had experienced an episode of intense chest pain 1 year earlier without seeking medical attention, and thereafter no further symptoms were reported.

Differential Diagnosis

Considerations included ventricular aneurysms (VAs) and pseudoaneurysms, which are outpouchings that may involve either ventricle, defined by abnormal or absent contractile motion of the affected wall. Most true VAs are caused by myocardial infarction, although they can also be caused by trauma, iatrogenic injury, Chagas disease, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, myocarditis, or sarcoidosis.

Serologic blood tests were unremarkable and excluded Chagas disease. Management

A conservative strategy was chosen given unfavorable coronary anatomy for revascularization, the transmural nature of the infarct making myocardial viability unlikely and thus limiting the potential benefit of revascularization, absence of clinical symptoms, and stable hemodynamics. Medical management included guideline-directed heart failure therapy, a high-intensity statin, and a direct oral anticoagulant.

Outcome

The patient was discharged asymptomatic, with scheduled close follow-up with the ischemic heart disease department.

Discussion

VAs are rare complications of transmural acute myocardial infarction (AMI), although cardiac, congenital, or metabolic disorders may also contribute to this condition.1 Approximately 80% of left VAs occur in the anterior or apical regions, usually as a result of occlusion of the left anterior descending artery.2

Inferior wall aneurysms account for 10% to 15% of left VAs, whereas basal inferior forms account for approximately 3% of cases.2 Aneurysms in the inferior and inferoposterior regions are rare, and involvement of the lateral wall is even less common.2,3 Even with conventional cardiac imaging modalities, it may be difficult to distinguish between a true VA and a pseudoaneurysm.1

Compared to patients without aneurysms, left VA is significantly associated with thrombus formation. Despite the widespread use of early reperfusion therapy, anticoagulation, and secondary prevention strategies that have reduced the overall incidence of aneurysm to <5%, aneurysmal remodeling continues to represent a significant substrate for intracavitary thrombus development, increasing the probability of a stroke and other systemic embolisms.3 Therefore, although documented cases of ischemic VAs of the inferior wall are rare,2,4 those with associated intracavitary thrombus are even rarer.

VAs and pseudoaneurysms are abnormal outpouchings of the ventricular wall that exhibit dyskinetic or akinetic wall motion. Unlike true aneurysms, pseudoaneurysms do not contain myocardium and are composed mainly of fibrous tissue, hematoma, and pericardium.5

The incidence of ischemic VA has significantly decreased, now reported at 0.2% in acute postinfarction settings, although it shares a common pathophysiologic mechanism with heart failure, affecting up to 35% of postinfarction patients. Both complications result from ongoing negative left ventricular remodeling, which may persist even after successful revascularization.1

A negative left ventricular remodeling pattern, characterized by infarct expansion, compensatory hypertrophy, and geometric transformation, results in diminished contractile efficiency and increased thrombogenicity, predisposing patients to mitral regurgitation, ischemic dilated cardiomyopathy, or cardiac rupture in severe cases.1

Left ventricular thrombus (LVT) often follows the development of a VA, especially in larger aneurysms, and it may cause systemic embolism, stroke, or visceral and limb ischemia, often without prior symptoms.1,6 LVT occurs more frequently after an anterior wall myocardial infarction and is typically associated with a VA, while it is less common after inferior infarctions. Aneurysmal hemodynamic stagnation promotes thrombus formation, initiating a self-perpetuating cycle of infarct thinning, endothelial disruption, and aneurysmal expansion.6

Apical VAs account for 92.2% of thrombus cases and are associated with a higher incidence of LVT compared with other locations.3,6 Apical aneurysms produce a blunt, distended ventricular contour with chamber enlargement and reduced apical flow velocity during mitral valve opening, leading to blood stasis and eddy currents that, combined with endothelial injury and hypercoagulability, create optimal conditions for thrombus formation.3

VA frequently presents with nonspecific clinical characteristics, primarily related to impaired systolic function, and is frequently accompanied by angina as a result of a high prevalence of multivessel coronary artery disease, which occurs in approximately 80% of cases.1

Evaluation of postinfarction VA requires a multimodal approach that includes echocardiography, CMR, and cardiac computed tomography (CCT). Echocardiography is often used initially given its availability, but suboptimal acoustic windows and prominent trabeculations may limit accuracy; in such cases, the use of contrast agents enhances endocardial border definition and facilitate thrombus detection.1

CCT and CMR have been described as valuable imaging modalities,5 although their use may be limited by time constraints, cost, and limited availability.7 CCT offers high spatial resolution for the diagnosis of VA and its distinction from pseudoaneurysms, which are identified by thin walls with low Hounsfield units compared with higher values in nonaneurysmal myocardium, while also enabling simultaneous coronary artery evaluation.1

CMR is considered the gold standard for evaluating left VA and LVT, offering superior spatial resolution and tissue characterization compared with echocardiography, particularly when acoustic windows are suboptimal.3 LGE sequences enable precise assessment of ventricular volumes, detection of LVT with high sensitivity and specificity, identification of myocardial scars and endocardial injury, and reliable differentiation between true aneurysms and pseudoaneurysms based on myocardial continuity.1,3,5

Distinguishing true aneurysms from pseudoaneurysms is challenging, as location and neck size are not definitive and may yield conflicting findings; diagnosis relies on integrating imaging features with clinical history. True aneurysms typically have a broad neck (>50% of the aneurysm diameter), contain fibrous tissue visible as delayed enhancement on CMR, and are separated from the pericardium by epicardial fat, whereas pseudoaneurysms lack this fat interface.5

Current guideline-directed therapy after AMI does not include specific management for patients with or at risk of VA, although heart failure treatment, anticoagulation for thrombus, and antiarrhythmic therapy are recommended. Symptomatic cases may require surgical revascularization and ongoing surveillance to identify progression or the need for advanced interventions.1

The identification of risk factors for LVT in patients with ischemic VA is essential for guiding anticoagulant therapy and reducing embolic risk, with LGE emerging as a predictor of thrombus formation.3 Management of LVT in patients with VA after myocardial infarction is divided into pharmacological and interventional approaches, with current guidelines recommending anticoagulation therapy for at least 3 to 6 months based on follow-up imaging.6

Vitamin K antagonist remains the standard therapy for LVT, but its limitations include strict monitoring, delayed onset, drug-food interactions, and increased bleeding risk in patients with antiplatelet therapy. Direct oral anticoagulants offer fixed dosing, predictable pharmacokinetics, and no monitoring requirements, achieving higher thrombus resolution than warfarin at 4 weeks and with comparable outcomes at 12 weeks, suggesting potential benefit in the acute phase, although robust randomized data remain lacking.8

Patients with VA remain at risk for heart failure, severe mitral regurgitation, ventricular arrhythmias, and sudden cardiac death, requiring ongoing surveillance to detect progression and treatment failure. Surgical resection is advised in selected cases, particularly with concomitant coronary artery bypass grafting, and it is recommended for patients with NYHA functional class III-IV, large VA, significant LVT, or arrhythmia arising from the aneurysm. Surgical ventricular reconstruction may also benefit those with advanced dilatation and associated mitral regurgitation.1

Persistence or recurrence of LVT despite treatment remains a challenging scenario.9 In patients with thrombus within an ischemic VA, thrombus nonresolution has occurred in 55.6% of cases and is associated with a significantly higher risk of adverse outcomes, including stroke, systemic embolism, and cardiac death. Risk factors for nonresolution include larger aneurysm area (>4.5 cm2) and thrombus area (>2.2 cm2) as well as myocardial infarction involving the left circumflex artery, emphasizing the need for individualized therapeutic approaches and consideration of prolonged or intensified antithrombotic strategies.9

We present a case of an asymptomatic patient with a large inferolateral VA secondary to an unrecognized AMI, complicated by an intracavitary thrombus, highlighting the importance of advanced cardiac imaging methods such as CMR in providing a comprehensive evaluation, precise anatomic delineation, and thrombus detection, as well as their crucial role in the assessment of postinfarction complications.

Conclusions

VAs complicated by an intracavitary thrombus represent a rare and diagnostically challenging postinfarction complication. In this context, CMR plays a pivotal role in distinguishing true aneurysm from pseudoaneurysm, enabling precise anatomic characterization, thrombus detection, and informed therapeutic decision-making.
