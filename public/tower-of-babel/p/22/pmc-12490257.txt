Lung virome convergence precedes hospital-acquired pneumonia in intubated critically ill patients
Authors: Hussein Anani, Grégory Destras, Simon Bulteau, Louise Castain, Quentin Semanas, Gwendolyne Burfin, Mélanie Petrier, Florian P. Martin, Cecile Poulain, Robert P. Dickson, Céline Bressollette-Bodin, Antoine Roquilly, Laurence Josset
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryHospital-acquired pneumonia (HAP) is one of the most common nosocomial infections, leading to significant morbidity and mortality in critically ill patients. HAP is previously associated with dysbiosis of the microbiota. However, the composition of the lung virome and its role in HAP pathogenesis remain unclear. Here, we longitudinally analyze the endotracheal virome in 87 critically ill patients, including 48 with HAP. Within the virome dominated by Caudoviricetes, a decrease in viral beta-diversity toward a bacteriophage-dominated signature and a distinct viral-bacterial interactome is observed 5–4 days before HAP onset. Lung virome composition, viral convergence before HAP onset, and conservation of 18% of the bacteriophage signature are validated in an external cohort of 40 patients. In silico causal inference further identifies bacteriophages associated with Streptococcus and Prevotella as a key regulator of HAP onset. These findings suggest an uncovered pathophysiological mechanism of HAP with virome involvement in lung microbiota dysbiosis. The discovery and validation studies are registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT02003196 and NCT04793568).

Introduction

Hospital-acquired pneumonia (HAP) is one of the most common causes of infection in intensive care units (ICUs) and has critical effects on patients, contributing to prolonged mechanical ventilation and increased mortality.1 Therefore, there is an urgent need to better understand the pathophysiology of this disease to develop innovative therapeutic options for prevention and treatment.

Recent studies have implicated the gut and respiratory bacterial microbiota in the pathogenesis of HAP. A longitudinal study combining 16S rRNA gene sequencing of rectal swabs and single-cell profiling of blood samples revealed that microbiota and immune function dynamics during critical illness are strongly interdependent. Intestinal dysbiosis was associated with compromised host defenses and increased susceptibility to HAP induction.2 Recently, a meta-analysis has questioned the respiratory bacterial composition in patients with HAP and ARDS (acute respiratory distress syndrome). Montassier and colleagues reported that HAP is associated with a depletion of the identified core group of microbes typical of healthy respiratory samples (Fusobacterium, Haemophilus, Prevotella, Streptococcus, and Veillonella), which have been shown to maintain lung homeostasis.3,4 However, to date, no studies have addressed the lung virome composition and diversity in ICU patients and its role in the pathogenesis of HAP.

In addition to the technical challenges associated with virome studies, the lung virome is particularly difficult to characterize due to the risk of environmental contamination in clinical respiratory samples,5,6 coupled with its low biomass and diversity.4 Virome studies have mainly focused on the diversity of viral communities in the gut and revealed their involvement in maintaining several functions such as intestinal homeostasis in balance with the bacteriome.7 Gastrointestinal and pulmonary virome studies have shown that Anelloviridae is the most abundant family in the eukaryotic fraction of the virome, but bacteriophages are the most dominant component in the whole virome and can influence the eubiosis of bacterial communities.7,8,9,10,11,12 In particular, bacteriophages are involved in the modulation of bacterial fitness, virulence, and abundance through different lifestyles (virulent/lytic or temperate/lysogenic prophage). In addition, they can directly activate the immune response through Toll-like receptor (TLR) signaling.13,14

Because of the role of the gut virome in the pathogenesis of human infections, we hypothesized that studying the respiratory virome response to critical illness would increase our understanding of the pathophysiological mechanisms of HAP. The aim of this study was therefore to characterize the dynamics of the respiratory virome in ICU patients in relation to different HAP susceptibilities.

Results

Clinical characteristics of the study population

We analyzed 121 endotracheal samples from 87 intubated brain-injured patients admitted in ICU from the Immunology and Brain Injury Study (IBIS). Baseline characteristics and clinical outcomes are described in Table S1. Samples were collected primarily during the fall-winter period (70 samples in total were collected between September and March) on day 1, 7, and/or 14 after ICU admission. Out of the 87 patients, 48 (55%) developed HAP at a median of 4 days (interquartile range [IQR] = 3–6 days) after admission, 31 (36%) developed ARDS, and 12 (25%) patients died before day 90. Except for gender, there were no demographic differences between HAP and no HAP patients. Additionally, no significant differences in comorbidities were observed between these groups. However, all patients with HAP (100%) received antibiotics during their ICU stay, compared to only 23% of no HAP patients. None of the patients in the discovery cohort received antivirals, antibiotics, or steroids before or at ICU admission (Table S1).

Lung virome composition in critically ill patients

Viral metagenomic sequencing was divided into four independent batches with a total of 42 sequencing controls (empty and MS2-spiked non-template controls to assess the risk of contamination along the process), and no batch effect was detected (Adonis permutation test, p value: p = 0.8 and Anosim test, p = 0.52) (Figure S1). After removal of non-viral reads (reads assigned to human, bacteria, fungi, or archaea), a median of 849,512 high-quality reads (IQR = 503,318–1,373,358) was obtained per sample (Table S1). The de novo assembly process followed by dereplication yielded a total of 256,304 operational taxonomical units (OTUs), of which 229,482 were unassigned and 26,822 were identified as viral OTUs (vOTUs) (Table S2). After remapping to vOTUs, normalization, and decontamination (Table S2), we obtained a median of 8,542 viral reads per kilobase per million mapped reads (RPKM) per sample with IQR = 4,900–12,344 (Table S1).

At ICU admission, we observed that the delay (in days) between HAP onset and sample collection had the largest effect on lung virome composition, accounting for 37% of the variation, followed by ARDS (20%) and HAP (17%), all of which showed significant effects (false discovery rate [FDR]-adjusted p < 0.05, Table S1 and Data S1). In contrast, variables such as mortality, age, gender, and antibiotics were not significant (Figure 1A). Then, we described the virome composition in the total ICU population. We found that Caudoviricetes bacteriophages and unclassified viruses in a second tier were the most abundant viruses (median of log10RPKM = 3.6 [IQR = 3.4–3.9] and 2.7 [IQR = 2.4–3.2], respectively) (Figure 1C; Table S3), corresponding to a median relative abundance of 64% (IQR = 41.2–77.3) and 6.7% (IQR = 3.5–12.3), respectively (Figure S2). Eukaryotic viruses were mostly represented by Alphatorquevirus (median log10RPKM = 1.9 with IQR = 0.3–2, and relative abundance of 0.7% with IQR = 0–1.2) and Enterovirus (median log10RPKM = 1.7 with IQR = 0–2, and relative abundance of 0.6% with IQR = 0–1.2) as shown in Figures 1C and S2. A total of 37 distinct Anelloviridae species were identified, including 19 torque teno viruses (TTVs), 17 torque teno mini viruses, and one torque teno midi virus (Figure S3). Additionally, we detected five Enterovirus genotypes, specifically Rhinovirus A49, A56, A71, and Rhinovirus B48 and B93 (Figure S3; Table S2). Only 0.4% of vOTUs within the lung virome were shared by >50% of the patients. This core virome was mainly composed of Caudoviricetes (73%), unclassified viruses (14%), and eukaryotic viruses (13%), with TTV19 being the most prevalent among them (Figure 1D).

Using PhaBOX2,16 5,201 out of 20,802 bacteriophage vOTUs were annotated in order to study their biological functions (Table S2). Among bacteriophages with known lifestyle, 58% of the lung virome exhibited a virulent lifestyle, which was significantly higher than the prediction made for previously published oral and gut viromes, with 42% and 40% of virulent lifestyle, respectively (Fisher’s test, p < 0.05) (Figure 1E).

Then, we questioned about the similarity of the lung virome composition in patients hospitalized in the ICU. We compared beta-diversity between specimens collected from a same patient (intra-patient) and from different patients (inter-patient) using three different indices: weighted Bray-Curtis and Hellinger to calculate distances based on viral abundance and Sorensen to account for viral presence-absence (Tables S3 and S4). High inter-patient dissimilarity indices were observed (Figure 1F), and they were significantly higher than intra-patient indices (Wilcoxon test p < 0.0001 for weighted Bray-Curtis; p < 0.001 for Hellinger, and p < 0.01 for Sorensen) (Figure 1F). These results demonstrated that the respiratory virome of critically ill patients exhibited considerable inter-individual variability, with moderate intra-individual stability.

Inter-patient respiratory virome diversity decreased in patients with HAP

To explore the global composition of endotracheal virome in relation to clinical outcome, we first studied differences between HAP and no HAP patients’ viromes at the alpha- and beta-diversity levels. Deceased patients without HAP (n = 9) were excluded from further analysis, as it was not possible to ascertain their HAP status. The total viral load (in log10RPKM) was not different between the HAP and no HAP groups (Wilcoxon test, p = 0.57) (Figure S4). The Shannon index, which measures diversity and evenness of the viral communities, were not statistically different between HAP and no HAP patients considering all time points (Wilcoxon test p = 0.13, Figure 2A, left) or over time with linear mixed modeling or locally estimated scatterplot smoothing (Figure S5; Table S4). Virome richness, representing the number of vOTUs, was not different between HAP and no HAP samples (Wilcoxon test, p = 0.39) (Figure 2B, left; Figure S6; Table S4). While principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity did not cluster HAP and no HAP patients (Adonis test, p = 0.544, r2 = 0.009) (Figure S7), non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) analysis showed that HAP samples were more similar to each other than no HAP samples (Anosim test, p = 0.01, stress = 0.2) (Figure S8). Moreover, inter-patient respiratory virome dissimilarity, measured by median Bray-Curtis, Hellinger, and Sorensen dissimilarity indices, was lower in HAP than in no HAP group (Figure 2C). Overall, the inter-individual variability of the virome was lower in HAP than in no HAP patients, suggesting the existence of a shared virome associated with the development of HAP in ICU patients.

Virome diversity scheme in patients before the beginning of HAP

We then sought to investigate whether the reduced interpersonal variability was associated with HAP onset, looking specifically at the period leading up to the onset of HAP. Therefore, we excluded samples from patients with HAP who already had HAP upon sampling and restricted the analysis to the first endotracheal aspirate in ICU patients who would later develop HAP (upcoming HAP group) and those without later documented HAP (no HAP group). Again, the virome alpha-diversity was not different between upcoming HAP and no HAP patients (Figures 3A and 3B. Nevertheless, PCoA showed that the virome in upcoming HAP was significantly different compared to no HAP (Adonis test, p = 0.001 and r2 = 0.062) (Figure 3C). These results were validated by NMDS analysis, which indicated a significant convergence of the upcoming patients with HAP who were closer to each other than no HAP patients (Anosim test, p = 0.001 and stress = 0.16) (Figure 3D). Finally, beta-diversity metric calculations validated the virome convergence in patients with future HAP where the median of Hellinger and Sorensen dissimilarities was significantly lower in comparison with no HAP patients (Wilcoxon test, p < 0.0001) (Figure 3E). Virome convergence 5 to 4 days prior to HAP induction

Then, we studied the virome dynamics over time to investigate when the virome convergence preceded HAP onset. We followed a sliding window spanning 2 days with a 1-day step between each window, counting backwards from HAP onset for cases and from median day of HAP onset (fourth day after ICU admission) for controls (Figure S9A). Median values of the three beta-diversity metrics remained unchanged over time in no HAP samples, confirming the absence of convergence in respiratory virome in controls (Figure 4A). In contrast, the median values of Hellinger and Sorensen dissimilarities were significantly lower in upcoming HAP than in no HAP samples in the 5–4 sliding window and increased over time in the HAP samples (Figure 4A; Table S5). These results confirmed the convergence of the respiratory virome 5 to 4 days prior to HAP onset, suggesting a possible causal role of the virome in the development of HAP. To identify vOTUs driving this convergence in upcoming patients with HAP, we compared the virome profiles of upcoming patients with HAP at the 5–4 day period to the virome profiles of no HAP patients using a Fisher’s exact test to identify differentially occurring (DO) vOTUs and linear discriminant analysis effect size analysis to identify differentially abundant (DA) vOTUs. In total, the signature comprised 571 vOTUs, including 220 DA vOTUs and 373 DO vOTUs (Figures 4B and S10; Table S6). Finally, we investigated whether the virome differed according to HAP etiology. We were able to define a viral signature specific to upcoming HAP caused by E. coli compared to other etiologies (Figure S11; Table S6 and Data S1).

Taken together, these findings suggest that the lung virome at ICU admission could discriminate patients at risk of HAP and even differentiate HAP etiology.

Virome influence on respiratory bacterial communities before HAP onset

Caudoviricetes bacteriophages have been described to contribute to bacterial depletion and affect intestinal homeostasis in inflammatory bowel diseases (IBDs),7 but the functions of these viruses in human lungs have not been thoroughly investigated. We thus questioned if the bacteriophages within the pre-HAP viral signature were associated with the composition of the lung bacteriome, particularly Streptococcus, Fusobacterium, Prevotella, Veillonella, and Haemophilus, whose depletion is associated with HAP and respiratory complications.3 We analyzed the 16S bacteriome profile in upcoming HAP and no HAP samples, and we checked the possibility of bacterial convergence before HAP onset. In contrast of virome, no significant convergence over time was observed using weighted Unifrac distance (Table S6; Figure S12). We then examined the correlations prior to HAP onset between the relative abundances of the bacteriophages from the viral signature (n = 455 vOTUs) and the core lung microbiome using Spearman’s rank correlation. Correlation profiles were significantly different between upcoming HAP and no HAP patients (Figure 4C). In particular, more inter-kingdom correlations were found in patients who later developed HAP (69/455 vOTUs significantly correlated in upcoming patients with HAP, vs. 27/455 in controls, Fisher’s test, p = 7.3265e−06) (Figure S13; Table S6). In addition, 49% of the vOTUs presenting a significant correlation were negative in upcoming patients with HAP, compared to only 38% in no HAP patients (chi-square test, p = 0.2). The majority of the viral-bacterial correlations identified before HAP involved Prevotella, with 93% of the vOTU-Prevotella correlations being negative. In contrast, viral-bacterial correlations in no HAP group involved mainly Fusobacterium with 85% of positive interactions (Figure 4C). By selecting all vOTUs with predicted hosts and lifestyle (n = 14/455), we found that the virulent bacteriophages Caudoviricetes_OTU_20114 and Caudoviricetes_OTU_51729 were significantly anticorrelated with their predicted hosts, Prevotella sp. HJM029 and Streptococcus parasanguinis, respectively, before HAP. However, in no HAP patients, there were no negative correlations between vOTUs and their predicted host (Figure 4C).

These data suggest that viral-bacterial lung interactions differ substantially between upcoming HAP and no HAP patients, with the viral signature mainly negatively associated with Prevotella in patients with HAP.

Validation of the HAP-associated viral convergence in an independent cohort

We validated our results in an external and independently recruited cohort. We analyzed samples from the PREV-HAP (preventive hospital-acquired-pneumonia) multicenter, randomized clinical trial, which tested interferon-gamma for the prevention of HAP in critically ill patients.17 The IBIS and PREV-HAP cohorts enrolled patients in different years and different clinical centers, with different inclusion and exclusion criteria but similar sample processing and sequencing (Data S1). Differences between the 2 cohorts are recapitulated in Table S7. We analyzed 58 endotracheal aspirates collected from 40 patients in the placebo group after ICU admission between April and October 2021 (Figure S9B). Only nine of them developed HAP with a median of 3 days (IQR = 2–5 days) upon admission. While there were more males among patients with HAP in the IBIS cohort, this was not the case in the validation cohort (Table S7). No differences in demographics, comorbidities, or antibiotics treatment were observed between HAP and no HAP patients. In addition, none of the patients received any antiviral or antibiotics before or at ICU admission. Additional clinical characteristics, sequencing, and viral assembly statistics are summarized in Tables S7 and S8.

Consistent with our previous results and among the 29,821 vOTUs identified in this cohort (Table S8), Caudoviricetes dominated the virome composition (log10RPKM median of 3.7 with IQR = 3.3–4.1 and relative abundance of 67.1% with IQR = 53%–78.9%) (Figures 5A and S14; Table S9), confirming the robustness of Caudoviricetes dominance in the respiratory virome of critically ill patients. We confirmed the bacteriophage lifestyle profile by observing 51% of vOTUs annotated as virulent, while 49% were predicted to be temperate (Figure S15; Table S9). In this validation cohort, since endotracheal specimens from patients with HAP were not available 4 days prior to HAP onset, we extended the sliding windows for dissimilarity analysis. The median dissimilarity indices in the upcoming HAP group were all lower than those in the no HAP group within the 5–3 day sliding window (Wilcoxon test p < 0.01 in Hellinger and Sorensen and p < 0.05 in Bray-Curtis) (Figure 5B; Tables S9 and S10). Altogether, these data confirm the previously observed viral convergence occurring up to 3 days before the onset of HAP, in addition to the dominance of the lung virome by Caudoviricetes.

Validation of the HAP-associated bacteriophage signature

To evaluate the ability of the viral signature from the discovery cohort to predict HAP cases in the validation cohort, we first sought whether vOTUs of the viral signature identified in the IBIS cohort were also present in the PREV-HAP cohort. Using a BLASTn analysis, we identified 121/571 (21%) vOTUs of the whole viral signature from the IBIS cohort as present within the virome of PREV-HAP pre-onset samples with alignments greater than 500 bp and a median sequence identity percentage of 91% (IQR: 86%–95%). By focusing on the bacteriophages, this represented 84/455 (18%) vOTUs of the bacteriophage signature from IBIS conserved in PREV-HAP pre-onset samples (median sequence identity percentage of 92%; IQR: 89%–96%) (Figure 6A; Table S11). Each PREV-HAP vOTU corresponded to 1–8 IBIS vOTUs; therefore, the 84 conserved vOTUs from IBIS corresponded to 66 vOTUs in PREV-HAP. These validated vOTUs were renamed as viral conserved OTU (vcOTU) and re-numbered vcOTU1-66. The conserved genetic signature consisted primarily of Caudoviricetes viruses with unclassified genus (82%). Among the 66 bacteriophages of the conserved signature, 40% were predicted to be virulent, and 54% had unknown lifestyle (Figure 6B). These 66 vcOTUS had 16 bacterial host predicted using PhaBOX2,16 with Mogibacterium, Veillonella, and Prevotella being among the most frequently identified bacterial genera. In addition, 874 viral proteins were annotated, including specific lytic functional proteins such as endolysin, a bacteriophage-encoded enzyme that hydrolyzes bacterial cell wall hydrogen bonds, and holin, a bacteriophage-encoded protein that triggers host cell wall degradation at the end of the lytic cycle (Figure 6B; Table S12). We then investigated how the bacteriophage conserved signature could classify patients in the validation cohort using pre-HAP onset samples. Finally, we applied a stacked machine learning approach, combining spectral clustering with fast-and-frugal trees (FFTs),18 to develop a parsimonious predictive model using a minimal set of criteria. The FFT-based model provided 10 single-factor decision trees that classified samples as either “upcoming HAP” or “no HAP” based solely on the abundance of a limited number of vcOTUs (between 2 and 4 vcOTUs) with an area under the curve of 78.4 calculated using a 10-fold cross-validation strategy. The best model included the abundance of two vcOTUs of virulent Caudoviricetes: vcOTU52 and vcOTU53 with Veillonella and Prevotella predicted as their respective hosts, achieving an accuracy of 76% (specificity of 63% and sensitivity of 89%) (Figure 6D).

Causal virome-bacteriome network before HAP

To further investigate the specific transkingdom interactions that may lead to HAP development, we used the transkingdom network analysis (TkNA),19 a recent developed method of causal inference. TkNA was used to reconstruct a comprehensive transkingdom network that captured interactions between the relative abundances of the 66 vcOTUS of the conserved viral signature and the core bacteriome by integrating data from both IBIS and PREV-HAP cohorts. After filtering for statistical significance and causality constraints (correlation inequalities), the obtained network contained 71 nodes including the 66 vcOTUs and five bacterial genera and 391 edges (Figure 6E; Table S13). To identify members with potential causal roles in the induction of HAP, we focused on two critical node properties: degree (the number of direct connections a node has) and bipartite betweenness centrality (BiBC), which measures a node’s bottleneck and indicates which node acts as the most effective “bridge” within a network. By evaluating both the degree and BiBC of each node, we identified 28 potential nodes involved directly in the progression to HAP (26 vcOTUs and 2 bacterial genera). Among these candidates, two unclassified Caudoviricetes vcOTUs with unknown life cycle, vcOTU11 and vcOTU43, showed the highest observed node degree (27) and BiBC (0.32), respectively (Table S14).

To further confirm these results, we generated 10,000 random networks and compared the BiBC and degree values of the candidates in the actual network to those in the random networks. Among the 66 vcOTUs and 5 bacterial genera, 26 vcOTUs (23 vcOTUs classified in the class Caudoviricetes, one vcOTU belonging to the genera Bohxovirus, one Lentivirus, and one Triplejayvirus) and 2 bacterial genera (Prevotella and Streptococcus) exhibited the lower likelihood of random finding probability in the reconstructed network (median of 1.09% [IQR = 0.09%–2.55%]) (Figure 6F) and were identified as the causal agents of HAP.

In conclusion, our results demonstrate that virulent bacteriophages from the Caudoviricetes class and both Streptococcus and Prevotella play a direct role in the development of HAP and may serve as a key factor in HAP pathogenesis.

Discussion

In this study, we aimed to characterize the lung virome in critically ill patients in relation to HAP susceptibility. We identified specific viral signatures preceding HAP onset, consisting mainly of Caudoviricetes bacteriophages showing specific interactions with the core lung bacteriome in patients with HAP. These findings suggest some interesting insights into the pathophysiology underlying HAP.

High interpersonal variability of the lung virome was observed in adult patients hospitalized in ICU. These findings are in accordance with those previously observed in the gut virome of healthy or diseased adults20,21,22 or infants.13 Despite this high dissimilarity at the strain level, at the family levels, the majority of the patients had a lung virome dominated by Caudoviricetes bacteriophages and unclassified viruses. Eukaryotic viruses were found to a lesser extent, with the Alphatorquevirus (belonging to Anelloviridae family) as the most abundant and prevalent genus. Anelloviridae are considered a common component of the human virome, present in nearly 90% of healthy individuals,23 and have been reported as the most prevalent family in the respiratory tract.24 We observed minor variations in the lung virome composition between the exploratory and the validation cohorts, the notable ones concerning the Picornaviridae and Redondoviridae families. The Redondoviridae family (Torbevirus), known to be associated with critical illness,25 was detected in 8% of patients in the IBIS cohort but 53% of patients in the PREV-HAP cohort. However, the prevalence of this family was not associated with HAP onset or patient outcomes, including death (Data S1). In addition, the Enterovirus (Picornaviridae) was positive in only 10% of the PREV-HAP patients while positive in 69% of patients from the IBIS cohort with 5 different genotypes detected. These differences in virome composition may be explained by the season of hospitalization, with patients in the IBIS cohort being hospitalized in autumn/winter and those in the PREV-HAP cohort mainly in summer. Seasonal variation in the respiratory microbiome, including the virome, has been previously described.26

Despite the complex variability in respiratory virome communities, we demonstrated a robust lung viral convergence as defined by a decrease in viral population dissimilarity up to 3 days before the onset of HAP. Virome convergence and viral signatures specific to HAP onset were mainly driven by Caudoviricetes viruses, a class of tailed bacteriophages previously associated with human pathology.7,27,28,29,30,31 These bacteriophages have been shown to lead the gut virome composition.24,32 They are enriched in the virome of patients with IBD and are also associated with gut virome dysbiosis, which subsequently contributes to bacterial depletion and affects intestinal homeostasis in IBD pathogenesis.7 Furthermore, Caudoviricetes bacteriophages interact with the human immune system, as demonstrated in the gut virome of 1-year-old children with interactions between 19 viral family clades in the Caudoviricetes and Toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9) significantly associated to the onset of asthma.33 In addition, several families within this class of bacteriophages have been shown to be involved in gut virome convergence preceding necrotizing enterocolitis in preterm infants.13

Viral convergence was lost at and after HAP onset, which may be related to different bacterial etiologies of pneumonia and different antibiotic treatments. Specifically, we found that antibiotics can affect the diversity of the lung virome, consistent with previous findings in the gut.34 In addition, in a previous study, we have shown that patients in ICU with HAP could be categorized into distinct sub-phenotypes based on their clinical progression: some developed a hyper-inflammatory HAP phenotype, which was associated with a higher incidence of septic shock, ARDS, fever, and hyperleukocytosis. This hyper-inflammatory group experienced significantly worse clinical outcomes compared to those with the hypo-inflammatory HAP phenotype.35 This phenotypic differentiation correlated with the emergence of different bacterial profiles in the subgroups. We can speculate that these variations in bacterial pathogens and pneumonia phenotypes may impact the lung virome profiles and are responsible for the increase in beta-diversity observed at the time of pneumonia. In the IBIS cohort, E. coli was the second most common cause of HAP after Staphylococcus. No specific signature has been identified with the latest. However, we were able to define specific signatures that discriminate upcoming patients with HAP caused by E. coli from those caused by other bacteria. This suggests that specific lung viromes are associated with bacterial pathogens.

In a recent meta-analysis of 738 critically ill patients with HAP from seven different studies, Montassier and colleagues defined and validated an HAP-associated signature in the respiratory bacteriome characterized by a decreased abundance of the healthy microbiota.3 In our study, causal inference identified Streptococcus and Prevotella as key factors of the lung microbiome interactome associated with HAP, altogether with unknown Caudoviricetes, some of which were negatively correlated with these bacteria. We can hypothesize that these bacteriophages are involved in Streptococcus and Prevotella lysis resulting in their decreased abundance observed later in HAP.3 Altogether, our results suggest that the lung virome may contribute to the dysbiosis of the respiratory bacteriome observed in HAP, similarly to what has been shown in several gut diseases7 and in the respiratory virome associated with asthma in preschool children.12 However, a direct interaction between these virulent bacteriophages and TLR9, independent of the bacteriome, is also possible as suggested in the study by Rodriguez et al.33

From a therapeutic perspective, we can speculate that specific measures could be taken to maintain respiratory microbiome eubiosis and prevent HAP induction through bacteriophage intervention. This could be done by avoiding environmental stressors or specific antibiotics that can activate prophages and stimulate the lysogenic-lytic lifestyle switch as shown for Prevotella and Streptococcus phages.36,37 Direct inhalation of intranasal phage administration has been previously used in animal models to treat multidrug-resistant bacterial pulmonary infections38,39,40 or to manipulate the lung bacteriome.41 In addition, recent studies have demonstrated the possibility of targeting and destroying Caudoviricetes bacteriophages (main component of the lung dysbiotic signature) using in vitro genome editing techniques and, in particular, clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats-CRISPR-associated systems.42,43,44,45,46,47

Overall, achieving and maintaining equilibrium in virome-bacteriome associations within the respiratory microbiome is challenging because it is influenced by dynamic interactions between the immune system, bacteria, bacteriophages, and their environment. Understanding these complex dynamics is critical to elucidating the principles of microbiome stability and avoiding HAP induction.

In conclusion, our analysis revealed the composition of the lung virome in critically ill patients and suggested a potential role of the virome in the pathophysiology of HAP. We identified a specific viral dysbiotic signature up to 3 days before HAP induction that targets beneficial bacteria and may cause the lung bacterial dysbiosis involved in HAP. This work significantly advances our understanding of HAP pathophysiology in relation to the lung virome and bacteriome.

Limitations of the study

This study has some limitations. First, the sample size was limited, especially in the sliding windows during the pre-onset period; in particular, in the external cohort, where only 21% of patients had HAP, validation in the HAP convergence window was limited to four patients with HAP and nine without HAP. Secondly, validation of the signature was limited to 18% of the training signature. Because of high individuality of the virome at the strain level, identification of signature at the genus or family level might help to identify conserved biomarkers.33 Third, the absence of non-hospitalized patients in the ICU limits our understanding of whether the lung virome changes observed are specific to ICU conditions or not. Fourth, we cannot conclusively prove direct causality in patients with HAP, which requires further analysis such as in vivo experimental models and additional evidence. Finally, despite some uncontrolled limitations, the major strength of our study lies in its ability to be validated through a multicenter prospective cohort.

Experimental model and study participant details

Study population

In the discovery cohort IBIS (Immunology and Brain Injury Study), patients were enrolled from 31st January 2017 to 5th May 2020 in two French Surgical Intensive Care Units of one university hospital (Nantes, France). The collection of human samples has been declared to the French Ministry of Health (Program de recherche “Immunologie”, DC-2017-2987), and was approved by the Comite de Protection des Personnes Ouest IV (7/04/2015 and 08/10/2020) (number clinicaltrial.gov NCT02003196). Written informed consent from a next-of-kin was required for enrollment. Retrospective consent was obtained from patients when possible. Appropriate consent was obtained for the release of information from deceased individuals. Participants received no compensation. Inclusion criteria were male or female, 18–80 years old, brain injury (Glasgow Coma Scale below or equal to 12 and abnormal brain-CT scan) and receiving invasive mechanical ventilation. Exclusion criteria were cancer with radiotherapy or chemotherapy in the last 90 days, AIDS, leukopenia in the previous five years, immunosuppressive drugs, preexisting immunosuppression and pregnancy. All patients were clinically followed up for 90 days. Intensivists blindly reviewed HAP and ARDS diagnoses for compliance with international definitions.48,49,50 Further details can be found in Montassier and colleagues study.3 In this study, endotracheal aspirates from 87 patients included in the IBIS cohort were selected for virome profiling based on sample availability.

The validation cohort consisted of patients in the placebo arm of PREV-HAP clinical trial.

The PREV-HAP trial was an investigator-initiated multicenter, parallel-group, double-blind, randomized clinical trial performed in 9 French ICUs. The Ouest II Angers (France) Ethics Committee approved the study protocol in March 2021. This trial complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was registered in March 2021 (number clinicaltrial.gov NCT0479356817). The patient’s legal surrogate provided written informed consent for participation. In agreement with the local laws, patients could be enrolled before the provision of legal surrogate consent if the next of kin could not be informed within the maximum delay for inclusion. When able to provide it, patient’s follow-up consent was requested up to 90 days after inclusion. Inclusion criteria were: Patients aged between 18 and 85 years, receiving invasive mechanical ventilation, with one or more acute organ failures at the time of inclusion were randomized to interferon gamma-1b (1b (100 μg/0.5 mL vials, IMUKIN, from Clinigen) or placebo (normal saline vials) in fixed blocks of 6, in a 1:1 ratio, with stratification based on the cause of hospitalization (sepsis vs. other) and the country (France, Greece, Spain). Exclusion criteria were pregnancy/breastfeeding (legal obligation); hypersensitivity to the interferon gamma-1b; a medical history of pre-existing immunosuppression (cancer with radiotherapy or chemotherapy in the last 90 days, AIDS, leukopenia); severe hepatic insufficiency (Child-Pugh score B or C); liver cytolysis (liver transaminases > 5N); chronic renal failure (MDRD glomerular filtration rate <10 mL/min/1.73m2), persisting coma after resuscitated cardiac arrest, hospitalization for cervical spinal cord injury, a previous episode of hospital-acquired pneumonia during the current hospitalization and sustained hyperlactatemia >5 mmol/L. Participants received five subcutaneous injections of 100 μg of recombinant interferon gamma-1b (interferon-gamma group) or matching placebo (placebo group) from day 1 to day 9 (i.e., one injection every 48h). Patients were followed-up for 90 days, and HAP and ARDS diagnoses were blindly reviewed by intensivists for compliance with international definitions.48,49,50 HAP was defined as pneumonia that occurred 48 h after hospitalization and required at least two clinical signs (body temperature >38°C; leukocytosis >12 000 cells per mL, leukopenia <4000 cells per mL, or purulent pulmonary secretions) with the appearance of a fresh infiltrate or worsening of an existing infiltrate on chest radiography, plus semi-quantitative or quantitative positive respiratory fluid cultures. Respiratory samples were obtained before starting any further antimicrobial therapy for pneumonia. An adjudication committee composed of intensivists, not involved in patient recruitment, reviewed all respiratory infection diagnoses and followed European guidelines for HAP diagnosis.48 ARDS was defined as the occurrence of severe hypoxemia (PaO 2/FIO2< 200 mmHg with a Positive End Expiratory Pressure ≥ 5 cm H2O) and bilateral opacities on chest radiograph or computed tomography scan within one week of worsening respiratory symptoms. In both cohorts, patients’ ancestry, race, ethnicity, or socioeconomic status were not available because these data cannot be collected in France for legal reasons.

Method details

Viral metagenomics sequencing

Endotracheal aspirates were preserved diluted (dilution factor of 1:7.4) in a viral transport medium (LMR1925VTM, Labomoderne) and stored at −20°C. Samples were treated according to an adapted version of the protocol from Bal and colleagues.5 Benchmarking of viral enrichment protocol against metagenomic shotgun demonstrates superior efficiency of viral enrichment method for analysis of the virome (Data S1). In addition, this approach demonstrates superior sensitivity for RNA and single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) viruses. Briefly, the MS2 RNA bacteriophage (MS2, IC1 RNA internal control; bioMérieux R-GENE ref. 71–110) was spiked in each sample to validate the whole metagenomic process. MS2 was spiked at the 10− 5 dilution for the IBIS cohort and at the 10−4 dilution for the PREV-HAP cohort. The samples were then filtered through 0.45 μm filters and incubated with DNase (Life Technologies , Carlsbad, USA) for 90 min. Total nucleic acid extraction was performed on the Qiagen EZ1 Advanced XL Extractor using the DSP Virus Kit, Qiagen. After extraction, both RNA and DNA amplification were performed using a Whole Transcriptome Amplification kit (WTA2, Sigma-Aldrich, Darmstadt, Germany). Amplified DNA was purified using QIAquick spin columns (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). No-template controls (NTC) and MS2 spiked-in NTC were prepared for each sample batch using nuclease-free water that followed the whole process starting from dilution in the transport media. The viral transport medium was also sequenced to confirm the absence of viral contamination. Before sequencing, quality control testing was performed with MS2 qPCR (R-GENE ref. 71–110). Libraries for the IBIS cohort were prepared using the Nextera XT kit, while those for the PREV-HAP cohort were prepared using the post-PCR portion of the Illumina COVIDSeq Test Kit (Illumina, San Diego, USA). The IBIS cohort samples underwent extraction, viral enrichment, and whole transcriptome amplification at CHU-Nantes, while the PREV-HAP cohort samples were processed at CHU-Lyon (Data S1). Both libraries were sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 at CHU-Lyon using a 2 × 100-bp paired-end sequencing protocol.

Bioinformatics virome analysis

Raw reads were dehosted using SraHumanScrubber v2.2.1 to filter out human reads and trimmed using Cutadapt v4.6 to remove sequencing adapters and low-quality ends, keeping only reads longer than 30 base pairs. Taxonomic assignment was then performed using kraken2 v2.1.2 (parameters: minimum-hit-groups of 2, confidence = 0.1) on a custom database containing viral taxonomy from RVDB (v24.1), Inphared (v2 Nov2022), archaeal, bacterial, fungal, and human taxonomy from RefSeq databases (downloaded with kraken2 24/11/2024).46 Using Krakentools,51 all kraken2 reads assigned to the taxa “Eukaryota,” “Bacteria,” “Archaea,” and “Fungi”as well as their corresponding taxonomic children, were discarded to retain only the potential viral reads. These reads were assembled into contigs with SPAdes v.3.14.0: mode “meta.” 52 De novo assembled sequences were dereplicated using cd-hit v4.753 to obtain operational taxonomical units (OTUs) with a minimum of 95% identity and 95% coverage. All OTUs smaller than 450 bp, the median contig size of the internal control MS2, were filtered out. The length-filtered operational taxonomical units were fed into PhaBOX216 and Diamond v2.1.6.16054 with the lowest common ancestor (LCA) algorithm using the NCBI nr database (version 4) in order to select the viral OTUs (vOTUs) and assign their taxonomy. The quality-filtered viral reads used for de novo assembly were realigned to the obtained vOTUs sequences using Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA) software v0.7.1755 and a count table was generated with samtools.

Virome decontamination and normalization

Only samples with positive detection of the internal quality control (MS2 bacteriophage: Emesvirus Zinderi) were kept for further analysis. vOTUs counts for each sample were normalized by RPKM (reads per kilobase per million mapped reads). Samples were then in silico decontaminated using proportions of vOTUs relative to the MS2 internal controls in samples and spiked-in NTC. For each vOTUi in sample j, viral decontaminated ratios were calculated as VDR i,j = xi,j/xMS2,j – max(xi,NTC/xMS2,NTC), with xi,j and xMS2,j the RPKM values of vOTUi and MS2 in sample j, respectively, and xi,NTC and xMS2,NTC the RPKM values of vOTUi and MS2 in spiked-in NTC from the same sequencing batch than sample j. vOTU with lower relative proportion in samples than NTC (i.e., VDR i,j < 0), were considered as contaminants and removed. To control false positive calls, only vOTU with a detection level at least one-tenth that of the MS2 (i.e., VDR i,j > 0.1) were considered positive for the IBIS cohort. For the PREV-HAP cohort, as the MS2 was spiked 10 times more concentrated, the offset to consider a vOTU positive was 10 times lower (i.e., VDR i,j > 0.01). We further excluded all sequences belonging to the families Retroviridae and Totiviridae because they were previously identified as contaminants in our metagenomic process as shown by Destras and colleagues.56 Finally, decontaminated RPKM for vOTUi in sample j were calculated as dRPKM i,j = VDR i,j ∗ xMS2,j.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis

Alpha (Shannon index and vOTUs richness) and beta (Hellinger, Sorensen, and weighted Bray-Curtis dissimilarity) diversities were calculated using the decontaminated vOTUS matrix (dRPKM for Bray-Curtis, Log10(dRPKM+1) for Hellinger, and presence/absence matrix for Sorensen) using the vegan v2.6.4 and adespatial v0.3-21 packages in RStudio (R v 4.2.3). Based on the vOTUs dRPKM matrix, Shannon index and richness plots were generated using ggplot2 v3.5.0. LOESS regression was used to generate trend lines and 95% confidence bands.

Analyses prior to the onset of HAP were carried out by counting backwards from a defined day 0 for each patient. This day 0 corresponded to the time of HAP onset in each case, and as the median day of HAP onset after ICU admission in all control patients. Longitudinal plots were based on a sliding window with a window size of two days, with one day overlap between windows. Note that starting with Figure 2, no HAP patients who died in the ICU were excluded from the analysis because we could not exclude that they might have developed HAP if they had survived.

LEfSe v1.1.257 was used to identify significant vOTUs associated with HAP development or not. PCoA was performed with the vegan v2.6.4, ggplot2 v3.5.0, ape v5.7-1, and cowplot v1.1.1 packages in R using clinical metadata. NMDS was also performed using the vegan and tidyverse v2.0.0 libraries in R. Phage host and lifestyle predictions were performed using the recent pipeline, PhaBOX216 and compared to VIBRANT58 v1.2.1 (Virus Identification By iteRative ANnoTation) as the gold standard (Data S1). Manual curation was applied to the lifestyle prediction of low quality contigs. PLS-DA analyses were performed using the mixOmics R package v6.22.0. FFT18 machine learning modeling and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) was used to assess the predictive power of the selected viral signature using FFTrees v2.0.0, pROC v1.18.5 and caret v6.0-94 R packages. Transkingdom Network Analysis (TkNA)19 was implemented to perform causal inference analysis using the relative abundance data from the conserved viral signature and bacteriome. The TkNA correlation network was visualized using Cytoscape59 v3.10.2. We used the Wilcoxon test, two-tailed Mann-Whitney U test, and Fisher’s exact test to compare all numerical and qualitative variables (rstatix v0.7.2 package). Statistical significance was defined as p ≤ 0.05. Symbols used: ns for not significant (p > 0.05), ∗ for p ≤ 0.05, ∗∗ for p ≤ 0.01, ∗∗∗ for p ≤ 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗ for p ≤ 0.0001.

Bacterial microbiome analysis

Previously published 16S ribosomal RNA gene sequencing raw dataset (Table S15) and assembled bacterial OTUs3 were downloaded from NCBI and the secure HAP2 data hub (CHU-Nantes), respectively. Unifrac distances were calculated using the phyloseq package v1.42.0 in R. We used to identify Spearman’s correlations between vOTUs and bacterial genera relative abundances using the R library corrplot v0.92. Only significant correlations with p < 0.05 were considered. A correlation network with the 16S respiratory core microbiome data3 was generated using the networkD3 v0.4, dplyr v1.1.2, and ggsankeyfier v0.1.8 packages in R.
