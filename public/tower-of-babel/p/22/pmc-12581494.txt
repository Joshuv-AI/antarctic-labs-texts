Mutational analysis of the mitochondrial tRNA genes and flanking regions in lymphocytes from long-term pediatric cancer survivors given doxorubicin chemotherapy for acute lymphoblastic leukemia
Authors: Vernon E Walker, Neha Bansal, Salina M Torres, Kristen Stevenson, Yael Flamand, Dale M Walker, Stephen E Sallan, Steven E Lipshultz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDoxorubicin induced cardiotoxicity in childhood cancer survivors is mediated by mitochondrial dysfunction. The aims of this research are to determine (i) the frequency of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) mutations in long-term survivors of childhood acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), and (ii) to determine if co-administration of dexrazoxane reduces the occurrence of mutations.MethodsPatients previously treated on Dana-Farber Cancer Institute Childhood ALL protocols and at least 4 years from the date of ALL diagnosis were enrolled in this study. MtDNA was isolated from samples of peripheral blood lymphocytes. A vertical denaturing gradient gel electrophoresis method was used for detecting sequence variants in the 22 transfer RNA (tRNA) genes and flanking regions of the human mitochondrial genome. The patients were divided into two cohorts, those with mutations and those without, and compared. The patients and variants were also compared to healthy controls. Mutational status was compared with echocardiographic measurements.ResultsOf the patients who received chemotherapy, there were 51/167 (31%) children who were found to have 61 different sequence variants in mtDNA with 9 patients having more than one variant. There was no association between mutation status and cumulative doxorubicin dose, though patients receiving < 300 mg/m2 had fewer mutations of any kind. At a median time of 8.5 years after diagnosis, the number of ALL patients with mtDNA sequence variants was 2.4-fold higher than the number of control children with sequence variants (8 sequence variants in 7/55 or 12.7%). Among patients receiving doxorubicin-based therapies, with (n = 34) or without dexrazoxane (n = 132), there were no statistically significant differences in the patient characteristics or in the frequencies, locations, types, and distribution of mtDNA sequence variants. The mutational status was not associated with echocardiographic changes.ConclusionsThe results of this study indicate that doxorubicin chemotherapy is associated with increases in mtDNA sequence variants in lymphocytes. The role of mtDNA mutations in late-onset cardiomyopathy of doxorubicin, and the potential antimutagenic activity of dexrazoxane, were not established but warrant further investigation.Keywords: Pediatric oncology, Survivorship, Cardiotoxicity, Doxorubicin, Ventricular function, Mitochondria, MtDNA mutations, MtDNA variants, Cardiomyopathy

Background

Doxorubicin chemotherapy is a cytotoxic agent used for effective treatment of a spectrum of childhood malignancies, including acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) [1–4]. The 5-year relative survival rate for all cancers combined improved from 58% during the mid-1970s to 83% during 2007 through 2013 for children and from 68% to 84% for adolescents [5]. Although doxorubicin has greatly improved outcomes in children with cancers, such as ALL and sarcomas, it is also among the most cardiotoxic of cancer therapies [4, 6]. The Childhood Cancer Survivor Study reported cardiac death as the second most common cause of late mortality in childhood cancer survivors, particularly in those receiving high doses of doxorubicin [7, 8]. The prevalence of chronic heart failure in survivors treated with doxorubicin ranges from 1% to 16%, but the true rate may be even greater with extended follow-up [9, 10].

Despite enormous amounts of research to elucidate the nature of doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity, the underlying molecular mechanisms remain controversial, are likely multifactorial, and warrant further investigation [reviewed in [11–16]. Current understanding includes a central role for oxidative stress [17–20] and mitochondrial dysfunction [9, 21–24] due in part to doxorubicin-induced effects on the mitochondrial electron transport chain [resulting in reactive oxygen species (ROS) formation], redox cycling, calcium dysregulation, and activation of cell death pathways [14, 25, 26]. Cardiac myocytes have abundant mitochondria that accumulate in quantity and function with increasing age in children, and they are the most injured intracellular organelles upon cell exposure to doxorubicin [14, 15]. Earlier studies have demonstrated a relationship between mitochondrial deletion mutations and doxorubicin cardiomyopathy in humans and experimental animals [17, 27–30]. Preclinical studies have associated doxorubicin exposure with irreversible cardiac mitochondrial dysfunction [31, 32]. Dexrazoxane is the only FDA-approved agent for prevention of doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity and, to date, its coadministration has been the most successful of various strategies for prevention and/or alleviation of doxorubicin cardiomyopathy [13, 27, 33–35] (see Table 1 in [14]).

The purpose of this research was to investigate the alterations in mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) in childhood cancer survivors treated with doxorubicin agents. The hypotheses of the current work were that (i) the frequency of small-scale mtDNA alterations in a cohort of long-term survivors of childhood ALL correlates with the cumulative dose of the doxorubicin treatment, (ii) the occurrence of mtDNA lesions is lower in children treated with doxorubicin plus dexrazoxane compared to those given doxorubicin alone, and (iii) echocardiographic parameters would differ between those with and without mtDNA mutations. Conclusive evidence that persistent mtDNA mutations are fundamental lesions contributing to late-onset cardiomyopathy might allow a distinction between the antitumor and cardiotoxic effects of doxorubicin for purposes of increasing its therapeutic index and enhancing rational strategies for mitigating cardiac dysfunction in patients receiving chemotherapy.

Methods

Patient population

This study utilized patients previously treated on Dana-Farber Cancer Institute (DFCI) Childhood ALL protocols. Eligible subjects were identified through the DFCI ALL Consortium database and were invited to participate in the study by their primary provider during a regularly scheduled clinic visit. Informed consent was obtained from all interested participants or their families. Patient enrollment began with all eligible patients identified by DFCI study coordinators. Patient eligibility for this study was defined as: (i) Previously diagnosed with ALL; (ii) Registered and treated with doxorubicin on any DFCI ALL Consortium Protocol from 1972 to 2000; (iii) At least 4 years from date of ALL diagnosis; (iv) No prior history of bone marrow or stem cell transplant; (v) No history of relapse; (vi) No history of secondary malignancies treated with chemotherapy or radiation. Patient eligibility was not restricted by risk group, age, or treatment protocol. All participants were registered with the DFCI Quality Assurance Office for Clinical Trials. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards at the participating institutions.

Sample collection

Peripheral blood was collected into heparin tubes at the time of enrollment and every two years thereafter via venipuncture. All samples were shipped overnight at room temperature to a central processing laboratory. Whole blood was diluted 1:1 with normal saline: peripheral blood sample. Samples were centrifuged on lymphocyte separation solution (Organon Biosciences, Kenilworth, NJ) and the mononuclear cell layer was isolated and washed twice with two volumes of PBS. Cell samples were enumerated and adjusted with PBS to achieve a concentration of 1 × 106 cells/mL and again centrifuged and aspirated. Resulting mononuclear cells constituting primarily lymphocytes were cryopreserved by freezing at −70 °C until mtDNA isolation and analysis. All samples were processed within 30 h of collection.

Identification of mtDNA sequence variants

Since doxorubicin-induced ROS can cause both large- and small-scale mutations in mtDNA, the present study focused on the potential occurrence of point mutations in the 22 mtDNA tRNA genes because they are thought to represent “hotspots” for heritable mutation-based mitochondrial diseases, including cardiomyopathies, in humans [36, 37]. The techniques for isolating mtDNA from samples of lymphocytes, screening for mtDNA sequence changes, sequencing of mtDNA variants, and estimating the degree of heteroplasmy (or mutant fraction) have been described previously [38]. In brief, a vertical denaturing gradient gel electrophoresis (DGGE) method, employing 14 unipolar psoralen-clamped polymerase chain reaction (PCR) primer pairs, was used for detecting sequence variants in the 22 tRNA genes and flanking regions (together spanning 22%) of the human mitochondrial genome (Fig. 1). The primer names and sequences, PCR variables, characteristics of amplified products, and tRNA gene(s) amplified using 13 primer pairs are listed elsewhere [38]. The corresponding information for a novel 14th primer pair is as follows: oligonucleotide names and primer sequences [“new” Human transfer RNA (H tRNA) 4 sense, AAAATGTTGGTTATACCCTTCC; “new” H tRNA 4 antisense phosphorothioate with a psoralen derivative linked to the 5’ end, CTCAGGGATGGGTTCGATTCTCA], PCR conditions (25 millimolar MgCl2, 30 PCR cycles), PCR product sequence positions (5’-nucleotide 4141, 3’-nucleotide 4360) and length (220 bases), and tRNA genes amplified (tRNAIle and ~ 50% of tRNAGln). The principles and advantages of DGGE and application of psoralen-oligonucleotide conjugates as PCR primers have been discussed elsewhere [38–43].

Positions of human mtDNA segments for analysis of tRNA genes and flanking regions sequence variations. Fourteen segments of human mtDNA were selected to screen all tRNA-coding regions using the Primo Melt 3.4 computer program to create corresponding pairs of unipolar psoralen-clamped PCR primers

Blood samples were coded with patient identification (PID) numbers at clinical centers, and subsequently were analyzed in a ‘blinded’ fashion for mtDNA sequence variants. The original PID numbers were changed for this report to provide an additional level of patient identity protection. There was no age-matched control group of children in the current study; thus, sequence variants found in biological specimens from ALL patients receiving doxorubicin chemotherapy, with or without dexrazoxane, were compared to those identified in a group of healthy infants (n = 55) who formed our control group, during the development of the vertical DGGE assay [38].

Echocardiographic measurements

Echocardiograms were obtained at diagnosis, at the end of doxorubicin therapy, and every two years thereafter locally and then centrally re-measured at a single facility by study staff blinded to treatment status. Structural left ventricular (LV) status was assessed with LV end-systolic and end-diastolic dimensions, LV mass, LV end-diastolic posterior wall thickness and septal wall thickness, and LV thickness-to-dimension ratio. Functional LV status was assessed with LV fractional shortening. To adjust for age, body surface area, and growth-related changes, echocardiographic measurements were standardized by calculating z-scores, which are the standard deviations above or below a normative population [44].

Statistical analyses

Means of normally distributed outcome variables were compared with t-tests; means of non-normally distributed variables, with Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Differences in proportions of dichotomous values (e.g., mutation carrier) between groups were compared with a Fisher exact test. For associations with echocardiographic z-scores, the mutation status was combined due to small sample size. For associations with cumulative doxorubicin dose, the mutation status was combined due to small sample size. All analyses were done using SAS (version 9.2).

Results

A total of 167 standard and high-risk ALL patients from 10 DFCI protocols were analyzed. Of these, 132 (79%) patients received doxorubicin alone and 35 (21%) patients received doxorubicin and dexrazoxane. There were 51/167 (31%) children who received chemotherapy having one or more different mtDNA changes for a total of 61 sequence variants (Table 1). Most patients (i.e., 42/51) had only 1 mutation, 8 patients had 2 mutations, and 1 patient had 3 mutations for a total of 51 patients with 61 mutations (Table 2). The median time from diagnosis to sample analysis for mutations was 8.5 years (range, 3.0–34.1.0.1) (Table 1).

Upon comparison between patients with and without mutations, patients with mutations were significantly younger at the time of diagnosis (p = 0.028) (Table 1). However, there were no differences observed between the two groups in terms of sex, age at sample analysis, cumulative dose of doxorubicin, or risk groups at initial diagnosis. No significant differences were observed in the frequency of occurrence, location and types, and distribution of sequence variants in patients who received only doxorubicin versus those who received dexrazoxane with doxorubicin chemotherapy (Table 2).

Table 3 summarizes the distribution and nature of mtDNA changes identified in doxorubicin-treated patients and provides additional information for each distinct sequence variant. The estimated mutant fraction, or proportion of mutant DNA to wild-type DNA, was assessed qualitatively based upon the banding patterns of the DGGE gels and was considered to reflect the degree of heteroplasmy (Table 3) [38]. Among the sequence variants found in ALL patients, 60/63 (95%) were identified first by the occurrence of mutant homoduplexes on primary DGGE gels. These mutant homoduplexes were identified because they had a different migration pattern than the wild-type homoduplexes for mtDNA of samples from children with no alterations in corresponding PCR fragments. Therefore, these sequence changes were likely homoplasmic [45]. A low degree of heteroplasmy was estimated for only three sequence variants (indicated by a single * in Table 3, Column 2), including the only novel mutation in the data set (T4330G in tRNAIle, Fig. 2A), based upon the occurrence of mutant/wild-type heteroduplex bands and no corresponding mutant homoduplex bands on primary DGGE gels.

Using published reports [37, 38, 46–59] and criteria for estimating pathogenicity of tRNA gene mutations [52, 59], the 28 distinct sequence variants identified in the current study were classified as 11 with pathogenic characteristics, 14 with no evidence of pathogenicity, and three with unclear effects on mitochondrial function. Among the 28 distinct sequence variants identified in the current study, 11 occurred in mitochondrial tRNA genes (Table 3). To estimate the effect of the tRNA gene mutations upon their secondary structure, the location of the sequence change(s) was identified in schematic cloverleaf structures for each mutated tRNA molecule [60] and individual variants were considered to have pathogenic characteristics if one or more criteria of McFarland and coworkers were met (Fig. 2A-E) [52]. A10005G and A10042G transitions occurring in the D-loop and T-loop of tRNAGly, respectively (Fig. 2C), as well as the single-base deletion at nucleotide 15,940 in the T-loop of tRNAThr (Fig. 2E) likely have little consequence because of the small size of the loops. The C4312T transition in tRNAIle arose in a larger T-loop of 7 bases and has been associated with thyroid tumor pathology [51]. The remaining seven tRNA sequence variants occurred in a D-loop stem (A15907G in tRNAThr), a T-loop stem (T4336C in tRNAGln and T10034C in tRNAGly), an anticodon stem (A15924G and G15928A in tRNAThr), or an amino acid acceptor stem (T4330G in tRNAIle or tRNAGln) and disrupt Watson-Crick base pairing, characteristic of ~ 73% of pathogenic mutations [52].

Several of these sequence variants in tRNA stems have been reported as mutations with associated clinical phenotypes (Table 3), albeit other published reports consider the pathogenicity of these point mutations as unclear [36]. For example, three groups have previously connected the G variant of the A15924G transition, observed in five patients in the current study, with respiratory enzyme deficiency, mitochondrial myopathy, and cardiomyopathy [53–56]. The A15924G transition in the anticodon stem of tRNAThr disrupts Watson-Crick base pairing, a characteristic of ~ 73% of pathogenic tRNA mutations [52]. The only novel sequence variant was found in one patient with three distinct mtDNA changes; this T4330G transversion was present at a low degree of heteroplasmy but is a remarkable mutation with high pathogenic potential because it simultaneously changes amino acid affinity for both tRNAIle and tRNAGln. Among the 17 distinct sequence variants found in regions flanking tRNA genes, only three have reported associations with clinical phenotypes (Table 3) including T4216C transitions in nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide dehydrogenase (NADH dehydrogenase) subunit 1, A10086G transitions in NADH dehydrogenase subunit 3, and A10398G variant in the nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide dehydrogenase subunit 3 (ND3) of complex I in the mitochondrial electron transport chain. Reports of clinical associations for these sequence variants are detailed in the footnotes of Table 3.

There was no association between mutation status and doxorubicin dose (Tables 2 and 4). Although patients who received a doxorubicin cumulative dose < 300 mg/m2 had fewer mutations of any kind (21% vs. 38% vs. 35% for cumulative doses of < 300 mg/m2, 300 to < 360 mg/m2, and ≥ 360 mg/m2, respectively), it did not reach statistical significance (Table 1). Furthermore, there were no statistically significant differences between cumulative dose and the number of pathogenic mutations. Patients who received doxorubicin alone had fewer mutations than those who received doxorubicin plus dexrazoxane; however, this difference was not statistically significant (Table 1). Furthermore, there was no difference in the number of pathogenic mutations between the two groups (Table 4).

The data from all the ALL patients were used in the following comparisons to healthy controls (Table 5). The number of survivors (with or without dexrazoxane) who had detectable sequence variants was significantly elevated compared with the number of control children with identifiable sequence variants (p = 0.008). The number of doxorubicin-treated children with sequence variants was 2.4-fold higher than the number of control children with sequence variants (8 sequence variants in 7/55 [12.7%] of control subjects) [38]. In addition, there was a 2.5-fold increase in the average number of sequence variants per subject in ALL patients (0.38 variants/subject) compared with controls (0.15 variants/subject). Nine of the ALL patients (6%) receiving chemotherapy had more than one sequence variant, which was higher compared with controls where only 1 of 55 subjects (~ 2%) had more than one sequence variant. Children receiving chemotherapy had an increase in the number of sequence variants at polymorphic sites (27 distinct variants) plus one novel mutation not found in controls (Table 3) [48, 49, 51–58]. Half of the sequence variants in coding regions of ALL patient mtDNA (i.e., 16/32 coding region variants) occurred at positions that resulted in an amino acid change (referred to as a nonsynonymous change), while only 20% of coding region variants in controls were nonsynonymous. A total of 13/63 sequence variants in 51/167 ALL patients were the same presumptive polymorphisms as found in control children (Table 3) [38]; the only distinct sequence variant occurring in controls not found among variants in ALL patients was a nonsynonymous transition (G9966A) [38]. The distinct variants in doxorubicin-treated children included 22 transitions, 4 transversions (one novel), a single-base deletion, and a 9-base deletion.

Discussion

There is abundant evidence in the literature to establish the risk for progressive and clinically significant cardiotoxic effects of doxorubicin [9]. The results of our study indicate that, several years after doxorubicin chemotherapy, there is a detectable increase in mtDNA sequence variants in lymphocytes in children treated with doxorubicin. The number of patients with these sequence variants was 2.4-fold higher when compared to healthy controls. In addition, even though no statistical significance was found between mutation status and doxorubicin dose, patients who received cumulative dose < 300 mg/m2 had fewer mutations of any kind. Further, there were no statistically significant associations between mutations and echocardiographic changes.

Proposed mechanisms responsible for the loss of ventricular and later functional consequences observed in doxorubicin-treated patients include oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, calcium dysregulation, inflammation, DNA damage, and activation of cell death by both intrinsic and extrinsic pathways [reviewed in [11–16]. A major mechanism of doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity is strongly linked to mitochondrial dysfunction, leading to an increased generation of intracellular ROS and oxidative stress [14]. A contributing factor is the high-affinity binding of doxorubicin to a mitochondrial membrane phospholipid, cardiolipin, known for maintaining mitochondrial structure, function, cardiac energy metabolism, and cell survival. Mitochondrial toxicity from doxorubicin is thought to be mediated mainly through oxidative stress [18] and inhibition of the respiratory chain by binding to cardiolipin or by interacting with mtDNA [17, 21].

MtDNA depletion, mtDNA mutations, and respiratory chain dysfunction initiated during doxorubicin chemotherapy may lead to formation of ROS and a cycle of secondary mtDNA and respiratory chain insults that accumulate long-term after treatment. Such events would explain, in part, the molecular mechanism of “dose memory” and delayed onset cardiomyopathy [17, 28]. In fact, mitochondrial dysfunction resulting in a bioenergetic crisis is a pivotal element of cardiotoxicity due to doxorubicin [61]. Doxorubicin-treated long-term survivors of childhood ALL have been observed to have significantly more mtDNA mutations or polymorphisms than healthy children [62]. A study of childhood ALL survivors found that gene polymorphisms of mitochondrial expression may affect mtDNA replication and possibly indicate susceptibility to doxorubicin [63]. Another study observed that doxorubicin-treated childhood ALL survivors had an increased number of mtDNA copies in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (primarily lymphocytes) and that the concomitant use of dexrazoxane was associated with a lower number of mtDNA copies per cell [32].

Doxorubicin induces free radicals (enzymatically or via formation of doxorubicin-iron complexes) and promotes myocardial oxidative stress leading to increased cytosolic calcium, mitochondrial dysfunction, and induction of myocyte apoptosis or necrosis [9, 21–24]. Due to the high-energy demands of the heart, 45% of myocardial volume is constituted by mitochondria, making them more abundant in cardiac tissue compared to other tissues [64, 65]. Hence, cardiac cells are more susceptible to free radical damage due to their highly oxidative metabolism, relatively poor antioxidant defenses, and high concentration of cardiolipin, a phospholipid concentrated in the inner mitochondrial membrane and critical to cell respiration [66, 67].

Doxorubicin is a cationic chemotherapeutic that readily amasses in the myocardium due to its strong affinity for negatively charged cardiolipin. Doxorubicin accumulates in the mitochondria of cardiomyocytes at concentrations 100-fold higher than in plasma, forming iron complexes, prompting oxidative stress and thus, DNA damage [68, 69]. A study found that the mitochondria in adults are “apoptosis refractory” (i.e., unable to induce programmed cell death) [70]. The mitochondria from the heart and brain tissues in young mice and humans are primed for apoptosis, predisposing them to undergo cell death in response to genotoxic damage. This predisposition makes children with cancer more vulnerable to the severe side effects of toxic chemotherapy than are the adults in whom this apoptotic machinery is almost absent [70].

Several studies have reported associations between doxorubicin treatment, nucleic acid damage or polymorphisms, and cardiotoxicity. Recent work has shown differential mRNA expression profiles in peripheral blood of doxorubicin-exposed childhood cancer survivors with and without cardiomyopathy [71]. Another recent study has also shown that high-dose doxorubicin combined with genetic variants involved in the profibrotic Slit-Robo signaling pathway promote cardiac fibrosis, thus giving credence to genetic association of doxorubicin-related cardiomyopathy [72]. The mtDNA deletion of about 4 kb produced by doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity was initially reported in 1993 in mice and the incidence of mutation increased with the duration and dose of doxorubicin [29].

A classic antitumor effect of doxorubicin is most often attributed to the inhibition of topoisomerase IIβ, which is abundantly present in the mitochondria of cardiomyocytes [13–15]. Topoisomerase IIβ forms a ternary cleavage complex with doxorubicin and DNA that induces DNA double-strand breaks leading to mitochondrial ROS generation, G [2]M-phase cell cycle arrest, and cell death, which are dependent upon both duration and dose of doxorubicin [13–15, 73–75]. The potential for testing mitochondrial topoisomerase 1 single–nucleotide polymorphisms to identify patients with increased susceptibility to doxorubicin cardiotoxicity has been explored [76, 77].

In our study, the elevations of mtDNA alterations in doxorubicin treated ALL patients involved increases in homoplasmic sequence variants that occurred in a seemingly “random” fashion, compared to increases in specific frequently occurring mutations associated with alkylating chemotherapeutic agents like cyclophosphamide [78, 79]. This random pattern of smaller mutations suggests that these mutations were most likely produced by ROS [80] generated by doxorubicin. In the future, analyzing the serial blood samples prior to administration of chemotherapy for the absence of these mutations as well as after the treatment (first immediately confirming the appearance of these mutations as heteroplasmic change and later confirming progression to homoplasmic mutations) would give stronger evidence of doxorubicin cardiotoxicity leading to acquisition, segregation, and expansion of these mtDNA changes. If ROS cause these mtDNA mutations, then a given sequence variant would be absent before treatment and would appear after treatment as a heteroplasmic change that becomes homoplasmic over time. Sequence changes in tRNA genes were of particular interest since they are responsible for more than half of all known mitochondrial pathogenic mutations even though they encompass only 10% of the human mitochondrial genome [59], while mutations in flanking regions examined have a lower probability of being pathogenic [37]. The relatively poor prognosis of heart failure in this population urges one to look for novel or offbeat agents to help with their quality of life.

Again, our study shows that the sequence variants in doxorubicin-treated children were 2.4-fold higher when compared to healthy controls. Some may argue that these mutations may be age-related given our control population was infants. However, age-dependent accumulation of these point mutations is not a correct explanation of these findings as they are observed mainly in the elderly [43, 49, 81, 82], with little differences between birth and early adulthood [43, 83]. Nonetheless, age-matched controls are needed in future work to confirm that differences seen in the present study are based on doxorubicin exposure and not age. The selection of peripheral blood lymphocytes for our analysis was based on their easy accessibility as well as the observation of doxorubicin-based chemotherapy induced mutations in the HPRT reporter gene of lymphocytes in pediatric ALL patients [84–87] with their persistence for 8 years of follow-up after treatment [87].

The cardioprotective effect of dexrazoxane was thought initially to act by binding iron and thereby preventing formation of doxorubicin-iron complexes, free radical formation, and cardiac damage [24, 33–35, 88–90]; however, dexrazoxane later was discovered to interact with topoisomerase IIβ and thereby to prevent enzyme binding to doxorubicin and induction DNA double-strand breaks and cell death [13–15]. In a rat study designed to mimic human treatment protocols, dexrazoxane prevented doxorubicin-induced late-onset cardiomyopathy and protected cardiac mitochondria from damage and mtDNA deletions [27]. While doxorubicin treatment is associated significantly with increased frequencies of mtDNA deletions [28] and small-scale alterations in humans, there currently is no evidence that improvement in cardiotoxicity due to co-administration of dexrazoxane in cancer patients is attributable in part to a reduction in mtDNA lesions. In our study, we did not find a statistically significant difference in mutation burden in those receiving dexrazoxane than those who received doxorubicin likely because of a small sample size.

Dihydroorotase also plays a key role in de novo pyrimidine synthesis, which is very low in non-replicating tissues like the heart and peripheral blood lymphocytes but is essential for mtDNA replication and nuclear DNA repair [91]. Presumptive ROS-mediated DNA damage is an early event in doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity [92] and such damage can lead to increased pyrimidine synthesis as an adaptational change. Thus, upregulation of dihydroorotase activity in the heart but not in lymphocytes may account in part for dexrazoxane-dependent protection against doxorubicin-induced mtDNA alterations in hearts of rats [27] but not in lymphocytes of childhood ALL patients. Thus, the absences of a difference between the groups that received dexrazoxane and the ones that did may be due to the small sample size of our study (Table 1) or due to the cell-specific differences in metabolism and activity of dexrazoxane.

Overall, this is the first long-term study of pediatric high-risk ALL survivors to show that doxorubicin therapy affects the mutational status of the mtDNA and that the dose of doxorubicin as well as concomitant dexrazoxane therapy may not show much difference than in the children treated with only doxorubicin. However, this study has its own limitations like the small sample size of the two populations. Cardiac tissue mitochondria would have been ideal to examine when studying doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity. However, it is significantly invasive and thus, we relied on peripheral blood lymphocytes. Studies in the past have shown correlations between the peripheral blood lymphocytes and pathological tissue, when studying inherited mitochondrial pathologies [93, 94]. We recognize that, in inherited mitochondrial disease, pathogenic mutations are sometimes detected in target tissues and surrogate tissues other than lymphocytes. For example, the A3243G mutation associated with mitochondrial encephalomyopathy with lactic acidosis and stroke-like episodes (MELAS) syndrome, diabetes, and deafness was detected in mtDNA of oral mucosa but not lymphocytes [95]. Buccal epithelial cells also have a high baseline proliferation index [96] and provide an accessible pool of epithelial cells that can indicate higher levels of ROS-induced DNA damage than circulating leukocytes [97]. Thus, buccal cells should be considered as an alternative surrogate for investigating the role of mtDNA mutations in the cardiotoxicity of doxorubicin and the antimutagenic potential of dexrazoxane.

Conclusions

In summary, results of this study in long-term survivors treated on DFCI Childhood ALL protocols indicate that doxorubicin chemotherapy is associated with increases in mtDNA sequence variants in lymphocytes but the potential antimutagenic activity of dexrazoxane in heart is not reflected in circulating lymphocytes. Therefore, the role of mtDNA mutations in late-onset cardiomyopathy of doxorubicin warrants further investigation for doxorubicin-treated survivors of childhood leukemia who either received doxorubicin alone or with dexrazoxane cardioprotection.

We have reported that children without cancer but with dilated cardiomyopathy and heart failure frequently have mitochondrial DNA mutations, mitochondrial DNA deletions, or abnormal mitochondrial function [98, 99]. Several mechanisms have been proposed for doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity such as oxidative stress, free radical generation, apoptosis, impaired mitochondrial function, a perturbation in iron regulatory protein, disruption of Ca2+ homeostasis, autophagy, the release of nitric oxide and inflammatory mediators, altered gene and protein expression that involved apoptosis, and altered DNA methylation [100–102]. Recent publications have suggested that emerging mitochondrial signaling mechanisms in cardio-oncology go beyond oxidative stress [103]. Therefore, our intent here was to see in children who had received doxorubicin and were at risk for clinical cardiotoxicity but who did not have heart failure whether there was a relation between left ventricular structure and function and mitochondrial DNA mutations, as one of many causes of cardiotoxicity. We did not find that association in this study. Adult non-oncology patients with idiopathic dilated cardiomyopathy with mild to moderate heart failure similarly did not find a causal relationship with the frequency of mitochondrial DNA deletions in their cardiac tissue [104]. This suggests that other mechanisms of early doxorubicin cardiotoxicity should be explored [11–13, 105].
