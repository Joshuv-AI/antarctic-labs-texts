Association between low muscle mass and depression in South Korean adults
Authors: Hyunkyu Kim, Yu Shin Park, Seung Hoon Kim, Hyun-Sang Cho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough the association between sarcopenia and depression is well-established, studies exploring the link between low muscle mass and depression in the general population are limited. This study aimed to investigate whether participants’ muscle mass proportion was associated with the prevalence of depression. Additionally, we examined whether individuals with lower muscle mass exhibited greater symptom burden, using both categorical and continuous analytic approaches.MethodsData from the 2022 Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey were used for this study. Altogether, 4185 participants who completed the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 were included. Muscle mass was measured using the bioelectrical impedance analysis method, and the participants were divided into low, medium, and high groups based on the proportion of appendicular muscle mass relative to body weight. The association between low muscle mass and depression was analyzed using multiple logistic regression.ResultsMen in the low muscle mass group exhibited 1.99 times higher odds of depression, (odds ratio = 1.99, 95% confidence interval = 1.03–3.85) and 1.61 times higher in women (OR = 1.61, 95% CI = [1.00-2.58]) using the medium muscle mass proportion group as a reference. Furthermore, in continuous analyses, higher muscle mass proportion was inversely associated with PHQ-9 scores in both men and women, suggesting a negative relationship between muscle mass proportion and depressive symptom burden.ConclusionThis study identified an association between reduced muscle mass and depression. Future prospective studies should explore whether this relationship is causal and determine whether interventions to increase muscle mass can help reduce the prevalence of depression. Such investigations could offer valuable insights into population-level strategies for mitigating depression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-026-07885-6.

Background

Depression is one of the most common mental disorders, with a high lifetime prevalence worldwide, ranging from 2% to 21% [1, 2]. Depression diminishes the quality of life and individual functionality and leads to a significant disease burden, since it may lead to suicides [3–5]. Depression had the highest years lived with disability (YLDs) and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) in the 2019 Global Burden of Disease Study, which analyzed 12 types of mental disorders [5]. In South Korea, the number of patients treated for depression increased by 35.1% between 2017 and 2021, reaching 933,471 [6]. During the same period, treatment costs rose by 73.5%, underscoring the growing socioeconomic burden of depression. Additionally, South Korea remains among the highest-ranking among Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development countries in terms of suicide rate (25.2 per 100,000 people in 2022) [7, 8]. Thus, it is crucial to identify the factors associated with depression and suicide and find interventions to alleviate them.

Exercise is suggested to be an effective modifiable factor that can be easily implemented at the population level to prevent depression [9]. A meta-analysis observed that the relationship between physical activity and depression follows an inverse curvilinear dose-response association, demonstrating that even levels of activity below the recommended amount offer some mental health benefits [10]. A previous study conducted in South Korea has also identified a correlation between physical exercise and the prevalence of depression [11]. However, these studies largely relied on surveys of participants’ exercise habits to examine the relationship between physical activity and depression. Consequently, they had limitations in assessing the relationship between the actual physical status achieved through exercise and depression. Although muscle mass is commonly viewed as a byproduct of physical activity, it may independently influence mental health through mechanisms such as myokine secretion [12]. Exploring its association with depressive symptoms could offer valuable implications for clinical practice and public health strategies. Therefore, we used muscle mass to reflect regular exercise results as an analytical indicator instead of relying on survey data.

Several studies have reported an association between muscle mass and depression. In studies conducted at two institutions in Korea, lower muscle mass was associated with depression, with the association being more pronounced in men [13]. Another study among older adults demonstrated a relationship between sarcopenia and depressive symptoms [14], while a study conducted in China reported that reduced muscle mass assessed using calf circumference was a risk factor for depressive symptoms [15]. However, these studies were limited by their focus on specific population groups or by the use of indirect measures of muscle mass, which restricts the generalizability and interpretability of their findings. These limitations highlight the need for population-based studies using direct and scalable measures of muscle mass to clarify its association with depression.

Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) is the gold standard for accurately measuring muscle mass; however, its limitations owing to cost and radiation exposure have made it less acceptable in clinical and public health settings. Bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) is a simple, noninvasive, and cost-effective method for assessing body composition, including skeletal muscle mass. Unlike more complex and resource-intensive techniques such as dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA), BIA can be easily applied in large-scale population studies and routine clinical practice. If skeletal muscle mass measured by BIA is found to be associated with depressive symptoms, this approach could serve not only as a proxy for physical health status but also as a practical screening tool for identifying individuals at elevated risk of depression. Consequently, BIA-based muscle assessment may provide a feasible means to inform and tailor preventive or therapeutic interventions in both clinical and community mental health settings.

This study aimed to investigate whether participants’ muscle mass was associated with the prevalence of depression. Additionally, we assessed whether individuals with lower muscle mass exhibited higher severity of depression to identify potential dose dependency.

Methods

Study population and data

The data used in this study were sourced from the 2022 Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES), a comprehensive nationwide survey conducted by the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency. The KNHANES aims to assess the health and nutritional conditions of the South Korean population, thereby providing essential information for formulating and assessing health policies. This includes setting the objectives for various initiatives under the Korea Health Promotion Institute and other related programs. Although the survey is conducted yearly, the specific parameters examined may vary slightly from year to year. All individuals involved in the KNHANES provided informed consent before participation. The survey adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and was exempt from institutional review board approval owing to government regulations. Given that KNHANES offers anonymized and publicly available data, obtaining ethical approval was not necessary for this study.

Measures

Patient health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9)

The Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) is a self-administered depression screening tool consisting of nine items from the Primary Care Evaluation of Mental Disorders Instrument [16]. It is a reliable and valid method for screening depression and assessing its severity [17]. Despite its limitations as an unstructured diagnostic tool, the PHQ-9 is widely used for its brevity. Each item is rated on a scale of 0–3, producing a total score between 0 and 27. An initial study suggested a score of 10 as a valid threshold for identifying major depression with a sensitivity and specificity of 88% [17]. The Korean version standardized by An et al., has internal consistency and test-retest reliability scores of 0.95 and 0.91, respectively [18]. With a cut-off score 10, its sensitivity and specificity were measured at 87.8% and 97.4% [18]. For this study, we defined depression as a PHQ-9 score of 10 or above. To examine the association between muscle mass and depression severity, PHQ-9 scores were categorized into mild (5–9), moderate (10–14), and severe (≥ 15) levels. These thresholds are based on the clinical cut-off scores proposed by Kroenke et al. [17]. In our analysis, we combined the “moderately severe” (15–19) and “severe” (20–27) categories into a single “severe” group due to the small number of participants with higher PHQ-9 scores.

Muscle mass variable

The participants’ muscle mass was measured using BIA equipment (Inbody 970, Biospace, Korea). This method is noninvasive, portable, and allows for easy body composition assessment [19]. It uses a weak alternating current to calculate the impedance of different tissues [20]. The 2022 KNHANES dataset includes fat-free mass, muscle mass, body fat mass, total body water, and whole-body phase angle. Appendicular muscle mass was defined as the sum of the lean mass in the limbs, excluding bone mineral content. Individuals with pacemakers or implantable cardioverter-defibrillators and pregnant women were excluded.

As body weight can influence the appendicular muscle mass, we analyzed the muscle mass proportion, which is the appendicular muscle mass divided by body weight. Participants were categorized into three groups based on sex- and age-specific tertiles (low, medium, and high).

Covariates

Demographic and socioeconomic factors such as age, educational level, equalized household income, marital status, and residential area were considered covariates. Additionally, health-related factors, such as smoking status, alcohol consumption, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and chronic kidney disease history were included to refine the analysis. All multivariable models were controlled for all covariates unless stated otherwise. The body mass index was also included in the model.

Statistical analysis

Chi-squared tests were used to analyze and compare the general characteristics of the study samples. Multiple logistic regression analysis was used to assess the association between muscle mass proportion and depression. This analysis incorporated potential confounding factors, including demographic, socioeconomic, and health-related variables. The findings were presented as odds ratios (ORs) and confidence intervals (CIs) to compare the prevalence of depression among groups with varying levels of exercise. To examine the relationship between muscle mass proportion and PHQ-9 scores as continuous variables, a generalized linear model was applied using a Poisson distribution, considering the distributional characteristics of PHQ-9 scores. The analysis used stratified sampling variables (kstrata) and weighted variables, as recommended by the KNHANES. All analyses were performed using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina, USA), with statistical significance determined at p < 0.05.

Participants were categorized into low, medium, and high groups based on muscle mass proportion, determined by tertiles within each sex and age subgroup. The tertile values for muscle mass proportion (categorized by sex and age) were presented in supplementary Table 1. In the chi-squared analysis, the distribution of depression severity differed across muscle mass proportion groups in females, but not in males.

Table 2 shows the multiple logistic regression analysis results of the association between muscle mass proportion and depression. When using the medium muscle mass proportion group as the reference, men in the low group had 1.99 times higher odds of depression (OR = 1.99, 95% CI = 1.03–3.850). There were no associations between medical conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, or chronic kidney disease and depression.

Table 3 presents the association between changes in muscle mass proportion and PHQ-9 scores. For male, a 0.1 increase in muscle mass proportion was associated with a 0.172-point decrease in PHQ-9 score. For female, a 0.1 increase in muscle mass proportion was associated with a 0.528-point decrease. Supplementary Table 2 presents the logistic regression analysis results, showing the relationship between muscle mass proportion and depression across mild, and moderate to severe levels based on the PHQ-9 cut-off scores divided by sex. Among men, the odds of mild depression in the low muscle proportion group did not differ from those in the medium group; however, the odds of moderate to severe depression were higher in the low group (OR = 2.06, 95% CI = 1.05–4.04). Similarly, among women, the low muscle proportion group did not show a difference in the odds of mild depression compared with the medium group, whereas the odds of moderate to severe depression were higher (OR = 1.75, 95% CI = 1.06–2.89).

Discussion

This study found that current depression was associated with a low proportion of muscle mass in Korean adults. Furthermore, we observed that an increase in muscle mass proportion was associated with a decrease in PHQ-9 scores in both men and women, supporting an inverse relationship between muscle mass and depressive symptom severity.

Previous studies investigating the relationship between muscle mass and depression are limited. Several studies have reported a correlation between sarcopenia and depression [21]. A cross-sectional study found that patients with sarcopenia (estimated by hand-grip strength) had 2.06 times higher odds of displaying depressive symptoms [22]. In a study defining sarcopenia using bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA), individuals with lower muscle mass showed higher odds of depression, with odds ratios of 1.13 in men and 1.07 in women [13]. In a prospective cohort study, participants diagnosed with sarcopenia based on BIA had a 1.6 times higher risk ratio for developing depressive symptoms than did those without sarcopenia [23]. Several studies have focused on older adults, a demographic often prone to sarcopenia, confirming that individuals with sarcopenia generally have a higher risk of depression [14, 21, 24, 25]. Our study results align with previous research in showing a higher prevalence of depression among individuals with lower muscle mass. However, our findings demonstrated an association between depression and muscle mass even in the lower terciles, unlike earlier studies that focused on extreme muscle loss, revealing this relationship across a broader spectrum of muscle mass.

To explore the association between muscle mass and the severity of depressive symptoms, we conducted two complementary analyses. First, we performed a logistic regression using PHQ-9 cut-off scores to categorize depressive symptoms into mild and moderate to severe levels (Supplementary Table 2). Although the analysis was based on a single-year dataset and the number of individuals in each severity category was limited—resulting in a lack of statistical significance—there was a consistent trend in both men and women: individuals with a lower muscle mass proportion had higher odds ratios as depression severity increased. In addition, we modeled the PHQ-9 score as a continuous variable and found an inverse association with muscle mass proportion, indicating that lower muscle mass was related to higher depressive symptom scores (Table 3). Taken together, these findings suggest a possible association between muscle mass and the severity of depressive symptoms. Although causality cannot be inferred, increasing muscle mass may be linked to reduced depressive symptom burden, with potential implications for clinical practice and public health. Further prospective and interventional studies are warranted to validate this relationship.

Several hypotheses have been proposed regarding the mechanisms that link low muscle mass and depression. Myokines secreted during muscle contraction may mediate this relationship. Some myokines can cross the blood–brain barrier (BBB) and affect the brain, along with brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), which are hypothesized to be associated with depression. BDNF affects regeneration and increases hippocampal function, and a decrease in BDNF is related to depression in older adults, suggesting that reserved muscle mass may reverse and prevent depressive symptoms by increasing BDNF [26]. Prior studies involving older adults have found baseline myokine levels to be associated with quality of life and depression [27]. Additionally, low muscle mass may be a proxy indicator of reduced physical activity. Exercise helps reduce depression [10, 11, 28–30]. Depression has been associated with atrophy in brain structures, such as the hippocampus, anterior cingulate cortex, and prefrontal cortex [31]. Exercise can enhance the volume of the hippocampus, nucleus accumbens, and prefrontal cortex, potentially contributing to its antidepressant effects [32–34].

This study had several limitations. First, using cross-sectional data makes it challenging to establish a clear cause-effect relationship between low muscle mass and depression prevalence. Determining the temporal relationship between muscle mass reduction and depression is challenging. However, it is plausible that muscle mass reduction occurred prior to the onset of depressive symptoms given that depression is assessed based on current symptoms and that reduced muscle mass likely results from long-term physical inactivity. Second, the reliance on survey data may have introduced a recall bias. Third, other crucial confounding variables such as a family history of mental disorders, medication history, and comorbid psychiatric conditions could not be considered because these factors were not included in the KNHANES dataset. Additionally, KNHANES is a sample survey of South Korea’s general population, and excludes individuals who were hospitalized or in facilities for severe depression during data collection. Therefore, patients with severe depression might respond differently to exercise.

Despite these limitations, our study has several strengths. First, data were collected through stratified sampling considering Korea’s demographic and regional structures. Thus, these findings are broadly generalizable and may apply to populations with similar demographic characteristics. Second, the muscle mass measurements used in this study were obtained using BIA, a practical and noninvasive method widely employed in clinical and public health settings. Unlike many previous studies that assessed sarcopenia through dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA)—methods that are precise but less accessible in routine settings—this study utilized muscle mass data derived from BIA, a simple and noninvasive method already incorporated in national health screening programs in Korea. The use of muscle mass proportion measured via BIA suggests a feasible and scalable approach to identify individuals at higher risk of depression based on an accessible physical health indicator. This contributes practical value by offering an easily implementable screening marker for depression risk in clinical and public health settings. Third, our results indicate that even modest reductions in muscle mass are associated with a higher risk of depression, unlike studies focusing on pathological sarcopenia. These findings emphasize the importance of maintaining a consistent muscle mass through exercise for potential mental health benefits.

Conclusions

This study identified an association between reduced muscle mass and depression, using data representative of the Korean adult population. Furthermore, we observed that an increase in muscle mass proportion was associated with a decrease in PHQ-9 scores in both men and women, supporting an inverse relationship between muscle mass and depressive symptom severity. Future prospective studies should investigate whether this relationship is indeed causal and whether interventions to increase muscle mass can reduce the prevalence of depression. Such research could provide valuable insights into interventions aimed at reducing depression at the population level.
