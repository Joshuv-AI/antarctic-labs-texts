Beyond traditional risk scores: Proteomic prediction of heart failure in MASLD
Authors: Jessica A Regan
Published: 2026

Alongside rising rates of obesity and other cardiometabolic diseases such as type 2 diabetes, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD; formerly non-alcoholic fatty liver disease [NAFLD]) now affects 30% of adults globally and is increasingly recognized as a systemic cardiometabolic disorder rather than a disease confined to the liver [1]. Similar to the general population, cardiovascular disease remains the leading cause of mortality in patients with MASLD, exceeding liver-related complications including cirrhosis. Heart failure (HF)—particularly HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), which itself is characterized by a substantial metabolic comorbidity burden—is increasingly recognized as a major cardiovascular manifestation of MASLD. However, despite evidence that MASLD confers an approximately twofold higher risk of incident HF, traditional HF prediction models rely largely on demographic and clinical risk factors and incompletely capture the inflammatory, fibrotic, metabolic, and multisystem biology linking MASLD to adverse cardiac remodeling [2]. Beyond natriuretic peptides such as NT-proBNP, a growing body of literature has sought to identify novel proteomic markers associated with HF pathophysiology and adverse cardiovascular outcomes [3]; however, proteomics-based HF risk stratification in MASLD populations remains underdeveloped. In this context, large-scale plasma proteomics has emerged as a promising strategy to characterize the liver–heart axis at a molecular level, offering new opportunities for biologically informed HF risk stratification, mechanistic discovery, and precision therapeutics.

In the current issue of the American Journal of Preventive Cardiology, Xiong and coauthors address this MASLD knowledge gap using the UK Biobank to identify proteomic markers of incident HF risk that add to clinical risk prediction beyond the PREVENT clinical risk score (Fig. 1) [4]. In over 17,000 individuals with baseline MASLD and free from history of HF, the authors leveraged the baseline Olink proteomic assays for 2911 plasma proteins to determine proteomic predictors of HF risk. Over a median follow-up of 13.56 years, 5.6% of the MASLD population studied developed incident HF events (953 events out of 17,091 individuals). Using a 3:1 training and testing split within this cohort, multivariable Cox models identified 301 proteins (10.3%) associated with incident HF in patients with MASLD.

Proteomic characterization of the liver–heart axis improves HF risk prediction in MASLD. MASLD is increasingly recognized as a systemic cardiometabolic disorder associated with heightened risk of incident HF. Traditional cardiovascular risk prediction models such as PREVENT incorporate demographic and clinical variables, but do not directly account for hepatic steatosis or proteomic molecular markers that may drive HF pathophysiology. In the current study by Xiong et al., integration of large-scale plasma proteomic profiling with clinical risk prediction improved HF risk stratification in patients with MASLD. The most predictive proteins identified converged upon biologic pathways central to the liver–heart axis, including fibrosis and extracellular matrix remodeling (LTBP2, BCAN), inflammation and hepatic stellate cell activation (WFDC2), myocardial stress (NT-proBNP), and kidney injury (HAVCR1, also known as KIM-1). Incorporation of these proteomic signatures enhanced discrimination between patients at low and high risk for future HF events beyond traditional clinical predictors alone. Potential clinical applications of proteomic profiling in MASLD include enhanced HF risk stratification, phenotyping with targeted cardiac imaging, enrichment of clinical trial populations, therapy monitoring, and precision therapeutics. Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) selected 37 out of the 301 HF-associated proteins for predictive modeling. Addition of the 37-LASSO selected proteins to the PREVENT score improved the C-index from 0.723 (95% CI: 0.691–0.755) to 0.805 (95%CI: 0.776–0.835). Net reclassification and integrated discrimination analyses suggested that proteomic profiling improved directional risk reclassification assignments and the separation of predicted HF risk between future HF cases and non-cases, beyond traditional clinical predictors. These findings were robust to multiple sensitivity models including in a magnetic resonance imaging–proton density fat fraction (MRI-PDFF) defined sub-cohort. Tiered protein approaches showed NT-proBNP added to PREVENT increased the C-index from 0.723 to 0.744, while the top 5 most predictive proteins (NT-proBNP, WFDC2, LTBP2, BCAN, HAVCR1) improved the C-index to 0.782, in contrast to the full 37-protein model C-index of 0.805. These findings suggest that even parsimonious protein panels may aid in HF risk prediction in patients with MASLD.

Applications for cardiovascular risk prediction in MASLD

With enhanced emphasis on Cardio-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) syndrome, contemporary risk scores like PREVENT, now include body-mass index, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and the option to integrate hemoglobin A1C (HbA1c) and urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR). Despite these enhancements, presence of hepatic steatosis or MASLD are not captured in the PREVENT prediction models. Importantly, the PREVENT equations have been recently evaluated for cardiovascular risk stratification in MASLD populations, showing weak predictive performance (C-index 0.60) for cardiovascular event prediction [5]. However, the work presented here by Xiong et al., highlights the potential to extend precision cardiovascular medicine approaches into MASLD populations through integration of clinical risk prediction and molecular phenotyping.

Decoding biologic and molecular underpinnings

The liver-heart axis linking MASLD to HF is distinct from traditional atherosclerotic mechanisms and includes ectopic fat deposition, systemic inflammation and activation of fibrotic pathways that promote myocardial remodeling and diastolic dysfunction. The true promise of proteomics in the liver-heart axis lies not only in predictive modeling, but in determination of mechanistic underpinnings and acceleration of precision therapeutics. Notably, the highest-performing proteins converged upon biologic pathways central to both MASLD and HF pathophysiology, including fibrosis and extracellular matrix remodeling (WFDC2, LTBP2), renal and microvascular injury (HAVCR1), and tissue structural integrity (BCAN). Although these pathways are not unique to MASLD-associated HF, their identification within a MASLD-specific cohort strengthens the concept that chronic multisystem inflammatory and fibrotic remodeling represent a defining feature of the liver–heart axis. Whether these proteins represent causal mediators of HF pathogenesis or integrated biomarkers of multisystem cardiometabolic stress remains uncertain.

Clinical implications

There are multiple potential clinical implications of these findings. First, application of proteomic profiling could aid risk stratification for patients with MASLD, identifying those with subclinical HF risk or who may benefit from advanced cardiac phenotyping (echocardiography, cardiac MRI, invasive hemodynamics). Second, proteomic biomarkers can be leveraged to improve clinical trial enrollment, enabling event enrichment and the ability to track therapy-responsive biomarkers. Third, the biologic underpinnings of these proteomic findings may uncover actionable biology for novel therapies to prevent progression to HF through targeting fibrosis and inflammation. Future integrated multimodal models inclusive of clinical variables, imaging phenotypes and genomic and metabolomic predictors alongside these proteomic markers could rapidly propel precision health approaches to MASLD care.

Limitations and future directions

Although this is the largest proteomic analysis of HF risk in patients with MASLD to date, certain important limitations must be noted. First is the absence of imaging- or biopsy-based disease ascertainment, where MASLD was defined as the presence of hepatic steatosis as assessed by a fatty liver index (FLI) ≥60 plus one or more cardiometabolic traits (body mass index (BMI) or waist circumference (WC), hypertension, dyslipidemia, elevated fasting plasma glucose or presence of type 2 diabetes). Notably, a previously reported biopsy-confirmed MASLD cohort reported nearly double (10.7%) rates of incident HF compared to the 5.6% reported here in the relatively healthy UK Biobank population [6]. Another key limitation is the absence of an external validation cohort or other orthogonal confirmation of plasma proteins as markers of incident HF risk in this population. Even following external validation, practical considerations and challenges to the implementation of proteomics into future clinical practice include (1) cost and scalability; (2) assay standardization and reproducibility across platforms; (3) generalizability across ancestral background and concomitant comorbidities.

Although both resmetirom and GLP-1 receptor agonists improve hepatic steatosis, fibrosis biomarkers, and cardiometabolic parameters, relatively little is known regarding how these therapies dynamically remodel circulating proteomic signatures associated with HF risk [7]. Longitudinal proteomic profiling before and after therapeutic intervention may help distinguish treatment-responsive pathways from static disease markers and identify molecular signatures linked to cardiovascular benefit. While we await these data, ongoing efforts and education should address the underutilization of currently validated tools to enhance identification of MASLD, including clinical risk stratification with the fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) score and assessment for fibrosis and fat content with liver elastography (Fibroscan). Just as we have seen PREVENT expand to include HbA1c, eGFR and UACR, might future clinical risk calculators for cardiovascular disease risk allow for the optional addition of presence of hepatic steatosis or fibrosis if known?

Conclusions

In summary, this American Journal of Preventive Cardiology study by Xiong et al. provides proteomic predictive modeling for HF in a MASLD-specific population. Proteins improved HF prediction beyond the clinical PREVENT score and identified biologic pathways of fibrosis, extracellular matrix remodeling and inflammation connecting the liver-heart axis. As MASLD increasingly emerges as a systemic cardiometabolic disorder, proteomics may ultimately provide a critical bridge between molecular pathophysiology, individualized HF risk prediction, and precision cardiovascular prevention.
