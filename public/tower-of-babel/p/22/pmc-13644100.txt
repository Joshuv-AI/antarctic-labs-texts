Effects of optimism and self-efficacy on subjective well-being: the mediating role of psychological well-being at work in health sciences faculty
Authors: Jesus Alfonso Beltran-Sanchez, José Moral de la Rubia, Angeles Dominguez, Cirilo Humberto García-Cadena, Mónica Teresa González Ramírez, Adrian Valle de la O
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTeacher well-being is a critical component for achieving academic quality; however, few studies have identified the psychological factors that explain this well-being within the context of health sciences education. This study aims to propose and validate an explanatory model of teacher well-being based on teacher self-efficacy, dispositional optimism, and psychological well-being at work.MethodA cross-sectional study was conducted with the collaboration of 189 professors of medicine and health sciences from a Mexican university. The constructs studied were measured using the Satisfaction with Life, Positive and Negative Experience, Psychological Well-being, and Teacher Self-Efficacy scales. The psychometric properties of the instruments were determined through Confirmatory Factor Analysis and Cronbach’s alpha and McDonald’s omega coefficients. The explanatory model was tested using Structural Equation Modeling.ResultsThe scales demonstrated the necessary psychometric properties to yield valid and reliable data. The proposed model obtained excellent fit indices (χ² = 197, df = 165, p = .045; χ²/df = 1.19; RMSEA = 0.03 [95% CI: 0, 0.05], p-close = 0.970, SRMR = 0.06, GFI = 0.99; CFI = 0.99, TLI = 0.99, NFI = 0.99, IFI = 0.99). The findings indicate that teacher self-efficacy and optimism, mediated by psychological well-being at work, successfully predict subjective well-being. Although the direct effect of teacher self-efficacy on subjective well-being was not confirmed, its indirect effect through psychological well-being at work was identified. This model explains 57.1% of the variance in the subjective well-being of medicine and health sciences professors.ConclusionsThis study provides an explanatory model of subjective well-being among medicine and health sciences professors, as well as scales with psychometric evidence to measure the constructs comprising said model. The findings serve as input to guide interventions or faculty development programs aimed at increasing teacher well-being.Keywords: Subjective well-being, Psychological well-being, Optimism, Self-efficacy, Health teachers, Explanatory model, Educational innovation, Higher education

Introduction

Education is a determining factor in the development of individuals and society as a whole. It is key to reducing global poverty, reducing inequalities, empowering people, and promoting values for peace. In this sense, its relevance is so high and essential that the pursuit and maintenance of educational quality is one of the Sustainable Development Goals for 2030, established by the United Nations [1].

Various problems affect the quality of education, including economic crises, financial constraints, the conceptions of educational models and their evolution, and changes in the paradigms of teachers’ roles and responsibilities [2]. Fundamentally, several actors are involved in the educational process, and teachers are central figures responsible for achieving success and academic quality through the advancement of knowledge and technology [3]. Traditionally, the teaching profession is conceived as rewarding and challenging due to its complex nature. It involves implicit ethical commitments in the pursuit of general and specific objectives that must satisfy societal demands, which, over time, have become intense and overwhelming [4, 5]. Therefore, the management and regulation of the teacher’s emotional state are fundamental, especially given that this profession is associated with high levels of stress and burnout. This vulnerability is accentuated by frequent exposure to social scrutiny and the complex responsibility of fostering meaningful learning and training for future professionals. In short, teaching is a highly demanding task that can compromise the well-being of those who practice it [4, 6–9].

The promotion of teacher well-being should be one of the primary objectives of education and policy, especially because teachers do not always receive quality initial training or training in emotional management, which is necessary for the emotional development of students and the fulfillment of teaching duties [10, 11] In this sense, the evaluation and understanding of well-being should become a fundamental indicator for any school [12], recognizing that the teacher’s work life and work environment represent strategic factors in achieving excellence [13].

Although studies on the relationship between teacher well-being and the quality of teaching and learning have recently emerged [8, 14] there remains a need for theoretical frameworks that contribute to a better understanding and the generation of knowledge. Therefore, teacher well-being should be explored in depth, given its potential to identify ways to address the challenges that teachers face [15]. In this context, a commitment to promoting teacher well-being leads to several positive outcomes, such as improved health, better relationships, higher performance, and greater resilience [16–20], and associated with student academic achievement [21, 22] and socioeconomic impacts [23].

The study of well-being is based on two philosophical traditions: the hedonic and the eudaimonic. The first perspective deals with subjective well-being, which is the evaluation that people make of their lives, determining their level of satisfaction with life and the perceived affective balance [24, 25]. A person’s satisfaction is understood as the pursuit or leveraging of opportunities that make life happy, content, comfortable, and/or rewarding [26], while affective balance consists of acknowledging the positive and negative events of life [27, 28] On the other hand, the second philosophical tradition encompasses psychological well-being, which consists of satisfying psychological needs [6], such as self-acceptance, positive relationships, purpose in life, personal growth, autonomy, and environmental mastery [29, 30], which leads to positive functioning [31].

Both perspectives have sparked debate about the best way to define and study human happiness. Sometimes, they have been understood as contradictory positions, where the hedonic approach—focused on subjective well-being—has gained greater acceptance than the eudaimonic one—focused on psychological well-being [32, 33]. However, subjective and psychological well-being should not be thought of as opposites, but rather as complementary approaches to a complex and multidimensional phenomenon [34]. In this sense, Taheri et al. have shown that life purpose, a focus on self-growth, attention to workers’ basic needs, and organizational justice are reliable predictors of positive affect and life satisfaction [35]. On the other hand, Gluyas et al. [36] highlight seven competencies that lead to teacher well-being: self-knowledge, self-esteem, emotional control, motivation, knowledge of others, appreciation of others, and leadership, the latter being reported as a facilitator or promoter of well-being [37]. In addition, there is evidence that autonomy at work, trust, commitment, and effectiveness are also contributing factors [21].

One of the variables that has attracted the attention of the scientific community due to its relationship with well-being is optimism [8, 38–40] Optimism is considered a generalized tendency to expect favorable results, leading people to work to achieve their goals, and if unsuccessful, to dismiss the failure without significant distress [41]. In this sense, there is a strong relationship between optimism and subjective well-being. An optimistic person tends to make positive assessments, assigns greater value to positive information, worries less, and tends to be more goal-oriented [42–44] resisting stressful or adverse events more effectively [45]. In education, optimistic teachers have high expectations of success and achievement for their students [40] based on their conviction that they can influence them [16]. Some correlates of optimism are hope, positive coping, self-esteem, self-efficacy, and well-being [40, 41, 44, 46, 47].

In addition, self-efficacy is another variable related to well-being and optimism [8, 48]. It is defined as the belief people hold about their ability to perform a task, activity, or responsibility successfully [6]. In education, teacher self-efficacy is defined as teachers’ beliefs about their competencies and effectiveness in planning, organizing, and carrying out their teaching activities and in addressing challenges [49]. These beliefs have several implications, including teacher job satisfaction, lower levels of stress and burnout, and a greater likelihood of experiencing positive emotions [50–52]. The latter variable links subjective well-being to emotional balance. However, there is still a need for studies that comprehensively investigate the relationship between self-efficacy and psychological factors [53].

Promoting faculty well-being is an obligation of educational institutions. Teachers have the right to live and work in a healthy environment, with the necessary conditions for their growth and holistic development—including physical, mental, spiritual, and social aspects—giving rise to a sense of well-being [8]. Teachers have the right to view their preparation and demonstrate competence positively, to feel that their efforts are worthwhile, to be recognized and valued, and to perceive that their leaders care about improving their working conditions.

Consequently, it is necessary to understand the relationships and mechanisms that promote teachers’ well-being. Based on this need, the present study proposes and validates an explanatory model of teachers’ subjective well-being in health sciences education, incorporating teacher self-efficacy, dispositional optimism, and psychological well-being, defined here as psychological well-being at work (see Fig. 1).

Methods

Design

A quantitative study was conducted using a non-experimental, predictive, cross-sectional design. The purpose of such designs is to identify the variables that predict a specific outcome or criterion [54, 55].

Sample

The sample consisted of 189 professors from a school of medicine and health sciences at a private university in Mexico. Of the total, 102 (53.97%) identified as women, 86 (45.50%) as men, and one (0.53%) preferred not to specify their sex. Regarding contract type, 76 were full-time professors, and 113 were part-time. Most participants were based at different campuses in the major cities of Mexico: Monterrey (n = 119), Mexico City (n = 37), Guadalajara (n = 21), Chihuahua (n = 9), and others (n = 3). The mean age was 45.9 years (SD = 11.1), and the mean length of employment was 10.8 years (SD = 7.7). Regarding academic degrees, 185 reported holding a postgraduate degree, and only four reported holding an undergraduate degree.

The data were collected in 2022 using non-probabilistic convenience sampling. Participants were invited via email, where they were given a general overview of the project, its objective, and the option to withdraw from the study at any time. The inclusion criteria stipulated that participants must have been active faculty members during the data collection period and have two or more years of experience at the university. Incomplete surveys were excluded.

Instruments

Satisfaction with life scale

The cognitive component of subjective well-being was measured using the Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS), developed by Diener et al. [56] and validated for the Mexican population by López-Ortega et al. [57]. For this purpose, items with established validity and reliability in Mexico were used, employing a five-point response scale (strongly disagree – strongly agree).

Scale of positive and negative experience

The affective component of subjective well-being was assessed using the Scale of Positive and Negative Experience (SPANE). Developed by Diener et al. [58], this instrument has demonstrated validity in Mexico, as reported by Daniel-González et al. [59]. It consists of two dimensions, each containing six items, measured on a Likert scale with five response options ranging from rarely or never to very frequently or always.

Psychological well-being scale

Psychological well-being at work was measured using the Psychological Well-Being Scale. We employed an institutional version validated for the teaching population, based on the Spanish adaptation by Díaz et al. [60] of the original model by Ryff and Keyes [30]. This university version is significantly shorter than its predecessors, comprising 11 items distributed across three dimensions: satisfaction with achievement, positive relationships, growth and acceptance.

Life orientation test-revised

Optimism was measured using an adaptation of the Life Orientation Test-Revised (LOT-R). Designed by Scheier et al. [61], this scale is widely used globally [62–65] However, because reliability in Latino populations has previously been reported as moderate, this study adapted the instrument by modifying the response system, shifting from an agreement-based scale to a frequency-based scale.

Ohio State Teacher Efficacy Scale (OSTES)

The construct of teacher self-efficacy was measured using the OSTES, developed by Tschannen-Moran and Woolfolk [66]. Although the scale is available in multiple formats, the 24-item and 12-item versions are the most prominent. This study utilized the short version (12 items), removing one item deemed inappropriate for the university context.

Ethical aspects

In accordance with the standards established by the American Psychological Association [67] institutional approval was obtained for data collection. Participants were informed about the purpose of the research, the estimated duration, and the different stages of the study. They were informed of their right to refuse to participate at any time and provided with contact information to resolve any doubts about the project. Likewise, the anonymity of the responses and the exclusive academic use of the data were guaranteed.

The institution’s ethics committee approved the study, classifying the project as “low risk” and assigning the reference number EMCS-22-003-1. It is important to note that under Mexican law, the data collected is not considered sensitive personal data [68].

Statistical analysis

The distributions of the variables, total scores, and scale factors were described using measures of central tendency, dispersion, and shape; specifically, the mean, standard deviation, skewness, kurtosis, and maximum and minimum values were calculated. Univariate normality was assessed based on skewness and excess kurtosis values within the range of [-3, 3] [69] and multivariate normality was verified using the Mardia test [70].

Regarding the psychometric properties of the scales, reliability and factor structure were estimated using Cronbach’s alpha (α; assuming tau-equivalence) and McDonald’s omega (ω; when tau-equivalence was not met). Tau-equivalence was verified through the homogeneity of the factor loadings; these were considered homogeneous when their estimation intervals overlapped [71]. Moreover, construct validity was verified using Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) with an estimation method of Diagonally Weighted Least Squares (DWLS), which is specifically for data obtained with Likert-type scales.

For the structural equation model, the correlation was calculated using Pearson’s product-moment correlation (r) with a 95% confidence interval. The parameters of the path analysis model were estimated using the DWLS method.

Psychological well-being at work

The scale measuring well-being in the teaching environment originally consisted of 11 items divided into three dimensions: (1) Satisfaction with achievement, (2) Relationships, and (3) Growth and acceptance. The DWLS estimation method was used because the assumption of multivariate normality was not met (b2M = 216.18, z = 29.75, p < .001).

Initially, the model was tested with three dimensions; however, the factors of “Satisfaction with achievement” and “Growth and acceptance” were found to be highly correlated (r = .90). Consequently, we chose to combine these into a single dimension labeled “Development in the Institution,” composed of the items from the two dimensions.

Subsequently, the model was re-specified using a two-factor structure; however, inspection of the modification indices showed that the item “I often feel alone because I have few friends at the institution with whom I can share my concerns” had a cross-loading with the other factor, leading to its removal. After this refinement, the Relationships dimension was left with only two items, which means it no longer provides sufficient information to yield stable estimates and errors. Therefore, following the principle of parsimony, the decision was made to adopt a single-factor model, which will be named Psychological Well-Being at Work.

This re-specification was applied, and the modification indices were reviewed again. It was found that the item “I haven’t experienced many close and trusting relationships at the institution ” shared high covariance with two other items, so it was also removed.

The final measurement model consisted of nine items with factor loadings above 0.50 and demonstrated excellent fit (χ² = 24.80, df = 27,p = .424; RMSEA = 0.01 [95% CI: 0, 0.06], p-close = 0.944, SRMR = 0.04, GFI = 0.99; CFI = 0.99, TLI = 0.99, NFI = 0.99, IFI = 0.99). Teacher self-efficacy

The teacher self-efficacy model was initially specified as a three-dimensional model, measured by 11 items. The DWLS estimation method was used because the assumption of multivariate normality was not met (b2M = 178.05, z = 14.24, p < .001). Explanatory model

The hypothesized model consisted of two exogenous variables (Teacher Self-Efficacy and Optimism), a mediating variable (Psychological Well-Being at Work), and an endogenous variable (Subjective Well-being). Prior to model evaluation, multivariate normality was assessed; the results indicated that the assumption was not met (b2M = 49.64, z = 12.03, p < .001). Consequently, parameter estimation was performed using the DWLS estimator, utilizing 95% confidence intervals calculated with a robust method.

The initial hypothesized model yielded an excellent fit to the data (χ² = 190, df = 160, p < .077; RMSEA = 0.03 [95% CI: 0.00, 0.05], p-close = 0.971, SRMR = 0.06, GFI = 0.99; CFI = 0.99, TLI = 0.99, NFI = 0.99, IFI = 0.99). Inspection of the parameter estimates, evaluated using 95% confidence intervals, revealed that the direct path from Teacher self-efficacy to Subjective well-being was not statistically significant (β = − 0.16, 95% CI [-0.32, 0.00], p = .051).

Following the recommended model trimming procedures to achieve parsimony, the non-significant path was constrained to zero (removed). The re-specified model (Model 2) also demonstrated an excellent overall fit (χ² = 197, df = 165, p = .045; RMSEA = 0.03 [95%: CI 0, 0.05], p-close = 0.970, SRMR = 0.06, GFI = 0.99; CFI = 0.99, TLI = 0.99, NFI = 0.99, IFI = 0.99). Model 2 explains 57.1% of the variance in teachers’ subjective well-being (see Fig. 2).

Discussion

The first objective of this study was to evaluate the psychometric properties of instruments measuring self-efficacy, optimism, psychological well-being at work, and subjective well-being among university professors. This objective was successfully met, as the validated scales demonstrated robust properties, yielding valid and reliable data regarding the psychological constructs they intend to measure.

Few studies have examined life satisfaction among teachers. Based on existing research, it appears that satisfaction is not consistently related to educational levels; while some studies report relatively high levels of satisfaction or scores above the theoretical average [33, 80–82], others have found low levels of satisfaction [13, 83, 84]. The results of the present research align with the group of teachers who report being satisfied with life.

Interestingly, the studies reporting greater life satisfaction share certain commonalities; for instance, Mexico (this study) and Indonesia [82] are countries often characterized by collectivist beliefs. In contrast, studies reporting lower satisfaction were primarily carried out in Europe (Austria, Croatia, the Czech Republic, Serbia, Slovenia, and Romania) and New Zealand—countries that tend to be more individualistic. It is advisable to continue this line of inquiry and further investigate the effect that general culture and institutional characteristics have on the subjective well-being of teachers [23].

Regarding the relationship between teachers’ self-efficacy and psychological well-being at work, our results are consistent with those reported by Cansoy et al. [6], who found a statistically significant correlation between the two variables, with a Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.73 and an R² of 0.53. A similar association was reported by Stanculescu [84] between teachers’ well-being and teaching self-efficacy (r = .32, p < .010). However, these data contrast with the findings of Ilisko et al. [21]. While they found a correlation between the two variables, they observed the opposite directionality in their model, in which Psychological Well-being was statistically related to Teacher Self-Efficacy.

Furthermore, the relationship between self-efficacy and life satisfaction (cognitive component of subjective well-being) in our study aligns with the findings of Bjorklund et al. [17], who reported a positive relationship of moderate-to-low magnitude (r = .29, p < .050; β = 0.26, p < .010). Similarly, Ngui and Lay [85] found a positive association between self-efficacy and subjective well-being (r2 = 0.45). These statistically significant correlations and modeled associations fully support our proposed hypothesis.

On the other hand, the correlation of Optimism on Psychological Well-being at Work has been supported (r = .32, p < .001; β = 0.33, p < .001), as has its relationship with Subjective Well-Being (β = 0.39, p < .001), composed of Life Satisfaction (r = .40, p < .001) and Affective Balance (r = .43, p < .001). Similar results were found by Yu and Lou [86] regarding the relationship between Optimism and Subjective Well-Being (r = .40, p < .010; β = 0.16, p < .001). Similarly, Stanculescu [84] reported a positive correlation between Optimism and Subjective Teacher Well-Being (r = .47, p < .010).

Likewise, Diener et al. [58] found that Dispositional Optimism was associated with Psychological Well-Being—or Flourishing, as the authors call it (r = − .59, p < .001)— and with Affective Balance (r = .61, p < .001). It is essential to note that the positive correlations of optimism measured by Diener et al. [58] indicated a positive relationship with pessimism or low optimism. Based on the above, it can be affirmed that the hypotheses regarding correlations between Optimism and Psychological Well-being at Work, and between Subjective Well-being and its dimensions, are supported, with coefficients similar to those reported in other studies.

Regarding the relationship between Psychological Well-being at Work and Subjective Well-being, this study has verified the association between the first variable and the dimensions of subjective well-being; that is, a relationship between Psychological Well-being and Life Satisfaction (r = .45, p < .001) and Affective Balance (r = .37, p < .001), as well as the predictive effect of Psychological Well-being at Work on Subjective Well-being ( β = 0.52, p < .001). A similar result was found by Diener et al. [58], who found a correlation of 0.62 (p < .001). The study conducted by Chen et al. [87], like the present one, demonstrated that the concepts or approaches to well-being were not the same and were distinct constructs, confirmed by a two-factor model using the frameworks of Carol Ryff [88] and Ed Diener [56, 58] which had an adequate fit (X2 = 2624.45, df = 667, N = 795, RMSEA = 0.061 [CI: 0.058, 0.063], SRMR = 0.051, CFI = 0.90).

Conclusions

A model with good data fit, valid for the sample of university professors, was obtained. This provides scientific evidence for designing teacher training interventions to increase teachers’ perceived self-efficacy and optimism. Likewise, the findings indicate that the greater the optimism, self-efficacy, and psychological well-being in the teaching environment, the greater the subjective well-being of the participants.

Additionally, the study identifies a gap in literature regarding the relationship between psychological well-being and subjective well-being. In this case, the findings indicate that psychological well-being is associated with subjective well-being. This result is logical because the assessment of subjective well-being is broader and more abstract than psychological well-being—particularly in this study, where the indicators used to measure the latter construct are limited to a particular context and its contribution to improving teachers’ lives. To advance this line of inquiry, it is essential to continue developing studies that incorporate control groups; however, there are indications that working at the participating university may lead to improvements in subjective well-being.

Similarly, this study provides instruments to collect valid and reliable data from samples similar to those used in this study. These instruments can be used to monitor the situation of teachers and implement timely interventions, thereby developing teacher support programs. Furthermore, this research contributes to the body of knowledge in developing countries, particularly by validating instruments with the psychometric properties necessary to obtain valid and reliable data, as well as an explanatory model of teachers’ subjective well-being based on modifiable variables. This brings developing countries closer to those considered developed, where the study of the variables in question is conducted through large-scale standardized surveys and advanced statistical analyses (e.g., modeling, multi-group CFA, or machine learning) [80, 89–92]. However, the cultural adaptation of measurement models remains necessary to ensure validity and to establish evidence of measurement invariance [93].

This study is subject to several limitations. One is sample size, which affects the interpretation of statistics and should be considered when making generalizations from the results. Likewise, the sample consisted only of professors from a private university in four Mexican cities. Thus, the recommendation is to increase the sample size and incorporate participants from more cities in the country, in addition to considering professors from disciplines other than Medicine and Health Sciences [94]. The research design could also be considered a limitation, as stronger inferences about the directionality of the relationship would require experimental or quasi-experimental studies, where the use of control groups would provide greater confidence in the interpretations. Similarly, a longitudinal study is recommended to observe the stability of these variables over time, which, if not manipulated or undergoing an abrupt change, should remain stable [23, 86]. Based on the above, it is advisable to continue the study of the instruments, analyzing them using invariance tests and with larger sample sizes. In addition, it would be desirable to consider designing scales that, from the outset, take into account the culture and cultural nuances of Mexicans.

Given the strength of the correlations and effects of the variables, the data from this study can serve the institution as inputs for training programs, which can aim to promote a positive, healthy, and resilient organization in the face of future challenges and unforeseen events [8]. Similarly, teachers with high well-being can influence students to behave and approach life similarly, effects that could enhance the learning environment. In the short, medium, and long term, promoting the general well-being of teachers contributes to the achievement of the Sustainable Development Goals of health and well-being, quality education, and decent work and economic growth [11].
