Intra-articular delivery of allogeneic bone marrow derived mesenchymal stromal cells (BM-MSCs) for painful lumbar facet arthropathy: a phase I clinical trial
Authors: Wenchun Qu, Dan Yan, Nisha C Durand, Zhen Wang, Vance L Lehman, Michael D Osborne, Jeffrey A Stone, David A Miller, Sahil Gupta, Erica Engelberg-Cook, Elani F Wiest, Drew M Witter, Kathryn A Siebenaler, Alfredo Quiñones-Hinojosa, Abba C Zubair

ABSTRACT
AbstractImportanceLumbar facet arthropathy (LFA) is one of the most common specific disorders associated with low back pain. Current treatments provide only symptomatic relief and are often limited in their long-term effectiveness.Objective To evaluate the safety and preliminary efficacy of intra-articular delivery of allogeneic BM-MSCs in patients with painful LFA.Design, setting, and participantsThis study was a prospective, Phase I, single-arm, open-label clinical trial. Ten patients with painful LFA were enrolled with nine completing the 2-year follow-up.InterventionsAll patients received a single intra-articular administration into two affected lumbar facet joints, with each joint injected 10 million allogeneic BM-MSCs suspended in 1 mL Lactated Ringer’s solution.Main outcomes and measures The primary endpoint was safety, as reflected by adverse events (AEs). Secondary endpoints (and the assessment tools) included low back pain (VAS), physical function (PROMIS CAT Physical Function), health status (PROMIS CATs), and the severity of facet joint degeneration on MRIs. Preliminary therapeutic benefits were observed, including back pain relief, improved physical function, and reduced facet joint degeneration. Further larger, randomized clinical trials are warranted to further assess its safety and efficacy. Introduction

Low back pain (LBP) is a prevalent chronic musculoskeletal condition and a leading cause of disability worldwide, affecting approximately 540 million people and imposing substantial socioeconomic and healthcare burdens globally [1, 2]. Although the LBP is often classified as non-specific, lumbar facet arthropathy (LFA) is one of the most common specific disorders associated with LBP [3, 4]. LFA is a degenerative and arthritic musculoskeletal condition that affects the lumbar facet joints (LFJs), which are paired synovial joints located on the posterior aspect of the vertebral column [5]. It has an estimated prevalence of 15% to 45% among patients seeking medical care for back pain [6, 7].

LFJs are synovial joints composed of a synovial membrane, hyaline cartilage, and a surrounding fibrous capsule, which collectively maintain spinal stability and enable controlled movements [3, 5]. LFA is essentially a type of osteoarthritis (facet joint OA). It involves complex pathophysiological processes that extend beyond simple cartilage wear and tear, including inflammation, altered cell signaling, and the involvement of the entire joint and surrounding soft tissues, particularly the synovium and subchondral bone [8]. Articular cartilage exhibits a poor intrinsic healing capacity due to its avascular nature, high biomechanical demands and limited cellular activity, all of which contribute to its progressive structural deterioration and pose significant challenges for effective management [8, 9].

The treatment of LFA typically begins with conservative measures, such as analgesics and physical therapy, followed by interventional pain management. These include corticosteroid or anesthetic injections, and medial branch rhizotomy [10]. Those non-surgical therapies primarily focus on symptom management and offer only short-term pain relief. They fail to address the underlying pathophysiological changes and are often limited in their long-term effectiveness.

Mesenchymal stromal/stem cells, known as MSCs or medicinal signaling cells, represent a promising regenerative medicine approach for symptomatic synovial joint disorders due to their anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, and trophic effects [11, 12]. Currently, human MSC studies on knee OA have demonstrated a favorable safety profile, along with improvements in pain, function, and cartilage repair [13–16]. No MSC therapy has been reported for symptomatic LFA, except for the previously published case describing the first patient enrolled in this clinical trial (NCT04410731). This patient experienced no drug-related AEs and demonstrated sustained improvements in pain, physical function, and facet synovitis [17]. Hereby, we present a Phase I clinical trial evaluating the safety and preliminary efficacy of intra-articular delivery of allogeneic BM-MSCs in nine patients who completed 2-year follow-up.

Methods

Standard protocol approvals—registration—patient consent

This study was a prospective, Phase I, single-arm, open-label, single-dose clinical trial evaluating the safety and preliminary efficacy of intra-articular delivery of allogeneic BM-MSCs in patients with painful LFA. The trial protocol was reviewed and approved by United States Food and Drug Administration (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier:NCT04410731) and Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board (IRB# 20–000330). Written informed consent was obtained from each patient prior to the enrollment. This trial was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, beginning in April 2020 and concluding in August 2024. Data from one of the patients in this study has previously been published as a case report [17]. Participation in the study was entirely voluntary, with no compensation provided.

Key eligibility criteria

Eligible patients were at least 18 years of age with a clinical diagnosis of symptomatic LFA, confirmed by double medial branch blocks at L3-S1 facet joints (>75% pain relief each time). Patients with chronic axial LBP persisting at least six months and failing at least three months of conservative back pain care were considered eligible. No sex or gender restrictions were applied to this trial. Detailed eligibility criteria are available on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04410731).

Study design

This Phase I clinical trial aimed to recruit a total of ten patients with painful LFA. The first three patients were enrolled in a staggered fashion with a two-week observation period between each patient. A subsequent patient’s injection proceeded only if no unexpected serious adverse events (SAEs) possibly or probably related to the investigational drug were reported from the prior patient during the two-week observation period. Open enrollment was then initiated for Patient 4–10. The dosing protocol in this trial was informed by prior MSC studies targeting synovial joints (e.g., knee, hip, ankle), which reported doses ranging from 2 to 100 million cells in volumes up to 5 mL.[15, 16, 18–24]. Reference trials demonstrated a favorable safety profile at the highest single intra-articular dose of 20 million cells in 1 mL, supporting the proposed dosing given the similarities in joint anatomy, pathology, and standard osteoarthritis care. The present trial was carried out as a single intra-articular administration into two affected facet joints; each joint received 10 million allogeneic BM-MSCs suspended in 1mL Lactated Ringer's solution. All treated patients underwent evaluations at the screening/pre-injection visit (Enrollment), the injection visit (Day 0), and standardized follow-up visits on Day 1, Day 4, Week 2, and Month 3, Month 6, Month 12, Month 18, and Month 24, as outlined in the clinical protocol (Supplement Fig. 1). A Data Safety Monitoring Board (DSMB) was established to ensure patient safety throughout the trial. The DSMB conducted periodic reviews with the focus of the AEs and any other factors that could compromise participant safety.

BM-MSC manufacturing, preparation, and administration

The manufacturing and dispensing of allogeneic BM-MSCs were conducted by the Human Cell Therapy Laboratory (HCTL) at the Mayo Clinic in Jacksonville, Florida, in compliance with current Good Manufacturing Practice (cGMP) standards. Bone marrow was harvested from a 28-year-old female healthy donor who underwent a comprehensive medical examination and completed a standard Donor History Questionnaire. Infectious disease marker testing, including HIV-1 and HIV-2 antibodies, HIV nucleic acid testing (NAT), HTLV I & II antibodies, syphilis screening, hepatitis B surface antigen, hepatitis B core antibody, HBV NAT, HCV antibody, HCV NAT, Trypanosoma cruzi antibody, West Nile Virus NAT, and Zika virus ELISA and PCR, was conducted by a Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA)-certified laboratory. After reviewing the donor’s medical evaluation, infectious disease test results, and donor history questionnaire, the HCTL Medical Director assessed and confirmed the donor’s eligibility.

Allogeneic BM-MSCs were derived from the mononuclear cell fraction of fresh bone marrow aspirate obtained from this single healthy donor and purified via adherence to Millicell HY 5-layer culture flasks (MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA). The cells were cultured and expanded in Minimum Essential Medium α (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA), supplemented with 5% pooled human platelet lysate (Sexton Biotechnologies, Indianapolis, IN) and 1X GlutaMAX™ (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA), until a sufficient cell dose was achieved. The cells were then cryopreserved in CryoStor CS10 (10% dimethyl sulfoxide) (STEMCELL Technologies, Vancouver, BC) and stored in vapor-phase liquid nitrogen below − 150 °C. Quality control of the BMSCs was performed at the time of cryopreservation, and the test results are presented in Supplement Table 1.

On the day of treatment, the cryopreserved BM-MSCs were thawed and reformulated in Lactated Ringer’s solution, with the designated dose of 10 million cells in 1 mL. Two 1 mL syringes, each containing 10 million BM-MSCs (Passage 3), were prepared in the HCTL facility and transported in a temperature-controlled shipping container (2–8 °C) to the procedure room. An aliquot of the final formulated product was reserved for cell count, viability testing, Gram stain, and bacterial/fungal culture evaluation. BM-MSCs were administered intra-articularly to all enrolled patients using standard techniques under local anesthesia with 1% lidocaine, fluoroscopic guidance, and contrast (Omnipause 180) by the principal investigator (PI) or a qualified physician delegate. Given that local anesthetics and contrast media can alter MSC viability and the MSC secretome in a dose-dependent manner, particularly in an inflammatory environment, [25, 26] lidocaine was superficially administered without filtration to the facet joints. The contrast media was delivered in a separate syringe exclusively for needle tracking to prevent intra-articular delivery and potential interactions with BM-MSCs during the fluoroscopy-guided procedure. A universal injection protocol was followed, including the use of a Becton Dickinson Quincke 22-gauge spinal needle, fill tubes, and syringes, to ensure the accurate evaluation of the procedure’s safety and to avoid any potential bias in product administration. All treated patients were observed for 60 minutes post-injection for assessing immediate AEs, and subsequently discharged from the clinic. Outcome assessments

The primary endpoint of this study was the safety of allogeneic BM-MSC treatment for symptomatic LFA, as reflected by the nature, incidence, and severity of AEs. AEs and serious adverse events (SAEs) were defined in our previous publication [27]. Acute AEs and delayed AEs were defined as those occurring within or after two weeks post-treatment, respectively. All AEs were comprehensively assessed and documented by the study investigators using a standardized AE reporting form in accordance with Good Clinical Practice (GCP) guidelines. This included spontaneous subject reports, interviews conducted by study personnel, review of medical records, and clinical assessments during follow-up visits. Blood samples were collected at screening and at 3-, 12-, and 24-month follow-ups. Complete blood count and basic biochemical panels were evaluated for safety surveillance.

The secondary endpoints of this study were changes in low back pain, physical function, and lumbar spine magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Back pain and physical function were assessed via the Visual Analog Scale (VAS), and PROMIS Computer Adaptive Tests (CATs) Physical Function (PF). In this trial, the minimum clinically important difference (MCID) was pre-defined as a 50% reduction in VAS, or a 2.3-point improvement in PROMIS CAT Physical Function [28, 29]. Other outcome questionnaire measures, such as work functional status, opioid pain medication use, and PROMIS CAT Pain Interference (PI), Anxiety, Depression, Social Functioning, Fatigue, and Sleep Disturbance were also included in this trial. For the PROMIS CATs, higher scores indicate a greater degree of the measured concept [30]. All subjects completed questionnaires using a HIPAA-compliant computerized outcomes tool at the screening/pre-injection visit (Enrollment), the treatment visit (Day 0), and standardized follow-ups scheduled on Day 1, Day 4, Week 2, and Month 3, Month 6, Month 12, Month 18, and Month 24. The change of facet anatomical structures, focusing on effusion, bone marrow lesions, peri-articular edema, osteophytes, and the extent of facet synovitis, were assessed in lumbar spine MRIs, including T1 (sagittal and axial), T2 fat-saturated (sagittal and axial) at the screening/pre-injection visit (within the past six months of enrollment), 3-, 12-, and 24-month follow-up visits. The MRIs were assessed independently by two board certified radiologists using the Fenton and Czervionke grading system [31]. In cases of significant disagreement between the two radiologists, a third radiologist was consulted for the final assessment. The schedule of the patient procedures is outlined in Supplement Fig. 2.

Results

Baseline demographics and clinical characteristics

Between April 2020 to August 2024, a total of 24 patients with symptomatic LFA were invited for screening, and ten patients were successfully enrolled and injected with allogenic BM-MSCs. The reasons for screening failure among 14 patients included not meeting the eligibility criteria (n = 9) and declining consent (n = 5). Of those ten treated patients, one permanently withdrew during the long-term follow-up due to COVID-19 travel restrictions. The remaining nine treated patients completed the 2-year study and constituted the outcome measure dataset. The trial stages are illustrated in Fig. 2. The mean (SD) age was 55.9 (10.0) years (range: 38 to 68), with 50% (n = 5) female. All patients were White, with an average BMI of 26.9 kg/m2. Nine patients had experienced low back pain for over three years and one patient had a history of back pain for two years. All patients experienced no long-term benefits from prior treatments, including pain medications, physical therapy, corticosteroid/opioid injections, nerve blocks, acupuncture, radiofrequency ablation, and transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS). At the time of BM-MSC administration, five patients were confirmed with the bilateral symptomatic LFA at L4-L5 (Patient 1, 2, 3, 6, 9), two at L5-S1 (Patient 4, 8), and three with unilateral LFA at two adjacent levels of L4-L5 and L5-S1 (Patient 5, 7, 10). The baseline measures for VAS, and PROMIS CAT PF were 6.65 ± 1.09, and 38.3 ± 6.45, respectively. The baseline demographics and clinical characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Safety and adverse events

A total of 72 AEs and 3 SAEs were reported among the ten patients in the study. Of these, 28 AEs were considered as acute, and 47 were categorized as delayed. Adverse events (AEs) were assessed for causality (possibly, probably, or definitely related) and further categorized as expected, expected and procedural-related, or not study-related. During the acute follow-up period, four patients experienced possibly procedure-related AEs, primarily manifesting as localized pain, itching, or discomfort at the injection site. Other acute AEs (n = 24) included skin rash, diarrhea, headache, low back and buttock pain/stiffness, pressure around the tailbone, pain localized to the posterior superior iliac spine, muscle tension, were resolved through over-the-counter medications, conservative management strategies, or spontaneous recovery and were considered expected or not related to the study. During the delayed follow-up period, three patients reported SAEs (n = 3), including knee arthroplasty (Patient 6), hemilaminectomy on the contralateral side of the injected site (Patient 7) and Stage 3 prostate cancer (Patient 10). All events were assessed to be unrelated to the study. The AEs and evaluations are summarized in Table 2. No abnormalities were found in the post-injection blood tests and physical examinations.

Visual analogue Scale, and PROMIS CATs

Patients completed self-reported questionnaires at eight scheduled follow-ups visits. VAS, and PROMIS CAT PF were the primary outcome measures used to assess the preliminary therapeutic benefits in pain and function. Patient-level responder analyses evaluated the proportion of patients achieving MCIDs in VAS and PROMIS CAT PF at 6, 12, 18, and 24 months, as illustrated in Fig. 3. The pain responder rate, defined as a ≥ 50% reduction in VAS scores, increased from 33.33% (n = 3/9) at 3 months to 75% (n = 6/8) at 6 months and sustained at 66.67% (n = 6/9) at 12, 18, and 24months post-treatment. A consistent physical function responder rate of 55.6% (n = 5/9), defined as a ≥ 2.3-point improvement in PROMIS CAT PF scores, was observed across all follow-up visits.

Discussion

This open-label, single-arm Phase I clinical trial demonstrated a favorable safety profile for intra-articular administration of allogeneic, culture-expanded BM-MSCs in the treatment of painful lumbar facet arthropathy. The manufacturing and delivery of the regimen were successful for all enrolled patients. No study-related SAEs occurred during the study. Aside from the expected event of injection-site discomfort following lumbar injection, no AEs were attributed to the investigational drug. One treated patient withdrew from the study after one year due to COVID-19-related travel restrictions, with no study-related AEs reported before withdrawal. The majority of patients met the MCID thresholds for VAS, and PROMIS CAT Physical Function at multiple follow-ups, highlighting the therapeutic benefits for pain relief and functional improvement.

Among emerging regenerative medicine strategies for treating OA, the use of culture-expanded MSCs has been extensively studied due to their versatile potential to reduce inflammation, facilitate cell communications, and mediate articular cartilage repair through immunomodulation [32–37]. Currently, substantial clinical evidence supports the safety of MSCs for knee OA. In the present study, no drug-related AE was observed. This finding is in agreement with evidence from pilot studies and early-phase clinical trials evaluating the safety of MSC therapy for knee OA [33, 34, 38]. The safety profile has also been corroborated in other clinical trials for treating discogenic pain caused by intervertebral disc disease(IDD) and extends beyond the musculoskeletal field [27, 39–41]. The preliminary efficacy findings are consistent with other studies using MSCs for the treatment of OAs [15, 33, 34, 42–44]. Additionally, three completed randomized controlled trials of allogeneic MSCs for discogenic pain caused by IDD reported significant pain relief on VAS at ≥ 24 months, along with functional improvement in ODI at ≥ 24 months than comparison groups of hyaluronic acid or mepivacaine [28, 41, 45, 46]. In our study, we also observed mental health improvements, as assessed in PROMIST CATs. This finding aligns with previous studies in musculoskeletal and neurological conditions that pain experience is multidimensional and closely correlated with improvements in functional outcomes, as well as enhanced mental well-being, emotional resilience, and overall quality of life [47–49]. In addition to the primary pain and functional outcomes, the present study assessed the severity of facet joint degeneration on T2-weight MRI [31]. No association was found between functional improvement and facet joint degeneration, revealing the multifactorial influences of biomechanical, neurological, and psychological factors on functional recovery [50].

Autologous MSC therapy is advantageous on patient-specific compatibility, whereas allogeneic MSC use is more cost-effective and logistically convenient for large-scale clinical applications. Excellent tolerance of allogeneic MSCs has been demonstrated to support their safety profile [51, 52]. Currently, there is no definitive clinical benefit of autologous MSCs over allogenic MSCs. The significant variability in the secretome composition and immunomodulatory potency of MSCs from different donors likely contributes to highly inconsistent outcomes, which can be properly regulated through potency assays for allogeneic donor selection [53–55].

This trial is, to the best of our knowledge, the first to evaluate allogeneic BM-MSCs in treatment of painful LFA. Our findings supported the safety of intra-articular delivery of BM-MSCs, along with the preliminary efficacy of pain relief and improved function. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution due to the inherent limitations of a Phase I clinical trial, including a small sample size, absence of a control group, and the exploratory nature of efficacy assessments. This trial was conducted at a quaternary academic health center with multidisciplinary specialized expertise, advanced diagnostics and comprehensive treatment plan which may pose challenges in generalizability due to potential patient selection bias and referral bias. A Phase II randomized placebo-controlled trial is currently underway to address most of the limitations discussed here (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT06001853).

With respect to the dosing regimen, no standardized guidelines or consensus have been established, and no documented human experience for LFA was available at the time of developing this trial. The dosing strategy was therefore based on related MSC trials for synovial joints, including those for the knee, hip, and ankle [15, 16, 18–24]. To ensure dose consistency, 10 million BM-MSCs in 1 ml Lactated Ringer’s solution were injected per facet joint, for a total of two LFJs per patient. The optimal dosing regimen remains an open discussion for further clinical investigations.

During the course of our clinical study, increasing evidence has ascribed the therapeutic effects of MSCs primarily to paracrine signaling, involving the secretion of bioactive factors with anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties that promote trophic (regenerative) effects at the injury site, rather than through long-term engraftment and differentiation into other cell lineages [36, 56–61]. Also, an increasing number of studies of biochemical markers are investigating inflammatory status and bone and cartilage degradation as indicators of pathophysiological changes within joint tissues, which may aid in monitoring and progression assessment of OA [62]. Unfortunately, the collection of synovial fluid or other biospecimens for cytokine analysis was not feasible in this trial, limiting the ability to investigate MSC preservation, mechanisms of action, and biomarker studies of disease progression in facet joint OA. Future studies may benefit from incorporating omics-based approaches for biochemical markers or assessments of systemic effects to enhance the understanding of MSC-mediated therapeutic mechanisms and disease progression.

Conclusion

This Phase I clinical trial (CellKine) suggests a favorable safety profile for intra-articular delivery of BM-MSCs for the treatment of symptomatic LFA. Preliminary therapeutic benefits were observed, including back pain relief, improved physical function, and reduced severity of facet joint degeneration. Additional larger, randomized clinical trials are warranted to further assess its safety and efficacy.
