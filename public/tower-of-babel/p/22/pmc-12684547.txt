Epicardial Adipose Tissue in Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy Cardiomyopathy
Authors: Eleanor Greiner, Kimberley G Miles, Zhiqian Gao, Sean M Lang, Thomas D Ryan, Nicholas J Ollberding, Benjamin S Mantell, Meilan M Rutter, Cuixia Tian, Chet R Villa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDuchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) has unique characteristics when compared with other forms of non‐DMD dilated cardiomyopathy. We sought to describe epicardial adipose tissue (EAT), the fat layer surrounding the myocardium, and investigate its relationship to longitudinal cardiac changes in DMD.MethodsThis single‐center, retrospective, cohort study included all DMD patients with ≥5 cardiac magnetic resonance studies between 2015 and 2022. EAT thickness was measured in the right atrioventricular groove during end diastole. Adiposity data were obtained from dual‐energy x‐ray absorptiometry scans. Linear mixed‐effects regression was used for statistical modeling.ResultsA total of 117 patients were included with 590 cardiac magnetic resonance studies, 333 dual‐energy X‐ray absorptiometry, and 255 NT‐proBNP (N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide) values. The median age at first cardiac magnetic resonance was 13.1 years and at any cardiac magnetic resonance study was 15.9 years. The median EAT thickness was 15.5±4 mm. EAT thickness was positively associated with age (P<0.001) and measures of general adiposity (P<0.01). Increased EAT was positively associated with NT‐proBNP and negatively associated with left ventricular volume and left ventricular mass indexed for body surface area (P<0.05). There was no association with left ventricular ejection fraction (P=0.98) or for left ventricular volume (P=0.61) or mass (P=0.52) when indexed for height.ConclusionsDMD is associated with early, appreciable EAT, which increases over the life span and is positively related to measures of general adiposity. Increased EAT is associated with higher levels of NT‐proBNP without a change in systolic function, suggesting it may contribute to diastolic dysfunction in DMD.Keywords: cardiomyopathy, Duchenne muscular dystrophy, epicardial adipose tissueSubject Categories: Cardiomyopathy, Heart Failure

Clinical Perspective

What Is New?

What Are the Clinical Implications?

The cardiomyopathy phenotype in Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) has classically been described as a form of dilated cardiomyopathy. However, some patients with DMD have minimal left ventricular (LV) dilation as they age, even in the setting of cardiac dysfunction. 1 A study by Mazur et al found that in a cohort of 21 DMD patients with reduced systolic function (defined by LV ejection fraction <55%), less than half had a dilated phenotype. 1 In addition to the absence of dilation, diastolic dysfunction is increasingly recognized as an early cardiac manifestation in DMD. 2 , 3 One potential pathologic contributor to this phenomenon that has yet to be studied in DMD is epicardial adipose tissue (EAT). EAT is a unique fat depot that has an unobstructed relationship with the underlying myocardium and has gained attention for its potential role in the development of cardiovascular disease in individuals without DMD. 4 , 5 Specifically, EAT can secrete proinflammatory and profibrotic cytokines and has a strong association with obesity. 5 , 6 Although the exact mechanism is unknown, EAT is thought to have an impact on ventricular remodeling. This theory has been postulated in previous studies showing that increased EAT is associated with less ventricular dilation 7 , 8 , 9 and more significant diastolic dysfunction, possibly through an inflammatory mechanism or by acting as a mechanical barrier surrounding the heart. 10 , 11 , 12

The current study aims to characterize the EAT burden in patients with DMD and explore the relationship between EAT thickness, overall body adiposity, cardiac biomarkers, and cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) findings.

Methods

Study Population

This was a single center, retrospective cohort study from Cincinnati Children’s Hospital Medical Center (CCHMC). The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at CCHMC (No. 2022‐0500), and no informed consent was required. Patients were identified through a CCHMC multidisciplinary DMD clinical database. Patients of any age evaluated between January 1, 2015 (when current CMR sequences were widely used clinically) and December 31, 2022, with ≥5 CMR studies were included (Figure 1). This deidentified clinical data may be available to other researchers upon request of the corresponding author. As patients typically have CMRs yearly, this number was selected to evaluate for longitudinal changes in each patient over about 5 years. CMR studies of poor quality that limited measurement acquisition were excluded, resulting in some subjects with <5 studies. Subjects with only 1 CMR of adequate quality to measure EAT were excluded from longitudinal CMR analysis. Adiposity data from dual‐energy x‐ray absorptiometry (DXA) scans were included for subjects if obtained within 3 months of a corresponding CMR. Demographic data, clinical data including results from pulmonary function tests, cardiac imaging, and serum biomarker data were obtained from the CCHMC database. Serum biomarkers were included if obtained within 3 months of a corresponding CMR. All data used in this research study were collected for clinical care purposes before December 31, 2022. Data collection did not require patient/subject interaction as it involved retrospective collection from existing medical records.

DXA Acquisition

Whole‐body DXA scans were obtained using a Horizon A DXA system (Hologic, Marlborough, MA) and were analyzed using software version 13.6.1.3. All DXA scans had been previously conducted as part of standard DMD medical care. Weights were obtained directly from the DXA scanner or a Hoyer‐based weight assessment. Heights were obtained using either standing measurement (for ambulatory patients) or calculated on the basis of ulnar length (for nonambulatory patients). 13 Body mass index was calculated using these values. For the purpose of this study, we recorded body mass index, percent body fat, and whole‐body fat from the previously read DXA reports. Fat mass index (FMI) was calculated by taking whole‐body fat (in kg) divided by height squared (in m 2 ). 14 , 15

CMR Acquisition

Per institutional practice, CMRs are performed yearly in patients with DMD beginning around age 7to 9 years or when scans can be tolerated without sedation. All CMR studies were performed using a 1.5T scanner (Ingenia; Philips Healthcare, Best, the Netherlands). A ventricular 4‐chamber view was prescribed using a 2‐chamber and pseudo–short axis acquisition for reference. Volumetric and functional assessments were made using a standard cine steady‐state free precession short‐axis stack from the mitral valve annulus to the apex (2‐chamber and 4‐chamber acquisitions were used for reference). Typical parameters for steady‐state free precession images were field of view 300×112 mm, matrix size 168×158 mm, slice thickness of 6 to 7 mm without gap, pixel resolution 1.8×1.8 mm, repetition time/echo time 2.3/1.17, 30 phases per R‐R interval, and parallel imaging factor 2. Contrast is routinely administered to assess for late gadolinium enhancement (LGE). Once LGE is definitively present, contrast administration is spaced to about every 3 years, to avoid unnecessary gadolinium exposure, decrease scan time and lessen cost, as has been previously reported. 16 For studies with contrast, a gadolinium‐based agent like Dotarem (gadoterate meglumine, Dotarem; Guerbet LLC, Princeton, NJ) was administered at a dose of 0.2 mmol/kg and LGE images subsequently obtained using a standard inversion recovery sequence protocol 8 to 10 minutes after injection. The presence of LGE was reported if present in ≥2 imaging planes. Cvi42 postprocessing software (Circle Imaging, Alberta, Canada) was used to measure cardiac volume, mass, and function.

Measurement of EAT Thickness

EAT thickness was measured retrospectively using Cvi42 postprocessing software. Linear measurements were obtained in the right atrioventricular groove during end diastole on 4‐chamber steady‐state free precession cine (Figure 2) with effort made to avoid including pericardial fat in the analysis. This is a previously validated, simple‐to‐conduct protocol that can be applied to previously sequenced CMRs. 17 , 18 , 19 A primary reviewer (E.G.) measured the EAT thickness on all imaging studies, and a secondary reviewer (K.M.) independently measured the EAT thickness on 10% of the studies selected by a random number generator to assess for interrater reliability. While no universally accepted normal values exist for EAT thickness, some studies suggest values >9.5 mm in men as abnormal. 20

N‐terminal Pro‐B‐Type Natriuretic Peptide

Per institutional practice, NT‐proBNP (N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide) levels are collected as part of routine monitoring at each DMD clinic visit, occurring either annually or biannually depending on patient age and the presence or absence of ventricular dysfunction or LGE. NT‐proBNP is an important cardiac biomarker and has been shown to be associated with all‐cause death in DMD. 21 At CCHMC, NT‐proBNP is performed on EDTA plasma (or lithium heparin plasma) on a Dimension Vista System (Siemens, Munich, Germany). The analytical measurement range of the assay is 5 to 35 000 pg/mL with a limit of detection of 0.8 pg/mL. A 1:5 automated dilution also extended the clinical reportable range to 175 000 pg/mL. The normal range for NT‐proBNP is age dependent; for patients aged 3 to 9 years the upper limit of normal is 255 pg/mL, for patients aged 10 to 14 years is 240 pg/mL, for patients aged 15 to 17 years is 180 pg/mL, and for adults aged 18 to 74 years is 124 pg/mL.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics are presented as frequency (percentage) for categorical variables and median (interquartile range) for continuous variables. Longitudinal data comparing a subject’s first and most recent CMR were compared using McNemar’s test or a paired t test. Interrater reliability of EAT thickness was calculated using intraclass correlation coefficients with 95% CIs. The mean difference in EAT thickness between raters was compared using a paired t test.

The association of EAT thickness with general adiposity (body mass index, percent body fat, and FMI) was assessed using linear mixed‐effects regression including a random subject‐specific intercept to account for the repeated measures within patients. Age was included as a covariate and restricted cubic spline terms (5 knots placed at the default percentiles) used to allow for potentially nonlinear relationships. Spline terms were dropped and linear associations assumed on the basis of the likelihood ratio test.

The effect of EAT thickness on LV ejection fraction, LV end‐diastolic volume (LVEDV) indexed to body surface area (BSA; LVEDVi), LVEDV indexed to height (m2.7), left ventricular mass indexed to BSA (LVMi), LV mass indexed to height (m2.7), and NT‐proBNP was also assessed using linear mixed‐effects regression. In addition to the conventional practice of indexing LVEDV and LV mass (LVM) by BSA, the values were also indexed by height to account for the effects of obesity. This is a previously reported method in cardiac imaging. 22 Covariates were selected a priori and included age, ambulatory status, the age by ambulatory status interaction, and age at steroid start. P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. The corresponding authors had full access to all data used in the study and take responsibility for its integrity and analysis.

Results

Demographics

During the 8‐year study period, 117 patients met the inclusion criteria. The median age at the time of the first CMR was 13.1 years (Table 1). The majority of patients were nonambulatory and on corticosteroid therapy at the time of their first included CMR, with a median corticosteroid treatment duration of 7 years. Aside from steroids, the next most common therapies included angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors and aldosterone antagonists. In addition to the 76 subjects who were nonambulatory at the time of the first CMR, an additional 20 patients lost ambulation over the course of the study period, representing 82% of the total cohort. The median age of ambulation loss was 13.5 years with an interquartile range of 12 to 16 years.

EAT Thickness

The mean EAT thickness was 15.5±4 mm with a range of 5.4 to 30 mm. EAT increased linearly with patient age (<0.0001). After adjusting for age, EAT was found to have a positive relationship with measures of general adiposity including body mass index (P<0.0001), percent body fat (P=0.002), and FMI (P<0.0001; Figure 3). Among these measures, the association with FMI showed the strongest linear fit (F[1, 108]=17.3; P<0.0001).

After adjusting for age, steroid duration, and ambulatory status, increased EAT thickness was found to have a negative association with LVEDVi (P<0.05) and LVMi (P<0.05) when using conventional indexing to BSA (Figure 4). However, there was no association between EAT thickness and LVEDV (P=0.61) or LVM (P=0.52) when indexed to height. EAT thickness was not associated with LV ejection fraction (P=0.98) or LGE burden (P=0.69) on multivariable analysis.

N‐Terminal Pro‐B‐Type Natriuretic Peptide

Of the 590 CMR studies, 255 had corresponding NT‐proBNP levels obtained for analysis. The median value was 57.0 (interquartile range, 31–120) pg/mL. Interrater Reliability

EAT thickness was evaluated by a primary reviewer for all studies and by a secondary reviewer for 10% of studies. The intraclass correlation coefficient between measurements was 0.73 (95% CI, 0.60–0.83) with a mean difference of −0.19 mm (−0.86 to 0.48), which was not significantly different from 0 (P=0.58), suggesting no important difference between reviewer measurements.

Discussion

The current study demonstrates that DMD is associated with early, appreciable EAT. EAT increases over the life span and is positively correlated with measures of general adiposity, particularly FMI. EAT is associated with elevated NT‐proBNP levels in the absence of systolic dysfunction, suggesting a potential role in impaired ventricular relaxation. Collectively, these findings indicate that EAT is prominent in DMD, correlates with adiposity, and may contribute to the development of diastolic dysfunction. Interestingly, FMI showed the strongest correlation with EAT thickness, supporting the notion that the hallmark fat accumulation in DMD could be a key driver in EAT formation in this population. These findings are relevant as patients with DMD have an elevated risk of obesity and adiposity with age due to pathologic fatty replacement of muscle. 23 Furthermore, EAT is a therapeutic target for glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists and sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors, providing a new potential mechanism for targeting DMD cardiomyopathy. 24 , 25

The EAT burden in DMD is considerable. Although no universally accepted thresholds for abnormal EAT thickness exist, a large echocardiography‐based study suggests a cutoff of >9.5 mm in men as abnormal, 20 and a previous CMR‐based study reported an average EAT thickness of 10.7±3 mm in a healthy adult population. 18 The current study demonstrates that patients with DMD have a considerably higher EAT burden.

DMD cardiomyopathy is characterized by fibrofatty infiltration of the myocardium, diastolic dysfunction, 2 , 3 and progressive LV dysfunction 26 and has a remodeling phenotype distinct from non‐DMD dilated cardiomyopathy, heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, and controls. 1 , 27 , 28 While the current study shows that EAT thickness is associated with less ventricular dilation and LVM when the traditional measure of LVEDVi and LVMi are used, the relationship is no longer present when LVEDV and LVM are indexed to height. This discrepancy suggests that the negative associations between EAT and both LVEDVi and LVMi are being driven by obesity, as both EAT and BSA increase as weight increases. It is important to note that because patients with DMD are at a high risk of obesity, traditional indexing practices relying on BSA may underestimate volume and mass measurements, which is a limitation previously noted in obesity literature. 29 It is also worth noting that indexing to BSA remains the most common approach in the DMD literature. In‐depth exploration of the optimal approach to assessing longitudinal changes in cardiac dimensions in DMD are lacking and generally needed given that loss of ambulation, increasing adiposity, and onset of cardiomyopathy tend to occur concurrently in the midteens. Understanding these relationships is important not only in that it will help characterize the pathogenic progression of DMD but also because the introduction of vamorolone is likely to change the athropometrics of patients with DMD. 30

Importantly, the current study demonstrates a positive association between EAT and NT‐proBNP. NT‐proBNP is a biomarker of myocardial stretch and volume overload and in patients with biventricular circulations with preserved systolic function has proven to be highly correlated with diastolic dysfunction. 31 , 32 It has been theorized that EAT contributes to diastolic dysfunction by acting as a mechanical barrier on the underlying myocardium, which impairs ventricular filling in a constrictive pericarditis‐like fashion. 33 This theory is based on data demonstrating that increased EAT is associated with higher filling pressures, higher LV eccentricity indices, and diminished exercise capacity in patients with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction. 11 , 12 Although our study lacks invasive hemodynamic data, the observed association between EAT thickness and NT‐proBNP, without corresponding systolic dysfunction, suggests a similar phenotype.

This relationship is particularly significant in light of recent studies indicating that diastolic dysfunction, as assessed by CMR, is common in patients with DMD. 3 Given the positive association between EAT thickness and NT‐proBNP levels in DMD, it is plausible that EAT contributes to impaired diastolic function. Moreover, both diastolic dysfunction and elevated NT‐proBNP have been linked to increased death in DMD, 3 , 21 underscoring the clinical importance of this association and its potential therapeutic implications. Notably, EAT expresses glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptors, positioning it as a potential target for glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists. These agents have been shown to significantly reduce EAT thickness in patients with type 2 diabetes, independent of weight loss. 34 , 35

The current study has limitations inherent to retrospective studies; however, a few specific topics are worth noting. For EAT quantification, we chose to measure EAT thickness. While this methodology has been previously validated in the literature, 17 , 18 , 19 EAT volume has been cited by some as a superior method, although it is more technically difficult and time intensive. 36 As EAT is not standardly measured or reported on CMR, we chose to use the more straightforward measurement approach, which could be easily replicated and applied by cardiologists caring for patients with DMD in real‐world settings. Additionally, the results garnered from this study are associations, and thus no definitive conclusions about cause and effect can be made. Moreover, only patients with ≥5 CMR studies were included to review trends over time. This inherently limits patients who moved out of our system, died at a young age, or were unable to have serial magnetic resonance imaging studies completed due to certain orthopedic hardware, pacemakers, and so on. Furthermore, our cohort primarily consisted of living patients, limiting the ability to assess EAT thickness in relation to cardiac‐specific death. We also did not have a cohort of healthy children in whom we could compare EAT.

Conclusions

Patients with DMD are at an increased risk of general adiposity, which is associated with EAT accumulation. Our findings suggest that a greater EAT burden in DMD is linked to markers of diastolic dysfunction. Further research is needed to explore the potential benefits of EAT‐targeting therapies, such as sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors and glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists in this patient population.
