Middle temporal vein approach for transvenous embolization of cavernous sinus dural arteriovenous fistula: A case report
Authors: Masaomi Takeuchi, Yoichi Morofuji, Eri Shiozaki, Yuki Matsunaga, Takayuki Matsuo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background: Transvenous embolization (TVE) is the preferred treatment for cavernous sinus (CS) dural arteriovenous fistula (dAVF), typically accessed through the inferior petrosal sinus (IPS). In cases where the IPS is inaccessible, alternative routes are required.Case Description: We report a case of an 87-year-old female with left conjunctival hyperemia and exophthalmos due to the left CS dAVF draining into the superior ophthalmic vein. Because of the IPS occlusion and severe facial vein tortuosity and stenosis, TVE was performed through the middle temporal vein (MTV).Conclusion: This case demonstrates the feasibility and effectiveness of the MTV route for TVE in anatomically challenging CS dAVF cases.

INTRODUCTION

Endovascular treatment has become the first-line therapy for cavernous sinus (CS) dural arteriovenous fistula (dAVF), with transvenous embolization (TVE) through the ipsilateral inferior petrosal sinus (IPS) considered the most preferred approach.[11] However, this route is occasionally not feasible because of the occluded IPS.[2] Alternative approaches, such as the facial vein (FV) route accessing the superior ophthalmic vein (SOV), have been reported. Yet, these routes can sometimes be challenging because of marked tortuosity and stenosis of the FV, angular vein, or SOV.[4] Here, we report a rare case of CS dAVF successfully treated with TVE through the middle temporal vein (MTV), demonstrating its potential as an alternative route in anatomically challenging cases.

CASE DESCRIPTION

An 87-year-old female referred to our department with progressive left conjunctival hyperemia and exophthalmos, which had worsened over 2-month period. Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) revealed a CS dAVF fed by the left middle meningeal artery, left accessory meningeal artery, left artery of foramen rotundum, and right inferolateral trunk. These feeding arteries converged at the posterolateral part of the left CS and drained into the left SOV [Figure 1]. Initially, we performed transarterial embolization to reduce shunt flow by coil embolization of the major feeding arteries. Subsequently, we proceeded with TVE. However, the left IPS was occluded, and the FV was markedly tortuous and stenotic, making these routes unsuitable. Therefore, an alternative approach was planned through the external jugular vein (EJV), superficial temporal vein (STV), and the MTV, whose diameter was 1.7 mm. Under general anesthesia, a 6-French guiding sheath (Flexor Shuttle Guiding Sheath 80 cm; COOK Medical, Bloomington, IN, USA) was inserted into the right femoral vein. After systemic heparinization, a 4-French diagnostic catheter was placed in the left common carotid artery for angiographic imaging as needed. Through a 6-French guiding catheter (Roadmaster 90 cm; Goodman, Aichi, Japan) and a 3.2-French distal access catheter (Guidepost 120 cm; Tokai Medical Products, Aichi, Japan), we advanced a 1.6-French microcatheter (Headway Duo 156 cm; Terumo, Tokyo, Japan) over a microguidewire (ASAHI CHIKAI 0.014 inch 200 cm; Asahi Intecc, Aichi, Japan) into the CS. Although there was resistance at several points due to venous valve structures, we successfully navigated the microcatheter through the left subclavian vein, EJV, STV, MTV, SOV, and into the posterolateral part of the left CS with the support of a quadruple coaxial catheter system. After filling the microcatheter with iopamidol 300, three-dimensional rotational angiography was performed to confirm the optimal positioning of the catheter tip. Selective embolization was performed in the compartment containing the shunt points, resulting in complete obliteration of the arteriovenous shunt [Figure 2]. Postoperatively, the patient’s ocular symptoms improved in the following days and have not recurred at 12-month follow-up.

DISCUSSION

TVE is the preferred treatment for CS dAVF, typically accessed through the IPS.[11] However, when the IPS route is not feasible due to occlusion, alternative approaches must be considered. When ipsilateral IPS cannulation fails, routes such as the contralateral IPS, superior petrosal sinus, or FV can be used.[2,12,14] Although less commonly employed, access through the pterygoid plexus[8] or the STV and MTV route[7] can be viable options, particularly when these vessels are dilated and serve as major drainage pathways. Other options include direct puncture or surgical exposure of the SOV, FV, and STV.[1,3,9] While these methods are occasionally necessary, they carry potential risks. The FV-SOV route, for example, involves navigation through a tortuous course between the angular vein and the SOV and lacks the bony and dural support typically found in the IPS route, making it more susceptible to complications such as venous injury from device manipulation. Surgical approaches may result in cosmetic issues, intraorbital hemorrhage, injury to the levator muscle or supraorbital/trochlear nerve injury, infection, or granuloma formation.[13] Direct puncture may lead to venous occlusion, potentially resulting in increased intraocular pressure and visual impairment, particularly if alternative drainage routes fail to develop. In cases where occlusion occurs in the SOV, it may lead to retrograde cortical venous drainage, potentially transforming into an aggressive dAVF with a risk of intracranial hemorrhage. Therefore, the transfemoral venous approach is generally preferred over direct puncture of the STV or SOV when feasible.

Our case demonstrates the successful use of the MTV as the alternative approach route as the conventional access through the IPS was occluded and the route from the jugular vein through FV was severely tortuous. Although only a few cases have been reported in which CS dAVF were treated by TVE through the MTV approach,[10] our case proved that TVE can be achieved through the MTV even when it is not the main drainer. Preoperative DSA showed that the FV was dilated as the main drainer. However, we identified the MTV as a potential approach route and assessed its feasibility using contrast-enhanced computed tomography and DSA. These imaging modalities have been reported to be useful in the preoperative evaluation of an alternative venous access route.[15] We considered that access to the CS would be feasible through the EJV and STV, which ran in a relatively straight course. Although the probability of successful access through the occluded IPS has been reported to be 50–80%,[5] we did not attempt the route. In cases with drainage into the SOV, a relatively straight path to the shunt points may be achieved through the SOV. Through a guiding sheath, a guiding catheter, a distal access catheter, with T-connector for each of them, we navigated a microcatheter over the long distance from the ipsilateral EJV to the STV, MTV, SOV, and, finally, to the ipsilateral CS. The efficacy of the quadruple coaxial catheter system has been described in our previous report, offering improved reachability through complex venous pathways and enhanced stability during the mechanically demanding process for target embolization.[6] In addition, skin compression has been reported as a useful technique to straighten the tortuous route between the STV and MTV, facilitating microcatheter navigation.[7]

CONCLUSION

Our case contributes to the limited existing literature by demonstrating the feasibility and effectiveness of the MTV approach for TVE in CS dAVF. This approach may serve as a valuable alternative in cases where conventional access routes are not feasible, offering a practical solution for anatomically challenging cases.
