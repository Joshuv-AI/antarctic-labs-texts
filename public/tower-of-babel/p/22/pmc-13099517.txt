Integrating nonlinear modeling with pT stage and nodal variables: A multi-center Con-pTN model for prognosis prediction in gastric cancer (pT2–4b)
Authors: Weiwei Zhu, Can Hu, Yanqiang Zhang, Ruolan Zhang, Qing Yang, Yizhou Wei, Mengya Zhou, Ruixin Xu, Jintao He, Ke Shen, Xiangdong Cheng, Zaisheng Ye, Zhiyuan Xu, Siwei Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryLymph node metastasis critically determines prognosis in gastric cancer (GC), yet conventional staging inadequately captures interactions between tumor invasion depth and nodal burden. In this retrospective multi-center study, we analyzed 2,370 patients (pT2–4b) undergoing curative gastrectomy and 724 patients with SEER to develop a contour-like pTN (Con-pTN) model using a Gaussian process-augmented Cox framework that integrates pT stage, retrieved lymph nodes, and positive lymph nodes as continuous variables. Con-pTN showed robust discrimination, with area under the curves (AUCs) of 0.797 (training), 0.804 (internal validation), 0.748 (external validation cohort 1), and 0.813 (external validation cohort 2). Calibration and decision curve analyses demonstrated good agreement with observed outcomes and favorable clinical utility. Compared with pTNM, rN, and LODDS, Con-pTN provided significantly improved prognostic stratification, particularly among high-risk patients. These findings support Con-pTN as a biologically informed, clinically applicable approach for postoperative risk assessment in GC.Subject areas: Oncology

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is the fifth most commonly diagnosed cancer and the fourth leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, posing a significant public health challenge. Since the early 21st century, advancements in radical gastrectomy, adjuvant chemotherapy, and immunotherapy have improved the prognosis for patients with GC; however, the five-year overall survival (OS) rate remains below 40%.1,2 In addition to enhancing early detection and optimizing treatment strategies, accurate staging has become a crucial factor in improving patient outcomes.3 The American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging system serves as a fundamental framework for assessing GC prognosis, incorporating tumor depth, lymph node (LN) involvement, and metastasis to inform clinical decision-making.4,5 Nevertheless, numerous studies have highlighted the limitations of this system, particularly its reliance on the number of positive lymph nodes (pLNs), which can be significantly affected by the number of retrieved lymph nodes (rLNs) during surgery.6,7,8

To overcome the limitations of traditional pN staging, innovative approaches such as the ratio-based N (rN) and the log odds of positive lymph nodes (LODDS) staging systems have been introduced. These methods incorporate both the number of rLNs and the number of pLNs, providing a more nuanced assessment of LN status. Research has shown that these approaches offer greater prognostic accuracy compared to conventional pN staging. For example, in a study by Sun, the LODDS classification revealed a significant hazard ratio in multivariate analysis (HR = 1.445, 95% CI: 1.369–1.525, p < 0.001), demonstrating superior prognostic performance compared to the traditional pN system.9,10

Moreover, the integration of these metrics into advanced staging systems, such as the T-revised N-M staging, has demonstrated exceptional prognostic capabilities, enhancing risk stratification and informing clinical decision-making.9,11,12 However, the current pTNM staging system primarily relies on a linear combination of categorical variables to classify patients based on prognostic evaluations. This method lacks the necessary granularity and does not fully account for the complexities of tumor behavior. This limitation is particularly noticeable in cases where patients within the same stage experience significantly different clinical outcomes, underscoring the need for more sophisticated prognostic tools.13

In this study, we introduce a contour-like pTN (Con-pTN) model that utilizes a nonlinear Gaussian process-augmented Cox proportional hazards (GP-Cox) fitting approach. This nonlinear fitting technique facilitates the identification of complex biological interactions and threshold effects, enabling more precise stratification of patient prognoses. By enhancing accuracy, the Con-pTN model serves as a valuable tool for optimizing individualized treatment strategies.

Both the number of pLNs and the number of rLNs are treated as continuous variables and critical factors in the assessment of LN status.14,15 However, a simple linear regression model is insufficient to fully capture the complex relationship between these variables and patient prognosis. We incorporated these metrics into the GP-Cox model, recognizing the critical importance of the pT stage in GC prognosis and including it as a key predictive variable. This integration resulted in the development of a three-dimensional Con-pTN model, which offers enhanced prognostic insights. Consequently, we created and validated this three-dimensional Con-pTN model, demonstrating improved prognostic performance compared to other staging systems.

Results

Clinicopathological characteristics

A total of 3,094 patients participated in this study, which included 2,184 patients from Zhejiang Cancer Hospital (comprising a training cohort of 1,530 patients and an internal validation cohort of 654 patients), 724 patients from the SEER database (external validation cohort 1), and 186 patients from Fujian Cancer Hospital (external validation cohort 2) (Figure 1). The detailed clinicopathological characteristics of all participants are summarized in Table 1. Among the entire cohort, 1,185 patients (50.0%) were aged 60 years or older, and 1,699 patients (71.7%) were male. The majority of patients (96.9%) had pathologic stage II or III GC. The average number of pLNs across the four cohorts was as follows: 7.3 ± 7.7 in the training cohort, 7.1 ± 7.8 in the internal validation cohort, 5.9 ± 7.5 in the external validation cohort 1, and 8.0 ± 9.0 in the external validation cohort 2. Similarly, the mean number of rLNs was determined to be 29.7 ± 10.6 in the training cohort, 29.8 ± 11.0 in the internal validation cohort, 21.9 ± 12.7 in the external validation cohort 1, and 38.2 ± 13.6 in the external validation cohort 2. The proportion of patients with more than 15 rLNs was high: 94.9% in the training cohort, 94.3% in the internal validation cohort, 66.0% in the external validation cohort 1, and an impressive 97.8% in the external validation cohort 2.

Construction of the Con-pTN model for predicting prognosis

Since the number of pLNs, rLNs, and pT stages were treated as continuous nonlinear variables, we utilized a nonlinear GP-Cox model to integrate these variables from the training cohort into our model construction. Importantly, higher RiskScore values generated by the model were associated with poorer survival outcomes, reflecting an inverse correlation with prognosis. The results of the GP-Cox fitting were visualized using contour plots (Figure 2A). Figure 2A depicts the three-dimensional prognostic risk surface derived from the GP-Cox model. To improve interpretability, Figures 2B–2D represent orthogonal two-dimensional slices extracted from this three-dimensional surface, each illustrating a distinct cross-sectional relationship between two variables while holding the third dimension implicit. We divided the RiskScore into six equal groups, labeled group 1 through group 6. The number of patients in each group was as follows: group 1 (n = 149), group 2 (n = 449), group 3 (n = 380), group 4 (n = 260), group 5 (n = 209), and group 6 (n = 87). To evaluate the distribution of clinicopathological features across risk strata, we compared baseline characteristics of patients in each of the six Con-pTN model groups within the training and validation cohorts (Tables S1–S4). Patients within the same group were assumed to have similar prognostic outcomes. Our findings indicated that an increasing pLNs, when accompanied by an adequate rLNs, aligns with previous studies,16 demonstrating that the risk of poor prognosis rises as the number of pLNs increases (Figure 2B). Additionally, the variability in the pT stage relative to the number of rLNs underscores the importance of thorough lymphadenectomy (Figure 2C). Furthermore, a higher number of pLNs was found to correlate with a more advanced pT stage, reinforcing the notion that LN involvement and retrieval are critical factors in both staging and prognosis (Figure 2D). We additionally provide an Excel-based lookup table (Table S5) that allows clinicians to directly obtain the corresponding RiskScore and group by inputting pT stage, pLNs, and rLNs.

Subgroup-enhanced prognostic stratification through the Con-pTN model outperforms traditional pTNM, pT, and pN staging in GC

Our Con-pTN model exhibited superior prognostic stratification compared to the traditional pTNM staging system in the training cohort (Figures 3A and 3B). In contrast, the pTNM system demonstrated limited discriminatory power, with considerable overlap among stages, particularly during the early survival periods. These results were consistent across both the internal validation cohort (Figure 3F) and the external validation cohorts (Figures 4B and 4F).

After stratifying patients into six groups (groups 1–6) using our model, we observed separated survival curves with minimal overlap, indicating a distinct gradient of risk across the groups (Figure 3A, p < 0.0001). In the training cohort, the five-year OS rates for groups 1 through 6 were 95.3%, 82.0%, 62.0%, 44.2%, 22.6%, and 8.7%, respectively. In contrast to the TNM-based pT (Figure S1A) and pN (Figure S1B) staging systems, the Con-pTN model provides a more detailed and precise stratification, particularly for high-risk populations. While the pT4 and pN3b stages do not effectively differentiate high-risk patients, our Con-pTN model successfully identifies distinct prognostic subgroups within these categories, allowing for a finer level of risk stratification.

These findings were consistent across the internal validation cohort (Figures S1C and S1D), external validation cohort 1 (Figures S1E and S1F), and external validation cohort 2 (Figures S1G–S1H). This consistency reinforces the robustness and clinical applicability of the Con-pTN model. Overall, our results indicate that this model provides a more precise and actionable framework for individualized risk assessment and treatment planning in GC.

Superior prognostic discrimination of the Con-pTN model compared to rN and LODDS staging systems

The prognostic efficacy of our Con-pTN model was thoroughly compared with traditional staging systems, including the rN and LODDS staging systems, within the training cohort (Figures 3A–3D). Specifically, the Con-pTN model revealed the following five-year OS rates: group 1 had an OS of 95.3%, group 2 had 82%, group 3 had 62.0%, group 4 had 44.2%, group 5 had 22.6%, and group 6 had 8.7%. The model demonstrated an impressive ability to capture survival heterogeneity across these groups, achieving distinct curve separations and minimal overlap between adjacent groups.

In the training cohort, the rN staging system (Figure 3C) demonstrated modest stratification, with five-year OS rates of 92.5% for rN0, 72.5% for rN1, 49.2% for rN2, and 15% for rN3. However, it fell short of effectively stratifying high-risk groups. The LODDS staging system (Figure 3D) provided improved stratification, with five-year OS rates of 92.8% for LODDS1, 82.1% for LODDS2, 67% for LODDS3, 46.6% for LODDS4, and 15% for LODDS5. Nonetheless, it still lacked the granularity offered by our Con-pTN model, particularly in identifying high-risk populations. These findings were consistent across the internal validation cohort (Figures 3E, 3G, and 3H), where group 1 had a five-year OS of 98%, group 2 had 83.7%, group 3 had 65.1%, group 4 had 46.3%, group 5 had 21.4%, and group 6 had 10.3%. Similar trends were observed in the external validation cohort 1 (Figures 4A, 4C, and 4D), with group 1 showing an OS of 79.4%, group 2 at 55.4%, group 3 at 40.5%, group 4 at 20.7%, group 5 at 16.3%, and group 6 at 9.1%. A comparable trend was also noted in the external validation cohort 2 (Figures 4E, 4G, and 4H), with group 1 showing an OS of 94.6%, group 2 at 92.8%, group 3 at 81.5%, group 4 at 59.4%, group 5 at 23.7%, and group 6 at 0%. The ability of our Con-pTN model to stratify patients into distinct risk groups suggests an improved capacity for survival prediction compared to traditional staging systems.

Comparison of the performances of the Con-pTN model with other staging systems

To thoroughly evaluate the robustness and predictive efficacy of our Con-pTN model, we compared its performance against traditional staging systems, including pTNM, rN, and LODDS, across the training, internal validation, and external validation cohorts (Figure 5). In terms of discrimination ability, the Con-pTN model showed higher area under the curve (AUC) values for predicting five-year OS. Specifically, in the training cohort, the Con-pTN model achieved an AUC of 0.797 (95% CI: 0.773–0.820), outperforming the LODDS (AUC: 0.779, 95% CI: 0.755–0.803), rN (AUC: 0.761, 95% CI: 0.737–0.785), and pTNM (3-group AUC: 0.643, 95% CI: 0.631–0.671; 6-group AUC: 0.772, 95% CI: 0.748–0.797) staging systems (Figure 5A). Similarly, in the internal validation cohort, the Con-pTN model attained an AUC of 0.804 (95% CI: 0.788–0.840), again surpassing the performance of traditional models (Figure 5B). This trend continued in external validations. In external validation cohort 1, the Con-pTN model achieved an AUC of 0.748, comparable to the pTNM 6-group model (AUC = 0.759), with the marginal difference likely due to limited sample size or clinical homogeneity. In cohort 2, the Con-pTN model outperformed all other staging systems (AUC = 0.813; Figures 5C and 5D). DeLong tests confirmed its statistical superiority over both 3-group and 6-group pTNM models in the training, internal validation, and cohort 2 datasets (p < 0.001). In cohort 1, although the Con-pTN model showed a favorable trend, differences were not always significant—highlighting its robust but context-dependent discriminatory power (Figures 5E–5H).

The DCA demonstrated that the Con-pTN model provided greater net benefits across various risk thresholds compared to the pTNM, rN, and LODDS staging systems (Figures S2A–S2D). Calibration curves further confirmed a strong concordance between predicted and observed five-year survival rates across the training, internal validation, and external cohorts, reinforcing the model’s robustness and clinical applicability (Figures S2E–S2H). In addition to its improved discriminatory performance indicated by AUC comparisons, the Con-pTN model also displayed enhanced fit and robustness, as summarized in Table 2. The model’s linear trend χ2 and likelihood ratio χ2 values were significantly higher than those of the pTNM, rN, and LODDS staging systems, suggesting stronger associations between the model’s predictors and five-year OS outcomes. Furthermore, the Con-pTN model exhibited lower Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) values compared to traditional staging systems, further supporting its improved predictive efficacy and optimal model fit.

Discussion

In clinical practice, the prognosis of patients with GC is influenced by a multitude of factors, making accurate predictions essential for guiding postoperative treatment decisions and monitoring strategies.12 Staging systems are critical for predicting long-term survival, particularly in assessing the significance of LN status for prognostic stratification following radical gastrectomy.17 Over the past decade, various approaches have been proposed to refine LN staging and scoring systems, with the goal of improving the accuracy of long-term outcome stratification in patients with GC.18,19,20 We utilized patient data from two major medical centers, supplemented by the SEER database, to develop the Con-pTN model based on nonlinear fitting techniques. This model improved stratification performance relative to traditional staging systems, allowing for potentially more detailed classification. These findings indicate that our Con-pTN model could serve as a personalized prognostic tool, empowering clinicians to refine treatment strategies and optimize therapeutic decision-making for individual patients.

The pTNM staging system remains the gold standard for cancer prognostic classification.21 The 8th edition refined the N staging by introducing the N3b category, which accounts for the presence of more than 15 rLNs.22,23 However, notable limitations still exist, highlighting the need for further enhancements to the staging system.13 The optimal extent of LN dissection during gastrectomy for GC and the average number of rLNs during surgery vary considerably.24 Consequently, there are substantial differences in both the scope of LN dissection and the average yield of LNs.25 To address these limitations of the pN staging system, several alternative staging systems have been proposed such as the rN and LODDS staging system.9,26 The rN staging method based on the LN ratio has been shown to outperform the conventional pN staging system.17 By classifying patients according to the ratio of positive LNs, the rN stage has demonstrated improved prognostic stratification compared to the traditional pTNM staging system in our validation. In the training cohort, the rN staging system provided more refined prognostic stratification, with five-year OS rates of 92.5% for rN0, 72.5% for rN1, 49.2% for rN2, and 15% for rN3. In contrast, the pTNM system showed five-year OS rates of 97% for pTNM I, 91.5% for pTNM II, and 50.4% for pTNM III. However, the stratification offered by rN remains suboptimal, particularly for patients with intermediate and advanced-stage disease. The LODDS are an emerging prognostic classifier that enhances the accuracy of LN evaluation for predicting prognosis. It has been found to outperform the rN stage in various malignancies.15,27 For instance, a study by Wang demonstrated that LODDS provided greater discriminatory performance and higher predictive effectiveness values compared to rN in both the SEER training dataset and an independent Chinese validation cohort (20.92 vs. 20.39).9 However, stratification within high-risk groups remains insufficiently refined, underscoring the need for further improvements to address these limitations.

These classifiers highlight the importance of incorporating both positive and negative LN counts into prognostic evaluations. However, they often fail to account for the critical influence of the pT stage. The pT stage reflects the depth of invasion, a key determinant of tumor aggressiveness and metastatic potential. rLNs and pLNs provide complementary insights into the extent and accuracy of LN assessment. When analyzed jointly as continuous variables, they offer a more nuanced representation of nodal burden and the systemic dissemination of disease, while also accounting for surgical thoroughness and potential immune response. These integrated measures allow for a more biologically faithful reflection of tumor behavior. Most existing models rely on simple linear combinations that include pT staging.13 To address this limitation, we developed a Con-pTN model that integrates three key variables—the number of pLNs, rLNs, and pT stage—within a nonlinear framework to create a prognostic model. Previous studies have utilized the GP-Cox model to predict recurrence in gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs). For instance, Heikki Joensuu and colleagues developed a model that achieved an AUC of 0.88 (95% CI: 0.86–0.90).28 For predicting recurrence risk. Similarly, Ryugo Teranishi et al. classified patients with GIST into seven groups, reporting five-year recurrence-free survival rates of 98.1%, 96.6%, 92.3%, 48.0%, 37.3%, 41.0%, and 42.4%, effectively reflecting their recurrence predictions.29 Additionally, Jun Lu13 and colleagues developed the nTNM model based on the distance from pN and pT to the coordinate origin, demonstrating significant survival differences between stages (all p < 0.001). However, their model has limitations, including susceptibility to missing values and reliance on two-dimensional linear parameters.

In our survival analysis, the Con-pTN model (groups 1–6) achieved a clear separation of survival curves (p < 0.0001). Notably, this model demonstrated consistent predictive accuracy across multiple validation cohorts, establishing a clear relationship between risk scores and patient outcomes. We observed a stepwise decline in survival outcomes as the group classification increased (p < 0.05), offering additional prognostic insights.

Our Con-pTN model, developed using GP-Cox nonlinear fitting, exhibited improved performance by effectively capturing the complex biological and clinical interactions that drive tumor progression and influence patient prognosis.28 By integrating LN involvement with the thoroughness of lymphadenectomy and incorporating the pT stage as a measure of local invasion, the model enhances the stratification of survival outcomes. The model, facilitated by nonlinear fitting, allows for more refined survival curve stratification with reduced overlap, resulting in more precise predictions.5,30 Additionally, the inclusion of the number of rLNs addresses variability in lymphadenectomy practices across different institutions and helps reduce variability in nodal evaluation across different institutions.31 This likely contributes to the model’s ability to provide consistent prognostic insights across diverse patient populations, as confirmed by its validation in both internal and external cohorts.

To improve clinical usability, the model can be visualized through a web interface that allows rapid calculation of individualized risk scores and stratification groups based on pT stage, rLNs, and pLNs in the future. This tool can be further embedded into clinical application environments, such as EMR-linked decision-support systems, to support real-time treatment planning.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the model is based on retrospective data from multiple sources, which may introduce variability in clinical practices, patient demographics, and treatment modalities. To ensure broader applicability and reproducibility, prospective validation in controlled settings is necessary. Second, our model does not account for other potentially relevant factors, such as molecular biomarkers, tumor genetic profiles, or the patient’s systemic immune status. Such as microsatellite instability (MSI), Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) status, and PD-L1 expression, which are increasingly recognized as essential prognostic and predictive factors in GC. Recent trials have shown that MSI-high tumors respond favorably to immune checkpoint inhibitors, while EBV-positive and PD-L1–high subtypes may also benefit from immunotherapy.32 Incorporating these variables in future research could enhance the model’s predictive capacity and improve its ability to stratify prognosis more precisely. Lastly, although the Con-pTN model utilizes nonlinear fitting techniques, it may not fully capture the dynamic nature of tumor progression and treatment responses over time. Then, because patients with pT1 were excluded, the applicability of the Con-pTN model may be limited to non-early (pT2–4b) resected GC, and additional studies are needed to extend and validate the model in early-stage disease. Future iterations could benefit from incorporating time-dependent modeling or longitudinal data to improve applicability in dynamic clinical contexts. In addition, the integration of multimodal information, such as radiomic features or molecular biomarkers, may further enhance the model’s predictive performance and individual-level applicability.

Experimental model and study participant details

Patient population

We conducted a retrospective analysis of GC patients who underwent surgery followed by adjuvant chemotherapy based on data sourced from Zhejiang Cancer Hospital, Fujian Cancer Hospital, and the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database from January 1, 2009, to December 31, 2019. Written informed consent for the use of clinical data and biological samples for research purposes was routinely obtained from patients at hospital admission. The inclusion criteria for the cases were (1) pathologically confirmed diagnosis of gastric adenocarcinoma; (2) pT2-4bN0-3bM0 based on the AJCC 8th edition staging system; (3) age 20–80 years; (4) having undergone curative gastrectomy. The exclusion criteria were (1) the presence of concurrent malignancies; (2) prior immunotherapy, radiotherapy, or other oncological treatments; and (3) incomplete clinical or pathological data. Patients with pT1 tumors were excluded from this study due to their distinct clinical characteristics and favorable prognosis. Lymph node involvement is uncommon in this subgroup, and the use of adjuvant chemotherapy is often variable, leading to substantial clinical and treatment-related heterogeneity. Based on these criteria, a total of 3094 patients were enrolled in the study. Of these, 2,184 patients were from Zhejiang Cancer Hospital, 186 from Fujian Cancer Hospital, and an additional 724 patients were obtained from the SEER database. To facilitate model training and validation, we assigned the Zhejiang Cancer Hospital cohort to a training and an internal validation cohort by using a 7:3 random split. The SEER database cohort served as an external validation cohort (Validation Cohort 1), while the cohort from Fujian Cancer Hospital served as a second external validation cohort (Validation Cohort 2). The work has been reported in line with the strengthening the reporting of cohort, cross-sectional, and case–control studies in surgery (STROCSS) criteria.33

Outcome

The primary outcome measure was OS, which was defined as the time from the date of surgery to the last effective follow-up or the time of death. Follow-up was conducted through outpatient visits, telephone calls, and social media platforms so as to determine the postoperative survival status of patients, with partly follow-up ending in May 2024. This retrospective analysis was approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhejiang Cancer Hospital (Ethics Approval No.: IRB-2023-960[IIT]) Fujian Cancer Hospital (Ethics Approval No.: K-2023-448-01), and informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Method details

SEER data source and patient selection

Data were retrieved from the SEER database using SEER∗Stat software (Version 8.3.8; Surveillance Research Program, National Cancer Institute, USA; www.seer.cancer.gov/seerstat). Data from the SEER database and institutional records, all patient information was anonymized prior to analysis. The use of de-identified SEER data adheres to U.S. federal regulations. Patients diagnosed with gastric adenocarcinoma between 2009 and 2019 were identified from the SEER Research Plus Data (17 Registries, Nov 2021 Sub, 2000–2019). Tumor staging was standardized to the AJCC 8th edition TNM staging system for consistency. Key variables collected included age, sex, year of diagnosis, tumor grade, pT stage, number of pLNs and rLNs, receipt of systemic therapy, and follow-up information. The inclusion and exclusion criteria adhered to those used in our institutional cohort to ensure methodological consistency.

rN and LODDS staging system

The rN staging classification classifies patients based on the LN ratio, defined as the percentage of pLNs relative to the number of rLNs. Specifically, rN staging is categorized as follows: rN0 (0%), rN1 (1%–20%), rN2 (21%–50%), and rN3 (>50%).18 The LODDS staging was calculated using the formula: LODDS = log(pnod+0.5tnod−pnod+0.5), where pnod represents the number of pLNs, tnod represents the number of rLNs, and a constant of 0.5 was added to both the numerator and denominator to prevent mathematical singularity. Patients with comparable prognoses were then grouped to establish the final LODDS staging, which consisted of the following categories: LODDS1 (LODDS ≤ −1.5), LODDS2 (−1.5<LODDS≤−1.0), LODDS3 (−1.0<LODDS≤−0.5), LODDS4 (−0.5<LODDS≤0), and LODDS5 (LODDS >0).10,19

GP-cox model

Non-linear modeling in this study was performed using the GP-Cox model, which replaces the log-linear predictor of the conventional Cox model with a Gaussian Process to capture smooth, non-linear effects and interactions. The baseline hazard was also modeled with a GP, allowing flexible estimation without prior categorization of continuous variables. Bayesian inference provided robust probabilistic interpretation.28,34 We minimized subjectivity in parameter selection by employing a fully Bayesian inference framework, where hyperparameters such as kernel width and noise variance were estimated directly from the data using marginal likelihood maximization to help mitigate overfitting and enhance model stability. The model was implemented using the GPstuff toolbox in MATLAB (GPstuff - Gaussian Process Models for Bayesian Analysis; https://research.cs.aalto.fi//pml/software/gpstuff/).29,35 Three-dimensional risk surfaces and color-mapped contour plots (Figure 2) were generated using MATLAB R2022b (MathWorks Inc.). A dense mesh grid was created using the meshgrid function, and predicted risk scores from the GP-Cox model were interpolated using scatteredInterpolant with the 'natural' method. The interpolated third variable (e.g., pT) was mapped to the z axis, and the risk score was visualized as a continuous color gradient. The final plots were rendered using surf for surface display and scatter3 to overlay raw data points, with colorbar indicating the risk scale. The resulting RiskScore forms a continuous, smooth risk surface across the input space of pT stage, pLNs, and rLNs. When visualized in two or three dimensions, this surface displays contour-like patterns, where lines or regions of equal predicted risk resemble topographic contours. This characteristic visualization motivated the term “contour-like pTN model.” Since this study excluded patients with pT1 tumors, the remaining population corresponds to stages IB through IIIC in the AJCC pTNM classification system. As such, patients can be stratified into six distinct prognostic categories according to their pTNM stages. To align our model output with this clinical framework, we divided the continuous RiskScore derived from the Con-pTN model into six ordered groups, enabling clinically interpretable risk stratification that corresponds to real-world stage-based management.

Model evaluation and validation metrics

To evaluate model performance, we utilized several statistical metrics. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and precision-recall curves were employed. The continuous probability score—defined as the model-predicted five-year probability of overall survival (alive vs. deceased)—served as the foundation for these analyses, ranging from 0 to 1. The DeLong test was employed to statistically compare the area under the ROC curves (AUCs) of different predictive models.36 This nonparametric approach evaluates whether the observed differences in AUCs are statistically significant. In addition, decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to assess the clinical utility of the predictive models. DCA quantifies the net benefit at different threshold probabilities, thereby providing valuable insights into the practical applicability of the models in clinical decision-making.3 Calibration curves were employed to assess the prediction accuracy and reliability of the model. The likelihood ratio χ2 test was employed to assess homogeneity, ensuring no significant survival differences among patients within the same stage. The linear trend χ2 test was applied to evaluate discriminatory ability and gradient monotonicity, thereby confirming significant survival differences across stages and verifying whether patients in the earlier stages had better survival outcomes than those in the later stages. Moreover, the Akaike information criterion (AIC) and Bayesian information criterion (BIC) were calculated within the Cox proportional hazards regression framework to further assess model discrimination. Lower AIC and BIC values reflected superior predictive accuracy for OS outcomes, indicating a more optimal model for clinical application.11,37

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. The survival rates were estimated using the Kaplan—Meier method, and hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated to assess the relative risk of events. All statistical tests were two-sided, with a significance threshold set at α = 0.05, where P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26 and R 4.4.1 software.
