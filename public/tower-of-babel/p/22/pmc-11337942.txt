Complete removal of a cavernous malformation in the dorsal pons, in contact with the expanded transpontine vein: illustrative case
Authors: Daisuke Yamamoto, Ichiyo Shibahara, Yuri Hyakutake, Madoka Inukai, Sumito Sato, Hiroyuki Koizumi, Takuichiro Hide, Toshihiro Kumabe
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDCraniotomy is required for the removal of brainstem cavernous malformations (CMs) with repeated hemorrhage, and this condition is often complicated by an accompanying developmental venous anomaly (DVA). However, a DVA of the brainstem or cerebellum with drainage penetrating the pons is an exceptional finding.OBSERVATIONSA 57-year-old man presented with double vision. Computed tomography revealed progressive enlargement of the hemorrhage in the dorsal pons. Contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance angiography revealed an expanded transverse vessel penetrating the center of the pons in contact with the CM. Digital subtraction angiography revealed that the DVA, comprising the expanded transpontine vein and some cerebellar medullary veins acting as normal venous drainage, coexisted with the CM. By utilizing the angioarchitecture and intraoperative neuronavigation system data, electrophysiological mapping, and indocyanine green videoangiography, complete removal of the CM was accomplished while preserving the DVA and brain function.LESSONSThis study presents the intraoperative images of an expanded transpontine vein as a DVA, which has never been depicted in a live patient before, accompanied by a CM in the dorsal portion of the pons. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE24314

The prevalence of cavernous malformation (CM) and developmental venous anomaly (DVA) has been reported as 0.3%–0.5% and 2.6%, respectively, with annual hemorrhage rates of 0.39%–1.3% and 0.1%–0.2%, respectively.1, 2 In the case of hemorrhage due to brainstem CM, the rebleeding rate reaches as high as 21%–60%.3, 4 In general, if the CM is large and symptomatic and does not improve or worsen after follow-up, craniotomy is indicated.

DVA is the most common vascular malformation coexisting with CM, reported in 13%–44% of cases.5–7 DVA is a structure in which the medullary vein expands into a collecting vein and is responsible for normal venous return.1, 2, 7 Therefore, when a CM coexists with a DVA, CM resection with preservation of the DVA is required. In the case of hemorrhage due to a CM with a DVA in the brainstem, the optimal surgical approach would avoid damage to the normal brainstem structure as much as possible and would preserve venous drainage from the DVA during CM resection.

The transpontine vein runs through the brainstem from the fourth ventricle floor to the ventral side of the pons and is collected from the deep medullary veins in the cerebellum.8 The transpontine vein has never been directly visualized in a living patient.

Here, we report a case of brainstem CM with a dilated transpontine vein in which detailed preoperative imaging findings, intraoperative microscopic findings, and hemodynamics of indocyanine green (ICG) videoangiography were reported. In this case, the transpontine vein was markedly dilated, as the DVA contacted the CM on the dorsal side of the pons. Therefore, the CM was removed by preserving the transpontine vein via craniotomy.

Illustrative Case

A 57-year-old man with double vision was referred to our hospital. Computed tomography (CT) revealed cerebral hemorrhage on the left dorsal side of the pons, while magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a CM in the same area. Conservative treatment was administered; however, 6 months later, the patient’s diplopia worsened. He was referred to our department with left abducens nerve palsy. CT demonstrated rebleeding, and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) revealed a CM with an approximately 1-cm hemorrhage on the left dorsal side of the pons, which was larger than the initial hemorrhage (Fig. 1). Contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance angiography (CE-MRA) revealed a dilated vessel penetrating the brainstem anteriorly and collecting vessels from the cerebellum (Fig. 2). Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) was subsequently performed, and the venous phase of the left vertebral angiography revealed a markedly dilated transpontine vein collected from the cerebellar medullary veins as normal venous drainage. Based on the MRI and DSA images, the coexistence of CM with DVA as a dilated transpontine vein was diagnosed. The CM was in contact with the dilated transpontine vein (Fig. 3). The CM was bleeding repeatedly and increasing in size, so a craniotomy for removal of the CM was performed using a neuronavigation system, electrophysiological mapping of the facial and lower cranial nerves, monitoring of transcranial motor evoked potentials (MEPs), and ICG videoangiography. A transcerebellomedullary fissure approach was applied as treatment. Several expanded veins running longitudinally were found within the subependymal parenchyma of the fourth ventricle, and hemosiderin deposits were found superiorly lateral to the left facial colliculus. ICG videoangiography revealed longitudinal venous drainage from posterior to anterior within the subependymal parenchyma of the fourth ventricle (Video 1). Electrical stimulation mapping revealed a facial colliculus; thus, we could identify the hemosiderin deposit on the surface of the fourth ventricle, located slightly outside the suprafacial triangle.

We decided to remove the CM through the hemosiderin deposit. The CM was on the caudal side and the dilated transpontine vein was on the rostral side of the hematoma. Blood flow was not observed between the CM and the transpontine vein as a DVA; thus, the CM was removed en bloc. During removal, there was no change in the transcranial MEPs.

ICG videoangiography was performed again, facilitating complete removal of the venous drainage within the subependymal parenchyma of the fourth ventricle toward the dilated transpontine vein (Fig. 4, Video 1). Postoperative MRI showed that the CM had been removed, and CT venography (CTV) confirmed that the DVA was preserved (Fig. 5). Immediately after the surgery, left facial nerve palsy was observed. Discussion

The transpontine vein penetrates the brainstem anteroposteriorly, connecting to the subependymal veins in the region of the fourth ventricle and dorsal pons, which drain from cerebellar medullary veins.9, 10 The transpontine vein is dilated by drainage from the infratentorial DVA.10 In previous studies, DVAs in the posterior cranial fossa have been classified into 5 drainage groups: 1) transpontine vein, 2) vein of the lateral recess, 3) precentral cerebellar vein, 4) vermian vein, and 5) hemispheric cerebellar vein.10 Our case falls into the first of these 5 categories. In our case, the transpontine vein was extremely dilated because of venous drainage from a broad area of the cerebellar medullary vein and subependymal vein as the DVA. Similar findings have been reported previously.10, 11

The coexistence of CM and DVA is characterized by a female predominance, cerebellar tentorium, and repeated bleeding, and surgery can be required in cases of hemorrhage.12 As the DVA is responsible for normal venous drainage from a wide area, surgery should entail removing only the CM while preserving the DVA.9 However, to achieve this, it is necessary to collect detailed information on the positional relationship between the CM and DVA and their vascularity prior to surgery.

Observations

In the present case, maximum intensity projection (MIP) images of CE-MRA13, 14 and three-dimensional (3D)–DSA allowed us to understand the positional relationship between the CM and DVA, surrounding structures, vascularity, and hemodynamics (Figs. 2 and 3). Intraoperative ICG videoangiography further showed the preservation of the DVA following removal of the CM by demonstrating venous drainage within the subependymal parenchyma of the fourth ventricle toward the dilated transpontine vein.

There have been few reports of ICG videoangiography for CM resection,15–17 and it has been reported that the contrast between the CM and surrounding vessels is useful for understanding the anatomical location of the CM.

This is the first report on the use of ICG videoangiography for CM resection, particularly in the brainstem. This supported our understanding of the vascularity of the well-developed transpontine vein and collecting veins, such as the DVA, on the floor of the fourth ventricle during the operation (Fig. 4).

Lessons

In this case, we observed a dilated transpontine vein coexisting with a CM in a living patient. The transpontine vein should be preserved for normal venous drainage during the resection of a CM in the pons. Preoperative CE-MRA and 3D-DSA MIP are useful for understanding the localization of the CM and the transpontine vein. In addition, intraoperative ICG videoangiography is useful for recognizing DVA hemodynamics, including the transpontine vein, and surgery can be safely performed with electrophysiological mapping.
