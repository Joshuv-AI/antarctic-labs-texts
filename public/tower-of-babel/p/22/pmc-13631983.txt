C-reactive protein-triglyceride-glucose index and risk of incident depressive symptoms in older adults: an eight-year follow-up study from ELSA
Authors: Mixue Guo, Mengyuan Cai, Huqiang Dong, Zongren Zhao, Ke Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDepression is a common mental health issue among older adults, yet few studies have explored whether metabolic biomarkers predicting its onset exist. The CRP-TyG index (CTI), combining C-reactive protein and the triglyceride-glucose index, may more effectively capture inflammatory-metabolic dysregulation than either marker alone. However, prospective evidence on the relationship between CTI and depression in aging populations is limited.MethodsWe analyzed data from 2,813 depression-free participants aged ≥ 50 years in ELSA, with follow-up through Wave 8. Baseline CTI was assessed from fasting blood biomarkers, and depressive symptoms were evaluated using the CES-D-8 scale. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs). To assess the potential influence of missing data, multiple-imputation analyses were additionally performed as sensitivity analyses.ResultsDuring follow-up, 136 participants developed incident depressive symptoms. After full adjustment including BMI, each 1-SD increase in CTI was associated with a higher risk of incident depressive symptoms (HR = 1.253; 95% CI: 1.002–1.479; p = 0.035). Multiple-imputation sensitivity analyses yielded directionally consistent results.ConclusionIn this nationally representative cohort of adults aged 50 years and older in England, higher baseline CTI was prospectively associated with an increased risk of incident depressive symptoms. CTI may serve as a scalable biomarker for identifying older adults at elevated risk of depressive symptoms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12991-026-00690-1.

Introduction

Depression remains one of the most common and disabling mental disorders worldwide, with particularly high burden among middle-aged and older adults [1]. It significantly impairs quality of life, social functioning, and physical health, and is associated with elevated risks for suicide, frailty, cognitive decline, and all-cause mortality [2–4]. Recent global estimates have shown that depressive disorders are rising in both absolute numbers and population-adjusted prevalence, particularly in aging societies [5–7]. As pharmacological treatments remain limited in efficacy and may have side effects or accessibility issues, identifying scalable and cost-effective biomarkers to detect individuals at risk for depression is essential for early intervention and public mental health planning [8].

Emerging evidence suggests that inflammation and insulin resistance (IR) may be critical biological mechanisms linking systemic metabolic dysregulation to depression. Among inflammatory markers, C-reactive protein (CRP) has been most extensively studied, with elevated levels found to correlate with depressive symptoms in cross-sectional studies [9–11]. Similarly, insulin resistance (IR), commonly assessed using the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, has been associated with depression and related metabolic and cardiovascular disorders [12–14]. Recently, the comprehensive CRP-TyG index (CTI), a novel biomarker combining CRP and TyG to simultaneously reflect systemic inflammation and insulin resistance, has been introduced to capture the interplay between these two physiological pathways [15]. Emerging evidence suggests that CTI is significantly associated with several adverse health outcomes, including cardiovascular disease, cancer prognosis, and all-cause mortality [16–19].

Although prior studies, including longitudinal analyses in other cohorts, have examined the association between CTI and depressive symptoms, further evidence from independent ageing populations remains warranted [20]. In particular, whether CTI predicts incident depressive symptoms among older adults in a European ageing cohort is still insufficiently clarified, given that late-life depression is shaped by age-related inflammation, metabolic dysfunction, multimorbidity, and social vulnerability [21, 22]. The English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA), a nationally representative cohort of adults aged 50 years and older in England with repeated depressive symptom assessments and standardized biomarker measurements, provides a valuable setting to address this question. Using ELSA, this study aimed to evaluate the prospective association between baseline CTI and incident depressive symptoms over eight years of follow-up, and to assess dose-response patterns and subgroup consistency.

Methods and materials

Participants

This study was based on data from the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA). ELSA was designed as a nationally representative ageing cohort of adults aged 50 years and older in England [23]. Therefore, the present study focused a priori on participants aged ≥ 50 years, consistent with both the design of ELSA and our research objective of examining inflammatory-metabolic predictors of late-life depressive symptoms. Participants younger than 50 years were excluded because they were outside the target population of this ageing cohort and outside the population to which the findings were intended to generalize. Although this observational analysis was not formally pre-registered, the age cutoff was specified before statistical analyses and was not determined based on the observed association between CTI and depressive symptoms.

In brief, participants have been followed biennially since 2002, with repeated assessments covering demographic and socioeconomic characteristics, family and employment status, physical and mental health, health-related behaviors, and cognitive function. Nurse-led health examinations were conducted during Waves 2, 4, 6, and 8 to obtain biological samples and anthropometric measurements, including body mass index (BMI). The present analysis included data from Wave 4 (2008–2009) through Wave 8 (2016–2017). The study flow diagram is shown in Fig. 1. We initially included 11,050 participants without depressive symptoms at Wave 4. We excluded individuals younger than 50 years (n = 301), those with depressive symptoms at baseline (n = 1525), and participants with missing information on exposures (n = 5786) or covariates (n = 625). After these exclusions, 2813 participants were available for the final analysis. During follow-up, 136 participants developed incident depressive symptoms, while 2677 remained free of depression. To address concerns regarding missing-data handling, participants excluded because of missing exposure or covariate data were further considered in multiple-imputation sensitivity analyses, as described below.

Exposure: Comprehensive CRP-TyG Index (CTI)

The comprehensive C-reactive protein-triglyceride-glucose index (CTI) was used as the exposure variable to capture the combined burden of systemic inflammation and insulin resistance [15]. CTI was calculated from baseline fasting blood biomarkers collected during the Wave 4 nurse visit, including C-reactive protein (CRP), triglycerides (TG), and fasting glucose. Following prior studies, CTI was calculated as: CTI = 0.412 × ln[CRP (mg/dL)] + ln[TG (mg/dL) × fasting glucose (mg/dL) / 2], where ln denotes the natural logarithm. The second term corresponds to the triglyceride-glucose index component, while the first term incorporates log-transformed CRP to reflect systemic inflammation. Because the published CTI formula is based on mg/dL units, biomarker values reported in other units were converted before calculation. Specifically, CRP measured in mg/L was converted to mg/dL by dividing by 10; triglycerides measured in mmol/L were converted to mg/dL by multiplying by 88.57; and fasting glucose measured in mmol/L was converted to mg/dL by multiplying by 18.02. CTI was analyzed as a continuous variable per 1-SD increase after standardization and was additionally categorized into tertiles according to its baseline distribution, with the lowest tertile used as the reference group.

Outcome: Incident depressive symptoms

At each follow-up wave, depressive symptoms were assessed using the 8-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression scale (CES-D-8), a widely applied and validated screening tool for depressive symptomatology in ageing cohorts, including ELSA. The CES-D-8 has been shown to retain measurement properties comparable to the original 20-item version while reducing respondent burden [24]. The scale asks participants whether, during most of the past week, they experienced three affective symptoms (“I felt depressed,” “I felt lonely,” “I felt sad”), three somatic or psychomotor symptoms (“I felt that everything I did was an effort,” “My sleep was restless,” “I could not get going”), and two positive affect items (“I was happy,” “I enjoyed life”), which were reverse-coded before scoring [25, 26]. Each item was answered with a binary yes/no response (“yes” = 1, “no” = 0). Total scores ranged from 0 to 8, with higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms. Scores were calculated when at least six of the eight items were completed, following the ≥ 75% completion rule used in prior ELSA analyses. For the main analysis, we defined incident depressive symptoms as a CES-D-8 score ≥ 4, consistent with published ELSA-based studies [27] .

Covariates

Covariates were selected based on established associations with both CTI and depressive symptoms in ageing populations. Different models were specified: Crude model included no covariates, Model 1 adjusted for age and sex, and Model 2 further adjusted for sociodemographic, lifestyle and health-related factors. Socioeconomic status was defined using educational attainment (yes or no), current employment status (not in work, routine or manual, intermediate, or managerial or professional occupations), household income quintiles (Q1–Q5), and marital status (married or cohabiting, single, or never married). Lifestyle factors included smoking status (never, former, or current), alcohol consumption (yes, no, or don’t know), and physical activity (yes or no). Health-related variables included body mass index (BMI, continuous) and self-reported history of hypertension, diabetes, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and cancer (all yes or no). All covariates were assessed at baseline (Wave 4). For the primary complete-case analysis, participants with missing information on exposure or covariates were excluded. Multiple-imputation sensitivity analyses were subsequently performed to evaluate the potential influence of missing data.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized as means with standard deviations for continuous variables and counts with percentages for categorical variables. Groups were defined by depression status during follow-up (0 = no incident depressive symptoms, 1 = incident depressive symptoms). Differences between the two groups were examined using t tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables, as appropriate. We used Cox proportional hazards models to estimate hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals for the association between CTI and incident depressive symptoms. Time-to-event was defined from baseline in Wave 4 to the first wave meeting the CES-D-8 threshold, last available follow-up, death, or Wave 8, whichever occurred first. Participants who did not develop depressive symptoms were censored at their last observation. Different models were specified in a prespecified sequence: Crude model included no covariates, Model 1 adjusted for age and sex, and Model 2 further adjusted for sociodemographic, lifestyle and health-related factors.

Restricted cubic spline models were used to examine the dose-response relation between CTI and the risk of depressive symptoms, and we reported the overall and non-linear P values to accompany the spline plot. Subgroup analyses were conducted by age group (50–60, 60–70, 70–80 years), sex (male or female), marital status (married or coupled, single, or never married), hypertension, diabetes and COPD (all yes or no), smoking status (never, former, or current), and alcohol consumption (yes or no). Interaction terms between CTI and each subgroup variable were fitted in the fully adjusted model to assess effect modification. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals and log-minus-log plots, with no evidence of violation. Analyses followed a complete-case approach as defined in the study flowchart. To address the potential influence of missing data, we additionally performed multiple-imputation sensitivity analyses using chained equations under the missing-at-random assumption. The imputation model included all variables used in the Cox regression models, follow-up time, event indicator, baseline CES-D-8 score, and available follow-up CES-D-8 information as auxiliary variables. The primary outcome variable was not directly imputed because incident depressive symptoms were derived from repeated CES-D-8 assessments and could not be reliably ascertained for participants without valid follow-up CES-D-8 data. Missing exposure and covariate data were imputed, and estimates from imputed datasets were pooled using Rubin’s rules. All analyses were performed in R version 4.5.0, and statistical significance was set at a two-sided P < 0.05.

Compared with participants who remained free of depressive symptoms, those who developed incident depressive symptoms were more likely to be female and had higher baseline CTI and BMI levels, lower educational attainment, higher prevalence of hypertension, and lower physical activity levels.

Association between CTI and incident depressive symptoms

Results from the Cox regression models are presented in Table 2. Higher baseline CTI was consistently associated with an elevated risk of incident depressive symptoms across all models. When modeled continuously, each 1-SD increase in CTI was associated with a 49.4% higher risk in the crude model (HR = 1.494; 95% CI: 1.187–1.880; p < 0.001), a 51.6% higher risk after adjustment for age and sex (HR = 1.516; 95% CI: 1.203–1.910; p < 0.001), and a 25.3% higher risk in the fully adjusted model including BMI (HR = 1.253; 95% CI: 1.002–1.479; p = 0.035). Findings were similar when CTI was analyzed in tertiles. Compared with the lowest CTI group (T1), participants in T2 and T3 had higher risks of developing depressive symptoms. In the fully adjusted model including BMI, the HRs were 1.679 (95% CI: 1.105–2.689; p = 0.021) for T2 and 1.356 (95% CI: 1.002–2.047; p = 0.043) for T3. The linear trend across ordered CTI tertiles remained statistically significant (p for trend = 0.042).

In the multiple-imputation sensitivity analysis, the association between CTI and incident depressive symptoms remained directionally consistent and statistically significant. Each 1-SD increase in CTI was associated with a higher risk of incident depressive symptoms in the fully adjusted model (HR = 1.283; 95% CI: 1.031–1.596; p = 0.025), and participants in the highest CTI tertile had an increased risk compared with those in the lowest tertile (Table S3).

Nonlinear association between CTI and depressive symptoms

Restricted cubic spline analysis was conducted to examine the dose-response relationship between CTI and the risk of incident depressive symptoms after adjustment all covariates. As shown in Fig. 2, higher CTI was significantly associated with an increased risk of incident depressive symptoms across its distribution (P for overall < 0.001). The test for nonlinearity was not statistically significant (P for nonlinear = 0.713), suggesting an approximately linear association. The spline curve showed that the risk of incident depressive symptoms increased progressively with higher CTI levels, although the confidence interval became wider at higher CTI values.

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses are shown in Fig. 3. The positive association between higher CTI and incident depressive symptoms was generally consistent across all examined demographic and clinical subgroups. No significant interactions were detected for age, sex, marital status, hypertension, COPD, diabetes, smoking status, or alcohol consumption (all p for interaction > 0.05), indicating no strong evidence of effect modification. Although the association reached statistical significance in several subgroups, including participants aged 50–60 years, both men and women, married or coupled participants, those with and without hypertension, those without COPD or diabetes, non-smokers, and both drinkers and non-drinkers, the absence of significant interaction indicates that the strength of association did not differ materially across strata.

Discussion

In this nationally representative longitudinal study of older adults from the ELSA, we found that higher baseline levels of the comprehensive CTI, a composite biomarker reflecting both systemic inflammation and insulin resistance, were significantly associated with increased risk of incident depressive symptoms over an 8-year follow-up. The association remained statistically significant after adjustment for sociodemographic, behavioral, clinical covariates, and BMI, and demonstrated an approximately linear dose-response pattern. After additional adjustment for BMI, the association was attenuated but remained significant, suggesting that adiposity may partly contribute to this inflammatory-metabolic pathway but does not fully explain the predictive value of CTI. Importantly, while sex- and age-specific effects have been reported in prior work, with some studies noting stronger associations in men or middle-aged adults, our stratified analyses showed broadly consistent associations across demographic and clinical subgroups, with no significant effect modification. This further supports the generalizability of CTI as a marker of depressive vulnerability across older populations, regardless of baseline metabolic risk or comorbidity profile. These findings support the hypothesis that inflammatory-metabolic dysregulation plays a critical role in late-life depression and suggest that CTI may serve as a scalable marker for early risk identification.

While earlier studies have established that both systemic inflammation and insulin resistance independently contribute to depression risk, the present study extends these findings by demonstrating that their combined expression, as captured by CTI, provides enhanced predictive value in a longitudinal context. This supports recent cross-sectional evidence linking elevated CTI to increased depressive symptoms in middle-aged adults in the U.S. general population [20]. The present findings should be interpreted as an external validation and population-specific extension of the emerging literature on CTI and depressive symptoms. Prior studies have suggested that higher CTI may be associated with depressive symptom burden or incident depression in other cohorts. Our study adds evidence from an independent European ageing cohort and focuses specifically on adults aged 50 years and older, a population in which immunometabolic dysregulation, multimorbidity, and late-life depressive vulnerability may differ from those observed in younger or general adult samples. By using repeated follow-up data from ELSA and conducting multiple-imputation sensitivity analyses, our findings provide additional longitudinal evidence that elevated CTI may precede the development of depressive symptoms in older adults.

The observed association between elevated CTI levels and subsequent depressive symptoms may be explained by a network of overlapping biological mechanisms involving systemic inflammation, insulin resistance, and neuroendocrine disruption. Both CRP and TyG, the core components of CTI, have been independently implicated in the development of depression through their roles in activating chronic low-grade inflammation and disrupting central nervous system regulation [22]. Inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α, frequently elevated in individuals with higher CRP levels, can cross the blood-brain barrier, activate microglia, and impair monoaminergic signaling, particularly in dopaminergic and serotonergic pathways, which are critical for mood regulation and reward processing [28].

Simultaneously, insulin resistance may contribute to neurobiological vulnerability through impaired insulin signaling in key brain regions such as the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex. These disruptions are associated with reduced synaptic plasticity, impaired neurogenesis, and deficits in cognitive-affective integration [29]. Additionally, both inflammation and insulin resistance are known to activate the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, resulting in sustained cortisol elevation and glucocorticoid receptor resistance, a profile that has been repeatedly observed in individuals with chronic depressive symptoms [21]. Recent transcriptomic analyses provide further support for the biological convergence captured by CTI. Individuals with elevated CTI scores exhibit upregulation of key immunometabolism pathways including PI3K/AKT, HIF-1, and insulin signaling, as well as downregulation of tyrosine metabolism, potentially impairing dopamine synthesis and reward circuit integrity [30]. The clustering of CRP, resistin, and insulin signaling molecules within peripheral biomarker networks and gene co-expression modules strengthens the case for CTI as a proxy of shared pathophysiological pathways linking metabolic and emotional dysregulation [31]. These effects may be especially pronounced in older adults, where immunosenescence and reduced metabolic flexibility further increase vulnerability to neuroinflammatory and neuroendocrine disruption. Taken together, these findings support the utility of CTI not only as a predictive marker of incident depression but also as a biologically informative index that captures systemic processes known to impair mood regulation at both structural and functional levels of the brain.

From a clinical perspective, these findings support integrating CTI into routine cardiometabolic assessment to enable earlier risk stratification for late-life depression. CRP and fasting triglyceride-glucose are already standard components of primary-care workups and cardiovascular prevention pathways, which facilitates automated calculation within electronic health records [32]. Using such risk information to prompt selective depression screening is consistent with contemporary recommendations that endorse screening adults, including older adults, when systems are in place for diagnosis and follow-up [33]. Evidence also supports targeting the inflammatory-metabolic axis in prevention. Randomized and meta-analytic data in middle-aged and older adults show that structured aerobic and resistance training can reduce circulating CRP and other inflammatory markers while improving insulin sensitivity [34, 35]. Nutritional interventions provide complementary benefits. A randomized controlled trial of a Mediterranean-style dietary program adjunctive to usual care improved depressive symptoms compared with an active social-support control [36], and similar effects were observed in a recent randomized trial among young men with depressive symptoms following dietary improvement toward a Mediterranean pattern [37].

Strengths and limitations

This study has several methodological strengths. It leverages data from the ELSA, a large and nationally representative cohort of older adults, with long-term follow-up and standardized biomarker assessment. The prospective design enabled temporal ordering between exposure and outcome, reducing the likelihood of reverse causation. The CTI was analyzed both continuously and categorically, allowing for robust interpretation of dose–response associations. Models were extensively adjusted for sociodemographic, behavioral, and clinical factors, and findings were consistent across multiple sensitivity and subgroup analyses, supporting their stability across different population strata.

Several limitations should also be noted. First, depressive symptoms were assessed using the CES-D-8, which is a screening instrument rather than a diagnostic tool, and some degree of misclassification is therefore possible [38]. Second, although a comprehensive set of covariates was included, residual confounding from unmeasured factors cannot be fully excluded. Third, although the primary analysis was based on complete cases, we compared included and excluded participants and performed multiple-imputation sensitivity analyses to assess the potential influence of missing data. Nevertheless, residual selection bias related to missingness cannot be fully excluded. Fourth, biomarkers were measured at a single time point, which may not fully capture changes in inflammatory–metabolic status over time. Fifth, the observational nature of the study prohibits causal inference; therefore, further intervention or longitudinal mechanistic studies are warranted to clarify whether modifying CTI could reduce the risk of late-life depression. Finally, because this study was restricted to adults aged 50 years and older in England, the findings should be interpreted within this target population and should not be generalized directly to younger adults or populations outside similar ageing cohorts. Future studies are needed to determine whether similar associations exist in younger and more geographically diverse populations.

Conclusion

In this nationally representative cohort of older adults, higher baseline CTI, a composite indicator jointly reflecting systemic inflammation and insulin resistance, was prospectively associated with an increased risk of incident depressive symptoms over eight years of follow-up. These findings suggest that inflammatory–metabolic dysregulation may contribute to the early development of late-life depression and highlight CTI as a potentially scalable biomarker for risk stratification in primary care. Given that both inflammation and insulin resistance are modifiable through lifestyle and metabolic interventions, incorporation of CTI into preventive mental health strategies may enable more targeted screening and earlier intervention. Future research should assess whether changes in CTI over time predict shifts in depressive risk and whether reducing CTI can modify the trajectory of depression in ageing populations.
