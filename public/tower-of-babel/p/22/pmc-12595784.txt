Examining the prevalence of reproductive tract infections (RTIs) and their correlation with knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP): a cross-sectional study among university-affiliated women
Authors: Jing Xiang, Xuan Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundReproductive tract infections (RTIs) remain a major public health concern among women of reproductive age, but limited evidence exists on their association with knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) in university settings. This study examined the prevalence of RTIs and their relationship with KAP and sociodemographic factors among university-affiliated women.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted from October 2023 to March 2024 among 457 women aged 18–48 years at a large comprehensive university in Tianjin, China, including students, faculty, administrative staff, and contracted workers. Participants were recruited through stratified convenience sampling and completed a validated 23-item KAP questionnaire, followed by standardized clinical examination and standardized clinical assessment with targeted laboratory testing. Associations between KAP, sociodemographic characteristics, and RTI prevalence were assessed using descriptive statistics and chi-square tests.ResultsThe mean KAP score was 50 (SD 14); 34.5% of participants were categorized as low, 46.3% as medium, and 19.0% as high. Overall RTI prevalence was 37.6%, with endometritis (17.7%) and salpingitis (17.2%) most common. Higher KAP levels were inversely associated with RTI prevalence (p < 0.001). Marked gradients were observed by education and expenditure: prevalence was 24.5% among women with a master’s degree or above versus 50.8% among college students, and 70.7% among those with monthly expenditure < 2,000 RMB.ConclusionsInstitutional affiliation alone did not confer adequate sexual health; persistent occupational and socioeconomic gradients indicate that structural socioeconomic and cultural barriers—rather than educational access per se—drive disparities. Equity-oriented, culturally appropriate, KAP-based interventions and longitudinal evaluation are warranted.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25111-y.

Introduction

Reproductive tract infections (RTIs) are a major global public health issue, associated with infertility, adverse pregnancy outcomes, and increased HIV susceptibility. RTIs include lower tract infections such as vaginitis and cervicitis, as well as upper tract conditions such as endometritis and salpingitis. Untreated infections may progress to pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), ectopic pregnancy, or tubal infertility. Globally, more than 374 million new cases of curable sexually transmitted infections (STIs) are reported annually, with a disproportionate burden among women in low- and middle-income countries [1, 2]. In addition to STIs, non-sexually transmitted RTIs such as bacterial vaginosis and candidiasis contribute substantially to reproductive morbidity. Alterations in vaginal microecology can impair mucosal immunity, facilitate ascending infections, and intensify inflammation, thereby increasing risks such as cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN), particularly when combined with high-risk HPV subtypes [3, 4]. Despite their clinical importance, RTIs are often underdiagnosed due to asymptomatic presentation, stigma, and limited access to screening and treatment.

Behavioral determinants, especially knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP), are recognized as modifiable risk factors for RTIs. The KAP framework has been widely applied in sexual and reproductive health research, but most studies focus on adolescents or outpatient populations. Evidence among women in higher education settings remains limited, and few investigations have combined behavioral assessments with laboratory-confirmed infections [5, 6]. This gap is noteworthy given the assumption that higher education ensures sufficient health literacy, yet whether this translates into effective reproductive health practices is uncertain. University-affiliated women—including students, faculty, administrative staff, and contracted workers—represent a heterogeneous but understudied population whose health outcomes may not correspond with their educational access [7–9].

This study aims to assess the prevalence of RTIs and to examine their association with KAP levels and sociodemographic factors among women in a university setting. By integrating a validated KAP scale with clinical diagnostics, the study seeks to provide evidence to guide targeted, behavior-based strategies for RTI prevention in institutional contexts.

Methods

Ethical approval and study design

This cross-sectional study was conducted between October 2023 and March 2024 in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Medical Ethics Review Committee of Nankai University, China (Approval No. NKUIRB2023140). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

The study population comprised women aged 18–48 years affiliated with a large comprehensive university, including undergraduate and postgraduate students, faculty members, administrative staff, and contracted service workers. Participants were recruited through stratified convenience sampling from three primary sources: (i) attendees of routine gynecological examinations at the university-affiliated outpatient clinic, (ii) participants in on-campus sexual and reproductive health (SRH) education sessions, and (iii) volunteers from other public campus venues.

Inclusion criteria were: female sex, age 18–48 years, university affiliation (student, employee, or contracted staff), and ability to complete both the survey and clinical examination. Exclusion criteria were: absence of sexual history, menstruation at the time of examination, recent (< 48 h) vaginal medication, lavage or sexual intercourse, and refusal or inability to provide informed consent.

A pilot survey of 40 students yielded a preliminary awareness rate of 48.6% for RTIs. Based on this estimate, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 385 to achieve 95% confidence with a 5% margin of error, using the standard prevalence-based formula for cross-sectional studies [10]. Considering an anticipated 10–20% attrition, 550 questionnaires were distributed. A total of 492 valid responses were obtained (89.5% response rate), and after applying exclusion criteria, 457 women were included in the final analysis. Development and validation of the RTI-KAP scale

The RTI-KAP scale was developed through an iterative process combining evidence review, expert input, and pilot testing. First, targeted searches of reproductive health literature were conducted to identify validated KAP instruments and previous studies relevant to RTIs. These sources informed the scope of knowledge items [11], guided the framing of attitude and practice domains [12, 13], and provided methodological standards for measurement design [14].

A multidisciplinary panel consisting of two gynecologists (each with ≥ 14 years of clinical experience), one public health physician, and one public administration expert then reviewed the draft instrument. The panel evaluated each item for content relevance, clarity, and cultural appropriateness, and revisions were made based on their feedback.

Subsequently, a pilot survey was conducted with 40 women to examine item clarity, logical flow, and comprehensiveness. Revisions were made to refine wording, remove redundancies, and improve internal consistency. Pilot participants were excluded from the final analytic cohort.

The finalized instrument consisted of 23 items across three domains: knowledge (9 items), attitudes (6 items), and practices (8 items). Knowledge items required factual responses, attitudes were assessed using a five-point Likert scale (strongly agree to strongly disagree), and practices were assessed using a five-point frequency scale (always to never). Total scores ranged from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better KAP. Full item content is provided in Supplementary Material 2[see Additional file 2].

Structured interview methodology

During the survey period, trained female interviewers administered an anonymous, structured questionnaire in a private setting to ensure confidentiality. The instrument followed a fixed sequence of standardized items with scripted wording, and responses were recorded verbatim to minimize interviewer effects and transcription errors. Information collected included sociodemographic characteristics, sexual health symptoms (e.g., vulvar pruritus, abnormal vaginal discharge, lower abdominal pain, dyspareunia), and responses to the validated 23-item RTI-KAP scale. To ensure data integrity, all data underwent double data entry and cross-checks, were de-identified, and were analyzed in IBM SPSS Statistics 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

RTIs screening and diagnostic procedures

All participants underwent standardized RTI screening by licensed gynecologists. Examinations were scheduled 3–5 days after menstruation, with ≥ 48 h of sexual abstinence and no prior intravaginal medication or lavage. The diagnostic protocol followed the national guideline for pelvic inflammatory diseases and was aligned with international recommendations for STI care [15, 16].

Detailed operational criteria (symptoms/signs, laboratory/ultrasound triggers, decision thresholds) are provided in Supplementary Table S1 [see Additional file 1].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The psychometric properties of the RTI-KAP scale were evaluated by assessing internal consistency with Cronbach’s α (≥ 0.70 considered acceptable [20]), split-half reliability, and Test–retest reliability was assessed in the full analytic sample (n = 457). Construct validity was examined through expert review, the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy, Bartlett’s test of sphericity, and exploratory principal component analysis (PCA) [21, 22].

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize sociodemographic characteristics, KAP scores, and RTI prevalence. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables as means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges, as appropriate. Associations between sociodemographic variables, KAP levels, and RTI prevalence were tested using chi-square analyses, with Cramer’s V reported as a measure of effect size. All tests were two-tailed, and p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Reliability and validity of the KAP scale

The RTI-KAP scale demonstrated high internal consistency at the total scale level (Cronbach’s α = 0.865) and acceptable to good consistency across knowledge (α = 0.739), attitudes (α = 0.710), and practices (α = 0.843) subscales. Sampling adequacy was supported by KMO values (overall = 0.731) and Bartlett’s test of sphericity (χ² = 7748.275, df = 253, p < 0.001).

Split-half reliability was satisfactory (Spearman–Brown coefficient = 0.792; Guttman split-half = 0.772), and test–retest reliability over a four-week interval indicated excellent stability (Pearson r = 0.919, Spearman ρ = 0.926, Kendall’s τ = 0.773; all p < 0.001). Inter-correlations among domains were moderate and significant (ρ = 0.395–0.532; all p < 0.001), supporting construct coherence.

Principal component analysis (PCA) extracted six components (eigenvalues > 1), cumulatively explaining 74.0% of variance; after Varimax rotation, the cumulative variance reached 78.0%. Factor loadings were coherent across domains, indicating a stable multidimensional structure. Detailed reliability and validity indices are presented in Supplementary Tables S3–S9[see Additional files 3–9].

Sociodemographic variables and KAP levels

Among the 492 baseline questionnaires collected, 35 were excluded due to lack of sexual history or participant withdrawal, resulting in 457 women included in the analysis (dropout rate: 7.1%). The mean age was 31.0 years (SD = 8.0; median = 30), and the mean knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) score was 50 (SD = 14). Based on tertile distribution of the total KAP scores, participants were categorized into three groups: low (≤ 47), medium (48–61), and high (≥ 62). Overall, 34.5% were classified as low, 46.3% as medium, and 19.0% as high. All participants were covered by health insurance.

Marked gradients were observed across sociodemographic strata (Table 1). Educational attainment showed the clearest association: 81.4% of women with a master’s degree or above were in the high KAP group, compared with only 0.8% of college students and 6.2% of those below bachelor level. Occupational categories displayed a similar pattern. Prevalence of RTIs across sociodemographic groups

The overall prevalence of RTIs among the 457 participants was 37.6% (n = 172). Upper RTIs predominated, with endometritis (17.7%) and salpingitis (17.2%) being markedly more common than lower tract infections (vaginitis: 13.5%; cervicitis: 5.9%; vulvitis: 4.3%).

Analysis revealed pronounced sociodemographic gradients in RTI risk, indicative of significant health inequities (Table 2; Fig. 2). Socioeconomic status, proxied by personal monthly expenditure and hukou registration, was a predominant predictor. A strong inverse correlation was observed between economic capacity and prevalence, which was highest in the lowest expenditure cohort (≤ 2,000 RMB/month: 70.7%) and lowest in the highest (≥ 4,000 RMB/month: 17.7%). Significant inverse gradients were also evident across occupational and educational strata. Taken together, these results demonstrate that socioeconomic position and educational attainment shape not only reproductive health literacy but also clinical outcomes, underscoring the structural basis of RTI vulnerability in this population.

Discussion

This study elucidated the complex interplay between RTIs and knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP), while underscoring the substantial influence of sociodemographic determinants including education, economic status, and residency. A robust inverse correlation was identified between elevated KAP levels and reduced RTI prevalence, substantiating the critical role of health knowledge in RTI prevention and underscoring the imperative for targeted health education initiatives.

Educational attainment emerged as a pivotal determinant of KAP proficiency. Women with advanced education, particularly those possessing master’s degrees or higher, demonstrated superior KAP scores and a correspondingly lower prevalence of RTIs. This observation corroborated extant literature positing that education enhances health literacy and facilitates the adoption of prophylactic health behaviors [23, 24]. Nevertheless, these associations do not imply that higher education universally guarantees adequate sexual health outcomes, as disparities persisted among non-academic staff and contract workers on the same campus. Conversely, diminished educational attainment, frequently compounded by economic disadvantage, was associated with compromised KAP performance and elevated RTI risk. Individuals within this demographic often encounter constrained access to health education and resources, thereby exacerbating their susceptibility to infections [25, 26].

Economic status was further identified as a significant predictor of RTI prevalence. Women reporting lower personal monthly expenditures—specifically, those utilizing less than 2,000 RMB monthly—exhibited a markedly higher RTI prevalence, reaching 70.7%. This finding aligns with prior research indicating that economic deprivation impedes healthcare access, culminating in heightened rates of untreated infections [27, 28]. Financial barriers restrict both preventive engagement and timely therapeutic intervention among women at risk of RTIs, as evidenced by substantial out-of-pocket and even catastrophic expenditures borne by STI patients. Policy expansions that reduce financial barriers—such as Medicaid expansion—are associated with increased STI testing, improving affordability and access for economically vulnerable populations [29, 30].

Geographic disparities, when scrutinized, revealed nuanced insights. Women possessing rural household registrations but residing in urban educational environments remained susceptible to diminished health literacy and persistent financial constraints, amplifying their RTI vulnerability. These outcomes suggest that socio-economic and cultural factors, rather than geographic locality per se, function as primary determinants of disparity. Although differential healthcare infrastructure contributes to urban-rural health gradients, rural hukou women in urban settings continue to experience compounded disadvantages due to socio-economic marginalization and insufficient sexual health education [31, 32]. Cultural impediments, including stigma and restrictive gender norms, further obstruct healthcare-seeking behaviors, complicating RTI prevention and management [33, 34]. Consequently, public health interventions must prioritize socio-economic inequities and cultural barriers over purely geographic targeting.

A salient methodological strength of this investigation lies in its integration of self-reported KAP metrics with objective clinical diagnoses across distinct anatomical zones (vulvitis, vaginitis, cervicitis, endometritis, and pelvic inflammatory disease). This approach mirrored the ascending trajectory of RTIs and affords a comprehensive perspective on reproductive tract pathogenesis. The results concurred with established theoretical models wherein vaginal microbiota dysbiosis initiates a pathogenic cascade, enabling opportunistic pathogens to transcend cervical barriers and precipitate ascending infections, potentially culminating in severe sequelae such as pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) [35–38]. This microbiota-oriented framework emphasizes the necessity of interventions addressing foundational microbial imbalances rather than merely ameliorating isolated clinical manifestations [39, 40].

Notwithstanding these contributions, several limitations warranted acknowledgment. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Although significant associations between KAP and RTI prevalence were discerned, temporal and causal relationships remain elusive. Longitudinal or randomized controlled designs are requisite to elucidate causal pathways between KAP modulation and RTI prevalence [41, 42]. Moreover, while clinical assessments mitigated self-report biases, subsequent research should perpetuate the integration of subjective and objective measures—such as advanced microbiological assays and standardized clinical evaluations—to bolster validity [43, 44].

Additionally, the exclusion of contextual determinants such as partner behaviors, cultural dynamics, and gender-specific roles constrains the holistic interpretation of RTI risk. Sociocultural constructs, including normative gender expectations and culturally entrenched taboos, likely modulate health-seeking behaviors and care accessibility. Future studies should incorporate these dimensions to cultivate a more nuanced understanding of RTI etiologies [45].

Conclusion

Women affiliated with a higher-education institution exhibited suboptimal KAP and a substantial RTI burden. Institutional affiliation alone did not confer adequate sexual health outcomes, as pronounced gradients persisted across occupational and socioeconomic subgroups within the same campus environment. These findings implicate structural socioeconomic and cultural barriers as the predominant drivers of reproductive health disparities in this cohort. Equity-oriented, KAP-based and culturally appropriate interventions that reduce economic barriers are warranted in university settings, and longitudinal studies should test whether improvements in KAP translate into sustained reductions in RTIs.
