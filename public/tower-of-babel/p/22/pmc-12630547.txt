Revisiting Cancer Cachexia Staging: Introducing an “At Risk” Category Based on AWGC Components
Authors: Tatsuma Sakaguchi, Keisuke Maeda, Makoto Yamasaki, Naoharu Mori
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCancer cachexia is a multifactorial syndrome characterized by progressive weight loss, muscle wasting, and systemic inflammation. Early identification of individuals at risk for cachexia is essential for timely intervention, yet a universally accepted definition of the “at risk” stage remains lacking. Building on the Asian Working Group for Cachexia (AWGC) framework, we propose that the presence of any one of the five components—low BMI (< 21 kg/m2), weight loss ≥ 2% over 3–6 months, anorexia, low handgrip strength, or elevated CRP levels—may indicate vulnerability to cachexia. We evaluated the prognostic value of this definition in a cohort of 364 patients with palliative cancer. The patients were categorized into three groups: non‐cachectic, at risk, and cachectic. Survival analyses demonstrated significant differences across groups (p = 0.005), with the median overall survival not reached in the non‐cachectic group, 381 days in the at‐risk group, and 157 days in the cachectic group. While low BMI and weight loss were not associated with survival in patients with edema, they became evident in those without edema (HR = 1.54 and 1.58), highlighting the confounding role of fluid retention in anthropometric assessment. Anorexia, low handgrip strength, and elevated CRP levels independently predicted poor prognosis in both full and non‐edematous cohorts. These findings support the clinical relevance of an “at risk” category based on AWGC components, especially in patients without edema. This simple and pragmatic definition may facilitate the early identification of patients who could benefit from supportive interventions before cachexia becomes refractory.Keywords: Asian Working Group for Cachexia, at risk of cachexia, cancer cachexia, precachexia

Introduction

Cancer cachexia is a complex multifactorial syndrome characterized by progressive weight loss, skeletal muscle wasting, and systemic inflammation. It affects a significant proportion of patients with advanced cancer and substantially contributes to functional decline, impaired quality of life, and shortened survival [1]. Despite the increasing recognition of the clinical impact of cachexia, its optimal management remains challenging. Multimodal treatment approaches that combine nutritional support, exercise, anti‐inflammatory interventions, and pharmacological therapies are generally more effective when implemented early in the disease trajectory [2, 3, 4]. The early implementation of such interventions has demonstrated excellent compliance and safety in elderly patients with newly diagnosed pancreatic and non‐small cell lung cancer undergoing chemotherapy [5]. These findings highlight the importance of identifying patients at risk of cachexia at an early stage. Therefore, systematic nutritional screening and assessment are essential components of standard cancer care [6, 7].

With improvements in the prognosis of non‐small cell lung cancer due to molecular targeted therapies and immune checkpoint inhibitors, the clinical importance of cachexia has become more prominent [8]. In lung cancer, cachexia frequently coexists with chronic pulmonary conditions such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, which exacerbate systemic inflammation and contribute to sarcopenia. These interactions further diminish the treatment response and survival outcomes. Although the pharmacological options remain limited, the approval of anamorelin, a ghrelin receptor agonist, in Japan has provided a new therapeutic avenue for improving appetite and lean body mass [9, 10]. However, its effects on muscle strength and function remain unclear. Notably, cachexia is associated with the diminished efficacy of immunotherapies, making early recognition and management increasingly important [11, 12]. Despite its impact, the awareness of cancer cachexia remains limited among patients and healthcare providers [13].

Fearon et al. proposed international diagnostic criteria for cancer cachexia based on weight loss, low body mass index (BMI), and sarcopenia, and introduced the concept of “pre‐cachexia” to describe an earlier stage of the syndrome [3]. However, a universally accepted definition of the “at risk” stage that can effectively guide timely clinical intervention is still lacking. Moreover, the existing international criteria developed mainly for Western populations may underestimate cachexia in Asian patients because of ethnic differences in body composition and BMI. To address this issue, the Asian Working Group for Cachexia (AWGC) was established to develop diagnostic standards optimized for Asian populations [14]. The AWGC proposed a diagnostic framework comprising five components that demonstrated prognostic relevance in cancer populations: low BMI, mild weight loss, anorexia, low handgrip strength, and elevated C‐reactive protein (CRP) levels. These components are practical for assessing and reflecting the key dimensions of the cachexia syndrome, including nutritional depletion, muscle dysfunction, and systemic inflammation. Notably, the AWGC criteria have been shown to identify a broader range of patients with cachexia than Fearon's criteria, particularly in patients with lung cancer undergoing chemotherapy [4]. The prognostic value of the AWGC framework has been validated in various types of cancer [15].

Building on these components, we propose a conceptual framework for defining the “at‐risk” stages of cancer cachexia. The presence of any one of the AWGC components may indicate increased vulnerability to cachexia and prompt early clinical attention. This minireview evaluates the independent prognostic value of each component and discusses the potential clinical utility and challenges of implementing an “at risk” definition in routine cancer care.

Proposed “At Risk” Criteria Based on AWGC Components

The AWGC diagnostic framework for cancer cachexia includes five key components: low BMI (< 21 kg/m2), weight loss ≥ 2% over the preceding 3–6 months, anorexia, low handgrip strength (male < 28 kg, female < 18 kg), and elevated CRP levels (> 0.5 mg/dL or > 5 mg/L) [14]. Each component is easily measurable in clinical practice and reflects distinct pathophysiological processes associated with cachexia, including energy imbalance, nutritional deficits, muscle wasting, systemic inflammation, and anorexia‐cachexia.

In the proposed “at risk” definition, we hypothesize that the presence of any one of these components, even in isolation, identifies a subgroup of patients at elevated risk for future cachexia development and adverse outcomes. This approach aligns with the understanding that cachexia is a dynamic process, and that early subclinical changes in body composition, appetite, and inflammatory status may precede overt syndrome manifestations.

This definition is pragmatic, based on readily available clinical parameters, and allows for early identification of patients who may benefit from proactive supportive care interventions.

Furthermore, the presence of edema, a characteristic of cachexia in patients with advanced cancer, is an independent prognostic factor that may mask weight loss and low BMI [15, 16]. Therefore, the presence of edema should be carefully considered in the diagnostic evaluation of cachexia [14].

Independent Prognostic Value of Each Component

BMI

A low BMI is a well‐established marker of poor prognosis in patients with cancer [17, 18, 19, 20]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that a lower BMI is associated with reduced overall survival, independent of the tumor stage and treatment modality. A BMI threshold of < 21 kg/m2 was selected by the AWGC based on analyses showing that this cutoff discriminates survival outcomes in Asian patients with cancer [14]. Importantly, a low BMI also reflects diminished nutritional reserves and is associated with an increased risk of treatment‐related toxicity and functional decline.

Weight Loss

Weight loss is a hallmark of cachexia [1, 3]. Even modest weight loss (≥ 2%) over a short period (3–6 months) has been shown to predict poor survival in various cancers [15]. The prognostic impact of weight loss is independent of the baseline BMI and reflects both the loss of fat mass and lean body mass [19, 20]. Recent data from our institution and other cohorts suggest that a ≥ 2% threshold captures early catabolic changes that may otherwise be overlooked by higher cut‐offs used in traditional cachexia definitions [15].

Anorexia

Anorexia, a persistent loss of appetite, is a common symptom in patients with cancer and a key driver of negative energy balance and weight loss. Self‐reported anorexia has been shown to independently predict poor survival and quality of life [21, 22]. Moreover, anorexia may serve as an early clinical marker of underlying metabolic and inflammatory changes that precede overt cachexia [3]. No standardized tool is available for assessing anorexia, and it is susceptible to chemotherapy‐induced nausea and cachexia [4].

Handgrip Strength

Handgrip strength is a simple, objective measure of muscle function and a surrogate for overall physical performance. Low handgrip strength has consistently been associated with poor survival in patients with cancer, independent of body composition and nutritional status [23, 24]. It also predicts treatment tolerance and hospitalization risk [25, 26]. The AWGC recommends using validated sex‐specific cut‐offs (male < 28 kg, female < 18 kg) from the consensus of the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia 2019 to identify low handgrip strength, which can be easily incorporated into routine assessments [27].

CRP

Elevated CRP levels are markers of systemic inflammation, which play a central role in the pathogenesis of cancer cachexia [28, 29]. High CRP levels are correlated with increased catabolism, anorexia, and poor survival across multiple cancer types. CRP levels are also independent predictors of adverse outcomes and may reflect the presence of tumor‐related or paraneoplastic inflammatory processes. Routine CRP measurement offers a feasible means of identifying patients with a heightened inflammatory burden who may be at an increased risk of cachexia progression.

Overview of Survival Analysis

This study is a retrospective, post hoc analysis conducted using the same dataset previously reported in a separate study by our group that focused on the clinical relevance of the AWGC‐based cachexia classification in palliative care settings [15]. To validate the prognostic utility of each AWGC component, we conducted survival analysis in a cohort of 364 patients with advanced cancer receiving palliative care at Aichi Medical University Hospital, Japan (Figure 1). Each component was evaluated using the log‐rank test and univariate Cox regression model. We also performed a prespecified subgroup analysis in patients without clinical evidence of edema (non‐edematous group, n = 252) to eliminate the confounding effects of fluid retention. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Aichi Medical University (approval no. 2019‐104). Given the retrospective and post hoc nature of the analysis, the requirement for written informed consent was waived, with an opt‐out option provided on the institutional website in accordance with national guidelines and ethical standards. The study was conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

After excluding patients in whom data collection was not feasible (n = 41) and those with missing information (n = 44), 364 patients were included in the final analysis.

Kaplan–Meier Curves

The patients were categorized into three groups based on their cachexia status: non‐cachectic, at risk of cachexia, and diagnosed with cachexia. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated for each group, with the median overall survival (OS) not reached in the non‐cachectic group, 381 days in the at‐risk group, and 157 days in the cachexia group (Figure 2). The log‐rank test demonstrated a significant difference in survival among the three groups (p = 0.005).

Univariate Cox Hazards Analysis

In the overall cohort, BMI < 21 kg/m2 was not significantly associated with worse survival (hazard ratio [HR] 1.25, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.95–1.66, p = 0.112). However, in the non‐edematous group, the prognostic impact was significant (HR = 1.54, 95% CI: 1.07–2.21, p = 0.020), supporting the reliability of BMI as a predictor when not confounded by fluid overload (Table 2).

Weight loss ≥ 2% over 3–6 months was not significantly associated with decreased survival (HR = 1.20, 95% CI: 0.88–1.64, p = 0.257). However, in the non‐edematous group, the prognostic impact was significant (HR = 1.58, 95% CI: 1.02–2.46, p = 0.041), emphasizing that modest weight loss identifies a high‐risk population (Table 2).

The Edmonton Symptom Assessment System [30] was used to assess anorexia at our institution. Self‐reported anorexia ≥ 4/10 on the numerical rating scale was significantly associated with poor survival in both the full cohort (HR = 1.60, 95% CI: 1.17–2.18, p = 0.003) and the non‐edematous subgroup (HR = 1.47, 95% CI: 1.01–2.15, p = 0.046), reinforcing its role as a clinically observable symptom of early cachexia (Table 2).

Patients with low sex‐specific handgrip strength had shorter survival in the overall cohort in both males and females (HR = 2.17, 95% CI: 1.49–3.16, p < 0.001; HR = 1.87, 95% CI: 1.20–2.92, p < 0.001). The prognostic association remained strong in the non‐edematous group in both males and females (HR = 2.51, 95% CI: 1.60–3.94, p < 0.001; HR = 2.16, 95% CI: 1.15–4.05, p = 0.017), indicating that handgrip strength is a fluid‐independent marker of declining reserve (Table 2).

Elevated CRP levels (> 0.5 mg/dL) were predictive of shorter survival in the full cohort and patients with no edema (HR = 2.58, 95% CI: 1.73–3.86, p < 0.001; HR = 2.72, 95% CI: 1.65–4.50, p < 0.001). This finding supports the role of systemic inflammation as a central pathway in the pathogenesis of early cachexia (Table 2).

Interpretation of Findings

Among 364 patients receiving palliative care, 87 (24%) did not meet the AWGC diagnostic criteria. However, 78 patients (90%) were classified as being at risk of cachexia based on the presence of one or more AWGC components. Our findings support the prognostic value of AWGC‐defined components in patients with advanced cancer receiving palliative care. Among the 364 evaluated patients, almost all met the criteria for cachexia or were at risk, with only 2.5% exhibiting no evidence of cachexia. This high prevalence underscores the importance of early detection and stratification to guide timely intervention.

Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated a clear survival gradient across the cachexia status groups, with the longest median survival in patients with no cachexia and the shortest in those diagnosed with cachexia. Notably, patients identified as “at risk” also showed significantly poorer survival compared to the non‐cachectic group, suggesting that this intermediate category represents a clinically meaningful and actionable stage in the cachexia trajectory.

Although low BMI and ≥ 2% weight loss did not significantly predict survival in the overall cohort, both were significant in the subgroup without edema. This highlights the masking effect of fluid retention on anthropometric indicators and reinforces the importance of a stratified analysis. In patients without edema, low BMI (HR = 1.54), and ≥ 2% weight loss (HR = 1.58) were independently associated with worse prognosis, suggesting that even mild anthropometric changes may signal early catabolism in the absence of confounding edema.

Anorexia emerged as a robust predictor of poor survival in both the full and non‐edematous cohorts. Its clinical simplicity and strong association with outcomes (HR = 1.60 and 1.47, respectively) make it a practical early indicator of metabolic dysregulation and negative energy balance. Given its subjective nature, the systematic use of validated tools, such as the Edmonton Symptom Assessment System, may enhance its reproducibility and clinical utility.

Low handgrip strength demonstrated the strongest prognostic signal among all components, with hazard ratios exceeding 2.0 in both sexes. This association persisted in the non‐edematous group, supporting its value as a fluid‐independent marker of physical decline. Because handgrip strength is easy to measure and reflects both nutritional and functional reserves, its inclusion in routine assessments is highly feasible and valuable.

Elevated CRP also strongly predicted poor prognosis, with hazard ratios above 2.5 in all analyses. This reinforces the idea that systemic inflammation is a key driver of cachexia and suggests that CRP may help identify patients in a pre‐cachectic inflammatory state. Importantly, CRP levels are unaffected by fluid retention, making it a stable and reliable prognostic marker.

Edema is a well‐recognized prognostic marker [16], and our findings are consistent with this observation. We did not conduct a multivariate analysis in the present study because such an analysis—including edema and the other AWGC components—was already performed and published in our previous work, where edema was confirmed to be the significant prognostic factor while the AWGC criteria also demonstrated independent prognostic relevance [15]. In the current analysis, our aim was to evaluate the prognostic impact of each AWGC component in an exploratory and straightforward manner. Because these components are interrelated (e.g., weight loss and low BMI), incorporating them into the same multivariate model may lead to multicollinearity and obscure their individual effects. In addition, the limited number of events in certain subgroups raises concerns about overfitting if multiple covariates are simultaneously adjusted. For these reasons, we intentionally presented univariate analyses to highlight the prognostic relevance of each component.

In the subgroup of non‐cachectic patients (n = 87), univariate Cox analyses showed that neither weight loss > 2% nor reduced handgrip strength in females was significantly associated with survival. This may be explained by the relatively small sample size of this subgroup, which could have limited the statistical power to detect modest effects. In addition, in patients without cachexia, minor weight loss or mild reductions in muscle strength in females may not strongly influence prognosis compared with more advanced disease‐related factors.

The AWGC components are interconnected through inflammatory and metabolic pathways, making causal separation challenging. Inflammatory activation may lead to anorexia and subsequent muscle wasting, whereas treatment‐related anorexia or pre‐existing sarcopenia can precede other cachexia features. In the multivariate Cox analysis, BMI < 21 kg/m2, weight loss ≥ 2%, and anorexia were not significantly associated with survival, whereas elevated CRP and low handgrip strength remained independent predictors of poor outcomes. The absence of significance for weight loss, low BMI, and anorexia likely reflects their interdependence and overlapping biological mechanisms. Similar trends were observed in the non‐edematous cohort, while no AWGC components were significant in the non‐cachectic group. These results suggest that systemic inflammation and impaired muscle strength represent the most stable prognostic domains within the AWGC framework, underscoring their clinical importance in the survival assessment of patients with advanced cancer.

Overall, our data provide empirical support for the AWGC component‐based model of cancer cachexia and justify the conceptualization of an “at risk” stage. These findings emphasize the prognostic validity of these components in patients with no edema, in whom diagnostic signals are not masked. Early identification of these patients may allow timely multimodal interventions before cachexia becomes refractory.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single‐center, retrospective, post hoc analysis, which inherently introduces risks of selection bias and unmeasured confounding. Second, referral practices and patient backgrounds may differ across institutions, which may affect the generalizability of our findings. Third, the assessment of muscle mass was based on calf circumference, which, while feasible in palliative care settings, may have led to misclassification compared to more precise methods such as computed tomography (CT), bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA), or dual‐energy X‐ray absorptiometry (DXA). Fourth, the number of patients categorized as non‐cachectic (n = 9) was very small, limiting the statistical power of comparisons with other groups. Fifth, the study lacked external validation, which restricts reproducibility. Finally, although we attempted to include key clinical variables in our analysis, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded. Despite these limitations, our study provides novel evidence supporting the prognostic value of an “at risk” category for cancer cachexia in palliative care populations.

Clinical Significance and Research Prospects

The proposed “at risk” definition offers several potential advantages for clinical practice. First, it enables the early identification of patients who may benefit from proactive supportive care interventions, such as nutritional counseling [31], physical activity programs [32], anti‐inflammatory treatments [29], and psychosocial support [33]. Second, it facilitates closer monitoring of at‐risk patients, allowing for the timely escalation of care as needed.

From a research perspective, establishing an operational “at risk” definition provides a foundation for clinical trials targeting early cachexia intervention. It also offers a framework for exploring biomarkers and novel therapeutic targets aimed at modulating the pathophysiology of cachexia before refractory tissue loss.

Conclusion

Cancer cachexia remains a critical and under addressed issue in oncology. Our findings demonstrate that a simple, clinically accessible “at risk” definition—based on the presence of any single AWGC component—can effectively stratify patients according to prognosis, particularly when fluid overload is absent. This approach enables early identification of individuals vulnerable to cachexia progression, facilitating timely supportive interventions before the condition becomes irreversible. Each component independently predicted survival, with handgrip strength and CRP level showing particularly strong prognostic associations. These results validated the AWGC‐based framework and supported its clinical relevance in palliative cancer care. While further prospective validation is needed, incorporating this “at risk” classification into routine practice may advance a more proactive, personalized model for cachexia management and inform future interventional research.
