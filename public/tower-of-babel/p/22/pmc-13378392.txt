The compliance patterns and personalized management of repeated red light therapy in children: A 4-year multicenter retrospective study
Authors: Guihua Liu, Na Li, Yuexuan Ma, Jinghui Wang, Desheng Song, Biying Wang, Yusheng Hu, Enpei Pei, Yingyu Tian, Jing Yang, Zhuoting Zhu, Bei Du, Ruihua Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryLimited real-world evidence exists on how repeated red light therapy (RRLT) compliance influences axial elongation patterns across multiple years. This 4-year multicenter retrospective real-world study included 2575 children aged 6–16 years (spherical equivalent −10.00 D to +3.00 D) using home-based RRLT. The annual axial length (AL) elongation ≤0.10 mm defined favorable efficacy. After 4 years, the cumulative AL increase was 0.32 ± 0.02 mm. Higher cumulative compliance correlated with slower AL elongation (β = −0.322; p < 0.001), particularly in children with higher baseline AL (6–9 years: >24.00 mm; 10–13 years: >25.00 mm). Spline models identified that cumulative compliance levels of approximately 58%, 69%, 58%, and 67% were associated with favorable efficacy outcome over 1–4 years, respectively. These findings indicate that at least 58% cumulative compliance is necessary for a favorable long-term response. This study provides suggestions for personalized treatment strategies to balance efficacy and usage burden in children receiving RRLT.Subject areas: health sciences, pediatrics, treatment

Introduction

Childhood myopia has reached epidemic proportions globally, posing a significant public health challenge due to its rising prevalence and associated risk of vision-threatening complications.1,2,3 Consequently, considerable research efforts have focused on identifying effective interventions to either prevent the onset of myopia or decelerate its progression.4 Among these strategies, repeated red light therapy (RRLT) has emerged as an innovative, non-invasive modality demonstrating remarkable potential in both myopia prevention and control.5,6

RRLT is a home-based device that emits 650 nm visible red light, and has demonstrated efficacy in slowing axial elongation and even inducing axial shortening.7 Data from randomized controlled trials (RCT) revealed that a 2-year RRLT intervention significantly reduced axial length (AL) elongation in myopic and premyopic children by 0.48 mm and 0.17 mm,8,9 respectively, compared to control groups. However, there remain two unresolved issues. On one hand, the comprehensive understanding of RRLT long-term sustained effects is insufficient. On the other hand, the role of treatment adherence, which is a common barrier in long-term home-based therapies, in influencing therapeutic outcomes among heterogeneous pediatric populations remains unclear. Consequently, it is essential to investigate the modulating effect of treatment adherence on therapeutic outcomes among diverse patient cohorts receiving sustained RRLT.

Therefore, this four-year, multicenter, real-world study aims not only to evaluate the long-term effectiveness of RRLT, but more importantly, to investigate the dose-response relationship between compliance and therapeutic outcomes. The purpose of our study is to identify personalized minimum effective compliance thresholds and, with the aim of optimizing home-based treatment strategies, to provide evidence for balancing therapeutic efficacy with usage burden in daily practice.

Results

Demographics

Between 1 September 2020 and 30 July 2025, 2911 children obtained RLRL device for therapy for at least 1 year, and a total of 2575 children undergoing RRLT treatment with complete information were included for final analysis (Figure 1). Baseline characteristics included sex (1360 [52.8%] boy), age (9.63 ± 2.80 years old), spherical equivalent (SE, −3.02 ± 2.53 D), AL (24.70 ± 1.24 mm) and best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA, LogMAR, 0.03 ± 0.10).

Axial elongation

The AL in the first year after RRLT treatment changed by −0.02 ± 0.01 mm. In the second year, it accumulated an increase of 0.09 ± 0.01 mm, followed by a cumulative increase of 0.19 ± 0.01 mm in the third year, and a cumulative increase of 0.32 ± 0.02 mm in the fourth year (Figure 2).

As shown in Figure 3, the cumulative mean changes in AL over the 1–4 years were comparable between boys and girls (1 year: p = 0.456; 2 years: p = 0.167; 3 years: p = 0.609; 4 years: p = 0.409). After RRLT treatment, children with older age, higher baseline SE or longer baseline AL experienced less cumulative AL elongation over the 1–4 years period.

Treatment compliance of RRLT

Over the course of 4 years, the annual treatment compliance rates for the 1st, 2nd, 3rd, and 4th years were 84.2%, 78.0%, 68.0%, and 57.6%, respectively. The cumulative compliance rates for years 1 through 4 were 84.2%, 81.4%, 78.2%, and 75.9%, respectively (Figure 4).

Association of axial elongation and treatment compliance

After adjusting for sex, baseline age, baseline SE, and baseline AL, the overall cumulative treatment compliance rate negatively correlated with the axial elongation in all participants following RRLT intervention (β = −0.322, p < 0.001; Table 1). This trend was consistent with that observed in both non-myopic (β = −0.230, p < 0.01; Table S2) and myopic subgroups (β = −0.350, p < 0.001; Table S2). For all participants, the negative association between the cumulative compliance and axial elongation was significant from year 1 through year 3 (all p < 0.001; Table 1). In year 4, the association showed a similar direction of effect, although it approached but did not reach statistical significance (β = −0.311, p = 0.078; Table 1). Figure 5A showed a significant non-linear association between the first-year axial elongation and the cumulative compliance rate. As the cumulative compliance rate increased, one-year axial elongation slowed down. To achieve the good response outcome (axial elongation of ≤0.10 mm/year) in the first year, a compliance rate of at least 58% was required. However, once the compliance rate exceeded 81%, the axial elongation remained stable. The cumulative compliance rates for years 2 through 4 were all linearly associated with the cumulative increases in AL (Figures 5B–5D). For the cumulative axial elongation over the second to fourth years to be limited to 0.20 mm, 0.30 mm, and 0.40 mm, cumulative compliance rates of at least 69%, 58%, and 67% were required, respectively. Subgroup analyses by geographic region found that the association between axial elongation and compliance rate did not differ across study sites (Figure 6).

Interaction of treatment compliance with baseline AL and age on axial elongation

Figure 7 showed that there was an interaction between cumulative compliance rate and baseline AL on axial elongation in different age groups and the three-way interaction term yielded a coefficient of 0.018 (95% CI: 0.002, 0.033; p for interaction, <0.001). The baseline characteristics for each age subgroup were shown in Table S3. For children aged 6–9, those with higher cumulative compliance (compared to a 50% compliance rate) had significantly shorter axial elongation over the 1 to 4-year treatment period when the baseline AL was greater than 24.00 mm (all p < 0.05). In children aged 10–13, increased compliance particularly influenced axial elongation during the first 1–2 years when the baseline AL exceeded 24.50 mm, and in the extended 3–4 years when the baseline AL surpassed 25.00 mm (all p < 0.05). For those aged 14–16, higher compliance notably reduced axial elongation, with the significant effects observed in the initial 1–2 years for a baseline AL between 25.00 and 26.00 mm, and at the 3-year stage for a baseline AL between 25.00 and 25.50 mm (all p < 0.05).

Safety

According to medical records, no functional damage, as indicated by BCVA, was observed during the 4-year RRLT intervention. Additionally, none of the children reported experiencing adverse effects, such as glare, flickering, or afterimages lasting longer than 6 min following treatment.

Discussion

In this 4-year, multicenter, real-world study of 2575 children, we demonstrated that home-based RRLT was associated with a substantially slowed axial elongation, with a mean cumulative increase of only 0.32 mm (0.08 mm/year) over four years. More importantly, by analyzing objectively monitored compliance data, we established a quantifiable dose-response relationship, that higher cumulative treatment compliance linearly was associated with better long-term control of axial elongation. A cumulative compliance rate of at least 58% was necessary to achieve a favorable therapeutic efficacy during long-term treatment. Moreover, the baseline AL and age may act as effect modifiers of the compliance-efficacy association. Thus, personalizing the RRLT compliance rate based on individual baseline age and AL was necessary.

Long-term efficacy of RRLT

The optimal long-term strategies for RRLT intervention have yet to be established, as most studies have focused on active treatment periods of only 1 to 2 years.7,8,9,10,11,12,13,14 In the present study, we analyzed data from large-scale medical records collected over 4 years, encompassing children with and without myopia who underwent RRLT, and found that RRLT was highly effective in controlling AL elongation during the first year, with a decline in effectiveness but sustained results over the next three years. Taken as a whole, the cumulative increase of AL over 4 years was 0.32 mm, with an average annual increase of 0.08 mm, which was even below the threshold of ≤0.10 mm/year, typically considered indicative of a good treatment response.15,16 This annualized rate is close to the lower limit of the predicted physiological eye growth range (0.04–0.33 mm/year) for children aged 6–16 years old,17 suggesting that long-term treatment with RRLT could significantly reduce the risk of future development of high myopia and related irreversible retinal changes caused by excessive axial growth. These findings were supported by one recent multicenter cohort study which demonstrated the 3-year RRLT treatment efficacy (annual AL change of 0.06 mm/year) among Chinese myopic children and adolescents.18 These fill the gap in understanding the long-term effects of RRLT on AL growth control, and provide evidence for its long-term value as an effective home-based photobiomodulation therapy.

Treatment compliance and treatment efficacy

Long-term compliance is a critical factor for influencing the effectiveness of home-based therapy.19,20 Unlike the self-reported adherence to other home-based myopia control strategies, such as low-concentration atropine,21 the compliance rate for RRLT in this study is derived from objective data collected through the automated diary function of the device system. Our study observes that axial elongation in the first year slowed as treatment compliance improved, but remained stable when compliance rate exceeded 81%. This finding indicates that maximizing the RRLT intervention frequency does not necessarily maximize the treatment effect at the initial stage. When the treatment duration is extended to 2–4 years, the cumulative axial elongation decreases with improved treatment compliance, showing a significant linear relationship. These findings underscore the crucial need to establish a proper incentive system that motivates children to consistently use the device. To balance the treatment efficacy and the burden of use, in long-term RRLT intervention, children need to maintain at least a 58% cumulative treatment compliance to achieve a good treatment response every year. Based on the prescribed treatment regimen (2 sessions per day, 7 days per week), this approximately corresponds to a minimum of 8 sessions of RRLT per week.

Recent study indicated that the myopia-control effect of RRLT is multifactorial.22 While changes in subfoveal choroidal thickness and peripheral refractive defocus are observed, mediation analyses indicate these factors account for only a portion of the therapeutic effect, with the majority attributable to direct mechanisms. The direct effects of RRLT involve both vascular and structural remodeling processes.23 This predominant direct mode of action likely underlies the dose-dependent efficacy observed in our long-term, real-world cohort. The adherence threshold may represent the minimum cumulative photobiomodulation dose required to consistently activate and sustain these direct biological effects at a level sufficient to achieve the predefined optimal clinical outcome. The plateau of therapeutic benefit is observedwhen the compliance exceeds 81% in the first year may indicate a saturation point for the initial biological response system. Conversely, the linear relationship between compliance and efficacy in subsequent years highlights that long-term treatment outcome critically depends on maintaining this “maintenance dose” to sustain cumulative structural changes.

The interaction between compliance, age, and baseline AL underscores the importance of risk-stratified management in children undergoing RRLT. For children aged 6–9 years with a baseline AL longer than 24.00 mm, and those aged 10–13 years with a baseline AL longer than 25.00 mm, it is recommended to increase the compliance as much as possible for less axial elongation. Conversely, for children with shorter baseline AL or older adolescents, a compliance at 50% may suffice. These findings indicate that during long-term RRLT treatment, clinicians should move away from the traditional view that higher compliance leads to slower axial elongation for all children. For the long-term RRLT treatment, clinicians should base on the scientific evidence, provide personalized recommendations for children due to the RRLT compliance rate, initial age, and baseline AL. This can not only alleviate the potential usage burden, but also enhance the adherence across the entire cohort of individuals as well.

As a home-based treatment option, the efficacy of RRLT depends on whether the patient can adhere to the treatment over an extended period. Based on the objective compliance data obtained from online treatment diary, this study shows that treatment compliance tends to naturally decline as the treatment duration increases, which is consistent with the challenges faced by other long-term home-based intervention programs.24 In the future, it is necessary to simultaneously integrate behavioral support strategies, such as digital reminders, parent participation plans, periodic monitoring feedback, in order to maintain treatment adherence. This is particularly important in the critical stages of the second year and beyond of the treatment where compliance significantly impacts cumulative efficacy.

Although no loss of BCVA or other adverse events was reported by children in this study, careful selection of device type is warranted in clinical practice,25 and regular monitoring of device optical output parameters, macular optical coherence tomography (OCT), cone density, and electroretinogram was recommended during treatment to ensure safety.18,26 Future long-term follow-up studies with larger sample sizes are needed to further evaluate the safety profile of RLRL therapy.

This 4-year multicenter real-world study demonstrates favorable long-term efficacy of RRLT in limiting axial elongation among children. A cumulative compliance rate of at least 58% should be maintained to achieve an annual AL progression no more than 0.1 mm. In addition, to balance the therapeutic effect and the usage burden, it is recommended that personalized treatment plans be formulated for myopic children using RRLT. These evidence-based findings provide guidance to clinicians in the long-term applications of RRLT for myopia management.

Limitations of the study

There are several limitations that should be noted. First, the retrospective, nonrandomized design may introduce selection bias, including the possibility that families opting for RRLT are more invested in visual health behaviors (healthy-user bias). Second, non-cycloplegic SE at baseline introduces accommodation-related measurement variability. Third, although the same device was intended for each child, real-world equipment upgrades or inter-device differences between Lenstar and IOL Master models may contribute to measurement variability. Fourth, OCT parameters were not available, preventing a mechanistic assessment of choroidal or retinal responses. Fifth, findings were derived from Chinese children and require validation in other populations. Sixth, the absence of a corresponding control group limits the ability to distinguish the treatment effect of RRLT from the natural progression of AL or refractive development. Future studies with a parallel untreated control group are needed to validate the specific effect of RRLT. Seventh, the marked sample attrition over the four-year follow-up (from 2575 to 1599 subjects) may have reduced statistical power and affected the interpretability of the later-year compliance rates. Lastly, the inclusion of only children who had used RRLT for at least one year may introduce a selection bias, specifically that those who discontinued early (e.g., due to poor efficacy) were excluded from the analysis.

Experimental model and study participant details

Participants were children aged 6 to 16 years, with no gender restrictions, receiving RRLT therapy for at least 12 months between 1 September 2020 and 30 July 2025 and having clinical data available at baseline and at least 12-month follow-up visits in hospitals of five provinces in China. All participants (n = 2575) were Chinese, with 1360 males and 1215 females. The mean age was 9.63 ± 2.80 years old.

This study was approved by the ethics committee of Tianjin Medical University Eye Hospital (ethics approval ID: 2025KY-41) and was registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (identifier: NCT07104513). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Method details

Study design and setting

This is a 4-year, retrospective, multicenter, real-world study based on the data derived from medical records in hospitals of five provinces in China from 1 September 2020 to 30 July 2025.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria for participants

Eligible participants were children aged 6 to 16 years, with an SE refraction ranging from −10.00 D to +3.00 D and BCVA (LogMAR) of 0.15 or better in both eyes, receiving RRLT therapy for at least 12 months between 1 September 2020 and 30 July 2025 and having clinical data available at baseline and at least 12-month follow-up visits. The study excluded participants with ocular abnormalities (e.g., strabismus, amblyopia), systemic diseases and ocular surgery history. Children who underwent myopia control treatments other than single-vision spectacles in the past or during the study period such as orthokeratology, defocus spectacles and atropine eye-drops, were excluded further. Figure 1 presents the study flow diagram.

RRLT therapy

The RRLT device (Eyerising; Suzhou Xuanjia Optoelectronics Technology, Suzhou, China), was purchased or provided free of charge for children included in the study period. Details of the device have been published previously.8,14 Briefly, the device incorporates a semiconductor laser diode which emits low-level red light with a wavelength of 650 ± 10 nm. Children were instructed to use the device at home under the supervision of their parents, and received the therapy twice daily for 3 min per session, spaced at least 4 h apart, and administered 7 days a week.

Treatment compliance monitoring of RRLT

The RRLT device automatically recorded the date and time of therapy sessions and sent them via the Internet to build an online treatment diary. A reminder message was sent to the supervisor, as well as to the parents or guardians of the children, whenever the system detected two consecutive days of inactivity in logins. The supervisor would immediately contact parents or guardians to facilitate improvements in treatment compliance. The treatment compliance rate was calculated by dividing the number of completed sessions (each session must last 3 min) by the total number of assigned treatment sessions (2 sessions per day, 7 days per week) throughout the entire treatment period.

Ophthalmic examinations

At baseline, all participants underwent a complete ophthalmic examination, including slit-lamp ophthalmic examination, BCVA, non-cycloplegic refraction, AL and fundus examination. Non-cycloplegic refraction was measured using auto-refractometer firstly, followed by subjective refraction performed by professional optometrists. The SE was calculated by summing the dioptric power of the sphere and half of the cylinder.

Measures of AL were performed at baseline and each follow-up visits using Lenstar (Lenstar LS-900, Haag-Streit AG, Koeniz, Switzerland) or IOL-Master (Carl Zeiss 500/700, Meditec, Oberkochen, Germany) depending on the devices available in each hospital. The same instrument was used for testing on the same participant throughout the study period. Axial elongation was defined as the difference between the measurements taken at baseline and those recorded at each follow-up visit. The annual axial elongation of ≤0.10 mm was considered a good response outcome.15,16

Quantification and statistical analysis

In this study, analyses were performed based on data from each subject’s right eye to exclude intra-individual correlations. Continuous variables were reported as means with standard deviations (SD), and categorical variables as frequencies with percentages. The differences among groups were assessed using χ2 tests for categorical variables, and two independent t-tests, analysis of variance (ANOVA), or Kruskal–Wallis tests for continuous variables, as appropriate.

To evaluate the longitudinal association between cumulative compliance rate and AL elongation, we employed linear mixed-effects models (LMMs), specifying a random-intercept and random-slope model for each participant to account for intra-individual correlation across repeated measurements, and the fixed effect was the cumulative compliance rate, treated as a continuous variable of interest after adjusting for sex, baseline age, baseline SE, baseline AL and time. As the interaction term was not statistically significant, subsequent models were presented without the interaction, focusing on the overall cumulative effect. Missing data were assumed to be missing at random and were handled using maximum likelihood estimation within the mixed-effects framework. In addition, we also conducted the analyses in the subjects having records in 1 year, 2 years, 3 years and 4 years to explore the consistency, respectively.

To investigate potential nonlinear relationships, we further modeled cumulative compliance rate using restricted cubic splines (RCS). Specifically, we selected the number of knots based on the minimum Bayes information criterion value. In our final model, 3 knots were used (located at the 10%, 50%, and 90% percentiles) according to the quantiles recommended by Harrell. Separate RCS models were estimated for each follow-up year to characterize possible year-specific nonlinear associations.

Subgroup analyses were also conducted by geographic region to assess the consistency of associations across study sites. To account for the potential influence of baseline refractive status, stratified analyses were conducted based on baseline myopia status. The baseline characteristics for non-myopic and myopic subgroups are shown in Table S1. Within each stratum, multiple linear regression models were employed to evaluate the association between compliance rate and axial elongation. Effect modification was additionally examined by introducing interaction terms between cumulative compliance rate and baseline AL, as well as baseline age. The differences in axial elongation corresponding to different cumulative compliance rates were tested using T tests where multiple comparisons of p value were adjusted by Tukey.

All statistical analyses were performed using R 4.4 software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and a two-sided p < 0.05 was defined as statistically significant.
