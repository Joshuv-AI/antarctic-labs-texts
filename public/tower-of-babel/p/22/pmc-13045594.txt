Resuscitated Cardiac Arrest From Cardiac Sarcoidosis in a Professional Basketball Player
Authors: Balakrishnan Pillai, Elizabeth Ashley Hardin, Mark S. Link, Koichi Kato, Benjamin D. Levine
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiac sarcoidosis is associated with malignant arrhythmias, and athletes with implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs) have historically been restricted from competition.Case SummaryA 34-year old professional basketball player experienced cardiac arrest due to monomorphic ventricular tachycardia during competition, however cardiopulmonary resuscitation with automated external defibrillator use led to immediate cardioversion. Work-up revealed findings of probable cardiac sarcoidosis according to current criteria. He was treated with steroids, and an ICD was placed with no further arrhythmia. With shared decision-making, he returned to competition.DiscussionThe evaluation of cardiac arrest requires coronary, genetic, and myocardial interrogation, with myocardial processes best evaluated using advanced imaging and, selectively, endomyocardial biopsy. Recent data suggest the safety of ICD use in athletes.Take-Home MessagesThe diagnosis of cardiac sarcoidosis relies largely on clinical findings and noninvasive imaging and exists on a spectrum of definite, probable, possible, or unlikely. Return to play after ICD implantation is an important consideration in competitive athletes with sudden cardiac arrest and requires shared decision-making.

History of Presentation

A 34-year old male African American professional basketball player was referred for evaluation after a resuscitated cardiac arrest while playing basketball in Japan. He was in his usual state of health during this most recent basketball season without exertional limitation or symptoms. His regimen consisted of playing basketball daily with competitive games 2 and 3 times per week and complementary strength training twice a week. Midway through the season, during the first half of the second game of a double-header, he felt acutely lightheaded while stopping to turn. He collapsed, and his teammates quickly initiated bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) for approximately 30 seconds. An automated external defibrillator (AED) was applied, and the presenting rhythm was noted to be monomorphic ventricular tachycardia (MMVT), after which a shock was delivered and sinus rhythm was restored (Figure 1). He was taken to a nearby hospital, where an extensive evaluation was performed. His physical examination on admission was unremarkable. Past Medical History

The patient's personal history was notable only for orthopedic injuries sustained during competitive sports. His family history was significant for a paternal aunt who died from unexplained sudden cardiac arrest (SCA) in her early 40s and a paternal grandparent who died from complications of systemic lupus erythematosus.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis for MMVT in an athlete includes idiopathic ventricular tachycardia (VT), VT in the setting of underlying atherosclerotic ischemic heart disease, anomalous coronary arteries, or myocardial bridging, as well as nonischemic cardiomyopathy, channelopathy, and myocarditis—either drug induced, viral/lymphocytic, sarcoidosis, or giant cell.

Investigations

At the hospital in Japan, a resting 12-lead electrocardiogram (Figure 2) showed sinus bradycardia with first-degree atrioventricular block and inverted T waves in the inferior and anterolateral leads. A high-sensitivity troponin assay, and other baseline laboratory results, were within normal limits for the Japanese laboratory (Table 1). Resting transthoracic echocardiography revealed 4-chamber dilation with eccentric left ventricular hypertrophy, mild global hypokinesis with a left ventricular ejection fraction of 50%, and severely reduced global longitudinal strain of −12.3% in the 2-chamber view, with large regional variation. Computed tomography coronary angiography disclosed normal origins of the coronary arteries without evidence of epicardial coronary disease. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) with gadolinium contrast was obtained and demonstrated hypokinesis and thinning of the mid-distal inferior and lateral walls, with corresponding epicardial late gadolinium enhancement (Figure 3, Figure 4, Figure 5), as well as prolonged T1 times, especially in the apex. Cardiac positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) with FDG (18F-fluorodeoxyglucose) was performed, without ketogenic diet, and showed focal-on-diffuse uptake of tracer in the mid-distal inferior and lateral walls of the left ventricle (Figures 6 and 7). An endomyocardial biopsy of the right ventricular septum was pursued, and pathology results showed mild fibrosis and very mild lymphocytic infiltration without epithelioid granuloma or amyloid deposits. A comprehensive 157 arrhythmia and cardiomyopathy genetic panel was obtained, yielding 5 variants of unknown significance including 1 in DSC2 (desmocollin-2) and 2 in DMD (dystrophin). Management

Based on the diagnostic evaluation performed in Japan, the patient was diagnosed with probable cardiac sarcoidosis with concomitant ocular involvement, and he was started on a prolonged prednisone taper. A subcutaneous implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) was placed for secondary prevention, and physical activity was restricted. Left ventricular function improved with these interventions, with ejection fraction increasing to 54%, global longitudinal strain to −20%, and concomitant normalization of chamber sizes over the course of 3 months. The patient noted no ICD shocks over this time frame, and additional 30-day rhythm monitors did not uncover any significant burden of ectopy. In the U.S. , a maximal cardiopulmonary exercise test was performed, showing a peak oxygen uptake of 31.2 mL/kg/min (86% predicted), 30% lower than expected for the average professional basketball player although consistent with detraining and prednisone use. Additionally, a sport-specific stress test simulating a full quarter of a basketball game was performed, and there were only rare couplet premature ventricular complexes and no VT at any time.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient continued taking low-dose prednisone without cardiovascular symptoms or recurrent ICD shocks. Based on the results of the maximal cardiopulmonary exercise test and sport-specific stress testing, a shared decision-making approach was used, and he returned to competitive basketball in Japan with plans to obtain repeat cardiac PET/CT testing with appropriate dietary preparation.

Discussion

Our patient experienced an out-of-hospital cardiac arrest due to sustained MMVT and was resuscitated, with no permanent postarrest related sequelae due to the prompt application of an AED, implementation of bystander CPR, and use of an emergency action plan. These practices are mandated by Japanese law, and they are strongly recommended by the most recent 2025 American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology scientific statement on competitive sport participation in athletes with cardiovascular abnormalities.1 He is representative of an at-risk population of athletes, as the rates of SCA in athletes have been shown in epidemiological studies to be highest in Black basketball players.2 The etiology of MMVT in athletes spans a diverse range, including ischemic heart disease (atherosclerotic, anomalous origins of the coronary arteries, myocardial bridging, vasospasm) to nonischemic cardiomyopathies, infiltrative and inflammatory disorders of the myocardium, and inherited channelopathies (although the latter predisposes most commonly to polymorphic VT).3

The patient's initial cardiovascular evaluation reliably excluded any coronary or ischemic etiologies of his presentation, however his initial echocardiogram showed findings of 4-chamber dilation with borderline left ventricular systolic function. These findings could have been consistent with a primary cardiomyopathy or exercise-induced cardiovascular remodeling, however the return to normal function and chamber sizes with detraining favored the latter.4,5 Ultrastructural evaluation with CMR showed evidence of regional hypokinesis with subepicardial late gadolinium enhancement—findings consistent with a primary cardiomyopathy or prior myocarditis, and not changes normally seen due to exercise.4,5 These regions additionally corresponded to areas of relative avidity on PET/CT, which was concerning for an ongoing inflammatory process of the myocardium.6 Unfortunately, his cardiac PET/CT was obtained without a preceding ketogenic diet, reducing its specificity, and a right ventricular septal endomyocardial biopsy—a notoriously insensitive procedure—did not capture a granulomatous process.6 Although he did not meet definite 2014 Heart Rhythm Society criteria for the diagnosis of cardiac sarcoidosis given the lack of granulomatous inflammation on endomyocardial biopsy, he met the 2016 Japanese Circulation Society criteria for clinical cardiac sarcoidosis with systemic involvement. Importantly, satisfaction of these diagnostic criteria is contingent on a high-quality cardiac PET/CT. Given the presentation of MMVT, resting first-degree atrioventricular block, and findings on CMR and PET/CT, in conjunction with granulomatous uveitis, the patient was treated empirically for cardiac sarcoidosis. At the time, other forms of myocarditis were considered less likely given the results of the endomyocardial biopsy, negative cardiac troponin levels, and lack of viral prodrome. He showed improvement in the burden of ventricular arrhythmia (with no recurrence) and in his ventricular function, the latter of which was consistent with a diagnosis of cardiac sarcoidosis according to 2014 Heart Rhythm Society criteria, however he is still awaiting a follow-up cardiac PET/CT to investigate resolution of inflammation, which would globally strengthen confidence in the initial diagnosis.6 The response to steroids with clinical quiescence during taper, coupled with potential doubt on initial diagnosis, led the team to delay prescribing other immunomodulatory agents.

Of note, our patient did have a family history positive for unexplained SCA in a second-degree relative, and his inherited arrhythmia and cardiomyopathy panel was positive for 5 variants of unknown significance, 1 in DSC-2 and 2 in DMD—desmosomal genes known to be linked to arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy. Taken together, these findings raise a possible alternative diagnosis of an inherited nonischemic cardiomyopathy as the etiology of his MMVT and SCA. Repeat CMR and cardiac PET/CT will be instrumental in clarifying these diagnoses. This case highlights the difficulties in the diagnosis of cardiac sarcoidosis; ultimately, if the diagnosis is confirmed, he may require revision of his subcutaneous ICD to a transvenous ICD given the frequent requirement of pacing in this condition.7

Return to competition in athletes with ICDs has been restricted for decades, but recent data from a multinational, prospective ICD registry have changed our understanding of the risks in this group, ultimately showing that athletes with ICDs may return to competitive sport without increased risk of device malfunction or death; only individuals with arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy showed higher rates of appropriate shocks with exercise.8 Mirroring the most recent American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology scientific statement,1 as ventricular systolic function recovered with empiric treatment for cardiac sarcoidosis in our patient, he underwent maximal, sport-specific stress testing as well as extended rhythm monitoring in order to frame a shared-decision making discussion regarding return to competition, and he ultimately returned for the 2026 season in Japan.

Conclusions

A professional basketball player experienced a witnessed out-of-hospital cardiac arrest and received effective bystander CPR with AED shock, ultimately diagnosed with MMVT due to probable cardiac sarcoidosis versus nonischemic cardiomyopathy. He received a secondary prevention subcutaneous ICD, and with empiric therapy for sarcoidosis he has seen recovery of his ventricular function and cessation of arrhythmia. Using shared-decision making, he has returned to sport while work-up is ongoing.
