Mark-recapture validates the use of photo-identification for the widely distributed blue-spotted ribbontail ray, Taeniura lymma
Authors: Ashlie J. McIvor, Collin T. Williams, Walter A. Rich, Anna M. Knochel, Neil M. Burns, Michael L. Berumen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The ability to identify individual animals can provide valuable insights into the behaviour, life history, survivorship, and demographics of wild populations. Photo-identification (photo-ID) uses unique natural markings to identify individuals and can be effective for scalable and non-invasive research on marine fauna. The successful application of photo-ID requires that chosen distinguishing markings are unique to individuals and persist over time. In this study, we validate the use of dorsal spot patterns for identifying individual blue-spotted ribbontail rays (Taeniura lymma) in conjunction with traditional tagging methods. Spot patterns were unique among T. lymma with 90.3% of individuals correctly identified using I3S photo-matching software from images taken up to 496 days apart. In comparison, traditional physical tagging methods showed a tag loss rate of 27% and a maximum tag retention period of only 356 days. Our findings demonstrate the effectiveness of photo-ID as a tool to monitor populations and better understand the ecology of the blue-spotted ribbontail ray without the need for physical tagging. The validation of photo-ID for this widespread species is important as it enables behavioural and demographic changes to be easily tracked in relation to coastal threats such as human development and habitat degradation.

Introduction

The ability to accurately identify individual animals is useful for effective species management. Differentiation among individuals of the same species enables researchers to investigate many aspects of a species’ biology, including social behaviour, reproductive patterns, population size, residency, site fidelity, connectivity, and habitat preferences1–4. Traditionally, populations have been monitored using mark-recapture techniques, where individuals are physically marked with distinguishing tags or catalogued using natural physical identifiers. To reduce the invasiveness of monitoring techniques, photo-identification (photo-ID) has frequently been employed as an alternative to more intrusive methods of identifying individual animals5.

The use of photo-ID has grown in recent years, especially among marine vertebrates, where pigmentation patterns, scale patterns, scarring, or other markings are used as distinguishing features6–10. Successfully identifying individuals of a given species with photo-ID assumes that such markings are unique and persist through time. Several studies have demonstrated that batoids in particular are suitable candidates for photo-ID and that photo-matching software can provide statistical validation for these methodological assumptions (e.g., reef manta rays Mobula alfredi11, spotted eagle rays Aetobatus narinari12–14, and smalleye stingrays Megatrygon microps15). However, for some species, spot patterns may change in size, shape, or quantity throughout an individual animal’s life which may hinder re-identification over time. For example, some batoid lineages appear to exhibit spot patterns that may not remain stable through time16, and the utility of photo-ID must be evaluated on a species-by-species basis5.

The bluespotted ribbontail ray Taeniura lymma (Forsskål, 1775) is a demersal dasyatid stingray that is widely distributed throughout the Indo-West Pacific Ocean, inhabiting coral reefs, mangrove habitats, and lagoonal systems17. This species is characterised by its conspicuous blue spots across the dorsal surface and two blue stripes along the dorsolateral edges of the tail (Supplementary Fig. 1). The reproductive strategy of T. lymma is viviparous18, with sexually mature individuals giving birth to live young that already exhibit a distinct spot pattern similar to that of adult individuals. Global declines in shark populations and the consequent reduction in predation has led to speculation about a potential increase in T. lymma populations via mesopredator release19,20. However, in order to comprehensively assess the status of T. lymma, it is necessary to gather more information on population trends and life history characteristics19,21. The ability to easily identify individuals of this species through photo-ID would greatly facilitate the collection of such information. This is particularly pertinent to regions undergoing rapid coastal development, such as the Red Sea, where planned developmental activities overlap with key habitats for T. lymma22. Monitoring regional populations of T. lymma through photo-ID can aid in understanding the species' behavioural and population-level responses to habitat degradation as well as provide evidence-based management insights for small-scale fisheries which harvest the species23,24. To systematically identify T. lymma individuals using photo-ID, the persistence of uniquely identifiable spot patterns needs to be first validated through a standardised approach. Although extremely short-term matches (i.e., < 60 minutes19,22) of T. lymma using spot patterns have been documented, there has been no investigation into the reliability and longevity of these markings over longer timescales relevant to population monitoring.

In this study, we not only assess the accuracy of using unique dorsal spot patterns of T. lymma for individual identification but also examine the longevity of these spot patterns to evaluate the use of photo-ID for long-term monitoring. To ensure the reliability of photo-ID, we combine this method with mark-recapture tagging to ensure that our photo-ID evaluations are grounded in verifiable recaptures. This dual approach not only confirms the accuracy of using photo-ID with T. lymma, but also validates the comparisons made between individuals. Determining the distinctiveness and temporal stability of dorsal spot patterns in T. lymma is a crucial prerequisite for enabling low-cost, scalable, and non-invasive monitoring of this widespread, coastal species.

Results

Tagging and recapture events

In total, there were 218 T. lymma capture events from Thuwal (n = 185) and the Farasan Banks (n = 33), Saudi Arabia (Fig. 1). From all capture events, 161 individuals were identified: 80 females, 56 males, and 25 individuals for which sex was undetermined. Average disc width in females was 22.9 cm and 22 cm in males (Supplementary Fig. 2). Of the 161 identified rays, 29 (18%) were physically recaptured at least once within the study period. Photo-ID validation

Mark-recapture efforts provided a robust validation of photo-ID for T. lymma by definitively confirming individual identity through verifiable recaptures. Each individual within the validation test-set (i.e., known individuals) was correctly identified in the randomised blind trials, confirming the applicability of photo-ID for the species. Dorsal spot patterns of T. lymma were found to be unique not only among tagged individuals, but all individuals examined in this study. This confirmation of distinct spot patterns, achieved by matching known individuals, enabled the application of I3S software to further test the efficiency of computer-aided photo-ID for all individuals.

The ROC analysis showed that individual spot patterns of T. lymma were sufficiently different to successfully discriminate between individuals using the I3S software. With an AUC value of 0.98, the I3S software demonstrated a high level of accuracy in its ability to successfully identify T. lymma individuals using dorsal spot patterns in the database test-set (Fig. 2). There was a notable exception with the individual with the longest recapture interval (496 days) which displayed observable changes in the number of spots. For example, a relatively small spot (< 3 mm diameter) appeared on the cranium and a spot (< 5 mm) on the outer pectoral disc disappeared (Fig. 3). Discussion

The current study establishes that photo-ID can be used effectively to identify T. lymma individuals using dorsal spot patterns, at least over a couple of years. Our results reveal a high degree of individual variation in T. lymma spot patterns (Supplementary Fig. 4). I3S photo-matching software correctly identified 161 different T. lymma individuals and proved to be an effective method to rapidly recognise individual spot patterns among T. lymma. The physical recapture of individuals demonstrated that the overall pattern of larger spots persists and allows for accurate identification for at least 496 days (Fig. 3). Given the longevity of spot patterns and the high probability of tag loss, we suggest photo-ID as a preferable, non-invasive alternative to traditional tagging methods for individual identifications of T. lymma.

The implementation of computer-aided identification with I3S confirmed that the spot patterns of T. lymma were unique and distinguishable, and validated its use for photo-ID of individuals. With a true-positive detection rate exceeding 90%, the results of our study affirm the reliability of I3S as a photo-matching tool. These findings are consistent with previous studies which also used I3S to identify individual teleost fish such as Paracirrhites forsteri25 and Stereolepis gigas10, and, more commonly, elasmobranchs such as A. narinari, Carcharodon carcharias, and Carcharias taurus12,26,27. Among these studies focusing on elasmobranchs, only C. taurus achieved a higher true-positive detection rate of 92% exceeding that established here, highlighting the suitability of T. lymma for photo-ID based identification. Our study also establishes a threshold score of 8.39 for the identification of T. lymma individuals, where match scores below this value could be considered true-positive matches (Fig. 2). This threshold score is also comparable to those previously established for other species of the family Dasyatidae, such as M. microps (10.0615).

Assessing the long-term applicability of photo-ID requires knowledge of the degree of change in T. lymma dorsal markings over time. Of the 29 individuals that were recaptured, only one individual had an observable change in the spot pattern (Fig. 3; adult female). However, this change was only observed in smaller spots (< 5 mm diameter) located on the outer edge of the disc and on the cranium, both of which had not been selected for the I3S fingerprint file. Despite this, the larger spot patterns in the centre of the disc remained unchanged, allowing for successful re-identification of the individual for up to 16 months using I3S. Specifically, the image of the re-captured individual had the lowest match score (i.e., closest match) to the image from the initial capture event, and retained its position as the most likely match within the database. This emphasises the importance of considering the stability of individual spots, as well as the role of larger and more central spots in maintaining the overall stability of the pattern for long-term identification. While the positive correlation between spot size and disc width suggests that photo-ID may be more reliable for adult individuals of T. lymma, it is unclear whether spot patterns change with age or developmental stage. For example, some species, such as the reticulate whipray, Himantura uarnak, appears to gain new spots with age instead of having its existing spots grow in size16. However, given that the blue spots characteristic of T. lymma are structural colours rather than pigment-based28, it is likely that these spots will remain stable over the individual's lifespan. To fully understand population dynamics and ontogenetic distribution shifts, it will be essential to confirm the stability of spot patterns over longer durations (i.e., > 500 days). While this study was conducted on wild populations of T. lymma, it would be valuable to independently validate these findings in a captive setting, such as those in aquaria. Based on these observations, it is advisable to use only spots larger than 5 mm in diameter and those located in the centre of the disc to ensure matching accuracy and minimise errors due to potential spot pattern changes over time. Due to the limited tag retention times observed in the current study, alternative marking methods (such as, microchips or Passive Integrated Transponder (PIT) tags29,30) should also be considered in future studies to assess spot-stability in T. lymma over greater timescales.

While substantial scars or deformities can serve as secondary features for identification (e.g., flapper skate, Dipturus intermedius29), the high healing capacity of T. lymma means that scar tissue cannot be relied upon as a defining feature for photo-ID. Anecdotally, in the current study recaptured individuals did not have any visible scarring at the tag attachment site three months after losing their tag. It is also important to note that injuries to the dorsal surface may not result in long-term scarring but may still have the capacity to alter boundaries of individual spots (see Supplementary Fig. 5). Though injuries may temporarily alter spot boundaries, they do not affect the relative position of spots or their pattern, and thus will not impact the placement of points in the fingerprint files and subsequent I3S matches, as the software relies solely on the central position of the spots rather than their shape.

Photo-ID offers several advantages over conventional tagging methods for T. lymma. Firstly, the physical risks associated with collecting stingrays for tagging, including the potential harm from their barbed tails, pose a significant challenge to both researchers and the animals. Additionally, the physical attachment of the tags can restrict movement and cause capture/handling stress, potentially impacting the survival or behaviour of the individual13,31. In contrast, photo-ID will not always require stingrays to be captured, thus avoiding the risks and impacts associated with traditional tagging methods when identifying individuals. It is still important to note, however, that the successful application of photo-ID in the wild is dependent on several factors, such as the clarity of the water, photo quality and clarity, the behaviour of the animals towards observers, and the accessibility of the population in terms of depth and habitat32. Nevertheless, photo-ID is likely to be more cost-effective than conventional tagging methods, particularly when the cost of tagging equipment and the labour required to attach and monitor the tags are considered33. Additionally, since tags can be shed, photo-ID is more effective at identifying individuals over longer durations as the dorsal spot pattern of T. lymma appears to be stable over the course of the study. However, similar to the loss of an identification tag, the degree to which identifiable markings change over time may affect the reliability of photo-ID confirmation, potentially leading to uncertainty in matching individuals5. As such, future research is needed to evaluate the factors that may affect dorsal spot pattern stability in T. lymma and to determine which parts of the body future photo-ID efforts should focus on to ensure maximum pattern stability. Provided that the assumption of being able to re-identify individuals over extended periods holds true, photo-ID could enable longer monitoring timescales while avoiding the negative impacts associated with invasive methods, making it a particularly suitable option for long-term research on this species.

The current study highlights the potential for computer-aided photo-ID to improve the efficiency of long-term monitoring of T. lymma populations. The utility of photo-ID extends beyond individual recognition, offering a powerful tool for addressing a range of ecological questions. For example, photo-ID has been used to assess courtship and mating behaviour in Mobula birostris34, social preferences and network structure in M. alfredi35, and site fidelity and connectivity in A. narinari14. Future applications of photo-ID may not even require physical capture as good quality photographs that meet grading criteria can be easily taken alongside common research methods, such as dive surveys10,13,31 or aerial surveys over shallow-water habitats22 (Supplementary Fig. 1). With increasing capabilities in machine learning, the process of identifying individual animals from photographs could likely be fully automated, making large-scale photo-ID projects even more feasible. Collaborative efforts between researchers and local stakeholders can also expand the spatial coverage of data collection and complement non-invasive monitoring programmes to increase knowledge on the biology and conservation of this species33. Recreational scuba divers or fishermen can also help collect photo identification data on stingrays by participating in community science programs or by contributing their own photographs to ongoing research projects, similar to MantaMatcher33 and the Common skate photo-ID database for Scotland36. Overall, by expanding photo-identification databases in both size and duration, the performance of photo-matching software will improve, as there will be increased availability of matches to draw upon26,37.

Conclusion

The present study has validated the accuracy and reliability of using natural dorsal spot patterns to identify T. lymma individuals. Our data suggests that T. lymma can be reliably recognised using photographs over at least 496 days. Considering tag retention rates and the potential impacts from tag application, the use of photo-ID can provide more reliable information with minimal impacts to individuals within a population. Long-term studies involving T. lymma can further validate the use of photo-ID to monitor this species over longer periods and could potentially be expanded to include other species with similar pigmentation patterns (e.g., bluespotted maskray, Neotrygon kuhlii; and Oceania fantail ray, Taeniura lessoni)19,38. With the validation of this method for T. lymma, long-term monitoring using photo-ID can provide new insights about the population trends, behaviour, and biology of T. lymma to better inform their management.

Methods

This study complied with the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia's animal welfare laws, guidelines and policies as approved by the King Abdullah University of Science and Technology (KAUST) Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) under approval #18IACUC14. Furthermore, this research adheres to the ARRIVE guidelines (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments) to ensure transparency and reproducibility in reporting39.

Collections and tagging

Taeniura lymma were collected at two locations in the Saudi Arabian Red Sea where the species is locally abundant22,40: Thuwal (22.3245, 39.0894) and the Farasan Banks (19.6207, 40.6067) (Fig. 1). The collection site in Thuwal lies along a fringing coastal reef system backed by a shallow, sandy lagoon and mangrove forest in the central Red Sea. Collections in the Farasan Banks were conducted off small islands (Dubarah, Thara, and Sirryan), which also contained both mangrove and coral reef systems, in the southern Red Sea.

Hand-seine nets (1.5 × 15 m) were used to capture T. lymma individuals in shallow water environments (< 1 m deep) and dip nets were used in deeper environments (> 1 m deep) while freediving. Once captured, T. lymma were transferred into a dip net if captured by hand-seine, and the barb(s) were immediately secured using fabric cloth. Disc width (DW; mm), sex, identifying features (e.g., physical deformities), and capture location were documented for each individual that was collected, when possible. Individuals were then briefly removed from the water and placed on a flat surface (< 30 s intervals), where 2 to 3 photographs were taken perpendicular to the dorsal surface using an Olympus TG waterproof camera, positioned above a metric measurement strip which was visible in every photo. All T. lymma individuals > 20 cm DW collected in Thuwal were fitted with polyolefin dart-style identification tags (Floy Tag Inc., Seattle, USA). Tags displayed unique identification numbers and were implanted into pectoral musculature away from internal organs. A minimum size criterion of 20 cm DW was established to avoid any potential negative impacts on locomotion for individuals with decreased pectoral muscle mass. Each T. lymma individual was released at their capture site following morphological measurements and photographs, with total work-up duration not exceeding five minutes (i.e., one capture event). Sample size was determined based on the availability of T. lymma individuals at the study locations. Efforts were made to ensure comprehensive coverage of the population by conducting multiple collection events at each location, resulting in a robust sample size that reflects the local abundance of the species.

Photo-identification

Photographs were retrospectively examined to select the image with the clearest spot patterns per individual, which was used as the final photo-ID shot for subsequent analyses. Only one image per individual was added to the main photo-ID database, while any additional images taken during the same capture event or upon recapture and identification from tags were retained in the main stingray database (Supplementary Fig. 6). To assess the accuracy of manual spot-pattern identification in identifying known individuals of T. lymma, a validation test-set consisting of 20 individuals that were recaptured, re-photographed, and already present in the main photo-ID database was created. These individuals were considered “known” due to identification from their physical tags, allowing the applicability of manual spot-pattern identification to be assessed. Each image from the validation test-set was visually compared against every image in the main photo-ID database in an iterative pairwise comparison process. Visual comparison of spot shape, their relative positions, and quantity were used as identifiable features during this process. The initial pairwise comparisons were verified by two independent researchers who conducted randomised blind trials on the validation test-set against every image in the main photo-ID database, consistent with methods used in previous photo-ID studies15,29. Once the applicability of individual identification was confirmed, this manual validation process was conducted for all images in the photo-ID database to check for duplicate or misidentified individuals in the database to account for possible recapture events of untagged rays (i.e., individuals < 20 cm DW) or tag losses.

The photo-matching software, I3S Classic (Version 4.0.241), was used to analyse a digital library of T. lymma photographs. Fingerprint files were created in I3S for each individual by defining an identification area based upon three fixed reference points on the dorsal surface: tip of rostrum, left pectoral insertion and right pectoral insertion (Supplementary Fig. 7). These points of reference were chosen as there is no clear apex to the pectoral disc, and this area is less prone to flex with pectoral movement15. The centre of 30 dorsal spots were user-selected in the software interface within the identification area to create a fingerprint file for each individual. To avoid selecting very small spots, the selection process began with all conspicuous spots on the individual’s cranium and then continued with those radiating from the centre of the cranium that were anterior of the eye stalks (Supplementary Fig. 7).

To evaluate the performance of I3S in matching photos of T. lymma individual, we tested rates of true-positive and false-positive identifications. In this instance, a true-positive was confirmed when two images were of the same T. lymma individual, such as an image taken from a recapture event identified from the physical tag or a consecutive image of the same individual from the same capture event. In contrast, a false-positive occurred when the algorithm incorrectly matched two images of different individuals, such as erroneously pairing an image taken in Thuwal with one taken in the Farasan Banks. Given that the home-range of T. lymma is assumed to be relatively small42, T. lymma captured in Thuwal and those from the Farasan Banks were considered to be different individuals as these locations are approximately > 350 km apart, making it highly unlikely for an individual T. lymma to travel such distances. We developed two test-sets to evaluate the performance of the algorithm: the database test-set and the error test-set. The database test-set was compiled using one image of each T. lymma individual captured in Thuwal directly from the main database (n = 128). The error test-set consisted of a subset of T. lymma individuals that were included in the database test-set, but with distinct images that were not used in the database test-set. This included photographs from recapture events (n = 31) and consecutive photos (n = 30) taken of the same individual to serve as examples of true-positives. In addition, the error test-set contained images of T. lymma individuals that were not included in the training database, such as those captured in the Farasan Banks (n = 15), which serve as examples of false-positives. The database test-set was used as the training dataset for the algorithm, with the error test-set used to evaluate the algorithm's ability to distinguish true-positives from false-positives.

Fingerprint files for each individual in the error test-set were queried against the photo-ID database test-set. I3S numerically ranks images within the database against the queried image based on how closely the fingerprint files match in terms of relative distance between the user-selected spots. The resulting match score of each image describes how closely the spot patterns match the query image, with a higher match score indicating a closer match. Once matched, a thorough manual comparison was conducted to assess ‘true-positives’ (matched images of T. lymma known to be the same individual) or ‘false-positives’ (known to be different).

The efficacy of I3S software to successfully match T. lymma individuals was assessed using a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve from the ROCR packaging43 of R version 4.0.344. The ROC analysis compared the rate of ‘true-positives’ against ‘false-positives’. A cut-off value was identified as the point at which the rate of ‘true-positives’ was highest and ‘false-positive’ was lowest using Youden’s index. This cut-off value indicates the optimal threshold score that should be considered when discriminating T. lymma individuals, where match scores below this value could be considered true-positive matches. The proportion of true-positive and false-positive identification rates were determined by analysing the matches from I3S that were below the aforementioned cut-off value. The true-positive rate represents the percentage of correct matches, while the false-positive rate represents the percentage of matches that were incorrect. Overall accuracy was assessed using the area under curve (AUC), where values close to one indicate higher accuracy of I3S software to correctly match images45. To determine the probability of I3S returning a ‘true-positive’ within the first five matched results against the query image, we used the I3S “Elaborate Evaluation” tool46.

Spot pattern stability was assessed by the ability of I3S to correctly match photographs of recaptured rays between the time of first, any subsequent, and final capture events, and was reported as the mean match score of all recaptured individuals. To account for potential changes through time, the relationship between spot pattern and disc width was explored using adapted methods and parameters outlined by Borsa et al.16,47 for a subset of 64 randomly selected individuals. The mean dorsal spot diameter was calculated in ImageJ48 using the known disc width of the individual to relatively measure the diameter of spots that were on the pectoral transect line in millimetres (i.e., mid-scapular point to the extremity of the right pectoral fin; Supplementary Fig. 8). The Pearson correlation method was used to measure a linear dependence between mean spot diameter and disc width and to plot a linear regression curve. A significance level of α = 0.05 was considered for all analyses. Normality and heteroscedasticity testing was conducted using Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s tests, respectively.
