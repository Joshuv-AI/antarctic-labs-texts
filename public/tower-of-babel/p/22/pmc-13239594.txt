Spatial variation and distribution of anaemia among children aged 6–59 months in Madagascar: a population-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Gebeyehu Lakew, Mathewos Mekonnen Gemmechu, Tiruye Menshaw Tiruneh, Yibeltal Assefa Atalay, Amlaku Nigusie Yirsaw, Eyob Ketema Bogale, Natnael Atnafu Gebeyehu, Tewodros Shitemaw Tekoye, Genanew Kassie Getahun, Kedir Seid, Abel Tibebu Goshu, Sintayehu Samuel Lorato, Mitiku Desalegn, Kelemu Abebe Gelaw, Yohannes Godie Ashebir, Olyad Kuma Getahun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo describe the prevalence and spatial distribution of anaemia among children aged 6–59 months in Madagascar and to explore individual-level and community-level correlates using data from the 2021 Madagascar Demographic and Health Survey.DesignA population-based cross-sectional study using secondary data from the 2021 Madagascar Demographic and Health Survey. Spatial statistical methods were used to assess geographic clustering and hotspot areas of anaemia.SettingThe study was conducted across community and household levels throughout Madagascar, covering both rural and urban populations within the primary and secondary healthcare delivery contexts.ParticipantsA weighted sample of 10 683 children aged 6–59 months was included. Sampling followed demographic health survey procedures, and only children with complete haemoglobin data were analysed.InterventionsNo interventions were applied.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe primary outcome was anaemia, defined as haemoglobin <11 g/dL. Spatial autocorrelation and hotspot analyses identified geographical clustering patterns.ResultsThe overall point prevalence of anaemia was 47% (95% CI 45.60 to 48.36), showing significant spatial clustering (Global Moran’s I=0.136, p<0.001), with hotspots mainly in the central and southeastern regions. Differences in anaemia prevalence were observed across population subgroups, including child age, maternal education, household wealth, place of residence and nutritional status.ConclusionsAnaemia among Malagasy children remains a major public health concern and shows clear geographic variation. The findings describe important differences in prevalence across regions and population subgroups, providing useful evidence for public health planning and for generating hypotheses for future analytical research.

Background

Anaemia is reduced haemoglobin or red blood cells, which carry oxygen to body tissues.1 The WHO defines anaemia in children under five as having a haemoglobin level of less than 11 g/dL.2 If the prevalence of anaemia in any group is 40% or more, it is considered a severe public health worry; if it is between 20% and 40%, it is considered a significant public health problem; and if it is between 5% and 20%, it is considered a mild public health problem.3 Children are particularly susceptible to anaemia at this critical developmental stage. The primary cause of anaemia in children under five is iron deficiency, with a lesser percentage linked to deficits in other micronutrients such as folate, vitamin A and vitamin B12.4 5

Anaemia in children under five is a major public health concern on a global scale. The WHO reported that 40% of children younger than five were anaemic.6 The frequency of anaemia in children under five was 39.8% worldwide in 2019. The prevalence of anaemia in children under five has remained constant since 2010, despite a gradual decline from 48.0% to 39.8% over time.7 Although the frequency varies by nation, South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) have the greatest percentages. In particular, the African region had the greatest records, with 60.2%.5 8

Anaemia is a major marker of inadequate nutrition and health and has a substantial influence on socioeconomic development. Children’s physical development, concentration, memory, learning, immunity and academic performance are all adversely affected by anaemia.9 10 11 12 Furthermore, a weakened immune system linked to childhood anaemia puts children at risk for serious morbidity and death.3

There are several reasons for anaemia, but in SSA, infectious diseases and dietary deficiencies are the main culprits.13 14 15

Previous studies have shown that a number of factors influence the development of anaemia in children under five.16 17 Significant determinants of anaemia in children under five included inadequate intake of iron and vitamin A, increased susceptibility to infection, sociodemographic characteristics of households such as region, mother’s employment status, wealth index, water source and maternal anaemia status, and child characteristics such as child age, birth order, nutritional status and birth size.3 18 19

Despite significant global advancements in community socioeconomic and health status, Sub-Saharan nations nevertheless have a high under-five mortality rate.20 21 22 In Africa, anaemia affects roughly 67.6% of children under five, accounting for 15%–18% of under-five deaths.22 23 Generating sufficient knowledge on the burden of anaemia and its drivers is essential for the development of timely interventions in anaemia prevention and treatment, which is vital to meet the Sustainable Development Goal-2030 targets of reducing child mortality.

A recent secondary analysis of the Madagascar Demographic and Health Survey 2021 (MDHS) found that among 5048 children aged 6–59 months, approximately 47% were anaemic.24 Determinants identified in this context include younger child age, maternal anaemia, maternal underweight, low maternal education, recent history of fever or diarrhoea and high birth order.25 Importantly, there is substantial spatial heterogeneity, with regional prevalence among provinces ranging from 27% in Haute Matsiatra to 62% in Atsimo Atsinanana.24 26

Despite nutritional and infection-control programmes (iron supplementation, malaria prevention, deworming) in Madagascar, the persistent high prevalence and spatial clustering suggest that these interventions may not be reaching all high-risk populations or may require adaptation to local conditions, such as rural versus urban differences.27 28 29 Unlike previous studies, this study uses the most recent national DHS data to examine both individual-level and community-level determinants as well as spatial clustering, providing updated evidence for targeted interventions. Understanding the spatial distribution and multilevel determinants among children aged 6–59 months is therefore critical to targeting resources and tailoring effective public health strategies.30

Thus, the purpose of this study was to describe the prevalence and spatial distribution of anaemia in children aged from 6 to 59 months in Madagascar by using the most recent DHS data.

Methods

Study area, data source and study period

Data from the MDHS, which were carried out between March and July of 2021, were used in this investigation. The DHS is a thorough and nationally representative survey that collects vital data on socioeconomic, population and health indices.

The administrative organisation of Madagascar is divided into layers that are arranged hierarchically. Since 2009, Madagascar has been organised into 22 regions, which are the primary administrative divisions. The second level of government is divided into 114 districts. Communities are a further division of districts. As a representative of the national government, each district has a chief of district (perfect). The communes (kaominina), which can be either rural or urban, are the smallest administrative divisions. In Madagascar, there are 1549 communes. Every commune has an elected communal council and is led by a mayor.

Sampling technique and study population

The Enumeration regions created during the 2018 Population and Housing Census of Madagascar served as the basic sampling frame for the MDHS’s well-structured sampling strategy.

By using the thorough and current data collected during the national census, this method guarantees the representativeness of the survey results. In order to obtain a balanced and varied representation of both urban and rural populations, the sampling process used a two-stage procedure. A total of 500 enumeration areas were carefully chosen in the first round, with 388 areas located in rural areas and 112 areas in urban areas.31 In the second step of the sampling procedure, 26 families were systematically selected from each enumeration area, yielding 13 000 households. The final sample size for the current study consisted of 10 683 children aged 6–59 months. This sample size refers to the weighted population used to ensure national representativeness.

Study variables

Dependent variable

Anemia

Parents or other responsible caregivers gave their agreement for the collection of blood samples from children aged 6 to 59 months. Blood samples were obtained in a microcuvette from a drop of blood extracted from a finger prick (or, in the case of children aged 6–11 months, a heel prick). A portable HemoCue analyser that ran on batteries was used to perform haemoglobin tests on-site. Before being categorised as anaemia, the haemoglobin levels measured with the HemoCue device were corrected for altitude. Haemoglobin levels between 10.0 and 10.9 g/dL are considered mild, those between 7.0 and 9.9 g/dL are considered moderate, and those below 7.0 g/dL are considered severe anaemia, according to the WHO haemoglobin level cut-off values. Consequently, a haemoglobin level below 11 g/dL of blood is considered anaemic; otherwise, it is normal.32

Independent variables

Factors considered at the individual level for their association with anaemia in Madagascar included the child’s age, child’s sex, maternal age, maternal educational status, breastfeeding status, marital status, sex of the household head, maternal working status, number of under-five children, stunting, wasting, underweight, place of residence, household wealth status, size of child and media exposure. Additionally, community-level factors such as community-level education and community-level poverty were also taken into account.

Operational definition

Media exposure

This study created a single category, ‘exposed to media’, to capture a wider range of participants. Individuals who reported using any of the three media sources—television, radio or newspapers—were placed in this group. By using this method, researchers can avoid the possible disadvantage of losing participants if they examine each media source independently. Some topics, for instance, might only be pertinent to particular subgroups, such as television for people with electricity or newspapers for women with education. The study guarantees a more comprehensive analysis by merging the data.

Birth size

Children who weigh less than 2.5 kg at birth, or who are described as ‘very small’ or ‘smaller than average’, are classified as having low birth weights; otherwise, they are considered normal. The MDHS sources state that the mother’s report or the existence of written documents, if any, were the basis for determining birth size (birth weight).33 34 35

Stunting

Moderately stunted was defined as height-for-age less than minus 2SD (−2SD) from the reference population’s median, and seriously stunted as being below −3SD from the reference population’s median.36 37 38

Underweight

Severe underweight was defined as weight-for-age below −3SD from the reference population’s median, and moderate underweight as weight-for-age less than −2SD from the reference population’s median.38 39

Wasted

Weight-for-height less than −2SD from the median of the reference population was regarded as moderately wasted, while below −3SD from the median of the reference population was considered severely wasted. These anthropometric indicators were measured based on the WHO growth standard.40 41

Wealth index

An indicator of social inequality, the wealth index gauges the socioeconomic standing of households. Using principal component analysis, as performed by the MDHS, the households were assigned scores based on their assets (television, bicycle/car, agricultural land size, number of livestock) and their dwelling characteristics (drinking water sources, sanitary facilities and building materials). The PCA method accounts for urban–rural differences in asset ownership and housing characteristics, so items that are rare in certain areas (eg, cars in rural areas, livestock in urban areas) are appropriately weighted in the analysis. The scores were then combined into five wealth quintiles (poorest, poorer, medium, richer and richest), each of which made up 20% of the population.34 35 42 43 44

Community level of poverty

We examined the percentage of households classified as ‘poorest’ or ‘poorer’ according to a wealth index in order to quantify community poverty. This wealth index takes into account things like facilities and household assets. Higher percentages of these lower-income households were associated with communities that were categorised as having a ‘low poverty level’. Communities with more households that were affluent and fell beyond the median wealth index, on the other hand, were deemed to have a ‘high poverty level’. The median, or middle point, was used as the dividing line because of the unequal distribution of wealth within communities. We classified community education levels using a similar methodology.

Community-level educational status

The survey also looked into the educational attainment of local populations. The percentage of moms or caregivers with at least an elementary education was examined. Communities were categorised as having a ‘high community level of educational status’ if a greater percentage of mothers or caregivers had completed primary school or above. Communities that had a high proportion of mothers or caregivers without a primary education, on the other hand, were deemed to have a ‘low community level of educational status’. In essence, this method assesses the general level of education in a given population.45 46

Data management and analysis

All analyses were conducted with a descriptive objective. The prevalence of anaemia was estimated overall and across population subgroups, including child, maternal, household and community characteristics. Results are presented using weighted proportions and corresponding 95% CIs.

Sampling weights provided by the DHS were applied to account for the complex survey design, including stratification and clustering, ensuring national representativeness.

Data cleaning and management were performed using Microsoft Excel, and statistical analyses were conducted using STATA V.17. Missing data were assessed for key variables, and a complete-case analysis approach was applied, excluding observations with missing values on selected variables.

No multivariable modelling was performed, as the aim of this study was to describe the distribution of anaemia rather than to estimate causal or predictive relationships.

Spatial analysis

Whether patterns of childhood anaemia were dispersed, clustered or randomly distributed across the study area is determined by the spatial autocorrelation (Global Moran’s I) statistic. Using ArcGIS V.10.7.1, the Global Moran’s-I (Moran’s-I) was used to measure the global spatial autocorrelation to determine whether geographical clustering and variability were present. Moran’s I was calculated to assess spatial autocorrelation of childhood anaemia across Madagascar. A positive Moran’s I indicates clustering of similar values, whereas a negative Moran’s I indicates dispersion.47 48

Hot spot analysis (Getis-Ord Gi* statistic)

The statistically significant hot spot and cold spot regions were determined using the local Getis-Ord spatial statistical technique. On a map, a hot spot is an area where there is a high prevalence of anaemia that clusters together, while a cold spot is an area where there is a low frequency of anaemia that clusters together.49 50 51 52

Spatial interpolation

From unobserved enumeration sites in Madagascar, a high frequency of anaemia was predicted using the Kriging interpolation approach. Since the ordinary Kriging spatial interpolation approach had a lower mean square error and residual than the other interpolation techniques, it was used for this investigation to predict childhood anaemia in Madagascar’s unobserved areas.53

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Results

Characteristics of the study population

About half were male and the majority lived in rural areas. Most mothers were 25–34 years old, married and had primary or no formal education. More than half of households were in the poor wealth categories, and most were male-headed. Over half of mothers lacked media exposure, and two-thirds lived in communities with high poverty levels (online supplemental file 2).

Prevalence of anaemia among children aged from 6 to 59 months in Madagascar

The point prevalence of childhood anaemia among children aged from 6 to 59 months old in Madagascar was 47% (95% CI 45.60 to 48.36). The prevalence of anaemia was calculated as a point prevalence, representing the proportion of children with anaemia at the time of the 2021 MDHS.

Discussion

The prevalence of anaemia among children aged from 6 to 59 months in Madagascar was 47% (95% CI 45.60 to 48.36). This figure is consistent with findings from other regions of Sub-Saharan Africa, where anaemia prevalence often exceeds 40% among children under five.54 In comparison, some studies in South Asia, Mozambique, Togo, Ghana, Zanzibar, Nigeria, Ethiopia and sub-Saharan African studies report high rates, with certain areas exceeding 60%.35561 Conversely, countries with robust public health interventions have reported lower prevalence rates, including a systematic and meta-analysis study conducted in 2022 with a prevalence of 16.42%,62 indicating that targeted strategies can effectively reduce anaemia.63

Spatial analysis revealed significant clustering of childhood anaemia (Global Moran’s I=0.136, p<0.001), indicating that anaemia is not randomly distributed across Madagascar. Hotspot analysis further identified high-risk areas predominantly in the central and southern regions, while lower-risk areas were observed in the northern and northwestern parts of the country. These findings suggest that geographic and contextual factors contribute to variations in anaemia distribution and highlight the importance of location-specific interventions.

The interpolation analysis further demonstrated marked geographic disparities in predicted anaemia prevalence, with higher concentrations in southern regions and lower levels in the northwest. This spatial heterogeneity may reflect differences in access to health services, food security and socioeconomic conditions across regions.

Descriptive patterns from the analysis indicated that anaemia was more common among younger children, children from poorer households, children of mothers with lower educational attainment and those from urban areas. Higher burden was also observed among underweight children and those from female-headed households. At the community level, children living in areas with higher maternal education showed lower levels of anaemia. These patterns are consistent with established evidence that child nutrition and maternal education are key drivers of child health outcomes.

The findings suggest that anaemia in Madagascar is a geographically clustered and socially patterned public health problem. The concentration of high-risk areas indicates that uniform national strategies may be less effective than geographically targeted interventions.

Implications and future research

The spatial clustering of anaemia highlights the need for geographically targeted nutrition and child health interventions, particularly in high-burden regions identified in this study. Strengthening maternal education, improving household food security and expanding child nutrition programmes may contribute to reducing anaemia burden.

Future research should incorporate dietary intake, infection status, parasitic diseases and micronutrient biomarkers to better understand the underlying biological and environmental drivers of anaemia in Madagascar.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study has several strengths. It used a large, nationally representative sample from the 2021 MDHS, which enhances the generalisability of the findings. The application of spatial analysis techniques allowed for the identification of geographic clustering and high-risk areas of childhood anaemia, providing valuable evidence for geographically targeted public health interventions. In addition, the use of standardised DHS measurement procedures, including haemoglobin assessment and sampling weights, improves the reliability and comparability of the findings.

However, the study also has limitations. Due to its cross-sectional design, the findings describe associations and spatial patterns at a single point in time and cannot be used to infer temporal relationships. Important variables such as dietary intake, parasitic infections and micronutrient status were not available in the dataset, which limits the ability to explore underlying biological and behavioural contributors to anaemia. In addition, some information was based on caregiver reports, which may introduce recall bias. Finally, because the analysis focused on descriptive and spatial approaches, it does not provide adjusted estimates of individual-level and community-level determinants.

Conclusion

Anaemia among children aged 6–59 months in Madagascar remains a major public health problem, with a high prevalence of 47% demonstrating clear spatial clustering. High-risk areas were identified in the central and southern regions, while lower prevalence was observed in the northern and northwestern parts of the country. The findings also show that anaemia is more common among younger children, children from poorer households and those with lower maternal educational background, highlighting important population subgroups that may require increased attention.

These findings provide useful evidence for geographic prioritisation of child health and nutrition interventions in Madagascar. Strengthening region-specific nutrition programmes and improving access to child health services in high-burden areas may contribute to reducing the overall burden of childhood anaemia.

Further research incorporating dietary intake, infection status and micronutrient biomarkers is needed to better understand the underlying drivers of anaemia and to support more targeted public health responses.
