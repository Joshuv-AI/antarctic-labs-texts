Sex-related differences in clinical characteristics and in-hospital outcomes of patients in acute type A aortic dissection
Authors: Fen Lin, Qiong Pan, Yaqin Chen, Yanchun Peng, Fei Jiang, Hong Ni, Lingyu Lin, Liangwan Chen, Yanjuan Lin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe aim of this study was to investigate sex-related differences in the clinical characteristics and hospital outcomes of patients undergoing surgery for acute type A aortic dissection (AAAD).MethodsThis study was a retrospective study. Patients who underwent surgery for AAAD at the Department of Cardiac Surgery, Fujian Medical University Union Hospital, from January 2014 to March 2023 were consecutively included. Data was extracted from electronic medical records. The primary outcome measure was in-hospital mortality, and secondary outcome measures included new-onset postoperative arrhythmia (POA), acute kidney injury (AKI), hepatic dysfunction, neurological complications, gastrointestinal hemorrhage, ICU length of stay, and hospital length of stay. Patients were divided into two groups based on sex, and data analysis was performed using SPSS 25.0 software.ResultsA total of 1137 subjects were included, with 863 males (75.9%) and 274 females (24.1%). There were statistically significant differences in age and BMI between the two groups (P < 0.05). There was no statistically significant difference in the incidence of pain at the onset between the two groups, but chest tightness in females was higher than in males (22.6% vs. 13.8%). Regarding primary outcomes, the in-hospital mortality rate was 11.1% for males and 10.6% for females (P = 0.803). There were no statistically significant differences between the groups in ICU days, length of hospitalization, neurological complications, or liver dysfunction (P > 0.05). The rate of POA in females was 4.7%, higher than in males (2.2%), but AKI and gastrointestinal hemorrhage were both higher in males than in females (P < 0.05). Multivariate analysis showed that age, white blood cell (WBC) counts, lactic acid, operation duration and prolonged mechanical ventilation (PMV) increased the risk of in-hospital mortality in male patients. Hypertension, WBC counts, lactic acid, and PMV increased the risk of in-hospital mortality in female patients.ConclusionDespite significant baseline characteristic differences between male and female AAAD patients, there were no significant differences in onset symptoms. The in-hospital mortality rates were similar between male and female patients, but the risk factors for in-hospital mortality differed.Keywords: Aortic dissection, Acute disease, Sex, Clinical characteristics, Hospital mortality, Critical care outcomes

Introduction

Acute type A aortic dissection (AAAD) is a severe cardiovascular disease with a rising incidence. However, determining the true frequency of AAAD is challenging due to pre-hospital mortality and potential misdiagnoses [1]. Patients with this condition require urgent surgery, as the mortality rate for those not undergoing surgery increases by 1–2% per hour within the first 48 h [2, 3]. In recent decades, the management of aortic dissection (AD) patients has been greatly improved, but compared with other cardiovascular diseases, the postoperative mortality rate is still higher, 10.1%~30% of patients die during hospitalization, which imposes a significant burden on patients’ families and society [1, 4, 5]. It is well known that the incidence of AD is higher in males than females, with a ratio of 1.5 ~ 2:1. However, female representation in aortic dissection studies is often insufficient [6]. Current cardiovascular guidelines also indicate that existing research has not adequately addressed sex-related issues, especially regarding the relationship between sex and AAAD [7, 8].

In recent years, the potential differences in clinical characteristics and prognosis of AAAD between sex have become a prominent area of research. However, the findings in this area are inconsistent and even contradictory [6, 8]. Several studies have reported conflicting age of onset and prognosis in both male and female patients [9–12]. Furthermore, the European Society of Cardiology guidelines emphasize the need for additional epidemiological research on AD to analyze outcomes in different treatment groups and evaluate potential sex differences [13]. Therefore, the aim of this study was to investigate sex-related differences in the clinical characteristics and hospital outcomes of patients undergoing surgery for AAAD, and to stratify the analysis based on sex and explore the risk factors for sex-related mortality.

Methods

This study was a single-center, retrospective study. Patients who underwent surgery for AAAD at the Department of Cardiac Surgery, Fujian Medical University Union Hospital, from January 2014 to March 2023 were consecutively included. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) confirmed type A AD by computed tomography angiography, with symptom onset within 14 days; (2) age ≥ 18 years. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) unclear record of symptom onset time; (2) traumatic dissection; (3) health check revealed asymptomatic aortic dissection; (4) history of malignant tumors; (5) coronary artery dissection. This study adhered to the Helsinki Declaration and received approval from the Ethics Committee of Fujian Medical University Union Hospital (2020KY082).

All operations were performed under intravenous and inhalation anesthesia combined with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB). CPB was established by intubation of the right femoral artery and right axillary artery. A mid-sternal incision is made, and while gradually cooling the body, the heart is fully exposed and the innominate artery and left carotid artery are freed. Reduce nasopharyngeal temperature, block the ascending aorta, and perfuse cardiac arrest fluid through the left and right coronary artery openings to protect the myocardium. After cardiac arrest, the lesions at the base of the aorta, aortic sinuses, aortic valve, and coronary artery openings are explored and treated accordingly. When the nasal temperature reaches 25 ℃, the perfusion flow of the aorta is reduced, and CPB is stopped. Unilateral anterograde cerebral perfusion is performed through the right axillary artery (or right subclavian artery) cannula. A perfusion catheter may be inserted into the left carotid artery during the anastomosis process for bilateral antegrade cerebral perfusion. The aortic arch is explored and treated, and any gas in the left heart and aorta is thoroughly removed. The occlusion clamps are released, and systemic CPB is restored, followed by rewarming. After cardiac resuscitation, CPB is discontinued and the surgery is completed.

A researcher extracts relevant patient data from electronic medical records. The general information includes the patient’s age, sex, height, weight, smoking and drinking history, and past medical history (Marfan syndrome, history of cardiac surgery, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, coronary heart disease). Clinical characteristics at symptom onset were recorded, including chest tightness, headache, chest pain, chest and back pain, lumbago and back pain, abdominal pain, limb numbness, limb weakness, dyspnea, syncope. The heart rate, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), and laboratory indicators (prothrombin time [PT], international standard ratio [INR], urea, creatinine, total protein, albumin, globulin, white blood cell [WBC], neutrophil, lymphocyte, monocyte, red blood cell [RBC], hemoglobin, platelet, platelet distribution width [PDW]) measured upon admission were documented. Record computed tomography results showing vascular involvement. Intraoperative data included operation duration, cardiopulmonary bypass time (CPB), aortic occlusion time, etc.

The primary endpoint of this study was patient discharge or in-hospital death, and the secondary outcomes included postoperative complications such as AKI, hepatic dysfunction, gastrointestinal hemorrhage, new-onset postoperative arrhythmia (POA), and neurological complications (postoperative stroke, epilepsy). AKI was defined as an absolute increase in serum creatinine ≥ 26.5 umol/L or an increase in serum creatinine to 1.5 times the baseline within 7 days [14]. Hepatic dysfunction was defined as a peak total bilirubin level > 7 mg/dl in the ICU after surgery [15]. Gastrointestinal hemorrhage was defined as the presence of obvious symptoms such as hematemesis, melena, or hematochezia. The POA, including sinus arrhythmias, ventricular arrhythmias (supraventricular tachycardia, ventricular tachycardia, ventricular fibrillation), atrial arrhythmias (premature, atrial flutter, atrial fibrillation), and atrioventricular block, were assessed by experienced cardiac surgeons based on postoperative ECG patterns [16].

Data analysis was performed using SPSS 25.0 statistical software. We divided patients into two groups based on sex and described baseline characteristics of AAAD patients in both groups, including age, BMI, smoking, and drinking. Medical history, including Marfan syndrome, history of cardiac surgery, hypertension, etc. Symptoms at onset, including chest tightness, headache, chest pain, etc. Heart rate, blood pressure, first laboratory test results (prothrombin time, international standard ratio, urea, etc.), and the extent of blood vessels involved in the dissection at admission. Intraoperative data, in-hospital mortality, ICU length of stay, hospital length of stay, and postoperative complications were also compared. Univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to assess the association and risk of in-hospital mortality for sex-specific variables. Measurement data conforming to normal distribution and homogeneous variance were expressed as (‾χ ± s), and a t-test was adopted. Non-normally distributed measurement data were expressed as median (interquartile range) and analyzed using the rank-sum test. Categorical data were presented as percentages and analyzed using the χ2 test. Results from the logistic regression analysis were reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). A significance level of P < 0.05 was used for all statistical analyses.

Results

The patient inclusion flow chart is shown in Fig. 1. A total of 1137 subjects were analyzed, comprising 863 males (75.9%) and 274 females (24.1%). The mean age for males was (51.98 ± 11.56) years with a mean BMI of (24.52 ± 3.65) kg/m2. Females had a mean age of (55.16 ± 12.47) years and a mean BMI of (23.39 ± 3.33) kg/m2. Statistically significant differences in age and BMI were observed between the two groups (P < 0.05). Smoking and drinking were more prevalent in males, with 474 (54.9%) and 367 (42.5%) subjects reporting these habits, respectively. There were no significant differences in the incidence of headaches and chest pain between the two groups (P > 0.05). However, females reported experiencing more chest tightness than males, and this difference was statistically significant (P < 0.05). Discussion

In this study, we compared the onset symptoms and clinical outcomes of AAAD patients of different sex. In our study, 24.1% of patients were female, with a male-to-female ratio of approximately 3.15:1, consistent with previous reported results [17, 18]. Research has indicated that over 50% of females experience AAAD after the age of 70, and the diagnosis is often delayed due to the lack of specific symptoms and signs, which may be a reason for the lower incidence rate [19, 20]. Our research results differ from those of other countries in terms of age. The results from the German Registry for AAAD showed that the age of males was (59.2 ± 13.3) years old and females was (65.5 ± 12.7) years old, P < 0.001 [21]. In the Nordic Consortium for AAAD, the age of males was (60 ± 12) years and that of females was (65 ± 11) years [11]. However, a study by Liu et al. [9, 21] on 884 patients with AD showed no statistically significant difference in age between them. In our study, males were (51.98 ± 11.56) years old and females were (55.16 ± 12.47) years old (P < 0.001), which is 10 years younger than other regions [11]. Multiple studies have showed that ovarian estrogen has a protective effect on the cardiovascular system in premenopausal female, and the decrease in estrogen after menopause may promote the occurrence of AAAD, which is one reason why females have a later age of onset compared to males [22, 23]. At the same time, estrogen has a protective effect on blood vessels, and female develop hypertension at a later age, which is one of the main risk factors for AAAD [24]. However, the specific pathological and physiological mechanisms underlying the significant age difference between females and males at the onset of AAAD are still to be explored.

Pain is the main symptom of AAAD, and the location of pain and other associated symptoms reflect the initial site of the intimal tear, which may change as the dissection extends along the aorta or involves other arteries or organ systems. A study by Yan Jie Liu et al. [9] showed that the incidence of chest pain in male patients was 82.2% and 78.7% in females. However, the incidence of back pain in female patients was significantly higher than that in males (P = 0.06), and the incidence of abdominal pain in males was significantly higher than that in females (P = 0.07). In this study, it was found that both male and female patients had the highest incidence of chest and back pain among self-reported symptoms, but the difference between different sex in multiple pain sites was not statistically significant, which was consistent with the findings of Nienaber et al. [20]. The incidence of females feeling chest tightness was much higher than that of males (P < 0.01). Females in menopause and low DBP exacerbate the insufficient blood supply to the coronary and aortic arteries [25, 26]. Numbness or weakness in the limbs is also noteworthy. The study’s results revealed a higher incidence of limb weakness in females, likely due to the impact of AD hematoma on spinal artery perfusion, leading to limb ischemia. Although these data are not statistically significant, they may contribute to a better understanding of postoperative care pathways for patients of different sex, thereby improving the current management model for AAAD patients. In clinicopathological baseline data, we noted that male patients had poorer outcomes than female patients, including urea, creatinine, and WBC counts upon admission. Due to the high proportion of smoking and drinking in male patients, it may not only lead to the production of pro-inflammatory factors in the body, promote inflammation, but also increase anti-inflammatory cells in the body [27, 28].

Researches have showed that females exhibit poorer short-term postoperative outcomes in various cardiovascular surgical procedures, such as aortic surgery and coronary artery bypass grafting [29, 30]. A recent report evaluated 2823 AAAD patients, including 969 females, and despite similar dissection involvement, surgical interventions, and hemodynamics, females experienced worse early surgical outcomes and higher in-hospital mortality compared to males [10]. Similarly, the Oxford vascular study demonstrated that females had higher 30-day and 5-year mortality rates compared to males [31] Recent observations suggest that the treatment course for AAAD is more aggressive in females. Although the time from symptom onset to surgery was relatively short, females have a higher frequency of death before reaching the operating room, with more females in a critical condition. The 30-day mortality rate (25.4% vs. 13.8%, P = 0.005), postoperative nerve injury rate (23.8% vs. 10.2%, P = 0.023), and long-term mortality risk are significantly higher in females than in males [32]. Conversely, several recent studies have shown no association between females and poorer early and long-term outcomes [12, 33–36], which our results also confirm. Although male patients had higher in-hospital mortality than females (11.1% vs. 10.6%), the difference was not statistically significant. In our study, there were no differences in postoperative neurological complications between two groups, but females had a higher incidence of POA, while males had higher rates of gastrointestinal hemorrhage and AKI.

Currently, there is limited research on stratifying the risk factors for AAAD mortality by sex. Francica et al. [33] found that chronic renal failure and CPB time were independent risk factors for 30-day in-hospital mortality in male AAAD patients, while LVEF level was a protective factor (OR = 0.9, P = 0.013), age and CPB time were independent risk factors for 30-day in-hospital mortality in female AAD patients. Nappi et al. [19] found that estimated glomerular filtration rate, lactic acid, and mesenteric ischemia increased the risk of death in female AAAD patients. Our study found that age, smoking, WBC count, lactic acid, operation duration, and PMV increased the risk of in-hospital mortality in male AAAD patients, while hypertension, WBC count, lactic acid, and PMV increased the risk of in-hospital mortality in female AAAD patients. A recent meta-analysis of sex-specific differences in thoracic aortic aneurysm repair found that female patients had higher 30-day mortality rates, which were independent of age [36], similar to our study results. Chinese females with AAD are in the menopausal transition phase, during which their estrogen antioxidants decrease, making them more susceptible to metabolic syndrome, insulin resistance, vascular aging, decreased vasodilation, and elevated blood pressure, thereby increasing the risk of cardiovascular diseases and death [37, 38]. Understanding the underlying mechanisms, biology, and hormonal factors of sex differences observed in AAAD, and developing sex-specific treatment strategies to better control the disease, will be crucial. Undoubtedly, further research is needed to better understand these differences and develop treatment methods that meet the needs of both sexes.

There are several limitations to this study. Firstly, it is a retrospective study conducted at a single center, and our findings should be confirmed through multi-center studies. Secondly, the neurological complications examined in this study are limited to stroke and epilepsy, with no data available on the incidence of delirium. Lastly, while potential confounding factors were taken into account and included in our multivariate model, there is still a possibility of residual or unknown confounding factors.

Conclusion

There is currently a lack of comprehensive understanding of sex differences in AAAD. Females are older than males at the time of diagnosis, but the symptoms at the onset of the disease are not significantly different between them. There was no significant difference in hospital mortality between the two sexes. Age and surgical duration can increase the risk of hospital death in males with aortic dissection. Hypertension increases the risk of hospital death in female AAAD patients. High WBC counts, high lactate levels, and PMV time are common independent risk factors for hospital death in both sexes. It should be emphasized that a better understanding of sex differences in clinical manifestations and treatment of AAAD patients may improve diagnostic accuracy and short-term and long-term prognosis.
