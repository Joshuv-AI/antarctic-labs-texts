Clinical risk factors for recurrence in adult-onset myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody-associated optic neuritis
Authors: Shanshan Cao, Tengyun Wu, Chunyan Pan, Shihui Wei, Huanfen Zhou, Yong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMyelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody-associated optic neuritis (MOG-ON) is a subtype of demyelinating optic neuritis (ON) characterized by a considerable risk of relapse; however, the demographic and clinical factors associated with recurrence remain poorly defined, posing ongoing challenges for patient management.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on adult-onset MOG-ON patients diagnosed in the Ophthalmology Department of the Chinese People’s Liberation Army General Hospital (PLAGH) from January 2019 to January 2024. Patients were divided into two groups based on their experience of a relapse course: the relapsing group and the monophasic group. Multivariate analysis was performed to examine the effects of various clinical factors on the risk of recurrence.ResultsAmong 126 screened participants, 56 were excluded. A total of 70 patients (median [IQR] age at onset, 35.50 [30.00, 48.75] years; 46 females [65.71%]) were included. During a median follow-up of 31.50 (IQR 21.25–52.75) months, disease relapse occurred in 54.29% (38/70) of patients. Multivariate analysis revealed that being female significantly elevated recurrence risk (hazard ratio [HR] 3.92, 95% CI 1.63–9.42, p = 0.002), while administration of immunosuppressive maintenance therapy after the first episode was associated with a lower likelihood of recurrence (HR 0.30, 95% CI 0.10–0.87, p = 0.026). At the final follow-up, the relapsing group had significantly worse visual outcomes compared with the monophasic group (median [IQR] VA, 0.40 [0.16–0.82] logMAR vs. 0.22 [0.10–0.40] logMAR; p = 0.012).ConclusionIn adult-onset MOG-ON, female sex is associated with an increased risk of recurrence, whereas early maintenance immunosuppressive therapy is associated with a lower recurrence risk. Furthermore, patients with a relapsing disease course exhibit poorer visual outcomes at follow-up compared with those with a monophasic course.Keywords: Myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody, Optic neuritis, Relapse, Clinical characteristics

Introduction

Myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody-associated disease (MOGAD) is an inflammatory demyelinating disorder of the central nervous system (CNS) triggered by MOG-IgG [1, 2]. Most individuals with MOGAD experience recurrent demyelinating attacks, with optic neuritis (ON) being the most commonly observed condition in adults [3–5]. MOG antibody-associated optic neuritis (MOG-ON) typically manifests with classic acute ON symptoms, including eye pain and visual impairment [6, 7].

Initially, MOG antibodies were mainly identified in patients with monophasic disease and favorable prognosis [8, 9]. However, recent studies have reported a higher proportion of patients with relapsing courses leading to permanent disability [6, 10]. Additionally, 80% of MOGAD patients with a history of ON experienced recurrence [4]. This high recurrence rate not only poses an ongoing threat to patients’ vision but also profoundly impacts their psychological well-being and quality of life.

Although high-dose corticosteroids are often administered for acute MOG-ON episodes, a consensus on the optimal long-term management strategy is lacking. This disagreement primarily arises from limited research on predictors of disease recurrence and the efficacy of medications in preventing relapses. Identifying key indicators early in the disease’s progression is essential—not only for forecasting the likelihood of recurrence but also for shaping treatment strategies over time and establishing a foundation for clinical studies testing new preventative therapies.

Current research findings on factors influencing MOG-ON recurrence remain inconclusive, as there are few studies dedicated to this topic, and most available data come from pediatric cohorts [11, 12]. This study aims to fill the gap in research on predictive factors for relapse in adult MOG-ON, with a particular focus on clinical, demographic, and treatment-related factors at disease onset, to support clinical decisions with scientific evidence.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective analysis was conducted at a single institution and examined the medical records and demographic information of adult patients diagnosed with MOG-ON. The study analyzed cases from the Ophthalmology Department of the Chinese People’s Liberation Army General Hospital (PLAGH) between January 2019 and January 2024. All patients underwent detection of serum MOG-IgG and AQP4-IgG using a fixed cell-based assay (CBA) method. Patients were classified into monophasic and relapsing groups based on disease recurrence. Baseline clinical data were compared between groups, and risk factors for MOG-ON relapse were examined using univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses. All enrolled patients underwent at least 12 months of follow-up, with clinical examinations serving as the source of follow-up data. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Chinese PLA General Hospital (S2026-404-01). All procedures were performed in accordance with the ethical standards set forth in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Diagnostic criteria

Inclusion criteria: (1) Patients diagnosed with ON, as established by the Optic Neuritis Treatment Trial (ONTT) [13] and 2022 diagnostic criteria [14]; (2) Age at onset ≥ 18 years; (3) Serum MOG-IgG antibody was positive; (4) Clinical diagnosis of MOG-ON without meeting the diagnostic criteria for multiple sclerosis (MS) or AQP4 antibody-positive neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD).

Exclusion criteria: (1) Undetermined AQP4/MOG antibody status; (2) Existing conditions such as glaucoma, anterior segment abnormalities, retinal/macular disorders, high myopia, severe refractive error or other ocular comorbidities; (3) Non-inflammatory optic neuropathies of other etiologies (e.g., ischemic, genetic, toxic, infectious, metabolic, infiltrative, or compressive); (4) A history of prior ON or other chronic eye diseases before enrollment; (5) Insufficient or incomplete clinical documentation.

Study variables and definitions at baseline

In the neuro-ophthalmologic examination, the primary outcome, best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), was measured at 5 m using a standard logarithmic visual acuity(VA) scale. For individuals unable to read letters at 1 m, additional assessments included finger counting, hand movements, and light perception tests. BCVA was recorded using the Snellen VA chart and converted to logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (logMAR) values(logMAR=-logBCVA). Non-numerical visual acuity was converted to logMAR as follows: no light perception = 3.00, light perception = 2.70, hand motion = 2.00, counting fingers = 1.70 [15]. Non-numerical visual acuity was converted to logMAR equivalents based on published criteria; however, these converted values are approximate and lack a universal standard, which may introduce minor bias. The Worst VA at nadir corresponds to the worst VA experienced during the initial attack. Follow-up VA was defined as the BCVA assessed at least 12 months after the initial ON episode or during the latest follow-up visit.

Body Mass Index (BMI), a standard measure for weight classification, reflects the relationship between weight and height. According to the Chinese Guidelines for Prevention and Control of Overweight and Obesity in Adults [16], the BMI classification criteria for the Chinese population are as follows: Underweight (BMI < 18.5), Healthy (18.5 ≤ BMI < 24.0), Overweight (24.0 ≤ BMI < 28.0), and Obese (BMI ≥ 28.0).

Acute phase treatment: Initiated within 30 days of symptom onset and generally lasting under 90 days. These interventions included intravenous methylprednisolone (IVMP), intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) administration, and plasma exchange (PE). IVMP therapy is defined as the administration of ≥ 500 mg of methylprednisolone per day. We defined treatment administered within one week after the initial attack as early treatment.

IVMP therapy is defined as the administration of ≥ 500 mg of methylprednisolone per day for 3 to 5 consecutive days, followed by oral prednisone. Physicians select different immunosuppressive therapies according to the patient’s condition. Immunosuppressive maintenance therapy refers to long-term immunosuppressive treatment (lasting ≥ 90 days) utilizing immunosuppressants, such as mycophenolate mofetil (MMF), azathioprine, cyclophosphamide, IVIG, and anti-CD20 therapy (Rituximab).

Peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer (pRNFL) measurements were obtained using spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT; Carl Zeiss Meditec, Dublin, CA, USA). Scans were performed in optic disc mode over a 6 × 6 mm area, applying a circular scan of 3.4 mm in diameter centered on the disc. Automated segmentation of the pRNFL boundaries was conducted by the device’s integrated software, and values for both the mean thickness and each of the four quadrants (superior, inferior, nasal, and temporal) were documented.

Baseline optic nerve magnetic resonance imaging (MRI): Abnormalities on optic nerve MRI, the presence or absence of T2 lesions, and the presence or absence of gadolinium-enhancing lesions were evaluated based on the earliest MRI performed within three months of disease onset. Lesion locations were categorized into five areas: orbital, canalicular, intracranial, optic chiasm, and optic nerve sheath.

Clinical relapse is defined as the recurrence of clinical symptoms at least 30 days after the last attack with new or worsening optic nerve symptoms and no other etiology [2]. Types of attacks include unilateral ON and bilateral ON.

Laboratory examinations

CBA using indirect immunofluorescence was employed to detect serum MOG-IgG and AQP4-IgG. Serum samples were deemed seropositive if the titer was 1:32 or higher. When the initial titer was 1:10, samples were retested, and a retest titer of 1:10 or higher was considered positive. All patients in this study tested negative for serum AQP4 antibodies.

Serum samples from all patients were tested for autoantibodies and cytokines, including antinuclear antibodies (ANA), human leukocyte antigen B27 (HLA-B27), anti-thyroglobulin antibodies (ATAs), anti-thyroid peroxidase antibodies (anti-TPOAb), Sjögren’s syndrome-related antigens A (SSA) and B (SSB), anticardiolipin antibodies (ACLs), anti-β2-glycoprotein I antibodies (anti-β2-GPI), anticentromere antibodies (ACA), antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), rheumatoid factor (RF), as well as interleukin-2 (IL-2), interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-8 (IL-8), interleukin-10 (IL-10), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α). All tests were performed at the Biomedical Research and Testing Center of the Chinese PLA General Hospital.

Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) variables were evaluated using data from lumbar puncture results in the acute onset of ON. The CSF analysis included assessments of white blood cell (WBC) count, protein concentration, ILs, and TNF-α levels.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and data processing were performed using SPSS 27.0 and R (version 4.2.1). To compare baseline characteristics between different phenotypes of the first attack, categorical variables were analyzed using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests. Continuous data are expressed as median (interquartile range, IQR), and between-group differences were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney test. For OCT pRNFL data, a Generalized Estimating Equation (GEE) model was used to compare differences between groups. A Cox proportional hazards regression model was used to identify factors associated with relapse. The time-to-event variable was defined as the interval from the onset of the first ON attack to the first confirmed relapse. Patients who remained relapse-free through the last follow-up were included as event-free observations in the survival analysis. Patients who did not receive immediate treatment within the first 30 days were assigned a value of 31 days [17]. Variables with a p-value < 0.10 in the univariate analysis were included in the subsequent multivariate Cox regression model. Variance inflation factor analysis showed no multicollinearity (all values < 10). All tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Of the 126 patients initially evaluated, 70 met the inclusion criteria (Fig. 1); 56 were excluded due to age at onset < 18 years (n = 39), incomplete medical history (n = 7), follow-up < 12 months (n = 9), or comorbid uveitis (n = 1). All enrolled patients presented with ON as the initial manifestation, and all relapses during follow-up consisted of recurrent ON episodes without other neurological syndromes, allowing focused analysis of recurrence predictors in MOG-ON.

Patients were categorized into relapsing (38/70, 54.29%) and monophasic (32/70, 45.71%) groups. The median (IQR) time to first recurrence in the relapsing group was 7.00 (4.00, 23.00) months. The relapsing group had a higher proportion of females than the monophasic group (84.21% vs. 43.75%; p < 0.001), whereas the median age at onset did not differ significantly between the two groups (37.50 vs. 34.00 years; p = 0.211).

Precipitating factors, including upper respiratory tract infections, vaccinations, sleep deprivation, and emotional fluctuations, were reported in 28 patients (40.00%). Optic disc edema at onset was present in 80 of 98 eyes (81.63%). No significant differences between groups were observed for optic disc edema, ocular pain, BMI, headache, MOG-IgG titer, autoimmune antibodies, cytokines, elevated CSF white blood cell count or protein levels, or IVMP use during the acute phase (all p > 0.05).

In contrast, patients in the monophasic group had a shorter interval to initial treatment (median 7.00 [IQR 4.75–10.00] vs. 12.50 [8.00–26.00] days; P < 0.001) and were more likely to receive immunosuppressive maintenance therapy after the first attack (17/32 [53.12%] vs. 4/38 [10.53%]; P < 0.001).

Univariate analysis of MOG-ON relapse factors

In univariate analysis, female sex was associated with a higher risk of relapse (HR 4.18, 95% CI 1.74–10.04; p = 0.001), whereas initiation of immunosuppressive maintenance therapy after the first episode was associated with a reduced risk (HR 0.26, 95% CI 0.09–0.73; p = 0.010). Other baseline variables, including MOG-IgG antibody titer, precipitating factors, laterality (unilateral vs. bilateral), ocular pain, optic disc edema, nadir logMAR, BMI, and time to treatment of the first ON attack, showed no significant association with relapse (Fig. 2A).

Multivariate analysis of MOG-ON relapse factors

In multivariate analysis, female sex was independently associated with an increased risk of recurrence (HR 3.92, 95% CI 1.63–9.42; p = 0.002), whereas initiation of immunosuppressive maintenance therapy before the second episode was independently associated with a reduced risk (HR 0.30, 95% CI 0.10–0.87; p = 0.026). Other baseline factors, including age, worst VA at first onset, presence of precipitating factors, and time to treatment of the first ON attack, were not significantly associated with recurrence (Fig. 2B).

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was used to estimate relapse-free survival in patients with MOG-ON, stratified by sex and by the use of immunosuppressive maintenance therapy after the first episode. Visual and structural outcomes of the optic nerve

The median worst VA at nadir was 1.70 (IQR, 1.00–2.30) overall, with no significant difference between groups (monophasic group: 1.70 [IQR, 0.85–2.30]; relapsing group: 1.70 [IQR, 1.50–2.15]; p = 0.632). At follow-up, VA differed between groups, with an overall median of 0.30 (IQR, 0.10–0.67); Monophasic group: 0.22 [IQR, 0.10–0.40]; Relapsing group: 0.40 [IQR, 0.16–0.82]; P = 0.012. A VA of 1.7 logMAR or worse was observed in 27 patients (62.79%) in the monophasic group and 40 patients (72.73%) in the relapsing group (P = 0.294). At subsequent follow-up, 15 participants (34.88%) from the Monophasic group and 36 participants (65.45%) from the Relapsing group exhibited VA worse than 0.3 logMAR (p = 0.003) (see Table 2).

A total of 98 eyes were included, of which 91 underwent final follow-up optic disc optical coherence tomography (OCT) examination. Among these, 40 (44.0%) were in the monophasic group and 51 (56.0%) in the relapsing group. At final follow-up, median average RNFL thickness was significantly higher in the monophasic group compared with the relapsing group (109.50 [IQR, 97.88–155.62] vs. 69.75 [IQR, 59.38–87.25]; P < 0.001). Similar differences were observed in the superior (144.00 [114.75–205.50] vs. 80.00 [63.50–107.50]), inferior (147.00 [118.00–214.25] vs. 91.00 [70.50–116.00]), nasal (82.00 [71.00–119.25] vs. 58.00 [53.00–67.50]) and temporal quadrants (76.50 [67.75–88.25] vs. 49.00 [40.00–57.50]); all P < 0.001.

All 70 patients (98 eyes) underwent MRI of the optic nerve. No significant between-group differences were observed in anatomical involvement: orbital segment (monophasic group: 90.70% vs. relapsing group: 85.45%; p = 0.432), canalicular segment (37.21% vs. 54.55%; p = 0.088), intracranial segment (13.95% vs. 10.91%; p = 0.648), optic chiasm (4.65% vs. 9.09%; p = 0.652), or perineural enhancement (30.23% vs. 38.18%; p = 0.412). Involvement of ≥ 2 sites did not differ between groups (37.21% vs. 50.91%; p = 0.176) (Table 2).

Discussion

This retrospective cohort study of 70 adult patients with MOG-ON investigated factors associated with relapse in adult-onset MOG-ON and compared long-term visual and structural outcomes between monophasic and relapsing phenotypes. While our group recently described the clinical features of MOG-IgG–associated optic perineuritis (OPN), a phenotype marked by inflammation of the optic nerve sheath [18], the present study focused on classic MOG-ON that affects the optic nerve parenchyma itself. Our analyses showed that female sex was independently associated with a higher risk of recurrence, whereas immunosuppressive maintenance therapy after the first attack was associated with a lower relapse risk. In addition, although visual acuity at the acute nadir was comparable between groups, patients with a relapsing course had worse long-term visual outcomes and thinner pRNFL at final follow-up. These observational findings supplement existing evidence regarding relapse-related factors in adult MOG-ON and may help inform future risk assessment in clinical practice.

Consistent with previous studies on MOG-ON [5, 19], the present study focused on adult patients and explored factors correlated with disease recurrence, as well as long-term visual differences between monophasic and relapsing disease courses. Prior studies have reported heterogeneous potential relapse indicators across the MOGAD [17, 20, 21]. Our study addresses this gap by focusing on an adult-only ON cohort, reducing phenotypic heterogeneity, and allowing a more focused assessment of factors associated with relapse.

The observed association between female sex and relapse is in line with previous reports suggesting a greater tendency toward recurrence in women with MOGAD [22, 23]. Rather than simply identifying sex as a statistical correlate, this finding may have potential clinical relevance for follow-up planning, as female patients may warrant closer monitoring for recurrent attacks. The mechanisms underlying this sex-specific predisposition remain incompletely understood, but several factors have been proposed, including hormonal influences, immunological differences, and genetic susceptibility. Fluctuations in estrogen levels may modulate immune activity and have been hypothesized to influence susceptibility to recurrent inflammatory events [24]. In addition, studies of CNS autoimmune diseases have shown that female patients often mount stronger peripheral immune responses and may have a more permeable blood–brain barrier, creating a permissive environment for autoreactive lymphocytes to infiltrate the CNS [25]. Together, these factors might contribute to the higher recurrence rate observed in female patients with MOG-ON.

We also found that maintenance immunosuppressive therapy after the first attack was associated with a lower risk of relapse. This observation is broadly consistent with recent observational studies suggesting that sustained immunosuppression may be linked to reduced relapse activity in MOG-ON [26, 27]. From a clinical perspective, this association may support consideration of maintenance therapy in selected patients perceived to be at higher risk of recurrence. However, the use of immunosuppressant maintenance therapy to prevent relapses after the first onset of MOG-ON is controversial [28, 29]. Some scholars contend that immunosuppressant maintenance therapy is unnecessary following the initial onset of MOG-ON, given that a substantial proportion of patients experience a monophasic course with favorable recovery outcomes [30]. Conversely, other emerging evidence indicates that early maintenance therapy following an initial MOGAD attack may be associated with fewer relapses and better long-term neurological outcomes [31]. While our data support that such treatment may be linked to fewer recurrences in high-risk individuals, the optimal timing, duration, and agent selection still require prospective validation.

Most baseline demographic and clinical factors, including age at onset, BMI, MOG-IgG titer, autoimmune antibodies, cytokines, and acute-phase IVMP treatment, were not associated with relapse risk in our cohort. This pattern may suggest that commonly available baseline markers have limited discriminatory value for relapse prediction in adult MOG-ON when used in isolation. Differences between our findings and prior reports may reflect variation in study populations, assay methods, relapse definitions, and treatment strategies. These considerations underscore the need for more robust and standardized prediction models integrating clinical, serological, and imaging markers.

Patients with relapsing disease showed worse visual recovery at final follow-up than those with monophasic disease. This finding suggests that relapse may have an adverse cumulative effect on visual prognosis, underscoring the importance of relapse prevention in preserving long-term visual function. Repeated inflammatory episodes may plausibly contribute to incomplete recovery through cumulative injury to the optic nerve, even when each individual attack appears to respond to acute treatment. This interpretation is consistent with prior studies in MOG-ON and other immune-mediated optic neuropathies showing that recurrent attacks are associated with less favorable long-term visual outcomes [3, 32, 33].

Our findings align with previous OCT-based studies showing progressive pRNFL thinning after each relapse in MOG-ON [4]. The diffuse pRNFL reduction observed in the relapsing group suggests that recurrent inflammation may cause both localized and widespread retinal nerve fiber injury, likely through repeated demyelination and secondary retrograde degeneration [34]. Clinically, such structural changes are important because pRNFL thinning is closely linked to irreversible visual dysfunction. Based on these correlative observations, early identification of patients at high risk of relapse and timely consideration of maintenance immunotherapy may be associated with a lower risk of cumulative neuronal loss and visual impairment in patients with MOG-ON. However, given the retrospective observational nature of our study, we cannot establish causal relationships between maintenance immunotherapy and reduced retinal neurodegeneration. Future prospective interventional studies are warranted to validate the present exploratory associations.

This study has several limitations. First, this was a retrospective, single-center study with non-standardized maintenance treatment regimens, which may introduce selection bias and limit the uniformity and comparability of treatment outcomes. Second, long-term continuous monitoring of MOG antibody titers was not performed, limiting analysis of dynamic antibody changes and recurrence. Third, we excluded pediatric patients, restricting the generalizability of our findings. In addition, baseline MRI and OCT structural parameters were not included in Cox regression models. Future prospective multicenter studies with standardized treatment, long-term antibody monitoring, and quantitative structural imaging are warranted to validate our results and optimize relapse prediction models.

Conclusion

Our study reveals that female sex was associated with a higher risk of relapse, whereas immunosuppressive maintenance therapy after the first episode was associated with a lower risk of recurrence. Patients with relapsing disease also had worse visual outcomes at follow-up than those with a monophasic course. These observational findings supplement current evidence regarding prognostic factors in adult MOG-ON and may support clinical risk recognition.
