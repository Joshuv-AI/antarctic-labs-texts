Biphasic oxygen-regulating cyanobacterial hydrogel for synergistic ischemic stroke gas therapy and post-stroke depression prevention
Authors: Haoxin Cheng, Haihua Yang, Hongmei Liu, Yihan Luo, Zhihao Zhou, Mengzhen Zhao, Chao Wang, Zhiyan Xiong, Shichen Liu, Xiaolei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ischemic stroke (87% of stroke cases) results from cerebral artery occlusion. Reperfusion therapy restores cerebral blood flow but predisposes to dual complications: ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI) caused by oxygen fluctuations exacerbating neural damage and post-stroke depression (PSD). This study develops a cyanobacteria hydrogel composite (PT-CUCBD) for comprehensive reperfusion protection through precisely regulating the oxygen (O2) microenvironment during distinct reperfusion phases. During pre-reperfusion phase, upconversion nanoparticles emit 660/450 nm light under 808 nm near-infrared irradiation for photosynthetic O2 production, thereby alleviating pre-reperfusion hypoxia, while ultraviolet light triggers nitric oxide (NO) release to promote angiogenesis. More importantly, during post-reperfusion phase, cessation of irradiation activates cyanobacterial respiration to deplete excess O2, mitigating reoxygenation injury. Simultaneously, PT-CUCBD efficiently scavenges reactive oxygen species (ROS), while its loaded diallyl trisulfide releases hydrogen sulfide (H2S) in response to glutathione to enhance neuroprotection. Furthermore, it promotes macrophage polarization toward the anti-inflammatory M2-type, suppressing neuroinflammation and apoptosis. Notably, experimental results demonstrate that, this multifunctional platform not only prevents IRI, but also significantly reduces the risk of PSD, establishing a promising therapeutic paradigm for ischemic stroke.

Introduction

Ischemic stroke (IS) constitutes approximately 87% of all stroke cases and represents the second leading cause of global adult mortality [1,2]. This condition arises from cerebral artery occlusion, resulting in cerebral hypoperfusion that initiates hypoxia, reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, and neuroinflammation, ultimately culminating in neuronal damage [3,4]. Globally, an estimated 13.7 million individuals suffer strokes annually, with incidence escalating markedly with age[[5], [6], [7]]. Current standard care involves reperfusion therapy (via thrombolysis or mechanical thrombectomy) to restore cerebral blood flow [8,9]. However, this approach is significantly limited by ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI), in which abrupt reoxygenation induces severe oxidative stress, triggering massive ROS release and pro-inflammatory cytokine production, thereby exacerbating neuroinflammation and neuronal apoptosis [10,11]. Consequently, over 60% of survivors sustain permanent disability despite treatment. Further complicating the issue, approximately 30% of stroke survivors develop post-stroke depression (PSD), a currently understudied complication [12,13]. Thus, developing strategies to precisely regulate peri-reperfusion oxygenation while preventing both IRI and PSD is critical for improving IS outcomes.

Synechococcus elongatus (S. elongatus) PCC 7942 (cyanobacteria, Cya), a freshwater unicellular cyanobacterium exhibiting plant-like photosynthetic activity, can continuously generate oxygen (O2) under illumination [14]. Distinct from typical Gram-negative bacteria, its lipopolysaccharide (LPS) exhibits low immunogenicity [15]. This property enables its safe deployment as a biological O2 source to sustain neuronal viability within the ischemic penumbra prior to reperfusion therapy. Due to its photosynthesis and good biocompatibility, the cyanobacteria have been used in biomedical fields such as photodynamic therapy[[16], [17], [18]] and tissue regeneration [19,20]. Studies have shown that oxygen-generating cyanobacteria driven by upconversion nanoparticles are feasible for supplying O2 to the infarct region in IS [21]. Notably, cyanobacteria possess biphasic O2 regulation capability: photosynthetic O2 generation under light irradiation alleviates hypoxic conditions, while respiratory O2 consumption in darkness prevents hyperoxic states [22]. Such dual-phase modulation facilitates dynamic O2 management during pre-reperfusion (maintaining moderate oxygenation) and post-reperfusion (preventing reoxygenation injury) phases. Effective intervention for IS requires not only initial hypoxia alleviation but also resolution of reperfusion-triggered pathological cascades, including excessive oxidative stress and neuronal apoptosis. Endogenous gas signaling molecules, including nitric oxide (NO) and hydrogen sulfide (H2S), show therapeutic potential in anti-inflammatory [[23], [24], [25]], anti-apoptosis [26,27] and angiogenesis promotion [28,29] due to their free transmembrane ability and highly effective regulatory characteristics. In theory, a synergistic strategy combining these molecules with oxygen regulation is expected to significantly improve the therapeutic outcome of IS.

As shown in Scheme 1, we proposed a cyanobacteria hydrogel (PT-CUCBD) that combines biphasic oxygen regulation with gas therapy to prevent IRI and its associated PSD complications. During the pre-reperfusion phase (Scheme 1a), the upconversion nanoparticles (UCNPs) core of PT-CUCBD was excited, using 808 nm near-infrared irradiation (NIR), to emit more energetic ultraviolet and visible light. The cyanobacteria specifically harnessed 660 nm red light and 450 nm blue light for photosynthetic oxygen generation to restore oxygen supply in the ischemic area, while the NO donor BNN absorbed 365 nm and 290 nm ultraviolet light to release NO gas, thereby promoting angiogenesis and facilitating blood flow restoration (Scheme 1b). During the post-reperfusion phase (Scheme 1c), restored blood supply elevated O2 concentration abruptly in the infarct region. Cessation of NIR initiated dark conditions, where cyanobacterial respiration actively depleted O2 to prevent reoxygenation injury. Additionally, PT-CUCBD exhibited rapid ROS scavenging capacity, and its loaded diallyl trisulfide (DATS) donor released H2S gas in response to glutathione (GSH) for neuroprotection. Concurrently, PT-CUCBD promoted macrophage polarization from pro-inflammatory M1 to anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, mitigating neuroinflammation and neuronal apoptosis (Scheme 1d). Ultimately, this multifunctional platform achieved effective treatment of IRI through biphasic O2 regulation (production/consumption), combined with ROS clearance, immunomodulation and NO/H2S gas therapy. We also evaluated the preventive effect of PT-CUCBD in rat models of PSD. Morphology and structure of the samples were characterized using a scanning electron microscope (SEM; ZEISS Sigma300, Germany) or a transmission electron microscope (TEM; JEOL JEM-2100F, Japan). The upconversion emission spectra of the samples were analyzed using a fluorescence spectrophotometer (FL970, China). The particle size distribution and Zeta potential of the samples were determined using a nanoparticle size and zeta potential analyzer (Zeta-sizer Nano ZS90, Malvern, UK). Synthesis of Core@Shell@Shell UCNPs

The NaYF4:20%Yb,0.5%Tm (Core) nanocrystals were initially synthesized via a modified thermal decomposition method [30]. Briefly, a 20 mL solution containing 4 mmol total rare earth acetates (Y:Yb:Tm = 79.5:20:0.5 M ratio) was introduced into a 250 mL three-neck flask, followed by the addition of 24 mL OA and 60 mL ODE. The mixture was heated to 120°C and maintained at this temperature for 60 min until achieving complete clarity and transparency, after which it was cooled naturally to room temperature. Upon reaching room temperature, a methanol solution containing 16 mmol NH4F and 10 mmol NaOH was added under vigorous stirring while heating the system to 60°C, held for 50 min. The mixture was then gradually heated to 100°C to evaporate methanol, followed by rapid heating to 300°C with a 90-min reaction period. The resulting product was precipitated with ethanol, collected via centrifugation at 11,000 rpm for 20 min, washed alternately with cyclohexane and ethanol three times, and ultimately redispersed in cyclohexane.

The synthesis of NaYF4:25%Yb,0.5%Tm@NaYF4:20%Nd,10%Yb (Core@Shell) followed a procedure similar with that of the Core. Briefly, a 10 mL solution containing 2 mmol total rare earth acetates (Y:Nd:Yb = 7:2:1 M ratio) was added to a reaction vessel, followed by 12 mL OA and 30 mL ODE. The mixture was heated to 120°C and maintained for 60 min until completely clear and transparent. Subsequently, the previously synthesized core UCNPs dispersed in cyclohexane were added to the mixture. After complete evaporation of the cyclohexane, the solution was allowed to cool naturally to room temperature. Upon reaching room temperature, a mixed methanol solution containing 8 mmol NH4F and 5 mmol NaOH was added under vigorous stirring while heating to 60°C, where it was held for 50 min. The mixture was then gradually heated to 100°C to remove methanol, followed by rapid heating to 300°C with a 90-min reaction period. The resulting core-shell product was precipitated with ethanol, collected by centrifugation at 11,000 rpm for 20 min, washed alternately with cyclohexane and ethanol three times, and ultimately redispersed in cyclohexane.

The synthesis of NaYF4:25%Yb,0.5%Tm@NaYF4:20%Nd,10%Yb@NaYF4 (Core@Shell@Shell) followed identical steps to that of the Core@Shell nanoparticles, with the sole modification being the replacement of the rare earth acetate mixture with 2 mmol Y(CH3COO)3.

Synthesis of COOH-UCM

To synthesize the mesoporous silica-coated nanoparticles, 0.2 g CTAB was dissolved in 100 mL ultrapure water, followed by the addition of 2 mL of UCNPs cyclohexane solution (10 mg/mL) and 200 μL TEA. The mixture was heated to 80°C under vigorous stirring. After complete evaporation of cyclohexane, the solution was sonicated until clear and transparent. The temperature was then maintained at 80°C, and the pH was adjusted to 11.0 by adding 2 M NaOH. Subsequently, 5 mL of a 10% TEOS anhydrous ethanol solution was slowly added dropwise into the reaction system under vigorous stirring to form the mesoporous silica shell. Following silica coating, 5 μL APTES was added and stirred for 6 h to functionalize the outer surface of UCNP@mSiO2 (designated UCM) with amino groups. The NH2-UCM particles were then extracted three times with 1 wt% NaCl methanol solution to remove the CTAB template, washed three times each with ethanol and water, and freeze-dried under vacuum to obtain the NH2-UCM product. For carboxyl functionalization, 200 mg of NH2-UCM was added to 10 mL DMSO solution containing succinic anhydride (60 mg) and triethylamine (60 mg), and stirred at 40°C for 48 h. The product (COOH-UCM) was recovered by centrifugation, washed three times with ethanol, and dried under vacuum.

Synthesis of UCBD

The compound N,N′-di-sec-butyl-N,N′-dinitroso-1,4-phenylenediamine (BNN) was first synthesized according to literature procedures. Briefly, 10 mmol BPA was dissolved in 20 mL ethanol, followed by the addition of 20 mL deionized water containing 0.12 mol NaNO2 under an argon atmosphere. After stirring for 30 min, 20 mL of 6 M HCl was added dropwise. The reaction mixture was then stirred for 6 h, after which the beige precipitate was collected by centrifugation. The precipitate was sequentially washed three times with ethanol and three times with water. The resulting BNN product was obtained by freeze-drying under vacuum under light-protected conditions. For UCBD formation, equimolar amounts of DATS, BNN, and COOH-UCM were added to DMSO and stirred overnight. The resulting product (UCBD) was then collected by centrifugation at 8000 rpm, washed several times with DMSO, and vacuum-dried.

Synthesis of CUCBD

Briefly, a solution of 4-aminophenylboronic acid hydrochloride (2.5 mg mL−1 in phosphate-buffered saline, PBS) was mixed with cyanobacterial cells at the logarithmic growth phase in the dark for 4 h. The resulting mixture was purified through centrifugation and repeated washing with PBS buffer. The aminated cyanobacteria were collected by centrifugation and washed three times with PBS. COOH-UCM was activated with DMTMM in pH 8.0 buffer for 15 min. Aminated cyanobacteria were then added, and the mixture was stirred in the dark for 6 h. CUCBD was collected by centrifugation.

Synthesis of PT-CUCBD

The compound N1-(4-boronobenzyl)-N3-(4-boronophenyl)-N1,N1,N3,N3-tetramethylpropane-1,3-diaminium (TSPBA) was synthesized according to literature methods. Briefly, TMPDA (0.2 g) and 4-(bromomethyl)phenylboronic acid (1 g) were dissolved in DMF (40 mL) and stirred at 60°C for 24 h. The product was precipitated by pouring the solution into tetrahydrofuran (THF, 100 mL), collected by filtration, washed three times with THF, and freeze-dried to obtain TSPBA. PT-CUCBD was prepared by adding 500 μL of 9% (w/v) PVA solution to 500 μL of 3% (w/v) TSPBA solution containing CUCBD at a concentration of 108 cells mL−1.

Hemolysis assay

Fresh Sprague Dawley (SD) rat blood was centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 15 min to isolate red blood cells (RBCs). The RBCs were washed three times with saline. Subsequently, 0.4 mL of purified RBCs was mixed with 9.6 mL saline to prepare a 4% (v/v) RBC suspension. Then, 100 μL of the RBC suspension was combined with 1.1 mL saline solution containing PT-CUCBD at concentrations of 0, 25, 50, 100, 200, or 400 μg mL−1. Negative control samples were prepared by mixing 100 μL RBCs suspension with 1.1 mL saline, while deionized water served as the positive control. After incubation at 37°C for 1 h, all samples were centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 15 min. The absorbance of supernatants was measured at 540 nm using a microplate reader. The hemolysis percentage was calculated using the following equation (Eq. (4)):(4)Hemolysis(%)=OD1−OD2OD3−OD2×100%where OD1 represents the absorbance of PT-CUCBD-treated sample, OD2 represents the absorbance of negative control (saline), and OD3 represents the absorbance of positive control (deionized water).

Cell culture

BV2 murine microglial cells were cultured in low-glucose DMEM medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution (100 U/mL penicillin and 100 μg mL−1 streptomycin). Human neuroblastoma cells (SH-SY5Y) were cultured in high-glucose DMEM medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution (100 U mL−1 penicillin and 100 μg mL−1 streptomycin). All cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

Synechococcus elongatus PCC 7942 was obtained from the Freshwater Algae Culture Collection at the Institute of Hydrobiology (FACHB-805; Chinese Academy of Sciences, Wuhan, China). Cyanobacteria were inoculated in 500 mL flasks containing BG-11 liquid medium and cultivated at 28°C under a 50 W plant fluorescent lamp (Philips, China) with a fixed 12 h light/12 h dark photoperiod. PCC 7942 cells was collected by centrifugation (2500 × g, 5 min), and the supernatant was discarded. The pellet was washed three times with PBS and then kept for later use. The number of PCC 7942 cells was calibrated by manual cell counting and OD450 measurement in a cuvette, and the growth curve was plotted accordingly. PCC 7942 cells were harvested at the mid-logarithmic phase (OD450 ≈ 0.1–0.15) for subsequent experiments.

Oxygen glucose deprivation/reoxygenation (OGD/R) model

To simulate in vitro ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI), an oxygen-glucose deprivation/reoxygenation (OGD/R) model was established using BV2 cells or SH-SY5Y cells. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates (1.0 × 104 cells well−1), 48-well plates (2.0 × 104 cells well−1), or 6-well plates (1.0 × 106 cells well−1). After 24 h incubation, cells were washed with PBS and incubated in glucose-free DMEM medium containing different test materials. To maintain an anoxic atmosphere, cells were cultured in sealed chambers equipped with AnaeroPacks (Mitsubishi Gas Chemical Company, Japan) containing 5% CO2 [31]. Following 4 h of OGD, reoxygenation treatment was initiated for subsequent experiments.

Cytotoxicity experiment

Following OGD/R model establishment in BV2 cells or SH-SY5Y cells seeded in 96-well plates, the medium was replaced with DMEM containing different test materials upon reoxygenation. After a specified duration, the medium was exchanged with 10% CCK-8 solution. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a multimode microplate reader. Cell staining

Following OGD/R model establishment in BV2 cells or SH-SY5Y cells seeded in 48-well plates, the medium was replaced with DMEM containing different test materials upon reoxygenation for 24 h co-culture. Subsequent staining procedures included: Live/dead viability staining using Calcein-AM and propidium iodide (PI); Intracellular ROS detection with DCFH-DA and DAPI; Intracellular NO detection with DAF-FM DA and DAPI; Intracellular H2S detection with WSP-5 and DAPI; Intracellular O2 detection with [Ru(dpp)3]Cl2 and DAPI; Mitochondrial membrane potential assessment with JC-1 and DAPI. All imaging was performed using an inverted fluorescence microscope (IX83, Olympus, Japan).

Cell apoptosis detection

After establishing the OGD/R model in BV2 cells or SH-SY5Y cells seeded in 48-well plates and subsequent reoxygenation, cells were washed once with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. Following PBS washing, cells were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 5 min at room temperature. After two additional PBS washes, the TUNEL reaction mixture was applied and incubated at 37°C for 60 min in the dark, with concurrent DAPI nuclear counterstaining. Imaging was performed using an inverted fluorescence microscope (IX83, Olympus, Japan).

Additionally, reoxygenated cells were harvested, washed, and resuspended in 500 μL binding buffer. Cells were dual-stained with Annexin V-FITC and PI for 20 min at room temperature in the dark. Anti-apoptotic effects of different materials were quantitatively analyzed by flow cytometry (FCM).

BV2 polarization assessment

Following OGD/R model establishment in BV2 cells seeded in 48-well plates and subsequent reoxygenation, cells were washed three times with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min. After blocking with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA, Servicebio, China) for 30 min, samples were incubated with fluorescence-labeled antibodies at room temperature for 1 h in the dark: anti-CD86 antibody (M1 phenotype marker, Servicebio) and anti-CD206 antibody (M2 phenotype marker, Servicebio), with concurrent DAPI nuclear counterstaining. Imaging was subsequently performed using an inverted fluorescence microscope (IX83, Olympus, Japan).

Comet assay

Following OGD/R model establishment in BV2 cells seeded in 6-well plates, cells were gently scraped after reoxygenation, pelleted by centrifugation, and resuspended in pre-chilled PBS. A 50 μL aliquot of cell suspension was mixed with 500 μL of 37°C molten low-melting-point agarose (LM Agarose). Immediately, 50 μL of the mixture was pipetted onto microscope slides. After 10 min dark incubation at 4°C, slides were immersed in pre-cooled lysis solution at 4°C for 1 h. Subsequently, slides were transferred to freshly prepared alkaline unwinding solution for 20 min at room temperature in the dark, followed by gel electrophoresis at 20 V for 30 min. After two PBS washes, slides were stained with 100 μL PI solution for 10 min in the dark. DNA damage was finally assessed by image acquisition using an inverted fluorescence microscope.

ATP content detection

Following establishment of the OGD/R model in BV2 cells seeded in 6-well plates, culture medium was aspirated after reoxygenation. Cells were lysed with 200 μL lysis buffer on ice for 10 min, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 5 min at 4°C. The resulting supernatant was collected for analysis. In a 96-well polystyrene plate, 100 μL ATP detection reagent was added to each assay well. After 5 min equilibration at room temperature, 20 μL sample supernatant was added to the assay wells. Relative Light Units (RLU) were measured using a multimode microplate reader.

Establishment of MCAO/R and PSD rat models

The middle cerebral artery occlusion/reperfusion (MCAO/R) rat model was established using published methods to simulate in vivo cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury [32]. Briefly, rats were fasted overnight with ad libitum access to water. Anesthesia was then induced with 2% isoflurane in a gas mixture of 70% N2 and 30% O2, followed by mechanical ventilation. PT-CUCBD + NIR group: Under aseptic conditions, the unilateral common carotid artery (CCA), external carotid artery (ECA), and internal carotid artery (ICA) were exposed. After ligating the CCA, a monofilament (0.26 mm diameter, silicon-coated nylon) was advanced through an ECA incision into the ICA to occlude the right middle cerebral artery. Following 30 min occlusion, 10 μL PT-CUCBD was injected perilesionally over 10 min using a gas-tight Hamilton syringe (coordinates: 0.2 mm posterior, 3.5 mm lateral to bregma; 2.0 mm ventral to skull surface). Concurrently, the perilesional area was irradiated with 808 nm NIR (1 W cm−2) for 40 min. Reperfusion was achieved by filament withdrawal after 60 min. NIR group: Identical surgical procedures without PT-CUCBD injection. PT-CUCBD group: Identical procedures without NIR irradiation. MCAO/R group: Identical procedures without NIR irradiation and PT-CUCBD injection. Ctrl group: Identical procedures without vascular occlusion, NIR irradiation, and PT-CUCBD injection.

The PSD model was established using reported methods [12]. Following MCAO/R modeling with the above group design, rats recovered for 3 postoperative days. Ctrl group remained under standard housing conditions.

Result and discussion

Preparation and characterization of PT-CUCBD

A light-controlled gas release nanoplatform based on UCNPs was prepared and characterized (Fig. 1a). First, UCNPs with hexagonal core@shell@shell structure (NaYF4:Yb,Tm@NaYF4:Nd@NaYF4, C@S@S) were synthesized by thermal decomposition method [40] (Fig. 1b). NaYF4:Yb, Tm was the upconversion luminescence (UCL) core layer (Fig. S1a) with a particle size of approximately 26 nm (Fig. S1c). To avoid potential tissue overheating caused by 980 nm NIR, NaYF4:Nd shell with a thickness of about 8 nm (Fig. S1b and d) that could be excited by 808 nm NIR was coated onto the core layer. The outermost layer was coated with an inert NaYF4 shell to avoid the loss of excitation energy in the nanocrystal, which could further enhance the UCL intensity [41]. The final UCNPs particle size of the core@shell@shell structure was about 39 nm (Fig. S1e), and the UCL intensity was significantly enhanced (Fig. 1c and S2). Subsequently, in order to load the gas donors, a layer of mesoporous silica (MSN) was coated on the surface of UCNPs to obtain UCM (Fig. 1d). As shown in the X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of UCNPs and UCM, all diffraction peaks could be attributed to the pure hexagonal phase β-NaYF4 (JCPDS No. 016-0334), and a broad peak of amorphous SiO2 at θ ≈ 22° could be observed in UCM (Fig. 1e). Next, surface functionalization of UCM with carboxyl groups allowed it to be attached to the surface of cyanobacteria, which could also block surface silanol groups to improve the biocompatibility of UCM [42]. Carboxyl groups were grafted onto UCM by a two-step process, and primary amine groups were first introduced on the UCM surface by 3-aminopropyltriethoxysilane (APTES) post grafting method [43]. The carboxyl groups were then grafted onto UCM by ring-opening reaction of succinic anhydride [44]. As shown in the Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra of UCM before and after grafting modification (Fig. S3a), the strong absorption peaks at 1046 cm−1 and 970 cm−1 were attributed to the asymmetric stretching of the Si–O–Si bond and the Si–OH bending vibration of UCM, respectively. The characteristic peaks at 3450 cm−1 and 1636 cm−1 were attributed to the adsorbed water molecules in UCM. For NH2-UCM, the peaks at 1639 cm−1 and 1559 cm−1 were assigned to the stretching vibration of amide I and the bending vibration of –NH2, respectively, indicating that the amino group of NH2-UCM was successfully functionalized. In addition, the appearance of a characteristic peak at 1410 cm−1 corresponding to carbonyl (C=O) stretching vibration confirmed the successful synthesis of COOH-UCM. Following surface modification, the zeta potential exhibited a characteristic negative-positive-negative transition, further supporting the successful carboxylation of COOH-UCM (Fig. 1f), which was consistent with the results of the ninhydrin reaction and pH measurement (Fig. S3b and c). FT-IR and ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) spectroscopy were used to characterize BNN (Fig. S4), indicating that BNN was successfully synthesized and could respond to UV irradiation. Finally, DATS and BNN were loaded in COOH-UCM to get UCBD (Fig. 1g). When the molar ratio of DATS and BNN was 1:1, the optimal modification rates of DATS and BNN were approximately 6.18% and 5.31%, respectively (Figs. S5–7). Elemental mapping (Fig. 1h) and thermogravimetric analysis (TGA, Fig. 1i) further confirmed the successful preparation of UCBD, which reduced the specific surface area of COOH-UCM from 279.8022 m2 g−1 to 83.3619 m2 g−1, and the pore volume from 0.797809 cm3 g−1 to 0.333501 cm3 g−1 (Fig. 1j and Table S1). It could be found from the UV-vis and UCL spectra (Fig. 1k) that the MSN shell had no significant effect on the luminous intensity of UCM, and the absorption spectrum of BNN partially overlapped with UCL spectrum. After modification with BNN, the UCL intensity of UCBD decreased significantly in UV region, indicating that BNN could effectively absorb the energy of UCL, which was conducive to the release of NO.Fig. 1Characterization of UCBD. a) Synthesis schematic of UCBD. b) Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) image of UCNPs. c) UCL spectra of UCNPs. d) TEM image of UCM. e) XRD patterns of UCNPs and UCM. f) Zeta potential of UCM. g) TEM image of UCBD. h) Elemental mapping of UCBD. i) TGA curves of UCBD. j) N2 adsorption-desorption isotherms of UCBD. k) UCL spectra of UCBD and UV-vis absorption spectrum of BNN. The synthesis scheme of PT-CUCBD was shown in Fig. 2a. The cyanobacteria exhibited a rod-shaped morphology, lacking flagella and possessing a smooth surface (Fig. S8). A standard curve of cyanobacteria was established (Fig. S9a). Based on the relationship between oxygen production rate and cyanobacterial growth phase (Fig. S9b–c), Cya in the logarithmic growth phase were used for the synthesis of CUCBD. In order to improve the connection efficiency with UCBD, amino groups were first modified on the surface of cyanobacteria to generate amino-modified cyanobacteria (NH2-Cya). 4-aminophenylboronic acid hydrochloride was used as an amination reagent, because the boron center could coordinate with the adjacent hydroxyl groups of polysaccharides on the surface of cyanobacteria to form borate esters [45]. 4-(4,6-dimethoxy-1,3,5-triazin-2-yl)-4-methylmorpholinium chloride (DMTMM) was used to activate the carboxyl groups on the surface of UCBD, and then reacted with NH2-Cya to obtain CUCBD (Fig. 2a). The standard curve (Fig. S9a) for cyanobacteria was established. CUCBD was synthesized at varying mass ratios. The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) results demonstrated that CUCBD synthesized at 106 cyanobacterial cells:1 mg UCBD ratio maintained intact cyanobacterial morphology (Fig. S10) with successful UCBD surface conjugation (Fig. 2b). The change in zeta potential demonstrated the successful modification of amino groups on the surface of cyanobacteria and the connection of UCBD (Fig. 2c). UV-vis spectra and UCL spectra (Fig. 2d) showed a significant decrease in UCL intensity at 450 nm, 472 nm (blue light), and 645 nm (red light), indicating that the cyanobacteria absorbed UCL energy, which was conducive to their photosynthesis. Live/dead staining results showed no obvious cell deaths after CUCBD co-cultivation under NIR irradiation (Fig. 2e). Subsequently, nuclear magnetic resonance hydrogen spectroscopy (1H NMR) showed the successful synthesis of the ROS responsive linker N1-(4-boronobenzyl)-N3-(4-boronophenyl)-N1,N1,N3,N3-tetramethylpropane-1,3-diaminium (TSPBA, Fig. 2f). Cross-linking of polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) and TSPBA at different mass ratios was investigated. When the TSPBA concentration was higher than 6%, a solid PVA-TSPBA (PT) hydrogel was formed regardless of the PVA concentration (Fig. S11a). In contrast, TSPBA at a concentration of 3% could be crosslinked with PVA at a concentration of 9% to form a flexible PT hydrogel. If the PVA concentration continued to decrease, no gel would be formed (Fig. 2g). The FT-IR spectrum showed a characteristic peak with borate bond at 1351 cm−1 (Fig. S11b), indicating the successful synthesis of PT hydrogel. PT hydrogel had a porous structure (Fig. 2h) and could be completely degraded in hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) solution within 10 min (Figs. S12 and 13). Based on the relationship between O2 production rate and Cya concentration (Fig. S14), CUCBD was ultimately loaded into the PT hydrogel at a concentration of 108 cells·mL−1 to obtain PT-CUCBD (Fig. S15). SEM image showed that CUCBD (green) was evenly distributed in the PT (Fig. 2i). Rheological analysis showed that with the increase of time (Fig. 2j) and angular frequency (Fig. 2k), the storage modulus (G′), which reflected elastic strength, remained significantly higher than the loss modulus (G′′), which characterized viscous behavior. The low G′ value indicated the ultrasoft property of PT-CUCBD hydrogel, likely matching the mechanical properties of ultrasoft tissues (e.g., brain). Notably, viscosity decreased as angular frequency rose, exhibiting shear-thinning properties (Fig. 2l). Continuous step-strain measurements further confirmed the self-healing ability of the PT-CUCBD hydrogel. Under high shear (300%) conditions, G′ decreased below G″, and the hydrogel transformed into a liquid state. When the shear strain was reduced back to 0.1%, G′ rapidly recovered to a level significantly higher than G″, and the hydrogel returned to a gel state (Fig. S16). Assessment of the NO, H2S and O2 release property of PT-CUCBD

The gas release performance of PT-CUCBD under NIR irradiation was subsequently evaluated. Under NIR irradiation, NO release continued to increase over 90 min and was positively correlated with concentration (Fig. 3a, S17). However, there was no significant NO release from the BNN donor alone, indicating that the NO release of PT-CUCBD was triggered by NIR. In addition, on-demand release behavior under repeated switching of NIR irradiation was also evaluated. As shown in Fig. 3b, NO was only released during NIR irradiation, indicating that NIR could achieve controlled release of NO. To further investigate the release properties of NO in vitro, the fluorescent probe 4-amino-5-methylamino-2,7-difluorofluorescein diacetate (DAF-FM DA) was incubated with mouse microglia (BV2) in vitro oxygen-glucose deprivation/reoxygenation (OGD/R) model to detect NO release. Green fluorescence in BV2 cells was detected exclusively when both PT-CUCBD and NIR irradiation were present (Fig. 3c and d), demonstrating that NIR irradiation precisely triggers on-demand NO release from PT-CUCBD in vitro. Concurrently, H2S release from PT-CUCBD in response to GSH was evaluated. The results demonstrated that free DATS triggered instantaneous H2S burst release without sustained delivery (Fig. 3e and S18). In contrast, PT-CUCBD enabled sustained H2S release (Fig. 3e) with concentration-dependent kinetics proportional to GSH levels (Fig. 3f), thereby functioning effectively at physiological concentrations to achieve continuous release. The H2S fluorescent probe (WSP-5) was used to further test the ability of PT-CUCBD to release H2S within cells. The results showed that both the PT-CUCBD group and the PT-CUCBD + NIR group produced strong fluorescence (Fig. 3g and h), indicating that PT-CUCBD released DATS into the cells and produced H2S. Finally, O2 release from PT-CUCBD was quantified using a portable dissolved oxygen meter. Under NIR irradiation, PT-CUCBD exhibited photosynthetic activity comparable to natural white light (Fig. 3i). NIR on/off cycling validated the light-regulated photosynthetic/respiratory switching (Fig. 3j). The O2 probe [Ru(dpp)3]Cl2 was employed to verify O2 generation in BV2 cells under oxygen-glucose deprivation (OGD). Scavenging of ROS in PT-CUCBD

Cerebral IRI induces excessive production of ROS as the initial phase of post-stroke brain damage [46]. The ROS-responsive behavior of PT-CUCBD was evaluated by monitoring weight loss and morphological alterations during immersion in H2O2 solution. The results demonstrated rapid degradation of PT-CUCBD in H2O2 solution, while CUCBD preserved the ROS-responsive characteristics of PT (Fig. 4a, S20). An oxidative stress (OS) cell model was established using hydrogen peroxide to assess cyanobacterial viability under high ROS conditions. Live/dead staining confirmed that PT-CUCBD protected cyanobacteria from H2O2-induced oxidative damage, probably owing to the ROS-scavenging capacity of PT. (Fig. 4b and S21). Artificial cerebrospinal fluid was used to culture cyanobacteria to assess their viability in a simulated brain tissue environment. The results indicated that the cyanobacterial cell membrane structure remained intact within 12 h, but exhibited varying degrees of shrinkage, collapse, and rupture after 24 h (Fig. S22). The ROS scavenging capacity of PT-CUCBD was systematically evaluated through a series of antioxidant experiments. First, total antioxidant activity was assessed via diammonium salt radical (ABTS), 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl radical (DPPH), and nitrotetrazolium blue chloride (NBT) methods. Concentration-dependent antioxidant potency was observed, with PT-CUCBD achieving 89.05% ABTS free radicals scavenging at 200 μg mL−1 (Fig. 4c), and exceeding 60% clearance for both DPPH free radicals and superoxide anions (•O2-) (Fig. 4d and e), demonstrating potent radical elimination. Subsequently, electron spin resonance (ESR) spectroscopy further quantified scavenging efficacy against three representative ROS species: singlet oxygen (1O2), hydroxyl radicals (•OH), and •O2-. Significant ESR signal attenuation confirmed efficient elimination of 1O2 (Fig. 4f), •OH (Fig. 4g), and •O2- (Fig. 4h). Finally, intracellular ROS levels were measured using 2′,7′-Dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) fluorescence probe. OGD/R-treated BV2 cells exhibited substantially elevated ROS versus controls, whereas PT-CUCBD treatment suppressed ROS generation to near-normal levels (Fig. 4i). Cerebral IRI induces neuronal apoptosis through ROS-mediated deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) oxidative damage [47]. DNA impairment in OGD/R cell models was consequently assessed via the comet assay. Excessive ROS levels led to DNA oxidation and comet formation in OGD/R-exposed cells. In sharp contrast, no significant comet tails were observed in the PT-CUCBD + NIR group (Fig. 4j and S23), confirming effective protection against oxidative DNA damage. In vitro therapeutic efficacy

Biocompatibility is critical for in vivo biomaterial applications. The hemolytic potential of PT-CUCBD was first evaluated. The hemolysis rate of medical materials is required to be less than 5% [48], while the hemolysis rate of PT-CUCBD at 400 μg mL−1 was 4.73%, which is closer to the safety threshold. Consequently, the 200 μg mL−1 group demonstrating lower hemolysis was selected for subsequent experimentation (Fig. 5a). An in vitro model of IS was established by exposing BV2 cells to OGD/R. This model was then employed to assess the neuroprotective efficacy of PT-CUCBD against neuronal injury induced by IS. The dissolved oxygen content in the cell culture supernatant was first measured (Fig. 5b). The results showed that during the OGD treatment period, the dissolved oxygen content decreased to 1.07 ± 0.18 mg L−1, whereas it increased to 6.31 ± 0.58 mg L−1 in the PT-CUCBD + NIR group, indicating that cyanobacterial photosynthesis can supply oxygen to hypoxic cells. After restoring oxygen supply for 2 h and then placing the cells in an anaerobic bag for 4 h of culture in the dark, the dissolved oxygen content in the OGD/R group was 5.66 ± 0.27 mg L−1, while that in the PT-CUCBD group and the PT-CUCBD + NIR group decreased to 3.89 ± 0.20 mg L−1 and 3.76 ± 0.23 mg L−1, respectively, indicating that cyanobacterial respiration alleviated post-reperfusion hyperoxia. Subsequently, cell viability was quantified via CCK-8 assay across treatment groups. Compared with the normal BV2 cells, OGD/R reduced viability to 39.84%, confirming the successful establishment of the IS model in vitro. Remarkably, PT-CUCBD intervention restored cell viability to 69.34%, with near-complete recovery (90.96%) achieved upon NIR irradiation, indicating robust neuroprotective effects against OGD/R-induced injury in vitro (Fig. 5c). Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) rapidly liberates extracellularly upon membrane integrity compromise, establishing it as a biomarker of membrane damage [49]. OGD/R significantly increased LDH leakage compared with the control group. Critically, PT-CUCBD + NIR achieved minimal LDH release, indicating potent protection against OGD/R-induced BV2 cells injury (Fig. 5d). Concurrently, live/dead staining analysis was performed to visualize the neuroprotective effect on BV2 cells subjected to OGD/R. The results showed that compared with normal BV2 cells, the OGD/R group represented the strongest red fluorescence, indicating severe cell damage after OGD/R induction. However, PT-CUCBD or PT-CUCBD + NIR treatment enhanced green fluorescence while diminishing red signals, indicating that cell damage was suppressed (Fig. 5e). Additionally, we established an OGD/R model of SH-SY5Y cells to investigate which component exerted the specific therapeutic effect. The results showed that both the PT group and the Cya-UCM + NIR group significantly increased cell viability, with the PT-CUCBD + NIR group achieving a survival rate as high as 80.95% (Figs. S24 and S25). This benefit was attributed to the synergistic effect of ROS scavenging by PT and oxygen production via cyanobacterial photosynthesis. Mitochondrial membrane damage from oxidative stress constitutes another key hallmark of neuronal apoptosis, closely associated with IS progression [50]. Therefore, we used the JC-1 probe to track the changes in mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) of BV2 cells exposed to OGD/R. At high ΔΨm, JC-1 accumulates as red-fluorescent aggregates within the mitochondrial matrix; conversely, it exists in green-fluorescent monomeric form at depolarized potentials [51]. Quantitative analysis of JC-1 aggregates (red) to monomers (green) revealed significantly reduced ΔΨm in OGD/R-treated BV2 cells versus normal controls (Fig. 6a and b). PT-CUCBD treatment diminished green fluorescence (indicating damaged mitochondria) while intensifying red fluorescence (indicating functional mitochondria). This mitochondrial protection was further potentiated by NIR co-treatment. In addition, mitochondrial function was reflected by measuring the adenosine triphosphate (ATP) content in BV2 cells. The results showed that PT-CUCBD significantly restored ATP production damaged by OGD/R (Fig. 6c and S26). Using the SH-SY5Y cell OGD/R model, we assessed the mitochondrial protective effects of each component (Fig. S27). Both the PT and Cya-UCM + NIR groups showed moderate protection. The UCM-Dats group outperformed the Free Dats group, likely due to sustained H2S release from UCM. The PT-CUCBD + NIR group exhibited the strongest protection, attributed to the synergy among H2S release, ROS scavenging, and photosynthetic oxygen production. Concurrently, mitochondria were immunolabeled with ATP synthase subunit β mitochondrial antibody (ATPB), a standard mitochondrial marker, to further evaluate the mitochondrial protective ability of PT-CUCBD. The administration of OGD/R led to a reduction in ATPB levels, whereas treatment with PT-CUCBD + NIR facilitated an increase in ATPB levels (Fig. 6d and e). In the pathological context of cerebral ischemia, microglia, which are the primary immune cells of central nervous system, exhibit rapid response characteristics [52]. Following a stroke, these cells can be swiftly activated and migrate to the ischemic region. Research has demonstrated that the M1 and M2 phenotypes exhibit significant functional differences: the M1 phenotype of BV2 cells exacerbates neuroinflammation and neuronal damage by releasing ROS and pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), whereas the M2 phenotype of BV2 cells confers neuroprotection by secreting anti-inflammatory cytokines like interleukin-10 (IL-10) and interleukin-4 (IL-4) [53]. Given these regulatory mechanisms, this study assessed the regulatory impact of PT-CUCBD on cell phenotypic polarization using the BV2 microglia model. The experiment initially developed an OGD/R model to simulate cerebral IRI. Immunofluorescence analysis (Fig. 6f and g) demonstrated that OGD/R stimulation significantly increased the expression of the M1-type marker CD86, confirming the successful establishment of an inflammatory microenvironment. Following intervention with PT-CUCBD, expression of the M2-type marker CD206 increased. Importantly, the combination of PT-CUCBD and NIR significantly altered the microglial phenotype, showing the highest red fluorescence intensity. Subsequently, levels of typical inflammatory mediators in BV2 cells subjected to various treatments were quantified using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). The findings indicated that treatment with PT-CUCBD combined with NIR significantly decreased the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α and IL-6, while significantly enhancing the secretion of anti-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-4 and IL-10, in BV2 cells. In vivo therapeutic efficacy

Based on the effective therapeutic efficacy of PT-CUCBD in vitro, its therapeutic effects were subsequently investigated in the middle cerebral artery occlusion/reperfusion (MCAO/R) animal model. Focal cerebral ischemia was induced in Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats through middle cerebral artery (MCA) occlusion using an intraluminal filament to establish MCAO/R and sham-operated models. With the sham-operated model serving as the Control group (Ctrl group), MCAO/R model rats were randomly assigned to four groups: MCAO/R, NIR, PT-CUCBD, and PT-CUCBD + NIR group. The experimental timeline and procedures are illustrated in Fig. 7a. After removing the infarcted brain tissue and homogenizing it, gas release was measured. The results showed that the NO level was increased in the PT-CUCBD + NIR group (Fig. S28a), and the H2S level was increased in both the PT-CUCBD group and the PT-CUCBD + NIR group (Fig. S28b). As oxygen availability decreases, cells shift to anaerobic respiration, thereby increasing lactate production and secretion [54]. Therefore, we measured the lactate content in the infarcted brain tissue. The results showed that, compared with the MCAO/R group, lactate secretion was reduced in the PT-CUCBD + NIR group (Fig. S28c–d), indicating that cyanobacteria supplied oxygen to the infarcted brain tissue through photosynthesis. Cerebral infarction volume was quantitatively assessed in MCAO/R rats by staining brain sections with 2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) solution (Fig. 7b). The MCAO/R group displayed severe brain injury with an infarction volume of 28.60 ± 3.04%, while NIR irradiation alone showed no visible therapeutic effect (Fig. 7c). However, PT-CUCBD treatment reduced the infarction volume to 17.12 ± 3.84%. Notably, the PT-CUCBD + NIR group significantly reduced infarction volume to 7.30 ± 2.78%. Brain water content was also measured to evaluate cerebral edema in MCAO/R rats following different treatments. Results indicated that PT-CUCBD, both with and without NIR irradiation, significantly alleviated cerebral edema in IS compared with the MCAO/R group (Fig. 7d). The prognosis for IS is often poor, frequently leading to disabilities such as paralysis and motor dysfunction. Therefore, the grid test (Fig. 7e1, S29a) and adhesive test (Fig. 7e2, S29b) were performed to assess motor function recovery in MCAO/R rats. The number of foot faults increased significantly in the MCAO/R group compared with the Ctrl group, whereas the PT-CUCBD + NIR group showed a significant decrease in foot faults compared with the MCAO/R group, demonstrating restored motor control (Fig. 7f). Simultaneously, the adhesive test was conducted to evaluate sensorimotor function. The Ctrl group typically detected and removed the adhesive within seconds in a single attempt. In contrast, the MCAO/R and NIR groups often required over 50 s to sense the adhesive and made no further attempts after one unsuccessful removal attempt. Interestingly, the PT-CUCBD group ceased removal attempts after 2-4 failures, but those receiving PT-CUCBD in combination with NIR irradiation eventually succeeded in removing the adhesive, indicating that PT-CUCBD + NIR significantly restored sensorimotor function (Fig. 7g). Furthermore, neurological deficit scores, including the modified Neurological Severity Score (mNSS), Bederson score, and Longa score, were evaluated in rats. Compared with the MCAO/R group, the PT-CUCBD + NIR group showed significant improvements in all scores (Fig. 7h–j, Tables S2–4). Neuroprotective effect and mechanism

To explore the neuroprotective mechanism of PT-CUCBD, brain tissues were collected for immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence staining. Firstly, hypoxia levels in the ischemic hemisphere were assessed via immunofluorescence staining of hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α). Relevant literature indicates that under normal oxygen saturation, the expression of HIF-1α is undetectable, whereas exposure to a hypoxic environment inhibits the degradation of HIF-1α, leading to a rapid increase in its protein levels [31]. The results showed that the PT-CUCBD + NIR group efficiently alleviated hypoxia in the ischemic hemisphere (Fig. 8a and b). PT-CUCBD + NIR also suppressed the expression of the hypoxia marker CA9 (Fig. S30). Secondly, neuronal survival in the ischemic hemisphere was evaluated using Nissl staining (Fig. 8c and d) and immunofluorescence staining for NeuN (Fig. 8e and f). Following MCAO/R, neuronal survival in the ischemic region decreased significantly compared with the Ctrl group. However, administration of PT-CUCBD + NIR resulted in greater neuronal survival. Additionally, TUNEL staining was performed to detect apoptosis in the ischemic area (Fig. 8g). TUNEL-positive cells increased markedly after MCAO/R. Nevertheless, rats treated with PT-CUCBD and PT-CUCBD + NIR exhibited significantly reduced TUNEL-positive apoptosis, indicating that the designed nanoplatform protects neuronal cells in the ischemic brain (Fig. 8h). Bcl-2, a key anti-apoptotic protein, inhibits cytochrome c release and reduces caspase-3 activity, while its antagonist Bax promotes apoptosis by facilitating cytochrome c release and activating the caspase-3 cascade. Based on this, expression changes in these hallmark apoptosis-related proteins were analyzed (Fig. 8i) to further validate the anti-apoptotic mechanism of the PT-CUCBD platform. Results revealed that compared with the Ctrl group, the PT-CUCBD + NIR group significantly downregulated pro-apoptotic proteins cleaved caspase-3 (Fig. 8j) and Bax (Fig. 8k), while significantly upregulating the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 (Fig. 8l). These findings demonstrate that PT-CUCBD + NIR effectively inhibits apoptosis by regulating key proteins in the apoptotic pathway, thereby mitigating cerebral IRI. The inflammation and immune response after stroke are intensified, resulting in severe limitation of angiogenesis. Therefore, post-stroke angiogenesis was evaluated by CD31/α-SMA/Ki67 immunofluorescence staining. Anti-inflammatory effect and mechanism

Notably, the reperfusion process itself triggers intense oxidative stress and activates inflammatory cascades, both of which are widely considered key pathological mechanisms mediating subsequent neural injury [55]. The free radical scavenging ability of PT-CUCBD in infarct lesions was tested using the DCFH-DA probe (Fig. 9a). Oxidative stress was significantly enhanced in the infarct region after MCAO/R, which was substantially suppressed in the PT-CUCBD group. Interestingly, the PT-CUCBD + NIR group showed no significant oxidative stress, approaching the level of the Ctrl group (Fig. 9b). Astrocytes and microglia are the primary inflammatory cells in the brain, which are activated and recruited to ischemic inflammatory regions [56]. The anti-inflammatory effects of PT-CUCBD were further investigated via glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) and ionized calcium binding adapter molecule 1 (Iba1) staining. The results showed that the number of GFAP-labeled astrocytes and Iba1-labeled microglia were significantly decreased in the ischemic hemisphere tissue of the PT-CUCBD + NIR group (Fig. 9c–f). Microglia, as highly plastic cells with phagocytic activity, are activated during cerebral IRI [57,58]. Their pro-inflammatory M1 and anti-inflammatory M2 phenotypes exert opposing effects on neural repair: the M1 phenotype exacerbates brain damage by releasing pro-inflammatory factors, while the M2 phenotype alleviates neuroinflammation and promotes repair through anti-inflammatory factor secretion. Therefore, promoting their polarization from the M1 to the M2 phenotype represents a key neuroprotective strategy. To investigate the regulatory effect of PT-CUCBD on microglial polarization, immunofluorescence co-staining (M1 marker: CD86; M2 marker: CD206) was performed on ischemic brain tissue sections. Results showed that CD86 expression increased significantly in the ischemic region of MCAO/R rats compared with the Ctrl group, indicating macrophage infiltration into the injury site (Fig. 9g). Notably, compared with rats in the MCAO/R group, brain sections from rats treated with PT-CUCBD + NIR exhibited a significant decrease in M1-like macrophages and a significant increase in M2-like macrophages (Fig. 9h). Simultaneously, macrophage phenotypic polarization was analyzed by flow cytometry (FCM). Results revealed that the ratio of CD206+ M2-like macrophages to CD86+ M1-like macrophages increased significantly after treatment with PT-CUCBD + NIR compared with the MCAO/R group (Fig. 9i and j). Furthermore, immune cell infiltration into the ischemic hemisphere was analyzed by FCM. CD11b+ myeloid cells were stained with anti-CD45 antibody to identify CD45high peripheral monocytes/macrophages (Mo/MΦ). Post-ischemic infiltration of Mo/MΦ was significantly attenuated after PT-CUCBD + NIR treatment, suggesting alleviated neuroinflammation in the ischemic region (Fig. 9k and l). In summary, the above findings confirmed that PT-CUCBD can effectively remodel the highly oxidative and inflammatory cerebral microenvironment following IS and protect neuronal cells from further damage.

To further elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying therapeutic effects of PT-CUCBD in IS, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed using ischemic brain tissues collected after 24 h. The boxplots demonstrated effective normalization of the RNA-seq data, indicating well-standardized samples across all groups (Fig. 10a). A total of 1970 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were detected (Fig. 10b). Among these, 359 and 803 unique genes were identified in the MCAO/R group and PT-CUCBD + NIR group, respectively, compared with the Ctrl group. Relative to the MCAO/R group, 243 upregulated and 125 downregulated genes were detected in the PT-CUCBD + NIR group (Fig. 10c). These DEGs were visualized in a heatmap analysis, demonstrating good inter-group consistency in the expression patterns of these differential genes (Fig. 10d). Subsequently, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was conducted on DEGs between the MCAO/R group and PT-CUCBD + NIR group. GO classification-based analysis of DEGs revealed broad distribution across biological processes, molecular functions, and cellular components, indicating that PT-CUCBD + NIR treatment significantly modulates functional activities at these biological levels compared with the MCAO/R group (Fig. 10e and Table S5). Prevention of PSD

PSD is a common and detrimental complication of stroke, occurring in nearly one-third of stroke survivors [59]. To validate the preventive effect of PT-CUCBD against PSD, a PSD model was established in SD rats. The experimental timeline is shown in Fig. 11a. Focal cerebral ischemia was induced via middle cerebral artery (MCA) occlusion using an intraluminal filament to establish MCAO/R and sham-operated models. With the sham-operated model as the Ctrl group, MCAO/R model rats were randomly assigned to four groups: MCAO/R, NIR, PT-CUCBD, and PT-CUCBD + NIR. Three days after surgical recovery, rats underwent a 3-week CUMS regimen to establish the PSD model. Behavioral validation was performed in the 4th week, followed by collection of hippocampal tissues from the ischemic hemisphere for histological analysis. MCAO/R rats subjected to CUMS exhibited significantly prolonged immobility time in the tail suspension test (TST) (Fig. 11b) and reduced sucrose preference in the sucrose preference test (SPT) (Fig. 11c), indicating marked depressive-like behaviors. Notably, PT-CUCBD + NIR treatment alleviated these depressive-like behaviors. Hippocampal dysfunction is a critical factor in depression pathogenesis, mechanistically linked to inflammation-mediated downregulation of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) expression, while antidepressant therapies exert effects by upregulating BDNF [60,61]. Therefore, the impact of PT-CUCBD on hippocampal BDNF expression was examined (Fig. 11d). Compared with the Ctrl group, PT-CUCBD + NIR treatment effectively prevented the CUMS-induced downregulation of BDNF protein levels in the hippocampus (Fig. 11e). Additionally, given that elevated expression of inflammatory proteins such as pStat3, NLRP3 and caspase-1 plays a critical role in depression progression, their expression levels were measured. Immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry analyses were performed on hippocampal tissues from the ischemic hemisphere of rats. The results of HIF-1α detection showed that the hippocampus was in a hypoxic state after MCAO/R surgery, while PT-CUCBD + NIR treatment significantly alleviated hypoxia (Fig. 11j and k). Simultaneously, PT-CUCBD + NIR reduced the recruitment of GFAP-labeled astrocytes in the hippocampus (Fig. 11l and m), indicating its potent anti-inflammatory effect. Furthermore, immunohistochemical staining revealed that PT-CUCBD + NIR treatment suppressed the expression of CD86 (a pro-inflammatory marker) while increasing the expression of CD206 (an anti-inflammatory marker) (Fig. 11n and o). These in vivo results demonstrate that PT-CUCBD + NIR shifts microglial polarization from the M1 to the M2 phenotype to exert anti-inflammatory effects. To further validate the anti-inflammatory activity of PT-CUCBD + NIR, levels of inflammatory cytokines in brain tissue were measured. Results indicated that PT-CUCBD + NIR treatment significantly inhibited the PSD-induced expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α (Fig. 11p) and IL-6 (Fig. 11q). In contrast, it enhanced the expression of anti-inflammatory cytokines IL-4 (Fig. 11r) and IL-10 (Fig. 11s). The ICP-OES results demonstrated a time-dependent decrease in the PT-CUCBD concentration within the brain, suggesting that the metal ions were gradually metabolized and eliminated from the body (Fig. S32). Finally, major organs from rats receiving different treatments were collected. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was used to evaluate the biosafety of the prepared PT-CUCBD, and serum biochemical markers were analyzed to assess systemic toxicity. As shown in the results, PT-CUCBD did not cause significant pathological changes in the brain tissue of normal rats (Fig. S33). No pathological changes in organ sections (Fig. S34) or abnormal blood routine parameters (Table S7) were observed in the treated rats, indicating that the prepared PT-CUCBD possesses good biosafety in vivo. In summary, PT-CUCBD suppresses post-MCAO/R hippocampal inflammation by promoting microglial polarization from the M1 to the M2 phenotype, thus effectively preventing CUMS-induced PSD.

Conclusions

This study successfully constructed a cyanobacteria hydrogel (PT-CUCBD) for the sequential treatment of ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) injury and the prevention of CUMS-induced post-stroke depression (PSD) complications. The innovations of this study are mainly reflected in the following three aspects: 1. First achievement of biphasic O2 regulation in the infarct zone: through the photosynthetic-respiratory dynamic metabolic characteristics of cyanobacteria, photosynthesis O2 production is used to maintain moderate oxygenation in the infarction area during pre-reperfusion, and excessive oxygenation is suppressed through respiratory O2 consumption during post-reperfusion. 2. Establishment of a three-pronged defense system (immunomodulation-redox balance-neuroprotection): PT-CUCBD synergistically releases (Ⅰ) NO (promoting angiogenesis)/H2S (protecting mitochondria), (Ⅱ) scavenges excess ROS, and (Ⅲ) induces microglial polarization towards the M2 anti-inflammatory phenotype, forming a multi-target synergistic therapeutic network for efficient treatment of post-stroke reperfusion injury. 3. Pioneering extension of stroke therapy to realize CUMS-induced PSD prevention: PT-CUCBD blocks neuroinflammation at its source within the post-stroke infarcted brain region (including the hippocampus), achieving effective prevention of CUMS-induced PSD. However, this study still possesses certain limitations: 1. Invasive delivery: intracranial injection surgery is difficult and potentially cause poor patient compliance, so brain-targeted nanocarriers for nasal or venous delivery need to be developed in the future; 2. The insufficient single-component controls in in vivo studies have not fully validated the synergistic therapeutic mechanism. 3. NIR penetration efficiency: limited tissue penetration depth restricts upconversion luminescence efficiency. In future research, while overcoming the limitations of existing delivery methods and light penetration, it is expected to expand its application to organ transplantation and ischemic pretreatment, chronic wounds and diabetic foot ulcers, myocardial/limb ischemia and retinal artery/vein occlusion and other fields. In the increasingly close combination of materials science and brain science today, this research may also generate beneficial impact on multiple future cutting-edge fields such as brain enhancement, emotion control and brain-machine association.
