Understanding garment durability through local lenses: a participatory study with communities across the globe
Authors: Hester Vanacker, Andrée-Anne Lemieux, Kirsi Laitala, Michelle Dindi, Sophie Bonnier, Samir Lamouri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The overproduction of garments, often of low quality, contributes significantly to environmental degradation, especially in the Global South. Therefore, assessing the durability of garments has attracted the attention of industry organizations and legislators. Recent research has identified both intrinsic and extrinsic dimensions of durability and their links to a circular transition. This study aims to deepen the understanding of garment durability by incorporating the local perspectives of five different global communities. Using a participatory action research methodology, semi-structured interviews were conducted with stakeholders across value chains in France, Ghana, Indonesia, Norway, and South Africa. The key findings of this study have been grouped into 8 trends that characterize garment durability: (1) quality is preferred over durability, (2) garment durability is dynamic, (3) price and brand are related to perceived durability, (4) local refers to geographical proximity, (5) local involves value creation, (6) local touches tradition, (7) traditional garments and textiles are more durable, and (8) local contexts influence garment durability. These trends indicate that local factors significantly influence the definition and practice of durability, suggesting that global legislation must consider such nuances when describing and quantifying durability in the context of garments and textiles.

Introduction

Overconsumption is a central concern, especially in the more affluent countries of the Global North1. However, it has emerged as a disproportional driver of environmental degradation in lower-income countries in the Global South2–4. The garment and textile industry is affected by overconsumption, which is driven by fast fashion brands known for the overproduction of cheap and disposable clothing5. The average European discards around 11 kg of clothing annually6. In the US, 85% of the discarded textiles are sent to landfills or incinerated owing to a lack of suitable end-of-life solutions7.

The second-hand clothing market, which is currently worth an estimated $197 billion8can extensively reshape the fashion industry9. However, the increasing production and consumption of low-quality garments affect this market. In Ghana, an estimated 40% of the 15 million second-hand garments that are imported weekly, mainly from countries in the Global North, end up as waste10. Mitigating and managing the fashion waste crisis requires data on annual garment production, which are currently unavailable10. Although the second-hand market offers a potential solution, it does not address the root of the problem, which is the excessive production of low-quality garments11.

Traditionally, the fashion industry is not regulated by authorities12; however, this has changed owing to growing concerns about its negative environmental and social impacts. Policymakers such as the European Commission (EC)13,14 and business advocacy groups such as the Ellen MacArthur Foundation15–19 have promoted circular economy strategies, after an era dominated by sustainable development20,21. The EC complemented this with its Waste Framework Directive, which, in its revised version for 2023, proposed an extended EU-wide extended producer responsibility (EPR) scheme for textiles and footwear22. This legislative proposal is the first attempt to regulate the environmental performance of the textile industry with a focus on the waste aspect6. However, France, Hungary, Latvia and the Netherlands are currently the only countries to have introduced an EPR policy, along with California, where the Responsible Textile Recovery Act was introduced in 202323.

Another regulatory framework is the Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation for Apparel and Footwear, which considers the intrinsic and extrinsic durability of products24. Intrinsic refers to wear, tear, and material decay, whereas extrinsic refers to external changes influencing user preferences25. The Product Environmental Footprint for Apparel and Footwear Category Rules (PEFCR) measures the environmental footprint of products following similar rules across the European Union. The standardized framework is based on the principles of a life-cycle analysis and considers circularity, repairability, and durability. Durability is defined as the lifespan of a product and the number of times it can be worn. It was originally quantified through physical tests12. The French equivalent of the PEFCR includes the extrinsic dimension and even prioritizes it over the intrinsic dimension by assessing the production volumes, speed cycles in garment sales, materials used, incentives for repairs, and traceability of manufacturing steps of a brand. This is reinforced by France’s latest Fast Fashion law proposition26which penalizes fast fashion brands and follows the rationale that physically durable new garments and footwear must have an extrinsic lifespan longer than their physical lifespan; otherwise, they will be discarded before their physical end-of-life11. For a holistic and representative approach, legislation should consider both the intrinsic and extrinsic dimensions of garments and their ability to withstand environmental changes.

In recent years, global and legislative debates have included garment durability, in response to concerns about the ever-decreasing quality and increasing quantity, as highlighted by different environmental and social justice organizations and a growing group of concerned researchers6,26–29. In a previous study, the authors conducted a literature review to obtain a clear definition of garment durability25, and considered it in the context of a just transition30. The authors argue that “garment durability” is the ongoing interaction between the garment and its changing environment(s) and user(s), allowing it to go through different life cycles through the practices of care, mending, and repair. “Change” evokes a local dimension that is associated with the context in which the garment is worn and the people wearing it. Hence, geographical variations in weather, climate, culture, customs, and dress habits must be considered29. Fletcher and Tham31 argued that work must seek situated responses rooted in the local context such that a multiplicity of approaches will replace a one-size-fits-all approach. This is crucial as clear assessment criteria for garment durability are almost non-existent or incomplete.

This study aimed to understand garment durability from the perspectives of different communities across the globe. Participatory Action Research (PAR) was applied, and a value-based framework was used for evaluation. Semi-structured interviews for data gathering were conducted with actors from the value chain in five regions: France, Ghana, Indonesia, Norway, and South Africa. By interviewing different communities in both the Global North and the Global South, legislative bodies in the Global North are challenged, as their policies and laws also impact communities in the Global South, who are often excluded from the decision-making process. The authors posed three research questions: (1) How do different global communities define and perceive garment durability? (2) How do different global communities define and perceive the notion of local in the context of garments and textiles? (3) How do durability and local factors intersect in the context of garments and textiles? The results can inform policy and practice by providing evidence of how different local contexts influence the understanding of garment durability, while monitoring global trends.

Results

Durability in the context of garments and textiles

Three major trends were identified in the responses concerning durability.

Trend 1: quality is preferred over durability

Many participants tended to reference “quality” and use it in their explanations together with or instead of durability. Participants mentioned high quality fabrics/materials, ensuring garment quality, quality construction, quality control, quality assurance, and physical quality testing. Such statements mostly come from actors who produce, sort, and/or sell garments, such as brands, second-hand retailers, and sorting centers; as part of their jargon, the term “quality” was mentioned spontaneously.

When asked about the meaning of durability, a French weaver explained that fabric quality is physically evaluated for pilling resistance, color stability, mechanical resistance, and tear resistance. These observations are consistent with the definition of intrinsic durability previously established by the authors25. A small-scale Norwegian fashion integrates durability into their design process by adding an extra seam to their garments to make them last. However, as an NGO employee from Ghana commented, “There is a difference between defining durability in a laboratory and defining it based on the local context. In the latter case, you look at how people use clothes, what the end of life looks like, and its impact.” An upcycling brand from France believes that a long-lasting garment goes through different loops that make it durable over time, not necessarily being in the exact same condition or use. In South Africa, a language barrier was identified as the word does not exist in isiZulu, one of the languages of the participants. In Sumba, Indonesia, no similar term exists in the local language.

Polyester garments often perform well on intrinsic durability tests but are deemed unfavorable in the local context of the participants. Two second-hand clothing sellers from Ghana avoided keeping polyester-lined clothing from their bales due to unsuitability to local weather and humidity. Similarly, upcyclers in the Kantamanto market in the country’s capital city struggle integrating polyester garments in their designs. Upcyclers from France also face the same problem as they find cheap and low-quality synthetic fabrics unsuitable for upcycling and are yet to find ways to integrate them in their remanufacturing process.

Trend 2: garment durability is dynamic

Various participants claimed that garment durability is “dynamic,” as opposed to “static” as they believe it is defined by the story created by the wearer and constantly evolves through cycles of wear, care, repair, and remanufacturing. An upcycler from France claims that extending a garment’s life is a shared responsibility as it depends 50% on eco-design and 50% on the actual care by the wearer. A designer and educator from Norway said that “It makes little sense to make things as durable as possible in the context of textiles and especially clothing, considering how people’s lives and bodies change over time.”

This highlights the risk of applying theories from related disciplines to fashion and textiles. An upcycling brand from France confirms this perspective, explaining that the multiple cycles intended to extend durability of a garment does not mean that it must retain its original condition. “There is the aspect of change, but also the aspect of use. A product can remain the same, but it can also be used differently; for example, a pair of trousers that I have worn for a long time can become a rag.” An upcycling designer in Ghana considers this as changing the destiny of a garment. Identifying “change” as a characteristic of durability contrasts with the initial definition of durability given by the authors as resistance to any change25. In contrast, this is in line with the concept of “resilience,” which also includes the ability to recover from severe damage through modularity, repair, replacement, or upgradability15.

Trend 3: price and brand are related to perceived durability

The concept of price was also mentioned by participants when describing the durability of garments. In all five regions, the price and brand of garments were associated with expected quality or durability. Consequently, only some items were considered worthy of repair. This leads to assumptions that certain brands at certain price points are high quality and that cheap clothes from fast fashion brands have poor quality. This was confirmed by the brand director of a garment rental service in South Africa, who admitted to judging the quality of garments by the brand.

Beyond financial value, luxury brands are seen as status symbols with a certain value in the country’s society, according to a South African designer. Several other participants from this region explained this as a “desire to conform to Western ideals” and that international brands are often unattainable, making them even more desirable. Clothing and second-hand retailers from all five regions cited brands as important differentiators in their sorting processes. In one sorting center in the Global North, sorters are trained to distinguish “good brands” from “cheap brands,” although the exact criteria for this remain unclear. The sorters mentioned that an increasing number of garments from fast fashion brands are often in poor condition. This causes problems for second-hand clothing sellers, who are unable to sell them. As mentioned in Trend 1, upcyclers struggle to give new value to these poor-quality garments.

Local in the context of garments and textiles

Across regions and actors, local was most often identified based on the geographical proximity. However, value creation and tradition were identified as additional trends.

Trend 4: local refers to geographical proximity

The sentiment that local ideally means that the fibers are produced locally, processed locally, woven locally, made locally into garments, and sold in the same country appeared in many of the participants’ responses. Some even extended the notion of local to the use phase, as a design student from France explained: “In the end, the garment is also worn in France, as it seems important that there is a minimum level of use in the neighborhood.”

The term “local” suggests a sense of proximity, “as close as possible,” “nearby,” “close,” or a “short cycle” taking place in a specific “region” with limited transport. The extent of this region is yet to be clearly defined. Some referred to it at a country scale, a group of neighboring countries, or even a continent. A measurable definition was provided by a participant from France, where local reuse was defined in the EPR regulation as being less than 300 km from the collection point. These views also have a human aspect, as local involves creating jobs for the local communities of the participants.

Trend 5: local involves value creation

The participants also emphasized the importance of value creation. An employee of an NGO in Ghana explained that a garment is local “if it creates local added value and ultimately stays in the region to be distributed locally.” In Sumba, an island isolated from other parts of Indonesia, an NGO founder stated that something local can interact with something global, claiming that the product can leave the island and still be considered local if the value created remains in the local community.

Another site of local-global interaction is the Kantamanto market in Ghana, where the remanufacturing center has developed local processes to work with materials imported from around the world. When adapted to local preferences and worn by the community, participants from this region consider them local, as they offer local added value through use. For example, the traders said that people like tank tops because you can combine them with a wrap skirt. Hence, they include tank tops in their first selection and tailor imported T-shirts into tank tops to meet local needs. Similarly, a French clothing exporter with Congolese roots explained that Western clothing is styled and worn by Congolese communities in a distinct way, making it part of the local clothing culture.

Trend 6: local touches tradition

Respondents across all regions explain how “local” refers to the culture of clothing that reflects the people who wear it. An educator from South Africa states that “it is not imported and resists the colonization of cultures.” To create something local, one needs local expertise and heritage, which has often been passed down from one generation to the next. According to an NGO, the patterns in Ikat from Sumba are “the voice of the voiceless women” who use techniques taught to them by their ancestors, just as Shweshwe textiles in South Africa have a cultural significance for traditional ceremonies and celebrations or Kente cloth in Ghana are an expression of culture, tradition, and identity. In Norway, different regions are identifiable by their traditional clothing designs, which are often unique to a particular village or community. As one designer from South Africa puts it, “local garments celebrate what is unique to us as a country, as a continent, and as a collective of people who share a similar perspective.”

Intersection between durability and local factors in the context of garments and textiles

The third research question deals with the overlap between the two concepts analyzed separately in questions one and two.

Trend 7: traditional garments and textiles are more durable

Traditional textiles are characteristic of a particular community in a particular region. In Norway, two participants involved in the preservation of traditional clothing emphasized the difference between the durability of traditional clothing made according to tradition by people who know their craft and mass-produced clothing from abroad. One of them explains: “How much we value our clothing is something we do not think about because it is just there. We demonstrate this by taking care of it and investing time in it.” This difference is even greater with modern clothing, as they claim that Norwegians cared for their traditional clothes more carefully than everyday clothes. Additionally, they are often passed down from generation to generation as they could also be quite expensive, making them last for decades.

One participant from Sumba, who works with ikat textiles, said that she inherited an ikat from her great-grandmother, which is now over 60 years old. An upcycler from South Africa stated that the perceived value of these traditional textiles and garments is often much higher when they are part of ceremonies or rituals. This value makes pieces more than just material objects, and people take great care of them.

Trend 8: local contexts influence garment durability

Garment durability is dynamic as people and their bodies change over time. Consequently, the environment in which clothing is worn also changes. This is particularly evident in the global second-hand market, in which garments are transported from one region to another. Climate and culture influence whether people can and want to wear garments for a long time. One participant from Ghana, who works for an NGO, emphasized: “If you are not able to keep the garment at home and feel that it does not belong there, why would someone want it elsewhere? We should think about the context, what material works in a certain environment, and what impact it has on the people who live in that environment.” The founder of a French NGO shares a similar sentiment: “Often garment exporters assume that they know what the importing countries want or do not want, but in reality, they do not.” Consequently, communities on the receiving end have begun to adapt garments to make them suitable for their local culture and climate, whether through upcycling or changing the way they are worn. This local value creation ensures that garments can last long.

Discussion

Here, the authors discuss the study findings with respect to the three research questions, followed by a revision of a self-developed framework.

Question 1: How do different global communities define and perceive garment durability?

Of the three main trends that emerged, the first two showed a clear split in their general understanding of garment durability. Some participants, particularly garment sorters, garment manufacturers and second-hand retailers, took a more objective and technical approach. They naturally referred to quality and tests to quantify physical properties. Other participants, particularly designers, educators, or members of NGOs, took a more personal and emotional approach, explaining that the durability of garments is the value that the wearer creates through use and is constantly evolving. To the authors’ knowledge, previous studies have not identified this difference in approach. Without factual evidence, many participants associated the durability of garments and textiles with brands. Thus, certain brands are seen as high quality and desirable, whereas others are not. The high value in garment brand has made it one of the most important criteria for clothing sorters. This also applies to price, as cheap clothing is seen as inferior, but branded clothing is perceived as high-quality, even though this is not always the case32,33.

Question 2: How do different global communities define and perceive the notion of local in the context of garments and textiles?

Participants in all regions understood “local” to mean something produced and sold in the same geographical region, which does not have a clearly defined size. This is in line with the vision of Fibershed, a non-profit organization that helps to create regional fiber systems and understands local in this context to mean the entire value chain of textile production that is located in a specific geographical area29. For some participants, the relevant parts of the local value chain begin with fiber production and ends with finished garments. For others, the inclusion of fiber production is unrealistic because of the scarce supply of raw materials and production facilities. Others claim that local presence extends to the utilization phase, which implies minimum use in the same area. A second trend questions whether employment-related value should remain in the region for it to be considered local. Local influence includes a sense of tradition shaped by specific expertise native to that region. These results correspond with the findings of a qualitative study on local clothing, in which five categories emerged from the data: production, place-specific garments, local clothing habits, home-based production, and local circulation17.

Question 3: How do durability and local factors intersect in the context of garments and textiles?

Several participants claimed that garments or textiles made according to local traditions, sometimes with ceremonial value, were inherently more durable. High cultural values lead to special care that is often not given to modern or conventional garments. This also applies to inherited pieces that last for decades over several generations. Furthermore, garments must be “place-specific”29 or suitable for the local context, that is, the culture and climate. A garment can be designed to be durable, but in practice and on a large scale, it does not mean it will last. Research shows that it can only lead to resource savings if the same product is maintained in active use for longer and an additional or replacement purchase is delayed34. Garments moved between regions (e.g., second-hand clothing trade), may not suit local contexts and often require adaptation, through upcycling or styling, to suit local settings.

A revised framework integrating the local dimension

Understanding garment durability has been an evolving process for the authors, with the aim of taking a holistic and representative approach. The authors previously developed an analytical framework, depicted in Fig. 1a, to conduct a semi-structured literature review25. This framework examines the impact of the different life cycle stages of a product on its intrinsic or extrinsic durability or resilience. It can also be used as a practical tool for the industry. The authors emphasized that the extrinsic dimension, which is often neglected, should be considered equally to the intrinsic dimension.

Evolution of the durability and resilience framework. (a) Original and (b) revised framework to suit the context of garment durability explained in this study.

Based on the findings of the present study, Fig. 1a was revised to Fig. 1b; level A now includes “intrinsic” and “extrinsic,” and level B only includes “garment durability” and not “resilience.” Recognizing that both the intrinsic and extrinsic dimension of garment durability are essential, as these terms have been widely adopted by industry organizations and legislators working on regulations, such as PEFCR11 and France’s environmental labelling35. However, treating them separately risks overlooking the need for a holistic approach. Hence, framing them under the umbrella of garment durability rather than two distinct concepts.

The authors have further developed the definition of garment durability as “an ongoing interaction between the garment and its changing environment(s) and user(s), enabling it to move through different life cycles via the practices of care, mending, and repair”18. The term “change” here coincides with the characteristics of resilience. Although the authors consider resilience to be relevant, they recognize that it would be confusing to add another term to the global discussion. Therefore, the focus should be on what durability distinctly means in garments and textiles. Level C remains unchanged and calls for the equal consideration of all life cycle phases. Based on the results of this study, a new level representing the local context is added to surround the framework so it considers changes in the users and the environment, which have a demonstrable influence on whether garments will last.

Global trends across local communities

Several common themes emerged in all regions, illustrating the connection between the communities of the Global North and Global South. Second-hand retailers recognize that garment quality continues to decline while their quantity continues to increase, making resale and remanufacturing increasingly difficult. This is exemplified by second-hand sellers from Ghana who cannot sell garments with polyester linings and upcyclers from France who cannot find ways to upcycle low-quality synthetic garments. Regardless of their geographical location, members of the community want to revitalize their local garment industry and ensure that the positive impact remains in the region. “By the locals for the locals,” as the founder of an NGO in Sumba phrased. The importance of traditional garments is also shared, with those from different communities “speaking their own language,” as two hand weavers from South Africa stated. Handmade lace from France, Kente fabrics from Ghana, Sumbanese Ikat from Indonesia, Bunad clothing from Norway, or Shweshwe cloth from South Africa were all cared for by their wearers. These garments and textiles are passed down from generation to generation and have an intangible value. In contrast, a participant working on the preservation of traditional clothing from Norway claimed that modern clothing does not receive the same level of care.

Conclusions

This study obtained multiple perspectives on durability and local factors, both as distinct and overlapping concepts, in relation to garments and textiles. The authors revised an existing framework to incorporate local notions and achieve a more representative approach to durability, focusing on unification instead of making distinctions regarding intrinsic or extrinsic dimensions, durability or resilience, and the Global North or Global South. Although laws currently being drafted may only apply to one region, their benefits and impacts are often global, albeit unevenly distributed. Garment durability can be part of a circular transition when considered holistically across the entire life cycle in changing local contexts. The results of this study, which used semi-structured interviews to understand the durability of garments from local communities spread across the globe, can be used to inform legislative bodies.

This study had some limitations. Although the selected participants were from five different regions of the world, the sample size may not be sufficiently representative. Despite the authors’ endeavors to identify the most appropriate respondents within the value chain, some gaps remain. In Norway, for example, the authors could not set up an interview within the data collection period with a sorting center, a design student, or a craftsman. In Sumba, Indonesia, the absence of a formally developed industry may lead to an incomplete and unrepresentative picture. Additionally, language barriers must be considered as most participants were not native English speakers. For one participant from South Africa, a word for durability in her native language of IsiZulu was not used in the context of textiles. A Norwegian participant explained that Norwegians do not have similar word diversity in English, which can lead to misunderstandings when the words are translated. Therefore, holistically understanding a term that is not well understood at the linguistic level by some local communities is difficult and perhaps even impossible. Furthermore, although the interviews were conducted by or with the help of locals, the interpretation and data analysis were performed solely by the authors, particularly Author 1, who was from the Global North. Therefore, the findings and results may be influenced by a Western perspective.

To improve the understanding of the intersection of durability and local context in clothing and textiles, other communities must be surveyed to make the data more robust. Furthermore, as upcycling was regularly mentioned as a method to make garments last. The potential environmental and social impacts of upcycling must be considered, given its link to local cultures, environments, and existing craft skills and abilities. Finally, the eight identified trends can be formalized as criteria to quantify garment durability and help actors throughout the value chain, such as brands that want to create locally durable garments.

Methods

Study design

We used a qualitative research methodology rooted in a value-based evaluation framework, which describes and understands rather than predicts and controls37. PAR is characterized by its participatory and action-oriented nature38. It enhances practice through its collaborative nature and encourages researchers to recognize knowledge co-construction through theory, practice, experience, and articulation processes3139. In particular, PAR assumes that “those who have been most systematically excluded, suppressed, or denied possess particularly insightful knowledge”40. Semi-structured interviews enable researchers to understand the behavior and interactions of actors41 and capture their voices42.

In this study, interviews were conducted in five different countries: France and Norway in the Global North and Ghana, Indonesia, and South Africa in the Global South. Table 1 lists the key indicators for the selected countries. They were chosen to enable us to analyze countries with different sociocultural, economic, and political landscapes, but also for practical reasons, as they were each accessible to at least one of the authors. Many global challenges such as climate change and economic inequality affect countries in the northern and southern regions differently, and the inclusion of both perspectives is important when studying these challenges. This allowed for comparisons across global communities and helped identify universal patterns and context-specific nuances. Such diversity enriched our findings and increased generalizability. Participants

The key informants (n = 73) were selected across the five regions through purposive sampling based on their technical and strategic knowledge of the design, production, use, reuse, sorting, repurposing, and recycling of garments and textiles. The geographical diversity supports the contextual generalizability of the results to achieve a certain degree of saturation43–45. Interview procedure

The interviews were conducted between January and May 2024, with each lasting an average of 45 min. This allowed for an in-depth exploration of the topic by understanding the behavior and interactions of the actors42. Approximately 47% of the interviews were conducted online, and the remaining 53% in person. The questions were aimed at understanding the participants’ personal experiences and professional positions in the fashion industry. Some questions focused on how the participants took care of their garments and the reasons for their preference regarding certain garments, which indirectly inquired about garment durability and local factors. The research topic was also directly addressed by questions on their knowledge of durability. Other questions were related to their professional position.

All the interviews were recorded to produce full transcripts. The authors emphasized the participatory nature of the interviews through open dialogues that shared power with the participants and created safe spaces for reciprocal and honest exchanges that captured their descriptive knowledge of the research subject46,47. Reciprocity was encouraged by sharing a summary of the transcripts with the participants to solicit their feedback, primarily to ensure an accurate representation of their perspectives and encourage an ongoing conversation between the researchers and participants. Twenty-five participants provided feedback on the transcripts, which were distributed across all five regions. Their feedback was incorporated into a revised version of the transcript, which was then shared with the participants.

Data analysis

To analyze the data, the transcripts were processed using Transcribe, a software that automatically generates transcripts from audio recordings. They were then read by the author who conducted the interviews for accuracy verification. The authors used inductive coding, which is exploratory in nature and allows themes to emerge from the data. The summary of the transcripts was structured by grouping the statements around themes that emerged from the interviews, such as on the durability of garments, repair, upcycling, design, and legislation. These summarized versions were further refined and linked to the statements of the other interviewees to investigate which trends emerged and whether they could answer one of the three research questions. The data were first grouped by region to identify local trends and the extent to which they were comparable to or different from those in other regions. In addition, the data were grouped by stakeholders across regions to determine, for example, whether fashion brands in all regions have different opinions on garment durability compared with second-hand retailers.

Ethical considerations and value-informed evaluation framework

The RIPH Committee (RIPH stands for «recherche impliquant la personne humaine”) » of the LAMIH UMR CNRS 8201 laboratory has reviewed and approved the research protocol used for this study. The committee concluded that the project complies with the ethical standards and regulations for research involving human participants and fulfils the ethical requirements of our institution. All participants signed a consent form to participate in the interview and allow their answers to be used for data analysis. They had the choice to give their full name, only their first name, or to remain anonymous. Although interviewing is a popular technique of data collection in qualitative research, it has been criticized for leaning towards individualistic westernized assumptions and theories48,49ignoring the values of those where colonizing research is used50. Therefore, this technique risks perpetuating the colonizing legacy of Western science, especially when interviewing people from communities in the Global South. Decolonizing research focuses on the concerns and worldviews of non-Western individuals by conducting research on Indigenous communities. This is an ongoing process of becoming, unlearning, and relearning about who you are as a researcher and educator, while taking responsibility for the participants55.

Part of this responsibility is to consult cultural insiders or brokers for regions where the interviewer is an outsider, such as Ghana, Indonesia, and Norway. Because these “insiders” belong to the culture of the respective groups, they have insights into and familiarity with it48,56. A value-explicit context served as the evaluation framework throughout the research process. This study adopted the framework developed by Fletcher and Tham3which outlines the need for action research in the fashion and textiles industry. Explicit values were derived from a paradigm or accepted model of how ideas relate to each other. Eight values were applied by the authors as follows: (1) Multiple centers, by obtaining perspectives from five different regions; (2) Interdependency, by linking concepts such as durability and local influence in a garment and textile context; (3) Diverse ways of knowing, by integrating perspectives from actors across the value chain from the different regions; (4) Co-creation, by collaborating with different authors and the interviewees; (5) Action research, by using PAR; (6) Grounded imagination, by allowing open dialogue through semi-structured interviews; (7) Care of world, by helping develop a global understanding rooted in local perspectives of garment durability; and (8) Care of self, by allowing participants and researchers to build lasting connections based on these shared values.
