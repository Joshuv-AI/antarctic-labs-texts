Differential transcriptomic landscapes of olive cultivars facing Xylella fastidiosa infection
Authors: Valentina Fanelli, Monica Marilena Miazzi, Silvia Procino, Carmine Fruggiero, Lucas Cafferati Beltrame, Mariateresa Volpicella, Francesca Taranto, Isabella Mascio, Vito Montilon, Leonardo Susca, Nunzio D’Agostino, Cinzia Montemurro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe bacterium Xylella fastidiosa subsp. pauca (Xfp) is the causal agent of Olive Quick Decline Syndrome (OQDS), a disease characterized by leaf scorch, irregular periderm development, branch desiccation, general growth arrest, and eventual plant death, leading to extensive damage to olive groves (Olea europaea L.) and severely impacting agricultural production in the Apulia region, Italy’s leading producer of olive oil. To date, no cure for the disease exists, and most olive germplasm is susceptible, with only a few varieties showing resistance. Among them, the Leccino cultivar displays resistance to the pathogen, exhibiting little to no symptoms after infection. In contrast, other varieties, such as Oliva Rossa, are highly susceptible, while cultivars like Donna Francesca exhibit an intermediate level of susceptibility. Only limited studies have investigated the genetic basis underlying these differential responses.ResultsComparative transcriptomic analysis of the above-mentioned olive cultivars revealed distinct and divergent defence strategies. The susceptible Oliva Rossa showed a broad but largely ineffective transcriptional reprogramming, including the downregulation of cytoskeleton-related genes. In contrast, the resistant Leccino displayed a more targeted activation of sulphur assimilation pathways, antioxidant responses, and cell wall–associated proteins, enabling more effective pathogen containment. The cultivar Donna Francesca exhibited a reduced transcriptional response overall, consistent with its intermediate level of susceptibility. Only 5 of 1,758 differentially expressed genes were shared across all cultivars, highlighting highly cultivar-specific responses and the overall complexity of the host–pathogen interaction. Resistance appears to be associated with a focused and limited transcriptional activation, susceptibility with widespread but ineffective gene reprogramming, and intermediate susceptibility with modest yet efficient transcriptional adjustments.ConclusionEach olive cultivar responds to Xfp infection by modulating a distinct set of genes, suggesting that resistance and susceptibility are governed by different molecular mechanisms. This cultivar-specific transcriptional behaviour highlights the complexity of host–pathogen interactions and expanding analyses to additional genotypes could provide further insights into the genetic and molecular bases of these diverse responses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09135-z.Keywords: Olea europaea L., Biotic stress, Bacterial infection, Host-pathogen interaction, Olive Quick Decline Syndrome, Transcriptional reprogramming

Introduction

Olive Quick Decline Syndrome (OQDS) has emerged as a severe phytosanitary threat to olive-growing regions worldwide since its first identification in 2013 in the Apulia region of southern Italy [1]. The disease is caused by the gram-negative bacterium Xylella fastidiosa subsp. pauca (hereinafter referred to as Xfp), which is transmitted by xylem-feeding leafhoppers (Phylaenus spumarious). Its spread is shaped by multiple epidemiological factors, including pathogen dynamics within host plants, interactions between the bacterium and its hosts, and prevailing environmental conditions [2, 3]. In Apulia, the rapid northward spread of the bacterium has been facilitated by insufficient political interventions, favourable conditions for the growth of vector populations, and the extensive cultivation of two highly susceptible native olive varieties, Cellina di Nardò and Ogliarola salentina [4, 5]. Xfp is highly polyphagous, capable of colonizing the xylem of at least 638 plant species [6], a number that continues to increase as new genomic data become available [4].

The external symptoms of OQDS include leaf scorch, irregular periderm development, branch desiccation, general growth arrest, and eventual plant death [1]. However, the processes governing host colonization and symptom development remain only partially understood [4]. It is widely accepted that these symptoms arise from xylem vessel occlusion, caused by bacterial aggregates embedded in an exopolysaccharide matrix, as well as tyloses and gums produced by the plant in response to infection [7–9]. Xfp spreads through the xylem by traversing adjacent vessels via pit membranes, which measure 5–20 nm in diameter. These membranes facilitate the movement of water and solutes and play a crucial role in regulating the cavitation threshold [10, 11]. To move between xylem vessels, Xfp enzymatically degrades the pit membranes, which are composed of hemicellulose, cellulose and pectins, triggering the formation of pectin gels, crystals and tyloses, ultimately leading to vessel occlusion [12].

In grapevine, where the disease has been extensively studied (Pierce’s disease caused by Xylella fastidiosa subsp. fastidiosa), significant transcriptional reprogramming has been linked to tyloses formation and carbon storage partitioning, occurring before visible symptoms appear [13]. Genes associated with tyloses formation, particularly those involved in ethylene signalling and cell wall biogenesis, as well as genes related to drought stress responses, were upregulated, whereas genes associated with photosynthesis and carbon fixation were downregulated. These molecular responses align with the observed degradation of starch and the synthesis of tyloses in ray cells [14].

Additionally, in grapevine, specific resistance genes have been identified in certain wild species such as Vitis arizonica, which contribute to varying levels of resistance [15]. Although PdR1 (Pierce’s disease resistance 1) is recognized as a major resistance locus, it accounts for only about 55% of the observed phenotypic variation. Other resistance-associated loci have been mapped to chromosomes 3, 7, 9, 10, and 15, highlighting the complex genetic basis of resistance [16–20]. Notably, resistance appears to be influenced by environmental factors, such as climate. Grapevine populations from regions with lower temperatures (< 10 °C) exhibit reduced infection rates, likely due to decreased pathogen pressure under colder environments [15]. This evidence suggests that the resistance in Vitis is polygenic and behaves as a quantitative trait.

Despite extensive research efforts, the aetiological mechanisms of OQDS remain poorly understood, and effective control strategies have yet to be developed. However, recent advances in next-generation sequencing (NGS) technologies for genome and transcriptome analysis, combined with functional gene studies, provides new opportunities to unravel the molecular processes driving disease development and host–pathogen interactions [21]. In infected areas, genotypes exhibiting varied responses to Xfp infection have emerged, ranging from low symptom severity or complete absence of symptoms to minimal bacterial loads [5, 22]. These putatively resistant genotypes within the local olive germplasm could help broaden the genetic base of cultivated olives in affected areas and may play a key role in elucidating the mechanisms of resistance to Xfp.

Few studies have attempted to explore the molecular responses of olive tree to Xfp, including the influence of variety, putative resistance mechanisms, bacterial transmission, and the role of the microbiome. Giampetruzzi et al. [23] were the first to investigate the transcriptomic responses of the resistant Leccino and susceptible Ogliarola Salentina varieties grown in infected areas, compared with healthy olive plants cultivated under non-infected areas. Their study revealed that the Leccino resistance to the pathogen is marked by the upregulation of genes associate with cell wall remodelling and receptor-like proteins, suggesting an activation of defence mechanisms that help limit pathogen proliferation. In contrast, Ogliarola Salentina showed comparatively weaker defence responses, consistent with its high susceptibility to Xfp infection. Arnholdt-Schmitt et al. [24] explored the roles of acetaldehyde release and cyanide-mediated respiration in olive tree resistance to Xfp. Their findings highlighted the alternative oxidase (AOX) gene family as a potential molecular marker of resilience under Xfp infection and various stress conditions. Complementary studies by La Notte et al. [25] and Abou Kubaa et al. [26], based on the evaluation of olive germplasm under high disease pressure, reported that certain Leccino offspring exhibit enhanced resistance, emphasizing the potential of incorporating these traits into breeding programs aimed at developing more resilient olive varieties. Amoia et al. [27] performed a transcriptomic analysis of Xfp under both in vitro and in planta conditions, revealing that overexpression of bacteriocins plays a critical role in bacterial survival and competitiveness of Xfp within the plant host. Additionally, transcriptomic profiling highlighted substantial changes in bacterial gene expression during host colonization, particularly in genes associated with biofilm formation, virulence, and adaptation to the olive xylem environment. Complementing these findings, a recent study on the role and composition of microbiome of resistant versus susceptible cultivars showed that the microbiome of the susceptible Cellina di Nardò was highly variable and sensitive to infection levels, while the resistant Leccino exhibited a higher abundance and diversity of beneficial endophytes producing antimicrobial compounds [28]. Collectively, these studies highlight the complex interplay between the genetic and physiological traits of both the olive host and Xfp, offering valuable insights into plant-pathogen interactions and informing future breeding and disease management strategies.

In recent years, the increasing application of global gene expression analyses to the study of plant-pathogen interactions has provided important insights into host defence responses and pathogen dynamics [29]. In the context of OQDS, these approaches offer a powerful framework for elucidating disease aetiology by enabling comparative transcriptome studies aimed at identifying key molecular pathways and genes involved in disease development [30]. The aim of this research was to compare gene expression across three olive cultivars with distinct responses to OQDS: the resistant cultivar Leccino, the susceptible cultivar Oliva Rossa, and moderately susceptible cultivar Donna Francesca. By analysing the whole transcriptome in both infected and non-infected plants, differentially expressed genes were investigated, thereby providing insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying host responses to OQDS.

Materials and methods

Plant material and infection assays

The plants were sourced from the olive collection at the Department of Soil, Plant and Food Sciences, University of Bari, Italy. Plant material was propagated by grafting scions of the three varieties onto healthy, two-year-old olive seedling rootstocks. Three biological replicates of each cultivar (Leccino, Donna Francesca, and Oliva Rossa) were artificially inoculated at 19 months of age with an Xfp bacterial suspension prepared in sterile phosphate buffered saline (PBS). The suspension was adjusted to an optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of 0.5, which corresponds to 1 × 10⁸ colony-forming units (CFU) per mL. Three additional plants per cultivar were injected with PBS alone and served as healthy controls. The bacterial strain used for inoculation was previously isolated from a naturally infected olive tree in the OQDS epidemic area and maintained on Buffered Charcoal Yeast Extract (BCYE) medium [31] at 28 °C, with periodic subculturing (every 8–10 days). Inoculation was performed by applying a 10 µL drop of the bacterial suspension onto the plant stem and puncturing the stem three to four times with a dissecting needle to facilitate infection [32]. For subsequent sampling, three inoculation points were marked on each plant. Plants were cultivated in 3 L pots in a quarantine greenhouse authorized for handling Xylella fastidiosa at the Department of Soil, Plant and Food Sciences, University of Bari, Italy. They were grown under natural light conditions, with 30–40% relative humidity (RH), an average temperature of approximately 25 °C, and drip irrigation every two days. The experiment followed a completely randomized design. All plants were monitored for twelve months to assess the development of characteristic symptoms and to confirm the presence of Xfp via real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) prior to sampling for transcriptome analysis (Fig. 1).

Assessment of symptom severity and diagnosis of Xfp infection

Symptom severity was visually assessed by estimating the percentage of shoots exhibiting leaf scorch and desiccation relative to the total number of shoots per plant. For diagnostic assays, 4–5 mature leaves were collected three, six and twelve months after inoculation from different positions around the inoculation site. Samples were stored in plastic bags at 4 °C and analysed within 3 days of collection. For DNA extraction, 0.5 g of leaf midrib and petiole tissue from each sample were placed into BIOREBA extraction bags (BioReba, Basel, Switzerland) containing 5 mL of CTAB extraction buffer (2% hexadecyl trimethyl-ammonium bromide, 0.1 M Tris-HCl pH 8, 20 mM EDTA, 1.4 M NaCl, 1% PVP-40). The plant tissue was crushed and homogenized using a HOMEX 6 homogenizer (Bioreba, Switzerland). A 1 mL aliquot of the homogenate was transferred to a 2 mL microcentrifuge tube for DNA purification. Genomic DNA extraction and PCR assays were carried out following the EPPO diagnostic standard PM 7/24 (5) [33]. DNA concentration was quantified using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Wilmington, DE, USA) to normalize nucleic acid input for subsequent qPCR reactions. The absolute number of bacterial cells per sample was determined using a standard curve generated from cycle threshold (Ct) values obtained from. ten-fold serial dilutions (from 10− 7 to 10− 4) of a bacterial suspension adjusted to an optical density (OD600) of 0.5, corresponding to 108 CFU mL− 1. For transcriptome analysis, three pathogen-positive plants of each cultivar were selected, together with three mock-inoculated (control) plants used as controls, twelve months after inoculation.

RNA isolation and sequencing

For each cultivar, three biological replicates of control plants and three replicates of Xfp-infected plants were analysed. Total RNA was extracted from leaf and petiole tissues using the Spectrum Plant Total RNA Kit (Sigma Aldrich, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA-seq libraries were prepared using the TruSeq Stranded Total RNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

This library preparation procedure includes a ribosomal RNA (rRNA) depletion step based on biotinylated probes that selectively hybridize to rRNA molecules. The probe–rRNA complexes are then removed, thereby minimizing ribosomal contamination.

RNA concentration was measured with a NanoDrop ND-1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA), and RNA integrity was assessed using an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent, USA). Libraries were generated from 10 µg of RNA from both control and Xfp-infected plants and sequenced on the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, USA). The raw Illumina reads have been deposited in the Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under the accession number PRJNA935531.

Read pre-processing, mapping and summarization

Data quality control of raw reads (FASTQ format, 100 bp paired end, Phred + 33) was performed using FastQC (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/), and the resulting reports were aggregated with MultiQC [34]. Illumina technical sequences and low-quality reads were removed using Trimmomatic version 0.40 [35], retaining reads with a minimum length of 75 nucleotides. To remove potential contaminants, reads were aligned to the Xylella fastidiosa strain Temecula1 genome (RefSeq assembly accession: GCF_000007245.1) using STAR version 2.7.10 A [36] with default settings. High-quality, contamination-free reads were then aligned to the reference genome of Olea europaea subsp. europaea var. europaea, cv. Leccino (OLGENOME Project - MIPAAF DM 13938) using STAR version 2.7.10 A with default settings. Both uniquely and multi-mapped reads were retained for downstream analysis. Gene-level raw counts were generated with featureCounts from the SubRead package [37], applying the “-M” and “–fraction” options to appropriately account for multi-mapped reads.

Differential gene expression analysis and gene ontology (GO) enrichment

To assess variability among biological replicates and identify potential outliers, Pearson’s correlation coefficients were calculated, and a principal component analysis (PCA) was performed. Raw read counts were normalized using both edgeR trimmed mean of M-values (TMM) method and the median of ratios approach implemented in DESeq2 version 1.36 [38] Low-abundance genes were filtered out using HTSFilter version 1.36.0 [39]. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using both edgeR and DESeq2, applying a false discovery rate (FDR) threshold of 5% and a minimum log2 fold change (FC) of ± 2. Only DEGs consistently detected by both methods were retained for downstream analyses. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis, covering molecular function (MF), cellular component (CC), and biological process (BP) domains, was conducted using BiNGO [40], a Cytoscape (version 3.9.1) plugin.

DEGs were annotated and automatically classified according to GO terms using the annotation provided in the Leccino GTF (Gene Transfer Format) file. In addition, Mercator 4 V2.0 [41] was used to assign each DEG to MapMan bins (functional categories/subcategories) [42, 43] The resulting mapping file was then imported into MapMan v3.0.0 for data visualization and pathway analysis.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Nine genes identified by RNA-seq as consistently upregulated or downregulated in response to Xfp across all three varieties were selected for validation by qRT-PCR. Gene-specific primers were designed using Primer3Plus (https://www.primer3plus.com) and Oligo Explorer 1.1.2 and are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using the iScript™ cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Quantitative RT-PCR reactions were prepared in a 10 µL volume containing 5 µL of SsoAdvanced™ Universal SYBR® Green Supermix (Bio-Rad), 0.4 µL of each 10 mM primer, and 1 µL of 20-fold diluted cDNA. Amplification was performed on a CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad) using a two-step cycling protocol: an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 30 s. A melting curve analysis was included to verify amplification specificity. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the ΔΔCt method [44], with EF1-α serving as the internal reference gene [45].

Results

Xylella fastidiosa infection

Symptom severity and bacterial population size were evaluated to determine cultivar susceptibility. Twelve months post inoculation, characteristic symptoms of OQDS appeared on Oliva Rossa which reached a mean severity of 64%, at the final scoring time. In contrast, all plants of Leccino and Donna Francesca remained asymptomatic throughout the trial (Figs. 1 and 2). Twelve months after infection, qPCR assay confirmed differential bacterial loads among the three varieties. In particular, Leccino exhibited the lowest bacterial load, with Cq values ranging from 28.3 to 31.0, whereas Oliva Rossa had the highest Xfp population, with Cq values between 22.1 and 23.9. Donna Francesca showed an intermediate bacterial load, with Cq values ranging from 24.4 to 29.5 (Fig. 2), although values were not significantly different from those of the resistant reference cultivar.

This approach aimed to identify DEGs associated with defence mechanisms and the cultivars’ resistance or susceptibility to the pathogen.

Overall count of RNA-seq reads

A PCA plot based on the normalized count matrix was generated to assess the similarity and variability among biological replicates (Supplementary Fig. S1 and S2). The analysis showed tight clustering of replicates for each sample, indicating consistent gene expression profiles. However, one replicate of the Oliva Rossa cultivar was identified as an outlier due to its distinct separation from the others and was excluded from further analyses, leaving two replicates for this group. Removing this outlier improved the reliability of downstream differential expression analyses. For each cultivar, gene expression profiles of control plants were compared to those of Xfp-infected plants and DEGs were identified using both edgeR and DESeq (Supplementary Fig. S3), resulting in a consensus set of 1,758 DEGs detected by both tools.

Transcriptomic reprogramming in response to Xfp infection was particularly pronounced in the susceptible cultivar Oliva Rossa, where 1,485 DEGs were identified. In contrast, the resistant Leccino and moderately susceptible Donna Francesca exhibited more moderate responses, with 230 and 198 DEGs, respectively. Notably, approximately 72% of DEGs in Donna Francesca were downregulated (Fig. 3a; Supplementary Table S2). Among the 1,758 DEGs, only 5 genes were differentially expressed across all cultivars (FDR < 0.05; |log₂ fold change| ≥ 2), highlighting the largely cultivar-specific nature of transcriptional responses. These 5 DEGs were associated with diverse biological processes (Supplementary Table S3). Oe11g759080 encodes an EG45-like domain–containing protein implicated in systemic acquired resistance, alternative respiration, and responses to hypoxia. Oe6g753850 encodes a PH-, RCC1-, and FYVE-domain–containing protein (Praf4) primarily involved in hormone signalling pathways, particularly cytokinin and abscisic acid signalling. Oe8g782300 encodes a poly(A)-binding protein associated with mRNA processing and RNA-binding functions. In contrast, no functional annotation was available for Oe1g727030 and Oe4g572670.

Functional annotation and pathway enrichment analysis of DEGs

To gain insights into the biological processes and molecular mechanisms involved in the response to Xfp infection, DEGs from the three cultivars were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis. This approach provided a comprehensive overview of the key functional categories represented among the differentially expressed genes (Fig. 4, Supplementary Fig. S4, S5, S6, and S7).

Each dot represents an enriched GO term; dot size reflects the number of genes associated with the term, and dot colour indicates the adjusted p-value. The x-axis shows fold enrichment relative to the genome background. A Leccino (LE-upregulated gene set); B Donna Francesca (DF-downregulated gene set); C Oliva Rossa (OR-upregulated gene set)

DEGs were distributed across several functional categories in the three varieties. In the Leccino cultivar, upregulated genes were significantly enriched for GO terms related to sulphate assimilation and ATP sulfurylase activity (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Fig. S5, Supplementary Table S4). Sulphate assimilation is crucial for hydrogen sulphide (H₂S) biosynthesis, a key signalling molecule that activates plant defence pathways against biotic agents. Additionally, the GO terms “plastid stroma” and “chloroplast stroma” were significantly enriched among the upregulated genes, consistent with the plastid-localized nature of sulphate reduction in plants. Conversely, downregulated genes were primarily associated with various classes of aminotransferase activity, indicating a possible inhibition of amino acid metabolism (Supplementary Fig. S4 and S5, Supplementary Table S5). As for the Donna Francesca cultivar, no significant functional enrichment was observed among the upregulated genes. In contrast, downregulated genes were significantly enriched for GO terms related to “response to stimulus” and “response to stress”, suggesting a downregulation of general defence-related pathways (Fig. 4B, Supplementary Fig. S6, Supplementary Table S7).

In Oliva Rossa, significant enrichment was observed for genes linked to defence responses, response to bacterial molecules, and response to bacteria, reflecting activation of pathogen recognition and immune signalling pathways. Several transferase and glucosyltransferase activities were also enriched, suggesting enhanced secondary metabolism and glycosylation processes. Additionally, genes associated with cell wall structure, including plant-type cell wall components, glucan-modifying activities, external encapsulating structures, and galactolipid biosynthesis, were upregulated, indicating structural reinforcement and remodelling of the cell wall. Genes associated with oxidoreductase activity, cellular responses to water deprivation, and signalling pathways, including calmodulin binding and cell surface receptor-linked pathways, were also upregulated, reflecting activation of stress signalling networks and reactive oxygen species-mediated defences (Fig. 4C, Supplementary Fig. S7, Supplementary Table S7).

Oliva Rossa also displays a pattern of downregulated genes including those involved in microtubule plus-end organization, establishment and maintenance of cytoskeleton polarity, and overall cytoskeletal regulation (Supplementary Fig. S4 and S7, Supplementary Table S8). Overall, these results reveal distinct patterns of transcriptomic reprogramming among the three cultivars, reflecting their differential molecular responses to Xfp infection.

An overview of the distribution of DEGs across functional categories in the three olive cultivars was obtained by mapping them onto MapMan bins to visualize gene expression changes within the context of cellular metabolism, with particular focus on the “Cell Function” category, which shows the largest differences and is biologically relevant during Xfp infection (Fig. 5). This category encompasses key pathways involved in basic cellular processes that can influence pathogen recognition, signal transduction, and cellular defence responses. The analysis revealed cultivar-specific transcriptional responses following infection.

Validation of RNAseq data by qRT-PCR and identification of candidate genes

To assess the reliability and generalizability of the RNA-seq findings, qRT-PCR was performed on nine genes selected based on their significant induction or repression in response to Xfp infection across all three cultivars (Fig. 6). Priority was given to genes that were upregulated in the resistant Leccino and concurrently downregulated in the susceptible Oliva Rossa, with the aim of identifying molecular components that may actively contribute to defence responses in Leccino.

The selected genes represented diverse biological processes, including heavy metal accumulation, disease response, and cell wall remodelling (Supplementary Table S1). Notably, two genes lacked functional annotation, suggesting they may correspond to novel or poorly characterized genes potentially involved in the defence response. The qRT-PCR expression profiles closely matched the RNA-seq data, with a r² > 0.84, confirming the robustness and accuracy of the digital transcriptomic analysis (Fig. 7). Expression patterns revealed that, apart from Oe11g759080, all validated genes were consistently downregulated in infected Oliva Rossa plants compared to mock-inoculated controls. In contrast, these genes were upregulated in infected Donna Francesca and even more strongly in the resistant Leccino, suggesting an active molecular response and supporting their potential role in mediating defence against Xfp.

Discussion

Xfp is the bacterial agent responsible for OQDS, one of the most devastating diseases affecting olive trees in Italy. Disease containment has proven largely ineffective, mainly due to the absence of fully reliable control measures and the complexity of managing both the pathogen and its insect vectors. At present, the most effective management strategy relies on the cultivation of resistant or tolerant varieties. Four cultivars, Leccino, FS17, Lecciana, and Leccio del Corno, have shown resistance traits [5, 46] and are currently authorized for replanting in infected areas. However, it is important to note that these cultivars share a partial genetic background with Leccino, raising concerns about the long-term durability of resistance. Available evidence indicates a form of partial resistance or tolerance rather than complete immunity, characterized by reduced bacterial colonization and slower symptom progression. This type of quantitative resistance is generally considered more durable than single-gene (qualitative) resistance, as it exerts lower selective pressure on the pathogen. Nevertheless, the long-term stability of resistance to Xfp remains an open question and warrants continuous monitoring under field conditions. In this context, the use of resistant cultivars should be integrated with additional management strategies, such as vector control, appropriate agronomic practices, and epidemiological surveillance, to reduce the risk of resistance erosion over time. Expanding the genetic base through the identification of new resistant genotypes or targeted crosses, requires a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying resistance. Advances in integrated omics approaches have substantially enhanced our understanding of plant–pathogen interactions by providing a multilayered view of host responses and identifying key regulators of pathogenesis [47, 48]. Among these, transcriptomics is particularly informative, as it captures dynamic gene expression changes during infection. Since resistance to Xfp in olive, as in grapevine, appears to be a quantitative, rigorous transcriptomic analyses are essential for dissecting its genetic basis. RNA-seq has proven effective in identifying defence-related genes [49] and characterizing temporal expression dynamics during plant–pathogen interactions [50, 51].

In the present study, the experimental design was intentionally focused on a biologically relevant stage of infection, corresponding to a phase in which phenotypic divergence among cultivars becomes clearly detectable. While expanding the temporal scope could provide additional insights into the dynamics of host–pathogen interactions, it would also introduce increased variability and complexity, potentially obscuring the specific transcriptional signatures associated with resistance and susceptibility. Therefore, this work was conceived to provide a high-resolution snapshot of a critical stage of infection rather than a comprehensive temporal atlas. Future studies incorporating multiple time points will be valuable to further elucidate the temporal progression of these responses.

Within this framework, young plants of three olive varieties with contrasting susceptibility, Leccino (resistant), Donna Francesca (moderately susceptible), and Oliva Rossa (susceptible), were artificially inoculated with Xfp. Oliva Rossa developed typical OQDS symptoms and exhibited a significantly higher bacterial load by qPCR, whereas Leccino and Donna Francesca remained asymptomatic, consistent with previous reports [5]. These contrasting phenotypes provided a robust framework for comparative transcriptome profiling, which revealed distinct gene expression patterns and regulatory pathways associated with resistance, moderate susceptibility, and susceptibility to Xfp infection.

Magnitude of transcriptional reprogramming reflects olive cultivar responses to Xfp

Using two independent methods to identify DEGs provides a more robust and conservative framework for transcriptomic analysis [52]. Each analytical tool applies distinct statistical models, normalization strategies, and assumptions about data distribution. Therefore, integrating results from multiple methods helps reduce method-specific biases and minimizes the risk of false positives. By focusing on DEGs consistently detected by both approaches, the analysis gains higher confidence and reproducibility, ensuring that the identified genes truly represent biologically meaningful expression changes rather than technical artefacts. This conservative strategy strengthens the reliability of downstream analyses and provides a more solid foundation for identifying candidate genes involved in disease resistance mechanisms [53, 54].

Transcriptome profiling of treated and control samples from the three olive cultivars revealed notable differences in gene expression, particularly among genes associated with diverse biological processes, some of which are likely involved in resistance to Xfp. Interestingly, the extent of transcriptional changes varied markedly among the cultivars (Fig. 3). The susceptible cultivar, Oliva Rossa displayed a broader reprogramming of gene expression compared to the more resistant cultivars, Leccino and Donna Francesca. Many genes were uniquely modulated in each cultivar, highlighting cultivar-specific responses to infection. The most extensive transcriptional changes, including both up and downregulation, were observed in Oliva Rossa, suggesting that susceptibility may be associated with widespread cellular perturbations during infection. In contrast, the resistant cultivars Leccino and moderately susceptible Donna Francesca maintained a more stable transcriptome, potentially reflecting effective containment of the pathogen and limited disruption of cellular homeostasis. This pattern aligns with observations in other plant-pathogen systems, where susceptible genotypes often exhibit more pronounced transcriptional reprogramming than resistant ones. Examples include rice responses to Magnaporthe oryzae [55] and Fusarium fujikuroi [56], wheat responses to Rhizoctonia cerealis [57] and Fusarium head blight [58], and grapevine responses to Flavescence dorée [59–61]. These parallels suggest that the magnitude of transcriptional reprogramming may be a common feature distinguishing susceptible and resistant genotypes across plant species. Overall, our findings emphasize a differential transcriptional landscape during Xfp infection in olive trees, suggesting that the extent and specificity of gene expression changes are shaped by cultivar-specific resistance traits underlying susceptibility and resistance.

Transcriptional dynamics in Oliva Rossa

The highly susceptible olive cultivar Oliva Rossa exhibited a pronounced transcriptional response upon infection with Xfp, characterized by upregulation of genes involved in stress and defence responses, cell wall modification, and secondary metabolism. GO enrichment analysis revealed a significant overrepresentation of genes associated with general responses to stimuli, biotic stress, and interactions with other organisms (Fig. 4C, Supplementary Fig. S4), indicating that the plant perceives the pathogen and mounts a robust, yet ultimately insufficient, defence response. In particular, genes linked to defence processes, response to bacterial molecules, and response to bacteria were upregulated, reflecting the activation of pathogen recognition and immune signalling pathways. Enrichment of transferase and glucosyltransferase activities suggests enhanced secondary metabolism and glycosylation processes that may contribute to the synthesis or modification of defence-related compounds.

Concurrently, genes associated with cell wall organization, including plant-type cell wall components, glucan-modifying activities, external encapsulating structures, and galactolipid biosynthesis, were upregulated, indicating structural reinforcement and remodelling of the cell wall, a typical strategy to limit pathogen invasion. Additional upregulation of genes associated with oxidoreductase activity, cellular responses to water deprivation, and diverse signalling pathways (including calmodulin binding and cell surface receptor-mediated signalling) reflects the activation of stress signalling networks and reactive oxygen species-mediated defences. In addition, the induction of genes involved in secondary metabolite biosynthesis further suggests stimulation of chemical defence mechanisms. Despite this broad activation of defence pathways, these responses appear insufficient in this highly susceptible cultivar to halt pathogen colonization.

Interestingly, Oliva Rossa also displays a distinct pattern of downregulated genes, particularly those involved in microtubule plus-end organization, establishment and maintenance of cytoskeleton polarity, and overall cytoskeletal regulation. Proper cytoskeleton dynamics are essential for intracellular trafficking, cell wall deposition, and targeted secretion of antimicrobial compounds; thus, their suppression may compromise the efficiency of the plant’s immune response. In the context of a xylem-limited bacterium that induces vessel occlusion and disrupts water transport, cytoskeletal destabilization may further impair the plant’s ability to compartmentalize damage and reinforce vessel walls.

Gene expression patterns in Donna Francesca

In the moderately susceptible cultivar Donna Francesca, GO enrichment analysis of downregulated genes revealed coordinated suppression of stress perception, immune responses, and defence mechanisms. Key categories, including “response to stimulus”, “response to oxidative stress”, and “innate immune response”, were repressed, indicating a reduced capacity to respond to environmental cues, redox imbalances, and pathogens. Downregulation of glycosyltransferases and secondary metabolism pathways suggests diminished detoxification and production of defensive metabolites. Hormone-mediated defence pathways, including jasmonic acid and salicylic acid signalling, as well as lipid- and oxylipin-related processes, were also suppressed, reflecting attenuated stress signalling. Abiotic stress responses were similarly downregulated, indicating compromised adaptive capacity. Sulphur compound metabolism was reduced, suggesting a reallocation of resources away from costly secondary defence compounds rather than a block of primary sulphur assimilation.

Overall, the transcriptional profile points to general repression of stress and defence networks. The results indicate limited engagement of classical defence mechanisms, with little evidence of stress- or pathogen-responsive transcriptional activity. Despite this subdued transcriptional response, Donna Francesca can constrain pathogen proliferation, although the mechanisms underlying its moderate susceptibility remain unclear and are not reflected in the differentially expressed genes identified here. Notably, upregulated genes were few and showed no significant functional enrichment, suggesting that factors beyond transcriptional regulation, such as constitutive defences, tissue structure, or post-transcriptional and biochemical processes, may contribute to its moderate susceptibility.

Taken together, these observations suggest that the moderate susceptibility of Donna Francesca cannot be fully explained by transcriptional responses alone and likely involves additional layers of regulation. Within this framework, the interpretation of the intermediate phenotype is grounded in a cautious and data-driven analysis. The transcriptional profile of Donna Francesca is clearly distinct from both the resistant and susceptible cultivars, supporting its classification as a genuine intermediate response rather than a diluted version of either extreme. This pattern is consistent with the concept of partial or quantitative resistance, where defence outcomes arise from a combination of modest transcriptional adjustments and additional regulatory or structural factors. Such an interpretation reflects the biological complexity of host–pathogen interactions and underscores that intermediate susceptibility represents a distinct and biologically meaningful response category rather than a limitation of the analysis.

Transcriptional reprogramming in Leccino

In Leccino, GO enrichment analysis revealed significant overrepresentation of terms related to sulphate assimilation and ATP sulfurylase activity (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Fig. S5), both crucial for hydrogen sulphide (H₂S) biosynthesis, a key signalling molecule that activates plant defence pathways against biotic agents. Previous transcriptomic analyses have shown, in the resistant Leccino, an enhanced expression of genes encoding cell wall–associated proteins, receptor-like kinases, and stress-response regulators, which contribute to limiting pathogen spread and maintaining xylem integrity [23, 62].

In our dataset, genes involved in sulphur metabolism, particularly ATP-sulfurylase (ATPS), the enzyme catalysing the first step committed in sulphate assimilation, showed notable upregulation. This represents a novel observation, aligning with the recognized role of sulphur metabolism in plant stress resistance [63, 64]. Physiological and metabolomic studies on Xfp-infected olives have reported the accumulation of defence-related metabolites such as azelaic acid and alterations in fatty acid or oxylipin profiles, particularly in trees capable of resprouting after infection [65]. Although direct information on sulphur assimilation during infection is limited, parallels from abiotic stress research are informative: under salinity, drought or heavy metal stress, olive and other plant species exhibit increased activity of ATP-sulfurylase and O-acetylserine(thiol)lyase (OASTL), elevating thiol levels and enhancing antioxidant capacity [64, 66–68].

Enhanced sulphate assimilation may therefore contribute to Xylella fastidiosa resistance through multiple, interconnected mechanisms. It strengthens antioxidant defences by supplying cysteine and glutathione precursors that detoxify reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated during infection [69, 70], facilitates redox reactions required for cell wall cross-linking and lignification to reinforce structural barriers against bacterial invasion [71], and modulates salicylic acid and jasmonic acid signalling, linking sulphur status with the regulation of systemic acquired resistance [72]. As ATP-sulphurylase functions as a regulatory bottleneck, its upregulation could determine both the speed and magnitude of defence responses. However, sulphate activation is energy-intensive, requiring ATP and reductants; thus, the ability to sustain this process under concurrent biotic and abiotic stress may distinguish resistant from susceptible cultivars [73]. Supporting evidence from model plants and crops indicates that reduced ATP-sulfurylase activity increases susceptibility to bacterial pathogens, whereas overexpression or adequate sulphur nutrition enhances resistance by elevating glutathione levels and promoting systemic acquired resistance [74–76]. Sulphur fertilization similarly improves bacterial disease resistance in crops such as oilseed rape and soybean by maintaining sufficient pools of sulphur-containing defence metabolites [72].

Emerging evidence also points to a role for the rhizosphere in sulphur-mediated defence. Microbial mineralization of sulfonates can increase sulphur availability in the rhizosphere, indirectly supporting the synthesis of antioxidant and defence-related sulphur compounds. Certain sulfonated molecules may also act as phytoalexins or signal intermediates during pathogen attack, though their specific roles in olive remain largely unexplored [72].

Together, these findings underscore the importance of sulphur availability and assimilation in plant immunity. However, direct biochemical and transcriptomic evidence linking sulphate assimilation to Xylella fastidiosa resistance in olive remains limited. Future research should focus on quantifying ATP-sulfurylase expression and activity in resistant versus susceptible cultivars, integrating sulphur metabolomics, particularly cysteine and glutathione dynamics, with transcriptomic data, and exploring the contribution of sulphur-cycling microbial communities in the rhizosphere. Such studies could inform sulphur-based nutritional and biotechnological strategies to strengthen olive defence systems and enhance resistance to Xfp and other vascular pathogens.

Subcellular localization GO terms such as chloroplast stroma and plastid stroma are also enriched, aligning with the well-established plastid localization of sulphate reduction in plants, where ATP sulfurylase, APS reductase, and sulphite reductase catalyse the core steps of the pathway [77]. Additionally, enrichment of selenium metabolic process likely reflects the shared assimilation pathways of sulphur and selenium, as selenate and selenite are taken up and metabolized via the sulphate assimilation machinery [78], indicating broader regulation of chalcogen metabolism. The presence of hydrogen peroxide catabolic process suggests a link between sulphate assimilation and redox homeostasis, as sulphate reduction is highly reductant-demanding and tightly coupled to cellular redox status and antioxidant systems. Overall, these enrichments collectively indicate a coordinated metabolic and stress-responsive activation of sulfur assimilation machinery in the upregulated gene set.

Commonly modulated genes in response to Xfp

Among the 1,758 DEGs identified, only 5 were shared across all cultivars. This strikingly small overlap points to highly cultivar-specific transcriptional responses and suggests that each variety activates and suppresses distinct gene sets upon infection. Such divergence reinforces the notion that resistance and susceptibility in olive may arise from fundamentally different molecular strategies. Previous studies provide complementary insights: Giampetruzzi et al., [23] showed that the resistant cultivar Leccino harbours structural genes and/or regulatory elements that counteract Xfp infection. More recently, La Notte et al. [25] demonstrated that three newly identified resistant genotypes exhibit altered expression of genes involved in responses to biotic and abiotic stresses, as well as in the regulation of plant hormone levels, thereby modulating the trade-off between defence and growth upon Xfp infection.

To explore components potentially underlying these contrasting responses, we selected nine candidate genes with particularly notable expression patterns (Supplementary Table S1). Our focus was on genes that were strongly upregulated in the resistant cultivar Leccino while being downregulated in the susceptible Oliva Rossa (and vice versa), as such patterns may indicate active contributors to defence in one genetic background and suppressed elements in another. Quantitative RT-PCR validation confirmed these trends, strengthening the notion that these genes represent robust biological signals with potential relevance for biomarker development and breeding for resistance.

Except for Oe11g759080, all selected genes showed a consistent pattern: they were downregulated in infected Oliva Rossa, moderately upregulated in infected Donna Francesca and most strongly upregulated in Leccino. This gradient of expression was the criterion used for their selection, suggesting that these genes may represent candidates potentially involved in mediating defence against Xfp.

Notably, two genes, HIPP 7 on chromosome 11 (Oe11g699090) and HIPP 6 on chromosome 23 (Oe23g820930) belong to the Heavy Metal–Associated Isoprenylated Plant Protein (HIPP) family. HIPPs genes localize to plasmodesmata, the membrane-rich nanochannels that regulate intercellular communication [79]. Because plasmodesmata undergo dynamic structural changes during stress responses [80, 81], HIPPs are well positioned to modulate host–pathogen interactions, and although their functions remain poorly characterized, increasing evidence implicates HIPPs in responses to both abiotic stresses and biotic challenges [82–84]. Importantly, some HIPPs can interact directly with pathogen effector proteins, facilitating their recognition by plant NLR (Nucleotide-binding Leucine-Rich Repeat) receptors and triggering effector-triggered immunity [85, 86]. In rice, for example, OsHIPP19 and OsHIPP28 bind fungal effectors to activate resistance [87, 88]. A similar mechanism in olive could explain the strong induction of these genes in resistant cultivars. Another gene of interest, Oe11g122070 encodes the putative disease resistance protein RGA3, a member of the resistance gene analogue (RGA) family. RGAs include both pattern recognition receptors and classical R genes, functioning as key components in effector recognition and downstream defence activation. RGA3 homologs have been associated with broad-spectrum resistance in potato [89] and with fire blight resistance in apple and pear [90]. Notably, RGA3 was recently found to be overexpressed in the spontaneous Xfp-resistant olive genotype S105 by La Notte et al. [25], further supporting its potential role in olive resistance. The gene Oe17g395980 encodes a WAT1-related protein, a member of a plant-specific gene family essential for secondary cell wall formation and auxin export [91]. WAT1 family members have been closely associated with plant immune responses against several vascular pathogens, including Ralstonia solanacearum, Verticillium spp., and Fusarium spp [92, 93]. Cell wall modification and remodelling is a typical defence response in woody plants and is associated with the formation of tyloses, which effectively block pathogen movement [8, 14, 94]. Notably, Castro et al., [95] observed temporal and spatial shift in cell wall modification in grapevine in response to X. fastidiosa, including the induction of pectin esterases, expansins, polygalacturonases, and cellulose synthases, highlighting the dynamic nature of cell wall–mediated defence. In addition, the gene CsWAT1-20 positively regulates cucumber defence against Podosphaera xanthii, the causal agent of powdery mildew [96]. The expression pattern of its homolog in olive may therefore provide valuable insights into potential susceptibility pathways that differ between cultivars. Another candidate implicated in cell wall modification is Oe11g759080, which encodes a protein containing an EG45-like domain, a member of the expansin-related family. Expansins facilitate cell wall loosening by enabling the passive release of cellulose networks in response to turgor-induced tensile stresses [97] and are involved in adaptive responses to environmental stresses and pathogens [98]. Expansins are secreted into the apoplast, especially under stress conditions, and contribute to systemic signalling that modulates plant physiological responses to both biotic and abiotic stresses, including pathogen attack, salinity, and drought [99, 100]. The divergent regulation of Oe11g759080 across the analysed cultivars suggests a complex, potentially context-dependent role in defence and stress adaptation.

For several other candidate genes, no clear role in stress responses has yet been described. The gene Oe20g247300 encodes a Noroxomaritidine synthase 2, a cytochrome P450 associated with alkaloid biosynthesis in Amaryllidaceae species [101], while the gene Oe6g243480 corresponds to a retrovirus-related Pol polyprotein, indicative of retrotransposon activity.

Collectively, these findings illustrate that resistant cultivars engage a distinct repertoire of genes, including those with established roles in defence signalling, effector recognition, and cell wall remodelling, while susceptible cultivars exhibit suppressed expression of the same elements. This differential regulation provides a valuable entry point for unravelling the molecular basis of resistance to Xfp and for identifying candidate targets to support future breeding programs and biotechnological interventions.

Conclusions

The comparative transcriptomic analysis of olive cultivars with differing susceptibilities to pathogen infection revealed distinct patterns of gene regulation and defence strategies. The integrative model presented in Fig. 8 summarizes these transcriptional dynamics and highlights both shared and cultivar-specific regulatory mechanisms underlying differential disease outcomes.

The model summarizes the key physiological, molecular, and/or biochemical pathways identified in this study, highlighting both shared mechanisms and variety-specific differences in response patterns. Oliva Rossa (susceptible) exhibited extensive transcriptional reprogramming, with numerous genes both up and downregulated. Despite the strong activation of defence responses, including stress-related pathways, secondary metabolism, and cell wall reinforcement mechanisms, these responses were largely ineffective. Notably, the downregulation of cytoskeleton-related genes likely impaired intracellular trafficking and the precise deployment of defence components. Consequently, Oliva Rossa displayed widespread cellular perturbation and failed to effectively contain the pathogen. Donna Francesca (moderately susceptible) showed a restrained transcriptomic response, with weak activation of classical defence pathways and only a few genes upregulated upon infection. The intermediate level of susceptibility observed is likely mediated by constitutive defence traits, tissue architecture, or post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms rather than by an induced transcriptional response. Consequently, pathogen containment was moderate, and overall transcriptomic adjustments were muted.

Leccino (resistant) demonstrated controlled and highly targeted transcriptional reprogramming. Key defence strategies included upregulation of sulphur assimilation pathways and ATP-sulfurylase, promoting H₂S production, redox balance, and antioxidant defences. Additionally, activation of cell wall–associated proteins, receptor-like kinases, and stress-response regulators further enabled efficient pathogen recognition and containment. Importantly, Leccino achieved an effective and precise defence response while minimizing collateral disruption to cellular processes.

Among the 1,758 DEGs identified, only 5 were shared across all cultivars, highlighting highly cultivar-specific transcriptional responses. Each variety appears to activate and suppress distinct gene sets upon Xfp infection, indicating that resistance and susceptibility in olive arise from fundamentally different molecular strategies and underscoring the complexity of host–pathogen interactions. Expanding the analysis to additional cultivars could further clarify the mechanisms underlying these diverse responses. Overall, resistance in olive cultivars is associated with a focused and strategic transcriptional reprogramming, susceptibility with broad but largely ineffective defence activation, and intermediate susceptibility with limited transcriptomic changes, likely relying on constitutive and post-transcriptional mechanisms.

While the transcriptional patterns identified here are consistent with established plant defence mechanisms, further validation at the biochemical and anatomical levels represents an important direction for future research. As such, the present work is intentionally designed as a discovery-driven study focused on gene expression profiling rather than a comprehensive physiological, biochemical, or structural characterization.

In this context, quantification of H₂S and GSH would help determine whether the activation of sulphur assimilation pathways translates into measurable changes in redox metabolism, while analysis of lignin content and callose deposition would provide direct support for the inferred cell wall remodelling. In parallel, detailed anatomical characterization of xylem architecture, such as vessel diameter, pore size, and pit membrane structure, assessed through microscopy approaches, could clarify the extent to which structural traits contribute to resistance and help disentangle their role from transcriptionally driven defence responses.

Integrating these biochemical and anatomical perspectives with transcriptomic data will be essential to achieve a more comprehensive and mechanistic understanding of resistance in olive, ultimately linking gene expression patterns to functional metabolic outputs and physical defence traits.
