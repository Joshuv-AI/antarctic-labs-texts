Letter to the Editor Regarding “Complementary Strategy of Maternal Immunization and Monoclonal Antibodies for the Prevention of Respiratory Syncytial Virus Among Italian Infants: A Cost-Effectiveness Assessment”
Authors: Claudio Costantino, Gabriele Giorgianni, Roberto Furnari, Vincenzo Di Gaetano, Fabio Massimo Contarino
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Polistena et al. recently published in Infectious Diseases and Therapy a cost-effectiveness analysis of a complementary strategy combining maternal immunization with respiratory syncytial virus prefusion (RSVpreF) vaccine and monoclonal antibodies for the prevention of Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV) infection among Italian infants [1].

While the analysis is of clear relevance, the manuscript contains an inaccurate description of the maternal RSVpreF immunization strategy in Sicily, the fourth most populous Italian region (approximately 4.8 million inhabitants).

Specifically, maternal vaccination is reported as recommended between 24 and 36 weeks of gestation [1]. This statement is not aligned with the Sicilian Regional Immunization Plan (SIP) 2024–2026 and with the subsequent operative decree for Sicilian health care professionals (HCPs) [2, 3].

According to the SIP, published in June 2024, and the subsequent Decree of the Sicilian Department of Health (September 2024), RSVpreF vaccination is recommended preferentially between 32 and 36 weeks of gestation, and only for pregnancies with an expected date of delivery between November 1 and March 31 [2, 3].

This distinction is not marginal, as it directly affects the target population and the estimated impact of the intervention in terms of seasonality and cost-effectiveness.

Moreover, this “seasonal” and “targeted” strategy is in accordance with the US Center for Disease Control and Prevention’s strategy for the use of RSVpreF vaccine in pregnant women published in September 2024 [4].

Furthermore, the SIP clearly states that RSVpreF should not be co-administered with Tdap vaccine, in line with the Summary of Product Characteristics approved by the European Medicines Agency [5].

It also explicitly excludes the administration of monoclonal antibodies (actually nirsevimab) at birth in infants born to vaccinated mothers with RSVpreF, to ensure appropriate complementarity and efficient resource allocation [2, 3, 6].

In parallel, the SIP identifies monoclonal antibodies as a cornerstone of universal RSV prevention, especially for newborns born from April 1 to September 30 (out of RSV season) and a milestone for newborns born from October 1 to March 31 (in RSV season) with a dual strategy based on seasonality, and summarized in Table 1 [2, 3, 6].

This approach has already demonstrated high uptake in Italy, with coverage rates between 70% and 90% during the 2024–2025 season [7, 8]. In Sicily, coverage reached approximately 75%, contributing to a measurable reduction in RSV-related hospitalizations [9].

Conversely, maternal immunization uptake remains extremely limited. During the 2024–2025 season, fewer than 300 pregnant women received RSVpreF vaccination in Sicily, corresponding to approximately 2% of the eligible population [10].

This is consistent with low coverage observed for other maternal vaccinations in Italy, including influenza (6.4%) and Tdap (41.0%) [11].

These data highlight a critical implementation gap. Without a substantial increase in maternal vaccine uptake, the assumption of effective complementarity between maternal immunization and monoclonal antibody strategies remains largely theoretical. Strengthening the involvement of obstetricians and gynecologists is therefore essential to ensure the feasibility of such integrated approaches.

Finally, the RSV immunization strategy defined in 2024 has been formally confirmed for the 2025–2026 season by the Sicilian Department of Health, following recommendations from the Sicilian Section of the Italian Society of Hygiene, Preventive Medicine and Public Health (SItI) [12].

The strategy remains unchanged, including both universal infant immunization with monoclonal antibody and targeted maternal RSVpreF vaccination as described above, and is extended with a booster of monoclonal antibodies before the second RSV season for at-high risk children [12].

In light of these considerations, we urge that the Sicilian immunization strategy be accurately represented in future analyses, as misclassification of key programmatic elements may lead to biased estimates and misinformed policy conclusions.
