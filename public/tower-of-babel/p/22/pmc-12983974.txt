Organ failure type in fatal and near-fatal anaphylaxis: a systematic review
Authors: Ben McKenzie, Stuart D Marshall, Lena Sanci, Jasmine Poonian, Rishabh Nair, Chris J Selman, Jo A Douglass
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesAnaphylaxis is a sudden onset multiorgan allergic reaction that infrequently but regularly causes fatalities which may be preventable with appropriate organ support. There is limited data about the type of organ failure leading to death or near-fatal episodes resulting in permanent neurological disability. To assist clinicians facing anaphylaxis in diverse clinical settings, we aimed to quantify the frequency of organ failure type contributing to death or neurological disability from anaphylaxis according to allergen trigger.DesignSystematic review of published peer-reviewed literature.Data sourcesThree databases were searched to January 2025: MEDLINE from 1946, Embase from 1947 and Web of Science from 1900.Eligibility criteriaStudies were eligible if they contained data about the type of clinical deterioration during anaphylaxis resulting in death or permanent neurological disability. No language restriction was implemented. Exclusion criteria were: hydatid anaphylaxis; five or more stings from an insect; death from acute atheromatous myocardial infarction and where anaphylaxis was only a differential diagnosis.Data extraction and synthesisWe extracted information using pre-specified criteria to determine the primary organ failure involved: either upper airway obstruction, lower respiratory obstruction (bronchospasm) or cardiovascular failure. Baseline demographics including age and asthma status were collected along with the allergen trigger, time course and treatment. We reported frequencies according to allergen trigger for case reports and a narrative analysis of case series weighted by risk of bias assessment.Results277 case studies and 14 case series were identified reporting 896 deaths and 28 disabilities. There were no other study types. Separate case series and case report analyses produced similar findings despite varying quality of published clinical data. Respiratory failure was the most common primary organ failure in case reports (73.4%), whereas primary cardiovascular failure was reported in 26.6% of case reports. Primary organ failure type differed in frequency by allergen trigger and was primarily caused by: respiratory failure when food was the allergen trigger (95%); respiratory failure in 65% of cases of drug allergen triggers; cardiovascular failure in 65% venom allergen triggers.ConclusionIn this review, respiratory failure (primarily bronchospasm) is the most common primary physiological event leading to decompensation in fatal anaphylaxis, particularly for food and drug allergen deaths. Emphasising the significance of respiratory involvement, particularly from bronchospasm, in both patient and clinician facing anaphylaxis treatment guidelines may help further reduce the risk of fatalities. Prospective anaphylaxis management registries or whole population data are needed to better capture primary organ failure present in fatal anaphylaxis to validate this finding.PROSPERO registration numberCRD42023434206.

Introduction

Anaphylaxis is an acute-onset, potentially fatal systemic allergic reaction that affects multiple organ systems either simultaneously or singularly in isolation.1 Its incidence has risen over decades, particularly for food-related anaphylaxis in younger age groups and drug-related anaphylaxis in older age groups.2 Intensive care admissions, including those requiring organ support, have increased dramatically over the last decade.3 There is conflicting data on the associated increase in anaphylaxis fatalities over time, with data being limited by the rarity of this event.4 5 Rising anaphylaxis incidence has driven necessity to recognise and respond to these unpredictable episodes effectively in diverse clinical settings as a global healthcare concern.6

Anaphylaxis reactions are brief, usually lasting <4 hours, and a rapid return to a person’s baseline can be expected if survival occurs without complication.7 The most devastating reactions result in death, or near-fatal episodes resulting in permanent neurological disability from hypoxic ischaemic encephalopathy. Current anaphylaxis treatment guidelines emphasise the non-invasive therapies of intramuscular epinephrine, oxygen by face mask and intravenous fluids.8 These interventions cater for early onset, less critical and cardiovascular reactions, but guidelines are generally silent on other advanced organ support therapies in anaphylaxis. If clinicians are to achieve successful treatment and resuscitation outcomes, a rigorous understanding of which organs fail, when and in what circumstances is important to inform tailored resuscitation therapy and targets that have a greater chance of being effective.

The end organ pathophysiology of anaphylaxis leading to loss of oxygenation and perfusion of the brain may be multifactorial and overlap through interdependent organ physiology, with the exact mechanism of some organ dysfunction still being elucidated.9 10 However, the primary organ system leading to fatal or near-fatal anaphylactic reactions can usually be categorised into either: upper airway obstruction leading to acute asphyxia; bronchospasm with lower airway obstruction also causing asphyxia (leading to secondary hypoxaemic cardiac arrest); primary cardiovascular collapse without respiratory involvement or a combination of these events.11

Mediators released from mast cells during anaphylaxis, such as histamine, platelet activating factor and leukotrienes, cause tissue oedema, vascular dilation and bronchospasm. These events, which evolve over minutes, lead to upper airway obstruction from oropharyngeal or laryngeal oedema and lower airway related respiratory failure principally from bronchospasm. Cardiovascular failure occurs due to vasodilation, fluid extravasation from the intravascular space with intravascular volume loss, and possibly cardiac depression.12 13 Each organ system failure is associated with symptoms and signs making it possible to record discrete organ involvement in clinical cases.14 Data regarding the frequency of organ failure type in anaphylaxis leading to death and disability is lacking, likely limited by the low frequency of these events and the diverse settings where specific allergen triggers occur. We therefore attempted to address this evidence gap by first undertaking a systematic review of the literature to identify the type and quantify the frequency of organ failure among published studies of fatal and near-fatal anaphylaxis.

Methods

Study design

Systematic review of incidence is a methodology useful in measuring disease burden and we formulated our question from guidelines published by the Johanna Briggs Institute (JBI).15 16 We later needed to amend this protocol to be a routine systematic review to determine the frequency of organ failure type because our comprehensive search strategy returned only case studies and case series (see Results). This study is reported in accordance with preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses (PRISMA) statement 2020. Our primary research question, using a population, condition and context approach, is: in published literature from 1900 to January 2025, what is the frequency of organ failure type contributing to death or permanent disability from anaphylaxis according to allergen trigger?

Data sources

We searched from the commencement of each database MEDLINE (1946), Embase (1947) and Web of Science (1900) to January 2025 for relevant articles. No language restriction was made (non-English articles were translated using Google Translate); full search strategies are available in the appendix (online supplemental tables 1-3), but in brief we searched for articles that included any death or permanent hypoxic ischaemic encephalopathy injury from anaphylaxis. Further records were identified by searching reference lists of articles included in the full text analysis stage of the study.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We included any primary data where there was a description of organ failure leading to cardiorespiratory arrest causing death or permanent neurological disability from anaphylaxis in humans.

We excluded studies where the causative role of anaphylaxis in death or disability was uncertain, namely: where anaphylaxis was one of multiple differential diagnoses leading to death; where there were five or more stings from an insect and venom toxicity may have played a direct role in death; and where acute atheromatous myocardial infarction occurred in the setting of recent anaphylaxis. We also excluded cases where anaphylaxis deaths were not generalisable to the broader anaphylaxis population, namely: hydatid anaphylaxis; and neonatal death from maternal anaphylaxis.

Data handling

All records were managed for each step up to and including data extraction using Covidence (Melbourne, Australia), a web-based collaboration software platform that streamlines the production of systematic reviews by allowing reviewers to conduct simultaneous independent screening and data extraction. Duplicate articles were identified by Covidence. All screening and data extraction was performed by the reviewers using the online platform. Two reviewers independently performed abstract and full text screening (BM and RN) with consensus reached by agreement. Full text was deemed unavailable if not able to be sourced within 8 weeks of screening. Study authors were contacted where possible when articles were identified as potentially recording relevant data but there were insufficient clinical details published to determine organ failure type. Cases were included as case reports if primary organ failure was described for individual patients regardless of the method of the primary publication. Case series allocation was used when organ involvement was reported as a pooled outcome for a group of patients.

Data extraction

The criteria for identification of organ system failure were derived through consensus of an expert panel incorporating the specialties of Allergy and Clinical Immunology, Emergency Medicine, Anaesthesia and Primary Care (see study protocol in the appendix for full derivation). Data was extracted independently by two critical care specialists (BM and JP) using these pre-specified criteria (included in table 1), reaching consensus by agreement with adjudication from the expert panel where disagreement occurred. Where data from case reports or case series explicitly specified organ failure type, this data was extracted unchanged. When more than one organ failure type was deemed to be present, then the data extractors were required to agree on the organ failure that predominantly led to cardiorespiratory arrest. Secondary organ failure was also recorded where descriptive criteria were met but deemed not to be the primary organ that led to cardiorespiratory arrest. The American Society of Anaesthesiologists (ASA) Physical Status Classification System17 was used to estimate comorbidities for each case. If reported, other variables collected include age, sex, allergen trigger, pre-existing asthma status, physical location of reaction in relation to healthcare, time from allergen exposure to symptoms, whether the patient was administered epinephrine before collapse, intubation status, presence of vomiting or aspiration clinically or on autopsy, whether the patient survived initial resuscitation longer than 2 hours, and the number of days to death if initial survival occurred.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias was assessed for case series using the JBI assessment tool18 with a scoring system identifying high and lower risk of bias (online supplemental appendix table 4). Risk of bias was assessed by BM and checked by the expert panel. Specific risk of bias was not conducted for case reports.

Data evaluation

Descriptive statistics (Stata 2023) Stata Statistical Software: release V.18 was used to summarise variables in the case reports. Specifically, counts and percentages were calculated for categorical variables and medians and IQRs for skewed data. Primary and secondary outcomes were summarised overall and split by allergen trigger using cross-tabulations. Case series were analysed with a simple narrative weighted by the risk of bias for each study.

Patient and public involvement

This study was performed in response to a network of families who have had a child die from anaphylaxis despite healthcare treatment. Their desire to seek opportunities for advances in treatment and resuscitation outcomes helped identify this as a research question important to the international allergy community.

Results

The article searches for the planned question on incidence of organ failure did not yield any population-based studies, so incidence could not be determined. The amended question on frequency of organ failure type was possible. The PRISMA diagram for this systematic review is shown at figure 1. Studies identified for inclusion comprised solely of case series and case reports. 277 studies were included describing 924 cases comprising 896 deaths and 28 cases of permanent disability. There were 14 case series111931 containing 533 cases and 263 case report publications32292 301 302 describing 391 individual cases. Six case reports included were removed after checking for matching demographics prior to analysis: one was a missed duplicate and five were identified as cases likely to have been published more than once, resulting in 385 case reports being analysed. Reviewers had agreement on initial data extraction for primary organ failure in 75.8% of cases. Disagreements resulted in primary organ failure being unable to be determined in 17.0% of case disagreements, with the remainder being able to be coded by agreement and adjudication.

There was a high burden of pre-existing asthma (60.8% overall) including 91.0% of food related cases, 34.6% for drug category and 35.0% for venom. For all triggers combined (table 1), the most common primary organ failure was bronchospasm (n=224, 60.9%), followed by cardiovascular (n=98, 26.6%) and then upper airway (n=46, 12.5%). The primary organ failure could not be determined in 17 (4.4%) cases. Combined upper and lower respiratory failure accounted for almost three quarters of the cases (n=270, 73.4%).

Drug anaphylaxis

Of 186 drug-related cases, 136 (73.1%) were from intravenous or intramuscular medications, 25 (13.4%) were from oral medications and 23 (12.4%) were from radiology contrast. Thirty-four cases occurred in the operative setting, 15 of which were from neuromuscular blockers (NMBs). Of the 15 NMB cases, 14 had bronchospasm and in 12 it was the primary organ failure. Overall, the primary organ failure for drug-related cases was primarily respiratory with bronchospasm (n=97, 54.2%), cardiovascular (n=63, 35.2%) and upper airway (n=19, 10.6%).

Food anaphylaxis

Food-related anaphylaxis deaths were a younger age group with a median age of 16 years (IQR 10–21), the majority (85.2%) were known and recorded as being allergic to the food involved and 91.0% were recorded as having pre-existing asthma. Major food allergens included peanut (25 cases), tree nuts (13 cases), cow’s milk (12 cases) and seafood (7 cases). Other food allergens included wheat, egg, buckwheat, soy, sheep’s milk, snail and Quorn. This group died almost exclusively of respiratory failure (n=76, 95.0%) with bronchospasm the primary organ failure in 85.0% of food allergen deaths (n=68). Upper airway obstruction was the primary cause of death in 10.0% (n=8). There were four deaths due to a food trigger where primary organ failure was recorded as cardiovascular, which were extracted from a single article. The reaction type in these cases was labelled “anaphylactic shock” which created uncertainty regarding whether these cases truly represented primary cardiovascular collapse, or the label was used more broadly for severe anaphylaxis. Of note, upper airway oedema was recorded in 19.5% (n=16) of food-related cases overall. Also, in the food allergen group, vomiting/aspiration was at a higher frequency than in other allergen triggers (n=18, 22%) with many cases otherwise not recording this level of detail.

Venom anaphylaxis

The 48 venom cases were all hymenoptera exposures except for seven myrmecia ant cases and one tick. The primary organ failure was cardiovascular in the majority of cases (65.0%). This group all had rapid symptom onset and generally progressed to collapse and cardiac arrest rapidly outside healthcare, making the type of organ failure difficult to both record for authors and extract for this review. The lack of quality observation in many instances renders judgement regarding the sequence of terminal organ failure prone to error. Airway involvement occurred in 29.2% (n=14) of cases and cardiovascular collapse in 66.6% of venom cases (n=32). Almost all of the published cases of survival with disability from hypoxic ischaemic encephalopathy were from insect exposures. Mast cell disorders (mastocytosis) were present in eight cases of the insect venom anaphylaxis cohort.

Allergy immunotherapy anaphylaxis

There were 63 cases of anaphylaxis deaths during allergy immunotherapy with an incidence of pre-existing recorded asthma of 81.3% (n=48). Primary organ failure was respiratory in nearly all cases (n=59, 93.7%) mainly comprising bronchospasm (n=47, 74.6%) with upper airway failure in a minority (n=12, 19.0%). Cardiovascular collapse was the primary organ failure in only 4 of the cases (6.3%).

Case series

A total of 14 published case series were discovered that described 533 cases. All 14 are presented by allergen group in order of risk of bias in table 4. Reporting of organ failure type leading to death and disability was generally low quality, with only one author reporting it as an explicit outcome, and this series did not describe the methodology for allocation. Four series with a high risk of bias were omitted from the narrative analysis.

Most series reported respiratory symptoms as a composite of airway oedema and bronchospasm and only three authors separated respiratory components. The predominant organ systems failure or sequence of organ failure was only reported by two authors. Cardiovascular failure ranged from 12% to 100%. Respiratory failure was present in most cases, ranging from 53% to 100%. Food-related fatalities were children and young adults who died almost exclusively from respiratory failure with a high frequency of bronchospasm reported (75%). The venom series was older with a very high frequency of cardiovascular collapse (59% to 81%) and lower frequency of respiratory involvement (19% to 53%) which was predominantly upper airway failure when present. Drug-related fatalities had high frequencies of both cardiovascular (47% to 100%) and respiratory (60% to 74%) failure. The four series reporting mixed allergen triggers were consistent with these trends.

Discussion

This is the first review to comprehensively examine published data regarding organ failure in published cases of fatal and near-fatal anaphylaxis. Importantly, the reports enabled a determination of primary organ failure in over 95% of cases. Several novel findings emerge from our study.

First, the primary organ failure type differed by the attributed causative allergen. Cardiovascular failure leading to shock appeared to be the major primary event in insect sting induced anaphylaxis (65.0%) and was important in drug induced anaphylaxis (35.2%). This is in distinct contrast to food-induced anaphylaxis where bronchospasm was the primary organ system affected in 85.0% of cases and combined upper and lower airway obstruction was critical in 95.0%. While pre-existing asthma rates were high in this group and asthma is a known risk factor for severe anaphylaxis,293 that food anaphylaxis may be exclusively a respiratory death has major implications for clinicians treating anaphylaxis. It is well-recognised that asthma is generally under-treated with asthma controller medications and patient under-perception of the symptoms occurs. Our findings therefore support the importance of recognising and treating asthma aggressively in food-allergic individuals as a preventative measure. But given that the propensity to develop bronchospasm cannot be eliminated when asthma patients develop anaphylaxis, resuscitation guidelines should recognise the importance of respiratory symptoms and treatment, especially in severe food anaphylaxis.

Furthermore, in relation to all allergens, lower respiratory failure from severe bronchospasm, was overwhelmingly present in the majority of cases, despite the source of the allergen, affecting 68.3% of individuals and being the primary organ failure in 60.9%. It was the most common cause of decompensation in drug anaphylaxis deaths. Upper airway involvement was present in 21% of all case reports and was the primary organ failure in 12.5%. Our findings therefore attest to the cardinal role of respiratory failure as a key primary physiological event in fatal anaphylaxis. Treatment for respiratory failure prioritises airway management and oxygenation before irrecoverable hypoxic damage occurs to cerebral and cardiac tissues,294296 yet ventilation of patients with severe anaphylaxis is not explicitly prioritised in anaphylaxis guidelines that traditionally emphasise cardiovascular therapies such as intravenous fluids.8 Improving recognition of respiratory failure and therefore the requirement for early ventilatory support may lead to more successful attempts at resuscitation.297

Our review also suggests that respiratory failure may be underappreciated if the trajectory of hypoxemic bradycardia, collapse and cardiac arrest is recorded as cardiovascular failure or “anaphylactic shock” by both clinicians and researchers. Our data reinforces that the latter label is unhelpful when used as a generic term for severe anaphylaxis and risks diverting therapeutic endeavours from addressing primary respiratory failure as a pre-terminal event. This phenomenon is recognised in other settings where hypoxaemic crises occur.298 While both cardiovascular and respiratory failure can have the same final common pathway of bradycardic arrest with hypotension and loss of pulse, the different organ symptoms and signs that lead to that deterioration, that is, the primary organ failure, need recognition so that clinicians can respond with appropriate targeted interventions that differ depending on the organs involved. In scenarios where primary organ failure is not observed prior to unconsciousness, our data suggests food anaphylaxis should be assumed to be respiratory in nature, and all allergen triggers should prioritise simultaneous respiratory and cardiovascular support.

Why respiratory failure is more common in anaphylactic deaths may reflect barriers in recognising and responding to critical respiratory symptoms where respiratory arrest can be sudden after a period of physiological compensation, technical difficulty in medically managing fulminant bronchospasm including appropriately timed advanced critical care techniques, and that hypoxic brain injury is rapid in the face of hypoxemic cardiac arrests. Specific interventions for fulminant bronchospasm such as tracheal intubation with protective ventilation strategies (to achieve oxygen delivery in the face of high airway resistance) and other advanced oxygenation interventions are available to manage acute respiratory failure.296 Our findings indicate that there may be merit in explicit use of these airway interventions early in managing acute respiratory failure during severe anaphylaxis resulting in cardiac arrest, respiratory arrest or in hypoxic decompensating pre-arrest patients.

Finally, this review identifies that it may be useful for both patients and clinicians to recognise and target respiratory symptoms as high risk early in the course of an anaphylactic reaction with reference to calling for help, treatment with escalating conventional epinephrine therapy, concomitant use of bronchodilators and supportive airway measures to avoid clinical deterioration.

Strengths and limitations

A strength of our study is that data was extracted using a rigorously designed systematic search methodology gathering all published cases of fatal and near-fatal anaphylaxis resulting in brain injury that include a description of preceding organ failure. This therefore represents the totality of available data on this subject. Another strength is that organ failure data was extracted using a rigorous predetermined categorisation based on careful consideration of anaphylaxis pathophysiology and double data extraction.

There are several limitations to this study. The data in this study are from case reports or case series and consequently they must be interpreted with caution. However, analysis using these data types is an appropriate method for rare, unpredictable events such as acute severe allergic responses where other study designs are either not feasible or unethical. Other limitations that might affect the analysis include reporting bias, lack of whole population data, low quality of secondary outcome clinical data reporting and lack of detailed description of treatment each patient received. Despite trying to limit information bias through the study protocol, the technical difficulty in extracting binary outcomes based on symptoms and signs that may overlap in pathophysiology may result in misclassification. Similarly, the historical nature of some reports may bias results because of treatment advances, such as the recognition of the superiority of intramuscular epinephrine and its uniform adoption over subcutaneous administration as first line agent.299 However, all cases were reported after epinephrine was widely available and recognised for use in anaphylaxis, and only 15% of case reports were published before 1980. Lastly, the number of cases published with survival after a brain injury sustained during anaphylaxis was small and largely confined to insect venom exposures, making conclusions regarding the primary outcome in this group limited.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our findings on all reported cases of organ failure preceding fatal anaphylaxis to date find that respiratory failure from bronchospasm is the most common cause of decompensation. Food-triggered anaphylaxis deaths were a consequence solely of acute respiratory failure. Respiratory failure, principally from bronchospasm, was found to be the most common organ failure in drug anaphylaxis deaths. These findings highlight the importance of respiratory symptom recognition for both patients and clinicians. Respiratory involvement as the most common cause of death should be appropriately emphasised in both patient-facing advice plans, and as a treatment endpoint in clinical guidelines. While early treatment with epinephrine may prevent respiratory complications and should be advocated, this may not be uniformly available nor effective. For physiologically decompensating patients, emphasising that respiratory failure needs aggressive management, including tracheal intubation during cardiac or respiratory arrest to reduce the risk of death should be considered. Further studies involving prospective or whole population data where the primary outcome is organ failure type and sequence would strengthen our conclusion and further inform resuscitation guidance.
