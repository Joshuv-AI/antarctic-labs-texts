Human urine-derived stem cells rescue cutaneous manifestation and suppress inflammation and fibrosis in vitro and in a mouse model of recessive dystrophic epidermolysis bullosa
Authors: Xingli Zhou, Jie Tan, Yuting Song, Pengcheng Liu, Xiwen Zhang, Xun Feng, Yue Xiao, Yiyi Wang, Guoqing Liu, Wenqian Zhang, Qingyi Zhang, Yanlin Jiang, Yuan Liu, Longmei Zhao, Huiqi Xie, Wei Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecessive dystrophic epidermolysis bullosa (RDEB) is a life-threatening disease characterized by persistent wound inflammation, tissue fibrosis, and even tumorigenesis in later stages. Despite its significant impact on patient health worldwide, treatment of RDEB has limited options. There is a clear need for now interventions. The goal of this study was to explore the potential efficacy of urine-derived stem cells (USCs) in RDEB.MethodsWe isolated human USCs from healthy donors, and assessed their therapeutic effects on RDEB both in vitro with tissue-engineered skin in a three-dimensional co-culture system and in vivo with COL7A1−/− (RDEB) mice.ResultsUSCs showed suppressive effects on expression of inflammation- and fibrosis-related genes involved in RDEB in vitro and in vivo. USCs could also extend the median life span (from 3 to 5 days), improve the expression of C7, and migrate to various organs of RDEB mice after intrahepatic administration.ConclusionsIn summary, these results suggest the potential effects of USCs on improving the expression of C7 and would repair of RDEB, which supports the future use of USCs for the treatment of RDEB patients.

Background

Epidermolysis bullosa (EB), a rare and heterogeneous disease, is distinguished by the mechanical fragility of the skin and mucosa [1]. Recessive dystrophic EB (RDEB) is one of the most life-threatening types of EB with a high mortality rate in young adulthood. Mutations in the COL7A1 gene, which codes for the type VII collagen (C7)–1 chain and leads to the aberrant expression of C7 produced by keratinocytes and fibroblasts, are involved in the pathogenesis of RDEB [2, 3]. Persistent wound inflammation and tissue fibrosis also lead to a marked increase in the incidence of aggressive cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (cSCC) in patients with RDEB (90.1% by the age of 55 years) compared to the general population (1% for those aged 50–59 years) [4, 5]. Research has demonstrated that the activation of transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) signaling [6–8], abnormal deposition of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) [9, 10], epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, and multiple inflammatory cytokines [11–13] were closely related to the tumorigenesis of patients with RDEB.

The current dominant management strategy for RDEB is amelioration of symptoms, but this provides only a slight benefit in cases of severe RDEB. Researchers have attempted to develop effective treatment strategies for RDEB using various approaches, such as gene repair and cell therapies. The first attempt to treat seven children with RDEB in a clinic with allogeneic bone marrow transplantation was made in 2007 by Wagner et al. Five patients showed improvement in erosions healing and less blistering after the therapy [14]. In the following years, different types of stem cells for the treatment of RDEB were gradually emerged, but to date, almost all stem cell therapies for RDEB patients were in preclinical research or clinical trial stage (Table S1, see online supplementary material) [14–19]. Among various types of stem cells for the treatment of RDEB, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), a type of pluripotent stem cell with the capacity for self-renewal and multi-directional differentiation, also showed the potential in changing the disease course of RDEB [17, 20–22]. Currently, the commonly studied MSCs for EB therapy are mainly derived from the bone marrow, umbilical cord, and placenta [6, 17, 18, 23]. However, the acquisition of these MSCs usually involves invasive procedures and limited sources, and some of them also have ethical issues. In 2008, a stem cell population identified in urine (termed urine-derived stem cells [USCs]) exhibiting characteristics of MSCs was first reported by Zhang, et al [24]. USCs can be isolated via a simple, harmless, and low-cost method, and our previous work confirmed that USCs can effectively repair skin defects, intrauterine adhesions, and bladder injuries [25–27]. All of the aforementioned studies have drawn attention to exploring the therapeutic value of USCs in RDEB.

Additionally, the most commonly used in vitro models for RDEB are keratinocytes and fibroblasts derived from the skin of patients/mice with RDEB; however, 2D monolayer cultures of cells have limitations owing to the absence of complicated cell–cell and cell-extracellular matrix interactions. To overcome these limitations, we prepared tissue-engineered skin (TES) [28, 29] by arranging RDEB mice-derived keratinocytes/fibroblasts in acellular dermal matrix (ADM). This RDEB-TES allows the interaction of different cell types with one another and the surrounding environment [30–32], providing us with a 3D physiological research environment.

Based on the aforementioned, this study was conducted as the initial stage in applying USCs in patients with RDEB. We obtained human USCs from healthy donors and demonstrated whether USCs express COL7A1. We examined the effects of USCs on the expression of inflammation- and fibrosis-related genes in keratinocytes and fibroblasts from RDEB-TES using a Transwell coculture system. Lastly, the therapeutic effects of USCs on the overall survival, C7 expression, and other functional outcomes in COL7A1−/− (RDEB) mice were also studied. We hope that this work can establish a research basis for the application of USCs in RDEB and provide a new alternative to stem cell therapy for the treatment of patients with RDEB.

Methods

Mice

C57BL6/J COL7A1+/− mice were crossed with the genotype identified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) to produce COL7A1−/− RDEB mice (Figure S1, see online supplementary material). COL7A1+/− mice were generously donated by Dr Chen Ting of the National Institute of Biological Sciences, Beijing. COL7A1+/− mice were developed by specifically targeting the c.6485G > A point mutation in exon 80 of mouse COL7A1 gene [33]. All animal studies were conducted following the procedures approved by the Animal Experiments Committee of Sichuan University (No. 20211261A).

Preparation of urine-derived stem cells

USCs were obtained from five healthy adult (aged 20–30 years) male donors using previously described methodologies [24, 34]. The Ethics Committee on Biomedical Research at West China Hospital of Sichuan University approved this research (2021-1066). All study participants provided written informed consent. The detailed steps and the flow chart of the USCs culture are presented in the Supplement (Figure S2, see online supplementary material). Briefly, 200 ml of urine was collected from each donor, subjected to a treatment regimen involving 1% penicillin and streptomycin (Gibco, USA), and centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 10 min. The resultant supernatant was decanted, and the sediment was subjected to dual rinsing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; ZSGB-BIO, China). From the pellet, the cells were seeded in a 25 cm2 culture flask containing a nurturing medium composed of the previously introduced constituents [26]. The cells were cultured at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. When cell clones had formed, the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium. After that, the medium was replaced every two days. Upon attaining 80%–90% confluency, the cells were passaged at a ratio of 1:3. USCs at the third passage were used for the subsequent experiments.

Characterization of human urine-derived stem cells

Flow cytometry analysis

The nonspecific antigen of USCs was blocked by incubating them with 3% bovine serum albumin (Sigma, USA) for 30 min. Monoclonal antibodies (BD Biosciences) against cluster of differentiation (CD)29-APC, CD34-APC, CD44-FITC, CD45-APC, CD73-PE, CD105-APC, human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-DR-BV510, and HLA-ABC-FITC were incubated for an additional 30 min At the end of the incubation, the cells were washed with PBS to remove unbound antibody and analyzed by a flow cytometry analyzer (Beckman Cytomics FC500, Beckman Coulter, USA).

Multipotent differentiation potential of urine-derived stem cells

Osteogenic differentiation

USCs cultured at Passage 3 were cultivated in a six-well plate. The medium was changed to osteogenic induction medium (Gibco) when the cells reached 80% confluency. The medium was replaced every 3 days. Alizarin Red S (Sigma) was used to stain the cells after 28 days of induction to identify the calcified extracellular matrix (Supplement file 1 for more details about methods).

Adipogenic differentiation

USCs cultured at Passage 3 were cultivated in a six-well plate. The medium was replaced with adipogenic induction medium (Gibco, USA) when the cells reached 80% confluency. The medium was replaced every 3 days. Oil Red O (Sigma) was used to stain the cells after 21 days of induction to visualize the lipid vacuoles (Supplement file 1 for more details about methods).

Chondrogenic induction

A pellet culture approach was used for chondrogenesis differentiation of USCs. In a 15 ml centrifuge tube, 1 × 106 cells were pelleted by centrifugation, and chondrogenic medium (Gibco) was gently poured into the pellets. The pellets were embedded in optimum cutting temperature (OCT) compound after 28 days of induction and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma). Toluidine blue staining was used to stain the 10 μm cryosections, which were then observed under an optical microscope (Leica, Germany) (Supplement file 1 for more details about methods).

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction analysis for COL7A1 expression in urine-derived stem cells

The total RNA was extracted from USCs using Trizol reagent. A real-time thermocycler (LC96, Roche, Switzerland) with TB Green® Premix Ex TaqTM II Mix (TaKaRa, Japan) was used to perform quantitative reverse transcription PCR (q-PCR) in triplicate. The following conditions were specified for q-PCR: 95°C for 30 s, 40 cycles of 95°C for 10 s, and 60°C for 30 s. A 2-△△Ct technique was used to compare the relative expression of the genes to glyceraldehyde-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH). Representative reverse transcription PCR (RT-PCR) analysis of the relative expression of COL7A1 in USCs was also performed. The primers used to detect human COL7A1 mRNA are listed in Table S2 (see online supplementary material).

Preparation and evaluation of tissue-engineered skin from wild-type and recessive dystrophic epidermolysis bullosa mice

Preparation of acellular dermal matrix from wild-type and recessive dystrophic epidermolysis bullosa mice

The dorsal skin of newborn WT and RDEB mice was digested by 10 mg/mL dispase II (Sigma) for 2 h at 37°C. The dermis was separated from the epidermis. The dermis was decellularized by freezing and thawing three times, added to a solution containing 0.5% Triton X-100 and 20 mM NH4OH for 24 h, treated with 100 mg/ml DNase I for 4 h, and rinsed with running water for 18 h. The acellular effects were observed using residual deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) quantification, hematoxylin–eosin (HE) staining, 4, 6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) staining, and Masson’s trichrome staining. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (EVO10, Carl Zeiss AG, Germany) was performed to observe the microstructure of ADM.

Preparation of keratinocytes and fibroblasts from wild-type and recessive dystrophic epidermolysis bullosa mice

The epidermis and dermis of newborn WT and RDEB mice were isolated using the aforementioned methods. Epidermis with the basal layer placed downward on a Petri dish was digested in 0.25% trypsin–EDTA buffer for 15 min at 37°C. The basal layer of the keratinocytes was separated from the epidermis. The cells were cultured in CnT-07 medium (CELLnTEC, Switzerland). The medium was changed after 24 h to remove unattached cells, and then every three days. Finally, rabbit anti-cytokeratin 14 (CK14) (Abcam, UK, 1:200) and rabbit anti-C7 (Abcam, UK, 1:500) antibodies were used to incubate keratinocytes. The keratinocytes were then incubated with goat anti-rabbit secondary antibodies (Abcam, USA). The nuclei were stained with DAPI (Sigma). A confocal laser scanning microscope (Nikon, Japan) was used to observe and photograph all the samples (Supplement file 1 for more details about methods).

The dermis was placed in a Petri dish and cut into 0.5 × 0.5 cm2-sized pieces; tissues were cut and explanted into culture dishes in an equilibrium manner with Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium-high glucose (DMEM-HG) (Gibco) containing 10% fetal bovine serum. The medium was changed every 2–3 days. Lastly, anti-α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) (Sigma, USA, 1:100) and anti-C7 antibody (Abcam, UK, 1:500) antibodies were used to incubate fibroblasts. The following process was performed as described for keratinocytes (Supplement file 1 for more details about methods).

Preparation and evaluation of wild-type tissue-engineered skin and recessive dystrophic epidermolysis bullosa tissue-engineered skin

Indirect cytotoxicity tests were conducted in accordance with the GB/T16886.5-2003/ISO 10993-5:199 standard. Complete medium with ADM extraction (medium volume (ml):material’s total surface area (cm2) = 1:6) prepared at 37°C for 24 h was used as an extract solution. The positive and negative controls were composed of complete medium containing 1% phenol and complete medium, respectively. In a 96-well plate, 3 × 103 cells/well of keratinocytes and fibroblasts were planted beforehand. The culture medium was changed to the corresponding extract solution after the cells were attached to the wall, and the liquid was changed every other day. Cell proliferation was measured at 1, 3, 5, and 7 days using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay (Dojindo Molecular Technologies, Japan) at 450 nm.

Next, 1 × 104 keratinocytes of passage 3 derived from WT mice skin were grown on the epidermal side (smooth side) of the acellular scaffold of WT mice (WT-ADM), keratinocytes derived from RDEB mice skin were grown on the epidermal side of the acellular scaffold of RDEB mice (RDEB-ADM); similarly, fibroblasts of passage 3 derived from WT mice skin were grown on the dermal side (rough side) of the WT-ADM; fibroblasts derived from RDEB mice skin were grown on the dermal side of the RDEB-ADM to establish the respective TES. After culturing for 72 h, a confocal laser scanning microscope (Nikon, Japan) was used to observe the growth status of the cells, which were stained with calcein-AM and propidium iodide (Sigma) for 30 min. SEM was used to observe the morphology of growing cells.

Wild-type tissue-engineered skin and recessive dystrophic epidermolysis bullosa tissue-engineered skin coculture with urine-derived stem cells

WT-TES and RDEB-TES established by keratinocytes and fibroblasts grown on the ADM were placed on the lower layer of a six-well transwell plate (Corning, USA), and 1 × 106 USCs were planted on the upper layer and then cocultured for 48 h. Total RNA was isolated from cocultured TES using Trizol reagent. Q-PCR was performed in triplicate to analyze the relative expression of inflammatory and fibrotic factors using a previously described method. As for WT/RDEB-TES established by fibroblasts grown on the ADM, coculture with USCs was also performed following stimulation with 5 ng/ml TGF-β1 for 24 h. Total RNA was isolated from the cocultured TES using Trizol reagent and analyzed by q-PCR, as described above.

Urine-derived stem cell administration in recessive dystrophic epidermolysis bullosa mice

Therapeutic regimen

The buffer, made up of a 1:1 mixture of 0.9% NaCl and 5% human serum albumin containing 10% dextran 40, was used to resuspend USCs at a concentration of 7 × 104 cells/μl. Fifteen microliters of the aforementioned solution (1 million cells) were injected into newborn RDEB mouse livers using a 31G insulin syringe (BD Biosciences, USA). RDEB mice that survived 5–7 days were randomly selected for the second injection. The vehicle control was prepared by administering the same volume of thawing buffer. All mice were euthanized on Days 7, 14, 21, and 28 after treatment.

Histological and immunofluorescence analyses

On Days 7, 14, 21, and 28 following USC administration, tissue samples from the selected mice were collected for histological and immunofluorescence investigations. The heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and part of the dorsal skin were sectioned into specimens measuring 4 μm in diameter after being preserved with 10% neutral formaldehyde and embedded in paraffin. The sections were then stained with HE. Dermis–epidermis junction (DEJ) separation percentage in each section was computed as the average ratio of the total separation/total DEJ of all paws per finger digit. Image J software 1.8.0 was used for quantification by tracing the DEJ around the perimeter of the biopsies.

Part of the dorsal skin, hind paws, heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney were prepared as frozen OCT-embedded tissue and processed into 5 μm diameter sections. The frozen sections of the dorsal skin were fixed using 10% neutral formaldehyde for a short time and stained with rabbit anti-C7 (OriGene, USA, 1:100), rabbit anti-collagen I (Affinity, USA, 1:200), and rabbit anti-TGF-β3 (Affinity, USA, 1:200) antibodies. The frozen sections of the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney were fixed using the same method as described before and stained with rabbit anti-HLA-ABC antibody (Abcam, USA, 1:200). Anti-fluorescent quenching coverslips were used to seal the sections that were counterstained with DAPI. A fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) was used to capture images, and ImageJ software 1.8.0 was used for analysis.

Ultrastructural skin examination

Dorsal skin biopsies were prepared for transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Skin samples were fixed overnight with 3% glutaraldehyde. After washing with 0.1 M cacodylate buffer, the skin was incubated for 2 h with 1% osmium tetroxide solution at room temperature. The samples were dehydrated, penetrated, and embedded in an epoxide resin after washing in distilled water. Ultrathin slices were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate and then observed under a TEM (Hitachi, Japan).

In vivo imaging to evaluate the distribution of urine-derived stem cells

USCs were transfected with the First Ubiquitin promoter - Green fluorescent protein - Woodchuck Hepatitis Virus Posttranscriptional Regulatory Element (FUGW) plasmid (YiBang, China) to label luciferase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), following standard procedures. USCs expressing luciferase were then thawed in a buffer composed of a 1:1 mixture of 0.9% NaCl and 5% human serum albumin containing 10% dextran 40 and injected into the liver of a newborn RDEB mouse. Subsequently, D-fluorescein potassium salt was injected intraperitoneally at different times. The distribution of USCs was dynamically observed using an in vivo imaging system (Caliper Life Sciences, USA).

Statistical analysis

Sample sizes for statistical analysis comprised three (in vitro) replicates per group at each time point. In the in vivo study, 61 mice were in the USC-treated group (n = 61) and 55 mice in the untreated group (n = 55). As for statistical analysis, all data were collected from at least triplicated samples and expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using R software (Version 4.3.1) and GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (GraphPad Software, USA). Differences between groups were analyzed using Student’s t-test or one/two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by post hoc Tukey’s test or Dunn’s test for multiple comparisons. Survival differences between groups were evaluated using Kaplan–Meier survival analysis, with statistical significance assessed by the log-rank test. A P-value < 0.05 was deemed to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Urine-derived stem cells met the criteria for defining multipotent mesenchymal stem cells

In USC culture plates, cell colonies were observed 7–14 days after the initial plating. A spindle- or rice-like morphology was observed in the majority of adhering cells (Figure S3a, see online supplementary material). USCs were positive for the antigens CD29, CD44, CD73, CD105, and HLA-ABC and negative for the antigens CD34 and HLA-DR according to flow cytometry analysis (Figure S3b, see online supplementary material). USCs could undergo osteogenic, adipogenic, and chondrogenic differentiation, as shown by the positive staining for Alizarin Red, Oil Red O, and Toluidine blue in osteogenic, adipogenic, and chondrogenic media, respectively (Figure S3c, see online supplementary material). Hence, USCs met the criteria for defining multipotent MSCs. Q-PCR and RT-PCR analyses showed that USCs expressed COL7A1 (Figure S4, see online supplementary material).

Characterization of wild-type acellular dermal matrix and recessive dystrophic epidermolysis bullosa acellular dermal matrix

Figure 1a shows a clear view of WT-ADM and RDEB-ADM, which are white and semitransparent. DNA quantification verified that the average DNA content of WT-dermis, RDEB-dermis, WT-ADM, and RDEB-ADM was 1362 ± 32.15 ng/mg, 1445 ± 29.05 ng/mg, 37.43 ± 2.56 ng/mg, and 40.93 ± 2.06 ng/mg, respectively (Figure 1b). The average DNA content of WT-ADM and RDEB-ADM satisfied the criteria for decellularization (<50 ng/mg). HE and DAPI staining revealed that no recognizable residual nuclear debris was left in WT-ADM and RDEB-ADM, and Masson staining of ADM confirmed that the arrangement of collagen fibers in ADM was not only similar to that of the dermis but also intact without obvious fracture (Figure 1c). SEM observations revealed that the collagen fibers on the smooth sides of WT-ADM and RDEB-ADM were neatly aligned in a certain direction. The rough side surface of WT/RDEB-ADM was multi-porous, and collagen fiber bundles were loosely interlaced (Figure 1d).

Urine-derived stem cells suppress inflammation and delay fibrosis progression of keratinocytes and fibroblasts from recessive dystrophic epidermolysis bullosa tissue-engineered skin

After coculturing USCs and TES, q-PCR results showed that the expression of inflammatory cytokine–related genes such as interleukin (IL)-6, IL-8, tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, and interferon (IFN)-γ, and MMP-related genes such as MMP-2, MMP-10, and MMP-13 in keratinocytes of the RDEB-TES + USCs group was downregulated compared to keratinocytes in the RDEB-TES group (P < 0.05). In contrast, IL-10 was upregulated in keratinocytes of the RDEB-TES + USCs group (P <0.05) (Figure 3). Q-PCR analysis of fibroblasts showed that USCs significantly decreased the expression of TGF-β1, IL-6, TNF-α, IFN-γ, MMP-9, and MMP-13 but upregulated the expression of IL-10 and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) in the RDEB-TES + USCs group under both the basal condition (Figure 4) and the TGF-β1-activated condition (Figure 5) (P < 0.05).

Urine-derived stem cells rescue cutaneous manifestation and suppress inflammation and fibrosis of recessive dystrophic epidermolysis bullosa mice

Urine-derived stem cells rescue cutaneous manifestation and extend survival time of recessive dystrophic epidermolysis bullosa mice

Figure 6a shows obvious blood blisters on the paws and chest of newborn RDEB mice. After 7 days of USC treatment, the skin lesions of newborn RDEB mice significantly improved. No new blisters, blood blisters, or finger deformities were found on Days 14, 21, and 28 in USC-treated RDEB mice. Kaplan–Meier survival curve analysis (Figure 6b) showed that USC administration extended the median life span of RDEB mice from 3 to 5 days (P < 0.0001), and the survival probability of USC-treated RDEB mice was higher than that of the RDEB group at each time point. Almost 11% of recipient mice survived to adulthood (>3 weeks).

Discussion

The study is the first to report the potential efficacy of human-derived USCs, a novel MSC source for the treatment of RDEB mice. USCs can not only be harvested via a noninvasive, painless procedure and possess a high proliferative capacity [35] but also express COL7A1. These features make USCs an ideal option for treating RDEB. Schosserer et al. successfully extracted USCs from urine derived from patients with EB in 2015 [36], providing a basis for obtaining autologous USCs from patients with RDEB or their families to avoid immune responses in the future. In addition, this study confirmed a significant improvement in the life span, blistering phenotype, and C7 expression in RDEB mice following USC administration.

RDEB mice have blood blisters on the skin at birth, followed by secondary erosion and infection; thus, mice usually cannot survive for >2 days without treatment [23, 37]. Because of premature death in RDEB mice, increasing the median life span and long-term survival are important for evaluating the therapeutic efficacy of USCs in animal models. In our study, the median life span of RDEB mice was extended to 5 days after intrahepatic administration of USCs. This phenomenon suggests that USCs themselves have regenerative activity in RDEB mice to a certain extent. We speculated that this may be related to the improvement of blister formation in the skin, oral cavity, and digestive tract of some recipients, resulting in better feeding after UCS infusion. Besides, USCs could release anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory cytokines (such as regulated on activation in normal T cell expressed and secreted [RANTES], growth-regulated oncogene-α [GRO-α], and granulocyte macrophage colony-stimulating factor [GM-CSF]) [38, 39], which may also improve the survival of RDEB mice.

Moreover, deposition of C7 was detected in the BMZ of skin from USC-treated RDEB mice, and TEM assays further showed that the lamina densa was significantly improved, with wisp-like structures of anchoring fibrils. At present, the mechanism of action of stem cells in EB treatment has not been fully elucidated. Raoufinia et al. reported that MSCs were found in the skin, lung, and other organs after bone marrow transplantation [40]. After migrating and homing to damaged skin and other organs, MSCs can mature into fibroblasts (stem cell genes shut off and genes for fibroblast markers including COL7A1 turn on) and then synthesize functional proteins that are lacking in patients with RDEB [41, 42]. In our study, because RDEB mice completely lacked C7, it was speculated that the detected C7 was only being produced by USCs. However, the identity of C7 and the specific mechanisms underlying its production remain unclear. It is also crucial to note that although some anchoring fibrils were formed, their morphology and quantity were significantly abnormal. This suggests that USCs do not completely reverse the disease phenotype in RDEB mice. Compton et al. reported that anchoring fibrils usually require 18 months to reach maturity in human skin regeneration [43]. Therefore, another factor related to this outcome to consider was the possibility that the C7 level obtained from USCs may not have been sufficient or have a considerably short life span for their proper assembly into normal anchoring fibrils. Nevertheless, in our study, discontinuous deposition of C7 was detected at the BMZ within 28 days in USC-treated RDEB mice but was not observed in older mice. The reason for this is probably C7 attenuation and depletion of USCs in vivo. Researchers have reported that the half-life of C7 is ~1 month [44], which coincides with the period during which we observed C7. The oldest mouse that survived after USC administration in this study was 48 days old. Although feeding behavior was similar to that of a healthy WT mouse, it was smaller and lighter than that of a healthy WT mouse of the same age. This may be related to the gastrointestinal involvement in RDEB mice (Figure S6).

In addition to the absence of C7, inflammation and fibrosis are also characteristic features of animal models and patients with RDEB. Chronic inflammation, fibrosis, and scarring during wound healing place patients with RDEB at a higher risk of developing aggressive cSCC than the general population [45]. Researchers have reported that patients with REDB have higher TGF-β1 levels, abnormal expression of multiple MMPs (MMP-2, 9, and 13) in the serum and skin wounds compared to healthy people [46–49]. In addition, Albrengues et al. reported that IL-6 family members can trigger the invasive activation of fibroblasts [50, 51], and a higher level of IL-6 expression was found in the plasma of patients with RDEB [52]. In this study, we used a 3D research mode (TES) to evaluate the efficacy of USCs in RDEB in vitro. Through the Transwell coculturing system, USCs downregulated the levels of IL-6, IL-8, and MMP-2, 10, and 13 in keratinocytes of RDEB-TES. Notably, the IL-6/IL-10 ratio of keratinocytes in RDEB-TES cocultured with USCs decreased nearly 10-fold compared with that in the control. Studies have shown that the higher the ratio of IL-6/IL-10, the worse the prognosis of patients with EB in later stages [53]; therefore, USCs may also be beneficial for the long-term prognosis of RDEB. TGF-β1, IL-6, TNF-α, IFN-γ, MMP-9, and MMP-13 of fibroblasts in RDEB-TES also decreased after co-culturing with USCs. As previously mentioned, studies have shown that USCs could regulate inflammation and immune reactions [38, 39]. Additionally, Zhou et al. observed that CD4 T-cell proliferation was inhibited and mRNA levels of IFN-γ and IL-17A were significantly reduced after coculturing with USCs [54]. Similarly, Kang et al. cocultured USCs with peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and observed a dose-dependent inhibitory effect of USCs on PBMC proliferation [55]. In this study, USCs showed similar suppressive effects on inflammation in RDEB-derived keratinocytes and fibroblasts. In contrast, expression of VEGF in fibroblasts of RDEB-TES was upregulated after coculturing with USCs. Jiang et al. demonstrated that USCs could release VEGF via paracrine effect [56], and Ouyang et al. found that USCs could provide proangiogenic-related microRNAs, such as miR-10, miR-21, and miR-30, which may result in an increase in VEGF expression [57]. USCs also upregulated the expression of TGF-β3 in fibroblasts of RDEB-TES. TGF-β3 is an isoform of TGF-β1; when TGF-β3 is combined with the TGF-βII receptor, different signal transduction pathways promoting scar-free healing are initiated [6, 58, 59]. A previous study demonstrated that the activation of the TGF-β signaling in fibroblasts of RDEB mice occurred as early as 1 week postnatally [6]. Since the fibroblasts in our study were extracted from neonatal mice, we also used TGF-β1 to stimulate fibroblasts in TES and then cocultured them with USCs. Except for the expression of TGF-β3 in fibroblasts, which was downregulated by USCs under an exogenous TGF-β1-activated condition, other inflammatory and fibrotic-related genes like TGF-β1, IL-6, MMP-9, and MMP-13 presented a similar variation trend to the normal condition. The expression of TGF-β3 was downregulated by 1-fold, but the TGF-β1 level was decreased by 10-fold under TGF-β1-activated conditions. Overall, USCs showed anti-inflammatory and antifibrotic effects against REDB in vitro.

To validate this speculation, we further analyzed the changes in the expression of inflammation- and fibrosis-related genes in the skin of RDEB mice after USC treatment. Q-PCR results of the skin tissue demonstrated that the administration of USCs acted similarly to suppress TGF-β1, IL-6, and MMP-2, 13 in RDEB mice on Days 7 and 14. This suggests that USCs play an anti-inflammatory role and delay the progression of fibrosis in a mouse model of RDEB. Actually, USCs have been reported to have anti-inflammatory properties in many diseases. Tian et al. reported that USCs can downregulate the expression levels of proinflammatory factors such as INF-γ and IL-1β in USC-treated rat model of ischemic acute kidney injury [60]. Li et al. found that USCs could improve the expression of inflammation-related factors, such as IL-6 and TNF-α, and reduce mast cell infiltration in a rat model of interstitial cystitis [61]. Moreover, USCs can reduce myeloperoxidase (MPO) activity and the number of MPO+ cells in tissues to reduce neutrophil infiltration [38, 54]. Since the skin of RDEB mice is prone to forming erosions accompanied by the infiltration of a large number of inflammatory cells (including neutrophils), the suppressive effect of USCs on neutrophils may subsequently lead to the downregulation of inflammatory factors. Additionally, Chen et al. found that tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinase 1 (TIMP1) (a natural inhibitor of MMPs) was highly enriched in USCs-extracellular vesicles [62], which may explain the suppressive effect of USCs through downregulating the MMP genes. However, it is worth noting that the expression levels of MMP-9 in keratinocytes from RDEB-TES and skin tissues were upregulated compared to those in the control group after USC intervention. MMP-9 is a gelatinase (termed gelatinase A) belonging to the large MMP family. In addition to MMP-9, MMP-2 is also a gelatinase (termed gelatinase B); the two work together to degrade gelatin and various types of collagen. The deletion of MMP-2 may be functionally compensated for by MMP-9 [63]. In our study, because of the inhibition of MMP-2 by USCs, the level of MMP-9 increased as a result of compensation. In fact, MMP-9 increased 1.5-fold in keratinocytes in RDEB-TES cocultured with USCs, but MMP-2 decreased nearly 4-fold compared to the control; an analogous variation trend was also exhibited in the skin tissue from USC-treated mice on Day 14. These results suggest that USCs could still inhibit the degradation of the extracellular matrix in RDEB mice and may play a role in reducing the invasion and metastatic ability of tumors in later periods. However, the causes and specific mechanisms of upregulation of the MMP-9 gene induced by USCs require further study.

We also investigated the distribution of USCs after intrahepatic administration in newborn RDEB mice and detected USCs in the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney after 7 days, and a greater number of USCs were distributed in the heart and lungs. In addition, we did not observe any abnormal changes in the structure of any organ (Figure S7). Lee et al. reported that within a few minutes of intravenous injection of MSCs in mice, >80% of them first accumulated in the lungs [64]. In vivo imaging examination revealed that the fluorescence signals were mainly located in the chest and abdomen, indicating that USCs injected into the liver first reached the lungs and then circulated to various organs. Importantly, USCs were also detected in the gastrointestinal tract of RDEB mice after treatment. This phenomenon may have vital therapeutic significance for patients with RDEB presenting skin injury as well as esophageal structural involvement. However, we did not perform further analyses of other changes in the gastrointestinal tract before and after treatment, leaving further exploration for future studies.

In summary, in this study, the efficacy of USCs in ameliorating skin lesions and improving the separation rates of DEJ and the expression of C7 in RDEB mice were investigated for the first time. RDEB-TES was successfully prepared in vitro. In vitro experiments based on RDEB-TES and in vivo experiments in RDEB mice showed that USCs manifested the effect of suppressing inflammation and delaying fibrosis. In addition, USCs could migrate to multiple organs of RDEB mice after intrahepatic administration, which may be an ideal “seeded player” for RDEB with multiple system involvement. These study findings reflect the potential of USCs in treating patients with RDEB. There were also some limitations of this study. Due to the poor survival of RDEB mice, a certain number of survivors at specific time points could not be attained in this study. Therefore, the conclusions drawn at subsequent intervals were based on the data from mice that survived, resulting in a certain degree of selectivity in the outcomes. In future studies, a greater number of RDEB mice will be needed to verify the specific therapeutic effects of USCs in RDEB. The other aspect that needs to be explored is how USCs are engrafted onto the skin and undergo division or proliferation in vivo.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that human USCs have several key properties in treating a mouse model of RDEB. First, USCs suppressed inflammation and delayed fibrosis progression of keratinocytes and fibroblasts in RDEB mice. Second, the life span, DEJ of skin, expression of C7, ultrastructure of the BMZ, and inflammation and fibrosis of RDEB mice were improved after intrahepatic injection of USCs. Third, USCs could migrate to different organs of RDEB without structural damage to vital organs or tumorigenic activity after circulation in RDEB mice. These findings support their future use for the treatment of RDEB. Taken together, this study justifies future clinical research on USCs as a general source of stem cells in patients with RDEB.
