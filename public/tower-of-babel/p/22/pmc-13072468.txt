Association between preoperative hypoalbuminemia and surgical site infection in abdominal surgery: a systematic review and meta-analysis

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSurgical site infections (SSIs) represent a significant postoperative complication following gastrointestinal surgery. Hypoalbuminemia has been proposed as a potential risk factor, but the strength of this association remains unclear. This review aimed to systematically evaluate the relationship between preoperative serum albumin levels and SSI risk in patients undergoing gastrointestinal surgery.MethodsA systematic search was conducted in PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus from inception through November 2025. Studies were eligible if they examined the association between serum albumin and SSI in adult patients undergoing gastrointestinal surgery, measured preoperative serum albumin levels, and reported SSI as an outcome. Two separate analyses were performed: odds ratios (OR) comparing hypoalbuminemia versus normal albumin groups, and standardized mean differences (SMD) comparing albumin levels between patients with and without SSI.ResultsTwenty-three studies, involving 681,181 patients, were included. The meta-analysis demonstrated that hypoalbuminemia significantly increased SSI risk (OR 2.37, 95% CI: 1.84–3.05, p < 0.001). Analysis of studies comparing mean albumin levels showed patients with SSI had significantly lower albumin concentrations than those without SSI (SMD − 0.80, 95% CI: -1.21 to -0.39, p < 0.001). Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of findings, and no publication bias was detected.ConclusionPreoperative hypoalbuminemia is strongly associated with increased SSI risk following gastrointestinal surgery. From a clinical and patient safety perspective, serum albumin should be incorporated into preoperative risk stratification to identify high-risk patients and guide targeted interventions.

Background

A surgical site infection (SSI), as defined by the National Healthcare Safety Network (NHSN), is an infection that develops at the surgical incision site or within the anatomical area involved in a surgical procedure, during a specified monitoring period of either 30- or 90-days post-surgery, based on the type of surgery performed [1]. SSIs are among the most frequently reported complications following abdominal surgery, leading to substantial consequences ranging from increased morbidity and healthcare costs to mortality [2]. To improve surgical outcomes, increasing attention has been directed toward implementing a preoperative nutritional assessment as an essential component of perioperative care; markers of nutritional status, particularly serum albumin, have been closely associated with postoperative wound healing and infectious complications following gastrointestinal surgery [3]. Hypoalbuminemia is a recognized key indicator of malnutrition, systemic inflammation, and postoperative complications [4].

Several studies suggest that low preoperative serum albumin levels independently predict SSIs, particularly deep infections, and tend to require prolonged hospitalization following abdominal surgeries [5–6]. However, the available evidence remains inconsistent due to substantial heterogeneity in study design, patient populations, outcome definitions across surgical literature, and variability in serum albumin cut-off values used to define hypoalbuminemia. Although many studies suggest an association between lower preoperative serum albumin levels and increased SSI risk, the lack of methodological standardization limits definitive conclusions [6].

Moreover, prior systematic reviews and meta-analyses have predominantly examined the relationship between preoperative hypoalbuminemia and overall postoperative morbidity or restricted their scope in specific surgical subgroups, such as colorectal cancer patients [7]. Consequently, the magnitude and consistency of the association between preoperative hypoalbuminemia and SSI following abdominal surgery remain insufficiently synthesized. To date, no systematic review and meta-analysis has comprehensively evaluated this association exclusively in abdominal surgical procedures. Clarifying this relationship may improve preoperative risk stratification and inform clinical decision-making and perioperative management. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to determine whether preoperative hypoalbuminemia is associated with an increased risk of postoperative SSI among patients undergoing abdominal surgery.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted and reported following the PRISMA guidelines, and the research protocol was previously registered (CRD420251186675). A comprehensive systematic literature search was conducted across major electronic databases (PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus) from each database’s inception through November 2025, using the following keywords to identify studies examining the relationship between serum albumin levels and SSI in adult patients undergoing gastrointestinal surgery: (“Albumin” OR “hypoalbuminemia” OR “hypo-albuminemia” OR “preoperative albumin” OR “pre-operative albumin”) AND (“Surgical Wound Infection” OR “surgical site infection” OR “SSI” OR “wound infection” OR “postoperative infection” OR “post-operative infection”) AND (“abdominal” OR “abdominal surgery” OR “gastrointestinal surgery” OR “hepato-pancreato-biliary” OR “hepatobiliary” OR “upper gastrointestinal” OR “lower gastrointestinal” OR “bowel” OR “gastric” OR “intestinal” OR “colon” OR “colorectal” OR “appendectomy” OR “laparotomy” OR “laparoscopy”).

Studies were included if they met the following criteria: adult patients (≥ 18 years) undergoing gastrointestinal or abdominal surgery, measurement of preoperative serum albumin levels, clearly defined SSI outcomes according to standardized criteria, and provision of sufficient data to calculate effect sizes. Studies were excluded if they lacked explicit data on albumin-SSI relationships, involved exclusively pediatric populations, or were review articles, case reports, or conference abstracts without full data.

Data extraction was performed systematically using a standardized form. The following information was collected from each study: first author, publication year, country of origin, sample size, patient demographics (mean age, sex distribution), type of surgical procedure, albumin cut-off values for defining hypoalbuminemia, SSI diagnostic criteria, and relevant outcome measures including odds ratios, mean albumin levels with standard deviations, and SSI incidence rates. Critical appraisal checklist provided by “Joanna Biggs Institute” was used to assess for risk of bias in the study design and conduct (http://jbi.global/critical-appraisal-tools).

For evidence synthesis, two primary outcomes were analyzed. First, the odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) for SSI development in patients with hypoalbuminemia compared to those with normal albumin levels. Second, the standardized mean difference (SMD) with 95% CI in serum albumin concentrations between patients who developed SSI and those who did not.

Meta-analysis was conducted using random-effects models to account for anticipated heterogeneity across studies. For dichotomous outcomes, pooled odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals were calculated. For continuous outcomes, standardized mean differences (SMD) were computed. Between-study heterogeneity was quantified using the I² statistic. Publication bias was assessed through visual inspection of funnel plots and formally tested using Egger’s regression test and Begg’s rank correlation test. Meta-regression analyses were performed to explore potential sources of heterogeneity, examining variables including patient age, sex distribution, and albumin cut-off definitions. Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of pooled estimates by systematically removing each study and recalculating effect sizes. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All analyses were performed using the meta package in R version 4.4.3.

Results

Study characteristics

The electronic database search produced a total of 1706 records. After eliminating duplicate entries, 1391 studies went through a process of screening titles and abstracts. At this phase, 1361 not relevant studies were eliminated, and complete texts of the remaining records were examined. The graphic flow of the studies selection process is demonstrated in (Fig. 1).

This systematic review included 23 studies with explicit data on the relationship between serum albumin and SSI [5, 8–29]. The studies were published between 2010 and 2025, including populations from Asia, the Middle East, Europe, and North America. All of the included studies assessed adult population. These studies involved diverse patient populations, ranging from small prospective cohorts of patients to large registry analyses (Table 1).

The surgeries generally involved abdominal or gastrointestinal procedures, with most of them were malignancy-related surgeries, such as radical resections for colorectal cancer or gastric cancer. Other inflammatory and benign conditions were included also, such as bariatric surgeries and procedures for Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis (Table 1). The follow-up period was consistent across studies (30 days postoperatively). Diagnosis of SSI was made mainly following the standards set by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and NHSN. Most studies assess the three standard anatomical depths (superficial incisional, deep incisional, and organ/space SSI); however, fewer studies focused on a specific subtype or group of SSIs (Table 2).

Impact of hypoalbuminemia on SSI risk

The first analysis investigated the association between hypoalbuminemia and the development of SSI across 14 independent studies. This pooled cohort comprised a total of 681,181 observations, consisting of 10,181 patients in the hypoalbuminemia (experimental) group and 671,000 patients in the normal albumin (control) group.

The meta-analysis revealed that patients with low albumin levels were significantly more likely to develop SSIs compared to those with normal albumin levels (OR 2.37; 95% CI: 1.84–3.05; p < 0.001) (Fig. 2). Heterogeneity was observed across studies (I² = 80.3%), and visual inspection of the funnel plot demonstrated a symmetric distribution of effect sizes around the pooled estimate (Fig. 3). There was no evidence of significant publication bias detected by Egger’s test (p = 0.53) or Begg’s test (p = 0.70).

Meta-regression was performed to explore potential sources of heterogeneity in the OR analysis. Patient age was a significant moderator (coefficient = 0.04, p = 0.022), suggesting that the association between albumin levels and SSI may vary across age groups. Meta-regression was performed to explore if heterogeneity was affected by the cut-off points used to define hypoalbuminemia across studies. However, the definition of albumin cut-off did not have a statistically significant influence on the effect size (coefficient = − 0.96, p = 0.09). In addition, proportion of male patients (coefficient = 0.02, p = 0.10) did not influence effect estimates.

A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis showed that the association between hypoalbuminemia and SSI remained remarkably consistent and statistically significant regardless of which study was omitted. The pooled OR fluctuated within a narrow range, from a minimum of 2.20 (95% CI: 1.72–2.81) when omitting Dong et al. study to a maximum of 2.60 (95% CI: 2.05–3.31) when omitting Khan et al. study. Additionally, the omission of any single study did not result in a substantial reduction of statistical heterogeneity.

Difference in serum albumin mean levels between SSI and non-SSI groups

Twelve studies comparing mean albumin levels between patients who developed SSI (n = 1,006) and those who did not (n = 22,152) were analyzed. The quantitative synthesis revealed that patients who developed SSI had significantly lower serum albumin levels compared to those without SSI, with a large effect size (SMD = -0.80, 95% CI: -1.21 to -0.39, p < 0.001) (Fig. 4). No evidence of publication bias was detected in the SMD analysis using either Egger’s test (p = 0.31) or Begg’s test (p = 0.58). The analysis demonstrated heterogeneity (I² = 96.8%), and visual inspection of the funnel plot demonstrated an asymmetric distribution of effect sizes around the pooled estimate (Fig. 5). To evaluate the stability of the results, a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed. Exclusion of each individual study in turn did not affect the statistical significance of the pooled effect size. However, the SMD shifted to -0.49 when the study by Ge et al. was omitted.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated that preoperative hypoalbuminemia is significantly associated with SSIs after abdominal surgery. Across 14 studies comprising over 680,000 observations, patients with low serum albumin levels were more than twice as likely to develop an SSI compared to those with normal levels (OR 2.37, 95% CI: 1.84–3.05, p < 0.001). The preoperative serum albumin could serve as a biomarker and infectious risk factor in people undergoing abdominal surgery.

The observed association is biologically plausible and consistent with established mechanisms. Serum albumin indicates nutritional status and systemic inflammatory burden, both of which are critical determinants of immune competence and wound healing. There is a link between hypoalbuminemia and increased incidence of postoperative wound infection. Some studies showed that hypoalbuminemia can impair collagen synthesis and fibroblast proliferation, cause delay in angiogenesis and impair cellular and humoral immunity, all of which leads to increased risk of postoperative wound infection [30]. Additionally, chronic illness, catabolic stress, or acute inflammation are often associated with a low albumin, making the occurrence of SSI after bowel surgeries more likely.

This meta-analysis supports the findings of previous observational studies showing that low albumin levels independently predict SSIs, independent of demographic factors, comorbidities and operative characteristics [5]. As per the studies reviewed, SSI rates were found to be high amongst patients with low preoperative albumin levels in numerous cohort studies irrespective of their abdominal site. These included colorectal, gastric, hepatobiliary, bariatric, and emergency surgical procedures [31].

Despite the consistency in the direction of effect, substantial heterogeneity was observed among the included studies. These differences in studies probably stem from the inherent clinical and methodological diversity of the included studies, such as variations in comorbidities, urgency of surgery, complexity of the abdominal procedure, and SSI surveillance protocols. Meta-regression indicated that patient age significantly moderated the effect, implying that the albumin-SSI relationship may strengthen in older populations, possibly due to compounded vulnerabilities in nutrition, immunity, and comorbidities among elderly patients [32–33].

From a clinical perspective, the findings have important implications for preoperative risk-stratification and perioperative optimization. From a patient safety perspective, serum albumin is an inexpensive and easily available biomarker that may be useful in identifying patients at increased risk for SSIs after surgery. If hypoalbuminemia is identified prior to surgery, it could encourage targeted interventions such as detailed nutritional assessment, optimization of inflammatory conditions, prehabilitation and/or timing of elective procedures. Given that serum albumin levels are influenced by both nutritional status and systemic inflammation, optimizing overall nutritional and inflammatory status may be more clinically meaningful than focusing solely on correcting albumin levels [34]. Incorporating albumin level in patient-related risk stratification should be considered along with the intraoperative preventive strategies that have been investigated to reduce SSI following laparotomy [35]. Future research should also prioritize the adoption of standardized albumin thresholds to improve comparability across studies. Additionally, examining albumin as a continuous variable may provide greater insight into its relationship with postoperative infectious outcomes.

A few limitations should be acknowledged. Because all of the studies included were observational studies, thus causal inference could not be conducted and residual confounding was possible. In addition, SSI subtypes were not uniformly reported, limiting further analysis by SSI depth or severity.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis of 23 studies involving over 680,000 patients demonstrates that preoperative hypoalbuminemia is significantly associated with increased SSI risk following gastrointestinal surgery. Identifying hypoalbuminemia preoperatively may enable targeted interventions such as nutritional optimization and enhanced perioperative monitoring. However, the optimal albumin cut-off thresholds require further investigation.
