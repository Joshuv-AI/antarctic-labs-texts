Broad-spectrum targeted next-generation sequencing: is it ready for routine deployment in intensive care units for severe pneumonia?
Authors: How-Yang Tseng, Yu-Chang Fu, Yue-Hua Su, Yu-Chu Kuo, Shinn-Jye Liang, Yi-Cheng Shen, Wei-Cheng Chen, Chieh-Lung Chen, Yu-Chao Lin, Meng-Yu Cheng, Chih-Hao Chen, Chih-Yen Tu, Zi-Lun Lai, Po-Ren Hsueh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTargeted next-generation sequencing (tNGS) has demonstrated higher analytical detection of respiratory pathogens than conventional microbiological tests (CMTs), yet its real-world clinical value in intensive care units (ICUs) remains uncertain. We conducted an interim analysis of a prospective study including 51 adult ICU patients with severe pneumonia at a tertiary medical center in Taiwan and examined the challenges to its routine implementation in the ICU. Bronchoalveolar lavage samples were analyzed using a hybrid capture–based Respiratory Pathogen ID/AMR Enrichment Panel (RPIP) and compared with standard testing (CMT combined with the FilmArray Pneumonia Panel [FAPP]). Causative pathogens were determined through multidisciplinary expert adjudication. RPIP showed a higher analytical detection rate than standard testing (90.2% vs. 82.4%). However, the proportion of adjudicated causative pathogens was similar between RPIP and standard testing (47.1% vs. 49.0%), with no significant difference (McNemar’s exact test, p = 1.00). After expert adjudication, RPIP provided additional clinically actionable yield in 23.5% of patients compared with CMT + FAPP, mainly related to antimicrobial resistance determinants, while 17.6% showed concordant results, 13.7% demonstrated lower yield with RPIP, and 45.1% of cases had no adjudicated causative pathogen after comprehensive review. Although tNGS broadens pathogen and resistance detection, its routine implementation in the ICU requires clearly defined indications, validated genotype–phenotype correlations, optimized turnaround time, strict contamination control, and structured multidisciplinary integration.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13054-026-05974-2.

Recent clinical interest in targeted next-generation sequencing (tNGS) is driven by its superior diagnostic yield relative to conventional microbiological tests (CMTs) for identifying respiratory pathogens [1–3]. However, challenges persist in integrating next-generation sequencing (NGS) into routine intensive care unit (ICU) workflow, and whether more comprehensive diagnostic approaches provide additional clinical benefit beyond CMTs and existing molecular assays in real-world practice remains uncertain. Li et al. note that molecular diagnostics often face barriers to routine clinical deployment despite high analytical accuracy [4]. Wu et al. further identified several critical hurdles to clinical translation, including distinguishing true pathogens from increased diagnostic yields, proving real-time feasibility, influencing clinical management, and improving patient outcomes [5].

This commentary evaluates the comparative diagnostic performance of tNGS relative to current standard-of-care testing and addresses challenges to its routine implementation in the ICU. We report an interim analysis of an ongoing prospective study conducted at China Medical University Hospital, Taiwan, enrolling 51 adult ICU patients with severe pneumonia since November 2023. Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) samples were analyzed using a hybrid capture–based Respiratory Pathogen ID/AMR Enrichment Panel (RPIP; Illumina) and compared with conventional microbiological tests (CMT) and the FilmArray Pneumonia Panel (FAPP). Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq platform and analyzed using DRAGEN Microbial Enrichment Plus v1.1.1 (Illumina, Inc.). Causative pathogens were adjudicated by three clinicians based on clinical, imaging, microbiological, and treatment-response data; sputum Gram stains were reviewed by an infectious disease specialist (Additional file 1: Supplementary methods).

In our study, the overall analytical detection rates were 90.2% (46/51) for RPIP and 82.4% (42/51) for standard testing (CMT combined with FAPP). After expert adjudication, the adjudicated causative pathogen proportion was 47.1% (24/51) for RPIP and 49.0% (25/51) for standard testing (Fig. 1). McNemar’s exact test demonstrated no statistically significant difference in adjudicated causative pathogen identification between RPIP and CMT + FAPP (p = 1.00; Additional file1: Table S1). RPIP provided additional clinically actionable yield in 23.5% (12/51) of patients, whereas 17.6% (9/51) demonstrated concordant clinically actionable yield and 13.7% (7/51) showed lower clinically actionable yield with RPIP. Notably, 45.1% (23/51) of cases remained without an adjudicated causative pathogen after clinical review (Fig. 2).

After expert adjudication, RPIP was compared with CMT + FAPP to determine whether it provided additional causative pathogen information for clinical decision-making. Patients were classified into four mutually exclusive diagnostic groups at the patient level (N = 51). Among 51 ICU patients, RPIP provided additional clinically actionable yield in 12/51 cases (23.5%), including antimicrobial resistance determinants (n = 9), viral pathogens (n = 2), and bacterial pathogens (n = 1). Concordant clinically actionable yield between RPIP and CMT + FAPP was observed in 9/51 cases (17.6%) (viruses, n = 4; bacteria, n = 4; Pneumocystis jirovecii, n = 1). Lower clinically actionable yield with RPIP was identified in 7/51 (13.7%) cases, most commonly involving Aspergillus species (n = 4), Pneumocystis jirovecii (n = 1), other fungal pathogens (n = 1), and bacteria (n = 1). No adjudicated causative pathogen was identified by either method in 23/51 (45.1%) cases, including 17 cases in which detected organisms were adjudicated as non-causative and 6 cases in which no pathogen was detected. Alternative noninfectious diagnoses were established after clinical review, including acute leukemia with tumor lysis syndrome (n = 1), virus-induced cytokine syndrome (n = 1), immune-related adverse events (n = 2), anti–MDA5-associated rapidly progressive interstitial lung disease (n = 1), lung collapse (n = 1), and fungal sinusitis (n = 1). Despite the emerging clinical adoption of tNGS for the diagnosis of severe pneumonia in critically ill patients, several challenges must be carefully addressed before its routine implementation in the ICU. First, the optimal timing for performing NGS remains unclear. In our study, tNGS yielded identical or less information than CMT and FAPP in 31.4% of cases. These findings suggest that while tNGS detects a broader range of pathogens and AMR genes, CMT and FAPP may be sufficient for pathogen identification in most patients with severe pneumonia in the ICU. A multicenter randomized controlled trial suggested that immunocompromised patients may benefit from NGS [6], whereas Chen et al. reported a low diagnostic yield in immunocompromised patients [3]. Given the relatively high cost of NGS, the optimal timing for its use in critically ill patients with pneumonia requires further clarification.

Second, the use of AMR information generated by tNGS to guide antimicrobial therapy in the ICU remains limited. In our cohort, most additional clinically actionable yield with RPIP involved AMR determinants. Although tNGS platform, such as RPIP, is capable of detecting over 1,200 AMR genes, discrepancies between genotypic AMR markers and phenotypic susceptibility remain common among the major bacterial pathogens causing pneumonia in the ICU (Additional file 2: Table S2) [2, 7]. While timely molecular detection may support antimicrobial stewardship and improve outcomes [8, 9], robust resistance mechanisms and local genotype–phenotype correlations are required before NGS-based predictions can be routinely applied in clinical practice.

Third, non-infectious conditions that mimic pneumonia, such as inflammatory or immune-mediated lung injury, warrant careful consideration. In our cohort, 45.1% of cases remained negative by all diagnostic methods, highlighting the potential role of NGS in avoiding unnecessary broad-spectrum antibiotic use in critically ill patients with noninfectious lung injury. A previous study demonstrated the impact of tNGS on antimicrobial de-escalation strategies [6]. In contrast, Chen et al. reported that nearly half of antimicrobial therapies remained unchanged after molecular diagnostics, likely due to disease severity in the ICU and limited clinician familiarity with the new tool [9]. Multidisciplinary review of negative molecular diagnostic results may increase confidence in avoiding or discontinuing broad-spectrum antibiotics, particularly in critically ill ICU patients. Furthermore, Kalantar et al. suggested that integrating host transcriptional profiling with metagenomic pathogen detection may provide a comprehensive framework for the future diagnosis of sepsis [10].

Fourth, several laboratory workflow considerations must be carefully managed to ensure the successful clinical implementation of tNGS (Table 1). Sample collection from non-sterile sites such as BAL requires strict standardization to prevent cross-contamination. Lai et al. demonstrated that background microbial signals in clinical metagenomic NGS arise from multiple sources, including extraction reagents ("kitome"), lot-to-lot variability, and site-specific environmental contaminants [11]. Optimizing workflow turnaround time (TAT) and integrating multidisciplinary reviews are essential for implementing clinical NGS in the ICU. In our real-world practice, the median analytical TAT was 6.5 days, positioning tNGS primarily as an adjunctive diagnostic tool rather than a same-day therapeutic driver within the current workflow. Charalampous et al. reported a median TAT of 6.7 h using respiratory metagenomics, enabling same-day ICU decisions through close multidisciplinary collaboration [12]. This underscores that clinical utility in the ICU depends on both speed and structured clinical governance. Furthermore, bioinformatic interpretation remains a significant hurdle. Although quantitative metrics like the Sepsis Indicating Quantifier have been proposed [13, 14], fully customized clinical pipelines are difficult to scale. To mitigate non-specific calls in BAL specimens, we employed a post-DRAGEN rule-based stratification. This approach integrates coverage, abundance, depth, and read support, and was calibrated against local data to minimize overcalling from background taxa or colonization.

In conclusion, NGS has the potential to reduce diagnostic gaps in critically ill patients with severe pneumonia in the ICU. However, its clinical impact depends on clearly defined indications for use, robust validation of AMR genotype–phenotype correlations, stringent contamination control, optimized turnaround time, accurate bioinformatic interpretation, and integration with expert clinical review. Future studies should move beyond pathogen detection rates and focus on demonstrable improvements in clinical decision-making and outcomes.
