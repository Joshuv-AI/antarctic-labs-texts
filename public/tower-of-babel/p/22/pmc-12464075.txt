Predicting postoperative fever in culture-negative patients undergoing mini-PCNL using MAP score-augmented machine learning: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Rong Xu, Jia-Jia Wang, Wei-Hong Zhao, Jin Xiong, Zi-wen Lu, Li-Cai Mo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposePostoperative fever is a common complication following percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) that occurs even in patients with sterile urine cultures. Traditional risk-assessment tools are insufficient in this subset of patients. This study aims to develop a risk prediction model for detecting postoperative fever in patients with sterile preoperative urine cultures by integrating the Mayo Adhesive Probability (MAP) score with machine learning (ML) techniques.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 730 patients with sterile urine cultures who underwent mini-percutaneous nephrolithotomy (mini-PCNL) at Taizhou Hospital from March 2022 to March 2025. The least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was employed to identify key variables. Ten ML models were built, and Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis was used to enhance model interpretability, and the optimal model was selected. An online tool was deployed for clinical use.ResultsPostoperative fever occurred in 17.4% of the patients. Logistic regression (LR) demonstrated the best predictive performance (AUC = 0.914, accuracy = 92.1%, specificity = 97.3%). Major predictive factors for fever risk included MAP score ≥ 3, diabetes mellitus, female sex, positive urine leukocytes, and low lymphocyte-monocyte ratio (LMR). SHAP analysis confirmed MAP score and urine leukocytes as the most influential variables. Limitations of the study include its single-centre design.ConclusionsThe proposed tool, combining MAP scores with ML, significantly enhances the accuracy of predicting the risk of postoperative fever following mini-PCNL in patients with sterile urine cultures. The LR model demonstrates strong utility and interpretability, offers personalised risk assessments preoperatively and constitutes a practical and accessible online tool for individualised clinical decision-making.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00345-025-05952-3.

Introduction

With advancements in minimally invasive surgery, percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) has become the leading option for managing large or complex kidney stones, offering higher stone-free rates than traditional open surgery [1]. Despite its efficacy, infection-related complications—especially postoperative fever—remain common, with urinary tract infection rates as high as 37% [2].

Previous studies have shown that patients with positive preoperative urine cultures have a significantly increased risk of developing postoperative fever or infection after PCNL [3]. However, some patients with negative preoperative urine cultures still experience postoperative febrile reactions, revealing limitations of current risk-assessment tools. Limited research exists for this specific population, and effective preoperative risk prediction models are yet to be established.

Recent advances in statistical methods and computational power have facilitated the widespread application of machine learning (ML) algorithms in medical research, particularly in disease prediction, therapeutic decision-making, and clinical risk-assessment [4]. For example, ML models have been successfully applied to predict acute kidney injury following nephrectomy in patients with renal cell carcinoma [5]. Notably, Castellani et al. developed ML model using multicentre prospective data from 1552 patients with kidney stones to predict sepsis risk following retrograde intrarenal surgery, thereby validating the clinical feasibility of ML for infection risk-assessment in urological surgery [6].

Beyond algorithmic models, imaging-based scoring systems have been employed for preoperative risk-assessment. The Mayo Adhesive Probability (MAP) score, originally developed for partial nephrectomies, uses computed tomography (CT) imaging features—posterior perinephric fat thickness and stranding patterns—to predict surgical complexity and postoperative complications, including infections [7, 8]. Fat thickness is strongly correlated with intraoperative bleeding risk, while stranding patterns are associated with postoperative infectious complications [9, 10]. While recent studies have explored the utility of the MAP score in predicting complications following PCNL [11, 12], its integration into ML frameworks remains unexplored, particularly in patients who have undergone mini-percutaneous nephrolithotomy (mini-PCNL) and have negative preoperative urine cultures.

This study aims to integrate the MAP score into ML models to predict post-PCNL fever, compare algorithm performance, and use Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis to enhance interpretability, offering a new online tool for individualised preoperative assessment.

Patients and methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective study was conducted at Taizhou Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University, spanning from March 2022 to March 2025. In total, 790 patients who underwent mini-PCNL were screened for inclusion. After applying the exclusion criteria, 730 patients with negative preoperative urine cultures were included in the final analysis. Ethical approval (approval number: K20250701) was obtained from the local ethics committee; all patient data were anonymised, and the requirement for informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective design.

Inclusion criteria were adults (≥ 18 years) deemed suitable for mini-percutaneous nephrolithotomy (mini-PCNL) with renal or upper ureteral calculi, complete clinical and imaging data, a non-contrast CT within 3 months before surgery, and a negative midstream urine culture within 10 days preoperatively. Exclusion criteria included concomitant lower urinary tract stones, congenital or acquired urological anatomical anomalies, concurrent malignancy with immunosuppressive therapy, prior ureteral stent placement, staged or bilateral PCNL, simultaneous additional procedures, positive or contaminated urine cultures or antibiotic use within 1 week of admission, and absence of preoperative CT data.

Diabetes mellitus was defined as a documented history of diabetes or the use of oral hypoglycaemic agents or insulin. Perioperative blood glucose was managed according to international guidelines, targeting 6–10 mmol/L [13]. Hypertension and other comorbidities were recorded based on established diagnoses. Urinary nitrite status was assessed by dipstick urinalysis within one week before surgery and classified as positive or negative.

Data collection and group definition

We collected baseline characteristics (age, sex, body mass index [BMI], diabetes history), preoperative laboratory parameters (complete blood count, inflammatory ratios including neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio [NLR], Lymphocyte-to-Monocyte Ratio [LMR], Platelet-to-Lymphocyte Ratio [PLR], renal function, albumin, urinalysis), and non-contrast CT imaging data were obtained, including hydronephrosis severity (SFU grading) [14], stone characteristics (Guy’s stone score), and MAP score [15]. Two experienced urologists independently assessed the Guy’s stone score and MAP score according to established criteria [15, 16]. Disagreements were resolved by a third senior urologist. MAP scores were categorised as low-risk (0–2) or high-risk (3–5) [11].

Surgical Technique: Patients with infection indicators received a 3-day course of cephalosporins or fluoroquinolones; others received a single prophylactic dose 30 min before surgery. mini-PCNL was performed under general anaesthesia with ultrasound-guided access, tract dilatation to 18–20 Fr, holmium laser lithotripsy (0.8–2.5 J, 15–25 Hz), and placement of nephrostomy tube and double-J stent. All standardised mini-PCNL procedures were performed by three senior surgeons, each with experience > 200 mini-PCNL cases, thereby minimising inter-operator variability. Nephrostomy tubes were removed at 2–3 days and double-J stents at 2–3 weeks. Postoperatively, antibiotics were discontinued within 48 h. In cases of perioperative infection, therapeutic antibiotics were continued for 3–5 days. Operative time was defined as the duration from renal puncture to nephrostomy tube placement after lithotripsy.

Group Definition: Patients were categorised into fever and non-fever groups based on body temperature >38 °C within 48 h of the procedure [17].

Modelling and feature selection

An ML framework was developed using Python 3.12. From 27 initial variables collected, predictors were selected via least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression with five-fold cross-validation on standardized features (zero mean, unit variance). The optimal penalty parameter (λ) was chosen by minimizing the mean cross-validated error. Variables with non-zero coefficients at the optimal λ were retained (n = 11) and entered simultaneously into the multivariable logistic regression model; no forward or backward stepwise procedures were applied. Numerical variables were standardised using 0–1 normalisation to mitigate scale differences. The dataset was randomly divided into a training set (70%) and a test set (30%) using the train_test_split function. Ten ML algorithms were employed: Logistic Regression (LR), Support Vector Machine (SVM), k-Nearest Neighbours (KNN), Random Forest (RF), XGBoost, LightGBM, AdaBoost, Extra Trees (ET), Decision Tree (DT), and Gaussian Naïve Bayes (GNB). The models were optimised using 5-fold cross-validation. Restricted cubic splines (RCS) analyse the nonlinear relationships between the variables and postoperative fever risk.

Model performance evaluation

The model performance was evaluated using multiple metrics: area under the curve (AUC), accuracy, recall, F1-score, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and Cohen’s κ coefficient. The model performance was visualised using heatmaps, and the “best model” was selected based on comprehensive performance. The selected metrics for the final model validation included: (1) confusion matrix to evaluate prediction accuracy, error types, and classification balance; (2) Receiver–Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve to assess sensitivity and specificity across decision thresholds, with the AUC quantifying discriminative ability; (3) learning curve to plot training and validation performance across varying sample sizes to detect potential underfitting or overfitting; and (4) precision–recall curve to evaluate classification stability under class imbalance conditions, supported by iso-F1 contours and average precision.

Model interpretability analysis

To address the “black-box” nature of ML models and enhance clinical interpretability, we utilised SHAP analysis implemented through the TreeExplainer module (Python SHAP v0.40.0) [17]. This approach provided global interpretability through mean SHAP value polar bar charts and beeswarm plots, whereas local explanations were visualised using decision, waterfall, and force plots. Feature contributions were colour-coded by risk direction (red = high-risk, blue = low-risk), and the stability of the importance rankings was validated using 1000 permutation tests.

Network calculator

We developed a Streamlit-based web tool that allows clinicians to input preoperative parameters for personalised postoperative fever risk estimation. The tool provides (1) predicted risk percentage, (2) SHAP-based variable contributions, and (3) ranked feature importance. This model is intended to aid clinical decision-making and not to replace professional judgement.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted using R (v4.1.3) and Python (v3.12). Continuous variables were compared using t-tests or the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. In the analysis, BMI was treated as a continuous variable to ensure a more accurate and nuanced understanding of its effect on postoperative fever risk. Univariate/multivariate logistic regression (odds ratio [OR], 95% confidence interval [CI]) and RCS were used to assess fever risk (p < 0.05) in R. ML tasks (feature selection, model training, validation, SHAP interpretation, and web tool development) were performed using the scikit-learn, pandas, numpy, matplotlib, shap, and streamlit Python functions.

Results

Baseline clinical characteristics

Among 730 mini-PCNL patients, 127 (17.4%) developed postoperative fever. Compared with the non-fever group, the fever group had higher preoperative monocyte counts (0.53 vs. 0.44, p < 0.001), NLR (4.13 vs. 2.97, p = 0.002), PLR (165.71 vs. 147.07, p = 0.004), but lower LMR (3.78 vs. 4.84, p < 0.001). Fever was also more common in females (43.3% vs. 26.7%), diabetics (32.3% vs. 9.0%), those with positive urinary leukocytes (96.1% vs. 71.0%), and MAP scores ≥ 3 (76.4% vs. 9.5%). They also had larger stones (2.68 cm vs. 2.35 cm, p = 0.003) and longer surgeries (119.5 min vs. 106.4 min, p = 0.002).The baseline characteristics are detailed in Table 1, and the study design is shown in Fig. 1.

Independent risk factors

Univariate analysis identified 15 significant predictors; multivariate logistic regression confirmed 8 independent risk factors: female sex, BMI, diabetes, urinary leukocytes, lymphocyte count, LMR, NLR, and MAP score ≥ 3 (Table S1).

Dose–response relationship

RCS analysis showed a strong nonlinear association between LMR and fever risk (p < 0.001), with lower LMR indicating higher risk. NLR also showed a trend (p = 0.002), while BMI and lymphocyte count were not significantly associated (Fig. S1).

Selection of predictor variables

Using Spearman correlation and LASSO regression, 11 predictors were selected: MAP score, diabetes, urine leukocytes, sex, BMI, urine nitrites, NLR, LMR, tract size, lymphocyte, and monocyte counts. MAP score had the highest positive coefficient, while LMR and BMI showed protective effects (Fig. S2).

Model development and performance comparison

Ten machine learning models were compared using five-fold cross-validation. LR achieved the best performance (AUC = 0.914, accuracy = 0.921, specificity = 0.973, κ = 0.699), followed by RF and XGBoost. Despite moderate recall, LR showed strong overall metrics and is the recommended model (Fig. 2).

Model explanation

To address concerns regarding “black-box” models, this study used SHAP to improve model interpretability through global and individual-level analyses. Global analysis revealed that the MAP score was the most influential predictor, followed by urine leukocyte status, sex, LMR, and tract size (Fig. 5a–b). The beeswarm plot (Fig. 5d) showed that MAP score ≥ 3, positive urine leukocytes, diabetes, and female sex were associated with a greater fever risk, whereas higher LMR, BMI, and NLR were associated with a lower fever risk. Individual analyses showed how each feature affected the predictions. The waterfall plot (Fig. 5c) depicted a patient with fever and whose baseline log-odds of −2.398 increased to 2.861; this increase was associated with MAP score ≥ 3 (+ 2.495), diabetes (+ 1.12), female sex (+ 0.89), positive urine leukocytes (+ 0.36), and low LMR (+ 0.26). The force plot (Fig. 5E) visually confirmed how positive factors outweighed negative factors, classifying the patient as having a high fever risk.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to develop an ML-based predictive model for postoperative fever following mini-PCNL in patients with negative preoperative urine cultures. Our model integrates multi-modal data encompassing imaging-based MAP scores, laboratory parameters, inflammatory markers, and demographic variables. We identified 11 independent predictors, systematically compared 10 ML algorithms, and selected the optimal model for clinical implementation. Additionally, we developed an accessible online calculator to translate these findings into practical clinical applications.

Urinary tract infection is a common postoperative complication of PCNL that, if misdiagnosed or if treatment is delayed, may precipitate high fever and rapidly progress to systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) or sepsis, which can be life-threatening in severe cases [18, 19]. Several studies have confirmed that a positive preoperative urine culture significantly increases the risk of post-PCNL fever [20, 21]. However, “negative” results do not guarantee safety; a randomised controlled trial demonstrated that the postoperative sepsis rate remained as high as 13% even with three-day preoperative antibiotic prophylaxis in patients with negative urine cultures [22]. Recent urinary microbiome research has revealed that low-biomass, high-diversity bacterial communities colonising the bladder may evade conventional culture detection [23]. Therefore, clinicians must remain vigilant about the blind spots of “negative” reports. Zhu et al. identified N + WBC + LE + and operative time >120 min as independent predictors of post-mPCNL sepsis in patients with negative preoperative urine cultures, achieving AUC values of 0.785 and 0.601, respectively [24]. However, this study did not explore the optimal integration of N + WBC + LE + with other variables or investigate additional factors that might enhance predictive performance.

Recently, explainable ML has provided new solutions. Jiang et al. developed an algorithm that integrates multi-modal data to enable early risk stratification and better prognosis assessment [25]. Castellani et al. created an ML model to predict the risk of sepsis in patients with kidney stones who are undergoing retrograde intrarenal surgery, offering a new tool for early sepsis prognosis in urolithiasis [6]. For UTIs, algorithms such as GBM, SVM, and RF can analyse early indicators to identify potential early warning signs [6, 26, 27].

A recent systematic review demonstrated that diabetes is associated with a 4.6-fold increased postoperative fever risk following PCNL [18]. Our study confirmed this finding, with multivariable logistic regression identifying diabetes as an independent risk factor and SHAP analysis ranking it among the top five positive contributors. Previous studies have established that female sex is an independent risk factor for post-PCNL infection, potentially due to sex-related genetic polymorphisms and oestrogen effects [28, 29]. Consistent with international findings, our research confirms that female sex is an independent risk factor for postoperative fever in patients who have undergone PCNL and have negative urine cultures. SHAP analysis identified female sex (SHAP = 1.54) as the third most important predictor, suggesting the need for enhanced perioperative monitoring in these patients.

The MAP score, first proposed by Daviduk et al., has demonstrated predictive value in surgical outcomes [15]. In our cohort, a MAP score ≥ 3 was independently associated with postoperative fever after mini-PCNL, underscoring its utility as a practical tool for preoperative risk stratification. This finding is consistent with previous evidence linking perirenal fat stranding to an increased risk of infection in endourological procedures [9, 11, 30, 31]. However, Haberal et al. reported no significant association between MAP ≥ 3 and postoperative fever in 130 patients undergoing standard-tract PCNL (12.3% vs. 19.2%, P >0.05) [32]. This discrepancy may be explained by surgical technical differences: mini-PCNL involves smaller working tracts and is associated with higher intrapelvic pressures, which may exacerbate two pathological mechanisms—(1) facilitating bacterial reflux and endotoxin absorption, and (2) amplifying the influence of pre-existing perirenal inflammation on postoperative infectious outcomes. These mechanistic distinctions may account for the stronger predictive performance observed in our cohort. From a clinical perspective, MAP scoring allows for early identification of high-risk patients who may benefit from optimized perioperative strategies, such as intensified antibiotic prophylaxis and closer postoperative monitoring [11, 31]. Therefore, the MAP score represents a simple, reproducible, and clinically meaningful parameter that can inform surgical planning and potentially reduce infection-related morbidity.

The presence of urinary calculi has been shown to induce the release of inflammatory mediators, including IL-6, IL-7, IL-8, TNF-α, and G-CSF, resulting in neutrophilia [33]. Excessive inflammatory responses further suppress the cytotoxic activity of lymphocytes, T cells, and natural killer cells, thereby leading to an elevation in NLR. Meanwhile, monocytes play a pivotal role in systemic inflammatory regulation, and their relative increase contributes to a reduction in LMR [33]. Thus, a preoperative elevation of NLR accompanied by a decrease in LMR often reflects ongoing systemic inflammation. Among the SIRS-related indicators, the NLR and LMR derived from routine blood tests are important for perioperative risk-assessment in urology [34]. Tang et al. identified NLR as an independent predictor of SIRS after PCNL (HR: 6.743, p < 0.001), while Kriplani et al. demonstrated that NLR, PLR, and LMR were independent predictors of SIRS after PCNL with AUC values of 0.596, 0.663, and 0.649, respectively [35, 36]. Notably, in sepsis, LMR appeared to outperform NLR, with an optimal cutoff of 2.88 (sensitivity 87.5%, specificity 55%, AUC 0.726) [36]. Similarly, Xu et al. reported LMR as an independent risk factor for SIRS following PCNL [37]. Consistent with these studies, our findings confirmed the predictive value of both NLR and LMR. However, their odds ratios were close to 1, indicating limited clinical impact despite statistical significance. This highlights the gap between statistical and clinical significance. Therefore, NLR and LMR should be considered supportive rather than decisive predictors and interpreted in conjunction with clinical and imaging parameters to improve predictive accuracy and reduce misclassification risk.

A recent meta-analysis of 101,363 cases found no significant association between BMI and PCNL outcomes [38]. Zhu et al. reported higher BMI as a risk factor for sepsis in univariate analysis (continuous variable), but this was not confirmed in multivariate analysis (cutoff ≥ 28 kg/m²) [24]. In our study, mean BMI did not differ between the fever and non-fever groups; however, multivariate regression suggested a slight protective effect (OR < 1). BMI was analyzed as a continuous variable without a predefined cutoff, and RCS analysis showed no significant linear or non-linear relationship. Overall, BMI appears to have limited predictive value and should be considered only as a supportive factor.

To reduce bias and enhance interpretability, we initially included 27 candidate variables, and then applied LASSO regression for dimensionality reduction, identifying 11 independent predictive features. This approach preserved key information while avoiding overfitting in high-dimensional datasets. Ten algorithms were trained using a five-fold cross-validation. LR demonstrated optimal performance, with a test AUC of 0.914 and an accuracy of 0.921, indicating excellent discriminative ability. However, the recall of the model was only 0.669, suggesting missed detection risks for some high-risk patients. The analysis of missed detections revealed two key limitations. First, the lack of intrarenal pressure data, which previous studies have shown to significantly increase postoperative sepsis risk, was unavailable due to the retrospective design [39]. Second, the absence of stone culture and composition data prevented the identification of high-risk stone types (struvite or staghorn stones), which are significantly associated with post-PCNL infections (OR ≈ 3–7), potentially leading to an underestimated infection risk in these patients [40, 41].

We developed a Streamlit-based online calculator that provides individualised PCNL fever risk predictions within 5 s, with SHAP visualisations showing feature contributions. This interpretable tool enables physicians to understand how several clinical indicators affect predictions, while improving perioperative decision-making efficiency.

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective design may have resulted in incomplete data collection, especially for variables such as stone composition and renal pelvic pressure, introducing potential bias. Residual confounding factors—including perioperative glucose control, surgeon experience, antibiotic use—may also have influenced the findings. Moreover, as a single-center observational study, the results have limited generalizability and cannot establish causality. Finally, the absence of external validation reduces confidence in the model. Larger multicenter prospective studies are warranted to confirm these results.

Conclusion

We developed an interpretable ML model that integrates the MAP score, NLR, LMR, and diabetes mellitus to accurately predict the risk of post-PCNL fever. Through a Streamlit-based online calculator, clinicians can obtain real-time, individualised risk assessments with SHAP visualisation results to support clinical decision-making. Given the retrospective single-center design and lack of external validation, the results should be considered exploratory and warrant confirmation in future studies.
