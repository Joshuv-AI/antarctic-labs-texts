Late presentation and diagnosis of concomitant ruptured sinus of valsalva and congenital gerbode defect in an elderly gentleman
Authors: M. R. Abdul Rashid, Y. Ahmad Ibrahim, J. Abdul Muiz, M. N. Mohd Arif, S. Ahmadi, M. K. Hamzah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Gerbode defect is a left ventricular-to-right atrial shunt. Sinus of Valsalva aneurysm is an abnormal outpouching at the base of the aorta caused by incomplete fusion between aortic media and annulus fibrosus. Both pathologies are rare entities, found as congenital heart disease mainly in pediatrics or young adults. A presentation of both pathologies in an elderly gentleman without evidence of acquired causes is extremely rare. We report a 58-year-old man who presented with acute congestive heart failure. Echocardiography showed features of ruptured Sinus of Valsalva aneurysm at the right coronary cusp and severe tricuspid regurgitation. With the intention of assessing the cardiac shunt in more detail, the echocardiography was repeated by an experienced pediatric cardiologist which yielded, to our surprise, a Gerbode defect on top of the ruptured Sinus of Valsalva aneurysm. The patient underwent repair of both defects with a single polytetrafluoroethylene patch through dual approach with good outcome. The diagnostic challenge, surgical technique and approach are discussed.

Introduction

Gerbode defect (GD) is a left ventricular (LV) to right atrial (RA) shunt which could be congenital or acquired. Congenital GD accounts for 1% of all congenital heart disease [1]. Sinus of Valsalva aneurysm (SOVA) is an abnormal outpouching at the base of the aorta caused by incomplete fusion between aortic media and annulus fibrosus. It can also be congenital or acquired and is noted to present in 0.14–3.5% of patients undergoing open heart surgery [1, 2]. Once it ruptures, it may cause fatal hemodynamic catastrophe needing prompt intervention. Both pathologies are rare entities, found as congenital heart disease mainly in pediatrics or young adults. A presence of both GD and ruptured SOVA (RSOVA) in an elderly patient without evidence of acquired causes is extremely rare. We report a diagnostic and surgical challenge in managing a patient with concomitant GD and RSOVA who presented with acute congestive heart failure, treated surgically with good outcome.

Case presentation

A 58-year-old man presented to a district hospital with New York Heart Association (NYHA) class II dyspnea and bilateral lower limb oedema. The cardiovascular examination showed a continous murmur best heard over the left lower sternal border radiating to the right lower sternal border. Diuretics were initiated. A transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) showed dilated cardiac chambers with moderate to severe tricuspid regurgitation (TR), with peak pressure gradient (PPG) of 55mmHg. There was a “windsock appearance” suggestive of SOVA rupture into right ventricle (RV) with continuous flow. He was the referred to our centre in which a repeat echocardiogram by an experienced practitioner yielded, an unexpected, GD with at least two flows into the RA, on top of the RSOVA of the right coronary cusp (RCC) measuring 3–5 mm with left to right shunt and a PPG 100mmHg (Figs. 1 and 2). A cardiac multi-slice computer tomography (MSCT) was done to study the coronary arteries, which showed normal coronaries.

We proceeded with the corrective surgery via a multiple approach (aortotomy and right atriotomy) under cardio-pulmonary bypass. Intra-operatively, there was a small defect noted at the right coronary sinus with aneurysmal tissue extending into the RA. Another opening was noted connecting the RA with the LV measuring 10 × 15 mm, which was buried under the tricuspid valve near the anteroseptal commissure. The anteroseptal commissure was under-developed with presence of redundant tissue. The defects were closed together using a single 0.4 mm polytetrafluoroethylene patch by continuous sutures (polypropylene 4 − 0) (Figs. 3 and 4). The aortic cross-clamp and the cardio-pulmonary bypass time were 136 and 174 min respectively.

Post-operatively patient developed stable fast atrial fibrillation which was chemically cardioverted with Amiodarone and Digoxin. A post-operative TTE was performed, which showed mild TR, normal RV size and systolic function, good LV systolic function, with no residual SOV and GD (Figs. 1 and 2).

Discussion

GD is a rare but well described type of intracardiac shunt [3, 4]. There were at least nine cases of congenital LV to RA communication reported since 1838 to 1957 before Gerbode et al. reported a series of successful surgeries on five patients with this anomaly in 1958, in which it was subsequently named as a Gerbode defect [4]. It can be congenital or more frequently acquired from either infective endocarditis (IE), trauma, cardiac surgery, or myocardial infarction (MI) [3]. The symptoms are of pulmonary over-circulation (dyspnea, fatigue and weakness) and are dependent on the size of the defect and shunting flow [1, 3]. There are 3 known subtypes of which are Indirect (Infravalvular), direct (supravalvular) or intermediate.

SOVA was first reported in 1840, but the first surgical repair was described in 1957. It is more commonly congenital, but can be acquired from either Infective Enodcarditis, syphilis, trauma, atherosclerosis, cystic medial necrosis, or Marfan’s syndrome. Symptoms arise due to either compression or rupture of the aneurysm [1]. Once it ruptures, it forms an aorto-cardiac fistula, most frequently into the RV, causing profound hemodynamic effects, manifesting as congestive cardiac failure [1, 2, 5]. These defects can also be subclassified based on it’s location of rupture. Type I ruptures into the right ventricle, just below the pulmonary valve; Type II ruptures into or just below the crista supraventricularis of the right ventricle; Type III ruptures into the right atrium or right ventricle near or at the tricuspid annulus; Type IV ruptures into the right atrium; and type V: Ruptures into the left atrium, left ventricle, or pulmonary artery.

Concomitant GD and RSOVA are extremely rare. A systemic review published in March 2015 revealed only four reported cases of concomitant GD and RSOVA, which were all acquired [3]. Meanwhile concomitant congenital GD and RSOVA, hitherto, had only been reported once as an abstract in a conference [6]. Our patient had neither a history suggestive of infective endocarditis, previous MI, surgery nor trauma. There was also no clinical feature of Marfan’s syndrome, nor signs of valvular vegetation in the echocardiography as well as intra-operatively. We postulate that the patient could possibly have dual congenital heart defects which were too trivial to cause symptoms at his younger age.

Two-dimensional echocardiography, either transthoracic or transesophageal, is highly sensitive to LV-RA shunts. The high flow velocity in color flow Doppler can reflect the gradient between the high-pressure LV and the low-pressure RA [4]. However, to differentiate between GD and RSOVA is challenging. The classic “windsock appearance” is seen in SOVA prior to rupture [1], but following rupture, left-to-right shunting is visualized across the defect, which is difficult to differentiate with other cause of left-to-right shunt.

Meticulous interpretation by imaging experts who are familiar with cardiac shunts is crucial to make an accurate diagnosis, avoiding unexpected findings during corrective surgery. GD produces left to right shunt during systole, while RSOVA produces diastolic shunting between the aorta and RA [4].

A detailed echocardiogram by an experienced operator is extremely important as a complete intracardiac assessment aid in pre-operative planning and avoids incomplete repair, especially in defects which doesn’t frequently present in this age group.

The treatment of chronic, asymptomatic or small GD is still debatable, however symptomatic ones clearly need surgical intervention, using either polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) or autologous pericardial patch closure. At the same time, if necessary, the tricuspid valve need to be reconstructed or even replaced [4]. Some authors advocate patch repair on the RA side to prevent postoperative heart block, while some advocate two-side patch repair. Nevertheless, good outcomes were reported from both groups [3].

Likewise, the treatment of asymptomatic SOVA is debatable. While some authors advocated surgical repair of all SOVAs, many authors reserve surgical repair only for aneurysms that rupture, compresses adjacent structures, or when the aneurysms are more than 50% of the average size of the other sinuses [1].

The goals of surgical repair of RSOVA are to close the ruptured aneurysm, remove the aneurysmal sac and repair any associated defects without causing heart block or valve dysfunction. This can be performed by primary or patch closure technique [1, 2] via a range surgical approaches namely trans-aortic, chamber only or dual approach [2].

Many reports found that primary closure caused recurrent rupture secondary due to suture line stress and worsening aortic regurgitation possibly due to deformation of aortic sinus [2], hence it is recommended only in small aneurysms. Therefore, due to the rarity of RSOVAs, recommendations for the best surgical approach and repair techniques are gained only from case series and systematic review, not clinical trials [1, 2].

Our case posed an interesting challenge as it presents in an adult with two rare congenital pathologies concomitantly. A transaortic-transatrial approach was opted to thoroughly inspect the deformities which involved aortic sinus as well as different heart chambers. This approach allowed us to securely repair the defect which resulted in a good surgical outcome.

Conclusion

When a cardiac deformity is encountered in an adult, it is important to exclude all possible acquired causes. If a congenital diagnosis is entertained, echocardiography by an experienced sonographer familiar with congenital heart deformities is crucial to determine the best treatment. Concomitant congenital GD and RSOVA are extremely rare, hence the surgical technique and approach for this case are contingent upon the anatomy of the defect and the experience of the attending surgeon.
