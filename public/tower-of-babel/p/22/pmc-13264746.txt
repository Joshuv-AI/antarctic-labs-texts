Systemic corticosteroid bridging followed by ultrasound-guided microwave ablation for recurrent male plasma cell mastitis: a case report
Authors: Hongxi Yao, Jieting Yin, Longquan Li, Wanqun Li, Aili Saiding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPlasma cell mastitis (PCM) is a chronic inflammatory breast disorder that primarily affects women. It is exceptionally rare in men and poses a diagnostic challenge due to its high recurrence rate and features that can mimic malignancy. Currently, there is no standardized treatment protocol for refractory PCM, particularly for recurrent cases in male patients. The aim of this case report is to present a unique instance of recurrent male PCM that was successfully managed with a stepwise approach: initial corticosteroid therapy followed by ultrasound-guided microwave ablation (MWA). Accordingly, this minimally invasive and tissue-preserving approach could be considered a treatment option alongside conventional surgical resection for managing such challenging recurrences.Case DescriptionA 30-year-old man presented with an ipsilateral recurrence of a retroareolar mass, accompanied by erythema and swelling, 18 months after initial surgical excision. Imaging and core needle biopsy confirmed recurrent PCM with suppurative inflammation. To avoid the trauma of repeat open surgery, the patient was treated with a step-up regimen: systemic corticosteroids (prednisone) were first administered to reduce the acute inflammatory burden, followed by ultrasound-guided MWA for definitive local control. At the 18-month follow-up, there was no clinical or sonographic evidence of recurrence, and the patient reported high cosmetic satisfaction and significantly improved quality of life compared with his initial surgery.ConclusionsThe main take-away lesson from this case is that a step-up approach—corticosteroid bridging followed by MWA—offers a promising, minimally invasive alternative to repeat surgery for recurrent male PCM. This strategy provides a significant clinical impact by balancing effective disease control with superior cosmetic outcomes. Further prospective studies are warranted to confirm its long-term implications.

Introduction

Plasma cell mastitis (PCM) is a rare chronic inflammatory breast disease that predominantly affects women, with only occasional cases documented in men (1,2). Despite its low incidence, PCM is notable for its tendency to recur and the significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges that it presents. Management of refractory disease can be especially difficult, as outcomes with conventional open surgery or empirical antibiotics are frequently suboptimal.

In the absence of a standardized treatment algorithm, care must be tailored to disease activity and patient priorities. Here, we report a unique case of recurrent male PCM managed with a novel step-up therapeutic approach: systemic corticosteroid therapy to rapidly attenuate inflammation, followed by targeted ultrasound-guided microwave ablation (MWA) to achieve anatomic clearance of residual foci. This sequence addresses the specific challenges of refractory PCM while preserving cosmesis and minimizing invasiveness. We present this article in accordance with the CARE reporting checklist (available at https://gs.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/gs-2026-1-0140/rc).

Case presentation

A 30-year-old man presented with a 3-day history of a left breast lump detected on self-examination. He noted a firm, pea-sized nodule without pain, erythema, swelling, warmth, nor nipple discharge; there were no systemic symptoms.

On examination, a firm, irregular mass with indistinct margins was palpable in the retroareolar region of the left breast, measuring approximately 1.5 cm × 1.0 cm. The overlying skin was unremarkable. No lymphadenopathy was detected in either axilla nor the supraclavicular fossae. The right breast was normal to palpation. Medical history was unremarkable, with no prior breast disease, trauma, nor significant comorbidity; there was no family history of breast cancer. He was a nonsmoker and reported no alcohol misuse.

Given the patient’s age, sex, and clinical features, the initial differential diagnosis included benign entities such as gynecomastia, duct ectasia/PCM, and (less commonly in males) fibroadenoma; however, the persistence of a discrete mass warranted further imaging to exclude malignancy.

Imaging assessment

Although mammography is the standard baseline modality for excluding malignancy, it was not performed in this case because the patient’s male sex and limited glandular tissue made adequate compression technically unfeasible. Breast ultrasound (Figure 1) initially showed a hypoechoic, irregular retroareolar mass with ill-defined margins measuring approximately 1.6 cm × 1.3 cm × 1.2 cm. Color Doppler demonstrated a significant increase in blood flow both peripherally and within the lesion, indicating perilesional hyperemia. On follow-up preoperative ultrasound, the mass had significantly enlarged to 2.4 cm × 1.0 cm × 2.2 cm, with increased echogenicity in the surrounding tissue. These findings suggest an inflammatory process, rather than a simple benign tumor.

MRI

Subsequent breast MRI (Figure 2) revealed mild enlargement of the left breast and an irregular retroareolar lesion (~2.7 cm × 2.1 cm) with relatively high signal on T1- and T2-weighted images, accompanied by heterogeneous enhancement on contrast-enhanced sequences. Diffusion-weighted imaging demonstrated central diffusion restriction, consistent with cellular inflammation in the tissue. Mild perilesional edema and subtle skin thickening further supported an inflammatory etiology. No pathologic lymphadenopathy was observed.

Axial MR images depict an irregular retroareolar lesion in the left breast with heterogeneous signal intensity and mild perilesional edema. No significant enlargement of lymph nodes was observed. MR, magnetic resonance.

Preliminary diagnosis and biopsy

In light of the imaging findings, an ultrasound-guided core needle biopsy (CNB) was performed to secure a histologic diagnosis. Histopathology (Figure 3) demonstrated a dense inflammatory infiltrate composed predominantly of plasma cells with a periductal distribution, alongside multinucleated giant cells and microabscess formation. Granulomatous inflammation was absent. These hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) morphological features supported the diagnosis of PCM and differentiated it from idiopathic granulomatous mastitis (IGM).

PCM is uncommon in men and often presents with malignant-mimicking clinical and imaging features, alongside a recognized propensity for recurrence. Given these considerations—and the absence of malignancy—we formulated an individualized, step-up treatment plan tailored to control inflammation while preserving cosmesis and minimizing invasiveness.

Initial treatment

After exclusion of surgical contraindications, the patient underwent local excision of the left retroareolar mass with abscess drainage under local anesthesia on January 6, 2023. Histopathology of the excised tissue confirmed PCM (Figure 4).

Histopathology of the excised left-breast nodule (H&E; magnification, 100×). (A,B) Sections show suppurative inflammation with abscess formation and granulation tissue proliferation, consistent with the postoperative diagnosis. No malignancy was identified. H&E, hematoxylin and eosin.

To control postoperative inflammation and reduce the risk of recurrence, an 8-week corticosteroid course was initiated, consisting of prednisone acetate 5 mg tablets. The dose started at 30 mg once daily and was tapered by 5 mg each week to 5 mg once daily, with weekly clinic reviews. The therapeutic intent was to suppress local inflammatory activity and limit fibroproliferative change. During treatment, local symptoms improved; the lesion’s size and induration decreased markedly, and the postoperative wound healed uneventfully.

Recurrence management and treatment

Approximately 18 months after surgery, the left breast lesion recurred. In this report, recurrence was defined as the reappearance of acute clinical inflammatory symptoms (erythema, swelling, and pain) in the ipsilateral breast, coupled with radiologic and histopathological evidence of PCM. The patient reported re-enlargement of the retroareolar mass accompanied by erythema, swelling, and warmth, in clear contrast to the initial postoperative recovery (Figure 5A). Imaging localized an irregular retroareolar lesion with perilesional edema and mild skin thickening. Breast ultrasound and MRI showed increased echogenicity/perilesional signal around the index lesion with interval enlargement, findings compatible with inflammatory relapse.

Given this profile, targeted aspiration was performed for microbiology. Aerobic, anaerobic, fungal, and acid-fast bacilli (AFB)/nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) cultures showed no growth at 48 hours. A repeat ultrasound-guided core biopsy demonstrated a dense inflammatory infiltrate composed predominantly of plasma cells and lymphocytes, consistent with the primary histopathological pattern of PCM. Based on the convergence of clinical, radiological, and pathological findings, the diagnosis was considered to be recurrent PCM.

To achieve definitive local control, a stepwise regimen combining medical and ablative therapy was undertaken.

Corticosteroid phase

The patient received two courses of systemic corticosteroids to suppress local inflammatory activity. Specifically, the dosing schedule for each course was identical to his initial postoperative regimen: an 8-week taper of oral prednisone, starting at 30 mg once daily and decreasing by 5 mg each week down to a maintenance dose of 5 mg. Following this medical bridging, clinical signs—erythema, swelling, warmth, and pain—improved markedly (Figure 5B).

Ultrasound-guided MWA

Building on the medical response, the patient underwent curative-intent MWA on May 21, 2024, under general anesthesia. At the time of the procedure, the target lesion presented on ultrasound as a mixed liquid-solid echogenic mass with a predominantly hypoechoic appearance and minimal peripheral vascularity, having slightly regressed in size following corticosteroid therapy. The procedure was performed using a 2,450 MHz MWA system (Key-2000, Nanjing Kangyou Medical Technology Co., Nanjing, China) equipped with a 16G cooled-shaft antenna. To protect the skin and nipple-areolar complex (NAC) from thermal injury, a hydrodissection barrier was created by injecting approximately 40–50 mL of isolation fluid (a mixture of saline and lidocaine) into the perilesional space. The ablation was executed with power outputs ranging from 25 to 35 W for a total duration of approximately 15 minutes. To minimize the risk of recurrence, the ablation zone was intentionally extended 2–3 mm beyond the visible sonographic margins of the lesion. Completion of the ablation was sonographically confirmed by the transformation of the target area into a slightly hyperechoic zone and the complete disappearance of internal blood flow signals.

Postoperative course

No peri-procedural complications occurred. Local erythema, swelling, and tenderness resolved progressively, and the incision healed uneventfully. On follow-up, the patient reported high cosmetic satisfaction with improved contour and texture of the left breast, and no evidence of recurrence was observed (Figure 5C).

Follow-up and outcomes

Over the 18-month surveillance period, recurrence-free survival (RFS) was confirmed both clinically and radiologically. Regular clinical evaluations (assessing physical signs and patient-reported symptoms) and breast ultrasound showed no evidence of local recurrence, and no new lesions were detected. The patient demonstrated adherence to the prescribed corticosteroid regimen. The step-up treatment was well-tolerated. There were no adverse or unanticipated events (Table 1).

Patient perspective

During a recent telephone follow-up, the patient shared his perspective on his treatment journey: “When the lump came back with redness and swelling, I was incredibly anxious about having to go through another open surgery and the resulting scars. However, this new treatment was much easier to endure. The MWA only left a tiny mark; the wound was significantly smaller than my first surgery. Furthermore, my recovery was much faster, and I experienced far less pain. I am extremely satisfied with the cosmetic result on my chest and deeply relieved to have my normal life back without any new flare-ups.”

Discussion

Key case features and differential diagnosis

Male PCM is uncommon and prone to recurrence. This case—relapsing after conventional surgery—has clear educational and clinical value. Although precise epidemiology is limited, the incidence of male PCM is reported to be far lower than that of breast cancer, with some series suggesting an order of magnitude difference (≈1/20) (3).

At initial presentation, the imaging profile showed malignant-mimicking features. Ultrasound demonstrated a hypoechoic, irregular retroareolar nodule (~1.6 cm × 1.3 cm × 1.2 cm) with ill-defined margins and punctate Doppler signals [color Doppler flow imaging (CDFI)]. MRI revealed an irregular retroareolar lesion with relatively high T1/T2 signal, heterogeneous contrast enhancement and a plateau-type (type II) time–intensity curve, together with restricted diffusion on DWI—findings that can be confused with breast carcinoma or granulomatous mastitis (4,5). Because PCM and IGM often present with overlapping clinical profiles, accurate histopathological differentiation is paramount. While IGM is classically characterized by lobulocentric non-caseating granulomas, PCM typically presents with a periductal, plasma cell-rich infiltrate without true granuloma formation, as observed in our case. Furthermore, it is essential to distinguish recurrent PCM from simple infectious abscesses and chronic inflammatory induration. Unlike common infectious abscesses, which usually present with localized fluctuance and distinct, larger fluid collections on ultrasound that yield specific pathogens, our case exhibited multi-focal microabscesses and sterile routine cultures. Conversely, chronic inflammatory induration typically lacks the acute signs of warmth and severe pain, as well as the active suppurative components and marked perilesional edema observed in this patient’s clinical and pathological profile.

Approximately 18 months after the initial operation and steroid consolidation, the patient developed ipsilateral recurrence with erythema and local warmth. Targeted aspiration cultures showed no growth at 48 hours, making a specific bacterial infection unlikely, and repeat biopsy again supported suppurative inflammation, favoring a diagnosis of recurrent PCM. This evidence-driven sequence—imaging triage → CNB confirmation → targeted microbiology → tailored therapy—helps individualize management while balancing disease control, cosmetic outcomes, and recurrence risk.

Treatment options and mechanisms

Given the recurrence after the initial operation, we adopted a step-up regimen: first, medically control the inflammation, then deliver minimally invasive definitive therapy. The etiology of PCM is increasingly recognized as an immune-mediated process. Emerging medical literature implicates profound immune dysregulation, including the activation of the IL-6/JAK2/STAT3 pathway (6), the role of the NLRP3 inflammasome (7), and injury-related deposition of the complement membrane attack complex (MAC) on the mammary duct epithelium (8). Because of this highly active, immune-driven microenvironment, primary surgical excision during an acute inflammatory phase may lead to suboptimal healing and recurrence.

To address this, systemic corticosteroids were utilized as a medical bridge to downregulate these pro-inflammatory cascades and stabilize the local tissue milieu. Once the acute inflammatory burden was attenuated, ultrasound-guided MWA was performed. The rationale for MWA in inflammatory breast disease differs from its application in oncology; rather than prioritizing strict safety margins, MWA utilizes electromagnetic fields to generate localized high temperatures (60–150 ℃), inducing predictable coagulative necrosis of inflammatory cells, granulation tissues, and micro-abscesses (9,10). This effectively devitalizes the pathological focus. Clinically, we opted for this approach because conventional repeat surgery often struggles to achieve complete clearance of diffuse inflammatory tissues. In contrast, the boundaries of PCM are frequently more distinct under real-time ultrasound, making ultrasound-guided MWA a highly feasible, targeted alternative. Mechanistically, while corticosteroids suppress the immunological pathways, MWA physically devitalizes the lesion through predictable coagulative necrosis and microvascular occlusion, creating a synergistic immunologic-anatomic intervention. Compared with open surgery or repeated drainage, this sequence provides anatomic “clearance” that may lower recurrence risk while offering smaller incisions, less scarring, and superior preservation of breast contour (11).

Insights and outlook

A limitation of this report lies in the restricted diagnostic workup. Microbiological cultures were not performed during the initial drainage, which represents a significant omission in distinguishing between a primary infectious process and non-infectious inflammation at the outset. Initially, special stains [e.g., periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) or acid-fast] were not performed to further differentiate PCM from IGM. Furthermore, during recurrence, the microbiological workup was limited to routine 48-hour cultures; extended incubation and molecular diagnostics [e.g., polymerase chain reaction (PCR)] were omitted. Future protocols should incorporate comprehensive histological (special stains) and microbiological (extended cultures/PCR) testing to reliably secure the diagnosis and exclude occult infections like Corynebacterium before definitive therapy. In inflammation-dominant presentations with malignant-mimicking imaging features, diagnosis should be secured through a standardized imaging-pathology evidence chain. Following a robust diagnosis, a step-up strategy—controlling inflammation first, then proceeding to minimally invasive intervention—can reduce overtreatment and empirical antimicrobial use while balancing effective disease control.

For patients with similar profiles (a history of recurrence and a strong preference for form preservation), ultrasound-guided MWA performed during a quiescent inflammatory phase, combined with systemic corticosteroid bridging, serves as a pragmatic candidate pathway. That said, an important limitation of this report is that current evidence derives largely from single-center experiences and small case series with limited follow-up. Future work should include multicenter registries or prospective cohorts that specify primary endpoints such as RFS and patient-reported outcomes (e.g., BREAST-Q scores) to further define the true clinical benefit of this step-up approach.

Conclusions

The primary take-away lesson from this case report is that a step-up regimen—consisting of systemic corticosteroid bridging followed by ultrasound-guided MWA—provides a highly effective, tissue-sparing alternative to repeat open surgery for recurrent male PCM. This minimally invasive approach successfully balances durable disease control with superior cosmetic outcomes and patient satisfaction, warranting further prospective validation in clinical cohorts.
