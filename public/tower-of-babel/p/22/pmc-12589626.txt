Experimental research on the fire resistance of columns in container-style steel modular buildings
Authors: Jintao Duan, Jianyong Liu, Jinghua Wei, HuaBing Wu, Shijie Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the fire resistance of columns in container-style steel modular buildings through fire tests. Three individual columns and one combined column were tested to evaluate the fire resistance performance of various protective measures: fire-resistant coatings, calcium silicate boards, and rock wool sandwich panels. The combined column protected by 75 mm rock wool sandwich panels achieved a fire resistance limit of 102.1 min. The study concludes that rock wool sandwich panels are a feasible, economical fire protection solution for such columns. The required thickness of rock wool for different fire resistance limits was calculated, providing valuable design insights for container-style steel modular buildings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-22642-7.

Introduction

Modular Integrated Construction (MIC) is an innovative construction method that involves prefabricating “modular units” in a factory and delivering them to the construction site for reliable connection to form a complete building. These modular units are typically divided by rooms or specific three-dimensional building spaces, and they include prefabricated parts such as the main structure, floors, ceilings, walls, and interior decorations. With a prefabrication ratio of up to 95%, MIC is a highly integrated construction method that demonstrates significant potential for enhancing construction efficiency, delivering whole life cycle cost savings, and promoting sustainable development. Moreover, due to its incredibly short construction period, it also offers excellent emergency response capabilities. In the initial stages of the MIC, it was primarily utilized for temporary and simplistic housing, including construction site housing, worker dormitories, migrant worker housing, and temporary port shops, etcs. However, with the advancement of construction industrialization and prefabricated steel structures, modular steel construction has extended its reach to low-rise villas, apartments, exhibition halls, budget-friendly hotels, schools, office buildings, fire stations, and other types of structures. During the epidemic of COVID-19, numerous centralized medical observation sites were rapidly constructed by packaged container-style modular houses, such as the Guangzhou International Health Station, Shenzhen International Health Station, and Xiamen International Health Station, which played an important role in combating the epidemic of COVID-19. Although the Health Station project is not a medical treatment facility, to avoid wasting public resources and to comply with the National Health Commission’s requirement for “integrating epidemic and civilian efforts”, many Health Stations have been converted to operate as regular hotels, dormitories, and talent apartments after the epidemic of COVID-19.

The primary design basis for steel modular buildings currently adheres to the “Technical Standard for Light Steel Modular Building” (JGJ/T 466–2019)1. However, JGJ/T 466–2019 merely states that the fire protection design of light steel modular buildings must comply with the existing national standard, i.e., “Code for Fire Protection Design of Buildings” (GB 50,016–2014)2. However, specific fire resistance requirements for container-style steel modular buildings are not provided. Due to steel’s high thermal conductivity, its strength and stiffness rapidly diminish as temperature increases, with the critical temperature for losing equilibrium stability under full load being approximately 500 °C. The fire resistance limit of unprotected steel structures is only around 15 min, significantly below the requirements of GB 50,016–2014. Currently, research on the safety of container-style steel modular buildings mainly focuses on structural load-bearing capacity3 and seismic performance4. There is relatively less research on the fire safety of container-style steel modular buildings, and even less on the fire resistance of its columns, which are the main load-bearing components of container-style steel modular buildings. Jiadi Liu5 investigated various fire protection methods for combined columns (four square steel pipes) used in modular dormitories, showing that gypsum boards effectively protect steel columns under fire conditions. However, rock wool, despite its good insulation properties, quickly loses its insulation properties when directly exposed to fire. Peng et al.6 conducted experimental research on combined columns (four square steel pipes) of container-style steel modular buildings under different fire protection conditions using a fire-resistant test furnace. The fire performance of gypsum board, fiber-reinforced calcium silicate board, and rock wool was verified. In addition, Huang et al.7 conducted fire tests on square steel pipe columns with different wall thicknesses under different fire protection methods, different load ratios, axial and eccentric loading conditions. This study found that thicker columns (20 mm) can achieve a fire resistance limit of up to 3.0 h, while thinner columns (6 mm) can achieve 2.0 h under the same protection.

To sum up, existing research on the fire resistance of columns in steel modular buildings primarily focuses on square steel pipes used in permanent structures, with wall thicknesses exceeding 6 mm, and in some cases, even reaching 20 mm. By contrast, container-style steel modular buildings, particularly recent projects such as International Health Stations, commonly use L-shaped thin-walled columns with a wall thickness of only 3.5 mm. These columns are distinguished by their light weight and high strength, enabling a reduction in material consumption while maintaining structural integrity. Such characteristics render them highly suitable for the requirements of modular integrated construction. Nevertheless, such L-shaped thin-walled columns generally require appropriate fire protection measures to ensure structural safety while achieving lightweight design, thereby offering an efficient and competitive solution for modular buildings. Notably, research on the fire resistance of such L-shaped thin-walled columns has not been reported. Therefore, this study investigates the effects of different fire protection strategies on their fire performance, including fire-resistant coatings, calcium silicate boards, and rock wool sandwich panels. The findings provide valuable theoretical insights and engineering references for the practical application of L-shaped thin-walled columns in modular integrated construction.

Experimental setup

The test columns

The test components used in this experiment were L-shaped thin-walled columns that are commonly employed in container-style steel modular buildings. The columns had a wall thickness of 3.5 mm and were made of Q235B steel. Their height was 2535 mm, and their cross-section is depicted in Fig. 1. Fixed supports were installed at both ends of the column, which were consistent with the actual structure. The column was secured to the support by high-strength bolts, with the fixed supports welded onto a steel plate. The structural details of the single column are shown in Fig. 2. In addition to single columns, a combined column between adjacent four containers was also selected for testing, as illustrated in Fig. 3. The installation of columns in actual container-style steel modular buildings is shown in Fig. 4. Axial loading was applied to the columns, with loads equivalent to those experienced by columns in their respective positions within actual structures. The single columns were subject to a load of 36.6 kN, and the load ratio is 0.4. The combined column was subject to a load of 131.8 kN, and the load ratio is 0.36. For this experiment, four test columns were designed, and three different fire protection measures were developed based on their feasibility in actual engineering. Among them, S1 served as the control group, without any fire protection measures applied. Experiment procedure

The column fire tests were conducted in a vertical component fire test furnace, measuring 2.5 m (L) × 2.5 m (W) × 3.8 m (H). Columns were installed vertically, with the bottom steel plate secured within the furnace and the top plate connected to the jack head. The heated section of each column was set at 2.0 m, as depicted in Fig. 5. Fire tests followed the “Fire-resistance Test—Elements of Building Construction—Part 1: General Requirements” (GB/T 9978.1–2008)8 guidelines. Prior to the fire test, mechanical loads were incrementally applied to the column, increasing by 20% every 10 min. After reaching the full load, the columns were kept under these loads for 30 min to stabilize before the fire test began. During testing, the furnace temperatures adhered to the ISO-834 standard fire. The ISO-834 standard fire provides a standardized thermal load curve, enabling the evaluation of the fire resistance performance of different materials and structural forms under uniform experimental conditions. This standardized approach allows us to exclude the influence of environmental factors, focusing on the fire behavior of the structure and materials themselves. However, it should be noted that this method does not fully replicate the complex heating patterns that may occur in real-world fires, particularly in cases where the fire conditions are highly uneven or complex.Fig. 5The columns installed in the fire test furnace.

The columns installed in the fire test furnace.

According to GB/T 9978.1–2008, the fire resistance limit for axially loaded components is determined when the axial deformation rate exceeds 3 h/1000 (mm/min) or when the axial deformation surpasses h/100 (mm), where h is the column’s initial fire exposure height. The fire test concluded once the column reached its fire resistance limit.

Experimental results and discussion

Column failure characteristics

During the fire resistance test of 4 columns, the furnace temperature curves are shown in Fig. 6, while the overall condition of the columns after fire testing is presented in Fig. 7. All 4 columns exhibited local buckling with a uniform mode due to their thin wall thickness (3.5 mm), which readily led to buckling during compression loading. The buckling was concentrated in the mid-to-upper regions of the columns, where higher temperatures were observed, resulting from uneven heating inside the furnace. Similar findings were previously reported by Huang et al.7, where square steel columns with dimensions of 200 mm × 200 mm and wall thickness less than or equal to 10 mm were found susceptible to localized buckling under high-temperature exposure during fire tests; the investigation of this fire tests confirmed these earlier conclusions.Fig. 6The furnace temperature curves.Fig. 7The columns after fire testing.

The furnace temperature curves.

The columns after fire testing.

In the case of column S2, which was coated with fire-resistant material, localized damage to the coating was observed due to the buckling of the column. However, no complete or extensive exfoliation of the coating occurred, the fire resistive coating could basically maintain its fire integrity under fire. For column S3, which was protected with calcium silicate boards affixed on the outside, following the application of fire-resistant coating on both sides of the column, partial exfoliation of both the coating and the boards was noted after the fire test. Due to the low toughness of the calcium silicate board, it could not deform well with the flexure of the column. Therefore, fiber-reinforced calcium silicate boards should be selected and properly fixed for fire protection of columns. Based on the actual installation conditions of the container-style steel modular buildings, the S4 utilized rock wool sandwich panels to provide fire protection for columns. It can be seen that the rock wool sandwich panel deformed with the buckling of the column and prevented direct exposure of the column from fire. This is due to its good toughness, which makes it resistant to cracking or exfoliating under fire. When not directly exposed to fire, rock wool has good insulation properties, and its surface color steel plate did not melt under high temperatures. Therefore, the rock wool sandwich panel effectively resisted high temperatures under fires. Ultimately, the S4 achieved a fire resistance limit of 102.1 min.

The deformations of the columns

The fire test of the column was carried out under constant mechanical loads with continuous heating. During the test, the column underwent thermal expansion and deformation until reaching its ultimate bearing capacity. As the temperature increased, both the strength and stiffness of the steel reduced rapidly leading to a decrease in its ultimate bearing capacity. When the latter approached that of mechanical loads gradually, the thermal expansion effect weakened resulting in a smoother axial deformation trend. Eventually, as the temperature continued to increase, in conjunction with mechanical loads, compression dominated causing an increasingly downward slope on its axial deformation curve. The maximum axial elongation of the four columns was observed to be within the range of 13–15 mm, as shown in Fig. 8. After reaching the maximum axial elongation, the columns underwent a rapid transition from elongation to compression. The columns had almost reached their fire resistance limit at this stage, and once the axial deformation curve entered the descending stage, the columns experienced significant deformation or deformation rate within a short span. Ultimately, the columns lost their bearing capacity upon reaching their fire resistance limit. Analysis of the axial deformation curves of the four columns revealed that the deformation characteristics were almost identical. Nonetheless, differences in the timing of significant axial deformation were attributed to differences in their respective fire protection measures.Fig. 8The axial displacement–time curves of the columns.

The fire resistance limit of the columns

The fire resistance limits of the columns with and without fire protection measures used in the fire test are demonstrated in Table 3. The reduction of the bearing capacity of the column under fire was attributed to the decrement of the strength and stiffness of the steel at high temperatures. As the bearing capacity reduced, the column gradually approached its fire resistance limit. By reducing the heating rate of the column under fire, the fire resistance limit of the column could be enhanced. Therefore, the thermal insulation performance of the fire protection measures utilized on the column determined its fire resistance limit. The fire protection measures adopted in container-style steel modular buildings mainly consider the construction site conditions, construction period, and costs. Compared to other fire protection measures, the rock wool sandwich panel possesses superior fire resistance performance. Although rock wool is prone to ablation when exposed to fire, it offers excellent insulation when not in direct contact with flames. Besides, the high melting point of the color steel plate on the surface of the rock wool sandwich panel offers excellent protection to rock wool. Therefore, the rock wool sandwich panel can effectively resist high temperatures under fire. In container-style steel modular buildings, rock wool sandwich panels are commonly used as partition walls due to their low cost and high feasibility. Conclusions

This paper conducted 4 fire tests on columns of container-style steel modular buildings, including three single columns and a combined column, to verify the fire resistance performance of the fire-resistant coating, calcium silicate board, and rock wool sandwich panel. The required protection layer thickness of rock wool sandwich panels for the columns under different fire resistance limits was analyzed by theoretical calculations. Based on the phenomena and data of the fire test and the theoretical calculation, the following conclusions can be drawn: Under the ISO-834 standard fire, the fire resistance limit of an unprotected column was 12.3 min, and with a regular fire resistive coating applied to one side, its fire resistance limit was 18.7 min. The failure mode was localized buckling at the upper-middle of the column. Under the protection of regular calcium silicate boards and fire-resistant coatings, the fire resistance limit of the columns was 34.9 min. Due to their low toughness, regular calcium silicate boards and fire resistive coating were susceptible to exfoliation from the column due to deformation. For components susceptible to deformation under fire, the fiber-reinforced calcium silicate board and fire-resistant coating should be used for fire protection. The combined column, protected with 75 mm thick rock wool sandwich panels, achieved a fire resistance limit of 102.1 min. The required thickness of the rock wool panels, calculated based on theoretical analysis (considering the detrimental effects of creep and modified critical temperature), showed only 4% difference compared to the experimental results, indicating good consistency. Due to its excellent insulation properties and toughness, rock wool, combined with color steel plates that prevent direct fire exposure, offers an effective and economical fire protection solution for columns in container-style steel modular buildings. These findings demonstrate that using rock wool sandwich panels is a practical and cost-efficient method to enhance fire resistance in such structures.
