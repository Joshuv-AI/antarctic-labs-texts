Hypertension among incarcerated individuals in the Ashanti Region of Ghana: examining prevalence and correlates based on the Social Cognitive Theory
Authors: Prince Kwabena Achoo, Joana Apenkwa, Ernest Osei, Dennis Bardoe, Denis Dekugmen Yar, David Ngmenbelle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHypertension remains a major cause of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. In Ghana, evidence indicates a rising prevalence across adult populations, yet little is known about the situation within prisons, where structural, behavioural, and environmental factors may increase vulnerability. This study, therefore, examined the prevalence of hypertension and its correlates among incarcerated individuals in six prison facilities within the Ashanti Region of Ghana, using the Social Cognitive Theory (SCT).MethodsA facility-based cross-sectional study was conducted among 382 incarcerated individuals. Data were collected using anthropometric measurements and closed-ended questionnaires. Guided by SCT, descriptive and inferential statistics, including chi-square tests, modified Poisson regression with robust standard error, and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), were performed with STATA 17 and IBM SPSS AMOS 29.ResultsOverall, 27.2% (95% CI: 22.7–31.7) of inmates were hypertensive. This was associated with behavioural factors (such as medication non-adherence, irregular blood pressure monitoring, smoking, excessive alcohol intake, unmanaged stress, inadequate sleep, and failure to follow health advice), personal factors (including low confidence in managing hypertension, limited ability to make lifestyle changes, poor coping of symptom, inadequate knowledge of risks associated with hypertension, and undervaluing a healthy diet), and environmental factors, (such as discomfort discussing hypertension, lack of support for exercise, absence of medication reminders, and inability to seek help). The structural model demonstrated robust fitness (AGFI = 0.911; CFI = 0.943; GFI = 0.915; IFI = 0.921; NFI = 0.901; RMSEA = 0.062), with strong correlations between personal and environmental factors (r = 0.987), behavioural and environmental factors (r = 0.593), and behavioural and personal factors (r = 0.576). Personal factors had the strongest direct effect on hypertension (β = 0.62), followed by behavioural factors (β = 0.33).ConclusionHypertension among incarcerated individuals was influenced by interacting behavioural, personal, and environmental correlates within the SCT framework. Addressing this burden requires integrated prison-based interventions that strengthen healthy behaviour, routine blood pressure monitoring, institutional support systems for physical activity, stress management, and access to care. Implementing coordinated health promotion and hypertension management programs within correctional facilities may help reduce the burden of hypertension among this vulnerable population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-26943-y.

Background

Hypertension remains one of the most significant contributors to the global burden of non-communicable diseases (NCDs), driving substantial morbidity, mortality, and economic loss [1]. As a major risk factor for cardiovascular disease, stroke, and renal failure, it accounts for a considerable share of premature deaths worldwide [1]. According to the World Health Organisation (WHO), approximately 1.4 billion adults aged 30–79 years were living with hypertension in 2024, with nearly two-thirds residing in low- and middle-income countries [2]. Limited access to screening, inadequate continuity of care, and shortages of essential medicines continue to undermine effective control in these settings [3]. In addition, an estimated 600 million (44%) of these adults with hypertension are unaware that they have the condition [2]. Sub-Saharan Africa has experienced a marked rise in hypertension prevalence, with regional estimates in West Africa reaching up to 34% [4]. In Ghana, national survey data indicate that 21.7% of adults have elevated blood pressure, with prevalence increasing substantially with age [5].

Several biological, behavioural, environmental, and structural factors have been implicated in these epidemiological patterns across the various sub-countries. From a biological standpoint, chronic stress, dysregulated neuroendocrine pathways, and comorbid metabolic conditions may promote persistent elevation of blood pressure [6]. Behaviourally, tobacco use, alcohol intake, high-sodium diets, and sedentary lifestyles remain important modifiable contributors [2, 7]. Regarding environmental factors, air pollution has been noted as a significant contributor to hypertension [2]. At the system level, gaps in screening, weak primary care infrastructure, poor continuity of chronic care, limited health promotion for lifestyle risk factors, medication stockouts, and weak health-seeking behaviour significantly impede control efforts [8, 9].

In response, WHO has articulated a coordinated public-health intervention to reduce the burden of hypertension at the population and health-system levels. These include multisectoral strategies targeting major behavioural risks, such as salt reduction, healthier food environments, tobacco and alcohol control, and promotion of physical activity [10]. At the service level, the HEARTS technical package provides a framework for strengthening cardiovascular disease prevention and hypertension management in primary healthcare through standardised treatment protocols, access to essential medicines and technologies, and improved monitoring systems [11]. Complementing these efforts, WHO guidelines emphasise timely treatment initiation, use of single-pill combinations where feasible, and sustained access to affordable long-acting medicines [12, 13].

Despite policy efforts aimed at improving chronic disease prevention and control, Ghana faces persistent barriers in ensuring equitable access to screening, timely diagnosis, and effective long-term management [14]. These challenges are often more pronounced among disadvantaged populations whose living conditions, socioeconomic constraints, or restricted access to healthcare limit their ability to engage in effective management of hypertension [14, 15]. People who are incarcerated represent one such vulnerable group. Evidence from correctional health literature suggests that incarcerated individuals often have a higher prevalence of chronic conditions compared to the general population [16, 17]. Correctional environments expose individuals to unique risk factors that may elevate the likelihood of developing or intensifying hypertension [18]. Overcrowding, chronic psychosocial stress, restricted autonomy, poor diet quality, reduced physical activity, and exposure to violence or uncertainty may contribute to elevated blood pressure levels [18, 19]. In addition, incarceration frequently disrupts continuity of medical care and medication adherence, which is essential for maintaining optimal blood pressure control [20].

Beyond the growing recognition of prisons as critical sites for public health intervention, evidence on the burden, determinants, and management of hypertension among incarcerated populations in Ghana remains limited. Existing studies [21, 22] have largely focused on community-based populations and outpatient settings, offering little insight into institutionalised and socially marginalised groups. This study aimed to assess the prevalence of hypertension among incarcerated individuals in six prison facilities within the Ashanti Region of Ghana. It also sought to identify key behavioural, personal, and environmental factors associated with hypertension, grounded in the Social Cognitive Theory (SCT). The study further aimed to explore the relationships between these factors and their collective influence on hypertension in this setting. The findings aim to inform the design of context-specific interventions to strengthen hypertension prevention and control within Ghana’s prison health system. In line with these objectives, the following hypotheses were tested:

Hypothesis 1

Null Hypothesis (H0): There is no significant association between behavioural, personal, and environmental factors (such as medication adherence, blood pressure monitoring, smoking, alcohol intake, self-confidence in managing hypertension, knowledge of hypertension risks, and support for exercise) and the prevalence of hypertension among incarcerated individuals.

Alternative Hypothesis (HA): There is a significant association between behavioural, personal, and environmental factors (such as medication adherence, blood pressure monitoring, smoking, alcohol intake, self-confidence in managing hypertension, knowledge of hypertension risks, and support for exercise) and the prevalence of hypertension among incarcerated individuals.

Hypothesis 2

Null Hypothesis (H0): The combined influence of behavioural, personal, and environmental factors does not significantly predict hypertension outcomes in incarcerated individuals, as measured through structural equation modelling (SEM).

Alternative Hypothesis (HA): The combined influence of behavioural, personal, and environmental factors significantly predicts hypertension outcomes in incarcerated individuals, as measured through structural equation modelling (SEM).

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This study employed a facility-based cross-sectional design conducted among incarcerated individuals in selected prisons within the Ashanti Region. It lasted over fifteen weeks, from March to July 2025 and included all incarcerated adults aged ≥ 18 years who were eligible and available during data collection.

Theoretical foundation of the study

This study was grounded in Social Cognitive Theory (SCT) (Fig. 1). This behavioural framework explains health actions as the result of dynamic interactions between personal, behavioural, and environmental determinants [23]. SCT emphasises the principle of reciprocal determinism, whereby these three domains continually influence one another to shape health-related decisions and practices [23, 24]. Within this framework, personal factors, including knowledge, beliefs, expectations, and self-efficacy, affect how individuals perceive their health risks and their capacity to manage chronic conditions such as hypertension [23, 25]. These personal determinants shape an individual’s capacity to initiate and sustain protective practices. Behavioural factors, such as adherence to medication, diet, physical activity, and self-monitoring of blood pressure, represent the overt actions that directly influence health outcomes [23, 25]. These behaviours serve as the direct mechanisms through which individuals manage their condition and reduce complications. In addition, environmental factors encompass the social and structural influences that support or constrain healthy behaviour, including social support, access to health services, institutional regulations, and the broader physical and social context of the prison setting [23, 25]. These factors influence both the opportunity and motivation to engage in healthy behaviours, especially in institutional settings such as prisons, where autonomy and resources are constrained. Applying SCT provided a structured lens to examine how incarcerated individuals understand hypertension, how they engage in its management, and how the prison environment shapes their ability to adopt and sustain recommended health behaviours.

Study setting and context

The study was conducted in the Ashanti Region of Ghana (Fig. 2). Geographically, the region lies within the forest ecological zone and spans diverse urban, peri-urban, and rural communities [26, 27]. It is administratively divided into several metropolitan, municipal, and district areas and serves as a major commercial and transportation hub in Ghana [26]. According to national census estimates, the region hosts a substantial share of the national population, with densely populated urban centres such as Kumasi alongside dispersed rural settlements [28]. Health infrastructure in the region includes 170 Government, 71 mission, 281 Private and 8 quasi-government [26]. The study focused on six correctional facilities within the region. These include Kumasi Central Prison (Kumasi Metropolis), Kumasi Female Prison (Kumasi Metropolis), Manhyia Local Prison (Kumasi Metropolis), Obuasi Local Prison (Obuasi Municipal), Amanfrom Camp Prison (Atwima Nwabiagya North Municipal), and Ejura Camp Prison (Ejura Sekyere-Dumase). These facilities vary in size and operational capacity but collectively house individuals in settings where access to healthcare, nutrition, physical activity, and routine clinical monitoring may be constrained [29, 30]. Overcrowding, limited health personnel, and resource shortages, which are some common challenges reported in many correctional environments [29, 30], may also influence the burden and management of chronic conditions such as hypertension. Therefore, conducting this study across multiple correctional sites in the Ashanti Region allowed for a broader understanding of how contextual and environmental factors shape hypertension-related outcomes among incarcerated individuals.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The study included incarcerated individuals aged ≥ 18 years, residing in selected facilities for at least one month, able to provide consent, and present during data collection. In contrast, individuals with cognitive impairment, those critically ill, or those who declined participation were excluded from the study.

Sampling procedure

This study employed a multi-stage sampling technique. Initially, a simple random sampling technique (balloting) was employed to select six out of the seven correctional facilities in the Ashanti region. Afterwards, a quota sampling method was used to allocate inmates based on the number of registered inmates serving jail terms or being on remand for more than three months in each of the six selected correctional facilities, as shown in Table 1. After accounting for a 0.8% non-response rate, the quotas were adjusted to maintain proportional representation by distributing non-respondents across the remaining units based on the initial sampling proportions. Within each correctional facility, simple random sampling was used to select inmates for participation. Each inmate within a correctional facility was assigned a unique identification number, and balloting was employed to ensure an equal chance of selection.

Consenting and enrolment of participants

Inmates were approached at the waiting area of the correctional facility to screen for eligibility. Those who met the inclusion criteria were informed of the study’s objectives, their right to withdraw at any time, and the measures to protect their confidentiality. Afterwards, those who consented signed or thumbprinted a consent form to affirm their engagement.

Confidentiality and privacy during data collection

Data collection was conducted in private spaces within the facility to prevent unintended disclosure of sensitive information. Interactions were conducted individually, and no third parties were present unless explicitly requested by the inmate. Responses obtained were recorded without personal identifiers; instead, unique study codes were assigned to each inmate. Completed questionnaires were stored in a password-protected file to ensure participant privacy, maintain confidentiality, and build trust throughout the study process.

Variables of interest

Outcome variable

The outcome variable was hypertension. This was measured using blood pressure readings (systolic ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic ≥ 90 mmHg). Results from the verified clinical assessment were dichotomously coded as (“1” - hypertensive and “2” - non-hypertensive).

Main explanatory variables

The main explanatory variables were derived from SCT, grouped into behavioural factors, individual factors, and environmental factors. These constructs were rated using a 4-point Likert scale to measure the degree of agreement or frequency of behaviours. Responses were later recoded or retained based on the type of analysis.

Data collection instrument, validity, and reliability

Data were collected using anthropometric measurements and a structured interviewer-administered questionnaire (Supplementary File 1). The questionnaire was developed based on previous literature [4, 7, 23–25, 33] and national hypertension screening guidelines. The instrument contained 42 items, stratified into four (4) sections. Section A captured sociodemographic characteristics, Section B assessed hypertension management behaviours, Section C examined personal factors related to self-efficacy and health knowledge, and Section D assessed environmental factors, including social support and external influences. Hand-labels were assigned to each item to clarify the measured constructs. A thorough validation process was performed to ensure the questionnaire’s reliability and validity. The first draft of the questionnaire underwent expert review by two public health professionals to assess its content validity. The instrument was pre-tested and refined based on feedback. Finally, the internal consistency of the questionnaire was assessed using McDonald’s omega (ω) for each SCT domain. McDonald’s omega (ω) values of 0.971 for behavioural factors, 0.973 for personal factors, and 0.965 for environmental factors. These indicate acceptable internal consistency across all constructs.

Measurement of blood pressure

Blood pressure was measured using the Omron HEM-705CP automated oscillometric blood pressure monitor (Omron Healthcare, Kyoto, Japan). This pressure monitor has been validated for accuracy and reliability. The measurement process was standardised to ensure consistency and minimise errors. This was according to the World Health Organisation (WHO) guidelines for the management of hypertension [2]. Before each measurement, consented inmates were asked to rest for at least 5 min in a quiet room, seated with their feet flat on the floor and their arms supported at heart level. They were instructed to avoid any physical activity, smoking, or consumption of caffeinated beverages 30 min before measurement. The appropriate cuff size for each inmate was selected based on arm circumference and placed on the upper arm approximately 2–3 cm above the elbow. Each inmate had their blood pressure measured twice, with a 5-minute rest interval between the two readings. The mean of the two measurements was recorded as the inmate’s blood pressure value. If the difference between the two measurements exceeded 5 mmHg, a third measurement was taken, and the average of the three readings was used. To ensure reliable data, this procedure was repeated over the course of three days, with a one-day interval between measurements on each day. This was done across all six selected correctional facilities. The mean of the measurements across the three days was recorded as the final blood pressure value for each inmate. Inmates with confirmed hypertension were referred for follow-up care.

Data management and statistical analyses

Data were double-entered, cleaned, and analysed with STATA 17 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, USA) and IBM SPSS AMOS 29 (IBM, USA). Preliminary data checks were performed to identify and address missing values, inconsistencies, and outliers. All questionnaires were fully completed, and after cleaning, the final dataset contained no missing values. Therefore, no imputation or case deletion procedures were necessary during the analysis. All categorical variables were appropriately coded and recoded as needed to ensure compatibility with the planned statistical analyses. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations, were computed to summarise the characteristics of the inmates. Pearson’s chi-square tests were used to assess associations between hypertension and the sociodemographic characteristics of the consented inmates. Modified Poisson regression with robust standard errors was performed to estimate prevalence ratios (PRs) using the command “glm outcome i.’var’, family(poisson) link(log) vce(robust) eform”. For convenience in modified Poisson regression model, responses from the 4-point Likert-scale items were recoded into dichotomous categories, where “Strongly Agree” and “Agree” were recoded as “Yes,” while “Disagree” and “Strongly Disagree” were recoded as “No.” Composite binary indices were then generated for each SCT domain and entered as explanatory variables in the regression analyses. Bivariate analysis (Model I) was first performed to assess crude associations between hypertension and explanatory variables. Variables with p ≤ 0.25 were retained in the multivariable model (Model II) to adjust for potential confounders. Adjusted prevalence ratios (aPRs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and p-values were reported to indicate the magnitude and statistical significance of the associations. In addition, Structural Equation Modelling (SEM) was used to test the conceptual relationships among the three domains of the SCT and their combined influence on hypertension. Responses on the original 4-point Likert scale were retained to preserve the ordinal and continuous structure of the data for structural equation modelling (SEM). A confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was first performed to validate the measurement model and confirm the loading of observed indicators onto their respective latent variables.

Assessment of model fitness and assumptions

The fit of the multivariable modified Poisson regression model was evaluated using several indices. Deviance and Pearson chi-square (χ2) were examined to assess model adequacy. In addition, information criteria, including the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), were reported to allow comparison of model performance. The overall significance of the model was confirmed with a p-value < 0.001. These measures collectively indicated that the model provided a reasonable fit to the data. Finally, the SEM model fit was assessed using indices such as Adjusted Goodness of Fit Index (AGFI), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Goodness of Fit Index (GFI), Incremental Fit Index (IFI), Normed Fit Index (NFI), and Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA). To assess potential multicollinearity among the items within each domain, variance inflation factors (VIFs) were calculated for each predictor in the model. This was performed using the vif, uncentered command in STATA. In addition, the Durbin-Watson statistic was applied to assess the assumption of independence among residuals. A result of 1.684 indicated no evidence of autocorrelation, as it falls within the acceptable range (1.5–2.5).

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of inmates

A total of 382 incarcerated individuals participated in the study, with a mean age of 32.3 ± 3.73 years. The majority were male (98.7%), married (80.9%), and had received formal education (54.2%). Before incarceration, most inmates were employed (60.2%) and resided in urban areas (67.8%). About two-thirds (66.5%) had been incarcerated for more than five years. 43.7% of inmates had a normal weight, whereas 30.1% were overweight and 19.4% were obese. Media exposure varied, with 40.8% reporting daily access to radio or television (Table 2).

Prevalence of hypertension and distribution across various sociodemographic factors

A total of 27.2% of inmates were hypertensive (95% CI: 22.7–31.7) (Table 2). Hypertension prevalence was higher among female inmates (40.0%) compared to males (27.1%), though the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.518). Prevalence varied by age, with rates ranging from 18.3% in those aged 20–30 years to 29.5% in those aged 31–40 years. Married inmates had a higher prevalence (29.1%) than unmarried inmates (19.2%). Those without formal education had a significantly higher prevalence (35.4%) compared to those with formal education (20.3%). Inmates who were previously employed had a higher burden (31.3%) than the unemployed (21.1%). While no significant difference was found by place of residence (p = 0.905), those incarcerated for more than five years had a higher prevalence (33.5%) compared to those incarcerated for less than five years (14.8%, p < 0.001). Prevalence was higher in those with obesity (44.6%) and overweight (40.9%) compared to those with normal weight (9.0%). Media exposure showed no significant association with hypertension (p = 0.455), but variation in prevalence was observed across correctional facilities, ranging from 5.9% at Manhyia Local Prison to 66.7% at Amanfrom Camp Prison (p < 0.001). The Akaike Information Criterion (AIC = 0.86) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC = − 2106.69) further demonstrated a parsimonious model with strong explanatory power, while the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC = 0.97) confirmed the model’s excellent discriminative ability. In the adjusted model (Model II), as shown in Table 3, individuals who were not willing to take medication if recommended by a healthcare provider had more than 25 times the prevalence of hypertension compared to those who were willing to adhere to medication (aPR = 25.3, [95% CI: 26.9–38.0], p < 0.001). Those who did not monitor their blood pressure regularly had a 1.5 times higher prevalence of hypertension compared to those who did monitor their blood pressure (aPR = 1.5, [95% CI: 1.2–2.0], p = 0.002). Individuals who did not avoid smoking had a significantly higher prevalence of hypertension (aPR = 1.3, [95% CI: 1.1–2.1], p < 0.001) compared to those who avoided smoking. Regarding alcohol consumption, individuals who did not control alcohol intake had a 1.9 times higher prevalence of hypertension compared to those who controlled their alcohol intake (aPR = 1.9, [95% CI: 1.2–3.1], p = 0.012). Similarly, those who did not manage stress had a 2.3 times higher prevalence of hypertension (aPR = 2.3, [95% CI: 1.7–3.4], p < 0.001). Individuals who did not get adequate sleep had a 3.2 times higher prevalence of hypertension compared to those who maintained good sleep quality (aPR = 3.2, [95% CI: 2.1–5.8], p = 0.014). Finally, those who did not follow health advice had a 2.4 times higher prevalence of hypertension compared to those who followed their healthcare provider’s advice (aPR = 2.4, [95% CI: 1.7–3.6], p = 0.004).

Discussion

Prevalence of hypertension among incarcerated inmates

This study quantified the proportion of incarcerated individuals living with elevated blood pressure within six correctional facilities in the Ashanti Region. It was found that 27.2% (95% CI: 22.7–31.7) of inmates were hypertensive. Reports from other studies among prison inmates have documented hypertension prevalence ranging approximately from 40% in Yaoundé Central Prison, Cameroon [34], 31.3% in Benin [35], 36.9% in Mozambique [36], and 54.5% in Northern Ghana [37]. Similarly, some national survey data from high-income countries have also reported higher levels of hypertension within correctional facilities [38], despite the availability of more structured clinical monitoring and established chronic disease management programs. This suggests that the proportion identified in this current study falls within the upper range of what has been observed in similar settings. Alignment with these proportions highlights that, despite the global push for the prevention of non-communicable disease, hypertension remains a persistent and significant chronic disease burden among incarcerated populations. The comparatively higher proportion observed in this study may therefore reflect gaps in preventive and routine care provision within Ghanaian prisons [37]. Several contextual elements may explain the observed burden. For instance, limited opportunities for physical exercise, monotonous diets often high in sodium, and high psychosocial stress associated with incarceration are commonly reported contributors to hypertension in correctional facilities [20, 34, 37, 38]. Moreover, restricted access to regular medical screening and delayed diagnosis may further exacerbate the risk of hypertension [36]. These systemic limitations may result in hypertension going unnoticed until complications arise. Taken together, the proportion identified in this study highlights persistent concerns regarding non-communicable disease management within correctional environments in low- and middle-income contexts. The findings point to the need for strengthened policies that ensure routine blood pressure monitoring, timely treatment initiation, and improved lifestyle-support interventions within prisons.

Correlates of hypertension based on the three domains of Social Cognitive Theory

Behavioural correlates of hypertension as conceptualised under the SCT model

This study examined the behavioural factors associated with hypertension among incarcerated inmates through the lens of the Social Cognitive Theory (SCT). Among the behavioural factors assessed, unwillingness to take prescribed anti-hypertensive medication emerged as a strong correlate. Thus, inmates who were unwilling to adhere to medication demonstrated a higher prevalence of hypertension. This association aligns with widely reported evidence that adherence to anti-hypertensive therapy plays a crucial role in maintaining blood pressure, particularly in settings where routine clinical follow-up and treatment monitoring may be limited [39, 40]. The observed relationship in this study may echo challenges related to medication access, limited understanding of treatment benefits, mistrust in prison healthcare systems, or perceived side effects of medication [40].

It was also revealed that inmates who were unwilling to monitor their blood pressure regularly had a higher prevalence of hypertension. Notably, regular monitoring allows early detection of elevated blood pressure and timely intervention [41]. Lack of self-monitoring may result from limited awareness of the risk of hypertension, lack of confidence in using measurement devices, or absence of institutional support for routine monitoring [42]. Hence, encouraging self-monitoring within prisons could serve as an important strategy for early identification and management of hypertension. In addition, behavioural risk practices such as smoking and inadequate control of alcohol intake were also associated with hypertension. These findings are consistent with widely acknowledged behavioural pathways linking substance use to elevated blood pressure [43]. Excessive smoking and alcohol intake elevate blood pressure through multiple physiological pathways, including increased sympathetic nervous system activity [44]. In the prison, there may exist limited formal interventions targeting alcohol use and smoking, and inmates with a history of heavy drinking or smoking before incarceration may continue maladaptive coping patterns [45]. Programs focused on reduction in alcohol intake and smoking, such as behavioural counselling, may help reduce hypertension prevalence in this population.

Furthermore, inadequate stress management and poor sleep quality appeared to be important correlates, with inmates who did not manage stress effectively and those who reported inadequate sleep having a higher prevalence of hypertension. Most correctional facilities are frequently characterised by overcrowding, rigid routines, noise, and psychological distress [19]. Chronic stress and sleep disturbances are likely to trigger hormonal dysregulation, sympathetic overactivity, and impaired vascular function, which are noted factors that increase blood pressure and cardiovascular risk [46]. Hence, teaching improving sleep hygiene, optimising sleeping environments, providing mental health support, and promoting coping strategies may support cardiovascular health among inmates.

Finally, failure to follow health advice was associated with an increased prevalence of hypertension. Following medical guidance is critical for controlling chronic conditions [47]. Non-adherence may stem from low health literacy, limited understanding of disease consequences, or scepticism toward institutional care [48]. Structured health education programs and motivational counselling could enhance adherence and improve hypertension outcomes in correctional facilities.

Personal correlates of hypertension as conceptualised under the SCT model

This study also revealed a series of personal correlates of hypertension among inmates. Inmates who lacked confidence in their ability to manage their blood pressure had a 5.4 times higher prevalence of hypertension. Self-efficacy, or confidence in one’s ability to perform health-related tasks, is a key determinant of chronic disease management [49]. In correctional settings, low confidence may limit proactive behaviours such as regular blood pressure monitoring, adherence to prescribed medication, and lifestyle adjustments. The constrained environment, with limited access to health professionals or peer support, may intensify this challenge [36, 37]. Enhancing inmates’ confidence through structured education, guided practice in blood pressure management, and motivational support could improve hypertension outcomes. Secondly, inmates who were not confident in making lifestyle changes had higher prevalence of hypertension. Adopting behaviours such as reducing salt intake, engaging in physical activity, and moderating substance use requires not only knowledge but also belief in one’s ability to implement change [50]. The low confidence observed in this study may reflect a perceived lack of control over the prison environment or prior unsuccessful attempts at behaviour modification. In line with this, interventions that include goal-setting, incremental behavioural targets, and mentorship programs may empower inmates to adopt healthier habits.

Furthermore, inmates who felt less confident in coping with hypertension symptoms had a 3.3 times higher prevalence of hypertension. Consistent with previous studies, managing the symptoms of hypertension requires both knowledge and practical skills [51, 52]. Low confidence may result in delayed care-seeking, poor adherence to self-management practices, or misinterpretation of symptoms [53]. Correctional facilities could implement symptom recognition workshops and a peer support programme to strengthen inmates’ coping abilities, enabling timely and effective responses to blood pressure changes. Also, inmates unaware of the risks associated with hypertension had higher prevalence of hypertension. Awareness of the potential complications of untreated hypertension is critical for motivating preventive and therapeutic behaviours [54]. Incarcerated populations may face gaps in health literacy due to limited education or a lack of structured health promotion activities [55]. Health education programmes tailored to the prison context, emphasising the consequences of uncontrolled hypertension and the benefits of treatment adherence, could substantially reduce risk.

Finally, inmates who did not perceive a healthy diet as important for managing or preventing hypertension had a higher prevalence of hypertension. Nutrition is a cornerstone of hypertension prevention and management. Perceptions undervaluing dietary quality may result in poor food choices and resistance to dietary interventions [56]. In prisons, structured access to nutritious meals, combined with education on the impact of diet on blood pressure, could enhance both knowledge and practice, reducing hypertension prevalence.

Environmental correlates of hypertension as conceptualised under the SCT model

As part of environmental factors, inmates who felt uncomfortable discussing their hypertension management had a higher prevalence of hypertension. This association aligns with observations that reluctance to communicate health concerns may limit opportunities to receive clarification, guidance, or emotional support [57, 58]. In correctional facilities, issues such as stigma, fear of being perceived as weak, mistrust of prison health staff, or concerns about confidentiality may discourage inmates from openly discussing their condition [59]. This reluctance may reduce engagement with available services and hinder timely adjustments in treatment plans, ultimately affecting blood pressure control. Improving communication environments within prisons, particularly by fostering respectful and confidential patient–provider interactions, may support more effective hypertension management. In addition, inmates who did not receive support to exercise regularly had a higher prevalenceof hypertension. Limited institutional support for physical activity, such as restricted access to recreational spaces, inadequate supervision, or lack of structured exercise programs, may contribute to sedentary behaviour within correctional facilities [38]. Physical inactivity has been widely described as a modifiable risk factor for hypertension, and the closed nature of prisons may intensify this challenge by restricting movement [60]. Ensuring consistent support for exercise, including organised physical activity and peer encouragement, may help improve cardiovascular health among inmates.

Furthermore, inmates who did not receive reminders to take their medication had a higher prevalence of hypertension. The structured routines of prisons may create dependence on external prompts for medication adherence, and the absence of reminder systems may result in missed doses [37, 61]. Missed medication schedules may lead to poor blood pressure control and complications. Incorporating reliable medication reminder mechanisms, such as supervised dispensing or scheduled announcements, may help promote adherence and improve outcomes. Finally, inmates who felt unable to seek help when struggling with hypertension management were more likely to be hypertensive. Barriers such as fear of punitive responses, uncertainty about care pathways, or limited trust in health personnel may discourage help-seeking [48]. This may lead to unmanaged symptoms, delayed treatment, and worsening health. Strengthening supportive structures that empower inmates to seek assistance without fear of negative consequences may improve hypertension management and overall well-being.

Structural relationships among the three domains of Social Cognitive Theory and their influence on hypertension

This study also conceptualised the influence of hypertension within three interrelated domains of the SCT. The confirmatory factor analysis confirmed the structural validity and reliability of these domains, with consistently high factor loadings and excellent internal consistency values across behavioural, personal, and environmental constructs. These findings indicate that the observed indicators were empirically coherent and theoretically aligned, reinforcing the multidimensional nature of hypertension determinants within correctional settings. The strong correlations identified between personal and environmental factors, as well as between behavioural and environmental factors, highlight the reality that hypertension outcomes are rarely shaped by a single domain. Rather, they emerge from a dynamic interaction between inmates’ beliefs and confidence, their health-related behaviours, and the surrounding institutional environment.

For instance, even when inmates possess the willingness or behavioural tendency to manage their hypertension, environmental constraints such as limited support, inadequate communication, or lack of assistance from correctional staff may still undermine effective management. This pattern aligns with broader findings in prison health research, where structural limitations, psychosocial stressors, restricted autonomy, and inadequate access to chronic disease support have been linked to poor health outcomes [62–64]. These findings emphasise the need for strategies that not only promote self-management but also strengthen environmental support systems within correctional facilities. Such an approach could contribute to improving hypertension control and reducing preventable complications among incarcerated populations.

Moreover, the structural path analysis revealed that personal factors had the strongest direct effect on hypertension, followed by behavioural factors. This observation highlights the dual importance of internal beliefs and behavioural engagement in shaping hypertension outcomes [65]. Personal factors such as confidence in symptom management and perceived importance of lifestyle modification directly influence inmates’ ability to act on recommended health practices. At the same time, behaviours such as medication adherence and regular blood pressure monitoring reinforce hypertension control [39, 40]. Together, these domains suggest that hypertension among inmates is not simply a matter of individual choice but reflects the interaction between personal capability and behavioural execution.

Interestingly, environmental factors did not show a statistically significant direct structural path to hypertension, despite demonstrating notable associations in the logistic regression. This suggests that environmental influences may exert their impact more indirectly by reinforcing or amplifying personal and behavioural barriers rather than serving as independent drivers of hypertension outcomes [66]. For instance, limited social or institutional support may weaken inmates’ confidence in managing their condition (a personal factor) or reduce adherence to recommended practices (a behavioural factor), thereby contributing indirectly [67]. This finding highlights an important conceptual implication of SCT: environmental support may be necessary but not sufficient to influence hypertension outcomes without corresponding personal readiness and behavioural engagement. Practically, this suggests that interventions within correctional settings should not address environmental factors in isolation. Instead, they may need to integrate environmental improvements with initiatives that strengthen personal beliefs and promote health-supportive behaviours. Such integrated approaches may be more effective in enhancing hypertension management and reducing complications among inmates by addressing interconnected barriers across all three SCT domains.

Implications for policy and practice

This study highlights an urgent need for comprehensive, multilevel strategies to address the substantial burden of hypertension among incarcerated individuals in Ghana. The results suggest that personal and behavioural factors exert the strongest influence on hypertension status, while environmental conditions within correctional facilities interact with these factors to reinforce poor health outcomes. This pattern indicates that improving individual self-management alone may be insufficient without corresponding institutional support. In light of this, it is recommended that the Ghana Prisons Service and the Ghana Health Service prioritise integrating routine hypertension screening, monitoring, and management into existing prison healthcare systems. Embedding blood pressure checks and medication reviews into regular clinic schedules could reduce missed opportunities for early detection and treatment. In addition, ensuring consistent access to essential antihypertensive medications and follow-up care may help limit preventable complications among inmates. Strengthening coordination between correctional health units and district health facilities could also support continuity of care.

At the same time, addressing personal-level barriers remains essential. Individuals with limited knowledge of hypertension, poor risk perception, and low confidence in managing their condition emerged as particularly vulnerable. Also, health education initiatives tailored to varying literacy levels, delivered through structured sessions and peer-support mechanisms within prisons, could improve understanding of hypertension risks and the importance of adherence. Providing repeated, clear messages about lifestyle modification, medication use, and symptom monitoring may empower inmates to take a more active role in their health.

Moreover, behavioural factors such as medication non-adherence, insufficient physical activity, and unmanaged stress also require targeted interventions. Therefore, introducing supervised exercise opportunities, structured medication administration systems, and stress management activities within daily routines may reinforce health-promoting behaviours. Incorporating counselling or brief behavioural interventions into clinical encounters could further support adherence. Finally, environmental influences, including limited access to healthcare personnel, inconsistent medical support, and restricted opportunities for healthy behaviours, must be addressed through institutional reforms. Beyond these, improving communication channels between inmates and health staff, expanding access to qualified healthcare providers, and creating supportive environments for health-seeking behaviour could enhance overall control of hypertension. As such, partnerships with external health organisations may also help alleviate resource constraints. Collectively, these recommendations recognise that while strengthening institutional healthcare delivery and routine hypertension management is an urgent priority, sustainable improvements will require simultaneous efforts to build personal capacity and enable supportive behavioural and environmental conditions within correctional settings.

Study’s strengths and limitations

This study has several notable strengths that distinguish it from much of the existing literature on hypertension in correctional settings. Unlike many prior studies [22, 34, 37, 44, 52, 54, 66] that focused primarily on isolated behavioural or clinical risk factors, this study applied SCT as a comprehensive analytical framework, integrating personal, behavioural, and environmental determinants within a single structural model. Methodologically, the use of confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and structural equation modelling (SEM) strengthened the analytical rigour by testing the validity, reliability, and structural relationships among latent constructs. These advanced techniques extend beyond traditional regression approaches by accounting for measurement error and revealing conceptual linkages across domains, thereby providing a stronger evaluation of the theoretical underpinnings of SCT in a correctional health context. The excellent model fit indices and high factor loadings further support the robustness of the measurement and structural models.

Despite these strengths, several limitations must be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design restricts causal inference, limiting the ability to establish temporal relationships between determinants and hypertension status. In addition, reliance on self-reported information for behavioural and personal constructs may have introduced recall bias and social desirability bias, particularly regarding medication adherence, physical activity, and stress management. Although the questionnaire distinguished clearly between behavioural practices and institutional constraints, these self-report limitations cannot be fully ruled out. Analytically, the examination of SCT domains separately before structural modelling may have led to potential overestimation of associations, as mutual adjustment across all categories was not achieved in preliminary analyses. Although SEM addressed inter-domain relationships, future studies employing longitudinal or integrated multivariate approaches may better clarify the strongest independent pathways influencing hypertension. A further limitation relates to possible ceiling effects in some measurement indicators, particularly within the environmental domain, where restricted variability in institutional conditions may have limited sensitivity to detect differences. Refinement of measurement tools to capture a broader range of environmental influences is recommended.

Finally, the study did not incorporate institutional audits or provider perspectives, which could have offered deeper insight into systemic barriers such as staffing capacity, availability of medications, and continuity of care within correctional facilities. Including these perspectives in future research may provide a more comprehensive understanding of structural challenges shaping hypertension management in prisons.

Conclusion

This study revealed a substantial prevalence of hypertension among incarcerated inmates in the Ashanti Region of Ghana. Beyond the headline prevalence, the findings demonstrated that the risk of hypertension is shaped by a constellation of determinants across the three domains of the Social Cognitive Theory (SCT). Personal factors, including limited knowledge of hypertension, low confidence in managing the condition, and poor perceived importance of lifestyle modification, emerged as the strongest direct predictors. Behavioural factors, such as medication non-adherence, irregular blood pressure monitoring, unmanaged stress, smoking, alcohol intake, and inadequate sleep, were also significantly associated with increased hypertension risk. In addition, environmental factors, including limited support, poor communication with healthcare staff, and restricted opportunities for healthy behaviours, contributed indirectly by reinforcing personal and behavioural barriers. Importantly, the study highlighted a strong interdependence among personal, behavioural, and environmental domains. This network of relationships indicates that hypertension outcomes are not determined solely by individual characteristics or isolated behaviours, but rather by their dynamic interplay within the correctional setting. For instance, even inmates motivated to adhere to treatment may remain hypertensive if institutional support is insufficient or if environmental constraints limit access to care and self-management opportunities. Therefore, interventions to reduce hypertension among incarcerated populations cannot rely on single-dimensional approaches. Effective strategies should integrate efforts to empower inmates with knowledge and self-management skills, promote adherence to healthy behaviours, and strengthen supportive institutional environments simultaneously. By addressing personal, behavioural, and environmental factors in a coordinated manner, correctional health services in Ghana could substantially improve hypertension management and reduce preventable morbidity and complications among inmates.
