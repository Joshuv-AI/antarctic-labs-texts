Exposure‐Response Associations Between Air Pollution and Laboratory‐Confirmed Influenza Cases in Anhui Province, China: A Two‐Stage Modeling Study
Authors: Harry Asena Musonye, Yi‐Sheng He, Lei Gong, Sai Hou, Junling Yu, Wei Chen, Peng Wang, Jun He, Hai‐Feng Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInfluenza remains a global public health and socio‐economic threat, and air pollution is believed to increase its risk. However, relatively few studies have focused specifically on laboratory‐confirmed influenza (LCI), which limits the precision and reliability of existing evidence on this association. We sought to explore the relationship between air pollution and LCI cases in Anhui province, China. We employed a two‐stage time series analysis to evaluate the associations between PM2.5, PM10, CO, SO2, O3, NO2 and daily LCI cases between January 2015 and March 2023. In the first stage, we applied distributed lag nonlinear model to characterize the lagged effects of each air pollutant in individual cities, followed by a meta‐analysis to pool city‐specific estimates in the second‐stage. We performed sub‐group analysis by age and gender, and explored modification effect of population density, median pollution levels, longitude, and latitude, using a meta‐regression. A total of 43, 872 LCI cases were reported in Anhui over study period. There was a positive associations between influenza and a 10 μg/m3 increase in PM2.5: RR = 1.0045 (95%CI: 1.0035–1.0054), SO2: RR = 1.0035 (95%CI: 1.0001–1.0069), NO2: RR = 1.0122 (95%CI: 1.0881–1.0162), PM10: RR = 1.0025 (95%CI: 1.0017–1.0033) and CO: RR = 1.2641 (95%CI: 1.1294–1.4150) in a single‐day lag model. In the cumulative‐day lag model, a 10 μg/m3 increase in PM2.5; RR = 1.0043 (95%CI: 1.0018–1.0068), NO2; RR = 1.0076 (95%CI: 1.0006–1.0145), PM10; RR = 1.0024 (95%CI: 1.0001–1.0046), and CO; RR = 1.3026 (95%CI: 0.8139–2.0849) positively associated with LCI cases. The associations were more pronounced in males and ages 5–24 years. Population density modified the associations. Our findings suggest the need to integrate air quality management in intervention strategies for influenza.Keywords: air pollution, influenza, time series analysis, relative risk, meta‐analysis

Introduction

Influenza, a contagious respiratory illness, remains a global public health concern due to its considerable morbidity and mortality. Annual seasonal influenza epidemics results in up to 650,000 deaths globally, with the high‐risk groups such as the elderly, the immunocompromised, chronically ill patients, children under five, and pregnant women bearing a heavy disease burden (Fell et al., 2017; Iuliano et al., 2018; Macias et al., 2021). It is estimated that influenza affects about 1 billion people each year, including 3–5 million severe cases (Chen et al., 2023). In China, the number of influenza reports increased substantially from 2018 to 2019, with the incidence rate rising from 55.09 per 100,000 in 2018 to 253.36 per 100,000 in 2019 (Zhu et al., 2022). The largest influenza‐related economic burden, amounting to 10.51 billion CNY, was recorded in East China, where Anhui province is located (Gong et al., 2021). The seasonal trend of influenza in China was disrupted after the outbreak of COVID‐19 epidemic, recording a yearly positivity rate of <1.0% (Chossière et al., 2021). However, this was followed by a resurgence of winter peak in the 2021–2022 and 2022–2023 seasons (Zeng et al., 2023). This trend coincides with the latest reports warning of a likely flu pandemic. Historically, influenza pandemics have occurred, with records documenting at least three before the 1918–1919 pandemic and in 1957–1958, 1968–1969, and 2009–1910 (Saunders‐Hastings & Krewski, 2016). Consequently, it is vital to urgently identify potential risk factors that may exacerbate influenza risks among different populations in China.

Research evidence reveals that exposure to environmental pollutants may significantly increase the likelihood of respiratory illnesses, including influenza (Clay et al., 2018; Domingo & Rovira, 2020; Huang et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2019b; Meng et al., 2021; Musonye et al., 2024; Somayaji et al., 2017; Xu et al., 2013; Zeng et al., 2020). Various explanations have been proposed to clarify the relationships. Air pollution exposure can impair immune function, increase susceptibility to infections, and exacerbate respiratory illness severity. For instance, exposure to particulate matter (PM) might obstruct airways and prompt production of free radicals, which strain the respiratory tract, decrease immunity to infections and exacerbate disease outcomes (Bourdrel et al., 2021; Li et al., 2019; Urrutia‐Pereira et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2020). In the blood, PM can trigger thrombogenic and pro‐inflammatory effects by producing metal induced‐oxidative stress and chemical compounds (Ciencewicki & Jaspers, 2007; Li et al., 2008; Mehta et al., 2013; Signorelli et al., 2019) that can worsen influenza outcomes. Exposure to sulfur dioxide (SO2), ozone (O3), carbon monoxide (CO), and nitrogen dioxide (NO2) can damage the lungs, cause oxidative stress and lead to endothelial dysfunction (Kowalska et al., 2020; Lai et al., 2021; Li et al., 2007; Pfeffer et al., 2019; To et al., 2020; Varon et al., 1999; Zhang et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2020). Despite the existing regulations that limit air pollution levels, exposure to pollutants continues to be associated with numerous adverse health effects, making it important to understand how common ambient air contaminants may worsen influenza risk.

While several epidemiological studies have recognized air pollution as a prospective precipitating factor for exacerbating influenza risks (Gao et al., 2025; J. Yang et al., 2023a; Yu et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2022), other studies have reported contrary results. For example, a study carried out in Brisbane, Australia related an increase in pediatric influenza cases with rising levels of O3 and PM10 (Xu et al., 2013). In another study, high concentrations of O3 and SO2 were linked to an increase in emergency room visits for influenza and pneumonia in São Paulo, Brazil (Martins et al., 2002). Some multicity studies in China have attributed increased influenza risks incidences to ambient air pollution exposure (Chen et al., 2017; Yu et al., 2023). Contrarily (Liu et al., 2019b; Su et al., 2019a), independently found that O3 and PM10 were both poorly linked with the probability of influenza‐like illness (ILI). The Instrumental Variable model, used to estimate the causal relationship, indicated that air pollution has small and statistically insignificant effects on influenza healthcare visits in Japan (He et al., 2025). Similarly, a study in the United States reported that environmental exposures including air pollutants did not significantly impact on the rate of influenza‐associated hospitalization (Somayaji et al., 2020). These discrepancies in epidemiological studies may stem from variations in study design, air pollution measurement methodologies, and data collection approaches. Furthermore, regional differences in population density, healthcare accessibility, socioeconomic status, age distribution, exposure histories, individual behaviors, immune responses, healthcare systems, climate, and public health strategies could all contribute to the observed discrepancies (Sarkodie & Owusu, 2021; Villeneuve & Goldberg, 2020).

Noteworthy, while previous studies (He et al., 2022; S. Yang et al., 2023) explored influenza patterns in Anhui Province, they primarily focused on influenza‐like illness (ILI) rather than laboratory‐confirmed influenza (LCI) cases. The current study extends this work by conducting a comprehensive multi‐city time‐series analysis of LCI cases over an extended period (2015–2023), capturing long‐term epidemiological trends with greater specificity. Unlike ILI, which can be caused by multiple pathogens, LCI data provide a more precise assessment of influenza burden, reducing diagnostic uncertainty and strengthening the validity of air pollution associations. Also, whether the specific exposure‐response associations for air pollution and LCI cases are modified by regional and population characteristics is uncertain. In view of all these, we utilized a two‐stage time series approach to explore the exposure‐response relationships linking air pollution and LCI cases in Anhui Province, China. The outcomes of this study carry important implications for public health policies and may form a basis upon which strategies aimed at reducing the burden of influenza in affected populations are developed.

Materials and Methods

Study Area

This study was conducted in the 16 prefecture‐level cities of Anhui province China (114°54′E to 119°37′E and 29°41′N to 34°38′N) (Figure 1). The province covers 140,100 square kilometers and has a population of 61.27 million people as of 2023. The substantial distance between the northern and southern regions, bisected by both the Yangtze and the Huai rivers, brings about variations in geographical features and climate across the area. The province experiences a monsoon climate characterized with medium rainfall, mild temperature, and four seasons: winter, spring, summer and autumn.

A map of China showing Anhui province and the study locations.

Data Collection and Processing

Data on daily LCI cases in 16 prefecture‐level cities of Anhui province was obtained from the “China Influenza Surveillance Information System” from January 2015 to March 2023. The data were originally collected from the laboratories of 16 municipal Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and 24 sentinel hospitals. These laboratories and hospitals handle a significant number of patients. For this study, every patient presenting with ILI at those 16 CDCs and 24 sentinel hospitals underwent testing for influenza viruses. Thus, the daily number of LCI cases in our study represents actual confirmed cases from a fully tested ILI population at these specific surveillance sites. Influenza is classified as a Class C notifiable disease by the “National Health Commission of China (NHCC).” The commission directs that once diagnosis is complete, all the observed cases must be reported to the CDC immediately. According to the “Diagnostic Criteria for Influenza (WS285‐ 2008)” published by the NHCC, a LCI incident is defined as an individual exhibiting influenza‐like symptoms (i.e., sore throat, cough or fever) and established to be infected with seasonal influenza A or B infection through either one or more of the subsequent laboratory tests: viral culture, reverse‐transcriptase polymerase chain reaction, real‐time reverse‐transcriptase polymerase chain reaction, antigen detection of influenza A or B virus from respiratory epithelial cells, sero‐conversion or a ≥4‐fold rise in precise antibodies to seasonal influenza A or B virus between acute and convalescent serum samples.

Daily data on air pollutants concentrations (SO2, NO2, PM2.5, PM10, CO, and O3) from January 2015 to March 2023 were retrieved from the China National Environmental Monitoring Centre. The daily measurement was based on the 24‐hr averages of SO2, NO2, PM2.5, PM10, CO, and O3 besides maximum value of 8‐hr moving averages of O3, consistent with the “Technical Regulation on Ambient Air Quality Index” (HJ633‐2012) guidelines set by the Ministry of Ecology and Environment of China (China SJSEQ, 2022). For the small number of missing air pollution data, the mean value of nearby points was used to fill in the gaps. If there were no influenza cases on a particular day, then the number of observed cases for that day was recorded as zero. The daily air pollution data set was matched with influenza cases based on the monitoring date. Excel software was used to compile the data, form a database and generate a time series file. The population data was obtained from the National Population Census of China.

Statistical Analyses

Descriptive examination of air pollutants was conducted and the distribution pattern of daily LCI cases in the 16 prefecture‐level cities summarized. Spatiotemporal distribution of LCI cases among different populations was determined. The Spearman rank correlation analysis plot between air pollutants and influenza was generated. Distributed Lag Non‐linear Models (DLNM) (Gasparrini, 2011; Gasparrini et al., 2010) was employed to assess the association between air pollution and LCI cases, based on the two‐stage time series model approach (Z. Yang et al., 2021; J. Yang et al., 2023), to enhance accuracy and statistical power (Acosta & Irizarry, 2022).

First Stage Analysis

In the initial phase of the model, we estimated city‐level relationship between air pollutants and LCI cases by fitting a quasi‐Poisson generalized linear model (GLM), accounting for over‐dispersion (Var(Y t) = φ·E (Y t)) and autocorrelation (via a smooth function of time) in city‐specific time series data. Within the DLNM structure, the φ parameter ensures that both the non‐linear exposure–response relationship and lag–response effects are estimated under appropriate variance assumptions. In the model, the time trend was smoothed using ns with 7 df for each year to account for the unmeasured long‐term trends and seasonality of influenza cases. This choice provided sufficient flexibility to capture periodic fluctuations without over‐fitting, aligning with standard practices in environmental epidemiology studies, where 6–8 df per year is commonly recommended. Additionally, the confounding effects of wind speed, temperature, and relative humidity were adjusted, and smoothed with ns (3 df) (Tian et al., 2018). This ensured adequate flexibility to capture non‐linear associations, such as pollutants thresholds influencing influenza risk, without introducing unnecessary complexity. Each air pollutant was modeled using the crossbasis () function from the dlnm R package (Gasparrini, 2011) with two components: (a) the exposure–response dimension and (b) the lag–response dimension. For the exposure–response dimension, a natural cubic spline (ns) with 3 df was specified (argvar = list (fun = “ns”, df = 3)) to allow sufficient flexibility for capturing potential non‐linear exposure–response relationships while minimizing overfitting. For the lag–response dimension, a ns with 3 df was also used (arglag = list (fun = “ns”, df = 3)) to model potential non‐linear and delayed effects over a 14‐day lag period. This modeling framework is appropriate given the non‐linear and time‐lagged effects of environmental exposures on respiratory outcomes (Chai et al., 2020; Yu et al., 2022). Knots for the exposure dimension were evenly distributed across the range of pollutant concentrations, while lag knots were logarithmically spaced up to a maximum of 14 days, allowing for greater flexibility in modeling rapid changes at shorter lags and coarser spacing at longer lags. To address collinearity, variables with high correlation (Spearman's correlation coefficient ≥0.7) were not included in the same model.

Literature indicate that effects of airborne emissions on influenza may be experienced for more than a week (Xu et al., 2013). So, to effectively analyze the lag structure, both single‐day lag (stretching from the current day [lag 0] up to the 14th day prior [lag 14]) models and cumulative‐day lag (lag 0–1 and lag 0–14) models were used to estimate the exposure‐response association. Here, lag 0 is the exposure on the index time, while lag 1–14 correspond to the fourteen prior days (Meng et al., 2021). Lag days are used to reflect the delay between exposure and the onset or reporting of infection. A maximum lag of 14 days is appropriate to capture the delayed effects of air pollution on influenza cases. Considering that developmental and behavioral differences at various ages and sex may influence the association, we conducted sub‐group analysis, at single‐day lag, to assess whether effects of air pollution varied by age and sex. The city‐specific estimated effect sizes were expressed as relative risk (RR) plus their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) in daily influenza cases per 10 μg/m3 increment of air pollutants (Higgins et al., 2019).

Second Stage Analysis

In the second‐phase of the model, exposure‐response relationships between the air pollutants and LCI cases for the city‐level, analyzed in the first‐stage, were incorporated into a random‐effect meta‐analysis model to obtain an overall effect size for the province. The lag day with the most acute and significant effect estimate (highest RR) was selected as the representative effect size for each city and pollutant (Richardson et al., 2011, Su Wei et al., 2019), which aligns with the exposure‐response nature of our study. Precisely identifying the critical exposure window through acute lag allows for the determination of the most susceptible period for implementing preventive measures. The random effects meta‐analysis was weighed using restricted maximum likelihood method (REML) (Partlett & Riley, 2017). REML accounts for both within‐study and between‐study variability, ensuring more accurate weighting of studies in the presence of heterogeneity. Effect modifications of city‐level factors comprising population density, average daily median (50th Percentile) air pollutant concentration, and geographic information (latitude and longitude), were investigated on single‐day lag data by fitting a multivariate meta‐regression model. Different cities might have varying levels of baseline air pollution, which can modify the effect size. Including median pollution helps to isolate the outcome of specific pollutants by controlling for overall pollution levels. Heterogeneity in the association between air pollution and LCI cases across cities was assessed using Cochran's Q test and quantified using the I 2 statistic. Heterogeneity was considered low when I 2 was less than 25%, moderate when I 2 ranged from 25% to less than 75%, and high when I 2 was 75% or greater, at a significance level of p ≤ 0.05 (Cumpston et al., 2019). The pooled effect estimates were tested for significance (p ≤ 0.05) using the Z test (Ades et al., 2005). We conducted sensitivity analyses by varying the lag structure, adjusting the df for the time trend (2–4 df per year), and the exposure–lag response functions of the pollutants (2–4 df). To further assess potential confounding from the COVID‐19 pandemic, we repeated the analyses using single‐day lag models after excluding data from 2020 onward. All the analyses were executed in R Version 4.3.1 (R Development Core Team) using “dlnm”, “splines”, and “metafor”/“mgcv” packages. A significance threshold of p < 0.05 was applied.

In total, there were 43,872 LCI cases reported in Anhui within the study period. The average incidence rate for influenza cases was 71.89 cases per 100,000 people. Annual incidences revealed spatiotemporal discrepancy between cities and years. Hefei reported the highest influenza incidence rate (115.66 per 100,000 people) and total case count (10,839), while Suzhou had the lowest influenza incidence rate (27.44 per 100,000 people) and total cases (1,461). The highest incidence of cases and rate was recorded in 2019, reaching 98.56 × 100 cases and 16.15/100,000 people, respectively. The lowest incidence cases were observed in the year 2020. The patterns of LCI cases differed by age, gender and virus type. Males exhibited the highest incidence of cases (23,928 cases) and rate (39.21 per 100,000 people), compared to females, a pattern consistent across years. Age‐wise, the highest incidence cases and rate was revealed among 5–14 years (19,200 cases and 31.46 per 100,000 people). Regarding virus type, H1N1 showed the highest incidence case (14,877 cases) and rate (24.38 per 100,000 people). Virus type showed decreased incidence rates (5.83 per 100,000 people) of the highest circulating virus type in 2019 compared to the first quarter of 2023 (10.05 per 100,000 people) (Figure 2).

Influenza outbreaks show consistent cyclical peaks across years, with varying intensity and timing, such as higher peaks during the 2017–2018 seasons. There is a temporal alignment between PM2.5 peaks and increased influenza cases, suggesting a possible association. Ozone showed negative correlation with all other pollutants plus influenza incidences at p < 0.01 (Table S1 in Supporting Information S1).

Distributed Lag Non‐Linear Model (DLNM) Analysis

The city‐specific DLNM analysis demonstrated significant lagged effects of air pollution on influenza cases, with impacts spanning up to 14 days (Table S2 in Supporting Information S1). The pooled effect estimates for a 10 μg/m3 positive increase in daily concentrations of PM2.5, SO2, NO2, PM10, and CO were RR: 1.0045 (95% CI: 1.0035–1.0054), RR: 1.0035 (95% CI: 1.0001–1.0069), RR: 1.0122 (95% CI: 1.0081–1.0162), RR: 1.0025 (95% CI: 1.0017–1.0033), and RR: 1.2641 (95% CI: 1.1294–1.4150), respectively, in the single‐day lag model. In the cumulative‐day model, there was a positive exposure‐response association for a 10 μg/m3 increase in the concentrations of PM2.5; RR: 1.0043 (95% CI: 1.0018–1.0068), NO2; RR: 1.0076 (95% CI: 1.0006–1.0145), PM10; RR: 1.0024 (95% CI: 1.0001–1.0046), and CO; RR: 1.3026 (95% CI: 0.8139–2.0849). O3 showed no association in both models while SO2 showed no association in the cumulative‐day lag model (Figure 3). Accounting for the potential impacts of COVID‐19 slightly altered the magnitude of the observed associations, but the directional consistency of associations remained. Most pollutants exhibited a slightly stronger positive association with influenza outcomes, while O3 showed similar positive but non‐significant associations (Figure S3 in Supporting Information S1). Ultimately, accounting for the potential impacts of COVID‐19 slightly altered the magnitude of the observed associations but did not fundamentally change the main conclusions of the study.

Table 2 and Figure S2 in Supporting Information S1 (a, b, c, d and e) show the pooled effect estimates for a 10 μg/m3 increase in the daily concentrations of air pollutants on the daily incidence of LCI cases, defined by age. The RR for PM2.5 is consistently above 1 across all age groups, indicating a positive association between PM2.5 and LCI cases. The highest effect is observed in the age group 5–14 years: RR = 1.005 (95% CI: 1.0037–1.0063), followed closely by the age groups 15–24 years: RR = 1.0045 (95% CI: 1.0029–1.0062) and 25–59 years RR = 1.0045 (95% CI: 1.0031–1.0058). The 95% CI in the 15–24 and 25–59 years subgroups overlapped. This calls for further differential analysis to draw accurate conclusions. The association between a 10 μg/m3 increase in the SO2 concentrations and influenza was more variable. Age groups 5–14 years: RR = 1.0084 (95% CI: 1.0026–1.0141) and 15–24 years: RR = 1.0114 (95% CI: 1.00044–1.0184) showed significant positive associations. In contrast, age group ≥60 years: RR = 0.9981 (95% CI: 0.9962–1.0000) showed a marginally negative association. Age group 0–4 years, RR = 0.9996 (95% CI: 0.99962–1.0029) and 25–59 years: RR = 1.0006 (95% CI: 0.9972–1.0039) showed no significant association. Discussion

The possible link between air pollution and the risk of influenza has raised serious concerns in China, particularly amid rapid urbanization and industrialization. However, epidemiological evidence on this association remains limited. In this study, we utilized a two‐stage time series modeling approach to examine the exposure‐response relationships between air pollutant concentrations and LCI cases in Anhui Province.

The Descriptive Statistics

The descriptive results demonstrated notable spatial variability in both pollutant concentrations and influenza incidence across the 16 cities, consistent with previous studies (Domingo & Rovira, 2020; Song et al., 2021; Yu et al., 2023). Hefei exhibited the highest influenza incidence and total case count, suggesting that densely populated urban centers with greater healthcare accessibility may facilitate both transmission and case detection. Conversely, Suzhou recorded a lower influenza incidence despite experiencing a relatively high PM2.5 levels, indicating that local public health measures and community health practices may have mitigated infection risks. The rise in pollutant concentrations during winter aligns with seasonal heating practices that is, use of coals and biomass, and the atmospheric conditions that limit pollutant dispersion.

Daily influenza incidence was positively correlated with SO2, NO2, PM2.5, PM10, and CO, supporting the hypothesis that air pollution may heighten susceptibility to respiratory infections (Meng et al., 2021). The highest incidence was recorded in 2019, potentially due to circulating influenza strains, increased transmission rates, or improved diagnostics and reporting. The sharp decline in 2020 and 2021 aligns with COVID‐19 public health non‐pharmaceutical interventions that reduced transmission of multiple respiratory pathogens (Chossière et al., 2021; Q. Yang et al., 2021). Influenza incidence was higher among males than females, which may reflect biological, social, or behavioral differences influencing exposure and immune response (Klein & Flanagan, 2016; Noymer, 2010; Organization, 2010). Children aged 5–14 years revealed the highest case incidences, consistent with evidence that school‐aged children are at higher risk for influenza due to frequent social contact and developing immune systems (Li et al., 2024; Maldonado et al., 2018). H1N1 was the predominant subtype, indicating its central role in the regional influenza burden and the need for subtype‐specific surveillance and vaccination strategies. While overall incidence peaked in 2019, H1N1 incidence rose again in early 2023, likely influenced by climatic seasonality, increased population mobility following relaxation of COVID‐19 restrictions, and possible waning immunity. This resurgence underscores the importance of continuous monitoring and adaptive vaccination planning in Anhui Province.

DLNM Analysis

We assessed the influence of air pollution on LCI cases, in Anhui province, entailing 43,872 cases. The results demonstrated statistically significant and positive associations between CO, PM25, PM10, NO2, SO2, and LCI cases. These findings are in line with previous studies, which associated air pollutants with ILI (Su et al., 2019a; J. Yang et al., 2023b). Specifically, PM demonstrated a consistent positive association with influenza cases, both in the cumulative‐day and single‐day lag models. Consistent with our findings, a research performed in Beijing using generalized summation model submitted that PM2.5 was strongly linked to an increased risk of ILI (Feng et al., 2016a, 2016b). Elsewhere, positive and negative associations between PM2.5 and PM10, respectively, and the cases of clinical influenza were documented in Hefei city, China (Liu et al., 2019b). Our results support existing literature, which shows that PM2.5 can penetrate deep into the respiratory tract, exacerbating respiratory infections and inflammation (Tian et al., 2019). In 2015, PM was ranked 6th important risk aspect for global burden of disease, leading to about 42.41 million deaths globally (Collaborators, 2016). The slight differences in effect size between the single‐day and cumulative‐day lag models imply that both short‐term and prolonged exposures to PM can influence influenza incidence. This necessitates continuous monitoring and stringent air quality regulations to protect public health.

The associations between gaseous pollutants and LCI cases varied. The NO2 consistently showed a significant positive association, particularly in the single‐day lag model. This is consistent with studies suggesting that NO2 exposure can impair immune function and increase susceptibility to respiratory infections (Larrieu et al., 2009). Exposure to NO2 has been related to respiratory symptoms through directly triggering airways inflammation and indirectly expediting susceptibility to inhaled allergens (Moriyama et al., 2020). SO2 showed a positive association in the single‐day lag model, but not in the cumulative‐day lag model. The marginal association observed for SO2 reflects its transient effects on respiratory health or variations in its emission sources and exposure levels. This depicts prospective role of SO2 pollution in increasing the risk of influenza, which is in tandem with previous studies that link air pollution to respiratory infections (Domingo & Rovira, 2020; Liu et al., 2019c). CO revealed the highest effect size among the pollutants studied, both in single‐day and cumulative‐day lag models. The significant association suggests that CO, even at low concentrations, can have acute health impacts. This finding is supported by evidence that CO exposure can lead to hypoxia, impairing respiratory and cardiovascular systems and potentially increasing vulnerability to infections like influenza (Kinoshita et al., 2020; Manisalidis et al., 2020).

Ozone showed no significant association in either model. This may be linked to O3 complex interaction with other pollutants or the fact that its respiratory effects are more pronounced in the context of chronic exposure rather than short‐term fluctuations (Bell et al., 2004). Notably, the association between acute respiratory infections and O3 exposure has remained contentious. Some research has shown a positive or no connection between O3 and influenza (Li et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2021), while other studies have established a negative relationship (Su et al., 2019b; J. Yang et al., 2023b). In the present study, whereas the initial correlation analysis indicated a negative relationship between O3 and LCI cases, our model, which controlled for various confounding factors, did not reveal any significant overall association. This contrasts with the findings of Su et al. (2019b), who described a negative effect of O3 on ILI in Jinan, China, and Ali et al. (2018), who found that O3 decreased influenza virus transmission in Hong Kong, China.

The lag‐specific variability indicates that the effects of air pollution on the risk of influenza can differ contingent on the timing of exposure relative to outcome. Single‐day lag analysis focuses on the immediate effects of air pollution, capturing acute responses within short time frames and identifying specific days of highest risk. This is crucial for immediate public health responses and short‐term interventions. Alternatively, cumulative‐day lag analysis considers the prolonged and cumulative effects of air pollution exposure over multiple days. This approach is important for understanding the build‐up of pollutants and the delayed health impacts, offering insights into chronic exposure scenarios. The cumulative‐day lag model showed slightly lower or non‐significant effect sizes for some pollutants. Thus, we propose that short‐term spikes in pollution levels might have more immediate health impacts compared to sustained exposure. While some studies have reported that exposure‐response is pronounced on a single day lag (Meng et al., 2021; Neuberger et al., 2004), others have submitted mixed findings regarding lag effect on exposure response association. For instance, Yu et al. (2023) submitted that the link between air pollution and influenza risks was short‐term and decayed over extended lags. The authors attributed this phenomenon to the relatively short incubation period. Similar observations were made by Su et al. (2019b).

The potential mechanisms driving the connection between air pollution and influenza risk are highly multi‐complex. Air pollution exposure may lead to the generation of free radical within the body, put strain on the respiratory system, reduce resistance to infections, and exacerbate disease outcomes. PM can infiltrate the lungs through the throat and nasal passage, subjecting those with constant exposure at greater risk of developing chronic airway inflammation (Ling & van Eeden, 2009). Such inflammation might lead to enhanced production of mucus and decreased cilia function, heightening susceptibility to influenza virus infections (Mehta et al., 2013). Once in the bloodstream, PM can provoke pro‐inflammatory and thrombogenic responses by releasing chemical compounds and inducing metal‐related oxidative stress (Ciencewicki & Jaspers, 2007; Li et al., 2008; Mehta et al., 2013; Santo Signorelli et al., 2019), both of which might trigger and exacerbate influenza risks. Urban air particulates can also alter how macrophage respond to respiratory viral infections (Becker et al., 1999). Toxic CO binds more readily to hemoglobin than oxygen, leading to lung tissue damage through hypoxia or asphyxiation (Zhao et al., 2019). SO2 exposure increases vulnerability to viral respiratory infections due to persistent immune‐related inflammation in in the respiratory system (Domingo & Rovira, 2020; Wu & Zha, 2021). Usually, the inflammatory response, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction are probable biological mechanisms that explain how air pollution increases susceptibility to influenza (Loaiza‐Ceballos et al., 2022).

The relationship between air pollutants and the risk of influenza differ between genders. CO, PM2.5, NO2, and PM10, showed a positive association with influenza cases in both gender with higher effect estimates recorded in males than females. Previous studies suggested that men may be more vulnerable to negative health effects of PM due to factors such as higher outdoor activity levels, exposure patterns, susceptibility, underlying health conditions, occupational exposures, and potential differences in respiratory physiology (Berlinger et al., 2024; Sacks et al., 2011; Wang et al., 2018). The stronger connection between exposure to NO2 and influenza incidence in males can be attributed to differences in occupational exposure. Males are more likely to work in environments with higher NO2 levels, such as traffic‐related occupations and industries (Anderson et al., 2013). The higher CO effect estimate in males might be due to differences in baseline health conditions, smoking rates, and occupational exposure (Jadoon et al., 2022). The RR for was slightly higher in females than in males, although the CI overlapped, implying no significant gender difference. Non‐significant association between O3 and influenza cases in both gender support previous studies that found the acute health effects of O3 to be less pronounced compared to other pollutants, possibly due to the body's ability to adapt to exposure (Bell et al., 2004).

Regarding age, air pollutants increase influenza risk with more pronounced effects in age 5–14 years (PM2.5, NO2 and CO) followed by age 15–24 years (SO2 and PM10). This results support literature which states that children are highly susceptible to respiratory infections, such as influenza, compared to adults (Feng, Gao, et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2019c; Sun & Zhu, 2019; Zheng et al., 2022). The observed increased influenza risk among children aged 5–14 years, particularly in relation to PM2.5, NO2, and CO, aligns with established biological plausibility for heightened vulnerability in developing respiratory systems. This age group exhibits immature immune responses and smaller airway diameters, rendering them more susceptible to pollutant‐induced inflammation and impaired mucociliary clearance. While our study utilized ambient pollution measurements, the pronounced effects in this age group may reflect the cumulative burden of both outdoor infiltration and potential indoor air quality challenges, such as those associated with school environments, which warrant further consideration in future investigations. The consistent positive association between PM10, PM2.5 and influenza spanning all age groups reinforces extensive literature highlighting PM's role in exacerbating respiratory infections (Anderson et al., 2013; Zheng et al., 2015). The intriguing strong positive association of CO in the 5–14 age groups suggests a need for deeper mechanistic understanding, possibly pointing to CO as a proxy for a broader mixture of traffic‐related pollutants known to trigger respiratory distress and immune modulation (Huang et al., 2012). The slightly lower effect in the ages 0–4 and ≥60 years across all pollutants could be attributed to reduced outdoor activity and potentially better healthcare access. Variation in effect estimates across age groups can be attributed to several factors. One is differential susceptibility and exposure. Children have less developed immunity and greater exposure in school settings, heightening susceptibility to both influenza and pollutants. Older adults may have lower susceptibility, but prolonged exposure in community settings, exacerbating underlying health conditions and leading to a different pattern of influenza cases. Second are behavioral and social factors, such as frequent social interactions in schools for younger individuals and reduced mobility for older adults. Lastly, healthcare seeking behavior may influence the effect size. Children receive prompt healthcare, enabling quicker detection of combined effects, while older adults may delay seeking care, obscuring immediate pollution effects.

Meta‐regression analysis revealed that population density modifies exposure‐response relationship between the studied air pollutants and influenza cases. This implies that effects of air pollution on influenza risks are more pronounced in densely populated areas. This result corroborates with studies that have shown higher disease transmission rates in crowded environments due to higher human interactions, closer contact and greater exposure to pollutants (Achakulwisut et al., 2019; Henneman et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2019a). This finding highlights the importance of considering urbanization and population characteristics when assessing the health impacts of air pollution. Elsewhere, median pollution levels moderated the association between SO2 on LCI cases. The statistically significant negative coefficient indicates that areas with higher median pollution levels may experience a reduced incremental effect of SO2 on influenza cases. This could indicate a threshold effect where once pollution levels reach a certain point, additional increases have a smaller impact on health outcomes (Burnett et al., 2014). Contrarily, median pollution levels do not significantly modify the relationship between CO, PM2.5, NO2, PM10, O3, and influenza cases. Latitude significantly modifies the associations between PM2.5, SO2 and LCI cases, with higher latitudes (locations further from the equator) being associated with fewer cases, as indicated by the statistically significant negative coefficients. This could be due to variations in climate and environmental factors that influence both pollution levels and influenza transmission (Fisman & Infection, 2012).

Longitudinal variations do not significantly affect the relationship between SO2, PM2.5 and influenza cases. This is consistent with previous findings that longitudinal variations may have less impact on pollution‐related health outcomes compared to latitudinal differences (Rodrigues et al., 2024). Both latitude and longitude were not significant predictors for exposure‐response relationship between NO2, PM10, CO and influenza cases (Rodrigues et al., 2024). Climate and seasonal variations might play a role but are not captured significantly in this model (Fisman & Infection, 2012). The high residual heterogeneity suggests that there are other influential factors not captured in this analysis that need to be assessed in upcoming research. The sensitivity analyses reinforce the reliability of our results, emphasizing the importance of accounting for varying temporal effects in air pollution studies. The observed significant lagged effects suggest both immediate and delayed risks associated with pollutant exposure, which have critical implications for influenza prevention strategies.

The current study's strengths are: first, the study offers novel insights into the relationship between environmental factors and influenza infectious dynamics, with significant implications for public health policy, climate change adaptation, and epidemiological research. Second, focusing on LCI cases provides specific, clear and accurate insights into the impact of air pollution eliminating the diagnostic uncertainty associated with ILI. Third, we employ a two‐stage modeling strategy for time series data that allows for the explicit estimation of the level of autocorrelation and over dispersion (via a smooth function of time) in specific cities. This strategy also enables inference on the appropriately lagged acute effects of pollution, aggregated across all cities or stratified by age and gender. Fourth, the study uses varying lag structure to determine effect estimates of air pollution on LCI cases, enhancing the understanding of temporal dynamics of influenza epidemiology in Anhui. Finally, the rapid economic growth and urbanization in China have substantially augmented health risks linked to air pollution for her citizens. The current findings contribute to the growing multi‐center evidence on the effects of air pollution on influenza risks in China.

Despite its strengths, this study has some shortcomings. First, because of the inherent nature of ecological studies, our results do not reveal a causal relation between air pollution exposure and LCI cases. Secondly, the vaccination rate, population mobility, healthcare‐seeking behavior, and changes in diagnostic practices over time were not considered in the study, which might be potential confounders. Third, LCI incidences may be underestimated, as not all patients go to hospital for medical attention and those with trivial influenza symptoms might have opted to buy over‐the‐counter drugs. Notably, this potential bias is likely random and unlikely to differentially impact the modelling outcomes. Fourth, since the relationship between air pollution and LCI cases can be influenced by population density, our findings need to be interpreted with caution when extrapolating to regions with different population densities. Finally, our study may not be generalizable to other regions with different environmental and demographic characteristics like Anhui.

Conclusion

In conclusion, PM10, CO, PM2.5, SO2, and NO2 intensify the risk of LCI cases in Anhui province, China, with more pronounced effects in males and age 5–24 years. Population density is a strong moderator of the exposure‐response relationship for these air pollutants. Median pollution levels moderate the association between SO2 and LCI cases, while latitude significantly modifies the associations between PM2.5, SO2 and LCI cases. Findings of this study suggest the need to incorporate aspects of air pollution and population density in policies and intervention strategies for influenza. Future studies should investigate mechanisms underlying these associations and the effectiveness of gender‐and‐age specific strategies in reducing the health burden of air pollution.
