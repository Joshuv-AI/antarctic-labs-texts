Pre-established ATF4 occupancy and chromatin organization instruct selective transcription activation during integrated stress response
Authors: Peipei Jiang, Qian Bian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCells rapidly and extensively remodel their transcriptome in response to stress to restore homeostasis, but the underlying mechanisms are not fully understood. Here, we characterize the dynamic changes in transcriptome, epigenetics, and 3D genome organization during the integrated stress response (ISR). ISR induction triggers widespread transcriptional changes within 6 h, coinciding with increased binding of ATF4, a key transcriptional effector. Notably, ATF4 binds to hundreds of genes even under non-stress conditions, priming them for stronger activation upon stress. The transcriptional changes at ATF4-bound sites during ISR do not rely on increased H3K27 acetylation, chromatin accessibility, or rewired enhancer-promoter looping. Instead, ATF4-mediated gene activation is linked to the redistribution of CEBPγ from non-ATF4 sites to a subset of ATF4-bound regions, likely by forming an ATF4/CEBPγ heterodimer. CEBPγ preferentially targets the sites pre-occupied by ATF4, as well as genomic regions exhibiting a unique higher-order chromatin structure signature. Thus, the transcriptional responses during ISR are largely pre-wired by intrinsic chromatin properties. These findings provide critical insights into transcriptional remodeling during ISR with broader implications for other stress responses.Subject terms: Epigenetics, Gene regulation, Epigenomics

Introduction

Organisms are constantly exposed to various stresses, including nutrient deprivation, oxidative damage, and unpredictable environmental fluctuations, all of which can jeopardize cellular function and survival1. In response, cells have evolved a range of sophisticated, efficient, and highly adaptable mechanisms to mitigate these challenges, collectively referred to as the stress response. The cellular stress response is characterized by its rapid activation and reversibility2. Upon encountering stress, cells initiate signaling pathways that temporarily suppress normal cellular processes and redirect resources toward stress management and repair. This often involves halting energy-demanding activities such as cell division and growth to resolve the stress3–6. Once the stressor is alleviated, these pathways are swiftly deactivated, allowing cells to restore homeostasis and resume normal functions seamlessly7. Thus, proper stress responses are crucial for preserving cellular integrity and function in adverse conditions, thereby ensuring the survival and resilience of organisms.

The protective effects of stress responses rely on extensive transcriptional remodeling, which involves the selective activation of specific pro-survival genes and the repression of many other genes, depending on the type of stress8–10. Over the past few decades, significant progress has been made in understanding the stress-sensing pathways and the transcriptional regulatory mechanisms underlying various stress responses11. Central to stress-triggered transcriptional reprogramming are several dedicated transcription factors (TFs). For instance, heat shock factor 1 (HSF1) is the master regulator of the heat shock response (HSR)12–15, while Nrf2 mediates transcriptional changes in response to oxidative stress16,17. XBP1, ATF4, and ATF6 play key roles in the endoplasmic reticulum stress response18–21. These key TFs reshape the transcriptional landscape by regulating transcriptional initiation and pausing, and by modulating chromatin structure at different levels22. During heat shock, activated HSF1 releases paused RNA polymerase II (Pol II) and promotes productive elongation, resulting in widespread transcriptional activation8,23. ER stress increases chromatin accessibility in active genes24. Acetylated histones H3 and H4, markers of transcriptional activity, rise in the promoters and enhancers of upregulated genes, while repressed genes show decreased acetylation in response to heat shock or oxidative stress10,25–27. Changes in chromatin organization have also been linked with stress-induced transcriptional reprogramming. Temperature stress weakens TAD boundaries, increasing inter-TAD interactions through cohesin28. In yeast, heat shock causes HSP genes on different chromosomes to interact, suggesting a role of 3D genome structure in transcriptional functions29,30. While these mechanisms exert significant influence on transcription, whether they play essential roles across different types of stress responses remains to be determined. Furthermore, the full extent to which these diverse transcriptional regulatory mechanisms contribute to the rapid, robust, and context-dependent transcriptional responses remains to be fully elucidated.

Among various cellular stress response pathways, the Integrated Stress Response (ISR) stands out as a crucial mechanism that integrates signals from various stress conditions into a coordinated cellular reaction, thereby protecting cells from metabolic, oxidative, and ER stress31. Chronic activation or dysregulation of the ISR can lead to numerous diseases, including neurodegenerative disorders, cancer, metabolic syndromes, and inflammatory diseases32. The ISR is primarily regulated by the phosphorylation of eukaryotic initiation factor 2 alpha (eIF2α) at the Ser51 site33,34 through four stress-sensing kinases: GCN2, PERK, HRI, and PKR35–40. eIF2α phosphorylation decreases global protein synthesis while promoting the translation of a key ISR-responsive TF, activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4)41,42. ATF4 subsequently relocates to the nucleus and triggers transcriptional remodeling to restore cellular homeostasis31,43–45. ATF4 belongs to the bZIP (basic leucine zipper) transcription factor family, and regulates transcription by forming both homodimers and heterodimers with other bZIP proteins, such as C/EBP (CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein) factors CHOP46,47, CEBPβ48,49, and CEBPγ50, thereby enhancing its regulatory versatility across different cell types and stress conditions. While the physiological and pathological significance of ISR has been extensively studied, the detailed mechanisms of transcriptional reprogramming during ISR activation are less well understood51. In particular, the impact of ISR on chromatin organization at various levels, and how these complex chromatin changes influence ATF4-mediated transcriptional reprogramming, have not been systematically explored.

In this study, we employed a multi-omic approach to dissect how the dynamic binding of ATF4 and its heterodimerization partners, along with different levels of chromatin structure, collectively shape the transcriptional landscape during the ISR. We found that ATF4 drives transcriptional activation at only a fraction of its binding sites during ISR. Notably, although ATF4 binds to many de novo sites following ISR induction, it also occupies the promoters of hundreds of genes involved in protein homeostasis before ISR induction, priming these genes for a more robust transcriptional activation. In contrast to previous findings in the heat shock response, ATF4-mediated transcriptional activation during ISR does not depend on increased H3K27Ac levels, enhanced chromatin accessibility, or 3D chromatin structure remodeling. Instead, the transcriptional outcome during ISR is largely determined by the genome-wide redistribution of CEBPγ from non-ATF4-bound sites to a subset of ATF4-bound sites, likely driven by the distinct chromatin-binding propensity of the ATF4/CEBPγ heterodimer. We further show that CEBPγ preferentially accumulates at sites that are pre-occupied by ATF4 and exhibit a unique higher-order chromatin organization signature. These findings suggest that intrinsic genome properties and pre-formed chromatin organization play a key role in guiding transcriptional responses during ISR, offering insights into the precise regulation of stress-induced transcriptional reprogramming.

Results

Progressive transcriptional remodeling during the ISR correlates with elevated ATF4 levels

To comprehensively characterize the dynamics of ATF4-driven transcriptional changes during ISR, we treated C2C12 cells with 100 nM Thapsigargin (Thap), which effectively activates ISR through PERK kinase52. By performing a time course experiment, we found that the expression of ATF4 increased noticeably as early as 2 h after Thap treatment and plateaued at around 6 h after induction (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Fig. 1a). Concordantly, immunofluorescence revealed that nuclear ATF4 levels peaked at 6 h after induction (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Fig. 1b).

We performed RNA-Seq on C2C12 cells treated with either DMSO or Thap for 2, 6, and 12 h and assessed the transcriptional changes at each time point (Fig. 1c). We found that Thap treatment induced rapid transcriptional remodeling, with 1132 genes already showing significant transcriptional changes (log2FC > 1 and padj <0.05) at 2 h (Fig. 1d), coinciding with ATF4 nuclear translocation. Upon further treatment, 3145 and 1854 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were detected at 6 h and 12 h, respectively, indicating more pronounced stress responses over time (Fig. 1e, f). Notably, the DEGs vary between each time point (Supplementary Fig. 1c). At 2 h, although some crucial stress response genes such as Sesn2, Chac1, and Ddit3 (CHOP) were upregulated, the most enriched GO pathways among the DEGs were related to apoptotic signaling and cell differentiation (Fig. 1g). In contrast, at 6 and 12 h, the transcriptome changes more closely resemble adaptive stress responses, with response to endoplasmic reticulum stress, response to topologically incorrect protein, and response to unfolded protein among the most enriched GO terms (Fig. 1h, i, Supplementary Fig. 1d). We further demonstrated that treating C2C12 cells with Thap for 6 h followed by a 48-hour incubation in a Thap-free medium reduced ATF4 protein expression to pre-treatment levels (Supplementary Fig. 1e). RNA-seq showed that the transcriptome of the cells after Thap withdrawal was almost identical to that of the DMSO-treated cells (Supplementary Fig. 1f, g), indicating that transcriptional responses during ISR are reversible.

We examined the transcriptional dynamics of genes related to the key stress response pathways, such as ER stress and UPR. In total, 83 key stress response genes were significantly upregulated at either 2, 6, or 12 h of Thap treatment (Fig. 1j, Supplementary Data 1). Overall, these genes only exhibited mild upregulation at 2 h. However, their expression was significantly elevated after 6-hour treatment and persisted through 12 h (Fig. 1j, k). In summary, our time-resolved transcriptional profiling revealed that extensive transcriptional remodeling occurs rapidly upon the induction of ISR, with full-scale transcriptional responses culminating at 6 h after Thap treatment.

We also induced ISR in HeLa cells using carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenyl hydrazone (CCCP), a known activator of ISR via HRI kinase53. Western blotting and immunofluorescence confirmed a marked increase in ATF4 expression after 6 h of CCCP treatment, with no further increase observed at 12 h (Supplementary Fig. 2a–d). We further performed RNA-Seq on HeLa cells treated with CCCP for 6 and 12 h (Supplementary Fig. 2e). At 6 h, 464 genes were significantly upregulated, showing strong enrichment for stress-related Gene Ontology (GO) terms (Supplementary Fig. 2f–h). By 12 h, although a larger number of genes were differentially expressed, the upregulated genes were no longer enriched for stress-related GO categories, suggesting a transition to post-stress responses (Supplementary Fig. 2i–k). Notably, 99 genes were commonly upregulated in both HeLa and C2C12 cells after 6 h of ISR induction, and these genes were strongly associated with stress responses such as endoplasmic reticulum stress and unfolded protein response (Supplementary Fig. 2l, m). The similar temporal patterns of transcriptional changes in HeLa and C2C12 cells indicate that these dynamics are not cell-type-specific or dependent on the ISR induction method, but rather reflect intrinsic properties of the ISR.

ATF4 elicits robust transcriptional upregulation at pre-occupied and de novo binding sites during ISR

Having demonstrated the efficacy of Thap in inducing ISR in C2C12 cells and CCCP in inducing ISR in HeLa cells, we next investigated how ATF4, the core effector of ISR, drives transcriptional responses during ISR. Because the transcriptional changes were most prominent after 6 h of Thap treatment, we compared the genome-wide distribution of ATF4 in C2C12 cells treated with either DMSO (control) or Thap for 6 h using CUT&Tag. To ensure accurate identification of ATF4-binding sites, we performed CUT&Tag with two different ATF4 antibodies and quantitatively assessed their performance by comparing the resulting profiles with each other and with previously published ATF4 ChIP-seq data sets54–58 (Supplementary Fig. 3). The antibody with higher specificity (Cell Signaling Technology, #11815) was selected for subsequent analyses.

As anticipated, genome-wide binding of ATF4 substantially increased upon Thap induction, with 803 and 8621 ATF4 peaks detected in DMSO-treated and Thap-treated cells, respectively (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Data 2 and 3). Notably, nearly all ATF4 peaks (799 out of 803) detected in DMSO-treated cells persisted in Thap-treated cells. In contrast, 7822 ATF4 peaks emerged only upon Thap induction, representing de novo ATF4-binding sites (Fig. 2a). We hereafter defined these two classes of ATF4 sites as pre-occupied and gained sites. Interestingly, the ATF4 signals at pre-occupied sites were substantially stronger than those at gained sites and only exhibited a mild increase upon Thap induction (Fig. 2b). The distinct ATF4-binding patterns at pre-occupied and gained sites suggest that they may exert different functions.

We examined the functions of genes linked to pre-occupied and gained ATF4 sites. Approximately 50% of both classes of sites were located at gene promoters, while the rest were found in introns or intergenic regions, likely corresponding to enhancers (Supplementary Fig. 4a). We therefore further categorized the ATF4-binding sites into four groups: pre-occupied ATF4 promoters, pre-occupied ATF4 enhancers, gained ATF4 promoters, and gained ATF4 enhancers (Fig. 2c), and annotated these sites to their nearest genes. GO enrichment analysis revealed that genes associated with pre-occupied ATF4 promoters were primarily involved in maintaining and restoring protein homeostasis, with GO terms such as cellular amino acid metabolic processes, tRNA aminoacylation for protein translation, and translation regulation among the most enriched terms. Conversely, while some genes associated with gained ATF4 promoters were also related to protein homeostasis, most were enriched in pathways related to the cell cycle, mRNA processing, and apoptotic signaling (Fig. 2d). Interestingly, the pre-occupied ATF4 promoters are also more enriched for genes directly involved in stress responses (Fig. 2d). On the other hand, the genes associated with the ATF4-bound enhancers are not directly involved in stress responses and the maintenance of cellular homeostasis but are enriched for GO terms related to tissue development. Given that enhancers often regulate genes over long distances and do not necessarily regulate the nearest genes, these observations could partly stem from the difficulty of accurately annotating enhancers to their target genes.

Concordant with the significant increase of ATF4 occupancy at the gained ATF4 promoters, we observed that genes associated with these promoters were generally upregulated, with 11.6% showing more than a twofold increase, a higher proportion compared to control promoters without ATF4-binding (6.9%) (Supplementary Fig. 4b, c). Moreover, only 4.4% of these genes were downregulated, which is lower than the percentage observed in control promoters (7.5%) (Supplementary Fig. 4b, c). Surprisingly, although ATF4 occupancy at pre-occupied promoters did not significantly change upon ISR, about one-third of these genes were significantly upregulated and very few downregulated upon ISR induction (Supplementary Fig. 4d). The upregulated genes associated with pre-occupied and gained promoters were enriched for genes involved in protein homeostasis and cell cycle regulation, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 2e–g). Similar patterns were observed when using a less stringent threshold for differential expression (|log2FC | > 0, p < 0.05) (Supplementary Fig. 4h–m). We further showed that the genes associated with the pre-occupied ATF4 sites exhibited greater increases in transcription levels compared to those associated with the gained ATF4 sites (Fig. 2e).

Similar to C2C12 cells, HeLa cells exhibited both pre-occupied and newly gained ATF4-binding sites upon CCCP-induced ISR, with pre-occupied sites showing stronger ATF4 occupancy (Supplementary Fig. 5a). GO enrichment revealed that ATF4 binding at gene promoters, particularly at promoters with pre-occupied ATF4, is associated with stress-related functions (Supplementary Fig. 5b–g). Notably, genes associated with pre-occupied ATF4-binding sites in HeLa cells also exhibit the most robust transcriptional activation during ISR (Supplementary Fig. 5h). Although only 54 pre-occupied ATF4 promoter peaks were shared between HeLa and C2C12 cells, their associated genes were significantly enriched for processes related to protein homeostasis, including responses to endoplasmic reticulum stress and amino acid activation (Supplementary Fig. 5i, j). Interestingly, unlike in C2C12 cells, where ATF4 occupancy at pre-occupied sites remained largely unchanged, ATF4 binding at these sites further increased after ISR induction in HeLa cells (Supplementary Fig. 5k). However, the extent of increased ATF4 binding did not correlate with changes in gene expression (Supplementary Fig. 5l), suggesting the robust transcription activation at the pre-occupied sites cannot be solely attributed to the increased ATF4 binding.

In summary, our results demonstrate that during ISR, ATF4 elicits robust transcriptional regulation via two different modes. While the de novo ATF4 binding can induce the transcription of some genes involved in cell cycle regulation and stress responses, the pre-occupation of ATF4 at the promoters of a subset of stress-related genes under unstressed conditions predisposes these genes for stronger transcription activation upon receiving stress signals.

ATF4 activates target gene expression without inducing significant changes in chromatin accessibility and H3K27Ac

Activation of transcription is often the result of elevated levels of histone modification H3K27Ac and increased chromatin accessibility. We reason that the differential transcriptional responses at ATF4 pre-occupied sites and gained sites may be linked to the different extent of chromatin changes induced by ATF4 at these two classes of sites. Previous studies reported that chromatin accessibility exhibited global increases during the UPR in Drosophila24. However, whether ATF4 induces global transcriptional changes during ISR via remodeling the chromatin landscape has not been examined. To elucidate the relationships between ATF4 binding, chromatin structure changes, and transcriptional induction during ISR, we performed H3K27Ac CUT&Tag and ATAC-Seq in C2C12 cells, both treated with DMSO6h or Thap for 6 h (Fig. 3a). ISR induction triggered widespread chromatin changes, with 3413 H3K27ac peaks upregulated and 4226 downregulated, alongside 1993 upregulated and 1513 downregulated ATAC-seq peaks (Fig. 3b, c). Globally, genes linked to increased H3K27ac or chromatin accessibility tended to be transcriptionally upregulated, whereas those associated with decreased peaks showed a general trend toward downregulation (Supplementary Fig. 6a, b).

We then examined whether ATF4 binding sites showed changes in H3K27Ac signals following Thap treatment. H3K27Ac CUT&Tag revealed that most pre-occupied and gained ATF4 sites colocalize with H3K27Ac peaks in DMSO-treated cells and exhibit stronger signals than control promoters not bound by ATF4 (Fig. 3d, Supplementary Fig. 6c, d), suggesting ATF4 preferentially targets transcriptionally active sites. However, most changes occurred at non-ATF4 sites (Supplementary Fig. 6e, f). At pre-occupied ATF4 promoters, the overall change of H3K27Ac was not significant (Supplementary Fig. 6d), with only 7% and 8% showing increased or decreased signals, respectively, while 85% remained unchanged (Fig. 3e). Similarly, gained ATF4 promoters showed limited H3K27Ac changes (Fig. 3e, Supplementary Fig. 6d). Interestingly, the proportion of a TF4-bound promoter with increased H3K27Ac was much lower than the proportion of genes showing transcriptional upregulation (Fig. 3e, Supplementary Fig. 4b–d). Although there was a positive correlation between the change of H3K27Ac and the change of gene transcription (Supplementary Fig. 6g), a vast majority of upregulated genes cannot be explained by the upregulation of H3K27Ac. We further grouped pre-occupied and gained promoters based on the changes in H3K27Ac and examined the transcriptional responses for each group. We found that although the vast majority of ATF4-bound promoters did not exhibit changes in H3K27Ac, they still exhibited substantial transcriptional upregulation upon ISR (Fig. 3f). These results suggest that ATF4-driven transcriptional remodeling during ISR occurs largely independent of H3K27Ac changes at its binding sites.

We performed similar analyses to evaluate the correlation between ATF4 binding and chromatin accessibility changes during ISR (Fig. 3g–i, Supplementary Fig. 6h–l). Unlike previously reported on UPR in Drosophila cells24, we found that chromatin accessibility in C2C12 cells did not exhibit a global elevation upon Thap treatment. Similar to H3K27Ac, the majority of upregulated genes do not exhibit significant increases in chromatin accessibility. Similar results were also observed in HeLa cells under CCCP-induced ISR (Supplementary Fig. 7a–f). Taken together, our data suggest that the increases in H3K27Ac signals or chromatin accessibility at ATF4-bound sites are neither necessary nor sufficient for transcriptional activation upon ISR.

Collectively, our results suggest that ATF4 preferentially targets sites exhibiting high H3K27Ac signals and chromatin accessibility, and effectively activates transcription without further remodeling chromatin landscapes at these locations. Upon gaining ATF4 sites, increased ATF4 binding at these pre-accessible sites results in transcriptional activation. Curiously, at pre-occupied ATF4 sites, as exemplified by the key stress-related genes Atf3, Xbp1, HERPUD1, and PPP1R15A (Supplementary Fig. 7g, h), despite minimal changes in ATF4 binding, chromatin accessibility, and H3K27Ac, transcript levels of these genes significantly increase after ISR. How the binding of ATF4 at these sites preconditions them for robust transcriptional activation remains to be explored.

ATF4 activates target gene transcription within the pre-formed 3D genome framework

Changes in 3D genome organization are intimately connected to transcriptional regulation. Previous studies have shown that some bZIP family TFs, such as AP-1, can induce the formation of regulatory chromatin loops and hubs that connect enhancers with gene promoters, thereby promoting gene activation59. These findings prompted us to examine whether ATF4 induces transcriptional remodeling by altering 3D genome organization during ISR and whether the different extent of transcriptional activation at pre-occupied or gained ATF4 binding sites is related to the changes in genome organization.

We performed Hi-C of DMSO-treated and Thap-treated cells to profile 3D genome architecture changes upon ISR (Supplementary Data 4). We then compared the genome architecture between DMSO-treated and Thap-treated cells at different levels, including A/B compartments, topologically associating domains (TADs), and chromatin loops. Upon Thap treatment, most of the compartments remain stable, with only 2.46% and 2.38% of genomic regions exhibiting compartment switch from A to B or B to A, respectively (Fig. 4a). In comparison, the changes at the TAD level are more pronounced. Upon ISR, while 1483 TADs remained stable, 318 and 216 TADs disappeared or emerged, respectively, corresponding to 17.7% and 12.7% of total TADs (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Fig. 8a). It should be noted that the TAD boundaries associated with the altered TADs exhibited more positive insulation scores compared to those associated with the unchanged TADs (Supplementary Fig. 8b), suggesting the changes of TAD organization mainly occurred at weak TAD boundaries. Chromatin loop landscapes also exhibit notable changes in ISR, with 7500, 1664, and 2484 loops unchanged, disappeared, or newly formed, respectively (Fig. 4c, Supplementary Fig. 8c). We next explored whether 3D genome reorganization during ISR is coupled with changes in epigenetic state or transcription. H3K27ac levels and chromatin accessibility remained largely unchanged at both dynamic and stable TAD boundaries (Supplementary Fig. 9a, b), and gene expression within these regions showed no significant differences (Supplementary Fig. 9c). Similar patterns were observed at chromatin loop anchors (Supplementary Fig. 9d–f).

Furthermore, we found that neither the pre-occupied nor gained ATF4 sites were enriched in changed TAD boundaries compared to the stable TAD boundaries (Fig. 4d). And TAD boundary containing pre-occupied or gained ATF4 binding sites exhibited negligible changes in insulation scores upon Thap induction (Supplementary Fig. 9g). Similarly, the anchors of lost or gained loops did not show enrichment ATF4 binding sites compared to the anchors of the stable loops (Fig. 4e). Taken together, these results strongly suggest that the changes in higher-order chromatin organization during ISR are not directly related to the genome occupancy of ATF4. Rather, these changes may be attributed to the secondary effects downstream of ATF4-induced transcriptional remodeling, such as redistribution of genome architecture proteins, including CTCF, or alterations in cell cycle composition.

Of particular note, some genes associated with pre-occupied or gained ATF4 peaks are linked to distal enhancers by chromatin loops in DMSO-treated cells. Upon Thap treatment, the strength of these E-P loops remains constant, while the expression of the genes was significantly upregulated (Fig. 4f). Moreover, pile-up analysis of chromatin loops revealed that the loops associating with ATF4 binding sites exhibited little changes in chromatin interaction frequencies (Supplementary Fig. 9h). Taken together, our data suggest ATF4 mainly acts upon pre-established chromatin architecture, rather than remodeling the architecture, to elicit transcriptional regulation during ISR.

Redistribution of CEBPγ among ATF4-binding sites upon ISR underlies selective transcriptional activation

ATF4 has been known to form heterodimers with various bZIP TFs such as CHOP and CEBP, both under normal physiological contexts and during stress responses, to regulate the transcription of target genes46,50. Given that neither the changes in ATF4 occupancy nor chromatin structure can predict the transcriptional outcomes at the ATF4 binding sites, we postulate that the differential recruitment of ATF4-heterodimerization partners may determine the differential transcriptional response at the ATF4-binding sites.

We employed several approaches to determine the key ATF4 heterodimerization partners involved in the transcriptional remodeling during ISR induced in C2C12. Motif enrichment analysis indicated that both the pre-occupied and gained ATF4 promoter regions exhibit strong enrichment for CHOP and CEBP family motifs (Supplementary Fig. 10a, b). By conducting ATF4 immunoprecipitation followed by mass spectrometry (IP-MS) in DMSO-treated and Thap-treated C2C12 cells, we found three TFs—CHOP, CEBPβ, and CEBPγ—are the main CEBP family TFs that exhibit increased association with ATF4 upon ISR (Supplementary Fig. 10c, Supplementary Data 5). Similar to ATF4, the expression levels of CHOP were significantly increased after Thap treatment, while the expression levels of CEBPβ and CEBPγ remained almost unchanged upon ISR (Supplementary Fig. 10d).

We performed CUT&Tag for each of these three TFs in DMSO-treated and Thap-treated C2C12 cells to examine the changes in their genome distribution during ISR (Supplementary Fig. 10e–g). We found that all three TFs exhibit greater occupancy at ATF4 sites than at non-ATF4 sites (Supplementary Fig. 10h–j). However, CHOP, CEBPβ, and CEBPγ exhibit distinct patterns of redistribution at different classes of ATF4 sites upon Thap induction (Fig. 5a). CHOP exhibits comparable occupancy at both ATF4 pre-occupied and gained sites in DMSO-treated cells (Supplementary Fig. 10h). Upon induction, the occupancy of CHOP throughout the genome elevates substantially (Supplementary Fig. 10e), in line with the increased expression level of CHOP during ISR, with the ATF4 pre-occupied and gained sites exhibiting a similar extent of elevation (Supplementary Fig. 10k). In contrast, the occupancy of CEBPβ exhibits an overall decrease throughout the genome upon Thap induction (Supplementary Fig. 10f). However, the occupancy of CEBPβ at the ATF4 pre-occupied sites, but not the ATF4-gained sites, remains constant, resulting in a relative enrichment of CEBPβ at the ATF4 pre-occupied sites (Supplementary Fig. 10l). Unlike CHOP and CEBPβ, CEBPγ exhibits greater occupancy at the ATF4 pre-occupied sites than ATF4-gained sites in control cells. After Thap treatment, CEBPγ occupancy at the ATF4 pre-occupied sites further increased, while its occupancy at the ATF4 gained sites and the non-ATF4 sites exhibited a slight decrease (Supplementary Fig. 10m). Taken together, these results suggest that CHOP, CEBPβ, and CEBPγ all exhibited pronounced redistribution upon the Thap-induced upregulation of ATF4, presumably due to the formation of a heterodimer with ATF4, with CEBPγ exhibiting the greatest enrichment at the ATF4 pre-occupied sites upon induction.

Interestingly, we further showed that both the ATF4 pre-occupied and gained peaks can be divided into two groups that either exhibit increased (class 1) or decreased (class 2) CEBPγ binding (Fig. 5b–e). Of the 344 pre-occupied ATF4 promoter peaks, ~45% (155 peaks) exhibit increased CEBPγ binding during the ISR (Fig. 5b, c, Supplementary Data 6). In contrast, of the 3545 gained ATF4 promoter peaks, only ~26% (935 peaks) exhibit increased CEBPγ binding (Fig. 5d, e, Supplementary Data 7). Although class 1 and class 2 peaks exhibit indiscernible patterns of ATF4 binding, transcriptional activation predominantly occurs at genes associated with class 1 peaks (Fig. 5f, g, Supplementary Fig. 11a, b). Moreover, the extent of transcriptional upregulation for genes associated with class 1 peaks exhibits a positive correlation with the increase of CEBPγ binding, but not with the increase of ATF4 binding (Fig. 5h, i, Supplementary Fig. 11c). Notably, genes associated with class 1 ATF4 pre-occupied promoters are functionally involved in the cellular amino acid metabolic process, response to endoplasmic reticulum stress, and response to unfolded protein (Supplementary Fig. 11d), which were highly relevant to the stress response. In contrast, class 1 gained promoters are linked to genes involved in proteasome-mediated ubiquitin-dependent protein catabolic process and cell cycle regulation. Similarly, in HeLa cells, CEBPγ accumulated at pre-occupied ATF4 sites following CCCP-induced ISR (Supplementary Fig. 12a). Genes associated with class 1 peaks exhibited stronger transcriptional activation than those in class 2 (Supplementary Fig. 12b–e), and GO analysis revealed that class 1 genes are enriched for pathways related to amino acid activation and the unfolded protein response (Supplementary Fig. 12f, g). Together, these findings underscore a critical role for CEBPγ in regulating gene expression during the ISR.

To further elucidate the function of CEBPγ, we performed shRNA-mediated knockdown of CEBPγ in C2C12 cells (Supplementary Fig. 13a). Transcriptomic analysis revealed that CEBPγ knockdown altered gene expression under both DMSO and Thap treatment (Fig. 5j, Supplementary Fig. 13b, c). Although genes downregulated by CEBPγ depletion were not significantly enriched for stress-related GO terms (Supplementary Fig. 13d, e), the activation of stress-responsive genes during ISR was markedly attenuated upon the depletion of CEBPγ (Fig. 5k). Notably, class 1 genes were more strongly affected by CEBPγ knockdown compared to class 2 genes (Fig. 5l). These findings suggest that the more robust transcription activation at ATF4-pre-occupied sites, as well as the selective transcriptional activation of both ATF4-pre-occupied and ATF4-gained sites, is likely driven by the preferential binding of CEBPγ.

In summary, our results paint a picture in which the increase of nuclear ATF4 levels upon ISR triggers the redistribution of CEBPγ throughout the genome, possibly in the form of ATF4/CEBPγ heterodimer, thereby selectively activating genes involved in stress responses. During ISR, the ATF4/CEBPγ heterodimer preferentially concentrates on a subclass of ATF4 binding sites, which are more enriched within the ATF4 pre-occupied sites compared to the ATF4 de novo binding sites, resulting in more robust transcriptional activation for genes associated with ATF4 pre-occupied sites.

Higher-order chromatin folding primes CEBPγ redistribution and transcription activation during ISR

We sought to understand why the class 1 sites, both within the ATF4 pre-occupied and gained peaks, became the preferential targets of the ATF4/CEBPγ heterodimer during ISR. One simple explanation for this phenomenon is that the class 1 sites harbor stronger motifs for both ATF4 and CEBPγ. To this end, we examined the motif strengths for ATF4 and CEBPγ in class 1 and class 2 sites in ATF4 pre-occupied sites, ATF4 gained sites, and control promoters not occupied by ATF4. We found that the ATF4 pre-occupied sites contain stronger ATF4 motifs than the ATF4 gained sites, consistent with the greater ATF4 occupancy in the former (Fig. 6a). However, class 1 sites within ATF4 pre-occupied sites or ATF4 gained sites exhibit comparable ATF4 motif strength to the class 2 sites (Fig. 6a). Similarly, whereas the ATF4 pre-occupied sites contain stronger CEBPγ motifs than the ATF4 gained sites, the CEBPγ motif strengths in class 1 sites are comparable or weaker than those in class 2 sites (Fig. 6b). Thus, while the ATF4 pre-occupied sites are overall more favorable sites for the binding of both ATF4 or CEBPγ, the preferential targeting of ATF4/CEBPγ heterodimers to class 1 sites cannot be attributed to the differences in their DNA sequences.

We postulate that the chromatin structure associated with different classes of sites may underlie the binding preference of the ATF4/CEBPγ heterodimer. However, we found that the chromatin accessibility at class 1 sites was either similar to or lower than that at the class 2 sites in untreated cells (Fig. 6c), suggesting the local chromatin accessibility is unlikely to make class 1 sites the more favorable targets for ATF4/CEBPγ heterodimer. Recent studies have shown that higher-order chromatin folding may profoundly influence the target search of TFs60–62. To visualize the chromatin organization surrounding the ATF4-binding sites, we created pile-up heatmaps for 200 kb genomic regions centered at different classes of sites. As reported previously, the genomic regions upstream and downstream of actively transcribed genes interact with each other at a lower frequency, resulting in a chromatin interaction pattern reminiscent of TAD boundaries63 (Fig. 6d). Intriguingly, although the class 1 and class 2 sites are largely indistinguishable based on motif strengths and chromatin accessibility, the higher-order chromatin organization surrounding these sites exhibits distinct characteristics (Fig. 6d). First, the chromatin “domains” upstream and downstream of class 1 sites exhibit less frequent intra-domain contacts than the domains surrounding the class 2 sites, suggesting the class 1 sites are located in a less compact chromatin neighborhood. Second, as indicated by the length of the cross-shape pattern extending from the center of the pile-up heatmaps, the class 1 sites could interact with their downstream and upstream regions within 20 kb. However, class 2 sites could interact with their neighboring regions over a greater distance, suggesting the class 1 sites are more spatially isolated within their genomic neighborhoods. Notably, these patterns are present before stress and maintained during the ISR, suggesting these higher-order chromatin organization characteristics are intrinsic to these genomic loci. Thus, the loosely structured genomic neighborhood with the ATF4 binding sites in a “stretched” conformation may serve as a structural cue for the targeting of the ATF4/CEBPγ heterodimer. Together, our results suggest that the pre-occupancy of ATF4 and higher-order chromatin organization may collectively instruct the binding of ATF4/CEBPγ at different genomic loci, thereby determining the transcriptional outcomes during ISR.

Discussion

Unlike transcriptional reprogramming during development, which drives stable changes in cell fate, stress-induced transcriptional reprogramming must be rapid, robust, and reversible to ensure cellular survival under adverse conditions while avoiding prolonged suppression of normal cellular functions. Therefore, stress-induced TFs likely mediate transcriptional responses in ways distinct from those regulating cell fate. During the ISR, increased translation of the master regulator ATF4 initiates the transcriptional upregulation of stress-responsive genes. While extensive studies have illuminated the stress-sensing and signaling pathways that cause ATF4 upregulation, the precise mechanisms by which ATF4 drives robust transcriptional responses remain less well understood. Through systematic genomic profiling, we discover that ATF4 induces potent transcriptional responses without causing significant chromatin remodeling at its binding sites. Instead, ATF4 leverages pre-existing chromatin features to selectively target and activate stress-responsive genes.

Recent studies have revealed that alterations in chromatin structure are associated with stress responses across various contexts28–30,64. Specific histone modifications, particularly acetylation of histones H3 and H4, are essential for ATF4-mediated upregulation of critical stress-responsive genes, including Jmjd3, Chop, and Snat2, during amino acid deprivation10,24–27,65. However, most investigations into the role of histone modifications in stress responses have focused on a limited set of target genes. Whether the upregulation of activating histone modifications represents a central mechanism of ATF4-induced transcriptional reprogramming remains unclear. Through genome-wide profiling, we demonstrate that ATF4 activates the majority of its target genes without inducing notable increases in chromatin accessibility or histone acetylation. These findings suggest that changes in epigenetic modifications may play a context-dependent and target-specific role, rather than a genome-wide function, in ATF4-mediated transcriptional activation.

At a higher level, 3D genome organization, including the compartmentalization of the genome into A/B compartments, the partitioning into TADs, and the formation of chromatin loops facilitating enhancer-promoter interactions, plays a critical role in precise transcriptional regulation66–68. Recent studies have shown that osmotic stress and heat shock can induce changes at the TAD and chromatin loop levels28,69. Consistent with these findings, we observed notable alterations in TADs and chromatin loops. However, further analysis revealed that these changes are not correlated with ATF4 binding or transcriptional changes, suggesting they may represent secondary effects rather than directly mediating stress-induced transcriptional responses. The limited changes at both the chromatin modification and genome architecture levels during ISR may minimize the risk of causing prolonged alterations to the transcriptional programs associated with normal cellular functions and cell proliferation, thereby ensuring the reversibility of stress responses.

Our findings suggest that the extent of ATF4-driven transcriptional activation is primarily determined by the selective recruitment of heterodimers formed between ATF4 and other bZIP TFs. While several ATF4 heterodimerization partners, including CHOP, CEBPβ, and CEBPγ, have been implicated in promoting stress-induced transcription in various contexts46–48,50, our results identify CEBPγ as the primary effector driving transcriptional activation during Thap-induced ISR in C2C12 cells. Upon ISR induction, CEBPγ exhibits genome-wide redistribution and concentrates on a subset of ATF4 sites. While we could not directly quantify the occupancy of different types of bZIP homodimers and heterodimers, the prominent colocalization of CEBPγ and ATF4 strongly suggests such redistribution is driven by the formation of ATF4/CEBPγ heterodimers.

The selective targeting of ATF4/CEBPγ heterodimers to specific genomic loci is linked to two key genomic properties. First, CEBPγ preferentially targets genomic sites that are pre-occupied by high levels of ATF4. These ATF4-pre-occupied sites are characterized by high chromatin accessibility and H3K27ac levels, irrespective of stress, and contain the strongest ATF4-binding motifs. Despite the significant increase in intranuclear ATF4 abundance during the ISR, ATF4 levels at these pre-occupied sites remain unchanged. However, CEBPγ occupancy at these sites increases substantially, accompanied by robust transcriptional upregulation. A plausible explanation for this phenomenon is that ATF4 primarily binds to these sites as homodimers under unstressed conditions. Upon stress induction, a fraction of ATF4 homodimers may be replaced by ATF4/CEBPγ heterodimers, which possess stronger transcriptional activation potential, thereby driving increased transcriptional output. Notably, while ATF4 protein levels are limited under unstressed conditions, CEBPγ protein levels remain comparable before and after stress induction and are likely in excess relative to ATF4. How the balance between ATF4 homodimers and heterodimers at different genomic loci is regulated under stress conditions remains an open question.

Our findings further reveal that the pre-formed higher-order chromatin organization surrounding ATF4 binding sites contributes to the selective recruitment of ATF4/CEBPγ heterodimers and transcriptional activation. Both pre-occupied and stress-induced ATF4 binding sites can be classified into two groups based on their higher-order chromatin folding patterns. While these two classes of sites exhibit similar chromatin accessibility as measured by ATAC-seq, class 1 sites are situated within loosely folded genomic neighborhoods, whereas class 2 sites reside in more compact chromatin domains. Notably, although class 1 and class 2 sites display comparable levels of ATF4 occupancy, class 1 sites show a greater increase in CEBPγ occupancy following ISR induction. This suggests that the more open chromatin environment surrounding class 1 sites provides a favorable context for the binding of ATF4/CEBPγ heterodimers. Interestingly, recent research has demonstrated that certain pioneer factors preferentially survey compact chromatin domains to drive chromatin remodeling, highlighting the significant influence of higher-order chromatin structure on TF target selection61. Future studies utilizing single-molecule imaging approaches may offer deeper insights into the target-search mechanisms and functional roles of different ATF4 heterodimers.

In summary, our study suggests that the transcriptional responses during ISR, which are essential for cell survival, are largely pre-wired by intrinsic chromatin properties. Variations in stress-induced transcriptional responses may arise from cell-type-specific chromatin landscapes and the expression patterns of different ATF4 heterodimerization partners70. While our investigation focused specifically on the ISR, these findings may offer broader insights into the mechanisms by which diverse stress conditions drive robust, reversible, and context-dependent transcriptional changes.

Methods

Cell culture and ISR induction

C2C12 (#SCSP-505) and HeLa (# SCSP-504) cell lines were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences and cultured in DMEM (high glucose) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution. The cells were maintained in a humidified atmosphere at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. To induce the ISR, C2C12 cells were treated with 100 nM Thap or DMSO (vehicle control) for various durations 24 h after seeding. Similarly, HeLa cells were exposed to 100 nM CCCP or DMSO for the indicated durations.

Western blotting

For protein extraction, cells were washed in PBS and lysed in ice-cold RIPA buffer (YEASEN #20101ES60) supplemented with protease and phosphate inhibitors for 10 min on ice. Samples were then centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was mixed with 5× Bolt Loading buffer and incubated at 95 °C for 10 min. Following a run onto Omni-PAGE™ Hepes-Tris Gels (4–15%) at 100 V for 1 h, the proteins were then transferred onto PVDF membranes using the Bio-Rad Trans Blot Turbo Transfer System (25 V, 2.5 mA, 10 min). Membranes were blocked in the blocking buffer containing 5% skim milk for 1 hour at room temperature and subsequently incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: ATF4 (Cell Signaling Technology #11815, 1:1000), β-Actin (Cell Signaling Technology#8457, 1:1000), CEBPβ (Thermo Fisher #MA1-827, 1:1000), CEBPγ (Thermo Fisher #PA5-80468, 1:1000), CHOP (Cell Signaling Technology#2895 T, 1:1000). Membranes were then incubated with corresponding horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (YEASEN #33101ES60, 1:10000; YEASEN #33201ES60, 1:10000) for 1 hour at room temperature. Protein detection was performed using ECL luminescence reagent (absin #abs920), and images were captured with the Amersham Imager 600.

Immunofluorescence

Cell samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 10 min, permeabilized with 1% Triton-X-100/PBST for 10 min, and blocked with 5% BSA/PBST for 1 hour at room temperature. The samples were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the ATF4 primary antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, #11815, 1:200) and subsequently incubated for 1 hour at room temperature with Alexa Fluor 594-labeled Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG secondary antibody (Thermo Fisher, #A-11012). Cell nuclei were stained with DAPI (1 μg/mL) for 10 min. Images were acquired using a Zeiss LSM 880 confocal microscope and analyzed with ZEN software (Carl ZEISS, Jena, Germany).

IP-MS

We performed co-IP/MS to identify proteins interacting with ATF4 during the ISR. Four biological replicates were prepared for C2C12 cells treated with either DMSO or Thapsigargin (Thap). Nuclear proteins were extracted using the Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction Kit (YEASEN, #20126ES50). The nuclear extracts were incubated overnight at 4 °C with rabbit anti-ATF4 antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, #11815, 1:50) or rabbit IgG as a negative control, followed by incubation with protein A agarose beads for 1 h at 4 °C. Beads were washed three times with IP buffer containing inhibitors, and bound protein complexes were eluted with sample buffer. The immunoprecipitated proteins were reduced with 10 mM tri(2-carboxyethyl) phosphine hydrochloride and alkylated with 25 mM iodoacetamide at 37 °C for 30 min. Five volumes of chilled acetone were added to the protein solution for protein precipitation. The protein pellet was reconstituted in 2 M urea in 100 mM Tris (pH 8.0) for trypsin digestion at 37 °C overnight (50:1 protein: trypsin, w/w; trypsin was provided by Promega). Digests were desalted using Stage-tip (in-house made, using 3 M EmporeTM C18 disk), the eluates were dried down in a Speedvac and stored at −80 °C until use.

All nano chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis was performed on a Q-Exactive HF mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled with an Easy-nLC 1200 UPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The lyophilized peptides were dissolved in buffer A (0.1% formic acid), loaded and separated onto a homemade analytical column (C18, 75 µm i.d., 15 cm) at a flow rate of 250 nL min-1 using a 60-min linear gradient (buffer B, 80% acetonitrile, 1–4% B over 2 min, 4–25% B from 2 to 40 min, and 25−40% B from 40 to 54 min, 40–80% B from 54 to 58 min, 80 % from 58 to 60 min). The column temperature was set at 55 °C. The electrospray voltage was set at 2.1 kV, and the temperature of the ion transfer tube was set at 275 °C. The S-lens RF level setting was 40%. MS data were acquired in a data-dependent mode, consisting of one full-MS survey scan (375–1500 m/z) with a resolution of 60 K and then MS/MS scans with a resolution of 15 K. For MS, the AGC was set to 4e5 ions, with maximum accumulation times of 50 ms. For MS/MS, HCD was used with 30% normalized collision energy, an isolation window of 1.6 m/z, and the AGC was set to 5e4 ions, with a maximum accumulation time of 35 ms. 2–6 charge state was included, and dynamic exclusion was 40 s.

The MS/MS data were searched against the human UniProt database (20,589 sequences) using Protein Discoverer software (Version 3.0). The search settings were as follows: trypsin, with the max missed cleavages set at 2. The carbamidomethylation of Cysteine (57.02 Da) was set as a fixed modification, while the oxidation of Methionine (15.99 Da) and the acetylation of the protein N-terminus (42.01 Da) were set as variable modifications. The mass error tolerance for parent and fragment ions was 10 ppm and 0.02 Da, respectively. The false discovery rates (FDRs) of protein identification were set to less than 1%. Peptide identification was restricted to a confidence level of ≥95% with at least one unique peptide per protein. For data analysis, protein levels were evaluated semi-quantitatively using the PSM (Peptide-Spectrum Matches) counts. Differences between ATF4 immunoprecipitates from Thap- and DMSO-treated C2C12 cells (with IgG as the control) were evaluated using a two-sided unpaired t-test.

Lentivirus preparation and infection

shRNAs targeting CEBPγ (Supplementary Data 8) were cloned into the pLVshRNA-EGFP(2 A)Puro lentiviral vector (NovoPro, #V012982). Lentiviral particles were produced by co-transfecting 293 T cells with the transfer plasmid, envelope plasmid pMD2.G (Addgene #12259), and packaging plasmid psPAX2 (Addgene #12260) using Lipo8000 transfection reagent (Beyotime, #C0533FT) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After 6 h, the transfection medium was replaced with fresh culture medium. Viral supernatants were collected 48 h post-transfection, filtered through a 0.22 µm membrane, and used to infect C2C12 cells in the presence of 10 µg/mL Polybrene (Sigma-Aldrich) for 48 h. Stable knockdown cells were selected with 1 µg/mL puromycin (Sigma-Aldrich).

RNA-seq experiments

Total RNA was extracted from 1 × 106 cells using the MolPure Cell RNA Kit (YEASEN, 19231ES50) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA sequencing libraries were constructed using the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (NEB England BioLabs). RNA-seq paired-end reads were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform.

RNA-seq data processing and statistical analysis

The quality of reads was assessed using the FastQC tool. The raw reads were trimmed using TrimGalore (v0.6.10) (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/trim_galore/) and then mapped to the transcriptome of mm10 by STAR (v2.7.1a)71 with default parameters. All mapped BAM files were converted to bigwig using bedtools (v2.31.0)72 for visualization in IGV. Differentially expressed genes (adjusted p value < 0.05 and abs (log2 fold change) > 1) were identified using DESeq2. The data were scaled and visualized in R using the heatmap2 package. Gene ontology enrichment analysis was performed using ClusterProfiler (v4.7.1.003). Genes were clustered by K-means. FPKM is calculated using the Homer analyzeRepeats.pl script (http://homer.ucsd.edu/homer/ngs/analyzeRNA.html). For all boxplots comparing gene expression changes, only genes with baseMean > 0 were included.

CUT&Tag experiments

The CUT&Tag assay was performed as previously described73 with modifications using the Hyperactive Universal CUT&Tag Assay Kit for Illumina (Vazyme, #TD903). Briefly, 1 × 10⁵ cells were collected, washed once with 500 μL wash buffer, and then incubated with 10 μL concanavalin A-coated magnetic beads in 100 μL binding buffer at room temperature for 10 min. The bead-bound cells were subsequently incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against H3K27Ac (Abcam, #ab4729, 1:50), ATF4 (Cell Signaling Technology, #11815, 1:50, and Santa Cruz Biotechnology, # sc-390063, 1:50), CEBPβ (Thermo Fisher, #MA1-827, 1:50), CEBPγ (Thermo Fisher, #PA5-80468, 1:50), and CHOP (Cell Signaling Technology, #2895 T, 1:50). The next day, corresponding IgG (1:100) in DIG wash buffer was added and incubated for 1 hour at 25 °C. The cells were then washed three times with DIG wash buffer and incubated for 1 hour at 25 °C with 0.04 μM pA/G-Tnp. After three washes with DIG 300 buffer, samples were resuspended in tagmentation buffer and incubated at 37 °C for 1 hour. The interactions were quenched by adding 5 μL of 20 mg/mL Proteinase K, 100 μL Buffer L/B, and 20 μL DNA extraction beads, followed by incubation at 55 °C for 10 min. The beads were washed once with Buffer WA and twice with Buffer WB before being resuspended in 22 μL nuclease-free water. For library amplification, 15 μL of purified DNA was mixed with 25 μL of 2× CAM, and 5 μL of uniquely barcoded i5 and i7 primers from the TruePrep Index Kit V2 for Illumina (Vazyme, #TD202). A total volume of 50 μL was placed in a thermal cycler with the following program: 72 °C for 3 min, 98 °C for 3 min, 12–16 cycles of 98 °C for 10 seconds, 60 °C for 5 seconds, and 72 °C for 1 min, followed by a hold at 12 °C. To purify the PCR products, 2× volumes of VAHTS DNA Clean Beads (Vazyme, #N411) were added and incubated at room temperature for 5 min. The beads were washed twice with 200 μL of fresh 80% ethanol and eluted in 22 μL ddH₂O. All CUT&Tag libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform in PE150 mode.

CUT&Tag data processing and statistical analysis

The quality of CUT&Tag reads was assessed using the FastQC tool. Raw reads were trimmed with TrimGalore (v0.6.10) (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/trim_galore/) and subsequently mapped to the mouse transcriptome (mm10) using Bowtie2. PCR duplicates and mitochondrial reads were excluded using Samtools (v1.9), and the resulting SAM files were converted to BAM files. CUT&Tag peaks in C2C12 were called using combined replicate data by MACS2 (v2.2.7.1, -f BAMPE) with a significance threshold of p < 0.05. Weak ATF4 peaks were filtered out using a cutoff of summit value > 100 (CST antibody) or >400 (SCBT antibody) in C2C12 cells. CUT&Tag peaks in HeLa cells were called using combined replicate data by MACS2 (v2.2.7.1, -f BAMPE) with a significance threshold of p < 0.05 and IgG as a control. Weak ATF4 peaks in HeLa cells were filtered out using a cutoff of summit value > 100. Genome-wide differential CUT&Tag peaks were identified using the DiffBind package (v3.6.1) based on edgeR analysis (FDR < 0.05, |log2FC | > 1). Peak annotation was performed using ChIPseeker (v1.30.2). To normalize signal intensity, Deeptools (v3.5.1) bamCoverage was applied to sorted BAM files. ComputeMatrix was used to calculate signals over designated genomic regions. Bigwigcompare was employed to compare BigWig files and obtain log2 ratios, which were visualized with plotProfile and plotHeatmap. Motif discovery and analysis in the regions of interest were conducted using Homer’s findMotifsGenome.pl (http://homer.ucsd.edu/homer/ngs/peakMotifs.html). Putative ATF4 and CEBPγ transcription factor binding sites (TFBS) were scanned using TFBSTools (v3.19). Position weight matrices for ATF4 and CEBPγ motifs were obtained from the JASPAR database (https://jaspar.elixir.no/).

ATAC-seq experiments

ATAC-seq libraries were generated from ~1 × 106 cell nuclei with the TruePrep® DNA Library Prep Kit V2 for Illumina (Vazyme #TD501) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were collected and lysed in 50 μL of ice-cold ATAC lysis buffer [10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 10 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl₂, and 0.1% (v/v) Igepal CA-630], and incubated on ice for 10 min. The cells were then centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min at 4 °C to pellet the nuclei. The pelleted nuclei were resuspended in 50 μL of transposition mix (10 μL of 5× TTBL, 5 μL of TTE Mix V50, and 35 μL of double-distilled water), and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. The reactions were purified using VAHTS DNA Clean Beads (Vazyme #N411) and PCR-amplified with the following program: 72 °C for 3 min, 98 °C for 3 min, 12–16 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s, 60 °C for 5 s, and 72 °C for 1 min, followed by a 12 °C hold. The final libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform in 150PE mode.

ATAC-seq data processing and statistical analysis

For all ATAC-Seq data sets, the data processing and statistical analysis were performed following the same procedures as for the CUT&Tag data.

Hi-C experiments

Hi-C 3.0 was performed as previously described with modifications74. Briefly, 5 × 106 cells were washed once with ice-cold PBS and crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature. Crosslinking was quenched by adding 0.2 M glycine for 5 min. Cells were further crosslinked with 3 mM DSG for 40 min, followed by quenching with 0.4 M glycine for 5 min. The crosslinked cell pellets were washed with cold PBS and resuspended in 0.5 mL of ice-cold Hi-C lysis buffer, rotating at 4 °C for 30 min. After an additional wash with 0.5 mL of ice-cold Hi-C lysis buffer, nuclei were resuspended in 0.5% SDS to open the chromatin and incubated at 65 °C for 10 min, followed by quenching with 1% Triton-X-100. Chromatin digestion was carried out overnight at 37 °C by adding 50 μL of 10× NEB Buffer 2400 U of DpnII (NEB #R0543M), and 400 U of DdeI (NEB #R0175L). After digestion, restriction enzymes were inactivated by incubating at 62 °C for 20 min. To fill in the DNA overhangs and add biotin tags, 35 U of DNA polymerase I (Klenow, NEB #M0210L), 10 μL of 1 mM biotin-14-dATP (Thermo Fisher #19524016), and 1 μL of 10 mM dCTP/dGTP/dTTP were added, and the reaction was incubated at 37 °C for 4 h with shaking at 950 rpm for 10 s every 5 min. Chromatin was then ligated with T4 DNA ligase (NEB #M0202M) at 16 °C for 4 h, followed by de-crosslinking with proteinase K treatment. DNA was extracted using phenol/chloroform and precipitated with isopropanol, with glycogen as a carrier. The DNA was sonicated using a Covaris S220 to 300–500 bp fragments, and biotin-labeled DNA was purified using Dynabeads MyOne Streptavidin C1 beads (Thermo Fisher #65002). DNA libraries were prepared using the VAHTS Universal DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina V3 (Vazyme #ND607), and barcoded adaptors were ligated using the TruePrep Index Kit V2 for Illumina (Vazyme #TD202). Size selection was performed with VAHTS DNA Clean Beads (Vazyme #N411). Library concentration was measured using the Qubit dsDNA High-Sensitivity Assay. All libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform in PE150 mode.

Hi-C data processing and statistical analysis

Raw reads were first trimmed using TrimGalore (v0.6.10) with default settings. The data were then processed using a standard Hi-C processing pipeline recommended by the 4D Nucleome Data Portal75 (https://data.4dnucleome.org/resources/data-analysis/hic-processing-pipeline). Briefly, the trimmed reads were first mapped to the mouse genome (mm10) using BWA MEM (v0.7.17-r1188) with the -SP5M option. The mapped reads were then parsed using pairtools (v0.3.0). After data aggregation and normalization, contact matrices in the cool file format were generated using the cooler package76 (v0.8.11). Hi-C heatmaps at selected regions were generated using the cooltools package (v0.5.0).

For A/B compartment analysis, Eigenvalue decomposition was performed on cooler matrices binned at 10 kb resolution using the call-compartments utility from the cooltools package (v0.5.0). The A/B compartment was defined using the first eigenvector values.

For TAD boundary analysis, TAD calling and insulation analysis were performed on cooler matrices binned at 10 kb resolution using the diamond-insulation utility from the cooltools. TAD boundaries with boundary strength above 0.5 were considered high-confidence boundaries and used in subsequent analysis. Chi-square test was used to compare the proportion of ATF4-binding promoters located on changed or stable TAD boundaries.

For loop analysis, mustache77 was used for calling the loops and different loops using p < 0.1 based on cooler matrices binned at 10 kb resolution. Chi-square test was used to compare the proportion of ATF4-binding promoters located on a changed or stable loop anchors. The heatmaps comparing the loop intensity in different regions of multiple matrices were plotted with R package GENOVA78 (https://github.com/dewitlab/GENOVA).
