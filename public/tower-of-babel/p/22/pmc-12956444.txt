Generalized Dystonia in Fahr’s Syndrome Secondary to Idiopathic Hypoparathyroidism: Improvement After Metabolic Correction
Authors: Jun Seok Lee, Sooyeoun You
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Fahr’s syndrome (FS) is a rare neurological disorder characterized by abnormal, bilateral, and symmetrical calcification of the basal ganglia. Unlike Fahr’s disease, which is primary and often genetic, FS represents the secondary form of brain calcification associated with metabolic, infectious, or degenerative conditions. Although FS may present with diverse neurological manifestations, generalized dystonia is exceptionally rare.12 Here, we report a case of generalized dystonia associated with longstanding, undertreated idiopathic hypoparathyroidism.

A 51-year-old male visited the neurology outpatient clinic with a one-month history of truncal turning movements (Supplementary Video 1 in the online-only Data Supplement). Neurological examination revealed a sustained dystonic posture characterized by rightward truncal rotation with mild ipsilateral tilt, which became more evident while standing still and was partially alleviated by voluntary movements. Asymmetric posturing was observed in the upper limbs, more pronounced on the right. Mild dysarthria was also present; however, no dystonic features were observed in the face or lower limbs, and no signs of parkinsonism or ataxia were identified.

The patient had a 10-year history of idiopathic hypoparathyroidism managed with calcium supplementation, without recent medication adjustments. Past medical history was otherwise unremarkable except for hypertension. He had no prior exposure to dopamine receptor-blocking agents, antiemetics, or other medications known to cause tardive dystonia. Laboratory tests showed hypocalcemia, with serum calcium (5.2 mg/dL; reference 8.6–10.0 mg/dL) and ionized calcium (1.28 mmol/L; reference 2.30–2.59 mmol/L), hyperphosphatemia (5.2 mg/dL; reference 2.5–4.5 mg/dL), and markedly reduced parathyroid hormone (2.2 pg/mL; reference 15–65 pg/mL). Brain computed tomography revealed extensive, bilateral, and symmetrical intracranial calcifications involving the centrum semiovale, basal ganglia, thalamus, occipital cortex, subcortical white matter, and dentate nuclei (Fig. 1). In addition, brain MRI performed to evaluate other structural abnormalities revealed no specific findings apart from the intracranial calcifications.

The patient’s advanced age and absence of a family history made genetic causes of dystonia, including DYT1, unlikely, and genetic testing was therefore not pursued; however, a genetic etiology cannot be completely excluded. Nevertheless, the combined clinico-radiological and biochemical findings were consistent with a diagnosis of FS secondary to idiopathic hypoparathyroidism. The patient was treated with intravenous calcium infusion for one week, in combination with oral calcitriol, alfacalcidol, and calcium supplementation. Following treatment, serum calcium normalized, and generalized dystonia gradually improved, although the symptoms did not completely resolve.

FS refers to intracranial calcification resulting from secondary causes, whereas Fahr’s disease represents a primary, often genetic disorder associated with mutations in SLC20A2, PDGFB, and PDGFRB.23 Differentiating between these two entities is clinically important, as FS arises from potentially correctable metabolic derangements. Among these, hypoparathyroidism is the most common cause, leading to calcium–phosphate imbalance and widespread intracranial calcification.3 Clinical manifestations are diverse, and include cognitive and psychiatric symptoms, epilepsy, and movement disorders, such as parkinsonism, chorea, tremor, dystonia, athetosis, orofacial dyskinesia, and cerebellar ataxia has also been reported.34

In chronic hypocalcemia, intracranial calcification has been variably reported, with prevalence ranging from 12% to 74% in patients with idiopathic hypoparathyroidism.135 The mechanisms underlying basal ganglia calcification in hypoparathyroidism are multifactorial. Chronic hypocalcemia and hyperphosphatemia promote calcium-phosphate deposition, while reduced parathyroid hormone levels further impair neuronal calcium homeostasis and facilitate calcium-phosphate precipitation in glial and perivascular tissues.13 We propose a “double-hit” mechanism whereby chronic structural disruption from widespread calcification interacts with acute hypocalcemia-related ionic imbalance. This putative mechanism may partly explain the axial predominance of dystonia and the partial reversibility of symptoms following metabolic correction.136789

Our patient’s dystonia improved after metabolic correction, with diminished truncal twisting and reduced persistence of abnormal posturing. This response is consistent with prior reports demonstrating that calcium and vitamin D supplementation can ameliorate neurological symptoms in FS.35 Recently, Kim et al.9 described a patient with idiopathic hypoparathyroidism who developed unilateral limb dystonia during acute hypocalcemia; the dystonia improved following correction, although other neurological deficits remained unchanged. Taken together, these observations suggest that while certain movement disorders in FS may show partial or even complete improvement with timely metabolic correction, recovery is often limited once metabolic derangements are long-standing and intracranial calcifications are established.

In conclusion, this case expands the clinical spectrum of FS associated with idiopathic hypoparathyroidism, illustrating generalized dystonia as a rare manifestation. It emphasizes the importance of considering metabolic causes in atypical dystonia, with evaluation encompassing not only neuroimaging but also serum electrolytes and parathyroid hormone levels. Early diagnosis and strict metabolic control are crucial to minimizing the risk of irreversible sequelae.
