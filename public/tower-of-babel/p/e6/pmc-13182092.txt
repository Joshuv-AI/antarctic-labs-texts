Factors influencing death anxiety among patients with end-stage chronic diseases: a scoping review
Authors: Yifan Ma, Xuanbo Zhang, Linxi Guo, Yixuan Li, Jing Shi, Yanmei Lang, Wenxia Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsTo systematically identify and map the influencing factors, assessment tools, and interventions for death anxiety among patients with end-stage chronic diseases.DesignA scoping review conducted in accordance with the JBI Global methodology and reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines.Data sourcesA systematic search was conducted across nine databases: CNKI, Wanfang, VIP, CBM, PubMed, CINAHL, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library. The search covered the period from database inception to December 15, 2025.Review methodsGuided by the Population, Concept, and Context (PCC) framework, two researchers independently performed literature screening, data extraction, and quality appraisal using JBI Global Critical Appraisal Tools.ResultsSixty-eight original studies were included. The prevalence of high death anxiety among terminal patients ranged from 25 to 80% (mean = 53.30%). Influencing factors were identified across demographic, socioeconomic, disease-related, and psycho-behavioral dimensions. Commonly used assessment tools included the Templer Death Anxiety Scale (T-DAS), the Death and Dying Distress Scale (DADDS), and the Death Anxiety Questionnaire (DAQ). Current interventions primarily involve Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT), Virtual Reality (VR)-integrated support, Life Review Therapy, Managing Cancer and Living Meaningfully (CALM) therapy, and Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT).ConclusionDeath anxiety is highly prevalent among patients with end-stage chronic diseases and is negatively correlated with quality of life. There is a critical need to implement culturally adapted measurement tools and disease-specific assessments.ImpactThis review highlights the necessity of shifting the care paradigm from "cure-centered" to "dignity-centered". Nursing professionals should prioritize a systematic "early screening–precise assessment–dyadic intervention" approach, focusing on "patient-caregiver" dyadic models to alleviate the fear of death and promote end-of-life quality.No patient or public contributionAs this study is a scoping review based on the synthesis of publicly available literature, no patients, service users, caregivers, or members of the public were involved in the design, conduct, analysis, interpretation, or preparation of this manuscript.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12904-026-02086-0.Keywords: Chronic disease, Terminal care, Death anxiety, Influencing factors, Scoping review

Background

Many chronic diseases last a long time and get worse slowly [1]. Examples include advanced cancer, late-stage heart disease, and severe lung problems (COPD). Even with treatment, these diseases cannot be stopped, and patients eventually enter the final stage of life. Today, more people are getting old and more people are dying from these chronic diseases. This means more patients are facing the end of their lives [2, 3]. In this final stage, patients have a lot of physical pain. They also feel a lot of stress and worry [4, 5]. Death anxiety (DA) is the fear and worry people feel when they think about dying [6]. This fear comes from knowing that life will end and not knowing what will happen next [7]. High death anxiety is very bad for a patient’s health and their quality of life. It can make it hard for nurses to plan care. It can also make patients want to hurt themselves or end their lives [8, 9]. Because of this, NANDA-International (NANDA-I) say that death anxiety is a very important problem to address [10]. Even though this is important, several critical gaps remain in our current understanding. First, while various factors influencing death anxiety have been identified, they are often scattered across different disease types, and we do not know enough about how these factors interact in a complex way. Second, there is a lack of systematic mapping regarding the assessment tools used to measure death anxiety in this specific population. Without identifying which tools are most reliable and appropriate for end-stage patients, clinical evaluation remains inconsistent. Furthermore, although many psychological and spiritual support strategies exist, the specific interventions targeted at reducing death anxiety have not been comprehensively synthesized in terms of their components and effectiveness. Therefore, this scoping review aims to not only identify the influencing factors but also to map the available assessment tools and interventions for death anxiety in patients with end-stage chronic diseases. We will look at many different areas to organize what we know. This will help doctors and nurses create better ways to support patients. It will help patients feel less pain and die with peace and dignity.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a scoping review methodology to map the evidence on death anxiety (DA) among patients with end-stage chronic diseases. The review was conducted in accordance with the JBI Global (formerly the Joanna Briggs Institute) manual [11] for evidence synthesis. The study protocol was prospectively registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF) (Registration: https://archive.org/details/osf-registrations-fh5y3-v1). The findings are reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines to ensure systematic rigor and transparency.

Research questions

Guided by the PCC (Population, Concept, Context) framework, this review addressed the following questions:

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion Criteria: 1. Population: Patients diagnosed with advanced cancer or non-cancerous progressive diseases (e.g., cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, chronic respiratory diseases). 2. Concept: Studies focusing on the prevalence, influencing factors, assessment tools, or interventions related to death anxiety. 3. Context: End-of-life or terminal care settings. 4.Study Type: Original studies, including cross-sectional, longitudinal, and experimental research. 5. Language: Peer-reviewed publications in Chinese or English.

Exclusion Criteria: 1. Records with unavailable full texts. 2. Conference abstracts, reviews, editorials, and purely qualitative studies (excluding the quantitative components of mixed-methods research). 3. Studies focusing on healthcare professionals, caregivers, or patients not in the terminal/end-stage of their disease.

Search strategy

A systematic search was performed across nine electronic databases: CNKI, Wanfang, VIP, CBM, PubMed, CINAHL, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library.

For an English language database, with Pubmed as an example, the retrieval formula was: ("death"[MeSH Terms] OR "death"[All Fields] OR "deaths"[All Fields]) AND "anxiety"[MeSH Major Topic] AND ("chronic disease"[MeSH Terms] OR ("chronic"[All Fields] AND "disease"[All Fields]) OR "chronic disease"[All Fields] OR (("disease progression"[MeSH Terms] OR ("disease"[All Fields] AND "progression"[All Fields]) OR "disease progression"[All Fields] OR "progression"[All Fields] OR "progress"[All Fields] OR "progressed"[All Fields] OR "progresses"[All Fields] OR "progressing"[All Fields] OR "progressions"[All Fields] OR "progressive"[All Fields] OR "progressively"[All Fields] OR "progressives"[All Fields]) AND ("disease"[MeSH Terms] OR "disease"[All Fields] OR "diseases"[All Fields] OR "disease s"[All Fields] OR "diseased"[All Fields])) OR ("terminal"[All Fields] OR "terminal s"[All Fields] OR "terminally"[All Fields] OR "terminals"[All Fields] OR "terminate"[All Fields] OR "terminated"[All Fields] OR "terminates"[All Fields] OR "terminating"[All Fields] OR "termination"[All Fields] OR "terminations"[All Fields] OR "terminator"[All Fields] OR "terminators"[All Fields]) OR "End-stage"[All Fields] OR "neoplasms"[MeSH Major Topic]).

For a Chinese language database, with CNKI as an example, the retrieval formula was: SU = Death anxiety AND All Fields = Chronic disease + Progressive disease + Terminal + End-stage + Cancer.

The search period ranged from the establishment of the databases until December 15, 2025. The full search strategy can be found in Supplementary Material.

Study selection and data extraction

Literature was managed using Zotero for deduplication. Two researchers, trained in evidence-based nursing, independently screened records based on the predefined criteria. Initial screening was based on titles and abstracts, followed by a full-text review. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third expert. Extracted data included: general information (author, year, country), study characteristics (population, sample size, design), and core findings (prevalence of DA, influencing factors, assessment tools, and intervention protocols).

Quality appraisal

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the JBI Global Critical Appraisal Tools for various designs (e.g., 8 items for cross-sectional studies, 13 items for RCTs). Two researchers performed the scoring independently to ensure the reliability of the evidence. While methodological quality was assessed using JBI Global tools, no minimum score threshold was established for exclusion. This approach was chosen to ensure a comprehensive mapping of all existing evidence regarding death anxiety in end-stage patients, regardless of study quality. However, the appraisal results (Tables 2, 3, 4 and 5) were used to weigh the strength of the evidence during the synthesis of findings.

Results

Literature search result

The initial systematic search yielded a total of 2, 240 records. After removing duplicates, 962 records remained. Following a preliminary screening of titles and abstracts, 523 articles were identified for further evaluation. After a full-text review, 455 articles were excluded for not meeting the predefined inclusion criteria. Consequently, 68 original studies were included in the final analysis, comprising 33 Chinese publications [12–44] and 36 English publications [6, 9, 45–77].Regarding the study designs of the included literature, there were 61 cross-sectional studies [6, 9, 12–23, 25–33, 35–39, 41–64, 66–68, 70, 73–77], 5 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) [24, 34, 40, 65, 72], 1 cohort study [69], and 1 quasi-experimental study [71] (Fig. 1).

Methodological quality appraisal results

Depending on the specific design of each study, the corresponding JBI Global checklists—including those for cross-sectional studies, case–control studies, and cohort studies—were utilized. Each included article underwent a rigorous, item-by-item evaluation. Studies with unacceptably low quality were excluded from this review to ensure the reliability of the findings. Since the assessment criteria vary across study designs, the most appropriate JBI Global tool was selected and applied according to the specific methodology of each included record.

Consequences of death anxiety in patients with end-stage chronic diseases

Based on data from 25 studies [12, 14–17, 20–22, 24, 25, 36, 37, 40–42, 45, 48, 54, 64, 66–68, 70, 74, 77], this report systematically analyzes the consequences of death anxiety. Research indicates that death anxiety exerts extensive and profound negative effects on both patients and their family caregivers. The impact on patients encompasses the following seven dimensions: Quality of life [12, 14, 15, 20, 25, 36, 37, 40, 45, 48, 64, 67, 68, 74, 77], Mental health [12, 15–17, 20–22, 24, 25, 36, 41, 42, 54, 66, 70, 74], Cognitive function [12], Social function [12], Treatment adherence [17, 24, 77], Physiological impact [17, 24, 25, 64] and Social relationships [74]. These aspects were referenced a total of 42 times. the impact on family caregivers is limited to only two areas: Quality of life [45] and Mental health [41].

The consequences of death anxiety are most prominently manifested in QoL and Mental Health. A significant negative correlation exists between death anxiety and QoL; as anxiety levels escalate, they consistently predict poorer functional outcomes and reduced life satisfaction. Mentally, death anxiety fosters a vicious cycle of reinforcing negative emotions, acting as a catalyst for psychological distress and, in extreme cases, increasing the risk of self-harm and suicide. Notably, this psychological burden possesses a "contagious" quality, where the anxiety of family caregivers can inadvertently aggravate the patient’s own distress. Beyond these primary concerns, death anxiety exerts a broad, multidimensional influence across other spheres. It impairs cognitive and social functions, disrupts treatment adherence, and triggers adverse physiological impacts. Collectively, these factors form a complex web of physical and social erosion that stems from the underlying psychological pressure of mortality salience.

Assessment tools for death anxiety

Among the 68 studies, a diverse range of assessment tools was found, with Templer’s Death Anxiety Scale (TDAS), the Death and Dying Distress Scale (DADDS), and the Death Anxiety Questionnaire (DAQ) being the most prominent. Although some studies relied on country-specific instruments, these are often limited by their geographical applicability.

Templer’s death anxiety scale

The Templer Death Anxiety Scale (TDAS), also known as the DAS, was developed by American psychologist Donald Templer in 1970. It remains the most widely used and frequently cited classic instrument in the field of death anxiety research. Based on Templer’s premise that death anxiety is a universal human experience, the scale is designed to measure an individual’s conscious perception of anxiety and fear regarding death.

The TDAS comprises 15 items. While originally designed as a unidimensional scale (measuring overall death anxiety), subsequent cross-cultural studies have generally identified four underlying dimensions: fear of death, fear of pain and disease, the sense of time, and the afterlife/unknown. To minimize "response set" bias (the tendency to automatically select "True"), the scale incorporates both positively and negatively worded items. It employs a dichotomous "True/False" scoring format, with total scores ranging from 0 to 15. The average score typically falls between 6 and 7; scores of 8 to 9 or higher indicate significant death anxiety, while scores exceeding 12 suggest severe fear that may impair daily functioning. Although designed as a general instrument, the TDAS has been extensively validated in specific populations, such as patients with chronic diseases and advanced cancer, demonstrating high clinical applicability.

Death and dying distress scale

The Death and Dying Distress Scale (DADDS) is a clinical assessment tool designed specifically to evaluate psychological distress related to death and the dying process in patients with advanced cancer or other life-limiting illnesses. Developed and validated around 2014 by a research team at the Princess Margaret Cancer Centre in Toronto, Canada, the DADDS differs from traditional death anxiety scales by focusing specifically on the unique psychological burdens faced by individuals approaching the end of life. The most commonly used version of the DADDS consists of 15 items, typically categorized into two core dimensions:

Finitude: Focuses on the brevity of life, missed opportunities, uncertainty about the future, the sense of being a burden to loved ones, and regrets over unfulfilled wishes.

Dying: Focuses on specific concerns regarding the actual event of death, such as the fear of dying alone, pain and suffering during death, and anxiety regarding the specific timing of death.

The scale utilizes a 6-point Likert scoring system to assess the level of distress caused by these thoughts over the past two weeks:

0: No distress

0: No distress

1: Very mild distress

1: Very mild distress

2: Mild distress

2: Mild distress

3: Moderate distress

3: Moderate distress

4: Severe distress

4: Severe distress

5: Extreme distress

5: Extreme distress

The total score ranges from 0 to 75, with higher scores indicating more severe death-related psychological distress. Generally, a total score exceeding 45 indicates clinically significant severe death anxiety, suggesting a potential need for psychological intervention.

The DADDS enables healthcare professionals to rapidly identify patients experiencing extreme fear or distress regarding death, allowing for the detection of high-risk groups and the timely provision of psychological support. It is frequently used to evaluate the effectiveness of palliative care (hospice care) or psychotherapeutic interventions—such as CALM therapy(Managing Cancer and Living Meaningfully)—in alleviating death anxiety. By analyzing specific scale items, clinicians can gain a concrete understanding of a patient’s specific fears—whether it be pain, burdening their family, or the suddenness of death—thereby enabling more targeted support.

The DADDS has been translated into multiple languages, including Chinese, German, Spanish, and Italian. Research indicates that the Chinese version (DADDS-C) demonstrates good reliability and validity among advanced cancer patients in China, making it one of the recommended professional scales in the domestic field of palliative care.

Death anxiety questionnaire

The Death Anxiety Questionnaire (DAQ) is a self-report scale developed by American psychologists H. Conte, M. Weiner, and R. Plutchik in 1982. It is primarily designed to measure an individual’s level of fear and worry regarding both death itself and the process of dying. Compared to Templer’s Death Anxiety Scale (DAS), which is commonly used in clinical settings, the DAQ emphasizes a multidimensional approach, offering more specific insights into the distinct sources of an individual’s anxiety. The questionnaire consists of 15 items categorized into four core dimensions derived from factor analysis: Fear of the Unknown, Fear of Suffering, Fear of Loneliness, and Fear of Personal Extinction. The DAQ utilizes a concise and intuitive 3-point scoring system: 0 (Not at all), 1 (Somewhat), and 2 (Very much). Total scores range from 0 to 30, with higher scores indicating higher levels of death anxiety. The scale is applicable to adults across a broad age spectrum (from 30 to over 80 years old) and demonstrates strong stability across gender and age groups. Furthermore, the DAQ exhibits good reliability and validity, with an internal consistency coefficient (Cronbach’s alpha) typically exceeding 0.83 and high test–retest reliability.

Interventions for death anxiety

Acceptance and commitment therapy

Chen Furong [24] employed Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) to address death anxiety in cancer patients. The intervention consisted of weekly, face-to-face individual sessions lasting 35 to 45 min over a period of eight weeks. It was delivered by qualified researchers and psychological counselors who had received systematic training in ACT. Results demonstrated that ACT significantly reduced death anxiety in cancer patients (P < 0.001), with effects sustained for up to three months post-intervention.

Virtual reality combined with psychological intervention

Cao Huahua [40] implemented a three-month intervention combining virtual reality (VR) with psychological support for breast cancer patients. The results demonstrated a significant reduction in death anxiety, with scores across all four dimensions of the T-DAS (affect, time awareness, cognition, and stress & pain) being significantly lower than those of the control group. Additionally, quality of life improved significantly; scores for functional domains and overall quality of life were higher than the control group, while symptom domain scores were lower. Patient satisfaction was also significantly higher, reaching 92% compared to 68% in the routine intervention group.

Life review therapy

Chen Shujuan [34] employed Life Review Therapy to investigate its efficacy in alleviating death anxiety among patients with malignant tumors. The intervention consisted of six one-on-one, semi-structured in-depth interviews, each lasting 1–3 h and conducted every other day, administered by a Master of Nursing with oncology experience and National Level 2 Psychological Counselor certification. The results demonstrated that T-DAS scores in the experimental group were significantly lower than those in the control group at 2 and 30 days post-intervention (P < 0.01). Furthermore, patients exhibited a cognitive shift regarding death—moving from ‘dread’ to ‘acceptance, ’ and from ‘avoidance’ to ‘cherishing the present’—which led to more active treatment participation, greater emotional stability, and improved family communication.

Managing cancer and living meaningfully and supportive psychotherapeutic intervention

Oberth [65] assessed the impact of short-term psychotherapy—specifically CALM and SPI—on death anxiety in advanced cancer patients. Findings revealed that both the structured CALM approach and the non-specific SPI effectively lowered death anxiety. These therapeutic benefits became apparent within three months and were sustained for up to six months.

Cognitive behavioral therapy

Moradi [71] investigated the efficacy of Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) on death anxiety and depressive symptoms in patients with heart failure. The intervention consisted of nine sessions, delivered either face-to-face or online (via the Skyroom platform), with each session lasting 45–60 min, three times a week for three weeks. All sessions were administered by psychological nursing staff trained in CBT. The results demonstrated that CBT effectively reduced death anxiety and depression levels in heart failure patients, with the effects remaining significant at the one-month post-intervention follow-up.

Illness perception-based educational protocol

Babaei Vahed [72] implemented an educational intervention based on illness perception to investigate its impact on death anxiety in patients with heart failure. The intervention consisted of three 30-min sessions of face-to-face, individualized education. The results indicated that illness perception-based education significantly improved the patients’ illness cognition and reduced their death anxiety.

Discussion

Review findings show a prevalence of high death anxiety in end-stage chronic disease patients ranging from 25 to 80% (mean: 53.30%), highlighting the extensive heterogeneity across disease groups. While cancer patients often experience heightened anxiety due to pain, body image changes, and the stigma of ’cancer as a death sentence, ’ patients with non-malignant conditions like heart failure and COPD are driven more by the fear of breathlessness and functional loss. Beyond clinical factors, cultural context plays a critical role. In Chinese culture, where death is a taboo subject, the resulting silence can intensify a patient’s cognitive distortion and loneliness. Therefore, nurses should acknowledge that death anxiety triggers differ by disease trajectory, requiring dynamic clinical assessments that are sensitive to both the specific condition and the patient’s cultural background.

Death anxiety is determined by complex demographic, socioeconomic, and psychological factors. Protective factors such as self-esteem and meaning in life can significantly reduce anxiety by fostering a sense of value and control. However, anxiety can also be transmitted from caregivers to patients via dyadic coping mechanisms. Notably, for patients with financial or familial burdens, death anxiety often reflects a fear of ’social death’—leaving responsibilities unfulfilled—rather than death itself. Therefore, nursing interventions should focus on the patient-caregiver dyad, prioritizing families with limited psychological and economic resources.

There is a growing trend in death anxiety assessment shifting from ’universal’ to ’specific’ tools. While Templer’s Death Anxiety Scale (T-DAS) remains the most widely used instrument due to its high generalizability and stable psychometric properties, its dichotomous scoring system limits its ability to capture subtle emotional fluctuations. Furthermore, as a generic tool, T-DAS may be insufficient in addressing the unique ’dying distress’ of terminally ill patients. The Death and Dying Distress Scale (DADDS) bridges this gap by focusing specifically on patients’ concerns regarding ’finitude’ and the dying process, offering higher clinical pertinence.

Given the significant cultural disparities between China and the West regarding death taboos, traditional family values, and religious beliefs, future research should prioritize the development of culturally sensitive, indigenous assessment tools. These tools must align with the Chinese context—incorporating concepts such as ’a good death within the family’ and ’reincarnation’—to enhance the cultural validity of assessments.

This review elucidates the bidirectional vicious cycle between death anxiety and patients’ quality of life, depression, and general anxiety. Beyond serving as a source of psychological distress, death anxiety possesses a physiologically and socially contagious nature: it can compromise treatment adherence, impede the implementation of advance care planning, and even exacerbate the psychological burden on family caregivers through ’emotional contagion.’ This phenomenon of ’double anxiety’ suggests that nursing interventions should not be restricted to the individual patient. Instead, a ’patient-caregiver’ dyadic coping approach is imperative to break this closed loop of anxiety by facilitating open communication about death within the family unit.

Despite the comprehensive review presented in this study, several limitations remain. The included studies were predominantly cross-sectional, lacking a longitudinal tracking of the dynamic trajectory of death anxiety. Furthermore, intervention studies have largely focused on cancer, with insufficient attention paid to patients with non-malignant end-stage diseases, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and renal failure. Future clinical inquiries should prioritize longitudinal studies to explore the dynamic evolution of death anxiety across different stages, from diagnosis to end-of-life. Additionally, incorporating narrative medicine approaches could facilitate the completion of ’life narratives’ during the final stages of life, thereby promoting a peaceful death.

Conclusion

Based on a scoping review of 68 studies, this research outlines the prevalence, predictors, measurements, and treatments for death anxiety (DA) in patients with end-stage chronic diseases. The findings reveal a high DA prevalence (25%–80%; mean 53.30%), which exacerbates physical and mental decline, lowers quality of life, and impedes palliative care, potentially leading to self-harm or suicide. While therapies like ACT, Life Review, VR, and CBT show promise, evidence-based protocols remain limited. Future nursing practice should prioritize a systematic ’early screening–precise assessment–dyadic intervention’ approach and expand longitudinal studies on non-cancer populations. Such measures are essential to shift the care paradigm from ’cure-centered’ to ’dignity-centered, ’ ensuring patients a peaceful end-of-life experience.
