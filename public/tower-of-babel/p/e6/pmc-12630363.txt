Effects of interventions to reduce clinical placement-related psychological distress among nursing students: A systematic review with meta-analysis
Authors: Ka Yan Ko, Jed Ray Montayre, Pak Lung Chiu, Timothy Kam Hung Lai, Kitty Chan, Harry Ho Tin Chan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClinical placements are essential in nursing education but often cause significant psychological distress, affecting students' well-being, learning outcomes, and patient safety. This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluate the effectiveness of interventions aimed at reducing psychological distress during clinical placements for nursing students.MethodsA comprehensive search was conducted across databases including CINAHL, PsycINFO, PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, ERIC, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, and Wan Fang Data. The review adhered to PRISMA guidelines and used Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) tools for quality appraisal. Meta-analysis employed a random-effects model for outcomes reported in at least three studies.ResultsFrom 2,680 citations, 14 studies met inclusion criteria, comprising five randomized controlled trials and nine quasi-experimental designs. Quality appraisal indicated low to moderate risk of bias. Most interventions were delivered before clinical placements and focused on stress and anxiety coping strategies (n=7), knowledge and psychomotor skills reinforcement (n=5), or mixed approaches (n=2). Coping strategies included cognitive-behavioral therapy, art therapy, and mindfulness. Outcomes primarily assessed stress and anxiety. Meta-analysis showed that pre-clinical interventions significantly reduced stress (SMD = -0.54, 95 % CI: -0.92 to -0.17, p = 0.005) and anxiety (SMD = -0.65, 95 % CI: -0.92 to -0.38, p < 0.001), with moderate effect sizes but high heterogeneity due to varied measurement tools.ConclusionsCurrent interventions effectively alleviate clinical placement-related psychological distress in nursing students despite outcome variability. Future research should incorporate theoretical frameworks, systematically assess student stressors, and standardize assessment tools. Additionally, interventions should address clinical mentors’ needs and provide adequate support to enhance their role in nursing education.Registration: The review protocol was registered on OSF (Open Science Framework) with the following registration number: DOI: https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/QWXF6.

Introduction

Clinical placements constitute a critical component of nursing education, facilitating the integration of theoretical knowledge with practical skills essential for delivering safe and effective patient care. However, clinical placements are also recognized as significant sources of stress for nursing students who report higher stress levels than peers in other healthcare fields (Cheng et al., 2023, Gülnar et al., 2024). Stress refers to the body’s response to external pressures, while psychological distress refers to the multifactorial, unpleasant experience characterized by symptoms like anxiety and depression, which is a manifestation of excessive or unmanaged stress (Barry et al., 2020). Approximately 70 % of nursing students experience moderate to severe stress during clinical practice (Salvarani et al., 2020), caused by demanding coursework, long hours, challenging environments, fear of mistakes, and interpersonal conflicts (Alkaissi et al., 2022, Wu et al., 2021). Moreover, psychological distress in nursing students may be linked to perceived clinical incompetence, including inadequate confidence in performing clinical skills and making clinical judgments (Wu et al., 2021, Sonmez et al., 2023). Excessive stress can lead to psychological and physical symptoms that harm student well-being and patient safety (Wu et al., 2021). While previous reviews highlight benefits of mindfulness and coping skills training for general stress reduction (Aloufi et al., 2021, Ji et al., 2024), there is limited synthesized evidence on interventions specifically targeting psychological distress during clinical placements. This review aims to evaluate the effectiveness of such interventions to inform the development of tailored, evidence-based strategies that enhance nursing students’ well-being and clinical learning outcomes.

Objectives

There are three study objectives, included: (i) to examine the effectiveness of the existing interventions for reducing clinical placement psychological distress among nursing students, (ii) to appraise the quality of existing evidence, and (iii) to identify gaps and inform future research.

Methods

This review title and inclusion criteria were developed using the PICO framework, to ensure a focused and systematic search strategy aligning with the review objectives.

Search strategy

To ensure a comprehensive literature search for this systematic review, multiple electronic databases were searched, including CINAHL, PsycINFO, PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and the Educational Resources Information Centre (ERIC), China National Knowledge Infrastructure and Wan Fang Data, chosen for their coverage of healthcare, psychology, and education. The search combined MeSH terms and keywords such as “psychological distress,” “stress,” “anxiety,” “clinical placement,” “intervention,” “nursing,” and “students.” Titles, abstracts, and index terms were screened independently by two researchers. Manual reference checks of included studies and relevant systematic reviews were also conducted to identify additional eligible articles. The literature search covered publications from the inception up to the year 2024 to include all relevant studies available to date.

Eligibility criteria

This review included the studies meeting the following criteria: (i) published in English or Chinese; (ii) experimental or quasi-experimental study which evaluate the effects of interventions reducing clinical placement related psychological distress; (iii) interventions that implemented in any time of the clinical placement. In this review, psychological distress is viewed as a broad and multifactorial concept that includes unpleasant emotional responses to the external or internal pressures (Cohen et al., 2016). This review primarily focuses on psychological distress outcomes, including stress, anxiety, and/or depression as manifestations of intense distress. Grey literature such as dissertations, policies, guidelines, or government reports were also included to minimise publication bias. Excluded studies with (i) interventions that not focused on clinical placements; (ii) interventions with no psychological distress-relieving components, or (iii) not targeting nursing students.

Data extracting

Data management and screening were conducted using the systematic review management software EndNote, to organize and track studies throughout the review process. Two researchers independently extracted data to ensure accuracy and reliability. No missing data were identified during the data extraction process. Key information from the included studies was compiled into a summary table, capturing essential details such as authors, publication year, study location, study design, population characteristics, intervention features, outcome measures, and results.

Critical appraisal of the evidence

To appraise the quality of the evidence, the JBI (Joanna Briggs Institute) framework was used to evaluate the methodological quality of the selected evidence (Joanna Briggs Institute, 2024). Specifically, the JBI-appraisal tool for RCTs (13 items) and the JBI-appraisal tool for non-randomized intervention studies (9 items) were employed. Two researchers, working independently, conducted a critical review of the evidence. Discrepancies on data extraction and quality appraisal were discussed, and the third researcher was consulted to reach a consensus in any cases of disagreement.

Synthesis of the results

The results of the critical appraisal of the selected articles are presented in various tables, clearly indicating the quality ranking of each study, alongside a summary of study characteristics. Each article was reviewed for additional details, and the literature was thematically analyzed by study design, methodology, intervention development, components, outcome types, and measurement tools. Data were synthesized and analyzed using Review Manager 5.3. For outcomes reported in more than three studies, meta-analyses calculated standardized mean differences (SMDs) with 95 % confidence intervals (CIs). Given the substantial heterogeneity observed across the included studies, included the study populations, interventions, and measurement tools, a random-effects model was employed to provide more generalizable estimates (Veroniki et al., 2016). Effect sizes were classified as negligible or very small (< 0.20), small (0.20 to < 0.50), medium (0.50 to < 0.80), and large (≥ 0.80) with significance set at p < 0.05 (Lakens, 2013). Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic; values >50 % with p < 0.05 indicated significant heterogeneity. For high heterogeneity, subgroup or sensitivity analyses to explore the potential sources. Publication bias was evaluated via funnel plots and Egger’s test when ≥10 studies were available on each outcome (Cumpston et al., 2019).

Protocol and registration

This review was designed, conducted and reported following JBI manual for evidence synthesis (Aromataris et al., 2024). To enhance the rigour and clarity of the current systematic review, the PRISMA 2020 statement was followed (Page et al., 2021). The review protocol was registered on OSF (Open Science Framework) with the following registration number: DOI: https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/QWXF6.

Results

Study selection

The literature search yielded a total of 9,418 citations. After the removal of duplicates, 2,680 remained. Titles and abstracts were screened to eliminate those involving irrelevant topics, non-nursing students, interventions unrelated to clinical placements, and outcomes not related to psychological distress. In the end, 14 articles met the criteria for inclusion. The screening process is presented by a PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1.).

PRISMA flow diagram of the study selection process.

General characteristics of the selected evidence

This review encompasses 14 studies conducted across a diverse range of countries and regions, namely, Australia (Olasoji et al., 2020), Spain (Lepiani-Díaz et al., 2023, Ortega et al., 2021), Iran (Mojarrab et al., 2020, Shahsavari et al., 2017), Turkey (Gülnar et al., 2024, Pelit‐Aksu et al., 2021, Torlak et al., 2024), Sweden (Terp et al., 2019), Scotland (Jones and Johnston, 2000), China (Wang et al., 2022, Lam et al., 2024), and Korea (Jang and Lee, 2016). Only a limited number of studies were conducted in Asia. The age of the literature included in this review spans from 2000 to 2024. The study designs consisted of five randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and nine quasi-experimental studies. Eleven studies incorporated comparison groups, while three did not. Sample sizes ranged from 52 to 177 participants, with a predominant representation of female students. With regard to the demographics of the participants, most were first-year students (n=6), with additional representation from final-year (n=2), second-year (n=4), and third to fourth-year students (n=2).

Characteristics of the interventions

The included studies utilized a diverse array of pre-clinical interventions designed to prepare nursing students for their clinical placement. Among these, seven interventions were implemented before the clinical placement, two were implemented both before and during the clinical placement, and five were implemented exclusively during the clinical placement. The interventions varied in duration and format. In duration, they ranged from one to 16 sessions. In format, they included scenario-based high-fidelity simulation workshops, clinical training seminars or programmes focusing on clinical skills and patient safety, telerehabilitation sessions, and online programmes.

Several interventions emphasized coping with clinical stress or anxiety (n=8) by introducing relaxation techniques such as breathing techniques, music relaxation, and progressive muscle relaxation exercises. Cognitive Behavioural Therapy was another common theme that was found in the included studies (n=3) (Gülnar et al., 2024, Ortega et al., 2021, Terp et al., 2019), with programmes integrating lectures on Cognitive Behavioural Therapy, art therapy, and mindfulness practices. Some interventions also focused on knowledge enrichment and psychomotor skills training (n=6), as gaining clinical knowledge and skills helps build students' confidence and reduces stress and anxiety during their clinical placements (Shahsavari et al., 2017). A summary of the interventions and outcomes is presented in Table 1. Most approaches aim to strengthen nursing students' emotional resilience and clinical skill competencies, thereby ensuring their readiness to effectively navigate clinical placements.

Characteristics of the outcomes

The included studies reported a diverse range of outcomes related to nursing students’ psychological well-being and clinical competencies. Psychological outcomes primarily focused on stress (n=10) and anxiety (n=7), with additional measures examining depression, burnout, resilience, self-esteem, self-efficacy and therapeutic engagement. These outcomes were assessed using validated instruments such as the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS) for stress, the State and Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) for anxiety, and the Depression Anxiety Stress Scale (DASS-21) for emotional well-being. Burnout and resilience were evaluated using the Oldenburg Burnout Inventory - Student (OLBI-S) and the General Self-Efficacy Scale, respectively. Additionally, the KEZKAK questionnaire, Perceived Stress Scale for Nursing Students (PSSNS), and Burnout Measure Short Version (BMS) provided insights into stressors, anxiety, and burnout specific to nursing students. Psychological distress and clinical competence are interconnected; enhancing clinical competence may help mitigate psychological distress in nursing students during clinical practice (Shahsavari et al., 2017, Karahan Okuroğlu et al., 2021). Several studies included outcomes related to clinical competence, such as placement preparedness and clinical performance, assessed using tools like the researcher-developed Clinical Self-Efficacy tool, which measures students’ perceptions of their ability to perform clinical tasks, and the Nursing Process Evaluation Form (NPEF), which evaluates students’ understanding and application of the nursing process (Karahan Okuroğlu et al., 2021). Critical appraisal of the evidence

The overall risk of bias across the studies ranged from low to moderate. Among the nine quasi-experimental studies, three lacked a comparison group. A majority (n=10) did not include a follow-up period, limiting assessment of long-term intervention effects. Nonetheless, all studies employed reliable outcome measures and appropriate statistical analyses, with clear definition of outcome variables. Appraisal of the five randomized controlled trials (RCTs) revealed methodological concerns, including unclear allocation concealment and ambiguous blinding of participants and outcome assessors. Additionally, potential response bias arising from power dynamics between students and educators was overlooked in all studies, raising concerns about the validity of findings. Meta-analysis results

The treatment effects of pre-clinical interventions were pooled for two outcomes: stress and anxiety. Other outcomes, including burnout, resilience, and self-esteem, were reported in fewer than three studies and thus were excluded from the meta-analysis but described narratively. Funnel plots to assess publication bias was conducted only for stress, which included the minimum requirement of 10 studies, one outlier presented that suggests possible publication bias or heterogeneity.

For stress, the pooled standardized mean difference (SMD) was -0.54 (95 % CI: -0.92 to -0.17, p = 0.005; n = 1205), reflecting a statistically significant moderate effect in reducing stress. However, substantial heterogeneity was observed (I² = 90 %). Sensitivity analysis excluding the outlier study by Wang’s study (Wang et al., 2022) reduced the SMD to -0.31 (95 % CI: -0.44 to -0.17) and markedly decreased heterogeneity (I² = 15 %). Although the effect size diminished after excluding the outlier, the intervention sustained statistically significance, confirming its continued effectiveness in reducing stress (Lakens, 2013).

For anxiety, interventions also showed a significant reduction compared to controls, with a moderate-to-high effect size (SMD = -0.65, 95 % CI: -0.92 to -0.38, p < 0.001; n = 940). Substantial heterogeneity was present (I² = 74 %), but funnel plot analysis was not feasible with fewer than 10 studies. The forest plots summarizing these results are presented in Fig. 2, Fig. 3.

Subgroup analysis

Two subgroup analyses were conducted. Interventions focusing on stress and anxiety coping strategies significantly reduced nursing students’ stress levels, showing a moderate effect size (SMD = -0.47, 95 % CI: -0.66 to -0.28, p < 0.001), while clinical skills and knowledge enhancement interventions produced a smaller but still significant effect (SMD = -0.19, 95 % CI: -0.35 to -0.04, p = 0.01). The difference between the effects of these approach was statistically significant (p = 0.03) with low heterogeneity (I² = 15 %), suggesting a consistent effect across all studies included. Regarding intervention timing, interventions delivered during clinical placement tended to have a larger impact (SMD = -0.51, 95 % CI: -0.82 to -0.19, p = 0.002) on reducing stress compared to those delivered before or before and during placement (SMD = -0.25, 95 % CI: -0.38 to -0.11, p < 0.001), although this difference between these two-subgroup effect was not statistically significant (p = 0.14). Narrative summary of other outcomes

Variability was observed across the measured outcomes. Regarding psychological outcomes, in addition to anxiety and stress, several studies assessed depression (n=2) (Lam et al., 2024, Jones and Johnston, 2000), burnout (n=2) (Pelit‐Aksu et al., 2021, Lam et al., 2024), self-efficacy (n=4) (Terp et al., 2019, Wang et al., 2022, Lam et al., 2024, Shahsavari et al., 2017), and self-esteem (n=1) (Terp et al., 2019). While two studies examining burnout demonstrated a reduction, but no significant changes in depression levels were reported. Beyond psychological measures, other outcomes included clinical performance (n=4) (Mojarrab et al., 2020, Torlak et al., 2024, Jang and Lee, 2016, Shahsavari et al., 2017) and stress coping and management competency (n=3) (Gülnar et al., 2024, Terp et al., 2019, Jones and Johnston, 2000). Findings on clinical performance were mixed, with some studies reporting improvement and others finding no significant change. In contrast, all studies evaluating stress coping and management competency consistently reported improvements.

Discussion

This review synthesized findings from 14 studies to evaluate the effectiveness of interventions aimed at mitigating psychological distress among nursing students during clinical placements. The interventions primarily fell into two categories: psychological coping strategies and skills-based training. Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) and mindfulness-based approaches are commonly used coping strategies that effectively address emotional challenges and foster resilience. Mindfulness interventions, such as mind-body practices and relaxation exercises, help reduce overwhelming emotions by stabilizing autonomic nervous system responses and improving emotional regulation (Mojarrab et al., 2020, Taylor et al., 2011). Meanwhile, skills-based training focusing on psychomotor development and theoretical knowledge boosts students’ confidence in clinical tasks, helping students to feel better prepared and reducing anxiety when come to patient care (Lepiani-Díaz et al., 2023, Shahsavari et al., 2017). Taken together, integrating psychological coping strategies with skills-based training may create a synergistic effect, supporting nursing students’ psychological well-being while enhancing clinical competence to optimize learning and patient care outcomes.

The timing of intervention delivery is a critical factor in managing nursing students’ stress and anxiety for the clinical placement (Lepiani-Díaz et al., 2023, Cruz Araújo et al., 2022). Our subgroup analysis showed that interventions delivered during clinical placement had a greater impact, highlighting the importance of timely and ongoing support for students when they face real clinical challenges. Conversely, skills and knowledge enhancement interventions administered before clinical placement also play a vital role by increasing students’ confidence, preparedness, and readiness, thereby helping to alleviate anxiety (Jang and Lee, 2016, Lam et al., 2024, Shahsavari et al., 2017).

Despite the overall effectiveness of the reviewed interventions, few studies explicitly identified students’ specific stressors prior to intervention implementation, and many lacked a guiding theoretical framework. Notably, only two studies explicitly referenced theoretical frameworks to support their interventions (Jones and Johnston, 2000, Lam et al., 2024). Lazarus and Folkman’s (1984) Stress and Coping Theory offers a valuable framework for understanding interventions aimed at reducing psychological distress in nursing students. This theory outlines a cognitive appraisal process with two key components: a primary and secondary appraisal (Lazarus and Folkman, 1984). Primary appraisal, where students assess the significance of clinical experiences and identify stressors such as unfamiliar environments or uncertainty; and secondary appraisal, where they evaluate available coping resources. This review revealed that most interventions focus on enhancing secondary appraisal, training students with coping strategies through mindfulness, mind-body techniques, muscle relaxation, and CBT to reduce stress and anxiety. Only two studies in this review (Lepiani-Díaz et al., 2023, Wang et al., 2022) explicitly identified the specific “stressors” of students. Therefore, to develop targeted and effective pre-clinical interventions is essential to identify students’ unique stressors and based the intervention development on a solid theoretical foundation.

Strengths and limitations

This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluate interventions aimed at reducing psychological distress among nursing students during clinical placements. The application of JBI frameworks and guidelines strengthens the review’s structure and reliability. The heterogeneity of outcomes highlighted the need for standardized measures and reduced publication bias in future research.

Limitations include the potential publication bias reflected by the funnel plot, as only published studies were available for inclusion and no grey literature could be identified. This may result in an overrepresentation of studies reporting effective interventions and underrepresentation of studies with neutral or non-significant results. Consequently, the pooled effect size may be overestimated, and the generalizability of findings may be limited. This review only included English and Chinese studies, potentially excluding relevant research in other languages. The variability in outcome measures, restricted the conduction of meta-analysis for secondary outcomes, and the lack of response bias consideration hindered the aggregation of findings and the ability to draw definitive conclusions. Furthermore, the long-term sustainability of interventions remains unclear due to limited follow-up data.

Implications

This systematic review provides valuable insights and recommendations for nursing educators, researchers, and policymakers to better support nursing students during clinical placements.

Nursing Educators

Clinical placements are a significant source of stress and psychological distress for nursing students, posing a distinct challenge from general academic stress. Consequently, it is vital to seamlessly integrate targeted interventions into nursing curricula. The findings of this review provide valuable insights for clinical mentors, emphasizing the importance of ongoing support throughout clinical placements. To effectively support students, clinical mentors should be equipped with the skills to early recognising signs of distress and proactively intervene. They should strive to understand individual student stressors and tailor support accordingly, fostering resilience and promoting successful learning outcomes within clinical environments. Additionally, institutions are encouraged to strengthen support systems for both students and educators during clinical placements, creating an environment conducive to learning and well-being.

Researchers

Although current interventions show benefits in managing students’ psychological distress, many lack prior identification of specific stressors and theoretical frameworks. Future research should incorporate these missing elements to develop more targeted and effective interventions. Additionally, exploring interventions that support clinical mentors and evaluating their effectiveness is recommended.

Policymakers

Institutions should mandate the implementation of structured and timely interventions aimed at reducing stress and anxiety both prior to and during clinical placements, thereby enhancing students' learning experiences and coping mechanisms. Furthermore, policies ought to promote collaboration between educational institutions and clinical sites, focus on allocating adequate resources to support both students and clinical mentors throughout the clinical placements.

Conclusion

This review highlights that clinical placement-induced stress and psychological distress among nursing students are common worldwide yet insufficiently addressed by researchers. Current interventions focus mainly on general coping, often neglecting specific clinical stressors and prevention. Future interventions should combine stressor identification with coping strategies, be grounded in robust theoretical frameworks, and incorporate support and training for clinical mentors. This review helps inform best practices for developing more targeted, comprehensive, and preventive interventions to better support nursing students during clinical placements.
