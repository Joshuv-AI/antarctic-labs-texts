Key factors impacting morning report learning in emergency residency training: a multicenter qualitative study
Authors: Omar Alghamdi, Salman Alotaibi, Ali Alkhulaif, Sarah Alqahtani, Ali Basfar, Muna Aljahany
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMorning reports are essential educational tools in medical training that promote clinical reasoning, communication, and decision-making. However, their impact in emergency medicine (EM) remains unexplored. This study identified factors that enhance or hinder learning in EM morning report sessions in Saudi Arabia.MethodsThis qualitative study was conducted at five major Saudi Board of Emergency Medicine training sites in Riyadh, where standardized morning reports were held at 7:00 AM for an average of 20 min, facilitated by EM residents under consultant supervision. Eligible participants included EM consultants actively supervising morning reports and EM residents mandated to attend and present. Data were collected through semi-structured interviews with consultants and focus group discussions with residents. Content analysis was used to thematically identify key dimensions influencing morning report effectiveness.ResultsA total of 47 EM residents and consultants participated. Three primary themes emerged regarding challenges and improvements in morning reports: (1) timing and scheduling – delays, inconsistent session durations, and conflicts with peak patient flow hindered participation; (2) content and teaching methods – overreliance on lecture-based formats, limited diversity, and insufficient interaction reduced engagement; (3) mentorship and engagement – passive participation, inconsistent guidance, and unprepared presenters diminished educational value.ConclusionsThis study highlights the key barriers and enablers in EM morning reports, emphasizing the need for structured scheduling, dynamic teaching methods, and enhanced mentorship. These recommendations can optimize morning reporting sessions, improve EM residency training, and enhance overall patient care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12873-025-01358-x.

Introduction

A morning report is defined as a teaching and learning experience conducted within clinical, reasoning, and evaluative contexts [1]. It is considered an effective educational tool across various branches of medicine. It primarily focuses on case- or topic-based discussions aimed at supporting clinical services. These discussions typically occur in the form of teaching conferences. Encouraging the audience to interact is an effective strategy for achieving meaningful educational outcomes [2].

There are different types of morning reports, including those that involve unprepared case discussions and others that are more structured, with a specific focus framed within broader themes [1]. The culture surrounding morning reports should emphasize flexibility, respect, and the efficient use of time and resources. Emphasis should be placed on participants’ satisfaction and the educational value derived from promoting communication skills, critical thinking, ethics, and professionalism in clinical medicine [2].

The environment should encourage participants to guide discussions, including those focused on initial diagnoses, complications, and treatment strategies. These discussions should comprise questions, answers, comments, and constructive feedback from the audience. In summary, morning reports are important tools for educating participants in clinical settings [2]. Morning reports have also been adapted successfully to outpatient venues, underscoring their flexibility across clinical settings [3].

Morning reports in the emergency department

Numerous studies have assessed the educational impact of morning reports, particularly within internal medicine. McNeill et al. [2] emphasized the value of morning reports in the emergency department (ED). Recognized globally for their effectiveness, morning reports incorporate diverse methods and settings and are considered excellent educational tools with distinct objectives. They facilitate interactive discussions about patient care, clinical development, and daily monitoring of hospital patients [4].

A newly- proposed structured learning model has been developed for trainees working night shifts in the ED [5].

This model is equivalent to a morning report and, as perceived by the targeted learners, results in a positive educational experience [6].

In summary, morning reports are vital in the ED, as they teach and reinforce clinical reasoning skills among all staff members in a productive manner [7].

Morning reports in emergency medicine training programs in Saudi Arabia

Morning reports are an essential part of educational activities within emergency medicine (EM) residency training programs worldwide. In Saudi Arabia, morning report sessions for EM training began in 2001 with the establishment of the Saudi Board of Emergency Medicine (SBEM). The Saudi Commission for Health Specialties has designated morning reports as a mandatory criterion for accreditation of EM training centers under the SBEM [8].

Morning reports are typically conducted during the transition between night and morning shifts. The facilitator is usually an EM resident supervised by the on-dutyan EM consultant on duty. Participants include trainees such as medical students, interns, and residents who worked either the previous night or the current morning shift. Each session lasts approximately 30–45 min and is held in a small conference room adjacent to the ED clinical area. These sessions are protected educational times for all trainees and are only canceled in urgent situations, such as ED disasters. The teaching methods used in morning reports vary and may include whiteboard case presentations, PowerPoint lectures, verbal case discussions, or brief hands-on sessions. While morning report sessions are widely recognized as valuable teaching tools in EM residency programs, limited data exist on the factors that most effectively enhance or hinder their success [4, 7, 9]. During the COVID-19 pandemic, many EDs reorganized clinical pathways via accelerated care units, further highlighting the need for adaptable teaching formats such as morning report [6, 10].

Importance of morning reports

Amin et al. (2000) emphasized the efficiency of morning reports in medical education. They are of immense importance for learning and appropriately demonstrating clinical reasoning. Additionally, they address key medical topics that enhance the application of effective teaching methods. Morning reports contribute to physicians’ learning by incorporating new strategies to manage urgent situations.Patients may also benefit through improved monitoring and timely decision-making as a result of structured case reviews. Ow et al. [11] offered additional insights into the significant value of morning reports. During these sessions, the internal medicine curriculum can be effectively provided, ensuring that the medical staff is reminded of all essential details. Morning reports are also effective assessment tools for increasing the quality of care. ED staff gain valuable knowledge as they often handle urgent cases that require appropriate pre-existing plans, supported by morning reports. Staff can also improve their productivity after morning reports, as they gain many skills, such as communication and problem-solving. This means that morning reports can facilitate a unique learning experience in which physicians discuss clinical cases with an educational purpose.

Strategies for improving morning reports

Elisseou and Holt [5] proposed recommendations to ensure the appropriate conduct of morning reports. First, they recommended selecting and mastering a case that provides realistic, engaging, challenging, and instructional themes. Second, they suggested focusing on building a differential diagnosis and outlining disease management. The main facilitator of the morning report must ensure a balanced conversation to maintain effectiveness throughout the entire session.

Methods for facilitating morning reports

Several tools support the creation of a psychologically safe environment for group learning, as toxic climates hinder deep learning participation [12]. Additionally, strategies aligned with the hierarchical structure of medicine should be used to ensure that opinions are valued. Using simple language and engaging appropriately with the audience helps foster constructive inquiry. Salazar, Chan, and Pietzak [13] also noted that introducing these tools within a warm and welcoming climate contributes significantly to establishing a positive morning report culture. Asking questions that foster clinical reasoning during each session encourages engagement and may enhance the overall learning experience [13]. This practice also supports regular repetition and summarization, encouraging consistent feedback from the audience. The systematic use of participants’ names is another essential element in conducting reports effectively.

In summary, fostering trust is critical to achieving respectful learning within the medical setting [13]. Given the limited literature on this topic, there is insufficient evidence regarding the effectiveness of morning reports for emergency medicine residents. This study aimed to identify the factors that enhance or impede learning experiences during EM morning report sessions.

Methods

Study design and study sample

This study was conducted at the five main training sites of the SBEM in Riyadh: King Abdullah bin Abdulaziz University Hospital, Ministry of National Guard Health Affairs, King Fahad Medical City, King Khalid University Hospital and Prince Mohammed bin Abdulaziz Hospital. These designated sites require morning reports for medical students and residents. Sessions begin at 7:00 am and last approximately 20 min, bridging the morning and night shifts. Typically, an EM resident facilitates the session under the supervision of an on-duty EM consultant. Attendees include trainees (medical students, interns, and residents) from the current morning shift or the previous night shift.

Eligible study participants were categorized into two groups:

Medical students and non-EM residents were excluded. Although their perspectives may be valuable, this study focused on factors specific to EM training. Medical students generally have limited clinical and academic experience, and rotating non-EM residents may not be fully immersed in the day-to-day practice of EM.

Data collection tool

Written informed consent was obtained before data collection. Participation did not affect their assessments. They were informed that they could withdraw at any time without consequence and that the study posed no risks or costs.

Upon consent, data were collected through semi-structured interviews and electronic surveys. One-on-one interviews were conducted with EM consultants, while small-group interviews were held with EM residents. Investigators explained the goals and objectives of the study and informed participants that interviews would be recorded. The interview guide was developed based on the investigators’ experiences as educators and trainees and aimed to identify factors influencing the morning report learning experience as well as the underlying mechanism. Two senior consultants reviewed and revised the questions accordingly.

Interviewers were permitted to adapt the question sequence and expand on topics as needed. Interviews continued until thematic saturation was reached. Each lasted 30–60 min. Focus groups included 5–8 EM residents and were conducted in person. Resident research members facilitated.

the resident focus groups, while consultant researchers led the consultant interviews to encourage open discussion.

All interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed for analysis. The interview structure is presented in Appendix 1. After the interviews, participants completed an electronic form via the Red Cap® system. The survey questions covered the demographics of the participants. Four independent investigators reviewed and coded the transcripts to identify themes. Thematic analysis was conducted through comparison and synthesis of coded data until a final framework emerged, by using (Braun & Clarke’s six phases), double‑coding of an initial subset, consensus reconciliation, iterative codebook refinement, and thematic saturation.

Ethical considerations

This research was conducted in accordance to the declaration of Helsinki and ethical approval was granted by the Institutional Review Board of Princess Nourah Bint Abdulrahman University (IRB log number 24–0043). All participating residents provided written informed consent.

Effect of the study protocol on teaching process of residents

All trainees received standard teaching and assessment, regardless of participation. No additional risks were associated with participation. The research team did not share participants’ identities with the program committee or external parties. Participating trainees may benefit from improved EM training if the findings are incorporated into future educational practices.

Data and safety monitoring

Each participant was assigned a unique identification number and access to the database was restricted to authorized personnel. All relevant variables were systematically collected using appropriate software. The study team met regularly to review procedures for completeness and data quality. Participation did not infringe upon participants’ rights and posed no significant risks.

Monitoring and quality assurance

The principal investigator reviewed the data and held discussions with the research team to ensure adherence to IRB-approved protocols and that the data were correctly obtained. All future publications will comply with the research standards and procedures of Princess Nourah Bint Abdulrahman University.

Privacy and confidentiality

We ensured a private physical setting for the interviews to minimize the risk of a privacy breach. The data collection sheets and audio recordings were stored on a password-protected hard disk accessible only to the research team.

Regarding content and teaching methods, participants noted an overreliance on lecture-style sessions, repeated material, limited content variety, and insufficient interaction. They also identified challenges in addressing a wide range of educational levels. A shift toward real-life, case-based discussions was recommended to ensure coverage of both common and rare cases.

Participants also suggested incorporating interactive tools (e.g., whiteboards and multimedia aids) to increase engagement. A combination of pre-announced topics and spontaneous discussions was proposed to maintain session dynamism.

Participants further identified several recurring issues affecting session quality, including inconsistent mentorship, passive audience engagement, and underprepared presenters. They suggested having at least one senior consultant present to facilitate—but not dominate—discussions. Encouraging active peer engagement and structured feedback was also emphasized.Helpful practices were categorized into five key dimensions: timing and scheduling, content and delivery, presenters and mentors, engagement and interactions, and environment and resources (Table 3).

Discussion

Morning report sessions are a valuable teaching component of EM residency programs worldwide. Using semi-structured interviews with EM consultants and residents, we identified several areas for improvement and practical strategies for enhancing the effectiveness of morning report meetings in EM training. Comprehensive reviews describe common standards for morning reports, yet no single universally adopted model exists, allowing programs to tailor sessions to local training needs [14]. Including high-impact domains such as behavioral and psychiatric emergencies can broaden relevance and reflect the multidisciplinary scope of EM practice [15].

Timing and scheduling

Participants reported significant issues with timing and consistency. Delayed starts, variable session lengths, and conflicts with peak clinical hours limited engagement This contrasts with previous findings highlighting the importance of punctuality and consistent timing, which should be handled by the morning report moderator [5]. Participants suggested enforcing strict start times and maintaining session durations between 20 and 30 min. Aligning sessions with shift changes and exempting night-shift residents–especially during demanding periods, such as the holy month of fasting (Ramadan) or exams—was also advised to improve attendance and focus.

Content and teaching methods

Many participants also expressed concerns about the content and structure of morning reports. The frequent use of lecture-based formats, repetition of basic cases, and lack of variety inteaching methods reduced educational value. Addressing the varying needs of interns through senior residents posed an additional challenge. To improve delivery, participants advocated a case-based approach focused on practical clinical scenarios. Tools such as whiteboards and multimedia aids were suggested to enhance session engagement. Incorporating both pre-announced topics and spontaneous discussions was recommended to maintain a dynamic format. The absence of a standardized model allows programs to tailor sessions to specific training needs. For instance, in settings with restricted resident work hours, morning reports can help maintain patient care continuity. Programs seeking to improve board certification pass rates may include relevant exam questions in sessions. Sites lacking complex cases may use published case reports as educational material. With these strategies, morning reports can serve as a powerful tool for program directors to address training gaps [4].

Mentorship and engagement

Mentorship and audience participation emerged as critical factors. Participants noted that passive audiences and unprepared presenters often compromised the quality of morning reports. They emphasized the importance of senior consultants providing guidance and instruction. Training facilitators in effective communication and facilitation skills was also recommended to elevate session quality. Fostering interactive discussions and structured feedback was considered a valuable strategy to promote active engagement and a more inclusive learning environment. Participants engaged in detailed discussions of selected cases chosen for their educational significance. In the final five minutes, they formulated questions and assigned them to residents for presentation the following day. Reilly and Lemon observed a positive department-wide impact after implementing this approach. Additionally, residents gained a deeper understanding of evidence-based medicine, including its principles, procedures, and the ability to formulate precise, clinically relevant questions [12].

Environment and resources

Participants identified the physical setting of morning reports as an area for improvement. They emphasized the need for small, well-equipped venues with access to essential tools such as whiteboards and audiovisual aids. Selecting accessible, appropriately sized locations with necessary resources could significantly enhance the learning experience. Emergency consultants emphasized the importance of punctuality and consistency, recommending standardized scheduling and improved communication.

They advocated focusing on critical thinking and diagnostic reasoning rather than solely on outcomes. Consultants were encouraged to facilitate rather than dominate discussions, ensuring relevance and depth. Granting residents greater autonomy was also seen as a way to boost confidence and teaching competence.

Morning reports provide a protected, uninterrupted time for education within a demanding morning schedule. They offer residents an opportunity to expand their medical knowledge while developing leadership, presentation, and problem-solving skills. However, evidence of their educational effectiveness remains largely anecdotal, and objectives are often implicit or unclear. Therefore, each training program should define specific goals and structure sessions to maximize their educational impact [12].

Implications and recommendations

This study provides several practical recommendations to address the identified challenges. Establishing a predictable schedule, introducing interactive and diverse teaching methods, improving audience engagement, and optimizing the physical environment are achievable steps that can significantly enhance the quality of morning reports. These improvements would strengthen the educational experience and better prepare residents for the demands of EM practice.

Limitations

First, as a methodological limitation of an interview-based study, there was a risk of interviewer influence, which may have affected some the participants’ responses. However, this was mitigated by using a standardized interview template. Second, participants were drawn exclusively from the Central Region of Saudi Arabia, although most EM residency trainees are based in this region.

Conclusion

This is the first study in Saudi Arabia to highlight the need for targeted improvements in the structure and delivery of morning reports in EM residency training. The recommendations provided by residents and consultants offer a clear roadmap for making these sessions more effective and relevant. Implementing these changes can enhance learning, maximize educational value, and optimize the role of morning reports in preparing residents for the challenges of EM practice.
