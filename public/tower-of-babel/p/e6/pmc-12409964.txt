Primary health care policy investments in the Latin America context: Health systems experiences from Brazil, Chile, and Colombia☆
Authors: Adriano Massuda, Michelle Fernandez, Marco Antonio Catussi Paschoalotto, Elisandréa Sguario Kemper
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study examines the policy investments in Primary Health Care (PHC) within the health systems of Brazil, Chile, and Colombia, highlighting their contributions toward achieving Universal Health Coverage (UHC). Employing a qualitative methodology, the research includes an institutional historical review and interviews with key stakeholders to analyze the development of PHC financing policies and practices in these countries. Brazil, with its Unified Health System (SUS), demonstrates federal leadership through initiatives like Requalifica UBS and the new PAC, albeit facing challenges in regional equity and monitoring. Chile emphasizes central governance but struggles with municipal funding capacity and infrastructure renewal. Colombia lacks targeted PHC investment policies but shows promise through emerging frameworks such as the Planes Maestros. The findings underline the critical role of integrated governance, sustainable funding, and advanced technological investment in strengthening PHC systems. Recommendations include enhancing territorial diagnostics, fostering public–private partnerships, and aligning investments with demographic and regional needs. Therefore, this research contributes to understanding PHC financing structures, offering actionable insights for improving equity and access. Limitations include the study’s focus on three countries and qualitative scope, suggesting future research should adopt broader comparative frameworks and mixed methodologies to evaluate the long-term impacts of PHC investments globally.

Introduction

Primary Health Care (PHC) is widely recognized as the cornerstone for achieving Universal Health Coverage (UHC), ensuring that all individuals have access to essential health services without enduring financial hardship [1,2]. PHC has been championed for its inclusivity, equity, cost-effectiveness, and efficiency in enhancing physical and mental health, as well as social well-being [3,4].

Investing in PHC is crucial for the financial sustainability of health systems [5]. Reforms in health financing, alongside investments in health infrastructure and the health workforce, are pivotal in strengthening PHC services [6,7]. Such investments not only improve service delivery but also ensure that health systems are resilient and capable of providing continuous care to populations [2,7].

In Latin America, characterized by significant health inequalities, robust PHC systems are essential [8,9]. The COVID-19 pandemic exposed vulnerabilities in the region's health systems, underscoring the need for resilient health systems that can provide equitable care [2,6,10]. Addressing these disparities requires targeted investments in PHC to ensure that all population segments have access to quality health services [8,11].

Countries like Brazil, Chile, and Colombia have made notable strides in strengthening their PHC systems. Brazil's Unified Health System (SUS) is lauded for its comprehensive PHC services, which have been instrumental in improving health outcomes and reducing inequalities [[12], [13], [14]]. Chile has implemented health reforms focusing on PHC to enhance service delivery and accessibility [15,16]. Colombia's health system reforms have aimed at expanding PHC services to ensure broader coverage and equity [17,18]. Despite that, there is still research and practical need to comprehend and share PHC policy investment initiatives between these three models. To fulfill this gap, this research aims to analyze the PHC policy investments in the Brazilian, Chilean, and Colombian health systems and propose policy recommendations.

Methods

The present analysis employed a qualitative, comparative methodology – multi-case design focused on Brazil, Chile, and Colombia – combined with an institutional historical analysis to examine the development trajectories of health system financing and policy initiatives directed toward investments in PHC in Brazil, Chile, and Colombia. The institutional historical review focused on identifying key milestones, reforms, and policy shifts that have shaped the current landscape of PHC investment in each country [12]. This approach enabled the study to contextualize investment patterns. The fulfilled “Consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative studies (COREQ): 32-item checklist” is presented in Appendix 1.

The comparative component – multi-case design – of the analysis sought to uncover both convergences and divergences in governance structures, funding sources, policy design, and implementation strategies [19,20]. By systematically contrasting the three cases, the study aimed to generate insights into the strengths and limitations of different PHC investment models in Latin America.

The selection of Brazil, Chile, and Colombia as case studies is justified by their relevance and diversity within the Latin American context, particularly concerning PHC development. All three countries have undertaken significant health reforms aimed at strengthening PHC systems in response to persistent health inequalities and, more recently, the vulnerabilities exposed by the COVID-19 pandemic [[12], [13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18]]. Brazil's Unified Health System (SUS) stands out for its comprehensive PHC model and its role in reducing health disparities [[12], [13], [14]]. Chile has advanced PHC through structural reforms designed to enhance access and service delivery [15,16]. Colombia, in turn, has implemented reforms focused on expanding PHC coverage and integrating services to promote equity [17,18]. These cases offer varied approaches to PHC governance, financing, and service delivery, providing a rich comparative basis for analysis. Despite their progress, a clear gap remains in the literature regarding how these countries are investing in PHC infrastructure, particularly in ways that promote long-term resilience and equity.

Case studies

Brazil's Unified Health System (Sistema Único de Saúde, SUS) is one of the world's most extensive public health systems, offering comprehensive, universal, and free healthcare services to the entire population [21]. Established in 1988, SUS operates on the foundational principles of universality, equity, and integrality, ensuring that every individual, regardless of socioeconomic status, has access to necessary health services [22]. The system encompasses various services, ranging from primary care to complex surgical procedures, including organ transplants. It also plays a pivotal role in public health initiatives such as vaccination campaigns, epidemiological surveillance, and health promotion activities [23]. SUS is structured across federal, state, and municipal levels, promoting decentralized management and encouraging community participation in health-related decision-making processes [14].

Chile's health system is characterized by a mixed public–private structure, resulting from historical reforms and political dynamics [24]. In the 1980 s, during the military dictatorship, Chile pioneered a radical health system reform in Latin America, expanding private sector participation in insurance and service provision, which influenced reforms in other countries in the region [25]. This period established a dual and segmented system with a strong private sector presence. The public subsystem, managed by the National Health Fund (FONASA), coexists with private insurers known as ISAPREs. This configuration has led to regulation, financing, and service provision challenges, perpetuating segmentation and inequalities in health access [26]. Despite efforts to integrate services and coordinate care through Primary Health Care (PHC), the system remains fragmented, with ongoing debates about balancing public and private roles in health service delivery [27].

Colombia's health system has undergone significant transformations since the enactment of Law 100 in 1993, which introduced a mixed public–private model to achieve universal health coverage [28]. This system is structured into two main regimes: the Contributory Regime (RC), funded by payroll contributions from employees and employers, and the Subsidized Regime (RS), designed to provide healthcare access to low-income populations through government subsidies. The implementation of this model led to a substantial increase in health insurance coverage [29]. However, this expansion has been accompanied by persistent challenges, including disparities in the quality of services between the two regimes and financial sustainability concerns [30]. The private sector's significant role in both the administration of resources and service provision has sparked debates regarding equity and efficiency within the system.

Primary data were collected through purposive interviews with key stakeholders of the implementation and/or formulation of PHC policies and infrastructure investment in PHC in Brazil, Chile, and Colombia. The criteria to select the key stakeholders were: i) being part of the Ministry of Health, or any national health commission or department related; and b) being involved in the implementation and/or formulation of the main PHC policies and infrastructure investment in PHC.

The invitation, with the project goals and researcher affiliations, was sent through the formal email contacts from the official websites. Seven interviews were conducted for the analysis of Brazil’s case, and three interviews for Chile, by two PhD female authors with experience in interviews and PHC financing through the Zoom platform. For Colombia, the Ministry of Health team chose to respond to the interview questions in written form. The semistructured interview guide was developed by the authors and passed by a pre-test with a practitioner expert in the Brazilian PHC policy investments. Also, it is available in Appendix 2.

Data analysis

The interviews, averaging 1 h and 27 min, were recorded and transcribed in their original languages, Portuguese and Spanish. Following the analysis, they were translated into English. The documentary research data and interviews were analyzed using the inductive content analysis approach [12]. Therefore, the data saturation during collection and analysis occurred when the interview data crossed with the documentary research data, also generating new findings. Results and discussion

Financing health systems comparison

The Unified Health System (SUS) is funded through general tax revenues, comprising contributions and taxes from all three levels of government. The financial investment percentages for municipalities, states, and the federal government in SUS are currently defined by Complementary Law n° 141 of 2012. State governments must allocate at least 12 % of their total revenue to health, while municipalities must dedicate a minimum of 15 % of their total revenue. The federal government, which initially was mandated to invest an amount equal to the previous year’s expenditure adjusted by GDP growth, is now required to invest 15 % of net current revenue [21]. Despite having a universal and comprehensive public health system like SUS, public expenditure on health in Brazil has historically remained lower than private health spending.

The Chilean health system is financed through general state taxes and mandatory contributions from workers and employers. Public health services are funded by general taxes, municipal contributions, and co-payments made by beneficiaries of the National Health Fund (Fonasa). Private sector resources come from mandatory and voluntary contributions, fees, and co-payments from affiliates of private health insurance institutions (Isapres), as well as direct payments by users for private health services. All workers are required to contribute to the health system and can choose to direct their contributions to either Fonasa or an Isapre. Contributions are voluntary for self-employed professionals. Since 2019, self-employed workers have also been required to contribute to social security and health insurance through the country’s fiscal system.

The Colombian health system is predominantly financed through mandatory contributions and fiscal resources. Social contributions are made by formal workers, employers, self-employed individuals, and pensioners. Contributions are mandatory for individuals earning more than ten minimum wages, amounting to 12.5 % of income. Salaried workers or pensioners contribute 4 %, with employers covering the remaining 8.5 % (ISAGS). Self-employed professionals with the ability to pay are responsible for the full amount. The General System of Social Security in Health (SGSSS) also relies on additional funding sources to cover the costs of the contributory and subsidized health regimes, payments for services outside the Capitation Payment Unit (UPC), and the provision of public health programs, as well as health promotion and prevention initiatives. Contributors must also pay co-payments whenever they access medical services. Co-payments for contributors and moderation fees for the use of tertiary services, such as hospitalizations or surgeries, are established to regulate service utilization and support system financing.

Table 2 reveals key trends in health expenditures for Brazil, Chile, and Colombia between 2005 and 2020. Total health expenditures as a percentage of GDP demonstrate consistent growth across all three countries, with Brazil showing the highest increase from 8.04 % in 2005 to 10.31 % in 2020. Chile and Colombia also exhibited significant increases, rising to 9.75 % and 8.99 %, respectively, by 2020. Regarding per capita health expenditures, Chile surpasses the other two countries, reaching $1,278 in 2020, indicating a robust financial commitment to health services. Conversely, Brazil experienced a decline from $894 in 2010 to $701 in 2020, reflecting economic constraints or shifts in policy. Colombia maintained a steady but modest increase, reaching $477 per capita in 2020.

Public health expenditures as a percentage of total health spending reveal contrasting approaches. Colombia consistently allocates the highest share to public health, exceeding 70 %, underlining its emphasis on universal health coverage. Brazil and Chile show a more moderate increase, with Chile reaching 56.4 % by 2020. Out-of-pocket expenditures declined across all three countries, indicating progress towards reducing financial barriers to healthcare. Brazil achieved the lowest levels (22.4 %), followed by Colombia and Chile, reflecting efforts to enhance equity in health access.

PHC policy investment analysis

Brazil

Since its inception, Brazil’s Unified Health System (SUS) has seen only three significant federal programmes dedicated to infrastructure investment: Reforsus, PAC-Saúde, and Requalifica UBS. Reforsus (1996–2000) was the first SUS programme to allocate financial resources to infrastructure and equipment investments. With approximately US$ 750 million in loans from international organisations such as the World Bank, it prioritised medium and high-complexity services, with limited focus on PHC. Despite its innovative approach, challenges such as excessive bureaucracy, delayed contract execution, and low financial implementation were noted. These issues were highlighted in an audit by the Federal Court of Accounts in 1999.“If a more robust bill is drafted, it is possible to create a regulatory model that provides greater credibility and more technical and legal security, not only to public partners but also to private ones (Brazil, Expert 4).”

“If a more robust bill is drafted, it is possible to create a regulatory model that provides greater credibility and more technical and legal security, not only to public partners but also to private ones (Brazil, Expert 4).”

In 2007, PAC-Saúde was launched to boost national development, including health infrastructure investments in its second phase (2010). This phase supported urban sanitation and the construction and refurbishment of healthcare facilities, including Primary Health Units (UBS) and Emergency Care Units. The Requalifica UBS, in 2011, exclusively targeted PHC, with over R$ 5 billion allocated to projects involving more than 25,000 UBS, nearly 50 % of all units at the time. It introduced an innovative fund-by-fund funding mechanism and digitalized processes through the SISMOB system. However, limitations such as insufficient monitoring, weak internal controls, and lacking criteria to address regional inequalities were reported.

Recently, the new PAC, in 2023, relaunched as a priority for federal investments, allocating R$ 31 billion for PHC, specialized care, Telehealth, and health emergencies. It aims to construct new UBS, polyclinics, and maternity facilities and improve vaccine production and access to Telehealth services.

Brazil's PHC investment strategy has evolved through targeted federal programs. The early Reforsus program aimed to improve health infrastructure but prioritised medium and high-complexity services, leaving PHC underfunded. Its effectiveness was further constrained by excessive bureaucratic hurdles and low levels of financial execution [31].

Subsequent innovations, including fund-by-fund transfer mechanisms and the adoption of digital monitoring tools, contributed to improved administrative efficiency and transparency [32]. Nonetheless, persistent limitations, such as inadequate consideration of regional disparities and weak monitoring and evaluation systems, continued to undermine policy effectiveness [33]. The launch of the new PAC (2023), which places strategic emphasis on PHC infrastructure, telehealth expansion, and health emergency preparedness, signals a renewed federal commitment.“The first thing we need to do is start investing in digital technology in primary care, especially in organizing the service. It's absurd that today, with the technology and availability of information we have, you still can't book an appointment online, reschedule it, or do a number of other things. (Brazil, Expert 4)”

Chile

In Chile, PHC policy formulation and implementation are centralized under the Ministry of Health, with municipalities managing service delivery. Most PHC funding originates from central government transfers, with limited municipal contributions [26], highlighting a strong national role in shaping healthcare networks. While municipalities can contribute to infrastructure funding, few have sufficient resources. PHC gained prominence more recently, driven by efforts to modernise healthcare and reduce hospital overcrowding [15].“Primary health care, in most cases, is the responsibility of the municipalities. In theory, municipalities can also fund health infrastructure. In general, large municipalities do so. But few municipalities have the resources. (Chile, Expert 3)”

“Primary health care, in most cases, is the responsibility of the municipalities. In theory, municipalities can also fund health infrastructure. In general, large municipalities do so. But few municipalities have the resources. (Chile, Expert 3)”

The interviews also emphasized the importance of regional diagnostics to identify priority investment areas. Regional diagnostics have highlighted the urgent need for infrastructure renewal [34]. Updated health network assessments suggest that over 60 % of existing facilities require replacement. Investment prioritisation based on demographic and geographic factors is critical for efficient resource allocation [35]. Although the national government leads PHC investments, regional and municipal contributions are recognized as supplementary sources.

There is a need for pre-investment diagnostics, project monitoring, and risk management in PHC infrastructure investments. Public-private partnerships and improved governance mechanisms are essential to address risk management and ensure investment efficiency [24]. Expanding PHC’s capacity to diagnose and resolve health issues requires advanced equipment and technology. Investments must enhance PHC’s problem-solving abilities, fostering a healthcare culture focused on proactive and effective primary care. Strengthening resource allocation and infrastructure is pivotal for advancing PHC in Chile. Enhancing PHC’s diagnostic and treatment capabilities remains a key focus.“It would be good if there were more resources to invest more in health, because investment in infrastructure—both the initial investment and ongoing maintenance—is not sufficient. (Chile, Expert 3)”

“It would be good if there were more resources to invest more in health, because investment in infrastructure—both the initial investment and ongoing maintenance—is not sufficient. (Chile, Expert 3)”

Colombia

In Colombia, PHC investment lacks a dedicated programme, with existing efforts integrated into broader health policies [36]. The current Comprehensive Health Policy acknowledges primary care providers' role and needs but lacks a clear plan to strengthen infrastructure. The National Development Plan 2022–2026, “Colombia: A Global Power of Life,” introduces the Planes Maestros de Inversiones en Infraestructura y Dotación en Salud (PMIDS), to be developed at departmental, district, and national levels every 10 years, aligned with the Decadal Health Plan. Additionally, departmental and district health secretariats must present four-year investment plans within the first six months of each government term. According to the Ministry of Health, the Comprehensive Health Policy references primary care needs but does not establish specific measures to enhance infrastructure or resources. However, these plans are still emerging, and formalised policies to strengthen PHC infrastructure are absent.

PHC funding in Colombia involves subnational governments, such as departments, districts, and municipalities. Subnational governments can invest directly using their resources or submit proposals to the Ministry of Health for funding based on prioritization criteria. Departments play a key role, coordinating projects for Integrated Health Service Networks (RISS), and approving and channeling requests from municipal levels.“It is necessary to clarify that the current Comprehensive Health Policy refers to the primary provider and their needs, but it does not specifically establish decisions aimed at strengthening the associated infrastructure or funding. (Colombia Ministry of Health)”

“It is necessary to clarify that the current Comprehensive Health Policy refers to the primary provider and their needs, but it does not specifically establish decisions aimed at strengthening the associated infrastructure or funding. (Colombia Ministry of Health)”

These frameworks highlight an emerging structure for PHC investment but underscore the need for formalized, targeted policies to address infrastructure and service delivery gaps [37].

The comparison reveals distinct national approaches to primary health care (PHC) investment, each shaped by specific institutional arrangements and historical trajectories. In Brazil, PHC policy is marked by a centralised federal focus, with the national government leading the design and funding of major initiatives. These programmes aim to strengthen service infrastructure and improve access. Funding is typically channelled through federal transfers to subnational governments via specific health funds. However, despite this strong federal role, the country continues to face significant challenges in regional equity and continuity [9]. Furthermore, weak monitoring and evaluation systems hinder the ability to track investment outcomes and guide adaptive policy measures.

Chile presents a contrasting model with centralised policy guidance combined with municipal-level service delivery [38]. Although PHC is integrated into broader health system planning, it lacks dedicated national programmes, relying instead on municipalities to implement and co-finance services. Central transfers are complemented by local contributions, which introduce disparities in PHC capacity across regions. Recent efforts have emphasised diagnostics-driven investment and infrastructure renewal, yet risk management and coordination remain challenging, particularly in ensuring that investments align with evolving health needs and demographic shifts.

Colombia follows a decentralised model, where subnational governments have substantial autonomy over PHC planning and implementation. While this allows flexibility and responsiveness to local contexts, it also contributes to fragmentation and inconsistencies in service quality [39]. The lack of targeted national policies and structured investment frameworks has resulted in uneven development of PHC infrastructure and limited scalability of successful local initiatives. Nevertheless, recent developments point toward the adoption of long-term investment planning tools, which aim to enhance strategic alignment across levels of government and improve investment efficiency.

These insights underscore the need for integrated frameworks that align national, regional, and local priorities to ensure equitable and sustainable PHC investment [40]. Overall, the comparison underscores the need for integrated PHC investment frameworks that balance national leadership with local responsiveness, ensure sustainable financing, and prioritize equity in both infrastructure and service provision.

Discussion and policy recommendations

Effective health system investment in PHC requires aligning domestic priorities with sustainable financing models, as underscored by WHO’s health financing diagnostics [41]. However, Brazil, Chile and Colombia demonstrate that this achievement is not an easy task. While the Brazilian case presented some interesting initiatives over time, but not in a continuous way, Chile and Colombia did not show sustainable policies for PHC investment.

Long-term investment planning must consider recurrent costs such as workforce salaries, infrastructure maintenance, and digital systems integration [42]. Also, WHO emphasizes the need to expand fiscal space for PHC through innovative financing, taxation reforms, and strategic donor coordination [43]. Complementary, PAHO suggests pooled regional funds and public–private partnerships to bridge equity gaps [44]. Despite all the advice and diagnostics of the international health organizations, Brazil, Chile, and Colombia struggle to increase fiscal capacity for PHC and to coordinate long-term investment and planning for PHC improvement.

Digital transformation and technological focus in PHC demand strong data governance, cybersecurity, and regulation to ensure equitable implementation [45]. Robust governance structures and clear legal mandates are essential for health system resilience [46]. Legal frameworks must evolve alongside service innovations to safeguard rights and ensure accountability in PHC [44]. In this sense, a) Brazil demonstrates potential to achieve a more equitable PHC, with clear responsibilities and some initiatives of digital transformations, b) Chile has difficulties with the lack of specific PHC policies, usually integrated and fragmented with other sectors, and c) Colombia depends on the subnational and local level to achieve a better PHC.

The health workforce is a key dimension for the development of PHC. In this regard, addressing workforce shortages at this level of care requires expanding education, improving working conditions, and enabling task-sharing models [43]. Regional frameworks promote interprofessional practice and equitable workforce distribution [47]. Even with all the international effort, all three cases analyzed here have difficulties in improving the PHC workforce integration and expansion, more specifically, in equity.

Finally, citizen engagement through participatory mechanisms enhances trust, responsiveness, and system legitimacy to the PHC action [43]. Community councils and digital feedback tools have proven effective in integrating public voice into health governance [44]. And in this case, Brazil and Chile showed great PHC initiatives of citizen engagement, while Colombia still tries to put the participation in the policy aspect.

In Colombia, the emphasis should be on long-term investment frameworks such as the Planes Maestros de Inversiones. Responsibilities for PHC financing must be clarified, and subnational governments should play an integral role in aligning investments with regional needs. Infrastructure development must focus on addressing resource gaps while improving coordination among stakeholders. Finally, Colombia must foster citizen participation in designing and implementing health policies, ensuring inclusive and transparent decision-making processes.

Final remarks

This study underscores critical lessons for improving health systems in Latin America and worldwide, with a focus on Primary Health Care (PHC). For the Latin American health systems, the study highlights the importance of integrating national and subnational efforts to overcome regional disparities. Countries like Brazil, Chile, and Colombia demonstrate the necessity of aligning investment strategies with regional needs through diagnostics and targeted resource allocation. Addressing inequalities requires robust legal frameworks and policies that prioritize equity in PHC funding and service delivery. Investments in infrastructure and technological modernization are pivotal for enhancing PHC's capacity to address health challenges effectively. Additionally, citizen engagement in health policy design can foster inclusivity and ensure that interventions meet population needs.

Globally, the findings stress the value of comprehensive and decentralized approaches to PHC investment. The need for sustainable financing models that integrate public and private sectors is evident. Strengthening governance structures and implementing risk management mechanisms are crucial for efficient resource utilization and accountability. The study also highlights the significance of monitoring and evaluation systems to assess the impact of health investments and adapt strategies as needed. Countries can benefit from regional cooperation and knowledge-sharing to adopt best practices tailored to local contexts. Emphasizing PHC as a cornerstone of Universal Health Coverage (UHC) can drive equitable and resilient health systems worldwide.

PHC is uniquely positioned to ensure continuity of care and to promote equity within health systems [48]. In this way, this study provides significant insights into PHC policy investments in Brazil, Chile, and Colombia, offering valuable practical and academic contributions. Practically, it highlights the importance of aligning investment strategies with regional and population-specific needs, advocating for robust governance frameworks, targeted resource allocation, and inclusive community involvement. Academically, the study expands the understanding of PHC financing structures and their implications for achieving equitable healthcare access in diverse socio-political contexts.

However, the study has limitations. The qualitative approach, while providing in-depth perspectives, may not fully capture the quantitative dimensions of PHC financing impacts. Furthermore, the analysis is restricted to three countries and with limited access to different PHC stakeholders, restricting generalizability to other contexts with different healthcare systems and funding mechanisms.

Future research should: i) expand the comparative framework to include additional countries or regions, which would also enrich the analysis, providing a broader perspective on best practices and challenges in PHC financing; and ii) evaluate the long-term sustainability and effectiveness of PHC investment policies in achieving Universal Health Coverage and reducing health inequalities. These efforts would contribute to a deeper understanding of the critical role of PHC in strengthening global health systems.
