Wavelength-dependent feedback behavior in light-gated polymersome nanoreactors
Authors: Uthaya Lathan, Beyzanur Kaya, Farzina Matubbar, Mohamed Chami, Jonathan Yeow, Omar Rifaie-Graham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Biological systems integrate multiple feedback processes to regulate their adaptation to evolving stimuli with high specificity. Inspired by wavelength-selective feedback processes in visual signaling, we present a synthetic nanoreactor system capable of producing orthogonal, wavelength-dependent positive and negative feedback loops. Polymersomes functionalized with a broad-spectrum pyrazolone-based Donor–Acceptor Stenhouse Adduct (DASA) encapsulate an esterase enzyme and undergo light-gated permeability switching. Upon irradiation, the nanoreactors operate out-of-equilibrium, hydrolyzing ethyl acetate to generate acetic acid in a wavelength-programmed manner. Under yellow light (590 nm), positive feedback is mediated by spectral unmasking: acid-induced protonation of a solubilized dye decreases competition for yellow light, enhancing membrane permeability and enzyme activity, yielding a positive feedback loop. In contrast, blue light (405 nm) irradiation leads to the accumulation of the same dye that spectrally competes with the photoswitch, progressively suppressing membrane permeability by formation of a negative feedback loop. These stimulus-specific feedback dynamics enable reversible and tunable control over enzymatic reaction kinetics. Importantly, this platform introduces a paradigm shift for light-programmed, autonomous regulation in synthetic cell mimics and could open a pathway to dynamic modulation of microscale environments and biointerfaces without genetic intervention.

Introduction

A defining hallmark of life is its ability to regulate molecular ensembles and biochemical reactions through hierarchical feedback loops, generating adaptive, out-of-equilibrium responses to dynamic environments1. A striking example is found in the human visual system, where cone photoreceptors selectively respond to different wavelengths of light, triggering distinct feedback mechanisms that refine color perception2,3. Short-wavelength (blue) light primarily activates S-cones, initiating a negative feedback loop mediated by horizontal cells that suppress signals from neighboring cones, thereby modulating contrast and preventing signal saturation4. In contrast, longer-wavelength (yellow) light predominantly stimulates M- and L-cones, engaging a positive feedback process through amacrine cell modulation and ON-bipolar cell activation, enhancing signal transmission and reinforcing color perception. This wavelength-dependent interplay between excitation and inhibition ensures precise visual processing.

Polymersomes, self-assembled vesicles formed from block copolymers, offer a versatile platform for mimicking the compartmentalization found in biological cells and organelles5. They offer tuneable permeability between the external environment and the inner lumen, spanning from fully impermeable to small-molecule permeable designs, and even advanced systems capable of dynamic switching in response to chemical or physical stimuli6. This tunability has been harnessed in enzyme-loaded polymersome nanoreactors, which operate exclusively in an out-of-equilibrium state triggered by specific stimuli7–16. Although several stimuli-responsive polymersomes have been reported, most designs rely on externally imposed switching events and do not operate under self-regulating, out-of-equilibrium conditions. A key strategy employed by living systems to sustain such out-of-equilibrium processes is the regulation of metabolite transport through selective channels13. Although selective channels have been successfully reconstituted in polymersome membranes, this approach remains technically demanding and often limited by difficulties in achieving the correct orientation17–20. As a simpler alternative, stimuli-responsive motifs offer the ability to change permeability in response to external and chemical stimuli10,11,21,22. In particular, polymersome membranes functionalized with photoswitches provide precise external control over molecular systems in a non-invasive manner owing to their ability to undergo structural, polarity, and absorption changes in response to light23–27. Among various photoswitches, Donor–Acceptor Stenhouse Adducts (DASAs) offer distinct advantages due to their tunable absorption across the visible spectrum, enabling selective activation based on the irradiation wavelength23. DASAs function as negative photoswitches, cycling between a colorful triene-enol in the dark and a colorless, more polar cyclopentenone upon visible-light exposure28–30. While the rational control of DASA populations allows for selective responses when stimulated with orthogonal wavelenghts11,31,32, DASAs are inherently multiwavelength-responsive systems, absorbing light from the blue region (∼400 nm) to the longest wavelength they are designed to detect33. This broad spectral tunability enhances their utility in the development of advanced photoregulated materials23.

Inspired by the ability of biological systems to regulate activity through wavelength-dependent processes, we designed a light-mediated feedback mechanism that enables dynamic control over enzymatic catalysis kinetics within a single pyrazolone-based DASA-functionalized polymersome nanoreactor containing an esterase enzyme (PYRA–esterase; Fig. 1A and Fig.1B). The photoswitch acts as a membrane permeability regulator in response to a broad spectrum of visible light wavelengths (380–700 nm), allowing passive transport of substrates across the vesicular membrane in an out-of-equilibrium manner that can be halted by withdrawing the light stimulus11,12. This property makes such nanoreactors ideally suited to establish distinct feedback loops, externally controlled by irradiation with blue (405 nm) and yellow (590 nm) light. When PYRA–esterase nanoreactors are exposed to yellow light, the membrane becomes permeable, facilitating the diffusion of ethyl acetate into the vesicle where it is hydrolyzed by the encapsulated esterase to generate acetic acid (Fig. 1C). In an initial alkaline state, the presence of bromocresol purple (BP-) results in spectral overlap between the dye’s absorbance and that of the photoswitch in its yellow-absorbing state. As acetic acid is formed, BP- undergoes protonation to yield its conjugate acid (BPH), leading to a reduction in its absorbance at the yellow-absorbing region of PYRA. Consequently, PYRA can absorb more yellow light, further increasing membrane permeabilization and accelerating substrate transport. This results in an autocatalytic increase in acetic acid formation, establishing a positive feedback loop that enhances the catalytic activity of the nanoreactors. However, over time, the progressive acidification of the medium eventually shifts the system beyond the optimal pH range for esterase activity, halting the reaction and establishing a delayed pH-mediated negative feedback loop. In contrast, irradiation with blue light (405 nm) produces an initial light-mediated negative feedback loop that inherently limits acetic acid formation (Fig. 1D). As the esterase catalyzes the hydrolysis of ethyl acetate, the resulting acid protonates BP- into BPH, which strongly absorbs in the blue region. This leads to spectral competition between BPH and PYRA, reducing the amount of blue light available to trigger PYRA photoisomerization. As BPH accumulates, the inhibition of PYRA photoswitching eventually prevents further membrane permeabilization, thereby restricting substrate transport into the polymersomes and terminating the reaction. Thus, this system establishes a hierarchy of feedback mechanisms governed by orthogonal light wavelengths: yellow light induces a self-reinforcing positive feedback loop that is ultimately constrained by a pH-mediated negative feedback response, whereas blue light generates a self-limiting negative feedback loop through spectral competition with the protonated dye. These findings illustrate how wavelength-dependent control over a photoresponsive nanoreactor can enable precise modulation of enzymatic activity, with potential implications for the design of synthetic systems that mimic biological signal transduction and regulation. Results and discussion

Synthesis of nanoreactors

Despite the development of polymersome nanoreactor systems capable of executing feedback loops, none have been preprogrammed to generate orthogonal feedback processes in response to distinct external or chemical stimuli7,9,12–16. This limitation constrains their ability to truly dynamically adapt to evolving environments as systems that aim to replicate the functions of living cells34–42. Therefore, we designed a strategy that enables selective activation of distinct feedback responses within a single system by applying orthogonal wavelengths of light11,12. Our approach employs a polymersome nanoreactor that alternates between small molecule semipermeability under orthogonal light stimuli and impermeability in the absence of light. To realize a light-responsive nanoreactor activated by either blue or yellow light (PYRA-esterase), we modified an amphiphilic block copolymer composed primarily of poly(ethylene glycol)-b-poly(hexyl methacrylate) (PEG-b-PHMA) with a broad spectrum third-generation pyrazolone-based donor–acceptor Stenhouse adduct (DASA). This scaffold was selected because its broad visible-light absorption profile enabled spectral overlap with the pH-sensitive dye, which was required to encode wavelength-dependent feedback behavior. The nanoreactors were assembled via solvent exchange while simultaneously encapsulating esterase followed by trypsinization of the non-encapsulated enzyme (for polymer synthesis and characterization, see Supplementary Figs. 1–4, and Supplementary Section 1; for particle characterization see Supplementary Figs. 5–6, and Supplementary Sections 2). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) revealed hydrodynamic diameters (Dh) of 116.0 ± 0.1 nm for PYRA-esterase nanoreactors while cryo-TEM confirmed the structure of polymersomes with membrane thickness of 12.8 ± 1.2 nm. Active trypsin remained present throughout all experiments; however, the encapsulated esterase remained protected within the polymersomes and retained full functionality, demonstrating that the nanoreactor membrane provides effective shielding from proteolytic degradation (Figs. 2–4). PYRA photoswitch-driven feedback loops in presence of yellow light

To achieve light-gated positive feedback in PYRA-esterase nanoreactors (Fig. 2A, B), we explored the removal of a product that could interfere with light absorption. The pyrazolone-based DASA (PYRA) utilized in this study exhibited a broad absorbance spectrum extending from the UV range to 700 nm (Fig. 2C), with a peak absorption at λ = 605 nm. Upon exposure to yellow light (λ = 590 nm), this compound underwent photoisomerization from the non-polar triene-enol to the more polar pyrazolone (Fig. 1A). Meanwhile, the BP chromophore transitioned from a purple, deprotonated form (λmax = 590 nm) in basic conditions (BP-) to a yellow, protonated form (λmax = 430 nm) in acidic conditions (BPH) (Fig. 2V). Given the spectral overlap between BP- and PYRA, a decrease in BP- concentration (due to pH decrease) resulted in diminished absorption of yellow light, thereby reducing its photomasking effect. To examine the light-mediated feedback behavior, PYRA-esterase nanoreactors were dispersed in solutions containing ethyl acetate and BP-. The dispersions were continuously irradiated with yellow light (λ = 590 nm) at intensities of 1.76 mW·cm⁻² and 1.57 mW·cm⁻², alongside a dark control, while periodically measuring absorbance at λ = 405 nm to track BPH formation via BP- protonation at concentrations of 0.2 mg·mL−1 (Fig. 2D and Supplementary Fig. 7). Under yellow light exposure, absorbance gradually increased due to enzymatic conversion of ethyl acetate into acetic acid, promoting BPH formation. This effect was absent in the dark, as the polymersome's permeability restricted the substrate (ethyl acetate) from accessing the enzyme, confirming the light-switchable nature of the PYRA-esterase nanoreactors. Prolonged irradiation led to an accelerated rise in absorbance, suggesting enhanced biocatalytic efficiency as BP- was depleted and BPH accumulated. To further investigate this effect, we monitored absorbance changes of the PYRA polymer in both organic solution and concentrated aqueous polymersome dispersions under alternating cycles of yellow light irradiation and darkness (Fig. 2E, Supplementary Fig. 8). The results indicated that the rate of isomerization from the blue triene-enol isomer to the colorless, more polar cyclopentenone isomer increased with higher irradiation intensities. Thus, higher light intensities led to greater membrane permeability changes, thereby enhancing the biocatalytic formation of acetic acid over time. Additionally, the rate of BPH formation correlated to light intensity (Fig. 2D). We hypothesized that under the initial alkaline conditions (pH = 8.5), the predominant BP⁻ species acted as a photomask, inhibiting the photoisomerization of membrane-bound PYRA, thereby reducing nanoreactor permeability despite yellow light irradiation. However, the low initial activity of PYRA-esterase under yellow light gradually facilitated the conversion of BP⁻ to its protonated form (BPH), diminishing its photomasking effect. This, in turn, led to increased PYRA photoisomerization, enhancing membrane permeability to ethyl acetate and accelerating the formation of acetic acid over 80 min (Supplementary Fig. 7A), therefore establishing an autocatalytic positive feedback loop. Importantly, following a period of deceleration of acetic acid formation (Supplementary Fig. 7A), the accumulation of BPH reached a plateau (Fig. 2D), indicating that acetic acid formation was self-limiting. Moreover, similar plateau values were observed across different light intensities. Importantly, while higher concentration of BP (0.4 mg·mL−1) generated UV-Vis signal saturation, lower concentration of BP (0.1 mg·mL−1) resulted in similar kinetic profiles as the intermediate (0.2 mg·mL−1) BP concentration, reaching plateau at similar irradiation times (Supplementary Fig. 9A). Thus, we hypothesized that the decreasing pH had shifted outside the optimal range for esterase activity, thereby limiting catalysis. To test this effect, free esterase was added to PYRA-esterase dispersions that had reached a plateau, at a concentration equivalent to that encapsulated within the nanoreactors (90 pmol), in the presence of BP and ethyl acetate (Fig. 2F and Supplementary Section 3). While the PYRA-esterase nanoreactors remained structurally intact following irradiation and pH cycling (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Figs. 10–11 and Supplementary Sections 4–5), the lack of further absorbance changes confirmed that esterase-mediated catalysis was suppressed, likely due to reaching a suboptimal pH (7.24). Consequently, the system transitioned from light-induced autocatalytic activation to pH-mediated inhibition, establishing a negative feedback loop that ultimately constrained acetic acid production. Interestingly, the hydrolysis of ethyl acetate proceeds at low overall conversion under these unbuffered aqueous conditions ( ≈ 10−3 %), generating only trace amounts of acetic acid; the observed pH shift therefore reflects changes in reaction rate rather than bulk turnover. To assess whether this behavior generalizes to substrates of differing hydrophobicity and molecular structure, we also evaluated the hydrolysis of phenyl acetate and ethyl propionate under identical conditions (Supplementary Fig. 12). The evolution of absorbance increased in the order phenyl acetate > ethyl propionate > ethyl acetate, consistent with enhanced nanoreactor uptake and hydrolysis rates for more hydrophobic esters. Nevertheless, we continued to use ethyl acetate as the reference substrate in further experiments, as it is a biologically relevant small molecule widely encountered in microbial metabolism and biotechnological processes.

PYRA photoswitch-driven negative feedback loops in presence of blue light

We aimed to showcase that the opposite feedback control mechanisms could be achieved by irradiation of PYRA-esterase nanoreactors with an orthogonal wavelength. The samples were irradiated with blue light (λ = 405 nm) in the same initial alkaline conditions as for yellow light experiments (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Fig. 13). As when exposed to yellow light, PYRA-esterase catalyzed the transformation of ethyl acetate to acetic acid inducing the protonation of the prevalent BP- (λmax = 590 nm) toward BPH (λmax = 430 nm) (Fig. 3B). Since there is a spectral overlap between BPH and the blue light spectral absorbing region of PYRA (Fig. 3C), an increase of BPH concentration resulted in competitive absorption of blue light, inducing a photomasking effect. Thus, to study the feedback-mediated behavior, dispersions of PYRA-esterase in the presence of ethyl acetate and BP (pH = 8.5) were continuously irradiated with blue light intensities of 1.88 mW·cm−2 and 1.54 mW·cm−2 (Fig. 3D). In contrast to experiments where PYRA-esterase was irradiated with yellow light, a drastic increase of absorbance under blue light irradiation was observed. While both blue and yellow light promoted the transformation of ethyl acetate to acetic acid that protonated BP- to BPH (Fig. 2B, 3B), under continuous blue light irradiation (Fig. 3D), this eventually led to a deceleration of absorbance increase, indicating that the biocatalysis became less efficient throughout the accumulation of BPH. This was in stark contrast to experiments irradiated with yellow light whereby a continuous decrease rate of change in absorbance over time was observed throughout the whole irradiation process (Supplementary Fig. 7A, B). Similarly to the experiments with yellow light irradiation, the photoisomerization of PYRA in both organic solution and concentrated aqueous polymersome dispersions after blue light irradiation cycles (Fig. 3E and Supplementary Fig. 14) resulted in the isomerization of PYRA to the colorless, more polar cyclopentenone isomer while being of higher magnitude with higher irradiation intensities, inducing higher changes in permeability. This resulted in increased enzymatic accumulation of acetic acid over time (Fig. 3D). The formation of the plateau also occurred at higher absorbance values for samples that were irradiated with higher intensities of blue light. We hypothesized that the increased accumulation of BPH in samples exposed to higher intensities of blue light was necessary to halt nanoreactor-mediated biocatalysis. Enhanced light penetration and increased photoisomerization of the PYRA moieties led to greater changes in membrane permeability, allowing ethyl acetate improved permeabilization to the encapsulated esterase and resulting in elevated conversion to acetic acid. This, in turn, promoted further formation of BPH, which acted as a blue light photomask (λ = 405 nm) to suppress additional PYRA photoisomerization, ultimately restoring membrane impermeability and arresting biocatalytic activity. Importantly, samples that contained lower concentration of BP (0.1 mg·mL⁻¹) were not interrupted by the formation of BPH during equivalent irradiation times indicating insufficient formation of blue light photomasking (Supplementary Fig. 8). This observation was further confirmed by spiking free enzyme into reactions that had reached stationary plateau via irradiation of blue light (0.1 mg·mL⁻¹) (Fig. 3F and Supplementary Section 4), resulting in a strong increase of absorbance, showcasing that the inhibition of biocatalytic activity of the nanoreactors was not due to a pH-mediated effect that decreased enzymatic activity, but was a light-induced negative feedback loop due to the formation of a photomask by the light-sensitive nanoreactors. These findings confirmed the presence of a light-responsive negative feedback mechanism within the DASA-based nanoreactor system, in which product formation inherently curtailed further catalytic activity. Importantly, the esterase retained its enzymatic function, while the BPH-induced photomasking selectively inhibited light-triggered catalysis, highlighting a light-gated feedback control over the biocatalytic process.

Alternation of feedback behavior in response to orthogonal blue and yellow light

The ability to generate distinct feedback responses to orthogonal external stimuli, such as two different wavelengths of light, represents a significant advance in multi-input sensing and processing within cell-mimicking systems. To explore this concept, we investigated whether PYRA-esterase could hydrolyze ethyl acetate and modulate feedback in a wavelength-dependent manner. Specifically, we evaluated the capacity of PYRA-esterase nanoreactors to reinitiate ethyl acetate transformation following catalytic interruption via light- or pH-mediated negative feedback loops. Dispersions containing BP (pH = 8.5) and PYRA-esterase were first irradiated with blue light (1.54 mW·cm⁻²), initiating ester hydrolysis and acetic acid release (Fig. 4A). This triggered a gradual accumulation of BPH, which plateaued over time due to the formation of a photostationary state (Fig. 3D, F). The buildup of BPH established a photoinduced negative feedback loop by reducing membrane permeability to ethyl acetate, resulting in a gradual deceleration of acetic acid formation (Supplementary Fig. 15A) inhibiting esterase activity. Subsequent yellow light irradiation (1.86 mW·cm⁻²) produced a sharp increase in absorbance (Fig. 4A) and an acceleration of acetic acid formation (Supplementary Fig. 15A), followed by a second plateau consistent with continuous yellow light controls (Fig. 2D, F). In contrast, reversing the irradiation sequence (yellow light followed by blue) did not restore activity, underscoring the directionality of the feedback control (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Fig. 15B). These results demonstrate that the kinetics and extent of the system’s response can be precisely tuned by the order of orthogonal light inputs, laying the foundation for programmable, stimulus-sequenced behavior in synthetic biochemical networks.

To further demonstrate the system’s capacity for sequential, wavelength-specific feedback, we evaluated its response to alternating orthogonal light inputs following restoration of the initial pH (Fig. 4C). Dispersions containing BP (pH = 7.9) and PYRA-esterase were first irradiated with blue light (2.40 mW·cm⁻²), triggering the hydrolysis of ethyl acetate and release of acetic acid. This led to the gradual and decelerated (Fig. 4D) accumulation of BPH and the establishment of a photostationary state at pH = 7.24, reflecting a photoinduced negative feedback loop in which decreased membrane permeability to ethyl acetate suppressed further esterase activity. After the solution was re-alkalinized to pH = 7.54, irradiation with yellow light (1.86 mW·cm⁻²) resumed. Under these conditions, BPH levels initially rose slowly (Fig. 4C), limited by the photomasking effect of BP⁻ at 590 nm, but then increased more rapidly as BP⁻ was depleted (Fig. 4D). This depletion enhanced membrane permeability to ethyl acetate, accelerating hydrolysis and feedback progression (Fig. 3D). Eventually, a second plateau was reached, coinciding with a pH drop sufficient to inhibit enzymatic activity once again. To assess both reversibility and input-sequence specificity, we performed the complementary experiment by inverting the irradiation order. The same solution was re-alkalinized and first exposed to yellow light (1.86 mW·cm⁻²), yielding a profile consistent with the previous yellow light response. Subsequent blue light irradiation (2.40 mW·cm⁻²) then produced a gradual deceleration in BPH accumulation, mirroring the previously observed blue light-induced feedback loop. These results confirm that the system exhibits reversible, sequence-dependent feedback behavior and establish that PYRA–esterase nanoreactors can process external stimuli in a programmable and adaptive manner, paving the way for life-like materials capable of logic-like decision-making in response to environmental inputs.

Stimulus-specific discrimination, a fundamental feature of sensory processing and signal integration in living systems, has yet to be replicated in synthetic cell-mimicking materials44–53. Here, we report a nanoreactor system capable of generating distinct, stimulus-dependent responses through wavelength-specific feedback loops. In dark, alkaline conditions, the nanoreactors remain in equilibrium. Upon irradiation, however, they shift to out-of-equilibrium states governed by two delayed, chemically encoded feedback mechanisms. Light controls membrane permeability to fuel, enabling conditional activation of an encapsulated esterase, while acid accumulation in the bulk phase drives further pH evolution. It is worth noting that the wavelength-dependent feedback dynamics reported here emerge at low substrate conversion ( ~ 10−3 %), underscoring the sensitivity of the system and demonstrating that meaningful regulation of reaction kinetics can be achieved without the need for bulk turnover. Crucially, the wavelength of light dictates whether the system engages in positive or negative feedback, leading to either acceleration or deceleration of acid production. Consistent with its inhibitory nature, the onset of negative feedback is comparatively slower than positive feedback. This kinetic asymmetry highlights the fundamentally different mechanistic requirements underlying activation versus inhibition in our system and frames the distinct temporal signatures generated by orthogonal stimuli. These programmable feedback loops allow the system to generate kinetically distinct outputs in response to orthogonal inputs, a key step toward synthetic materials with autonomous, adaptive behaviors. Unlike conventional enzyme-based systems, which typically yield a single predefined response54–57, our platform exhibits multiadaptive dynamics modulated by the nature of the stimulus. This offers a paradigm shift for out-of-equilibrium materials that more closely emulate the responsive complexity of living cells. Beyond signaling, the ability to modulate the rate of pH evolution has direct implications for processes such as crystallization, where kinetic control dictates crystal number and size. Furthermore, similar systems operate in biologically relevant buffers, paving the way for future integration with living cells12. By interfacing with cellular systems without genetic modification, these nanoreactors may enable on-demand, feedback-controlled modulation of cell behavior, advancing the development of truly interactive synthetic–biological hybrids. In a broader context the present system establishes fundamental design principles and paves the way toward sharper differentiation between multiple inputs. Although the current implementation employs an externally added photomask and a single model enzyme, the underlying architecture is inherently modular: the dye component and catalytic cargo can, in principle, be integrated, exchanged, or hierarchically compartmentalized in future designs without altering the fundamental wavelength-dependent feedback logic demonstrated here. While the current implementation demonstrates clear wavelength-specific responses, it does not yet achieve full stimulus discrimination (e.g., mechanical stress vs light); we explicitly acknowledge this limitation and view the present work as an essential intermediate step toward truly selective, stimulus-discriminating synthetic reaction networks. Moreover, effects arising from wavelength-dependent modulation of reactivity may become more pronounced with substrates of different molecular weight or polarity. Such substrates could exhibit more dramatic permeability-dependent rate modulation, further amplifying the responsiveness and discriminative capacity of future system iterations.

Methods

Materials and equipment

All materials were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich and used without further purification unless noted otherwise. Methanol was used to recrystallize azobisisobutyronitrile (AIBN). Biobeads S-X3 (600–14,000 g mol−1 range) were sourced from Bio-Rad Laboratories.

UV–vis measurements were performed using a Molecular Devices Spectramax M5 plate reader.

The hydrodynamic diameter (Dh) and polydispersity of the nanoreactors were assessed by DLS in ultrapure water. Each sample was measured in triplicate at 25 °C on a Malvern Zetasizer Ultra. Size distributions and polydispersity indices (PDI) were obtained in backscatter mode (173° angle), and the resulting data were analyzed with ZX Xplorer (version 2.2.0.147, Malvern Panalytical Ltd., Malvern, U.K.).

NMR spectra were acquired at 298 K on either a Bruker AVANCE 400 or a Bruker AvanceNEO 600 spectrometer. Residual solvent signals served as the internal reference for 1H NMR, while 19F NMR spectra were collected at 400 MHz.

Number-average molecular weight (Mn, GPC) and dispersity (Ð) of the polymers were determined on a 1260 Infinity II GPC MDS system using refractive index detection only. The instrument was fitted with a PSS GRAM guard column (8 × 50 mm, 10 µm) followed by two PSS GRAM linear columns (8 × 300 mm, 10 µm, 500–1,000,000 Da), with HPLC-grade DMF (0.075 wt% LiBr) flowing at 1 ml·min−1 and 40 °C as the mobile phase. Calibration relied on near-monodisperse poly(methyl methacrylate) standards (EasiVial, Agilent). To prepare each sample, polymer was dissolved at 2 mg·ml−1 in DMF containing 0.075% w/v LiBr and allowed to swell fully over 12 h prior to filtration through Millex-LRC 13 mm philic PTFE 0.45 µm filters (X100). A 100 µL injection volume was used, with analyses run at 25 °C against the poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) calibration curve.

ATR-FT-IR spectra were collected on a Perkin Elmer Spectrum 65 FT-IR spectrometer, with absorptions reported at their peak maxima (cm−1).

DASA light stimulation was achieved using a LEDA-Y LED array (λ = 590 nm) driven by a Teleopto LAD-1 light-emitting diode (LED) array driver.

A Mettler Toledo FiveEasy plus pH meter equipped with an InLab micro pH-electrode was used for pH measurements.

For cryo-TEM, 4 µl of each nanoreactor sample was deposited onto a holey carbon-coated grid (Lacey), blotted with Whatman 1 filter paper, and vitrified by plunging into liquid ethane at −180 °C with a Leica GP2 plunger (Leica Microsystems). The vitrified grids were loaded onto a Talos F200C electron microscope (FEI) via a Gatan 626 cryo-holder (GATAN). Imaging was performed at 200 kV under low-dose conditions (40 e⁻/Å2) with the specimen held at −175 °C and defocus set between −2 and 3 µm. Images were captured on a 4k x 4k Ceta CMOS camera, and ImageJ was used for brightness/contrast adjustment and for measuring inter-object distances.

Synthesis of pentafluorophenyl methacrylate (1)

This compound was synthesized by modification of a procedure published by Théato and colleagues58. Pentafluorophenol (20.0 g, 108.6 mmol, 1.0 eq.) was weighed and transferred to a round-bottomed flask and dissolved in dry dichloromethane. The solution was cooled to 0 °C in an ice bath, and 2,6-lutidine (12.9 mL, 111.2 mmol, 1.02 eq.) was added. Methacryloyl chloride (12.4 mL, 118.3 mmol, 1.09 eq.) was then added dropwise over 30 min, and the reaction mixture was maintained at 0 °C for a further 2.5 h, after which it was allowed to warm to room temperature and stirred overnight. The lutidine hydrochloride salt was removed by filtration through filter paper, and the organic filtrate was washed sequentially with 1 M HCl (100 mL), water (100 mL), and brine (100 mL). The solvent was removed under reduced pressure, and the crude product was purified by column chromatography on aluminum oxide, eluting with hexane, to afford pentafluorophenyl methacrylate as a colorless oil (67% yield).

Synthesis of poly(ethylene glycol)-b-(poly(hexyl acrylate)-co-poly(pentafluorophenyl methacrylate)) (PEG b-(PHMA-co-PPFPMA) (2)

This compound was synthesized according to previously published protocols59,60. Chain extension was carried out using reversible addition–fragmentation chain-transfer (RAFT) radical polymerization on the macro chain transfer agent (macroCTA), poly(ethylene glycol)−4-cyano-4-(phenylcarbonothioylthio)pentanoate (PEG-CPADB), to obtain an amphiphilic diblock copolymer incorporating randomly distributed activated ester groups. A mixture of PEG-CPADB (121.8 mg, 48.7 μmol) and AIBN (1 mg, 6.09 μmol) was prepared in 0.77 mL of 1,4-dioxane. In parallel, hexyl methacrylate (HMA) was purified to remove inhibitors by passing it through a basic alumina column to eliminate monomethyl ether hydroquinone. The initiator/macroCTA solution was then combined with pentafluorophenyl methacrylate (PFPMA; 44 μL, 61 mg, 0.24 mmol) (1) and the purified HMA (0.432 mL, 0.373 g, 2.19 mmol). The resulting reaction mixture was degassed under nitrogen for 1 hour to eliminate dissolved oxygen. Polymerization was performed using a feed ratio of initiator:CTA:monomer = 1:8:400, with a monomer composition of HMA:PFPMA = 9:1 by molar ratio. The reaction proceeded at 90 °C for 2 h, after which it was quenched by exposure to air. To purify the polymer, the reaction mixture was precipitated into a 60:40 methanol:water (v/v) solution. The precipitated material was collected by centrifugation at 5000 g for 15 min at room temperature. To further remove unreacted monomer, the pellet was redissolved in 2 mL of THF, and a small amount of Sudan Blue II was added as a tracking dye for preparative size-exclusion chromatography (SEC). SEC purification was carried out using Biobeads S-X3 resin (fractionation range: 600–14,000 g·mol⁻¹) with inhibitor-free THF as the eluent. The polymer fraction was collected by monitoring elution prior to the appearance of the dye, then concentrated under reduced pressure. The purified product was subsequently dried in a vacuum oven at 40 °C overnight, yielding 435 mg of purified polymer (78% yield).

Removal of the chain-transfer agent end group from PEG-b-(PHMA-co-PPFPMA)

The RAFT end group was cleaved from PEG-b-(PHMA-co-PPFPMA) following a modified thermal treatment protocol inspired by prior work59–61. Briefly, 360 mg of the copolymer was dissolved in 3 mL of DMF alongside AIBN (200 mg, 1.2 mmol) in a round-bottom flask. The solution was degassed under a gentle nitrogen stream for 1 h to eliminate oxygen and then sealed under a nitrogen atmosphere to maintain inert conditions. Polymer end-group removal was initiated by heating the reaction mixture in an 80 °C oil bath for 4 h, allowing for thermal cleavage of the RAFT agent. After completion, the reaction mixture was cooled to room temperature. The crude polymer was purified by preparative size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) using Biobeads S-X3 resin (fractionation range: 600–14,000 g·mol⁻¹) as the stationary phase and inhibitor-free THF as the mobile phase. A small amount of Sudan Blue II was introduced as a tracking dye to identify the elution of small molecules. The purified polymer (3) was collected prior to the appearance of the dye fraction, then concentrated under reduced pressure. Finally, the polymer was dried overnight in a vacuum oven at 40 °C to yield the RAFT-cleaved copolymer.

Synthesis of the second generation DASA donor precursor N-(4-methoxyphenyl)−1,3-diaminopropane (4)

The compound was synthesized following a procedure published by Han et al62. To a 1.75 mL vial equipped with a small stirrer bar were added CuBr (15 mg), powdered KOH (112 mg, 2.0 mmol), and 1,3-propanediamine (250 µL, 3.37 mmol) at room temperature. The mixture was stirred for 1 min, after which 4-iodoanisole (234 mg, 1.0 mmol) was added. The reaction was stirred overnight at room temperature. The reaction mixture was diluted with DCM (25 mL) and filtered through filter paper into a separating funnel (50 mL). The reaction vial was rinsed with water (3 × 5 mL) and the washings filtered into the same separating funnel. The aqueous and organic layers were separated, and the aqueous layer was extracted with DCM (3 × 20 mL). The combined organic layers were dried over anhydrous magnesium sulfate, filtered, and concentrated under reduced pressure to afford a brown oil. The crude product was dissolved in DCM (10 mL) and anhydrous 2 M HCl in diethyl ether was added dropwise, producing a white precipitate. The precipitate was collected by filtration and washed with cold diethyl ether (2 × 3 mL) to afford the title compound as a light brown solid (isolated as the HCl salt, 63% yield).

Synthesis of aromatic amine-functionalized diblock copolymer

To introduce aromatic amine functionality, PEG-b-(PHMA-co-PPFPMA) capped with an AIBN-derived end group was reacted with N-(4-methoxyphenyl)−1,3-diaminopropane59,60. Specifically, the polymer was dissolved in 3 mL of anhydrous DMF alongside the amine reagent (100 mg, 0.55 mmol) and triethylamine (0.10 mL, 0.73 g, 1 mmol), which served to neutralize the resulting pentafluorophenol byproduct. The solution was degassed under a gentle nitrogen stream for 30 min to remove dissolved oxygen. The mixture was then sealed and incubated at 60 °C under inert atmosphere for 7 days, allowing sufficient time for nucleophilic substitution of the activated ester moieties by the aromatic diamine. Following the reaction, the product was purified via preparative SEC using Biobeads S-X3 resin (fractionation range: 600–14,000 g·mol⁻¹) and inhibitor-free THF as the eluent. A trace amount of Sudan Blue II was used as a visible marker to differentiate small-molecule fractions from polymeric material. Elution was monitored, and fractions collected prior to the appearance of the dye were pooled and concentrated under reduced pressure. The purified polymer (5) was then dried in a vacuum oven at 40 °C overnight, yielding the functionalized diblock copolymer.

Synthesis of 4-(furan-2-ylmethylene)−2,5-diphenyl-2,4-dihydro-3H-pyrazol-3-one (6)

The furan adduct was synthesized according to a method described by Rifaie-Graham et al11. Synthesis of 2,5-diphenyl-2,4-dihydro-3H-pyrazol-3-one: Phenylhydrazine (2.36 mL, 24.0 mmol, 1.0 eq.) and ethyl benzoylacetate (4.16 mL, 24.0 mmol, 1.0 eq.) were combined in a round-bottomed flask, and acetic acid (0.4 mL) was added as a catalyst. The reaction mixture was heated to 100 °C under reflux with a nitrogen atmosphere. Solidification of the mixture was observed after approximately 3 minutes; heating was continued at 100 °C for a further 1 h, after which TLC analysis confirmed complete consumption of starting materials (single spot, heptane/ethyl acetate 3:2). The crude solid was recrystallized from ethanol, washed with cold ethanol, and dried under vacuum to afford the title compound as white crystals (4.21 g, 17.7 mmol, 72%). Synthesis of 4-(furan-2-ylmethylene)−2,5-diphenyl-2,4-dihydro-3H-pyrazol-3-one (Knoevenagel condensation): 2,5-Diphenyl-2,4-dihydro-3H-pyrazol-3-one (1.0 g, 4.2 mmol, 1.0 eq.) and L-proline (49 mg, 0.42 mmol, 0.1 eq.) were dissolved in ethanol (30 mL) in a round-bottomed flask equipped with a magnetic stirrer. Furfural (0.70 mL, 8.45 mmol, 2.0 eq.) was introduced and the mixture was heated to reflux under a nitrogen atmosphere for 30 minutes. The mixture was then cooled to −20 °C to induce precipitation, and the resulting solid was collected by suction filtration and washed with ice-cold ethanol. The red crystalline solid was dissolved in DCM (30 mL) and passed through a short pad of silica (approximately 4 cm) eluting with DCM. Removal of the solvent under reduced pressure afforded 4-(furan-2-ylmethylene)−2,5-diphenyl-2,4-dihydro-3H-pyrazol-3-one as a red solid (87% yield).

Functionalization of the aromatic amine diblock copolymer with DASA moieties

This compound was synthesized according to a previously reported procedure59,60. The secondary aromatic amine groups on the functionalized diblock copolymer (5) were further reacted with a pyrazolone-derived furan adduct to generate the corresponding DASA-conjugated copolymer (7). In a typical reaction, 100 mg of the amine-bearing polymer (approximately 12 μmol) was dissolved in a solution of the furan adduct (0.9 M) in 3 mL of a 1:9 HFIP:DCM solution in a round-bottom flask. The flask was sealed with a rubber septum to maintain anhydrous conditions, and the mixture was stirred at room temperature for 7 days, allowing for the complete formation of the DASA chromophores via condensation. Upon completion of the reaction, excess furan adduct and small molecule byproducts were removed by preparative SEC using Biobeads S-X3 resin (molecular weight range: 600–14,000 g·mol⁻¹) and inhibitor-free THF as the eluent. In this case, no tracking dye was required, as the distinct colors of the DASA-modified polymer and unreacted furan adduct enabled visual monitoring of the separation. The purified product was then concentrated under reduced pressure and dried overnight at 40 °C in a vacuum oven, affording the final DASA-functionalized diblock copolymer.

Preparation of PYRA–esterase nanoreactors

To assemble PYRA–esterase nanoreactors, 1 mg (approximately 0.11 μmol) of the pyrazolone-functionalized DASA diblock copolymer (PYRA polymer) was weighed into an HPLC vial and dissolved in 0.1 mL of 1,4-dioxane. The self-assembly was carried out under low-light conditions to stabilize PYRA in its triene-enol isomer, the more hydrophobic and colored form, which facilitates nanoparticle formation. Separately, 1 mg of esterase from porcine liver (5.95 nmol) was dissolved in 1 mL of ultrapure water in a second HPLC vial equipped with a magnetic stir bar. While magnetically stirring, the PYRA polymer solution was added gradually in 10-μL increments to the enzyme solution to allow for uniform encapsulation and nanoreactor formation. After complete addition, the dispersion was stirred for an additional 5 min. To enzymatically remove any non-encapsulated esterase, the mixture was transferred to a third HPLC vial containing 1 mg of trypsin (42 nmol) and stored in the fridge for 1 week. The resulting PYRA–esterase nanoreactors were stored at 4 °C for subsequent characterization and application. To facilitate cryo-TEM imaging, 4 mg (2.2·10−1 μmol) of the of the DASA block copolymer was used instead of 1 mg.

Photoswitching of PYRA polymer in THF

A solution of the PYRA polymer in tetrahydrofuran (THF) was prepared at a concentration of 0.14 mg·mL⁻¹ and transferred into a 1 mL quartz cuvette. Absorbance measurements at 590 nm were performed using a SpectraMax M5 plate reader (Molecular Devices) equipped with a cuvette holder. After baseline recording, samples were irradiated with yellow light (590 nm) for 15 s at varying intensities: 0.10, 0.19, and 0.57 mW·cm⁻². Thermal back-isomerization was subsequently monitored over 15 min, with absorbance recorded every 10 s. For blue light irradiation, samples were exposed to 405 nm light at intensities of 1.06, 1.54, and 3.55 mW·cm⁻². The light–dark cycle was repeated three times for each sample. Absorbance values were normalized to the initial peak absorbance of the first cycle to correct for potential variations. Data from the three switching cycles were compiled sequentially for comparative analysis.

Photoswitching of PYRA polymer in aqueous dispersions

For experiments in aqueous conditions, tetrahydrofuran (THF) was replaced with ultrapure water, and the PYRA polymer was dispersed at a concentration of 0.2 mg·mL⁻¹. A 300 μL aliquot of the dispersion was transferred into a non-binding polystyrene 96-well microplate (Corning). The same measurement protocol used for the organic phase was followed. Samples were irradiated at 590 nm using light intensities of 0.10, 0.19, and 0.57 mW·cm⁻² for 10 min. After irradiation, thermal recovery was monitored by recording absorbance every 10 s for 30 min. For 405 nm irradiation, samples were exposed to light intensities of 1.06, 1.54, and 1.88 mW·cm⁻² for 15 min between each of the three cycles. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and data were processed using the same normalization and cycle-tracking procedure as described for the organic phase measurements.

Evaluation of feedback control by PYRA–esterase nanoreactors

Unless otherwise stated, 2 µL of PYRA-esterase nanoreactors were added to 300 µL of a 0.2 mg·mL⁻¹ BP solution (0.37 µmol·mL⁻¹) saturated with ethyl acetate in a 96-well microplate (Corning). Samples were irradiated with either yellow light (590 nm) or blue light (405 nm). Following an initial UV–vis absorbance measurement, subsequent measurements were recorded every 10 min at 405 nm. All experiments were conducted in triplicate.
