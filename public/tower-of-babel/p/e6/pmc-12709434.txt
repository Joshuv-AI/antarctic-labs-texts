Reactive oxygen species counteract zebrafish wound contraction and promote wound healing
Authors: Chang Ding, Linlin Li, Yueyang Wang, Hong-Anh A. Nguyen, Deva D. Chan, David M. Umulis, Adrian T. Buganza, Qing Deng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are second messengers that drive wound closure. However, the mechanism by which ROS regulate wound contraction to facilitate wound healing remains unclear. Here, we report that ROS counteract wound contraction by inhibiting the phosphorylation of myosin regulatory light chain. Acute ROS inhibition, through pharmacological perturbations, disturbs wound relaxation, delays wound closure, and impairs regrowth after amputation. Moreover, actomyosin inhibition relaxes tailfin contraction without impairing wound closure or regrowth. Overcontraction, on the other hand, impedes wound closure. Meanwhile, chronic depletion of epithelial ROS during embryonic development, achieved through morpholino-mediated knockdown of the duox gene, alters tissue stiffness, as measured using atomic force microscopy-based nanoindentation. Despite a reduced contraction force, the wound also appears to be overcontracted, with delayed healing and regrowth. An in silico linear elasticity simulation to calculate the second principal stress based on node-wise prescribed displacement recapitulated the contraction dynamics during acute and chronic ROS inhibition. Together, our results provide a novel understanding of how ROS facilitate wound closure, a process instrumental in restoring tissue integrity and maintaining homeostasis.

Significance

This study demonstrates that reactive oxygen species regulate wound kinetics, ultimately facilitating efficient wound closure. Acute inhibition of reactive oxygen species with pharmacological inhibitors leads to prolonged wound contraction, likely due to the activation of actomyosin. Meanwhile, long-term reactive oxygen species depletion resulting from morpholino treatment also leads to wound overcontraction due to a significant reduction in tissue rigidity and elasticity. Furthermore, this study reveals that contraction is not required for wound healing. Additionally, excessive wound contraction can hinder the wound healing process. These findings challenge the conventional view that actomyosin ring-mediated contraction provides the primary driving force for wound closure, offering novel insights into the context-dependent role of contraction in wound healing.

Introduction

Wound closure is essential for reestablishing the epithelia’s barrier function after injury (1). Epithelial wounds in aquatic environments, such as those in the zebrafish tailfin, and injuries in the mucosal lining of the upper respiratory and digestive tracts in mammals, are exposed to excessively high microbial burdens in environmental fluids (2). An efficient wound repair mechanism is essential for the survival of aquatic species and the homeostasis at mucosal surfaces.

Upon wounding, multiple signals, including electric potential differences, reduced junction signals, osmolarity differences, and chemical signals, coordinate to close the wound (3,4,5). Most wound signals converge on the second messenger, calcium. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are other second messengers that facilitate wound healing in various species, including Drosophila and zebrafish (6,7). In both systems, calcium triggers H2O2 accumulation at the wound edge within 10 min postamputation (mpa) through the activation of NADPH oxidase (NOX) (6,7,8).

ROS, including superoxide, hydroxyl radical, hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), and singlet oxygen, are redox-sensitive species that orchestrate complex physiological signaling processes (9). Among all ROS species, H2O2 is the most relevant in conveying long-range signals due to its longer half-life, higher permeability, and significantly reduced cellular toxicity compared with other ROS species (10,11,12). H2O2 facilitates wound healing by mediating various wound-signaling pathways (13,14,15,16). Firstly, H2O2 regulates the activation of the transcription factor (TF) nuclear factor erythroid-derived 2-like 2 (Nrf2), promoting re-epithelialization (15,16). Secondly, H2O2 leads to lipid peroxidation, which facilitates cell proliferation (14). Upon wounding, a low level of 4-hydroxynonenal is synthesized through the oxidation of H2O2, which phosphorylates the epidermal growth factor receptor and activates downstream proliferation-related kinases, such as Akt and ERK 1/2, to promote wound healing. Thirdly, H2O2 may directly modulate kinase activities, such as phosphorylating focal adhesion kinase (FAK), to facilitate cell adhesion and migration during wound closure (13). Fourthly, H2O2 can directly or indirectly regulate actin cytoskeleton dynamics, either through the direct oxidation of actin or the regulation of gene expression of actin-binding proteins, including profilin, ARP-3, MRLC, and 14-3-3ζ, thereby inducing wound closure (17). It is worth noting that the H2O2 level is tightly regulated during the wound healing process. Excessive H2O2 levels elevate inflammatory TF activation, such as activator protein 1 (AP-1) and nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB), leading to extracellular matrix protein degradation and delayed wound healing (18,19,20,21). A too low level of H2O2 also impedes wound healing by impairing fibroblast function (22). However, the mechanisms underlying how H2O2 promotes mucosal wound healing in multicellular tissue remain to be elucidated, mainly due to the complexity of the signaling networks.

The zebrafish larvae tailfin model has been utilized to characterize the role of H2O2 in mucosal wound healing. It mimics a similar microbial selection pressure in the surrounding aquatic environments (2). In addition, the high regenerative ability and transparency of the tissue make it an ideal model for studying wound closure and tissue regeneration (23). In this model, H2O2 is primarily generated from the enzyme Dual oxidase (Duox), an isoform of NADPH oxidase (7,8). Oliveira et al. demonstrated that calcium activates Duox and produces H2O2 to trigger NF-κB signaling through the p2yR/PLC/calcium signaling pathway for wound closure (6). Similarly, injury-generated H2O2 at the tailfin induces Fynb activation, a family member of Src family kinase, for wound regeneration (24). Although previous studies have suggested that H2O2 plays a pivotal role in wound closure, the understanding of how H2O2 regulates cytoskeleton compartments to seal the wound remains limited.

Here, we report a novel perspective on the function of ROS in facilitating wound healing, whereby ROS relax the wound mechanically to promote wound closure. The zebrafish fin fold amputated wound contracts for 20–30 min postwounding, and then gradually relaxes to its initial width (5). The formation of the actomyosin ring provides the main driving force for wound contraction and has long been regarded as beneficial for wound closure (25,26,27,28,29). Surprisingly, we found that the contraction is dispensable for the closure of amputation wounds. ROS relaxes wounds to prevent overcontraction, which impedes wound closure. Together, our work reveals that ROS promote relaxation and closure of amputation wounds, demonstrating a context-dependent role of actomyosin in mediating mucosal wound closure, which is instrumental in our understanding of wound biology.

Materials and methods

Animals and stable line generation

The zebrafish experiment was conducted in accordance with internationally accepted standards. The Animal Care and Use Protocol was approved by the Purdue Animal Care and Use Committee, adhering to the Guidelines for the Use of Zebrafish in the NIH Intramural Research Program (protocol number: 1401001018). MATLAB and the sampsizepwr function were used to calculate the sample sizes required for each experiment, based on conservative estimates of variability in the controls for each type of experiment, with a power of 0.9 (significance level of 0.05) in a two-sample t-test. Data were quantified blindly by an investigator who was not involved in data collection.

For generating stable zebrafish lines, microinjections of fish embryos were performed by injecting 1 nL of a mixture containing 25 ng/μL plasmids and 35 ng/μL Tol2 transposase mRNA in an isotonic solution into the cytoplasm of embryos at the one-cell stage. The stable lines were generated as described previously (30). At least two founders (F0) were obtained for each line. To minimize artifacts associated with random insertion sites, experiments were conducted using F2 larvae derived from F1 fish produced by multiple founders.

Molecular cloning

For gene expression in the fish epidermal layer of epithelia, a Tol2-krt4 vector reported previously was used in this study (31). Target genes were inserted into the Tol2 backbone, which contains the krt4 promoter and SV40 polyA for ubiquitous expression. The MRLC2 gene was cloned from the pTK91_GFP-MRLC2 plasmid (Addgene, Watertown, Massachusetts, no. 46355). The mScarlet fragment used in fish was cloned from our previous plasmid constructs in the lab and inserted into the Tol2-krt4 vector. Similarly, the timer gene was cloned from the construct pMitoTimer (Addgene, no. 52659) and fused into the Tol2 backbone, which contains the krt4 promoter and SV40 polyA. The In-Fusion cloning (In-Fusion HD Cloning Plus Kit, Clontech, San Jose, California) was used for fragment fusion. The incorporated primers were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies (Coralville, IA) and are listed as follows:

mrlc2-F: 5′- TACAAGTGAGCGGCCGCGCGCCACCATGTCGAGCAAACGCGCC -3′; mrlc2-R: 5′- GGCGACCGGTGGATCCCTTGTACAGCTCGTCCATGCC -3′; timer-F: 5′- GACACGCAGAGGATCCTCGCCACCATGGCCACAAC -3′; timer-R: 5′-ACCGCGGTGGCGGCCGCCTACAGGAACAGGTGGTGGCG -3′.

Inhibitor treatments and ROS intensity measurements

Inhibitors were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare stock solutions, which were then further diluted in E3 medium (5 mM NaCl, 0.17 mM KCl, 0.33 mM CaCl2·2H2O, 0.33 mM MgSO4·7H2O) to achieve working concentrations. DMSO was utilized as a vehicle control, with its volume matched to that of each corresponding treatment. Diphenyleneiodonium chloride (DPI) (Sigma-Aldrich, no. A7250) and N-acetyl-L-cysteine (NAC) (Cayman, no. 20261) were used as ROS inhibitors, with final concentrations of 100 and 200 μM, respectively (32,33,34). A final concentration of 200 μM ROCKIOUT (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, Missouri, no. 555553), ML-7 (Cayman, Ann Arbor, Michigan, no. 11801), or 25 μM (S)-4′-nitro-blebbistatin (Bleb) (Cayman, no. 24171) was incorporated to disrupt actin dynamics (35,36). The final concentration of DMSO for all inhibitor treatments is as follows: DPI and NAC, 0.5%; ROCKOUT and ML-7, 0.27%; Bleb, 0.05%). Larvae were pretreated with the inhibitor for 30–40 min before tailfin amputation and maintained in the inhibitor for wound dynamics experiments. Inhibitors were washed out 1 h postamputation for regeneration assays, a final concentration of 5 μM pentafluorobenzene sulfonyl fluorescein (Cayman, no. 10005983) was utilized for ROS intensity measurements, which was also dissolved in DMSO. Embryos were pretreated with the ROS dye 30 min before amputation and were washed with E3 buffer three times before amputation. The intensities for each group were measured immediately after amputation and 20 min postamputation with a Zeiss Axio Zoom V16 fluorescent microscope. Emission was excited with a 470/40 band-pass filter and acquired with a 525/50 band-pass filter.

Morpholino injections and RT-PCR

Morpholino (MO) oligonucleotides (Gene Tools, Philomath, Oregon) in Danieau buffer (58 mM NaCl, 0.7 mM KCl, 0.4 mM MgSO4, 0.6 mM Ca(NO3)2, and 5.0 mM HEPES [pH 7.1–7.3]) were injected (3 nL) into one-cell-stage embryos. For duox knockdown, 100 μM duox splice MO1 (5′-AGTGAATTAGAGAAATGCACCTTTT-3′) was used (8). For p53 knockdown, 300 μM p53 MO (5′-GCGCCATTGCTTTGCAAGAATTG-3′) was used (8). For morphotyping of the splicing MOs, RNA was prepared from 3-dpf larvae using the RNeasy kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany), and one-step RT-PCR was performed using the SuperScript III One-Step RT-PCR System with Platinum Taq DNA Polymerase kit (ThermoFisher, Waltham, Massachusetts). The oligonucleotide sequences used for RT-PCR were as follows: duox forward: 5′-CCACAATTACCTGGCCTCCA-3′; duox reverse: 5′-AGGTGTTGCATAAACGCAGG-3 ′.

Wound dynamics and regeneration assays

The tailfin transaction was carried out on a 3-pdf larva using a razor blade at a standardized location proximal to the caudal notochord to ensure comparable wound sizes across samples, as described previously (24). For wound dynamics assays, the width of the tailfin wound was measured 1 h postamputation and normalized to the initial tailfin width immediately after amputation (approximately 2 min postamputation). Imaging was conducted at 1-min intervals with bright-field microscopy (Zeiss Axio Zoom V16, Oberkochen, Germany). For regeneration assays, regrowth length was quantified by measuring the distance between the caudal tip of the notochord and the amputated wound at 0, 1, and 2 days after wounding. Regrowth measurements were performed at approximately the same time each day after amputation to minimize variability.

Wound closure assay

The Tg(krt4: NLS-timer)pu50 embryos were fixed at 0 and 30 mpa in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at 4°C overnight, and the fixed samples were imaged with a Zeiss LSM 880 Inverted Confocal microscope. The Z-stack images were then 3D-rendered in Imaris software to acquire the x-z plane of the amputated wound, and the relative distances between nuclei at the wound edge and the wound central plane were calculated utilizing the codes deposited at GitHub: https://gist.github.com/ding275/7bee9327f85b79c4d9c732969bc6b29b#file-ros-jcs-2025-linear-regression-nucleus-ipynb.

Dextran entry assay

The embryos at 3 dpf were incubated with 2 mg/mL 10 kDa dextran (Alexa Fluor 488; ThermoFisher, no. D22910) at 0, 7, 17, or 27 min postamputation, as described previously (37,38). The inhibitor-treated groups were pretreated with inhibitors 30–40 min postamputation, and the inhibitor concentration was maintained in the dextran solution. After incubating embryos with dextran for 3 min, the dextran was washed twice with Tricane containing E3. Next, the embryos were fixed with 4% PFA overnight, and the dextran penetration was assessed using confocal microscopy.

H2O2 supplement

Morpholino-treated embryos at 3 dpf were transferred to E3 containing 3 mM H2O2 immediately postamputation, followed by a wound dynamics assay. Similarly, embryos at 3 dpf were treated with DPI for 30–40 min and then transferred to E3 containing DPI and 3 mM H2O2 immediately after amputation.

Immunofluorescence staining

Fish embryos were fixed in 4% PFA overnight at 4°C. After fixation, embryos were permeabilized by proteinase K (10 mg/mL, 15 min for embryos at 3 dpf), and fixed again with 4% PFA for 15 min at RT. Embryos were washed three times with PBS-Tween 20 (0.1%) for 5 min each on the rocker, and then blocked in PBS containing 0.3% Triton X-100 and 4% BSA for 3 h at room temperature. Staining was performed in blocking buffer with p-MRLC primary antibody (1:50 dilution) (Cell Signaling, Danvers, Massachusetts, no. 3675) overnight at 4°C, followed by three washes with PBS containing 0.3% Triton X-100 (15 min each on the rocker) as described previously (39). For nuclear staining and F-actin labeling, DAPI (ThermoFisher, no. D21490) and phalloidin (ThermoFisher, no. A12380) were added to the secondary antibody mixture (ThermoFisher, no. A-21236) and incubated at room temperature for 4 h, followed by three washes with PBS containing 0.3% Triton X-100. Confocal imaging was performed using a Zeiss LSM 880 Inverted Confocal microscope with a 20× objective (N/A 0.8).

Fluorescence confocal microscopy

Fluorescent microscopy imaging of duox morphant was obtained with a Nikon (Tokyo, Japan) A1R fluorescent confocal microscope. Larvae at 3 dpf were settled on a glass-bottom dish (Mattke, Freiburg im Breisgau, Germany, no. P35G-1.5-10C) and mounted with 1% low-melt agarose as described previously (40). Z-Stack imaging was performed at 28°C (41). The far-red (membrane label) channel and red (myosin light chain) channel were acquired sequentially using 640- and 561-nm lasers at 3% power, with a Nikon oil immersion 60× N/A 1.40 objective.

Fluorescent microscopy imaging data from immunofluorescent staining and wound closure assays were acquired using a Zeiss LSM 880 Inverted Confocal microscope with a 20× N/A 0.8 objective at 2% power. For immunostaining Z-stack imaging, the blue, red, and far-red channels were acquired using the 405-, 561-, and 641-nm lasers, respectively. For wound closure assay Z-stack imaging, the green channel was acquired with a 488-nm laser.

Atomic force microscopy

An Asylum Research MFP3d Bio AFM system (Santa Barbara, CA) was used to measure the effective modulus of p53 control and duox morphants, as well as the tissue stiffness and stress approximation at the wound edge of the DMSO control or Bleb-treated group. A Nanoandmore PPP-CONTAuD-10 probe (Watsonville, CA) with a 450-μm spherical probe and 0.2-N/m nominal stiffness cantilever was used for these experiments. The optical lever sensitivity of the cantilever was calibrated with a static force-displacement curve, which was performed on the part of the polystyrene Petri dish not covered by the embryos. The cantilever stiffness was determined using the thermal tuning method in the E3 medium (42). Atomic force microscopy (AFM) indentation experiments were performed using MO-injected embryos at a constant temperature of 28°C. For imaging, a sufficient amount of E3 buffer was used to maintain embryo viability, and a 200-μL drop of E3 was placed on the AFM cantilever tip to avoid the formation of air bubbles between the cantilever holder and the sample. Force-displacement curves were acquired for three biological replicates of the MO-injected embryo at three positions along the tailfin region. Three indentations were taken at each position for each fish. Force spectroscopy was conducted using a single loading-unloading cycle, with the trigger threshold set at 50 nN based on the cantilever deflection. To account for stress relaxation, a minimum interval of 45 s was maintained between successive indentations within the same embryo. For each measurement location, the force-displacement curve calculated during the cantilever’s approach phase was used to determine the effective modulus. This calculation also incorporated the cantilever’s material characteristics and applied the Hertzian contact model for a spherical indenter interacting with a flat surface (43). Based on this model, the force measured during indentation (F) is correlated with cantilever indentation (δ) as described by Johnson (44).F=4ERtip1/23(1−ν2)δ32where ν is the Poisson’s ratio (ν = 0.5, assumed for biological samples), E is the effective elastic modulus, and Rtip is the radius of the spherical cantilever tip (45,46). The embryo is assumed to be incompressible with μ = 0.5. The force curves were divided into two regions: one before the contact point of the cantilever tip with the tissue and the other after contact. Force data after contact with the embryo was used to calculate the effective modulus.

Here, ρ is the (constant) mass density, u is the displacement vector, σ is the Cauchy stress tensor, and f is the body force per unit volume.

Image data analysis

Image analysis was performed using Imaris software and Fiji/ImageJ (https://imagej.net/ij/). For immunofluorescent staining images and optogenetic imaging data, all images at the same time point of an embryo tailfin were projected to 2D for fluorescence intensity measurements. The images were background subtracted, and the mean intensity values were acquired using Fiji/ImageJ. The myosin distribution and cell morphology data were analyzed using Fiji/ImageJ and Imaris software. The images were Z-projected, background subtracted, and median filtered in Fiji/ImageJ. The perimeters and aspect ratios were measured directly using Imaris software. The wound closure data were 3D rendered with Imaris (RRID: SCR_007370) software, and nuclei were masked using the built-in functions as described.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Prism 6 (GraphPad). To determine the statistical significance of differences between groups, an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test or ANOVA was used. The Shapiro-Wilk test was conducted to assess the normality of the data. The Turkey test was performed after ANOVA analysis when the data met the assumption of normality, while Dunn’s test was conducted after a nonparametric test. Similarly, a Student's t-test was performed when the data met the assumption of normality, while the Mann-Whitney test was conducted as a nonparametric test. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

ROS promote wound relaxation

To address the function of ROS in regulating wound contraction, we incorporated a NOX inhibitor, diphenylene iodonium (DPI), a ROS scavenger, NAC, and duox MO to reduce ROS (Fig. 1). Pretreatment with DPI and NAC significantly reduced H2O2 level at the wound edge at 20 min postamputation (mpa), as reflected by the increase in pentafluorobenzene sulfonyl fluorescein, which has a higher selectivity of H2O2 over other ROS species (Figs. 1, A, B, and S1) (8). Consistent with previous observations, the DMSO control contracted for 15 mpa and gradually relaxed to its initial tailfin width 1 h after wounding (5). However, the tailfin of both DPI-treated and NAC-treated larvae continued to contract without returning to the relaxed tailfin width (Figs. 1, C–E and S2; Video S1). Similarly, duox morphants were defective in duox pre-mRNA splicing and displayed significantly reduced H2O2 production at the wound edge (Fig. 1, F–H). In addition, the duox morphants displayed more robust and prolonged wound contraction compared with the p53 morphant control (Fig. 1, I and J; Video S2). To demonstrate whether the impaired wound relaxation observed was attributable to the loss of H2O2, we supplemented exogenous H2O2 in both conditions (Fig. 2; Videos S3 and S4). H2O2 supplement significantly rescued the overcontraction in the DPI-treated group and the duox MO-injected groups compared with their respective control, indicating that H2O2 is required for wound relaxation (Fig. 2, C and F). ROS are required for wound closure and regrowth

To determine whether ROS regulate wound closure, we utilized a transgenic line Tg(krt4: NLS-timer)pu50 to quantify the wound opening by measuring the distances of nuclei of the enveloping epithelial layer to the rendered wound center at the wound edge (Fig. 3 A). We observed reduced wound opening at 30 mpa compared with 0 mpa (Fig. 3, B, C, and E), consistent with the previous work; an amputated wound takes approximately 30 min to seal (47). In contrast, DPI treatment or duox knockdown expanded the wound opening at 30 mpa (Fig. 3, B–F). In addition, we performed a dextran entry assay to assess barrier function in both the DPI-treated and duox knockdown conditions and their respective controls, as described previously (48). In our hands, the majority of the wound is sealed by 20 min postwounding. Complete wound closure is achieved by 30 min, as indicated by the absence of dextran penetration into the tissue (Fig. S4 A). Consistent with the morphological wound measurements, we found that, at 30 min postwounding, both DPI-treated wounds and duox MO-treated wounds allowed dextran penetration (Fig. 3, G and H). Together, these results indicate that ROS depletion impairs wound closure. We measured regrowth after amputation to further determine the physiological consequence (Fig. 3 I). DPI was washed out at 1 hpa to prevent prolonged ROS inhibition. Again, DPI treatment or duox knockdown significantly impaired regrowth compared with their control groups at 2 days postamputation (dpa), consistent with the wound closure defect (Fig. 3, J and K). However, the reduced regrowth in duox morphants may partly result from their general developmental delay (Fig. 5). Furthermore, duox knockdown increased mortality at 2 dpa, whereas no mortality was observed in the controls (Fig. 3 L). Contraction is not required for wound closure

The Rho-kinase (ROCK) and myosin light chain kinase (MLCK) are the two kinases responsible for MRLCII phosphorylation. We then employed ROCK inhibitor ROCKOUT and MLCK inhibitor ML-7 to inhibit p-MLRC, confirming that wound contraction is indeed a result of actomyosin activation. ROCKOUT treatment reduced initial contraction, whereas ML-7 completely blocked wound contraction (Fig. 4, A–F and H; S5). ML-7 prevented DPI-induced overcontraction (Fig. 4 J), indicating that ROS deficiency-induced overcontraction depends on p-MLRC activity. Furthermore, ML-7 completely abolishes while ROCKOUT significantly reduces F-actin accumulation at the wound margin (Fig. S5, A and B). Surprisingly, neither ROCKOUT- nor ML-7 treatment impaired wound closure or regrowth (Fig. 4, G–I, K, and L), suggesting that wound closure is separable from wound contraction. In addition to inhibiting MRLC phosphorylation, we used Bleb to inhibit nonmuscle myosin II (NMII) ATPase activity, which directly abolished wound contraction (Fig. 4 M; Video S6). No dextran entry is observed at 30 min postwounding (Fig. 4 N), again supporting the conclusion that wound contraction is not required for wound closure.

Distinct mechanisms contribute to wound overcontraction upon acute and chronic ROS depletion

These developmental defects in duox morphants may contribute to the dynamics of wound contraction. We measured the elastic modulus of the duox and p53 morphants using AFM-based nanoindentation. Indeed, consistent with the observed decrease in F-actin levels in unwounded tissue, duox morphants had a significantly lower tissue rigidity (Fig. 6, A and B). Therefore, we hypothesized that reduced tissue rigidity is the primary reason driving overcontraction in duox morphants, despite reduced contraction force, since tissue with reduced rigidity is more susceptible to deformation under the same load (51). To examine this hypothesis, we performed an in silico linear elasticity simulation to calculate the second principal stress based on the node-wise prescribed displacement, reflecting the compression stress at the amputated wound margin for the MO-injected groups (Figs. 1, I, J and 6, C–F). The in silico wound margin tip displacement was fitted to the experimental data with the Fourier series (Figs. 1 D and S6). The maximum compressive principal stress of the p53 control continues to increase as the wound contracts. It peaks at approximately 1000 s postamputation and gradually diminishes as the wound relaxes back to its initial tailfin width (Fig. 6 E; Video S7). The midrange of elastic modulus for each group was used to simulate the principal stress at the wound margin, which was 45,175 or 10,942 Pa (Fig. 6 B). The maximum stress at the wound edge in the p53 and duox morphants was estimated to be 11,386.99 and 6207.64 N/m2, respectively. The significant increase in the maximum compression principal stress between the two groups aligns well with the twofold increase in p-MRLC intensity. Together, the prolonged contraction period postamputation and delayed wound closure in duox morphants result from reduced tissue rigidity rather than p-MRLC overload at the wound margin (Fig. 6 F; Video S7). Moreover, we demonstrated that the DPI treatment caused overcontraction due to significantly elevated stress at the wound margin through linear elasticity simulation, assuming that the DPI-treated group and the DMSO control group share the same Young’s modulus as the p53 control (Fig. 6, G–I). The simulated elevated compression stress at 1000 s postamputation in the DPI-treated group, compared with the DMSO control group, yields a consistent result, with a significantly increased p-MRLC level in the DPI-treated group at 15 mpa (Figs. 4, C–E and 6, H, I; Video S8). The continued increase in compressive principal stress over time in the DPI-treated group suggests a dramatic rise in force at the wound margin at 1 hpa, which effectively fills the gap in our experimental data (Fig. 6 H). In addition, the linear elasticity simulation in the Bleb treatment aligns well with the MRLC live imaging (Fig. S7; Videos S9 and S10). At 1000 s, the simulated stress at the wound margin in the Bleb-treated group was 1053.27 N/m2, significantly reduced compared with the 12,496.23 N/m2 observed in the DMSO control group, consistent with impaired actomyosin formation after amputation. Discussion

Here, we report that ROS counteract overcontraction to relax wounds and promote regeneration. Although contraction is regarded as beneficial in wound closure across various models, our study reveals a distinct aspect in the zebrafish tailfin amputation model, where overcontraction disrupts efficient healing. The actomyosin “purse string” drives wound edge cells to reconnect (25,26,27,28,29). In pinch wound, puncture wound, or laser wound models in Drosophila, the actomyosin ring localizes at the wound margin and the rear of wound-adjacent cells (26,27), in response to upstream signaling cues, including altered tension, JNK signaling, and calcium signaling (26,52). The medial actomyosin network, together with the actomyosin “purse string” anchored by E-cadherin-mediated adherent junctions in the epithelial cells at the wound edge, collectively contributes to wound contraction and closure in Drosophila (27,50,53). Furthermore, eliminating branched actin leads to the chiral swirling of linear F-actin filaments, thereby maintaining a stable actomyosin ring and facilitating wound closure (54). Additionally, in other animal models, such as Xenopus oocyte wounds and human colonic specimens, actomyosin also accumulates at the wound sites to promote wound contraction and closure (28,29). Here, we propose an opposing perspective that the contraction process postamputation is not required for wound closure. This idea is supported by our data, which show that MLCK or ROCK inhibition significantly relaxes the wound without impairing wound closure or subsequent wound regrowth (Fig. 3). A slight reduction in contraction postamputation may even facilitate wound closure, as the relative distances of nuclei to the wound central plane in the ROCK inhibitor-treated group are slightly smaller than those in the DMSO control group.

The opposing role of wound contraction in zebrafish versus other animal models, particularly in Drosophila, may result from multiple reasons. Firstly, it may arise from variations in epithelial structures and different healing strategies. Compared with the monolayer of Drosophila epithelium, zebrafish epithelia are composed of two interconnected layers (5). The actomyosin ring is restricted to the suprabasal layer, and cells in the basal layer protrude and crawl to the wound edge. The basal layer may compensate for the loss of the contraction force. Secondly, it may result from a distinct wounding geometry. The zebrafish fin fold amputation requires the reconnection of two enveloping epithelial sheets. In contrast, in the Drosophila laser wounding model, a single epithelium closes the circular wound. In addition, the aquatic environment of the zebrafish may activate strikingly different wound signals compared with Drosophila wounding models, such as osmolarity differences, varied mechanical forces at the wound edge, and distinct activated growth factors and cytokines, leading to variations of wound healing strategies across species (5,24,26).

Furthermore, we provide evidence that ROS counteract actomyosin during wound closure. ROS play an essential role in regulating cytoskeleton dynamics through direct and indirect actin modulation (13,55,56,57,58,59,60,61,62). DalleDonne reported that ROS directly oxidize actin at Cys374, thereby decreasing the speed of actin polymerization and compromising F-actin stability (55). In addition, ROS indirectly tune actin dynamics and cellular processes by regulating a broad spectrum of proteins, including actin-binding proteins, Rho GTPases, kinases, and protein tyrosine phosphatases (13,56,57,58,59,60,61,62). ROS oxidize actin-binding proteins, such as myosin II and cofilin, to modulate F-actin stability (56,63,64). Oxidation of Met394 in myosin II heavy chain decreases ATPase activity and actin contraction levels. Oxidation of cofilin at Cys39, Cys80, Cys139, and Cys147 inhibits actin severing and depolymerization, stabilizing the cytoskeleton. Moreover, redox-sensitive GTPases containing the NKCD motif, for instance, Rac1 and RhoA, are susceptible to ROS-induced oxidation. Rac1 oxidation at Cys18 accelerates the GDP exchange rate and enhances lamellipodia formation and cell migration (57,58). Simultaneously, ROS may accelerate nucleotide dissociation and, therefore, inhibit RhoA activity by oxidizing Cys16 and Cys20 in RhoA (59). Furthermore, ROS orchestra cytoskeleton dynamics by regulating kinases such as FAK, protein kinase A (PKA), and Src kinases. ROS enhances cell survival rate through FAK phosphorylation at various tyrosine sites, including Y397, Y925, and Y577 (13). ROS-mediated PKA oxidation at Cys199 exposes Thr197 in its adjacent activation loop, rendering it more susceptible to phosphatases. The conformational change results in PKA inactivation and reduced cell migration (60). Similarly, ROS oxidize Src kinase at Cys245, and Cys487 enhances its activation, leading to enhanced cell migration and invasion (61). Additionally, ROS inactivate low-molecular-weight protein tyrosine phosphatase through the oxidation at Cys12. The reduced p190-RhoGAP phosphorylation level downregulates RhoA activity, facilitating cell spreading (62).

The ability of ROS to stabilize and disassemble F-actin, as observed in previous studies, highlights the complexity of ROS signaling in multicellular tissues (13,56,57,58,59,60,61,62). In this study, we demonstrate that ROS inhibit the phosphorylation of MRLC to relax the wound, providing a novel context where ROS inhibit actomyosin during wound closure in zebrafish.

We observed smaller epithelial cell areas in duox morphants. This phenotype is likely due to increased cell division, as elevated ROS levels delay the G1-S cell-cycle transition and cell proliferation, resulting in cell enlargement in Drosophila eye imaginal disks (65). In addition, the duox MO induces long-term ROS depletion in the tissue, resulting in a significant reduction in tissue stiffness. This finding is supported by the AFM measurements shown in Fig. 6 (37,66). This observation is consistent with a previous report that eliminating ROS with NOX inhibitors in zebrafish embryos delays epiboly progression through interference with E-cadherin and cytoskeleton dynamics (67,68). Moreover, ROS may mediate the expression of actin and actin-binding protein genes, thereby modulating actin abundance. ROS elimination through NOX1 RNAi silencing significantly reduces SM α-actin expression in rat aortic smooth muscle cells, and ROS inhibition downregulates actin cytoskeletal regulatory proteins transcription, including profilin, ARP-3, MRLC, and 14-3-3ζ, in mesangial cells (17,69). We propose that this drastic reduction in mechanical rigidity in the duox knockdown group renders the tissue more susceptible to deformation when subjected to compression stress, as demonstrated in our simulation results in Fig. 6 (37,66). It is worth noting that actomyosin is still required in this condition, which is again modulated by ROS, as indicated by the results showing that a ROS supplement rescues wound overcontraction (Fig. 2). Together, a smaller compression force deforms a much softer tissue in the duox morphants.

In addition to modulating wound dynamics, ROS has been well characterized to promote leukocyte recruitment, including neutrophils, macrophages, and monocytes, to facilitate wound closure (8,51). Upon injury, various TFs, including NF-κB, AP-1, STAT3, IRF3, and HIF-1α, can be activated by the upregulation of ROS. Among these TFs, STAT3 and NF-κB bind together to the promoters and enhancers of specific inflammatory genes, such as IL-6, IL-8/CXCL8, and CCL2/MCP-1, thereby boosting the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (70,71). In addition, ROS trigger AP-1 formation through c-JUN phosphorylation, leading to the accumulation of pro-inflammatory chemokine CXCL8. Moreover, other TFs such as HIF-1α and IRF3 often upregulate classic pro-inflammatory cytokines synergistically with NF-κB, such as IL-1β and CXCL10 (72). After chemotaxis to the injury site in response to pro-inflammatory cytokine cues, leukocytes can (1) phagocytose debris and pathogens (73,74); (2) secrete additional cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β to amplify the inflammation signal for recruiting more immune cells (75); (3) produce ROS to eliminate pathogens and regulate the inflammation response to maintain homeostasis postwounding (76,77). Although leukocytes such as neutrophils are known to generate ROS at sites of injury, their recruitment kinetics do not align with the wound edge relaxation time course. Specifically, ROS production at the wound margin peaks between 20 and 30 mpa, coinciding with the initiation of tissue relaxation observed around 20 mpa (8). In contrast, maximal neutrophil accumulation peaks approximately at 4–6 h postamputation (hpa), well after the relaxation phase has occurred (78,79). Moreover, the specific NADPH oxidase responsible for H2O2 production at the wound margin has been identified as Duox, rather than NOX2, which is typically expressed in neutrophils. Together, leukocyte-derived ROS likely play a minimal role in modulating wound dynamics.

In summary, ROS play a crucial role in driving cytoskeleton dynamics and morphogenesis during development. Their robust production at the wound margin is pivotal in facilitating wound closure. In this work, we demonstrate that ROS modulate the contraction process after amputation, promoting healing and regrowth. Overcontraction impedes wound closure and subsequent healing and regrowth. These observations are instrumental in understanding and treating mucosal wounds. In the future, we aim to incorporate tissue-specific ROS reporters and inducible perturbations to dissect the spatiotemporal sequence of ROS production and contractile responses at single-cell resolution.
