Hydroxychloroquine and pregnancy outcomes in patients with anti-phospholipid syndrome: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Aya Berman, Gili Kenet, Aharon Lubetsky, Nancy Agmon-Levin, Shelly Soffer, Assaf A Barg, Sarina Levy-Mendelovich, Omri Cohen, Liat Waldman-Radinsky, Shadan Lalezari, Eyal Klang, Orly Efros
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAbout 20% of female patients with anti-phospholipid syndrome (APS) experience obstetric complications despite standard treatment with aspirin and heparin. Treatment with hydroxychloroquine (HCQ) was shown to reduce disease flares during pregnancy in patients with systemic lupus erythematosus and to improve pregnancy outcomes.ObjectivesTo evaluate the impact of HCQ on live birth and obstetric complication rates in pregnant women diagnosed with APS.MethodsWe systematically searched PubMed and EMBASE databases in April 2024 for original, peer-reviewed studies on HCQ efficacy and safety in APS patients. The main outcomes measured were live births and obstetric complications, comparing HCQ plus standard therapy to standard therapy alone.ResultsSeven observational studies met our inclusion criteria, comprising a total of 750 patients included in the meta-analysis. The overall pooled proportions of live births in the HCQ group were 89.9% (95% CI 76.5% to 96.0%; I2=81%) and 73.9% (95% CI 51.8% to 88.2%; I2=81%) in the non-HCQ-treated group. The overall pooled proportion of obstetric complications occurred in 19.3% (95% CI 9.4% to 35.4; I2=81%; four studies) of the HCQ group and 55.0% (95% CI 39.4 to 69.7; I2=79%; four studies) of the non-HCQ treated group. Treatment with HCQ was associated with higher rates of live births (OR 2.66; 95% CI 1.44 to 4.91) and lower obstetric complications (OR 0.19; 95% CI 0.19 to 0.39) compared with non-HCQ treatment. HCQ treatment during pregnancy was well tolerated.ConclusionsOur findings suggest that HCQ may offer potential benefits to APS patients during pregnancy by increasing the probability of live birth rates and lowering the risk for obstetric complications.PROSPERO registration numberThe study was registered with PROSPERO, the prospective international register of systematic reviews (identification number CRD42024574097).

Introduction

Anti-phospholipid syndrome (APS) involves higher rates of pregnancy complications and undesirable foetal outcomes. These include recurrent miscarriages and foetal death, intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), preterm birth and APS-associated thromboembolism and pre-eclampsia.1 2 In women with APS, triple antibody positivity (lupus anticoagulant (LA), anti-cardiolipin and anti-beta-2-glycoprotein-I), previous pregnancy loss, a history of thrombosis and systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) are all associated with increased risk of negative pregnancy outcomes.3 4 In the multicentre prospective GR2 study conducted in France, both clinical and serological factors were highlighted for their contribution to maternal morbidity in APS patients, with LA and placental insufficiency notably emerging as significant risk factors for thrombotic and haemorrhagic events.5 These findings align with an earlier study, the PROMISSE study (Predictors of pregnancy outcome: biomarkers in antiphospholipid antibody syndrome and systemic lupus erythematosus), which independently validated LA as the strongest predictor of adverse pregnancy outcomes among anti-phospholipid anti-bodies (aPL) antibodies in aPL-positive women, thereby reinforcing its critical role in risk stratification.6

Current standard treatments for APS primarily revolve around the administration of anti-coagulation therapy with low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) and anti-platelet therapy with low-dose aspirin (LDA) during pregnancy.2 7 This therapy has notably improved pregnancy outcomes, increasing live birth rates, birth weight and gestational age. It has also reduced the prevalence of IUGR, pre-eclampsia and miscarriages among APS patients.8 9 However, approximately one in five pregnancies in patients diagnosed with APS and treated with the standard therapy do not result in live births.7 10 Some studies indicate an even higher risk for those with triple-positive APS, where foetal loss rates can reach up to 70%.11

Hydroxychloroquine (HCQ) is a widespread drug primarily used to treat malaria, viral diseases and autoimmune conditions, demonstrating immunomodulatory effects.12 HCQ treatment is generally well-tolerated, and its use is safe during pregnancy. In women with systemic autoimmune diseases, HCQ is commonly continued throughout gestation due to its maternal benefits and several studies showing consistently a lack of increased risk of adverse foetal outcomes.1315 Common side effects include gastrointestinal symptoms, such as nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea and abdominal discomfort. While rare, severe side effects include cardiotoxicity with rhythm disorders and retinopathy.12

The HCQ mechanism of action is thought to involve multiple anti-inflammatory pathways. Among these pathways are the inhibition of pro-inflammatory cytokine production and the interference with toll-like receptor signalling.16 Besides its anti-inflammatory effect, HCQ was demonstrated to have an anti-thrombotic effect by inhibiting platelet adhesion and platelet-coagulation factors interaction.17

Due to its immunomodulatory properties, HCQ stands as a milestone in the treatment of SLE. It has been shown to decrease the rates of disease flares and lower mortality and long-term complications.18 Its use was also reported to decrease aPL titre.19 These findings possibly explain recent studies demonstrating a reduction in the incidence of thrombosis in APS patients treated with HCQ.20 Treatment with HCQ was shown in several studies to improve pregnancy outcomes in selected APS patients, particularly refractory cases.21 22 Consequently, the recent guidelines from the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology considered the addition of HCQ treatment during the first trimester of pregnancy in patients with refractory APS.7 Yet, the effectiveness of HCQ in reducing APS complications during pregnancy is still not well-established. Although multiple individual studies have reported potential benefits, their conclusions are constrained by small sample sizes, methodological heterogeneity and inconsistent outcome definitions.23 24 Consequently, the strength and generalisability of the evidence remain limited. To address this gap, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to quantitatively assess the impact of HCQ, when added to standard therapy, on live birth rates and obstetric complications in pregnant women diagnosed with APS, and its safety in pregnant women diagnosed with APS.

Methods

Search strategy and selection criteria

This study was conducted under the Preferred Reporting Items for a Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis 2020 guidelines.25 No ethical approval was required. The study was registered with PROSPERO, the prospective international register of systematic reviews (identification number CRD42024574097).

We conducted a comprehensive search of PubMed and EMBASE databases for studies published up to April 2024. Keywords and medical subject headings (MeSH) included “antiphospholipid syndrome”, “antiphospholipid antibody syndrome”, “APLA”, “APLS”, “antiphospholipid antibody syndrome”, “Hydroxychloroquine”, “Plaquenil”, “antimalarial” and “HCQ”, used in various combinations to ensure a broad capture of relevant studies (online supplemental 1).

Inclusion criteria were original, peer-reviewed research articles that provided data on the efficacy or safety of HCQ in adult APS patients. For consistency, APS was defined according to the revised Sapporo classification criteria (updated Sydney criteria, 2006), which require the presence of at least one clinical and one laboratory criterion, with laboratory abnormalities confirmed on two or more occasions at least 12 weeks apart.26 Studies were required to compare outcomes of interest (live births and obstetric complications, including preeclampsia, placental abruption, IUGR and foetal loss) between APS patients treated with HCQ in addition to standard therapy vs those receiving standard therapy alone. Only English publications were included. Review articles, case reports, abstracts and studies not reporting outcomes of interest were excluded.

Data analysis

Data were extracted by two independent reviewers (AB, OE) using a standardised form. The full-text article was reviewed to determine eligible or in case of any uncertainty. Conflicts were resolved by a third reviewer (SS). Extracted information included study characteristics (authors, year of publication, study design), participant demographics, treatment regimens and outcomes of interest. A detailed list of excluded studies is in online supplemental 2. The risk of bias in included studies was assessed using the ROBINS-I tool (Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies - of Intervention).27

The primary outcomes analysed were the OR of live births and obstetric complications between the group treated with HCQ in addition to standard therapy vs standard therapy alone. The ORs were computed using the Mantel-Haenszel method to account for the potential effect of study size and within-study variance. Pooled proportions of these outcomes within the HCQ group were also computed. For the analysis of pooled proportions, a generalised linear mixed model with a logit transformation was employed, using maximum likelihood estimation to accommodate the variability among studies. Fixed and random-effect models were considered, with the choice between them based on the heterogeneity among study outcomes assessed by the I² statistic. Heterogeneity was considered significant at I² > 50%. In the presence of significant heterogeneity, random-effect models were used to provide a more conservative estimate of the pooled effects.

Thrombosis events during pregnancy were rare and reported in only a few studies without HCQ treatment status specification; therefore, this outcome was not included in the analysis.

To address the potential confounding effect of SLE,28 a sub-analysis was conducted focusing exclusively on patients with APS who did not have SLE. This sub-analysis employed the same methods as the primary analysis.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to explore the impact of individual studies on the overall meta-analysis result. The potential for publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s regression test with a significance level set at p<0.1.

Statistical analyses were performed using R V.4.2.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing) using the packages meta and metafor.

There was no funding source for this study.

Six studies reported on live birth rate, comprising a total of 426 receiving HCQ and 282 patients receiving standard of care.2324 2932 The overall pooled proportion of live births in the random effects meta-analysis was 89.9% (95% CI 76.5% to 96.0%; I2=81%; six studies) in the HCQ group (figure 2A). In the non-HCQ treated group, live births were described in 184 APS patients out of 282 (65.2%). The pooled proportion of live births in this group was 73.9% in the random effect meta-analysis model (95% CI 51.8% to 88.2%; I2=81%; six studies) (figure 2B). Treatment with HCQ was associated with higher rates of live birth (OR 2.66; 95% CI, 1.44 to 4.91) compared with non-HCQ treatment (figure 2C). A funnel plot for possible publication bias (figure 2D) had a symmetrical appearance, suggesting that there was no publication bias, as further supported by the Egger test (t=0.43; p=0.69).

Two out of the six studies examined the refractory APS population, all showed that the addition of HCQ resulted in higher live birth rates.24 30 Gerde et al showed that the addition of HCQ in patients with refractory APS significantly increased live birth rate (97.1% in HCQ vs 62.5%, p<0.001) and decreased pregnancy complications (8.7% vs 37.5% in non-HCQ, p<0.001), even though the HCQ group represented the highest-risk population with a history of severe pregnancy complications under conventional treatment in previous pregnancies.24

Zhou et al conducted an additional comparative analysis of the live birth rate in primary APS and secondary APS (SLE-APS; SAPS) patients treated with HCQ. This study showed a significantly higher birth rate among HCQ-treated SAPS patients, 81.8% in SAPS versus 33.3% in primary APS (p < 0.001).32

Four studies evaluated obstetric complications, including pre-eclampsia, placental abruption, intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), and early and late foetal loss, in 245 patients treated with HCQ and 217 patients receiving standard of care.23 24 30 33 In the group treated with HCQ, obstetric complications were observed in 22.4% of the cases (55 patients) with a pooled proportion of 19.3% in the random effect meta-analysis (95% CI 9.4% to 35.4; I2=81%; four studies) and in 58.1% of the cases in the group not treated with HCQ (126 patients) with a pooled proportion of 55.0% in the random effect meta-analysis (95% CI 39.4 to 69.7; I2=79%; four studies) (figure 3A,B). Treatment with HCQ was associated with lower rates of obstetric complications (OR 0.19; 95% CI 0.19 to 0.39) compared with non-HCQ treatment (figure 3C). A funnel plot for possible publication bias (figure 3D) had a symmetrical appearance, suggesting that there was no publication bias, as further supported by the Egger test (t=−0.84; p=0.49).

In the study conducted by Latino et al, HCQ treatment prevented early severe pre-eclampsia in high-risk APS patients (8.3% vs 40.0% conventional treatment, p=0.03).33

Three studies reported thrombotic events during pregnancy, but the categorisation into HCQ treatment groups was not specified.23 30 32 Ruffatti et al reported that 5.1% of patients experienced thrombotic events during pregnancy, Sciascia et al found none and Zhou et al described a 0.67% incidence during pregnancy.

Sciascia et al observed higher rates of spontaneous vaginal delivery in the HCQ group (37.3% in HCQ vs 14.3% in non-HCQ, p=0.01).23

In the study by Sciascia et al, 32 out of 96 patients had persistent aPL antibodies without meeting full APS classification criteria; 15 were in the non-HCQ group and 17 in the HCQ group, of whom 11 had SLE.23 A sensitivity analysis excluding this study was conducted to assess its impact on the overall results. This analysis, presented in online supplemental 3 and 4, showed similar results.

The sub-analysis conducted to evaluate live birth outcomes in patients with APS, excluding those with SLE, included a total of 438 patients. For the live birth, the analysis included three studies24 30 31 : 322 patients in the HCQ-treated group and 116 in the non-HCQ group. Among APS patients without SLE treated with HCQ, the pooled proportion of live births was 95.6% (95% CI 83.8 to 98.9%; I² = 88.1%), while patients not treated with HCQ had a pooled live birth proportion of 84.6% (95% CI 53.5 to 96.3%; I² = 79.6%). The OR for live births comparing HCQ-treated patients to non-HCQ-treated patients was 3.73 (95% CI 0.68 to 20.51; I² = 73.6%) (online supplemental 5).

In the sub-analysis evaluating obstetric complications among patients with APS without SLE, two studies24 30 were included, comprising a total of 250 patients: 182 in the HCQ-treated group and 68 in the non-HCQ group. The pooled proportion of obstetric complications in the HCQ-treated group was 16.0% (95% CI 7.4% to 31.2%; I² = 85.1%), compared with 57.3% (95% CI 30.1% to 80.7%; I² = 89.1%) in the non-HCQ group. The OR for obstetric complications comparing HCQ-treated patients to non-HCQ-treated patients was 0.13 (95% CI, 0.06 to 0.25; I² = 0%) (online supplemental 6).

HCQ treatment during pregnancy was generally well tolerated. None of the included studies reported any major side effects associated with HCQ use. Two studies reported minor side effects of mild cutaneous reactions24 and diffuse dermatitis.30

One study compared HCQ dosage and timing of administration, showing that a daily dose of 400 mg versus 200 mg and administration before pregnancy, compared with initiating treatment during pregnancy, resulted in a significantly higher live birth rate.30

Two studies showed the triple-positive rate was higher in the HCQ-treated group, compared with non-HCQ (10% HCQ vs 3% non-HCQ; 66% HCQ vs 50% non-HCQ).24 33 A statistical difference between the groups was not tested.

According to the ROBINS-I tool, the included studies exhibited mostly a moderate risk of bias, primarily attributed to their retrospective and observational design, as well as the heterogeneity of the patient populations included (online supplemental 7).

Discussion

This meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of HCQ in improving obstetric outcomes in patients diagnosed with APS. Our findings suggest that HCQ, when added to standard therapy with LMWH and LDA, significantly improves live birth rates and reduces the incidence of obstetric complications in APS patients.

The meta-analysis included seven retrospective cohort studies that demonstrated a significant increase in live birth rates among APS patients receiving HCQ in addition to standard therapy compared with those receiving standard therapy alone. Furthermore, the analysis demonstrated a significant reduction in obstetric complications in the HCQ group. These findings are consistent with previous studies and suggest that HCQ plays a beneficial role in mitigating the adverse pregnancy outcomes associated with APS.21 34

These effects were also demonstrated in the studies examining the refractory APS population, with the addition of HCQ resulting in higher live birth rates and decreased pregnancy complications, even in high-risk patients with prior severe complications under conventional treatment.24 30 In addition, Zhou et al showed a significantly higher birth rate also among HCQ-treated SLE-APS patients.32 These findings highlight the potential of HCQ to significantly improve pregnancy outcomes even in higher-risk APS cases and in secondary APS.

While the exact mechanisms by which HCQ may improve pregnancy outcomes in APS remain unclear, several potential mechanisms are suggested. In APS patients, aPL anti-bodies bind to placental cells, triggering inflammation and complement activation and promoting the release of inflammatory molecules like TNF-α by neutrophils, further amplifying the inflammatory response.35 The immunomodulatory properties of HCQ may reduce the inflammatory processes, including complement inhibition, involved in APS pathogenesis. Bertolaccini et al, using a mouse model of obstetric APS, demonstrated that HCQ’s protective effects against foetal death and placental abnormalities are mediated through the inhibition of complement activation, rather than by preventing aPL antibody binding as initially hypothesised.36

The study by Sciascia et al also included patients with persistent aPL antibodies without APS.23 We chose to include this study because a substantial proportion of these individuals had SLE and persistent aPL antibodies—features associated with higher adverse pregnancy outcomes.3 Since this higher-risk population belonged to the HCQ group, any observed benefit of HCQ might be underestimated, ensuring that our meta-analysis conclusions would not be compromised. This assumption was supported by a sensitivity analysis excluding the Sciascia et al’s study, which yielded similar effect estimates, further confirming that the inclusion of this study did not materially affect the overall results.

An additional sub-analysis specifically examined patients with APS in the absence of SLE, aiming to reduce potential confounding by SLE. In this subgroup of 438 patients, the pooled proportion of live births among those treated with HCQ was 95.6% (95% CI 83.8% to 98.9%; I² = 88.1%), compared with 84.6% (95% CI 53.5% to 96.3%; I² = 79.6%) in the non-HCQ group. While the OR for live births favoured HCQ (3.73), the CI was wide, preventing the result from reaching statistical significance. In the sub-analysis of obstetric complications among 250 patients with APS without SLE, those treated with HCQ exhibited a pooled obstetric complication rate of 16.0% (95% CI 7.4% to 31.2%; I² = 85.1%), compared with 57.3% (95% CI 30.1% to 80.7%; I² = 89.1%) in the non-HCQ group. Notably, the OR for obstetric complications was significantly lower in the HCQ group compared with the non-HCQ group, suggesting a potentially protective effect of HCQ. However, only two studies contributed to this specific analysis, limiting the robustness of these findings. Overall, these sub-analyses are consistent with the broader meta-analysis.

HCQ has also been shown to have anti-thrombotic effects. These include reduction of platelet adhesion and a dose-dependent inhibition of aPL antibody-mediated platelet activation in the presence of a thrombin agonist. It has also been described to interfere with the binding of aPL antibody complexes to phospholipid surfaces, potentially reducing their pro-thrombotic effects.37 Although HCQ’s anti-thrombotic properties may have some impact, the unexpected rarity of placental thrombosis in APS pregnancies suggests that thrombosis may not be the main pathophysiology of obstetric complications in this population.35

The included studies reported no major side effects associated with HCQ use during pregnancy, confirming its safety and tolerability in this population. Minor side effects, such as mild cutaneous reactions, were rare and did not pose significant concerns.24 30 HCQ has demonstrated a favourable safety profile during pregnancy, with several prior studies in women with SLE or aPL showing no increase in congenital anomalies, miscarriage or other major adverse foetal outcomes.1315 It is important to note that a large population-based cohort study conducted by Huybrechts et al investigated the risk of congenital malformations associated with HCQ use during the first trimester of pregnancy. The findings suggest a small increase in the risk of malformations in pregnancies exposed to higher doses of HCQ (≥400 mg per day).38 This led the European Medicines Agency to recommend updating safety information for HCQ regarding its use in pregnancy.39 The decision, however, sparked debate within the medical and scientific community, with many experts challenging the findings of Huybrechts and colleagues and calling for the need to consider previous evidence, including observational data and a meta-analysis, which showed no statistically significant increase in congenital malformations at HCQ daily doses ranging from 200 mg to 400 mg.40

Limited data suggest that initiating HCQ treatment before pregnancy may be associated with better outcomes compared with starting during pregnancy. Additionally, one study indicated that higher doses of HCQ and initiation before pregnancy might be more effective than lower doses and administration during pregnancy, respectively.30 However, further research is needed to confirm these findings and establish optimal treatment protocols.

While the findings of our meta-analysis suggest a potential benefit of HCQ in improving live birth rates and reducing obstetric complications in APS patients, some limitations need to be considered. The included studies were retrospective and observational, with small sample sizes, inherently limiting the ability to establish causality. Additionally, the studies reported data from highly heterogeneous patient populations (eg, varying degrees of APS severity) receiving varying combinations of therapy, often with differing dosing strategies for HCQ, LDA, LMWH and glucocorticoids. This variability in standard care within both HCQ-treated and non-HCQ-treated groups represents a significant source of bias and potential confounding, which could impact the reliability of pooled analyses. Further research is needed to disentangle the individual contributions of each treatment component and optimise therapeutic strategies for APS. Another important limitation is the incomplete reporting of aPL profiles across studies. While triple-positivity is known to confer a higher risk for adverse obstetric outcomes, and LA has been shown to be a strong predictor of adverse pregnancy outcomes beyond the first trimester,5 6 only five of the seven included studies reported aPL profiles. However, there was insufficient data on stratified outcome data by individual antibodies, which limited our ability to evaluate whether the observed effects of HCQ differ across aPL subtypes. Notably, two studies (Latino et al, Gerde et al) compared the prevalence of triple positivity between treatment groups and found it to be higher in the HCQ group than in the non-HCQ group (10% vs 3% and 66% vs 50%, respectively).24 33 This finding reinforces our conclusions, as it highlights the benefit of HCQ treatment even in higher-risk populations. Finally, although we conducted a sub-analysis specifically excluding SLE patients to examine HCQ’s effect in primary APS, data limitations in several studies restricted the clarity of this differentiation. Consequently, it remains possible that the observed benefit of HCQ could still be influenced, in part, by its known effects on SLE rather than solely on APS-related obstetric complications. These limitations underscore the need for randomised controlled trials (RCTs) to more definitively assess the efficacy of HCQ in different subpopulations of APS patients and across various clinical characteristics. Such a multinational RCT, the HYPATIA study (EUDRACT: 2016–0 02 256–25), is currently in progress.41

In conclusion, by systematically aggregating data across multiple studies, our meta-analysis provides a more robust estimate of HCQ’s effect in pregnant women with APS. The findings suggest that incorporating HCQ into the treatment regimen for APS during pregnancy is associated with higher live birth rates and lower obstetric complications, thereby reinforcing its potential role in clinical practice and strengthening the evidence base for its use in this high-risk population. However, these findings are based on retrospective observational data and should be interpreted cautiously. Overall, HCQ demonstrates a favourable safety and tolerability profile during pregnancy. Further investigations, including RCTs, are needed to definitively establish its benefits on pregnancy outcomes and to identify the specific subgroups of APS patients who would benefit most from this treatment. Until such evidence is available, decisions regarding HCQ use in pregnant women with APS should be made on a case-by-case basis.
