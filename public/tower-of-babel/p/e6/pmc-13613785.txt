Regional wasteosome accumulation across neurodegenerative diseases points to a shared underlying mechanism potentially related to glymphatic insufficiency
Authors: Raquel Alsina, Marta Riba, Marina Sartorio, Clara Romera, Jordi Riu, Gerard Vilaplana, Iban Aldecoa, Yaroslau Compta, Raquel Sánchez‑Valle, Jaume del Valle, Carme Pelegrí, Laura Molina-Porcel, Jordi Vilaplana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The glymphatic system plays a key role in clearing waste products from the brain and is essential for maintaining brain homeostasis. When dysfunctional, it appears to contribute to pathological changes that exacerbate brain disorders, including neurodegenerative diseases. Additionally, wasteosomes, also known as corpora amylacea, are structures that function as waste containers and are thought to increase in response to chronic glymphatic insufficiency. Hence, in this study, we evaluated whether the accumulation and distribution of wasteosomes are compatible with both the potential role of wasteosomes as a hallmark of the chronic glymphatic insufficiency and the presence of this insufficiency in certain neurodegenerative diseases. Accordingly, brain tissue from 185 donors was analysed, including cases of Alzheimer’s disease, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis with TDP-43 proteinopathy, frontotemporal lobar degeneration with TDP-43 or tau proteinopathy, and non-diseased controls. Wasteosomes were examined across 28 brain regions comprised within 5 major brain areas, using region-specific scoring systems. Analysis was conducted through variance and covariance analyses, along with decision tree procedures. The findings reveal that wasteosomes are consistently found in specific critical regions, with a higher burden in donors with neurodegenerative diseases compared with controls. These regions are independent of the regional distribution of the underlying proteinopathy, and are potentially associated with glymphatic drainage pathways. From an integrated perspective, although further studies are required, the increased presence of wasteosomes in these critical regions across all diseased groups is consistent with the potential presence of chronic glymphatic insufficiency in these diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40478-026-02362-3.

Introduction

The glymphatic system, in coordination with the meningeal lymphatic system, facilitates fluid movement across the brain parenchyma and contributes to the clearance of protein aggregates and other waste products [1]. The fluid movement originates with cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) inflow to the periarterial spaces, primarily driven by arterial wall pulsatility [2, 3]. From there, CSF enters the brain parenchyma and mixes with interstitial fluid (ISF), through a process facilitated by aquaporin-4 water channels densely expressed at the vascular astrocytic endfeet [4]. The mixture of CSF and ISF moves through the brain parenchyma and exits the brain via perivenous spaces and along cranial and spinal nerves, ultimately being transported by traditional lymphatic vessels located in the meninges and the surrounding soft tissues of the skull [5–7].

As a key element in the cleaning of the brain, the glymphatic system, when dysfunctional, leads to the accumulation of waste products and the disruption of cerebral homeostasis, and growing evidence supports a causal relationship between the failure of this system and the progression of neurodegenerative diseases [7–13]. In fact, impaired glymphatic function has been described in Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Parkinson’s disease and frontotemporal lobar degeneration (FTLD), among others [14–22]. Furthermore, meningeal lymphatic dysfunction, which is closely related to the glymphatic system, may also be an aggravating factor in AD pathology, age-associated cognitive decline, and other neurodegenerative diseases [23, 24].

Although there are currently no established markers of glymphatic dysfunction, accumulating evidence suggests a potential role for wasteosomes as a hallmark of chronic glymphatic insufficiency [25].

Wasteosomes, or corpora amylacea, are spherical bodies ranging from 2 to 50 μm in diameter that appear in the human brain and act as waste containers [26–28]. Consequently, their composition can vary depending on the waste elements they entrap [29]. For instance, wasteosomes from AD patients can contain tau protein [30, 31], while those from FTLD patients can contain tau, TAR DNA-binding protein 43 (TDP-43) or Fused in Sarcoma (FUS) proteins, aligning with the specific proteinopathy involved in the disease [32].

Wasteosomes can be present throughout the central nervous system but tend to accumulate in areas closely associated to CSF-filled spaces and glymphatic flow pathways, including specific periventricular, subpial, and perivascular regions, as well as cranial and spinal nerves, with singular predominance of the olfactory bulb [25, 27, 33, 34]. Generated by a specific subset of CD44 + astrocytes, wasteosomes can be released into the CSF and have been detected in deep cervical lymph nodes, where CSF drains via the meningeal lymphatic system [27, 35, 36]. Eventually, they can be phagocytosed and eliminated by macrophages [37–39].

While a link between wasteosomes and glymphatic dysfunction remains hypothetical, it is noteworthy that several disruptors of the glymphatic system, such as sleep disorders, aging, and certain cardiovascular diseases, have been associated with increased wasteosome accumulation [25, 40–42]. Moreover, wasteosomes have also been found to accumulate in significant numbers in several brain diseases, including AD [30, 31, 43–45], Parkinson’s disease [46], Huntington’s disease [43], FTLD [32], amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) [46], multiple sclerosis [47, 48], temporal lobe epilepsy [49–51], vascular encephalopathy [52], and sleep apnoea [53].

Taken together, these observations suggest a potential relationship between glymphatic system dysfunction, neurodegenerative diseases, and the presence of wasteosomes. However, this relationship has not yet been systematically investigated, and the potential causal links among these factors remain unclear. To help to address this gap, the present study provides a detailed analysis of wasteosome burden and regional distribution in a well-characterized cohort of donors with AD, FTLD with TDP-43 proteinopathy (FTLD-TDP), FTLD with tau proteinopathy (FTLD-Tau), and ALS with TDP-43 proteinopathy (ALS-TDP), as well as in non-diseased control donors. By examining wasteosome accumulation and distribution across these conditions, we aim to assess whether the observed patterns are consistent with the presence of glymphatic insufficiency. It should be noted that direct measures of glymphatic system function are not available for these donors, as the analyses are based on postmortem tissue. Nevertheless, if chronic glymphatic insufficiency contributes to increased wasteosome formation and is present in these diseases, a higher burden of wasteosomes would be expected across the diseased groups. To date, no detailed comparative studies have systematically evaluated the wasteosome distribution and burden across several neurodegenerative diseases and control individuals.

Materials and methods

Study subjects

Participants were 185 brain tissue donors from the Neurological Tissue Bank (NTB, Biobank-Hospital Clínic-Fundació de Recerca Clínic Barcelona (FRCB)-Institut d’Investigacions Biomèdiques August Pi i Sunyer (IDIBAPS), Barcelona). The NTB was established in 1988 to support the diagnosis and research of neurological diseases. Patients were enrolled from memory and movement disorders units of major university hospitals across Catalonia, Spain (population: 7.7 million), as well as from geriatric clinics and primary care centers. All participants and/or their legal representatives provided written informed consent before obtaining the brain tissue samples. The Ethics Committee of our institution approved the use of brain tissue and associated clinical data for research purposes, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (IRB00003099).

This study included patients with AD, FTLD-TDP, FTLD-Tau, and ALS-TDP. We selected 20 patients with a primary diagnosis of high AD (A3B3C3), as defined by Montine et al. [54], excluding those with limbic or neocortical Lewy Body Dementia copathology, according to McKeith et al. [55]. All available FTLD-TDP cases were included (N = 42) (subtype A: N = 8; B: N = 11; AB: N = 9; C: N = 13; undefined: N = 1). For FTLD-Tau, 75 patients were selected, including all available cases of corticobasal degeneration (N = 28) and Pick’s disease (N = 14), as well as 33 cases of progressive supranuclear palsy with minimal concomitant pathology. Regarding the term FTLD, it should be noted that it is an umbrella that indicates a specific anatomical involvement (frontotemporal lobar degeneration) when this is not always the case. For instance, in cases of progressive supranuclear palsy and corticobasal degeneration, where tau pathology is mainly of 4R type, there is little involvement of the temporal lobe. In the first case, the subthalamus, striatum, and brainstem are predominantly affected, while in the second case, the primarily affected regions are some subcortical regions, the basal ganglia, and the parietal region. For ALS-TDP, cases with Brettschneider stages 2 and 3 in accordance with Brettschneider et al. [56] were included (N = 19). Cases classified as stage 4 were excluded due to significant hippocampal involvement. Genetic cases were excluded from all disease groups. The study also included 29 non-diseased control participants. Inclusion and exclusion criteria are detailed in Supporting Information (SI) Table S1. Participant details, including number, age at death, disease duration, post-mortem delay, and sex, are summarized for each group in SI Table S2.

Neuropathology

Neuropathological examination was performed following the standardized protocols of the NTB and in accordance with international consensus criteria [54]. For each donor, one-half of the brain was fixed by immersion in a 4% formaldehyde solution for 3 weeks. At least 25 brain regions were embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 5 μm thickness, and stained with haematoxylin and eosin, as well as luxol fast blue for specific brain areas. Immunohistochemistry was performed using antibodies anti-amyloid β, anti-phospho-tau, anti-RD3 and anti-RD4 tau, anti-α-synuclein, anti-phosphorylated-α-synuclein, anti-FUS, anti-TDP-43, anti-phosphorylated-TDP-43, anti-ubiquitin, anti-p62, and anti-internexin (see SI Table S3 for details).

Brain areas and regions

Initially, a preliminary examination was conducted on 10 cases out of a total of 185 donors, specifically 2 cases from each disease group and 2 controls. In this preliminary analysis, the distribution of wasteosomes and proteinopathy across 25 brain areas was assessed. The aim of this examination was to identify (a) brain areas containing wasteosomes and affected by the proteinopathies under study, and (b) brain areas containing wasteosomes but lacking proteinopathy in any of the disease groups. Accordingly, 5 brain areas were selected for subsequent analysis: (1) the superior frontal gyrus, (2) the hippocampus, (3) the lentiform nucleus, and (4) the medial superior temporal cortex, which are characteristically affected in the neurodegenerative diseases included in the study, and (5) the calcarine sulcus of the occipital cortex, included as a control region due to its relative preservation in the conditions examined (Fig. 1). Subsequently, wasteosome burden was assessed across the 5 areas. In the superior frontal gyrus, lentiform nucleus, medial superior temporal cortex, and calcarine sulcus, the presence of wasteosomes was assessed in subpial and intraparenchymal regions, as well as in 3 defined perivascular regions within each area. In the area of the lentiform nucleus, we also evaluated the presence of wasteosomes in the periventricular region. In the hippocampal area, which has been extensively studied in other pathologies and in different ages, we focused on 7 distinct regions: the dentate gyrus, fimbria, subpial region of the fimbriodentate sulcus, subpial region of the hippocampal sulcus, subpial region of the subiculum, subpial region of the pre-subiculum, and periventricular area. All these pre-selected regions, a total of 28, are indicated in Fig. 1 over brain images adapted from [57].

Wasteosome analysis

To assess the wasteosome accumulation in brain tissue, we examined haematoxylin and eosin-stained paraffin-embedded sections from the pre-selected 28 regions.

As illustrated in SI Fig. S1 with images taken with a BX41 microscope (Olympus, Hamburg, Germany), distinct patterns of wasteosome accumulation were observed across the different regions. In the subpial region, wasteosomes tended to align in rows along the tissue border near the pia mater, with a predominant concentration in the sulcal depths. In the periventricular area, wasteosomes accumulated in rows adjacent to the ependyma. In the perivascular region, they were typically organized in rows within the tissue surrounding the perivascular space, although some were scattered within the surrounding tissue, deviating from the linear arrangement. In contrast, intraparenchymal wasteosomes did not show any consistent pattern.

Based on the distinct patterns of wasteosome accumulation, a specific scoring scale was developed for each region, adapting a previously described approach [32], as described below and illustrated in SI Fig. S1. In the subpial regions, we focused on the depth of sulci, assigning a score of 0 (no wasteosomes) to 5 (≥ 2 rows + high density of wasteosomes surrounding the rows) to each sulcus, and then calculated the mean score across all sulci. In the perivascular regions, we examined all vessels in 3 specific fields of view (FOV) at 100 × (3,80 mm2) and scored each vessel from 0 (no wasteosomes) to 5 (≥ 2 rows of wasteosomes + high density of wasteosomes in the tissue around the vessel), and averaged the scores on each FOV. In the periventricular and intraparenchymal regions, we focused on the FOV at 400 × (0,24 mm2) with the highest accumulation of wasteosomes. For the selected FOV in the periventricular region, we used the same semiquantification method as in the subpial region. In the intraparenchymal region, where no specific accumulation pattern was observed, we counted the number of wasteosomes and assigned a score from 0 (no wasteosomes) to 5 (> 60 wasteosomes). The dentate gyrus and the fimbria were scored as an intraparenchymal area. Wasteosomes were manually quantified by a single researcher blinded to the pathological diagnosis.

Of the 28 pre-selected and semiquantified regions, 21 (marked in red in Fig. 1) were excluded of the statistical analysis due to limited signal resolution and lack of reliable dynamic range. Consequently, a total of 7 regions (marked in green in Fig. 1) were ultimately included in the statistical analysis (see SI Fig. S2 for details). These regions are specifically the intraparenchymatous region of the fimbria (IP_Fi), the subpial region of the fimbriodentate sulcus (SP_FDS), the subpial region of the hippocampal sulcus (SP_HpS), the subpial region of the subiculum (SP_Sb), the subpial region of the presubiculum (SP_PrS), a subpial region in the area of the brain containing the lentiform nucleus (SP_L), and the perivascular region around the thalamostriate vein (or their perforant branches) of the same brain area (PVa_TS). It is important to note that, as can be observed in Fig. 1, all selected regions (with the exception of PVa_TS) are aligned consecutively in the basal part of the brain; specifically, two are in the peripheral regions of the hippocampus (SP_HcS and SP_FDS) and four in adjacent regions (IP_Fi, SP_Sb, SP_PrS and SP_L). Notably, localization in the basal brain was not an a priori inclusion criterion, but rather a subsequent observational finding.

To obtain the wasteosome load index for each subject, the average of the scores from the selected regions per subject was calculated. Accordingly, the wasteosome load index ranges from 0 to 5.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with the IBM SPSS Statistics (IBM). Specifically, we used the ANOVA to test the global effects of the different factors and, in case of significance (F test, p < 0.05), we applied Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) test for post hoc comparisons. Dunnett’s pairwise multiple comparison t test was also used on post-hoc comparison to compare the single control mean against the means of the different diseases. When a covariate was introduced in the GLM, the marginal means, adjusted for the covariate, were compared using the Sidak adjustment. Additionally, we employed the decision tree procedure. We used the method CHAID, and the significance values for splitting nodes and merging categories were set at 0.05 and adjusted using the Bonferroni method for multiple comparisons.

However, and in reference to the statistical analyses, it should be highlighted that (1) using only p values to determine significance can lead to some analyses being biased, some false positives being overhyped and some genuine effects being overlooked; and (2) logic, background knowledge and experimental design should be considered alongside p values and similar metrics to reach a conclusion and decide on its certainty [58]. Accordingly, in the present study, p values were used alongside other observations to reach conclusions based on an overall perspective.

Regarding age at death, the ANOVA indicates significant differences between the groups (p < 0.05). Post-hoc comparisons, performed using Tukey’s HSD test, show that the mean age at death for the AD group is higher than that of all other groups, and the mean age at death for the ALS-TDP group is lower than that of all other groups (in all cases, p < 0.05). Figure 2A shows the means for each group and the significant differences between them. As a result, Tukey’s HSD test generates three homogeneous subsets: one containing the AD group, one containing the ALS-TDP group, and a third subset including the control, FTLD-Tau, and FTLD-TDP groups (Fig. 2B). Note the age at death in the control group does not differ significantly from that in the FTLD groups, iIt is lower than in the AD group and higher than in the ALS-TDP group.

In reference to brain weight, the ANOVA also indicates that there are significant differences between the groups (p < 0.05), and the post-hoc comparisons, also conducted using Tukey’s HSD test, indicate that the mean brain weights of the control and ALS-TDP groups are higher than the means of the AD, FTLD-TDP, and FTLD-Tau groups (in all cases, p < 0.05; Fig. 2C). Accordingly, Tukey’s HSD test generates two homogeneous subsets: one containing both the control and ALS-TDP groups, and the other containing the AD, FTLD-TDP, and FTLD-Tau groups (Fig. 2D). It is worth noting that these results are expected, since AD and FTLD are associated with brain atrophy, whereas this atrophy is not present or minimal in the controls and in ALS-TDP patients, which in this study have Brettschneider stages lower than 4.

Analysis of the amount of wasteosomes in the different regions and diseases

After semiquantifying the amount of wasteosomes in each of the selected regions for each donor, an initial ANOVA was performed, defining disease and brain region as independent variables and the amount of wasteosomes (wasteosome score) as the dependent one. The ANOVA indicates that both disease and region have a significant effect on the amount of wasteosomes (p < 0.05 in both cases) and shows that the interaction between these factors is not significant (p > 0.44). Thus, there are differences in the amount of wasteosomes that can be attributed to the disease factor, and there are differences that can be attributed to the region. The lack of a significant interaction between these two factors indicates that there is no evidence suggesting that the increase in wasteosomes attributable to a specific disease is not homogeneous or uniform across the different regions. In Fig. 4A, the mean number of wasteosomes in each region for each disease and the control group is shown. Post-hoc comparisons (Tukey’s HSD test) indicate that the mean number of wasteosomes in all disease groups is higher than in the control group (p < 0.05), and that there are no differences between the different disease groups (Fig. 4B). The test generates one subset comprising all the diseases and another subset consisting only of the control group (Fig. 4C). Regarding the studied regions, Tukey’s HSD test indicates that there are significant differences among some of them (Fig. 4D) and four subsets are identified (Fig. 4E).

Analysis of the effects of the diseases on the wasteosome load index

After confirming that there is no interaction between the disease factor and the region factor, a wasteosome load index was calculated for each patient. This index was defined as the average number of wasteosomes across the selected regions. This approach allows for a direct assessment of the effects of the disease factor on the amount of wasteosomes, avoiding redundancy from the patient factor introduced by the region factor. The ANOVA performed using the factor disease as the independent variable and the wasteosome load index as the dependent one indicates that there are significant differences in the wasteosome load index attributable to the disease factor (p < 0.05). Post-hoc comparisons (Tukey’s HSD test) indicate no significant differences among the different disease groups (p > 0.05), and significant differences between each disease group respect to the control group (p < 0.05), except for the ALS-TDP group, where the mean score is higher than that of the control group but does not reach significance (p ≈ 0.066) (Fig. 5A). Additionally, considering that the primary interest lies in identifying differences between the control group and the disease groups, Dunnett’s T-test was applied, defining the control group as the control on the test. The results indicate that in all diseases, including ALS-TDP, the wasteosome score is higher than that from the control group (p < 0.02 for ALS-TDP and p < 0.01 for all other groups). The decision tree procedure (which allows recoding the group predictor categories with minimal information loss) also separates in the first division the subset control on one branch, and the AD, FTLD-TDP, FTLD-Tau, and ALS-TDP in the other one (Fig. 5B).

Finally, an additional ANOVA was performed, again defining the disease factor as the independent variable and the wasteosome load index as the dependent one, but including age at death as a covariate since aging may influence the number of wasteosomes. Note that, as shown earlier in the results section, there are significant differences in the age at death among the patient groups, with the AD group having a higher age at death and the ALS-TDP group a lower one compared to the other groups. As expected, the results indicate that the covariate age at death has significant effects, as well as the disease factor (p < 0.01 in both cases). After estimating the marginal means for the disease factor at an age of 72.83 years, pairwise comparisons (with Sidak correction for multiple comparisons) indicate in this case that the mean wasteosome scores in FTLD-Tau, FTLD-TDP, and ALS-TDP are significantly higher than that from the control group (p < 0.05 in all cases), while the difference between AD and control groups does not reach significance (p ≈ 0.056), despite the mean of AD is higher than that of the control group (Fig. 5C). The mean for AD has decreased compared to the values presented in Fig. 5A, due to the fact that in Fig. 5A the AD patients had an average age at death of 85.15 years, and in Fig. 5C the mean wasteosome score is estimated at 72.83 years. Conversely, the mean for the ALS-TDP group has increased because their average age at death in Fig. 5A was 61.37 years. In this case, and with the age-corrected wasteosome load index, the decision tree also separates in the first division the control group, with a lower mean wasteosome score, from all other groups (Fig. 5D).

Overall, from a global perspective, and considering all test performed and the different p values obtained for each test, the results collectively reveal an increase in the wasteosome load index in all diseases groups compared to the control group.

Discussion

The main findings of this study are that wasteosome burden increases across all studied neurodegenerative diseases, and that the regions showing this increase are not directly related to those affected by their proteinopathy.

The increase in wasteosome burden has previously been described in several disorders, including AD [43–45, 48, 59], FTLD [32], Huntington’s disease [43], and Parkinson’s disease [46, 59], among others. However, the dissociation between wasteosome distribution and disease-specific proteinopathy patterns is a novel finding. This dissociation is evidenced by several observations. In Pick’s disease and FTLD-TDP type A, the anterior frontal lobe is prominently affected [60], yet no corresponding increase in wasteosome accumulation is observed in this region. Likewise, while the hippocampus is among the earliest regions involved in AD, it is not affected in ALS-TDP cases at Brettschneider stages 2–3 [56], which correspond to the ALS-TDP cases included in the present study. Nevertheless, an increase in wasteosomes was detected in the hippocampal regions of all the diseases studied, even in the ALS-TDP donors. These findings indicate that the regional increase in wasteosomes is not directly caused by local proteinopathy and therefore cannot be explained by the spatial distribution of disease lesions. Accordingly, a common underlying factor linking both processes is likely involved.

Aging is a key factor implicated in both the onset of neurodegenerative diseases and the increase in wasteosome burden [40, 41, 61–63]. As the primary risk factor for neurodegenerative diseases, aging encompasses a range of hallmarks, like cellular senescence and altered intercellular communication, all of which are associated with increased susceptibility to these disorders [62, 64]. Likewise, aging has been consistently described as a key contributor to wasteosome formation [40, 41, 61, 63, 65].

However, as shown in the present study, the ALS-TDP group exhibited a higher amount of wasteosomes than the control group, despite dying at younger ages, and the FTLD groups also showed higher wasteosome levels than controls, despite having similar ages at death. More robustly, both the ANOVA and decision tree analyses, performed using an age-corrected wasteosome load index, revealed significantly higher wasteosome levels in the disease groups than in the control group. Taken together, these results indicate that age can be ruled out as the common underlying factor.

Having excluded aging, and among other potential factors, our results reinforce that chronic glymphatic insufficiency may be the common factor that influences both the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative diseases and the accumulation of wasteosomes. As indicated in the introduction, there is evidence that alterations of the glymphatic system contribute to the onset and progression of neurodegenerative diseases [7, 9–11, 13], and there are also indications that they may increase the wasteosome burden [25]. Here, we observed that the increase of the wasteosome burden in the studied neurodegenerative diseases affects some specific regions unrelated to the primary distribution of disease proteinopathy. As mentioned above, these critical regions include the basal regions of the brain as well as areas near the thalamostriate veins. These regions have been suggested to form part of the main drainage pathways of the glymphatic system [25]. It should be noted that we cannot rule out the possibility that wasteosomes may also increase in other, as yet unstudied, glymphatic drainage regions, such as the cranial nerves, including the olfactory nerve, which is also generally characterised by high levels of wasteosomes.

In this context, chronic glymphatic insufficiency may represent an underlying mechanism that simultaneously drives both neurodegenerative disease and wasteosome formation (as illustrated in Fig. 6A). More specifically, and as represented in Fig. 6B, chronic failure of the glymphatic system leads to a persistent increase in waste products in the brain parenchyma. This accumulation, including aggregation-prone proteins, could facilitate the development of neurodegenerative diseases [10]. In addition, the chronic accumulation of waste products is accompanied by an increase in wasteosomes, which predominantly appear in the drainage regions of the glymphatic system.

This hypothesis, further supported by the results obtained, cannot be conclusively demonstrated, as the state of the glymphatic system in the donors cannot be determined. However, taking everything into account, this model explains why wasteosomes increase at the observed specific sites across all the neurodegenerative diseases studied. In addition, the model may also account for the limited but noticeable age-dependent increase in wasteosomes in critical regions of individuals without neurodegenerative diseases, as a consequence of the gradual deterioration of glymphatic function.

Given that glymphatic failure may constitute a significant risk factor for the development of neurodegenerative diseases [7, 9–11, 13–17, 19, 21, 22], there is a current need for an indicator to assess the functional state of this system. Such an indicator would be valuable in experimental research, enabling precise determination of variables affecting this system and studying intervention mechanisms to restore its functionality. Additionally, it could hold diagnostic utility. To date, most approaches for determining the functionality of the glymphatic system in mice rely on single-time-point measurements of the dynamics of tracers either administered into the subarachnoid space (commonly in the cisterna magna) or directly into the brain parenchyma. The main limitation of these studies is that while they provide a snapshot of the system status, they fail to offer insights into its functionality over time. From this perspective, we previously postulated that wasteosomes could serve as a hallmark of chronic glymphatic insufficiency [25]. Now, with the present work, it seems that wasteosomes in the defined critical regions may be particularly relevant for this purpose. Moreover, based on the findings of the present study, we suggest that the presence of wasteosomes specifically in the critical regions might also provide information about the risk of neurodegenerative diseases. For this purpose, techniques for inferring wasteosome-related microstructural changes in vivo, such as diffusion-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (DWI or DW-MRI) or synchrotron X-ray spectromicroscopy techniques, would be of interest [66–70].

Furthermore, it is well-known that most neurodegenerative diseases exhibit a high frequency of co-pathologies [71–73]. For example, a recent study involving 144 AD cases found that all patients with late-onset AD had at least one non-AD pathological diagnosis, and at least one co-pathology was also found in 98% of early-onset AD patients [73]. These co-pathologies complicate clinical diagnosis, epidemiological studies, and pharmacological interventions. Indeed, neuropathological examination is required to precisely identify the primary disease and co-pathologies in each individual. The high prevalence of co-pathologies indicates that some predisposing factors are shared among these diseases. From this perspective, the increase in wasteosomes observed in the critical regions across all diseases studied in the present work suggests that the chronic glymphatic insufficiency may be one of these common underlying factors. This insight helps to explain, at least in part, the abundant and widespread co-pathologies and mixed patterns observed in these conditions.

Further research is needed to determine the exact role of chronic glymphatic insufficiency in neurodegenerative diseases and to assess whether wasteosomes could serve as a reliable marker of this condition. Moreover, it remains to be established whether these findings can be extended to other neurodegenerative diseases not included in the present study. As a final consideration, we emphasize that understanding these diseases, mitigating their risk factors, and developing effective treatments will require approaches that go beyond purely biochemical or genetic frameworks. The inclusion of physiological perspectives, including the glymphatic system, will therefore be essential to achieve meaningful progress in this field.
