Quality of life for pediatric patients with short bowel syndrome‐associated intestinal failure treated with teduglutide
Authors: Danielle Wendel, Paul W. Wales, Samuel Kocoshis, Robert Venick, Beth Carter, Valeria Cohran, Cheney Matthews, Bram P. Raphael, Ian Robinson, Susan Hill
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe complex care needs of pediatric patients with short bowel syndrome‐associated intestinal failure (SBS‐IF) can negatively impact the health‐related quality of life (HRQoL) of patients and their caregivers. We assessed the impact of teduglutide on HRQoL of pediatric patients with SBS‐IF.MethodsTwo long‐term extension (LTE) studies (NCT02949362, NCT02954458) assessed HRQoL over 96 weeks using the Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory (PedsQL) 4.0 Generic Core Scales and the PedsQL Family Impact Module. Patients included in this analysis received teduglutide in one of three randomized Phase 3 parent studies and the respective LTE study. We assessed changes in HRQoL over time, as well as the impact of attaining enteral autonomy on HRQoL and predictors of change in HRQoL.ResultsIn pediatric patients with SBS‐IF receiving teduglutide (n = 69), the total mean (standard deviation) patient‐reported PedsQL score was 75.0 (16.2). Higher mean number of stools per day at LTE study baseline (p = 0.03) predicted increased patient‐reported HRQoL. Longer teduglutide exposure (p = 0.047), increasing length of remnant small intestine (p = 0.034), and older age (p = 0.026) predicted higher caregiver‐proxy‐reported HRQoL. Mean PedsQL scores remained stable over 96 weeks regardless of patients achieving enteral autonomy.ConclusionsHRQoL of pediatric patients with SBS‐IF treated with teduglutide was generally stable over 96 weeks. Predictors of improved HRQoL included higher baseline stool number, longer teduglutide treatment duration, greater length of small intestine, and older age. To better understand these observations, future studies should assess HRQoL before initiation of treatment.Clinical Trial RegistrationClinical trials NCT02949362 and NCT02954458.

INTRODUCTION

Short bowel syndrome (SBS) is a chronic, life‐threatening malabsorptive disorder that is the most common cause of intestinal failure (IF) in children. Pediatric patients with SBS‐associated IF (SBS‐IF) require parenteral nutrition and/or intravenous fluids (PN/IV) for adequate nutrition and to support growth. 1

Health‐related quality of life (HRQoL) outcomes have become important in contributing to understanding the potential benefit of medical treatments, and can be collected via patient‐reported surveys to obtain the patient's self‐assessment of the effects of chronic disease and treatment on their functioning and well‐being, 2 , 3 and via caregiver‐proxy‐reported surveys. 4 Because survival has been improving for children with SBS‐IF over the past two decades, clinical aims have shifted from simple survival to survival with a high HRQoL. 5 , 6 , 7 Furthermore, there is an increasing awareness of the importance of improving the HRQoL of caregivers of children with IF. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 HRQoL can be measured in children using the Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory (PedsQL) surveys. In the healthy population, mean total patient‐reported PedsQL scores and total caregiver‐proxy‐reported PedsQL scores have been reported to be 83.9 and 82.3, respectively. 12 A cutoff score of one standard deviation below the healthy population means can be used to estimate risk of impaired HRQoL. 12

The complex care needs of pediatric patients with IF and SBS‐IF, including enteral nutrition, specialized diets, medical and surgical interventions, symptoms, and long‐term home PN/IV, 1 can negatively impact the HRQoL of patients and their families and caregivers. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 Real‐world data generally report lower HRQoL of pediatric patients with SBS‐IF compared with healthy children or those with other complex health conditions. 13 , 21 Reduced HRQoL can manifest as sleep disturbance, anxiety, depression, and reduced participation in social and school activities. 22 Reduced caregiver well‐being can stem from poor sleep and lack of support or resources. 17 However, families report managing SBS‐IF with competence and view family life as ‘fairly normal’; 16 patients report feeling happy and positive about the future. 13

Teduglutide, a recombinant analog of the human glucagon‐like peptide‐2, increases intestinal adaptation, reduces PN/IV requirements, improves tolerance of enteral nutrition, 23 , 24 , 25 and is approved for use in many countries in children with SBS requiring PN/IV (i.e., SBS‐IF). 26 , 27 , 28 , 29

Clinical trial data have demonstrated the potential benefits of teduglutide on SBS‐related quality of life in adults. 30 , 31 Furthermore, a longitudinal real‐world study reported significant improvements in HRQoL of adults with SBS‐IF treated with teduglutide compared with those not treated with teduglutide, over a median of 4.3 years. 32 Increased HRQoL has also been reported in families of patients with IF following attainment of enteral autonomy. 8 However, to our knowledge, there are no studies investigating the effect of teduglutide or attaining enteral autonomy on HRQoL in pediatric patients with SBS‐IF.

Here, the primary objective was to evaluate long‐term HRQoL in children with SBS‐IF after treatment with teduglutide over 96 weeks. Secondary objectives included evaluating the HRQoL of caregivers of children with SBS‐IF, investigating predictors of change in HRQoL and identifying differences in patients who did or did not achieve enteral autonomy.

METHODS

Ethics statement

Studies were conducted in compliance with the International Council for Harmonisation Good Clinical Practice guidelines and the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki and its amendments. Local institutional review boards or medical ethics committees approved all protocols; central institution review boards or medical ethics committees were Western Institutional Review Board, Washington, USA and South West REC Centre, Bristol, UK. Informed consent was provided by patients, guardians, or parents before study participation.

Study design

Data were pooled from two open‐label, multicenter, Phase 3, long‐term extension (LTE) clinical trials of teduglutide (NCT02949362 [conducted 2016–2020], NCT02954458 [conducted 2017–2020]; Supporting Information: Figure S1). The study design has been described previously. 33 Eligible pediatric patients who enrolled in the LTE studies were PN/IV‐dependent and had previously completed one of three randomized, Phase 3 parent studies (NCT01952080, NCT03571516, and NCT02682381) during which they received teduglutide or standard of care and no teduglutide treatment. 24 , 25 , 34 Patients were eligible for teduglutide in the LTE studies if they met one or more of the treatment inclusion criteria and none of the exclusion criteria previously described (Supplemental Methods). 33 During the LTE studies, patients received teduglutide 0.05 mg/kg once daily subcutaneously or no teduglutide treatment for treatment cycles lasting 24 weeks, followed by 4‐week follow‐up periods during which patients did not receive treatment. Only patients who received teduglutide in both the parent and LTE studies were included in the present analyses. HRQoL data were only available from the beginning of the LTE studies because PedsQL surveys were not administered during the parent studies.

Baseline patient characteristics

Baseline was defined as the point of entry into the LTE studies. Patient demographics and characteristics (bowel anatomy, cause of SBS‐IF, and PN/IV requirements) were recorded at baseline, unless otherwise stated.

Quality of life assessment

PedsQL 4.0 Generic Core Scales 35 were used to collect patient‐reported (age 5–18 years) and caregiver‐proxy‐reported (age 2–18 years) HRQoL data prospectively during the LTE studies. The PedsQL Family Impact Module 36 assessed caregiver‐self‐reported HRQoL. PedsQL questionnaires were completed at Day 1, Week 12, and Week 24 of each treatment cycle for patients receiving teduglutide. Further details are in Supporting Information.

Statistical analysis

Data from patients who completed at least one PedsQL questionnaire either partially or fully in the 96 weeks after baseline were included. Continuous and categorical variables were summarized using descriptive statistics. PedsQL scores were summarized by 12‐week intervals from baseline over 96 weeks. The PedsQL score and PN/IV volume measurement closest to the 12‐week interval date were included in the summarized score. Change in PedsQL scores was investigated over 96 weeks. Using PedsQL scores as the outcome variable, subgroup analyses were performed on those with sustained enteral autonomy (patients who achieved 100% reduction in PN/IV volume for ≥90 consecutive days in the LTE studies) and those without sustained enteral autonomy (patients who did not achieve 100% reduction in PN/IV volume for ≥90 consecutive days in the LTE studies). Predictors of change in PedsQL scores were explored by modeling patient‐level characteristics in a univariate analysis with the outcome variable as change in each PedsQL score at each visit. Characteristics reaching significance of p < 0.2 were included in a multivariable regression model to determine individual predictors of mean change in PedsQL scores at each visit through 96 weeks for patient‐reported and caregiver‐proxy‐reported total PedsQL scores only. An α value of < 0.05 was deemed significant, and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for the parameter estimates (PEs). All analyses were conducted with SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

RESULTS

Baseline demographics and characteristics

Overall, 69 patients who received teduglutide in both the parent and LTE studies were included (age range: 1.0–17.0 years). Baseline demographics and characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The most common causes of SBS were gastroschisis (33.3%) and volvulus/ischemic bowel (31.9%). The median (range) length of remnant small intestine was 30.0 (0.0–147.0) cm. The mean (standard deviation [SD]) duration of teduglutide exposure was 83.3 (41.9) weeks. The age range of patients with or without a stoma was 3.0–10.0 years and 1.0–18.0 years, respectively.

Of the 69 patients, the response rate to at least one patient‐reported PedsQL survey was 75.4% (n = 52; age range: 3–17 years); the age range of patients who did not respond to the patient‐reported PedsQL survey was 1–16 years (n = 17; 24.6%). In total, caregiver‐proxy response was obtained for 92.8% of patients (n = 64; age range of patients: 2–17 years). Twelve patients (age range: 2–15 years) only had caregiver‐proxy response reports available.

Mean PedsQL scores over 96 weeks

The mean PedsQL sub‐scores ranged from 67.9 to 78.1, with a total mean patient‐reported PedsQL score of 75.0 and a total mean caregiver‐proxy‐reported PedsQL score of 71.6 (Figure 1). The mean PedsQL score for caregiver‐self‐reported HRQoL was 72.3.

Predictors of change of HRQoL

Univariate analysis indicated that change in HRQoL over time may be affected by aspects such as cause of SBS, bowel anatomy, and demographics (Supporting Information: Table S1). Following multivariable regression, the following characteristics were positively associated with total patient‐reported PedsQL score: intestinal atresia (PE = 13.65; CI = 7.31, 19.98; p < 0.001) and higher mean number of stools per day at baseline (PE = 1.42; CI = 0.15–2.70; p = 0.03). The following were positively associated with total caregiver‐proxy‐reported PedsQL score: longer duration of teduglutide exposure (p = 0.047; PE = 0.05; CI = 0.00–0.09), greater length of remnant small intestine (p = 0.034; PE = 0.05; CI = 0.00–0.10), and older age at baseline (p = 0.026; PE = 0.60; CI = 0.08–1.11). Hirschsprung's disease was negatively associated with total caregiver‐proxy‐reported PedsQL score (p = 0.041; PE = −6.99; CI = −13.69, −0.30; Figure 2).

DISCUSSION

In this analysis of data from two LTE studies, we found that the HRQoL of children with SBS‐IF receiving teduglutide was generally stable over time and that longer teduglutide exposure was associated with improved HRQoL. We investigated HRQoL of children with SBS‐IF in the clinical trial setting using a validated tool, the PedsQL. The PedsQL uses a modular approach to assess HRQoL of children, with symptom‐ and treatment‐specific modules, and can be used for those with or without acute and chronic health conditions, and their caregivers/families. 9 , 10 , 11 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 As there are no SBS‐IF‐specific modules, we used the global measures.

PedsQL scores remained generally stable across the 96‐week study despite reductions in PN/IV requirements. Stable HRQoL could indicate patient resilience in managing their condition, or a lack of sensitivity to detect changes in this patient population. Previous studies have reported on the resilience of families to cope with the complex needs of children with SBS and/or IF, 11 , 16 which may positively affect the resilience of the children under their care. School functioning scores were consistently low, in line with previous observations, 13 , 21 and thus may be a target for improving HRQoL. 37 School functioning may relate to the impact of SBS‐IF on behavioral and psychological changes that may not be readily improved by teduglutide treatment alone.

Improvements in patient‐reported PedsQL scores over 96 weeks were significantly associated with higher mean number of stools per day at baseline, which may indicate that this group of patients has more potential for clinical‐ and therefore HRQoL‐related improvement during teduglutide treatment than those with a lower mean number of stools per day at baseline.

Improvements in caregiver‐proxy‐reported PedsQL scores were significantly associated with older age at baseline, but this was not identified as a predictor of patient‐reported HRQoL. Teduglutide might improve HRQoL of older children but caregivers may be more sensitive to these changes over time than patients themselves. Improved HRQoL with increasing age could reflect various factors; however, a previous report in pediatric patients with SBS and SBS‐IF did not detect differences in HRQoL between families of children over or under 5 years old. 13 In a group of pediatric patients with IF requiring home PN/IV, children older than 7 years were reported to be well‐informed about their condition and treatment, and wished to take responsibility for their own care 38 ; however, the age range of children in that study was 7─17 years compared with 1─18 years here. Pediatric patients with IF and their families have reported reasonably favorable HRQoL compared with other chronic conditions, and this HRQoL may improve with age. 8 Improvements in caregiver‐proxy‐reported total PedsQL scores were also associated with increased exposure to teduglutide, suggesting that caregivers may have a positive bias toward the impact of teduglutide treatment on HRQoL.

Greater length of remnant small intestine was a significant positive predictor of caregiver‐proxy‐reported HRQoL, possibly partly due to the association with less dependency on PN/IV, 33 thus reducing caregiver burden and reflected in their perception of HRQoL. These findings align with previous reports that greater length of remnant bowel in children with SBS‐IF is positively associated with HRQoL. 39

The presence of intestinal atresia was positively associated with patient‐reported PedsQL scores, and the presence of Hirschsprung's disease was negatively associated with caregiver‐proxy‐reported PedsQL scores; however, sample sizes were small, limiting generalizability of these results.

Summarized PedsQL scores over 96 weeks were generally below the means reported in healthy children, but were within one SD, which may be a meaningful cutoff point for being at risk of impaired HRQoL. 12 Patients who achieved enteral autonomy had lower PN/IV requirements at the start of the LTE studies than patients who did not, which could explain the lack of association between change in PN/IV volume and PedsQL scores over time (i.e., they may have had higher and more stable HRQoL from baseline owing to lower PN/IV requirements). A previous study that investigated change in family members' HRQoL found that total family impact module score and caregiver HRQoL improved following achievement of enteral autonomy. 8 Here, the family impact module was not investigated in relation to change in PN/IV volume or HRQoL over time, because the focus of this study was on patient‐reported scores in relation to enteral autonomy. Change in PedsQL scores due to teduglutide treatment may not be fully captured here because scores were assessed from the start of the LTE studies rather than from the initiation of teduglutide treatment. This meant that patients had already been exposed to teduglutide and HRQoL may have improved before the start of the LTE studies. Measuring HRQoL before teduglutide initiation would be of interest for future studies.

This study has several strengths. Pooled data from two multicenter, longitudinal LTE studies provided a large sample size for a pediatric rare disease and provided cross‐sectional survey data covering various stages of the patient journey. We used PedsQL, a validated tool with published mean scores in healthy children and children with various chronic conditions, 12 to evaluate and compare the HRQoL of pediatric patients with SBS‐IF. The patient‐reported response rate here (75%) was consistent with those reported in previous studies, 13 , 21 while the caregiver response rate was higher than a previous study (93% vs. 72%). 16 Finally, this study encompassed patient self‐reported, caregiver‐proxy‐reported, and caregiver‐self‐reported data, providing a wide overview of HRQoL of patients and their families.

Some limitations should be considered. Measures of HRQoL were collected at the start of the LTE studies only, therefore the initial impact of teduglutide treatment may not have been captured. Not all social determinants of health could be captured in this study. The impact of comorbidities could not be assessed in the current study owing to the small sample size, but is an important consideration for future research, in particular within real‐world evidence studies. Furthermore, symptoms specific to SBS‐IF such as diarrhea/stomal output, gastrointestinal symptoms, diet, and eating and drinking habits were not assessed in this study. Selection bias may have occurred due to strict enrollment criteria in clinical trials; pediatric patients and caregivers participating in clinical trials might have a different outlook than those in routine clinical practice, which may impact HRQoL measurements. Subgroup analyses by age groups were not performed, thus the possible inconsistencies in self‐reported measures in younger children has not been fully accounted for. 40 Challenges exist when collecting both child and caregiver reports in parallel, particularly when results differ, which may reflect differing experiences of the same construct. 40

CONCLUSIONS

HRQoL of pediatric patients with SBS‐IF receiving teduglutide remained stable across the 96‐week study and teduglutide treatment was positively associated with HRQoL. Pediatric patients who are older and have more remnant small intestine might experience the greatest HRQoL benefit from teduglutide treatment. Improvement in HRQoL among patients who achieved enteral autonomy was not detected, although this may be impacted by the lower PN/IV volume reported by these patients at baseline. Future studies will assess HRQoL from before initiation of treatment to better understand how teduglutide can improve HRQoL and to evaluate unmet needs in domains such as school functioning.
