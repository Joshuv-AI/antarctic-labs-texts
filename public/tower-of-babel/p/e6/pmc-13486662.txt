Millennial clonal persistence and transposable element-biased somatic mutations in an herbaceous plant (Typha latifolia)
Authors: Chichi Zhao, Ziyu Wang, Hui Zeng, Yifeng Yao, Dan Yu, Xinwei Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractClonal reproduction represents a fundamental strategy in plant propagation, enabling the formation of extensive genetically identical populations through persistent asexual growth. Understanding the spatial-temporal dynamics of these clonal systems, particularly with regard to their age, size, and somatic mutation patterns, is crucial for unravelling the evolutionary ecology of long-lived plant species. While such investigations have been conducted in woody perennials, herbaceous clonal systems remain markedly understudied. We address this knowledge gap through an integrated genomic study (including de novo assembly of a gap-free reference genome and whole-genome resequencing of 72 spatially distributed samples) of Typha latifolia, a rhizomatous perennial inhabiting a 103.2 ha alpine wetland in Sichuan, China. We identify this population as a clonal population and estimated the clone’s age to be ~1900 ( ± 100) years, with a species-level mutation rate and radiocarbon. Somatic mutations are significantly enriched in transposable elements (TEs), 71% occurring in TEs with one-third genomic coverage ( ~ 33% of callable sites and ~34% of total genome), and are significantly fixed in TEs in 50 out of 72 sampled ramets. This study provides insights into both clonal persistence and clonal expansion capacity and somatic mutation accumulation in herbaceous plants, advancing understanding of clonal plant survival and evolution.Subject terms: Molecular ecology, Evolutionary genetics, Plant ecology, Genomics

Introduction

Clonal plants exhibit a remarkable ability to replicate parental genetic information, a key factor in their success1–3. In agriculture, a considerable number of crops are propagated asexually to maintain stable and desirable traits across generations2,4,5. Similarly, invasive plants often use asexual reproduction to rapidly colonise local habitats and form dense populations, which enhances their competitive ability against native species and disrupts local ecosystems6,7. Native plants likewise employ vegetative propagation to optimise resource use and niche occupancy3, forming large clonal patches or populations that span from metres to hundreds of kilometres in scale8.

Accurate quantification of asexual reproduction duration and clonal population size is essential for advancing our understanding of plant survival strategies9. Traditional approaches for identifying clonemates relied on phenotypic traits, patch structure analysis, and multilocus genotyping10–12. Recent advances have incorporated reduced-representation genome sequencing and whole-genome resequencing, utilising single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) for higher-resolution clonal discrimination8,13–16. Although methodological approaches differ, most studies employ pairwise genetic distance metrics or similarity matrices with predefined thresholds to assign clonal membership. A persistent challenge arises in populations with low genetic diversity, where high shared similarity between individuals can obscure the distinction between clonemates and non-clonemates17. This methodological limitation can be addressed through a theoretical principle: all members of the same genotype should share many heterozygous loci, as mitotic inheritance preserves the heterozygous SNPs of the ancestral zygote in all clonal descendants18. Building on this principle, Yu et al. developed and theoretically and experimentally validated a robust clonemate identification method based on genome-wide shared heterozygosity17.

Estimating the age of herbaceous clonal populations remains a significant challenge in plant biology. Traditional methods, such as measuring annual clonal growth rates or employing size- and stage-structured matrix models, often yield imprecise estimates9. Recently, Heinze and Fussi proposed a promising approach using genetic divergence generated by somatic mutations to estimate genet lifespan, which is based on the fact that constant division of mitotic cells in clonal plants leads to the accumulation of somatic mutations over time19. Early observations of genetic differentiation patterns at microsatellite loci in multiple clonal populations of Populus tremuloides were consistent with models of increasing population size, enabling pairwise divergence to serve as an age proxy20. Despite its potential, somatic mutation-based aging has been underutilised due to technical challenges in detecting somatic mutations and distinguishing them from allelic variation9. Recent advances in sequencing technologies and genomic analysis methods now enable high-resolution somatic mutation detection18,21. Notably, Yu et al. introduced a novel molecular clock based on genome-wide accumulation of fixed somatic mutations, demonstrating that fixed somatic mutation accumulation occurs near-linearly after an initial lag phase, with a rate determined by mitotic mutation frequency rather than asexual reproduction time22. This genetic clock represents a powerful tool for studying the longevity and population dynamics of clonal species23–25.

Somatic mutations represent a fundamental source of genetic variation in clonal plants and play a crucial role in their evolutionary dynamics3,26,27. Consequently, elucidating somatic mutation patterns is a prerequisite and basis for understanding the evolution of clonal plants27. In herbaceous clonal plants, ramets naturally become physically independent because their connections, such as the rhizome, disintegrate within a few years18, and they exhibit significantly shorter lifespans than their woody counterparts. These differences in ramet autonomy and clone regeneration rates between herbaceous and woody plants likely lead to distinct somatic mutation patterns and differential responses to environmental changes. Despite their evolutionary significance, studies of somatic mutation patterns in natural populations have predominantly focused on trees21,28–30, with herbaceous plants remaining largely unexplored except for a single study on seagrasses18. This knowledge gap leaves the evolutionary fate of somatic mutations in large, long-lived herbaceous clonal populations poorly understood, highlighting the need for further investigation.

Here, we focus on Typha latifolia (common cattail), a rhizomatous perennial herb capable of both sexual and asexual reproduction31. This species exhibits vigorous clonal growth through lateral rhizome production, generating new ramets that establish as independent units. Notably, T. latifolia demonstrates remarkable clonal expansion capacity: a single plant can develop a network of rhizomes with dozens of ramets covering an area 10 feet in diameter within a single growing season32. The clonal system features relatively short-lived ramets (≤3 years lifespan) that typically separate from parent plants within 2–3 years31, suggesting strong potential for extensive clonal population formation. We found a population of T. latifolia spanning 103.2 hectares in an alpine wetland of Sichuan, China. Using a combination of gap-free reference genome assembly and high-depth whole-genome resequencing (72 samples across the wetland), we first confirmed the clonal nature of this population. Subsequent genomic dating, calibrated with a species-specific mutation rate and supported by radiocarbon dating of Typha-bearing sediments, estimated the clone’s age at ~1900 ± 100 years. Analysis of somatic mutation patterns in the population revealed several findings: 71% of somatic mutations occurred in transposable elements (TEs); this enrichment was significant, given that TEs comprise only ~33% of callable genomic sites and ~34% of the total genome; fixed somatic mutations showed even stronger TE association. This study provides insights into both the spatial-temporal dynamics of herbaceous clonal populations and the genomic patterns of somatic mutation accumulation in long-term clonal lineages of herbaceous plants.

Results

Analysis of the reference genome

We de novo assembled the high-quality genome of T. latifolia through Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) long-read sequencing of a representative individual from an alpine wetland of Sichuan, China population (Fig. 1A). The final gap-free assembly spans 216,955,151 bp organised into 15 chromosomes and two unplaced scaffolds (Table S1), with the ultrahigh sequencing depth (Fig. 1B) enabling a remarkably contiguous draft comprising just 17 primary contigs that remained structurally intact throughout the assembly process. Chromatin conformation capture (Hi-C) scaffolding confirmed the chromosomal assignment of the 15 largest scaffolds (Fig. 1C, Table S2). The assembly demonstrates exceptional completeness, with 99.86% and 99.84% mapping rates for ONT and Illumina short-read (next-generation sequencing; NGS) reads, respectively, and achieves a BUSCO completeness score of 99.92% (including 99.01% of single-copy and 0.81% of duplicated genes out of 1614 conserved orthologs). Further quality metrics included: a 21 k-mer analysis estimating 204,247,699 bp genome size (indicating 106.2% completeness relative to our assembly); contig and scaffold N50 values exceeding 14.5 Mb; a long terminal repeats (LTRs) Assembly Index (LAI) score of 11.84 confirming reference-grade quality, and Merqury Quality Values of 41.60 (NGS) and 42.08 (ONT) validating reference-grade base-level accuracy. Our annotation pipeline identified that 35.23% of the genome is repetitive, comprising 33.81% transposable elements (TEs)—including 2.33% DNA transposons, 0.73% LINEs, 24.60% LTRs, 0.003% SINEs, and 6.14% unclassified TEs—and 1.42% non‑TE repeats (satellites, simple repeats, and TRF/tandem repeats). Repeat analysis revealed abundant Gypsy and Copia LTR retrotransposons (Fig. 2A). We also annotated 19,984 protein‑coding genes, and assigned functional annotations to 98.14% of them (19,612 genes).

The high-quality, gap-free chromosome-level assembly generated here represents a substantial advance over the previously published scaffold-level draft genome of T. latifolia33. Compared with the earlier scaffold assembly (286.77 Mb, 1158 scaffolds, N50 = 8.71 Mb, BUSCO = 96.03%; generated from an Ontario, Canada accession), our assembly is fully anchored and ordered into 15 pseudomolecules and shows markedly improved contiguity (N50 > 14.5 Mb) and near-complete gene-space recovery (BUSCO 99.92%). Notably, our reference was built from a high-elevation East Asian population (the clonal population analyzed here). In contrast, the previous reference was derived from North America (Table S3). This geographic separation suggests potential lineage divergence and intraspecific genome-size variation, and our assembly should better support read mapping and somatic variant discovery in East Asian populations by reducing reference mismatch and mapping bias. Consistent with its accuracy, our assembly size (216.96 Mb) closely matches the genome size independently estimated by k-mer analysis (204.25 Mb; 106.2%). Together, these improvements provide a more reliable and regionally relevant genomic resource for evolutionary and ecological studies of T. latifolia, particularly in East Asia.

Our phylogenetic analysis placed T. latifolia within the Poales clade, with all major nodes receiving strong support (posterior probability = 1.00), confirming its divergence from other emergent monocots in agreement with established timelines (Fig. 2A, Table S4). The divergence time between T. latifolia and its close relative Ananas comosus (pineapple) was estimated to be 93.28 Mya (95% Highest Posterior Density, HPD: 91.67 – 95.09 Mya), consistent with previous estimates of 99.8 to 95.6 Mya34,35.

Using the ζ whole genome duplication (WGD) event – a shared paleopolyploidization in seed plants dated to 310 Mya36 – as a calibration point, we identified three distinct WGD events in T. latifolia (Fig. 2B-F). Synonymous substitution rate (Ks) distribution analysis revealed: (1) the ancient ζ WGD event, evidenced by concordant Ks peaks in T. latifolia (Ks = 3.42), A. comosus (Ks = 3.61), Oryza sativa (Ks = 3.72), and Sorghum bicolour (Ks = 3.76); (2) the σ WGD event ( ~ 130 Mya), marked Ks peak in T. latifolia (Ks = 1.44), A. comosus (Ks = 1.42), O. sativa (Ks = 1.45), and S. bicolour (Ks = 1.55), coinciding with early monocot radiation (Fig. 2B–F); (3) A recent, lineage-specific WGD event (Fig. 2A) in T. latifolia (Ks = 0.767, ~69 Mya) that aligns temporally with the Cretaceous-Paleogene boundary, potentially associated with post-extinction adaptive radiation37.

We calculated the species-level mutation rate for T. latifolia as 5.52 ± 0.16 × 10−9 site−1∙year−1, where Ks = 3.42 ± 0.10 (synonymous substitutions per site) and T = 310 Mya (divergence time) were obtained from the ζ WGD calibration in T. latifolia. This rate is comparable to the spontaneous mutation rates reported in Arabidopsis thaliana: 6.95 × 10−9 per site per generation38.

Verifying clonal population and identifying somatic mutations

We verified the clonal nature of the T. latifolia population through comprehensive genomic analyses of 72 samples across the wetland (Fig. 1A). Each sample yielded approximately 10 Gb of whole-genome resequencing data, with filtered reads aligning to the reference genome, at high efficiency (98.30– 99.88% mapping rate) and an average sequencing depth of 45× coverage (Supplementary Data 1, Table S5). GATK variant calling revealed 13,808–18,567 mutation sites per sample, including 7009-10,768 heterozygous indels (72.37–78.83% of total indels per sample) and 1909–2651 heterozygous SNPs (46.71–51.62% of total SNPs per sample) (Fig. 3A). The clonal origin of all 72 samples was robustly supported by two key lines of genomic evidence. First, histograms of shared heterozygous SNPs exhibited a pronounced peak at the full-sample level, with no intermediate peaks across the range from 1 to 72 samples—a pattern sharply contrasting with expectations for a sexually reproducing population (Figs. 3B, C, S1). Second, Identity-by-descent (IBD; pairwise PI_HAT > 0.9979) and identity-by-state (IBS; pairwise value = 1.000) analyses confirmed near-complete genetic identity among the samples, effectively excluding detectable signals of sexual reproduction (Supplementary Data 2, 3). Compared to the high numbers of shared heterozygous SNPs at the full-sample level reported in clonal seagrass studies17,18, the relatively low number observed here may reflect the initially low heterozygosity of the clonal founder plant in this population. Because these heterozygous variants are shared across all 72 ramets, they most likely represent founder-derived standing heterozygosity inherited through clonal propagation, rather than independent de novo mutations arising repeatedly at the same loci.

Validation through Sanger sequencing of 11 selected loci (representing 44 heterozygous SNPs) confirmed the presence of these variants in all 72 individuals (Figs. S2, S3, and Table S6), further supporting clonal identity. Interestingly, our Sanger results showed better concordance with GATK calls than with hard-filtered variants (Supplementary Data 4), diverging from previous reports18 and highlighting the importance of specialized somatic mutation tools for accurate variant detection in clonal populations.

To improve mutation detection reliability, we implemented a robust pipeline incorporating four somatic mutation callers: GATK Mutect239, VarScan240, LoFreq41, and Strelka242, with SomticSeq43 integration. This pipeline identified 1434-2434 somatic mutation sites per sample, including 251–795 heterozygous indels (80.45%-92.48% of total indels per sample) and 556-1109 heterozygous SNPs (49.60–66.81% of total SNPs per sample) and produced variant distribution patterns consistent with GATK results (Fig. 3D, E). When compared across three datasets (variants from GATK; variants from all four somatic variant callers; and variants supported by at least two somatic variant callers), the latter had significantly fewer errors (Fig. 3G, F, Supplementary Data 4) and the lowest false positive rate (FPR = 0.17; Table S7), providing the most reliable variants for downstream analyses. Kinship analyses showed exceptionally high genetic similarity among individuals, with pairwise values of IBD PI_HAT > 0.864 (Supplementary Data 5) and IBS > 0.944 (Supplementary Data 6), conclusively demonstrating their derivation from a single clonal lineage.

Age and development routes of the clonal population

Using the species-level mutation rate of 5.52 ± 0.16 × 10−9 site−1∙year−1, we estimated the clonal population’s age to be 1781 ± 196 years (Supplementary Data 1). This suggests that the ancestral lineage from which the current population has persisted in this wetland ecosystem for an estimated two millennia.

Radiocarbon dating of Typha pollen-bearing sediment cores confirms independent support that Typha has been continuously present in this ecosystem over the estimated two-millennia timeframe, verifying the long-term ecological suitability for the persistence of this lineage. Our age-depth model, constructed from four Accelerator Mass Spectrometry (AMS) 14C dates, showed that the core encompasses approximately 4300 years before the present (yr BP) (Fig. 4A–D, Table S8). Continuous Typha pollen presence in the upper 20 cm (samples Nos. 1-8) of the core defined the earliest definitive appearance of Typha at 1859-1964 yr BP, though pollen abundance remained low relative abundance (R.A.: 0.6%, 3 grains/500 counted) (Fig. 4E).

Analysis of the pollen assemblages revealed a high degree of taxonomic diversity, indicating four stages of climate change during the presence of Typha pollen (Figs. 4E, S4). Stage I (2160–1960 yr BP, samples Nos. 9 and 10): dominance of Pinus (R.A.: 26.3%, likely Pinus tabuliformis and P. armandii) and minor cold- and dry-tolerant taxa (Picea: 6.5%, likely Picea brachytyla and P. neoveitchii; Abies: 3.3%, likely Abies fargesii var. faxoniana; Ephedra: 0.2%), indicating warm and arid conditions. Stage II (1960–1280 yr BP, samples Nos. 6-8): sharp decline in Pinus (11.5%) and increased Picea (12.5%), reflecting a colder and wetter climate. Stage III (1280–670 yr BP, samples Nos. 4 and 5): Pinus resurgence (44.1%) alongside reduced Picea (4.3%), signalling renewed warmth and aridity. Stage IV (670 yr BP to the present, samples Nos. 1–3): herbaceous expansion (Artemisia: 26%; Poaceae: 9.3%) alongside declining Pinus (19.4%), suggesting cool and moist conditions.

Spatial-genetic analyses revealed a network-like growth pattern (Figs. 5 and S5), suggesting a history of complex back-and-forth movement rather than simple linear expansion. Although relationships among extant samples are traceable, the precise geographic origin remains unresolved, though a single founding ancestor is inferred.

Patterns of somatic mutations

Analysis of somatic mutations across 72 samples revealed significant enrichment in TEs. Although TEs comprise ~34% of the genome and ~33% of callable sites, they accounted for 71% of observed somatic mutations (Figs. 6A, S6–S77; Supplementary Data 7). Fisher exact tests confirmed this strong enrichment in all samples (Tables S9–S80), indicating that TEs are major mutation hotspots. When summarized by TE superfamily (as a fraction of all somatic mutations in each sample), somatic mutations were unevenly distributed among TE categories. LTR retrotransposons contributed the largest fractions, with Gypsy elements comprising 13.787% of the genome yet accounting for 23.30–26.64% of somatic mutations per sample, and Copia elements comprising 5.740% of the genome yet accounting for 9.16–11.05% of somatic mutations. In contrast, LINE/L1 represents only 0.674% of the genome but accounts for 5.42–8.25% of somatic mutations (Supplementary Data 7). Mutation type statistics of SNPs showed a 2:1 ratio of transitions to transversions, with C > T and G > A as the most frequent changes and G > C and C > G as the rarest (Fig. 6B, S78). The number of non-synonymous mutations per sample ranged from 7 to 21, with an average of 13.67 ± 3.08 (Figs. 6C, Supplementary Data 8). Notably, fixed somatic mutations were detected in 50 of 72 samples and were significantly enriched in TEs (Tables S81–S152).

Discussion

We present evidence for the existence of an exceptionally large (103.2 ha) and long-lived (~1900 ± 100 years) clonal population of T. latifolia, establishing a significant temporal and spatial framework for herbaceous clonal plants on the continent. Genomic analysis using whole-genome resequencing data offers higher accuracy in clonality identification than microsatellite genotyping17,18. Using the same method employed for eelgrass18 in conjunction with kinship analysis against negative controls, we confirmed that all 72 T. latifolia samples across the wetland belong to a single genet, a finding further validated by Sanger sequencing. This supports the existence of a massive clonal population, with a maximum linear span exceeding 2 km (Fig. 1A), surpassing all previously documented continental herbaceous plant clones9,11. The remarkable size of this clone may be attributed to T. latifolia’s capacity for rapid clonal expansion through multidirectional rhizome elongation and persistent clone regeneration31,32. We also inferred the age of the clonal population to be close to 2000 years old, based on the species-level mutation rate of T. latifolia: 5.52 ± 0.16 × 10−9 site−1∙year−1. This finding is further supported by evidence from radiocarbon dating of the sediments bearing Typha pollen. This T. latifolia clonal population is older than dated eelgrass clones, which have been dated to a maximum of 1403 years22. Extreme clone longevity is often associated with either exclusively vegetative propagation21,44 or extremely low levels of sexual reproduction8,45. Interestingly, our field observations reveal that most individuals produce fruit containing a certain proportion of mature seeds. We propose that this population, located at the southernmost edge of T. latifolia’s East Asian range in a high-elevation, marginal environment, persists due to the selective advantages of clonal propagation under such stressful conditions. Future research should focus on identifying the specific ecological or physiological barriers—such as microsite availability, temperature thresholds, or hydrological dynamics—that inhibit seedling establishment and have allowed clones to persist for millennia.

Somatic mutations accumulate in ramets, leading to genetic mosaicism within clonal populations18,27. However, detecting these mutations presents significant challenges as they occur stochastically at low allele frequencies, requiring both complete genomic information and highly reliable variant calling28,30,43. Somatic mutation detection often produces false positive candidates due to sample quality and systematic errors43. Our study overcomes these limitations through several key advantages: (1) A completely gap-free reference genome assembled from ultra-deep ONT reads (over 150× coverage), providing a foundation for accurate somatic mutation analysis; (2) Sampling the reference genome directly from the clonal population, ensuring perfect genetic background matching; and (3) High-depth resequencing data (average 45× coverage) from representative samples across the wetland, offering credible material for population-level somatic mutation analysis. This comprehensive genomic resource package enables precise identification and rigorous validation of somatic mutations across the entire clonal population.

Somatic mutations represent a natural source of genetic variation that can be inherited through sexual and asexual reproduction in plants, playing a potential role in evolutionary adaptation27,30,46–48. In the clonal T. latifolia population, analysing somatic mutation patterns may provide insights into how the population has responded to climate change over the past ~1900 ± 100 years—a timespan supported by shifts in pollen assemblages across different stratigraphic layers (Fig. 4E, S3). Somatic mutations are strikingly enriched in TEs (Figs. 6A, S7–S78, and Table S9–S80), in a pattern similar to that observed in a 330-year-old Populus trichocarpa tree29, suggesting that TEs serve as primary drivers of genetic variation in clonal plants. This finding aligns with studies in the model plant A. thaliana, where TEs facilitate high mutation rates and thus offer potential adaptive advantages5,49–51. This study reveals that an accelerated accumulation of somatic mutations within TEs serves as a genomic hallmark of long-term clonality in T. latifolia. Notably, this enrichment is not uniform across TE families. TE enrichment is heterogeneous across TE classes (Supplementary Data 7), suggesting that TE-specific chromatin and epigenetic contexts contribute to differences in mutation accumulation. In plants, TE families are differentially regulated by epigenetic silencing (e.g., DNA methylation and heterochromatin), and these contexts can shape the local mutational landscape52–54. While the direct contribution of these small-scale mutations to past climate adaptation remains unclear, they may function as a neutral molecular clock for estimating clonal age. Simultaneously, TEs represent a potential reservoir of genetic variation that could become relevant in future environmental contexts—a hypothesis that merits further experimental investigation. Future studies should experimentally test the fitness effects of candidate mutations under controlled conditions mimicking reconstructed paleoclimates and integrate epigenomic data to assess whether TE-associated mutations correlate with regulatory changes.

In conclusion, this study establishes a temporal and spatial framework for understanding clonal propagation in herbaceous plants, demonstrating through genomic analysis and radiocarbon dating that a T. latifolia clone has persisted for approximately 2000 years across a 103.2 ha alpine wetland. We reveal two key patterns of somatic mutation accumulation in this clonal population: a striking enrichment of mutations in TEs and a strong association of fixed mutations with TEs. These findings significantly advance our understanding of long-term clonal dynamics in perennial plants and provide crucial insights into the genomic mechanisms that may underlie the adaptation and evolution of clonal plants.

Methods

Sample collection, DNA extraction and sequencing

The study site was located in the Ganhaizi wetland, Jiuzhaigou County, Sichuan, China (33.25° N, 103.76° E; elevation: 2,625 m) (Fig. 1A). This area represents the southernmost extent of T. latifolia’s distribution in East Asia55. Although T. latifolia predominantly reproduces asexually in natural environments56, the clonal structure of this population remains uncharacterized. Asexual reproduction is hypothesized to occur more frequently at niche edges57. To comprehensively assess the genetic structure of this T. latifolia population at the whole-genome scale, we first conducted a wetland survey using drone imagery, revealing that T. latifolia occupies approximately 12.5 ha of the 103.2 ha wetland (Fig. 1A).

For high-quality reference genome assembly, we collected an intact T. latifolia specimen, which was transported live for genomic DNA extraction. We employed a multi-platform sequencing strategy, combining: Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) long reads (~150× coverage, 36.58 GB clean data) generated from fresh leaf tissue using the SQK-LSK109 kit on the PromethION platform; chromatin conformation capture (Hi-C) scaffolding (DpnII-digested library, ~110× coverage, 24.17 GB clean data) sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (150 bp PE reads); Illumina short-read (next-generation sequencing; NGS) polishing (~60× coverage, 15.47 GB clean data) from sheared DNA (350 bp fragments, 150 bp PE reads); PacBio HiFi full-length transcriptome (39.29 GB clean data) from a mixed RNA sample (leaves, shoots, roots); and Illumina transcriptome sequencing (RNA-Seq) of leaf/shoot RNA (Table S1).

For population genetic analysis, we collected 72 individuals across accessible wetland regions, maintaining at least 10 m spacing to minimize the likelihood of sampling rhizome-connected ramets58. Due to vegetation patchiness, some samples were collected at shorter intervals (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Data 1). To establish a robust baseline for clonal identification, we incorporated 20 negative control samples from two geographically distinct populations: one from the Gaya River in Jilin, China (43.02° N, 129.80° E; elevation 109 m; n = 10), and another along the Hutubi River in Xinjiang, China (44.28° N, 86.88° E; elevation 461 m; n = 10) (Table S5). All samples were preserved on dried ice (−80 °C) during transport. DNA was extracted, sheared (~350 bp), and sequenced on the NovaSeq 6000 platform (150 bp PE reads), achieving over 45× coverage (10 GB clean data) per individual.

Genome assembly, annotation, and analysis

ONT and PacBio HiFi reads were filtered using Nanofilt59 v2.8.0 with the parameters: -q 10 -l 500, while Hi-C, NGS, and RNA-Seq reads were filtered using fastp60 v0.24.0 with default parameters. Genome size and heterozygosity were estimated using jellyfish61 v2.2.10 with the parameters: -m 21 -s 1000000000, and genomescope262 v2.0.1 with the parameters: -k 21 -l 150. For draft assembly, >150× coverage ONT reads were assembled using NextDenovo63 v2.5.0 with the following parameter settings: read_cutoff = 1k, genome_size = 220 m, pa_correction = 2, sort_options = -m 50 g -t 30, minimap2_options_raw = -t 8, correction_options = -p 15, minimap2_options_cns = -t 8, nextgraph_options = -a 1. The yielding 17 contigs were polished with NextPolish64 v1.4.1 using ONT and NGS reads under the options: sgs_options = -max_depth 150 -bwa, lgs_options = -min_read_len 1k -max_depth 300, lgs_minimap2_options = -x map-ont. For chromosome-level scaffolding, Hi-C reads were aligned to the polished genome using 3D-DNA pipeline65 v180922, and scaffolds were manually curated into 15 chromosomes with Juicebox66 v2.20.00 (Table S2). We assessed the completeness of the reference genome by mapping ONT reads and NGS reads to it using Minimap267 v2.14 and BWA68 v0.7.17, respectively. Mapping statistics were calculated using the flagstat function in SAMtools69 v1.15.1. We evaluated assembly quality using the embryophyta_odb10 database via BUSCO70 v5.3.0. The assembly’s continuity was validated using the LTR Assembly Index (LAI)71, which was calculated using LTR_retriever72 v2.9.0. Overall consensus quality was estimated using Merqury73 v1.3.0.

To identify repetitive sequences, a combined strategy of de novo prediction and homology-based search was employed prior to gene annotation. De novo prediction was performed using RepeatModeler74 v.2.0.2 to generate an initial set of repetitive sequences. Furthermore, a non-redundant set of long terminal repeats (LTRs) was identified using LTR_FINDER_parallel75 with parameters “-size 1,000,000 -time 300” and refined with LTR_retriever76 v.2.9.0. Homology-based searching was performed by RepeatMasker77 v.4.1.2 against the RepBase RepeatMasker Edition library (RepBaseRepeatMaskerEdition-20181026). TE proteins were then predicted using RepeatProteinMask in RepeatMasker. All identified sequences were clustered using CD-HIT78 v.4.8.1 at a 90% identity threshold (-c 0.9 -aS 0.8) to create a non-redundant consensus library. For final masking, RepeatMasker was executed with rmblast (-e rmblast), soft-masking (-xsmall), GFF output (-gff), slow sensitivity (-s), and parallelization (-pa 8). Overlapping matches were resolved using the priority order TE protein > RepeatMasker nucleotide matches > TRF > other repeats, and coordinates were merged using custom Python scripts so that the reported masking percentage (35.23%) reflects non-overlapping genomic loci.

For protein-coding gene prediction, an integrated approach incorporating transcriptomic evidence, homology information, and ab initio prediction was applied. Ab initio annotations were performed using the Braker279 v2.1.6 pipeline. RNA-seq-based annotations were performed using Trinity80 v2.13.2 to de novo assemble the RNA reads. These assembled reads were then aligned to the reference genome using Hisat281 v2.2.1 and annotated using StringTie82 v2.2.1. Homologous predictions were made using Arabidopsis thaliana (TAIR10), Zea mays (B73 RefGen v4), and Oryza sativa (IRGSP-1.0) as homologous prediction species with GenomeThreader83 v1.7.1. Annotations were integrated using EVidenceModeler84 v1.1.1, with weights of 2 for ab initio annotations, 6 for RNA-seq-based annotations, and 2 for homologous predictions. The PASA85 v2.4.1 pipeline was then used to refine the final structure annotations. Functional annotation was performed using the Swiss-Prot86, Nr87, KEGG88, InterPro89, GO90, and Pfam91 databases. To assess the completeness of the final gene set, BUSCO v5.3.0 was run against the embryophyta_odb10 dataset. To contextualize assembly quality relative to the previously published T. latifolia assembly33, we also compared key assembly metrics between the two assemblies (Table S3).

A species-level phylogenetic tree was reconstructed using assembled genomes from T. latifolia and ten other species (Amborella trichopoda, Vitis riparia, Spirodela intermedia, Phalaenopsis equestris, Althaea officinalis, Elaeis guineensis, Musa acuminata, O. sativa, Sorghum bicolour, and Ananas comosus) (Table S4). Orthologous genes were identified using OrthoFinder92 v2.2.7, yielding 101 single-copy orthologous genes across all taxa. These gene sequences were then aligned using MAFFT93 v7.505. Divergence times were estimated using BEAST94 v2.7.7 with a relaxed molecular clock model, a chain length of 10,000,000, and 10,000 pre-burnin. Two calibration points were obtained from TimeTree95 5: 1) Sorghum–Oryza at 49 ± 1million years ago (Mya) and 2) Musa–Ananas at 113 ± 1 Mya.

Syntenic blocks in the genomes of O. sativa, S. bicolour, A. comosus, and T. latifolia were identified using MCScanX96 v1.0.0 with the default parameters. To infer WGD events, nonsynonymous (Ka) and synonymous (Ks) substitution rate values were calculated between the syntenic blocks using KaKs_Calculator97 v2.0. WGD events were inferred from Ks distribution curves fitted to Gaussian models using the MATLAB 2022b curve fitting toolbox.

Mutation identification and clone verification

The raw NGS reads of 72 main samples and 20 negative controls were filtered using fastp60 v0.23.2 with the expression “-w 8 -q 20 -u 20 -n 5 -l 50 -y 20 -x --umi --umi_loc read1 --umi_len 10”. The clean reads were then mapped to the reference genome using BWA68 v0.7.17 with the MEM algorithm. The mapping rate was calculated using the flagstat function in SAMtools v1.15.1. After PCR duplicates were removed using GATK39 v4.2.2.0, variants were called using the GATK HaplotypeCaller and GenotypeGVCFs pipelines. SNPs were filtered using the expression “DP < 30 || DP > 8000 || QD < 15.0 || MQ < 58.0 || FS > 2.0 || SOR > 3.0 || MQRankSum < -12.5 || ReadPosRankSum < -4.0” in GATK v4.2.2.0. Further filtering was performed using VCFtools98 v0.1.14 with the expression “--maf 0.01 --max-alleles 2 --max-missing-count 4 --min-alleles 2” to select credible variants. The histogram of shared heterozygous SNPs in both raw and filtered SNPs indicated that the 72 samples belonged to a single clone (Figs. 3B, C and S1). We also used PLINK99 v1.9 to evaluate genetic relatedness and clonality based on the SNP data, with negative controls included as a reference. Identity-by-descent (IBD) was estimated with the “-- genome” function to generate PI_HAT values. The IBD components Z0, Z1, and Z2 represent the probabilities that a pair of individuals share 0, 1, or 2 alleles identical by descent at a given locus, respectively. PI_HAT values summarize these components as the overall proportion of the genome shared IBD, while identity-by-state (IBS) values reflect the fraction of alleles identical in state regardless of ancestral origin. Genetically identical (clonal) samples are characterized by Z0 = 0, Z1 = 0, Z2 = 1.0, PI_HAT = 1.0, and IBS = 1.0. In contrast, first‑degree relatives (e.g., parent–offspring or full siblings) typically show PI_HAT ≈ 0.5 with Z2 ≤ 0.25, and unrelated individuals exhibit PI_HAT ≈ 0.0. To account for long‑term clonal evolution with somatic mutation accumulation, we applied relaxed thresholds, classifying samples as clonal if they met the following criteria: Z0 = 0, Z1 > 0, Z2 > 0.5, and PI_HAT > 0.75. Consistent with these expectations, our analysis confirmed the clonal identity of the 72 samples: pairwise IBD Z2 and PI_HAT values ranged from 0.9979 to 1.000 (Supplementary Data 2), and IBS values were uniformly 1.000 (Supplementary Data 3), sharply contrasting with results from negative control comparisons.

To validate the reliability of our variant calls and assess shared heterozygosity among individuals, we selected 11 heterozygous loci spanning 10 chromosomes (Fig. S2). These loci were chosen based on the following criteria: (1) high-quality variant calls supported by filtered credible variants, (2) heterozygous genotypes in most individuals, and (3) sufficient read depth (>30×) to enable reliable PCR amplification. The selection was not focused on mutation hotspots, but rather aimed to represent genome-wide polymorphic regions. Primer pairs were successfully designed for 11 regions using Primer3100 v4.1.0, and each primer pair was subsequently screened against the reference genome to ensure single-locus specificity. For loci located within transposable elements (TEs), we further evaluated primer suitability using an additional confirmation at the Chr07 locus by redesigning an anchored primer set spanning a longer interval, with one primer located within the TE near its boundary and the other placed in a unique flanking sequence outside the TE. The anchored-primer design produced results consistent with the original primer pair, supporting locus-specific amplification. PCR and Sanger sequencing confirmed the expected heterozygous alleles at all validated loci (Fig. S3, Tables S6, and Supplementary Data 4), thereby supporting the accuracy of our SNP calls and the shared (clonal) genetic background of the population.

To obtain high-confidence variants for downstream analyses of this clonal population, we employed four established somatic variant callers in tumour-only mode: GATK Mutect239 v4.2.2.0, VarScan240 v2.4.3, LoFreq41 v2.1.5, and Strelka242 v2.9.10. All callers were run with default parameters on all 72 samples. To increase reliability and reduce tool-specific bias, the outputs of these four callers were integrated using SomaticSeq43 v3.7.3, which employs a consensus-based ensemble strategy to score and classify variants based on the features derived from each tool’s output. Low-confidence variants were further filtered and merged with BCFtools101 v1.21. To evaluate the performance of each tool, we calculated false positive rates (FPR) and false negative rates (FNR) by separately comparing three datasets (mutation calls from GATK; variants from all four somatic variant callers; and variants supported by at least two somatic variant callers) with validated mutation sites from 11 Sanger-sequenced regions (Table S7). Based on these assessments, variants supported by at least two somatic variant callers were retained for downstream analyses.

Additionally, we used PLINK99 v1.9 to assess genetic relatedness and clonality based on credible somatic mutations supported by at least two somatic variant callers. IBD was estimated with the “-- genome” function to generate PI_HAT values (Supplementary Data 5). IBS similarity was also calculated using the “-- distance ibs square” function, producing a square IBS matrix (Supplementary Data 6).

Inferring the age and development routes of the clonal population

We inferred the population age using the species-level mutation rate, estimated from the WGD event 5.52 ± 0.16 × 10−9 site−1∙year−1. First, we used SAMtools v1.15.1 to calculate the number of callable sites per individual, defining these as genomic regions with sufficient coverage (>15×). Next, we counted the number of mutation sites per individual based on the final set of high-confidence variants supported by at least two somatic variant callers. The age (T) of an individual ramet was estimated using the following formula: T=mutationsitescallablesites×genomesizegenomesize×μ×1−falsenegativerate1−falsepositiverate. This formula assumes a diploid genome and incorporates the proportion of mutated sites per callable site. Finally, the population age was derived as the mean of all ramet ages.

We independently estimated the population age through radiocarbon dating of Typha pollen-bearing sediments collected from the Ganhaizi wetland. A 90-cm sediment core was extracted and analysed, revealing three distinct lithological units: the upper 0–30 cm layer consisted of grey-yellowish sandy mud with abundant plant remains; the intermediate 30‒60 cm layer of grey-yellowish sandy mud; and the basal 60‒90 cm layer of grey-black sandy mud. Four bulk sediments from depths of 14‒16 cm, 58‒60 cm, 60‒62 cm, and 88‒90 cm were subjected to Accelerator Mass Spectrometry (AMS) 14C dating at Beta Analytic Testing Laboratory (Miami, FL, USA). The measured 14C ages, quoted in conventional years BP with two-sigma level of confidence (95% probability), were calibrated using IntCal 20102 and rounded to the nearest 10 years103, with final calibrated ages presented in Table S8. An age-depth model for the core was constructed using the Bayesian age-depth modelling software BACON104 version 3.2 in R105 v 4.3.2 based on the four AMS 14C dates.

A total of 45 samples were taken from the core at 2 cm intervals for palynological analysis. Each sample (10 g) underwent treatment, including heavy liquid flotation followed by acetolysis, to extract pollen grains. One tablet of Lycopodium spores (batch: 2013001; 27560 ± 2643 spores/tablet) was added to each sample before treatment to calculate the pollen concentration. Pollen identification and counting were conducted under a Leica DM 4000 light microscope at × 400 magnification, with a minimum of 500 pollen grains and spores counted per sample to ensure statistical reliability. Taxonomic identification was achieved by comparing fossil pollen with modern pollen slides (Fig. S4) and consulting established palynological literature106–108. The relative abundance (R.A.) of each pollen taxon was calculated using R.A.=N/Nt (N, the count of a specific pollen type in the sample; Nt, the total sum of pollen grains and spores in the sample). Climate shifts at the study site were inferred based on variations in pollen assemblage composition. In the modelling, the prior age ( − 13 ± 30 BP) assigned to the surface was used.

To elucidate the relationships among the 72 clonal samples, we developed a novel analytical approach based on somatic mutation patterns. As all samples originated from a single ancestral clone and accumulated mutations independently without genetic recombination18, we utilized pairwise comparisons of somatic mutation profiles to reconstruct their divergence history. The analysis relied on share heterozygous sites as reliable markers of common ancestry109, with genetic distances calculated from the number of differential mutation sites between sample pairs. We constructed a hierarchical clustering structure analogous to a phylogenetic tree, where branch lengths quantitatively reflected the accumulation of shared variants within subgroups. The analytical procedure involved: (1) identifying the most genetically divergent sample pair (maximum mutation site differences); (2) bifurcating remaining samples based on their variant sharing patterns with these two anchors; (3) iteratively repeating this subdivision process until reaching terminal branches containing individual samples. The resulting dendrogram was subsequently georeferenced, enabling visualization of potential expansion pathways across the wetland landscape.

Exploring patterns of somatic mutations

To comprehensively characterize somatic mutation patterns in clonal T. latifolia, we performed detailed genomic distribution analyses using the reference GFF3 annotation to classify genomic features into four categories: gene regions (including exons, introns, and UTRs), promoters (2 kb upstream of gene regions), transposable elements (TEs), and intergenic regions (IGRs)29. Our custom Python scripts analysed somatic mutations in each sample to determine their genomic localization, accounting for potential overlaps between different genomic feature types. We quantified the relative proportions of somatic mutations occurring in each genomic feature to assess potential distribution biases. Additionally, we categorized mutations as transition or transversion based on nucleotide substitution types. The number of somatic mutation types in each sample was counted and summarised in Python scripts (Fig. S5). To investigate evolutionary implications, we reconstructed phylogenetic relationships using ordered somatic mutations, with ancestral variants (appearing in more samples) positioned closer to the root (Fig. S6). All analytical pipelines, including scripts for mutation classification, phylogenetic reconstruction, and selection analysis, are available in the Code Availability section110.

Statistics and reproducibility

Genomic analysis was performed on 72 spatially distributed samples of T. latifolia collected from the Ganhaizi wetland, supported by an average sequencing depth of 45× coverage per individual. To establish a rigorous baseline for clonal identification, 20 negative control samples from two geographically distinct populations in Jilin and Xinjiang, China, were included. Clonal membership was quantified using Identity-by-descent (IBD) and identity-by-state (IBS) analyses, with a PI_HAT threshold of > 0.75 defining membership; confirmed clonal values in the study population ranged from 0.9979 to 1.000.

To ensure the reproducibility and reliability of variant calling, we utilized a gap-free reference genome assembly built with ultra-deep Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) reads (150× coverage). An integrated bioinformatic pipeline employed four somatic mutation callers (GATK Mutect2, VarScan2, LoFreq, and Strelka2) via SomaticSeq. High-confidence somatic variants were defined as those supported by at least two callers, a method validated to achieve a low false positive rate (FPR = 0.17). These computational calls were further validated through Sanger sequencing of 11 selected loci, representing 44 heterozygous SNPs across all 72 individuals.

For palaeocological and temporal reconstruction, four AMS 14C dates were utilized to construct a Bayesian age-depth model using BACON. Palynological reliability was maintained by counting a minimum of 500 pollen grains and spores per sediment sample. Finally, the statistical significance of somatic mutation enrichment in transposable elements (TEs) compared to other genomic features was confirmed for all samples using Fisher’s exact tests.
