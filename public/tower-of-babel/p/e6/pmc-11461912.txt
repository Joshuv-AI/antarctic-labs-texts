Causal impacts of smoking on pain conditions and the mediating pathways: a mendelian randomization study
Authors: Kai Zhang, Hongjin Liang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Smoking is a risk factor for multiple diseases. We performed mendelian randomization (MR) analyses to investigate the causal association of smoking initiation on pain conditions and the potential mediating pathways. Genetic associated with smoking initiation at the genome-wide significance level were selected as instrumental variables. Genetic associations with 10 pain conditions were derived from the FinnGen and UK Biobank study. Multivariable MR analysis was conducted to explore the mediation effects of depression, insomnia and sedentary behavior. A series of sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the stability of our research findings. Genetic liability to smoking initiation was associated with an increased risk of angina pectoris, dorsalgia, low back pain, pain in limb, pain in joint, pain in thoracic spine and sciatica in both FinnGen and UK Biobank study. These causal associations were largely mediated by major depression (2.9- 39.5%), sedentary behavior (13.0- 31.2%), insomnia (10.3- 33.1%) and combination of all three mediators (30.2- 65.3%). The effects of smoking on outcomes were partly attenuated after adjusting for depression, sedentary behavior and insomnia respectively, and the direct effect of smoking initiation on pain was diminished toward null after adjusting for combined three mediators. These results were robust to sensitivity analyses. Our findings illustrated the causal effect of smoking and a broad range of pain conditions, and major depression, sedentary behavior and insomnia mediate many of these associations. Introduction

According to the definition by the International Association for the Study of Pain (IASP), pain is an unpleasant subjective and emotional experience related to tissue damage or potential tissue damage1. With economic development and changes in the living environment, the incidence of pain has significantly increased in recent years2. It is reported that the global prevalence of low back pain is 18.1%, musculoskeletal pain is 25%, headaches are 42%, and pelvic pain is 2-16%2,3.Previous studies have found that lifestyle habits such as dietary habits, sedentary behavior, and sleep can increase the risk of pain4–6. However, the mediating factors involved have been rarely studied.

Smoking continues to be a significant public health problem7. Research over the past two decades has documented an association between smoking and chronic pain8,9. A meta-analysis of cross-sectional data revealed that smokers have approximately a 30% higher likelihood of experiencing lower back pain10. Additionally, a prospective cohort study also confirmed this correlation11. However, previous observational studies are easily affected by confounding factors, and prior research did not explore the mediating factors between smoking and pain. The global prevalence of smoking is estimated to be approximately 1.18 billion individuals12, underscoring the importance of in-depth investigation into the adverse health consequences of smoking. This includes exploring the relationship between smoking and pain, an aspect that has been overlooked in previous studies.

While the majority of studies have documented a positive association between smoking and pain, many of these studies suffer from methodological limitations such as small sample sizes, short study durations, and a lack of effective consideration of potential confounding factors or spurious correlations13.

MR is a widely employed method that incorporates summary data from genome-wide association studies (GWAS) to determine causal associations between risk factors and outcomes14. MR leverages genetic variants as instrumental variables, which strengthens its ability to infer causality. This approach mitigates the potential confounding effects of environmental factors on the estimated relationships. In this study, we conducted a comprehensive MR analysis to explore the causal effects between smoking and pain conditions. Then we explored mediators in the pathway from smoking to pain conditions.

Methods

Study design

Figure 1 shows the study design overview. This MR study consisted of two analysis stages. In the first stage, we initially investigated the causal effects of four smoking initiation factors on ten prevalent pain conditions separately in the FinnGen and UK Biobank datasets by using univariable MR (UVMR). Only pain conditions that demonstrated significant associations in both resources were deemed meaningful and included for the subsequent mediation analysis in the second stage. In the second stage, we evaluated the mediating influence of three common risk factors on the causal relationships between smoking and these pain conditions by using MVMR. Given that the mediator variables involve extensive data from the UK Biobank, to mitigate potential biases arising from population overlap, the FinnGen database was utilized as the primary outcome in the mediation analysis. We performed MR analyses based on three key assumptions:1 significant associations exist between the single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and the exposure;2 the SNPs should be independent of any confounding factors;3 the SNPs only impact the outcome directly through the exposure variable15. We reported this study according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology using Mendelian Randomization (STROBE-MR)16. All data used in this study were obtained from public sources.

Finally, 106 significant instruments variants (P < 5 × 10−8) were obtained after linkage disequilibrium (r2 < 0.01 within 10,000 kb). These SNPs explained approximately 2.8% of the variation in smoking initiation. Detail information about GWASs included in our study was shown in Table 1 and Table S1-2.

Data source for outcomes

Genetic associations with ten pain conditions, including headache syndromes, facial pain, angina, dorsalgia, low back pain, pain in joints, pain in limbs, cervicalgia, pain in thoracic spine, and sciatica, were derived from the FinnGen study and the UK Biobank study. Genetic and electronic health record data were collected in the FinnGen study, which served as a comprehensive nationwide genetic investigation. Gastrointestinal diseases were identified through the utilization of ICD-8, ICD-9, and ICD-10 codes. To ensure accuracy and control for confounding factors, genome-wide association analyses were adjusted by accounting for sex, age, genetic components, and genotyping batch. The investigation into genetic associations with pain conditions utilized summary-level data from the latest publicly available FinnGen R9 data release (https://r9.finngen.fi). The UK Biobank study is a multicenter cohort study conducted in the United Kingdom between 2006 and 2010. This large-scale study recruited over of 500,000 participants18. To ensure comprehensive coverage of pain conditions, the UK Biobank summary statistics of European ancestry from GWAS conducted by Lee lab database (https://www.leelabsg.org/resources) and the MRCIEU database (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk) were used. In cases where the same outcome disease was present in both databases, we selected the one with the largest sample size. As a result, we obtained data for nine pain conditions, excluding angina.

Data source for mediators

Previous studies have indicated that sedentary behavior, depression, and insomnia are commonly associated with various pain conditions19–21. Hence, in this study, we incorporated these those three risk factors into our mediation analysis. For depression, a meta-analysis utilizing data from three large-scale genome-wide association studies, involving a total of 807,553 individuals (246,363 cases and 561,190 controls), investigating depression. The study encompassed various subtypes of depression; however, in our analysis, we only utilized Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) data obtained from the PGC dataset within that study22. The largest recently conducted GWAS provided publicly accessible data on sedentary behavior23. Sedentary behavior in this study was characterized by the duration participants spent watching television and included 408, 815 European-ancestry individuals. The reported mean daily leisure television watching from the original study was 2.8 h (standard deviation [SD] 1.5 h). The GWAS of insomnia included 453,379 European participants in the UK Biobank was used24. To evaluate insomnia symptoms, participants were queried using the following question: “Do you experience difficulty falling asleep at night or do you wake up during the night?” Response options included “never/rarely,” “sometimes,” “usually,” and “prefer not to answer”24.

Ethical approval statement

All GWAS data used in this study were obtained from public sources. The ethical approval and consent information for each GWAS included in this study can be found in the original articles17,18,22–25.

Mediation MR analysis

In the UVMR analysis, smoking initiation was association with seven out of ten pain-related disease outcomes. Furthermore, three selected mediators were found to be commonly associated with those seven pain-related diseases. Consequently, these seven pain-related diseases and three mediators were taken forward for MVMR mediation analysis26. the overall effect of exposure on each outcome can be deconstructed into direct and indirect effects. This enables the estimation of potential mediating effects and determines the proportion of the exposure’s impact on the outcome that operates through other considered exposures27,28. The considered mediators were included in this model both individually and all together. In order to determine the mediated proportion of the association between genetically predicted smoking initiation and the risk of pain, the estimate for the effect of smoking initiation on pain risk after adjusting for genetically predicted levels of the mediator factors was divided by the total effect of smoking initiation on pain risk estimated in the IVW univariable MR and subtracted from 127,29,30.

MR sensitivity analysis

Following the estimation of the effect size for each independent variable (IV) using the Wald ratio, we proceeded to combine these ratios by employing the inverse variance weighting (IVW) method. In this study, the main analysis involved utilizing the multiplicative random-effects IVW method. Additionally, we employed several sensitivity analysis methods to assess the stability of our results alongside the inverse variance weighting (IVW) approach. The MR-Egger and weighted median methods were utilized in estimating the causal effect, accounting for the possibility of some invalid instrumental variables31,32. MR-Egger constitutes a reliable method for evaluating pleiotropy, allowing the interpretation of the intercept term estimates as average pleiotropic effects across SNPs. To identify potential outliers and obtain adjusted results, we utilized the MR pleiotropy residual sum and outlier (MR-PRESSO) test. To evaluate the heterogeneity among instruments, we employed the Cochran’s Q test. Additionally, we utilized conditional F-statistics as a means to test the validity of the instruments. All analyses were conducted using R packages TwoSampleMR, MVMR, and MRPRESSO in R software 4.1.1.

Procedures of MR analysis

In order to mitigate the impact of heterogeneity on result bias, our study followed a three-step approach (Supplementary Fig. 1). Step 1 involved conducting MR analysis using all selected instrumental variables (IVs). Following this, Step 2 entailed assessing the MR analysis again by removing any outliers identified through MRPRESSO with a significance level of P < 0.05. If heterogeneity persisted, Step 3 involved excluding IVs with P-values less than 1 in the MR-PRESSO test and re-evaluating the MR analysis.

Results

Smoking initiation on ten pain conditions

106 independent genetic instruments were used in this MR analysis, and all F-analysis were over 10, suggesting no weak variants exited (Table S3).

Genetically predicted smoking initiation was association with a higher risk of angina pectoris (FinnGen [OR: 1.21, 95% CI:1.11, 1.33]; UK Biobank [OR: 1.38, 95% CI:1.22, 1.57]), dorsalgia (FinnGen [OR: 1.26, 95% CI:1.15, 1.34]; UK Biobank [OR: 1.01, 95% CI:1.01, 1.02]), low back pain (FinnGen [OR: 1.30, 95% CI:1.19, 1.43]; UK Biobank [OR: 1.01, 95% CI:1.01, 1.02]), pain in limb (FinnGen [OR: 1.26, 95% CI:1.15, 1.38]; UK Biobank [OR: 1.46, 95% CI:1.26, 1.68]), pain in joint (FinnGen [OR: 1.16, 95% CI:1.06, 1.28]; UK Biobank [OR: 1.32, 95% CI:1.12, 1.55]), pain in thoracic spine (FinnGen [OR: 1.40, 95% CI:1.15, 1.71]) and sciatica (FinnGen [OR: 1.27, 95% CI:1.01, 1.43]; UK Biobank [OR: 1.01, 95% CI:1.00, 1.01]) in both FinnGen and UK Biobank study (Fig. 2). In addition, smoking initiation was association with headache (OR: 1.37, 95% CI:1.18, 1.59) and facial pain (OR: 1.01, 95% CI:1.00, 1.01) in UK Biaobank but not FinnGen study, and association with cervicalgia (OR: 1.20, 95% CI:1.01, 1.43) in FinnGen but not UK Biobank.

Genetically predicted smoking initiation was associated with a high risk of major depression (OR: 1.31, 95% CI:1.25, 1.37), insomnia (OR: 1.03, 95% CI:1.02, 1.05) and longer sedentary behavior (OR: 1.14, 95% CI:1.10, 1.18). Heterogeneity was observed across the studies, but no evidence of potential horizontal pleiotropy existed.

Mediation effect of mediators in the associations between smoking initiation and seven pain conditions

MVMR mediation analyses were performed to investigate the mediating effect of genetically predicted smoking initiation on pain risk and the results were shown in Table 2. Smoking is a high-risk factor for pain and is attenuated after adjusting for major depression, sedentary behavior and insomnia respectively. But the direct effect of smoking on angina, pain in joint, pain in thoracic spine and sciatica was diminished toward null with adjustment for all three mediators together. Major depression explained 2.9– 39.5% of the effect of smoking on seven pain conditions. The proportion mediated by sedentary behavior in the associations between smoking initiation and outcomes was range from 13.0 to 31.2%. Insomnia explained 10.3– 33.1% and all those three mediators explained approximately 30.2- 65.3% of seven pain conditions (Table 2).

Discussion

This MR study investigated the causal association between smoking initiation and 10 pain disease, and whether these association impacted through some causal pathway. We provided novel evidence that genetically determined smoking was associated with high risks of angina pectoris, dorsalgia, low back pain, pain in limb, pain in joint, pain in thoracic spine and sciatica in both FinnGen and UK Biobank study. In particular, this MR study identified the mediating effect of three mediators in the effect of smoking initiation on seven pain disorders, with mediation proportions of 2.9– 39.5% for major depression, 13.0– 31.2% for sedentary behavior, and 10.3- 33.1% for insomnia. We notice that the proportion mediated by combination of three mediators was over 30% for the causal effect of smoking on seven pain conditions.

Tobacco smoking are established as primary drivers of the worldwide disease burden and significant contributors to premature mortality33. Numerous physiological changes occur due to the inhalation of cigarette smoke, which serves as a means of delivering nicotine to the human body. Although experimental studies have suggested that nicotine possesses analgesic properties, epidemiological evidence indicates that smoking is, in fact, associated with an increased risk of multiple pain conditions34,35. Several observational studies had been determinate cigarette smoking was a risk factor for back pain10, sciatica36, headache37 and oral pain38. Moreover, there is a positive correlation between lifelong cigarette exposure and the likelihood of developing persistent pain39. In the analysis of patients at a large chronic pain registry at a tertiary pain management center, smokers exhibited significantly poorer outcomes during initial consultation and first follow-up visits when compared to non-smokers13. Furthermore, a recent MR study provides additional support for the correlation between smoking and the occurrence of low back pain40. Our MR analysis extended previous studies by adding evidence for a causal effect of smoking on other pain conditions.

Numerous mechanisms have been proposed to substantiate the documented positive correlations between smoking and pain conditions. Prolonged nicotine consumption has been linked to the development of atherosclerosis, resulting in musculoskeletal malnutrition and reduced bone formation, particularly accelerating muscle degeneration in the back, intervertebral discs, and joints41. In addition, smoking is often associated with unhealthy lifestyle habits, such as sleep disturbances, depression, and anxiety. These factors, in turn, serve as high-risk factors for pain13. Furthermore, nicotine intake has been shown to enhance pain threshold and alleviate symptoms during the early stages of pain42. A cross-sectional study involving 151 chronic pain patients further revealed that nearly 50% of smokers reported using cigarettes as a coping mechanism for their pain43. The acute analgesic effects of nicotine are primarily mediated through nAChR. However, chronic exposure to nicotine can lead to nAChR desensitization and tolerance13,44. Therefore, smoking behavior appears to trigger a detrimental feedback loop, as pain prompts smoking behavior, which in turn exacerbates pain over time.

More importantly, this MR study found the mediating role of three common risk factors and quantified the proportions mediated by those mediators between smoking initiation and pain conditions. It is notice that the proportion mediated by combination of three mediators was over 30% for the causal effect of smoking on seven pain conditions. The effects of smoking on outcomes were partly attenuated after adjusting for depression, sedentary behavior and insomnia respectively, and the direct effect of smoking initiation on pain was diminished toward null after adjusting for combined three mediators, indicating that this effect was largely attributable to three mediators. A nationally representative study identified depressive symptoms as a crucial mediating factor and the proportion was approximately 62.7% for the causal association of smoking and self-reported pain conditions45. Similarly, several observational studies have provided support for the mediating role of insomnia in the relationship between smoking and pain46. Our results extended previous studies by adding evidence for the mediation role of sedentary behavior. Notably, depression, sedentary behavior and insomnia often co-occur as comorbidities and share common biological mechanisms, such as genetics and energy metabolism. Considering the interrelated nature of these three mediators, there may be an overlap in the proportion mediated by each mediator in our analyses.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first MR study to assess the causal association of smoking and pain conditions, and to identify causal mediators in the pathway. This work has several strengths. Firstly, our study design is based on MR study, which helps minimize the impact of confounding factors and mitigates bias caused by reverse causation. Secondly, to enhance the credibility and robustness of our findings, our outcomes are derived from two independent databases. While the majority of the mediator variables are obtained from the UK Biobank, to prevent bias resulting from overlapping populations, FinnGen database was used as primary data in the MVMR mediation analysis. Additionally, we employed a comprehensive MVMR analysis to investigate the mediating pathways, which enhanced our understanding of the underlying mechanisms and furnished supporting evidence for the development of preventive strategies.

Limitations should also be noted. First, the persistent presence of SNP heterogeneity has the potential to introduce bias and impact the robustness of our MR results. Second, we did not assess the bidirectional associations between smoking and pain conditions because the lack of genetic instruments for all pain conditions. Third, this study was carried out specifically in individuals of European ancestry, thereby potentially restricting the generalizability of our findings to other populations. Fourth, we were unable to assess the potential nonlinear associations between smoking and pain conditions due to the limitation of summary-level data. Fifth, Due to data limitations, other sedentary times and total sedentary time cannot be analyzed as mediating factors. In the future, more summary-level data and non-European GWAS data are necessary to confirm and update the findings of this study.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this MR study found that smoking is a risk factor for a broad range of pain conditions, and depression, sedentary behavior and insomnia mediate many of these associations. This study contributes valuable insights into the etiology of pain conditions and advocates for the reduction of tobacco consumption as promising strategies to mitigate the prevalence and burden of pain conditions. Additionally, effective management of the identified causal mediators holds potential in this regard.
