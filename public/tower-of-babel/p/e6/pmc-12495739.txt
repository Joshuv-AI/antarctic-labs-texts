Association between residential proximity to major roadways and sleep health in older Chinese people: the moderating role of living environments
Authors: Chi Zhang, Yinan Zhao, Jundan Huang, Mingyue Hu, Yongzhen Guo, Ruotong Peng, Xiaoyang Li, Xi Chen, Juan Yang, Siliang Wu, Hui Feng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn developing countries, the relationship between residential proximity to major roadways and sleep health in older adults remains to be seen, and more emphasis needs to be placed on the modulating role of the living environments on sleep health. For this study, we utilized population data obtained from the 2018 wave of the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS).MethodsThis study used logistic hierarchical regression to examine the relationship between residential proximity to major roadways and sleep quality and duration, controlling covariates. Moreover, the moderating effect of the living environments was explored through subgroup analysis and interaction. Sensitivity analysis was used to verify the robustness of the results.ResultsThe study included 7798 participants aged ≥ 65. The median age of participants was 82.00 years. Compared with those living near major roadways < 50 m, in the good sleep quality group, those living at a distance of 101–200 m had worse sleep quality (OR = 0.82, 95%CI: 0.67, 0.99; P < 0.05), and the remaining distance was unrelated to good sleep quality (P > 0.05). The distance wasn’t significantly associated with bad sleep quality and duration (P > 0.05). Moreover, those living in detached houses had better sleep quality than those in the good sleep quality group (OR = 1.30, 95%CI: 1.14, 1.50; P for interaction = 0.019). The bad sleep quality group, with a musty smell, had worse sleep quality (OR = 1.13, 95%CI: 1.09, 1.62; P for interaction = 0.023). High indoor ventilation frequency was associated with longer sleep duration in the long sleep duration group (OR = 1.26, 95%CI: 1.07, 1.47; P for interaction = 0.011).ConclusionThe residential proximity to major roadways 101–200 m was associated with worse sleep quality, but a distance of > 200 m didn’t improve sleep quality. A home’s proximity to major roadways was not associated with sleep duration. Therefore, determining the moderate distance between the house and major roadways may be a strategy for optimal sleep health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24684-y.

Introduction

High - quality and sufficient sleep can improve the body’s immunity, reduce energy consumption, and help the lymphatic system remove toxins and waste from the body, which is particularly important for older people’s physical and mental health [1]. However, the sleep status of older adults is susceptible to environmental factors, and the incidence of sleep disorders increases with age. Sleep disorders mainly include insomnia, sleep apnea syndrome, chronic sleep deficiency, etc., which are important risk factors for increasing the overall mortality of older people [2]. Existing studies showed that the prevalence of sleep disorders among older adults in Denmark, Japan, and Poland is 16.6%, 28.9%, and 31.2%, respectively [3, 4]. In comparison, the prevalence of sleep disorders among older in Chinese communities was as high as 41.2% [5]. The National Sleep Foundation recommended that older adults should sleep for 7–8 h [6]. According to the U - shaped dose - response relationship, too short or too long sleep duration can increase the risk of cognitive disorders, cardiovascular diseases, stroke, and other diseases in older adults [7, 8]. Sleep problems are a tremendous challenge in the health promotion process for older people, so it is essential to improve sleep health actively through the strategic planning of healthy ageing.

The residential proximity to major roadways is a multi - factor exposure that includes living arrangements, socioeconomic factors, and noise and air pollution, such as carbon monoxide, nitrogen oxides, and PM2.5 [9]. Sleep is particularly sensitive to noise because the body’s auditory system functions as a watchman, constantly monitoring the environment for potential threats during sleep. A French study of 1,244 adults suggested that nighttime aircraft noise exposure may reduce subjective sleep quality and quantity [10]. A survey of 363,886 adults pointed out that air pollution, especially fine particulate matter, can cause accelerated biological ageing that can be mitigated by better sleep [11]. A systematic review indicated that the effects of air pollution on sleep may vary by age, and the relationship needs to be explored in the elderly population in the future [12]. Now, many researchers have addressed the influence of the proximity of houses to main roadways on the physical and mental health of different age and disease groups. Regarding sleep, scholars mainly discussed the impact of sleep disorders on health issues or separately analyzed the adverse effects of noise and air pollution on individual sleep health. However, the relationship between residential proximity to major roadways and sleep health has yet to be well documented, particularly in developing countries. Therefore, we need more national studies to explore the underlying mechanisms further and validate this association.

Since older adults live in residential areas most of the time, they are easily affected by the external and cultural environment of the housing. The study found that physical environment (e.g., urban density, recreational facilities) [13], social environment (e.g., social cohesion [14], sense of security [15]), neighbourhood environment (e.g., neighbourhood barriers, building conditions) [16, 17], and other factors will have an impact on the sleep health of older people. A systematic review also indicated that housing security, home environment, and neighbourhood environment can impact sleep health among older adults [18]. In addition, some studies have demonstrated that light, noise, and indoor temperature can influence sleep health [19–21]. For example, a small heat load may reduce sleep quality and trigger an overactive sympathetic nervous system in older adults [22]. Similarly, inadequate ventilation can lead to the accumulation of carbon dioxide in the bedroom, ultimately resulting in poorer sleep quality [23]. Remarkably, there is a notable lack of information regarding the potential moderating role of other living environments in the relationship between residential proximity to major roadways and sleep health. For example, it remains unclear how the frequency of indoor ventilation impacts sleep health or whether musty odours contribute to sleep - related issues. In addition, because older people are more restricted in their mobility and outdoor activities, they tend to be more confined to their residential areas [24]. We need to pay more attention to the relationship between the living environments of housing (e.g., housing type, separate bedrooms, indoor ventilation frequency, musty smell) and the sleep health of older adults.

Given this background, two hypotheses are proposed in this study: (1) The residential proximity to major roadways is correlated with both the sleep quality and sleep duration of older adults; that is, the closer the residence is to the major roadway, the worse the sleep quality and the shorter the sleep duration. (2) Four living environment factors (housing type, bedroom ownership, indoor ventilation frequency, and musty smell) can moderate the relationship between residential proximity to major roadways and sleep quality and sleep duration in older adults. To test these two hypotheses, we used cross - sectional data from the 2018 wave of the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS) to conduct this study. Therefore, this study aimed to explore the potential relationship between residential proximity to major roadways and sleep quality and duration among older adults and examine the living environment’s moderating role. This may provide a basis for policy formulation, medical resource allocation, and public health practice of healthy ageing.

Methods

Study population

The present study used the CLHLS. It began in 1998 and covers 23 provinces in China, with a sampling frame covering about 85% of the total population (Beijing, Tianjin, Hebei, Shanxi, Liaoning, Jilin, Heilongjiang, Shanghai, Jiangsu, Zhejiang, Anhui, Fujian, Jiangxi, Shandong, Henan, Hubei, Hunan, Guangdong, Guangxi, Chongqing, Sichuan, Shanxi, and Hainan). The surveys were conducted using structured questionnaires by trained interviewers in the participants’ homes. When participants couldn’t answer questions, proxy respondents were usually spouses or other close family members, but the participants themselves responded to questions about cognitive function and mood. To ensure representativeness, a targeted random sample design was adopted, which means that octogenarians (80–89 years) and nonagenarians (90–99 years) are randomly selected, matching with centenarians (≥ 100 years) by sex and living area [25, 26]. The CLHLS study was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of Peking University (IRB00001052–13074), and all participants or their proxy respondents provided written informed consent. Details of the study design have been reported elsewhere [26, 27]. In the 2018 wave of CLHLS, residential proximity to major roadways was collected for the first time, so we included the data for this wave. The total sample consisted of 15,874 participants. 95 participants aged under 65 years and 8 participants missing information in age were excluded. 1,436 participants were excluded due to missing data and unclear residential proximity to major roadways. There were 1137 participants missing data in sleep quality, 124 participants missing data in sleep duration, and 73 older adults with sleep duration < 2 h or > 16 h per night [28]. To ensure the validity of the analysis, any samples with missing values in the other included variables of interest or whose answers were “don’t know” or “not sure” were excluded (n = 5203). Lastly, a total of 7798 participants aged more than 65 years old were included in the current study. Figure 1 shows the process of the inclusion and exclusion of participants.

Residential proximity to major roadways

Using a question to assess it: “How many meters is your home’s horizontal distance from the main traffic artery (m)?”. The major traffic artery/line was defined as “dual carriageway with at least 4 lanes”. The question has five options: “< 50m”, “50–100m”, “101–200m”, “201–300m”, and “>300m” [29].

Outcomes

Sleep quality was assessed by a single item: “How is your sleep quality now?” The answers were scored using a 5 - level Likert scale. The single - item sleep quality scale is a self - reported measurement that can be used when time is constrained or quick identification of sleep impairments [30]. In this study, “very good” and “good” emerged as good, and “bad” and “very bad” emerged as bad in the statistical analysis. Therefore, sleep quality was divided into three groups: bad, fair, and good [31]. The following question assessed sleep duration: “How many hours do you usually sleep each day?” This variable is continuous. Previous evidence suggests that subjective reports of habitual sleep duration are moderately correlated with objective measures of sleep duration (e.g., sleep logs, wrist accelerometers) (r = 0.60–0.78) [32, 33], so subjective reports of sleep duration can also reflect real sleep in the population. This study redefined 7–8 h per Day as the normal sleep duration for older adults, < 7 h per day as short, and > 8 h per day as long [2].

Moderator variables

The residential environment includes bedroom ownership (yes/no), housing type (detached house/others), musty smell (yes/no), and indoor ventilation frequency. The question “How often do you open windows for ventilation in spring, summer, autumn, or winter?” was used to assess indoor ventilation frequency. We coded the answer as follows: do not open windows (0), 1–5 times/week (1), and > 5 times/week (2). The score ranges from 0 to 8 points [34].

Covariates

To control for potential confounding factors, we considered including the following covariates based on previous studies and data accessibility. These factors included demographic characteristics, residential environment, health status, and lifestyle factors as the covariates.

Demographic characteristics [35, 36] include gender (male/female), age, residence (urban/rural), living with family members (yes/no) [37], years of education, marital status (married and living with their spouse/single), economic status, and chronic diseases (hypertension, diabetes, heart disease, stroke or cerebrovascular disease, and cancer) (yes/no).

Health status includes visual and hearing function (yes/no) [38], jaw joint pain (yes/no), mental health [39], the Chinese version of the modified Mini - Mental State Examination (CMMSE), activities of daily living (ADL), and instrumental ADL (IADL) [36, 40]. Mental health has seven questions, each with 5 response levels. The score range is from 0 to 28, with higher scores indicating higher levels of mental health [41]. The CMMSE consists of 24 items; its scores range from 0 to 30, with low scores implying poor cognitive function [42]. ADL was measured across six items: bathing, dressing, toileting, indoor transferring, continence, and feeding. IADL was rated using eight indicators, including visiting neighbours, shopping, making food, washing clothes, walking one kilometre, carrying 5 kg weight, crouching and standing for three times, and taking public transport. ADL and IADL score ranges are 0–12 and 0–16, respectively [43].

Lifestyle factors include fruit and vegetable intake, smoking (yes/no), drinking (yes/no), tea, and leisure activities [35]. The frequency of fruit and vegetable intake includes two items. The answer to each entry was recorded as “almost every day (3)”, “except winter (2)”, “occasionally (1)”, or “rarely or never (0)”. The score ranges from 0 to 6 points. Leisure activities were assessed using 8 types of activities. Each leisure activity has 5 answer levels, with a total score of 8 to 40. The higher the score, the more frequent leisure activities [44]. Details of all variables are in Supplementary Information (Table A.1).

Statistical analysis

In this study, the variables’ normality is tested using the histogram, and descriptive statistics were analyzed using frequencies and composition ratios, mean (SD), and median (25th, 75th). The association between residential proximity to major roadways and sleep health was assessed using the logistic regression models as follows: unadjusted model (Model 1); Model 2 adjusted for gender, age, residence, living with family members, years of education, marital status, economic status, and chronic diseases (hypertension, diabetes, heart disease, stroke or cerebrovascular disease, and cancer); Model 3 included the Model 2 covariates plus bedroom ownership, housing type, musty smell, and indoor ventilation frequency; Model 4 included the Model 3 covariates plus fruit and vegetable intake, currently smoking, currently drinking, drinking tea, and leisure activities; Model 5 had the Model 4 covariates plus visual function, hearing impairment, jaw joint pain, mental health, CMMSE, ADL, and IADL.

In subgroup and interaction analysis, residential proximity to major roadways was assessed by dichotomization (≤ 200 m or > 200 m), according to the data - driven cut point and experience from previous research [45]. We explored whether the association between residential proximity to major roadways and sleep health varied according to bedroom ownership, housing type, musty smell, and indoor ventilation frequency.

We further performed the following sensitivity analyses to evaluate the robustness of our results: (1) excluding participants aged 100 or older; (2) excluding participants with severe cognitive impairment with scores of CMMSE < 18 [46]; (3) excluding participants with complete physical functional disability (ADL score = 0 or IADL score = 0). All statistical analyses were performed using Stata SE 17.0. Two - sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Basic characteristics

A total of 7798 participants were included as the study population. The median age of participants was 82.00 years. Among the participants, 52.1% were female, 56.1% were urban residents, and 46.8% had residential proximity to major roads greater than 300 m. The results showed that housing type and musty smell significantly influenced the relationship between residential proximity to major roadways and sleep quality: in the group with good sleep quality, older adults living in detached houses had better sleep quality (OR = 1.30, 95%CI: 1.14, 1.50; P for interaction = 0.019). In the bad sleep quality group, older adults with indoor air musty smell had worse sleep quality (OR = 1.13, 95%CI: 1.09, 1.62; P for interaction = 0.023) (Table 3). Sensitivity analyses

Supplementary Information (Table A.2) presents the results of sensitivity analyses. Sensitivity analyses of logistic regression, excluding older adults aged 100 years and older with severe cognitive impairment and complete physical dysfunction, respectively, did not change the estimated effect or significance of residential proximity to major roadways and sleep quality and duration. The results of sensitivity analysis are robust.

Discussion

In a study of 7,798 Chinese people aged 65 years and older, we assessed the relationship between residential proximity to major roadways and sleep quality and duration. In addition, the regulating effect of the living environments is also discussed. When residents lived 101–200 m from major roads, their sleep quality was significantly worse compared to those within 50 m. However, distances of 50–100 m, 201–300 m, and > 300 m were not significantly related to sleep quality. Moreover, residential proximity to major roadways was not significantly associated with bad sleep quality and sleep duration in older adults. We also found that detached houses promote better sleep quality, indoor musty smell leads to worse sleep quality, and the higher the indoor ventilation frequency, the longer the sleep duration of older adults. However, having a bedroom was not associated with either sleep quality or duration.

Residential proximity to major roadways is a known source of several environmental exposures, including traffic - related air pollution and noise [47, 48]. Hunter et al. found that road traffic noise and air pollution disrupt sleep in a dose-response manner. They also noted that sleep quality declines as people live closer to noise sources [19]. A Swiss study of 1,383 adults found that living near roads increased sleep fragmentation, a condition linked to sleep deprivation and poor sleep quality [49]. A Spanish study also found that higher air pollution and traffic density levels were associated with lower sleep efficiency [50]. These are generally consistent with our results. Specifically, we found that older adults 101–200 m away from the main roadways have worse sleep quality. Evidence has also suggested that traffic pollution may not affect people above 200 m [45]. A reasonable explanation is that older people less than 100 m away from the main road have been exposed to traffic - related noise and air pollution for a long time, and the body has been tolerant. In addition, some homes will install soundproof glass, air purifiers, and other equipment to eliminate adverse factors.

This study also showed that residential proximity to major roads was not associated with sleep duration in older adults. However, Aasvang et al. found that traffic noise was associated with shortened sleep time during REM in young and middle - aged people [51]. In addition, long - term exposure to traffic - related air pollution can lead to shorter sleep duration in adults (30–79 years), especially in men and people of low socioeconomic status [52]. Evandt et al. found that traffic noise had a stronger Link to difficulty falling asleep and early awakenings than to difficulty staying asleep. This led to shorter sleep duration in adults aged 39–85 [53]. These are generally inconsistent with the results of this study. This may be because these studies included older adults and young or middle - aged people. However, the sleep patterns of older people change with age, usually falling asleep earlier and waking up earlier, and the total amount of sleep is reduced under natural circumstances [54]. Therefore, extensive sample studies should be carried out in the future to analyze further the relationship between residential proximity to main roads and the sleep duration of older adults. To sum up, older people should live 200 m away from the main roadways, away from outdoor noise. If the residence must be near main roads due to unavoidable factors, soundproofing measures (e.g., earplugs, soundproof windows) should be adopted.

Subgroup and interaction analysis found that owning a detached house can improve sleep quality in older people. Janson et al. also found that compared with adults living in detached houses, adults living in apartments have a higher prevalence of insomnia and are more disturbed by night traffic noise [55]. Older adults who live near major roads or in non - detached houses are likely to have a lower socioeconomic status. In contrast, higher socioeconomic status was positively associated with better sleep quality [36, 56]. Another interesting finding was that having a musty indoor air smell was associated with worse sleep quality in older adults. In humid environments, mould overgrowth releases more spores, which irritate the respiratory mucosa and may trigger respiratory diseases in older adults. These effects ultimately harm sleep quality. Therefore, older adults should have a separate house or room to reduce interference. At the same time, keep the room dry to prevent mould growth. We can use dehumidifiers and air conditioning equipment to control indoor humidity and reduce moisture accumulation. While ventilation frequency does not affect sleep quality or short sleep duration, higher ventilation rates are linked to longer sleep (> 8 h). However, other studies have shown that indoor low ventilation frequency has a significant negative impact on sleep quality, and ventilation noise of 50 dB or above may interfere with sleep [23, 57]. This may be because insufficient ventilation leads to higher indoor pollutants that affect older adults’ respiratory and autonomic nervous systems. In addition, long sleep time is significantly associated with increased mortality and the incidence of cardiovascular diseases [58]. Thus, older adults’ rooms should have proper ventilation to remove stale air and allow fresh air circulation.

Several limitations to our study bear mentioning. First, based on cross - sectional data, it cannot determine the causal relationship between residential proximity to major roadways and sleep health. However, it provides reference information, such as distance to major roads and indoor air quality, for older adults when selecting a residence. Longitudinal studies would provide more robust evidence regarding causality. Further studies are needed to explore the differences in sleep behavior trends among older adults. Second, residential proximity to major roadways, sleep quality, and sleep duration measures were based mainly on self - reports, with the possibility of recall bias. We clearly define what constitutes a main road, thereby reducing subjectivity to some extent. During data cleaning, we removed sleep duration outliers to reduce errors. In addition, we performed a sensitivity analysis by excluding those with severe cognitive impairment, and the results remained robust. Third, despite efforts to collect comprehensive covariates, database limitations prevented inclusion of confounders such as seasons, weather, and healthcare access. These omissions may limit the generalizability. Despite some limitations, the CLHLS national survey covers 85% of China’s population with a large, representative sample and diverse distribution, making its results reliable. It is possible to generalize our results to other low - and middle - income countries.

Implications

Residence is a modifiable factor, and this study may encourage urban planners to develop housing away from the most heavily trafficked major roads. Our results also show that older adults in independent houses sleep better. Older adults with an indoor musty smell have worse sleep quality. More frequent indoor ventilation also increases older adults’ sleep duration. These findings can help older adults choose homes that reduce traffic - related sleep problems. In addition, we can take various measures to adjust the indoor ventilation frequency and humidity for older adults. This improves indoor air quality, increases sunlight exposure, and reduces mould breeding. Possible solutions include installing dehumidifiers and ventilation systems in housing, among other solutions.

Conclusions

Among older adults in China, in the good sleep quality group, compared with a distance < 50 m, sleep quality is worse when the distance is between 101 and 200 m. When the distance was 50–100 m, 201–300 m, and > 300 m, there was no significant association with good sleep quality. Furthermore, the residential proximity to major roadways was not significantly associated with bad sleep quality and sleep duration. In addition, detached houses can promote sleep quality, indoor air musty smell can reduce sleep quality, and high indoor ventilation frequency can lead to longer sleep duration in older people. Future research could explore interventions to mitigate the potential adverse effects of residential proximity to major roads on sleep health. In addition, more large - scale cohort studies are necessary to confirm our findings.
