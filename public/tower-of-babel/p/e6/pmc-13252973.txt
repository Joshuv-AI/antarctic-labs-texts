Psychometric Validation of the Chinese Versions of the Effort Assessment Scale and Vanderbilt Fatigue Scale for Adults–10 for Assessing Listening Effort and Fatigue
Authors: Zongyun Zhang, Zhaohao Zhang, Junyu Liu, Zhaomin Fan, Haibo Wang, Dongjie Jiang, Ruijie Wang, Lei Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objectives:Listening effort and listening-related fatigue are clinically important yet rarely assessed in routine audiology. This study culturally adapted the Effort Assessment Scale (EAS) into Chinese and psychometrically evaluated the Chinese EAS (C-EAS) and the 10-item Vanderbilt Fatigue Scale for Adults–10 (C-VFS-A-10) in Chinese-speaking adults. Associations with better-ear pure-tone average (PTA), tinnitus, and key demographic factors were also examined.Design:A two-phase study was conducted. Phase 1 followed established guidelines to translate and culturally adapt the EAS. Phase 2 was a cross-sectional validation in 701 adults recruited from a tertiary hospital in China (normal hearing, n = 180; unilateral hearing loss, n = 22; bilateral hearing loss, n = 499). A subsample of 85 participants with hearing loss completed repeat testing over 7 to 21 days to assess test–retest reliability. All participants completed the C-EAS, C-VFS-A-10, the Hearing Handicap Inventory for the Elderly/Adults-Screening version (HHIE/A-S), and pure-tone audiometry. Psychometric evaluation included internal consistency, test–retest reliability, factor analyses, convergent validity with PTA and HHIE/A-S, and item response theory (IRT) modeling, alongside ANCOVA and multivariable regression.Results:Both scales showed excellent internal consistency and high short-interval stability. Scale-specific confirmatory factor analyses supported essential unidimensionality adequate for total-score use. Convergent validity was supported by moderate associations with better-ear PTA and HHIE/A-S (ρ = 0.47 to 0.63). Item-level diagnostics indicated localized local dependence for both instruments. For the C-EAS, item-level IRT parameter estimates were unstable under local dependence, supporting the use of summed total scores. For the C-VFS-A-10, a graded-response testlet model produced stable item parameters and high measurement precision across the range where most respondents fell. Scores on both measures increased with hearing-loss severity. In multivariable models, higher better-ear PTA and tinnitus independently predicted higher effort and fatigue scores, while age showed a small additional association with effort only.Conclusions:The C-EAS and C-VFS-A-10 are reliable, culturally adapted measures of listening effort and listening-related fatigue for Chinese-speaking adults. Associations with better-ear PTA and tinnitus support clinical relevance.

INTRODUCTION

Hearing loss is commonly quantified using standard audiological tests such as pure-tone and speech audiometry, but these measures do not fully capture everyday listening difficulties. In many situations, the burden of hearing loss extends beyond reduced audibility per se and reflects the sustained cognitive and attentional demands required to understand speech in complex or adverse listening environments (McGarrigle et al. 2014; Pichora-Fuller et al. 2016). Listeners with hearing loss may therefore experience greater listening effort and downstream fatigue in everyday communication (Hornsby & Kipp 2016; Davis et al. 2021). However, this “hidden” burden of listening effort and fatigue is not routinely assessed in audiological practice, despite evidence that it can affect communication, social participation, and quality of life (Gustafson et al. 2021; Holman et al. 2021b).

Listening effort refers to the deliberate allocation of cognitive resources to overcome obstacles during listening, particularly when communication is hindered by degraded signals, high task demands, or listener-related limitations (McGarrigle et al. 2014; Shields et al. 2022). Within the Framework for Understanding Effortful Listening, listening effort is conceptualized as active cognitive resource management shaped by task complexity, listener motivation, and available capacity (Pichora-Fuller et al. 2016). When auditory input is degraded by hearing loss, background noise, poor signal quality, or reverberation, listeners may need to allocate more cognitive resources to the listening task and rely more heavily on contextual cues, working memory, and top-down linguistic processing to maintain comprehension (Picou et al. 2013; Holmes et al. 2018).

Whereas listening effort reflects moment-to-moment cognitive engagement, listening-related fatigue is commonly conceptualized as a more sustained state reflecting the longer-term consequences of prolonged or highly demanding auditory processing. Listening-related fatigue has been defined as a subjective experience of exhaustion and depletion of cognitive and emotional resources that is attributed to extended or effortful listening activity (Bess & Hornsby 2014; McGarrigle et al. 2014; Davis et al. 2021). Hornsby et al. (2016) distinguish between transient fatigue, which emerges shortly after demanding auditory tasks, and longer-term fatigue, characterized by more persistent exhaustion associated with chronic listening demands.

Listening effort and listening-related fatigue are closely related but distinguishable patient-reported constructs, both commonly reported by adults with hearing loss (Hornsby 2013; Alhanbali et al. 2017; Ohlenforst et al. 2017; Davis et al. 2021). Conceptual models and emerging empirical work suggest that sustained engagement in effortful listening may contribute to the development or exacerbation of listening-related fatigue, although the mechanisms and boundary conditions of this relationship remain to be clarified (Hornsby 2013; McGarrigle et al. 2014; Pichora-Fuller et al. 2016). Clinically, adults with hearing loss tend to report higher levels of listening effort and listening-related fatigue than those with normal hearing, and these experiences extend beyond physical tiredness and have been associated with reduced work functioning, social withdrawal, and psychological distress (Hornsby & Kipp 2016; Holman et al. 2021b).

Despite growing recognition of their clinical importance, listening effort and listening-related fatigue are not routinely assessed in clinical practice. Objective and behavioral measures (e.g., pupillometry, dual-task paradigms) are often time-consuming and not easily implemented in routine settings (Alhanbali et al. 2019; Francis & Love 2020). Self-report scales offer a practical, patient-centered alternative because they are easy to administer, reflect real-world listening demands, and capture subjective experience directly. However, the availability of instruments that are specifically designed, psychometrically validated, and culturally adapted for individuals with hearing loss remains limited, particularly outside English-speaking contexts (Alhanbali et al. 2017; Hornsby et al. 2021, 2023). This gap is particularly relevant for Chinese-speaking adults, highlighting the need for validated instruments in this population.

The Effort Assessment Scale (EAS), developed by Alhanbali et al. (2017), is a concise six-item self-report instrument that measures subjective listening effort in everyday communicative situations. Each item is rated on a 0 to 10 visual analog scale, providing a continuous index of perceived effort. The original English version showed high internal consistency (Cronbach α = 0.94) and a unidimensional factor structure, with subsequent studies demonstrating convergent validity through associations with hearing handicap and speech-in-noise performance (Alhanbali et al. 2018). The EAS has been translated and evaluated in several languages, including Arabic, Danish, and French, supporting its international applicability (Alhanbali et al. 2023; Cañete et al. 2023; Ferschneider & Moulin 2023). However, no formally translated and psychometrically validated Chinese version of the EAS is currently available, limiting its use in Chinese-speaking clinical and research settings.

Listening-related fatigue has traditionally been assessed using generic fatigue measures such as the Numerical Rating Scale-Fatigue, the Fatigue Severity Scale, and the Checklist Individual Strength (Machado et al. 2021). However, as these tools were not specifically developed for individuals with hearing loss, they may lack sensitivity to the unique manifestations of listening-related fatigue (Hornsby et al. 2021). Recognizing this gap, Hornsby and colleagues developed the Vanderbilt Fatigue Scale for Adults–40, a multidimensional instrument specifically designed to assess the emotional, social, and physical dimensions of fatigue associated with auditory demands. A shortened version, the Vanderbilt Fatigue Scale for Adults–10 (VFS-A-10), has demonstrated strong psychometric properties, including reliability, validity, and responsiveness to hearing intervention outcomes in clinical settings (Hornsby et al. 2023). A Chinese translation of the VFS-A-10 (C-VFS-A-10) is available on the Vanderbilt Fatigue Scales website and was used in this study in accordance with the developers’ stated terms of use (i.e., freely available for clinical and research use). We were not involved in the translation; the website provides a reconciliation report and notes that translations follow the guidelines recommended by Hall et al. (2018). To our knowledge, this Chinese version has not undergone systematic psychometric validation in Chinese-speaking adult populations.

Previous studies have further examined the relationship between listening effort and listening-related fatigue using self-report measures. Alhanbali et al. (2018) found only weak associations between the EAS and a generic fatigue scale, suggesting that general fatigue tools may not adequately capture auditory-specific demands. By contrast, Hornsby et al. (2023) reported moderate correlations between the EAS and the VFS-A-10 and showed that the EAS tended to relate more strongly to hearing handicap, whereas the VFS-A-10 was more closely associated with broader fatigue and emotional outcomes. These patterns indicate that the EAS and VFS-A-10 may provide related but non-identical information about the burden associated with hearing loss, reinforcing the need for validated versions in underrepresented populations.

Given the potential impact of listening effort and listening-related fatigue on communicative efficiency, cognitive resources, and psychosocial well-being, culturally validated assessment tools are needed for Chinese audiological practice. However, psychometrically validated Chinese instruments specifically targeting these constructs remain scarce, which may hinder accurate assessment, individualized intervention planning, and outcome monitoring. To address this gap, we culturally adapted the EAS to create the Chinese version (C-EAS) following established translation guidelines. We evaluated the psychometric properties of the C-EAS and the C-VFS-A-10 in adults with and without hearing loss across a wide range of audiometric status using classical test theory (CTT) and item response theory (IRT). We also examined associations between scale scores, hearing loss severity, and key demographic and clinical factors.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Design

This two-phase study was conducted at a tertiary hospital. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board (blinded for review), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. In phase 1, the EAS was translated into Chinese following the translation and cross-cultural adaptation guidelines outlined by Hall et al. (2018) to produce the Chinese EAS (C-EAS). A small sample of adults with hearing loss (n = 20) participated in cognitive debriefing to evaluate clarity, relevance, and acceptability of the translated items (see Table S1 in Supplemental Digital Content 1, https://links.lww.com/EANDH/B854). In phase 2, a large-scale cross-sectional validation study was conducted to examine the psychometric properties of the C-EAS and the C-VFS-A-10.

Participants

For the large-scale cross-sectional study, 701 Chinese-speaking adults were recruited from an initial pool of 711 respondents. Ten participants were excluded during data quality screening because their records suggested invalid responding (substantial missingness and/or implausibly short completion times). Participants were first-time outpatients or their accompanying family members; none had prior experience with hearing aids or cochlear implants. Inclusion criteria were age ≥18 years, being a native Chinese speaker, and adequate cooperation. Individuals with recent, sudden, or fluctuating sensorineural hearing loss were excluded. Twenty-two participants were identified as having unilateral hearing loss (UHL); this subgroup was documented but not included in some analyses due to small sample size (see Statistical analysis).

To assess test–retest reliability, a convenience subsample of 85 participants with hearing loss completed the same assessments twice over a 14-day interval (range: 7 to 21 days). This subgroup was chosen to minimize floor effects in normal-hearing participants and to ensure sufficient score variability for stable reliability estimates.

Measures

Chinese Version of the Effort Assessment Scale

This scale comprises six items rated on a 0 to 10 visual analog scale anchored at “no effort” and “maximum effort.” Item scores are summed (range = 0 to 60) to yield a total score representing overall listening effort. Higher totals indicate greater perceived effort. The Chinese translation and instructions of C-EAS are available in Supplemental Digital Content 2, https://links.lww.com/EANDH/B855.

Chinese Version of the Brief VFS-A-10

This 10-item scale assesses listening-related fatigue. Each item is rated on a five-point Likert scale (0 = Never/Almost Never to 4 = Always/Almost Always), yielding a total score from 0 to 40; higher scores indicate greater listening-related fatigue. The Chinese version and instructions used in this study are provided in Supplemental Digital Content 2, https://links.lww.com/EANDH/B855.

Hearing Handicap Inventory for the Elderly/Adults-Screening Version

This 10-item scale evaluates perceived hearing difficulties, with items divided into emotional and social subscales. The validated Chinese version of the Hearing Handicap Inventory for the Elderly/Adults-Screening Version (HHIE/A-S) (Wang et al. 2017) was used to assess convergent validity in this study. Each item is scored 0 = No, 2 = Sometimes, and 4 = Yes. Total scores range from 0 to 40, with higher scores indicating greater perceived hearing handicap.

Audiometric Assessment

Hearing thresholds were measured at octave and inter-octave frequencies from 0.125 to 8 kHz using a GSI AudioStar Pro audiometer with TDH-39 supra-aural earphones or ER-3A insert earphones. Equipment was calibrated to ANSI/ASA S3.6-2018 within the preceding 12 months. Testing was conducted in a sound-treated booth meeting ANSI/ASA S3.1 to 1999 (R2018) standards. Hearing-loss severity was classified according to the World Health Organization (2021) based on the better-ear pure-tone average at 0.5, 1, 2, and 4 kHz.

Questionnaire Administration

Each questionnaire includes standardized written instructions in Chinese, outlining the study’s purpose and the available response options. Participants read these instructions and then completed the questionnaires on-site. When necessary (for example, if participants had questions about the response format), an audiologist briefly clarified the written instructions. Completed questionnaires were checked solely for missing or multiple responses, without content-related feedback or altering any selected answers.

Statistical Analysis

Analyses were performed in R (version 4.5.0) using the lavaan, psych, mirt, and mice packages. Normality assumptions for linear models were evaluated by visual inspection of Q–Q plots and histograms of residuals; where these assumptions were violated, robust or non-parametric methods were used. Significance was set at p < 0.05 (two-tailed).

Missing Data Handling

Missingness was limited to C-EAS item 4 (24/701, 3.4%), possibly because some participants felt that questions regarding “discussion in a class, a meeting, or a lecture” were not applicable. Participants with missing data tended to be older, had poorer (higher) better-ear PTA, and differed in education level (all p < 0.05), suggesting that missingness was related to observed covariates and was plausibly compatible with a missing-at-random assumption after conditioning on these variables. Because IRT estimation requires complete response vectors, we used multiple imputation with predictive mean matching (m = 20), imputing only item 4 using the remaining C-EAS items, better-ear PTA, age, sex, tinnitus, and education level as predictors. For IRT analyses, we report results from the first imputed dataset as a pragmatic approach given the lack of standard pooling procedures for testlet-IRT parameter estimates; inferential analyses based on linear models (group comparisons, correlations, and regressions) were repeated across all 20 imputations and pooled using Rubin’s rules. Complete-case analyses (n = 677) yielded substantively similar conclusions.

Reliability

Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach α, McDonald ω, and split-half coefficients for each scale (Revelle & Condon 2019). Test–retest reliability was assessed in a subsample of adults with hearing loss (n = 85), the primary clinical population, to avoid range restriction from normal-hearing participants. Temporal stability was evaluated using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC; two-way mixed-effects, absolute-agreement, single-measure), and Spearman ρ.

Validity

Structural validity

To examine construct overlap between the C-EAS and C-VFS-A-10, a joint exploratory factor analysis (EFA) of all 16 items was conducted to test whether effort and fatigue items formed separable yet correlated factors. Consistent with recommendations for polytomous IRT models, C-EAS items (0 to 10) were discretized into five ordered categories (0 to 2, 3 to 4, 5 to 6, 7 to 8, 9 to 10; coded 0 to 4) to reduce sparse category usage and to align the response format with the ordinal C-VFS-A-10. Factor retention followed parallel analysis and the minimum average partial test, with oblimin rotation and a loading cutoff of ≥0.30. Sensitivity analyses indicated that discretization did not materially alter factor retention or salient loadings.

One-factor confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) models were fitted separately for each scale. The C-EAS was analyzed using the original 0 to 10 scoring treated as quasi-continuous and estimated via robust maximum likelihood. The ordinal C-VFS-A-10 was analyzed using weighted least squares mean and variance-adjusted estimation. Model fit was summarized with the CFI, TLI, robust root mean square error of approxiamation (RMSEA) (90% confidence interval [CI]), and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR).

Convergent validity

In the combined normal-hearing (NH; n = 180) and bilateral hearing-loss (BHL; n = 499) sample (N = 679), Spearman correlations were computed among the total scores of the C-EAS, C-VFS-A-10, better-ear PTA, and HHIE/A-S as well as their Emotional and Social subscales. Correlation magnitudes were interpreted using Akoglu (2018) absolute-value guidelines (0.00 to 0.10 negligible, 0.10 to 0.39 weak, 0.40 to 0.69 moderate, 0.70 to 0.89 strong, 0.90 to 1.00 very strong).

IRT Analysis

For the C-EAS, all IRT analyses used five-category discretized response scores; collapsing rarely endorsed extreme categories is recommended for rating-scale data to reduce sparse cell counts and stabilize parameter estimates in polytomous models (DeMars 2010). For both scales, graded response models were fitted under the assumptions of essential unidimensionality and local independence. Essential unidimensionality was supported by the joint EFA and the scale-specific one-factor CFA described earlier. Local dependence (LD) was screened using adjusted Yen’s Q3 statistics and CFA-based modification indices, with Holm adjustment applied to control for multiple testing across item pairs (Chen & Thissen 1997). When LD was detected, residual dependencies were accommodated using graded response testlet models (Wainer et al. 2007). Model performance was evaluated using global model-fit and item-level fit diagnostics (e.g., S-X2), and only models demonstrating adequate fit were used for parameter reporting. For such models, we summarized item parameters including general-factor discrimination (aG), category thresholds (t1 to t4), and conditional standard errors of measurement (CSEM). Measurement precision across the latent continuum was quantified by converting CSEM to conditional reliability, R(θ) = 1 – CSEM2(θ), assuming Var(𝜃) = 1.

Group Comparison Analyses

Unadjusted score distributions across hearing-loss levels were visualized using jittered boxplots. To test whether these patterns persisted after controlling for demographic and clinical variables, analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) models compared scale scores across six WHO-defined severity levels, with age, sex, education level, and tinnitus as covariates. Adjacent (consecutive) contrasts of estimated marginal means (EMMs) were pre-specified, and p values were adjusted using the Holm procedure.

Multivariable Regression Analysis

Multiple linear regression models were fitted in the combined NH + BHL sample (N = 679) to examine associations of better-ear PTA, age, sex, tinnitus status, and education level with each scale score. Results are reported as adjusted regression coefficients with 95% CIs. As a prespecified sensitivity analysis, we included a PTA × Group (NH versus BHL) interaction term to assess whether the PTA–score association varied by hearing-status group and, where appropriate, derived group-specific simple slopes. In a further exploratory sensitivity analysis, we additionally included a Tinnitus × Group term to evaluate whether allowing this effect to vary by group materially influenced the PTA × Group findings.

Tinnitus was reported by 338 participants (48.2%). Detailed demographic and audiological characteristics by hearing status are shown in Table 1.

Reliability

Internal consistency indices were high for both scales. For the C-EAS, Cronbach α = 0.97, McDonald ω = 0.98, and the Guttman split-half coefficient = 0.97. For the C-VFS-A-10, Cronbach α = 0.95, McDonald ω = 0.96, and the Guttman split-half coefficient = 0.95. Corrected item–total correlations (r) were uniformly strong (C-EAS: 0.78 to 0.95; C-VFS-A-10: 0.69 to 0.89), indicating good item homogeneity for both instruments.

Test–retest reliability was assessed in a subsample of 85 participants with hearing loss (median retest interval = 15 days; interquartile range = 13 to 17; range = 7 to 21). For the C-EAS, time 1 and time 2 means (M, SD) were 46.06 (10.65) and 44.81 (9.71), respectively (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, p = 0.074), indicating high short-interval stability (ICC = 0.86, 95% CI: [0.79, 0.91]) and a strong rank-order association (Spearman ρ = 0.83, p < 0.001). For the C-VFS-A-10, means were 18.06 (9.59) and 18.18 (8.58) at time 1 and time 2, respectively (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, p = 0.869), with high agreement (ICC = 0.93, 95% CI: [0.90, 0.96]) and a strong rank-order association (Spearman ρ = 0.94, p < 0.001).

Validity

Discretizing the C-EAS did not materially alter its factor structure: agreement between the original and discretized scores was extremely high (Mantel r = 0.998, p < 0.001; Tucker congruence = 1.00). An EFA on all 16 items (C-EAS + C-VFS-A-10) showed excellent sampling adequacy (KMO = 0.96) and a significant Bartlett’s test (p < 0.001). Parallel analysis (Figure S1 in Supplemental Digital Content 3, https://links.lww.com/EANDH/B856) and the minimum average partial test supported a two-factor solution. The one-factor solution explained 74.4% of the variance, whereas the two-factor solution accounted for 76.3%, with effort and fatigue items loading primarily on separate but correlated factors.

In a confirmatory follow-up, a two-factor CFA (with C-EAS and C-VFS-A-10 as separate dimensions) fit better than a unidimensional model. Fit indices were CFI = 0.996, TLI = 0.995, RMSEA = 0.087, 90% CI: [0.081, 0.093], and SRMR = 0.030 for the two-factor model, versus CFI = 0.990, TLI = 0.989, RMSEA = 0.136, 90% CI: [0.130, 0.143], and SRMR = 0.071 for the unidimensional model.

Scale-specific CFAs broadly supported essential unidimensionality. For the C-EAS, the robust one-factor CFA indicated good incremental fit (CFI = 0.982, TLI = 0.970), borderline acceptable absolute fit (RMSEA = 0.080, 90% CI: [0.065, 0.097]), and a very small SRMR (0.011; Table S2 in Supplemental Digital Content 1, https://links.lww.com/EANDH/B854). Standardized factor loadings were uniformly high (0.79 to 0.97; Table S3 in Supplemental Digital Content 1, https://links.lww.com/EANDH/B854).

For the C-VFS-A-10, the initial robust one-factor model showed strong incremental fit (CFI = 0.990, TLI = 0.987) and a small SRMR (0.034), but elevated RMSEA (0.131, 90% CI: [0.121, 0.142]; Table S2 in Supplemental Digital Content 1, https://links.lww.com/EANDH/B854). Model diagnostics indicated two pairs with substantial residual dependence (items 1 to 2 and 8 to 9), reflected by large modification indices (modification indices = 102.0 and 34.5, respectively); corresponding local dependence diagnostics (adjusted Yen Q3 and adjusted p values) are reported in Table S4 in Supplemental Digital Content 1, https://links.lww.com/EANDH/B854). Alternative two- and three-factor CFAs were tested as sensitivity analyses but did not meaningfully improve fit relative to the residual-covariance specification and were characterized by highly correlated latent factors (Table S2 in Supplemental Digital Content 1, https://links.lww.com/EANDH/B854), consistent with localized LD rather than additional substantive dimensions. Allowing these two residual covariances improved fit to an acceptable level (CFI = 0.996, TLI = 0.995, RMSEA = 0.084, 90% CI: [0.073, 0.096], SRMR = 0.024; Table S2 in Supplemental Digital Content 1, https://links.lww.com/EANDH/B854), while standardized loadings remained strong (0.76 to 0.96; Table S3 in Supplemental Digital Content 1, https://links.lww.com/EANDH/B854).

In the combined NH + BHL sample (N = 679), Spearman correlations indicated that the C-EAS total was associated with better-ear PTA (ρ = 0.63), the HHIE/A-S total (ρ = 0.47), and the HHIE/A-S Social (ρ = 0.51) and Emotional (ρ = 0.34) subscales. The C-VFS-A-10 showed a similar profile (better-ear PTA ρ = 0.58; HHIE/A-S total ρ = 0.56; HHIE/A-S Social ρ = 0.51; HHIE/A-S Emotional ρ = 0.50). The C-EAS and C-VFS-A-10 were strongly intercorrelated (ρ = 0.79, p < 0.001; Table 2).

IRT Analysis

Consistent with the validity analyses, both the C-EAS and C-VFS-A-10 were treated as essentially unidimensional for IRT modeling. Pre-IRT diagnostics indicated localized violations of LD for the C-EAS, particularly for item pairs 3 to 4 and 3 to 5 (Table S4 in Supplemental Digital Content 1, https://links.lww.com/EANDH/B854), and for the C-VFS-A-10 between items 1 to 2 and 8 to 9.

To address these issues, graded response testlet models were fitted for both scales. For the C-EAS, the testlet model did not yield stable estimates: item discrimination parameters were extremely large (a ≈ 8.94 to 9.19), and item fit assessment (S-X2 tests) indicated significant misfit for four items (items 1 to 4; all p < 0.005). Attempts to derive a shorter core scale (5-item, 4-item, 3-item, and 2-item subsets) produced similar problems, with at least two items in each model showing significant misfit and extreme discrimination (a > 6.0). Consequently, no C-EAS item parameters were retained or reported, and subsequent analyses relied on total scores under classical test theory.

For the C-VFS-A-10, the testlet model provided stable parameter estimates and improved model fit (Table S5 in Supplemental Digital Content 1, https://links.lww.com/EANDH/B854). General discrimination parameters (aG) ranged from 1.57 to 5.49, and category thresholds (t1 to t4) were appropriately ordered, spanning from −1.30 to 2.73, suggesting that the items span a wide portion of the fatigue continuum in this sample. As shown in Figure 1, measurement precision was highest around 𝜃 ≈ 0.59, where the minimum CSEM was 0.22. Importantly, the CSEM remained below 0.30 across 𝜃 ∈ [−0.69, 1.88], corresponding to test information values ≥11.1 [i.e., CSEM ≤0.30 under Var(𝜃) = 1] and matching the information level reported for the original scale in its high-precision region (Hornsby et al., 2023). This region encompassed approximately 84% of the sample, indicating that the C-VFS-A-10 yields consistently high precision for the vast majority of respondents.

Multivariable linear models (Table 3) for the combined NH + BHL sample (N = 679) showed that higher C-EAS scores were independently associated with higher better-ear PTA (β = 0.46, 95% CI: [0.41, 0.51], p < 0.001), older age (β = 0.11, 95% CI: [0.03, 0.19], p = 0.005), and the presence of tinnitus (β = 9.80, 95% CI: [7.45, 12.16], p < 0.001). Sex and education level were not significant predictors (all p > 0.05). Similarly, for the C-VFS-A-10, higher better-ear PTA (β = 0.23, 95% CI: [0.20, 0.26], p < 0.001) and the presence of tinnitus (β = 4.81, 95% CI: [3.45, 6.16], p < 0.001) were significant predictors, while age, sex, and education level were not (all p > 0.05). Model fit was acceptable (adjusted R2 = 0.46 for the C-EAS; 0.39 for the C-VFS-A-10).

DISCUSSION

In a large clinical sample of Chinese-speaking adults, we evaluated the psychometric performance of the C-EAS and the C-VFS-A-10 as brief measures of listening effort and listening-related fatigue. Both scales demonstrated very high reliability and essentially unidimensional measurement at the scale level, while joint analyses suggested substantial shared variance with meaningful differentiation between effort and fatigue. Both measures related to higher better-ear PTA and tinnitus. In contrast, item-level IRT results diverged: the C-EAS showed pronounced local dependence and inadequate fit even under testlet modeling, whereas the C-VFS-A-10 yielded stable testlet-model estimates, supporting IRT-based scoring for this scale, particularly for research applications.

Psychometric Performance and Dimensionality

Across both instruments, reliability was consistently very high in this clinical sample across multiple indices, and short-interval test–retest results indicated good stability. For the C-EAS, reliability estimates were broadly comparable to, or slightly higher than, those reported for the original English scale and other translated versions (Alhanbali et al. 2017, 2023; Cañete et al. 2023; Ferschneider & Moulin 2023). Published test–retest evidence for the EAS appears limited, and our findings provide additional support for its short-term stability. For the C-VFS-A-10, internal consistency was similarly high and aligned with the English version, and the short-interval test–retest ICC observed here closely matches the brief longitudinal evidence reported by Hornsby et al. (2023).

Joint analyses of the combined item pool indicated two correlated but separable latent dimensions. Model fit favored a two-factor representation with loadings clustering by instrument, although a single-factor solution still accounted for most of the common variance, consistent with prior conceptual and psychometric work treating them as related yet separable dimensions of listening burden (McGarrigle et al. 2014; Alhanbali et al. 2017). At the same time, the high interscale correlation and broadly similar patterns of external associations indicate substantial construct overlap, so the present findings are more consistent with substantial shared variance alongside some differentiation than with fully independent domains.

At the single-instrument level, both measures showed essential unidimensionality adequate for total-score use. For the C-EAS, the one-factor CFA yielded good incremental fit, acceptable absolute fit, and uniformly high loadings, consistent with reports from English, Danish, and Arabic samples that treat the scale as dominated by a single effort factor (Alhanbali et al. 2017, 2023; Cañete et al. 2023). For the C-VFS-A-10, a one-factor model achieved acceptable fit once two conceptually coherent residual covariances (items 1 to 2 and 8 to 9) were freely specified. Items 1 and 2 both derive from the Physical domain of the original Vanderbilt Fatigue Scale for Adults–40 and, in Chinese, use similar wording of general tiredness, whereas items 8 and 9 both refer to socio-emotional consequences of listening (relationship strain and feeling emotionally drained). These small, theory-guided residual covariances were therefore treated as item-specific dependencies within this CFA model and not as evidence against an overall unidimensional structure (Brown 2015).

Item-Level Information From IRT Models

The present study extends the evidence base for the EAS by adding an IRT perspective, providing item-level information in a large clinical sample. Despite extensive model exploration, including graded response models, testlet specifications, and item deletion schemes, none of the fitted models achieved acceptable overall fit in this sample. Poor fit appeared to be driven mainly by local dependence among conceptually overlapping items within this short, homogeneous scale. Such dependence is known to inflate discrimination estimates and degrade IRT model performance (Yen 1984, 1993). In line with previous work on patient-reported outcome measures, the C-EAS nonetheless showed high internal consistency and a coherent unidimensional structure under CTT, illustrating the now well-documented dissociation between favorable CTT indices and inadequate IRT fit (Reise & Waller 2009; Petrillo et al. 2015). Practically, the C-EAS may be most appropriately used as a brief, highly saturated CTT index of listening effort, with routine clinical and research applications relying on summed scores rather than on unstable item-level IRT parameters (DeMars 2010). Any future IRT work would likely require models that explicitly accommodate local dependence. In this context, the evidence of pronounced local dependence in a short, homogeneous scale is suggestive of substantial item redundancy and a relatively restricted effective trait range in this clinical cohort, rather than optimally efficient coverage of the full listening-effort continuum. This pattern should be regarded as a limitation when generalizing beyond similar populations.

In contrast, the C-VFS-A-10 yielded more conventional and informative IRT results. As in the CFA, diagnostics supported an essentially unidimensional general factor but indicated local dependence for items 1 to 2 and 8 to 9. Representing these pairs as testlets in a graded response testlet model substantially improved global fit and reduced residual dependence while preserving a strong general factor. The resulting general-factor discrimination parameters (aG = 1.57 to 0.49) were similar in magnitude to those reported for the original English VFS-A-10 (2.26 to 4.95), with item 2 again showing the highest discrimination. These findings suggest that, once local dependence is accommodated via testlets, the Chinese version preserves comparable item functioning and provides informative measurement of listening-related fatigue across the range where most respondents fall.

Measurement precision was summarized using the CSEM, defined in IRT as CSEM(θ)=1/TIF(θ) (Nguyen et al. 2014). Applying the high-precision criterion used for the original English VFS-A-10 (CSEM ≤ 0.30, corresponding to TIF ≥11.1; Hornsby et al. 2023), the Chinese C-VFS-A-10 showed a minimum CSEM of 0.22 at 𝜃 ≈ 0.59, indicating maximal precision around moderately elevated levels of listening-related fatigue and closely matching the original peak around 𝜃 ≈ 0.5. The high-precision region (CSEM ≤ 0.30) extended across 𝜃∈[−0.69, 1.88] and encompassed 84% of respondents. Although this band was narrower than that reported for the original English scale (𝜃∈[−1.5, 2.4], approximately 93% of respondents), it still covered most clinic-referred adults with hearing loss in our sample, suggesting that the C-VFS-A-10 provides adequate precision for the target population.

Associations With Hearing and Clinical Characteristics

In this sample, greater audiometric severity indexed by better-ear PTA was associated with higher scores on both listening effort and listening-related fatigue and remained an independent predictor in multivariable models. Consistent with this, across WHO severity categories, adjusted scores were generally higher at greater hearing-loss severity, although adjacent-category differences were not uniformly detectable. This pattern is consistent with conceptual models and experimental work indicating that, when listeners remain engaged in communication, poorer audibility and more degraded speech signals increase the cognitive resources required for successful understanding, which can manifest as higher perceived effort and cumulative fatigue (Noble & Gatehouse 2006; Pichora-Fuller et al. 2016; Ohlenforst et al. 2017). At the instrument level, our findings are also in line with evidence that the VFS-A-10 relates more strongly to hearing-specific difficulties than to generic fatigue (Hornsby et al. 2023) and that the EAS differentiates adults with and without hearing loss and is sensitive to the impact of hearing difficulties on everyday listening effort (Cañete et al. 2023; Ferschneider & Moulin 2023). More broadly, robust epidemiological evidence links greater hearing loss with increased hearing handicap and poorer cognitive and psychosocial outcomes (Dawes et al. 2015).

At the same time, the PTA gradients observed here were somewhat larger than those typically reported, especially in older, predominantly aided cohorts where associations between audiometric thresholds and self-reported listening effort or fatigue are often weak or absent once hearing handicap and speech recognition are taken into account (Alhanbali et al. 2017, 2018; Ferschneider & Moulin 2023). Our effect sizes, therefore, fall toward the upper end of the range suggested by previous work rather than representing a qualitatively different pattern. Several design features are likely to contribute to these comparatively stronger PTA effects. First, the cohort included a wide range of audiometric severity, with a sizeable subgroup who had bilateral moderate-to-profound hearing loss, rather than being restricted to near-normal or mildly impaired ranges. Consistent with this, prespecified interaction models provided evidence that the association between better-ear PTA and both outcomes differed by hearing-status group. In the BHL subgroup, higher PTA was associated with higher C-EAS and C-VFS-A-10 scores after covariate adjustment, whereas in the NH subgroup, the PTA slopes were not clearly different from zero and did not show a consistent positive pattern within the restricted PTA range. In addition, all participants were unaided at the time of assessment. In such an unaided context, changes in sensory input are likely to translate more directly into perceived listening effort and fatigue than in cohorts where routine amplification partially restores audibility, reduces hearing handicap, and lowers day-to-day listening demands (Johnson et al. 2016; Holman et al. 2021a).

Beyond audibility, tinnitus emerged as an independent correlate of both effort and fatigue after adjustment for PTA and other covariates, indicating additional listening-related burden not reducible to pure-tone thresholds alone. Experimental and clinical data link tinnitus to increased attentional and executive-control demands during listening, consistent with elevated perceived effort even at comparable audiometric levels (Degeest et al. 2017; Sendesen et al. 2023), and systematic reviews associate tinnitus with broader cognitive strain and fatigue phenotypes (Tegg-Quinn et al. 2016). In our data, tinnitus showed robust associations with both the C-EAS and C-VFS-A-10, although exploratory interaction analyses suggested that tinnitus-related effects on effort may differ by hearing-status group, whereas such heterogeneity was not clearly supported for fatigue. Taken together, these findings are consistent with tinnitus contributing to both momentary listening effort and cumulative listening-related fatigue over and above audiometric severity.

Age, in contrast, showed only a minimal association with C-EAS scores and no clear association with C-VFS-A-10 scores. A cautious interpretation is that older age may be linked to small, context-dependent increases in perceived moment-to-moment effort rather than to systematically higher levels of cumulative listening-related fatigue, which is compatible with previous work on age, listening demands, and cognitive load (Degeest et al. 2015; Lemke & Besser 2016; McGarrigle et al. 2021). However, these age effects were small and model-dependent, and the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; more definitive conclusions about age-related trajectories in listening effort and fatigue will require longitudinal or interventional data.

Clinical Implications, Strengths, and Limitations

The C-EAS and C-VFS-A-10 showed acceptable psychometric properties and captured related but partially distinct aspects of listening burden, namely self-reported listening effort and listening-related fatigue. Used together, they may help clinicians gain a broader picture of how patients experience listening difficulties beyond what is reflected in audiometric thresholds alone, while our findings also indicate that their total scores contain a high degree of shared variance. In clinical practice, both scales can be scored as summed totals and interpreted alongside audiometric results and case history; more detailed testlet-based item-level estimates from the full 10-item C-VFS-A-10 are likely to be most relevant in research settings.

A key strength of this study is the large, unaided clinical sample spanning the full WHO severity range, combined with detailed CTT and IRT/testlet diagnostics. This design allowed us to examine PTA × Group interactions and tinnitus effects across a broad spectrum of impairment and to illustrate circumstances in which IRT-based refinements may add information beyond classical scoring. Few cross-cultural validation studies have applied this combination of methods to effort and fatigue measures, particularly in non-English-speaking populations.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The study was single-center and clinic-based, which may limit generalizability to community samples or to patients already using amplification. The UHL subgroup was small and analyzed descriptively only, precluding detailed ear-symmetry comparisons. Convergent validity was examined with the 10-item HHIE/A-S rather than the full HHIE/A, which may reduce comparability with some prior work. For the C-EAS, responses were discretized for IRT diagnostics, and no stable, well-fitting testlet solution was obtained, so item parameters were not retained. Future work in larger, more heterogeneous samples, or with extended item content, could clarify whether more flexible IRT specifications are tenable. Finally, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference about the pathways linking hearing loss, tinnitus, effort, and fatigue.

CONCLUSION

Overall, the C-EAS and C-VFS-A-10 showed high reliability and supported essentially unidimensional measurement at the total-score level for assessing listening effort and listening-related fatigue in Chinese-speaking adults. The C-EAS is best used as a brief summed-score measure of perceived listening effort, whereas the C-VFS-A-10 additionally supported IRT-based scoring with good precision across the fatigue range most commonly observed in this cohort. Among unaided adults with bilateral hearing loss, higher effort and fatigue scores were associated with poorer audibility and tinnitus.
