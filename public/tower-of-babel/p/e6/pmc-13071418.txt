Real-world Application of the International Anorectal Physiology Working Group Standardized Protocol and London Classification: A Multi-country Cross-sectional Survey of Anorectal Manometry Practice in Asia
Authors: Seon-Young Park, Kee Wook Jung, Myeongsook Seo, Han Hee Lee, Ju Yup Lee, Soo In Choi, Jong Wook Kim, Chong Il Sohn, Suck Chei Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/AimsThe International Anorectal Physiology Working Group (IAPWG) has proposed a standardized protocol and the London classification to enhance the consistency and diagnostic accuracy of anorectal manometry (ARM). However, real-world adoption in Asian countries has not been systematically assessed. This study aims to evaluate current ARM practices and adherence to the IAPWG protocol across Asian centers.MethodsA cross-sectional, 50-item web-based survey was distributed to gastroenterologists and motility specialists practicing in Asian countries.ResultsThirty-one centers from 8 countries responded (20 in Korea, 3 in Japan, 2 in Taiwan, 1 in China, and 7 in other countries). High-resolution ARM was used in 80.6% of centers, primarily with solid-state catheters. While all centers performed rest and short squeeze maneuvers, only 58.1% conducted all maneuvers recommended by the IAPWG protocol. Considerable variation was observed in test methodology and interpretation, including definitions of resting pressure, squeeze duration, push maneuver repetition, and rectal balloon volume for rectoanal inhibitory reflex. For balloon expulsion and rectal sensory testing, centers differed in patient positioning, balloon type, inflation methods, and threshold definitions. Only 38.7% of centers reported having institutional normative values for test interpretation, and 64.5% applied the London classification.ConclusionsAcross Asian centers, ARM practice shows marked regional variation and incomplete implementation of the IAPWG standardized protocol and London classification, highlighting persistent gaps in standardization. Strengthening procedural guidance, regionally appropriate normative data, and interpretation criteria through coordinated education and international collaboration is needed to support more consistent and clinically meaningful use of ARM in routine practice.Keywords: Education, Manometry, Practice patterns, Protocol, Survey

Introduction

Anorectal manometry (ARM) is an established diagnostic tool for identifying pathophysiological anorectal disorders, including fecal incontinence, constipation, rectal prolapse, and anorectal pain. However, protocols for performing, interpreting, and reporting ARM results differ substantially among institutions.1 Recently, the International Anorectal Physiology Working Group (IAPWG) published a consensus guideline providing a standardized protocol for ARM methods and interpretation, as well as the first classification system for anorectal dysfunction based on objective physiological measurements (the London classification) developed from expert opinion.2 Nonetheless, limitations remain, such as the absence of validated normal values accounting for demographic factors including age, sex, parity, body mass index, and ethnicity.3 Additionally, further modifications to equipment and manometry software are required. Detailed, standardized methodological protocols are also lacking for procedures such as rectal sensory testing (RST), rectoanal inhibitory reflex (RAIR) testing, simulated defecation testing, and balloon expulsion test (BET).4

The development of performance guidelines and a classification system has promoted broader acceptance and standardization of ARM. Despite this, information on the clinical adoption of the IAPWG protocol is limited. Variability in equipment, institutional protocols, and clinician familiarity with ARM may affect implementation of the standard protocol. Therefore, we aim to investigate the current real-world application of the IAPWG standard protocol in Asian countries through a multi-institutional survey following its release.

Materials and Methods

A questionnaire assessing real-world clinical practice for ARM was developed by members of the Education Committee Group under the Korean Society of Neurogastroenterology and Motility. The Committee drafted the initial English questionnaire, which was then professionally edited by an English editing service and subsequently refined by committee members to ensure clinical relevance and linguistic clarity. The finalized English version was distributed to non-Asian centers, whereas a Korean version was used for Korean centers (Supplementary Methods). To develop the Korean version, the English questionnaire was translated into Korean by a Korean researcher familiar with the study objectives, and this version was back-translated into English by an independent bilingual translator; the back-translated and original English versions were compared, and any discrepancies were resolved by the Committee through iterative review to ensure conceptual equivalence. Terminology related to key anorectal manometry concepts, including the RAIR, rectal sensation testing, the balloon expulsion test, the London classification, and IAPWG maneuvers etc, was harmonized across language versions. The questionnaire was pilot tested by members of the Education Committee, who assessed item clarity, terminology, and response options, leading to minor modifications based on their feedback. Although automated skip logic was not available in the web platform, respondents at centers not performing high-resolution anorectal manometry (HR-ARM) or not using the London classification were explicitly instructed to skip HR-ARM- and London-specific items, and brief definitions/examples for key terms were embedded in the survey to minimize misclassification.

The survey comprised 50 items covering center activity, manometry technique and equipment, patient preparation, test protocol, data analysis, and interpretation. The survey was designed to assess compliance with the standard protocol, specifically focusing on the IAPWG protocol. Data were collected via multiple-choice questions, primarily closed-ended, with some open-ended questions for responses not otherwise provided. The questionnaire was professionally translated and implemented as a web-based survey (Google Survey). Detailed information on the variables used in this study is available in the Supplementary Method (Data dictionary). The target population were Asian medical centers performing ARM. We invited one designated representative from each center affiliated with the Korean Society of Neurogastroenterology and Motility that performs ARM, and we additionally sent email invitations to representatives of member countries in the Asian Neurogastroenterology and Motility Association from March 2023 to June 2023. Respondents were instructed to complete the questionnaire as the center’s designated representative. All submissions were reviewed to confirm that no center contributed more than one response.

The protocol was subsequently reviewed and the study was approved by the Inje University College of Medicine Institutional Review Board and a waiver of documentation of informed consent was granted due to the anonymous, minimal-risk nature of the study in accordance with institutional and ethical guidelines (IRB No. 2024-08-005).

Data are presented as number (percentage). We analyzed adherence to IAPWG-recommended maneuvers including BET and use of the London classification according to the center characteristics (country, center volume and equipment types of ARM system). Associations between categorical variables were assessed using χ² tests or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate, and Cramér’s V was reported as a measure of effect size. Given the modest sample size, interpretation emphasized effect sizes and their magnitude rather than formal hypothesis testing.

Results

Center Activity

A total of 136 centers were invited to participate in the survey, of which 31 (22.8%) responded. All responding centers were included in the final analysis. These 31 responses were received from centers in 8 countries: 20 in Korea, 3 in Japan, 2 in Taiwan, 2 in Thailand, and one each in China, Afghanistan, India, and Indonesia. Twenty-six centers were based in tertiary hospitals, and 5 in secondary referral centers.

Fifteen centers (48.4%) reported performing at least 5 studies per month (non-low-volume centers), and 16 centers (51.6%) reported fewer than 5 studies per month (low-volume centers).

Patient Preparation

Patients were instructed to fast before ARM in 21 centers (67.7%), and enemas were used in 18 centers (58.1%). Medication review was conducted in 24 centers (77.4%), with 21 centers (67.7%) recommending discontinuation prior to the procedure. Regarding medication discontinuation prior to ARM, 18 centers reported discontinuing relevant medications for at least 3 days, 1 center for more than 5 days, and 2 centers for more than 7 days before the procedure.

Manometry Technique and Equipment

Equipment varied among centers. High-resolution ARM (HR-ARM) or high-definition ARM (HD-ARM) was used in 25 centers (80.6%), including 23 with solid-state catheters and 2 with water-perfused systems. Use of HR-/HD-ARM was common in both low- and non–low-volume centers, with 13 of 16 low-volume centers (81.3%) and 13 of 15 non–low-volume centers (86.7%) reporting access to HR-ARM equipment (Supplementary Figure). The 2 most frequently used devices were HR-ARM (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA) in 8 centers and UNI-ANO-M0138 (Diversatek, Milwaukee, WI, USA) in 6 centers. Study Protocol and Measurement Reporting

Maneuvers performed

All centers performed rest and short squeeze maneuvers. Long squeeze was performed in 22 centers (71.0%), cough in 26 centers (83.9%), push (simulated defecation) in 25 centers (80.6%), RAIR in 27 centers (87.0%), and RST in 28 centers (90.3%). Of 31 centers, only 18 (58.1%) performed all maneuvers recommended by the IAPWG protocol. Detailed responses for HR-ARM maneuvers are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Stabilization period

To allow anal tone to return to baseline after catheter insertion, 13 centers (41.9%) reported a stabilization period of less than 3 minutes.

Resting pressure assessment

For resting pressure measurement, 11 centers (35.5%) reported a recording period of 60 seconds, 7 centers (22.6%) for over 60 seconds, and 11 centers (35.5%) for 30 seconds. The definition of resting pressure varied: 15 centers (48.4%) used the mean maximum pressure from the entire anal canal, 8 centers (25.8%) used the median maximum pressure, and 6 centers (19.4%) used the maximum pressure measured. The presence of ultraslow wave was reported by 11 centers (35.5%), while the remaining 20 centers did not report their presence of ultraslow wave.

Short squeeze measurement

For short squeeze assessment, 3 attempts were performed in 24 centers (77.4%). Seventeen centers (54.8%) instructed patients to squeeze for 5 seconds, and 9 centers (29.0%) for at least 10 seconds. The most commonly used parameter for short squeeze pressure was maximum incremental pressure, measured by 23 centers (74.2%).

Long squeeze measurement

Among 22 centers performing long squeeze, 11 (50%) instructed patients to squeeze for 30 seconds, while 8 centers (36.4%) asked patients to squeeze for as long as possible. Fourteen centers (63.6%) reported the duration of squeeze above 50% of maximum pressure as the outcome parameter.

Cough reflex and simulated defecation (push)

Among 25 centers performing the push maneuver, 13 (52.0%) performed 3 attempts, 1 (4.0%) performed 4 or more attempts, and 8 (32.0%) performed 1 or 2 attempts. A repeated push maneuver after distension with a 50 mL rectal balloon was conducted in 20 centers (80.0%). Maximal rectal pressure and residual anal pressure during push were recorded in 21 centers (84.0%) and 18 centers (72.0%), respectively. Cough reflex was assessed in 26 centers (83.9%).

Rectoanal inhibitory reflex

Assessment of RAIR was performed in 27 centers (87.1%). The question regarding methods for provoking RAIR allowed multiple responses. RAIR was assessed by stepwise balloon distension with 10 mL of air in 16 of 27 centers (59.3%), by maximum balloon distension with 50 mL or 60 mL of air in 8 centers (29.6%), and by minimum balloon distension with 30 mL of air in 7 centers (25.9%).

Rectal sensory testing

RST was performed in 28 centers (90.3%). The questions regarding RST reporting permitted multiple responses. “First constant sensation volume (in mL)” was reported in 26 of 28 centers (92.9%), “desire to defecate volume (in mL)” in 25 centers (89.3%), “maximum tolerated volume (in mL)” in 25 centers (89.3%), and “sustained urgency volume” in 2 centers (7.1%, Fig. 1). Among 28 centers, 21 (75.0%) identified patient-reported sensory thresholds by gradually increasing the balloon volume. In 7 centers (25.0%), once a sensory threshold was identified, the balloon was deflated and then reinflated to assess the next threshold.

Balloon expulsion test

The methodology and balloon type used for BET varied among the 23 centers performing this test. Regarding patient positioning, 13 of 23 centers (56.5%) conducted BET in the sitting position, while 10 of 23 centers (43.5%) used the left decubitus position. The threshold time cutoff for defining a normal BET also differed; 14 centers (60.9%) considered expulsion within 1 minute as normal (Table 2). Only 7 centers (30.4%) used a commercial balloon for BET. The remaining centers used alternative materials as follows: Foley catheter (n = 1), medical condom (n = 2), medical glove (n = 10), and party balloon (n = 2). Eight centers (26.7%) did not provide information regarding the balloon type used (Fig. 2).

Additional Information

Among 31 centers, 12 centers (38.7%) reported institutional standard reference values. The London classification for anorectal physiologic function was applied in 20 centers (64.5%), while 11 (35.5%) either denied or did not respond regarding its clinical usefulness.

Figure 3 showed the adherence to the IAPWG-recommended maneuvers across center characteristics: center volume and country. When centers were stratified by procedural volume (< 5 vs ≥ 5 studies per month), the proportions performing each standard maneuver were comparable between low-volume (n = 16) and non-low-volume centers (n = 15), with no apparent differences in the use of stabilization time, squeeze maneuvers, cough, push, RAIR assessment, rectal sensation testing, or balloon expulsion test (Supplementary Table 2). In addition, the proportions performing each standard maneuver were comparable between Korean centers (n = 20) and other Asian centers (n = 11) with no apparent differences (Supplementary Table 3). Centers adhering to the London classification were more likely to perform push maneuvers, RAIR, and RST than centers not using the London classification (Table 3), whereas performance of other maneuvers was similar between the groups.

Discussion

This multinational survey study assessed current ARM practices in Asian countries, focusing on real-world implementation of the IAPWG standardized protocol and the London classification.2 Our results indicate increased adoption of advanced technologies and partial standardization in performance technique, data interpretation, and reporting. Nevertheless, clinical application and utility of the London classification remain limited in routine practice.

Recent advances in ARM equipment and software have substantially improved diagnostic accuracy and clinical value. These include high-resolution manometry (HR-ARM), high-definition manometry (HD-ARM), and advanced software for data acquisition and interpretation.5 Compared to the previous international survey from 2017,1 which also reported wide variability in ARM practices, our findings suggest higher uptake of HR-ARM with solid-state catheters in the present study. In this Asia-focused survey conducted after publication of the IAPWG protocol, 80.6% of centers reported using HR-ARM with solid-state catheters, whereas the 2017 survey (conducted predominantly in Western centers and prior to publication of the IAPWG protocol and London classification) reported a rate of 53%. However, because the 2 surveys differ in sampling frame, geographical mix, and survey methodology, these figures should be interpreted as contextual rather than as evidence of a direct temporal trend. HR-ARM systems offer advantages such as multi-channel catheters with closely spaced sensors and topographical pressure mapping.6 Resting and squeeze pressures measured by HR-ARM have been shown to correlate with those obtained by conventional ARM.7,8 Moreover, HR-ARM enables simultaneous assessment of anorectal function and enhanced diagnosis of anorectal dysfunction.9,10 These benefits likely contribute to the rapid transition from conventional ARM to HR-ARM in clinical practice.

The IAPWG protocol recommends minimum standards for physiological assessment of anorectal function, including 6 HR-ARM maneuvers, RST, and BET. However, our findings identified several limitations in its clinical applicability. In particular, the protocol lacks detailed guidance on the performance and interpretation of key components such as BET and RST. Our survey revealed marked variability among centers in patient positioning for BET (sitting vs left decubitus), balloon type (commercial vs non-commercial), and thresholds for normal expulsion time. The use of alternative, non-commercial materials such as medical gloves and condoms represents a significant lack of standardization and raises concerns regarding the consistency of material properties and infection control procedures compared to approved medical devices. Similarly, for RST, inflation techniques and sensory threshold definitions differed. This lack of procedural detail may have affected adherence levels across centers. These inconsistencies suggest that future protocol revisions should incorporate more comprehensive operational instructions and standardized criteria to improve consistency. Indeed, only 18 centers (58.1%) performed all maneuvers recommended by the IAPWG protocol. This relatively low adherence rate may be attributable to several factors. First, full compliance requires a time-intensive evaluation by highly trained personnel. In practice, many centers are limited by insufficient numbers of trained staff and lack of standardized training or awareness of the IAPWG protocol. The observed heterogeneity may also reflect differences in training or subjective interpretation of protocol requirements. Second, patient-related factors and clinical judgment influence protocol adherence. ARM is often perceived as uncomfortable and invasive, leading to incomplete adherence due to patient discomfort, anxiety, or inability to cooperate. Clinicians may also omit maneuvers based on the patient’s condition or findings from other physiologic tests. Supporting such selective approaches, a recent study showed that a modified IAPWG protocol with fewer and shorter maneuvers in patients with fecal incontinence did not compromise diagnostic yield, suggesting that certain protocol elements may be redundant.11 Third, healthcare system and reimbursement limitations may impede protocol adherence. Comprehensive assessments using expensive equipment and requiring skilled personnel are frequently under-reimbursed, discouraging full protocol implementation, especially for resource-intensive components. Collectively, these clinical and procedural challenges underscore the need for system-level solutions to support standardized ARM practice. Advancing the field will require standardized training programs, evidence-based protocol refinement, and adequate reimbursement for complex, time-consuming assessments performed by highly trained personnel.

The IAPWG has proposed standardized definitions for reporting outcomes of each maneuver. However, our survey found considerable variability in outcome definitions across centers, reflecting a lack of detailed procedural guidance and structured educational efforts, such as workshops and training modules addressing ARM performance and interpretation. Additionally, the IAPWG protocol does not specify normative or cutoff values for outcome interpretation, which are known to vary by equipment type, technique, and patient characteristics such as gender, parity, or age.2 The use of artificial intelligence models may help overcome limitations of conventional diagnostic parameters.12 Furthermore, technological advancements such as Fecobionics may enhance understanding of anorectal physiology.13 Future protocol iterations should provide comprehensive descriptions of maneuvers, normative parameters, and outcome interpretation, incorporating technological advances.

In this study, the London classification was applied in approximately 65% of participating centers. However, about 25% of respondents expressed reservations regarding its clinical utility, indicating uncertainty and variability in its interpretation and use in daily practice. Although most centers in our survey (80.6%) used HR-ARM, the London classification system is specifically designed for interpretation with HRM data. Consequently, applying the London classification to centers that use conventional ARM may be problematic, owing to differences in measurement techniques, normative values, and diagnostic criteria. This limits both the accurate application of the London classification and the generalizability of our findings to non-HRM settings. Future studies should address this issue and clarify whether the London criteria can be appropriately validated for conventional ARM systems. Recent studies suggest that defining rectal hyposensitivity based on 1 or more abnormal sensory thresholds is more clinically meaningful, as it better correlates with patient characteristics.14-17 Another multicenter prospective study demonstrated the prevalence and potential clinical relevance of the London classification in patients with chronic constipation, fecal incontinence, or overlapping symptoms.18 However, these findings require further prospective validation before revisions to the London classification criteria can be considered. Additional international and multicenter studies are needed to establish diagnostic efficacy and clarify its role in personalized, pathophysiology-based treatment strategies and outcome prediction.

This study has several limitations. The survey did not include questions about clinical indications for ARM at each center, so indication-specific data could not be analyzed. Future research should address this issue. Additionally, our findings are subject to limitations inherent to survey methodology, including potential selection bias, as the sample favored academically engaged centers, and information bias due to the self-reported nature of the data, which may overestimate adherence. Furthermore, the small sample size and significant heterogeneity of center volume heterogeneity across 9 countries limit the generalizability of the findings and the power to draw strong associations between center characteristics and protocol adherence.

In conclusion, our study demonstrated increasing adoption of advanced ARM technologies and partial implementation of international standards in Asian countries. Nonetheless, considerable variability persists in protocol adherence, measurement techniques, and interpretation criteria. These results emphasize the need for structured training, international consensus on performance and interpretation standards, and appropriate reimbursement models to support comprehensive and standardized ARM practice.
