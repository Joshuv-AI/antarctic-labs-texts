Mega-floods over the past 30,000 years in western yunnan, southwest China
Authors: Qi Suo, Qifa Sun, Qian Shi, Min Wang, Hongwei Meng, Linpei Huang, Lixiong Xiang, Caiming Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Global warming has been leading to a significant increase in the frequency and intensity of extreme rainfall events, however, few paleoflood records hinder our understanding of its long-term patterns and underlying mechanisms. Here, we present a record of mega-floods over the past 30,000 years revealed by fossil cladoceran and geochemical proxies from Lake YunlongTianchi (YLTC) in western Yunnan, SW China. Seven mega-floods are identified in terms of abrupt changes in percentages of planktonic cladocerans, planktic-to-littoral (P/L) ratios, cladoceran flux, sedimentation rates, and terrestrial elements. They occurred at 20,410, 16,940, 15,340, 13,930, 11,540, 3,730, and 1,270 BP, when the lake catchment witnessed extreme precipitation and reduced land cover. They were triggered by extreme rainfall induced by monsoon instability, and amplified by decreased infiltration capacity due to reduced land cover. Our results suggest that these mega-floods were likely associated with monsoonal precipitation extremes as well as land cover change in the lake catchment. Our findings provide a reference for both understanding the driving mechanisms of mega-floods during geological times in western Yunnan and assessing regional extreme hydrological risks and adaptive strategies under global warming scenarios.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46783-5.

Introduction

Future warming scenarios are projected to increase South Asian monsoon precipitation, and thus extreme rainfall events and flood risks1. As the most biodiverse yet ecologically fragile region in China2–4, Southwest China is strongly influenced by the South Asian summer monsoon (SASM)5–7, and it is therefore particularly susceptible to hydroclimatic disruptions. Mounting evidence indicates that global warming has elevated both the frequency and intensity of extreme hydrological events8–10. Observational and satellite data reveal a significant increase in the global extent of floods11 and the proportion of the population exposed to floods12. Data reanalysis indicates that increased monsoon instability leads to more extreme rainfall events13, which in turn trigger flood events. Historical records indicate that flood exposure risk on the Tibetan Plateau rose markedly, and it is expected to intensify under future warming14. Proxy evidence shows that extreme hydroclimatic events, such as floods, became more frequent in southwestern China over the past centuries, exerting severe impacts on human livelihoods15. Climate models also project that flood regimes will strengthen under ongoing warming, primarily driven by extreme precipitation16,17. However, projections of future extreme events remain highly uncertain18,19, largely because long-term paleoflood records are scarce2,20–22, limiting robust attribution of the mechanisms underlying these events.

Lake sediments uniquely offer long and continuous records. Their wide geographic distribution, hydroclimatic sensitivity, and stratigraphic integrity provide exceptional insights into flood regimes in geological times23,24. Recent studies of Holocene flood records25–27 suggested that flood dynamics arose from interactions between external forcings and internal climate variability25,27. The Last Glacial Maximum (LGM, ~ 26,500–19,000 BP) (BP = cal yr BP), the last deglaciation (LD, ~ 19,000–11,700 BP), and current interglacial (the Holocene) provide an opportunity to better understand long-term natural rhythm of extreme hydroclimate events in monsoonal Asia. However, few studies provide continuous, high-resolution records of extreme floods over the past ~ 30,000 years, particularly addressing how flood occurrence varied under warm and cold climate periods. This lack of long-term records limits our mechanistic understanding of flood–climate linkages.

To address this knowledge gap, we present a 30,000-yr high-resolution paleoflood record from Lake YLTC in western Yunnan, Southwest China. This multi-proxy study integrates (1) cladoceran assemblages (planktonic percentages, planktic-to-littoral (P/L) ratios, and cladoceran flux), (2) geochemical proxies (XRF-derived elemental and loss-on-ignition), and (3) rate of lake water depth changes to identify mega-floods. Specifically, we aim to reconstruct the temporal patterns of mega-floods, reveal their relationship with environmental changes, and clarify how mega-floods responded to warm and cold climate periods, as well as the role of SASM in controlling flood occurrence. This mega-flood record is further compared with regional paleoclimate data, including speleothem δ¹⁸O and pollen-based reconstructions, to reveal possible flood-climate linkage, providing insights for both paleoclimate reconstruction and contemporary flood risk assessment.

Results

Cladoceran Record over the past 30,000 years

Zone III (1716–1236 cm; 29,850–19,000 BP; the LGM): This zone is characterized by the overwhelming dominance of Chydorus sphaericus (facultative planktonic) and some littoral taxa such as Alona guttata/rectangula and Leydigia leydigi. Other littoral taxa, including A. affinis and A. costata, were also present but in low abundances. Planktonic taxa were largely absent or rare in this zone, as reflected by low planktonic percentages, low P/L ratios, and minimal Cladoceran flux. An abrupt shift occurred around 20,410 BP, marked by a sharp increase in the planktonic species such as Bosmina longispina, accompanied by a coeval rise in planktonic percentages, P/L ratios, and Cladoceran flux.

Zone II (1236–699 cm; 19,000–11,700 BP; the LD): This zone is marked by a substantial decline in the abundance of C. sphaericus and A. guttata/rectangula, alongside a significantly increase in (A) affinis at the expense of L. leydigi. Other littoral taxa were either rare or nearly absent. Three intervals, with marked increases in the planktonic taxa (B) longispina, Daphnia longispina group, and D. pulex group as well as pronounced rises in planktonic percentages, P/L ratios, and Cladoceran flux, appeared at 16,940, 15,340, and 13,930 BP.

Zone I (699–0 cm; 11,700 BP–present; the Holocene): This zone is dominated by a diverse assemblage of planktonic and littoral Cladocera, reflecting a marked shift in community structure compared to earlier zones. Cladocera assemblages of this zone contain abundant planktonic taxa such as B. longispina, B. longirostris, D. longispina group, and D. pulex group, together with consistently elevated planktonic percentages and P/L ratios. C. sphaericus and A. guttata/rectangula increase notably, whereas A. affinis declines markedly. Other littoral species, including L. leydigi, A. intermedia, A. quadrangularis, A. costata, Acroperus harpae, and Alonella excisa, are common but at low abundance levels, and show a gradual decreasing trend over time. Abrupt increases in the planktonic taxa Bosmina and Daphnia are observed at approximately 11,540, 3,730, and 1,270 BP, coinciding with marked peaks in planktonic percentages, P/L ratios, and Cladoceran flux.

Identification of mega-floods

Previous studies showed that fluctuations in lake water depth significantly influence Cladoceran assemblages, i.e., increasing water depth typically suppresses the growth of littoral taxa28–30. The proportion of planktonic taxa has successfully been used as a semi-quantitative indicator for past water level changes28,31,32. Studies from Lake YLTC further showed that planktonic percentages are significantly positively correlated with lake water depth33, consistent with these previous findings. Additionally, similar patterns have been observed in Haixi Lake, located in the same region, where increased water levels correlate with higher planktonic percentages30. The same results have also been verified in Bosten Lake34 and Jili Lake35 in Northwest China. Similarly, the P/L ratio, which reflects lake water depth changes, has been proposed as an indicator of lake water level changes in many lakes and reservoirs worldwide29,36. For instance, similar findings have been reported in East Taihu Lake37 and Shengjin Lake38. However, multi-proxy validation is needed for this interpretation of P/L ratios, as they are influenced by various factors39.

Our multi-proxy dataset shows several concurrent peaks at 20,410, 16,940, 15,340, 13,930, 11,540, 3,730, and 1,270 BP (Fig. 2). These peaks are especially evident in the cladoceran assemblages, as shown by sudden increases in planktonic percentages, P/L ratios, and cladoceran flux (Fig. 2a-c), indicating the rapid deepening of lake water. Simultaneously, detrital inputs, such as Ti, K, Al, and Si, typically linked to catchment erosion40, increase significantly (Supplementary Fig. 8d–g). These elements are the dominant contributors to XRF-PC1 (Supplementary Fig. 5), a proxy of erosion, showing strong correlations with other detrital input indicators, such as redness26(r = 0.59, p < 0.001) and mineralogical composition41(r = 0.71, p < 0.001). Sedimentation rate also increases synchronously (Fig. 2d). Overall, we interpreted these abrupt and concurrent changes across multiple proxies as seven episodes of extreme hydrological disturbance, referred to as F1 to F7 mega-floods. They resulted in sudden increases in lake water depth, more detrital inputs, and significant ecological shifts in the lake ecosystem. Notably, during the F2-F3 and F5 events, minor changes in sedimentation rates may be related to the less dating samples in the nearby sediment layers (Fig. 2). The F8 event occurred around 140 BP. However, historical documents indicated that a simple dam was constructed at the YLTC at that time42, so this event is not classified as a mega-flood. The timing, magnitude, duration, and age uncertainty of each event are summarized in Supplementary Table 2.

To validate those identified mega-floods, we further applied rate-of-change (ROC) analysis and a long short-term memory (LSTM) model into Cladoceran PC2 (a proxy of water depth) and planktonic percentage data, respectively. Except for the F4 mega-flood, six mega-floods were also identified at 95% confidence level by the ROC analysis (Supplementary Fig. 6). For the F4 mega-flood, the lake depth showed a deepening trend, although the ROC analysis did not capture a significant change. Given its synchronous multi-proxy signal, F4 was nevertheless retained as a mega-flood event. The LSTM model detected seven abrupt increases in planktonic percentages that surpassed the threshold of 40% (Supplementary Fig. 7), supporting our interpretation. Moreover, element S content reflects reducing conditions in sediments and serves as a proxy for lake-level fluctuations26,43, showing marked increases during these mega-floods (Fig. 2e). Additionally, elevated C/N ratios observed during the identified mega-floods (Supplementary Fig. 8a) indicate increased allochthonous inputs44, further supporting our interpretation.

Discussion

Mega-Floods over the Past 30,000 Years in Western Yunnan, SW China

Regional flood dynamics within the context of long-term climatic variability is crucial for understanding the dynamic relationship between climate change and hydrological extremes on different timescales45,46. In this study, we identified seven mega-floods (F1-F7) around 20,410, 16,940, 15,340, 13,930, 11,540, 3,730, and 1,270 BP over the past 30,000 years. More mega-floods (F2-F7) occurred in the LD-the beginning of the Holocene, and late Holocene, whereas less mega-floods appeared in the LGM and early-middle Holocene.

,,,A comparison of regional paleoflood archives shows that some mega-floods were not only confined to Southwest China but also occurred in India (Fig. 3). For example, the F4 mega-flood (13,930 BP) corresponds with sediment evidence from the Indian River on the Tibetan Plateau47. Similarly, paleoflood phases on the Tibetan Plateau between 13,000 and 11,000 BP47,48 coincide with the F5 mega-flood, and abundant flood sediments are also observed in India at 11,600 BP49. Among late Holocene events, the F6 mega-flood (3,730 BP) shows the greatest regional synchrony and societal relevance, drawing attention due to its concurrence with the rise and fall of multiple ancient civilizations50. Archaeological evidence at the Zhongba, Baodun, Jinsha, and Hongqiaocun sites in Sichuan basin reveals that paleofloods at 4,100–3,790 BP may have facilitated the transformation of the ancient Shu civilization51,52,53. Lacustrine sediments from an alpine lake of the Southeastern Tibetan Plateau record a flood event around 3,900 BP54, while fluvial sediments in the western Himalayas48 and India55 provide evidence for a flood event around 3,600 BP. The F7 mega-flood (1,270 BP) corresponds with the severe flood recorded in historical records of Chongqing in 648 AD (~ 1,300 BP)56. Sedimentary records from the Tibetan Plateau confirm a flood event around 1,200 BP54, and further evidence indicates increased flood frequency between 1,600 and 1,200 BP47. Additionally, flood sediments from the western Himalayas48 and India55,57,58,59,60 corroborate the F7 event of the late Holocene.

Possible causes of mega-floods

In this study, we identified seven mega-flood events (F1–F7). Overall, flood events were more frequent during the LD-the beginning of the Holocene, and late Holocene, periods characterized by pronounced climatic variability, whereas fewer mega-floods occurred during the LGM and early-middle Holocene, when climate was comparatively stable.

These observations suggest that mega-flood occurrence and magnitude result from the complex interaction between climatic forcings and catchment characteristics (Fig. 4)61. As the primary hydrological events, the major dynamic source of mega-floods is extreme monsoonal rainfall; meanwhile, vegetation cover is another dominant factor in the catchment, which significantly influences the transformation of extreme rainfall into surface runoffs and the infiltration capacity, soil stability, and water retention in the catchment62,63,64. Accordingly, mega-flood occurrence in Lake YLTC was controlled not simply by mean monsoon intensity, but by the interplay among hydroclimatic instability, the frequency of extreme rainfall, and catchment buffering capacity. On millennial timescales, regional hydroclimate in the is governed by various climate drivers, including summer insolation (Fig. 5a)65,66, Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) strength (Fig. 5b)67, Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ) (Fig. 5c-d)68, and El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO) variance27. These factors influence both the amount and seasonality of precipitation, determining the major dynamic source of mega-floods. On the other hand, low vegetation coverage increases the exposure of the surface soil and reduces its capacity to buffer the high-energy runoff resulting from the heavy rainfall, as suggested by previous studies69,70. In the Lake Caohai catchment of SW China, a negative correlation between LOI550% and Ti concentrations, similarly observed in Lake YLTC (r= −0.81, p < 0.001, n = 337), suggests that denser vegetation limits runoff-induced erosion71. Since the positive relationship between evergreen forest % and LOI550% (r = 0.23, p < 0.05, n = 95) in the YLTC catchment, LOI550% seems reasonable as a valid vegetation cover proxy. During the LD period, detrital input sharply increased (Fig. 2f-h), and it was primarily driven by the combined effects of increased precipitation and reduced vegetation cover, i.e., enhanced erosion processes in the catchment (Fig. 5g and Supplementary Fig. 8c), as suggested by the sedimentary study of Lake Erhai in western Yunnan72. Overall, extreme rainfall induced by monsoon instability, together with vegetation cover, exerts a dominant control on the frequency and magnitude of mega-floods.

The LGM was a period with fewer mega-floods, just with only one occurring around 20,410 BP (F1 mega-flood). This suggests that, despite the generally low vegetation cover in the catchment, land cover was not the primary driver of mega-floods (Supplementary Fig. 8c). The δ18O records from Qunf Cave and Mawmluh Cave indicate that the monsoon was relatively stable during the LGM66,73,74, leading to reduced SASM rainfall and fewer extreme rainfall events74,75,76. A sudden increase in rainfall occurred around 20,410 BP (Fig. 5g-h)77,78, which, coupled with a sharp rise in catchment erosion (Supplementary Fig. 8d-g) and detrital inputs (Fig. 2f-h), ultimately led to the occurrence of the F1 mega-flood. Although vegetation cover was low, the less extreme rainfall led to fewer mega-floods during the LGM. In addition, enhanced ENSO variability and northward shifts of the ITCZ (Fig. 5d) may have increased monsoonal rainfall27,79 thereby leading to the F1 mega-flood.

More mega-floods occurred in the LD-the beginning of the Holocene, specifically around 16,940, 15,340, 13,930, and 11,540 BP (F2-F5 mega-floods). In contrast to the LGM, the monsoon became unstable during this period80,81,82 leading to more extreme rainfall events74. Although catchment vegetation was gradually recovering (Supplementary Fig. 8c)83, mega-floods were more frequent compared to other periods. This was likely due to the unstable summer monsoon, which triggered extreme rainfall, causing higher detrital input (Fig. 2f–h) and catchment erosion (Supplementary Fig. 8d–g). In addition to SASM instability, enhanced ENSO variability and northward shifts of the ITCZ (Fig. 5d) may further modulate monsoon rainfall intensity, thereby influencing the occurrence of these mega-floods27,79. For example, the F2 mega-flood occurred during the early Heinrich Stadial 1 (HS1), coinciding with an increase in SASM intensity recorded in the Arabian Sea78,84, with unstable SASM possibly triggering extreme rainfall. The F3 mega-flood occurred during the early Bølling-Allerød warming, synchronized with monsoon intensification and increased precipitation in the Arabian Sea86,87 and also aligns with records from the Bay of Bengal88,89 and southwestern India90. Similarly, the F4 mega-flood reflects renewed SASM intensification, supported by marine records from the Eastern Arabian Sea87 and the Bay of Bengal89, with unstable SASM likely again triggering extreme rainfall. The F5 mega-flood occurred at the end of the Younger Dryas, and its occurrence may be linked to extreme rainfall triggered by SASM instability, supported by independent proxy evidence, including enhanced SASM in the Arabian Sea84,85,87 and peaks in magnetic susceptibility in Bay of Bengal sediments89.

The relatively stable monsoon conditions during the MH80 resulted in fewer floods26,27 with no mega-floods occurring in the YLTC. This pattern can be explained by a coupled “vegetation-erosion-flood” feedback mechanism. Favorable hydrothermal conditions promoted vegetation growth (Supplementary Fig. 8c)83, which in turn inhibited catchment erosion (Supplementary Fig. 8d-g)71. More mega-floods occurred in the late Holocene, specifically around 1,270 and 3,730 BP (F6-F7 mega-floods). The monsoon changes during the LH period were abrupt and unstable80,91 accompanied by increased ENSO variability, which led to frequent floods26,27. During this period, the SASM weakened (Fig. 5e) and monsoon precipitation decreased (Fig. 5g), but more mega-floods occurred, deviating from this pattern, highlighting the combined influence of environmental and human activities. Archaeological evidence records significant agricultural expansion around 3,800 BP, resulting in a marked reduction in land cover92,93. Simultaneously, sediment cores from Lake Erhai show an increase in copper concentrations around 3,500 BP, indicating intensified copper smelting activities in the region94. Following the establishment of the Nanzhao Kingdom around 1,210 BP (738 AD), significant population growth and agricultural expansion occurred, particularly in mountainous areas95,96 which further reduced vegetation cover. Although vegetation cover sharply declined during this period (Supplementary Fig. 8c), overall coverage remained higher than in earlier stages83, suggesting that vegetation loss alone could not fully account for the flood intensity. Other factors, such as regional drought (Fig. 5h), may have also played a significant role. Previous studies have shown that soil compaction97 and enhanced surface hydrophobicity98 can reduce soil permeability and increase surface runoff, especially after prolonged dry periods99. It should be noted that human activities and drought-like conditions likely increased catchment sensitivity to runoff generation, but did not necessarily produce a proportional increase in catchment erosion and detrital export. In addition, the rapid increase in water depth in Lake YLTC around 3,700 BP100, the rise in lake levels on the Tibetan Plateau around 1,300 BP101, and the increased flow of the Irrawaddy River around 1,200 BP102, along with flood deposits in India around 1,300 BP55,59 provide further evidence for the occurrence of the F6-F7 mega-floods.

In summary, the F1–F7 mega-floods over the past 30,000 years reveal that flood dynamics have been governed by complex, temporally variable interactions among extreme rainfall, vegetation cover, and surface hydrological conditions. Importantly, mega-flood occurrence depended on hydroclimatic instability and the efficiency with which extreme rainfall was converted into runoff. These findings underscore the importance of integrating both climatic forcings and ecological responses in reconstructing paleoflood histories.

Conclusions

This study provides a 30,000-year paleoflood reconstruction of Lake YLTC, revealing seven mega-floods around 20,410, 16,940, 15,340, 13,930, 11,540, 3,730, and 1,270 BP. More mega-floods occurred, primarily triggered by extreme rainfall associated with the unstable summer monsoon during the LD to the beginning of the Holocene and late Holocene, amplified by reduced vegetation cover and soil water retention causing large runoff and detrital input. Fewer mega-floods occurred during the LGM and early-middle Holocene, when stable summer monsoon brought about fewer extreme rainfalls prone to mega-floods. Fewer mega-floods were also partially attributed to the suppressive role of dense vegetation in mitigating flood generation, especially the early-middle Holocene. These findings offer new insights into the hydrological events of monsoon-dominated regions and valuable paleoclimate references for assessing the increasing frequency of mega-flood in the context of ongoing global warming.

Materials and methods

The setting of Lake YLTC

Lake YLTC (25°52′12.89″ N, 99°16′49.75″ E) is located in western Yunnan, Southwest China (Fig. 6a, b). The lake has a maximum water depth of 18.9 m, a surface area of 1.28 km2, and a catchment area of 11.3 km2. It has a natural outflow in its east bank, which flows into a branch of the Lantsang River. Observational data from 1981 to 2010 AD at the Yunlong Meteorological Station (the nearest station) show that the mean annual temperature is ~ 16.2 °C; the mean annual precipitation is ∼780.8 mm, of which 672.1 mm (about 86.1%) is concentrated in the rainy season from May to October mostly brought about by the SASM (Fig. 6c). Modern vegetation in the catchment is primarily composed of evergreen broadleaved forest dominated by Lithocarpus variolosus and Rhododendron irroratum, sclerophyllous evergreen forest dominated by Quercus guyavaefolia and Quercus rehderiana, deciduous broadleaved forest dominated by Alnus nepalensis, subtropical coniferous forest dominated by Pinus yunnanensis and Pinus armandii, temperate coniferous forest dominated by Tsuga dumosa, Abies delavayi, Picea likiangensis, and Larix speciosa, alpine shrubs and meadows, with the vegetation cover reaching 93%110.

In 2019, An 1880-cm sediment core (YLTC-2) was obtained from Lake YLTC at a water depth of 16.4 m using the above-water platform and the Austrian UWITEC piston sampling equipment (UWITEC GmbH, Mondsee, Austria). Its age-depth model was established through AMS14C dating of 22 samples (8 bulk organic sediments, 5 pollen concentrates, and 9 plant macrofossils) using Bacon 2.2 package111 implemented in R 3.0.2 (https://www.R-project.org) (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Text 1). The dating was conducted at the Beta Laboratory in the United States. The analysis was conducted at Yunnan Key Laboratory of Plateau Geographical Processes and Environmental Changes, Yunnan Normal University. Cladoceran analysis was conducted on this core’s upper 0–1716 cm, where fossil Cladoceran were present. A total of 196 samples were analyzed at ~ 10 cm intervals. A minimum of 50 individuals112 were counted per sample. Nineteen samples contained insufficient numbers of fossil Cladoceran, and were excluded from subsequent analyses. Cladoceran procedure was done using the standard method (Supplementary Text 4)113. The core was split lengthwise in the laboratory. One half was scanned using a Dutch Avaatech XRF scanner to obtain element abundances and redness, and the other half was sliced and freeze-dried for measurements of LOI and Cladocera (Supplementary Text 2–4). Principal component analysis (PCA) of major cladoceran percentage data was also performed to help the climatic interpretation of cladoceran spectra and zones (Supplementary Fig. 3). The PC2 of Cladoceran data were further analyzed using the rate of change114.

Definition of mega-floods

We defined a mega-flood as an extreme hydrological disturbance represented by the synchronous occurrence of anomalies across multiple independent proxies within a stratigraphic interval. In practice, a stratigraphic interval was considered a potential mega-flood only when at least two of the following three proxy groups showed clear anomalies, providing a quantitative and reproducible criterion for event identification: (1) Cladoceran assemblage features, such as increased planktonic percentages (~ 40%), high P/L ratios (~ 0.7), and elevated cladoceran flux, reflecting abrupt changes in lake water depth and habitat structure; (2) sedimentation rate, indicating enhanced detrital input due to the catchment flooding; and (3) proxies of external detrital influx (e.g., redness, minerogenic %, XRF-PC1), signaling increased external detrital influx of lake sediments.

We also validated these mega-floods using multiple approaches. First, we performed rate-of-change (ROC) analysis on cladoceran PC2 scores, a proxy of lake water depth, to capture abrupt shifts in lake water depth dynamics. The ROC analysis quantified changes in cladoceran PC2 within a 170-year moving window, consistent with the average temporal resolution of the core sequence, and significant peaks were identified at the 95% confidence level; detailed procedures are provided in Supplementary Text 6. Peaks in the ROC series coinciding with multi-proxy anomalies supported the interpretation of mega-floods. Second, we employed a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) neural network to detect anomalies in the planktonic percentage series, enabling recognition of complex deviations from normal patterns115. The thresholds for planktonic percentage (~ 40%) and P/L ratio (~ 0.7) were statistically determined from the LSTM analysis: we first predicted the planktonic percentage series using the LSTM model and calculated the residuals between observed and predicted values; potential anomalous events were identified using a threshold defined as the mean residual plus two standard deviations (mean + 2σ). Among the seven events identified, the event with the lowest peak magnitude (F2) was used to define the final planktonic percentage threshold (~ 40%), and the corresponding P/L ratio (~ 0.7) was adopted as the associated threshold.

Only mega-floods that simultaneously exhibited multi-proxy anomalies and were validated by at least one of the two statistical or machine learning methods (ROC, LSTM) were ultimately classified as mega-floods. In addition, the abrupt increases in S content and C/N ratios provided supplementary evidence for mega-flood identification, as these indicators respectively reflect changes in lake water depth and the sources of organic matter. The ~ 10 cm sampling interval corresponds to an average temporal resolution of approximately 170 years, although this varies among core sections depending on sedimentation rate. Therefore, flood events of shorter duration or lower intensity may not be fully resolved or detected in the record.
