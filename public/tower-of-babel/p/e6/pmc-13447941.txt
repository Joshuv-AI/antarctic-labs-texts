Physics-informed machine learning for cross-study prediction of oil–water membrane fouling
Authors: Carine Lourenco Alves, Silvio Edegar Weschenfelder, Maria Alice Prado Cechinel, Mariane Carolina Proner, Regina de Fátima, Peralta Muniz Moreira, Alan Ambrosi, Marco Di Luccio, Sergio Yesid Gómez González
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPerformance decay driven by coupled transport, accumulation, and removal processes remains a central challenge in many chemical engineering systems. Membrane fouling in oil–water separation is examined here as a representative case. While numerical and empirical models can capture specific cases, their predictive performance across independent studies remains limited. In this work, we combine a reduced-order finite-element model of transport and fouling resistance with a data-driven framework that maps experimental metadata to flux-decline behavior across 112 datasets involving various membranes, oils, and operating conditions. The reduced representation reproduces flux–time trajectories with high fidelity (average R2 ≈ 0.95). Mapping experimental metadata to curve descriptors enables stage-resolved prediction of normalized flux, yielding mean absolute errors of 0.08–0.14 under grouped cross-validation. External validation using publication-level holdouts shows that differences between studies, rather than model capacity, limit predictive performance, highlighting the need for data standardization. The analysis indicates links between crossflow and pressure and early-stage fouling, while porosity and permeance relate to steady-state flux. Based on these results, we propose a minimal checklist of parameters needed for reproducible fouling models. The results quantify the impact of metadata completeness on model transferability and provide a reproducible framework for cross-study analysis of time-dependent membrane performance.Subject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Membrane fouling1 remains a major limitation in oil–water separation processes, leading to progressive flux decline and reduced operational efficiency. Although the mechanisms governing fouling, such as pore blocking, surface deposition, and shear-induced removal are qualitatively well understood, predictive models often remain system-specific and difficult to generalize across different membranes, feed compositions, and operating conditions2–4.

Rapid growth in the global economy and the oil sector has intensified the generation of domestic and industrial oily wastewater streams5,6. Treating this wastewater is not only crucial for environmental protection and resource recovery but also aligns directly with UN Sustainable Development Goal 6 (clean water and sanitation)7. Achieving this goal requires innovative, energy-efficient, and sustainable technologies capable of treating complex industrial effluents while minimizing environmental impact. Traditional oil–water separation techniques, such as centrifugation, flocculation, and air flotation, are often effective for bulk-phase separation but may struggle to remove stable emulsions, particularly those containing surfactants8. In contrast, membrane separation technologies offer advantages in handling finely dispersed and emulsified oil–water mixtures. While membrane processes are generally more energy-efficient than thermal separation methods, their energy consumption and operational costs can be comparable to or higher than conventional mechanical separation techniques, depending on operating conditions and fouling behavior. Their main advantage lies in their ability to achieve high separation efficiency for stable emulsions under relatively compact and controllable operating conditions1. This technology can also effectively handle a wide variety of oil-water mixtures. Because of its adaptability, membrane separation technology has broad applications for treating oily wastewater and is considered one of the most effective methods available9–11.

Advanced membrane technologies, such as nanofiltration and reactive membranes, are increasingly critical for achieving higher flux, superior separation, and sustainability goals and targets in water treatment. While these advances create new opportunities, they also introduce significant complexity to the design and scale-up processes12. A key technological obstacle in membrane separation is the reduction of permeate flow. This is caused by resistance to transmembrane flow, which occurs when pores are blocked and when molecules adsorb onto the membrane surface and within the porous media during the microfiltration process13. Beyond this, challenges with scalability, cost, and maintenance remain significant hurdles, as outlined by Sutrisna et al.9. The issues of fouling, scaling, and the durability of membranes—especially in harsh environments- have therefore become the central focus of research on ceramic membranes for oil-water separation14–17.

Other membrane technologies, such as reverse osmosis (RO), are also frequently used in treating produced water, especially when total dissolved solids (TDS) levels are high18,19. That said, the main fouling mechanisms in RO systems are distinct from those in MF/UF systems, in which osmotic effects, concentration polarization, and scaling are more influential, whereas pore blocking and surface deposition are less dominant20. Consequently, the current framework is centered on MF/UF-type systems, where the decline in flux is mainly driven by fouling from particulates and emulsions. Adapting this approach to RO systems would require adding further transport and physicochemical parameters that account for high-pressure and high-salinity environments.

Advances in computer science have enabled the use of numerical simulation methods, such as computational fluid dynamics (CFD), Monte Carlo (MC) simulations, and artificial neural networks (ANNs), for modeling membrane fouling21–25. While these tools can capture specific scenarios more richly than classical theory, they have struggled to generalize across studies due to heterogeneous materials, operating conditions, and oil chemistry. Yang et al. emphasized that, despite the critical need to understand fouling mechanisms, fouling characteristics, and fouling control measures, few studies have been published that provide an overview of fouling modeling, particularly for numerical simulation of fouling21. This gap is even wider for oil–in–water applications.

Recent reviews have highlighted the increasing use of machine learning in membrane fouling prediction and desalination processes, while also emphasizing challenges related to model selection, descriptor definition, and data consistency across studies3,16,26. While simulations can describe specific operating conditions, their ability to generalize across studies is limited by variations in membrane materials, operating parameters, and feed compositions reported in the literature. In this context, the present work focuses on analyzing this lack of transferability by combining a reduced-order finite-element model (ROM) with a data-driven analysis of experimental datasets. Rather than developing a new predictive model in isolation, the approach examines how experimental metadata relates to flux-decline behavior across heterogeneous studies.

The framework quantifies between-study heterogeneity as a primary limitation for predictive modeling and evaluates how experimental metadata can be used to reconstruct flux-decline behavior across heterogeneous datasets. The results show that predictive performance is primarily constrained by metadata completeness rather than model capacity, highlighting the need for more consistent reporting to enable transferable fouling models. Within the scope of this study, the framework provides a reproducible approach to analyzing time-dependent fouling behavior across independent studies and directly supports global clean water initiatives, such as the United Nations Sustainable Development Goal 6. This work provides (i) a transferable physics-informed learning framework, (ii) a quantitative measure of cross-study generalization loss, and (iii) a minimal metadata standard required for reproducible fouling prediction.

A parallel analysis was conducted to evaluate the extent to which normalized flux trajectories encode information about experimental metadata. Features were extracted from the curves using two complementary representations: convolution-based features (MiniRocket)29 and physically interpretable shape descriptors (e.g., AUCs, segment slopes, and variability). Model performance was assessed using each representation individually and in combination to determine how different feature types capture information relevant to the underlying process conditions.

Two combination strategies were evaluated. In the first, feature vectors were concatenated after their respective preprocessing steps. In the second, a logarithmic transformation was applied to the concatenated features to account for skewed distributions. Model performance for numeric targets was quantified using mean absolute error (MAE), while categorical targets (membrane material and oil type) were evaluated using macro-F1 and accuracy. Results are summarized in Fig. 3a-c, respectively.

Under publication-level GroupKFold, shape-based features consistently outperformed convolution-based features (MiniRocket) for categorical targets. For membrane material, shape features achieved a macro-F1 of 0.41 (accuracy = 0.49), compared to 0.08 for MiniRocket (Fig. 3c). For oil type, macro-F1 reached 0.20 (accuracy = 0.54) for shape features versus 0.07 for MiniRocket (Fig. 3b). Combining feature sets increased accuracy (0.50 for membrane material and 0.63 for oil type) but reduced macro-F1, indicating sensitivity to class imbalance and uneven representation across studies.

For numerical targets, the recoverable signal was limited but measurable. Applying a logarithmic transformation improved prediction accuracy, with the most reliable estimates obtained for crossflow, clean-water flux (Jwh), oil concentration, pressure, and porosity. In contrast, pore size and salt concentration were more difficult to infer quantitatively from the flux trajectories, indicating weaker regression performance.

When analysed from a classification and time-localized perspective, however, certain variables such as crossflow velocity and pore size exhibited consistent and detectable signals within specific time segments of the flux trajectory (Fig. 4a–c). This indicates that, while these variables may be difficult to recover accurately in a global regression sense, they still leave identifiable signatures in the temporal structure of the flux decline.

The normalized flux trajectory was divided into six equal segments (S1–S6) along the time axis. Segment S1 corresponds to the initial phase immediately after the onset of filtration, when flux remains close to its initial value and changes are minimal. As a result, S1 provides limited discriminatory information and is not emphasized in the analysis, whereas subsequent segments (S2–S6) capture the evolution of fouling dynamics across the early, transition, and late stages. Segments S2–S3 represent early-stage fouling, S4 corresponds to transition behavior, and S5–S6 capture late-stage resistance stabilization.

Time-window permutation analysis revealed that the information content is not uniformly distributed along the normalized time axis. Crossflow was most influential in early segments (S2), whereas porosity and clean-water permeance contributed more strongly to the late segments (S5–S6). Pore size showed peak importance in the mid-time region (S4), while oil type exhibited comparatively weaker and more diffuse contributions (Fig. 4d). These temporal patterns are consistent with the expected roles of operating conditions and membrane properties during different stages of fouling. This temporal decomposition provides a direct link between statistical importance and physically interpretable fouling stages. The observed importance patterns were stable across repeated permutations, indicating that the temporal trends are robust with respect to random shuffling variability. The relative importance across segments (S2–S6) highlights that different physical variables dominate distinct fouling stages, reinforcing the multistage interpretation of flux decline dynamics.

Classification of membrane material and oil type directly from normalized flux curves using shape features yielded a macro-F1 score of 0.196 [95% CI: 0.119–0.262] for membrane material (9 classes), exceeding both majority and uniform baseline models. In contrast, classification performance for oil type was close to baseline levels, indicating limited discriminative information in the flux trajectories for this variable.

Models were trained to map experimental metadata to curve descriptors derived from normalized flux trajectories, using publication-grouped cross-validation to prevent cross-study leakage. A PCA basis fitted to the normalized curves captured approximately 99.3% of the total variance, indicating that the flux trajectories can be represented in a low-dimensional space. This suggests that prediction errors are more likely to be associated with estimating the corresponding coefficients from metadata rather than with limitations in the curve representation itself30–32.

The regression model was evaluated using two complementary validation schemes (Fig. 5a). Grouped cross-validation represents within-study prediction, in which training and test samples originate from the same experimental dataset. In contrast, publication hold-out evaluation excludes entire studies from training and therefore reflects true generalization across independent experimental conditions and therefore represent out-of-distribution predictions.

This clustering behavior confirms the temporal relationships identified by the permutation importance analysis: early-stage factors, such as crossflow and pressure, define the initial phase, while late-stage factors, such as porosity and clean-water permeance, dictate the late-term behavior. The proposed model was further validated by laboratory experiments, yielding high agreement (R2 = 0.98; s. Figure 8). The experimental validation, however, is limited to dead-end conditions and therefore does not capture shear-induced removal mechanisms typical of crossflow systems. These experiments therefore provide validation of the model structure under simplified conditions, while highlighting the need for further validation under crossflow regimes.

Three main findings can be highlighted. First, normalized flux trajectories are intrinsically low-dimensional and can be accurately reconstructed using reduced-order representations. Second, predictive performance degrades significantly under publication-level hold-out, demonstrating that cross-study variability and incomplete metadata are the primary limitations for generalization. Finally, the temporal analysis reveals that different physical parameters govern distinct stages of fouling, with structural descriptors (e.g., porosity) dominating late behavior, while operational parameters (e.g., crossflow) influence early-stage dynamics. These findings indicate that the main barrier to predictive modeling in membrane fouling is not model complexity; rather, cross-study variability and limited metadata availability constrain predictive performance. This performance degradation is referred to here as a cross-study generalization penalty, arising from heterogeneous experimental reporting.

Discussion

The reduced-order framework achieved high agreement across the compiled dataset, with an average R² of approximately 0.95. The performance of the hybrid formulation can be attributed to its structural flexibility, which combines early exponential decay with late-time algebraic relaxation and an optional nonzero asymptote.

This functional form is consistent with multistage fouling behavior, in which an initially rapid and partially reversible decline transitions into slower, history-dependent growth of resistance. Such behavior aligns with pore-blocking and cake-formation mechanisms described in classical fouling models, including Hermia-type frameworks and fractional relaxation approaches, where early-time responses resemble exponential decay and long-time behavior follows algebraic trends 34–36. The inclusion of an asymptotic resistance further captures the residual irreversible contribution typically captured in resistance-in-series descriptions.

It is worth noting that the compiled datasets span a range of fouling regimes that may not be equally well represented by the hybrid functional form. Systems dominated by biofouling may exhibit lag–growth behavior, while scaling processes can involve induction periods followed by accelerated deposition. These dynamics may deviate from the monotonic decay patterns captured by the present formulation, potentially leading to reduced agreement in specific cases. Such deviations reflect additional physicochemical processes not explicitly represented in the reduced-order description and highlight the limitations of applying a single functional form across heterogeneous datasets.

The observed associations between early-time AUC metrics and both terminal flux level and half-time further support the multistage interpretation of fouling dynamics. Systems exhibiting a steeper initial decline tend to sustain lower long-term normalized flux, indicating coupling between early deposition kinetics and subsequent resistance buildup. This behaviour is consistent with classical blocking and cake-growth mechanisms in membrane filtration.

Several of the extracted descriptors (e.g., AUC, initial slope, half-time, and terminal flux) are derived from the same fitted flux trajectory and are therefore not statistically independent. As a result, some of the observed correlations among these features reflect inherent mathematical coupling rather than independent physical relationships. In contrast, correlations between these descriptors and external experimental variables (e.g., pore size, pressure, or crossflow conditions) provide more meaningful insight into the underlying transport and fouling mechanisms. This distinction is important when interpreting the correlation structure, as it separates intrinsic curve properties from physically driven associations.

The correlations observed between curve descriptors and external experimental variables provide insight into the dominant mechanisms governing fouling dynamics. For example, the association between larger pore sizes and higher terminal flux or longer characteristic times (t₅₀) suggests reduced susceptibility to rapid pore blocking, consistent with a transition toward cake-dominated filtration regimes 36. Similarly, the influence of operating pressure on early-time descriptors reflects its role in accelerating initial deposition rates, which may enhance pore blocking or compaction effects depending on the system.

Crossflow-related trends, when present, are consistent with shear-mediated mitigation of fouling, particularly in early stages where particle transport and deposition are most dynamic3,37. While these relationships do not provide direct predictive rules due to cross-study variability, they indicate that the extracted descriptors capture physically meaningful signatures of transport and fouling behaviour. As such, they can support comparative analysis across systems and guide the interpretation of fouling dynamics under varying membrane properties and operating conditions. These trends are consistent with established fouling theory but should be interpreted qualitatively due to cross-study variability.

Despite the descriptive accuracy of the reduced-order fits, the mapping between the fitted parameters () and experimental metadata did not reveal stable monotonic relationships. Screening across pressure, pore size, droplet size, oil concentration, crossflow velocity, porosity, and clean water permeance showed that no single parameter could consistently explain the fitted coefficients. This absence of clear univariate trends suggests that fouling dynamics within the compiled dataset are governed by multivariate interactions rather than isolated experimental factors.

Consequently, although the reduced-order model accurately reconstructs individual flux trajectories, it does not directly generalize to unseen conditions without recalibration. The fitted coefficients, therefore, function primarily as descriptive representations of observed behavior, rather than predictive descriptors directly linked to explicit processing variables.

The comparative analysis further indicates that normalized flux trajectories encode measurable information about certain operational and structural parameters. Shape-based descriptors consistently outperformed convolution-based representations under publication-level validation, suggesting that physically interpretable curve features capture more robust signals across heterogeneous studies. In contrast, MiniRocket29 features appear more sensitive to distribution shifts between publications.

The greater recoverability of the membrane material relative to the oil type reflects differences in how structural and chemical descriptors are expressed in flux decline behavior. Membrane material and porosity directly influence hydraulic resistance and permeance, leading to persistent signatures across both early and late stages of filtration. In contrast, the effects of oil chemistry depend strongly on interfacial properties, such as droplet size distribution, surfactant content, and oil–membrane affinity, which were inconsistently reported across studies. As a result, oil type alone acts as an incomplete surrogate for the underlying physicochemical interactions governing fouling dynamics.

Time-window permutation analysis further revealed distinct temporal patterns in how different parameters influence the flux trajectory. Crossflow exhibited its strongest influence in the early stage (S2), indicating that the initial flux decline is highly sensitive to shear conditions36. Pressure showed a bimodal contribution (S3 and S6), consistent with both an early acceleration of fouling and a later influence on the terminal flux. In contrast, porosity was most influential in the late stage (S6), reflecting its control over steady-state resistance once the fouling layer is established 38. Pore-size peaks in the mid-time regime (S4), highlighting the transition phase in which pore-scale mechanisms interact with forming cake layers. Clean-water permeance (Jwh) is late-weighted (S5–S6), matching its influence on the asymptote/throughput after fouling sets in. Finally, the oil type is weak overall, with only a small mid-time signal (S4), suggesting that chemistry leaves a modest, diffuse signature in the trajectory. These results highlight that the interpretability of flux trajectories is inherently limited by the level of detail in the reported experimental descriptors.

The influence of membrane material and oil type on the “process identity” encoded in the flux trajectory was analyzed by classifying both targets directly from normalized curves using shape-based features. For the membrane material (9 classes), the classifier outperformed baseline models, achieving a macro-F1 score of 0.196 [95% CI: 0.119–0.262] and an accuracy of 0.188 [95% CI: 0.116–0.259], compared to a majority baseline of approximately 0.053 and a uniform random baseline of 0.120. In contrast, classification performance for oil type was close to baseline levels, indicating limited discriminative information in the flux trajectories for this variable.

It is worth noting that this difference reflects how structural and chemical descriptors manifest in fouling behavior. Membrane material and porosity directly influence hydraulic resistance and permeance, producing consistent signatures across the flux trajectory. In contrast, oil chemistry depends on interfacial properties, such as droplet size distribution, surfactant content, and oil–membrane affinity, which were sparsely and inconsistently reported across studies. As a result, oil type alone acts as an incomplete surrogate for the underlying physicochemical interactions governing fouling dynamics. These findings indicate that, while flux trajectories encode information related to membrane structure and permeability 39–42, their ability to resolve oil-specific mechanisms is fundamentally constrained by cross-study variability and incomplete metadata, rather than by limitations of the modeling approach. This further supports the interpretation that structural descriptors leave more persistent and recoverable signatures in flux decline than chemical descriptors under heterogeneous reporting conditions.

This interpretation is subject to several limitations, including inconsistent publication identifiers (which precluded strict GroupKFold validation), class imbalance, and inherent label noise. In particular, the “material” label often conflates base polymer type with surface modifications (e.g., coatings, grafting, or fillers), which likely contributes to the observed reduction in macro-F1 performance. These factors indicate that incorporating richer and more consistently reported metadata, as well as increasing sample size, would improve model robustness and classification performance.

The high variance retention achieved by PCA (99.3%) indicates that normalized flux trajectories are intrinsically low-dimensional. This suggests that the observed behavior can be effectively represented in a compact latent space, and that residual reconstruction errors are primarily due to estimating the corresponding coefficients from metadata rather than to limitations in the representation itself 30–32. Consequently, the degradation in predictive performance under cross-study conditions is more likely driven by incomplete or heterogeneous metadata than by insufficient model expressiveness. This distinction is critical for interpreting predictive performance in heterogeneous datasets, as it shifts the focus from model development toward data quality and reporting practices.

The approximately twofold increase in MAE under publication hold-out highlights the sensitivity of the model to cross-study heterogeneity. While grouped cross-validation evaluates interpolation within comparable experimental settings, publication hold-out probes generalization across differing membrane materials, operating ranges, and reporting practices. The observed performance degradation, therefore, reflects domain shift rather than overfitting.

Given the low-dimensional representation of the flux trajectories established earlier, this degradation is unlikely to arise from limitations in the modeling framework. Instead, it indicates that incomplete or inconsistently reported metadata restricts the model’s ability to infer the correct mapping under unseen conditions. In this context, cross-publication variability emerges as a primary constraint on predictive transferability.

The calibration results further show that, within the bounds of available metadata, the regression models provide quantitatively consistent predictions. The widening of uncertainty bands during periods of rapid flux decline suggests coherent uncertainty propagation rather than random dispersion, supporting the interpretability of the predicted trajectories.

The reduction in NRMSE with increasing metadata dimensionality highlights the importance of descriptor richness. The plateau observed at approximately 10–12 parameters suggests that a minimum set of physical descriptors is required to constrain flux-decline dynamics. Below this threshold, under-specified models struggle to capture the multivariate interactions governing fouling.

Feature-importance analysis identifies porosity as the dominant structural determinant of curve reconstruction, consistent with its role in setting baseline hydraulic resistance and influencing late-stage behavior. Crossflow and pore size follow as influential parameters, while droplet size, salt concentration, and clean-water flux exhibit secondary but systematic effects. Oil concentration and clean-water flux primarily contribute to early- and mid-stage dynamics, whereas crossflow exerts its strongest influence in the initial phase. The consistency between global importance metrics and SHAP-based directional analysis supports a physically coherent hierarchy of parameter influence. These rankings quantify influence on predictive reconstruction rather than direct mechanistic causation. The remaining degradation in publication hold-out performance further highlights the limitations imposed by incomplete metadata, as key parameters influencing fouling kinetics are not consistently reported across studies.

Collectively, these results indicate that predictive performance is primarily constrained by metadata completeness and cross-study variability rather than by limitations of the modeling framework. This finding underscores the need for more consistent and comprehensive reporting practices and motivates the standardized metadata checklist proposed in this work to improve reproducibility and cross-study transferability in membrane fouling modeling.

The remaining limitations in cross-study generalization are expected to decrease with the inclusion of more descriptive and consistently reported metadata (e.g., emulsifier chemistry, temperature, and pre-treatment history) and with larger, more diverse datasets. The observed collinearity between porosity, pore size, and clean water permeance reflects their shared physical basis in governing intrinsic membrane permeability. As a result, once porosity and pore size are included, clean water permeance contributes a limited additional predictive signal. This explains its reduced relative importance in SHAP analysis despite its well-established experimental relevance.

Alternative representations of the flux trajectories using image-based encodings (GAF/MTF) were also explored. These representations preserve temporal correlations and provide a qualitative view of the flux-decay patterns, capturing differences in early-stage decline and long-term behavior. Incorporating these features into the combined model improved agreement with experimental data, particularly for targets sensitive to subtle changes in curve shape. However, these improvements did not change the main conclusions, which remain governed by metadata availability and cross-study variability.

The temporal alignment between clustering behavior and permutation importance reinforces the mechanistic coherence of the framework. Early-time dominance of crossflow and pressure reflects their influence on incipient deposition and shear-mediated removal, whereas porosity and clean-water permeance exert stronger control over late-stage resistance stabilization. The agreement between statistical feature importance and physically interpretable parameters indicates that the learned representations capture structured transport dynamics rather than arbitrary correlations.

Experimental validation further shows that the reduced-order model reproduces flux-decline trends under controlled laboratory conditions. The strong agreement observed across distinct tubular geometries suggests that the reduced-order representation retains sufficient physical structure to remain consistent under moderate variations in configuration.

From an operational perspective, these results highlight the limitations of predictive fouling models when applied across heterogeneous datasets. They emphasize that reliable model-based analysis depends strongly on the availability and consistency of experimental metadata. This type of predictive capability is consistent with previous work on fouling-informed process optimization.

The key outcome of this study is outlined in Fig. 8. The proposed model reproduces experimental flux-decline trends across different membrane geometries (Fig. 8a), while the accompanying metadata framework (Fig. 8b) defines a minimal set of descriptors required for reproducible and comparable modeling. These include intrinsic membrane properties (e.g., porosity, pore size), feed characteristics (e.g., oil concentration, type), and operational variables (e.g., crossflow velocity, pressure), which together provide the basis for transferable predictions of fouling behavior.

Although demonstrated here for oil–water membrane filtration, the proposed framework may be applicable to other transport-limited systems exhibiting time-dependent growth in resistance, provided that sufficiently detailed and consistent datasets are available. Its broader applicability is therefore expected to depend on the availability and quality of experimental metadata across studies. From an engineering perspective, these results provide practical insight into the limitations of predictive fouling models across studies. They show that model performance is strongly constrained by the availability and consistency of experimental descriptors, rather than by model formulation alone. This has direct implications for experimental design, data reporting, and the reliability of model-based analysis in membrane processes. These results indicate that reliable fouling prediction for process optimization is fundamentally limited by the availability of metadata, implying that improvements in data reporting may yield greater gains than increasing model complexity.

Methods

The conceptual framework integrates physical modeling, reduced-order abstraction, and machine learning, as illustrated in Fig. 9:

Figure 9 illustrates the overall conceptual workflow of the proposed framework. This schematic is intended to provide a high-level overview rather than a detailed mathematical formulation. The process is organized into three main stages. In the first stage (physical modelling), the system is defined through operating conditions, including feed streams, crossflow, and boundary conditions. In the second stage (reduced-order model abstraction), the governing transport processes (advection, diffusion, and Darcy-based flow) are used to construct a simplified representation of the system, from which key descriptors of flux decline are extracted and reduced using techniques such as principal component analysis (PCA). In the final stage (machine learning integration), these descriptors, together with experimental metadata, are used to train models for prediction and classification tasks. The workflow enables reconstruction of flux trajectories and prediction of fouling behaviour under unseen conditions. The schematic elements in the lower part of the figure illustrate the transformation from the physical system representation to reduced descriptors and finally to predicted flux curves.

A database was assembled from peer-reviewed studies on oil–water separation by membranes 43–53, published from 1998 to 2025. The compiled dataset consists of 112 standardized flux–time curves gathered from ten separate published studies on oil–water membrane filtration. Inorganic (ceramic) microfiltration and ultrafiltration membranes make up most of the data, which aligns with their common application in produced-water treatment and their frequent use in longer-term, high-pressure experiments documented in the literature. Polymeric membranes are underrepresented, mainly because of scarce high-resolution flux-time data and insufficiently detailed operational and material information needed for cross-study comparisons. Consequently, the current framework is assessed chiefly within the scope of inorganic membrane systems, and the influence of material type is directly addressed during the discussion. The size and composition of the dataset reflect the current state of the literature, where consistent time-resolved flux data with complete metadata remain limited.

For each experiment, operating conditions (transmembrane pressure, crossflow velocity, temperature, salt concentration), feed descriptors (oil type/chemistry, oil concentration, droplet size), and membrane properties (material, geometry, porosity, nominal pore size), together with performance metrics (permeate flux, oil rejection), were extracted and incorporated in meta-variables set. All records were de-duplicated, units were harmonized (e.g., pressure in bar, flux in LMH), categorical fields were standardized (controlled vocabularies for oil type and membrane material), and water flux values () were collected to enable water-normalized analysis (given by the flux ratio ). To ensure data integrity, any missing or unclear data points were flagged and removed from the database. Reduced-order finite element method (ROM) in python

A Python workflow was implemented to analyze fouling dynamics over time across all experiments. The geometries were drawn using SolidWorks according to the described conditions, including channel width/height, fiber radius, length, and other geometry details 43–53. To prevent over-parameterization, principal component analysis (PCA) was applied to the geometric descriptors. This process identified and retained a compact set of orthogonal components that captured the variation most relevant to flux. These reduced features were incorporated into our low-dimensional model, improving computational efficiency while preserving the critical information needed for accurate simulation.

The objective of the reduced-order model is to provide a physics-consistent, low-dimensional representation of established membrane transport and fouling concepts that can be used across heterogeneous datasets in literature. In this sense, the formulation is grounded in classical advection–diffusion transport, Darcy-type permeation, resistance-in-series reasoning, and the balance between foulant deposition and hydrodynamic removal, as widely discussed in the membrane fouling literature2,37. The novelty lies not in the governing equations themselves, but in their integration into a reduced-order, transferable representation compatible with heterogeneous datasets from the literature.

Detailed interfacial phenomena, such as oil–membrane affinity, interfacial tension, and surfactant-mediated interactions, are not explicitly resolved in the ROM, as the corresponding parameters (e.g., contact angle, droplet size distributions, surface energies) are reported sparsely and inconsistently across the literature.

Instead, their aggregate influence is implicitly reflected through the observed flux-time trajectories and associated operating metadata. In this sense, the ROM provides a physics-informed but deliberately reduced representation that constrains the learning problem while enabling transferable analysis across heterogeneous experimental datasets. Parameter bounds were constrained to physically admissible ranges to avoid non-identifiable solutions and overfitting of empirical functional forms.

Several assumptions were carried. First, it is assumed that the emulsion is a diluted water-oil emulsion with high oil rejection typical of MF/UF produced-water filtration. It is also assumed that bulk transport is governed by advection-diffusion, while permeate flow through the membrane wall is described by Darcy’s Law 54,55. Fouling was represented through a time-dependent resistance term, , that accounts in reduced form for the combined effects of pore blocking and surface deposition, together with shear-mediated removal under crossflow conditions. Machine learning time series regression

The objective of the data-driven component is not to develop a new machine learning methodology, but to evaluate how experimental metadata relates to flux-decline behavior across heterogeneous studies and to quantify the limits of predictive transferability. The literature dataset was separated into two components: (i) a metadata table detailing experimental conditions and membrane characteristics, and (ii) a curve table containing flux–time extracted from the cited studies.

For the metadata prediction analysis, following data integration, each flux curve was pre-processed to ensure consistency and comparability across datasets (Fig. 1). This included enforcing monotonic behavior through a cumulative minimum transformation and normalizing the flux by its initial value to obtain a scale-independent representation62,63. Each permeate flux curve was resampled to a fixed length \:L=60 on a normalized time axis [0,1] and transformed into a normalized curve . A row-wise cumulative minimum filter was then applied to enforce the physically expected non-increasing trend. From each normalized curve, a set of physically interpretable descriptors was extracted and used as targets for the machine learning models. For visualization, compact curve summaries and predictive features, the standardized curve matrix was compressed via PCA. The PCA basis retained more than 99% of the variance, providing compact latent codes for each curve suitable for efficient subsequent modeling. Metadata included transmembrane pressure, pore size, porosity, crossflow, droplet and solute concentrations, clean-water flux, membrane material, and oil type. For continuous parameters with varying scales (e.g., transmembrane pressure, crossflow velocity, porosity), the data were discretized into three quantile bins, thereby transforming the task into a multi-class classification problem to improve model stability. Model selection and performance are evaluated using a GroupKFold cross-validation strategy65,66, with publication as the grouping variable. All transformers are fitted exclusively on the training folds. Performance is quantified using out-of-fold predictions, specifically macro-F1 and accuracy. Time-window permutation importance was also computed by permuting one-sixth segments (S1.S6) of the normalized curve to pinpoint when in time the most informative data reside, measuring the drop in macro-F1 as a metric of importance67,68. The permutation procedure was repeated across multiple random shuffling realizations to ensure that the observed importance patterns were consistent and not driven by a single permutation instance.

The supervised model maps from experimental metadata to fouling curves using three regressors: RidgeCV69, Kernel Ridge (RBF)70, and Random Forest71. To stabilize targets, a Yeo–Johnson transform was fitted on the training fold and inverted at prediction, and the outputs were clipped to physical ranges72,73. The predicted descriptors were converted back into full flux curves using a hybrid function that combines a smooth early-time exponential decay with a late-time logistic relaxation. For the model evaluation, due to the publication-level hold-out evaluation, the classical R2 metric becomes unstable under distribution shift and can be negative even with moderate absolute errors. The segment-wise mean absolute error (MAE) is consequently reported as the primary metric, and cross-validation is included for completeness. To prevent leakage across publications, GroupKFold was applied with publication as the grouping variable, fitting all preprocessing transformers (standardization, PCA) and the regressor only on the training folds74. To assess early and late segments, time-weighted losses were also evaluated, along with tail-only metrics (the last 40% of time). An optional ensemble of the three regressors is tuned to the chosen metric (early- or late-weighted) and used to evaluate if it improves robustness. Additionally, to understand the global feature importance for the curve prediction from metadata, a Shap global SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) analysis was carried out75,76.

To predict discrete metadata directly from the time series while avoiding leakage, two complementary feature sets are extracted from the normalized curves. Convolution-based features (MiniRocket)29 were extracted to capture temporal patterns in the flux trajectories. These features were then simplified and compressed to maintain efficiency using TruncatedSVD77,78 before standardization. The second set included shape statistics, including measurements of the curve’s profile, such as the total area under the curve (AUC), the steepness (slope) across early, mid, and late sections, and measures of global variability. A logistic-regression classifier was trained and evaluated with GroupKFold by publication, and performance was reported as macro-F1 and accuracy on out-of-fold predictions. To localize informative time regions, time-window permutation importance was applied, shuffling one-sixth segments (S1…S6 ) and measuring the drop in macro-F1.

In addition to scalar descriptors, image-based representations of the normalized curves (Gramian Angular Fields and Markov Transition Fields) were explored to capture temporal patterns33. These features were used as auxiliary inputs and slightly improved prediction accuracy without altering the main conclusions. Specifically, Gramian Angular Fields were applied to capture late-range shape and phase dependencies, and Markov Transition Fields (MTF) to model short-range transition dynamics. Before encoding, the curves were z-normalized, in which fitting was restricted to the training fold. Each curve was then mapped to a fixed-size 64 × 64 image. When used as features, these images were compressed using TruncatedSVD77 to a small set of components (16–32) before concatenation with the metadata. All encoders and reducers were fitted inside the training fold to avoid leakage. These pyts images served not only to visualize curve structures that are hard to see in 1D but also as auxiliary features that modestly improved robustness by including global shape information that local metrics (like slopes or AUCs) failed to capture.

All models were implemented in Python 3.11 using scikit-learn 1.x. Random seeds were fixed to ensure reproducibility.

Conclusion

A hybrid workflow combining a reduced-order 2D finite element model (ROM) with a data-driven mapping approach was developed to analyze flux decline during membrane oil–water separation. The ROM showed high agreement with individual experiments, achieving an average of 0.95. PCA demonstrated that normalized flux trajectories are intrinsically low-dimensional, with more than 99% of the variance retained. This indicates that prediction errors arise primarily from estimating model coefficients from experimental metadata rather than from limitations in the curve representation itself.

Under grouped cross-validation, the model achieved segment-wise MAEs of approximately 0.08 (early) and 0.14 (mid and late). These errors increased significantly under publication hold-out, demonstrating that cross-study heterogeneity, rather than model expressiveness, is the primary limitation on predictive performance.

Feature importance and temporal analyses provided physically consistent insights: crossflow dominates early-stage behavior, pressure influences both early- and late-stage behavior, porosity and clean-water permeance govern long-term response, and pore size contributes to the mid-time regime. In addition, flux trajectories encoded more information about membrane structure than about oil chemistry, reflecting the limited and heterogeneous reporting of interfacial descriptors across studies.

These results show that predictive performance is primarily constrained by metadata completeness and cross-study variability rather than by the modeling framework itself. The proposed approach provides a structured way to analyze fouling behavior, combining reduced-order physical constraints with data-driven analysis to improve interpretability and assess transferability across heterogeneous datasets.

Although demonstrated here for membrane fouling, the framework may be applicable to other transport-limited systems exhibiting time-dependent growth in resistance, provided that sufficiently detailed and consistent datasets are available. Its broader applicability, therefore, depends on the availability and quality of experimental metadata across studies.

More broadly, this work demonstrates how combining reduced-order physical constraints with data-driven analysis can improve the interpretability and transferability of fouling models across heterogeneous studies. The results emphasize that consistent, sufficiently detailed metadata are essential for reliable predictive modeling, shifting the focus of fouling prediction from model development toward data standardization as the primary enabler of transferable, engineering-relevant predictions.
