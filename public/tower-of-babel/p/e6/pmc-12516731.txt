ABSTRACT
Neglected tropical diseases, such as human African trypanosomiasis (HAT), require novel treatments that can populate the clinical pipeline in the event of rising resistance and treatment recrudescence. Following a high-throughput screen of almost 42,000 human kinase inhibitor chemotypes, we identified a 2,4-disubstituted azaindole that had good antiparasitic activity, selectivity, and predicted brain exposure, but that failed to meet our criteria for advancement into an efficacy study. Following hit-to-lead optimization, we arrived at 18a, which offered the best combination of potency, properties, and pharmacokinetic parameters, and, while not curative, this work leads to opportunities for continued optimization for HAT and cutaneous leishmaniasis.

Introduction

The World Health Organization (WHO) has defined 20 neglected tropical diseases (NTDs) that include a variety of parasitic, bacterial, viral, and fungal diseases, which, in 2019, affected 1.74 billion people. Analysis of the global burden of NTDs estimates that the incident cases and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) in 2019 due to NTDs were 57.99 million and 13.18 million, respectively. While the figures for 2025 are not yet available, ongoing efforts and interventions are in place with the goal of reducing DALYs associated with these diseases.

Human African trypanosomiasis (HAT) is endemic in sub-Saharan Africa. There are 65 million people at risk for HAT, and infection can cause death if left untreated. , In 2021 and 2020, there were 802 (747 attributed toT. b. gambiense, responsible for a chronic infection) and 837 (799 attributed to T. b. gambiense) new cases reported, respectively, though the actual case numbers are likely higher with poor healthcare infrastructure, indistinguishable symptoms, and coinfections with other diseases believed to contribute to underreporting. HAT is caused by an infection with the protozoan parasite Trypanosoma brucei. The disease infects the host in two stages, hemolymphatic (Stage I) and meningoencephalitic (Stage II), which introduces treatment challenges. Currently, there are no vaccines available for the treatment of HAT, and the disease is managed through vector control and chemotherapeutics. Fexinidazole was approved by the European Medicines Administration, and Acoziborole successfully completed Phase III trials. , While this represents a significant improvement to the treatment options for HAT, there is still a need to ensure that additional options are progressing through the drug discovery pipeline in case of clinical failure or the development of resistance.

The drug discovery process is expensive and time-consuming, and the field of NTDs has historically been resource-constrained. We employed a “lead repurposing” strategy, leveraging conserved target families shared between humans and parasites to identify chemical matter suitable for initiating a hit-to-lead optimization campaign. The highly conserved kinome of kinetoplastids, previously explored by our group, served as a productive starting point for medicinal chemistry efforts. − In collaboration with GlaxoSmithKline, we previously identified 797 potent and selective hits from a high-throughput screen of a 42,000 compound human kinase-targeted library against T. brucei. One of the promising chemotypes was a class of 2,4-disubstituted azaindoles, represented by NEU-1200; the targeted properties and profile are listed in Table . These properties were proposed based on the well-established target product profile (TPP) for HAT, enabling focused drug design and synthesis. The TPP indicates that the first priority is to develop a safe and effective treatment that can be used for Stage I and II HAT (caused by T. b. gambiense at a minimum), with good selectivity, and dosed orally, or via intramuscular injection.

Compound Properties and Targeted Values for NEU-1200

Compound NEU-1200 was originally disclosed as part of an effort to truncate GSK1070916, a human Aurora B kinase inhibitor. Aurora B is involved in cytokinesis and leads to polyploidy in cells. When NEU-1200 was described, it failed to lead to polyploidy, which led the authors to hypothesize that removal of the phenylurea led to a reduction in kinase selectivity. Medina et al. hypothesized that the introduction of a 5-chlorine on the central scaffold could fill space in the binding pocket, which restored the Aurora B activity. Our approach differed in that we sought to introduce bulk off the 6-position to modulate selectivity, while also seeking to improve solubility (increasing sp3 character and decreasing LogD), and metabolic stability (through modification of metabolic liabilities predicted by MetaSite). Ultimately, our efforts led to the identification of 18a, which had improved in vitro metabolic clearance and in vivo pharmacokinetic (PK) profile. Further, we describe the structure–activity (SAR) and structure–property relationships (SPR) for this chemotype. Finally, we have applied a “parasite hopping” approach and assessed compounds against two other trypanosomatid parasites: Trypanosoma cruzi and Leishmania major.

Results and Discussion

The starting compound, NEU-1200 (Table ), showed good overall properties with excellent aqueous solubility, good lipophilic ligand efficiency (LLE), and blood–brain barrier (BBB) score, which were predictive of CNS exposure, necessary to treat stage 2 HAT. Other compounds (S1–S13) identified in the original screen are tabulated in the Supporting Information (Table S1). Given that NEU-1200 met nearly all our stated lead-like compound criteria, we first assessed it in a mouse PK study at 5 mg/kg following intraperitoneal administration in female NMRI mice (Table and Figure ). However, it was metabolically unstable and found to be rapidly cleared (t 1/2 = 1.2 h) with exposure below the limit of quantification at 4 h for all but one mouse.

Truncated matched pair analogs (Table ) were synthesized by choosing similar substituents at C-2 and C-4 from other confirmed hits (Supporting Information, Table S1) in the original screen. Truncation at either position resulted in a complete loss of potency against T. brucei, which indicates that both C-2 and C-4 substitutions are necessary to maintain antiparasitic activity (Table ).

The N-demethylated analog of NEU-1200, 15b (Table ), was synthesized as a potential metabolite, and while potency was maintained it displayed higher microsomal clearance (64 μL/min/mg protein vs 26 μL/min/mg protein for NEU-1200) and similar intrinsic clearance in hepatocytes, indicating that it is unlikely to be the main driver of the lack of observed in vivo stability. The methyl groups at the 3-position of the pyrazole (15a and 15c) were tolerated in terms of potency with little or no change in clearance compared to NEU-1200. Replacement of the pyrazole with the phenyl methyl sulfone (15d) resulted in a slight reduction in potency and loss of aqueous solubility, though the LLE was acceptable, and there was a moderate improvement in metabolic clearance. However, replacement with sulfonamides (15e and 15f) and phenyl (15g) led to a reduction in potency, LLE, and aqueous solubility. Replacement with pyridines (15h–15j) led to the recovery of solubility but no improvement in terms of potency, LLE, or clearance values.

MetaSite predicted the methyl groups on the tertiary nitrogen and the methylene carbon as potential sites of metabolism (Figure a). Removal of one of the methyl groups to form a secondary amine (18d, Table ) was well tolerated from a potency perspective; however, there was little impact on the intrinsic clearance in either microsomes or hepatocytes. Removal of the methylene carbon (15k) led to drastically increased clearance, though this was associated with a 10-fold loss in potency and abrogation of aqueous solubility. The attempt to block metabolism at the methylene of 18a with a methyl group (15m) resulted in little impact on the intrinsic clearance. Compounds 15q and 15r were synthesized as potential oxidative metabolites of 1; only 15r maintained potent activity against T. brucei (still a half-log lower than NEU-1200).

We explored the replacement of the dimethyl amine with a range of aliphatic amines. The piperazine (15n, Table ) or piperidine (18b) did result in a boost in potency but led to a ∼ 20-fold reduction in solubility. Though compounds 17, 18d, and 18i resulted in an improvement in potency, metabolic clearance, and aqueous solubility. There was some loss of selectivity compared to NEU-1200; however, it was within the desired range (>100-fold selectivity). Extension of the methyl groups to the ethyl (15l) resulted in similar potency and clearance while the bulkier isopropylamine (18f) resulted in a compound with excellent potency and metabolic stability, though it was toxic against host cells. Further, replacing the acyclic disubstituted amine with a pyrrolidine ring provided 18a with improved overall physicochemical and ADME properties and >1000-fold selectivity.

Given the superior intrinsic clearance profile of 18f, coupled with its potent activity against T. brucei, we selected this substituent to progress into the synthesis of matched pair analogs with varied C4 substituents (Table ).

The analysis of potency, selectivity, physicochemical, and ADME properties of compounds generally showed that most of the cross-over analogs maintained or improved clearance relative to 18f. Replacement of the methyl on the pyrazole with a tetrahydropyran (15w) or piperidine (20) further improved the intrinsic clearance for both human liver microsomes and rat hepatocytes, but potency was reduced. Changing the attachment position of the pyrazole to the 5-position (15x) reduced the potency 1.5 log units compared to 18f, though the clearance rates were still acceptable. Replacement of the pyrazole with an unsaturated piperidine (23) resulted in a compound with excellent intrinsic clearance, but there was a 2-log unit reduction in potency as compared to 18f. Other matched pair analogs with pyridine, sulfonamide, and methyl sulfone groups either resulted in a reduction in potency or did not provide an improved selectivity window compared to 18f.

The target candidate profile has identified the need for compounds to be trypanocidal and, preferably, fast-acting. Compounds with pEC99 > 6.0 (the concentration that inhibits 99% of parasite growth recovery when compared with DMSO control) at 18 h of incubation, followed by a wash-out step, are considered cidal (Supporting Information, Table S2). Of those compounds tested, all were deemed to be trypanocidal with the exception of 19. Compounds are deemed fast-acting if they reach a pEC50 > 6.0 by18 h of incubation. All analogs tested from this series were found to be fast-acting (Supporting Information, Figure S1).

Based upon the overall profile of 18a (Table ), which had excellent potency, selectivity index, in vitro ADME, and physicochemical properties, it was progressed to a PK study in female NMRI mice at a dose of 10 mg/kg following intraperitoneal administration. PK parameters, including C max, t max, t 1/2, and AUC, are summarized in Supporting Information, Table S4, and Figure . We aimed to achieve plasma drug levels of >10 × EC50 for 4–6 h. The average C max of 18a in plasma was found to be 520 ng/mL (1.5 μM), and this concentration is approximately 75-fold higher than the EC50 of 18a (0.02 μM), and our goal of >10 × EC50 plasma concentration of 18a was achieved for >5 h. Further, there was a slight increase in the half-life of 18a (t 1/2 = 2 h). Significantly, the BBB score (4) for 18a predicted that it would cross the BBB; therefore, the drug levels were measured in the brain at 0.5 and 4 h. As predicted, exposure levels exceeded 10 × EC50 for the time points measured. The brain/blood ratios of 18a are summarized in Supporting Information, Table S5.

The PPB for both compounds was measured in human plasma, and there was 20% free for 1 and 11% free for 18a. These data are unavailable in mouse plasma, and due to species differences, an adjusted EC50 cannot be calculated. However, based upon the plasma and brain exposure, 18a was progressed to an acute efficacy study in female NMRI mice infected with T. b. brucei STIB795 parasites. Following a highest tolerated dose (HTD) study, which revealed signs of toxicity at 50 mg/kg, and considering the PK profile, we advanced this compound into the acute efficacy study at 10 mg/kg/day following intraperitoneal administration (Figure ). Though no cure was observed, mice treated with 18a showed an increase in survival rate with an average life extension of 8.8 days compared to the control group (Supporting Information, Figure S3). We note that one mouse in the 18a-treated group died during the treatment period; however, this was not due to the toxicity of 18a, but rather due to a failure of the parasitemia to respond to treatment (Supporting Information, Table S6). This may have been caused by issues with administration or absorption of 18a, or other factors that were not identified in the PK study.

Given that substituted azaindoles are known human kinase inhibitors, and the origin of the hits from this screen are from a kinase-targeted library we profiled 15i and 18a against a panel of 45 human kinases at 5 μM. Both compounds were found to potently inhibit several kinases (defined as > 50% inhibition; Supporting Information Figure S2). Compound 18a potently inhibited 14 kinases, while 15i inhibited 31. This highlights that optimization of kinase selectivity can be achieved in this series, though there is still additional work required to improve the kinome selectivity of 18a.

Finally, we have previously demonstrated the value of leveraging our chemical matter against multiple pathogens by cross-screening. , In this context, we screened selected compounds against T. cruzi (causative agent of Chagas disease) and L. major (one of the causative agents of cutaneous leishmaniasis), and the results are summarized in the Supporting Information Table S3. For Chagas disease and leishmaniasis, a hit is defined as having EC50 < 5 μM (ideally EC50 < 1 μM) against the intracellular parasite. Compounds from this chemotype had host cell toxicity against L6 cells, which meant that they were not suitable for advancement for Chagas disease, given the poor selectivity over the host cell. Screening the compounds against L. major intracellular amastigotes revealed 8 compounds with low nanomolar activity, 11 compounds with submicromolar activity, and 4 compounds with micromolar activity, along with desired selectivity over the macrophage host (>10-fold). This presents an opportunity to further explore this chemotype as an antileishmanial agent, though it is important to acknowledge that the L6 activity of these compounds could indicate off-target toxicity that needs to be addressed in any optimization campaign. However, the lack of in vivo toxicity at 10 mg/kg in NMRI mice suggests that this is not a problem for the series as a whole.

Conclusion

A lead repurposing approach was utilized, and a rational design and synthesis campaign was run to explore a cluster of 2,4-substituted azaindoles with the aim of improving the metabolic stability that could be used to demonstrate proof-of-concept efficacy in a murine model of HAT. This work resulted in the development of SAR and SPR and led to the identification of 18a with enhanced plasma exposure (>10 × EC50 for 4–6 h), good brain exposure necessary to treat Stage 2 HAT, and notable improvements in the metabolic stability in mice. Based upon the in vitro and in vivo PK, 18a progressed to an efficacy study where it showed an average life extension of 8.8 days compared to the control group.

While the observed efficacy was encouraging, we acknowledge several factors that may have contributed to the limited systemic exposure of 18a in mice, including potential mouse-specific metabolism or plasma instability. Although active efflux or poor absorption appear less likelygiven the compound’s ability to cross the blood–brain barrierthese possibilities were not fully explored, as detailed ADME studies (e.g., obtaining species-matched mouse liver microsomal or hepatocyte clearance) were not conducted due to resource constraints. However, given the modest efficacy observed and the likely need for significant optimization, this chemotype has been deprioritized in favor of more promising series.

Experimental Section

Cell and Whole-Organism Assay Protocols

Strains and Media

Bloodstream T. brucei brucei Lister 427 was cultured in Hirumi’s modified Iscove’s medium (HMI-9), supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS, at 37 °C, and 5% CO2 in a T-25 vented flask (Corning). The MRC5-SV2 cell line (SV40-transformed human lung fibroblast cell line) was cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in a T-75 vented flask (Corning). The T. cruzi Tulahuen C4 strain, expressing the β-galactosidase gene (LacZ) and L6 rat skeletal muscle cells, used as host cells, was cultured in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% iFBS, 2 mM l-glutamine, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Leishmania donovani MHOM/ET/67/HU3 cells with the luciferase gene integrated into the parasite genome were grown at 28 °C in RPMI 1640-modified medium (Invitrogen) supplemented with 20% iFBS and 100 mg/mL of hygromycin B. The human myelomonocytic cell line THP-1 was grown at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% iFBS, 2 mM glutamate, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 mg/mL streptomycin.

Preparation of Compound plates

For dose–response experiments, compound plates were prepared for each analogue by serial 3-fold dilutions in 100% DMSO. Five concentration points (mammalian cytotoxicity) or 10 concentration points (parasite growth inhibition) were made in 96-well transparent Nunclon plates. Pentamidine was routinely included in compound plates as internal quality control, and plates were stored sealed at −20 °C for no more than four weeks.

Determination of T. b. brucei pEC50

Midlog growing cultures of T. brucei bruceiwere dispensed at a cell density of 2500 cells per well in Nunclon 96-well transparent plates. Maximum concentration for compounds was 40 μM in 0.4% DMSO per well, and serial 3-fold dilutions were used for dose response curves.

Assay plates were incubated for 72 h at 37 °C and 5% CO2; after incubation, 20 μL of a 440 μM resazurin solution in prewarmed HMI-9 was added. Fluorescence was read in an Infinite F200 plate reader (Tecan) at 550 nm (excitation filter) and 590 nm (emission filter). A four-parameter equation was used to fit the dose–response curves, and determination of the EC50 as the concentration that reduces 50% viability compared to solvent-treated control was determined using SigmaPlot 14.5 software. Four μM pentamidine was used as 100% inhibition control. Assays were performed in triplicate at least twice.

Rate of Action assays

Midlog T. brucei brucei cultures were diluted to the required cell density, according to the different incubation time points described. Cultures (90 μL per well) were dispensed in the final assay Nunclon 96-well flat bottom solid white plates, and 10 μL of intermediate plates were added to each well, as described before. Four sets of assay plates were arranged to assay in order to be sequentially stopped at each indicated time point. The top and bottom rows were dismissed for the compound assay to reduce evaporation effects.

Plates were incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for the indicated time points; incubation was stopped by the addition of 10 μL of prewarmed Cell Titer Glo reagent (Promega), and after shaking, the plates were incubated at room temperature for 10 min to allow the signal to settle. Plate luminescence was read on an Infinite F200 plate reader (Tecan), and raw data were processed and analyzed as previously described. Assays were performed in triplicate at least twice.

Β-d-Galactosidase Transgenic T. cruzi Assay

A Thermo Scientific Multidrop Combi dispenser (MTX Lab Systems, Vienna, VA) was used to dispense 90 μL of T. cruzi amastigote–infected L6 cell culture (4 × 103 infected L6 cells per well) into 96-well Corning assay plates (Corning Inc., Corning, NY) already containing 10 μL of the compounds to be screened and controls. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 96 h. Then, 30 μL of 100 μM CPRG and 0.1% NP40 diluted in PBS were added to each well, and the plates were incubated for 4 h at 37 °C in the dark. Absorbance at 585 nm was measured in a Vmax kinetic microplate reader (Molecular Probes). Compound activities were normalized using the in-plate 100% inhibition (50 μM benznidazole) and 0% inhibition (0.2% DMSO) growth controls. Assays were performed in triplicate at least twice.

Cytotoxicity Assay in MRC5

Intermediate plates were made as described, adding 95 μL of DMEM complete media to 5 μL of compound per well, setting a 5% DMSO amount. Log-phase MRC5 cells were removed from a T-75 TC flask using TrypLE Express (Thermo) and dispersed by gentle pipetting. Cell density was adjusted to working concentration in prewarmed DMEM medium: 25,000 cells in 90 μL of culture were plated in 96-well transparent Nunclon plates and allowed to settle for 24 h at 37 °C and 5% CO2. After settling incubation, 10 μL of the freshly made intermediate plate was added per well: final maximal concentration for compounds was 50 μM in 0.5% DMSO per well. Plates were incubated for 48 h at 37 °C and 5% CO2. At 4 h prior to fluorescence measurement, 20 μL of 500 μM resazurin solution was added. Fluorescence was read in an Infinite F200 plate reader (Tecan) at 550 nm (excitation filter) and 590 nm (emission filter).

A four-parameter equation was used to fit the dose–response curves and determination of EC50 by SigmaPlot 13.0 software. Assays were performed in triplicate at least twice.

Resazurin-Based L6 Assay

One hundred microliters (100 μL) per well of culture medium containing the compounds and controls were added to L6 cells previously cultured (4 × 103 L6 cells per well). After 72 h at 37 °C, the medium was exchanged and the viable cell number was determined by resazurin (Sigma-Aldrich) reduction. 20 μL of resazurin (1.1 mg/mL) was added to each well and incubated in the dark for 2 h at 37 °C. Cell viability was estimated by measuring the final fluorescence at 570–590 nm in an Infinite F200 plate reader (Tecan). Assays were performed in triplicate at least twice.

Determination of L. major Amastigote Efficacy

The intracellular amastigote assay is a microtiter plate drug sensitivity assay that uses the activity of luciferase as a measure of proliferation of luciferase-expressing L. major parasites developing intracellularly inside RAW 264.7 macrophages in the presence of antileishmanial drugs or experimental compounds. As the activity of luciferase and its associated luminescence after addition of the substrate, luciferin, is relative to parasite growth, a test compound that inhibits the growth of the parasite will result in a lower luminescence. The luciferase-expressing L. major parasite used in this assay is genetically modified by adding the luciferase coding region to the pLEXSY-hyg2 vector (Jena Biosciences). The luciferase expression construct was created by digesting the luciferase coding region (1.66 kbp) of pGL3-Basic (Promega) by using two restriction enzymes, NcoI/EagI, followed by electrophoretic separation of the luciferase coding region on a 1% agarose gel. The luciferase coding region was then ligated into the pLEXSY-hyg2 vector (Jena Biosciences), which had previously been digested with NcoI/NotI. The vector was linearized with SwaI and subsequently gel-purified prior to transfection into L. major parasites. Transfections were carried out by electroporation at 480 V, 13 Ω, and 500 μF (0.4 mL of 1 × 108 parasites/mL, and 0.1 mL of 10 μg DNA). Selection for transfectants was then carried out using hygromycin B (100 μg/mL). RAW 264.7 macrophages were cultured in DMEM media supplemented with heat-inactivated 10% FBS. To begin the assay, macrophages were harvested from culture by removing all spent media, adding 10 mL of fresh media, scraping cells, and counting using Trypan Blue. The cells were resuspended in DMEM/10% HIFBS media at 2.0 × 105 cells/mL and then dispensed in a volume of 50 μL to yield a final concentration of 10,000 macrophages/well in 384-well tissue-culture-treated sterile white plates using a Tecan EVO Freedom robotics system. The plates were then incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO2 atmosphere for 24 h. After incubation, the media was removed from each well using the Tecan EVO Freedom robot, and L. major promastigotes were added to each well and allowed to invade the RAW macrophages. Promastigotes were cultured in Schneider’s medium supplemented with 20% heat-inactivated FBS. Metacyclic stage promastigotes provide the best invasion for this assay, and cultures should be grown to increase the percentage of metacyclic promastigotes for the best results. Promastigotes were harvested from culture, counted, and suspended at 2 × 106 promastigotes/mL in DMEM/HIFBS media, and 100,000 promastigotes were dispensed per well in a volume of 50 μL. After overnight incubation, the media were removed from each well using the Tecan Freedom EVO robot, and each well was subsequently washed three times with 40 μL of fresh DMEM/HIFBS medium to remove all extracellular promastigotes. After the third wash, 69.2 μL of DMEM/HIFBS medium was added to each well using the Tecan EVO Freedom robot. Drug plates were prepared with the Tecan EVO Freedom using sterile 96-well plates containing twelve duplicate 2-fold serial dilutions of each test compound suspended in DMSO. 7.8 μL of diluted test compound was added to the 69.2 μL of media present in each well, providing a 10-fold final dilution of compound. The final concentration range tested was 0.5–10,000 ng/mL for all assays. The plates were next incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 96 h. After 96 h of incubation, 7.5 μL of a luciferin solution (Caliper Life Science) diluted to 150 μg/mL was added to each well, and the plates were incubated for 30 min at 37 °C, in the dark. Each plate was read using an Infinite M200 plate reader. The 50% inhibitory concentrations (IC50s) were then generated for each dose response test using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA) using the nonlinear regression (sigmoidal dose–response/variable slope) equation.

Cytotoxicity Assay in HepG2 Cells

HepG2 cells were cultured in complete minimal essential medium (MEM) prepared by supplementing MEM with 0.19% sodium bicarbonate, 10% heat-inactivated FBS, 2 mM l-glutamine, 0.1 mM MEM nonessential amino acids, 0.009 mg/mL insulin, 1.76 mg/mL bovine serum albumin, 20 units/mL penicillin–streptomycin, and 0.05 mg/mL gentamycin. HepG2 cells cultured in complete MEM were first washed with 1× Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (Invitrogen #14175095), trypsinized using a 0.25% trypsin/EDTA solution, assessed for viability using trypan blue, and resuspended at 250,000 cells/mL. Using a Tecan EVO Freedom robot, 38.3 μL of cell suspension was added to each well of clear, cell culture-treated 384-well microtiter plates for a final concentration of 9570 liver cells per well and plated cells were incubated overnight in 5% CO2 at 37 °C. Drug plates were prepared with the Tecan EVO Freedom using sterile 96-well plates containing 12 duplicate 1.6-fold serial dilutions of each test compound suspended in DMSO. Diluted test compound (4.25 μL) was then added to 38.3 μL of media in each well, providing a 10-fold final dilution of compound. Compounds were tested in the range of 57–10,000 ng/mL for all assays. Mefloquine was used as a plate control for all assays with concentrations ranging from 113 to 20,000 ng/mL. After a 48 h incubation period, 8 μL of a 1.5 mg/mL solution of MTT diluted in complete MEM media was added to each well. All plates were subsequently incubated in the dark for 1 h at room temperature. After incubation, the media and drugs in each well were removed by shaking the plate over the sink, and the plates were left to dry in a fume hood for 15 min. Next, 30 μL of isopropanol acidified by the addition of HCl at a final concentration of 0.36% was added to dissolve the formazan dye crystals created by the reduction of MTT. The plates are put on a three-dimensional rotator for 15–30 min. Absorbance was determined in all wells using a Tecan iControl 1.6 Infinite plate reader. The 50% toxic concentrations (TC50) were then generated for each toxicity dose–response test using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA) by a nonlinear regression (sigmoidal dose–response/variable slope) equation.

ADME Experiment Protocols

Aqueous pH 7.4 Solubility

Compounds are dried down from 10 mM DMSO solutions using the centrifugal evaporation technique. Phosphate buffer (0.1 M pH 7.4) was added, and StirStix was inserted in the glass vials; shaking was then performed at a constant temperature of 25 °C for 20–24 h. This step is followed by double centrifugation with a tip wash in between to ensure that no residues of the dried compound are interfering. The solutions are diluted before analysis, and quantification using LC/MS/MS is performed.

log D 7.4

The shake-flask octanol–water distribution coefficient at pH 7.4 (log D 7.4) is determined. The aqueous solution used is a 10 mM sodium phosphate pH 7.4 buffer. The method has been validated for log D 7.4 ranging from −2 to 5.0.

Human Plasma Protein Binding

PPB is determined using equilibrium dialysis (RED device) to separate the free from the bound compound. The amount of compound in plasma (10 μM initial concentration) and in dialysis buffer (pH 7.4 phosphate buffer) is measured by LC–MS/MS after equilibration at 37 °C in a dialysis chamber. The fraction bound is reported.

Human Liver Microsomal Clint

In vitro intrinsic clearance is determined from human liver microsomes using a standard approach. Following incubation and preparation, the samples are analyzed using LC/MS/MS. Refined data are uploaded to IBIS and are displayed as Clint (intrinsic clearance) in μL/min/mg.

Rat Hepatocyte Clint

In vitro intrinsic clearance is determined from rat hepatocytes using a standard approach. Following incubation and preparation, the samples are analyzed using LC/MS/MS. Refined data are uploaded to IBIS and are displayed as Clint (intrinsic clearance) μL/min/1 million cells.

Calculated log P and log D Values

Both log P and log D predictions are based on a modified version of the method where the predicted partition coefficients are composed of the molecules’ atomic increments.

Pharmacokinetics Protocols

Animals and Ethical Statement

Studies were ethically reviewed and carried out in accordance with European Directive 2010/63/EEC and the GSK Policy on the Care, Welfare and Treatment of Animals. Compound was administered intraperitoneally (IP) to two groups of female NMRI mice (group 1 n = 3; group 2 n = 6) supplied by Charles River (Germany) Ltd. The compound was prepared in 1% (v/v) DMSO: 99% (v/v) 20% (w/v) sulfobutyl ether-beta-cyclodextrin (SBE-β-CD/Captisol, used as a solubilizing agent) in water, and the dosing volume was 10 mL/kg for a total dose of 10 mg/kg. Food and tap water were available ad libitum. Following IP dosing, group 1 blood samples were collected from the tail vein into capillary tubes containing K2EDTA at the following time-points: 0.0833, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 24 h.

In order to obtain simultaneous blood and brain samples, group 2 mice were placed under terminal anesthetic (isoflurane) and blood samples (0.3 mL) collected from the retro-orbital sinus into K2EDTA tubes at 0.5 h (n = 3) and 4 h (n = 3) after compound administration. Immediately following blood sample collection, death was confirmed by cervical dislocation, and the brain was removed. Aliquots of each blood sample were diluted in an equal volume of water. Mouse brain samples were weighed, and water was added at a 1/2 (w/v) ratio (brain/water) and then homogenized. Both blood and brain samples were stored −80 °C until analysis.

Diluted blood and brain homogenates were processed under standard liquid–liquid extraction procedures using CAN containing an internal standard (Nifedipine) and analyzed by LC–MS/MS. Noncompartmental analysis was performed using the Phoenix PK software version 1.4 (Certara), and C max, t max, AUClast, AUC, and t 1/2 were estimated.

Procedures were approved by the ethical committee of the Institute of Parasitology and Biomedicine Lopez-Neyra (Spanish National Research Council, CSIC), code MNC.2/2015. This institute has joined the Agreement on Transparency in Animal Experimentation, promoted by the Confederation of Scientific Societies of Spain (COSCE), with the collaboration of the European Association for Animal Research, released on September 20, 2016. Female NMRI mice (Charles River Laboratories) were provided with sterilized water and commercial pellets ad libitum and maintained under the standard conditions in a conventional room at 20–24 °C with a 12/12 h light/dark cycle. Compound 18a solution for treatment was resuspended in vehicle: 5% DMSO in 20% Captisol sulfobutyl ether β-cyclodextrin that improves solubility and stability for drug dosing.

Highest Tolerated Dose

Prior to the efficacy model, the HTD was determined. The HTD was assessed by dosing two female NMRI mice i.p. with 18a every 2 h with increasing doses starting at 5 mg/kg up to a maximum of 50 mg/kg, or starting at 50 mg/kg up to a maximum of 150 mg/kg. The mice were observed for symptoms of toxic and subtoxic activity and evaluated according to the Irving Test. As soon as symptoms of toxicity were observed, the administration of the drug was stopped and the cumulative dose was recorded. Subsequently, the mice were observed for 24 h to perceive potential delayed adverse effects, and necropsy was performed post assay.

Blood-Stage Efficacy Model

Infection was performed by i.p. injection of 104 bloodstream forms in 0.2 mL TDB glucose of T. brucei brucei (STIB795) from a cryopreserved stock. Three days later, nine infected animals with confirmed parasitemia were divided into two groups: control (infected mice treated with vehicle, n = 4) and treated (infected mice treated with 10 mg/kg/day of 18a, QD for 5 days). For treatment, both control and 18a were heated at 50 °C for 10 min to solubilize. Control and drug-treated mice received a 0.2 mL i.p. injection on the third day after infection for 5 consecutive days. Parasitemia was individually checked by direct microscopic counting of parasites in a Neubauer chamber using 2 μL of blood from the infected mice’s tail, diluted in 100 μL of TDB glucose. Parasitemia of all mice was periodically checked by tail blood examination up to day 31st. The day of death was recorded.
