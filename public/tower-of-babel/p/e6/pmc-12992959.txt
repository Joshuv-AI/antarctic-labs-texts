Development of a customizable vascularization strategy using a modular microfluidic platform for fibroblast-free vascularized tissue
Authors: Geonho Jin, YoungWon Koo, Jongwoo Ahn, Dohyung Kim, Seonghun Mun, Minkyeong Jeong, Jiyoung Song, Hong Nam Kim, Sunghan Lee, Youngwon Kim, Jaehun Lee, Jiseok Lim, Bongseop Kwak, Seokyoung Bang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn vascularized microphysiological systems, co-culture of fibroblasts and endothelial cells is commonly employed to induce capillary network formation through angiogenesis. However, when recapitulating organs such as brain or pancreas, which contain few or no fibroblasts, conventional fibroblast-dependent co-culture poses a limitation to physiological relevance. Here, we developed a modular microfluidic platform that allows reversible connection and disconnection of fibroblast and endothelial cell modules. When the modules were connected, fibroblast-induced angiogenesis of endothelial cells was promoted, and upon disconnection, a vascularized module composed solely of endothelial cells for fibroblast-free capillary network was prepared and able to be maintained for a certain period. We analyzed vascular morphogenesis under different conditions of fibroblast concentration and co-culture duration. We also confirmed that the preformed capillary network remained stable for up to 2 days after module disconnection. Furthermore, upon module reconnection after 2-day of disconnection, angiogenic activity was reactivated through the reestablished co-culture. This approach overcomes the limitations of conventional co-culture methods, enabling the application to organ-specific fibroblast-free vascularization conditions, and provides a foundation for investigating the interaction between preformed capillary networks and other tissues or organs.Keywords: Microfluidics, Modulation, Fibroblasts, Angiogenesis, Microphysiological system

Introduction

Vascular system plays a central role in supporting physiological homeostasis through the regulation of metabolic exchange and intercellular signaling across various tissues and organs [1]. To recapitulate these physiological functions in vitro, three-dimensional (3D) vascular microphysiological systems (vMPSs) have been widely utilized in drug screening, disease modeling, and tissue engineering. Accordingly, extensive research is underway to develop precisely controllable vascular systems under in vitro conditions [2]. 3D vMPSs encompass a wide range of types depending on the intended application and fabrication strategies [3,4]. Among them, vascular networks formed by seeding endothelial cells on hydrogels followed by inward sprouting are particularly notable for their structural relevance, closely resembling in vivo morphology and reproducing human capillary diameters down to 6 μm [5]. In addition, these 3D vMPSs exhibit favorable physiological characteristics, including low permeability and high expression of junctional proteins [6].

However, successful vascularization in these 3D vMPSs typically requires co-culture with supporting cell types such as fibroblasts or mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), which can secrete angiogenic factors [7,8]. Since certain human tissues, such as brain or pancreas, contain no or only locally distributed fibroblasts [9,10], systems capable of selectively removing fibroblasts after initial vascularization are required to accurately recapitulate such microenvironments of those tissues. To address this issue, Song et al. introduced fibroblasts engineered with an inducible caspase-9 (iCasp9) gene, allowing for the selective elimination of the cells via chemical induction with chemical inducer of dimerization (CID) after their contribution to early vessel formation. Their findings demonstrated that vascular networks can remain functional even after the fibroblast removal, suggesting the feasibility of maintaining vascular structures in the absence of fibroblasts [11]. This approach provides insights into the role of fibroblasts and potential alternatives but requires a complex genetic engineering process and incurs costs for the use of expensive chemical inducers.

To overcome such constraints, modular microfluidic platforms (MMPs) have emerged as a promising alternative [12,13]. MMPs function as a versatile system, enabling adaptation to diverse experimental objectives through modular configuration [14]. Modules in the platforms can be independently cultured and manipulated prior to integration, and upon assembly, allow precise control of microfluidic flow and intercellular interactions [15]. Kim et al., for instance, developed an MMP in which different tissue modules were cultured separately and later integrated, establishing a foundation for inter-tissue communication [[16], [17], [18]]. Such systems provide precise spatiotemporal modulation by allowing the physical decoupling and reassembly of modules at defined time points. If this technology allows for controlled integration and separation of fibroblast-containing modules, it would enable the induction of vascularization to a desired extent, followed by application to fibroblast-free yet vascularized microenvironments.

In this study, therefore, we investigated fibroblast-dependent angiogenesis by employing a spatiotemporally controllable MMP with an endothelial cell-containing module (HUVEC module) and a fibroblast-containing module (LF module). Angiogenesis in HUVEC module was quantitatively evaluated under variation of fibroblast presence or concentration in LF module and the co-culture duration. At defined time points, LF module was detached to establish a fibroblast-free condition, and the vascular stability of HUVEC module was subsequently assessed. Thereafter, HUVEC module was reconnected to LF module to evaluate the restoration of angiogenic responsiveness. Through this approach, we demonstrate that our platform serves as a foundational technology for temporally analyzing and controlling fibroblast-dependent vascular formation and its maintenance following fibroblast removal. This study may provide a foundation for future advances in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine.

Materials & methods

Fabrication of the modular device

Modular devices were fabricated using conventional soft lithography. A master mold was prepared by patterning SU-8100 photoresist (Kayaku Advanced Materials, USA) on a 4-inch silicon wafer. Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS, SYLGARD™ 184; Dow, USA) was mixed at a 10:1 (w/w) ratio of base to curing agent, degassed, poured onto the mold, and cured in a dry oven at 80 °C for 1 h. After curing, the PDMS replica was peeled off from the mold, and individual modules were separated along predefined boundaries using a surgical blade. Reservoirs were created by punching 2 mm and 6 mm holes with biopsy punches (KAI Industries, Japan). Each module was permanently bonded to two parallel glass coverslips (22 × 40 mm; Marienfeld, Germany) via O2 plasma treatment (CUTE, Femto Science, Republic of Korea). To eliminate the temporary hydrophilicity induced by plasma activation, the bonded assemblies were stored in a dry oven at 80 °C for at least 48 h. The interface between the two contacting coverslips was gently pried open to expose the PDMS region, where a shallow incision was introduced using a utility blade to define a controlled fracture initiation point. Subsequently, lateral force was applied to manually cleave the PDMS along this predefined incision, separating the assembly into two modules (Supplementary Video). For connecting two modules, a holder, tailored to the specifications of coverslip, was fabricated using stereolithography (SLA) 3D printing with high resolution (Form 3; Formlabs, USA) and black resin. Prior to experiments, both the holder and modules were sterilized by UV exposure for 30 min. This holder enabled stable positioning and reversible parallel connection of the two modules, allowing spatiotemporal control of intermodular communication during experiments.

Cell culture

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs; Lonza, Switzerland) and human brain microvascular endothelial cells (hBMECs; Cell Systems, WA, USA) were cultured in Endothelial Growth Medium-2 (EGM-2; Lonza, Switzerland). HUVECs at passages 4–5 and hBMECs at passage 8 were used for all experiments. Human lung fibroblasts (LFs; Lonza, Switzerland) were cultured in Fibroblast Growth Medium-2 (FGM-2; Lonza, Switzerland), and passages 5–6 were used. Human astrocytes (HA; ScienCell Research Laboratories, USA) were cultured in Astrocyte Medium (ScienCell Research Laboratories, USA), and cells at passage 7 were used for experiments. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 and used at approximately 80% confluency. For passaging, cells were detached using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Gibco, USA), centrifuged at 169×g for 3 min, and resuspended in their respective culture media.

3D angiogenesis assay

For efficient induction of vascularization, a fibrin hydrogel-based cell seeding method previously reported in the literature was employed [5]. Briefly, bovine fibrinogen (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was dissolved in Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS; Welgene, Republic of Korea) at 10 mg/mL (final concentration of 2.5 mg/mL are used). The fibrinogen solution was mixed with aprotinin (final concentration of 0.15 U/mL; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) at a 25:4 vol ratio to prevent fibrinolysis. Harvested LFs were suspended in this fibrinogen solution at final concentrations of either 8 × 106 or 4 × 106 cells/mL. Thrombin (final concentration of 0.5 U/mL; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was added at a 50:1 vol ratio, and the mixture was immediately loaded into the LF module. The mixture was stably confined within the hydrogel channels by the restored hydrophobicity of the PDMS surfaces and a capillary burst–mediated pinning effect at the micro-pillar interfaces, which prevented gel leakage into adjacent medium channels [19,20] (Supplementary Fig. S1 and Supplementary Video). Gelation proceeded for 5 min at room temperature, followed by medium filling via vacuum-assisted loading. In parallel, acellular fibrin gel was prepared by mixing the fibrinogen solution with thrombin in the same ratio and loaded into the HUVEC module. After 5 min of gelation at room temperature, the module was filled with medium by vacuum-assisted loading. On the following day, HUVECs were harvested, resuspended in EGM-2 medium at 5 × 106 cells/mL, and introduced into the HUVEC module containing the preformed acellular fibrin gel. After cell seeding, the module was immediately tilted at 90° and incubated for 30 min to promote HUVEC attachment on the fibrin gel surface. After attachment, both modules were placed onto a custom-designed 3D printed holder and assembled together (Supplementary Fig. S2). The assembled modules were maintained at 37 °C and 5% CO2 with daily media exchange. During the media change, a higher EGM-2 level was intentionally supplied to the reservoir on the LF module side, while a lower level was maintained on the HUVEC module side. This setup promoted directional flow across the fibroblast-laden and acellular fibrin gels, facilitating transient delivery of angiogenic factors toward the HUVECs.

To recapitulate a BBB microenvironment, HUVECs were replaced with human brain microvascular endothelial cells (hBMECs), and vascularization was achieved by co-culturing with the LF module for 5 days under the same conditions as the HUVEC experiments. Human astrocytes were harvested and resuspended at a final concentration of 5 × 106 cells/mL. After LF module was disconnected, astrocytes were seeded onto the surface of the fibrin gel in the hBMEC module. Then, hBMEC module was immediately tilted at 90° and incubated for 30 min to allow attachment to the other side of the vascularized endothelium. Following astrocyte attachment, Astrocyte Medium was supplied to the astrocyte-facing reservoir, while EGM-2 was independently supplied to the opposite reservoir corresponding to the endothelial compartment. This dual-medium culture condition was maintained to support cell-type-specific requirements and to establish a BBB-like cellular interface.

Immunofluorescence staining and imaging

To analyze vascular networks and identify lung fibroblasts, immunofluorescence staining for CD31 and Vimentin was performed. Samples were washed twice with DPBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; Biosesang, Republic of Korea) for 30 min at room temperature. After fixation, samples were washed twice with DPBS. The blocking solution was prepared by dissolving 1% (wt/vol) bovine serum albumin (BSA; MP Biomedicals, USA), 2% (vol/vol) fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA), and 0.2% (vol/vol) Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) in DPBS, and applied to the samples for 1 h at room temperature. For CD31 staining, the primary antibody (CD31 Monoclonal Antibody [JC/70A], #MA5-13188; Invitrogen, USA), diluted 1:200 in blocking solution, was applied (150 μL per channel) and incubated overnight at 4 °C. For Vimentin staining, the primary antibody (Anti-Vimentin antibody [EPR3776], #ab92547, Abcam, UK), diluted 1:150 in blocking solution, was applied (150 μL per channel) and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After washing, the secondary antibody for CD31 (Goat anti-Mouse IgG [H + L] Alexa Fluor™ Plus 488, #A32723; Invitrogen, USA), diluted 1:250 in blocking solution, was applied (150 μL per channel) and incubated overnight at 4 °C. For Vimentin, the secondary antibody (Goat anti-Rabbit IgG [H + L] Alexa Fluor™ 594, #A-11012, Invitrogen, USA), diluted 1:500 in blocking solution, was applied (150 μL per channel) and incubated for 2 h at room temperature. For astrocyte staining, a directly conjugated primary antibody against GFAP (GFAP [E4L7M] Rabbit Monoclonal Antibody, Alexa Fluor® 488 Conjugate, #94242; Cell Signaling Technology, USA) was diluted 1:100 in blocking solution, applied to the channels (150 μL per channel), and incubated overnight at 4 °C. For nuclear staining, Hoechst 33342 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) diluted 1:2000 in DPBS was perfused into the channels and incubated for 1 h at room temperature, followed by a 30-min DPBS wash. Fluorescence images were acquired using a confocal microscope (K1-Fluo, Nanoscope Systems, Republic of Korea).

Live/dead cell assay

Hoechst/ethidium homodimer (EthD-1)/calcein-AM (HEC) staining was performed to evaluate LF viability. The staining solution contained 1 μg/mL Hoechst 33342 (Invitrogen™, USA), 4 μM EthD-1 (ABP Biosciences, USA), and 2 μM calcein-AM (Invitrogen™, USA) in DPBS. Cells were treated with the HEC solution and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 30 min. Following the live/dead assay, z-projection images were acquired using a confocal microscope.

Quantification of vascular morphology

To quantitatively analyze the vascular morphology, z-projected images of stained HUVEC samples were acquired using a confocal microscope. Defined ROI were set within the vascularized zones, and the area of CD31-positive structures within each ROI was measured using ImageJ software (NIH, USA) measurement tools. These measurements were used to calculate vascular density (percentage of CD31-positive area within the total ROI) and vascular coverage along defined distances from the module boundary. For branching analysis, the z-projected images were analyzed using AngioTool (National Cancer Institute, NIH, USA) to quantify the number of branching nodes (junctions) within each ROI.

For section-based comparisons (e.g., Figs. 2d and. 3d), two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons post-test was conducted to assess interaction and group-wise differences. For all other comparisons, ordinary one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-test was used. Results and discussion

Modular design for controlled vascularization and fibroblast-free microenvironments

The modular microfluidic platform (MMP) consisted of two independent modules—a lung fibroblast (LF) module and a human umbilical vein endothelial cell (HUVEC) module—both were capable of stable culture and analysis in either connected or disconnected configurations (Fig. 1a). Following the connection of the two modules to verify fluidic behavior through the central channel, unidirectional flow was observed from the higher-level blue solution toward the lower-level red solution (Fig. 1b). This observation suggests that, when the LF and HUVEC modules are connected and co-cultured, angiogenic factors secreted by LF can diffuse through the central channel into the HUVEC module, thereby inducing angiogenesis (Fig. 1c). To recapitulate this configuration, LFs and HUVECs were first seeded into their respective modules, followed by medium loading (Fig. 1d–i). LFs were embedded within the fibrin gel, while HUVECs were attached to the acellular fibrin gel surface. Each module contained an independent medium reservoir and channel, enabling separate culture prior to connection. After cell loading and attachment, the two modules were connected using a 3D-printed holder (Fig. 1d–ii). Upon connection, the medium channels of both modules became aligned, forming a continuous fluidic pathway through which angiogenic factors such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) secreted from LF could diffuse toward the HUVEC module (Fig. 1d–iii). Subsequently, when the modules were disconnected and cultured separately (Fig. 1d–iv), the preformed vascular structures within the HUVEC module remained stable (Fig. 1d–v). Collectively, an MMP-based culture system was well-established to maintain a stable capillary network under fibroblast-free conditions.

A 3D-printed holder was fabricated to enable precise connection and disconnection of modules. The holder was manufactured using stereolithography (SLA) 3D printing, which provides a smoother surface and higher dimensional accuracy than fused deposition modeling (FDM), allowing for precise alignment between modules [21]. However, since the resolution of SLA printing does not reach the micrometer-scale precision achievable by photolithography-based micro-patterning, polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) devices fabricated via soft lithography were employed for the formation of microchannels within each module. In addition, structural integrity was maintained while the holders were assembled; however, the individual components often underwent hygroscopic deformation when detached, particularly due to the humidity in the incubation environment (also due to internal irregularities caused by nonuniform polymerization during the SLA curing process). This deformation could reduce assembly precision and disrupt the fluidic interconnection between modules. To overcome this issue, the original holders were replaced with new ones for the reconnection step after disconnection, thereby preventing structural deviation and ensuring consistent alignment during experiments.

The transport of angiogenic factors from the LF module to the HUVEC module occurred via two mechanisms: (i) diffusion through the connected fluidic channel, and (ii) interstitial flow induced by a controlled height difference between the two medium reservoirs positioned on both sides of the HUVEC compartment [22]. The latter mechanism recapitulates in vivo interstitial flow driven by hydrostatic pressure gradients between the vascular and extravascular tissues, thereby providing a more physiologically relevant environment for angiogenesis [[22], [23], [24], [25]].

In this study, transient co-culture with fibroblasts was chosen over the direct administration of angiogenic factors as the strategy to form fibroblast-free capillary networks. Fibroblasts are known to secrete a variety of angiogenic factors, including vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) [26], basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF) [27], cytokines such as angiopoietin-1, angiogenin, hepatocyte growth factor (HGF) [28,29], and interleukin-8 (IL-8) [30]. These factors act synergistically to regulate angiogenesis in HUVECs [31]. For instance, in the study by Eckermann et al., the combined treatment of VEGF and bFGF significantly increased the CD31-positive vascular area compared to the single treatment of VEGF [32]. This finding supports that co-culture with fibroblasts, which secrete various angiogenic factors simultaneously, is more effective for inducing angiogenesis than supplementing a single factor such as VEGF. Moreover, this approach differs fundamentally from the use of fibroblast-conditioned medium (CM). In CM-based methods, angiogenic factors gradually degrade or lose biological activity over time. Variations in concentration and functionality may also arise during collection, storage, and reinjection. Additionally, CM is typically derived from fibroblasts cultured under 2D conditions, which fail to fully recapitulate the 3D-specific gene expression and growth factor secretion patterns of fibroblasts. In contrast, real-time co-culture allows HUVECs to continuously receive fresh and dynamically regulated angiogenic cues from LFs at physiologically relevant concentrations, thereby more accurately recapitulating in vivo tissue microenvironments. Consequently, real-time co-culture is considered superior to CM-based approaches in promoting vascular network formation and stabilization. Consistent with previous microfluidic studies that induced endothelial angiogenesis through fibroblast co-culture systems, our study also adopted the co-culture approach within the MMP. To further optimize the fibroblast-free vascularized tissue system and validate its function, the LF module was co-cultured with the HUVEC module under different fibroblast concentrations, connection durations, and disconnection time points. These experiments were performed to quantitatively evaluate the effects of each condition on vascular formation and maintenance within the MMP.

Optimization of LF concentration for HUVEC angiogenesis

Since the concentration of LFs regulates the secretion of angiogenic factors and influences vascular formation in HUVECs, LF modules were loaded with different LF concentrations (0, 4, and 8 × 106 cells/mL) and connected to HUVEC modules for seven days to evaluate angiogenic outcomes. Immunofluorescence staining for CD31 revealed that vascular formation in the HUVEC module increased markedly with higher LF concentrations (Fig. 2a). In particular, HUVEC module connected to LF module with LF concentration of 8 million (M) cells per mL exhibited the most pronounced enhancement in both vessel formation and sprouting, characterized by thicker and longer vascular structures. These features were further confirmed through 3D reconstruction of confocal z-stack images (Fig. 2b), where lumen-like hollow structures were clearly observed in XZ cross-sections. Quantitative analysis of CD31-positive regions demonstrated a significant increase in total vascular density within the HUVEC module as LF concentration increased, consistent with the visual observations (Fig. 2c). Analysis of vascular coverage across defined distances from the module boundary showed that under the 8 M condition, vessels extended to the farthest regions of the HUVEC module (Fig. 2d, Supplementary Fig. S1). In particular, at the 480 μm region—where minimal vascular structures were detected under the 0 M condition—a similar statistical trend to the results of total vascular density was shown (Fig. 2e). Furthermore, quantification of branching nodes revealed that the highest network complexity occurred under the 8 M condition (Fig. 2f). Given that in vivo capillaries possess highly interconnected 3D architectures [33], the vascular network formed under the 8 M condition most closely recapitulates physiological capillary morphology. These results collectively demonstrate that higher LF concentrations promote more active angiogenesis within the HUVEC module, likely due to increased levels of LF-secreted angiogenic factors. Meanwhile, LFs exhibited stable growth within the module regardless of initial seeding density (Supplementary Fig. S2).

This finding is consistent with previous microfluidic studies reporting that LF concentrations around 7.5 × 106 cells/mL effectively induce stable angiogenesis under similar chip-based culture conditions [5]. Because most angiogenic factors also act as mediators of vascular permeability, an inflammatory response, excessive fibroblast concentrations may cause undesirable effects such as excessive extracellular matrix degradation, nutrient depletion, and disturbance of angiogenic gradients [[34], [35], [36], [37]]. Therefore, additional experiments are needed to examine the influence of higher fibroblast concentrations on endothelial viability and vascular integrity within the MMP.

Analysis of angiogenic progression in HUVEC module

As shown in Fig. 2, the 8 M cells/mL LF condition exhibited the most active sprouting. Therefore, this concentration was selected to analyze the temporal progression of vascular sprouting and structural maturation within the HUVEC module after connection. Consistent with Fig. 2, CD31 immunostaining was used to evaluate vascular morphology (Fig. 3a). At Day 1, short and thin protrusions were observed without discernible lumen formation. By Day 3, sprouting length increased, accompanied by partial branching and a denser vascular pattern. At Day 5, the vessels thickened and began to form interconnected networks, and by Day 7, a complex vascular network with well-defined lumens had formed. Examination in the opposite micro-pillar regions revealed that at Day 5, lumen structures were not yet clearly observed (Fig. 3b–i), whereas by Day 7, distinct lumen morphology was evident in the same region (Fig. 3b–ii). Quantitative analysis showed a significant increase in total vascular area throughout the 7-day culture period (Fig. 3c), accompanied by a corresponding increase in mean vessel length (Fig. 3d and e). Furthermore, analysis of branching nodes showed the highest values at Day 7 (Fig. 3f). These findings indicate that angiogenic factors secreted from LF continuously diffused through the fluidic channel, promoting sustained angiogenesis within the HUVEC module throughout the 7-day culture period.

The MMP developed in this study is a detachable system, which has a structural difference—additional reservoirs for independent culture—from conventional monolithic microfluidic platforms in which LFs and HUVECs are co-cultured within a single chamber [5]. Due to the independent reservoirs, there is a possibility that fibroblast-secreted growth factors may leak out to the other reservoirs, potentially reducing the delivery efficiency of the growth factors to HUVEC. Nevertheless, the temporal progression of angiogenesis observed in this study closely recapitulates that reported in previous monolithic devices, confirming that the MMP functions effectively as an integrated vascularization system. However, further validation of vascular functionality will be necessary to confirm the continuity and perfusability of the formed lumen structures.

Analysis of vascular structure in HUVEC module after disconnection

Fibroblasts play a key role in supporting endothelial cells by supplying growth factors essential for both the formation and maintenance of vascular structures. Previous studies have reported that in the absence of supporting cells such as fibroblasts, endothelial networks fail to form or remain stable [38,39]. Similarly, in the present platform, the disconnection of the LF module from the HUVEC module could potentially compromise the stability of the vascular structures due to the absence of continuous angiogenic support. To investigate this, the modules were disconnected after being co-cultured for seven days for which a mature 3D capillary network were established. Subsequently, the vascular morphology within the HUVEC module was monitored over 1, 2, and 3 days after disconnection (DAD 1, 2, 3) (Fig. 4a). Up to two days after disconnection (DAD 1 – DAD 2), no notable morphological changes were observed in the vascular network. At DAD 3, however, although the lumen structures remained intact (filled white arrows), distinct morphological signs of vascular regression appeared, including rounded and retracted tip cells (empty white arrows) (Fig. 4b). Quantitative analyses of vascular density (Fig. 4c), vascular length (Fig. 4d), and branching node number (Fig. 4e) at each time point revealed no significant change until DAD 2, but a marked reduction in all parameters by DAD 3.

These results indicate that even a preformed 3D capillary network within the HUVEC module cannot be stably maintained over extended periods once disconnected from the LF module. In particular, the rounded morphology observed at the vessel termini on DAD 3 suggests that tip cell retraction occurs in response to growth factor deprivation, marking a transition from vascular formation to regression. The accompanying reduction in vascular length and branching node count serves as additional evidence of this regression process [40]. In contrast, under continuously connected conditions without LF removal, the HUVEC network retained its stable morphology up to Day 10 (Supplementary Fig. S3). Additionally, upon further culture for 5 and 7 days after separation, the luminal structure gradually deteriorated, ultimately leading to the loss of the vascular network (Supplementary Fig. S6). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that vascular structures within the HUVEC module begin to degenerate approximately three days after disconnection, indicating that re-supply of angiogenic cues should occur within two days to preserve network stability. Notably, Song et al. reported that vascular structures remained stable for up to three days following selective fibroblast removal [11], suggesting that slight variations in maintenance duration may arise depending on cell condition and culture environment. Therefore, to ensure structural stability of the capillary network, subsequent experiments employed a strategy of temporarily disconnecting the modules for up to two days before reconnection, to examine whether this reestablished co-culture could maintain or further promote angiogenic growth within the HUVEC module.

Angiogenic response to module reconnection after temporary separation

To evaluate the effect of reconnection following temporary disconnection on vascular maintenance, HUVEC modules that had been co-cultured with LF modules for five days were disconnected for different time periods and then reconnected for an additional two days, resulting in a total co-culture period of seven days. The results were compared to those from continuously connected control samples maintained for seven days without disconnection (Fig. 5a). Experimental groups were classified according to the interval between disconnection and reconnection, defined as days before reconnection (DBR): modules that were reconnected immediately after disconnection (DBR 0), after one day (DBR 1), or after two days (DBR 2). Quantitative analyses including vascular density, vessel length, and branching node number showed no significant differences between the continuously connected control and the temporarily disconnected groups (Fig. 5b–e). These results suggest that short-term disconnection of up to two days does not critically affect the structural integrity of preformed vascular networks. These results were further validated by extended culture up to 7 days, which confirmed that the vascular network was not only maintained but also connected across the hydrogel channel in the module, exhibiting an open and continuous luminal structure (Supplementary Fig. S7).

(c–e) Quantitative analyses of vascular density (c), vessel length (d), and branching node number (e), showing no significant differences between control and any temporarily disconnected groups, indicating that short-term disconnection of up to 2 days did not affect the structural integrity of the vascular networks. Data in (c–e) are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD, n = 5). Statistical significance is indicated where applicable (ns: non-significant, ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001).

Previous studies have reported that continuous supply of growth factors and extracellular matrix proteins from supporting cells such as fibroblasts is indispensable for maintaining endothelial stability and network persistence [29,[41], [42], [43]]. However, our findings indicate that a transient absence of fibroblast-derived support for approximately two days does not cause irreversible damage to the vascular network. Once the LF module is reconnected and growth factor exchange is resumed, angiogenic activity and vascular maintenance are restored to levels comparable to those of continuously connected systems. These findings demonstrate that the MMP allows flexible temporal control of intermodular communication, enabling modular vascular systems to remain stable even after short disconnection periods. Such controllability provides a foundation for developing ‘plug-and-play’ type inter-organ systems, where the vascular module can be dynamically reconnected to other tissue modules—such as tumor modules—to recapitulate physiological or pathological crosstalk [[44], [45], [46]].

Nonetheless, our current MMP fabrication process, which relies on manual cutting to create paired modules, limits stable reconnection to the original counterparts produced simultaneously. To enable reliable reconnection with heterogeneous organ modules, fabrication precision should be improved to allow cross-assembly compatibility. Furthermore, to maintain vascular integrity over longer disconnection periods, optimized culture conditions during disconnection, such as supplementation with VEGF-containing media, should be established [47,48]. Overcoming these limitations would enable the development of a fibroblast-free vascular module that can be freely connected to diverse organ modules to study vascular interactions in a modular and dynamic manner. Such a system could supply nutrients and remove metabolic waste in organ models lacking typical fibroblasts—such as the brain or pancreas—and ultimately serve as a core technological basis for ‘multi-organ integration’ and ‘human-on-a-chip systems’. As an example, to model the blood-brain barrier (BBB), a brain microvascular structure reported to devoid of fibroblasts [[49], [50], [51]], we performed additional studies on a brain-specific vMPSC model by co-culturing human brain microvascular endothelial cells (hBMECs) with astrocytes instead of conventional HUVECs (Supplementary Fig. S8). Vascularization of the hBMEC module was first induced via co-culture with the LF module, after which the LF module was removed to generate a fibroblast-free hBMEC module. Astrocytes were then seeded onto the hydrogel surface within the hBMEC module. As a result, astrocytic endfeet structures aligned along the hBMEC capillaries were observed, demonstrating that our system recapitulates key features of the BBB microenvironment in the absence of fibroblasts. Collectively, the findings of this study provide fundamental data on the stability of modular vascular systems and highlight the potential of the MMP for applications in customizable tissue modeling, precision drug testing, and advanced tissue engineering.

Conclusions

In this study, we established a customizable vascularization strategy using a modular microfluidic platform (MMP). The platform enables controlled connection and disconnection between an LF module and a HUVEC module, thereby recapitulating a fibroblast-free 3D capillary network. Through seven days of co-culture under connected conditions, a well-developed microvascular structure was formed within the HUVEC module. Subsequent disconnection experiments demonstrated that the vascular architecture remained stable for up to two days in the absence of the LF module. Furthermore, upon reconnection, angiogenic activity was reactivated, indicating that vascular network growth could be temporally modulated as desired. Collectively, these findings highlight the feasibility of maintaining and reactivating vascular networks without continuous fibroblast support. This approach provides a foundation for modeling fibroblast-lacking microenvironments—such as those of the brain or pancreas—where traditional fibroblast co-culture methods are unsuitable. Moreover, the modular configuration of the MMP enables dynamic reconnection with disease-specific or organ-specific modules, offering new opportunities to recapitulate pathological remodeling of vascular networks, such as those occurring in patient-specific tumor microenvironments.
