Stomata in-sight: Integrating live confocal microscopy with leaf gas exchange and environmental control
Authors: Joseph D Crawford, Dustin Mayfield-Jones, Glenn A Fried, Nicolas Hernandez, Andrew D B Leakey
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractStomatal anatomy (aperture area, length, and width) influences leaf-level physiology traits including conductance to water vapor. Stomatal anatomy can be visualized in situ by microscopy, but the difficulty of regulating the atmospheric environment of a microscope stage means that the conditions under which imaging is done are rarely physiologically relevant. Alternatively, leaf gas exchange instruments that measure gas fluxes reflect stomatal anatomical characteristics in aggregate, but the relative strengths of anatomical traits to control water use (e.g. size vs density) cannot be firmly established. To reconcile the microscopic stomatal characteristics with leaf-level gas exchange, we describe a tool that combines laser scanning confocal microscopy, gas exchange instruments, and machine-learning image analysis to simultaneously observe anatomical characteristics of many (>40) stomata alongside leaf-level traits like photosynthesis, transpiration, and stomatal conductance. We demonstrate how the tool has the resolution capable of quantifying aperture sizes and variability in maize (Zea mays) leaves under 5 steady-state light/pCO2 treatments while tightly controlling other environmental variables like relative humidity and temperature. A model used to calculate stomatal conductance from measured apertures and stomatal density accurately matched stomatal conductance measured by gas exchange. This technical advancement will provide insight on how stomatal anatomy and function trade off to influence stomatal conductance and leaf-level water use efficiency.

Introduction

Stomata in the leaf epidermis of land plants control photosynthetic CO2 uptake and transpiration (Lawson and Leakey 2024). Stomatal complexes feature a pair of guard cells and in some phylogenetic groups, including grasses, are surrounded by subsidiary cells (Cheng and Raissig 2023). Variation in guard cell turgor allows opening and closing of the stomatal pore in response to environmental and physiological signals (Lawson and Blatt 2014; Jezek and Blatt 2017). Stomatal conductance (gsw) is a measure of how easily water vapor and CO2 can be exchanged between the leaf internal airspace and the atmosphere (Lawson and Matthews 2020). Well-established theory describes maximum potential stomatal conductance (gsw-max) as a function of stomatal density, pore depth and maximum pore aperture, with pore aperture being typically estimated from measures of stomatal complex size (Parlange and Waggoner 1970; Franks and Farquhar 2007; Franks and Beerling 2009; Lawson and Matthews 2020). The role of stomatal complex size and density as phenotypes in determining maximum stomatal conductance has been important in understanding how the stomatal anatomy and physiology of stomata can drive ecological adaptation (Taylor et al. 2012). However, actual operational stomatal conductance rarely approaches gsw-max because under most growing conditions the balance between carbon gain and water loss is optimized by the pore being partially closed (Lawson and Morison 2004; McElwain et al. 2016; Lawson and Matthews 2020). The environmental responsiveness of operational aperture means it is highly variable over time and space (Lawson and Blatt 2014). Unfortunately, existing methods for direct measurement of stomatal aperture under controlled and physiologically relevant conditions have significant limitations (Mott and Buckley 2000). Consequently, data describing stomatal aperture has rarely been integrated with data on stomatal density and leaf gas exchange, leaving significant gaps in our knowledge about how the mechanical diversity of stomata enables their performance characteristics, and how aperture is regulated in response to environmental conditions (Mott and Buckley 2000; Kaiser 2009; Grantz et al. 2018; Lunn et al. 2024; van den Berg et al. 2025). Improved understanding of the genetic, environmental, and physiological regulation of gsw must be achieved if crops are to be engineered for the improved water use efficiency (Lawson and Blatt 2014; Leakey et al. 2019; Harrison et al. 2020; Ferguson et al. 2024) needed to support resilient production of food, fuel, fiber, feed and valuable biobased products.

Techniques to directly measure stomatal aperture are limited in precision, accuracy, and especially in throughput. For example, epidermal impressions are a technique where a malleable paste is applied to the leaf epidermis and seeps into the stomatal pores, resulting in a negative mold that can be measured microscopically (Matthaeus et al. 2020). This technique can be effective in some circumstances, but results are often variable, and it can fail when apertures are small or hard to access (Karavolias et al. 2023; Lunn et al. 2024). Alternatively, the epidermis can be removed and measured with a microscope, but this technique removes stomata from their native state and subjects them to unrealistic conditions (Mott et al. 2008; Shen et al. 2015). A live leaf can be measured under a microscope, but without the ability to tightly control conditions of the microscope stage, stomata will close or change in seconds to minutes and will lack coherent correspondence with stomatal conductance (Clark 2019). In contrast, although technically challenging, imaging leaves within cuvettes that regulate the environment they experience and measure gas exchange has been achieved by customized instruments (Kaiser 2009; Grantz et al. 2018). It is challenging in part because the leaf cuvette blocks an objective lens from reaching the close working distance typically required for stomata to be in focus. Notably, it has recently become possible to measure stomatal apertures in situ, with growth chambers being used to control environmental conditions (van den Berg et al. 2025). A challenge common to all methods is the difficulty of sampling sufficient stomata to accurately represent the mean and variance of the distribution of apertures on a leaf under stable, realistic conditions.

Due to the challenges of assessing operational stomatal aperture, plant physiologists most commonly use gas exchange instruments to measure stomatal conductance. In this technique, the magnitude of measured water vapor flux coming from the leaf can be used as a proxy for aperture area given appropriate staging of the experiment (Mott and Parkhurst 1991). A benefit of modern, commercial gas exchange systems is that they have fine control over leaf environmental conditions and can be portable. However, the limitation of using gas exchange to measure stomatal conductance (gsw-gasex) is that the enclosed leaf area measures the average effect of between 104 and 105 stomatal complexes (Mott and Buckley 2000). In other words, measuring gsw via gas exchange measures the effect of aperture in aggregate and assumes that the distribution of apertures is spatially uniform and normally distributed. If those assumptions are not held, then gsw-gasex would not accurately estimate leaf gas exchange, leading to misinterpretation of how it responds to endogenous or environmental factors. In addition, reliance on leaf gas exchange data alone prevents investigation of how stomatal density, stomatal complex size, and pore aperture interact to control gsw-gasex.

Difficulties in directly measuring stomatal aperture, especially simultaneously with leaf gas exchange measurements, have left many questions about stomatal structure and function unanswered. For example, the distribution of stomatal apertures and how they correspond to rates of stomatal conductance has only been published a few times (Laisk et al. 1980; Van Gardingen et al. 1989; Kaiser 2009). It is therefore possible that there is non-uniform distribution of apertures with widespread, unforeseen spatial variation in aperture size. Additionally, there is a negative relationship between stomatal density and stomatal size. This relationship has been well-documented in ecology and over evolutionary time (Franks and Beerling 2009; Taylor et al. 2012). Nevertheless, since apertures are technically challenging to measure, there is uncertainty about how variations in aperture interface with variations in stomatal size and/or density. Transgenic reduction of stomatal density has offered a direct test of the trade-offs between stomatal size, density, and aperture and their impact on stomatal conductance. Recent engineering of sugarcane reduced stomatal density but did not alter gsw. In the absence of changes in stomatal complex size, it seems likely the low-stomatal density plants were compensating through a larger aperture (Lunn et al. 2024). Which means there is an adjustment to stomatal behavior with a mechanism that is currently unknown. There are many other knowledge gaps around structure-function of stomata and dynamic stomatal conductance, such as the relationship between stomatal size and closing speed (Ozeki et al. 2022), or the speed advantages of dumbbell-shaped guard cells in grasses (McAusland et al. 2016; Lawson and Matthews 2020). Many of these open questions hinge on whether stomata respond to environmental factors independently or through a poorly understood emergent behavior (Mott and Buckley 2000; Peak et al. 2023). In summary, development of a method that can directly measure the apertures of a sufficient population of stomata distributed across a leaf under controlled environmental conditions while also measuring photosynthetic gas exchange would create the opportunity to address many strategic research priorities.

Results

Simultaneous measurement of leaf gas exchange and pore/stomatal complex anatomy under controlled environmental conditions

Modified gas exchange equipment was coupled to the microscope stage and functioned with full use of the light supply, gas flow, and fans as the leaf was exposed to a series of different environmental conditions (1: dark, ambient pCO2; (2) light, ambient pCO2; (3) light, low pCO2; (4) light, high pCO2; and (5) return to light, ambient pCO2) with the abaxial leaf epidermis being visualized with 2-photon confocal microscopy once the leaf reached equilibrium at each condition (Figs. 1 and 2; Supplementary Fig. S1). The modified equipment generated data that corresponded closely with output from a standard instrument (Supplementary Fig. S2) and stomatal aperture was spatially uniform throughout the leaf cuvette providing evidence of appropriate boundary layer conductance (Moran statistic = 0.039, P = 0.079; Supplementary Fig. S3). A machine-learning model was trained to estimate the area, length, and width of stomatal complexes, pores, guard cells, and subsidiary cells from microscope images. The correlations between the machine-learning model output and human measurements were strong (Fig. 3), being best for area estimates of stomatal complexes (r2 = 0.98), pores (r2 = 0.91) and subsidiary cells (r2 = 0.91). Correlations between machine-learning- and human-generated trait estimates still explained a large portion of variation in length (r2 = 0.67 to 0.94) and width (r2 = 0.73 to 0.9; Fig. 3) of all targets, with outcomes generally being better for stomatal complexes and pores than guard cells and subsidiary cells. The slope and bias of the lines of best fit were such that machine-learning results generally did not deviate substantially from the human-generated data. However, the machine-learning model did consistently estimate greater values of guard cell length and lower values of subsidiary cell length than human evaluators (Fig. 3, H and K). For the key trait of pore area, machine-learning and human-generated data were highly correlated but the machine-learning estimates were greater compared to human-generated data by 20%, on average (Fig. 3D).

Bootstrap re-sampling to determine how many stomata to measure

As the number of stomata sampled from a leaf increased from 2 to 76, the 95% confidence interval (CI) among 1,500 re-sampling estimates of the mean and standard deviation decreased exponentially (Fig. 4, A and B). Based on this analysis, data from 40 stomatal pores were needed to generate mean and standard deviation values with 95% CIs within 96% and 73%, respectively, of the 95% CIs of the true mean and standard deviation of pore area across the leaf (Fig. 4, A and B).

Stomatal conductance and pore area variation in response to PPFD and pCO2

Under dark/ambient pCO2 conditions, gsw was indistinguishable from zero (Fig. 5A). First increasing PPFD to 1,800 μmol m−2 s−1 before switching to low pCO2, and elevated pCO2 altered the average gsw-gasex to 0.14 mol m−2 s−1, 0.26 mol m−2 s−1, and 0.13 mol m−2 s−1, respectively (Fig. 5A). Upon returning to ambient pCO2, there was no evidence of hysteresis, since gsw-gasex was not significantly different from measurements under the equivalent conditions earlier in the sequence (Fig. 5A). The aperture of approximately 40 stomata was assessed at each environmental condition. Under dark-adapted conditions, most stomata were closed (i.e. zero pore area) and the median aperture area was 0, while the mean aperture was indistinguishable from zero at 1.13 ± 7.0 µm2 (Fig. 5B). Across the sequence of environmental conditions, variation in mean pore area (Fig. 5B) matched changes in gsw-gasex, increasing significantly when PPFD was greater and when pCO2 was lower.

Frequency distribution of open stomata

The frequency distribution of stomatal pore apertures was non-normal under all 5 environmental conditions that were tested (Fig. 6). In the dark, 95% of stomata were closed (Fig. 6A). Maximum stomatal aperture in the dark was 32.8 µm2. After photosynthetic induction in the light at ambient pCO2, only 33% of stomata remained closed and the apertures of stomata that were open was highly variable with a median of 23.2 µm2, a mean of 31.2 µm2, and a maximum of 172 µm2 (Fig. 6B). After decreasing to a low pCO2, pore apertures increased, but were still widely distributed with a median of 65.3 µm2, a mean of 68.3 µm2, and a maximum of 193 µm2 (Fig. 6C). At elevated pCO2, the distribution of stomatal apertures shifted to lower values and 53% of stomata were closed (Fig. 6D). However, a significant number of stomata still had apertures > 15 µm2. As a result, the mean aperture remained 22.9 µm2 despite a drop in the median aperture to 0 µm2. This is consistent with the leaf gas exchange data in suggesting that insufficient time was spent at elevated pCO2 for full equilibration after transition from low pCO2. Upon returning to ambient pCO2, there was no evidence of hysteresis and the distribution of stomatal aperture matched those observed under the equivalent conditions earlier in the sequence (Fig. 6E).

Spatial variation in stomatal aperture across the leaf surface

The 1 × 3 cm gas exchange chamber was placed on the abaxial surface of the leaf blade, while avoiding the midrib, and oriented with the long side parallel to the longitudinal (base-to-tip) axis of the leaf (Supplementary Fig. S4A). Pooling data from all leaves assessed, there was no systematic trend in stomatal pore area or the presence of closed stomata along either the longitudinal or medial axes of the leaf (Supplementary Fig. S4, B and C). Additionally, there was no trend in stomatal pore area from the effect of the temporal sequence of imaging (Supplementary Fig. S5) nor from randomization of leaf sites visited (Supplementary Figs. S6).

Reconciling operating stomatal conductance calculated from pore area and stomatal density versus leaf gas exchange

There was a strong correlation between gsw-gasex measured by leaf gas exchange and gsw-anatomy, which was estimated from observations of stomatal aperture and stomatal density (R2 = 0.85; Fig. 7). The line-of-best-fit intercept was not significantly different from zero (P = 0.58) but the slope of the relationship (m = 0.89) indicated that estimates of gsw-anatomy were greater compared to gsw-gasex.

Leaf structure trait correlations

The analysis of trait–trait correlations of leaf structures used data from the gas exchange coupled to the confocal microscope in the ambient treatment and included 506 paired observations of guard cells, subsidiary cells, and pores forming stomatal complexes. Since many stomata are closed, the number of apertures captured and compared was 329. All but 5 of the 64 correlations were statistically significant (P < 0.01; Fig. 8). Variation in gsw-gasex was most strongly correlated with measures of pore size (Pearson correlation [ρ] = 0.51 to 0.67), moderate for measures of guard cell size (ρ = 0.41 to 0.43), and weak for measures of stomatal complex size (ρ = 0.18 to 0.40). Pore width explained as much variation in gsw-gasex as pore area did. Variation in pore area was most strongly correlated with measures of guard cell size (ρ = 0.49 to 0.62), moderate for measures of stomatal complex size (ρ = 0.26 to 0.47), and very weakly or not related to subsidiary cell sizes. Variation in stomatal pore length was strongly correlated with the length of complexes (ρ = 0.87) and guard cells (ρ = 0.89) but only moderately correlated with subsidiary cell length (ρ = 0.71).

Discussion

This study demonstrated a technical advance that can reconcile variation in stomatal aperture and stomatal density with variation in operational stomatal conductance (gsw-gasex), by successfully addressing 5 aims. First, a tool was developed that combined (1) the resolution of 2-photon confocal microscopy to measure open stomatal pores, (2) precise control of the environment experienced by the leaf, and (3) the ability to simultaneously measure gsw-gasex (Figs. 1 and 2). Second, a high-throughput, automated image analysis pipeline was trained to automatically quantify stomatal aperture and related anatomical traits (Fig. 3). Third, bootstrap resampling determined that sampling 40 stomatal pores was sufficient to provide an accurate estimate of the mean stomatal aperture for the portion of the leaf in the gas exchange chamber (Fig. 4). Fourth, it was demonstrated that gsw-gasex can be accurately predicted from independent estimates of stomatal apertures, stomatal density, and pore depth data extracted from images of the epidermis (Fig. 7). Fifth, stomatal complex size influences pore aperture and gsw-gasex via correlations between pore length and stomatal complex length or guard cell length (ρ = 0.87–0.89; Fig. 8). However, pore width explains the most variation in pore area (ρ = 0.96; Fig. 8) and gsw-gasex (ρ = 0.66; Fig. 8), while being less accurately predicted from the widths of guard cells (ρ = 0.62; Fig. 8) or stomatal complexes (ρ = 0.19; Fig. 8). This integration of a confocal microscope with a leaf gas exchange system is significant because it has the potential to explore long-standing unknowns about stomatal structure-function relationships in eco-physiology and stomatal behavior, which can aid efforts to engineer stomatal traits for improved WUE in agriculture (Leakey et al. 2019).

Gas exchange

Measurement of aperture concurrent with gas exchange is a long-standing technical challenge. Although previous work has observed leaf structures while controlling reference conditions on the microscope stage, very few studies have measured sample gases coming off the leaf (Kaiser 2009). The Stomata In-Sight instrument can measure gsw-gasex concurrent to imaging (Fig. 1, A to C). The long working-distance objective also stayed completely outside of the flow of measured leaf gases (Fig. 1). This had the benefit of not having adsorption of water to the uncoated metal which would influence gas flux measurements. Additionally, it avoided adding any impediment to air stirring that drives down the boundary layer of the leaf. If the boundary layer is large or the air is poorly stirred then estimates of stomatal conductance would be inaccurate (Nobel 2009). The custom chamber was confirmed to have sufficient fan speeds to achieve boundary layer conductance rates larger than gsw, as needed to accurately assess gas exchange, while not significantly distorting microscope measurements (Supplementary Fig. S7, A and B). The instrument was converted to a 1-sided system (Fig. 1A; Supplementary Fig. S8, A to E). This was key to reconciling gsw-gasex and gsw-anatomy since abaxial and adaxial stomatal densities and sizes are often substantially different, and there is evidence that their behaviors are independently regulated in amphistomatic leaves (Richardson et al. 2017; Wall et al. 2022). Importantly, the adaxial leaf surface not being measured was still provided some flow of the same reference gas concentrations because without it the flux measurements of water vapor and CO2 can be biased (LI-COR Inc. 2020).

Stomatal patterning and anatomy

This study demonstrates a tool that overcame the technical challenge of measuring open pores of stomata under controlled conditions (Fig. 1, A to C). Confocal microscopy blocks out of focus light from detection and so the high-intensity LED lamp can remain on during measurement (Fig. 1B). The long-working distance objective and confocal microscope together provided the ability to measure pore area with a measurement resolution limit of 0.25 μm2 pixel−1 (Fig. 1C). This was critical since stomatal apertures in grasses are often narrow rectangular shapes, where opening involves increases in width of just a few microns that are then multiplied across a length that may be an order of magnitude greater in scale (i.e. changing aperture width; see Supplementary Video S1).

The challenge of measuring apertures in conjunction with gas exchange is also limited by throughput (Laisk et al. 1980; Kappen et al. 1987; Mott and Buckley 2000). The throughput problem was overcome by training and validating a machine-learning model to detect and measure stomatal complexes, apertures, guard cells and subsidiary cells which increased the efficiency of data (Figs. 1C and 3). The agreement between human and machine measurements was strong for pore area (r2 = 0.91; Fig. 3D) and pore width (r2 = 0.9, Fig. 3F) even though pore width was the smallest structure assessed (Table 1). The slightly diminished agreement for measurements of pore length were likely driven by uncertainty about the location of the boundary between guard cells and the pore. The tendency of the computer vision model to overestimate pore area is likely to explain why gsw-anatomy was slightly overestimated relative to gsw-gasex (Fig. 7). It is likely that the machine learning model can be retrained to improve performance in predictions of pore area and gsw-anatomy if additional annotation of training images can define aperture boundary more precisely. Alternatively, this could indicate that the model is too simple and that the 3D geometry of the pore throat is an important factor in accurately modeling diffusion (Kaiser 2009). It is important to note that machine learning models are powerful, but typically context dependent, so a new model will need to be trained to estimate pore area in experiments on images from grass species with stomata that are different in size or form to maize, and in dicot species with their different stomatal form (Tan et al. 2024). Stomatal complexes were the largest structures observed and were accurately predicted by the machine-learning algorithm (Fig. 3, A to C). In attempt to maximize efficiency during data collection, bootstrap re-sampling was used to assess how many stomatal complexes were sampled under each gas exchange condition. Approximately 40 stomatal complexes were sampled per leaf since, on average, this generated a mean and standard error of pore area that was within 99% of the estimated true mean and standard error for the leaf (Fig. 4).

Reconciling estimates of stomatal conductance from gas exchange and anatomy

We demonstrated how variation in stomatal conductance could be driven by a sequence of light and pCO2 treatments on a leaf and could generate measurable variation in stomatal apertures (Fig. 2). Starting from a baseline of a dark-adapted leaf where gsw-gasex was zero, we quantified the mean and variance of the population of stomatal apertures alongside gsw-gasex (Fig. 5). The mean aperture increased or decreased tracking gsw-gasex as expected (Figs. 5, A and B; 6, A to E). Others have observed the large variance in apertures and have suggested that there is some biological significance and that variance may be a feature of the stomatal system such as potentially non-uniform signal reception amongst stomata (Laisk et al. 1980; Van Gardingen et al. 1989; Kaiser 2009). There is of course the simple factor of within-sample anatomical variation that larger guard cells could generate larger variation in aperture or that different size classes of individual stomata have different time constants for closure (Spence 1987). Supporting this, van den Berg et al. found that individual guard cell length was correlated with stomatal closing speed (2025). A review of stomatal aperture variability found coefficients of variation (CV) range from 30% to 300% (Spence 1987). Some variability has been attributed to inadequate measurement resolution and technique (Spence et al. 1983) (i.e. cellular damage from epidermal peels vs intact leaves). But even when measurement of aperture area was not limited by instrument resolution a CV range of 30% to 200% was observed in oat leaves at a similar conductance observed in this paper (Van Gardingen et al. 1989). When the leaf maintains a large variance in aperture, it may serve the purpose of having at least a few stomata poised to respond best to the next unforeseen, rapid environmental change. This topic deserves additional investigation to build on the observation that using the mean of stomatal pore area predicted gsw-gasex with a high degree of accuracy (Fig. 7).

The proportion of open stomata under different treatment conditions was not normally distributed (Fig. 6, A to E). In the low pCO2 treatment, nearly all stomata were open, but under the high pCO2 treatment, the distribution was non-normal with 50% of stomata closed (Fig. 6D). Having a large portion of stomata that are completely closed in many treatments may contribute to the large dispersion of apertures. At low conductance in oats, the distribution of stomatal apertures observed through flash-freezing and scanning electron microscopy was non-normally distributed with 70% completely closed. Oat open apertures were skewed to the low end with about 5% of apertures with small, non-zero area, and this was observed in this study in the 0.25 to 15.2 µm2 bins (Fig. 6, B, D, and E) (Van Gardingen et al. 1989). However, inclusion in this bin could be a measurement resolution limitation artifact. Alternatively, this could be evidence of a bimodal distributions and reflect a subpopulation of stomata whose feedback mechanisms are oscillating between opening and closing similar to individual maize stomata reported by van den Berg et al. (2025). This deserves future study with better measurement resolution. Studies with similar instrumentation capabilities have noted that some stomata appear to always be closed (Kappen et al. 1987). Time required to reach steady state may also contribute to large dispersions in aperture. van den Berg et al. demonstrated that individual stoma showed a delayed response to light changes and that the aperture area fluctuated when perturbed by changing light conditions but eventually tighten their dispersion after steady state is reached (2025). In this study, the shift from low to high pCO2 condition may have required more time to reach steady state (Supplementary Fig. S1).

A well-established theory describes how maximum stomatal conductance (gsw-max) can be calculated from the density and size of entire stomatal complexes (Parlange and Waggoner 1970; Lawson and Matthews 2020). This theory has been powerful for comparative studies that calculate gsw-max from stomatal size and density and then correlate it to factors such as ecological habitats, atmospheric pCO2 over geologic time, photosynthetic functional types, and stomatal kinetics of intra-specific species (Franks et al. 2009; Franks and Beerling 2009; Taylor et al. 2012; Yoshiyama et al. 2024). However, operational stomatal conductance is always below maximum potential conductance (gsw-max), but to a variable and unpredictable extent, which means aperture is regulated in a manner that varies across species and environmental conditions (McElwain et al. 2016). Being able to reconcile stomatal anatomy with leaf gas exchange with Stomata in-Sight will allow investigation of how aperture interacts with SD and stomatal complex size to determine genetic and environmental variation in gsw. In this data set, stomatal complex area and the best measures of aperture (area and width) were relatively weakly correlated (0.27 to 0.47) (Fig. 8). In fact, data sets from large diversity panels that measured stomatal anatomy, gsw, and intrinsic water use efficiency in a common garden often did not find strong relationships between these factors as would be expected (Ferguson et al. 2021; Pignon et al. 2021; Xie et al. 2021). This further highlights the need for a direct measurement of aperture that relates to gsw-gasex. The technical development presented here will allow validation of model estimates of pore aperture from gsw-gasex, stomatal density, and pore depth, something that has been very challenging up until now.

Additionally, transgenic sugarcane plants engineered to have reduced stomatal density are a direct test of the interplay between density, size, and aperture. Despite having no differences in stomatal size or photosynthetic capacity, gsw was the same which suggests that because of reduced density their apertures compensate through some fundamentally unknown stomatal behavioral adjustment mechanism (Lunn et al. 2024). Those mutants and this combination of microscopy and gas exchange will be a key system to understand this barrier to engineering higher leaf-level water use efficiency in agriculture (Leakey et al. 2019).

Materials and methods

Plant cultivation

Seeds of Zea mays genotype B73 were sown directly into 6 L pots and grown in the University of Illinois Plant Biology greenhouse (Urbana, IL). The growth media was 3:1:1:1 soil mix of BM6 (Berger, Saint-Modeste, QC, Canada, BM6-All-Purpose), sterilized field soil, peat, and perlite. The greenhouse room temperature settings were 28 °C in the day and 22 °C at night. Supplemental LED lighting was used to maintain a 16-hour photoperiod. The plants were fertilized twice per week with 300 ppm 15-5-15 NPK, STEM micronutrients, and 0.1 L chelated iron fertilizer powder (Sprint 330). Plants were grown consecutively in 2 grow-out groups (statistical block). Plants were on automatic twice daily drip irrigation that started at 7 Am and 7 Pm and watered for 30 min.

Custom chamber fabrication and assembly

A customized gas exchange chamber was designed with the goal of measuring abaxial gas exchange (i.e. single-sided data collection) while also doing confocal imaging of stomata inside the gas exchange chamber. The adaxial surface was illuminated by a light source and was also supplied with a controlled [CO2] to avoid CO2 gradients that would otherwise create artifacts in gas exchange data from the abaxial surface, but no data were collected from it. A customized 2-piece, clamp-on leaf gas exchange chamber was developed to be compatible with the LI-6800 photosynthetic gas exchange system (LI-COR Bioscience, Inc., Lincoln, NE, USA; Fig. 1A, Supplementary Fig. S8, A to C). The lower half of the chamber (Fig. 1A; Supplementary Fig. S8D) was milled from aluminum and nickel plated (Precision Plating of Quincy, Inc.) before a large microscopy cover glass (4.98 cm × 7.5 cm, No. 1 Thickness, Ted Pella, Inc.) was adhered to the base to form a transparent window for leaf imaging from below. The top of the lower half of the chamber had centrally located 1 × 3 cm area opening around which a standard gasket was used to interface with the abaxial surface of the leaf where gas exchange and aperture was measured. The total distance from the outside of the glass window to the abaxial surface of a leaf in the chamber was less than in a standard chamber bottom so that it remained within the working distance of the objective lens (13 mm).

The upper half of the chamber (Fig. 1A; Supplementary Fig. S8, B and E) was a standard chamber top (LI-COR 6800-02P) fitted with standard a 1 × 3 cm insert (LI-COR P/N 9868-563) that was customized so that leaf gas exchange was only measured on the abaxial leaf surface, i.e. matching the subject of imaging by the confocal microscope. Propafilm covered the upper chamber cavity above the leaf and reference air was routed from the reference gas line to the upper leaf cavity and then exhausted into the room (Supplementary Fig. S8E). This design used the established operating principles of the commercially available 1-sided gas exchange kit (LI-COR P/N 9968-313) to exclude adaxial surface gas fluxes from being measured while avoiding gradients in [CO2] or water vapor that could create artifacts in gas exchange of the abaxial leaf surface. To measure contact leaf temperature, 2 E-type leaf temperature thermocouples (Omega Engineering, Inc., Item# FF-E-20-SLE-25) were routed through a silicone-sealed piercing in the Propafilm that seals the top half of the leaf chamber (Supplementary Fig. S8, B and D). Before use, the 2 thermocouples were given unique calibration offsets based on their deviation from the temperature of water in an ice slurry (0.01 °C).

The handle of the LI-6800 head was removed so that the head could be inverted and fastened to a custom-made microscope stage adapter (Fig. 1A; Supplementary Fig. S8F) that could be moved with microscope controls. With the handle and clamping mechanism disabled, an alternative clamping mechanism was made by compressing the top lid of the cuvette onto the bottom lid against the custom stage mount (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Fig. S8, A, B and F). Aluminum blocks were fastened perpendicularly to the top lid. The bottom half of the cuvette was modified by extending rubber baffles and served to disconnect fan vibrations from the stage (Fig. 1A; Supplementary Fig. S8G). The entire head of the LI-6800 was hung from shock cord connected to a swiveling arm above the microscope (Supplementary Fig. S8H).

The custom chamber's boundary layer conductance (gblc) was measured following manufacturer recommendations (https://www.licor.com/support/LI-6800/manuals.html). Briefly, water-saturated Whatman #1 filter paper without drips was placed in the leaf cuvette and therefore the only resistance to water evaporation is the boundary layer. An auto-program manipulated mixing fan percent power of the LI-6800 internal mixing fan in a sequence of 0, 10, 30, 50, 70, 90, 100, 80, 60, 40, and 20%. Without a light source on in the cuvette, the balance of energy in the chamber comes from water vaporization (measured as transpiration), and the sensible heat loss of evaporation which is temperature difference between the paper and air. With all other variables measured, the boundary layer conductance can be calculated as a result of fan power and flow rate. The 2 instruments executed the same timed, sequence of changes in light and pCO2.

Gas exchange measurements

Simultaneous measurements of leaf gas exchange and imaging of stomata with confocal microscopy were performed with the LI-6800 fitted with the custom chamber and mated to the microscope stage (Fig. 1). Environmental conditions in the chamber were set to be: flow rate to abaxial leaf surface—500 µmol s−1; diverted flow rate from the reference gas to the adaxial leaf surface—200 µmol s−1; partial pressure of CO2 (pCO2)—420 µmol mol−1; photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD)—1,800 µmol m−2 s−1 with light quality of 90% red and 10% blue; air temperature—28 °C; relative humidity—70%. A microscope stage heater was used to heat the humidifying column on the back of the console LI-6800 to help achieve high humidity.

Image quality control during gas exchange measurements

There was the potential for confocal images to be blurred or “noisy” due to vibration from fans in the gas exchange system when the 2 pieces of equipment were coupled together. However, after the modifications to the gas exchange system described were implemented there was no significant effect of mixing fan power on the clarity of acquired images, as assessed in terms of measurement deviation of distances between fixed objects (Supplementary Figs. S7 and S9). Additional testing also confirmed no loss of image quality due to operation of the fans associated with the temperature exchanger and infrared gas analyzer cooling (Supplementary Fig. S10), and therefore no need to apply averaging across larger numbers of frame scans that would otherwise slow image acquisition (Supplementary Fig. S10). However, during data collection on the microscope, selecting fields of view and focusing occurs in “Live Mode” which does not have any frame averaging of pixel intensities (Zeiss, LSM 710 manual). High mixing fan power did distort the “Live Mode” image feed making operation of the microscope more challenging. Therefore, a fan power of 40% was chosen because it maximized image quality and ease of microscope operation while still resulting in a boundary layer conductance of 1.8 mol m−2 s−1, which greatly exceeded all measured values of gsw-gasex as is needed to achieve accurate estimates of leaf gas exchange (Supplementary Fig. S7).

Gas exchange data were collected from 10 independent replicate plants, arranged in 2 blocks, after time was given for the leaf to reach equilibrium under each of the following conditions:

Confocal microscope

A 50× magnification objective (Plan Apo Infinity Corrected) with 0.55 numerical aperture and 13.0 mm working-distance (Mitutoyo Corporation, Kangawa, Japan) was installed on a Zeiss Laser Scanning Microscope (LSM) 710 (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) with a custom-made adapter. A Ti-Sapphire Laser (Spectra Physics Mai Tai, Milpitas, CA, USA) with 70 fs pulse duration operating at 80 mHz was used for 2-photon excitation imaging of auto-fluorescence. The main beam splitter dichroic mirror was 760+ nm. Typical laser settings were 30% laser power of 2-photon excitation at 780 nm and emission detection from 390 to 605 nm, corresponding to <10 mW of laser power at the leaf. Laser scans were taken with 2× averaging of the pixel intensities from scanning laser lines. Image settings were standardized for optimal resolution with image width × height of 202 × 202 µm (824 × 824 pixels), a pixel size of 0.2457 × 0.2457 µm2 which determines the XY resolution as 4.07 pixels µm−1. One field of view was taken at 1× digital zoom in ∼2 s. Z axis stacks were collected with intervals of 1.8 µm and 11 slices per stack in ∼22 s. Although not exploited by the data collection in this manuscript, this is rapid enough to allow capture of time-lapse movies of stomatal opening and closing (Supplementary Video 1). The XY stage position was set to zero at the top left corner of the leaf cuvette area. To prepare for a standard data collection event, 16 randomly located positions across the cuvette leaf area were designated as sampling targets before the positions were visited in random order to collect images. Sixteen Z-stack images with 11 layers each were taken in parallel with steady-state gas exchange data under a given set of conditions, then the environmental condition was changed, leaf gas exchange was given time to reach a new equilibrium, and then 16 new random positions were visited in random order for imaging. Z-stack images in CZI format were transformed with a maximum intensity projection with ImageJ-Fiji batch scripts and converted to tif format (Supplementary Text S1). The maximum intensity projection created a summarized image from the maximum intensity value of each pixel within the Z-stack for each XY pixel coordinate.

Machine learning tool for detection and segmentation of stomatal pores and cells

A commercially available, browser-based machine-learning image analysis software was trained to automate (1) detection of stomatal pores, stomatal complexes, guard cells, subsidiary cells and (2) quantification of their area, length, and width (Biodock; www.biodock.ai/). The initial model was trained on 62 images by annotating stomatal complexes, guard cells, subsidiary cells, and pore areas with digital outlines. A total of 1,304 labels were made with 227 stomatal complex labels, 428 subsidiary cell labels, 445 guard cell labels, and 204 pore labels. For training, 55 images were used for training and 7 images for testing. The training cycled through random horizontal flip, vertical flip, brightness, contrast, rotation, and rescale augmentations. The training also applied contrast normalization transformations.

To verify that the Biodock outputs were consistent with manual counting, an independent set of 19 images was randomly selected from 9 plants that had not been used for training and represented a range of stomatal densities across various treatment conditions. In each image, stomatal complexes, including 283 subsidiary cells, guard cells, and apertures, were traced by a single operator using ImageJ's polygon selection tool and saved as regions of interest. Each individual object was then compared to its corresponding Biodock selection for area, length, and width. In total, data from 55 stomatal complexes and their components were manually assessed.

Optical tomograghy leaf impressions and imaging

Leaf epidermal impressions were taken using nail polish from the abaxial leaf surface at the same position as leaf gas exchange had been assessed. Impressions were removed from the leaf using double-sided tape and adhered to a microscope slide. Each resulting slide was then imaged with a MarSurf CM Explorer (Mahr) microscope at 4 positions in a transect across the leaf between the midrib and the margin. Each field of view was 0.8 mm × 0.8 mm when imaged with a 20× objective lens (Xie et al. 2021). Stomata in each image were counted to assess stomatal density (SD).

Bootstrap sampling

A preliminary experiment was done to assess how many stomata across a leaf had to be imaged for the mean pore area of that sample to reliably match the true mean pore area of the leaf. When gsw-gasex was at steady-state (0.12 to 0.13 mol m−2 s−1) under “ambient” chamber conditions (CO2 425 µmol mol−1; temp 28 °C; RH 70%; PPFD 1,800 µmol m−2 s−1), images were collected at 27 randomly located positions on one leaf. This resulted in the acquisition of data describing the area of 74 pores. The resampling procedure was to draw with replacement a bootstrap sample from 2 to 74 apertures. For each sample size the simulation was replicated 1,500 times to create a resampled distribution of means and standard deviations. The mean of bootstrap samples, the 95% CI of bootstrap means, the mean of bootstrap sample standard deviations, and the 95% CI of bootstrap sample standard deviations were calculated.

Statistical analysis

Analysis of variance for the block, treatment, and interaction terms was analyzed with the lmerTest function from the lme4 package in R and used an α threshold of 0.05. Tukey-adjusted post-hoc mean separation was done with the emmeans function with the emmeans package. Simple and multiple regression used the lm function. Correlations used a Pearson correlation in the cor function and with complete observations. Stomatal aperture, including closed stomata (i.e. zero area), was not normally distributed, so a Kruskall–Wallis non-parametric rank sum test was performed on the treatment factor using the kruskal.test function. Pairwise post-hoc tests used the pairwise.wilcox.test function and did not have a P-value adjustment.
