Endoscopic Vascular Grading Directs Stepwise Therapy in Hereditary Hemorrhagic Telangiectasia
Authors: Hong‐Gang Duan, Fang Ji, Hui Yuan, Lin‐Rong Li, Ke‐Jia Yu, Zhen‐Yuan Wei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo establish a nasal endoscopic vascular grading system for predicting epistaxis severity and guiding stepwise therapy in hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia (HHT).MethodsA retrospective cohort of 32 HHT patients from 22 families was classified via 1080p endoscopy into three grades: Grade I: Sparse punctate telangiectases; Grade II: Dense confluent vessels; Grade III: Protruding hemangiomas. Demographics, Epistaxis Severity Score (ESS), hemoglobin levels (Hb), systemic vascular lesions, and ENG/ACVRL1 mutations were analyzed. Treatment outcomes were compared across grades.ResultsGrade I accounted for 18.8%, Grade II for 34.4%, and Grade III for 46.9%. Grade III patients showed the highest ESS (6.31 ± 2.13 vs. 1.21 ± 0.92 for Grade I, p < 0.01) and the lowest Hb (97.2 ± 22.4 vs. 129.5 ± 4.9 g/L for Grade I, p < 0.05), and had more systemic lesions (80.0% of Grade III patients had hand/oral/visceral hemangiomas vs. 16.7% in Grade I, p < 0.01). Local therapies or tranexamic acid treatment sufficed for Grade I/II patients, who maintained stable ESS. Among Grade III patients who showed no improvement after treatment with tranexamic acid, bevacizumab (administered at a dose of 5 mg/kg every 3–6 months) improved the ESS and increased the Hb in 62.5% of the patients (5 out of 8), and no severe adverse events occurred.ConclusionThis study preliminarily confirms a significant correlation between nasal endoscopic vascular morphology and HHT epistaxis severity, guiding disease assessment and treatment planning.Level of Evidence3.Keywords: bevacizumab, endoscopic vascular grading, epistaxis, Epistaxis Severity Score, hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia

Introduction

Hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia (HHT) is an autosomal dominant vascular disorder caused by mutations in ENG, ACVRL1, or SMAD4 and other genes, leading to dysregulation of the transforming growth factor‐beta (TGF‐β) signaling pathway [1]. Pathologically, it is characterized by mucocutaneous telangiectases and visceral arteriovenous malformations (AVMs) in the lungs, liver, and brain. The absence of smooth muscle layers and elastic fibers in affected vessels results in impaired contractility and recurrent spontaneous bleeding [2]. Epistaxis occurs in 90%–96% of patients, while gastrointestinal bleeding affects 15%–33% of patients, often leading to chronic anemia or hemorrhagic shock. Epidemiological studies in Europe report an HHT prevalence of 1/5000–1/8000, though systematic data are lacking in Asia [3]. Extrapolating from China's population (1.4 billion) and European prevalence rates, the estimated number of HHT patients in China ranges from 175,000 to 280,000. Most patients with HHT are unaware that they have the disease, and the actual number of patients may be higher [4].

Epistaxis represents the most debilitating clinical manifestation of HHT, affecting 97% of patients, with 49% requiring specialized interventions and 23% necessitating combined therapies [5, 6]. Bleeding frequency intensifies significantly after age 40, and 18% of patients require blood transfusions due to severe anemia (Hb < 80 g/L) [7]. The 2020 international guidelines recommend a stepwise therapeutic approach for epistaxis management, sequenced as follows: moisturizing topic therapy, oral tranexamic acid, nasal ablation therapy, systemic anti‐angiogenic therapies (e.g., bevacizumab), nasal septal dermoplasty, and Young's procedure (nasal nostril closure) [1]. However, determining optimal treatment strategies poses a significant clinical challenge. If all patients initiate treatment with topical moisturizers, those with severe epistaxis inevitably experience delayed interventions. The development of a validated dual‐function tool for objective epistaxis severity assessment and therapeutic guidance represents an urgent clinical need.

The Epistaxis Severity Score (ESS)—which quantifies bleeding frequency, duration, volume, anemia severity, and healthcare utilization—remains the primary tool for assessing epistaxis severity [8]. However, ESS has notable limitations in Chinese populations: (1) recall bias introduces significant scoring error, especially among the elderly and those with a lower level of education; (2) higher transfusion thresholds in China (Hb < 60 g/L for chronic anemia) lead to underestimation of bleeding burden via ESS anemia metrics [9]. Hb is an objective indicator, but it may also be influenced by other systemic diseases. ESS and Hb share a key limitation: they exhibit rapid fluctuations in response to changes in patient status, including acute bleeding exacerbations or blood transfusion events. In contrast, nasal vascular changes progress relatively slowly and can effectively reflect the long‐term severity of nasal bleeding in patients over an extended timeframe. These gaps underscore the urgent need for objective grading systems to optimize HHT management.

Recent imaging studies reveal progressive nasal mucosal vascular remodeling in HHT, which evolve from punctate telangiectases to confluent vascular plaques and protruding hemangiomas [10]. A cross‐sectional study demonstrated significantly higher ESS scores in patients with hemangioma‐type lesions compared to those with telangiectases alone, suggesting a link between vascular morphology and bleeding severity [11]. Building on this evidence, we hypothesized that endoscopic vascular grading could objectively stratify epistaxis severity and guide stepwise therapy in HHT. By analyzing endoscopic imaging, ESS scores, and treatment responses in 32 patients from 22 HHT families, we established a modified three‐tier grading system (Grades I–III) based on Reh D D's classification [12], aiming to address the limitations of subjective scoring in clinical decision‐making.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Participants

This single‐center retrospective cohort study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (Approval No: 2025‐0649). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Patients diagnosed with HHT between December 2019 and February 2025 were enrolled according to the following criteria:

Diagnostic criteria: Fulfillment of ≥ 3 Curaçao criteria: spontaneous recurrent epistaxis, mucocutaneous telangiectases, visceral arteriovenous malformations (AVMs), or family history of HHT; Confirmation of pathogenic ACVRL1/ENG/SMAD4 mutations via genetic testing [2].

Exclusion criteria: Coexisting bleeding disorders (e.g., hemophilia); Contraindications to bevacizumab, including uncontrolled hypertension and proteinuria > 1 g/24 h.

Data Collection and Assessment

Baseline data: Age, sex, ESS, and Hb were recorded.

Nasal vascular grading:

Equipment: An Olympus CV‐190 high‐definition endoscope was used, featuring a resolution of 1920 × 1080 and a cold light source with an intensity of ≥ 1800 Lux. Procedure: Two independent rhinologists evaluated the nasal septum and lateral walls.

Grade I: Sparse punctate telangiectases; Grade II: Dense confluent vascular plaques, involving ≥ 50% of the observed area; Grade III: Protruding mucosal hemangiomas, presenting with raised berry‐like telangiectasia spots (Figure 1).

(A) Grade I: Sparse punctate telangiectases. (B) Grade II: Dense confluent vascular plaques, involving ≥ 50% of the observed area. (C) Grade III: Protruding mucosal hemangiomas, presenting as raised berry‐like telangiectasia spots.

During the research process, to minimize potential bias, independent reviewers (two attending rhinologists) were maintained in a blinded state to other clinical characteristics of the patients—including disease severity, prior treatment history, and other relevant metrics—when conducting vascular grading assessments. None of the reviewers were involved in the clinical management of the patients; instead, they solely evaluated the relevant imaging and data in strict accordance with pre‐established vascular grading criteria.

Systemic Vascular Lesion Evaluation

Cutaneous/oral telangiectases: Counts were performed under standardized lighting conditions. For the hands, the fingers and palms were examined; for the oral cavity, the tongue tip and lower lip mucosa were inspected. If the patient presents with hemoptysis, laryngoscopy under topical anesthesia is performed to visualize any vascular malformations in the throat (Figure 2).

(A) Vascular malformations on the laryngeal surface of the epiglottis. (B) Vascular malformations of bilateral fingers. (C) Vascular malformations of the lower lip.

Visceral AVMs: Brain: Contrast‐enhanced MRI was carried out using a 3.0 T Siemens Magnetom Skyra. Lung: CT angiography with a slice thickness of 1 mm was employed. Liver: Abdominal ultrasound was conducted using a GE Logiq E10 probe. Gastrointestinal: Endoscopy was performed with an Olympus CF‐HQ190. Imaging interpretation: Two blinded radiologists independently evaluated the scans. In case of discrepancies, a consensus was reached through discussion.

Genetic Testing Protocol

DNA extraction: Peripheral blood (5 mL) was collected, and genomic DNA was isolated using the QIAamp DNA Blood Midi Kit (Qiagen). The isolated DNA had a concentration of ≥ 20 ng/μL and an A260/A280 ratio within the range of 1.8–2.0.

Library preparation: DNA was fragmented into 200–250 bp fragments using a Bioruptor Pico. Subsequently, end repair, A‐tailing, adapter ligation (using the KAPA HyperPlus Kit), and hybridization capture (with the xGen Exome Panel) were performed.

Sequencing: The prepared libraries were first validated using a Qsep100 fragment analyzer and StepOne qPCR. Then, sequencing was carried out on an Illumina HiSeq X Ten with paired‐end 150 bp reads (PE150), aiming for a sequencing depth of ≥ 100× and a Q30 score of ≥ 85%.

Bioinformatics: Raw data were filtered using AfterQC and aligned to the hg19 reference genome with BWA‐MEM. Variants (single nucleotide variants and insertions/deletions) were called using GATK and filtered based on a minor allele frequency (MAF) of less than 1% in the 1000 Genomes, gnomAD, and ExAC databases. Pathogenicity of the variants was assessed using dbNSFP, HGMD, ClinVar, and ACMG guidelines. Candidate variants were further validated by bidirectional Sanger sequencing with an ABI 3500xL sequencer, and the results were analyzed using CodonCode Aligner. Variants with a peak height ≥ 20% of the wild‐type were considered valid.

Stepwise Therapeutic Strategy

Treatment protocols were designed following WHO guidelines [1]: Grade I: Observation was adopted, with endoscopic follow‐up conducted every 3 months. For patients with symptoms of epistaxis or nasal dryness, saline nasal sprays are recommended. Grade II: Saline nasal sprays were performed three times daily, combined with oral administration of tranexamic acid at a dosage of 500 mg twice a day for 3 months. Grade III: Initially, the Grade II therapy was administered for 3 months. For non‐responders, intravenous bevacizumab was given at a dosage of 5 mg/kg every 3–6 months. During the treatment, monthly blood pressure monitoring and urine dipstick testing were performed. If the urine dipstick test was positive, 24‐h protein quantification was carried out.

Statistical Analysis

SPSS 26.0 was utilized for the following statistical analyses: ① One‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed to compare the differences in age, ESS score, and Hb among different grades; ② Chi‐square test was applied to analyze the distribution of gender, gene mutations, and superficial hemangiomas; ③ Spearman correlation test was conducted to evaluate the correlation between blood vessel grading and the severity of epistaxis, as well as the Hb; ④ Paired t‐test was performed to compare the changes in ESS score and Hb before and after treatment with tranexamic acid and bevacizumab. A significance threshold of p < 0.05 (two‐tailed test) was set for all analyses.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

A total of 32 clinically diagnosed HHT patients were analyzed (Table 1). The cohort included 20 females (62.5%) and 12 males (37.5%), with a mean age of 49.0 ± 14.2 years (range: 20–73). Age and Sex Distribution

Age progression: Grade III patients were significantly older (59.1 ± 10.7 years) compared to Grade II (49.8 ± 6.9) and Grade I (27.3 ± 4.2) (p < 0.05, ANOVA), indicating age‐dependent vascular severity.

Sex distribution: No significant differences in sex ratios were observed across grades (male: 37.5%, female: 62.5%; p > 0.05, χ 2).

Genetic Mutations

Among 23 patients who underwent genetic testing: ENG mutations: 7 cases (30.4%), ACVRL1 mutations: 15 cases (65.2%), mutations in different families are located at different loci. No pathogenic mutations: 1 case. Mutation types showed no grade‐specific association (p > 0.05, χ 2).

Systemic Vascular Lesions

The incidence of mucocutaneous hemangiomas (hands, oral cavity, and viscera) increased significantly with vascular grade: Grade I: 16.7% (1/6), Grade II: 36.4% (3/11), and Grade III: 80.0% (12/15, p < 0.01, χ 2).

Epistaxis Severity and Hb

ESS scores: Grade I: 1.21 ± 0.92, Grade II: 3.87 ± 1.00, and Grade III: 6.31 ± 2.13 (p < 0.01, ANOVA). A strong positive correlation was observed between vascular grade and ESS (r = 0.76, p < 0.01). Hb: Grade I: 129.5 ± 4.9 g/L, Grade II: 126.0 ± 17.7 g/L, and Grade III: 97.2 ± 22.4 g/L (p < 0.05, ANOVA). Vascular grade inversely correlated with hemoglobin (r = −0.562, p < 0.001).

Therapeutic Outcomes

Grades I: Observe, there was no epistaxis during the observation period. Grades II: Conservative management (nasal saline + tranexamic acid) effectively controlled epistaxis. Grade III: Bevacizumab treatment resulted in a 62.5% response rate (5/8), with significant reductions in the ESS and improvements in Hb compared to tranexamic acid (p < 0.05; Table 2, Figures 3 and 4). No severe adverse events were observed. One non‐responder demonstrated improvement after receiving estrogen therapy for climacteric syndrome, with her ESS score decreasing from 6.8 to 4.7.

Discussion

This study focused on patients with HHT, aiming to explore the correlation between the morphological characteristics of blood vessels under nasal endoscopy and the severity of epistaxis, and to evaluate the correlation's guiding value for the selection of individualized treatment plans. Through in‐depth analysis of 32 clinically diagnosed patients, a series of clinically significant results were obtained, providing a new perspective for the diagnosis and treatment of HHT.

Comparison With Existing Classification Systems

Prior studies have established foundational frameworks for nasal vascular assessment in HHT. Mahoney and Shapshay [13] proposed a three‐grade system: (I) isolated punctate telangiectasias, (II) diffuse interconnecting vasculature, and (III) large solitary arteriovenous malformations, demonstrating laser hemostasis efficacy in Grades I/III but limited response in Grade II. Reh et al. [12] developed a more comprehensive scoring system integrating vascular morphology, distribution, thickness, perforation, and crusting, which correlated strongly with ESS.

Our classification system, derived from intergenerational clinical observations of a single family, uniquely emphasizes age‐related vascular progression. Unlike prior systems, it excludes septal perforation and crusting—rare in Chinese patients with minimal surgical history. In patients with nasal septal perforation and crusting, hemangiomatous changes remain visible in the nasal lateral wall mucosa and residual nasal septal mucosa. These changes show no significant difference from those in patients without nasal septal perforation and do not affect the classification of such patients. This simplification enhances clinical applicability while maintaining strong ESS correlation, offering better temporal continuity than Mahoney's system and greater simplicity than Reh's.

Patient Characteristics and Genetic Insights

Grade III patients exhibited a significantly higher mean age than Grades I/II, aligning with HHT's age‐progressive epistaxis pattern. However, age overlap between Grades II/III suggests unexplained intra‐age heterogeneity. Gender ratios showed no intergrade differences, consistent with prior findings ruling out gender bias in HHT vascular pathology [14].

Genetic analysis of 23 patients revealed no significant differences in ACVRL1/ENG mutation types across vascular grades. This may stem from the small sample size, which limited statistical power to detect subtle associations. Alternatively, mutation types may not directly dictate vascular morphological severity or epistaxis intensity, suggesting a potential role for environmental factors. Further research is needed to elucidate the synergistic effects between genetic and environmental influences [15].

Treatment Efficacy and Clinical Implications

Treatment strategies were stratified by grade: Grade I: Observation and reproductive counseling, as bleeding is minimal and diagnosis often follows familial genetic testing. For patients with symptoms of epistaxis or nasal dryness, saline nasal sprays are recommended. Grade II: Saline nasal sprays were performed three times daily, and oral tranexamic acid for severe cases, with stable clinical outcomes during follow‐up. Grade III: Bevacizumab demonstrated 62.5% efficacy, though 3 cases were unresponsive; one improved with estrogen therapy for menopausal syndrome, underscoring individualized treatment needs. The conservative bevacizumab dose (5 mg/kg, 3–6 months) may limit efficacy, warranting dose/frequency optimization [6, 16].

Compared to WHO's stepwise therapy, our vascular morphology‐guided approach bypasses ineffective initial steps, improving treatment precision and patient compliance. Based on intrafamilial age–group observations, we hypothesize Grade III (prominent hemangiomas) represents progressive Grade II (diffuse vascular dilatation), as both show diffuse vascular networks. Thus, Grade III requires more aggressive initial treatment.

Role of Surgical Intervention

Given that nasal laser equipment is rarely available in Chinese hospitals, patients in this group (Grade III) had undergone multiple sessions of general anesthesia—assisted bipolar electrocoagulation for hemostasis prior to admission. However, the therapeutic effect was short‐lived, with recurrent bleeding typically occurring within 1–2 months. Therefore, we did not perform further surgical intervention on these patients, though we do not negate the role of surgery in the treatment of HHT.

For patients receiving bevacizumab injections, patient feedback indicated that although nasal bleeding was significantly reduced after injection, recurrent bleeding persisted from hemangiomas in specific locations, leading them to request surgical treatment. For such patients, nasal laser therapy or electrocoagulation hemostasis may serve as excellent treatment options. In cases where both repeated laser electrocoagulation and bevacizumab therapy fail to control recurrent nasal bleeding, nasal septal skin grafting emerges as a crucial alternative. While Young's procedure yields significant therapeutic effects, it substantially impacts patients' quality of life [17]. Intermittent anterior nostril occlusion with adhesive tape or packing with ointment—impregnated cotton balls can be used as viable alternatives [18].

Limitations and Future Directions

A key limitation is that our patients were recruited exclusively from Zhejiang Province, China, with potential regional specificity leading to distinct characteristics compared to other geographic cohorts. All received initial diagnosis at our institution, having only undergone routine symptomatic management for nasal bleeding (with infrequent transfusions) beforehand. Notably, our cohort's genetic mutation profile—predominated by ACVRL1 mutations—differs significantly from overseas reports, further reflecting regional distinctions. These geographic features may limit our grading system to greater applicability in Chinese patients, particularly those from similar regions; further validation is needed for overseas or geographically diverse populations.

Another limitation is the lack of molecular mechanism exploration. Larger, geographically diverse cohorts are required to generalize findings, clarify gene–environment interactions, and develop objective biomarkers (e.g., VEGF) to supplement endoscopic assessments, thereby enhancing epistaxis severity prediction.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study preliminarily confirms a significant correlation between nasal endoscopic vascular morphology and HHT epistaxis severity, guiding disease assessment and treatment planning.
