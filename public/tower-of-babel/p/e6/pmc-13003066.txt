K63-linked ubiquitylation of RMI1 by RNF8 is essential to its recruitment to stalled forks
Authors: Rimpy Arun, Sadananda Kumbhakar, Tavleen Kaur, Simran Kaur, Sagar Sengupta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
RMI1 forms an evolutionarily conserved complex known as the BTR complex along with BLM and TOP3A. This complex plays a crucial role in DNA repair, particularly in the resolution of Holiday Junction like intermediates which are formed during homologous recombination. Despite the crucial role of this complex, very little is known about the recruitment of RMI1 to the sites of DNA damage like stalled forks. Here we demonstrate that RMI1 is recruited to sites of replication stress in a ubiquitin-dependent manner. We identified an essential role of the RING-type E3 ubiquitin ligase, RNF8, in the recruitment of RMI1 to the sites of stalled replication through K63-linked polyubiquitylation at three lysine residues, Lys428, Lys453 and Lys566. This modification is crucial for the proper localization of RMI1 along with the entire BTR complex at these sites of DNA damage, thereby ensuring its downstream functions. Further, the ubiquitylation of RMI1 is essential for replication fork recovery. Taken together, this study deciphers how RNF8-dependent ubiquitylation cascade is recruited to the chromatin where it is involved in preserving genome stability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-026-06156-7.

Introduction

In humans, there are five homologs of RecQ helicases. Germline defects in four of these helicases (RECQ1, BLM, WRN and RECQL4) lead to distinct syndromes, namely RECON syndrome, Bloom syndrome (BS), Werner syndrome (WS), and Rothmund–Thomson syndrome (RTS), respectively [1, 2]. BS arises from biallelic mutations of the BLM gene. BS patients and patient-derived fibroblasts and lymphoblastoid lines show hyper-recombination, which consequently causes chromosomal instability. BS patients are genetically predisposed to the early onset of multiple types of cancers [3, 4].

BLM, along with TOP3A and RMI1, forms the evolutionarily conserved BTR complex, which is essential for processing homologous recombination (HR) intermediates by preventing genetic crossovers to maintain genomic integrity [5]. Apart from BLM, mutations in RMI1, RMI2 and TOP3A have also been linked to multiple human genome instability syndromes which are collectively termed Bloom-like Syndrome, thereby highlighting the crucial role of this complex in maintaining genomic integrity [6, 7]. Mechanistically, the BTR complex facilitates the dissolution of double Holliday Junctions (dHJs) arising during HR [8–10]. Using an array of biochemical studies, the intricate cooperativity between the three members of the BTR complex has been well studied [11]. The role of RMI1 in the formation of the BTR complex is also known [12]. Evidence exists that once RMI1 relocates to the site of damage, it is involved in multiple DNA metabolism processes to resolve the replication stress [13] and thereby contributes to DNA repair [14, 15]. Hence knockdown of RMI1 impairs the DNA repair of the stress generated during stalled replication [16] .

It has been known that BLM and TOP3A reside in PML NBs in absence of DNA damage [17, 18]. In fact, BLM is known to be constitutively phosphorylated at Ser646 in the PML NBs [19] and gets transported to the sites of stalled replication via RNF8/RNF168 mediated ubiquitylation [20]. Sumoylation of BLM has also been proposed to be the factor which dictates its interaction with RAD51 at the stalled forks [21]. However unlike BLM, much less is known about the RMI1 functions within the cellular context - both in asynchronous culture or after exposure to HU which causes stalling of the replication fork.

In this study we demonstrate that RMI1 undergoes K63-linked polyubiquitylation mediated by RNF8, in response to HU induced replication stress and this facilitates its recruitment to stalled replication forks. We identified the three lysine residues on RMI1 that are targeted by RNF8 in cells and demonstrate that the ubiquitylation on these residues is necessary for proper localization of the BTR complex to the sites of stalled replication. Furthermore, we provide evidence that RNF8 mediated ubiquitylation of RMI1 is critical for the maintenance of replication fork progression as well as facilitating fork restart following DNA damage. Thus the study highlights the importance of this post-translational modification on RMI1 for efficient localization to the sites of stalled forks.

Materials and methods

Reagents, antibodies, recombinants and primers

All antibodies used are listed in Table S1. All recombinant DNAs used have been described in Table S2. All reagents (including chemicals, oligonucleotides, cell lines and other materials) used are described in Table S3. All primers are described in Table S4.

Cell culture treatments

HEK293T, U2OS, GM03509 GFP Clone 100, GM03509 GFP BLM Clone 4.3.4 cells were maintained with DMEM (Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, L-glutamine, and penicillin-streptomycin solution. U2OS shRNF8 and U2OS shRNF168 were maintained according to [22, 23]. Hydroxyurea (HU) treatment was for 16 h with 1mM concentration. Plasmid and siRNA transfections were carried out with Lipofectamine 2000, according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. 200 picomoles of siRNA and/or 1–3 µg plasmids were transfected in a 1:3 DNA: Lipofectamine ratio. Lysates or the slides for immunofluorescence were made 48 h post-transfection. Cells were always maintained at 37 °C and 5% CO2.

Stable cell line generation

Viral particles were produced by co-transfection of lentiviral packaging plasmids (pMD2.G, psPAX2) and lentiviral expression plasmids (pLKO.1 shRMI, pLVX-IRES-hygro-GFP RMI1 WT, pLVX-IRES-hygro-GFP RMI1 3KR, pLVX-IRES-hygro-Flag RMI1 WT, pLVX-IRES-hygro-Flag RMI1 3KR) in a molar ratio of (1:3:4) in HEK293T cells using Lipofectamine 2000 (in 1:2 ratio) as the transfection reagent, in 10 cm plates. The media was changed 6 h post-transfection. Viral supernatants were collected at 24- and 48-hours post-transfection and filtered through a 0.45 μm filter. Virus was concentrated by using PEG 8000 (in 1:3 ratio) overnight at 4 °C, followed by centrifugation at 1600 x g for 1 h. The viral pellet was resuspended in 1XPBS. Transduction was carried out in U2OS and HeLa cells in 12-well cluster plates using polybrene (8 µg/ml). Both U2OS and Hela shControl and shRMI1 cells were selected and maintained in 2 µg/ml of puromycin. U2OS shRMI1 GFP RMI1 WT, U2OS shRMI1 GFP RMI1 3KR, U2OS shRMI1 Flag RMI1 WT, U2OS shRMI1 Flag RMI1 3KR cells were selected and maintained in 200 µg/ml of hygromycin. Hela shBLM cells were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, L-glutamine, and penicillin-streptomycin, containing 0.5 µg/ml puromycin.

Immunoblotting

For western blotting, cell lysates were prepared using M2 lysis buffer (1mM Tris-Cl, pH 7.4, 150mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 0.5mM EDTA, and 0.5mM EGTA, supplemented with 1x Protease Inhibitor Cocktail [PIC]) or RIPA buffer (1mM Tris–Cl, pH 7.8, 150mM NaCl, 1% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS, and 2% Triton X-100, supplemented with 1x PIC). Lysates were mixed with SDS-PAGE loading buffer (375mM Tris-Cl, pH 6.8, 6% SDS, 48% glycerol, 9% β-mercaptoethanol, and bromophenol blue) and boiled for 10 min. Samples were then resolved by SDS–PAGE or Nu-PAGE 4–12% gels using 1x MOPS buffer and analyzed by immunoblotting using specific antibodies. Typically, 50 µg of cell lysates was used for direct westerns or input for immunoprecipitation reaction.

Immunoprecipitation

All immunoprecipitation reaction were carried out in native (non-denaturing) conditions. For each immunoprecipitation, 900 µg lysate was incubated with 1 µg of specific antibody (anti-RMI1 or the corresponding IgG rabbit control) overnight at 4 °C on an end-to-end rotor. Post-incubation, 30 µl of Protein G Sepharose bead slurry (equilibrated with 1XPBS + 0.1% NP-40) was added for additional 3 h. Following binding of the Protein G Sepharose beads to antigen-antibody complex, the beads were washed three times with 1 ml of 1XPBS containing 0.1% NP-40. 6XSDS-PAGE loading buffer [62.5 mm Tris-HCl pH 6.8, 10% glycerol, 2% (w/v) SDS, 5% β-mercaptoethanol (βME), 0.01 mg/ml bromophenol blue] was added to the beads and boiled for 12 min. Samples were resolved either in 10% SDS-PAGE gel or Nu-PAGE 4–12% gels using 1XMOPS buffer and transferred to nitrocellulose membrane for subsequent immunoblotting.

Immunofluorescence

Cells were seeded on 22 mm coverslips in 6 well cluster plates. Cells were treated with hypotonic lysis buffer (10mM Tris-HCl, pH7.4, 2.5mM MgCl2, 1mM PMSF and 0.5% Nonidet P40) on ice. Cells are washed with 1XPBS and fixed with ice-cold absolute ethanol for 5 min on ice, followed by permeabilization with 1XPBS containing 0.2% Triton X-100. Cells were blocked with 10% Normal Chicken Serum and washed with PBST (1XPBS containing 0.2% Tween-20), followed by incubation with the respective primary antibodies for 2 h in humidified chamber at room temperature. Cells were washed with PBST, followed by incubation with respective secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Coverslips were mounted with mounting medium containing DAPI. Cells were visualized and imaged at 63X/1.4 magnification (using an oil immersion objective) in a Zeiss LSM980 Meta confocal microscope with Airyscan,, using the laser lines 405/561/647 nm.

Protein purification and in vitro interaction

E. coli BL21 DE3 Cells were used for all GST and His-tagged protein expression. Proteins were purified by binding with either Glutathione-S-Sepharose (for GST proteins) and Nickel-NTA beads (for His tagged proteins) using standard protocols. Bound proteins were eluted with a gradient of glutathione (for GST-tagged proteins) or imidazole (for His-tagged proteins). Eluted fractions were pooled and dialysed. For in vitro interactions, 20nM of Glutathione-S-Sepharose bead-bound proteins (GST, GST RNF8, GST RNF168) were incubated with 20nM soluble His-tagged RMI1 protein for 1 h at 4 °C. Post-incubation, the mixture was centrifuged, and beads were washed with GST Buffer (50mM Tris-Cl, pH 7.5, 10mM MgCl2, 100mM KCl, 10% glycerol, 0.5% NP-40). 6XSDS sample buffer [62.5 mm Tris-HCl pH 6.8, 10% glycerol, 2% (w/v) SDS, 5% β-mercaptoethanol (βME), 0.01 mg/ml bromophenol blue] was added to the beads and boiled for 12 min. Samples were resolved in a 10% SDS-PAGE gel and transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane for subsequent immunoblotting.

In vitro ubiquitylation assays

Ubiquitylation assay was adapted from [20]. The assay was carried out using 20nM of His-tagged RMI1 WT and its mutants. Assays were set up using recombinant His RNF8/ GST RNF168 (40nM and 100nM, respectively) as the E3 enzymes, 0.2µM of indicated E2 enzymes Ubc5a or Ubc13, 0.0125µM E1 enzyme and 1µM of ubiquitin or its mutants at 37 °C for 3 h. The reactions were carried out in 50mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 1mM DTT and initiated by the addition of 2mM ATP and 5mM MgCl2. Two types of ubiquitin mutants were used: “R” mutants (where lysine at a particular position in ubiquitin has been mutated to arginine on ubiquitin) and “O” mutants (where only one lysine is present in ubiquitin, while rest of the six lysines are changed to alanine). The reactions were stopped by boiling with SDS-PAGE loading buffer (375mM Tris–Cl, pH 6.8, 6% SDS, 48% glycerol, 9% β-mercaptoethanol, and bromophenol blue (βME), and samples were run on a 10% SDS-PAGE gel or Nu-PAGE 4–12% gels in 1XMOPS buffer, transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane, and probed with the indicated antibodies.

Subcellular fractionation

Cells were grown until they reached 80%-90% of confluency. Lysis was performed using Buffer I (50mM HEPES, pH7.5, 150mM NaCl, 1mM EDTA, 0.05% NP-40, supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors) for 5 min on ice. The lysate was then centrifuged at 1575 x g for 5 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatant was collected as the soluble fraction. The pellet was washed once with Buffer I, followed by treating the cells with extraction buffer II (50mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 150mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS, and protease and phosphate inhibitors). Following 20 min of incubation on ice, the extracts were centrifuged at 25,200 x g for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected as the ribonucleoprotein-enriched fraction, representing the chromatin-bound fraction.

DNA fiber assay

To assess nascent replication fork velocity, cells were seeded in a 6-well plate cluster and were pulse labelled with 25µM CldU followed by 250µM IdU for 20 min each. For replication fork restart assay, cells were labelled with 25µM CldU for 20 min, treated with 2mM HU for 2 h, followed by labelling with 250µM IdU for 30 min after washing. For the replication fork protection assay, cells were labelled with 25µM CldU followed by 250µM IdU for 30 min each, followed by treatment with 2mM HU for 5 h after washing. DNA fiber spreads were prepared following the protocol from [24]. After trypsinization, cells were resuspended in ice-cold PBS, labelled and unlabeled cells were mixed in a ratio of 2:1 and stretched onto glass slides in DNA lysis buffer (200mM Tris-HCl, pH7.4, 50mM EDTA, 0.5% SDS). The stretched DNA fibers were fixed overnight at 4 °C in 3:1 methanol to acetic acid solution, followed by denaturation with 2.5 M HCl for 1 h. After 3 washes with 1XPBS, slides were blocked with blocking solution (2% BSA in 0.1% Tween-20 PBS) for 45 min. Slides were incubated for 2.5 h with primary antibodies, washed followed by incubation with the respective secondary antibodies. Slides were then air-dried and mounted using ProLong Gold Antifade. Fibers were imaged using Zeiss LSM980 microscope with 63x/1.4 oil objective lens, using the laser lines 488/561 nm. Data analysis was done using Ziess Zen 3.8 software.

iPOND assay

iPOND was adapted from [25]. 70–80 million exponentially growing U2OS shRMI1 GFP RMI1 WT and GFP RMI1 3KR cells were pulse labelled with 10µM EdU for 15 min, followed by treatment with 1mM HU for 16 h after washing. Alternatively, cells were washed and subjected to the indicated treatments after labelling with EdU. For chase conditions, EdU was chased with thymidine (10µM) for 5 h. Cells were then either left untreated, treated with 1mM HU for 16 h, or subjected to HU treatment followed by a thymidine chase (10µM) for 5 h prior to iPOND analysis. Cells were fixed with 1% formaldehyde for 20 min, quenched with 0.125 M glycine, washed thrice with 1XPBS. Cells were permeabilized using 0.25% Triton X-100 in 1XPBS for 30 min at room temperature, followed by PBS washes twice. EdU was conjugated to biotin azide through a click reaction consisting of 20µM biotin azide, 10mM sodium ascorbate and 2mM CuSO4 in PBS for 2 h at room temperature. Cells were washed with 1XPBS, lysed in RIPA lysis buffer (50mM Tris-HCl pH 8, 150mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS, 1mM EDTA, 10µM sodium fluoride, 100µM sodium orthovanadate, 20mM sodium pyrophosphate, 7mM β-glycerophosphate, 1mM DTT, and 1XPIC). The lysate was sonicated using Biorupter Pulse Sonicator (Diagenode), 30 s on and 30 s off for 30 cycles. Lysates were centrifuged at 15,000 x g for 30 min at 4 °C. Supernatants were diluted with 1:1 ratio of RIPA lysis buffer containing 1XPIC and incubated overnight with streptavidin agarose beads to capture Biotin-tagged EdU-labelled DNA-protein complexes. Beads were washed with RIPA lysis buffer twice, followed by washes with low salt buffer (20mM Tris, pH 8.0, 2mM EDTA, 150mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100), high salt buffer (20mM Tris, pH 8.0, 2mM EDTA, 500mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100), and RIPA lysis buffer again. The captured proteins were eluted by boiling the beads with 2XLaemmli buffer for 20 min and run on Nu-PAGE 4–12% gels in 1XMOPS buffer, transferred to nitrocellulose membrane and probed with indicated antibodies.

Results

RMI1 undergoes ubiquitylation mediated by RNF8 after replication arrest

BLM is known to be recruited to the sites of replication stress through the activity of two chromatin bound E3 ligases RNF8 and RNF168 [20]. Given the fact that RMI1 is part of the BTR complex, we hypothesized that RNF8 and RNF168 may also regulate the recruitment of RMI1 to the stalled forks through ubiquitin-dependent DDR response pathway. To determine whether RMI1 is indeed ubiquitylated in response to HU induced replication stress, HEK293T cells were either mock treated or exposed to HU and immunoprecipitation was carried out using anti-RMI1 antibody. Probing of the immunoprecipitate revealed a significant increase in RMI1 polyubiquitylation following HU treatment (Fig. 1a). To determine whether RNF8 and RNF168 are indeed the designated E3 ligases for RMI1, in vitro pull-down experiments were carried out with purified recombinant proteins (Fig. S1a). Direct interactions between RMI1 and both the E3 ligases were observed (Figs. S1b, c), which was subsequently confirmed by the colocalization of RNF8/RNF168 with RMI1 as well as TOP3A in cells only after HU-induced replication stress and not in cells growing under asynchronous conditions (Figs. 1b, S2a–c).

We next wanted to determine the parameters which mediated RMI1 ubiquitylation. Both RNF8 and RNF168 wild-type (WT), but not their catalytic dead (CD) counterparts, facilitated RMI1 ubiquitylation using Ubc13 as the E2 enzyme (Figs. 1c, d). In vitro ubiquitylation assays revealed that RNF8 ubiquitylated RMI1 in a Ubc13-dependent manner (Fig. S2d). Using U2OS cells expressing doxycycline (Dox) inducible shRNA to either RNF8 or RNF168, we observed that cells lacking RNF8 failed to efficiently promote polyubiquitylation of RMI1, whereas cells lacking RNF168 still maintained a robust ubiquitylation pattern following HU treatment, thereby indicating RNF8 to be the major E3 ligase for ubiquitylation of RMI (Figs. 1e, f). Subsequently, when siRNA-mediated shutdown of RNF8 was carried out on U2OS shRNF168 cells (+ Dox condition, i.e., when RNF168 was already ablated), the ubiquitylation of RMI1 was also abrogated (Fig. 1g). These results together suggest that while RNF8 is the primary E3 ligase, RNF168 plays a secondary effect with respect to the ubiquitylation of RMI1 in vivo. In contrast to RMI1, both RMI2 and TOP3A showed a constitutive ubiquitylation pattern, with RMI2 showing mono-ubiquitylation and TOP3A showing poly-ubiquitination. However, ubiquitylation of RMI2 and TOP3A were independent of either RNF8 or RNF168 and not dependent on HU treatment (Figs. S3a–f).

RNF8-dependent RMI1 ubiquitylation via K63-linkage facilitates its recruitment to replication damage sites

RNF8 is known to catalyze ubiquitylation of its substrates via K11 or K63 linkages [26]. To determine the linkage via which RNF8 ubiquitylates RMI1, in vitro ubiquitylation assays with wildtype (WT) ubiquitin, or its “R” and “O” mutants were carried out. Usage of K63R mutant ubiquitin resulted in loss of polyubiquitylation of RMI1 (Fig. 2a), while K63O ubiquitin mutant showed the robust ubiquitylation of RMI1 (Fig. S4a), indicating that RNF8 ubiquitylated RMI1 via K63-linkage. Enhanced K63-linked ubiquitylation of RMI1 was observed only upon HU treatment and not in asynchronous cultures. Hence, robust ubiquitylation was detected in HU-treated cells expressing the K63O ubiquitin mutant, while it was abolished in cells expressing the K63R mutant (Fig. 2b, right). Co-localization of RMI1 foci with K63-linked ubiquitin chains only upon HU treatment was also observed (Figs. 2c, S4b). Similar co-localization post HU treatment was also observed for TOP3A (Fig. S4c), possibly because of the presence of the entire BTR complex at the stalled forks.

Since K63-linked ubiquitin chains predominantly facilitate the recruitment of repair proteins to the DNA damage sites, we sought to investigate the role of RNF8/RNF168 ubiquitin cascade in regulating the recruitment of RMI1 upon HU treatment. Depletion of RNF8 or RNF168 significantly reduced the recruitment of RMI1 as well as BLM to the chromatin, as also observed when the E3 ligases were depleted simultaneously (Fig. 2d). To further validate the localization of RMI1 vis-à-vis the presence or absence of the two E3 ligases, immunofluorescence was carried out in doxycycline inducible U2OS shRNF8 and U2OS shRNF168 cells (in + Dox conditions). In these experiments 53BP1 (which is a known target of RNF8/RNF168) was used as a positive control to validate the knockdown of RNF8 and RNF168 [23, 27]. The loss of either RNF8 or RNF168 (i.e. in + Dox condition) resulted in decreased RMI1 foci formation in response to HU damage (Figs. 2e, h). Notably, the absence of RMI1 and BLM foci correlated with a failure to form TOP3A foci, confirming the functional co-dependency of the recruitment of BTR complex members to the stalled forks (Figs. S4d–g).

RNF8 mediated ubiquitylation of RMI1 at K428, K453, and K566 facilitates its recruitment to sites of stalled replication arrest

To understand the biological functions of RNF8/RNF168-dependent ubiquitylation of RMI1, it was essential to identify the specific sites of ubiquitylation on RMI1. To narrow down the lysine residues within RMI1 that could serve as potential ubiquitylation targets, PhosphositePlus (https://www.phosphosite.org/homeAction.action) was used. PhosphositePlus, highlighted twelve lysines at positions 109, 125, 130, 205, 334, 376, 387, 428, 453, 566, 573, and 580 as potential ubiquitylation sites in RMI1 (Fig. 3a). To investigate whether one or more of these lysine residues were ubiquitylated by RNF8, the twelve recombinant mutant His RMI1 proteins (where the lysines were changed to arginines) were purified (Fig. S5a) and used as the substrates for in vitro ubiquitylation reactions in parallel with wildtype RMI1. K428R, K453R, and K566R mutants of RMI1 showed a partial reduction in the level of RNF8-mediated RMI1 polyubiquitylation (Fig. 3b). Subsequently, RMI1 2KR (both K453 and K566 mutated), and 3KR (all K428, K453, and K566 mutated) were generated and used as substrates for in vitro ubiquitylation reactions. RNF8-dependent ubiquitylation was completely ablated in the 3KR mutant, indicating that RNF8 targets K428, K453, and K566 in RMI1 for ubiquitylation (Figs. 3b, S5b–d).

Next, to determine whether the three identified lysine residues are indeed ubiquitylated in vivo after stalling of the replication forks, U2OS cell line with stable knockdown of endogenous RMI1 protein was created and subsequently rescued with either Flag or GFP-tagged wildtype RMI1 or with its corresponding 3KR mutant (Figs. 3c, left, S6a, left). Using whole cell extracts, we first established that cells expressing 3KR RMI1 mutant retained the ability to form the BTR complex, as confirmed by co-immunoprecipitation of Flag-tagged RMI1 WT and RMI1 3KR mutant (Fig. S6a, right). Immunoprecipitation assay revealed that cells expressing the RMI 3KR mutant showed almost complete loss in ubiquitylation levels of RMI1 (Fig. 3c, right). Furthermore, subcellular fractionation revealed that compared to ubiquitylated wildtype RMI1, the non-ubiquitylated 3KR mutant showed diminished recruitment of the entire BTR complex onto the chromatin fraction (Fig. 3d).

Next we wanted to determine whether the entire BTR complex is recruited to stalled replication forks. Indeed, by iPOND we found that BTR complex is recruited to the stalled forks, a process which gets dissipated upon thymidine chase, both without or after HU treatment (Fig. S6b). Next, to examine the role of ubiquitylation of RMI1 on the recruitment of the BTR complex to replication forks, we carried out iPOND on cells expressing either RMI1 wildtype or RMI1 3KR mutant after exposure to HU. The association of the BTR complex with the stalled forks was significantly reduced in cells expressing the RMI1 3KR mutant following HU-induced replication stress (Fig. 3e). Immunofluorescence carried out in the isogenic cells indicated that unlike wildtype RMI1, the RMI 3KR mutant exhibited a significantly reduced ability to form HU-induced foci (Figs. 3f, g). Notably, the failure of RMI1 foci formation in the 3KR mutant also abrogated the formation of BLM and TOP3A foci, indicating that the lack of recruitment of RMI1 abrogated the localization of the entire BTR complex to the sites of stalled replication (Figs. S6c, d). Notably, equivalent levels of BLM ubiquitylation was observed in cells expressing either RMI WT or RMI1 3KR cells, suggesting that the defect in BLM recruitment is not due to a loss of its ubiquitylation (Fig. S6e) but possibly due to the stability of the BTR complex at the stalled forks due the lack of functional wildtype RMI1.

It was earlier demonstrated that the impaired recruitment of BLM to stalled replication forks due to the lack of the ubiquitylation machinery could be rescued by direct chromatin targeting of BLM by its fusion with GFP-tagged histone, H2AX [20]. Hence, we examined whether the GFP tagged H2AX-BLM fusion can also rescue the recruitment of both BLM and RMI1 3KR recruitment in U2OS shRMI1 cells expressing RMI1 3KR mutant. It was observed that expression of GFP tagged H2AX-BLM fusion restored the localization of both BLM and RMI1 3KR to the sites of stalled replication (Figs. 4a, b), thereby indicating that forced targeting of BLM to the chromatin can overcome the lack of RMI1 ubiquitylation.

Ubiquitylated RMI1 is required for replication progression and restart

Next, we wanted to understand how ubiquitylated RMI1 affects the recognition and repair of the stalled forks. For this purpose, we first checked the levels of various proteins which recognize the stalled forks in cells expressing either GFP RMI1 WT or GFP RMI1 3KR mutant after HU treatment. In cells expressing wildtype RMI1, we observed activation of the DNA damage response pathway in response to HU treatment. Hence, the levels of pATR, pChk1, BLM were all enhanced. Interestingly, the levels of these sensors of stalled forks (pATR and pChk1) and the marker for residual DNA damage (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma$$\end{document}H2AX) were further increased in cells expressing RMI1 3KR (Fig. 5a), suggesting an elevated DNA damage response in the mutant expressing cells. The increased level of residual DNA damage in RMI1 3KR cells was also confirmed by the alkaline comet assay (Figs. S7a, b).

To determine whether the RMI1 3KR mutant affects the cell-cycle progression, we analyzed cell-cycle profiles of U2OS shRMI1 cells rescued with GFP RMI1 WT or the 3KR mutant following HU treatment. No significant differences in cell-cycle distribution were observed between WT and 3KR mutant cells under untreated conditions. However, upon release from HU-induced replication arrest, cells expressing the RMI1 3KR mutant progressed into G2/M more slowly than RMI1 WT expressing cells. Notably, RMI1 3KR cells showed prolonged retention in the G2/M phase from 8 to 18 h post-HU removal compared to RMI1 WT cells (Figs. 5b, S7c, d), indicating that loss of RMI1 ubiquitylation impairs the recovery from replication stress, likely due to reduced efficiency of DNA repair, resulting in a prolonged G2/M arrest.

Since DNA replication and DNA damage repair are coupled, we next assessed the impact of RMI1 ubiquitylation on these processes by using cells expressing either RMI1 wildtype or 3KR mutant. Measurement of the normal replication fork velocity indicated that the RMI1 3KR mutant expressing cells, like shRMI1 cells, displayed a marked reduction in replication fork velocity compared to RMI1 WT cells (Figs. 5c, d). Loss of RMI1 itself or the inability of RMI1 to undergo ubiquitylation both led to a significant increase in the fraction of stalled forks (as determined by CldU+, IdU- tract length) (Fig. S7e). Additionally, the length of IdU-labelled tracts in restarted forks (i.e. the CldU+, IdU+ tracts) was shorter in both shRMI1 and RMI1 3KR mutant cells, indicating that fork restart is also compromised in both shRMI1 and RMI1 3KR mutant cells (Figs. 5e, f). The replication restart defect was infact comparable in HeLa shRMI cells expressing RMI1 3KR mutant and HeLa BLM cells expressing BLM 3KR (Figs. 5g, h, S7f). However, the extent of fork protection is not altered between cells expressing either wild-type RMI1 or RMI1 3KR mutant cells (Figs. S7g, h), thereby indicating that ubiquitylation of RMI1 has very specific roles during the replication-coupled repair process.

Finally, we wanted to understand the epistatic relationship between the loss of ubiquitylation in RMI1 3KR mutant and the loss of RNF8. For this purpose, we carried out DNA fiber restart assay and comet assay in U2OS shRMI1 expressing either GFP RMI1 WT or GFP RMI1 3KR, without or after depletion of RNF8. In both assays, loss of RNF8 did not alter the replication restart defect or the levels of the residual DNA damage observed in RMI1 3KR mutant cells (Figs. S8a–f), suggesting that RNF8 and RMI1 ubiquitylation act in the same pathway to regulate efficient replication restart after stalling of the replication forks.

Discussion

It is known that BLM and TOP3A reside in PML-NBs in the absence of DNA damage [17, 18]. In fact, BLM is known to be constitutively phosphorylated at Ser646 in the PML NBs [19] and gets transported to the sites of stalled replication via ubiquitylation [20]. Sumoylation of BLM has also been proposed to be a factor which dictates its interaction with RAD51 at the stalled forks [21]. However, unlike BLM, much less is known about the RMI1 localization and functions within the cellular context while functioning as a member of the BTR complex and regulating HR. This report has elucidated that RMI1 (but not RMI2 and TOP3A) undergoes RNF8dependent K63-linked ubiquitylation at three residues after replication arrest post-HU treatment, thereby allowing RMI1 to be recruited to the sites of stalled replication (Fig. 6). We also provide evidence that the lack of such ubiquitylation has consequences on DNA replication, DNA damage response and how cells recover from the stalled forks.

Our findings also indicate that while RMI1 requires RNF8 for its ubiquitylation in vivo, whereas RNF168 seems to be dispensable for this modification. Nevertheless, the loss of RNF168 impairs RMI1 foci formation. This dichotomy is most likely due to the established role of RNF168 in modifying the chromatin environment rather than directly affecting only RMI1. It is known that in wildtype cells, RNF8 ubiquitinates nucleosomal histone H1 as well as non-nucleosomal proteins [28]. The recruitment of RNF168 is triggered by RNF8-mediated ubiquitination of histone H1, which then monoubiquitinates H2A/H2AX at lysine 13/15. RNF168 amplifies these modifications to form a robust ubiquitin-rich chromatin region that facilitates the recruitment of DDR proteins [28–30]. Hence, the histone-directed ubiquitination activity of RNF168 is crucial, occurring upstream of RMI1 and is thus separate from its function on RMI1.

Evidence suggests that RNF8 mainly catalyzes two distinct types of ubiquitin chain linkages: K11 or K63 [26]. In this study, we demonstrate K63-linked polyubiquitylation of RMI1 in response to replication stress and that this ubiquitylation is directly mediated by RNF8. Through in vitro ubiquitylation assays, we identified three specific lysine residues (K428, K453, K566) in RMI1 as the sites of ubiquitylation by RNF8. Hence, mutating all three of these residues to arginine (3KR mutant) resulted in reduced ubiquitylation of RMI1 and disrupted its recruitment to the sites of replication damage. Previous studies have shown that RMI1 depletion leads to reduced cellular levels of TOP3A protein, while BLM expression remains largely unaffected, indicating a critical role of TOP3A-RMI1 interaction for TOP3A stability [13]. Further, while it is known that RMI1 colocalizes to the stalled forks with BLM and TOP3A [13], the mechanism behind such an ability of RMI1 has remained elusive. In this study, we demonstrate that the lack of K63-linked polyubiquitylation of RMI1 in cells expressing the RMI1 3KR mutant resulted in defective recruitment of not only RMI1 but also the entire BTR complex to the sites of stalled replication. Thus, RNF8-dependent RMI1 ubiquitylation is a positive regulator of DNA damage response when the cells are exposed to replication stress. Reports about post-translational modifications on RMI1 with functional validation are rare. For example, RMI1 is known to be phosphorylated at Ser284 and Ser292 by CDK1 kinase during M phase of the cell cycle [31]. We now provide evidence that ubiquitylation of RMI1 also regulates its functions.

One of the key cellular responses to replication stress is the activation of the ATR signalling pathway [32]. Our findings reveal that compared to RMI1 WT, cells expressing RMI1 3KR mutant exhibit significantly enhanced ATR and CHK1 phosphorylation in response to HU-induced replication blockade. This enhanced activation of the ATR signalling is reminiscent of the response observed in RMI1-depleted cells [16]. Furthermore, an increased accumulation of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma$$\end{document}H2AX in the RMI1 3KR cells was observed, indicating an enhanced accumulation of damaged DNA. This persistent damage was also substantiated by comet assays, reinforcing the idea that defective RMI1 ubiquitylation signalling leads to abrogation of the DNA damage response, which in turn causes enhanced accumulation of replication-associated DNA damage. Consistent with this, the prolonged G2/M delay observed in RMI1 3KR cells following replication stress is likely a consequence of persistence of the damaged DNA due to impaired completion of the repair processes, thereby causing prolonged checkpoint engagement.

It has been reported that cells depleted of RMI1 show a reduced replication fork velocity, which might be attributed to the role of the BTR complex in promoting dHJ dissolution activity [13]. Similarly, BLM, through its interaction with condensin II, plays an important role in replication fork progression and in stabilizing fork stalling during replication stress [33]. Using DNA fibre assays, we observed a reduction in replication fork velocity of RMI1 3KR mutant cells, which was comparable to RMI1 depletion. This can be attributed to the failure to optimally recruit the BTR complex. Furthermore, the efficiency to restart a stalled fork upon HU damage was significantly reduced in cells with RMI1 3KR mutation, resulting in an increased fraction of stalled forks and shortened restarted tracts. Additionally, cells expressing the BLM ubiquitylation dead mutant (BLM 3KR) also exhibited a comparable replication restart defect, similar to that observed in RMI1 3KR mutant, and BLM-depleted cells. The defective fork recovery is indicative of impaired fork remodelling and the failed recruitment of critical repair factors (like the BTR complex proteins), which are essential for proper fork restoration.

It is also interesting to note that we demonstrate that both RMI1 WT and 3KR retain their ability to interact with BLM, TOP3A, and RMI2, indicating that the ubiquitylation status of RMI1 does not interfere with the formation of the complex BTR. While our data does not directly measure the helicase activity of BLM, it is conceivable that the helicase function of BTR complex will be affected. This is because the recruitment of the entire BTR complex to the chromatin is diminished due to the lack of RMI1 ubiquitylation and the helicase function of BTR complex should occur in vivo on the chromatin.

Mechanistically, the BTR complex facilitates the dissolution of double Holliday Junctions (dHJs) arising during HR. BLM promotes the convergent branch migration of the dHJs to form a hemicatenane, which then is resolved by TOP3A in association with RMI1 and RMI2, enabling a non-crossover outcome [8]. RMI1 enhances the strand passage activity of BLM-TOP3A and also stabilizes the whole complex [9]. Using an array of biochemical studies, the intricate cooperativity between the three members of the BTR complex has been well studied [11]. The role of RMI1 in the formation of the BTR complex is also known [12]. Evidence exists that once RMI1 relocates to the site of damage, it is involved in multiple DNA metabolism processes to resolve the replication stress [13] and thereby contributes to DNA repair [14, 15]. Hence, knockdown of RMI1 impairs DNA repair post-replication stress in the cells [16]. It will be interesting to know in future whether ubiquitylation of RMI1 at the three identified residues contributes to any of the above known functions of RMI1 and also to functions yet unknown, all of which may contribute to genome instability syndromes like Bloom-like Syndrome.
