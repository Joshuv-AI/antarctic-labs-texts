Combining GLP-1 Receptor Agonists and SGLT2 Inhibitors for Cardiorenal Prevention
Authors: Hertzel C Gerstein, Naveed Sattar
Published: 2026

Drugs that are analogs for native glucagon-like peptide-1 (glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists [GLP-1RAs]) and those that inhibit sodium-glucose–linked transporters in the kidney (the sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor [SGLT2i] drugs) were originally developed to lower glucose in people with type 2 diabetes (T2D). When drugs from both these classes were tested in long-term, large randomized and placebo-controlled outcomes trials to establish their cardiovascular and cerebrovascular (CV) safety in people with T2D, they reduced the incidence of serious, life-threatening CV and kidney outcomes.1 Subsequent large outcomes trials demonstrated similar effects in people without diabetes who were overweight or living with obesity and who had evidence of prior CV disease.2,3 As a result, representatives of these 2 drug classes are quickly becoming mainstays of preventive therapy for a variety of serious health outcomes, and are being increasingly prescribed by generalists, cardiologists, and nephrologists in addition to endocrinologists and diabetologists. Indeed, a recent analysis of a large American health network reported that the proportions of 121,508 people with T2D who were at high risk of CV or kidney disease and who were taking a GLP-1RA, SGLT2i, or both together were 22%, 17%, and 6%, respectively.4

Differences in the structure and proposed mechanisms of actions of these 2 classes support the hypothesis that combining them could potentially magnify their cardiovascular and kidney benefits. Unfortunately, no randomized controlled outcomes trials of combination therapy have been reported to date. Nevertheless, several of the completed trials using a representative of either one or the other class permitted participants to take the alternate drug class as background therapy. The researchers then analyzed whether the CV or kidney effects of the tested drug differed according to this background therapy. Syntheses and scrutiny of these analyses provide potential insights regarding the CV and kidney efficacy of using both in combination. The relevant data are reviewed and summarized herein, along with a worked estimate of the potential impact of the combination use to help inform clinical practice.

As noted previously, several drugs with GLP-1RA properties reduce the hazard of both CV and kidney outcomes in middle aged and older overweight or obese participants with T2D and additional CV risk factors. A meta-analysis that synthesized the observed findings from 10 large outcomes trials comprising 77,419 participants followed for a mean of 3.3 years1 reported significant reductions (i.e., HR: 0.86; 95% CI: 0.81-0.90) in major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) (ie, a myocardial infarction, stroke, or cardiovascular death), each component of this composite (HR: 0.86 for all), all-cause mortality (HR: 0.88; 95% CI: 0.82-0.93), heart failure hospitalization (HR: 0.86; 95% CI: 0.79-0.93), and worsening kidney function (HR: 0.78; 95% CI: 0.69-0.88). One large outcomes trial conducted in 17,604 people living with overweight or obesity and prior cardiovascular disease but without diabetes reported broadly similar effects.3

The effects of SGLT2i drugs on serious CV and kidney outcomes have also been tested in 13 randomized controlled trials comprising more than 90,000 middle-aged and older high-risk individuals followed for more than 2.5 years; more than 80% of participants had T2D. Two recent meta-analyses of these trials reported clear overall reductions in the hazard of MACE (HR: 0.91; 95% CI: 0.87-0.96), CV death (HR: 0.86; 95% CI: 0.81-0.92), CV death or heart failure hospitalization (HR: 0.77; 95% CI: 0.74, 0.81), and kidney disease progression (HR: 0.63; 95% CI: 0.58-0.69),2,5 with no evidence of different effects in people based on whether they had T2D (interaction P > 0.30). Notably, there was not a statistically significant reduced hazard of either myocardial infarction or stroke,2 suggesting that the MACE benefit is conferred by reduced CV death.

As noted previously, some of the completed trials of either GLP-1RA or SGLT2i drugs analyzed their effect on CV and kidney outcomes in subgroups defined by the presence or absence of background therapy with a representative of the alternate drug class. In these analyses, the absence of a statistically significant interaction between the randomly assigned drug therapy and the subgroup supports the hypothesis that the 2 drug classes are likely to have largely independent or orthogonal effects. For the GLP-1RA outcomes trials, these interaction analyses were summarized in a meta-analysis of 3 trials6 (Figure 1) and in a subsequent report of a fourth trial.8 These analyses did not detect any statistically significant interactions for MACE, its components, CV death, heart failure, or kidney function decline (interaction P > 0.25 for all outcomes).

Twelve SGLT2i outcomes trials reported their CV and kidney effects in subgroups defined by baseline GLP-1RA drug use. A meta-analysis of these trials7 showed no evidence of any statistically significant interaction for MACE, a composite of CV death or heart failure hospitalization, CV death alone, heart failure hospitalization alone, all-cause death, or a decline in kidney function (interaction P > 0.30 for all outcomes). All the data summarized previously suggests that GLP-1RA and SGLT2i drugs likely have independent effects on CV and kidney outcomes. This means that if used together, the CV and kidney benefits of a representative of 1 of these 2 classes would likely augment any established effects of the other. The relative effect size of such combination therapy can be estimated by multiplying the HRs of the 2 classes for each outcome, with the CIs of the resulting HR estimated from the SEs of the 2 multiplied HRs. This approach suggests that combining representatives of both classes of drugs would reduce the hazard of MACE and a kidney function outcome (comprising a sustained fall in estimated glomerular filtration rate <15 mL/min/1.73 m2, end stage kidney disease, or death from kidney failure) by 22% (HR: 0.78; 95% CI: 0.73-0.84) and 51%, respectively (HR: 0.49; 95% CI: 0.42-0.57). These HRs can then be applied to the incidence of the outcome during some time interval to generate the absolute risk benefit and the number of people who would need to be treated to prevent one from experiencing an outcome during that period. Thus, assuming a 5-year incidence of MACE of 20% in untreated people, those on combination therapy would have an incidence of 15.6% (ie, a 4.4% absolute risk reduction over 5 years), with number-needed-to-treat for 5 years of 23. Similarly, assuming a 5-year incidence of a kidney function outcome of 7% in untreated people, those on combination therapy would have an incidence of 3.4% (ie, a 3.6% absolute risk reduction over 5 years), with number-needed-to-treat for 5 years of 28.

In summary, the available large outcomes trials of GLP-1RA and SGLT2i drugs show that both classes of drugs reduce the hazard of MACE, CV death, heart failure, all-cause death, and progression of kidney disease, with no evidence of a differential effect in people with or without T2D. They also show that the GLP-1RA drugs additionally reduce the hazard of myocardial infarction and stroke, and that the SGLT2i drugs reduce the hazard of heart failure and progression of kidney disease to a greater degree than GLP-1RA drugs. Finally, the absence of any evidence of an interaction between GLP-1RA drugs with the background use of SGLT2i drugs and between SGLT2i drugs with the background use of GLP-1RA drugs suggests that the tested representatives of these classes are likely to have independent effects on the outcomes studied.

The likely independence of these drugs’ effects on CV and kidney outcomes is in keeping with evidence that their CV and kidney-related mechanisms of action fundamentally differ and so should be complementary. It also suggests that when used together they will have a large impact on CV and kidney outcomes and supports serious consideration of the combined use in the highest risk people who are likely to glean the largest absolute benefits, as already discussed in some guidelines.9 Notably, at least 1 outcomes trial has reported independent effects of these 2 classes on risk factors such as glycated hemoglobin, weight, and blood pressure.8

The fact that these conclusions were based on several carefully performed meta-analyses of large trials suggests that they are likely robust. Nevertheless, they are limited by the heretofore small numbers of participants using combination therapy, as reflected in the CIs around their effects in the combination subgroups, particularly when GLP-1RAs are added to SGLT2i class for chronic kidney disease outcomes. Thus small, but clinically relevant interactions may have been missed. They are also restricted to the groups studied who were all middle-aged and older at relatively high risk of serious CV and kidney outcomes, most of whom had T2D. Different findings might be observed in different populations. Nevertheless, these data lend some confidence to the conclusion that if combination therapy is used for the appropriate patients, they are likely to experience larger reductions in serious CV and kidney outcomes. These insights may help clinicians identify those patients who stand to benefit the most from combination therapy.
