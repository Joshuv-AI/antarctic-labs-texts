Protocol for in vivo transfection of mRNA-encapsulated lipid nanoparticles in mouse testis
Authors: Liying Wang, Huafang Wei, Yiyao Wu, Wei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryMale infertility affects 10%–20% of the population in most countries, with approximately half of the cases attributed to male factors. Here, we present a protocol for in vivo transfection of mRNA-encapsulated lipid nanoparticles (mRNA-LNPs) into the mouse testis. We describe steps for mRNA-LNP preparation, rete testis delivery, and assessment of transfection and spermatogenic rescue. This protocol establishes a relatively safe and efficient testis transfection system, offering a potential therapeutic strategy for patients with spermatogenic failure.Subject areas: Cell Biology, Developmental biology, Genetics

Before you begin

This protocol describes the combination of mRNA-encapsulated lipid nanoparticles with a rete testis microinjection system to treat spermatogenic failure. The mRNA-LNPs were prepared at Shanghai CYNBIO Co., Ltd. The rete testis microinjection system enables the delivery of exogenous mRNA-LNPs into the germ cells of live mice. Successful expression of the target protein was detected 24 h post-injection and maintained for up to 10 days. Here, we focus on the detailed steps encompassing mRNA-LNPs preparation, microinjection and transfection of the mouse testis, and subsequent data analysis, including quantification of transfection efficiency and evaluation of spermatogenic rescue efficiency.

Innovation

The most severe form of male infertility is spermatogenic failure, which manifests as azoospermia or oligozoospermia.1 Assisted reproductive technology (ART) remains largely ineffective for spermatogenic failure due to the absence of functional haploid germ cells.2,3,4 Gene delivery to germ cells represents an optimal strategy to rescue spermatogenic failure caused by genetic defects. Current approaches for gene delivery to germ cells include genetically modified viruses, ex vivo modified spermatogonial stem cells (SSCs), and in vivo electroporation.5,6,7,8 However, ethical concerns and the risk of genomic integration limit their clinical application, highlighting the need for a non-integrating and efficient gene delivery system for germ cells. Here, we present the integration of mRNA-LNPs with rete testis microinjection to successfully deliver target genes into germ cells, thereby effectively rescuing spermatogenic failure. This approach provides a potential non-viral, non-integrating platform for male infertility therapy.

Step-by-step method details

Preparation for target mRNA

Timing: 1 day

The preparation of mRNA-LNPs was completed at Shanghai CYNBIO Co., Ltd.

1.Amplify the target gene vector by PCR using primers with the desired Poly(A) length and high-fidelity DNA polymerase to obtain the linearized DNA template required for transcription.

Amplify the target gene vector by PCR using primers with the desired Poly(A) length and high-fidelity DNA polymerase to obtain the linearized DNA template required for transcription.

2.Purify the PCR product to obtain the target linearized DNA template.

Purify the PCR product to obtain the target linearized DNA template.

3.Measure the concentration and A260/280 ratio of the product using Nanodrop, and assess the product quality by agarose gel electrophoresis.

Measure the concentration and A260/280 ratio of the product using Nanodrop, and assess the product quality by agarose gel electrophoresis.

4.Perform in vitro transcription of the linearized DNA template using T7 RNA polymerase in a system containing NTPs/Cap1AG at 37°C for 2 h.

Perform in vitro transcription of the linearized DNA template using T7 RNA polymerase in a system containing NTPs/Cap1AG at 37°C for 2 h.

5.Digest the DNA template by incubating with DNase I at 37°C for 30 min, then purify the crude product to obtain the target mRNA and dissolve it in RNase-free water.

Digest the DNA template by incubating with DNase I at 37°C for 30 min, then purify the crude product to obtain the target mRNA and dissolve it in RNase-free water.

Encapsulation method

mRNA-LNPs were prepared using the: NanoAssmblr Ignite (Precision NanoSystems) with an Ignite microfluidic chip.

6.Aqueous phase preparation: Prepare mRNA working solution at a concentration of 0.35 mg/mL in 100 mM sodium acetate buffer (pH 4.0).

Aqueous phase preparation: Prepare mRNA working solution at a concentration of 0.35 mg/mL in 100 mM sodium acetate buffer (pH 4.0).

7.Organic phase preparation: Dissolve lipids in anhydrous ethanol at a concentration of 25 mM with the following molar ratios: SM-102: DSPC: cholesterol: DMG-PEG2000 = 50:10:38.5:1.5.

Organic phase preparation: Dissolve lipids in anhydrous ethanol at a concentration of 25 mM with the following molar ratios: SM-102: DSPC: cholesterol: DMG-PEG2000 = 50:10:38.5:1.5.

8.Encapsulate mRNA into LNPs using the Ignite microfluidic chip at an aqueous-to-organic phase flow rate ratio of 3:1 and a total flow rate of 12 mL/min.

Encapsulate mRNA into LNPs using the Ignite microfluidic chip at an aqueous-to-organic phase flow rate ratio of 3:1 and a total flow rate of 12 mL/min.

9.After encapsulation, dilute the resulting mRNA-LNPs with 10 volumes of Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS) (without calcium and magnesium).

After encapsulation, dilute the resulting mRNA-LNPs with 10 volumes of Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS) (without calcium and magnesium).

10.Concentrate the diluted solution to the original volume using a 100-kDa centrifugal filter unit at 4°C and 3,000 × g for 30 min.

Concentrate the diluted solution to the original volume using a 100-kDa centrifugal filter unit at 4°C and 3,000 × g for 30 min.

11.Exchange the buffer three times with DPBS to achieve 99% buffer exchange efficiency.

Exchange the buffer three times with DPBS to achieve 99% buffer exchange efficiency.

12.Dilute the final mRNA-LNPs sample in DPBS containing 5% sucrose to obtain the desired concentration.

Dilute the final mRNA-LNPs sample in DPBS containing 5% sucrose to obtain the desired concentration.

13.Characterization of the mRNA-LNPs, including mRNA integrity, PDI, size, zeta potential, and encapsulation efficiency, was provided by Shanghai CYNBIO Co., Ltd. (Figures 1A and 1B). The final mRNA-LNPs were stored at −80°C.

Characterization of the mRNA-LNPs, including mRNA integrity, PDI, size, zeta potential, and encapsulation efficiency, was provided by Shanghai CYNBIO Co., Ltd. (Figures 1A and 1B). The final mRNA-LNPs were stored at −80°C.

CRITICAL: Alternatively, the tip can be cut using fine surgical scissors under a microscope, though this method may result in less consistent tip quality. Preparation of mRNA-LNP microinjection solution mix

Timing: 20 min

18.Install the microinjection needle onto the glass capillary holder (Figures 1F and 2A).

Install the microinjection needle onto the glass capillary holder (Figures 1F and 2A).

Note: Before installation, remove the stopper from the glass capillary holder to evacuate the air inside.

19.Mix 2.5 μL of mRNA-LNPs (1.0 mg/mL) with 7.5 μL of RNase-free water and 1 μL of 0.1% (w/v) Fast Green (Figure 2B).

Mix 2.5 μL of mRNA-LNPs (1.0 mg/mL) with 7.5 μL of RNase-free water and 1 μL of 0.1% (w/v) Fast Green (Figure 2B).

Note: The total volume of the injection mixture is 11 μL for 4-week-old male mice and 15 μL for 6-week-old gene knockout male mice.

20.Load the microinjection pipette with the mRNA-LNPs microinjection solution mix (Figures 2B and 2C).

Load the microinjection pipette with the mRNA-LNPs microinjection solution mix (Figures 2B and 2C).

CRITICAL: Gently and slowly aspirate the sample with the needle tip close to the bottom of the dish. Slow aspiration prevents the sample from being drawn into the glass capillary holder and avoids the formation of air columns or bubbles. Air columns or bubbles injected into the seminiferous tubules of the testis may cause germ cell damage.

Anesthesia and surgery

Timing: 10 min

21.Sterilize all surgical instruments, including surgical and suturing tools (Figures 1G and 1H).

Sterilize all surgical instruments, including surgical and suturing tools (Figures 1G and 1H).

22.Prepare mice for the experiment.

Prepare mice for the experiment.

Note: In this protocol, we used 4-week-old C57BL/6J male mice for detection of target protein expression and 6-week-old male mice for evaluating spermatogenic rescue in gene knockout mice. Mice are housed under standard conditions. All efforts were made to minimize animal suffering.

23.Anesthetize mice with 1.25% tribromoethanol solution at a dose of 0.2 mL per 10 g body weight (∼250 mg/kg) by subcutaneous injection between the pelvis and the hind limb to avoid organ damage and injury.

Anesthetize mice with 1.25% tribromoethanol solution at a dose of 0.2 mL per 10 g body weight (∼250 mg/kg) by subcutaneous injection between the pelvis and the hind limb to avoid organ damage and injury.

Note: Deep anesthesia is typically achieved within 5 min after injection.

24.After anesthesia, place the mouse on a stereo microscope equipped with a light source (Figures 1I and 1J). Use surgical forceps to part the hair along the abdominal midline.

After anesthesia, place the mouse on a stereo microscope equipped with a light source (Figures 1I and 1J). Use surgical forceps to part the hair along the abdominal midline.

25.Make a ventral incision directly above the preputial glands in the center of the abdominal area. First, pull the skin away and make a small transverse cutaneous incision of approximately 10 mm. Then, continue the incision through the abdominal muscular layer.

Make a ventral incision directly above the preputial glands in the center of the abdominal area. First, pull the skin away and make a small transverse cutaneous incision of approximately 10 mm. Then, continue the incision through the abdominal muscular layer.

Note: The incision should be as small as possible to reduce harm to the animal and to minimize the risk of postoperative infection.

26.Place sterile gauze over the abdominal incision. Carefully pull up the abdominal fat pads to expose the attached testis and place it on the sterile gauze beside the incision site (Figure 2D).

Place sterile gauze over the abdominal incision. Carefully pull up the abdominal fat pads to expose the attached testis and place it on the sterile gauze beside the incision site (Figure 2D).

Note: The exposed surface of the testis should clearly reveal the efferent duct. The efferent duct is a transparent white tube attached to the fat pad, connecting the testis to the epididymis and forming a 45° angle with the aorta. A helpful tip to confirm proper exposure is to check the visibility of the aorta. If the aorta is fully exposed, the testis is not positioned correctly. If the aorta remains partially buried under fat, the correct surface of the testis is exposed.

Rete testis microinjection

Timing: 30 min

27.At the beginning of the injection, position the microinjection needle over an avascular region of the testis, with the tip pointing toward the efferent duct and extending into the rete testis (Figure 2E).

At the beginning of the injection, position the microinjection needle over an avascular region of the testis, with the tip pointing toward the efferent duct and extending into the rete testis (Figure 2E).

Note: For an 11 μL injection volume, the injection duration is approximately 120 s, corresponding to an injection rate of approximately 0.09 μL/s. Do not puncture the rete testis, as this may lead to injection failure.

28.Generate injection pressure using a 1 mL syringe connected to the glass capillary holder.

Generate injection pressure using a 1 mL syringe connected to the glass capillary holder.

CRITICAL: When the syringe is advanced to deliver approximately 50–100 μL of air, the mRNA-LNPs microinjection solution mix is observed to flow out from the needle tip, as monitored with the aid of Fast Green. Approximately 10 s later, the rete testis becomes fully filled with the mRNA-LNPs solution mix. The syringe is then advanced further to deliver approximately 200–300 μL of air pressure to increase the injection pressure, at which point the mRNA-LNPs solution mix enters the seminiferous tubules and fills the entire testis within approximately 2 min. A successful rete testis microinjection is first indicated by filling of the entire rete testis with the mRNA-LNPs microinjection solution mix, followed by flow into the seminiferous tubules (Figure 2F). Misdirected injection—where the needle tip is not correctly positioned within the rete testis—results in the mRNA-LNPs microinjection solution mix being delivered into the interstitial space between seminiferous tubules rather than into the tubules themselves (Figure 2G). Over-injection can cause germ cell damage and subsequent spermatogenic impairment (Figure 3D).

29.Rapidly withdraw the needle when only approximately 0.6 cm of the solution remains in the needle tip.

Rapidly withdraw the needle when only approximately 0.6 cm of the solution remains in the needle tip.

CRITICAL: Prolonged needle dwell time before withdrawal may allow air bubbles to enter the seminiferous tubules, leading to germ cell damage. The success rate of rete testis microinjection is operator-dependent and requires hands-on practice. For researchers with a background in reproductive biology, familiarity with the anatomy of the rete testis and efferent duct, and steady hand motor skills, the success rate can reach approximately 50% after more than 10 injection attempts during the initial learning phase. Success rates are expected to improve with increased experience.

Homogenize the tissue using a Dounce homogenizer at 60 Hz for 60 s. After brief sonication (2 s on, 2 s off), incubate the lysates on ice for 30 min.

Centrifuge the lysates at 14,000× g for 15 min at 4°C to pellet debris. Collect the supernatant for further analysis.

Denature protein lysates as required based on the protein of interest, then resolve them by SDS-PAGE and transfer onto nitrocellulose membranes.

Wash the membranes three times with PBS (5 min each wash), then visualize the protein bands using an Odyssey infrared imaging system.

Expected outcomes

To evaluate target protein expression, we used mCherry mRNA-LNPs as an example. At 48 h post-injection, testes were collected. The injected mRNA-LNPs concentration did not affect testis size (Figure 3A). Successful protein expression was detected by western blotting (Figure 3B). Observation of injected testes under a fluorescence microscope revealed mCherry fluorescence expressed in the seminiferous tubules, consistent with the western blot results (Figure 3C). Immunofluorescence staining of testis sections further confirmed that mCherry was successfully expressed in germ cells, as demonstrated by colocalization with the germ cell marker DDX4 (Figure 3E). Co-staining with the Sertoli cell marker SOX9 also showed mCherry expression in Sertoli cells (Figure 3F). The efficient delivery into germ cells is consistent with recent reports demonstrating high efficiency of mRNA delivery for germ cell transfection.9,10,11

To assess the rescue efficiency, we used a mouse model of oligoasthenoteratozoospermia by knocking out Pdcd5 gene in mice. At 14 days after injection of the target mRNA-LNPs, the testes showed increased size compared to non-injected controls. Sperm count was significantly increased, and the percentage of abnormal sperm morphology was markedly reduced. These results demonstrate effective rescue of the spermatogenic defect in the gene knockout mice.12

Limitations

One limitation of this protocol is the inconsistent expression levels of different target genes encapsulated with LNPs following injection into the seminiferous tubules. Generally, mRNA with shorter coding regions exhibits higher protein expression efficiency, whereas those with longer coding regions show lower expression efficiency post-injection. In addition to coding region length, batch-to-batch variability in mRNA-LNPs preparation may also pose a major challenge.

Another limitation is the relatively short duration of mRNA-LNPs-mediated protein expression in the seminiferous tubules, which currently persists for approximately 10 days. This limited expression window results in reduced rescue efficiency. Consequently, oligoasthenoteratozoospermia mice that achieve rescue must rely on intracytoplasmic sperm injection and embryo transfer to produce offspring, rather than through natural mating.

Furthermore, the efficiency of target gene delivery to spermatogonial stem cells remains low. This limitation precludes the use of this method to rescue all cases of azoospermia. Future efforts aimed at modifying RNA or optimizing LNP encapsulation methods may achieve higher rescue efficiency and broader applicability.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

High doses of mRNA-LNPs may cause germ cell damage. Although this protocol suggests an optimal treatment range of 0.25 mg/mL, the actual working concentration may vary depending on the specific LNP formulation and the target mRNA used.

Potential solution

If germ cell damage is observed using this protocol, we recommend decreasing the mRNA-LNPs concentration. Two days post-injection, evaluate testis size, target RNA expression level, sperm motility, and sperm count to assess the extent of germ cell damage. Additionally, perform H&E staining to examine seminiferous tubule morphology and determine whether the injection concentration is appropriate. Refer to Steps 57–65 of this protocol.

Problem 2

During sample loading, aspiration of air columns or bubbles into the microinjection needle may cause germ cell damage.

Potential solution

Gently and slowly aspirate the sample with the needle tip close to the bottom of the dish. Slow aspiration prevents the sample from being drawn into the glass capillary holder and avoids the formation of air columns or bubbles. Refer to Step 20 of this protocol.

Problem 3

During the injection procedure, injecting at an excessive speed may also cause germ cell damage.

Potential solution

During injection, the speed should be kept as slow as possible, allowing the mRNA-LNPs microinjection solution mix to fill the seminiferous tubules gradually and appropriately. Refer to Steps 27–29 of this protocol.

Problem 4

Gene knockout mice do not achieve successful spermatogenic rescue after target mRNA-LNPs microinjection.

Potential solution

Since different genes exhibit distinct expression localization patterns across various stages of germ cells, and the phenotypic consequences of their deficiency also vary accordingly, the optimal mouse age at injection and post-injection collection time should be experimentally determined for each specific knockout mouse line. It is recommended to perform a time-course analysis by evaluating testis size, target protein expression, sperm count, sperm motility, and seminiferous tubule morphology at multiple time points (e.g., 7, 10, 14 days post-injection) to identify the optimal window for assessing rescue efficiency. Additionally, confirming the expression of the target protein in the appropriate germ cell types by immunofluorescence may provide further guidance for optimizing the collection time. Refer to Steps 57-65 of this protocol.
