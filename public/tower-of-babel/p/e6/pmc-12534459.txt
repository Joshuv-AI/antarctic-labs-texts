Burden and risk factors of depression in seniors from 1990 to 2021: a multi-database study based on EMR mining methods
Authors: Site Xu, Mu Sun, Yu Xiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Depression in seniors is a growing public health concern worldwide. Despite the rising prevalence of depression in this demographic, comprehensive data on its burden and trends over an extended period remain limited. This study aims to assess the trends in the burden of depression among seniors from 1990 to 2021, utilizing the Global Burden of Diseases, Injuries, and Risk Factors Study (GBD) database, and to further explore the risk factors using China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) database and National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) database. We utilized data from the GBD 2021, reporting incidence and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) per 100,000 population, average annual percentage change (AAPC), and risk factors at global, and regional levels. Trends were analyzed by age, sex, and social development index. Joinpoint regression identified significant changes in global trends. We established an interpretable machine learning (ML) model with high efficiency and robustness that identifies depression based on CHARLS (2015, 2018, &2020) and NHANES (2013-2020.3). We chose a best-performing eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGB) with Genetic Algorithm (GA) for identification, and used SHapley Additive exPlanation (SHAP) to illustrate the potential risk factors. From 1990 to 2021, the overall global incidence of depression among seniors remained broadly stable (AAPC 0.01, 95% CI −0.07 to 0.08), although marked changes emerged in specific regions and population subgroups. The incidence of depressive disorders increased globally for males (AAPC 0.06 [95% CI −0.02 to 0.15]) while it decreased for females (AAPC −0.01 [95% CI −0.09 to 0.07]). Regional analysis showed the highest incidence rates in low-SDI countries, while middle-SDI countries experienced the most significant increases in the burden of depression (AAPC 0.25 [95% CI 0.17 to 0.34]). Risk factor analysis using machine learning models identified key predictors of depression in elderly populations in both China and the United States. The burden of depression among seniors has significantly shifted globally, with marked regional and demographic variations. These findings underscore the urgent need for targeted interventions, policy modifications, and early screening programs to address the rising burden of depression in this vulnerable age group. The use of advanced machine learning models provides valuable insights into the risk factors, facilitating the development of more effective and tailored intervention strategies.

Introduction

Depression among the elderly is a critical public health issue, contributing significantly to the global burden of disease. As the world’s population ages, the incidence of depression among older adults is rising, with significant implications for disability and mortality. According to the Global Burden of Disease Study 2019, depressive disorders accounted for a substantial proportion of the mental health burden among individuals aged 65 and above, particularly in both ageing and aged countries [1]. Countries like China, India, and Indonesia, categorized as ageing populations, and countries like Japan, Italy, and Portugal, considered aged populations, exhibit distinct trends in the incidence and DALYs associated with depression [2].

The increasing prevalence of depression among the elderly is driven by various factors, including demographic shifts towards older populations, increased life expectancy, and higher exposure to risk factors such as chronic diseases, social isolation, and economic dependency [3]. These factors underscore the need for targeted health policies, early screening programs, and effective intervention strategies to mitigate the impact of depression on this vulnerable age group.

To further understand these trends, this study leverages data from the GBD database to analyze the epidemiological trends of depression in elderly populations from 1990 to 2021. Additionally, by employing advanced machine learning techniques, including genetic algorithms, XGB, and SHAP, the study delves into the risk factors specific to elderly populations in China and the United States using data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) and the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) databases [4, 5]. This multi-database approach aims to construct interpretable models that provide actionable insights for policymakers and healthcare providers to address the growing burden of depression among the elderly.

The study can be condensed into two aims: Primary aim – to chart global, regional, and national trends in depressive-disorder incidence, prevalence, and DALYs from 1990 to 2021 using GBD-2021; Secondary aim – to identify and rank individual-level risk factors for late-life depression in China and the United States with interpretable machine-learning models (XGBoost + SHAP) applied to CHARLS and NHANES.

By focusing on both global and regional trends and employing innovative analytical methods, this study seeks to contribute to the understanding of depression in elderly populations and inform the development of effective strategies to reduce its prevalence and impact.

Methods

Study population and data collection

For our analysis of the GBD 2021, we used repeated cross-sectional data from the Global Health Data Exchange (GHDx) [6], which includes the global burden of 372 diseases and injuries in 200 countries and territories from 1990 to 2021, including depressive disorders. For our analysis of the CHARLS [7], which is carried out by the National School of Development and the Institute for Social Science Surveys to monitor the health status of seniors in 28 provinces across China, we used longitudinal data (18152 participants) from the most recent three waves (2015, 2018, and 2020). For our analysis of the NHANES [8], we used cross-sectional data (60971 participants) of 3 contiguous cycles from 2013 to March 2020.

GBD 2021 synthesises more than 17 000 data inputs in the Bayesian DisMod-MR 2.1 framework to produce harmonised estimates of depressive-disorder incidence and DALYs for 204 countries and territories (age ≥55 y); CHARLS is a nationally stratified, multistage cohort that has followed ~18 000 Chinese older adults every three years since 2011, with weights adjusting for non-response; NHANES is a rolling two-year multistage survey of the U.S. civilian population that has assessed ~61 000 adults aged ≥55 y (2013–2020.3) with the PHQ-9 under rigorous field audits; none of the individual CHARLS or NHANES records enter the public GBD model, so analytical independence is maintained, and GBD converts age–sex-specific prevalence into DALYs via standard disability weights and reference life expectancy, propagating uncertainty through 1 000 posterior draws as detailed in the 2024 IHME methods report.

GBD 2021 supplies harmonised, population-level estimates of depressive-disorder incidence and DALYs for all world regions, establishing the macro-epidemiological frame. CHARLS adds longitudinal, individual-level data from community-dwelling older adults in rapidly ageing, upper-middle-SDI China, whereas NHANES provides repeated, nationally representative examinations of U.S. seniors in a high-SDI setting. Together these complementary sources permit cross-validation of ecological trends with person-level risk-factor models across divergent socio-economic contexts, and overlap is negligible because raw CHARLS and NHANES micro-data are not embedded in the GBD input files.

Although CHARLS and NHANES are widely cited in geriatric research, their individual-level records are not among the primary data sets ingested into the publicly released GBD 2021 depression model. Consequently, no sample duplication exists across the three repositories, and our design avoids analytic circularity. Instead, the independence of the micro-datasets strengthens external validity: person-level evidence from China and the United States provides an independent test bed for the macro-level trends produced by the multi-country GBD synthesis, enhancing generalisability without inflating the effective sample size.

WHO defines seniors as individuals aged 60 years and above [9]. For this study, ‘seniors’ were defined as individuals aged 55 years and above. GBD 2021 estimated the SDI for each country, defined as a composite index based on fertility rates for women under 25, mean years of education for those aged 15 and older, and income per capita, ranging from 0 to 1, grouping countries into five categories: lower, low-middle, middle, high-middle, and high SDI. Incident cases, prevalent cases, DALYs, incidence, and prevalence rates were extracted directly from GBD 2021 and reported per 100,000 population. The 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs) were defined by the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of 1000 estimates based on GBD’s algorithm. Our analysis followed the Guidelines for Accurate and Transparent Health Estimates Reporting for cross-sectional studies.

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the CESD in CHARLS, which has been demonstrated as a reliable and valid mental health assessment tool for Chinese elders [10]. The CESD score was calculated cumulatively with a total score ranging from 0 to 30, and depressive symptoms was recognized as more severe with the score increasing. In the study, participants with CESD scores greater than or equal to 10 were defined as having depression. Ultimately, a total of 120 variables (referred to as features within the field of ML) were selected form CHARLS. These variables were obtained either through surveys or physical measurements. Data from surveys were classified into 7 categories, including demographic, health status, lifestyle, treatment, mental, home environment, and socioeconomic factors. Data from physical measurements were mainly classified into 2 categories, including physical abilities, and blood markers.

Since the 2013–2014 data release cycle, professional physicians have used the code F32.9 & F33.9, which was labeled in accordance with the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, Tenth Revision (ICD-10), to diagnose depression in NHANES [11]. Ultimately, a total of 89 variables were selected from NHANES. These variables were obtained either through surveys or physical measurements. Data from surveys were classified into 5 categories, including demographic, dietary, examination, laboratory, and questionnaire data.

We harmonised case definitions across the three data sources as follows. GBD 2021 reports depressive disorders, an ICD-10 aggregate comprising major depressive disorder and persistent dysthymia; we use this label except when sub-analyses isolate either sub-code. CHARLS measures depressive symptoms with the 10-item CESD; a score ≥ 10—validated in Chinese elders—indicates a positive screen and carries no clinical diagnosis. In NHANES, depression is identified through clinician-reported ICD-10 codes in the Medical Conditions Questionnaire: respondents affirming MCQ220 are coded by certified NCHS abstractors as F32.9 (single episode, unspecified) or F33.9 (recurrent, unspecified0029. Throughout the manuscript we therefore use four non-overlapping terms: depressive disorders (GBD aggregate), major depressive disorder (MDD) (ICD-coded results in NHANES or GBD sub-analyses), dysthymia (when analysed separately) and depressive symptoms (CESD screens). Where the manuscript discusses findings from all three datasets collectively—e.g., in the Abstract or the overall conclusion—we use the umbrella term ‘depression’ for readability; in all dataset-specific passages, the precise labels defined above (depressive disorders, MDD, dysthymia, depressive symptoms) are retained.

Statistical analysis

For GBD. The main step was to determine global trends in the incidence, prevalence, and DALYs of depressive disorders. We computed age-specific rates and their average percentage changes per year using linear regression, treating each variable as continuous, taking values from the rank order log of dependent variables. The average annual percentage changes (AAPC) were calculated as a weighted average of APCs, summarizing trends over a specified interval. An AAPC describes the yearly percentage change as an increase, decrease, or no change. For example, an AAPC of 0.1 indicates a 0.1% annual increase. Temporal trends were represented by AAPCs and their 95% CIs for the periods 1990–1999, 2000–2009, and 2010–2021. The second step was to determine the year of the most significant trend transitions using joinpoint regression analysis, one of typical EMR mining methods and epidemiological steps [12–19], which fits data with connecting line segments known as joinpoints, starting with the simplest model of zero joinpoints (a straight line). Additional joinpoints were tested using a Monte Carlo permutation method. The final model was chosen using professional judgment and the Weighted Bayesian Information Criterion in Joinpoint software. Multiple separate Joinpoint regressions—one each for age-standardised incidence, prevalence, and DALY rates—were run in so that inflection years could be identified for every metric; these segment-specific slopes form the temporal backbone of the subsequent risk-attribution analysis. The third step was to stratify global trends by age group, sex, and SDI, and regions using the same AAPC method. Statistical tests’ inferences included effect sizes, CIs, rates, uncertainty intervals, and exact p-values. The fourth step was to describe risk factor estimation. GBD 2021 includes risk factors that are broadly categorised into 3 groups: (1) environmental and occupational, (2) behavioural, and (3) metabolic. For percentages of risk-attributable depressive disorders DALYs reported, the GBD comparative risk assessment framework was used to compute the fraction of depression-specific burden attributable to each risk factor. Percentages of risk-attributable DALYs were derived with the GBD 2021 CRA framework. For each risk factor we first computed a period-specific PAF, The PAF was then multiplied by the total DALYs for depressive disorders in that region and segment to yield risk-attributable DALYs, which we expressed as a percentage of the total burden.

For CHARLS and NHANES. The main analytical strategy was to explore potential risk factors as a supplement to the findings from the GBD 2021. In this study, a data-driven approach was employed to develop a machine learning model for the identification and prediction of depression using the XGB algorithm [20]. The model’s optimal performance was achieved through the use of GA. Subsequently, SHAP was utilized for interpretability analysis of the model [21], highlighting the most significant features. Initially, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and the Select K Best (SKB) algorithm were used for feature extraction [22]. The dataset was divided into training and testing sets using K-Fold cross-validation [23].

Model performance was evaluated with a stratified 10-fold cross-validation routine; area under the ROC curve (AUROC), accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score were averaged over the ten validation folds, and 1 000 bootstrap replicates were used to obtain 95% confidence intervals. To check the stability of feature importance, we recalculated SHAP values on 500 bootstrap resamples and observed that the membership of the top-ten predictors remained unchanged in more than 90% of resamples. Hyper-parameter tuning by genetic algorithm was confined to the training folds only, thereby avoiding information leakage.

Statistical tests’ inferences included effect sizes, CIs, rates, uncertainty intervals, and exact p-values. All analyses were conducted using Python version 3.9.13.

Global trends by sex

From 1990 to 2021, the incidence of depressive disorders increased globally for males while it decreased for females. Males showed an AAPC of 0.06 (95% CI −0.02 to 0.15), increasing from 4577.91 per 100,000 population (95% UI 3812.81 to 5650.17) in 1990 to 5037.96 per 100,000 population (95% UI 4175.4 to 6196.22) in 2021. Females had an AAPC of −0.01 (95% CI −0.09 to 0.07), decreasing from 7060.82 per 100,000 population (95% UI 5940.22 to 8599.03) to 7627.53 per 100,000 population (95% UI 6388.58 to 9314.36) during the same period (Table 2). In 2021, 63% of the 95,229,492.7 global incident depressive disorders cases occurred in males (59,988,096.37 cases).

Global trends by age groups

The most significant increase trend in depressive disorders incidence from 1990 to 2021 was observed in the 75+ age group (AAPC 0.02 [95% CI −0.02 to 0.07]). The 95+ age group experienced a decrease in incidence during this period (AAPC −0.33 [95% CI −0.4 to −0.27]). A similar situation occurred with prevalence and DALYs.

Global trends by SDI

The highest increase trend in depressive disorders incidence by SDI quintile was observed in the middle-SDI quintile, rising from 5180.87 per 100,000 population (95% UI 4289.12 to 6380.67) in 1990 to 6092.24 per 100,000 population (95% UI 5090.41 to 7542.7) in 2021 (AAPC 0.25 [95% CI 0.17 to 0.34]). Only middle-SDI and high–SDI quintiles showed increasing trends in depressive disorders incidence during this period. A similar situation occurred with prevalence and DALYs (Table 2).

Regional trends

Regionally, the largest increase in depressive disorders incidence between 1990 and 2021 was observed in Western Pacific Region, increasing from 3892.22 per 100,000 population (95% UI 3242.32 to 4739.45) in 1990 to 4936.12 per 100,000 population (95% UI 4137.71 to 5984.68) in 2021 (AAPC 0.62 [95% CI 0.52 to 0.73]) (Table 2). This region also showed the largest increase in prevalence and DALYs from 1990 to 2021 (Fig. 2). Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to describe the burden and risk factors of depression among seniors (ages 55+) across 200 countries and territories from 1990 to 2021 by multi-database, analyzed at global, regional, and national levels. The first paragraph of the Discussion has been rewritten and is highlighted in the manuscript. It now restates both the trend findings and the main risk-factor results: “Primary findings. From 1990 to 2021 the age-standardised incidence of depressive disorders in adults ≥55 years was flat overall (AAPC 0.01, 95% CI −0.07 to 0.08), yet it increased in men and middle-SDI regions, declined slightly in women, and remained highest in low-SDI countries. Secondary findings. Behavioural risks accounted for 6.24% of global DALYs in 2021; SHAP analysis pinpointed insomnia and low life-satisfaction as leading predictors in China, and recent prescription-medicine use, age, and diabetes in the United States. Together, these patterns show that both macro-level development status and micro-level modifiable factors shape the late-life depression burden.

The joinpoint analysis of depressive disorders from 1990 to 2021 reveals significant shifts in prevalence, incidence, and DALYs. Notably, in 2019, the prevalence of depressive disorders increased significantly (APC = 0.38%, p < 0.001), while both incidence (APC = −0.44%, p < 0.001) and DALYs (APC = −0.11%, p < 0.001) showed a significant decline. This paradoxical trend indicates a complex interplay of factors influencing the burden of depressive disorders [24, 25].

The rise in prevalence suggests that more individuals are living with depressive disorders, possibly due to chronicity and improved survival rates [26]. Enhanced management and treatment of depressive disorders may contribute to patients living longer with the condition. On the other hand, the decline in incidence indicates fewer new cases being diagnosed, which could be attributed to effective prevention strategies, increased mental health awareness, and early intervention programs [27]. The reduction in DALYs may reflect improved treatment outcomes, better management of symptoms, and enhanced quality of life for patients with depressive disorders [28].

The COVID-19 pandemic, starting in late 2019, has had a profound impact on mental health globally. The stress, isolation, and economic uncertainties brought about by the pandemic could have exacerbated existing mental health conditions, leading to higher prevalence. However, the same factors might have limited access to healthcare services, reducing the number of new diagnoses (incidence) and lowering the reported DALYs due to delays in healthcare reporting and management [29, 30]. Improvements in diagnostic criteria and increased awareness among healthcare professionals can lead to more accurate and earlier diagnosis of depressive disorders, thereby increasing prevalence rate [31]. Improved health information systems and mandatory reporting mechanisms can contribute to more comprehensive data collection, reflecting a higher prevalence but potentially lower incidence as the existing cases are better tracked over time [32].

The global maps depicting the incidence and DALYs of depressive disorders in 2021, along with the average annual percent change (AAPC) from 1990 to 2021, provide valuable insights into regional variations [33]. High incidence and DALYs are observed in regions with significant socio-economic challenges, suggesting that social determinants of health play a critical role in the burden of depressive disorders [34]. Regions with lower incidence and DALYs might benefit from better healthcare infrastructure, mental health awareness, and effective public health policies [35]. Regions showing a decline in incidence and DALYs over time might indicate successful implementation of mental health interventions and policies [36]. Conversely, regions with increasing trends highlight the need for targeted mental health services and interventions to address the growing burden of depressive disorders [37].

The relative contribution of various risk factors to DALYs, as illustrated by the GBD database for global, Chinese, and U.S. populations, underscores the multifaceted nature of depressive disorders [38]. Behavioral risks, such as substance abuse and lifestyle factors, along with social determinants like bullying victimization and intimate partner violence, significantly contribute to the DALYs associated with depressive disorders [39]. Differences in the relative contributions across regions highlight the importance of culturally tailored interventions and policies to address specific risk factors effectively [40]. Childhood sexual abuse and bullying are notable contributors to depressive disorders’ DALYs, emphasizing the long-term impact of early life experiences on mental health [41]. Preventive measures and early interventions targeting these adversities could mitigate their long-term effects on mental health [42].

The SHAP value analysis from ML modeling of NHANES and CHARLS databases provides additional insights into key factors influencing depression [5]. Factors such as insomnia, health satisfaction, stroke treatment, life satisfaction, and job satisfaction are critical predictors, indicating the importance of addressing both physical and psychological aspects in managing depressive disorders [43]. Socio-demographic variables, including age, gender, and socio-economic status, also play significant roles, reinforcing the need for comprehensive and personalized approaches in mental health care [44].

The observed trends in depressive disorders’ prevalence, incidence, and DALYs reflect the intricate dynamics of chronicity, treatment advancements, and socio-economic factors. The COVID-19 pandemic’s impact further complicates these trends, necessitating ongoing surveillance and adaptive mental health strategies. Addressing behavioral and social risk factors, particularly those rooted in childhood adversities, remains crucial in reducing the long-term burden of depressive disorders. Regional variations and predictive modeling insights underscore the need for tailored interventions to effectively manage and mitigate the impact of depressive disorders globally.

Our results accord with the plateauing global trend reported by Santomauro et al. [45] and further reveal sex-specific divergence together with a pronounced rise in middle-SDI regions—a pattern echoed in the 30-country analysis by Lim et al. [46]. Unlike single-country investigations such as the 10/66 population-based study in Latin-America, China and India [47] and the SHARE-based longitudinal work [48], our multi-database design triangulates GBD estimates with CHARLS and NHANES micro-data, thereby strengthening causal inference on behavioural and social determinants. Importantly, the predictors highlighted by our machine-learning models converge with Scandinavian registry evidence from Sivertsen et al. [49] and a population-based cohort from Singapore [50], both showing that sleep disturbance and low life satisfaction are leading modifiable drivers of late-life depression. These convergent signals argue for integrated programmes that couple routine insomnia screening with community-based psychosocial interventions, especially in rapidly ageing middle-SDI economies. Our study has several limitations. Firstly, the results are influenced by the methodologies employed in the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021. In cases where data were missing, model predictions were used; when data were available, preferred definitions or methods might not have been applied, leading to variability, such as differences in the definition of depression across various data sources. Although techniques were employed to minimize bias in the Global Burden of Disease Study, it cannot be entirely eliminated. Additionally, we used confidence intervals instead of uncertainty intervals after age standardization, necessitating cautious interpretation of our findings and highlighting the need for further real-world studies for validation. Secondly, the burden of early-onset depression is influenced by detection methods, screening quality, and data registries, all of which vary according to socioeconomic status. Significant disparities in healthcare services across countries likely lead to an underestimation of early-onset depression in countries with low SDI. Thirdly, the CHARLS and NHANES components of this study only reflect data characteristics from China and the United States, and can only identify associations between depression and potential factors, as current machine learning models do not support causal inferences. Fourthly, in CHARLS, the study only used predictor values assessed at baseline, which did not account for potential variations over time. Fifthly, in CHARLS, the CESD results may be subject to bias due to subjective tendencies. Sixthly, in NHANES, although the ICD-10 is normative, the diagnosis of depression was self-reported by participants through questionnaires, potentially introducing information bias. Seventhly, Cross-sectional CHARLS and NHANES data, differing case definitions, potential class-imbalance bias in XGBoost, lack of low-SDI micro-data, and uncertainty propagated from GBD exposure and relative-risk inputs together limit causal inference and external generalisability. Lastly, because GBD and NHANES rely on clinician-assigned ICD-10 diagnoses while CHARLS uses a symptom scale, numerical estimates are not directly interchangeable; cross-dataset comparisons are interpreted qualitatively, and residual misclassification cannot be entirely excluded.
