Posterior-stabilized vs cruciate-retaining TKA in osteoarthritic patients with genu recurvatum: a 2-year comparative analysis
Authors: Rui Sheng Wang, Jia Ying Lee, Mitra Amit Kanta, Lincoln Ming Han Liow, Jerry Yongqiang Chen, Paul Chee Cheng Chang, Seng Jin Yeo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPre-operative genu recurvatum (GR) in patients with knee osteoarthritis (OA) presents a surgical challenge during total knee arthroplasty (TKA). It remains unclear whether cruciate-retaining (CR) or posterior-stabilized (PS) TKA offers superior functional recovery and postoperative stability in this population.MethodsIn this single-centre retrospective cohort study, we analysed 102 patients with knee OA and physiological GR > 5° who underwent either CR-TKA (n = 60) or PS-TKA (n = 42). Clinical outcomes were assessed preoperatively, at 6 months, and at 2 years postoperatively using range of motion (ROM), and validated functional scores, International Knee Society Score (IKSS), Oxford Knee Score (OKS), Short Form-36 (SF-36). Recurrence of GR, complication rates, and revision procedures were also recorded. Statistical comparisons were performed using independent t-tests and chi-square tests.ResultsBoth groups demonstrated significant improvements across all functional outcomes. At the 6-month and 2-year follow-ups, the PS-TKA group demonstrated significantly better end range flexion and ROM than the CR-TKA group. At 2 years, recurrence of GR was 9.5% in the PS-TKA group versus 18.3% in the CR-TKA group (p < 0.05). There were no significant differences in IKSS, OKS, SF-36, patient satisfaction or expectation fulfilment. There were no significant differences in complications or revision rates between the groups.ConclusionBoth CR-TKA and PS-TKA provided substantial functional improvement in patients with pre-operative GR. PS-TKA demonstrated superior flexion and lower recurrence rates of GR but no difference in functional outcomes or revision rates. Given its enhanced extension stability, PS-TKA may be preferable in cases with significant posterior laxity. Implant selection should remain individualized based on intraoperative findings and patient-specific anatomical considerations.Keywords: Total knee arthroplasty, Genu recurvatum, Posterior-stabilized, Cruciate-retaining, Functional outcomes, Knee osteoarthritis.

Introduction

Arthritic knees with genu recurvatum (GR) pose unique challenges during total knee arthroplasty (TKA) [1, 2]. Genu recurvatum is a condition characterized by excessive hyperextension of the knee joint beyond its normal range > 5° [3]. This abnormal movement of the knee can lead to instability which occurs in the sagittal plane, manifesting as excessive hyperextension [4], pain, and impaired function, significantly affecting the quality of life [5] for those affected. GR may result from a combination of factors including quadriceps weakness, capsuloligamentous laxity, neuromuscular disorders, or previous trauma [6]. In the context of osteoarthritis (OA), GR is often attributed to asymmetric wear and chronic instability of the joint, leading to posterior capsular attenuation and extension imbalance [7] The prevalence of GR in patients undergoing TKA is estimated to range between 1% and 5% [8, 9], though this figure may vary based on patient population and diagnostic criteria. While mild GR (< 5°) may be asymptomatic, physiological GR > 5°—as considered in this study—poses important surgical considerations. If left unaddressed, GR can contribute to postoperative instability, recurrent hyperextension, anterior knee pain, and early implant failure [10, 11].

Cruciate Retaining Total Knee Arthroplasty (CR-TKA) and Posterior Stabilized Total Knee Arthroplasty (PS-TKA) are commonly used to treat knee OA [12–14]. In CR-TKA, the posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) is preserved [15, 16] while in PS-TKA, the PCL is resected and a post-cam mechanism compensates for its function [17–19]. Previous meta-analyses have demonstrated satisfactory survivorship and similar outcomes comparing the CR and PS-TKAs, with no difference in clinical scores, ROM, midterm survival rate and quadriceps muscle recovery [12, 14, 20–25]; only two studies reported better ROM in PS-TKAs [26, 27].

​In this study, the choice of CR-TKA versus PS-TKA was determined by each surgeon’s standard practice, with individual surgeons consistently performing only one implant type. Implant selection was therefore based on surgeon preference rather than patient-specific characteristics, introducing a potential selection bias but providing a pragmatic reflection of real-world clinical practice. While some studies advocate for CR-TKA as the standard approach in patients with genu recurvatum (GR) [28], other evidence suggests that PS-TKA may yield superior postoperative flexion and range of motion [29]. Direct comparative data on CR- versus PS-TKA outcomes in OA patients with preoperative GR, however, remain limited.

Patients with GR represent a unique subset, as the deformity may persist or recur following TKA, potentially leading to inferior alignment correction and functional outcomes [29]. Conversely, when appropriate surgical techniques—such as posterior-stabilized implants and accurate component positioning—are used, several studies have reported outcomes comparable to those of patients without GR [28].

Given this ongoing controversy, the present study aims to compare functional outcomes, the incidence of postoperative GR recurrence, and revision rates between CR- and PS-TKA in patients with preoperative GR. Our null hypothesis is that there would be no significant differences between CR- and PS-TKA with respect to functional outcomes, recurrence of genu recurvatum, or revision rates at two years postoperatively.

Methods

Study population

All patients who underwent primary TKA at our institution between 2000 and 2018 were screened using the institutional TKA registry. Inclusion criteria were: (1) documented preoperative genu recurvatum, defined as knee hyperextension > 5° on preoperative standing lateral radiographs, (2) treatment with either a CR- or PS-type implant, and (3) availability of a minimum of two years’ postoperative clinical and radiological follow-up. Recurvatum was constitutional (idiopathic) in all included cases. The contralateral knee was not analysed as part of this study. No upper limit was placed on the degree of recurvatum for inclusion, provided the deformity was corrected with a standard primary CR or PS implant.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) any history of prior surgery on the index knee (TKA, patellectomy, high tibial osteotomy, ligament reconstruction), (2) previous knee trauma causing malunion or instability, (3) septic arthritis, (4) neuromuscular or neuropathic disorders (e.g., poliomyelitis, Charcot joint), or (5) inflammatory arthritis.

3960 primary TKAs were performed in total by 3 specialist Orthopaedic surgeons between 2000 and 2018, of which 123 knees (3.1%) had pre-operative recurvatum. Only 102 knees had physiological recurvatum of which 42 underwent PS-TKA and 60 underwent CR-TKA.

Eligible patients were stratified into two groups based on implant type: PS-TKA (Group 1) and CR-TKA (Group 2). No patients were lost to follow-up during the two-year study period, ensuring complete outcome data for all included cases. The study was approved by the SingHealth Centralized Institutional Review Board under the reference number 20,190,900,089.

Surgical procedure

All surgical procedures were performed by fellowship-trained orthopaedic surgeons. Two surgeons (AKM, CCC) exclusively performed PS-TKA, while the third surgeon (YSJ) exclusively performed CR-TKA. Implant selection was determined by the operating surgeon’s routine practice rather than patient-specific factors. All operative procedures were performed under either general or spinal anaesthesia. Anaesthesia was not considered a potential source of bias in this study and therefore was not included among the study limitations. A single dose of intravenous antibiotic was given 30 min before tourniquet inflation. Each surgeon employed a similar surgical technique consisting of a midline skin incision with a medial parapatellar arthrotomy along the line of the quadriceps tendon proximally. For PS-TKA, the Zimmer NexGen® Complete Knee Solution was used, whereas CR-TKA was performed using the Johnson & Johnson P.F.C.® SIGMA® Total Knee System with standard CR inserts.

A hybrid approach combining measured resection and gap-balancing techniques was used with a combination of mechanical and kinematic alignment. Ligamentous balancing was performed using spacer blocks or trial implants. Femoral cuts were referenced from intramedullary instrumentation, while tibial cuts were made using an extramedullary guide.

In the coronal plane, resection levels were adjusted as needed to restore neutral mechanical alignment. In the sagittal plane, tibial posterior slope was reproduced within the recommended range for each implant type (slightly greater for CR designs to preserve PCL tension and slightly reduced for PS designs to optimise cam-post engagement). This step was critical to achieving balanced flexion and extension gaps and preventing hyperextension instability. Resecting the distal femur or proximal tibia with a 1-mm under-resection was used as needed to tighten the extension gap, as each millimetre corresponds to roughly 2–3° of flexion correction and assists in controlling recurvatum. All implants were cemented, and slope targets were standardised to minimise bias.

Postoperative protocol

After surgery, the knee was immobilized in a Jones bandage that was removed on Day 1 or Day 2 for wound inspection. After that, a light dressing was applied allowing the patient to go to physical therapy and full weight bearing. Suture removal occurred at 2 to 3 weeks.

Data collection and extraction

Medical records and information were reviewed retrospectively by independent clinicians and researchers not directly involved in the care of the patients. Demographics, preoperative and post-operative ranges of motion, and functional scores were extracted. Patient demographics include age, sex and body mass index (BMI).

Ranges of motion (ROM) and recurvatum were measured independently using goniometers in physiotherapy-led assessments, ROM were characterised by maximum flexion, maximum extension, and total range of motion.

Management details included type of procedure, method of arthroplasty and any relevant post-surgical complications were obtained from follow-up outpatient records and radiographs post-operatively at 6-months and 2-years including post-operative pain, infection, and readmission.

Recurrence of GR was defined as knee hyperextension greater than 5°, whereas absence of recurrence was defined as knee hyperextension of 5° or less.

Functional Outcomes were monitored at three time points: preoperatively, 6-months and 2-years postoperatively via International Knee Society Scores (IKSS) [30], Oxford Knee Score [31], Short Form-36 (SF-36): Physical Component Summary (PCS), Mental Component Summary (MCS) [32].

Statistical analysis

Variables including age, BMI, ROM, and functional scores were analysed. Independent t-test and Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test were used for continuous and categorical variables, respectively. McNemar’s and Cohen’s h test was also performed. A P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analysis was performed with SPSS Statistics for Windows (Version 26.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp Released 2019).

Results

A total of 102 patients with pre-operative genu recurvatum > 5° were identified. 60 (58.8%) underwent CR-TKA and 42 (41.2%) underwent PS-TKA. The patients had a mean age of 66.9 years (SD 7.6) and 86 (84.3%) were female. The median extension angle was − 5.0° (Range: −20.0° to −5.0°). Otherwise, there were no statistically significant differences in preoperative BMI, degree of recurvatum, range of motion, IKSS or OKS scores (Table 1).

Knee Range of Motion Over Time

At the 6-month follow-up, both patient groups exhibited significant correction of pre-operative recurvatum. The mean correction in recurvatum did not show a statistically significant difference between the groups (CR: 6.1° vs. PS: 7.8°, p = 0.090) (Table 1). A significant difference was observed in end range flexion (CR: 110.0° vs. PS: 122.5°; p = 0.005) and range of motion (CR: 110.5° vs. PS: 124.0°; p = 0.021), favouring the PS patients with reference to Fig. 3. In the CR group, there were 3.3% recurrences of GR (2), while the PS group had no recurrences (0)—a statistically significant difference (CR: 2 vs. PS: 0; p < 0.001) (Table 2). At the 2-year follow-up, there was a statistically significant difference between the groups in terms of mean correction in recurvatum from baseline (CR: 4.2° vs. PS: 6.0°, p = 0.044) (Table 1) and greater end-range flexion (CR: 115.0° vs. PS: 124.0°; p = 0.023), favouring the PS patients. In the CR Group there were 18.3% recurrences of GR and only 9.5% in the PS Group (CR: 11 vs. PS: 4; p < 0.001) (Table 3). The mean preoperative extension angle did not differ significantly between patients with recurrent GR (121.3° ± 16.9°) and those without recurrence (122.4° ± 19.5°; p = 0.72). There were no significant differences observed in terms of IKSS functional scores, IKSS knee scores, improvement in Oxford scores, or improvement in SF-36 physical component scores from baseline between the groups at 6 months and 2 years (Table 1). There was also no difference in postoperative satisfaction between the groups at 6 months and 2 years (Table 1). A post hoc power analysis was performed to assess the ability to detect a difference in GR recurrence rates between groups. Based on the observed proportions (18.3% for CR-TKA vs. 9.5% for PS-TKA), a Cohen’s h of 0.26 was calculated. With a total sample size of 102 (60 CR-TKA, 42 PS-TKA) and an alpha of 0.05, the power to detect this difference was approximately 73.9%.

Discussion

The aim of this study is to compare the clinical and functional outcomes of two commonly employed TKA techniques, namely CR-TKA and PS-TKA, in patients with pre-operative genu recurvatum (GR). We highlight the following key findings:

Patients in both groups achieved significant correction of their recurvatum deformity, however, higher rates of recurrence of GR was observed in CR-TKR compared to PS-TKR at 2-years.

Our findings confirm that both techniques are effective in improving joint function, but PS-TKA demonstrated biomechanical advantages in terms of greater range of motion (ROM) and lower GR recurrence rates at 2 years postoperatively.

Both groups exhibited significant immediate postoperative correction of preoperative GR at the 6-month follow-up, indicating that the surgical intervention was initially effective across the cohort. Specifically, the mean correction in recurvatum was 6.1° for CR-TKA and 7.8° for PS-TKA at 6 months, with no statistically significant difference (p = 0.090). This suggests that both implant types were similarly capable of achieving early correction of hyperextension deformity.

At 2 years, PS-TKA demonstrated superior durability of correction compared with CR-TKA (6.0° vs. 4.2°, p = 0.044), with fewer patients developing recurrent recurvatum beyond 5° (9.5% vs. 18.3%). The rise in recurrence within the CR cohort from 3.3% at 6 months to 18.3% at 2 years indicates a true recurrence of recurvatum rather than inadequate initial correction, underscoring the greater stability afforded by the post-cam mechanism of PS implants.

This pattern may be attributed to several factors. In CR-TKA, preservation of the posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) can lead to posterior capsular laxity or insufficient extension gap control [33]. In contrast, PS-TKA, through resection of the PCL and use of a post-cam mechanism, may offer better anteroposterior stability in extension, potentially explaining its lower rate of late GR recurrence.

The mechanical advantages of PS-TKA, particularly its post-cam mechanism, likely contributed to the observed reduction in GR recurrence and improved range of motion. By resecting the PCL and mechanically guiding femoral rollback and extension restraint, PS-TKA offers a more predictable method for achieving and maintaining sagittal stability in knees with preoperative recurvatum [34, 35]. In contrast, CR-TKA relies on the tension and integrity of the native PCL for AP stability—an approach that may be insufficient in patients with posterior capsular laxity or chronic hyperextension [33, 36, 37]. These biomechanical distinctions are supported by our findings, which showed significantly lower GR recurrence and greater flexion in the PS-TKA group at 2 years, despite no measurable differences in functional scores. Prior studies have also demonstrated that PS designs are more effective in managing flexion gap balance and extension stability, especially when soft tissue conditions are suboptimal [35, 38].

Despite these differences, no significant disparities were observed between groups in functional scores (IKSS, Oxford, SF-36) or patient satisfaction. This highlights that even with biomechanical recurrence of GR, clinical outcomes may remain stable, at least within the 2-year period assessed. It is also possible that patients adapt functionally to minor hyperextension without perceiving a decline in outcome—an area that warrants further biomechanical and long-term follow-up study.

One of the study’s strengths is its ability to differentiate between early correction and longer-term recurrence through time-point comparisons. While allocation was not randomized, consistent operative protocols and surgeon expertise helped ensure comparability. Nevertheless, the observed recurrence pattern underscores the importance of intraoperative stability assessment, especially in CR-TKA, where long-term correction of GR may be more challenging.

In clinical practice, these findings suggest that PS-TKA may be a preferred option in patients with marked preoperative recurvatum, not only for its improved flexion outcomes, but also for its potential to maintain deformity correction over time. However, as both techniques led to substantial improvements in quality of life and function, implant selection should remain individualized, factoring in surgeon experience, soft tissue condition, and specific anatomic considerations.

It is important to note that osteoarthritis can lead to genu recurvatum due to the degeneration of the medial condyle, resulting in a deformity characterized by both varus and recurvatum. According to Meding and colleagues [39], in cases without neuromuscular disorders (such as in our study population), the presence of preoperative genu recurvatum does not necessarily hinder the success of a total knee arthroplasty (TKA). However, it is essential to take specific precautions during surgery to prevent even minor residual instability in the coronal plane, as this instability is associated with increased extension problems during the postoperative phase. This is particularly relevant in cases of genu recurvatum, where imbalances in the surrounding soft tissues, including ligaments and tendons, significantly impact joint stability. In the realm of total knee arthroplasty, achieving precise ligament balance stands as one of the most critical surgical techniques for ensuring a successful outcome [40] and achieving proper soft tissue balance is essential for optimal joint function post-TKA. Various intraoperative methods can be employed to correct recurvatum, such as under-resection of the bone ends by using thicker components and tightening the extension gap to avoid recurvatum [2, 41]. In the case of PS-TKA, the absence of the posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) can lead to increased flexion gap laxity, which can be mitigated by inserting a thicker poly component to tighten the extension gap and reduce recurvatum during extension. Conversely, in CR-TKA, it’s essential to perform a more posterior femoral cut and less distal femoral cut to anteriorize the femoral component for proper balance. It appears that as long as joint stability can be achieved without a substantial alteration in joint line height during TKA, satisfactory clinical outcomes are likely, regardless of the degree of pre-operative hyperextension or the specific implant and insert utilized [42].

Consistent with prior comparative studies [12, 14, 20–25, 43–45], our results show no significant differences between CR- and PS-TKA in patient-reported outcomes or functional measures at both 6 months and 2 years.

Additionally, the absence of significant differences in complications and revision rates between the CR-TKA and PS-TKA groups reinforces the overall safety and reliability of both surgical approaches in the context of GR. However, it’s crucial to emphasize that achieving positive postoperative outcomes relies heavily on precise surgical techniques and the careful balancing of the knee joint [45, 46].

In the 6-month and 2-year follow-up, while there was a significant difference favouring PS-TKA in end range flexion, range of motion and recurrence of GR, this did not translate into statistically significant differences in patient-reported outcomes. It’s important to note that an increased knee flexion, as reported by Thomsen and colleagues [26] doesn’t necessarily correlate with improved patient-perceived outcomes.

The authors acknowledge that while statistically significant differences may emerge, they might not necessarily translate into clinically significant differences. This observation is in line with the findings of Li et al., who also noted improved knee joint flexion outcomes with PS-TKA compared to CR-TKA. However, their study, like ours, found no significant difference in IKSS scores [14, 47, 48]. This suggests that both TKA techniques offer comparable functional and patient-reported outcomes in patients with pre-operative genu recurvatum [49].

The higher rates of recurrence of GR after CR-TKA compared to PS-TKA do not compromise outcomes in patients with preoperative recurvatum. The overall results of the present study are consistent with previous research, demonstrating that existing knee implant designs for TKA can achieve significant correction of recurvatum deformity with low rates of GR recurrence. Mesnard and colleagues [50] conducted a case-controlled study and demonstrated that PS-TKA in the setting of severe preoperative genu recurvatum achieves good clinical and radiological outcomes at a minimum follow-up of 5 years and similar to TKA without preoperative recurvatum. Jiang and colleagues [51] reported that in patients with preoperative genu recurvatum they benefit from better 2-year functional outcomes after total knee arthroplasty, with improvement of hyperextension angle, IKSS, Oxford, and SF-36 scores.

Complications amongst the studied group included postoperative infection (2 patients, 1.9%) and stiffness (1 patient, 0.9%). The rate of postoperative complication of these GR patients is comparable with that of international registries. Post-operative infection occurring in 0.88–1.28% [52] of patients and post-operative stiffness occurring in 5–6% of TKAs. Manipulation under anaesthesia remains the intervention of choice for stiffness [53].

This study presents several notable strengths that enhance the validity and clinical relevance of its findings. First, it specifically addresses a distinct and underexplored subset of patients with knee osteoarthritis complicated by preoperative genu recurvatum, a population often excluded or underrepresented in broader TKA outcome studies [54–57]. By focusing on this unique clinical scenario, our study contributes to filling an important gap in the orthopaedic literature. Second, the study utilized robust clinical assessment protocols, including standardized physiotherapy-led measurements of range of motion and recurvatum using goniometers, as well as validated and widely accepted functional outcome tools—International Knee Society Score (IKSS), Oxford Knee Score, and the SF-36 health survey. This allowed for a comprehensive, multidimensional evaluation of postoperative recovery, encompassing not only objective knee function but also patient-reported satisfaction and quality of life.

Additionally, the inclusion of both short-term (6-month) and intermediate-term (2-year) follow-up periods provides insight into the trajectory of functional recovery and the durability of recurvatum correction over time. Despite the challenges of recruiting patients with this uncommon deformity, the sample size of 102 cases represents a meaningful cohort and allows for meaningful statistical comparisons between implant types.

Importantly, the study was conducted in a real-world clinical setting with uniform surgical techniques and perioperative care protocols, enhancing the external validity of the findings for similar institutions and patient populations. By stratifying outcomes between PS-TKA and CR-TKA, the study offers practical guidance for clinical decision-making, particularly in managing patients with challenging extension deformities.

This study was conducted with a well-defined patient cohort and consistent perioperative protocols; however, several factors should be considered when interpreting the findings. The allocation of patients to the CR or PS implant groups was based on surgeon preference, with two surgeons performing only PS-TKA procedures and a third performing only CR-TKA, which may have introduced variation in technique and experience between groups. Furthermore, differences in alignment philosophy, with a mix of mechanical and kinematic alignment being employed, may also have contributed to variability in outcomes. Nonetheless, all procedures were performed by fellowship-trained surgeons using standardised approaches, which helps to mitigate these potential sources of bias.

The study was retrospective in nature, with two follow-up intervals at 6 months and 2 years. While these time points are appropriate for evaluating early and mid-term outcomes, longer-term data would further clarify the durability of deformity correction and functional performance. Additionally, although the sample size was modest due to the specific inclusion criteria, it remains one of the largest dedicated cohorts examining GR in the context of TKA.

Finally, while validated functional and quality-of-life outcome measures were used, the study did not incorporate gait analyses, which could complement the clinical findings. These areas offer opportunities for future research to build on the insights provided here.

Conclusion

Both CR and PS TKA demonstrated significant improvements in knee function, range of motion, and quality of life in patients with osteoarthritis and GR. While the PS-TKA group showed superior end-range flexion and lower rates of GR recurrence at 6 months and 2 years, these differences did not correspond to improved patient-reported outcome measures.

These findings suggest that both CR and PS implants are appropriate options for managing extension deformities associated with GR. However, PS-TKA may offer biomechanical advantages in cases of pronounced recurvatum or in knees with poor posterior soft tissue integrity, due to its post-cam mechanism that enhances extension stability.

From a clinical perspective, PS-TKA may be preferred in patients at higher risk of posterior laxity or where sustained correction of deformity is a surgical priority. Implant selection should remain individualized, guided by preoperative deformity severity, intraoperative soft tissue balance, and surgeon expertise.
