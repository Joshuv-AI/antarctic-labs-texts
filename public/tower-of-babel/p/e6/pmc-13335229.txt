Microscopic evaluation of Li, Cd, and Co removal from aqueous solutions using equisetum and teucrium: statistical thermodynamic modeling
Authors: Mohamed Ben Yahia, Fatma Aouaini, Beriham Basha, Salah Knani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study provides a microscopic interpretation of Li+, Cd2+, and Co2+ adsorption onto Equisetum and Teucrium, integrating experimental isotherms with a grand-canonical statistical physics framework. Li+ and Cd2+ follow Type I monolayer adsorption behavior, whereas Co2+ exhibits Type II multilayer behavior. Li+ adsorption was best described by an ideal-gas Langmuir monolayer model, with high site densities (Wm = 152.05 and 145.32 mg g−1) and the highest capacities (Qm = 147.48 and 139.50 mg g−1), consistent with the highest configurational entropy peak (Smax = 100–106 J K−1) and a larger number of accessible adsorption microstates. Cd2+ obeys a Langmuir monolayer model with lateral interactions (Qm ≈ 104 mg g−1), while Co2+ follows a BET-type multilayer model with two adsorption energies (E1 = 31.7–29.75 kJ mol−1; E2 = 24.21–22.34 kJ mol−1) and lower capacities (Qm = 34.19 and 22.62 mg g−1). Internal energy profiles and dual–plateau isotherms support the occurrence of Co multilayer adsorption. The n values obtained for all three metals are close to unity, suggesting that each adsorption site on the wild herbs accommodates at most one ion. Energetic analysis suggests partial chemisorption for Li+ (E1/2 = 42.14 and 40.88 kJ mol−1) and physical adsorption for Cd2+ (38.69–38.22 kJ mol−1) and Co2+ (31.7–22.34 kJ mol−1), consistent with their predominantly exothermic adsorption behavior. Excellent agreement with the models (R2 > 0.99, χ2 < 0.22), together with negative Gibbs free energy values, suggests the spontaneous nature of the adsorption process and highlights Equisetum and Teucrium as efficient biosorbents for Li+ and promising adsorbents for Cd2+ and Co2+.

Introduction

Heavy-metal contamination of water is a critical environmental issue with far-reaching ecological and human health implications. Toxic elements such as cadmium (Cd), cobalt (Co), and lithium (Li) are released into aquatic environments primarily through industrial effluents, mining activities, battery production, electroplating, and chemical manufacturing.1,2 Unlike organic pollutants, heavy metals are non-biodegradable and tend to accumulate in soils, sediments, and living organisms, ultimately entering the food chain.3 Even at low concentrations, these metals pose severe risks, including nephrotoxicity, neurological damage, carcinogenicity, and developmental disorders in humans, as well as biodiversity loss and disruption of aquatic ecosystems.4,5

To mitigate these risks, a variety of conventional treatment methods have been employed, such as membrane separation, chemical precipitation, coagulation–flocculation, ion exchange, and electrochemical processes.6 Although these technologies can be effective under controlled conditions, they often suffer from several drawbacks, including high operating costs, incomplete removal at trace concentrations, sensitivity to pH variations, and the generation of secondary sludge requiring further treatment.7 These limitations highlight the urgent need for more sustainable, efficient, and cost-effective approaches to heavy-metal remediation.

Adsorption has emerged as one of the most promising alternatives due to its simplicity, low cost, and ability to achieve high removal efficiencies.8 Activated carbon, zeolites, and clays have been widely studied as conventional adsorbents,9 but their production and regeneration costs can restrict large-scale applications. In contrast, biosorption, a process utilizing natural, renewable, and low-cost biomaterials, has attracted increasing attention in recent years.10 A wide variety of biosorbents have been explored, including agricultural by-products (rice husk, sawdust, peanut shells),11 algal biomass,12 microbial and fungal biomass,13 as well as medicinal and aromatic plants.14 These materials typically contain functional groups such as carboxyl, hydroxyl, and amino moieties, which facilitate heavy metal binding through ion exchange, complexation, or electrostatic interactions.

Despite these advances, systematic investigations of wild herbs as biosorbents remain limited. Wild plants offer several advantages because they are naturally abundant, often considered invasive or waste biomass, and their harvesting does not compete with food resources.15 In addition, their diverse surface chemistry, associated with secondary metabolites such as polyphenols and flavonoids, may enhance their affinity for metal ions.16 Among them, Equisetum and Teucrium appear as promising candidates for sustainable bioremediation due to their favorable physicochemical characteristics. Their lignocellulosic matrices and secondary metabolites provide multiple oxygen-containing functional groups, including hydroxyl, carbonyl, and carboxyl groups, which may contribute to metal uptake via ion exchange, electrostatic attraction, or surface complexation mechanisms.17,18 Equisetum is particularly characterized by its silica-rich and highly porous structure,17 whereas Teucrium contains polyphenolic and aromatic constituents that may enhance surface reactivity toward metal species.18 The use of untreated biomass further reduces chemical processing requirements, thereby lowering operational costs and minimizing secondary environmental impacts. These combined features make both plants attractive low-cost biosorbents for remediation of heavy-metal-contaminated water systems.

In this study, Equisetum (commonly known as horsetail) and Teucrium, were selected as adsorbents for Cd, Co, and Li removal from aqueous solutions. Although both species are widely distributed and traditionally recognized for their medicinal properties,19 their environmental applications remain scarcely explored. The objective of this work was not only to evaluate their adsorption capacities but also to provide a microscopic interpretation of the adsorption phenomenon. For this purpose, adsorption isotherm experiments were conducted, and the data were analyzed using statistical physics-models developed within the grand canonical ensemble framework.20

Reliable interpretation of adsorption processes fundamentally depends on accurate isotherm modeling, as experimental data alone cannot reveal molecular mechanisms such as site occupancy, interaction energies, or layer formation. Adsorption models therefore play a central role in quantifying the affinity between adsorbent and adsorbate, predicting performance under various environmental conditions, and supporting the rational design of efficient biosorbents for water purification. Yet classical empirical models such as Langmuir, Freundlich, and BET provide only simplified descriptions and their parameters generally lack direct physical meaning, which limits their ability to describe phenomena such as lateral interactions, multi-energy sites, or multilayer growth. Advanced statistical-physics models overcome these limitations by incorporating molecular thermodynamics into the fitting procedure, enabling the extraction of microscopic parameters including the number of ions per active site, site density, and adsorption energies. In this paper, four models were applied: two monolayer adsorption models (with and without lateral interactions) and two multilayer adsorption models. The monolayer statistical model offers a mechanistic improvement over the classical Langmuir approach, while the multilayer model represents a physically consistent extension of the BET framework capable of describing infinite-layer formation and real-gas interactions. Using these rigorous models in this work provides a more reliable interpretation of Li+, Cd2+, and Co2+ adsorption onto wild herbs and yields mechanistic insights unattainable with empirical isotherm models.

Chemicals and adsorbent materials

The selected adsorbents were two wild herbs: Equisetum (horsetail) and Teucrium (Fig. 1), obtained from a certified herbal supplier. Equisetum is a perennial herbaceous plant with fertile, spore-bearing stems growing from rhizomatous underground structures, widespread in temperate and arctic regions of the Northern Hemisphere. This species contains bioactive compounds with medicinal potential. Teucrium is a genus encompassing annual and perennial herbs, shrubs, and subshrubs, commonly found in Mediterranean climates and recognized for various bioactive metabolites.19

Wild herbs: Teucrium and Equisetum (horsetail).

Preparation of adsorbent materials

The herbs were dried, cut into small pieces, and then milled into powders using a laboratory planetary ball mill-(DECO-PBM-V-0.4L). The resulting powders were sieved to obtain particle sizes of 50_ 400 µm using an Octagon D200 Digital-Sieve Shaker. The powders were stored in sealed glass containers and used directly without further chemical pretreatment (Table 1).

Preparation of metal solutions and batch adsorption experiments

Aqueous solutions of the target heavy-metals were prepared using distilled water. Stock solutions were diluted to achieve the desired metal concentrations. Adsorption experiments were conducted in 100 mL flasks containing fixed amounts of adsorbent and metal solution. The mixtures were continuously agitated for 16 hours at 120 rpm using a Rotaterm-orbital shaker to ensure equilibrium. After agitation, the solutions were filtered, and the residual metal concentrations were determined using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) (Table 2).

Determination of experimental adsorption capacity

Batch adsorption experiments were conducted over a range of initial metal concentrations (0.01–0.5 g L−1) and contact times (1–6 hours). Temperature effects were investigated using 0.5 g of adsorbent in solutions with an initial metal concentration of 0.1 g L−1 from 30 °C to 60 °C for 2 hours. To further elucidate the influence of chloride ions on the adsorption behavior of Co, Li, and Cd, additional batch experiments were carried out at 30 °C under identical operational conditions (adsorbent dosage: 0.5 g, solution volume: 100 mL, agitation speed: 120 rpm, and contact time: 1–6 h). The NaCl concentration was systematically varied from 0 to 5.844 g L−1 to assess the effect of ionic strength and chloride complexation on metal uptake. In addition, Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy analyses were performed on the adsorbents before and after adsorption to identify the surface functional groups potentially involved in the adsorption process and to evaluate possible changes in the chemical structure of the materials following metal uptake.

Comparison of adsorption capacities and ion characteristics

Both Equisetum and Teucrium demonstrated comparable adsorption performances toward the investigated metal ions, suggesting the presence of similar surface functionalities and adsorption sites. For both adsorbents, the adsorption capacities followed the order:Qa(Li) ≈ Qa(Cd) > Qa(Co).

Li and Cd were adsorbed in significantly larger amounts than Co over the investigated concentration range. This behavior indicates that Li+ and Cd2+ possess more favorable physicochemical characteristics for interaction with the functional groups present on the herb surfaces, including hydroxyl, carboxyl, and oxygen-containing moieties.

The observed adsorption differences are strongly influenced by ionic radius, ionic charge, hydration behavior, and ion mobility. Li+ possesses a small bare ionic radius (∼76 pm), a monovalent charge, and a relatively low hydration energy (≈−520 kJ mol−1). These properties facilitate ion mobility and diffusion within the porous structure of wild herbs, enabling efficient access to adsorption sites despite the presence of hydration shells. Cd2+ exhibits a larger ionic radius (∼95 pm) and a higher hydration energy (≈−1809 kJ mol−1), which moderately restrict diffusion into smaller pores. Nevertheless, Cd2+ maintains relatively high adsorption capacities because of its strong affinity toward surface functional groups. In contrast, Co2+ shows the lowest adsorption performance despite its relatively small ionic radius (∼74 pm). This behavior is mainly attributed to its very high hydration energy (≈−2010 kJ mol−1), which results in a strongly bound hydration shell that limits dehydration and reduces effective interaction with adsorption sites.

Saturation behavior and surface affinity

Distinct adsorption behaviors were observed for the three metal ions. Cd adsorption progressively approached saturation at higher concentrations, indicating gradual occupation of the available active sites. The reduced adsorption increase observed at elevated concentrations may reflect limitations in site accessibility and possible lateral interactions occurring at high surface coverage.21

In contrast, Li adsorption did not exhibit clear saturation within the studied concentration range, suggesting strong affinity between Li+ ions and the herb surfaces as well as the presence of abundant and accessible adsorption sites. The continuous increase in adsorption capacity indicates that the adsorbents retain high accessibility toward Li+ even at relatively high equilibrium concentrations.

For the Co–Equisetum system, partial saturation was observed between 0.05 and 0.25 g L−1, followed by a rapid increase in adsorption capacity at higher concentrations. This behavior may indicate the occurrence of multilayer adsorption,22 where additional Co ions are retained through weaker ion–ion interactions after formation of the initial adsorbed layer. Such behavior is generally associated with heterogeneous surfaces containing adsorption sites with different energy levels.

Implications for adsorption mechanisms and modeling

The experimental adsorption trends provide valuable insight into the mechanisms governing metal uptake on the investigated biosorbents. The absence of clear saturation for Li+ suggests highly accessible adsorption sites and strong adsorbate–surface interactions, whereas the progressive saturation observed for Cd2+ reflects gradual site occupation and possible lateral interactions between adsorbed species. The behavior observed for Co2+ indicates the possible contribution of multilayer adsorption mechanisms associated with weaker surface interactions.

These observations constitute the experimental basis for subsequent statistical physics modeling. Fitting the adsorption isotherms using advanced grand canonical ensemble models allows the determination of important microscopic parameters, including the number of ions adsorbed per site, adsorption energies, and the role of intermolecular interactions. Such analysis is essential for achieving a molecular-level understanding of the adsorption process and for optimizing the performance of these low-cost and environmentally friendly biosorbents for selective metal removal from aqueous media.20–22

Theoretical framework for microscopic adsorption modeling

Traditional adsorption models (Langmuir/Freundlich) provide a macroscopic description of adsorption equilibria but are limited in their ability to reveal microscopic mechanisms such as site occupancy, energy distribution, and lateral interactions among adsorbed ions.23–26 To overcome these limitations, the present work applies a statistical physics framework based on the grand canonical ensemble, allowing a detailed and quantitative microscopic interpretation of the adsorption of Cd, Co, and Li onto Equisetum (EH) and Teucrium (TH).20–22

This approach enables the formulation of four advanced models: two monolayer adsorption models, one neglecting lateral interactions and the other including them, as well as two multilayer adsorption models (layer-by-layer, L.B.L). These models enable the calculation of key microscopic parameters such as the number of ions per adsorption site (n), the density of available sites (Wm), and adsorption energy distributions (E, E1, E2), as well as van der Waals parameters (a and b) representing lateral interactions among adsorbates.21,22and27

Statistical physics-based monolayer and multilayer models

The adsorption process is conceptualized as a reversible equilibrium between ions in solution and ions bound to the adsorbent surface:282n(H) + W ⇆ (H)n − Wwhere H represents the metal ion, W the active site of the herb, and n the number of ions per site. Under grand canonical conditions, the solution is treated as a reservoir with fixed chemical potential, while the number of adsorbed ions varies depending on site availability and energy interactions.27,28

The multilayer (L.B.L) models (Type II equivalent to BET adsorption) further extend this framework by allowing sequential adsorption. The first adsorbed layer interacts directly with the herb surface with energy −E1, while subsequent layers interact with previously adsorbed ions with weaker energy −E2. This approach is particularly relevant for Co adsorption, where experimental data indicate the formation of multiple layers and interactions of Co ions with Cl− ions.22,27 Multilayer adsorption models capture both adsorbate–adsorbent and adsorbate–adsorbate interactions, providing a comprehensive description of complex surface phenomena.22,30

Numerical fitting and parameters extraction

To quantitatively interpret the experimental adsorption isotherms, all four statistical physics models monolayer without lateral interactions, monolayer with lateral interactions, and two multilayer (L.B.L) models were fitted to the data obtained for Cd, Co, and Li on both Equisetum (EH) and Teucrium (TH). The fitting procedure was performed using nonlinear regression,31,32 optimizing the microscopic parameters such as the number of ions per site (n), site density (Wm), adsorption energies (−E, −E1, −E2), and van der Waals parameters (a and b).

Two statistical metrics were employed to evaluate the quality of the fit. First, the correlation coefficient (R2) measures the proportion of variance in the experimental data explained by the model.32 Values approaching unity indicate excellent agreement. Second, the chi-square (χ2) statistic quantifies the deviation between experimental and predicted values, providing an additional tool to compare different models.22 The model yielding the highest R2 and lowest χ2 was considered the most suitable for describing the adsorption mechanism at the microscopic level.22,31

Lithium adsorption

The monolayer model without lateral interactions provided the best fit (R2 > 0.99, χ2 < 0.06) for both EH and TH. This indicates a uniform occupation of adsorption sites, with negligible inter-ionic interactions. The relatively high adsorption energy derived from the fit suggests stronger adsorption interactions, consistent with the observed endothermic process in the experimental data.

Cadmium adsorption

A monolayer model including lateral interactions was required to describe Cd adsorption accurately. Partial saturation observed in experimental isotherms is attributed to lateral forces among free-state ions, which reduce their interaction with the surface and modulate the adsorption equilibrium. The derived van der Waals parameters (a and b) quantify these lateral interactions and help explain the faster saturation of Cd compared to Li.

Cobalt adsorption

Experimental Co isotherms indicated multilayer adsorption, which was successfully modeled using the L.B.L multilayer framework. The model distinguishes the first adsorbed layer (energy −E1) from subsequent layers (energy −E2), capturing both adsorbate–adsorbent and adsorbate–adsorbate interactions. The multilayer behavior may also be influenced by chloride-dependent aqueous speciation effects (Fig. 3), which can modify the availability of free Co2+ species during adsorption.

Effect of NaCl concentration (0–5.844 g L−1) on the adsorption of Co(ii), Cd(ii), and Li(i) onto Equisetum (EH) and Teucrium (TH) at 30 °C under fixed conditions (0.5 g adsorbent, 100 mL solution, 120 rpm, 1–6 h contact time).

The results, shown in Fig. 3, demonstrate a pronounced and systematic decrease in Co2+ adsorption capacity with increasing Cl− concentration for both Equisetum and Teucrium. In contrast, Li and Cd adsorption capacities were only weakly affected or remained nearly constant over the same chloride concentration range. This distinct behavior indicates that chloride ions exert a selective inhibitory effect on Co2+ uptake under identical experimental conditions. The observed reduction in Co2+ adsorption is therefore more likely related to Co-specific aqueous speciation changes, possibly involving the formation of Co-chloride complexes that reduce the fraction of free Co2+ available for interaction with adsorption sites, rather than being solely attributed to a general ionic strength effect. These findings provide stronger mechanistic evidence for the role of chloride ions in modulating Co2+ adsorption behavior.

Interpretation of numerical fitting

The numerical fitting results of advanced models explain well the experimental results given in Section 2.5: the Lithium can be removed more efficiently from aqueous solution by Equisetum and Teucrium compared to the adsorption of the other heavy metal, cobalt and cadmium since the process is established without lateral interactions (Cd adsorption) and because chloride ions remain predominantly in solution and have no impact on the Li adsorption mechanism (case of Co adsorption).

Overall, this detailed numerical analysis establishes a direct connection between the experimental adsorption data and the underlying microscopic mechanisms. By linking the macroscopic isotherms to parameters such as site occupancy, energy distribution, and lateral interactions, this approach provides a rigorous theoretical framework to predict and interpret the adsorption performance of wild herbs for different heavy metals.

This methodology not only explains the superior removal efficiency of Li but also clarifies the differences observed for Cd and Co, thereby guiding the rational design of low-cost, eco-friendly adsorbents.

Evaluation of maximum adsorption capacity (Qm) and temperature effects

The parameters Wm and n are considered steric control factors in the adsorption process. The parameter n represents the maximum number of heavy metals that can occupy the wild herb cavity, while the fitted Wm values indicate the number of available adsorption sites at each concentration and temperature. These parameters determine the maximum adsorption capacity (Qm = n × Wm), thereby offering a quantitative description of the adsorption behavior occurring within the first adsorbed layer.

As shown in Table 5, the n values for all three metals are close to unity. This suggests that electrostatic repulsion prevents multiple ions from aggregating at a single adsorption site. The parameter Wm is particularly useful in assessing the effect of temperature on the adsorption process (Fig. 5).

Effect of temperature on metal adsorption obtained from 2 h experiments performed at 30–60 °C.

According to Fig. 5, temperature variations exert little effect on the adsorption of Cd and Co onto Equisetum (Qa(Cd) ≈ 16.7 mg g−1 and Qa(Co) ≈ 16.5 mg g−1 at T = 30–60 °C). In contrast, the adsorption of these metals on Teucrium appears exothermic, a typical feature of physical adsorption, where weak bonds form between the metal ions and the adsorbent.

In contrast, Li adsorption shows a clear endothermic behavior on both adsorbents. As temperature increases, the adsorbed quantity rises, reflecting stronger interactions between Li+ and the sorbent surfaces. This trend is evident in Fig. 5, where the bar heights for Li increase from 12.14 mg g−1 at 30 °C to 18.99 mg g−1 at 60 °C. The proportional increase in Qa indicates that thermal activation facilitates Li binding and mobilizes additional sorption sites. Correspondingly, the site density Wm increases with temperature, showing that thermal agitation enhances the availability of active sites and accelerates Li adsorption dynamics.

The relatively low adsorption of Co is attributed to competition with Cl− ions, which disrupt the adsorbed layers and limit metal uptake. Li and Cd are not significantly affected by such anionic interference, enabling more efficient incorporation into the adsorbent structure. However, Cd adsorption is influenced by lateral interactions, as reflected by the cohesion pressure parameter (a) and the covolume parameter (b). These interactions make the free phase thermodynamically more favorable than the adsorbed phase, thereby reducing Cd uptake. Li adsorption is not subject to these restrictions, which explains its more efficient incorporation into the sorbent structure.

The combined interpretation of Fig. 2 and 5 and the statistical-physics parameters yields a consistent classification. Cd2+ and Co2+ exhibit predominantly physisorption-controlled adsorption, rapid saturation, and exothermic behavior. Li+ shows strong interactions, increasing adsorption with temperature, and clear endothermic behavior. These tendencies match the isotherms' trends Qa(Li) > Qa(Cd) > Qa(Co) in Fig. 2 and the theoretical capacity sequence Qm(Li) > Qm(Cd) > Qm(Co), confirming full coherence between experiments and modeling.

Energetic insights into mono- and multilayer adsorption models

The evaluation of adsorption mechanisms primarily relies on the analysis of model parameters. Specifically, the interaction nature between the three adsorbates and the two wild herbs is governed by their corresponding adsorption energy values. The adsorption behavior of Li is governed by a single energy term c1/2, whereas Cd follows the monolayer real-gas model which yields the parameter w1/2. In contrast, Co adsorption is best described by a multilayer model involving two distinct energy levels c1 and c2. These parameters indicate the strength of the interaction between the active sites of Equisetum and Teucrium and each adsorbate.

The energy values in Table 7 directly support the adsorption tendencies observed experimentally in Fig. 2 and 5. The low energies of Cd and Co (<40 kJ mol−1) confirm their physisorption behavior, which explains the rapid saturation shown in Fig. 2 and the exothermic trend observed in Fig. 5. In contrast, the higher Li adsorption energies (>40 kJ mol−1) are fully consistent with the stronger chemical interactions indicated by the steeper isotherm slopes in Fig. 2 and the clear increase of Qa with temperature in Fig. 5. Thus, the energetic analysis validates the experimental classification: Li exhibits endothermic chemisorption, whereas Cd and Co undergo exothermic physisorption.

After metal adsorption, slight modifications in band intensity and local spectral deformation were observed, suggesting the involvement of oxygen-containing functional groups in the adsorption process. These spectral changes remained relatively limited for Cd and Co, suggesting predominantly weak surface interactions consistent with physisorption, in agreement with their adsorption energies below 40 kJ mol−1 and their exothermic adsorption behavior (Fig. 5).

Within this weak-interaction regime, Cd2+ adsorption is mainly governed by non-specific surface associations, where lateral interactions between adsorbed species may contribute to a redistribution of surface occupancy without significantly strengthening metal–surface bonding. This behavior is consistent with the statistical-physics parameters (cohesion pressure a and covolume b), which suggest the presence of adsorbate–adsorbate interactions that limit the effective stabilization of Cd species on the surface. In contrast, Co2+ exhibits similar physisorption characteristics but with a stronger dependence on aqueous-phase speciation. In particular, chloride-induced speciation changes may reduce the fraction of free Co2+ available for surface interaction, which, combined with the weak perturbations observed in FTIR spectra, supports a predominantly physisorption-controlled uptake mechanism sensitive to chloride concentration effects. By comparison, Li adsorption appears to induce more pronounced spectral perturbations in the O–H, CO, and C–O/C–C vibration regions, indicating stronger involvement of surface functional groups. This behavior is consistent with the endothermic adsorption profile observed in Fig. 5 and the progressive increase in adsorption capacity with temperature, reflecting enhanced activation of binding sites and stronger Li–surface interactions. Although the overall structure of the adsorbent remains preserved, these spectral and thermodynamic features collectively suggest a partial chemisorption contribution for Li uptake.

Overall, the combined interpretation of FTIR data, adsorption thermodynamics (Fig. 5), isotherm behavior (Fig. 2), and statistical-physics parameters establishes a coherent mechanistic classification: Cd2+ adsorption is dominated by physisorption with significant lateral interactions, Co2+ adsorption is primarily governed by physisorption and chloride-dependent aqueous speciation effects, while Li+ adsorption involves stronger interactions with partial chemisorption character, leading to its higher adsorption efficiency and temperature-dependent enhancement.

Entropy analysis and configurational disorder

As shown in Fig. 7, entropy reaches its maximum value near the characteristic energetic parameter specific to each adsorption system (c1/2 for Li, w1/2 for Cd, c1 and c2 for Co). At these points, the configurational disorder of the adsorption process reaches its maximum value.27,28 Notably, the entropy profiles of Li and Cd display a single peak (Smax (Li) = 100–106 J K−1 and Smax (Cd) = 65–74 J K−1), consistent with their monolayer adsorption mechanism involving a single energy level. In contrast, the entropy profile of Co exhibits two distinct maxima (Smax1 (Co) = 33–47 J K−1 and Smax2 (Co) = 59–78 J K−1). The first peak corresponds to the disorder associated with the formation of the initial adsorbed layer, while the second peak represents the overall disorder of the system once multilayer adsorption is achieved.22,30 These peak shapes are explicitly linked to the multilayer nature of the Co adsorption process, clarifying the origin of the two intensity maxima in Fig. 2 and 4. Moreover, the entropy peak of the Li system is significantly higher than those of Cd and Co, reflecting its much greater maximum adsorption capacity (Qm). This strong disorder increase is consistent with the superior site occupancy achieved by Li, which displays the highest number of effectively mobilized adsorption sites among the three metals.

Beyond the configurational interpretation provided by the statistical-physics model, the entropy variations may also reflect interfacial reorganization phenomena occurring during adsorption. In aqueous systems, metal ions are initially surrounded by hydration shells, while the adsorbent surface itself is associated with structured water molecules and hydrogen-bonded surface functionalities. During adsorption, partial desolvation of hydrated ions together with the displacement or redistribution of interfacial water molecules may contribute to modifications in the entropy of the system. Such solvent-replacement effects can increase the number of accessible microscopic configurations at the solid–liquid interface, particularly near the characteristic energetic parameters where adsorption probability becomes maximal. The more pronounced entropy response observed for Li+ may therefore be associated not only with its higher adsorption capacity, but also with stronger restructuring of the interfacial environment during adsorption, consistent with its stronger interaction with surface functional groups and its endothermic adsorption behavior.

Gibbs free energy and adsorption spontaneity

Fig. 8 shows that Gibbs free energy values are negative for all systems, confirming that the adsorption of heavy metals onto Equisetum and Teucrium proceeds spontaneously up to saturation. Moreover, the Gibbs free energy profiles of Li and Co reach stable saturation states, indicating that their adsorption processes evolve spontaneously toward equilibrium at high concentrations. In contrast, Cd exhibits an increasing enthalpy magnitude from the concentration c = 0.15 g L−1, which suggests that lateral interactions hinder its adsorption on both adsorbents at higher concentrations.21,34

Internal energy and stability-state analysis

Fig. 9 illustrates the internal energy behavior of the three metals. For Li and Cd, a single stability state is observed, consistent with their monolayer adsorption mechanism. In contrast, Co exhibits a biphasic profile, with two distinct stability states: the first (from c = 0.05 g L−1 to c = 0.25 g L−1) corresponds to partial stabilization associated with the saturation of the initial adsorption layer, while the second begins (from c = 0.33 g L−1) reflects full stabilization after the occupation of additional layers.22,30 This biphasic behavior is fully consistent with the two plateau regions observed in the Co isotherms in Fig. 2. These observations suggest that adsorbate–adsorbate interactions in the upper layers limit direct Co–wild-herb binding, thereby reducing the overall adsorption intensity. Collectively, the isotherm and internal energy analyses provide a comprehensive understanding of the physical interactions governing the stepwise multilayer adsorption mechanism of Co on the wild herbs.

The adsorption isotherms in Fig. 2 reveal that both Equisetum and Teucrium exhibit strong affinities toward Cd2+, Co2+ and Li+ ions. However, the distinct shapes and slopes of the curves clearly indicate that the three metals follow different adsorption mechanisms. To clarify these tendencies, advanced statistical physics models were applied to interpret both the curvature and the intensity of the experimental isotherms.

Lithium adsorption mechanism

In Fig. 2, the Li isotherms display a continuous increase in uptake without reaching a saturation plateau, suggesting a strong affinity and the persistence of accessible adsorption sites. This behavior is fully consistent with the modeling results, where the ideal monolayer model (no lateral interactions between adsorbates) provided the best fit. The calculated adsorption energies (>40 kJ mol−1) indicate a chemisorption-driven process. This interpretation is reinforced by the endothermic nature of Li uptake (Fig. 5), where thermal agitation facilitates the activation of additional adsorption sites. Consequently, Li exhibits the highest theoretical maximum adsorption capacities (Qm) among the three metals. Thermodynamic analysis further shows that Li systems exhibit the highest configurational entropy peaks (Fig. 7), highlighting the large number of microstates associated with the continuous availability of empty or newly activated sites.

Cadmium adsorption mechanism

The Cd isotherms in Fig. 2 are characterized by well-defined plateau regions, indicative of monolayer saturation and the absence of additional sites. Statistical modeling confirmed that the monolayer model including lateral interactions at the free phase is the most suitable to describe Cd uptake, consistent with attractive interactions among Cd2+ ions at high coverage. The process is exothermic (Fig. 5), dominated by physical interactions, as supported by adsorption energies below 40 kJ mol−1. This interpretation aligns with the Gibbs free enthalpy trend (Fig. 8), where the gradual increase at high concentrations reflects non-negligible lateral interactions that locally hinder additional adsorption.

Cobalt adsorption mechanism

The Co isotherms in Fig. 2 exhibit a distinct profile: partial saturation between 0.05 g L−1 and 0.25 g L−1 (obviously shown for Equisetum) followed by a marked increase in uptake at higher concentrations. This stepwise behavior indicates multilayer ionic adsorption, which agrees with the selected multilayer model. Despite this multilayer formation, Co presents the lowest Qm values, reflecting weaker interactions with the biosorbents, as confirmed by adsorption energies below 40 kJ mol−1 and by the exothermic nature of the process (Fig. 5). The multilayer mechanism is further supported by the internal energy profile (Fig. 9), which exhibits two stability zones: the first corresponds to the partial saturation of the first layer (matching the mini-plateau in Fig. 2) and the second corresponds to full multilayer stabilization, consistent with the sharp rise in the high-concentration region.

The characteristic shapes of the isotherms in Fig. 2 continuous uptake increase for Li, single-plateau behavior for Cd, and stepwise multilayer profile for Co highlight the diversity of adsorption mechanisms. Statistical physics modeling provides a coherent interpretation of these tendencies by quantifying the adsorption energies, site densities and the number of layers. The thermodynamic profiles (Fig. 7–9) further validate these mechanisms, demonstrating full consistency between theoretical predictions and experimental observations.

Conclusion

This study provides a detailed microscopic and macroscopic interpretation of Li+, Cd2+, and Co2+ adsorption onto Equisetum and Teucrium, combining experimental isotherms with grand-canonical statistical-physics modeling. The adsorption mechanisms were clearly established: Li+ and Cd2+ follow Type I monolayer behavior, while Co2+ exhibits Type II multilayer adsorption with two distinct energetic levels. Quantitative analysis revealed that Li+ exhibits the highest site densities (Wm = 152.05–145.32 mg g−1) and adsorption capacities (Qm = 147.48–139.50 mg g−1), indicating its strong affinity for the biosorbents. Cd2+ showed monolayer adsorption with lateral interactions and moderate capacities (Qm ≈ 104 mg g−1), whereas Co2+ formed multilayers with two adsorption energies (E1 = 31.7–29.75 kJ mol−1, E2 = 24.21–22.34 kJ mol−1) and lower capacities (Qm = 34.19 and 22.62 mg g−1) likely due to chloride-related speciation effects. Energetic and thermodynamic analyses demonstrated chemical adsorption for Li+ (|E| > 40 kJ mol−1) and physical adsorption for Cd2+ and Co2+ (|E| < 40 kJ mol−1). Negative Gibbs free energies confirmed the spontaneity of all processes, while entropy and internal energy profiles supported the distinction between monolayer and multilayer adsorption behavior. Overall, accurate isotherm modeling is essential for reliably interpreting adsorption phenomena. While classical Langmuir and BET models offer simplified views, the advanced statistical-physics models reveal microscopic parameters such as number of ions per site n, site density Wm, and adsorption energies E providing mechanistic insights at the molecular level beyond the reach of empirical approaches. This study highlights Equisetum and Teucrium as efficient and sustainable biosorbents for Li+ and as competitive adsorbents for Cd2+ and Co2+, thereby advancing the understanding of selective toxic-metal remediation.
