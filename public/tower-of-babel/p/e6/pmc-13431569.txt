Cystic Thymoma-Associated Fulminant Myasthenia Gravis with Rapid Progression in a Cat: Diagnostic and Therapeutic Challenges
Authors: Ji-Hyun Park, Geon-Ung Byun, Joon-Woo Lee, Dae-Hyun Kim, Dong-In Jung, Dongbin Lee, Chang-Hwan Moon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Simple SummaryMyasthenia gravis is a disease in which nerve signals to muscles are blocked, leading to weakness, difficulty in walking, and, in severe cases, respiratory muscle paralysis. This condition is well described in dogs but rarely reported in cats. A particularly severe form, fulminant myasthenia gravis, can progress within a few days and carries a poor prognosis. In this report, we describe the case of an 11-year-old Persian cat that developed acute hindlimb weakness, which rapidly progressed to tetraplegia and respiratory muscle paralysis. Because the initial signs resembled those of other acute neuromuscular disorders, diagnosis was challenging. A chest mass was surgically removed, and the diagnosis was established based on the presence of a cranial mediastinal mass, markedly elevated acetylcholine receptor antibody levels, and histopathological and immunohistochemical confirmation of a cystic thymoma. However, despite surgery and medical treatment, the cat remained ventilator-dependent and was euthanized on postoperative day 3. This case highlights that cats can develop this rare and severe form of myasthenia gravis and emphasizes the importance of early diagnosis and advanced supportive care.

1. Introduction

Acquired myasthenia gravis (MG) is an acquired autoimmune disorder of the neuromuscular junction characterized by the production of autoantibodies against acetylcholine receptors, impairing neuromuscular transmission. Clinical manifestations include generalized muscle weakness, gait abnormalities, dysphagia, and respiratory failure [1]. In dogs, MG has been relatively well documented with various clinical presentations, while in cats, it is rarely reported, and information regarding diagnosis and treatment remains limited [2,3]. In cats, acquired MG most commonly presents as generalized weakness, either with or without megaesophagus, and may be associated with a cranial mediastinal mass. Reported feline cases have shown a guarded prognosis, with limited spontaneous remission and a relatively high euthanasia rate [3].

Acquired MG is commonly classified by clinical presentation into focal, generalized, and fulminant forms. The fulminant form is distinguished by rapid onset of tetraparesis with respiratory muscle weakness, often progressing to respiratory failure within days and requiring mechanical ventilation [4]. This subtype is typically associated with poor therapeutic response and a grave prognosis [4,5]. Although canine cases have been described, feline fulminant MG is exceedingly rare. Acquired MG can also be categorized etiologically into thymoma-associated and non-thymoma types [3]. Thymoma-associated MG has been sporadically reported in cats, and thymectomy is sometimes considered a therapeutic option [5,6]. Although the exact immunopathogenesis remains incompletely understood in cats, thymoma-associated MG is thought to involve impaired thymic immune tolerance and antibody production against acetylcholine receptor–related antigens [7,8,9].

This case report describes a feline presentation of cystic thymoma-associated fulminant MG characterized by the absence of megaesophagus, initial clinical resemblance to other acute neuromuscular disorders, and rapid progression to respiratory muscle paralysis within several days. Unlike previously reported feline thymoma-associated MG cases with variable clinical courses, the present case illustrates a rapidly progressive clinical course associated with cystic thymoma and highlights the diagnostic challenge of recognizing MG before the development of ventilatory failure. This report provides clinically relevant insight into the disease spectrum, diagnostic challenges, and therapeutic limitations of fulminant MG in cats.

2. Case Presentation

An 11-year-old spayed female Persian cat weighing 2.9 kg was admitted to our hospital with tetraplegia as the chief complaint. The owner reported exercise intolerance beginning 5 days before admission, followed by progressive neurological deficits over the subsequent 4 days. No dysphagia, regurgitation, voice change, weight loss, anorexia, or respiratory abnormalities were reported before admission. Physical and neurological examination revealed depressed mentation, reduced palpebral reflex, menace response that was not reliably assessable owing to depressed mentation, postural reactions and nociceptive responses that could not be reliably assessed in all four limbs due to profound generalized weakness and severe debilitation, and generalized muscle weakness. Complete blood count, serum biochemistry, and electrolyte analysis revealed no clinically relevant abnormalities. Electromyography was performed in all four limbs, targeting the largest accessible appendicular muscles, including the bilateral triceps brachii muscles in the thoracic limbs and the bilateral quadriceps femoris muscles in the pelvic limbs. After aseptic preparation, a concentric needle electrode was inserted into each muscle, and spontaneous electrical activity was evaluated at rest at multiple sites within each muscle. Positive sharp waves and fibrillation potentials were observed in the examined muscles (Figure 1).

Thoracic radiographs identified a soft-tissue mass in the cranial mediastinum (Figure 2A,B). Ultrasonography revealed a well-defined, oval-shaped, anechoic cystic mass measuring 2.4 × 1.0 cm that was clearly delineated from the left atrium (Figure 2C). The lesion was not associated with the aorta or heart. Computed tomography identified a single well-defined, round-to-oval, non-contrast-enhancing, fluid-attenuating mass measuring 1.9 × 1.0 × 2.3 cm cranial to the heart, with a mean attenuation value of 22 Hounsfield units (HU). No invasion into adjacent vascular structures, pleural effusion, or gross evidence of metastatic disease was observed. Mild enlargement of regional thoracic lymph nodes was noted but was considered reactive. Based on the predominantly cystic imaging appearance, cystic thymoma and mediastinal cyst were considered the primary differential diagnoses (Figure 2D–F). Megaesophagus was not observed. Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain and vertebral column was performed to further evaluate the acute neurological signs. MRI revealed only mild degenerative intervertebral disc changes, with no clinically relevant intracranial or spinal cord abnormalities associated with the neurological signs.

Based on the history, physical examination, neurological assessment, electromyography, and imaging findings, thymoma-associated fulminant MG and acute polyradiculoneuritis (APN) were included in the differential diagnosis. The neurological deficits progressed from the pelvic to the thoracic limbs, resulting in acute tetraplegia, supporting the inclusion of APN in the differential diagnosis. However, the presence of a cranial mediastinal mass and rapid onset of respiratory muscle paralysis suggested fulminant MG as the more probable diagnosis. The day following admission, because thymoma-associated fulminant MG was strongly suspected in association with the cranial mediastinal mass and worsening respiratory muscle paralysis, urgent surgical removal of the mass was performed for diagnostic and therapeutic purposes.

Pre-anesthetic medications included cefazolin (Sephazolin inj., Korea Kolmar Pharmaceutical, Seoul, Republic of Korea) 25 mg/kg, tramadol (Shin Tramadol HCl inj., Shin Poong Pharmaceutical, Seoul, Republic of Korea) 3 mg/kg, and midazolam (Midazolam inj., Bukwang Pharmaceutical, Seoul, Republic of Korea) 0.2 mg/kg, followed by induction with alfaxalone (Alfaxan Multidose, Jurox, Rutherford, Australia) 2 mg/kg and maintenance with isoflurane (Ipran, Green Cross Corp., Yongin, Republic of Korea). Immediately before anesthesia, thoracic expansion was markedly reduced, and spontaneous ventilation was considered insufficient after endotracheal intubation. Therefore, controlled mechanical ventilation was maintained throughout anesthesia. Standard anesthetic monitoring, including electrocardiography, pulse oximetry, capnography, noninvasive blood pressure measurement, and body temperature monitoring, was performed during the procedure. Analgesia was provided using constant-rate infusions of tramadol at 0.2 mg/kg/h and ketamine at 0.6 mg/kg/h, which were continued postoperatively. No clinically relevant anesthetic complications or intraoperative vital sign abnormalities were observed. The total surgery time was 1 h.

A left fourth intercostal thoracotomy was performed to access and remove the cranial mediastinal mass. Intraoperatively, the mass appeared well-encapsulated and showed no adhesion to the adjacent tissues (Figure 3A). The lesion was completely excised without complications, and the resected specimen measured 26 × 14 mm, exhibiting a cystic lumen on gross examination (Figure 3B,C). The slight differences in size among imaging modalities and the resected specimen were considered attributable to modality-dependent measurement methods and ex vivo measurement of the cystic mass.

Following anesthesia recovery, oxygen supplementation was continued; however, the patient exhibited persistent respiratory distress. Serial venous blood gas analyses demonstrated severe respiratory acidosis without improvement [pH 7.17, venous partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PvCO2) 66.7 mmHg, and bicarbonate (HCO3−) 24.8 mmol/L], indicating inadequate ventilation. As a result, re-intubation was performed, and mechanical ventilation was initiated. Attempts to transition the patient to spontaneous ventilation were unsuccessful, with persistent hypercapnia observed. Continued mechanical ventilation was required. Volume-controlled ventilation was used with initial settings of an inspired oxygen fraction (FiO2) of 0.6, respiratory rate of 16 breaths/min, tidal volume of 8 mL/kg, and peak inspiratory pressure maintained below 20 cmH2O. Ventilatory parameters were adjusted according to serial monitoring of peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2; target ≥ 95%) and end-tidal carbon dioxide concentration (EtCO2; target 35–45 mmHg). Because fulminant MG was strongly suspected, pyridostigmine bromide (Pirinol, Myungmoon Pharmaceutical, Seoul, Republic of Korea) was initiated postoperatively at 0.02 mg/kg/h as a constant-rate infusion and was subsequently administered at 0.2 mg/kg IM q12h. This pyridostigmine regimen was selected to provide anticholinesterase therapy in a patient in which oral administration was not feasible because of respiratory failure and mechanical ventilation. Prednisolone acetate (Prednisolone Injection, Samwoo Median, Seoul, Republic of Korea) was initiated at 2 mg/kg SC q12h for immunosuppressive treatment. No clinically meaningful improvement in respiratory function or generalized weakness was observed after medical treatment. Because prolonged mechanical ventilation with an endotracheal tube requires continuous general anesthesia, a temporary tracheostomy was performed to facilitate long-term ventilatory support without general anesthesia. Despite continued intensive supportive care, the patient could not be weaned from mechanical ventilation by postoperative day 3, and no clinically meaningful improvement was observed. Given the short postoperative observation period and the delayed immunological effects expected following thymectomy, the long-term therapeutic effect of tumor removal could not be assessed. After discussion with the owner regarding the poor prognosis, humane euthanasia was elected at the owner’s request. A chronological summary of the clinical course is provided in Table 1.

Histopathological examination was performed (Figure 4). The cranial mediastinal mass demonstrated large polygonal cells consistent with thymic epithelial origin. Cystic structures were observed within the lesion, characterized by a lumen lined with flattened to cuboidal epithelial cells, some of which were continuous with Hassall’s corpuscle–like formations. The surrounding tissue contained mixed populations of epithelial cells and immature T lymphocytes, with reduced lymphocyte density compared with conventional thymoma. The mitotic count was less than 1 per 10 high-power fields, and no vascular invasion was observed. Based on these features, the mass was definitively diagnosed as a thymoma. Immunohistochemically, the epithelial component showed diffuse cytoplasmic positivity for cytokeratin (AE1/AE3) (Figure 4C,D), while scattered lymphocytes were positive for CD3 (Figure 4E,F). Based on these histopathological and immunohistochemical findings, the mass was diagnosed as a cystic thymoma. Additionally, a blood sample for serum acetylcholine receptor antibody testing was collected immediately before surgery; however, the result became available postoperatively. The acetylcholine receptor antibody level was markedly elevated at 4.48 nmol/L (positive threshold > 0.3 nmol/L), confirming seropositive MG.

3. Discussion

This report describes a rare case of thymoma-associated fulminant MG in a cat that deteriorated rapidly, progressing to respiratory muscle paralysis within several days. Feline MG is infrequently reported, and fulminant cases are even rarer [2]. Such rapid progression from hindlimb weakness to ventilatory failure has rarely been documented in cats.

The clinical course initially resembled acute APN, complicating the differential diagnosis. Neurological deficits progressed from the pelvic limbs to involve the thoracic limbs, culminating in acute tetraplegia. Findings supporting APN included the apparent ascending progression of weakness, acute tetraplegia, and electromyographic evidence of spontaneous activity. During the initial neurological examination, the palpebral reflex was reduced, whereas the menace response was not reliably assessable owing to depressed mentation. Postural reactions and nociceptive responses could not be reliably assessed in all four limbs due to profound generalized weakness and severe debilitation, and these findings were therefore not interpreted as evidence of primary sensory pathway dysfunction. Electromyography demonstrated positive sharp waves and fibrillation potentials, typically associated with APN. However, previous studies have noted similar electrodiagnostic changes in MG secondary to muscle fiber denervation [10]. In contrast, findings favoring fulminant MG included the presence of a cranial mediastinal mass, rapid progression to respiratory muscle paralysis, absence of clinically relevant intracranial or spinal cord abnormalities on MRI, and markedly elevated serum acetylcholine receptor antibody levels. This overlap highlights the limitation of relying solely on electromyographic findings to differentiate fulminant MG from APN and emphasizes the importance of acetylcholine receptor antibody testing for definitive diagnosis. Therefore, the combination of compatible clinical progression, thymoma, and positive acetylcholine receptor antibody testing strongly supported thymoma-associated fulminant MG as the primary diagnosis. The absence of megaesophagus also had diagnostic relevance. Although megaesophagus is an important manifestation of acquired MG, feline MG may present as generalized weakness without megaesophagus [3]. Therefore, the absence of megaesophagus did not exclude MG in this cat, particularly given the cranial mediastinal mass, rapid respiratory muscle paralysis, and positive acetylcholine receptor antibody testing. In the present case, nerve conduction studies and repetitive nerve stimulation were not performed, limiting further electrodiagnostic assessment of peripheral nerve and neuromuscular junction function. In addition, pharmacological testing with edrophonium or neostigmine was not performed because of rapidly progressive respiratory compromise and concern for adverse effects that could exacerbate ventilatory failure. Therefore, the combination of compatible clinical progression, thymoma, and positive acetylcholine receptor antibody testing strongly supported thymoma-associated fulminant MG as the primary diagnosis.

The association between thymoma and MG is well established in humans and dogs, with sporadic reports in cats [11,12,13]. Thymectomy has been considered a therapeutic option; however, previous studies report variable outcomes, ranging from clinical improvement following tumor removal to postoperative onset or exacerbation of MG. In previously reported feline cases of thymoma-associated MG, treatment outcomes have also varied, with some cats showing clinical improvement or long-term remission after thymectomy and medical management, including therapeutic plasma exchange, whereas others experienced postoperative worsening of MG [11,12,14]. Importantly, clinical improvement after thymectomy may not occur immediately, and surgical removal of the thymoma alone may be insufficient to reverse an acute fulminant myasthenic crisis. Surgery was not performed to relieve mechanical airway obstruction by the cranial mediastinal mass, but rather because thymoma was considered a potential immunological driver of MG and because excision was necessary for definitive diagnosis and treatment. Moreover, thymectomy does not immediately eliminate pre-existing circulating acetylcholine receptor antibodies; therefore, tumor removal alone may not provide rapid clinical improvement during an acute myasthenic crisis. In the present case, complete excision of the cranial mediastinal mass was achieved, but respiratory muscle paralysis persisted, reflecting the fulminant course and poor prognosis of this subtype [5]. This lack of immediate improvement was therefore interpreted as a reflection of the severe fulminant disease course rather than failure of tumor excision alone. Compared with previous feline thymoma-associated MG reports, this case was notable for extremely rapid progression to ventilatory failure, absence of megaesophagus, cystic thymoma morphology, and lack of clinical response despite thymectomy and medical treatment. These features emphasize the diagnostic difficulty and severity of feline thymoma-associated fulminant MG.

In feline cranial mediastinal masses located in the thymic region, primary differential diagnoses include thymoma, thymic lymphoma, and thymic cyst [14]. The cystic nature of the mass in the present case was clinically relevant because it influenced the initial diagnostic interpretation and broadened the differential diagnosis. On ultrasonography and computed tomography, the lesion appeared as a well-defined, predominantly cystic, fluid-attenuating mass, making cystic thymoma and mediastinal cyst the main preoperative considerations. Cystic or mixed solid–cystic thymomas may be difficult to distinguish from non-neoplastic thymic cysts or other cystic mediastinal lesions based on imaging findings alone. Therefore, although cystic morphology may not necessarily indicate a distinct biological behavior, it has important diagnostic significance in preoperative assessment. Definitive diagnosis requires histopathological and immunohistochemical evaluation to confirm the presence of neoplastic thymic epithelial cells and to distinguish thymoma from other mediastinal diseases, including lymphoma. Cytokeratin (AE1/AE3) immunostaining plays a pivotal role in identifying thymic epithelial proliferation, whereas CD3 labeling assists in characterizing the lymphoid population. In the present case, cystic thymoma was confirmed by proliferative cytokeratin-positive thymic epithelial cells forming cystic structures, together with admixed CD3-positive T lymphocytes. These findings were not consistent with a simple thymic cyst, which lacks neoplastic epithelial proliferation, or mediastinal lymphoma, which would be expected to show a predominantly monomorphic lymphoid population without a proliferative cytokeratin-positive epithelial component. Therefore, additional immunohistochemical markers were not considered necessary to establish the diagnosis in this case. Overall, integration of imaging findings with histopathological and immunohistochemical results is crucial for accurate classification and appropriate clinical management of thymic masses in cats.

Despite treatment with an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor and corticosteroids, clinical improvement was not achieved, and the patient remained dependent on mechanical ventilation. Pyridostigmine was selected as anticholinesterase therapy, whereas prednisolone was used to target the presumed immune-mediated pathogenesis of thymoma-associated MG [15,16]. The prednisolone regimen was selected as an immunosuppressive treatment because the disease was considered immune-mediated and the patient had a severe, rapidly progressive fulminant presentation. However, corticosteroid initiation has been associated with transient worsening of neuromuscular weakness in some patients with MG, particularly in severe or generalized cases. Therefore, although the persistent respiratory failure in this cat most likely reflected the fulminant disease course, a potential contribution of early corticosteroid-associated exacerbation could not be completely excluded. In acute myasthenic crisis, advanced immunomodulatory therapies such as therapeutic plasma exchange and intravenous immunoglobulin may be considered as adjunctive treatments to achieve more rapid immunological control. Therapeutic plasma exchange can rapidly reduce circulating acetylcholine receptor antibody levels and has been reported as an adjunctive treatment in dogs with MG [5,17,18]. More recently, therapeutic plasma exchange was reported as part of successful multimodal management in a cat with thymoma-associated MG, suggesting that it may represent a clinically relevant option in selected feline cases [12]. Intravenous immunoglobulin is used as a rapid immunomodulatory therapy in myasthenic crisis in human medicine and may be practically attractive because it does not require extracorporeal circulation or specialized apheresis equipment; however, evidence supporting its use in feline MG remains limited. These options were discussed but were not pursued because of limited practical feasibility and financial constraints. These considerations highlight the clinical relevance and practical limitations of advanced immunomodulatory therapy in feline thymoma-associated fulminant MG.

Although acetylcholine receptor antibodies are central to the pathogenesis of MG, a single serum antibody titer does not always reliably reflect clinical severity, short-term outcome, or immediate treatment response [6,8]. Studies have shown that antibody levels may decline following thymectomy; however, this reduction is not always associated with concurrent clinical improvement [9]. Nevertheless, postoperative monitoring provides important insight into the immunological changes induced by tumor removal and may contribute to long-term prognostic evaluation. In this case, a blood sample for serum acetylcholine receptor antibody testing was collected only once immediately before surgery, but the result became available postoperatively. The acetylcholine receptor antibody level was markedly elevated, supporting the final diagnosis of thymoma-associated fulminant MG. However, no postoperative sample was obtained; therefore, serial perioperative changes in antibody titers could not be evaluated, and the effect of thymectomy on circulating antibody levels could not be assessed. This limitation highlights the importance of serial titer monitoring for a comprehensive understanding of disease dynamics.

4. Conclusions

This report presents a rare feline case of thymoma-associated fulminant MG that progressed acutely to respiratory muscle paralysis. The clinical and electrodiagnostic features overlapped with those of other acute neuromuscular disorders, particularly APN, underscoring the diagnostic challenges associated with fulminant MG in cats. Fulminant MG should therefore be included among the differential diagnoses in cats presenting with rapidly progressive weakness and a cranial mediastinal mass. In this context, acetylcholine receptor antibody testing was essential for confirming seropositive MG. Although complete surgical excision of the thymoma was achieved, the patient remained ventilator-dependent, and no early clinical improvement was observed during the three-day postoperative period. The long-term therapeutic effect of tumor removal could not be assessed given the short observation period. Clinically, this case emphasizes that respiratory compromise may develop rapidly in feline fulminant MG and should prompt early recognition, immediate assessment of ventilatory function, and timely implementation of intensive ventilatory support. This case further highlights the importance of prompt definitive serological testing and consideration of adjunctive immunomodulatory therapies alongside tumor resection in the management of feline thymoma-associated fulminant MG.
