Barriers to delivering optimal care in epilepsy surgery candidates during transition of care: a short case series
Authors: Quynh Vo, Kathryn Lalor
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•In drug-resistant epilepsy periodically reassess potential for surgical intervention.•Concurrent surgical workup and transfer can lead to logistical issues and confusion.•Many factors play a role in surgical timing for drug-resistant epilepsy.•Counseling to give young adults knowledge of epilepsy aids in decision making.•Relationship with pediatric vs adult epileptologist affects epilepsy surgery process.Keywords: Epilepsy, Transition, Neurosurgery, Adolescents, Epilepsy surgery, Drug resistant epilepsy

Introduction

Approximately 33–50 % of children with epilepsy will require continued management into adulthood [1]. Adolescents with drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE) face additional stressors during this already challenging time, including increased psychosocial complications, social stigma, academic difficulties, reproductive concerns, concerns regarding driving, and difficulty developing autonomy [2,3]. The difficulties of the transition processes are compounded when the need for epilepsy surgery arises [5]. Approximately one-third of people with epilepsy (PWE) will meet criteria for consideration of surgical management [12]. This process can vary in length from months to years and involves two phases: 1) non-invasive imaging, functional, and electroencephalographic (EEG) testing; and, 2) surgical intervention with or without preceding invasive intracranial EEG monitoring.

PWE transitioning to adulthood are reported to have disengagement from their adult providers, poor compliance with medication, and increased hospitalizations leading to complications such as higher rates of cognitive and behavioral issues and increased rates of sudden unexplained death in epilepsy [2]. Literature suggests that creation of a transition program may alleviate some of these concerns [2,4]. Transfer of care has been defined as a formal hand-off of a patient from one provider to another whereas, transition of care has been described as a process of preparation of patients and their families for a change in their care [6]. The Ontario Epilepsy Task Force recommends introducing the transition process as early as 12 years-old, to prepare for transfer of care around 18–19 years-old [10]. Creation of a transition clinic allows for a dedicated service and set of providers to focus on the goals of transition without placing the majority of burden on the pediatric health care system [Fig. 1].

In recent years, the topic of transition has been given more attention, and high quality resources now exist for providers seeking both general and disease specific transition information [8]. Clark, et al developed a validated questionnaire to assess the transition readiness of young adults with epilepsy called the Epilepsy Transition Readiness Questionnaire (EpiTRAQ) [7].

Despite the increase in knowledge and resources surrounding the transition process, literature is sparse regarding the transition of care for epilepsy surgical candidates. This case series aims to highlight challenges met by epileptologists and young adults undergoing epilepsy surgical evaluation while facing transfer of care.

Methods

Patient data was collected from patients being transferred from Children’s of Alabama and the University of Alabama at Birmingham (UAB). Both of these are free standing hospitals that are both connected with the UAB Heersink School of Medicine, but do not share the same electronic medical records (EMR) and operate separately. Each hospital has a Level IV National Accredited Epilepsy Center, pediatric and adult, respectively. The patients in Cases 1 and 2 were directly transferred from pediatrics to adult epilepsy. The patient in Case 3 was seen in the newly created Epilepsy Transition Clinic at UAB, staffed by both pediatric and adult epileptologists. The patient in Case 4 was being considered for the Epilepsy Transition Clinic, but had not formally transitioned or transferred care out of pediatric epilepsy. IRB approved consent was obtained for each case.

Results

Case 1

A 21-years-old, right-handed man with global developmental delay and refractory multifocal epilepsy presented to the adult epilepsy clinic. He was under the legal guardianship of his parents. Onset of focal impaired consciousness (FIC) seizures occurred at 2-years-old, described as loss of awareness, lip smacking and complex motor movements which sometimes spread to become generalized (FBTC). FIC seizures occurred daily, while FBTC seizures clustered every few months. At the time of transfer to the adult clinic, he was on therapeutic doses of five anti-seizure medications (ASMs), having failed four others. He also suffered from immune thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP), with chronically low platelets.

He had undergone pre-surgical evaluation while under the care of his pediatric epileptologist at 15-years-old. His parents reported completion of both noninvasive and invasive testing without localization of the seizure onset focus. From their understanding, what was surgically possible for their son had been performed. Due to this understanding and his comorbid ITP, they were reluctant to pursue more testing, especially invasive intracranial monitoring.

Medical records sent to adult epilepsy with the initial transfer-of-care did not contain the detailed surgical workup. Despite multiple official written requests for both the EEG and imaging reports as well as the raw data for phase 1 and 2 testing, the correct records were not obtained until a non-neurology pediatric specialist went to the effort to directly obtain them.

The medical records detailed two epilepsy monitoring unit (EMU) admissions, capturing a total of 15 seizures. Semiology lateralized to the left hemisphere with relative hemiparesis of the right side at the onset of seizures and left arm automatisms. Fourteen seizures, with his typical semiology, showed a left frontal onset on EEG, which was concordant with his ictal single photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) results. One FBTC seizure evolved to nonconvulsive status epilepticus and appeared to arise from the left occipital region on EEG. 3 Tesla (3 T) magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain showed thickening of the cortical gray matter of the medial temporal lobes bilaterally and nodular heterotopia along the medial aspect of the atrium of the right lateral ventricle.

Given the significant seizure burden localizing to the left frontal region, he underwent intracranial EEG with subdural grids placed in the left lateral frontal, orbitofrontal, and interhemispheric regions. Three electroclinical seizures and one electrographic seizure were captured. Electrographic changes were first noted in the orbitofrontal region; however, it was indicative of a spread pattern rather than seizure onset. Additional placement of electrodes was discussed, but due to ITP, the risk was felt to be too high. No resection was performed and a VNS was placed. Detailed record review of prior surgery work up showed the potential for further exploration in previously unmonitored areas of the left temporal and occipital lobes.

This information was novel to the patient’s parents and two years after transfer, following many discussions about repeat surgical work-up, family agreed to proceed. EMU was repeated with ictal SPECT injection consistent with the left temporal region as part of the ictal network. With the assistance of the prior testing results from pediatrics, a repeat invasive monitoring plan was formulated included left frontal, temporal, insular, and occipital coverage.

Key Points

-During the process of transfer-of-care, health information can easily be left out, especially the details of extensive testing and monitoring done during a surgical work up. It is crucial for the adult epileptologist to have the complete medical records in order to provide a thorough assessment and offer the best treatment options.

Case 2

A 19-year-old, right-handed woman with focal DRE was transferred to the adult epilepsy clinic. Seizures started at 14-years-old with FIC and FBTC seizures of various semiologies including: 1) behavioral arrest with loss of awareness 2) repetitive vocalizations with loss of awareness. 3 T MRI brain showed left perisylvian polymicrogyria, bilateral periventricular nodular heterotopia, and malformations of the cerebral cortex in the left and right temporal lobes. VNS was implanted at 14-years-old when seizures became drug-resistant. Despite this intervention and trials of eight ASMs, multiple seizures occurred per month. Presurgical workup was subsequently done with four EMU stays capturing seven FIC seizures – three from the left hemisphere, one from the right frontotemporal region, two with right hemispheric lateralization, and one non-lateralizing. Ictal SPECT localized to the right temporal lobe. She underwent stereotactic EEG (sEEG) monitoring at 17-years-old with ten seizures captured all localizing to the left anterior hippocampus. Language dominance was present in the left temporal lobe. At the time of this case, the pediatric epilepsy center was not yet performing all available neuromodulation procedures that have not been FDA approved for patients under 18-years-old. The decision was made to transfer her to the adult center to ensure all options would be available for consideration. EEG and imaging source data were not transferred with the patient.

In the adult hospital, she underwent repeat EMU admission which captured seizures that were difficult to lateralize and localize. Records were eventually obtained from her pediatric epileptologist and presented at the adult multi-disciplinary surgery conference. The consensus decision was that Deep Brain Stimulation (DBS) was the best option given the widespread multi-focality of the seizures. At this point she was 19-years-old and living on her own with less family support. Referral for surgical implantation of DBS was delayed by another two years during which time she had ongoing seizures.

Key Points

-Concurrent surgical workup and transfer of care can lead to logistical issues and confusion for youths and physicians, which negatively impact outcomes.

-If the surgical workup is close to completion around the age to transition, it is reasonable for the pediatric epileptologist to complete the workup and intervention. Alternatively, this case speaks to the benefits of a combined pediatric and adult surgery conference where it is easy to complete verbal hand-offs.

-During surgical workup, evaluation should be done to assess the youth’s supportive network and readiness for surgical intervention.

Case 3

A 19-year-old, right-handed woman with focal DRE was seen in a joint pediatric and adult epileptologist-run Epilepsy Transition Clinic (ETC), located within the adult epilepsy clinic, after transfer of care was initiated by her pediatric neurologist. Onset of epilepsy was at 17-years-old with FIC seizures described as staring and hand automatisms. Routine EEGs had shown indications of a possible right temporal focus. 3 T MRI brain was non-lesional. She continued to have seizures every few months despite two ASMs. Non-adherence to medication regimen was also an issue.

Per transfer protocol of the clinic, all records were obtained prior to transfer to the ETC. At the first clinic visit, the EpiTRAQ was given. Her initial score was low in all areas of transition readiness. She reported struggles with retaining knowledge. She was not working or driving, with no plans to do so in the future. She had very little knowledge about her epilepsy and was still fully dependent on her mother for medical care. Extensive counseling was done regarding her epilepsy and medications. It was discovered that she did not like to take her medication due to side effects that she was experiencing, thus medications were changed. The potential for surgical options was introduced.

At her second visit in the ETC, her EpiTRAQ score had risen in specific areas of epilepsy knowledge. She remained very reliant on her mother for medical and functional needs. Epilepsy surgery work-up was again discussed and she decided to pursue this avenue.

In the adult EMU, four typical seizures occurred emanating from the right temporal region with spread to the left temporal region. Magnetoencephalography (MEG) showed right mesial, lateral, and temporoparietal spike sources. PET showed subtle, bilateral temporal hypometabolism. Overall, findings suggested a right temporal lobe onset, but an independent left temporal lobe focus could not be ruled out. The consensus from epilepsy surgery conference was sEEG with evaluation of the bilateral temporal lobes and mimics.

SEEG captured four FIC seizures localizing to the right hippocampus with rapid spread to the left hippocampus. Language and memory dominance was left-sided. She underwent right anterior temporal lobectomy at 20-years-old with Engel class I outcome.

Following surgery, she continued to struggle with information retention and significant anxiety. She remained very dependent on her mother. However, with ongoing seizure freedom, she began to develop some goals for the future.

Key Points

-Young adults with epilepsy need continual counseling to become autonomous and make decisions regarding their own care. Having a dedicated epilepsy transition clinic facilitated a smoother transition process.

Young adults with epilepsy need continual counseling to become autonomous and make decisions regarding their own care. Having a dedicated epilepsy transition clinic facilitated a smoother transition process.

-While seizure-freedom is the goal in medical and surgical treatment of epilepsy, providers involved in transition-of-care must understand the importance of and address the psychosocial factors and epilepsy comorbidities which also impacts a young adult’s quality of life regardless of seizure control.

Case 4

A 20-year-old, right-handed, man with focal DRE underwent a surgical work-up. Epilepsy onset was at 14-years-old following a right frontal lobe abscess requiring debridement. His epilepsy was deemed drug-resistant at 18-years-old with a seizure every 5–6 months preventing driving and making it hard for him to retain a job. He had comorbid anxiety which was tied to his ongoing seizures; due to age and loss of Medicaid he was unable to find treatment. He was very hesitant to undergo surgery due to his prior experience. He had strong social support from his mother and girlfriend. With repeated discussions and ongoing seizures, he did agree to pursue a surgical work-up at 20-years-old. While he would normally be transferred to the adult epilepsy clinic to start this work-up due to hospital policies about admitting people over 21 years-old, he had only agreed to undergo the testing and potential surgery with his pediatric epileptologist and the pediatric neurosurgeon who did his original abscess debridement.

Key Points

-As opposed to the delay of epilepsy surgery until after transfer of care, this case demonstrates that transfer to an adult epilepsy care team can be delayed for surgical work up.

As opposed to the delay of epilepsy surgery until after transfer of care, this case demonstrates that transfer to an adult epilepsy care team can be delayed for surgical work up.

-Strong patient and family relationships with pediatric providers can make transfer of care difficult. When transfer is eventually made, the adult provider often faces an uphill battle to gain a similar level of trust in the doctor-patient relationship.

Discussion

The goal of health care transition, in any specialty, is to improve the ability of young adults to manage their own health care and effectively use health services [8]. The process itself involves the facilitation of transition readiness, transfer to an adult provider, and integration into adult-centered care. In order to achieve these goals, there needs to be inclusion of professional and environmental support, family support, decision-making and consent readiness for adolescents, and professional sensitivity to psychosocial issues and shared responsibilities between pediatrics and adult providers [9].

Barriers limiting transition of care often results in decreased health service utilization, interrupted treatment, and worsening of symptoms. These issues can become compounded in the epilepsy population, especially in those with DRE considering the arduous process of surgical workup. As highlighted in the cases above, young adults can face social isolation, delays in treatment, behavioral and psychiatric problems, and failure to move towards independence.

Adult clinicians often face many unknowns when taking over the care of young adults with epilepsy, at times leading to unnecessary repeat testing and delays in care. These issues can be mitigated with support from pediatric colleagues. Given the complexities of the work-up and need for multi-disciplinary involvement, surgical epilepsy cases can be especially challenging to transition. Poor communication and coordination of care are common. The existence of a transition program may enhance medical and psychosocial outcomes for young people with DRE. Critically, adult clinicians need to re-evaluate the diagnosis, management, and psychosocial needs of young adults with the assistance of data and background information from their pediatric colleagues.

In DRE, exploring the options for surgical interventions should not be delayed, however the timing of transfer for surgical candidates is crucial to a smooth process. The Ontario epilepsy implementation task force recommended postponing surgical evaluation until the patient is established with an adult care team, when the patient is close to the transition-of-care age for improved continuity and efficiency [10]. However, due to social support and financial factors as well as relationships with pediatric epileptologists/surgeons, it is beneficial to delay transfer in favor of surgical work up at a pediatric facility in some cases.

When diagnosed with a chronic disease as children, many young adults are not knowledgeable about their own condition, having grown accustomed to relying on their parents for health care decision making and communication with providers. The process of transition should be started early to avoid learned dependence. Pediatricians are used to directing education and counseling to parents who are the primary source of information and questions. Focus should be shifted to providing adolescents with developmentally appropriate education about their epilepsy, clinical course, and treatment options [11].

The main limitation of this paper is that it remains a short case series, which cannot cover the complete scope of factors affecting young adults with DRE facing transition-of-care. A deeper discussion on psychosocial issues and roles of parents and guardians during transition-of-care is warranted but falls out of the scope of this paper. Some potential solutions are mentioned, but this paper falls short of recommending any single standardized process.

Conclusions

As young adults move from the pediatric to adult medical system, a complete transfer of relevant patient data is crucial; having a formal process can be highly beneficial. When DRE is present, psychosocial, financial, and relational factors should be considered when deciding on the timing of surgical evaluation and transfer of care. Upon accepting the care of a young PWE, epileptologists should readdress the diagnosis and management of seizures; mental and psychosocial needs; and financial, community and legal supports [10]. Young adults with DRE should be educated as neuro-cognitively appropriate so that they can participate effectively in decision making.
