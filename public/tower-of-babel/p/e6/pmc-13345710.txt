Efficacy of high‐frequency low‐dose rituximab and acalabrutinib in chronic lymphocytic leukemia
Authors: Charles C. Chu, Clare B. Heffernan, Nydia C. Jaimes‐Delgadillo, Christine A. Herne, Jennifer E. Bruno, Andrea M. Baran, John M. Ashton, Timothy R. Mosmann, Paul M. Barr, Ronald P. Taylor, Michael R. Elliott, Clive S. Zent
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnti‐CD20 monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) are effective but not curative treatment for chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL). To study rituximab efficacy, we examined serial blood samples from CLL patients receiving initial monotherapy with high‐frequency low‐dose (HFLD) rituximab for 1 week followed by the addition of acalabrutinib. Low‐dose rituximab (intravenous infusion of 25 mg over 1 h) decreased the circulating CLL cell count by 85% with no additional changes during infusion of the next 25 mg of rituximab. Limited clearance of CLL cells was not attributable to low concentrations of rituximab or complement and was most likely caused by exhaustion of innate immune cytotoxicity as previously described for larger rituximab doses. Circulating CLL counts and CD20 levels rebounded by 48 h when subcutaneous administration of 50 mg of rituximab caused a small but significant decrease in CLL cell counts. Administration of 100 mg of rituximab in the first week of therapy decreased palpable lymphadenopathy by 66%. Addition of acalabrutinib, a second‐generation Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitor (BTKi), on Day 8 of treatment was followed by decreases in CLL cell expression of MS4A1 coding for CD20, CD20 levels, and CLL cell sensitivity to rituximab‐induced antibody‐dependent cellular phagocytosis. Based on these data and previous reports, we propose that rituximab therapy in CLL patients is optimized by HFLD regimens, which achieve maximum CLL cell clearance at dosing intervals sufficient for recovery of immune cytotoxicity. Our data also suggest that the efficacy of rituximab, and possibly other CD20‐targeting mAbs, could be decreased by the addition of acalabrutinib or other BTKi.

INTRODUCTION

Unconjugated CD20‐targeting monoclonal antibody (anti‐CD20 mAb) therapy is an important component of treatment for B‐cell–mediated diseases, including chronic lymphocytic leukemia/small lymphocytic lymphoma (CLL). 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 The primary mechanism of clearance of circulating B‐cells by anti‐CD20 mAbs in humans is antibody‐dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP) mediated by fixed‐tissue macrophages in the liver (Kupffer cells) and spleen as recently reviewed. 7 We have shown in vitro that primary human macrophages rapidly engulf large numbers of mAb opsonized primary B‐cells for a limited period (<1 h) followed by cessation of ADCP for up to 24 h (hypophagia). 8 , 9 , 10 Murine studies support the primary role of fixed macrophage ADCP in the clearance of circulating mAb opsonized B‐cells. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 Additional lower capacity immune cytotoxic mechanisms are NK‐cell–mediated antibody‐dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) and complement‐mediated cytotoxicity (CDC). 8 , 9 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 Standard doses of intravenous (IV) rituximab, 20 ofatumumab, 21 , 22 , 23 or obinutuzumab 24 decrease but do not completely eliminate circulating CLL cells. An improved understanding of the cytotoxic mechanisms of anti‐CD20 mAbs and the limitations in clearance of circulating B‐cells could allow for the design of more effective therapeutic regimens.

Potential limitations of CLL cell clearance by CD20‐targeting mAbs include inadequate mAb concentration, persistence of a subpopulation of CLL cells with low levels of membrane CD20, loss of CD20 from circulating CLL cells opsonized by mAb, intrinsic resistance of CLL cells to immune cytotoxicity, limited penetration of large mAb molecules into lymphoid tissue, and finite immune cytotoxic capacity. CLL patients receiving their first treatment with standard (large) doses of IV anti‐CD20 mAb have rapid but limited‐duration decreases in their circulating B‐cell counts. Remaining circulating CLL cells have reduced CD20 levels and are resistant to in vitro CDC. 22 , 25 , 26 Cell membrane CD20 levels are primarily decreased by mAb‐induced trogocytosis, a non‐lethal removal of rituximab–CD20 immune complexes from the surface of CLL cells by immune effector cells. 22 , 25 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 Previously reported clinical trials have shown that the loss of CD20 by trogocytosis can be overcome by repeated small doses of rituximab. 31 , 32 , 33

B‐cell receptor (BCR) signaling pathway inhibitors can mobilize B‐lymphocytes from lymphoid tissue into the circulation, which could overcome the therapeutic barrier of low mAb tissue penetration. 34 , 35 , 36 This led to trials combining the first‐generation Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitor (BTKi) ibrutinib with rituximab, which showed no improvement in efficacy compared to ibrutinib monotherapy. 37 In vitro studies showed that off‐target effects of ibrutinib decreased rituximab‐induced ADCP, but this did not occur with the more specifically targeted second‐generation BTKi acalabrutinib. 38

Based on these preclinical data and experience using high‐frequency low‐dose (HFLD) rituximab therapy to limit acquired resistance to rituximab in patients with CLL, 31 , 32 , 33 we designed and conducted a Phase II clinical trial for the initial treatment of therapy‐naïve patients with progressive CLL. 39 The study was specifically designed to (1) evaluate an initial 1 week of HFLD rituximab (50 mg twice a week) with a focus on factors limiting treatment efficacy and (2) evaluate the effect of the addition of acalabrutinib on rituximab efficacy. HFLD rituximab caused rapid but limited clearance of circulating CLL cells and decreased CLL tumor burden in Week 1, and addition of acalabrutinib was associated with a significant decrease in CLL cell expression of MS4A1 and levels of membrane CD20 and reduced sensitivity to in vitro rituximab ADCP.

METHODS

Patients and specimens

Serial peripheral blood specimens were collected from 38 previously untreated CLL patients with progressive disease enrolled in a Phase II clinical trial (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT03788291) 40 with written patient consent and institutional review board approval. Patients received 50 mg IV rituximab with a planned infusion rate of 25 mg/h on Cycle 1 Day 1 (C1D1) and 50 mg of subcutaneous (SQ) rituximab on Cycle 1 Day 3 (C1D3) (Figure 1A). Rituximab 50 mg SQ was administered on Days 1 and 3 of the subsequent 23 weeks. Oral acalabrutinib therapy (100 mg every 12 h) started on Cycle 1 Day 8 (C1D8) for a minimum of 1 year (Figure 1B). Blood specimens were collected as detailed in Figure 1. COVID‐19 pandemic restrictions (March–May 2020) prevented the collection of 23 specimens from 7 patients (3.5% of 608 anticipated specimens, Supporting Information S2: Table 1).

Blood cell counts and processing

Blood specimens were processed immediately after collection as previously described. 41 , 42 An aliquot of each specimen was used for cell counts (Supporting Information S2: Table 2) and initial flow cytometry. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated and preserved as viably frozen cells for subsequent analysis. 41 , 42 , 43

Flow cytometry

Fresh or viable defrosted cells were stained with antibodies against CD3, CD5, CD19, and CD20 plus a live/dead marker (Supporting Information S2: Table 3), collected on an LSRII flow cytometer (BD), and analyzed using FlowJo 10.5 software (BD) to gate CLL and T‐cell populations (Supporting Information S1: Figure 1). 41 , 42 CLL counts were determined by multiplying the percentage of viable CLL cells by the absolute lymphocyte count (Supporting Information S2: Table 4). CLL CD20 surface molecules/cell was determined from the CD20 AF488 geometric mean fluorescence intensity (gMFI) ratio of CLL and T cells calibrated to an AF488 Molecules of Equivalent Soluble Fluorochrome (MESF) beads (Bangs Laboratories) standard curve (Supporting Information S2: Table 5). 41 , 42 Viably defrosted PBMCs were stained with a panel of antibodies against multiple CLL antigens and deposited C3b‐d fragments (Supporting Information S2: Table 3), collected and unmixed on an Aurora spectral flow cytometer (Cytek), and analyzed by FlowJo 10.5.

Lymph node measurement

Palpable lymph node size was estimated before initiation of therapy and on Day 8 prior to treatment using a standard clinical measurement of the sum of the products of the perpendicular diameters (SPDS) of the largest lymph nodes in the cervical, axillary, and inguinal areas bilaterally 39 , 44 in the 35 patients with clinically evaluable adenopathy and recorded data.

Serum measurements

Serum was collected at time points shown in Figure 1 using serum separator tubes (BD). After centrifugation, ~2 mL serum was recovered and stored in 1 mL cryovials at −80°C for future analysis. Thawed serum specimens were assayed for rituximab concentrations (Supporting Information S2: Table 6) using an ELISA kit (Eagle Biosciences, Inc.) 41 , 45 , 46 , 47 and for complement activity (CH50, Supporting Information S2: Table 7) at a clinical reference laboratory (ARUP Laboratories). 41

Complement activation and immune cytotoxicity

Complement activation was assessed by flow cytometry by measurement of complement C3 fragments (C3b/iC3b/C3dg/C3d [C3b‐d]) deposited on CLL cells (Supporting Information S2: Table 8). 25

Immune cytotoxicity was assessed by measuring in vitro ADCP using a kinetic video microscopy method. 10 , 48 Briefly, human monocyte derived macrophages (hMDMs) were generated from healthy donor blood, 9 labeled with Cell Tracker Deep Red (CTDR, Thermo Fisher Scientific), 10 , 48 and co‐cultured with CLL target cells (20:1 per plated monocytes) in 10% C5 depleted human serum (Complement Technology) with saturating concentrations of 10 μg/mL rituximab or the anti‐CD52 mAb alemtuzumab which was used as a non‐CD20–targeting mAb control. 10 , 25 , 48 , 49 ADCP was quantitated by measuring the phagocytic engulfment that appear as voids in the CTDR‐labeled hMDMs (Void or Phagocytic Index) and then calculating the area under the curve (Cumulative Phagocytic Index) of the normalized void count over time (0–2.5 h). 10 , 48 Experiments used 3 wells for each time point from the 17 patients with adequate numbers of CLL cells (Supporting Information S2: Table 9). The selected patients were significantly older and had significantly higher baseline absolute CLL cell counts than the other 21 patients enrolled in this study. There were no significant differences between these groups in gender, biological prognostic parameters, and other measurable parameters that could influence ADCP analysis results (Supporting Information S2: Table 10).

CLL single‐cell mRNA sequencing

Single‐cell RNA sequence (scRNAseq) of seven CLL patients was obtained on six specimens (C1D1‐pre, C1D1‐post, C1D3‐pre, C1D8‐pre, C1D8‐post, and C1D15‐pre) by the Wilmot Cancer Institute Genomic Shared Resource. 50 Individual cell suspensions were labeled with cellular indexing of transcriptomes and epitopes by sequencing (CITE‐seq) and cell hashtag oligonucleotide barcoded antibodies, 51 processed into amplified single‐cell–barcoded cDNA libraries (10x Genomics) which were pooled equally per cell count for each patient time point and sequenced (Illumina), targeting 100,000 reads per cell for gene expression, 2000 reads per cell for hashtag, and 10,000 reads per cell for CITE‐seq. These raw sequencing files were processed with Cell Ranger v9.0.0 (10x Genomics) and the Seurat 4.1.1 R package to retain high‐quality B‐cells (Supporting Information S2: Table 11) 52 , 53 for subsequent Seurat analysis. B‐cells were clustered and visualized on reduced uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) dimensions. Counts for target genes (MS4A1 and CD52) and surface protein measures via CITE‐seq were aggregated within each patient and time point to generate pseudobulk data for downstream differential expression analysis.

Statistical methods

CLL and CD20 molecule counts are reported as percentage of pre‐treatment counts and summarized using medians and associated 95% distribution‐free confidence intervals due to the skewed distribution of the data. CLL and CD20 molecule counts are also reported as a percentage of C1D8 pre‐acalabrutinib counts. The estimated palpable lymph node size is reported as a percent change from baseline lymph node size. Serum rituximab, C3b‐d, and CH50 are summarized by medians and associated 95% distribution‐free confidence intervals. Rituximab or alemtuzumab‐induced ADCP is reported as median fold change from baseline time point (C1D1‐pre or C1D8‐pre) and associated 95% distribution‐free confidence intervals. Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests compared outcome measures between pairs of time points.

Mixed‐effects modeling of associations of single cells was used to assess cluster membership of single cells in any of multiple cellular subsets between time points, while accounting for technical confounders. 54 The effect of rituximab mobilization on cluster usage was assessed by comparing cluster cell counts at C1D1‐pre to C1D3‐pre, and the effect of acalabrutinib mobilization on cluster usage was assessed by comparing cluster cell counts at C1D8‐pre to C1D15‐pre. DESeq2 was used to analyze changes in MS4A1 and CD52 expression over time, adjusting for the patient. 55 Negative Binomial Mixed Models were used to assess differential expression of surface proteins between time points, with a random effect for patient and an offset for total antibody count.

SAS (version 9.4, SAS Institute Inc., 2023) and R Statistical Software (version 4.4.2, R Core Team, 2024) were used for data analysis. As this study involved exploratory analyses from a Phase II clinical trial, the aim was to identify potential leads in interesting biology to explore in future work. No adjustments for multiple comparisons were made, and unadjusted P‐values are provided. These analyses are considered hypothesis‐generating and will require future confirmatory studies with appropriate sample size justification for each outcome of interest.

RESULTS

Rituximab monotherapy

Circulating CLL cells (median percentage of C1D1‐pre count) after the first 1 h of IV rituximab (maximum 25 mg) decreased to 15% (95% CI 8%–27%, P < 0.0001) (Figure 2A) with no further change in CLL counts during the completion of the 50 mg rituximab infusion (C1D1‐post vs. C1D1‐1h, P = 0.94). Day 3 (C1D3‐pre) CLL counts rebounded to 76% (95% CI 70%–83%) (C1D3‐pre vs. C1D1‐post, P < 0.0001). The first 50 mg SQ rituximab dose reduced the CLL count to 68% (95% CI 60%–80%) (C1D3‐post vs. C1D3‐pre, P = 0.004), with no significant reduction at Day 8 (median 78%, 95% CI 71%–90%)(C1D8‐pre vs. C1D3‐pre, P = 0.32).

Palpable lymph node size: During the first week of rituximab therapy, the median palpable lymph node size decreased to 34% (95% CI 22%–48%) of baseline (Figure 2B).

To investigate why the CLL cell counts decreased only during the first hour of the IV infusion, we measured C3b‐d opsonization of CLL cells, serum complement activity (CH50) until the initiation of SQ rituximab, serum rituximab concentrations, CLL cell CD20 levels, expression of MS4A1 mRNA coding for CD20, and sensitivity of CLL cells to in vitro mAb‐mediated ADCP.

C3b‐d opsonization of CLL cells measured in 21 patients increased throughout the infusion of 50 mg rituximab to reach 17.2‐fold of baseline at C1D1‐post (Figure 2C). These data show that complement activation can be achieved at low serum concentrations of rituximab (Figure 2C). C3b‐d opsonization decreased at 48 h (C1D3‐pre) but remained elevated compared to baseline throughout the first week of therapy (all P < 0.0001).

Complement activity (CH50) decreased throughout the initial rituximab infusion to a nadir at 55 units/mL (95% CI 47–64, P < 0.0001) and then rebounded at 48 h (C1D3‐pre) to 65 units/mL (95% CI 62–72) which was still lower than baseline (P < 0.0001) (Figure 2D) confirming that low‐dose rituximab activated complement. 25 Only six patients (15.8%) had any post‐infusion CH50 measurements below the normal range of 38.9–89.9 units/mL, suggesting that depletion of serum complement did not limit clearance of CLL cells in most patients (Figure 2D).

Rituximab serum concentrations reached 2.2 μg/mL (95% CI 1.3–2.7) at 1 h and 10.3 μg/mL (95% CI 5.3–13.4) at the end of the rituximab infusion (both P < 0.0001) (Figure 2E) showing that availability of rituximab was not a limiting factor for clearance of circulating CLL cells. Of note, rituximab opsonization of CLL cells in vitro has been shown to achieve maximum binding at 10 μg/mL. 43 , 56 Administration of 50 mg rituximab SQ on Day 3 did not increase rituximab concentrations measured 2 h later (P = 0.94) or on Day 8 (P = 0.63) (Figure 2E).

CD20 levels on circulating CLL cells decreased during the first hour of the IV rituximab infusion to 67% (95% CI 53%–73%) of baseline (P = 0.002) (Figure 2F). Based on data from our previous studies, 9 this decrease would be expected to have only a limited effect on rituximab‐induced CLL cell clearance. In contrast, at the completion of the rituximab infusion, CD20 levels on surviving circulating cells were decreased to 42% (95% CI 33%–55%) of baseline (C1D1‐post vs. C1D1‐1h, P < 0.0001), which could potentially decrease CLL cell clearance by rituximab. By Day 3 (C1D3Pre), CD20 levels rebounded to 81% (95% CI 59%–106%) of baseline (C1D3‐pre vs. C1D1‐post, P = 0.0003). There was insufficient evidence of a change in CD20 levels after administration of the first dose of SQ rituximab (C1D3‐post vs. C1D3‐pre, P = 0.15, C1D8‐pre vs. C1D3‐pre, P = 0.51).

MS4A1 was measured in CLL cell samples from seven patients. Compared to baseline (C1D1‐pre), CLL cell MS4A1 expression decreased by 20% (fold change = 0.80, P = 0.02) after the completion of the rituximab infusion, rebounded to 12% below baseline expression at 48 h (C1D3‐pre, fold change = 0.88, P = 0.17) and then decreased to 29% below baseline after administration of SQ rituximab at Day 8 (C1D8‐pre, fold change = 0.71, P = 0.0004) (Figure 2G). The expression of CD52 coding for the target of alemtuzumab was only significantly decreased after initiation of therapy with rituximab at C1D1‐post (Figure 2G). These findings suggest that rituximab treatment could select for the survival of CLL cells with lower expression of MS4A1 and CD20. We have not determined the mechanism of reduction of CD52 expression at C1D1‐post.

In vitro ADCP of CLL cells collected from 17 patients prior to rituximab (C1D1‐pre), during (C1D1‐1h), and after (C1D1‐post, C1D3‐pre) IV rituximab infusion, and before initiation of acalabrutinib therapy (C1D8‐pre) was measured in co‐cultures of hMDM and CLL cells using medium containing 10% C5 negative serum with 10 μg/mL rituximab or alemtuzumab. Under these conditions, the cells can be opsonized with C3 fragments but not lysed by downstream complement. Alemtuzumab‐induced ADCP, used as a non‐CD20–dependent comparison, was considerably higher than rituximab‐induced ADCP at all time points in our study as expected. 9 In vitro rituximab‐induced ADCP of CLL cells was significantly decreased compared to pre‐treatment at all time points (C1D1‐1h, P = 0.01; C1D1‐post, P = 0.005; C1D3‐pre, P = 0.006; and C1C8‐pre, P = 0.03) (Figure 2H). In contrast, alemtuzumab‐induced in vitro ADCP of these specimens was decreased at C1D8‐pre (P = 0.049) but not at other time points (C1D1‐1h, P = 0.09; C1D1‐post, P = 0.07; and C1D3‐pre, P = 0.13). These data suggest that a subpopulation of CLL cells could be resistant to rituximab‐induced ADCP.

Addition of acalabrutinib

The first 100 mg oral dose of acalabrutinib was administered with 50 mg SQ rituximab on Day 8, and blood was sampled as detailed in Figure 1B.

Circulating CLL cell counts were compared to the level measured prior to initiation of acalabrutinib therapy (C1D8‐pre). There was no significant change at 2 h (C1D8‐post, P = 0.22) but at 1‐week (C1D15‐pre) counts increased to 210% (95% CI 160%–298%, P < 0.0001) and then decreased to 2.7% (95% CI 1.2%–6.9%) at C9D1‐pre (P < 0.0001) (Figure 3A).

Rituximab serum concentrations: Rituximab 50 mg SQ was administered on Days 1 and 3 of each week for 23 weeks after initiation of acalabrutinib therapy. The rituximab serum concentration increased to 8.6 μg/mL (95% CI 4.1–13.0) at the end of the first cycle of therapy (C2D1) (Figure 3B). The highest serum rituximab concentration measured was 40.9 μg/mL (95% CI 12–52) 4 days after administration of the last dose of rituximab (C7D1). The serum rituximab concentration decreased to 7.6 μg/mL (95% CI 4–10) 8 weeks later at C9D1.

C3b‐d opsonization of CLL cells measured after initiation of acalabrutinib was compared to C1D8‐pre levels in 30 patients. Levels were not increased 2 h after the 50 mg SQ dose on C1D8. There was a small but significant increase in C3b‐d opsonization at 1 and 3 weeks after initiation of acalabrutinib therapy (C1D15‐pre, P = 0.04; C2D1‐pre, P = 0.007). The 1.47‐fold increase in C3b‐d opsonization following acalabrutinib therapy (C2D1‐pre) was considerably smaller than the 17.2‐fold increase caused by the first IV dose of rituximab (C1C1‐post). The C3b‐d levels then decreased over the next 4 weeks (C3D1) to pre‐acalabrutinib levels (Figure 3C). Despite the increase in rituximab levels after C1D5 (Figure 3B), there was only a small and non‐sustained increase in C3b‐d opsonization of circulating CLL cells, suggesting limited complement activation by rituximab after the first cycle of therapy.

CD20 levels on circulating CLL cells were compared to levels prior to the initiation of acalabrutinib (C1D8‐pre). After 1 week, CD20 levels decreased to 56% of pre‐acalabrutinib levels (C1D15‐pre, 95% CI 44%–71%, P = 0.0004), remained stable for the duration of rituximab therapy (C2D1‐pre vs. C1D15‐pre, P = 0.06; C2D1 to C7D1, P = 0.68), and rebounded in the 8 weeks following cessation of rituximab therapy (C7D1 to C9D1, P = 0.004) (Figure 3D). One week of acalabrutinib therapy was associated with a 52% reduction in CLL cell expression of MS4A1 (fold change =0.48, 95% CI 0.39–0.58, P < 0.0001) and a lesser but still significant decrease in expression of CD52 (fold change 0.81, 95% CI 0.73–0.90, P = 0.0004) (Figure 3E). Administration of a single dose of ibrutinib has been previously shown to decrease MS4A1 expression in circulating CLL cells by an equivalent ratio to that demonstrated for acalabrutinib in this study. 57 , 58 We are not aware of any published data on the effect of acalabrutinib on MS4A1 expression in vivo in CLL. Addition of acalabrutinib to therapy was thus associated with significantly decreased CLL cell expression of MS4A1 and cell membrane CD20.

ADCP of circulating CLL cells collected from 17 patients after addition of acalabrutinib to therapy at 2 h (C1D8‐post), 1 week (C1D15‐pre), 3 weeks (C2D1‐pre), and 7 weeks (C3‐pre) was measured in vitro and compared to ADCP of CLL cells collected at C1D8‐pre. Addition of acalabrutinib was associated with further decrease in the sensitivity of CLL cells to ADCP induced by rituximab (P = 0.03 at C3D1) and alemtuzumab (P = 0.005 at C2D1 and P = 0.0005 at C3D1) (Figure 3F). These data show that the addition of acalabrutinib to therapy is associated with additional decreases in susceptibility of circulating CLL cells to ADCP.

IV rituximab effect: Compared to the pre‐treatment CLL cells (Figure 4A, C1D1‐pre), Cluster 2 appears to be dominant after the infusion of 50 mg of rituximab, and thus the size of all other clusters was attenuated (C1D1‐post). Eight of the top 10 discriminating marker genes in Cluster 2 are induced by interferonγ (Table 1). These findings support our previously published data from this clinical trial showing that initiation of rituximab therapy causes a unique cytokine release syndrome characterized by high serum levels of interferonγ induced cytokine IP10 (CXCL10). 41 Increased levels of serum cytokines have been reported after activation of complement, which generates the anaphylatoxins C3a and C3b. 59 , 60 The source of the cytokines in patients receiving rituximab therapy has not yet been determined and is being investigated.

CLL cells mobilized into circulation: In untreated patients, CLL cells continually traffic between lymphoid tissue and the circulation. 61 , 62 We investigated the mobilization of CLL cells into the circulation after initiation of rituximab therapy (rituximab mobilization, C1D1‐post to C1D3‐pre) and subsequent addition of acalabrutinib therapy (acalabrutinib mobilization, C1D8‐pre to C1D15‐pre) by analysis of changes in cluster usage and expression of selected proteins measured via CITE‐seq. Rituximab and acalabrutinib mobilizations are associated with discrete patterns of cluster usage (Figure 4B) and expression of selected proteins (Figure 4C). These differences suggest that the mechanism of CLL mobilization induced by rituximab is different from mobilization induced by acalabrutinib.

DISCUSSION

Small doses of IV rituximab (≤25 mg) rapidly caused a large (85%) reduction in circulating CLL cells with activation of complement and induction of an interferonγ pattern of mRNA expression in surviving circulating CLL cells. Despite adequate amounts of rituximab and complement, clearance of circulating CLL cells was limited to <1 h. Circulating CLL cell counts rebounded at 48 h suggesting that CLL cells were mobilized from the lymphoid tissues into the circulation. In 1 week, 100 mg of rituximab monotherapy decreased palpable lymphadenopathy by 66%. Addition of acalabrutinib to therapy caused a > 2‐fold increase in circulating CLL cells, which decreased to <3% of baseline at 8 weeks after the last dose of rituximab. Acalabrutinib therapy was associated with decreased CLL cell MS4A1 expression and CD20 levels, and lower sensitivity to ADCP.

The limited duration of rapid clearance of CLL cells from the circulation is similar to observations from in vitro and murine studies showing that mAb including rituximab and obinutuzumab rapidly induce limited duration macrophage ADCP of opsonized B‐cell. 8 , 9 , 10 In vitro studies with primary human cells and murine model studies could provide additional data on the mechanisms that limit macrophage ADCP. These data could be used to design novel regimens with lower doses sufficient to achieve maximum innate immune cytotoxicity that are repeated after recovery of innate immune cytotoxicity.

The current standard of care doses of rituximab and obinutuzumab (>500 mg) far exceed those needed to achieve maximum clearance of circulating B‐cells. 63 The mAb opsonized B‐cells not cleared by the innate immune system become resistant because of loss of CD20 by trogocytosis. 22 , 25 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 In contrast, use of low‐dose rituximab limits both the extent and duration of decreases in CLL CD20 levels 31 , 32 , 33 as confirmed in this study. Repeated clearance of CLL cells by administration of small doses of rituximab at intervals of at least 48 h shows that innate immune cytotoxicity recovers from exhaustion with use of the HFLD regimen. 31 , 32 , 33 This rituximab regimen can be adopted in future clinical trials of combination therapy, and this principle could be generalizable to other mAbs including obinutuzumab and daratumumab. 64

We have previously reported that the initial dose of rituximab in this clinical trial induced a unique cytokine release syndrome (CRS) in 95% of patients. 41 Examination of scRNA of circulating CLL cells collected at the end of the rituximab infusion showed an increase in expression of interferonγ induced genes (Table 1). The source and biological role of this CRS is currently being studied, and these data could potentially be useful for improving the management of rituximab infusion reactions.

The rebound of CLL cell counts after initial rituximab‐induced reduction (Figure 2A) has been previously reported. 26 , 33 Our study shows that this rebound is associated with a decrease in lymph node size (Figure 2B) suggesting that CLL cells are being mobilized from lymphoid tissue into the circulation. Similar mobilization has been previously described in CLL patients after treatment with leukapheresis, 34 corticosteroids, 34 and anti‐idiotype mAb. 65 Differences in the single‐cell mRNA and protein expression cluster ratios (Figure 4B) and fold change of protein expression (Figure 4C) of CLL cells from the “rituximab mobilization” compared to the “acalabrutinib mobilization” suggest different mechanisms of mobilization. CLL cell mobilization by low‐dose IV rituximab therapy could enhance efficacy by overcoming the therapeutic barriers of slow lymphoid tissue penetration by mAb and limited numbers of immune effector cells in lymphoid tissue.

The efficacy of rituximab could be limited by CLL cell resistance to ADCP. Our study shows that CLL cells surviving in vivo treatment with rituximab have decreased in vitro sensitivity to ADCP (Figure 2H). Loss of CD20 by CLL cells could contribute to this resistance, but other mechanisms may also be involved because decreased sensitivity to ADCP persists when CLL cell CD20 levels have recovered to near baseline levels and was also demonstrated for alemtuzumab (Figure 2F). Additional studies of the mechanisms of resistance of CLL cells to ADCP are required.

CLL cells generally have low levels of CD20 expression, and CDC is not usually a major mechanism of action of rituximab in CLL. 66 We show that infusion of 50 mg of rituximab activates complement, and circulating CLL cells are opsonized with C3‐derived fragments, which could provide targets for complement‐dependent ADCP (cADCP) (Figure 2C). Although standard dose rituximab has been previously shown to deplete complement with recovery taking 1–4 weeks, 25 this does not occur after low‐dose IV rituximab therapy. The role of cADCP in the clearance of circulating CLL cells requires further study.

The design of the clinical trial providing the specimens and data for this study was based on our preclinical data from in vitro studies of the effects of ibrutinib and acalabrutinib on mAb‐mediated macrophage ADCP. 9 , 38 These data showed that ibrutinib, but not acalabrutinib, inhibited macrophage ADCP of CLL cells and led to the development of the HFLD rituximab and acalabrutinib regimen. In this study, we show that addition of acalabrutinib to therapy was associated with large decreases in MS4A1 expression (52%) and CLL cell CD20 levels (44%) at C1D15‐pre (Figure 3D) and decreased sensitivity to ADCP (Figure 3F). We propose that acalabrutinib could decrease CLL cell CD20 to levels that reduce the efficacy of CD20‐targeting mAbs resulting in decreased efficacy of the HFLD rituximab and acalabrutinib regimen. Of note, only one of 38 treated patients achieved an undetectable measurable residual disease (uMRD) complete response after 12 cycles of therapy, 40 which was the primary clinical goal of this regimen. These data suggest that the addition of rituximab did not improve outcome compared to acalabrutinib alone, and if proven in future studies, this finding could have important implications for the design of future studies of regimens to treat B‐cell–mediated disease that combine BCR pathway inhibitors and mAb. These findings could also explain why a recently published randomized controlled trial of initial treatment of CLL patients suggested limited benefit of the addition of obinutuzumab to the combination of acalabrutinib and venetoclax. 67

In conclusion, our results suggest that mAb targeting CD20 on circulating pathological B‐cells can be optimized by regimens designed using data on the mechanisms of action of the mAb and the target cell resistance to cytotoxicity. One such approach is the HFLD regimen, which uses the smallest dose that achieves maximum immune cytotoxicity at dosing intervals sufficient for recovery of immune cytotoxicity. In addition, while multidrug therapy is useful in overcoming target cell resistance, the addition of BCR pathway inhibitors to anti‐CD20 mAb therapy could be deleterious.
