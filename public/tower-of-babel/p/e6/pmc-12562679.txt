Crosstalk between microRNAs and autophagy in adipocyte differentiation: emerging therapeutic targets for obesity
Authors: Shan Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObesity remains a global health challenge, underscoring the need for more effective therapeutic strategies. Recent evidence highlights the central role of adipose tissue autophagy in metabolic homoeostasis, with its dysregulation contributing to obesity-related pathophysiology. MicroRNAs (miRNAs) have emerged as critical regulators of autophagy in adipocytes, influencing differentiation, lipid metabolism, and systemic energy balance. This review synthesizes current knowledge on how specific miRNAs modulate autophagic activity in adipose tissue and discusses their functional impact on adipocyte biology. Based on the accumulated evidence, we conclude that targeting miRNA-mediated autophagy pathways holds significant therapeutic potential for obesity and related metabolic disorders. Future research should focus on translating these mechanistic insights into targeted interventions, overcoming delivery challenges, and advancing miRNA-based therapeutics towards clinical application.

Introduction

Obesity is a chronic condition characterized by excessive energy accumulation and adipose tissue expansion. The rising global prevalence of obesity has contributed to a public health crisis, primarily driven by poor dietary habits and reduced physical activity. This trend is associated with an increased incidence of obesity-related comorbidities, such as type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, and certain cancers [1–3]. Although several clinical approaches are available to manage obesity, including lifestyle modifications, pharmacological agents, and surgical procedures, their effectiveness remains limited and often accompanied by significant side effects. In recent years, research on adipose tissue biology – focusing on its development, distribution, and regulatory mechanisms – has become a major scientific endeavour.

Autophagy, traditionally recognized as a catabolic pathway activated under nutrient-deficient conditions, is primarily regulated by the inhibition of mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) and activation of AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK). Conversely, nutrient abundance – mediated by signals such as insulin – suppresses autophagy [4]. Interestingly, initial studies reporting autophagy activation in adipocytes suggested that insulin resistance-induced reduction in mTOR activity could underlie this process [5]. Transcriptional regulation may further contribute to autophagy induction under conditions of nutrient overload, endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, inflammation, and mitochondrial dysfunction [6–8].

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) represent a layer of post-transcriptional regulation that remains relatively underexplored. These small non-coding RNAs do not encode proteins but anneal to specific target mRNAs, leading to transcript degradation or translational repression [9]. Accumulating evidence indicates that miRNA-mediated regulation constitutes an integral part of the autophagy network and significantly influences autophagy-related physiological and pathological conditions. In obesity and metabolic disorders, miRNAs that modulate autophagy and regulate adipogenic differentiation have garnered increasing attention [10–12]. This review outlines the molecular mechanisms of autophagy and discusses the roles of miRNAs in adipose tissue biology and metabolic homoeostasis.

Adipose tissue: white, brown, and brite

Mammals possess two primary types of adipose tissue: white adipose tissue (WAT), which serves as the main energy reservoir, and brown adipose tissue (BAT), which mediates non-shivering thermogenesis. Notably, brown-like adipocytes can emerge within WAT depots in response to thermogenic stimuli, a process termed “browning. These inducible adipocytes, often referred to as ‘beige’ or ‘brite’ (brown-in-white), originate from distinct precursor cells that differ from those of classical BAT and may constitute a third adipocyte class [13]. Beige adipocytes share the thermogenic capacity of brown adipocytes but can be distinguished by specific surface markers such as Tmem26 and CD137 [14,15].

White adipose tissue is distributed throughout the body in two major depots: subcutaneous and visceral (intra-abdominal). Visceral WAT includes mesenteric, omental, epicardial, and gonadal fat. In healthy adults, WAT comprises approximately 20% of body weight and functions primarily as a triglyceride storage site. Additionally, WAT exhibits endocrine functions, secreting hormones and cytokines such as leptin, TNF-α, adiponectin, and resistin, which are implicated in obesity and insulin sensitivity [16]. White adipocytes are characterized by a large, unilocular lipid droplet that occupies most of the cell volume, a flattened peripheral nucleus, and few mitochondria.

Brown adipose tissue was initially thought to be functional only in human infants and is primarily located in the interscapular, paravertebral, perirenal, and axillary regions. However, recent studies have identified active BAT deposits in adults – containing both classical brown and beige adipocytes – in cervical, supraclavicular, mediastinal, paravertebral, and suprarenal areas upon mild cold exposure [17]. Brown adipocytes are generally smaller and exhibit multilocular lipid droplets, a central nucleus, abundant mitochondria, and scant endoplasmic reticulum. Beige adipocytes display phenotypic plasticity: under basal conditions, they resemble white adipocytes with a unilocular morphology; upon cold exposure or β-adrenergic stimulation, they adopt a multilocular, mitochondria-rich phenotype resembling brown adipocytes [18,19].

The origin and recruitment mechanisms of beige adipocytes remain incompletely elucidated. They may arise from a distinct progenitor population within subcutaneous adipose tissue [20] or from the transdifferentiation of existing white adipocytes [21]. Park et al. stimulated living mice with the selective b3AR agonist CL-316243, the results of their study showed that at least a subpopulation of unilocular adipocytes underwent conversion to multilocular mitochondria-rich adipocytes [22]. Moreover, recent research has shown novel mechanism of the beige adipocytes formation. Wang et al. monitored that most beige adipocytes stem from de novo-differentiated adipocytes during cold-induced ‘browning’ of subcutaneous adipose tissue [23]. Vargas et al. further showed that EP300 interacting inhibitor of differentiation 1 (EID1) transfection into adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells reduced triglyceride accumulation and enhanced expression of thermogenic markers (e.g. PGC1α, TFAM, UCP1), along with beige-specific markers CITED1 and CD137 [24].

Regulatory roles of autophagy in adipose tissue

Significant progress has been made in understanding the molecular and cellular mechanisms of autophagy. Macroautophagy (hereafter autophagy) is a highly conserved process involving the encapsulation of cytoplasmic components within double-membraned vesicles (autophagosomes) for lysosomal degradation [25,26]. Autophagy plays crucial roles in energy and metabolic homoeostasis by facilitating the degradation of glycogen, proteins, and lipids [27]. In adipose tissue, autophagy is influenced by multiple factors, including insulin resistance, inflammation, mitochondrial function, and lipolysis.

Insulin resistance

Autophagy activation has been identified as a response to endoplasmic ER stress-induced insulin resistance (IR), leading to degradation of the insulin receptor. ER stress can trigger autophagy independently of calcium signalling via eukaryotic initiation factor 2α (eIF2α) phosphorylation [28]. Inhibition of autophagy using 3-methyladenine, bafilomycin A1, or siRNA against Atg5 and Beclin1 attenuated insulin receptor degradation and improved insulin sensitivity. Conversely, AMPK activation – induced by an increased AMP/ATP ratio following F0F1-ATPase inhibition – enhanced autophagy and insulin sensitivity [29]. Chronic insulin exposure in hepatoma cells impaired mitophagy, contributing to aberrant glucose utilization [30]. Adipose-specific Atg7 knockout mice exhibited WAT browning, increased fatty acid β-oxidation, and improved insulin sensitivity and glucose homoeostasis [31]. However, whether these protective effects directly result from autophagy impairment remains unclear, and the causal relationship between autophagy and insulin resistance requires further investigation.

Adipose tissue inflammation

Obesity-associated inflammation is a key contributor to insulin resistance [32], and autophagy may regulate inflammatory responses. On one hand, autophagy can exacerbate inflammation: circulating endotoxins like lipopolysaccharide activate autophagic signalling in adipose tissue inflammatory cells via toll-like receptors (TLRs) [33]. Enhanced autophagy may also hypersensitize cells to inflammatory stimuli, promoting IL-1β secretion [34]. Thus, autophagy may fine-tune the inflammatory tone in obese adipose tissue. On the other hand, global haplodeficiency of Atg7 in obese mice exacerbated insulin resistance, increased lipid-associated inflammation, and promoted pro-IL-1β processing to IL-1β [35]. In 3T3-L1 adipocytes, oxidative stress, sympathetic activity, and pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g. TNF-α, IL-6) suppressed adiponectin expression – an effect reversed by the autophagy inhibitor 3-methyladenine [36]. Given adiponectin’s anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties [37], autophagy may modulate inflammation both directly (via cytokine production) and indirectly (via adiponectin regulation).

Mitochondrial function

Mitochondria are central to cellular processes such as apoptosis, detoxification, and calcium buffering and are major sources and scavengers of reactive oxygen species (ROS). Alterations in mitochondrial mass and function have been implicated in insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, and obesity. Autophagy maintains cellular homoeostasis by degrading damaged organelles, including mitochondria – a selective process known as mitophagy [38]. In adipose tissue, autophagy promotes adipocyte differentiation, possibly through mitochondrial clearance. Recent evidence indicates that autophagy-mediated mitochondrial turnover is essential for beige adipocyte maintenance and energy expenditure [39]. Beige adipogenesis is induced by various stimuli, including cold exposure, exercise, and PPARγ agonists, likely through active mitochondrial biogenesis. Transcriptional regulators of mitochondrial biogenesis (e.g. Pgc1a, Tfam) remain elevated in BAT after β3-AR agonist withdrawal, suggesting that the beige-to-white transition involves mitochondrial decline [40]. ROS-modulated autophagy links numerous pathologies, including cancer, neurodegeneration, diabetes, and cardiovascular diseases. Mitochondrial ROS and lipid oxidation play key roles in autophagy induction: in mammalian cells, starvation-induced ROS generation triggers AMPK-mediated autophagy [41]. ROS can also upregulate Beclin-1 and p53, while H₂O₂ induces Beclin-1-dependent autophagic cell death [42,43].

Adipose tissue lipolysis

Adipose-specific Atg7-knockout mice displayed altered fatty acid metabolism, with increased β-oxidation and reduced hormone-induced lipolysis [44]. Although activated autophagy might be expected to enhance lipolysis, autophagy deficiency in adipocytes may reflect developmental alterations rather than direct regulatory roles. Cold exposure modulates autophagy in proopiomelanocortin (POMC) neurons and activates lipophagy in BAT. Hypothalamic rapamycin injection targeted autophagy activation in POMC neurons, sufficient to stimulate lipid utilization in mice at room temperature [45,46]. Pharmacological inhibitors of autophagy should be used cautiously: in differentiated 3T3-L1 adipocytes, 3-methyladenine increased lipolysis via PKA-dependent pathways, independent of autophagic inhibition [47]. The protein Bif-1 regulates autophagic lipolysis: autophagy induces Bif-1-dependent perilipin1 degradation, and Bif-1 deficiency reduces triglyceride hydrolysis, indicating that perilipin1 degradation is necessary for lipid droplet breakdown [48]. Autophagy may also regulate lipolysis in non-adipocytes within adipose tissue. Macrophages increase autophagy to metabolize lipids without altering activation status, suggesting that macrophage autophagy buffers obesity-associated lipid overload [49]. However, some studies report no effect of autophagy inhibition on macrophage lipid stores, possibly due to macrophage heterogeneity and environmental context [50].

MiRnas regulate autophagy in adipose tissue

MicroRNAs are short (19–23 nt) non-coding RNAs that repress gene expression by inhibiting translation and promoting mRNA decay. miRNAs are expressed across tissues and regulate over 60% of mammalian genes [51], making them crucial post-transcriptional regulators. They modulate various pathways, including insulin signalling, inflammation, adipokine expression, adipogenesis, and lipid metabolism [52–56]. Tissues central to metabolic syndrome – pancreatic β-cells, liver, muscle, and adipose tissue – are all influenced by miRNAs. Growing evidence indicates that miRNAs modulate autophagy to adjust adipose tissue function.

Autophagy regulation and its molecular complexes

Autophagy involves several stages: induction, phagophore nucleation, vesicle elongation and autophagosome formation, autophagosome retrieval, fusion with lysosomes/endosomes, and cargo degradation [57]. Core molecular machinery includes: (a) UNC-51 kinase 1 (ULK) complexes; (b) ubiquitin-like proteins Atg12 and Atg8/LC3 and their conjugation systems; (c) class III PI3K complex; (d) transmembrane proteins mAtg9 and vacuole membrane protein 1 (VMP1) [58]. The cargo receptor p62 delivers ubiquitinated proteins to autophagosomes for selective degradation [59].

MiRNAs regulate the major autophagy cascades

Starvation, ER stress, hypoxia, genotoxic stress, and ROS activate signalling pathways that induce autophagy. The ULK complex initiates autophagy cascades. MiR-20a and miR-106b (miR-17 family) regulate leucine deprivation-induced autophagy by suppressing ULK1 in C2C12 myoblasts [60]. The AMPK-mTORC1 axis integrates diverse stimuli to regulate ULK activity. Several miRNAs target this pathway: miR-26b suppresses transforming growth factor-β-activated kinase 1 (TAK1) and transforming growth factor-β-activated kinase 1 binding protein 3 (TAB3), sensitizing hepatocellular carcinoma cells to apoptosis [61]; miR-148b targets AMPKα1, inhibiting pancreatic cancer progression; miR-99a suppresses mTOR and lung cancer tumorigenicity [62]; miR-199a-3p regulates mTOR and c-Met, influencing chemotherapy sensitivity [63].

Regarding class III PI3K, miR-338-5p silences PIK3C3/Vps34, suppressing autophagy [64]. Beclin-1, a key component, is directly targeted by miR-30a, miR-376b, and miR-216a, reducing autophagic activity [65–67]. MiR-181a, miR-519a, miR-374a, and miR-630—downstream of p-ΔNp63α—modulate ATG5, BECN1, ATG10, ATG12, ATG16L1, and ultraviolet radiation resistance-associated gene (UVRAG) [68–71]. For class I PI3K, miR-503 targets the regulatory subunit p85α (PIK3R1), suppressing tumour proliferation and metastasis [72]. MiR-193a-5p inactivates AKT/mTOR by targeting p55γ (PIK3R3) and mTOR, inhibiting non-small cell lung cancer progression [73]. PTEN, a negative regulator of PI3K signalling, is targeted by miR-128, miR-196a, miR-221, miR-93, and miR-32 [74–78]. MiR-375 suppresses 3-phosphoinositide-dependent protein kinase 1 (PDK1), another PI3K pathway effector [79], while miR-302–367 inhibits AKT1, cyclin D1, and AKT1 translation [80]. Rheb, a negative autophagy regulator upstream of mTORC1, is suppressed by miR-155, accelerating autophagy [81]. Members of the Ras-Raf-MEK-ERK pathway are also miRNA targets: Raf-1 is targeted by miR-195 [82]; MEK1 by miR-1826 and miR-34a [83,84]; extracellular regulated protein kinases 1 (ERK1) by miR-483-5p [85].

MiRNAs also regulate ubiquitin-like conjugation systems. MiR-204 targets LC3B, impeding autophagy [86]. ATG4D is targeted by miR-101, inhibiting basal and induced autophagy [87]. MiR-376b controls starvation- and mTOR inhibition-related autophagy by targeting ATG4C [67], and miR-376a shares targets including ATG4C and BECN1 [88]. ATG12 is a target of miR-23b-3p; reduced miR-23b-3p increases ATG12 expression and autophagic activity [89].

VMP1, critical for phagophore formation, is a direct target of hypoxia-induced miR-210 [90]. Hypoxia increases miR-210, which suppresses BNIP3 expression and promotes cell survival [91]. MiR-495 post-transcriptionally suppresses REDD1, a downstream component of BNIP3 signalling under hypoxia [92,93] (Figure 1). MicroRNAs are key regulators of brown adipogenesis

Brown adipose tissue has therapeutic potential, and miRNAs play vital roles in BAT formation—e.g. miR-193b/-365 [94], miR-196a [95], miR-155 [96], and miR-133a/b [97]. Deep sequencing reveals varying miRNA expression levels between brown and white adipocytes, with the miR-27 family among the most abundant [98]. The miR-193b-365 cluster promotes brown adipogenesis by targeting Runx1t1, a brown fat inhibitor, and is positively regulated by PRDM16/PPARα [99]. Muscle-specific miR-133a targets PRDM16; its inhibition promotes brown adipocyte differentiation and mitochondrial activity [100]. MiR-455, induced by BMP7, targets Necdin, Runx1t1, and HIF1an, initiating adipogenesis and activating AMPK to drive brown adipogenesis [101]. Global miRNA processing influences adipocyte determination: Dicer deficiency in WAT with ageing reduces miRNA levels, causing BAT whitening and partial dystrophy [102]. MiR-365 May contribute to this whitening phenotype [103]. MiR-30 family members, miRNA-30b and miRNA-30c, have been shown to promote thermogenesis and development of beige fat [104]. Upon cold exposure or β-adrenergic receptor activation, their expression increased during brown adipocyte differentiation. miRNA-30b/c promotes thermogenesis by upregulating the expression of thermogenic genes (UCP1 and Cidea) and inducing mitochondrial respiration in brown adipocytes. In vivo, miRNA-30b/c antagomir treatment results in a downregulation of UCP1 expression and reduction in mitochondrial respiration in BAT. The effect of miRNA-30b/c on UCP1 and Cidea expression is due to its direct targeting of receptor- interacting protein 140 (RIP140), a corepressor of thermogenic genes [105]. Independent datasets further support a role for miR‑30 in human and murine adipose remodelling. In primary human adipocytes and mouse fat, miR‑30a levels correlate with browning signatures and insulin sensitivity, and miR‑30a overexpression promotes a pro‑thermogenic transcriptional state [106]. Dietary or pharmacologic cues can converge on this axis: omega‑3 fatty acid (EPA)/FFAR4 signalling elevates miR‑30b/miR‑378 in brown fat and augments UCP1‑dependent thermogenesis in vivo. Beyond adipocytes, the miR‑30 family constrains canonical autophagy by targeting key initiation/nucleation components such as BECN1. In cardiomyocytes, Ang II – induced hypertrophy is accompanied by down‑regulation of miR‑30, excessive autophagy, and pathological remodelling, while miR‑30a directly represses BECN1 to restrain autophagic flux [107]. Exosomal transfer of miR‑30a modulates post‑ischaemic autophagy between cardiomyocytes [108]. These mechanisms are pertinent to adipose biology because autophagy tonically shapes adipocyte fate and organelle quality control during thermogenic activation. Taken together, we propose that miR‑30 family members may couple thermogenic programming with a tempered autophagy set‑point in beige/brown adipocytes – an attractive therapeutic angle to boost energy expenditure while avoiding maladaptive catabolism. Clinically, this mechanistic axis converges with recent translational work linking miRNAs to metabolic bone – adipose crosstalk. A 2025 systematic review of miRNA therapeutics in obesity and osteoporosis [109] underscores how specific miRNAs can be harnessed to rebalance adipogenesis versus osteogenesis, reinforcing the therapeutic promise of pro‑thermogenic miRNAs such as miR‑30b/c within comprehensive obesity strategies.

Adipocyte-specific Atg7-knockout mice exhibited adipocytes with features more characteristic of brown fat than of white adipose tissue, being heterogeneous, smaller, containing multiple small lipid droplets and a significant amount of cytoplasm, in contrast to the typical homogenous unilocular cells of wild-type mice. Cells also exhibited a markedly increased number of mitochondria. Mitochondria are multifunctional cellular organelles, which are primarily responsible for energy production via oxidative phosphorylation in the inner mitochondrial membrane. The mitochondrial network is a dynamic and adaptable system that must remain healthy in order to meet the various needs of host cells in response to the bioenergetic and environmental changes [38,110]. MiR-634 overexpression induces mitochondrial injury, featuring fragmented morphology, reduced membrane potential, increased ROS, and cytochrome c release, triggering apoptosis [111]. Damaged mitochondria are degraded via the ubiquitin-proteasome system (outer membrane proteins) or mitophagy (whole organelles) [112]. MiR-137 inhibits hypoxia-induced mitophagy by targeting FUN14 domain-containing protein 1 (FUNDC1) and NIX, impairing LC3 binding and mitochondrial targeting to autophagosomes [113]. Relative contribution of miRNA control over autophagy nodes with overlapping targets

Autophagy is indispensable for adipocyte differentiation: genetic disruption of core ATG genes (e.g. Atg7, Atg5) impairs adipogenesis in vitro and in vivo, yielding lean, insulin-sensitive phenotypes in mice. Against this backdrop, multiple miRNAs converge on a limited set of autophagy ‘bottlenecks’, creating target overlap that is functionally non-redundant during adipocyte maturation. Nucleation hub – BECN1/VPS34 complex (strongest convergence). Several miRNAs independently repress BECN1 and associated nucleation machinery. The miR-30 family directly targets BECN1 and blunts rapamycin-induced autophagy [116]; miR-376b co-targets BECN1 and ATG4C to limit starvation- and mTOR-inhibition – induced autophagy [117]; miR-216a also binds the BECN1 3’UTR and suppresses BECN1-mediated autophagy [118]. Because autophagosome nucleation is rate-limiting, convergent repression at BECN1 likely exerts a greater functional effect on autophagy flux during adipogenesis than miRNAs acting downstream. Beyond nucleation, miR-30d has been shown to directly repress ATG5 (in addition to BECN1 and PI3K components) and inhibit autophagy. miR-376b targets ATG4C (cysteine protease that processes LC3) [117], while other contexts implicate additional miRNAs (e.g. miR-101/ATG4D/RAB5A/STMN1) as elongation modulators that curtail autophagosome formation [119]. Because elongation can be partly buffered by parallel ATG8/LC3 processing pathways, the net effect is moderate compared with BECN1-directed control, but still sufficient to attenuate adipogenic autophagy when multiple elongation targets are co-repressed.

(iii) LC3 system and cargo selection – MAP1LC3B (targeted, context-dependent impact).

MiR-204 directly targets LC3B (MAP1LC3B), reducing LC3B expression and autophagy in several models [120–122]. Given LC3’s central role in membrane conjugation and cargo sequestration, miR-204 exerts a node-specific brake on autophagy flux. However, because LC3 paralogs and upstream nucleation/elongation modules can partially compensate, the relative contribution of LC3-directed miRNAs is context-dependent and generally lower than BECN1-centric repression, unless LC3B is the dominant ATG8 paralog in the adipocyte state.

(iv) Upstream nutrient-sensing modulators (indirect, integrative control).

miR-34a suppression of SIRT1 (and consequent mTOR activation) indirectly restrains autophagy [123]. While this does not create overlapping 3′UTR targeting within core ATG transcripts, it integrates with the above nodes by globally lowering autophagy tone during adipocyte commitment and maturation.

Perspectives

Numerous miRNAs are implicated in metabolic syndrome and diabetes, yet no unified miRNA signature characterizes metabolic disease subphenotypes. The involvement of miRNAs in various autophagy stages underscores autophagy’s importance in cellular homoeostasis and disease. Although debated whether miRNA-regulated autophagy is protective or detrimental in obesity, it is clear that autophagy does not operate in isolation but is integrated with other signalling networks cross-regulated by miRNAs. Factors such as insulin resistance, inflammation, mitochondrial mass, and lipolysis influence adipose tissue autophagy, and miRNAs target multiple autophagy-related proteins. Thus, miRNAs represent promising tools for manipulating adipocyte function.

In addition to defining miRNA – autophagy interactions, it is increasingly important to leverage new technologies for precise, controllable modulation of miRNAs in vivo. Recent advances span chemical modifications, targeted delivery vehicles, gene therapy vectors, and programmable nucleic acid systems. Below we briefly summarize key approaches and their preclinical or early clinical progress.

(i) Chemically modified miRNA mimics/inhibitors (antimiRs/antagomiRs/antisense oligonucleotides)

Synthetic miRNA mimics (for miRNA upregulation) or antisense inhibitors (for miRNA suppression) remain foundational tools. To enhance stability and reduce off-target effects, modifications such as 2’-O-methyl, locked nucleic acids (LNAs), phosphorothioate backbones, or peptide nucleic acids (PNAs) are widely used. Emerging designs include LNA-antimiRs with high affinity and specificity, and γPNA-based inhibitors encapsulated in biodegradable nanoparticles for enhanced delivery [125].

(ii) Nanocarrier-based delivery (lipid-, polymeric, inorganic, and exosome systems). A major bottleneck is delivering miRNA agents to the correct tissue/cell type with minimal toxicity. Several innovative delivery platforms are under development:

Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs), especially ionizable cationic lipids, are widely studied to package miRNA mimics or inhibitors, offering favourable biocompatibility andosomal escape.

Polymeric nanoparticles, e.g. PLGA (poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid)) and cationic polymeric complexes, enable controlled release and surface targeting modifications (ligands, antibodies) to improve specificity.

Inorganic nanoparticles (such as gold, silica) and mesoporous systems provide high loading capacity and tunable surface chemistries, though their biocompatibility and long-term safety remain under scrutiny [126].

Extracellular vesicle/exosome-based delivery exploits the natural packaging and cell-targeting capacity of vesicles, sometimes engineered with specific membrane proteins or targeting ligands to improve tissue tropism [125].

(iii) Viral/gene therapy – based miRNA modulation

For more durable intervention, miRNA regulation can be achieved via viral vectors (e.g. adeno-associated virus, lentivirus) encoding miRNA precursors/‘miRNA sponge’ decoys or CRISPR-based regulatory cassettes that modulate endogenous miRNA genes. These approaches enable sustained expression or suppression but carry risks of insertional mutagenesis and immunogenicity [127].

(iv) Programmable nucleic acid systems and responsive platforms

Newer approaches integrate stimuli-responsive release, aptamer-guided targeting, or logic-gated miRNA switches, allowing miRNA activity to be controlled spatiotemporally (e.g. pH, redox, light-triggered release). Computational optimization and machine learning models are being harnessed to predict miRNA – mRNA networks and design optimized multi-miRNA combinations with minimal off-target effects [128].

(v) Translational/clinical prospects and challenges

Although miRNA-based therapeutics have shown promise in numerous preclinical models, clinical translation remains nascent. As of now, fewer than 20 miRNA therapeutics have entered clinical trials, and none have reached Phase III or obtained regulatory approval in major jurisdictions. One illustrative example is MRX34 (a liposomal miR-34a mimic), which entered Phase I trials for solid tumours but was terminated due to immune-mediated adverse events [129]. Major challenges include off-target effects, immunogenicity, toxicity, dosing control, efficient and specific delivery to adipose tissue, and long-term safety [130]. In the context of adipose tissue and metabolic disease, adaptation of these technologies (e.g. adipocyte-targeted LNP, exosome tropism for adipose, inducible miRNA vectors) is still in early-stage exploration. Incorporating these innovations into the miRNA – autophagy – adipocyte axis may accelerate the development of precision miRNA therapeutics for obesity.
