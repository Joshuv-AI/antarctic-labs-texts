Letter by Rachana Mehta and Ranjana Sah Regarding Article, Cholesterol and Cardiovascular Risk in Type 2 Diabetes: The Role of Kidney Function
Authors: Rachana Mehta, Ranjana Sah
Published: 2025

We read with great interest the nationwide cohort study by Kim et al.,1 which examined the interaction between low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and kidney function in determining cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk among patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). These findings have important implications for individualizing lipid targets; however, several methodological issues warrant clarification to better contextualize the results for clinical application.

First, the study’s reliance on baseline LDL-C values without incorporating longitudinal changes or treatment dynamics is a notable limitation. More than 50% of participants with estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) <30 mL/min/1.73 m2 were taking statins, yet no time-varying covariate models were applied.2 This omission raises concern for treatment bias, especially since individuals with eGFR <30 exhibited a 3.23-fold increased risk of CVD (95% confidence interval [CI], 2.87–3.64) even at LDL-C <55 mg/dL. This persistent risk suggests that non-lipid factors or reverse causality, including cachexia or malnutrition, may play a substantial role.3

Second, the lack of inflammatory and nutritional biomarkers (e.g., serum albumin, urine albumin-creatinine ratio) limits mechanistic interpretation. In chronic kidney disease (CKD), lipoproteins are altered by uremic toxins and oxidative stress, which modify their atherogenicity.4 Notably, in patients with eGFR <30, higher non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (non-HDL-C) remained strongly associated with increased CVD risk, even above 190 mg/dL (hazard ratio [HR], 3.98; 95% CI, 3.43–4.63). This finding suggests that lipoprotein quality, not merely quantity, may be a more relevant determinant of risk in this population.5

Third, the use of Cox proportional hazards models assumes constant risk over time, yet this assumption is unlikely to hold across progressive T2DM and CKD. Without formal testing of the proportional hazards assumption or application of time-varying approaches, the reported HRs may obscure dynamic shifts in lipid effects across disease stages.6 Techniques such as landmark analysis or extended Cox modeling could better capture these evolving risk patterns.

Fourth, the analysis does not account for competing risks, which are especially relevant in advanced CKD, where non-CVD mortality (e.g., infection or malignancy) is common.7 Standard Cox models tend to overestimate risk in this context. In older diabetic populations with proteinuria or low eGFR, sub-distribution hazard models would provide more accurate risk estimation.8 The finding that proteinuria was associated with nearly a twofold increase in CVD risk even at LDL-C <70 mg/dL (HR, 1.70; 95% CI, 1.63–1.78) underscores the importance of non-atherosclerotic factors.

Fifth, although the authors referenced recent Korean guidelines recommending LDL-C targets <55 mg/dL for high-risk groups, the applicability of this threshold to advanced CKD remains uncertain. The small number of patients achieving such targets, together with residual confounding and lack of randomization, raises the possibility of treatment indication bias.9 Using methods such as inverse probability weighting or marginal structural models could mitigate this issue.

Regarding the interpretation of this study, we concur with the authors that kidney function modifies lipid-associated CVD risk, but we propose that this modulation reflects not only LDL-C thresholds but also broader pathophysiologic alterations in CKD. The data suggest that non-HDL-C elevation correlates with CVD risk more consistently than LDL-C across declining eGFR and proteinuric states, highlighting its potential as a superior therapeutic target in diabetic nephropathy.

We respectfully recommend that future studies incorporate time-varying exposures, competing risk models, and inflammatory and nutritional markers. These refinements would enhance precision in defining lipid targets for patients with T2DM and CKD and strengthen the evidence base for clinical guidelines.
