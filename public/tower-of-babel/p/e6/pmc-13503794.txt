Structural complexity and groundwater circulation in mountain karst systems: an integrated approach combining hydrodynamic data and environmental tracers
Authors: Fatima Raibi, Mouad Maaziz, Mohammed Hssaisoune, Ahmed El-Azhari, Meryem Miftah, Lhoussaine Bouchaou, Tarik Tagma, Yassine Ait Brahim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractKarst springs are essential freshwater resources in semi arid mountain regions, yet their recharge areas, connectivity, and vulnerability remain difficult to constrain in structurally complex settings. This uncertainty limits the ability to separate climate driven variability from structure-controlled flow pathways, which weakens both vulnerability assessment and management decisions. This study investigates the hydrogeological functioning of the transition zone between the High and Middle Atlas Mountains located in the upstream Oum Er Rbia Basin. Utilizing an integrated approach, we combined hydrodynamic time-series analysis with hydrochemical characterization and environmental isotopes (δ18O, δ2H, 3H) to assess aquifer recharge, connectivity, and vulnerability. Hydrodynamic analysis of spring discharge records from 2000 to 2012 reveals a stark contrast between inertial, storage-dominated systems and flashy, conduit-controlled overflows, a divergence governed largely by local structural compartmentalization. Geochemical data uniformly identify a Ca–Mg–HCO3 facies derived from carbonate dissolution, with negligible saline influence from Triassic evaporites. Stable isotopes confirm exclusively meteoric recharge following a distinct altitude gradient, identifying a regional mixing zone that integrates convergent flows from both mountain domains. Crucially, a significant positive correlation between nitrate concentrations and tritium activity demonstrates that aquifer vulnerability is intrinsically linked to the rapid transmission of modern recharge. These findings underscore the necessity for differentiated management strategies that incorporate the specific hydrodynamic memory and transit times of these critical karst systems to ensure regional water security.Subject terms: Environmental sciences, Hydrology, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Mountain carbonate aquifers and their springs are strategic freshwater sources in Mediterranean and semi-arid regions, where surface water is increasingly limited by climate variability and rising aridity1–4. In Morocco, this pressure is especially strong where springs sustain domestic supply, irrigation, and local economies5. This makes a clear understanding of spring functioning essential for water security and sustainable management.

The upstream Oum Er Rbia (OER) Basin lies at the junction between the High Atlas and the Middle Atlas, within a transition zone commonly referred to as the “Dir”6,7. This setting combines strong relief contrasts, major tectonic structures, and extensive karst development within Lower–Middle Jurassic carbonate rocks8, creating a hydrogeological system where recharge partitioning, storage, and flow organization can change sharply over short distances.

Independent regional work highlights how exceptionally dense and diverse the spring resource is in the Dir of Beni Mellal, with more than 130–140 springs reported, spanning wide ranges of altitude and discharge9, and including seasonal “overflow” springs that appear only for a few months after winter rain and snow10,11. These studies also document strong sensitivity of these karst springs to drought periods, including reduced discharge and temporary drying of some outlets, which underlines the need for robust diagnostics of recharge effectiveness and storage buffering.

Despite this importance, two scientific gaps remain. First, spring behavior in the Middle Atlas–High Atlas system is often assessed in a fragmented way: high-frequency studies can describe event responses, but they rarely translate into a transferable, regional understanding of how different springs integrate rainfall and snowmelt over time12,13. Second, the role of structural compartmentalization (faults, folds, and detached Liassic units) in shaping both circulation pathways and water-quality contrasts are still not well constrained14–16, limiting our ability to separate “climate forcing” from “connectivity control” in explaining spring variability.

Addressing these gaps requires an integrated framework that combines hydrodynamic analysis of spring discharge with conservative tracers to identify recharge sources and groundwater flow paths. In karst hydrology, correlation and spectral analyses are commonly used to derive response times and aquifer memory from rainfall–discharge relationships17, but they generally assume temporal stationarity. Time–frequency analysis overcomes this limitation by revealing how spring responses vary under contrasting wet and dry conditions, thereby capturing non-stationary system behavior18. Hydrochemical and isotopic tracers provide complementary constraints. Electrical conductivity and major-ion chemistry reflect water–rock interaction and help identify mixing between recharge domains with contrasting mineralization19,20. Stable isotopes (δ¹⁸O, δ²H) trace recharge conditions because they vary systematically with elevation, season, and moisture source and are largely preserved during transport in carbonate aquifers21. Tritium constrains groundwater residence time and distinguishes modern from pre-modern recharge21–23, while nitrate indicates surface connectivity and anthropogenic vulnerability24–26. Together, these indicators resolve both rapid conduit flow and delayed matrix drainage more effectively than any single method alone.

In this study, we therefore propose an integrated framework for the upstream OER Basin that combines three complementary lines of evidence. (1) Rainfall–discharge diagnostics derived from hydrological time series, using correlation-based lag indicators and event filtering metrics, supported where appropriate by time–frequency analysis; baseflow separation is performed using a recursive digital filtering approach that is widely applied for automated hydrograph partitioning. (2) Hydrochemical characterization based on major ions and electrical conductivity to describe dominant water–rock interactions and identify mixing tendencies across the High Atlas, the Middle Atlas, and the Dir transition zone. (3) Environmental tracers, including stable water isotopes (δ²H, δ¹⁸O) interpreted against appropriate meteoric reference lines, and tritium (³H) as an age-sensitive tracer of modern recharge and transit time information; combining nitrate with the isotope and age evidence further supports a process-based interpretation of vulnerability in karst systems.

Study area

The study area is located at a critical geographical transition zone where the High Atlas (HA) and the Middle Atlas (MA) ranges meet, primarily through the Oued El Abid syncline. This HA-MA junction, known as the “Dir,” serves as a transitional piedmont or foothill between the high-altitude mountains and the vast Tadla Plain. Topographically, the area is structured into three distinct units: high mountain peaks exceeding 2400 m, such as Jbel Ighnayene (2401 m) and Jbel Tacemit (2248 m), The Tadla Plain situated at approximately 400 m, and the Dir piedmont, which acts as the intermediate transition zone with altitudes typically ranging between 600 m and 1,000 m (Fig. 1). The region is characterized by significant climatic diversity, ranging from humid conditions at the Atlas summits to semi-humid and sub-arid environments at the foothills. Long-term meteorological data indicate a mean annual temperature of approximately 20 °C, with monthly averages fluctuating between 10.8 °C in January and 29.5 °C in August. Precipitation is marked by high spatial and temporal irregularity, with a decade-long average of roughly 490 mm. The hydrologic year is divided into a wet period spanning from October to April and a pronounced dry season from May to September. This climatic regime is increasingly influenced by drought cycles, which have resulted in reduced snowfall and shorter melting periods in the high mountains27–30.

The lithology of the region is dominated by Liassic (Lower Jurassic) and Dogger (Middle Jurassic) formations, primarily consisting of massive and bedded limestones and dolomites31,32. These carbonate rocks form the primary Atlasic relief and are heavily karstified, especially along the North Atlasic Fault, which defines the contact between the mountain range and the plain. Structural arrangements reveal an anticlinal overlapping toward the Tadla Plain, with a high density of lineaments oriented primarily NE-SW. Satellite imagery and structural profiles, such as the NW-SE section A-A’ (Fig. 1), highlight complex assemblies of faults and folds where Paleozoic and Meso-Cenozoic rocks coexist, including Triassic formations with basaltic intrusions and various Cretaceous deposits31–33. These geological and structural features directly govern the regional hydrogeology, where the Liassic karstic system constitutes the most vital groundwater resource in Morocco34,35. This system feeds over 130 natural springs emerging along the North Atlasic Fault. These emergences are categorized into three types based on their structural origin: (1) springs emerging directly from the Liassic massif, such as Asserdoune spring, which maintains a high historical average flow of approximately 1100 L/s, (2) springs issuing from detached Liassic units in the Dir, such as Foum El Ancer, and (3) springs emerging from Tertiary formations and travertines in the piedmont36,37. The aquifer is primarily recharged by rainfall and snowmelt from the High Atlas summits. While the system exhibits a relatively regular hydrological regime with an interannual variability index of 0.54, total discharge has declined in recent years due to decreasing precipitation in the primary recharge zones. While groundwater extraction in the region is confined to the Tadla Plain, where irrigation pumping targets the alluvial and deep Liassic aquifers located downstream of the spring outlets.

Asserdoune exhibits intermediate behavior, with moderate baseflow support (BFI = 0.74), higher amplification (Q95/Q50 = 1.80), and a larger quickflow fraction (25%). It shows the strongest RQ relationship among the three springs (r: 0.38 to 0.48) and the highest short-term responsiveness, with an average 0–1 month responding rate of 54.7%. The most responsive station pairings are Ait Ouchene (68%) and Taghzit (64%), indicating that these rainfall signals most consistently translate into discharge changes within one month. Tamegnounte is the most event-dominated system in terms of discharge variability, with low baseflow dominance (BFI: 0.35 to 0.37), extremely high amplification (Q95/Q50: 9.22 to 9.93), and very high quick flow fraction (63 to 65%). Despite the flashy discharge signature, its RQ correlation remains only moderate (r: 0.26–0.38), and the mean 0–1 month responding rate is 39.0%, with the strongest pairing observed for Ait Ouchene (50%).

The topographical context supports a first-order separation between an upland, and piedmont springs. Aghbalou, located farther upslope within the mountain domain, likely integrates recharge over a broader high-elevation contributing area. This setting favors diffuse infiltration, thicker vadose storage, and longer groundwater residence, which naturally explains its high BFI, low peakiness, and the dependence on multi-month cumulative wetness rather than individual storm events.

A key result is that Asserdoune and Tamegnounte are less than 1Km apart yet behave very differently. This proximity rules out climate alone as the main cause and supports the fact that these outlets likely drain different aquifer compartments, have different degrees of conduit connectivity, or are fed by flow paths with different storage and transmissivity. Asserdoune’s higher BFI and higher 0–1 month responding rate suggest a mixed system where event recharge is efficiently transmitted but still superimposed on a persistent storage component. The strong linkage to Ait Ouchene and Taghzit is consistent with effective recharge coming from the adjacent upland flank that is topographically and structurally connected to the spring. In contrast, the Tamegnounte time series plots corroborate a more threshold-controlled behavior: sharp rainfall peaks are commonly followed by muted or delayed discharge increases unless wet conditions persist over successive months. This suggests that recharge must first overcome antecedent deficits, or fill intermediate storages before producing a measurable spring response. The weaker coupling with Taghzit, despite its relevance for Asserdoune, further implies that Tamegnounte taps a hydraulically distinct compartment, potentially influenced by perched conditions, diffuse recharge, or disconnected fracture networks. In addition, while Asserdoune responds more consistently to upstream rainfall, Tamegnounte responds more nonlinearly, producing strong peaks only when recharge conditions exceeds activation thresholds.

Aghbalou has a very high BFI, low peakiness, and only moderate R–Q correlation, with the lowest 0–1 month responding rate among the three springs. The spring, therefore, does not react strongly to single rainy months. Instead, rainfall is likely stored and released slowly before it reaches the spring. As a result, discharge stays stable and is mainly supported by baseflow coming from a thick unsaturated zone, or water stored in small fractures, rather than fast flow through large conduits. Aghbalou’s location is in the MA-HA junction zone, and can be seen as the piedmont of both domains. Its similar response to Tizi n’Isly (HA) and Taghzout (MA) suggests that the spring receives recharge from both sides. As a result, discharge increases are more strongly linked to several wet months in a row and to wet conditions before the event, rather than to a single wet month. In other words, the system needs time to build up storage before spring flow rises clearly.

In contrast, very strong correlations are observed between EC and Na⁺, Cl⁻, and SO₄²⁻. However, the hydrochemical data indicate that these relationships are primarily driven by a limited number of highly mineralized samples, mainly located in the Middle Atlas. These waters are associated with circulation through Triassic evaporitic formations, where the dissolution of halite and gypsum leads to elevated salinity.

Most springs, particularly in the High Atlas and the junction zone, exhibit moderate EC values and relatively low Na⁺ and Cl⁻ concentrations, indicating that carbonate processes dominate their hydrochemistry (Fig. 3). HCO₃⁻ shows only a weak correlation with EC, suggesting that while carbonate dissolution is widespread, it does not control the highest levels of mineralization.

Nitrate (NO₃⁻) displays no significant correlation with EC or other ions, confirming its independent behavior and suggesting limited anthropogenic influence in the recharge areas.

Spatially, patterns in groundwater chemistry reflect contrasts in lithology, structural setting, and flow organization across the three domains. High Atlas samples generally show higher EC and stronger Ca²⁺–Mg²⁺ enrichment, consistent with longer residence times and prolonged interaction with massive carbonate formations. Middle Atlas waters exhibit greater chemical variability, likely driven by lithological heterogeneity and more complex flow paths. Samples from the junction zone show intermediate compositions in both the Piper diagram and EC–ion relationships, supporting hydrogeochemical mixing between the two groundwater systems, facilitated by structural connectivity and convergent flow conditions.

Water isotopes and recharge

High Atlas springs are relatively isotopically enriched, whereas Middle Atlas springs display more depleted signatures, reflecting differences in recharge altitude and climatic conditions between the two domains. Tritium levels across the High Atlas, Middle Atlas, and their junction zone range from 0 TU to 1.65 TU, reflecting variable recharge conditions and residence times. In the High Atlas, values generally fall between 0.6 and 1.1 TU, indicating recent recharge and active infiltration, often facilitated by karstic and fractured media. Higher levels (> 1 TU), as in Ain Foum Oudi and Ain Ou Sefrou R.G, signal rapid flow paths, while zero values in springs like Aghbalou suggest deeper, older groundwater. junction zone springs show mixed signatures (0 to 1 TU), combining old, tritium-free waters with recent inputs, indicating these areas act as hydrodynamic buffers between Atlas domains. Middle Atlas springs typically show 0.76–1.14 TU, consistent with recent recharge through permeable lithologies. However, isolated zero values (e.g., Ain Oum Er Rbia) imply contributions from deeper or older groundwater, highlighting the region’s complex karst flow dynamics.

Recharge conditions, mixing, and evaporation

The relationship between δ¹⁸O and δ²H provides key constraints on groundwater recharge conditions, mixing processes, and the extent of isotopic fractionation prior to infiltration. Across the OER Basin, groundwater isotopic compositions cluster predominantly along the Local Meteoric Water Line (OER LMWL: δ²H = 8.05 × δ¹⁸O + 14.79) (R² = 0.84). The slope close to 8 indicates a predominantly meteoric origin with limited evaporation effects, while the relatively high intercept reflects regional moisture conditions typical of Mediterranean influences.

Despite this overall coherence, a noticeable dispersion of individual rainfall samples around the regression line highlights significant event-scale variability in isotopic composition. This variability is consistent with changes in atmospheric conditions, moisture sources, and rainfall processes between successive events. However, such short-term isotopic fluctuations are expected to be attenuated in karst systems due to mixing processes within the vadose zone and aquifer. Consequently, the isotopic composition of groundwater and karst springs represents an integrated recharge signal rather than individual precipitation events.

Groundwater emerging from the Middle Atlas is characterized by the most depleted δ¹⁸O and δ²H values observed in the dataset, while remaining closely aligned with meteoric water lines. This isotopic depletion reflects recharge derived from isotopically light precipitation, consistent with recharge dominated by cold-season inputs or precipitation that has undergone substantial atmospheric rainout. The narrow dispersion of Middle Atlas samples further indicates relatively homogeneous recharge conditions and limited isotopic fractionation prior to infiltration, suggesting rapid percolation through karstified carbonate formations and short residence times in the unsaturated zone.

In contrast, High Atlas groundwater displays less depleted isotopic compositions compared to the Middle Atlas, while still plotting along meteoric water lines (Fig. 5). This pattern indicates a meteoric recharge origin but reflects different recharge conditions, likely integrating precipitation formed over a broader range of climatic conditions and seasons. The relative enrichment of High Atlas groundwater compared to Middle Atlas waters may indicate a greater contribution from mixed-season precipitation or a weaker influence of cold-season recharge. Nevertheless, the absence of systematic deviation toward lower slopes confirms that evaporation prior to recharge remains limited. In this context, recharge may include contributions from rainfall and, locally, from snowmelt, particularly during winter and early spring, without inducing strong evaporative fractionation.

Groundwater from the junction zone between the Middle and High Atlas occupies intermediate positions in δ18O-δ2H relationships and defines linear trends connecting the two mountain domains. These trends are characteristic of mixing between isotopically distinct recharge components rather than recharge from a single homogeneous source. The preservation of meteoric slopes along these mixing trajectories indicates that mixing occurs without significant evaporative fractionation and is likely facilitated by structural features, including faults and fracture networks, that enhance hydraulic connectivity between the two mountain systems.

A temporal comparison of High Atlas groundwater sampled in 2017 and 2020 shows that isotopic compositions from both periods remain aligned with meteoric water lines (Fig. 6), indicating stable recharge mechanisms over time. However, subtle interannual variability is observed, with samples collecet in November 2020 displaying tighter clustering and slightly less depleted isotopic compositions compared to those collected in March 2017. This difference should only reflect variations in recharge conditions between the two periods, such as changes in precipitation amount, seasonality, and the relative contribution of rainfall versus snowmelt, rather than a fundamental change in recharge pathways41,42. Importantly, both datasets remain clearly distinct from modern precipitation, indicating that recent rainfall does not dominate the groundwater isotopic signal.

Comparison of δ¹⁸O–δ²H relationships for High Atlas groundwater sampled in 2017 and 2020.

Despite the close alignment of groundwater isotopic compositions with meteoric water lines, groundwater remains systematically more depleted in heavy isotopes than modern precipitation, which is isotopically enriched in δ18O and δ2H. This offset indicates that contemporary rainfall does not directly represent the dominant recharge signal preserved in the aquifer system. Instead, groundwater isotopic composition integrates recharge formed under climatic conditions different from those prevailing today, reflecting either older recharge inputs or limited effectiveness of modern recharge under recent drought conditions.

These stable isotope observations are consistent with the tritium evidence discussed in previous sections. The predominance of low tritium activities supports the interpretation that groundwater storage is largely dominated by older recharge components, while the presence of low but detectable tritium confirms that limited modern recharge still occurs. Tritium thus provides independent confirmation that the δ18O-δ2H signatures primarily reflect recharge operating over longer timescales, with only minor contributions from recent precipitation.

Altitudinal isotope gradients and recharge elevation

The relationship between δ18O and recharge altitude provides further insight into groundwater origin and circulation mechanisms (Fig. 7). A clear negative isotopic gradient with elevation is observed across the basin, consistent with regional precipitation patterns in Morocco and North Africa.

Relationship between groundwater δ¹⁸O values and recharge altitude for springs from the High Atlas, Middle Atlas, and their junction zone within the OER Basin.

Middle Atlas recharge zones are characterized by significantly depleted δ18O values at elevations exceeding 1200 to 1500 m.a.s.l., confirming that these areas act as major recharge reservoirs for the basin. The steep isotopic gradient reflects orographic effects and lower condensation temperatures at higher elevations. High Atlas recharge occurs at comparatively lower altitudes, resulting in less depleted isotopic signatures.

The junction zone displays a broad vertical spread in δ18O values over a relatively narrow altitude range, indicating vertical and lateral mixing between waters originating from different elevations. This observation suggests that groundwater flow paths are not strictly confined within individual geological units but instead cross structural boundaries, facilitated by fault systems and karst conduits.

For Asserdoune spring, despite only five sampling campaigns over eleven years (Table S1), the convergence of hydrodynamic, hydrochemical, and isotopic evidence supports a coherent conceptual model. The available data indicate that Asserdoune is supplied by a large, structurally organized karst recharge system rather than by a small local catchment. Its hydrodynamic behavior is complex, combining rapid, intermediate, and delayed flow responses, and thus reflects the dual functioning typical of karst systems, where fast conduit flow coexists with slower diffuse infiltration and storage. The sustained high baseflow, together with rapid responses to rainfall recorded at distant upland stations, implies hydraulic connectivity to an extensive recharge domain. The marked contrast with nearby Tamegnounte, despite the short distance separating the two springs, further indicates that this behavior is controlled primarily by structural recharge organization rather than by local climatic differences alone. The January 2022 drop in EC, following cumulative seasonal rainfall rather than a single extreme event, suggests that dilution is triggered by progressive catchment wetting and conduit-network activation. Conversely, the persistence of high discharge during the exceptional 2015–2016 drought demonstrates the presence of a large matrix reservoir capable of sustaining flow during prolonged dry periods.

The isotopic evidence further refines this interpretation. In such karst systems, the vadose zone, including the epikarst, plays a key role in regulating recharge by promoting temporary storage and mixing of infiltrated water, which dampens short-term variations in recharge signals. Accordingly, although precipitation in the study area may display strong event-scale isotopic variability, the isotopic composition of Asserdoune is more consistent with an integrated recharge signal than with the direct imprint of individual rainfall events. The strongly depleted δ¹⁸O value recorded in February 2017 indicates recharge at high altitude, and its occurrence after two years of drought points to renewed input from High Atlas snowmelt sustaining a baseflow that remains above 500 L/s. The February 2023 sample, which combines moderate discharge, relatively depleted isotopic values, and elevated tritium, confirms that the influence of high-altitude recharge is not merely episodic but structurally embedded in system functioning. Altogether, these observations support a mixed-flow karst model integrating rapid conduit recharge from a structurally connected upland zone, diffuse matrix recharge from mid-altitude carbonate slopes, and delayed seasonal snowmelt contributions from the High Atlas ridge. The interaction of these pathways explains both the spring’s responsiveness to rainfall and the relative temporal stability of its isotopic composition.

Oxygen-18 and Tritium versus NO3-

The positive correlation (R = 0.40, p = 0.03) between total nitrate (NO3−) and δ18O (Fig. 8a) highlights a clear link between nutrient loading and isotopic enrichment across the study area. Springs in the High Atlas, such as Ain Foum Oudi and Ain Ou Sefrou, are characterized by enriched δ18O values (−7.5 to −6‰) and higher nitrate levels, while Middle Atlas springs show more depleted isotopic signatures (−9 to −7.29‰) (Fig. 8a). This spatial variability indicates that the MA-HA junction serves as an intermediate transition zone, where the isotopic composition of recharge waters is directly coupled with the occurrence of nitrate.

Bivariate plots correlating total nitrates (NO3−) concentrations, (a) Tritium, and (b) δ18O.

Although isotopic enrichment in Mediterranean aquifers is frequently attributed to irrigation return flows or evaporation prior to infiltration43–46, this study identifies rapid rainfall-driven recharge as the primary control. Intense precipitation episodes mobilize soil-bound nitrates, transporting them through preferential flow paths with minimal residence time for evaporative modification. This process, supported by rapid spring discharge responses and observed in other fractured aquifers47, allows nitrates to reach the groundwater while preserving an isotopic signal reflective of the original rainfall rather than significant surface evaporation.

The significant positive correlation between nitrate (NO3−) and tritium (3H) activity (Fig. 8b) (R = 0.54, p = 0.002) indicates that groundwater vulnerability in the Atlas system is closely linked to modern recharge. Since tritium is a robust tracer for water infiltrated within the last few decades22,23,48, elevated activities indicate short residence times and rapid transfer from the surface to the aquifer. This interpretation is consistent with studies showing that young groundwater commonly contains higher nitrate concentrations owing to surface-derived anthropogenic inputs24–26.

In the Atlas aquifers, nitrate likely originates from fertilizers, livestock activities and wastewater49, while its transport is facilitated by the fractured and karstified structure of the system. These hydrogeological conditions promote preferential flow paths that bypass much of the unsaturated zone, thereby reducing opportunities for attenuation through denitrification, which generally requires longer residence times and reducing conditions48. Intense rainfall may further enhance this process by mobilizing nitrate stored in soils and generating recharge pulses enriched in young contaminated water50.

Although the nitrate–tritium relationship could partly reflect nitrate reduction along longer flow paths, this mechanism is unlikely to dominate in the present system. Denitrification is typically favored under reducing conditions associated with low dissolved oxygen, organic carbon availability and stagnant or waterlogged environments51. In the study area, the scarcity of such settings and the likely predominance of oxic groundwater conditions suggest that denitrification is limited. The observed pattern is therefore more consistently explained by recent recharge affected by anthropogenic inputs and by rapid transport through fractured and karstified formations. This interpretation is supported by the higher nitrate and tritium values recorded in High Atlas springs, particularly Ain Foum Oudi, indicating greater vulnerability to recent contamination (Fig. 9). By contrast, Middle Atlas springs generally show lower tritium and nitrate contents, consistent with dilution by older groundwater and recharge from less disturbed high-altitude zones, whereas the MA–HA junction displays intermediate signatures reflecting mixing between distinct flow systems52,53.

Protection areas

Groundwater protection priorities are established through an integrated multi-tracer framework that utilizes NO3- concentrations, δ18O signatures, and 3H activities to evaluate anthropogenic pressure and residence times (Table 4). While the World Health Organization (WHO) sets the drinking water limit for total nitrate at 50 mg/L, concentrations exceeding 10 mg/L are utilized here as an early indicator of anthropogenic vulnerability, such as agricultural or wastewater infiltration, distinguishing these from natural background levels54. High protection priority is assigned to springs combining elevated nitrates (> 10 mg/L), enriched δ18O values (−6 to −6.5‰) indicative of evaporative recharge or irrigation return flows, and modern tritium activities (> 1 TU) reflecting rapid hydrological connectivity. Conversely, low-priority springs exhibit depleted isotopic signatures (< −7.5‰) and low tritium levels, suggesting older groundwater with higher natural attenuation capacity.

Spatial classification reveals a distinct contrast between the fractured High Atlas aquifers and the more stable Middle Atlas systems. In the High Atlas, five springs (Ain Ourbia, Ain Sidi Bouyakoub, Ain Ou Sefrou, Ain N’oudaine, and Ain Foum Oudi) are categorized as high protection priority due to their extreme susceptibility to rapid surface-derived contamination. In comparison, only Ain Assoul in the Middle Atlas meets these high-priority criteria, as the region generally benefits from greater groundwater storage and reduced sensitivity to surface inputs. These regional variations corroborate findings in other mountainous karst regions, where the fraction of young groundwater is the primary driver of nitrate transport and aquifer vulnerability50,55.

Conclusion

The combined stable isotope and tritium evidence provides key insights into basin-scale groundwater functioning in the OER Basin. The persistence of meteoric δ18O-δ2H signatures and the absence of significant evaporative enrichment indicate that groundwater recharge is dominantly controlled by atmospheric precipitation and that isotopic signals are preserved during subsurface transport. This suggests that groundwater flow systems integrate recharge over large spatial scales rather than being dominated by localized surface processes.

The hydrodynamic analysis reveals a functional gradient among the three springs governed by structural complexity rather than climate. Aghbalou integrates recharge from both mountain domains over multi-month timescales, Asserdoune combines persistent high baseflow with rapid conduit response consistent with an extensive, hierarchically organised recharge area, and Tamegnounte operates as a threshold-controlled conduit outlet tapping a hydraulically distinct compartment.

Hydrochemical data confirm carbonate dissolution as the universal geochemical baseline. The progressive EC increase from High Atlas to Middle Atlas reflects a residence-time gradient, while the evaporite signature of the Middle Atlas and the intermediate compositions of the junction zone attest to active hydrogeochemical mixing facilitated by structural connectivity.

The isotopic record documents recharge sources and storage dynamics across contrasting hydrological states, from drought-induced enrichment and mineralisation to post-drought high-altitude snowmelt input at approximately 1867 m. The persistent correlation between tritium and nitrate establishes that aquifer vulnerability to surface contamination is intrinsically linked to the modern recharge fraction transmitted through conduit pathways.

These findings underscore the necessity of high-resolution continuous monitoring of flow rate, electrical conductivity, and stable water isotopes at key spring outlets. Such a program, capturing the critical window of days before, during, and after rainfall events, and sustained across contrasting wet and drought cycles, would transform the indicative conceptual model developed here into a quantitatively constrained framework capable of supporting source protection zoning and sustainable management of these critical karst resources under an increasingly variable climate.

Hydrochemical and isotopic analysis

During sample collection field parameters such as electrical conductivity (EC), temperature (T) and pH were measured in situ using a multiparameter probe (Multi 440 Conductivity-pH meter). The total alkalinity (as HCO3) was determined through titration with 0.1 M HCl against phenolphthalein and helianthin indicators. Samples for major ion analysis (Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻, NO3-, SO₄²⁻) were filtered using 0.45 µm Millipore filter paper and then placed in a refrigerator at 4 °C upon collection. Immediately following the collection of water samples, the chemical and isotopic analysis was performed at the Hydro Geochemistry Laboratory of the National Center for Energy Sciences and Nuclear Techniques (CNESTEN), Rabat, Morocco. Major ions were analyzed using ion chromatography (Thermo Scientific Dionex ICS 6000). The reliability of the hydrochemical data was assessed by checking ion balances, which were found to be within ± 5%.

The OER LMWL was constructed using a record of precipitation collected at Sidi Jaber station between September 2024 and March 2025 (Supplementary Table S2).

Stable isotopes (δ²H, δ¹⁸O) of both spring and rain samples were analyzed using a Picarro L2130i cavity ring-down spectrometer (CRDS) (Picarro, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The data were expressed in delta notation (δ) as parts per thousand (‰) relative to the Vienna Standard Mean Ocean Water (V-SMOW), with precision of ± 0.2‰ for δ18O and ± 1‰ for δD.

Most tritium (³H) analyses were conducted at the CNESTEN laboratories in Morocco, which have successfully participated in several IAEA tritium proficiency testing programs, supporting the reliability of the analytical results. The measurements were performed using electrolytic enrichment following the method of58, followed by liquid scintillation counting according to Thatcher et al59.. In this procedure, approximately 500 mL of each water sample was concentrated by electrolysis and then mixed with a scintillation cocktail prior to counting. The analytical detection limit was 0.2 tritium units (TU), with 1 TU corresponding to 0.118 Bq/L.

Some samples were reported by the laboratory as 0 TU, indicating activities indistinguishable from instrumental background rather than a complete absence of tritium. These values should therefore be interpreted as old or pre-modern water, which reflects groundwater with no contribution from recent recharge.
