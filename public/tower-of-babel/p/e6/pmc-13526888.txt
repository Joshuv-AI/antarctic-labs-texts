Legume cover cropping enhances soil function and yield stability in wheat–maize systems under groundwater overexploitation in the North China Plain
Authors: Tao Wang, Mengmeng Sun, Zijie Fan, Zhenlei Li, Xiaoli Liu, Wenchao Zhen, Yandong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The North China Plain (NCP) is one of China’s most important wheat–maize production regions, but the conventional winter wheat–summer maize system relies heavily on groundwater irrigation and high fertilizer inputs, intensifying the conflict between grain production and resource sustainability. Legume cover cropping and intercropping have been proposed as potential strategies to improve resource use and soil health; however, field evidence linking soil water regulation, soil multifunctionality, and yield stability under reduced water and fertilizer inputs remains limited. Here, we conducted a five-year field experiment in a groundwater-overexploited area of the NCP to evaluate four cropping systems: the conventional winter wheat–summer maize system (TWM), a reduced-input winter wheat–summer maize system (WM), wheat–white clover strip intercropping with relay-intercropped maize (CI), and wheat–white clover mixed cropping followed by maize (CM). The results showed that CI improved soil moisture conditions in the wheat strips, whereas CI-C reduced water consumption during the wheat season by 26.2%, 19.3%, and 16.9% compared with TWM, WM, and CM, respectively. CI also enhanced soil nutrient availability and biochemical activity, as indicated by higher contents of alkali-hydrolyzable nitrogen, available phosphorus, available potassium, and soil organic carbon, together with increased soil alkaline phosphatase, urease, and saccharase activities. Consequently, CI-C showed the highest soil multifunctionality index across all sampling periods, while WM consistently showed the lowest values. Yield analysis further revealed that WM reduced wheat and maize yields by 18.6% and 11.3%, respectively, compared with TWM. In contrast, CI increased maize yield by 9.7%, 23.6%, and 16.0% compared with TWM, WM, and CM, respectively, and maintained an annual yield of 18.2 Mg ha⁻¹, comparable to that of TWM. Redundancy analysis indicated that SOC, SAP, SAN, SSC, SUE, SCAT, and ET jointly contributed to yield formation. These findings demonstrate that wheat–white clover strip intercropping with relay-intercropped maize can achieve synergistic improvements in water regulation, soil nutrient activation, soil multifunctionality, and yield stability. This system provides a promising ecological intensification strategy for sustainable wheat–maize production in groundwater-overexploited areas of the NCP.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-57436-y.

Introduction

The North China Plain (NCP) is one of China’s most important grain-producing regions. It receives around 500 mm of precipitation annually, with significant interannual variability, and most rainfall is concentrated in July and August during the maize season1,2. The winter wheat–summer maize cropping system in this region relies heavily on groundwater irrigation and produces about two-thirds of the country’s wheat and more than a quarter of its maize3,4. However, this system has led to a rapid decline in groundwater levels by 17.8 mm annually, creating the world’s largest groundwater depletion area and exacerbating the conflict between water resource security and sustainable agricultural development5. In the NCP, improving irrigation practices is essential for increasing water-resource use efficiency. Introducing intercropping systems with cover crops may further increase crop yield and enhance the sustainability of agricultural ecosystems6,7.

Global meta-analyses have shown that cover crops and other conservation measures can significantly reduce surface runoff and soil erosion while improving soil health8. Research by Tonitto et al. indicates that cover crops can also increase soil organic carbon storage and enhance nutrient cycling, thereby reducing dependence on chemical nitrogen fertilizers by substituting winter fallow, providing biological nitrogen fixation, and removing residual nutrients9. Among the numerous leguminous cover crops, clover (Trifolium repens) stands out as a commonly used agricultural cover crop due to its excellent nitrogen fixation ability, frost resistance, year-round coverage, and strong regeneration capacity. In this study, clover is used primarily in intercropping systems to perform its functional role through vegetation coverage, and the benefits of clover in a wheat–clover intercropping system are clearly demonstrated. Li et al.10 found that the amount of nitrogen transferred from clover to wheat can reach 34.4–57.5 kg N ha−1, providing an important slow-release nitrogen source for wheat. A review by Bedoussac et al.11 highlighted indications that intercropping can significantly increase wheat grain protein content, improving its nutritional and processing quality. Additionally, a study on poor soils by Xia et al.12 found that an intercropping system can increase soil organic matter by 13%, enhance microbial activity, and improve nutrient cycling, with a land equivalent ratio exceeding 1.3, achieving synergistic improvements in both yield and soil fertility. Moreover, well-established clover can effectively assist in weed suppression in the field. However, it is also important to note that clover’s growth and functionality may be limited under water stress conditions, which is a factor to consider when applying it in arid or semi-arid regions.

Intercropping has, in recent years, been widely applied in global agriculture as an agricultural management strategy to enhance crop diversity and optimize resource utilization13,14. Studies by Jensen et al. indicate that intercropping can significantly improve soil health, increase soil organic carbon storage, enhance crop yield, and optimize resource use through complementary interactions between different crops15,16. Intercropping systems involving cereals and legumes have, in particular, been shown by Peoples et al. to improve nitrogen use efficiency and reduce dependence on chemical nitrogen fertilizers, thereby enhancing crop productivity and reducing environmental pollution15,17. Moreover, by intercropping with cover crops, soil fertility and crop water use efficiency can be further improved, while also reducing dependence on external chemical fertilizers18. In dry regions such as the NCP, optimizing irrigation methods is a key approach to improving water-resource use efficiency. When combined with cover crops, intercropping systems may also increase crop yield and strengthen the sustainability of agricultural ecosystems19. Therefore, intercropping cover crops with cereals can not only diversify cropping systems but also maximize the ecological benefits provided by cover crops, offering a viable strategy for achieving sustainable agricultural development.

Compared to conventional spring irrigation with two irrigation phases, cutting back to one round of irrigation or no irrigation at all in spring led to yield reductions of 23.0% and 50.9%, respectively19. Therefore, continuing to reduce irrigation based on the “one spring irrigation” policy cannot maintain economic benefits through simple irrigation reduction. Hence, there is an urgent need to establish a new planting system that can maintain food production capacity, improve the agricultural ecological environment, and enhance precipitation resource use efficiency.

Although cover crops and intercropping systems have significant theoretical potential, their practical application in groundwater overexploitation areas of the NCP remains insufficiently understood. In particular, whether cereal–legume intercropping can maintain soil fertility and crop yield while reducing water and fertilizer inputs still requires further verification. In addition, existing studies have mainly focused on optimizing single agronomic practices20,21, whereas the integrated effects of legume cover cropping and intercropping on soil water dynamics, soil nutrient supply, soil biochemical processes, soil multifunctionality, and crop productivity under strict water-resource constraints remain unclear. Therefore, this study established four cropping systems: a conventional winter wheat–summer maize system, a reduced-input winter wheat–summer maize system, a wheat–white clover strip intercropping system with relay-intercropped maize, and a wheat–white clover mixed cropping system followed by maize. The objectives were to compare soil moisture dynamics, crop water consumption, soil nutrient availability, soil enzyme activity, soil multifunctionality, and wheat–maize yield under different cropping systems, and to identify the key soil and water-related factors associated with yield variation. This study hypothesized that: (1) compared with the reduced-input wheat–maize system, wheat–white clover strip intercropping with relay-intercropped maize would improve soil water regulation and reduce water consumption during the wheat season; (2) the introduction of white clover would enhance soil available nutrient contents, enzyme activities, and soil multifunctionality; and (3) by improving soil water conditions, nutrient availability, and enzyme-mediated soil processes, the CI system would maintain or improve wheat–maize productivity compared with WM and CM under reduced water and fertilizer inputs.

Methods

Experimental site

A five-year field experiment was conducted from 2019 to 2024 at the Xinji Experimental Station of Hebei Agricultural University in Xinji City, Hebei Province, China (37.47°N, 115.18°E) (Fig. 1). The experimental station is owned and managed by Hebei Agricultural University, and all field experiments were conducted with permission from the station administration. The experimental site is located in the central Haihe Plain at an elevation of approximately 43 m and has a warm temperate, semi-humid continental monsoon climate. The long-term mean annual precipitation is 473.6 mm, with rainfall mainly concentrated in July and August. The long-term mean precipitation is 128.6 mm during the wheat season and 345.0 mm during the maize season. The mean annual temperature is 12.5 °C, with mean temperatures of − 3.9 °C in the coldest month and 26.5 °C in the warmest month. The winter and spring seasons are characterized by strong winds, low precipitation, low temperature, and high evapotranspiration. During the experimental period from 2019 to 2024, annual precipitation ranged from 487.7 to 840.5 mm (Figure S1). The soils in this region are typically classified as Cambisols. The soil at the experimental site had a bulk density of 1.34 g cm−3 and an organic matter content of 19.10 g kg−1. The contents of total nitrogen, total phosphorus, and total potassium were 1.37, 0.94, and 17.52 g kg−1, respectively. The contents of alkali-hydrolyzable nitrogen, available phosphorus, and available potassium were 143.70, 51.12, and 152.50 mg kg−1, respectively.

Experimental design

A randomized complete block design was adopted with four treatments: wheat–white clover strip intercropping with relay-intercropped maize (CI), wheat–white clover mixed cropping followed by maize (CM), a reduced-input winter wheat–summer maize system (WM), and a conventional winter wheat–summer maize system (TWM). Each treatment was replicated three times, and each plot measured 46.2 m² (6.6 m × 7.0 m). Winter wheat cultivar Malan 1 was sown at 150 kg ha−1. Maize cultivar Zhengdan 958 was planted at a density of 72,000 plants ha−1 with a row spacing of 60 cm. White clover (Trifolium repens L.) was sown at 75 kg ha−1.

In the CI treatment, wheat was sown into pre-established white clover strips, with both wheat and clover strip widths set at 60 cm. Six rows of wheat were sown within each wheat strip, with a row spacing of 12 cm. Maize was relay-intercropped into the white clover strips two weeks before wheat harvest. CI-M and CI-C denote the wheat strip and white clover strip within the CI treatment, respectively. In the CM treatment, wheat was sown between pre-established white clover rows, with a row spacing of 30 cm. The TWM and WM treatments followed conventional winter wheat–summer maize planting practices. In the TWM, WM, and CM treatments, maize was planted after wheat harvest (Figure S2).

During the wheat season, micro-sprinkler irrigation was applied once at the jointing stage. The irrigation amount was 90 mm for TWM and 45 mm for WM, CI, and CM. In the CI treatment, irrigation was applied only to the wheat strips at 90 mm, resulting in a plot-level equivalent irrigation amount of 45 mm. During the maize season, irrigation was applied once after sowing, with 45 mm applied to all treatments.

During the wheat season, the TWM treatment received a total of 240 kg N ha−1, applied in two equal splits as basal fertilizer and topdressing. The basal fertilizer consisted of 120 kg N ha−1 as urea, 53 kg P ha−1 as superphosphate, and 100 kg K ha−1 as potassium sulfate, and was incorporated into the topsoil before sowing. An additional 120 kg N ha−1 was applied as topdressing at the jointing stage. Fertilizer inputs in the WM, CI, and CM treatments were reduced by 50% compared with TWM, and fertilizers in the CI treatment were applied only to the wheat strips. During the maize season, a total of 240 kg N ha−1 was also applied in two equal splits. The basal fertilizer consisted of 120 kg N ha−1 as urea, 53 kg P ha−1 as superphosphate, and 100 kg K ha−1 as potassium sulfate, and was applied to the topsoil at sowing, followed by 120 kg N ha−1 at the V12 stage. Weeding and pest control were conducted regularly throughout the growing period.

Soil moisture and water consumption

In the 2021–2022 and 2022–2023 growing seasons, soil moisture content (v/v) was measured using a time domain reflectometry instrument (TDR, IMKO, Germany). Measurements were taken in the 0–200 cm soil profile at 20 cm intervals during key growth stages. For wheat, measurements were conducted at pre-sowing, pre-wintering, jointing, flowering, and maturity. For maize, measurements were conducted at pre-sowing, seedling, jointing, silking, and maturity.

Soil collection and data acquisition

Soil samples were collected at the maturity stages of wheat and maize in the 2021–2022 and 2022–2023 growing seasons. In each plot, soil samples were collected from the 0–20 cm soil layer using a 5 cm diameter soil auger and a five-point sampling method. The five subsamples from each plot were thoroughly mixed to form one composite sample. Fresh soil samples were passed through a 2.0 mm sieve to remove visible roots, plant residues, stones, and other debris, and then stored at 4 °C for subsequent determination of soil physicochemical properties and enzyme activities. Soil total nitrogen (TN) was determined using the Kjeldahl method. Soil total phosphorus (TP) was measured using the HClO4-H2SO4 digestion method, whereas soil total potassium (TK) was determined by flame photometry. Soil alkali-hydrolyzable nitrogen (SAN) was measured using the alkaline diffusion absorption method. Soil available phosphorus (SAP) was extracted with NaHCO3, and soil available potassium (SAK) was extracted with CH3COONH4 and then determined by flame photometry. Soil organic carbon (SOC) was determined using the potassium dichromate oxidation method with external heating. Soil catalase (SCAT) activity was measured by back-titrating residual H2O2 with a standard KMnO4 solution. Soil alkaline phosphatase (SCAP) activity was determined using the disodium phenyl phosphate colorimetric method. Soil urease (SUE) activity was determined using the permanganometric method, and soil saccharase (SSC) activity was determined using the 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid colorimetric method.

Soil multifunctionality index

To comprehensively evaluate soil nutrient supply and enzyme activity under different cropping systems, soil multifunctionality index (SMI) was quantified using the standardized averaging approach.

A total of 11 soil variables were included in the multifunctionality analysis, including TN, TP, TK, SAN, SAP, SAK, SOC, SCAT, SCAP, SUE, and SSC.

Grain yield and its components

At wheat harvest, 1 m double-row wheat plants were selected to determine the number of spikes, kernel number per spike, kernel weight, and aboveground biomass. Moreover, 1 m2 of wheat spikes were harvested to measure grain yield, and grain moisture content was adjusted to 13%. The wheat yield in the CI treatment was adjusted for land area. At maize harvest, a 5-meter section of twin-row maize plants was selected to determine the number of ears, kernels per ear, kernel weight, and grain yield, with the water content adjusted to 14%.

Statistical analysis

Partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) was performed to identify the major soil variables driving differences among treatments. Treatment was used as the response variable, whereas TN, TP, TK, SAN, SAP, SAK, SOC, SCAT, SCAP, SUE, and SSC were used as explanatory variables. The PLS-DA models were constructed using the opls function in the ropls package with orthoI = 0. Analyses were conducted separately by year and season, and permutation testing was performed to assess model stability and the risk of overfitting. The interpretation of the PLS-DA results mainly focused on variable importance in projection (VIP) values; soil variables were ranked according to their VIP values, and variables with VIP > 1 were considered key indicators distinguishing different treatments. The PLS-DA score plots were used to present the overall pattern of differences among treatments. Model quality was evaluated using R2X, R2Y, Q2, RMSE, pR2Y, and pQ2. R2X and R2Y represent the explanatory ability of the model for the predictor and response variables, respectively; Q2 reflects predictive ability; RMSE indicates model fitting error; and pR2Y and pQ2 were used to assess model significance. In general, higher R2X, R2Y, and Q2 values together with lower RMSE values indicate better model performance. Models with Q2 > 0.5 and pR2Y and pQ2 < 0.05 were considered to have good stability and reliability.

Redundancy analysis (RDA) was performed to examine the effects of soil nutrients, enzyme activities, and water consumption on wheat and maize yield. The RDA was conducted separately for wheat and maize. Crop yield-related indicators were used as response variables. Since the variable importance in projection (VIP) values of total soil nutrients in the PLS-DA analysis were all lower than 1, SAN, SAP, SAK, SOC, SCAT, SCAP, SUE, SSC, and ET were selected as explanatory variables. Prior to the analysis, the response matrix was Hellinger-transformed to reduce the influence of differences in scale and extreme values. Detrended correspondence analysis (DCA) was then used to determine the appropriate ordination method. Because the gradient lengths of the first DCA axis for both wheat and maize were less than 4, RDA was selected as the linear constrained ordination method. To avoid the influence of multicollinearity among explanatory variables, variance inflation factors (VIFs) were calculated, and all variables with VIF values lower than 10 were retained in the model. The RDA models were constructed using the rda function in the vegan package in R, and the explanatory ability of the models was evaluated using adjusted R2. The overall significance of the RDA models and the relationships between explanatory variables and yield indicators were assessed using permutation tests. The envfit function was used with 1000 permutations, and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. RDA ordination plots were used to visualize the relationships among treatments, yield indicators, soil nutrients, enzyme activities, and water consumption. In the ordination plots, the direction of each arrow indicates the trend of variable change, whereas the arrow length represents the relative explanatory strength of that variable for yield variation.

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to evaluate the effects of treatments on soil nutrients, enzyme activities, and water consumption. The ANOVA was performed using the aov function in R. When treatment effects were significant, multiple comparisons were conducted using the least significant difference (LSD) test at P < 0.05. The multiple comparison procedure was carried out using the LSD.test function in the agricolae package, and significant differences among treatments were indicated by different lowercase letters. Data for each treatment were expressed as mean ± standard error (SE), and bar plots were generated using ggplot2. To analyze the effect of different treatments on yield while accounting for the influence of experimental year, a mixed-effects model was fitted with treatment as a fixed effect and year as a random effect using the lme4 package in R.

Results

Effects of different planting systems on soil moisture

The dynamics of soil moisture in the 0–200 cm profile under different cropping systems are shown in Fig. 2. The WM treatment maintained lower soil moisture compared with TWM. The CI treatment significantly improved soil water content in the wheat strips (CI-M), with overall soil moisture higher than that in the TWM and WM treatments; however, soil moisture in the clover strips (CI-C) during the maize season was markedly lower due to increased transpiration. In contrast, the CM treatment exhibited lower soil moisture than the TWM, WM, and CI treatments because of increased transpiration between crops.

Crop water consumption differed significantly among cropping systems (Fig. 3). During the wheat season, the TWM treatment showed the highest water consumption, which was significantly greater than that of the CM, CI-M, and CI-C treatments. In contrast, the CI-C treatment had the lowest water consumption, with significant reductions of 26.2%, 19.3%, and 16.9% compared with the TWM, WM, and CM treatments, respectively. During the maize season, water consumption in the CI treatment was significantly higher than that in the other treatments. On an annual basis, total water consumption was lowest in the CI-C treatment and highest in the CI-M treatment, indicating that CI-C had a clear advantage in reducing annual water consumption in the wheat–maize rotation system. Clear interannual variation in crop water consumption was also observed, which was mainly associated with rainfall distribution. Rainfall during the 2021–2022 wheat season was greater than that during the 2022–2023 wheat season, resulting in higher water consumption in the former season. In contrast, higher rainfall during the 2023 maize season led to increased water consumption in that season. Overall, total annual water consumption was higher in 2022–2023 than in 2021–2022.

Effects of different planting systems on soil nutrients

The PLS-DA results showed clear differences in soil nutrient characteristics among cropping systems (Fig. 4). The R²Y and R²X of the models were greater than 0.5, and the Q² ranged from 0.44 to 0.64. In addition, all cross-validation permutation tests were significant (P < 0.01), indicating that the models showed good or moderate predictive ability (Table S1). The score plots showed clear separation among treatments along the first or second component in both the wheat and maize seasons, suggesting marked differences in soil nutrient characteristics among cropping systems. VIP analysis indicated that SAP, SAK, SCAP, SCAT, and SSC were the major contributing variables in the 2022 wheat season, whereas SAP, SAK, SOC, SCAP, and SCAT were the main contributors in the 2022 maize season. As the rotation period progressed, the cumulative effects of white clover introduction on soil organic matter accumulation and microbial activity became increasingly evident, leading to a marked increase in the VIP value of SOC, which gradually emerged as an important factor in both the 2023 wheat and maize seasons. Overall, the differentiation of soil properties among cropping systems was mainly driven by SOC, available nutrients, and key soil enzyme activities.

The measurements of soil nutrients during the wheat and maize growing seasons showed that the contents of total nutrients, including TN, TP, and TK, did not differ significantly among treatments (Figure S3). However, soil available nutrients and SOC content varied significantly among the different cropping systems (Figure S4). Overall, the CI-C treatment maintained higher levels of SAN, SAP, SAK, and SOC, while the CI-M treatment also showed relatively high levels of available nutrients. In contrast, the WM treatment generally exhibited lower available nutrient contents. Soil enzyme activities also differed significantly among cropping systems across the crop growing seasons (Figure S5). Compared with the TWM and WM treatments, the CI-C treatment maintained lower SCAT activity but higher SCAP, SUE, and SSC activities. The CI-M treatment also showed relatively high soil enzyme activities, although they were generally lower than those observed under CI-C. Overall, white clover intercropping increased soil available nutrient contents and enhanced key soil enzyme activities, suggesting that it improves soil nutrient supply capacity and biological activity in the wheat–maize rotation system.

Effects of different planting systems on soil multifunctionality

The effects of different cropping systems on overall soil functioning were evaluated by calculating the soil multifunctionality index (SMI) (Fig. 5a). The results showed that intercropping significantly enhanced soil multifunctionality in farmland. Compared with the WM, CM, and TWM treatments, the CI-C treatment exhibited significantly higher SMI values across all four periods. The CI-M treatment also showed relatively high SMI values. In contrast, the WM treatment consistently had the lowest SMI across all periods. In addition, the heatmap analysis indicated that soil available nutrients (SAN, SAP, and SAK), SOC, and soil enzyme activities were the main factors influencing farmland soil multifunctionality (Fig. 5b). Specifically, the increases in SMI under the CI-C and CI-M treatments were strongly associated with higher enzyme activities and greater availability of soil nutrients.

Effects of different planting systems on crop yield

Mixed-effects model analysis based on five-year yield data (Fig. 6) revealed that the system’s productivity was jointly determined by planting system and inter-annual climatic variation. Regarding treatment effects, the five-year average yields for the wheat and maize seasons under TWM were 8.1 Mg ha−1 and 10.1 Mg ha−1, respectively. Under WM, the corresponding yields were 6.6 and 9.0 Mg ha−1, representing reductions of 18.6% and 11.3% compared to TWM. Conversely, CI, which introduces species complementarity through structural redesign, performed better—especially in the maize season. The five-year average maize yield under CI was 9.7%, 23.6%, and 16.0% higher than under TWM, WM, and CM, respectively. Meanwhile, its annual average yield reached 18.2 Mg ha−1, essentially matching that of the TWM treatment, demonstrating the yield-enhancing potential of innovative planting systems. Analysis of year random effects further highlighted the importance of environmental variation for system yield and the differences in stability among systems (Figure S6). In terms of annual yield, 2022 exerted a strong positive effect, likely due to favorable precipitation, whereas adverse conditions in 2021 clearly constrained yield. Notably, in unfavorable years with negative random effects, yield declines under WM were often more severe. In contrast, the CI treatment exhibited greater buffering capacity and more stable yields.

Relationships between grain yield and soil environmental variables

RDA analysis revealed the relationships between soil properties and crop yield as well as yield components under different cropping systems (Fig. 7). During the wheat season, the first two ordination axes explained 98.92% of the total variation (RDA1: 84.76%; RDA2: 14.16%). The ordination plot (Fig. 7a) showed that the CI and CM treatments increased soil SOC, SAP, SSC, SUE, and SCAP, thereby enhancing wheat grain number and grain weight. In contrast, the TWM and WM treatments were associated with higher SCAT and ET, as well as greater spike number. Overall, the TWM and CI treatments achieved relatively higher wheat yields. Environmental vector fitting (Fig. 7b) further indicated that SOC, SAP, SSC, and SCAT were the major drivers of wheat yield variation. During the maize season, the first two ordination axes explained 99.79% of the total variation (RDA1: 82.03%; RDA2: 17.76%). The ordination plot (Fig. 7c) indicated that the CI treatment increased soil SAN, SOC, SSC, and SUE, thereby promoting maize yield. In contrast, the CM and WM treatments were associated with higher SCAT, SAK, and ET values. Environmental vector fitting (Fig. 7d) showed that SAN, SCAT, ET, and SOC were the primary factors driving maize yield variation.

Discussion

The North China Plain, as a core grain-producing region in China, has long faced a conflict between water scarcity and intensive agricultural production25. The traditional winter wheat–summer maize double-cropping system has high grain productivity but depends heavily on groundwater irrigation. In contrast, winter fallow or spring maize monocropping can reduce irrigation pressure during winter and spring, but may also lead to underutilization of winter light and thermal resources, bare soil exposure, wind erosion, dust emissions, and reduced farmer income26. Therefore, achieving coordinated water saving, yield stability, and soil fertility improvement without substantially compromising grain production has become a key challenge for agricultural green transformation in groundwater-overexploited areas of the North China Plain. In this study, four cropping systems were established: the traditional winter wheat–summer maize system (TWM), the reduced water and fertilizer input winter wheat–summer maize system (WM), the winter wheat–white clover strip intercropping system with relay-intercropped maize before wheat harvest (CI), and the wheat–white clover mixed cropping system followed by maize (CM). The results showed that, compared with WM, which relied solely on reducing water and fertilizer inputs, the CI system formed through optimized crop spatial configuration and phenological coordination exhibited more comprehensive advantages in soil water regulation, nutrient supply, soil multifunctionality, and system productivity maintenance.

From the perspective of water dynamics, WM mainly reduced resource consumption by decreasing irrigation and fertilizer inputs, but this approach did not alter the soil–crop–water relationship. The results showed that soil moisture under WM was generally lower than that under TWM, indicating that simple input reduction may weaken soil water supply capacity and increase water limitation during crop growth. In contrast, CI did not merely reduce water input; rather, it reconstructed crop spatial distribution and water use processes through white clover strip coverage, wheat strip planting, and late-stage relay intercropping of maize. CI significantly increased soil water content in the wheat strips, and its overall soil moisture was higher than that of TWM and WM. Meanwhile, CI-C had the lowest water consumption during the wheat season, which was significantly reduced by 26.2%, 19.3%, and 16.9% compared with TWM, WM, and CM, respectively. These results indicate that the water-saving advantage of CI did not arise from passive suppression of crop water demand, but from improved soil water retention and optimized spatiotemporal water allocation driven by ground cover, root activity, and strip configuration27.

This water-regulation mechanism has important agronomic implications. White clover coverage can reduce bare soil exposure, thereby decreasing non-productive evaporation and wind erosion. Its root activity and residue input may also improve surface soil pore structure and enhance the retention of precipitation and irrigation water28. At the same time, wheat and white clover were spatially separated in CI, while maize subsequently used the white clover strip space before and after wheat harvest. This shifted water consumption from a single crop demand peak to a more distributed pattern across different crops and growth stages. It should be noted that soil moisture in CI-C was lower during the maize season because of increased transpiration by white clover. However, the period of stronger clover water consumption coincided with the maize season, when high temperatures and seasonal rainfall coincide and seasonal precipitation is relatively high. This suggests that cover crops do not consistently increase soil moisture across all periods; rather, their effects depend on the balance among cover crop growth, transpiration consumption, and the water demand of the main crop. Therefore, when promoting the CI system, attention should be paid to regulating white clover biomass, optimizing strip width, and managing irrigation at critical stages to avoid excessive water competition between the cover crop and the main crop.

Changes in soil nutrients and enzyme activities further indicate that the advantages of CI were not limited to improved water dynamics, but also involved enhanced internal nutrient supply capacity and soil biochemical processes. In this study, total nutrients, including TN, TP, and TK, did not differ significantly among treatments, whereas available nutrients and SOC varied significantly. CI-C maintained higher SAN, SAP, SAK, and SOC contents, and CI-M also showed relatively high available nutrient levels, while WM generally had lower available nutrient contents. These results suggest that, in the short term, different cropping systems did not significantly alter the total soil nutrient pool, but they strongly affected the transformation of nutrients from “potential pools” to “available pools”. The increase in available nutrients under CI may be associated with greater inputs of labile organic substrates from white clover root exudates, fine-root turnover, and residue return. These organic substrates can provide carbon sources and energy for soil biochemical processes, promote organic nutrient mineralization, and enhance the activation of nitrogen and phosphorus.

Changes in soil enzyme activities support this mechanism. Compared with TWM and WM, CI-C had higher SCAP, SUE, and SSC activities, and CI-M also showed relatively high enzyme activities. SCAP is closely related to organic phosphorus mineralization and available phosphorus release, SUE is associated with organic nitrogen transformation, and SSC reflects carbohydrate decomposition and carbon cycling29. The increase in these key enzyme activities under CI suggests that white clover coverage and strip intercropping may enhance soil carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus cycling. As a result, nutrient supply may shift from being mainly dependent on external fertilizer inputs to being partly driven by internal cycling processes involving rhizosphere activity, residue decomposition, and enzyme-mediated mineralization. This also explains why CI maintained relatively high available nutrient levels and favorable crop performance even under lower water and fertilizer inputs than TWM. In contrast, although WM reduced water and fertilizer inputs, it did not introduce additional biological drivers. Relatively lower crop biomass and rhizosphere carbon input may have limited the activation of soil nutrient cycling processes.

The soil multifunctionality results further confirmed the positive effects of CI on overall soil functioning. The SMI results showed that CI-C had significantly higher SMI values than WM, CM, and TWM across all four periods, while CI-M also showed relatively high SMI values. In contrast, WM consistently had the lowest SMI. Heatmap analysis showed that SAN, SAP, SAK, SOC, and soil enzyme activities were the main factors affecting SMI, and the improvement in SMI under CI-C and CI-M was closely associated with higher enzyme activities and greater nutrient availability. These results indicate that the improvement of soil function under CI was not driven by a single indicator, but by the combined enhancement of nutrient availability, carbon accumulation, and enzyme-mediated processes30. In other words, through the integration of cover cropping and strip intercropping, CI enhanced the soil’s capacity to maintain nutrient supply, promote organic matter turnover, and support biological activity.

Yield results showed that the advantages of CI were also reflected in yield stability and coordinated resource use. The mixed-effects model based on five-year yield data showed that the average wheat and maize yields under TWM were 8.1 Mg ha−1 and 10.1 Mg ha−1, respectively, whereas those under WM were 6.6 Mg ha−1 and 9.0 Mg ha−1, representing reductions of 18.6% and 11.3%, respectively, compared with TWM. These results indicate that simply reducing water and fertilizer inputs can cause substantial yield losses. In contrast, CI performed particularly well in the maize season. The five-year average maize yield under CI was 9.7%, 23.6%, and 16.0% higher than that under TWM, WM, and CM, respectively, and its annual average yield reached 18.2 Mg ha−1, which was essentially comparable to that of TWM. Therefore, the yield stability of CI did not depend on high inputs, but rather on the synergistic use of water, nutrients, and growth resources through system-level structural optimization.

RDA further revealed the key environmental factors underlying this yield advantage. During the wheat season, CI and CM were associated with higher SOC, SAP, SSC, SUE, and SCAP, which promoted kernel number and grain weight. In contrast, TWM and WM were associated with higher SCAT and ET, as well as greater spike number. Environmental vector fitting showed that SOC, SAP, SSC, and SCAT were the main factors driving variation in wheat yield. During the maize season, CI increased SAN, SOC, SSC, and SUE, thereby promoting maize yield, while SAN, SCAT, ET, and SOC were the primary drivers of maize yield variation. These results indicate that the yield-enhancing and yield-stabilizing effects of CI were not determined solely by water supply, but by the combined effects of soil carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus availability, enzyme-mediated nutrient release, and crop water use. In particular, the increase in SAN and SOC under CI during the maize season may have played an important role in supporting late-stage maize growth and grain formation.

In addition, the system-level advantages of CI were also associated with crop niche complementarity and improved use of light and thermal resources. In the northern North China Plain, maize production is often constrained by delayed sowing after wheat harvest and insufficient accumulated temperature during the growing season. Through wheat–white clover strip intercropping and late-stage relay intercropping of maize, CI created temporal and spatial complementarity among different crops. Wheat preferentially used spring light and thermal resources from early spring to early summer, while maize continued to grow before and after wheat harvest and could utilize the light, heat, and space released after wheat harvest. This extended the annual period of effective photosynthetically active radiation use in the field. Unlike systems that rely on high inputs within a single crop season, CI expanded yield formation to the scale of annual resource coordination. This mechanism helps explain why CI maintained an annual yield comparable to TWM under reduced water and fertilizer inputs during the wheat season, while showing higher productivity during the maize season.

From a practical perspective, CI provides clear implications for groundwater-overexploited areas of the North China Plain. For farmers, CI indicates that water saving does not necessarily mean simply reducing irrigation and fertilizer inputs, nor does it have to come at the cost of substantial yield loss. Through white clover strip coverage, wheat strip planting, and maize relay intercropping, it is possible to reduce water and fertilizer inputs during the wheat season while maintaining relatively high annual yield and improving soil fertility. Therefore, extension efforts should focus on technical training in strip configuration, white clover biomass regulation, maize relay-intercropping timing, reduced fertilization, and irrigation management at critical growth stages. For policymakers, existing policies should not focus only on fallow subsidies or simple reductions in irrigation. Instead, they should support cropping system innovations that simultaneously achieve water saving, yield stability, and soil function improvement. For example, subsidies could be provided for cover crop seeds, strip-sowing machinery, relay-intercropping equipment, and demonstration fields, while an integrated evaluation system based on water-saving effects, yield stability, and soil health improvement should be established. For water resource managers, CI can serve as an important agronomic pathway for irrigation quota management and agricultural water-saving assessment in groundwater-restricted areas. In the future, crop system optimization could be incorporated into regional water-saving evaluation, and CI demonstration trials could be conducted in areas with different groundwater extraction restriction intensities to promote synergy between groundwater control policies and agronomic water-saving measures.

In summary, CI achieved synergistic improvements in water regulation, nutrient activation, enzyme-mediated soil processes, and crop niche complementarity through white clover coverage, wheat strip intercropping, and late-stage relay intercropping of maize. Compared with WM, which relied mainly on reduced inputs, CI emphasized system structural optimization and activation of internal soil processes. Compared with the high-input yield-maintaining pathway of TWM, CI maintained relatively high yield and better soil functioning under lower water and fertilizer inputs. Therefore, CI can serve as an important alternative ecological intensification strategy for groundwater-overexploited areas of the North China Plain. Nevertheless, this study still has some limitations. First, it was mainly based on soil nutrients, enzyme activities, water dynamics, and yield indicators, and did not directly measure microbial community structure or functional genes. Therefore, the microbial mechanisms underlying changes in soil biological processes remain unclear. Second, this study did not systematically evaluate the economic benefits, mechanization adaptability, or farmer acceptance of CI. Future studies should combine long-term field experiments, microbiome analysis, cost–benefit evaluation, and regional demonstration to further clarify the applicability of CI under different soil types, groundwater restriction intensities, and farm management conditions.

Conclusions

The field experiment quantified the synergies and trade-offs among productivity, resource use, and soil health in groundwater-overexploited areas. Compared with WM, which mainly relied on reducing water and fertilizer inputs, CI redesigned the spatial and temporal crop configuration through white clover coverage, wheat strip planting, and late-stage relay intercropping of maize. This system improved soil moisture conditions in the wheat strips and reduced water consumption in the clover strips during the wheat season by 26.2%, 19.3%, and 16.9% compared with TWM, WM, and CM, respectively. CI also enhanced soil nutrient availability and biochemical activity, as indicated by higher contents of soil alkali-hydrolyzable nitrogen, available phosphorus, available potassium, and organic carbon in CI-C, as well as higher soil multifunctionality in both CI-C and CI-M, whereas WM consistently showed the lowest soil multifunctionality. The five-year yield analysis further showed that WM markedly reduced wheat and maize yields relative to TWM, while CI exhibited a clear advantage in the maize season. The average maize yield under CI was 9.7%, 23.6%, and 16.0% higher than that under TWM, WM, and CM, respectively, and its annual yield reached 18.2 Mg ha−1, which was comparable to that of TWM. RDA further indicated that soil–water variables, such as SOC, SAP, SAN, SSC, SUE, and ET, jointly contributed to yield formation. Overall, CI maintained crop productivity not by relying on high water and fertilizer inputs, but by promoting crop niche complementarity, soil nutrient activation, and the spatiotemporal redistribution of water use. Therefore, CI represents a promising ecological intensification strategy for winter wheat–summer maize systems in groundwater-overexploited areas of the North China Plain. Future studies should combine long-term field experiments, microbial mechanism analysis, cost–benefit assessments, and evaluations of mechanization adaptability to clarify its regional applicability and extension potential.
