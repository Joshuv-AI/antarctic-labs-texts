Melody, Harmony, and Discord: A Complication During Pulmonic Valve Intervention in a Patient With Tetralogy of Fallot
Authors: Quinn J Carbol, Pearl M Huynh, Aaron Kao, Ross Hanson, Timothy R Petersen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Patients born with tetralogy of Fallot (ToF) require early corrective surgery, often involving staged procedures and palliative shunts. Interventions may lead to complications as patients reach maturity, which causes them to become candidates for different treatments based on their specific anatomy, physiology, and the evolving structural heart technologies. Especially in advanced percutaneous interventions, each patient’s unique physiology coupled with procedure-specific considerations makes perioperative planning a key factor in successful treatment.We present a 17-year-old male with a history of ToF who underwent valve-sparing surgical correction as an infant. Follow-up through his teenage years revealed worsening pulmonic insufficiency and right ventricular (RV) function necessitating pulmonic valve replacement. The recently approved Medtronic Harmony valve provided a well-suited percutaneous option.Pre-operative anesthetic planning included consideration of the impact of his RV function as well as complication contingency planning. Under general anesthesia, the Harmony valve was deployed but soon after embolized into the RV. This required emergent open surgical device retrieval and placement of an RV-pulmonic artery conduit with a homograft.Percutaneous therapies are appealing options, and the available technology is changing rapidly. Though exciting, these treatment options are not without complications. A Harmony valve embolizing to the RV after deployment is one of these very rare complications, but it enforces the salient point that contingency planning is absolutely necessary prior to attempting these interventions. Though the framework for this planning may be well established in some facilities, it becomes much more complicated in lower volume centers.

Introduction

The most common cyanotic congenital heart disease, tetralogy of Fallot (ToF), occurs in approximately 1 in 3,000 births [1]. Increasingly understood to be a family of diseases sharing similar intracardiac anatomy, patients born with ToF require complex corrective surgery early in life. Frequently requiring staged procedures and early palliative shunts, surgical management of ToF may lead to different complications as patients reach maturity. As subsequent complications develop, patients become candidates for different treatments based on their specific anatomy, physiology, surgical history, and rapidly evolving structural heart technologies. In this patient population, worsening right ventricular (RV) function, pulmonary artery dilation, and pulmonic insufficiency (PI) herald the need for further intervention on the pulmonic valve. Percutaneous options are appealing as they avoid additional sternotomy incisions and speed recovery time. With increasingly advanced percutaneous interventions, each patient’s unique physiology coupled with procedure-specific considerations makes perioperative planning by the anesthesia team a key factor in successful treatment of teenage and adult ToF patients.

We present a complicated case of pulmonic valve replacement (PVR) in a teenager with surgically corrected ToF, with specific focus on the clinical course and considerations for perioperative anesthetic and multidisciplinary planning.

Case presentation

A 17-year-old male with a history of ToF was initially scheduled to undergo percutaneous PVR with a Medtronic Harmony transcatheter pulmonary valve in the setting of moderate to severe PI, progressive RV enlargement, and functional decline.

Following birth at 36 weeks' gestation, cyanosis prompted initial workup demonstrating a large sub-aortic perimembranous ventricular septal defect (VSD), mild to moderate RV outflow tract obstruction, a small patent ductus arteriosus with left-to-right shunting, a left-sided aortic arch, normal coronary arteries, and confluent branch pulmonary arteries of adequate size. The patient underwent an uneventful valve-sparing ToF repair with VSD patching at three months of age and was subsequently closely monitored by pediatric cardiology throughout childhood.

Serial cardiac MRIs at ages 12, 14, and 16 demonstrated progressive RV enlargement and functional impairment in the setting of moderate PI, and the patient began preoperative evaluation for PVR at age 16. Right-heart catheterization demonstrated mildly elevated RV pressures with normal pulmonary vascular resistance, and the pulmonic valve annulus was measured at 24-26 mm by balloon sizing.

After extensive risk-benefit discussions, the patient and his parents chose to pursue temporization with a trans-catheter bioprosthetic valve with plans for definitive surgical intervention at a later date. Due to the nature of his previous operation and his enlarged pulmonary valve annulus, he was deemed not a good candidate for the Medtronic Melody valve, leaving his only option the Edwards SAPIEN valve at the time of initial PVR workup. As the patient was asymptomatic, catheterization lab RV end diastolic pressures were not alarming, and there was concern of causing coronary compression with the Edwards SAPIEN valve; intervention was then deferred.

The FDA approval of the Medtronic Harmony valve in 2023 provided a new percutaneous option. Following FDA approval, the patient underwent Harmony valve candidacy evaluation at age 17. Cardiac MRI at this time, with the images included in Figures 1, 2, revealed an RV ejection fraction of 38%, worsened RV end-diastolic volume, and pulmonary artery dilation. Transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) findings were consistent with the MRI, confirming severe RV dilation and moderate to severe PI. This workup determined that the patient had favorable anatomy for Harmony valve intervention, and a procedure date was scheduled.

After extensive preoperative planning, the case proceeded in the cath lab with surgical backup. Induction of general anesthesia was uneventful with lidocaine, fentanyl, and propofol, and paralysis was achieved with rocuronium. Following induction, a 9 French MAC central line was placed under ultrasound guidance as well as a femoral arterial line by the procedural team. Venous cannulation for device deployment was performed without issue and the Harmony valve was deployed in the RV outflow tract under fluoroscopic and trans-esophageal echocardiography (TEE) guidance. Shortly after deployment, the valve apparatus became malpositioned and embolized into the RV, where the self-expanding device filled the available space in the RV with mechanical compromise of RV contraction and herniation through the tricuspid valve, causing severe tricuspid regurgitation. Device embolization appeared to be due to positioning fractionally too inferior in the RV outflow tract, as sizing and anatomic suitability were carefully analyzed prior to the procedure. Fortunately, this complication was able to be seen in real time under fluoroscopic and TEE guidance. As the complication was happening, expected hypotension from impaired RV contraction and severe tricuspid regurgitation ensued. Filling pressures at the time of device embolization are unavailable. Hemodynamic instability was managed with vasopressors and aggressive volume resuscitation to promote passive RV cardiac output, and the decision was quickly made to proceed to the operating room for device retrieval and open surgical intervention.

All lines were left in place, including the right femoral vein sheath with guidewire left traversing the expanded Harmony device. The patient was transported under general anesthesia from the catheterization lab to the operating room. A radial arterial line was placed, a TEE probe was inserted, and open PVR proceeded via redo sternotomy. The right atrium was opened, and the Harmony device was found to be filling the RV as seen on TEE in Figures 3, 4, herniating through the tricuspid valve, and wrapped in the chordal structures of the tricuspid valve. It remained snared in this position by the deployment wires, which were then removed through the groin. The device was then able to be carefully removed through the atrium without damage to the tricuspid valve. A 5 mm patent foramen ovale was identified and closed, followed by replacement of the pulmonic valve with a 27 mm homograft in an RV-pulmonary artery conduit. Cardiopulmonary bypass was discontinued exactly one hour after initiation, and RV and pulmonary artery pressures were measured and both found to be 31 mmHg. Coming off bypass, the patient required a low-dose epinephrine drip as well as intermittent nitroglycerin boluses and infusion but was off all vasoactive medications when leaving the operating room.

The patient was transported to the ICU, intubated and recovered uneventfully with successful discharge home on post-operative day 4. He resumed close outpatient cardiology follow-up after the procedure and post-operative visits over the three months after discharge noted that he was doing well, EKG was unchanged from pre-operative baseline, and transthoracic echocardiography imaging demonstrated signs of expected RV remodeling and no evidence of pulmonic stenosis or pathological insufficiency. The patient was able to start college classes in the fall as planned after undergoing this procedure in the spring.

Discussion

Tetralogy of Fallot pathology and prevalence

ToF is a congenital heart defect characterized by four anatomical derangements: VSD (most frequently perimembranous), overriding aorta, RV outflow tract obstruction, and RV hypertrophy. The RV outflow tract obstruction is often characterized by a hypoplastic pulmonic valve annulus and a stenotic or dysplastic valve. A right-sided aortic arch is also present in 20-25% of cases. Clinical presentation and pathophysiology are variable due to the possibility of associated cardiac abnormalities such as aberrant pulmonary vasculature, anomalous coronary arteries, atrioventricular septal defects, and aortic incompetence. ToF is due to genetic dysregulation during embryological development; however, this dysregulation is often isolated, as 75-80% of cases are non-syndromic. In syndromic cases, the most common associations are with trisomy 21 or 22q11.2 deletion syndromes. ToF is the most common cyanotic congenital heart disease, has an equal sex distribution, and presents in approximately 1 in 3,000 births [1]. In uncomplicated ToF, the survival rate without surgical correction is 40% at three years and decreases to 11% at 20 years. However, therapeutic advances have allowed for increased prevalence of patients with congenital heart disease surviving into adulthood [2]. With appropriate surgical repair, 20-year survival is estimated to be around 90% [3].

Clinical implications of varying ToF presentation

One of the early determining factors of clinical severity is the degree of RV outflow tract obstruction. Significant RV outflow tract obstructions will direct blood flow towards the aorta, bypassing the pulmonary circulation, and lead to significant cyanosis. Defects with minimal RV outflow tract obstruction in which RV outflow tract resistance remains less than systemic resistance will preserve adequate flow to the pulmonary bed, and these patients may be acyanotic [2]. As a result, maintaining ductus arteriosus patency or performing early surgical correction has varying significance and urgency. In cases of severe cyanotic disease, ductus patency is maintained with prostaglandin therapy to bridge to procedural correction [4]. Uncorrected ToF may present with “tet spells” characterized by episodes of severe cyanosis requiring aggressive intervention. During these “tet spells,” children often resort to a body position that increases systemic vascular resistance and allows a greater proportion of blood flow to move through pulmonary circulation, relieving cyanosis, such as knees to chest. Medical interventions that achieve the same physiologic consequence of decreasing pulmonary vascular resistance include increasing FiO2 and decreasing CO2 tension. Beta blockers and fluid boluses may aid RV filling. Phenylephrine therapy will increase systemic vascular resistance, allowing for a relatively lower pulmonary vascular resistance, which increases the proportion of flow directed to the pulmonary circulation. All these interventions must be considered in the context of the individual patient’s pathophysiology and balanced with the need to maintain systemic flow and organ perfusion.

ToF treatment considerations

The primary concern of procedural intervention is to restore and maintain adequate pulmonary blood flow. Timing and options to achieve this end are debated and dependent on individual pathophysiology. Severe anoxemia is the primary indication for infantile intervention, and a sequela of prolonged hypoxemia - severe polycythemia - becomes the primary indication for intervention in later infancy and early childhood [5]. Valve-sparing or replacing surgical repairs involve closing the VSD and relieving the RV outflow tract obstruction, which may be through a transatrial or transventricular approach. Other options for intervention include modified Blalock-Taussig shunting, stenting of the ductus arteriosus, stenting of the RV outflow tract, and pulmonary artery banding. During surgical closure of the VSD, care is taken to avoid the Bundle of His in order to avoid lifelong conduction abnormalities. The transatrial approach is gaining popularity as this approach confers the benefit of limiting transannular incisions [6]. An atrial septal defect is often preserved or created to allow for right atrial decompression in cases where RV compliance is a concern. An RV-pulmonary artery conduit is chosen in cases of severely hypoplastic RV outflow tract, significant conal branch coronary artery crossing the RV outflow tract, or cases requiring reoperation due to severe PI or stenosis, as in our case presented here [7]. Vigilance is advised for warning signs of complications, including electrophysiologic abnormalities, evidence of fibrosis on MRI, RV ejection fraction less than or equal to 35%, and rising B-type natriuretic peptide. In 2023, Bokma et al. found that in patients with repaired ToF, PVR when clinically indicated-as evidenced by the above criteria-had a lower risk of composite endpoint of death or sustained ventricular tachycardia [8].

Valve-sparing vs transannular patch repairs for ToF

PVR procedures are rare in early ToF repair as they will necessitate re-operation and valve replacement during childhood as the heart develops and grows to adult size. The most common options for intervening on RV outflow tract obstructions and RV hypertrophy are valve-sparing and transannular patch procedures, each with unique long-term consequences. Though the discussion is more involved, the primary concern of valve-sparing repair is residual and worsening stenosis as obstructive tissue may remain or the repaired tissue scars while the heart continues to grow throughout childhood. Conversely, the primary concern of transannular patch repair is the introduction of PI, which will likely lead to chronic RV overload and dilation and require re-intervention at regular intervals. A 2021 study published in JAMA involving a cohort of 528 patients has provided data that valve-sparing repair may be a more favorable approach, as 30-year survival rates are improved versus trans-annular patching even if moderate residual stenosis remains [9]. A 2022 study provides an additional layer of nuance to this discussion. In a study group of 71 patients, Siddiqi et al. found it reasonable to convert from a valve-sparing to transannular patch repair if the pulmonic pressure gradient is greater than 45 mmHg or RV pressure is higher than half of systemic pressure intraoperatively, as the risk of pulmonic valve reintervention is significantly higher when these parameters are met [10]. Available records indicate that the patient in this case underwent valve-sparing repair with VSD patch closure as an infant but developed the common sequelae of a trans-annular patch repair. Potentially providing clarity to this incongruence is the diagnosis of previous valvotomy in the cardiac surgery operative report from the day of the described operation, not mentioned in the records elsewhere. Unfortunately, we were unable to obtain further records to clarify these details. Regardless of the nature of the intervention that led to the presentation, at age 17 this patient had significant PI, an extremely large pulmonic annulus, and was progressing toward RV failure and other feared complications necessitating intervention. Complicating the considerations for how to address the issue at hand was the fact that the patient and his family were understandably averse to surgery if another reasonable intervention was available, and the available data for the Harmony valve suggest that a good transcatheter intervention does exist.

PV treatment options

The first transcatheter PVR was performed by Dr. Bonhoeffer in 2000, in which he implanted a Medtronic Melody valve [11]. Since that time, multiple devices have been developed or are currently in the process of development and approval, including Edwards SAPIEN Transcatheter Heart Valve, Medtronic Harmony Valve, Venus P Valve, Edwards Alterra Adaptive Prestent, Pulsta Valve, and Med-Zenith PT-Valve. The only FDA-approved options for the patient presented here were the Medtronic Melody and Harmony valves and the Edwards SAPIEN valve. The SAPIEN valve is specifically noted to have the risk of coronary compression, and thus coronary compression testing is routinely performed. The Melody valve is approved for PVR in RV-pulmonary artery conduits or surgical bioprosthetic valves. Though not included in initial FDA approval, many centers are implanting this valve into native or patched RV outflow tracts with prestenting [12]. The Harmony valve received FDA approval in 2023 as the first self-expanding valve designed specifically for patients who have regurgitation in native or patched RV outflow tracts, and initial mortality, arrhythmia, and infection data from this valve have been very promising. However, there remains a concern that balloon-expandable and self-expanding pulmonary valves may trigger ventricular tachycardia as well as make ablation procedures challenging due to device interaction with the muscular RV outflow tract, which covers the critical isthmus for the ablation procedure [11,12].

Anatomic suitability for the Harmony valve is determined through a screening report and perimeter plot that accounts for the perimeter-derived radius of the RV outflow tract and projects appropriate device oversizing. As this valve is intended to be implanted into previously intervened-upon RV outflow tracts, the anatomy is expected to be abnormal. However, some anatomies are unsuitable, and thus the length of the RV outflow tract, presence/precise location of a pinch point, and geometry of the tract may make Harmony valve placement more tenuous. In a 2024 study, McElhinney et al. published data suggesting that Harmony valve placement may still be suitable in perceived unfavorable RV outflow tract anatomies [13].

Core valve vs Harmony valve

The concept of self-expanding percutaneous valve replacement is not novel in the Harmony valve. Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) gained FDA approval in the narrow clinical indication of patients with aortic stenosis in 2011, deemed to be too high risk for open surgical replacement and since that time has gained additional approvals as well as further data on long-term consequences. The Medtronic CoreValve (and its successors) is one option on the market for TAVR and, like the Harmony valve, is a bioprosthetic self-expanding valve. Improvements are continuously being made in each generation of this valve technology, but self-expanding aortic valves have been linked to an increased incidence of paravalvular leak as compared to balloon-expanding valves, though they are easier to reposition intraoperatively if necessary. Paravalvular leak is likely not as significant a concern for self-expanding pulmonic valves as the right heart is a lower pressure system, but a different concern in the TAVR literature may be significant. From the start of TAVR programs, device embolization was a concern, and it was quickly discovered that the presence of significant amounts of calcium on the aortic valve/annulus provided a better environment for the device to anchor and remain anchored. In the older adult population in which TAVR interventions are generally pursued, calcific disease is a common occurrence. Extrapolating this finding to the transcatheter pulmonic valve intervention is not necessarily easy to do, but the possibility exists that a pulmonic valve or RV-pulmonary artery conduit may have a better chance of remaining in its intended position if calcific disease is present. As these transcatheter pulmonic valve interventions are more likely to happen in clinical circumstances such as those presented here, it is unlikely that severe calcific disease will be present [14]. To this point, long-term data regarding the likelihood of device migration or expected timeframes for reintervention are lacking.

Thorough pre-operative planning

Though device complications with transcatheter pulmonic valve technologies are rare, mechanical complications due to interaction of wires or inferior positioning-both of which were suspected here-may lead to emergent life-threatening complications which require escalation of care and open surgical intervention. While these issues may be due to precise technical aspects of the procedure, they lead to a broader conversation on operative planning. In high-volume centers, the interventional pediatric cardiologist and pediatric cardiothoracic surgeon are almost certainly operating in the same system and likely to be in the same physical facility when interventions are taking place. Hybrid operating suites with percutaneous technologies in the same physical space as capabilities to perform open surgical interventions may be commonplace. However, in the setting of lower volume centers such as ours, the interventional cardiologist and cardiothoracic surgeon may not be operating in the same system, let alone be in the same physical facility, and the catheterization lab may be physically separated from the operating space where surgeons are comfortable operating and staff can access equipment. A dismissive reply to these issues may be to simply say to build hybrid operating rooms and bring the providers under the same umbrella, but in some situations, this is not feasible. It also may not be feasible for patients who rely on a safety net hospital to travel far out of state for interventions that can potentially be offered at home. Thus, the issue arises: where do providers and systems draw the line for what facilities and personnel are required to safely and effectively pursue an intervention such as percutaneous valve replacement in a pediatric patient? In this case, the adult cardiothoracic surgeon was immediately available should a complication arise, and pediatric cardiothoracic surgery was emergently credentialed to be on stand-by status to help if necessary. This proved to be apropos in our case, and preoperative contingency planning led to expeditious transfer to a prepared operating room for definitive surgical correction.

Post-operative care also requires careful consideration. At our institution, intensive care staff are only trained to handle cardiac surgery recovery care issues in the adult units, making the adult cardiothoracic care unit a much more suitable location for postoperative recovery than the pediatric intensive care unit for this patient. A thorough discussion was held pre-operatively to ensure that all care team members were aware of this plan and had the required resources to ensure proper care.

Conclusions

Given the prevalence of ToF, it is likely that the clinician will encounter one or more patients with this pathophysiology in their career. An understanding of the underlying disease process as well as the early corrective procedural options provides a framework to work from when taking care of these patients. Depending on the type of early procedural correction-most likely either valve sparing or transannular patching-residual RV outflow tract obstruction or progressive PI and RV dysfunction may be expected. It may not always be obvious which intervention a patient has undergone, and complications may not always follow the expected trajectory. The landscape of technologies to address these problems is rapidly developing and provides exciting options that patients once did not have. If faced with the prospect of caring for a patient undergoing transcatheter intervention on the pulmonic valve, thorough preoperative planning is essential to ensure a successful outcome should a complication arise. This becomes much more complicated in low-volume and potentially discordant healthcare delivery systems. However, even in the event of the most serious complications, effective planning and care will allow these patients to overcome these obstacles.
