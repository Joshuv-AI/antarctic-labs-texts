Evidence-Based Assessment of the Role of Pharyngeal Gonorrhea and Commensal Neisseria Species in the Emergence of Antimicrobial Resistance in Neisseria Gonorrhoeae: Data Gaps and Future Research
Authors: Amber D Kimble, Yukari C Manabe, Johan H Melendez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAntimicrobial resistance in Neisseria gonorrhoeae (NG) represents a global public health threat. NG has progressively developed resistance to current and previously recommended antibiotics, including the currently recommended treatment, ceftriaxone. Pharyngeal NG infections can contribute to the sustained transmission of gonorrhea as pharyngeal infections are often asymptomatic, can persists, and remain undiagnosed. Pharyngeal NG infections play a major role in the emergence of antimicrobial resistance in NG as horizontal gene transfer of resistance markers is common between NG and commensal Neisseria species which colonize the oropharynx. We review the evidence on the contribution of pharyngeal NG infections and commensal Neisseria species to the emergence of antimicrobial resistance in NG, outline research gaps, and propose potential research topics. Improved understanding of pharyngeal NG pathogenesis and how NG acquires antimicrobial resistance markers through horizontal gene transfer from commensal Neisseria species is critical to curtail the rapid evolution of antimicrobial resistance in NG and preserve the clinical utility of currently available and future antimicrobials.Keywords: Neisseria gonorrhoeae, pharyngeal Neisseria gonorrhoeae, antimicrobial resistance, commensal Neisseria species, horizontal gene transfer

Gonorrhea, the second most commonly reported bacterial sexually transmitted infection (STI) with an estimated global incidence of 82.4 million cases in 2020 [1], increases HIV transmission risk and can lead to long-term sequalae, especially in women [2]. Neisseria gonorrhoeae (NG) can infect the urogenital tract, rectum, and oropharynx [2]. Although, routine testing of extragenital sites is increasing, testing at these sites remains inadequate when compared with urogenital sites [3]. Notably, pharyngeal NG (pNG) infections often remain undiagnosed as they are primarily asymptomatic [4] and are more difficult to treat and prone to treatment failures than urogenital NG infections [2, 5, 6].

NG has developed antimicrobial resistance (AMR) to penicillin, fluoroquinolones, macrolides, and extended spectrum cephalosporins [2]. Treatment options for gonorrhea are limited, with ceftriaxone as the only recommended first-line treatment [6]. However, NG ceftriaxone resistance has been reported globally and is rapidly increasing in the Asia-Pacific region [7] and increasing in England [8].

NG, a naturally competent organism, can acquire AMR through acquisition of resistance determinants from closely related species. For example, the ceftriaxone-resistant NG FC428 clone, isolated in Japan in 2015, harbored a mosaic penA allele [9], which was acquired via transformation with the commensal Neisseria species (CNsp) Neisseria subflava [10]. Previous studies have shown that oropharyngeal CNsp can function as a reservoir of AMR genes, which can be acquired by pathogenic Neisseria species [10–12]. Taken together, these findings suggest that pNG infections, which can persist [13], and CNsp play a critical role in the emergence of AMR in NG.

As noted by another review, CNsp are “a bubbling cauldron of adaptive solutions for pathogenic Neisseria” [12] and as such, are essential for gaining insights into the current and future patterns of gonococcal resistance. Here, we provide a distinct perspective on the importance and intersection of pNG infections and CNsp by reviewing what is known about pNG infections, describing the evidence linking the emergence of AMR in NG to recombination events with CNsp, and discussing how new antimicrobials might be affected by CNsp-derived AMR mechanisms. Lastly, we highlight research gaps and offer our perspective on potential research topics on pNG infections and CNsp with the goal of preserving currently available and future antimicrobials.

UNVEILING THE HIDDEN PATHOGENESIS OF THE PHARYNX AND PNG: OUTLINING THE KEY PATHWAYS TO PERSISTENCE AND RESISTANCE

Pharyngeal NG infections are hypothesized to play a major role in the sustained transmission of gonorrhea as they can have a prolonged duration of infection and antimicrobials are less effective at the pharynx than at other anatomical sites [4–6, 13, 14]. Epidemiological and modelling studies suggest a central role for the oropharynx in transmitting gonorrhea to, or acquiring gonorrhea from, the partner's oropharynx, penis, or anorectum through either direct contact or via saliva [14–16]. In contrast, others have questioned the efficiency of transmission of pharyngeal gonorrhea to other anatomical sites and the population-level impact of transmission from an infected oropharynx [17]. Regardless of its contribution to population-level transmission, the oropharynx represents an ecologically important niche where NG and CNsp coexist, interact, and exchange genetic material, with implications for AMR evolution [4, 12]. Here we discuss three key aspects of the pharynx and pNG infections to highlight the importance of pharyngeal infections in the emergence of AMR in NG.

A. Asymptomatic or Lack of Specific Symptoms for Pharyngeal Gonorrhea

A prospective study (the EXPLORE study) of men who have sex with men (MSM) found that pharyngeal gonorrhea was asymptomatic in 92% of cases [18]. A study assessing the duration of pNG infections found that only 26% of participants reported a sore throat during the first 2 weeks of infection [13]. A retrospective study at an STI outpatient center found that 92.2% of patients with PCR-confirmed pNG were asymptomatic [19]. Because the oropharynx is susceptible to infections, such as streptococcal pharyngitis, the symptoms of pharyngeal gonorrhea can be nonspecific making it difficult to identify individuals who should be tested for pNG. In the EXPLORE study, having a sore throat before each visit was not consistently associated with a positive pNG test [18]. Similarly, Barbee et al. reported that having a sore throat was not associated with a positive nucleic acid amplification test (NAAT) for pNG [13].

B. Limited Pharmacokinetic Activity in Oropharyngeal Tissue

A systematic review of >16 000 NG infections reported that cure rates at the oropharynx were significantly lower than at other sites for both males (79.2%) and females (83.7%) [20]. A review of the first 11 cases of verified ceftriaxone treatment failures reported that all were pharyngeal infections and that most were caused by ceftriaxone-susceptible NG [21]. A meta-analysis of NG treatment failures showed that pNG infections accounted for 67.6% of ceftriaxone treatment failures [22]. Treatment failures of pharyngeal gonorrhea with dual-therapy combinations have also been reported [22]. The challenge in treating pNG infections may be related to the inability of antimicrobials to achieve high extracellular concentrations in tonsillar and oropharyngeal tissue [23]. The concentrations of β-lactams in the pharynx are lower than 10% of serum concentrations [24]; in saliva, the concentration of cefixime is 5-fold lower than serum levels [25]. An open-label, nonrandomized, phase I study of cefixime in healthy individuals found no cefixime in pharyngeal fluid after multiple doses of cefixime at increasing concentrations [26]. The low concentration of drug in the pharynx highlights the uncertainty of pNG infection cure [23].

C. The Oropharyngeal Microbiome is a Reservoir for Antimicrobial-Resistant CNsp

The oropharyngeal microbiome is the second-largest microbiome in the human body composed of an array of microbial communities, including the CNsp, which play an essential role for immune function and health [27]. CNsp generally do not cause disease or infection and contribute to protection against harmful bacteria, even demonstrating competitive inhibition of NG [28]. However, CNsp are also reservoirs for AMR genes that can be transferred to their pathogenic Neisseria counterparts [11, 12]. The pharynx provides a unique niche for NG to acquire AMR as NG can acquire resistance determinants through horizontal gene transfer (HGT) from CNsp [12, 23, 29]. There is increasing evidence (later discussed in this review) that NG resistance to various antimicrobials was mediated through acquisition of AMR markers from CNsp [2, 29]. As shown in Figure 1, oropharyngeal CNsp can acquire AMR through bystander selection [30], the pharynx can serve as a reservoir of resistance determinants which can then be transferred to NG, leading to the emergence of antimicrobial-resistant NG [12]. The risk associated with the CNsp-derived AMR emergence mechanism is heightened by the asymptomatic nature of pNG infections, which allows for antimicrobial-resistant NG to circulate undiagnosed [31]. Even when pNG is diagnosed, treatment may not eradicate the infection, thereby facilitating the dissemination of antimicrobial-resistant NG through sexual networks [31].

CNsp are common in the oropharynx (Table 1). In a study of healthy participants, 86% had at least one CNsp, with 66% colonized by multiple CNsp; N. subflava was isolated from 74% of participants [11]. Similarly, a study from India found that N. flava/subflava/perflava was the most frequently isolated oropharyngeal CNsp [32]. A 5-month continuous assessment of healthy participants found that N. subflava, N. mucosa, and N. oralis were the most prevalent oropharyngeal CNsp over time [33]. The studies by Miari et al. and Sunil et al. also reported that 5% and 13.9% of CNsp were resistant to ceftriaxone, respectively [11, 32]. Decreased cefixime susceptibility was first reported in a 2007 study of N. subflava isolated from the oropharynx of Japanese men and female commercial sex workers [34].

As pNG infections are common in MSM, considerable effort has been made to characterize the profiles of oropharyngeal CNsp in this population. A study of 207 MSM from Vietnam reported that N. flavescens and N. subflava were the most commonly isolated CNsp and that 93%, 31%, and 28% of CNsp displayed reduced susceptibility or resistance to ciprofloxacin, cefixime, and ceftriaxone, respectively [35]. Additionally, antibiotic use in the past month was strongly associated with increased cefixime and ceftriaxone minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) when compared with participants who had used antibiotics one to six months prior to study enrollment [35]. Similarly, a Belgian study [36] reported that the ceftriaxone MICs of the most commonly isolated CNsp, N. subflava, were considerably higher (range, 0.023–2.0 mg/L) than the MICs (range, 0.001–0.12 mg/L) reported by a previous study [34]. A follow-up study of Neisseria species isolated from MSM and the general population reported that the CNsp from MSM were less susceptible to ceftriaxone and ciprofloxacin than the CNsp from the general population and that CNsp had higher ceftriaxone, azithromycin, and ciprofloxacin MICs than the pathogenic Neisseria species [37]. Higher azithromycin MICs were also reported for the CNsp of MSM when compared with the general population in a study from Italy [38]. A study of men on HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) found that 85% of isolated CNsp were resistant (MIC ≥ 1 µg/mL) to azithromycin, including 25% with high-level resistance (MIC ≥ 256 µg/mL) [39]. Additionally, 6% and 54% of CNsp were resistant to ceftriaxone and doxycycline, respectively [39]. A cross-sectional metagenomic-based analysis showed that the oropharynx of MSM on PrEP is a reservoir enriched with AMR genes [40].

ASSESSING THE EVIDENCE OF HGT BETWEEN CNSP AND NG

The emergence of ceftriaxone-resistant NG harboring mosaic penA alleles has highlighted the importance of CNsp as reservoirs of AMR markers which through HGT can be acquired by NG [9, 10, 12]. HGT plays an important role in the microevolution of Neisseria species dating back to the acquisition of sulfonamide resistance in N. meningitidis which was acquired via natural transformation from a CNsp [12]. NG penicillin resistance emerged from the acquisition of modified penA alleles from CNsp [41]. Phenotypic and genomic characterization of antimicrobial-resistant CNsp demonstrated that mutations associated with NG resistance to fluoroquinolones, penicillin, and tetracycline are also present in CNsp [42]. A. ESC

The oral ESC cefixime gained widespread adoption in the late 1990s and 2000s but was discontinued due to increasing resistance and treatment failures [2]. A study of NG isolates from men who failed cefixime treatment showed that the NG penA gene displayed a mosaic-like structure, with regions similar to the penA from CNsp [43]. Furthermore, the study demonstrated that cefixime-susceptible NG could be transformed to cefixime-resistant using penA DNA from a cefixime-resistant CNsp [43].

Transformation studies using mosaic penA DNA from the CNsp N. subflava and N. cinerea, identical or closely related to that of the FC428 clone, demonstrated that acquisition of the mosaic penA allele by NG results in ceftriaxone resistance [10, 44]. Genomic analyses showed that the penA gene of ceftriaxone-resistant N. subflava strains exhibited a high degree of sequence homology (89.8%–100%) to the mosaic penA allele of the FC428 clone [10]. A follow-up study showed that all N. subflava isolates included in the analysis contained penA FC428-like sequences and found a high degree of homology between publicly available CNsp penA sequences and FC428-like penA sequences [45]. In silico analyses of 1700 recombinant penA alleles from 35 513 Neisseria isolates, comprising 15 different CNsp, showed that N. subflava and N. cinerea were the most common sources of recombinant sequences in NG penA [46]. Collectively, these results suggest that FC428-like penA sequences are common in CNsp and that the mosaic penA alleles in ceftriaxone-resistant NG originated from CNsp penA sequences.

B. Macrolides

The macrolide azithromycin was used as part of dual-therapy with ceftriaxone, but due to increasing resistance, dual treatment is no longer widely recommended [47]. Previous studies have reported an association between resistances to azithromycin and mosaic multiple transferable resistance (mtr) efflux pump alleles in NG [2]. A study aimed at assessing the boundaries of HGT at mtrRCDE (MtrC-MtrD-MtrE cell envelope proteins, which export antimicrobials) found that the entire mtr region is a hot spot of interspecies recombination which has introduced multiple mosaic alleles, likely from N. meningitidis and N. lactamica, into NG [48]. A study characterizing NG isolates with decreased susceptibility to azithromycin reported a NG mosaic mtr efflux pump locus which had 95% sequence homology with the mtr locus of the N. polysaccharea [49]. Comparative genomic analyses from 11 644 NG and 15 CNsp revealed evidence of HGT in the NG 50S ribosomal genes rplB, rplD, and rplY and that the mutations in the ribosomal proteins originated from CNsp [50]. Frost et al. showed that after 20 days of selection, decreased azithromycin susceptibility repeatedly emerged in N. cinerea and N. subflava, and that in almost all of the cases, resistance emergence converged on mutations within ribosomal genes or the mtrRCDE efflux pump [51]. Although this study did not show evidence of HGT between CNsp and NG, it demonstrated that azithromycin resistance in CNsp can emerge through pathways which are similar to those in NG, and that these mutations, as previously demonstrated [12], can be acquired by NG through HGT.

C. Fluoroquinolones

NG fluoroquinolone resistance is associated with amino acid changes affecting gyrA and parC [2]. A comprehensive genomic analysis of 20 047 globally sourced NG (1928–2020) and CNsp reported evidence of HGT in the NG quinolone resistance-determining regions (QRDR) of gyrA/B and parC/E [52]. In gyrA, recombination events of NG as the recombinant (recipient) were identified with N. cinerea and N. lactamica, and for parC, recombination events were reported with all Neisseria species [52]. Although there are no studies assessing HGT of fluoroquinolone-resistance genes between CNsp and NG, Gaspari et al. reported that 58% of CNsp in their study were ciprofloxacin-resistant [38].

ASSESSING THE THREAT OF CNSP-DERIVED RESISTANCE EMERGENCE IN NOVEL ANTIMICROBIALS

Two novel, oral antimicrobials, gepotidacin and zoliflodacin, received (December 2025) Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approval for urogenital gonorrhea [53]; the first approval of a new class of antibiotic for NG in over three decades. Gepotidacin [54], targets gyrA and parC while zoliflodacin, targets type II topoisomerase (gyrB) [55]. There are concerns, however, regarding their effectiveness for extragenital infections and a low barrier to resistance [55]. Phase 2 trial results showed that a single dose of zoliflodacin (2 grams) successfully only treated 50% of pharyngeal infections, compared with 96% of urogenital infections, and 100% of rectal infections [56]. Although studies have shown that it is difficult to induce NG zoliflodacin resistance [57], AMR can emerge through the CNsp-mediated resistance pathway. A study reported that zoliflodacin resistance could be induced in CNsp through serial passage and that resistance was associated with previously described mutations in gyrB (D429N and S467N) [58]. Furthermore, zoliflodacin-susceptible NG was transformed into zoliflodacin-resistant with the DNA from transformed CNsp [58]. This finding is concerning because N. subflava is one of the most prevalent CNsp in the oropharynx [33, 41], which may facilitate the acquisition of zoliflodacin resistance by this CNsp and subsequent transfer of resistance marker(s) to NG.

RESEARCH GAPS AND FUTURE RESEARCH

Given the controversy surrounding the role of pNG infections in gonorrhea transmission, it is critical to define what constitutes an actual infection, as NAATs cannot differentiate between infection and detection of residual nucleic acids [13]. While viability-based methods, which only detect viable NG, could help differentiate between these scenarios [59, 60], they are technically complex and not currently used for NG diagnosis. These tests, however, could be used to address critical questions regarding pNG infections. Additionally, a better understanding of what factor(s), other than poor drug penetration in pharyngeal tissue, contribute to persistence of these infections is warranted. For example, a review of the natural history of pNG infections showed that 19–42% of individuals with pharyngeal gonorrhea clear their infection between screening and treatment [61]. As such, identification of factor(s) (ie, host microbiome, immunological factors, etc.) is critical to understand pNG infections pathogenesis.

Experimental models could provide critical information on the pathogenesis of pNG infections [62]. Although an estradiol-treated mouse model is available for genital tract NG infection [63], such a model is currently not available for pNG infections, and if available, it could never fully represent the complex human oropharyngeal microbiome [20]. The advent of human organoid systems, however, provides an opportunity to re-create the architecture of human organs to study health and disease. A study used an in vitro human oropharyngeal epithelial cell model to investigate infection dynamics and evaluate treatment strategies and reported that NG was highest in tonsillar cells and that cefixime showed variable efficacy in clearing NG [64]. A review of 2D and 3D tissue models for NG research described how organoid-based systems can be used to study interactions between commensals and NG as well as host cell-pathogen interactions [65]. Further development of organoid systems could enable the introduction of the microbiome, including the oropharyngeal microbiome, thereby enabling exploration of HGT between CNsp and NG. Additionally, establishing controlled human infection model (CHIM) protocols could provide critical information on the natural history of pNG infections [62]. A male urethritis CHIM developed in the1980's has not reported serious adverse events [66]. The use of a pNG CHIM would allow for detailed microbiological assessments of pharyngeal infections, including how CNsp might affect NG colonization and infection. Additionally, as noted by Williams et al., the use of these models, combined with metagenomic sequencing of saliva and throat swabs, would allow for direct monitoring of HGT events between CNsp and NG under natural conditions [62].

Despite the extensive use of ceftriaxone, the rates of ceftriaxone-resistant NG in most countries remains low. The low rates of resistance might reflect the success of increasing dosing of ceftriaxone—initially at 125 mg and now up to 1 g in some countries [67]—which is preventing the spread of ceftriaxone-resistant strains, paralleling the historical experience with penicillin, where incremental dose escalation delayed—but did not prevent—the spread of resistance [2]. Alternatively, it is plausible that ceftriaxone resistance in NG driven by the acquisition of penA sequences from CNsp might not be an efficient process. An in-vitro transformation study using the penA gene from a ceftriaxone-resistant N. cinerea strain to transform NG estimated the transformation frequency as 1 in 107 recipient cells [44]. Additional studies are warranted to assess the frequency of transformation, including those using an oropharyngeal gonorrhea human infection model [62].

Doxycycline post-exposure prophylaxis (doxy-PEP) has emerged as an effective prevention strategy for bacterial STIs [68, 69] but has prompted concerns about the potential for AMR in NG [70, 71]. A modeling study reported that doxy-PEP would have minimal impact on extending the lifespan of ceftriaxone and that it would accelerate the spread of tetracycline resistance, ultimately resulting in the loss of clinical effectiveness of doxy-PEP for prevention of gonorrhea [70]. Another study from King County, Washington, USA, showed that tetracycline-resistant NG, including high-level resistance, rapidly increased between 2021 and 2024, and that tetracycline-resistant S. aureus and colonization with Group A Streptococcus were more common among doxy-PEP users [71]. Another study showed that doxycycline resistance in CNsp was significantly higher among participants who reported doxycycline use in the past 6 months, suggesting that doxy-PEP may also affect CNsp [72]. Notably, doxy-PEP may select for NG with dual resistance to tetracycline and ceftriaxone [73] and could accelerate the emergence of NG ceftriaxone resistance [74]. Despite these concerns, the impact of doxy-PEP on AMR is still unclear. As such, enhanced surveillance practices are warranted to monitor the evolution of AMR in NG in populations using doxy-PEP. Additionally, given the risk of AMR mediated by HGT between CNsp and NG, resistance trends among CNsp should also be monitored. Finally, additional studies are warranted to understand if and how doxy-PEP can induce resistance to other antimicrobials, especially ceftriaxone.

Given that the use of the new antibiotics zoliflodacin and gepotidacin will increase in the near future, selection pressure will likely select for resistance to either or both of these antimicrobials. As such, surveillance practices need to be enhanced globally to monitor antimicrobial susceptibility trends in NG to zoliflodacin and gepotidacin using phenotypic antimicrobial susceptibility testing. While molecular assays can reliably predict susceptibility/resistance to ciprofloxacin, they are not suitable for novel antimicrobials for which resistance mechanisms have not been fully elucidated [75]. Emerging approaches and technologies may enable faster, high-throughput assessment of AMR in NG to support enhanced surveillance practices. Additionally, as CNsp serve as a reservoir of AMR genes for NG, surveillance efforts need to be expanded to assess the evolution of AMR in CNsp, which could help to predict the future emergence of AMR in NG.

CONCLUSION

In this review, we highlighted the evidence supporting the role of pNG infections and CNsp in the emergence of AMR in NG. The implications of AMR for the future successful treatment of gonorrhea and for public health at large are significant and addressing the known, and unknown, at the intersection of pNG infections and CNsp-mediated AMR emergence is essential for preventing transmission of antimicrobial-resistant NG. A deeper understanding of the dynamics of pNG infections and how they contribute to emergence of AMR is also warranted to develop effective biomedical prevention strategies, which will be critical in managing the evolving landscape of AMR in NG, preserving the efficacy of current treatment options, and hopefully preventing emergence of resistance in novel antimicrobials.
