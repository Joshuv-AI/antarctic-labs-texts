Finite element biomechanical evaluation of a novel double-door screw-plate system for posterior cervical laminoplasty
Authors: Zhihao Kang, Kaibin Wang, Haipeng Si, Chongyi Wang, Yanwei Zhang, Wencan Zhang, Junfei Chen, Chen Liu, Yunze Feng, Wanlong Xu, Le Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo address the surgical challenges and postoperative implant displacement associated with conventional internal fixation techniques in posterior cervical double-door laminoplasty, we developed the double-door screw-plate system (DDSPS).PurposeThis study aimed to evaluate the biomechanical effects of DDSPS using finite element (FE) analysis.MethodsAn intact adult cervical spine FE model and three postoperative fixation models representing the DDSPS, the LA lamina staple, and the hydroxyapatite (HA) spacer construct were developed. Under a 75 N follower load and a 2.0 N·m moment applied in six loading directions, ROM, mean von Mises stress, implant-bone interface micromotion, and DDSPS stress distribution were evaluated.ResultsAcross the six loading directions, all three postoperative models showed a similar overall kinematic pattern, with the greatest increase in ROM occurring during flexion and only limited changes in the remaining directions. The DDSPS and LA lamina staple models demonstrated markedly lower implant-bone interface micromotion than the HA spacer model (DDSPS CSLIP1/2: 0.0008/0.0021 mm; LA lamina staple: 0.0020/0.0019 mm; HA spacer: 0.2506/0.1560 mm). In the DDSPS model, implant stress was mainly concentrated around the connecting axis, the plate surrounding the axial-side screw holes, and the axial-side fixation screws, with maximum stresses of 48.70 MPa in the plate and 67.09 MPa in the screw.ConclusionWithin the present finite element framework, the double-door screw-plate system (DDSPS) demonstrated a more favorable initial biomechanical profile, including lower interface micromotion and greater construct stability than the reference constructs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-026-09909-4.Keywords: Cervical spondylotic myelopathy, Posterior cervical double-door laminoplasty, Double-door screw-plate system, Internal fixation comparison, Biomechanical properties, von Mises stress, Finite element analysis

Introduction

Cervical spondylotic myelopathy (CSM)‌, a prevalent degenerative cervical spine disorder, primarily results from ‌cervical disc herniation (CDH)‌ and ‌ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament (OPLL)‌‌ [1, 2]. Surgical intervention demonstrates superior efficacy in restoring neurological function, alleviating symptoms, and improving quality of life, while maintaining lower complication rates than non-surgical alternatives [3, 4]. Early surgical intervention is strongly recommended following a confirmed CSM diagnosis, especially in patients with progressive neurological deficits [5].

Posterior cervical laminoplasty (PCL)‌, a widely used surgical treatment for CSM, ‌demonstrates significant clinical efficacy‌ [6–9], ‌primarily comprising two techniques: single-door laminoplasty and double-door laminoplasty [10–13]. Although open-door laminoplasty has been more commonly reported in many clinical series, double-door laminoplasty remains a relevant posterior decompression strategy in selected settings. From a biomechanical perspective, double-door laminoplasty creates a distinct reconstructive environment characterized by bilateral laminar opening and midline canal expansion, in which the maintenance of laminar position and fixation stability are critical [14]. An effective fixation technique for the laminae is critical to prevent postoperative reclosure. ‌The prevailing technique employs hydroxyapatite (HA) spacers combined with non-absorbable sutures [15–18]. However, during the early postoperative period, laminar stability relies entirely on suture fixation, thereby carrying inherent risks of HA spacer displacement and dislodgement [19, 20], which may potentially precipitate severe delayed dural tears [21, 22]. ‌The LA lamina staple (“LA” refers to the device designation used for this comparator) has emerged as a viable alternative fixation modality in recent years [23]. Nevertheless, its deployment entails a non-negligible intraoperative risk of laminar perforation that could potentially culminate in iatrogenic spinal cord injury. The screw-plate system is a well-established internal fixation construct in spinal surgery, particularly in anterior cervical procedures and posterior single-door laminoplasty [11]. However, its application in posterior double-door laminoplasty represents a novel approach.

To address these limitations, our team developed a novel double-door screw-plate system (DDSPS), the main innovations of which can be summarized in three aspects. First, it translates the classic screw-plate fixation principle to the specific requirements of the double-door technique. Compared with traditional fixation methods such as hydroxyapatite (HA) spacers, which rely on spacer placement and suture fixation, or the LA lamina staple, which resists reclosure through elastic engagement and dentation anchorage within the laminar bone, the DDSPS is designed to provide more rigid and direct fixation [19]. Second, screw fixation provides enhanced initial stability. Finite element analysis in this study showed lower implant-bone interface micromotion than that in the traditional flexible fixation constructs, suggesting a potentially more stable initial fixation and possible biomechanical advantages in the early postoperative period. Finally, from a surgical perspective, the DDSPS enables unilateral, sequential fixation. This allows the surgeon to first secure one side as a stable fulcrum before addressing the contralateral side, simplifying the surgical workflow, reducing technical challenges associated with simultaneous bilateral manipulation, aligning better with spine surgeons’ operative preferences, and potentially enhancing procedural controllability. Therefore, the DDSPS is not merely an application of an existing technology but represents a novel class of implant specifically engineered to overcome the limitations of both traditional laminoplasty techniques (sutures/HA spacers) and existing screw-plate systems, which are ill-suited to the posterior cervical architecture. Despite its clinical potential, biomechanical investigations of DDSPS remain scarce. Therefore, the primary aim of this study was to characterize the time-zero biomechanical behavior of the DDSPS in double-door laminoplasty using a cervical finite element model, with the LA lamina staple and HA spacer construct included as reference comparators to provide procedural context. The primary outcome measures included cervical range of motion (ROM), mean von Mises stress in relevant spinal and implant structures, and implant–bone interface micromotion, with additional analysis of DDSPS stress distribution.

Materials and methods

Construction of intact C2-T1 FE model

Figure 2 shows the FE model of the C2-T1 spine. The raw CT data were acquired from a healthy adult volunteer with written informed consent. All 221 DICOM-format CT images were imported into the medical image processing software Mimics (Materialize, Leuven, Belgium), where grayscale thresholds were adjusted to achieve precise bone contours. Following processing, the files were exported in STL format. The STL files were then imported into Geomagic Studio 12 (Geomagic, USA) for solid surface reconstruction. SolidWorks 2021 (Dassault Systèmes, USA) was utilized to model the endplate, facet cartilage, nucleus pulposus, annulus fibrosus and DDSPS. Hypermesh 14.0 (Altair Engineering, USA) was employed to generate the mesh for the solid model. Finally, the meshed FE models were analyzed in Abaqus 6.10 (Dassault Systèmes, France) after incorporating ligaments, applying boundary conditions, and setting computational parameters. The DDSPS system includes multiple plate-catch sizes, plate lengths, and screw lengths for different anatomical situations. For the present FE analysis, one representative plate configuration and one corresponding screw specification were selected for modeling. Figure 3 shows the engineering drawing and principal geometric specifications of the DDSPS plate used in the present FE model. The titanium plate measured 13.23 mm in length, 4.6 mm in width, and 11 mm in height, with a thickness of 1.0 mm and three fixation holes of 2.6 mm in diameter. The center-to-center distance between the two distal holes was 4.0 mm, and the distance from the proximal hole center to the distal paired-hole centerline was 7.8 mm. For the present FE model, the DDSPS screws had an outer diameter of 2.3 mm, a core diameter of 1.5 mm, a thread pitch of 1.0 mm, a thread depth of 0.4 mm, and a length of 7 mm.

Construction of surgical FE model

The three surgical configurations are visually presented in Figs. 4 and 5. Model A corresponds to the DDSPS, Model B to the LA lamina staple, and Model C to the HA spacer. The supraspinous ligament, interspinous ligament, and ligamentum flavum from C2 to C7 were digitally removed in all three postoperative models. Bilateral laminar troughs were created at the lamina–facet junctions with an approximate width of 3 mm. During trough creation, the outer cortical bone and part of the cancellous bone were removed, whereas the inner cortical layer was preserved or locally thinned to serve as the bilateral hinge. The laminae were opened symmetrically by approximately 35° relative to their preoperative position, with the bilateral troughs serving as the hinge axis, corresponding to a target opening distance of 10 mm at the midline opening level. The final opening configuration was kept identical across the three postoperative models. For spinous process handling, complete removal was performed in the DDSPS and LA lamina staple models, whereas the spinous process was preserved in the HA spacer model to maintain the geometric support required for spacer placement.

The posterior view of the surgical FE model of C2-T1 segments. A DDSPS; (B) LA lamina staple; (C) HA spacer

Top view of the surgical FE model of the C5 segment. A DDSPS; (B) LA lamina staple; (C) HA spacer

Three distinct fixation techniques were employed to stabilize the opened lamina. Model A incorporated a titanium alloy plate positioned unilaterally on the lamina, with three-point screw fixation. A single screw was placed within the medullary canal of the expanded lamina, whereas two additional screws were secured in the cortical bone layer of the axial lamina. In Model B, an LA lamina staple was placed on the bilateral lamina, and its dentations were inserted into the cancellous bone of the lamina. The staple was intended to maintain laminar opening through elastic clamping and dentation-based anchorage at the laminar interface. To eliminate areas of overlap between the screws and the lamina in Model A, as well as between the dentations and the lamina in Model B, Boolean calculations were performed at the screw-bone and dentation-bone interfaces [24]. This procedure established standardized time-zero implant-bone interface conditions for comparative FE analysis. In Model C, an HA spacer was placed into the gap between the laminae after opening. The HA spacer was connected between the two side laminae using multiple three-dimensional two-node truss units (T3D2) with a cross-sectional area of 1.77 mm². This representation was used as an effective time-zero connector model for the spacer-lamina linkage, rather than as a direct geometric reproduction of a clinical multi-strand suture construct.

Material properties

Table 1 presents the properties of all the elements used in this study [25–28]. Ligaments, nucleus pulposus, annulus fibrosus, endplate, posterior structures, articular cartilage, cancellous bone, and cortical bone were defined as isotropic linear elastic materials [29]. The measured thicknesses were 0.5 mm for the cartilage endplate and 1.0 mm for the cortical bone layer. Parameter settings included a 0.5 mm articular cartilage gap with a Coulomb friction coefficient of 0.3 [30]. Ligaments were characterized as T3D2, which are exclusively subjected to axial tension and not compression. The DDSPS, LA lamina staple, and HA spacer were meshed using linear tetrahedral elements (C3D4), which were selected to accommodate the geometric complexity of the cervical spine and implant constructs within a whole-model finite element framework. The material properties assigned to the HA spacer were used as simplified linear-elastic parameters for comparative time-zero analysis and did not incorporate porosity-dependent variation, biodegradation, or progressive bone ingrowth.

Boundary and load conditions

Both the surgical and intact models were subjected to the same loading and boundary conditions. The inferior margin of T1 vertebra was rigidly constrained. A 75 N follower load was applied to simulate the weight of the head [25, 28, 31]. A 2.0 N·m moment was applied to the odontoid process of C2 to simulate flexion, extension, lateral bending, and axial rotation (Fig. 2A) [25, 31]. All material interfaces (screw-bone, plate-bone, screw-plate, LA staple-bone, and HA spacer-bone) were assigned identical friction properties with µ = 0.3 [30].

Mesh convergence test

The goal of the mesh convergence test was to determine an appropriate mesh resolution with a moderate number of elements, ensuring simulation accuracy and efficiency under the same loading conditions used in the main FE analyses. Therefore, a 75 N follower load and a 2.0 N·m pure moment were applied to the intact cervical spine model. Axial rotation was selected as the test condition because it imposed relatively demanding deformation and contact responses on the posterior cervical structures, making it a practical condition for mesh sensitivity evaluation [32]. Table 2 shows the number of elements and nodes for each mesh resolution. Three mesh resolutions (Mesh 1, Mesh 2, and Mesh 3) were generated through progressive mesh refinement while preserving the same geometry, material properties, and boundary conditions. Mesh 3, the finest mesh, was used as the reference. In the present study, mesh convergence was evaluated using mean von Mises stress under axial rotation as a representative metric for the comparative FE analyses. Mesh convergence was assessed according to the percentage difference in mean von Mises stress, and a difference of less than 5% between Mesh 2 and Mesh 3 was considered acceptable for the present comparative analysis [33].

Model validation and data analysis

For model validation, the intact model’s range of motion (ROM) was evaluated. The ROM and mean von Mises stresses of the components were analyzed and compared among the three surgical models. ROM was determined from the relative angular displacement between defined vertebral reference regions of the corresponding motion segment. The mean von Mises stress is the arithmetic average of the stresses at all nodes or integration points within a specific area [34]. It indicates the overall stress distribution characteristics of the region rather than localized stress extremes. In the Abaqus visualization module, the mean von Mises stress was calculated by averaging the von Mises stresses of all elements within each component.

Figures 9 and 10 summarize the percentage changes of mean von Mises stresses in the annulus fibrosus and nucleus pulposus across the three surgical models. Peak micromotions at the implant-bone interface

CSLIP1 and CSLIP2 represent the tangential relative motions between the implant and bone in Abaqus and were used to characterize tangential interface micromotion in two orthogonal directions [40, 41]. The reported values correspond to peak tangential contact-slip outputs within each construct and were interpreted as comparative biomechanical outputs within the present FE framework. Normal separation at the interfaces was not analyzed as a primary endpoint in the present study. Across the six loading directions, the DDSPS and LA lamina staple models showed similarly low micromotion, whereas the HA spacer model exhibited substantially greater values. Table 7 present the peak micromotions at the implant-bone interface in different models. The peak micromotions of DDSPS (CSLIP1: 0.0008 mm; CSLIP2: 0.0021 mm) and the LA lamina staple (CSLIP1: 0.0020 mm; CSLIP2: 0.0019 mm) were therefore both markedly lower than those of the HA spacer (CSLIP1: 0.2506 mm; CSLIP2: 0.1560 mm). These values represent the maximum CSLIP1 and CSLIP2 values observed across the tested motion directions. Within the present FE framework, these micromotion values should be interpreted as comparative contact outputs under the standardized friction setting.

Discussion

For patients with cervical spondylotic myelopathy (CSM) involving three or more segments, posterior cervical laminoplasty (PCL) is regarded as the surgical treatment of choice [42, 43]. PCL predominantly comprises two techniques: single-door and double-door laminoplasty [11, 23]. Standard posterior double-door laminoplasty methods include the hydroxyapatite (HA) spacer and the LA lamina staple, both of which carry potential risks, including laminar perforation and implant detachment. To mitigate these issues, we developed a double-door screw-plate system (DDSPS). This system uses screws to secure titanium plates, thereby reducing the risk of implant displacement or detachment. Furthermore, it enables independent fixation of bilateral laminae, obviating the need for simultaneous bilateral fixation in conventional techniques, thereby decreasing surgical complexity and enhancing procedural safety. As the screw-plate system is the most widely adopted fixation method in cervical spine surgery, it aligns closely with spine surgeons’ operative practices, making it a clinically relevant construct for biomechanical evaluation, although its practical applicability still requires further validation. In this study, finite element (FE) analysis was used to characterize the biomechanical performance of the DDSPS and to compare it with two reference surgical constructs in posterior cervical double-door laminoplasty.

It should be noted that the finite element analysis used herein constitutes a computational simulation method. Comparisons among different internal fixation models-DDSPS, LA lamina staple, and HA spacer-represent a qualitative study based on the deterministic outputs of each model under standardized loading conditions, aimed at elucidating their relative biomechanical behavior and trends.

Under flexion loading, the C2-T1 range of motion (ROM) increased by more than 30% in the DDSPS, LA lamina staple, and HA spacer models compared to the intact model. In comparison, changes in other movement directions were limited to 10% or less. This observation aligns with a previous finite element study by Kode et al. [28], which reported a greater than 20% increase in C2-T1 ROM during flexion for the LA lamina staple model, with minimal alterations (< 5%) in other motions. All three surgical techniques aim to stabilize the detached laminae without achieving segmental fusion, which accounts for their limited overall effect on cervical ROM. The pronounced increase in ROM, particularly during flexion, is likely attributable to intraoperative disruption of the posterior ligamentous complex.

The percentage changes in mean von Mises stress within the annulus fibrosus and nucleus pulposus were comparable across all surgical models. A marked increase in mean von Mises stress was observed during flexion in all models. Stress changes in the remaining directions were generally limited, except for localized increases at C2–C3 during rotation. This pattern is consistent with the findings of Nishida et al. [44] in a finite-element analysis of cervical open-door laminoplasty using HA spacers. The phenomenon may be explained by a compensatory redistribution of stress within the intervertebral discs following the disruption of the posterior ligamentous complex. This mechanism helps maintain overall cervical stability. Compared with the intact model, the three surgical models showed only minor differences in mean articular cartilage stress, except at specific spinal segments during specific maneuvers (e.g., the C2-C3 segment during lateral bending).

Relative to the DDSPS and LA lamina staple models, the HA spacer model exhibited markedly reduced mean von Mises stresses in both the surgically addressed vertebral lamina and the implant itself. This difference is likely attributable to their distinct fixation mechanisms. Unlike the rigid fixation provided by DDSPS and LA lamina staples, the HA spacer employs a comparatively flexible, non-rigid interface. It is hypothesized that this flexible fixation affords less complete stabilization of the implant-lamina complex than rigid systems. Consequently, the mechanical interaction force between the lamina and the implant is reduced, resulting in lower stress levels within the HA spacer model’s lamina and implant components.

The DDSPS and LA lamina staple models demonstrated similarly low peak micromotion values, both of which were markedly lower than those of the HA spacer model within the present FE framework. From a biomechanical perspective, this finding suggests that both constructs may provide stronger initial resistance to laminar reclosure than the HA spacer model. For the LA lamina staple in particular, such interface behavior is relevant because its fixation relies on elastic engagement and dentation anchorage within the laminar bone; therefore, insufficient anchorage, local loosening, or laminar perforation may be mechanically related to reduced fixation security. Excessive micromotion is detrimental to bone-implant integration and is associated with implant loosening, migration, and clinical failure [45, 46]. In laminoplasty, inadequate fixation and resultant micromotion directly increase the risk of laminar reclosure-a complication that can reverse surgical decompression and lead to symptom recurrence or neurological compromise. Within the present FE framework, the rigid screw-plate construct of the DDSPS was associated with lower tangential interface micromotion, suggesting a more favorable initial fixation environment than that of the HA spacer construct.

Von Mises stress is a key metric for evaluating the initial stability and fracture risk of internal fixation devices [47]. Our results show that under the six loading directions, the maximum peak stress on the DDSPS occurred during extension, with values of 48.70 MPa for the plate and 67.09 MPa for the screws. These stresses are well below the yield strength of the titanium alloy used (σ = 780–950 MPa) [48]. Stress distribution maps indicated that concentrations primarily arose during flexion and extension. Specifically, the stress nephogram revealed that high stresses were localized to three critical regions: the plate’s connecting axis, the periphery of the screw holes on the axial side, and the axial fixation screws themselves. This distribution pattern can be interpreted using principles of structural mechanics. The connecting axis serves as the primary load-bearing component, transferring bending moments between the opened laminae. During flexion and extension, which induce significant bending on the construct, this region naturally experiences the highest stress. The screw holes, as geometric discontinuities, act as intrinsic stress risers, explaining the elevated stress around them. The peak stress concentration in the axial-side screws indicates that these components bear the predominant shear and tensile loads resisting lamina closure or displacement. This behavior is consistent with the intended design function of a rigid, screw-based fixation system. Importantly, stress concentration in these key areas does not represent a design flaw but instead reflects an efficient load transfer pathway from the mobile spinal segments through the implant to the fixed bony structures.

The present study should be interpreted primarily as a time-zero finite element characterization of the DDSPS in the setting of double-door laminoplasty. This study has several limitations inherent to its computational methodology. First, excluding the cervical musculature simplifies the simulation of physiological load transfer and dynamic stabilization, potentially affecting the absolute magnitudes of predicted motions and stresses. Second, the assumption of isotropic, linear elastic material properties for bone and soft tissues-while common in finite element (FE) studies-does not fully capture the nonlinear, viscoelastic, or anisotropic behaviors of biological tissues under complex loading. Third, modeling ligaments as linearly simplified tension-only elements may influence segmental mobility and stress distribution patterns. Notably, the use of quasi-static loading conditions precludes predictions of long-term performance, such as implant fatigue, screw loosening under cyclic loading, or bone remodeling. In addition, no dedicated sensitivity analysis of suture pretension, contact parameters, or friction-related assumptions was performed, which further limits the generalizability of the comparative findings involving the HA construct. Although these simplifications were necessary to ensure computational feasibility, they necessitate cautious interpretation of the absolute quantitative results. The present study is more informative for comparative time-zero analysis at the construct level than for the absolute quantification of highly localized stress concentrations. Nevertheless, this FE model provides valuable insights into the relative performance and potential mechanical advantages of the DDSPS compared to existing techniques.

While this study preliminarily validates the biomechanical efficacy of the DDSPS, its design offers significant potential for optimization. Future research could employ parametric FE analysis to refine results systematically. Regarding materials, exploring biomaterials with an elastic modulus intermediate between that of a titanium alloy and cortical bone may help mitigate stress-shielding effects and promote bone ingrowth while maintaining sufficient strength [34]. Geometrically, the observed stress concentrations-particularly on the plate’s connecting axis and axial-side screws during flexion/extension (Fig. 14)-suggest specific areas for structural reinforcement, such as locally increased thickness or the addition of reinforcing ribs. Furthermore, optimizing the plate contour could improve anatomical conformity across a diverse patient population. Implant stability may also be enhanced through screw design optimization; for instance, evidence suggests that pedicle screws with an undercut thread profile offer superior resistance to migration and rotation compared to V-shaped threads [49]. Such simulation-driven optimization would provide crucial theoretical guidance for subsequent prototype development, in vitro biomechanical testing, and future clinical translation.

Conclusion

Within the assumptions of the present FE model, the DDSPS demonstrated lower interface micromotion and greater construct stability than the comparator models. These findings support a more favorable initial biomechanical profile for the DDSPS within the standardized comparative framework of the present study. Further validation through biomechanical testing, animal studies, and clinical investigation is required before broader clinical conclusions can be drawn.
