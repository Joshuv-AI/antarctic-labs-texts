Tretinoin synergistically enhances the antitumor effect of combined BRAF, MEK, and EGFR inhibition in BRAF V600E colorectal cancer
Authors: Yuya Yoshida, Masanobu Takahashi, Sakura Taniguchi, Ryunosuke Numakura, Keigo Komine, Chikashi Ishioka
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPatients with BRAF‐mutated colorectal cancer (BRAF V600E CRC) are currently treated with a combination of BRAF inhibitor and anti‐EGFR antibody with or without MEK inhibitor. A fundamental problem in treating patients with BRAF V600E CRC is intrinsic and/or acquired resistance to this combination therapy. By screening 78 compounds, we identified tretinoin, a retinoid, as a compound that synergistically enhances the antiproliferative effect of a combination of BRAF inhibition and MEK inhibition with or without EGFR inhibition on BRAF V600E CRC cells. This synergistic effect was also exerted by other retinoids. Tretinoin, added to BRAF inhibitor and MEK inhibitor, upregulated PARP, BAK, and p‐H2AX. When either RARα or RXRα was silenced, the increase in cleaved PARP expression by the addition of TRE to ENC/BIN or ENC/BIN/CET was canceled. Our results suggest that the mechanism of the synergistic antiproliferative effect involves modulation of the Bcl‐2 family and the DNA damage response that affects apoptotic pathways, and this synergistic effect is induced by RARα‐ or RXRα‐mediated apoptosis. Tretinoin also enhanced the antitumor effect of a combination of the BRAF inhibitor and anti‐EGFR antibody with or without MEK inhibitor in a BRAF V600E CRC xenograft mouse model. Our data provide a rationale for developing retinoids as a new combination agent to overcome resistance to the combination therapy for patients with BRAF V600E CRC.Keywords: BRAF inhibitor, colorectal cancer, MEK inhibitor, retinoid, tretinoin

INTRODUCTION

Activated BRAF plays an important role in cancer development and progression by regulating proliferation, differentiation, the cell cycle, angiogenesis, apoptosis, and migration. 1 , 2 BRAF mutation occurs in various malignancies, such as melanoma, thyroid cancer, CRC, and non–small‐cell lung cancer. 1 , 2 , 3 Previous studies have proposed a BRAF mutation classification based on kinase activity, RAS dependence, and dimerization. 4 Of the three proposed classes, BRAF V600E, a RAS‐independent monomer, belongs to class 1. BRAF V600E accounts for 70%–80% of BRAF mutations in patients with CRC. 5 , 6

Compared with patients having BRAF–wild type CRC, those whose tumors harbor the BRAF mutation have a poor prognosis and a poor response to chemotherapy. 7 , 8 , 9 BRAF inhibitor monotherapy is effective against BRAF‐mutated melanoma or thyroid cancer. 10 , 11 In contrast, BRAF inhibitor monotherapy has been less effective against BRAF‐mutated CRC. 12 A main mechanism of intrinsic resistance to BRAF inhibitor monotherapy in BRAF‐mutated CRC is the EGFR‐mediated reactivation of MAPK signaling. 13 , 14 The addition of EGFR inhibitor to BRAF inhibitor led to overcoming the intrinsic resistance of BRAF V600E CRC to BRAF inhibitor alone in vitro and in vivo. 13 , 14 In a randomized open‐label phase III study in patients with BRAF V600E CRC, triplet therapy with ENC, BIN, and CET, and doublet therapy with ENC and CET, compared with investigators' choice of irinotecan or FOLFIRI plus CET, were associated with improved OS. 15 However, acquired resistance to the combination therapy of BRAF inhibition and EGFR inhibition, which has been associated with various alterations in the RAS–RAF–MEK–ERK signaling pathway, 16 , 17 is another problem for treating patients with BRAF V600E CRC. Further development of BRAF‐targeted therapeutic strategies is therefore required for patients with BRAF V600E CRC.

In the present study, we aimed to identify compounds that enhance the antitumor effect of BRAF inhibitor, MEK inhibitor, and anti‐EGFR antibody in BRAF V600E CRC for potential clinical application. We also aimed to clarify the underlying mechanisms of the synergistic effect between the identified compounds and BRAF‐targeted therapy.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Cell culture

CRC cell lines used in this study are listed in Table S1. Cell lines were cultured in RPMI 1640 or DMEM with 10% FBS in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 in air at 37°C. Cell line authentication was performed by short tandem repeat analysis using the GenomeLab human STR primer set (Beckman Coulter, CA, USA).

Reagents

DAB, TRA and TRE, RET and TAM, BEX, and CET were purchased from LC Laboratories (Woburn, MA, USA), Cayman Chemical (Ann Arbor, MI, USA), Selleck Chemicals (Houston, TX, USA), MedChemoExpress (Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA), and Merck Serono (Tokyo), respectively. ENC and BIN were purchased from Cayman Chemical and MedChemoExpress.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed 72 h after treatment using the Cell Counting Kit‐8 assay (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) according to the manufacturer's protocol. If each drug revealed an antiproliferative effect, CI was calculated using the CompuSyn software application (version 1.0: ComboSyn, Inc., Paramus, NJ, USA). Combination effects were classified as strong synergism (CI < 0.3), synergism (CI 0.3–0.7), slight synergism/additive effect (CI 0.7–1.0), or antagonistic effect (CI > 1.0). 18 , 19

Screening compounds to enhance BRAF inhibitor and MEK inhibitor

We used the SCADS inhibitor kit IV, which contains 78 chemical compounds developed for other diseases, to screen compounds that might enhance the antiproliferative effect of DAB and TRA in RKO cells. Cells were incubated in 500 nM of each compound alone in the SCADS inhibitor kit or with added DAB 50 nM and TRA 5 nM for 72 h (Figure 1A). The relative cell viability of cells treated with DAB/TRA was calculated by normalization to that treated with DMSO alone in the SCADS inhibitor kit alone.

Tretinoin (TRE) was identified as promising for enhancing the antiproliferative effect of BRAF/MEK/EGFR inhibition. Cell cycle analysis

Cells were collected and washed after the treatment, fixed overnight in 70% ethanol at 4°C, and then incubated with RNase and propidium iodide. Samples were analyzed in a CytoFLEX LX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter Life Sciences, Indianapolis, IN, USA).

Annexin V‐FITC apoptosis assay

Cells were collected after the treatment, and apoptosis was detected using the Annexin V‐FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit (Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto). The measurements were obtained using the CytoFLEX LX flow cytometer.

Microarray analysis

The SurePrint G3 Human Gene Expression 8 × 60 K microarray kit (version 3.0: Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) was used to identify genes that were differentially expressed by DMSO‐ and TRE‐treated cells and by ENC/BIN‐ and TRE/ENC/BIN‐treated cells. RKO and HT29 cells were incubated with each treatment for 24 h. After incubation, total RNA was extracted from the cells using the RNeasy Mini kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), and labeled cRNA was prepared using standard Agilent protocols. The change multiplier was calculated for each sample using the GeneSpring software application (version 14.5: Agilent Technologies). Genes whose change multiplier was >2 were defined as altered genes. The microarray data were registered to the Gene Expression Omnibus database (GSE269880 and GSE269881; http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov.geo/).

Kinex antibody microarray

The Kinex KAM‐2000 antibody microarray (Kinexus Bioinformatics Corporation, Vancouver, BC, Canada) was used to analyze the total protein including unphosphorylated and phosphorylated proteins, and phosphorylated protein expression profile of cells treated with DMSO, TRE, ENC/BIN, or TRE/ENC/BIN. RKO cells were incubated with each treatment for 48 h. After incubation, the samples were sent to the Kinexus Bioinformatics Corporation for hybridization and analysis. Proteins altered by TRE/ENC/BIN except for those altered by TRE alone and ENC/BIN were analyzed. Total proteins and phosphorylated proteins whose percentage change from control was 45 or greater, or −45 and greater, were defined as significantly changed.

Pathway analysis

Using the mRNA microarray and antibody microarray results, the Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery tool (Laboratory of Human Retrovirology and Immunoinformatics, Frederick, MD, USA) was applied for pathway analyses. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways (Kanehisa Laboratories, Kyoto) belonging to the Environmental Information Processing and Cellular Processes categories were selected for analysis. 20 , 21 , 22 Signaling pathways were regarded as significantly changed when they met the screening criteria (p ≤ 0.05) and included at least 10 genes or proteins.

Western blot analysis

Western blot analyses were performed following the standard protocol. 23 Primary antibodies used are listed in Table S2. The band intensities were quantified by densitometry using the ImageJ software application (version 1.53a, US National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA), 24 and the expression of each protein was normalized to that of internal protein controls such as tubulin, GAPDH, or β‐actin.

RNA transfection

siGENOME Human RARA siRNA SMARTpool (M‐003437‐02‐0005), siGENOME Human RXRA siRNA SMARTpool (M‐003443‐02‐0005), and siGENOME Non‐Targeting siRNA Pool (D‐001206‐13‐05) were purchased from Horizon Discovery Ltd. (Waterbeach, Cambridge, UK). Cells were transfected with 25 nM of the siRNAs using Lipofectamine 2000 transfection reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Animal experiments

Each mouse received an injection of HT29 cells in DMEM mixed 1:1 with Matrigel (Corning Life Sciences, Tewksbury, MA, USA) subcutaneously into the flank region. When the tumor volume reached 150–200 mm3, mice were randomly assigned to six treatment groups: vehicle control (n = 7), TRE (n = 7), ENC/CET (n = 7), TRE/ENC/CET (n = 7), ENC/BIN/CET (n = 7), and TRE/ENC/BIN/CET (n = 7). TRE, ENC, and BIN were administered orally at doses of 10, 5, and 1.75 mg/kg, respectively, daily for 28 days. CET was administered intraperitoneally at a dose of 20 mg/kg twice weekly for 28 days. Tumor size and body weight were measured every 3 days, and the tumor volume in cubic millimeters was calculated using the formula 0.5 × length (mm) × [width (mm)]. 2

Immunohistochemistry

Immunostaining of tumor cells with an anti–Ki‐67 antibody was performed on formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded tissue sections of tumors resected from mice in each group. Five fields were randomly selected using an optical microscope and imaged at ×400 magnification. In each group, more than 500 tumor cells were counted, and the Ki‐67 labeling index was calculated.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed in JMP Pro (version 15.0: SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) and Microsoft Excel for Microsoft 365 MSO (version 2202: Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA). Except for screening using the SCADS inhibitor kit, mRNA microarray, antibody microarray, and animal experiments, all procedures were performed as three or more independent replicates. The significance of differences between each group was estimated using the unpaired Student's t‐test. Differences were considered significant when the p‐value was ≤0.05. Error bars are defined in the individual figures in which they appear.

RESULTS

Screening for potential compounds to enhance BRAF inhibitor and MEK inhibitor

We began with the hypothesis that certain compounds might potentiate the therapeutic efficacy of combined BRAF and MEK inhibition. To screen candidate compounds that might enhance the antiproliferative effects of DAB and TRA, we used the SCADS inhibitor kit IV. Treatment of DAB/TRA decreased the viability of the cells by 52%. Each compound in the kit was classified as a candidate compound if it, together with added DAB/TRA, decreased the viability of the cells by >20%, but alone decreased the cell viability by <20%, compared with the viability of the cells treated with DAB/TRA. TRE, a retinoid, was identified as the compound with the most promise of enhancing the efficacy of BRAF and MEK inhibitors (Figure 1B and Table S3).

TRE enhanced the antiproliferative effect of combined ENC/BIN on BRAF V600E CRC cells

To evaluate whether TRE could synergistically enhance the antiproliferative effect of combined ENC/BIN on BRAF V600E CRC, we assessed the viability of cells cultured with ENC/BIN with or without TRE at various concentrations. TRE, at least with concentrations ranging 0.01–20 μM, was shown not to affect cell viability in NCM460, which is a normal colonic epithelial cell line. 25 The addition of TRE to ENC/BIN resulted in a strong synergistic effect (CI < 0.3) on the RKO, HT29, Co‐115, and WiDr cell lines (Figures 1C and S1; Table S4). Furthermore, the strong synergistic effect on the RKO and HT29 cells was maintained when CET was added to the TRE/ENC and the TRE/ENC/BIN combination, which are current standard regimens in clinics for patients with BRAF V600E CRC (Figures 2A and S2A–C; Tables S5 and S6). To elucidate whether enhancement of the antiproliferative effect by TRE in combination with ENC/BIN was TRE specific or common to retinoids, we performed a cell viability assay using other retinoids: RET, TAM, and BEX (Figures S3–S5; Tables S7–S9). Next, we evaluated whether TRE also synergistically enhanced the antiproliferative effect of ENC/BIN on BRAF–wild type CRC. The TRE/ENC/BIN combination did not result in a strong synergistic effect on BRAF–wild type CRC cells (Figure S6A,B; Table S10). The synergistic enhancement of retinoids was not associated with MSI status (Table S1). These results demonstrated that retinoids, including TRE, could synergistically enhance the antiproliferative effect of ENC/BIN specifically on BRAF V600E CRC cells.

TRE, when added to combined ENC/BIN, induces apoptosis in BRAF V600E CRC cells

To clarify the mechanism of the synergistic antiproliferative effect of adding TRE to ENC/BIN, we next used flow cytometry to assess the effect of the combination on the cell cycle and induction of apoptosis. The addition of TRE to ENC/BIN was associated with a sub‐G1 increase in RKO cells. In contrast, compared with ENC/BIN alone, the addition of TRE to ENC/BIN was not associated with any significant changes in the G1, S, and G2‐M fractions in RKO or HT29 cells (Figure 2B). Next, when TRE was added to ENC/CET and ENC/BIN/CET, a similar tendency was observed (Figure S7A). The Annexin V‐FITC assay showed that the percentage of apoptotic cells increased significantly with the addition of TRE to ENC/BIN, even though TRE alone, compared with DMSO alone, did not increase apoptotic cells (Figure 2C). In addition, we validated that TRE showed a synergistic effect of inducing apoptosis both in RKO and HT29 cells even when combined with ENC/CET and ENC/BIN/CET as well as to ENC/BIN (Figure S7B). These results indicated that adding TRE to the BRAF/MEK/EGFR inhibition induced apoptosis, one of the mechanisms of the synergistic antiproliferative effect. In addition, these results indicated that adding TRE to ENC/BIN induced apoptosis, one of the mechanisms of the synergistic antiproliferative effect.

Adding TRE to ENC/BIN was associated with altered expression of various genes and proteins involved in cell proliferation

Although TRE alone had a poor antiproliferative effect, our results revealed that the addition of TRE to ENC/BIN synergistically enhanced the antiproliferative effect. We therefore hypothesized that TRE induced different gene expression changes depending on the presence or absence of ENC/BIN. To address that hypothesis, a mRNA microarray analysis was performed using RKO and HT29 cells. With the addition of TRE to DMSO or ENC/BIN on RKO cells, 790 and 1222 genes, respectively, were judged to be significantly altered. After the same additions to HT29 cells, 1274 and 1619 genes were significantly altered. Pathway analysis indicated that the addition of TRE to ENC/BIN was associated with alterations in various genes belonging to the molecular signaling pathways involved in cell proliferation (Tables S11 and S12). Next, to elucidate whether the addition of TRE to ENC/BIN affected the expression or phosphorylation of specific proteins, we used the Kinex antibody microarray KAM‐2000 to analyze the expression of total proteins including unphosphorylated and phosphorylated proteins, and phosphorylated proteins by RKO cells with each treatment (Table S13). As a result, the addition of TRE to ENC/BIN was observed to result in specific changes to 106 proteins. Pathway analysis using those proteins revealed that nine pathways were enriched with proteins belonging to molecular signaling pathways involved in cell proliferation, such as the MAPK, PI3K‐Akt, FoxO, and ErbB signaling pathways (Table 1). These results indicated that TRE added to ENC/BIN is associated with alterations to many genes and proteins involved in cell proliferation. Those differences might be one of the factors underlying the synergistic antiproliferative effect of TRE/ENC/BIN.

TRE, when added to ENC/BIN, upregulated cleaved PARP, BAK, p‐H2AX

To elucidate the mechanisms involved in the enhancement of antiproliferation when TRE is added to ENC/BIN, we assessed the expression of proteins associated with cell growth. Based on the mRNA and antibody microarray results, the expression of proteins associated with the MAPK signaling pathway and the PI3K‐Akt signaling pathway was assessed by western blot analysis. Our study revealed that the addition of TRE to ENC/BIN induced a level of apoptosis. Retinoids were shown to regulate Bcl‐2 family proteins, leading to DNA damage and apoptosis in CRC cells. 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 Accordingly, we assessed the expression of proteins involved in Bcl‐2 apoptotic pathways or the DNA damage response. Compared with ENC/BIN alone, TRE/ENC/BIN resulted in a significant downregulation of p‐MEK expression in both RKO and HT29 cells (Figures 3A and S8). However, the addition of TRE to ENC/BIN did not significantly affect the expression of p‐ERK, a protein downstream of MEK (Figure 3B). Compared with ENC/BIN, TRE/ENC/BIN resulted in significant downregulation of p‐AKT expression in RKO, but not in HT29 (Figures 3C and S8). TRE/ENC/BIN resulted in significant downregulation of pS6 in RKO (Figure S9). Endogenous expression of p‐S6 was not detected in HT29 cells. To analyze whether TRE/ENC/BIN affected the feedback of the EGFR pathway, we also analyzed the expression of p‐CRAF and p‐EGFR. We found a trend in which p‐CRAF was downregulated in RKO cells, but there was no endogenous expression of p‐CRAF in HT29 cells (data not shown). Endogenous expression of p‐EGFR was not detected in RKO and HT29 cells (data not shown). When TRE was added to ENC/BIN, cleaved PARP, BAK, and p‐H2AX all significantly increased in both RKO and HT29 cells (Figures 3D,E and S8). These results suggested that the MAPK and PI3K‐Akt signaling pathways were not a key part of the mechanism underlying the synergistic antiproliferative effect in all cell lines, and that the mechanism may involve modulation of the Bcl‐2 family and the DNA damage response that affects apoptotic pathways.

Enhancement of the antiproliferative effect observed when retinoid is added to ENC/BIN is mediated in part by RARα or RXRα

Retinoids have been reported to have an antiproliferative effect in various tumors as agonists against RARα or RXRα. 30 , 31 , 32 When TRE isomerizes into 9‐cis retinoic acid, it acts as an agonist for RXR and also as a ligand for RAR. 33 To evaluate the mechanism for the enhancement of the antiproliferative effect observed when adding TRE to ENC/BIN involved RARα or RXRα, we assessed the relation of the endogenous protein expression of RARα or RXRα with the synergistic antiproliferative effect of TRE. Endogenous expression of RARα was detected in RKO cells, but was very low or almost undetectable in the other five cell lines (Figure 4A). Endogenous expression of RXRα was highest in WiDr cells, with expression declining, in order, in HT29, LIM2405, RKO, COLO205, and Co‐115 cells (Figure 4A), indicating that the degree of endogenous RARα and RXRα expression did not correlate with the level of the TRE‐induced synergistic enhancement of the antiproliferative effect.

We next aimed to elucidate whether enhancement of the antiproliferative effect exerted by TRE is affected by alterations in the expression of RARα or RXRα. Considering that only RXRα is endogenously expressed in BRAF V600E CRC cells, except in RKO, we used both the RAR and RXR agonist TRE and the selective RXR agonist BEX for all cell lines. At 72 h after transfection with si‐RARα and si‐RXRα, the protein expression of RARα and RXRα was reduced in RKO cells (39% and 87% respectively; Figure 4B). In HT29 cells, which lack endogenous RARα expression, we used only siRNA against RXRα and confirmed reduced expression of RXRα (by 60%; Figure 4A,C). When the expression of RARα and RXRα was reduced, the enhancement of the antiproliferative effect observed with TRE/ENC/BIN in RKO was inhibited (Figure 4D). The enhancement of the antiproliferative effect observed when BEX was added to ENC/BIN was canceled, at least in part, only when RXRα expression was reduced (Figure 4E). The reduced expression of RXRα partly inhibited the enhancement of the antiproliferative effect observed with the addition of TRE or BEX to ENC/BIN in HT29 and RKO cells alike (Figure 4F,G). Next, we validated the similar reduction of enhancement of the antiproliferative effect by the addition of TRE to ENC/CET and ENC/BIN/CET in RKO cells (Figure S10A). These results indicated that, at least in part, mediation of RARα or RXRα stimulation by TRE and BEX enhances the antiproliferative effect. Then, to confirm that the RARα‐ or RXRα‐mediated enhancement of the antiproliferative effect observed when TRE is added to ENC/BIN leads to apoptosis, we assessed cleaved PARP expression in RKO cells transfected with si‐NC, si‐RARα, or si‐RXRα. When either RARα or RXRα was silenced, the cleaved PARP expression that had increased with the addition of TRE to ENC/BIN or ENC/BIN/CET decreased (Figures 4H and S10B). Taken together, our results indicated that the addition of TRE to ENC/BIN, with or without CET, exerts a synergistic antiproliferative effect, at least in part, through RARα‐ or RXRα‐mediated apoptosis.

TRE, when added to ENC/CET or ENC/BIN/CET, inhibited tumor growth in vivo

To validate in vivo the antitumor effect and toxicity of the addition of TRE to ENC/BIN/CET but also to ENC/CET, another current standard therapy for BRAF V600E CRC, we used a xenograft mouse model. Tumor volume was significantly smaller in the TRE/ENC/CET group than in the ENC/CET group and in the TRE/ENC/BIN/CET group than in the ENC/BIN/CET group; no significant difference between vehicle or TRE alone was evident (Figure 5A). Tumor volume did not significantly differ between ENC/BIN/CET group and TRE/ENC/CET group, nor between the TRE/ENC/CET group and TRE/ENC/BIN/CET group. Mild dry skin was observed on all mice treated with tretinoin. At 28 days, body weight did not significantly differ between the groups treated with or without TRE (Figure 5B). To evaluate differences in cell proliferation between the groups treated with and without TRE, we determined the Ki‐67 labeling index in resected tumor tissues. No significant differences in the Ki‐67 labeling index were evident between the vehicle group and the TRE alone group; however, the Ki‐67 labeling index was significantly lower in the TRE/ENC/CET and TRE/ENC/BIN/CET groups than in the ENC/CET and ENC/BIN/CET groups (Figure 5C,D). Taken together, those results indicated that the addition of TRE to ENC/CET or ENC/BIN/CET was associated with synergistic antitumor effects in vivo as well as in vitro.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we identified compounds that would enhance the antitumor effect of combined therapy with BRAF inhibitor, MEK inhibitor, and anti‐EGFR antibody in BRAF V600E CRC. To the best of our knowledge, this report is the first to demonstrate that retinoids, including TRE, synergistically enhance the antiproliferative effect of BRAF and MEK inhibitors in BRAF V600E CRC. In addition, the enhancement of the antiproliferative effect was demonstrated to operate, at least in part, through RARα‐ or RXRα‐mediated apoptosis, and TRE enhances the antitumor effect of BRAF inhibitor and anti‐EGFR antibody with or without MEK inhibitor in vivo.

In current clinical practice, TRE and TAM are used in the treatment of acute promyelocytic leukemia, 34 and BEX is used for cutaneous T cell lymphoma. 35 Few studies have explored the therapeutic potential of retinoids in solid tumors. 36 Several studies have analyzed the ability of retinoids to inhibit the proliferation of CRC cells in vitro or in vivo. 27 , 30 Several clinical trials have analyzed the efficacy of treatments combining a retinoid with other drugs in CRC. 37 , 38 , 39 However, the efficacy of these combinations has not been validated in randomized studies. The specific effects of TRE on BRAF V600E CRC cells and the clinical role of TRE in patients with BRAF V600E CRC remain unclear. Our study demonstrated that TRE enhances the antitumor effect of BRAF, MEK, and EGFR inhibition on BRAF V600E CRC. The addition of TRE to a current standard therapeutic regimen of BRAF inhibitor, MEK inhibitor, and anti‐EGFR antibody could be a new strategy for patients with BRAF V600E CRC. In addition, TRE is expected to have synergistic efficacy when TRE is combined with not only a BRAF‐targeted therapy, but also a combination of BRAF‐targeted therapy and cytotoxic agents, or BRAF‐targeted therapy and immune checkpoint inhibitors. Azarifar et al. suggested that TRE suppressed the self‐renewal capability of cancer stem cells and can increase the therapeutic efficiency of 5‐FU. 40 TRE, which may enhance the efficacy of a combination of BRAF‐targeted therapy and cytotoxic agents. Next, targeting MDSCs is an attractive strategy to enhance the efficacy of immunotherapies. TRE acts on MDSCs to promote their differentiation and leads to decreased frequency of circulation MDSCs via ERK1/2 activation, glutathione synthase upregulation, and glutathione generation. 41 A phase Ib/II clinical trial, which evaluated the safety and efficacy of the combination of TRE with pembrolizumab in patients with melanoma, has shown that the combination therapy had favorable tolerability and high response rate. 42 In addition, Tobin et al. reported that TRE decreases MDSC immunosuppressive gene expression and immunosuppressive function, and suggested that the combination of TRE with immunotherapies represents a promising strategy to improve outcomes in cancer patients. 43 Oncogenic BRAF can induce changes that facilitate tumor immune escape, and the inhibition of BRAF is associated with significant immunological changes in the tumor microenvironment that are considered favorable for antitumor immunity. 44 , 45 Cooper et al. reported that immune checkpoint blockade using anti‐PD‐1 or anti‐PD‐L1 synergizes with BRAF‐targeted therapy in an in vivo model. 46 These combination therapies with TRE could be another promising strategy that has to be explored in future studies.

There is a significant association between the BRAF V600E mutation and MSI in CRC. 47 MSI‐high tumors are more immunogenic than other tumor subtypes, as the truncated proteins resulting from errors in unrepaired DNA strands function as neoantigens that activate the immune system. 48 The KEYNOTE‐177 study demonstrated that a BRAF mutation was not significantly associated with the worse survival of patients with MSI‐high CRC treated with pembrolizumab. 49 Conversely, patients with BRAF V600E and MSI‐stable CRC have been associated with a poor prognosis, 50 , 51 , 52 raising high potential medical needs particularly for treating patients with MSI‐stable and BRAF V600E and MSI‐stable CRC. In our study, the synergistic effect of the addition of TRE to BRAF, MEK, and EGFR inhibition was observed in RKO cells characterized with BRAF V600E and MSI‐high and BRAF V600E, as well as in HT29 cells characterized with BRAF V600E and MSI‐stable, which demonstrated that TRE enhanced the antitumor effect of BRAF, MEK, and EGFR inhibition on BRAF V600E CRC, regardless of MSI status.

The activity of retinoids is mediated primarily by members of the RAR and RXR subfamily. 36 RARs form heterodimers with RXRs, while RXRs form heterodimers with various nuclear receptor superfamily members. 53 The RXR dimers can be divided into the RXR homodimer, permissive heterodimers, and non‐permissive heterodimers. The RXR homodimer and the permissive heterodimers become transcriptionally active in the sole presence of an RXR‐selective ligand. 54 In contrast, the non‐permissive heterodimers are unresponsive to an RXR agonist alone. RAR/RXR is interestingly categorized as a non‐permissive heterodimer. Our study indicated that not only TRE, but also the selective RXR agonist BEX, was associated with the synergistic enhancement of the antiproliferative effect of ENC/BIN, in part through an RXRα‐mediated pathway. This suggests that the synergistic antiproliferative effect mediates a pathway activated by RXRα stimulation alone. Dillard et al. reported that, in vitro, RET increased the RXR‐mediated proteasomal degradation of β‐catenin in TRE‐resistant CRC cells, reducing the transcription of both exogenous and endogenous β‐catenin target genes. 30 In both in vitro and in vivo models, Cesario et al. demonstrated that the activation of the RXR/PPARγ permissive heterodimer inhibited CRC cell growth. 31 Our results in the present study, demonstrating that retinoids can potentially synergistically enhance the antiproliferative effect mediated by an RXRα‐only mechanism, are consistent with the earlier reports. Furthermore, we demonstrated that a selective RAR agonist TAM enhances the antiproliferative effect of ENC/BIN and that reduced expression of RARα canceled the enhancement of the antiproliferative effect of ENC/BIN in RKO cells. The latter result suggests that an RARα‐only mechanism also exists. Further studies are needed to elucidate the exact mechanisms that underlie the synergistic enhancement effect of TRE.

Our data indicate that the Bcl‐2 family is involved in TRE enhancement of the antiproliferative effect of ENC/BIN. BAK and BAX are the pro‐apoptotic effector proteins of the Bcl‐2 family. 55 Several in vitro studies have shown that a retinoid antiproliferative effect on CRC cells is mediated by Bcl‐2 family proteins. 26 , 28 , 56 Furthermore, the synergistic induction of apoptosis by inhibition of the Bcl‐2 and MEK/MAPK pathways has been clarified in various tumors. For instance, a potentially therapeutic MAPK‐targeted therapy, combined with inhibition of Bcl‐2 family member Mcl‐1 for BRAF V600E CRC, has been proposed based on the results from an in vitro model. 57 , 58 Our observations, that the mechanism of TRE's synergistic enhancement of the antiproliferative effect of ENC/BIN is mediated in part through Bcl‐2 protein family‐associated apoptosis, are in line with those previous findings.

In summary, using an in vitro BRAF V600E CRC model, we first demonstrated that TRE synergistically enhances the antiproliferative effect of BRAF and MEK inhibition at least in part by activating RXRα‐ or RARα‐mediated apoptosis. Subsequently, we demonstrated in vivo that TRE synergistically enhances the antitumor effect of either BRAF and EGFR inhibition or of BRAF, MEK, and EGFR inhibition. Our findings suggest the possibility of developing a novel therapeutic strategy for patients with BRAF V600E CRC, whose prognosis is poorer than that of their counterparts with other molecular CRC subtypes.
