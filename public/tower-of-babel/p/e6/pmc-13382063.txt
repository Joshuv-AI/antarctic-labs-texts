CHIP protects rheumatoid arthritis progression by targeting TRAF6 in arthritis animal models
Authors: Jing Liu, Kaijian Fan, Yilei Yang, Yidan Tang, Song Guo Zheng, Tingyu Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCarboxyl terminus of HSC70-interacting protein (CHIP) serves as a quality control protein that involves in inflammation and immunity, bone remodeling and tumorigenesis. However, the role of CHIP in joint disease such as rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is less explored.MethodsWild-type and Chip knockdown mice were used to establish a collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) model to assess arthritis progression through clinical index and histopathological markers (synovial hyperplasia, inflammatory infiltration and cartilage damage). SiRNA and lentivirus were respectively used to knockdown and overexpress CHIP in CIA fibroblast-like synoviocytes (FLS), investigating its regulatory effect on proliferation, migration, invasion and inflammation. Western blotting and immunofluorescence analysis were performed to assess the effect of CHIP on Tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated factor 6 (TRAF6) and to elucidate the underlying mechanism. Adeno-associated virus (AAV) mediated delivery of Chip was used to evaluate its therapeutic effects in CIA mice.ResultsCHIP significantly decreased in the synovium of CIA mice and RA patients compare to control. CHIP deficiency exacerbated synovial inflammation, cartilage degradation, and bone erosion in CIA, further underscoring its protective role in joint homeostasis. Moreover, knockdown of CHIP promoted the proliferation, migration and expression of inflammatory cytokines in CIA-FLS, while CHIP overexpression in CIA-FLS showed the opposite results. Mechanistically, CHIP interacted with TRAF6 and promoted the degradation of TRAF6. CHIP inhibited the migration and pro-inflammatory phenotypes of CIA-FLS via TRAF6/p65 pathway. Further, AAV mediated CHIP gene therapy by intra-articular administration effectively attenuated the progression of CIA by inhibiting the synovial inflammation and expression of TRAF6.ConclusionCollectively, these findings demonstrated the critical regulatory role of CHIP in RA. Therefore, exploiting the protective effects of CHIP may represent a potential therapeutic strategy for RA.The translational potential of this articleThese findings not only advance our understanding of CHIP's regulatory role in RA but also highlight the value of genetic animal models in orthopaedic drug discovery.

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a common systemic autoimmune disorder and is characterized by the over synovial hyperplasia, inflammatory cell infiltration, pannus formation along with progressive, irreversible bone and cartilage destruction [1]. In the past 20 years, a variety of new Disease-Modifying Antirheumatic Drugs (DMARDs) have been developed, targeting various inflammatory and immune mediators including TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6, CTLA-4, GM-CSF. While these biological agents have demonstrated clinical efficacy in ameliorating disease activity and attenuating or delaying joint structural damage, there are still a considerable number of RA patients whose symptoms cannot be effectively controlled [2]. Consequently, there is imperative need to comprehensive elucidate the molecular mechanism of RA progression in order to develop novel effective therapeutic agents of RA.

Fibroblast-like synoviocytes (FLS), a pivotal cellular component of the synovial membrane, maintain joint homeostasis under physiological conditions through the synthesis of extracellular matrix components and lubricating factors [3]. However, in RA, FLS undergo tumor-like phenotypic changes, marked by hyperproliferation and increased invasion [4]. Additionally, activated FLS secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, perpetuating the immune-inflammatory cascade and driving bone and cartilage destruction [4]. Given their critical role in RA pathogenesis, FLS have merged as a potential therapeutic target.

E3 ubiquitin ligases regulate diverse cellular processes—such as cell cycle progression, apoptosis and immune regulation—by mediating protein ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation [5]. Notably, special E3 ligases—Mid1 [6], cIAP1/2 [7,8], and UHRF1 [9]—have been linked in RA pathogenesis. Carboxyl terminus of Hsp70-interacting protein (CHIP/STUB1), is an important E3 ligase that plays a pivotal role in protein quality control [10]. Dysregulated CHIP expression has been implicated in critical pathophysiological processes including neuroprotective, immune-inflammatory modulation, metabolic homeostasis, bone remodeling and tumorigenesis [10,11]. While previous studies of CHIP have predominantly explored its roles in cancer and immunity [[12], [13], [14]], our research group focuses on musculoskeletal disorders, particularly RA. RA is characterized by dysregulated immune-inflammatory responses and FLS exhibiting tumor-like phenotypes [3]. CHIP has been shown to regulate osteoclastogenesis through TRAF6 degradation [15], our prior work demonstrated its role in bone homeostasis by inhibiting osteoclast formation via TRAF family interactions [16]. Given the critical involvement of CHIP in tumor suppression and immune-inflammatory regulation, we are motivated to investigate its potential involvement in RA pathogenesis.

The tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated factor (TRAF) family consists of seven conversed adaptor proteins (TRAF1-7) that serve as critical mediators of immune and inflammatory signaling pathways [17,18]. Specifically, TRAF6 functions as an E3 ubiquitin ligase and an essential signaling hub that integrates signal inputs from receptor families including Toll-like receptors (TLRs), interleukin-1 receptors (IL-1Rs), and the receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB (RANK) [19]. TRAF6 catalyzes K63-linked polyubiquitination of downstream substrates, including TAK1 and IKK, thereby transducing signals from activated receptors to trigger NF-κB and MAPK activation [20]. This distinctive positioning allows TRAF6 to integrate multiple pro-inflammatory and osteoclastogenic signals, making it a central regulator in diseases characterized by chronic inflammation and bone destruction, such as RA [[20], [21], [22]]. Alternatively, TRAF6 can also be targeted by other E3 ligases for ubiquitination-mediated regulation, which modulates its stability, interactions, and signaling transduction [23]. In the context of inflammatory arthritis, multiple lines of clinical and preclinical evidence have confirmed that TRAF6 signaling is hyperactivated in RA synovial tissues and cells [24,25]. Genetic studies have also identified functional variants of the TRAF6 gene that are associated with increased susceptibility to RA and low bone mineral density in RA patients, further supporting a pathogenic role of TRAF6 in RA [26].

In current study, we established collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) model, the experimental model of human RA, in wild-type (WT) and Chip knockdown mice to demonstrated the role of CHIP in inflammatory arthritis as well as the potential mechanism involved. Our results demonstrated that CHIP deficiency aggravated arthritis progression in CIA mice by promoting synovial hyperplasia and inflammation. Additionally, CHIP-TRAF6 axis could simultaneously participate in the CIA-FLS-mediated inflammatory response. Intra-articular administration of adeno-associated virus (AAV)-Chip attenuated the progression of arthritis in mice by inhibiting synovial inflammation and TRAF6 expression. Thus, CHIP may be a promising target for RA treatment.

Materials and methods

Animals

Chip knockdown (Chip+/−) mice on a C57BL/6 background were originally generated using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing targeting exon 2-3 of Stub1 gene (encoding CHIP protein). Homozygous knockout mice were not used for the CIA model due to severe neonatal lethality. Chip knockdown (Chip+/−) mice were generated by crossing male and female heterozygous mice. Genotyping was performed by PCR analysis of genomic DNA extracted from tail biopsies. Male DBA/1 J mice and C57BL/6 mice, were purchased from Shanghai Jihui Laboratory Animal Care Co., Ltd (China). All the mice were housed in the SPF facility of the Laboratory Animal Center of Shanghai Ninth People's Hospital, affiliated to Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine. Mice were maintained under conditions with a 12-h light-dark cycle and constant temperature at 22 ± 2°C. Mice used in all the experiments were male and between 68 weeks old. Animal welfare and experimental protocols were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Shanghai Ninth People's Hospital (Ethical Approval NO. SH9H-2020-A347-1).

CIA model

6-8 weeks male DBA/1 J mice were used to establish the CIA model as previously described [27]. Basically, the emulsion for immunization was prepared by bovine type II collagen (2 mg/mL, Chondrex, USA) with complete Freund's adjuvant (CFA, 4 mg/mL, Chondrex, USA) at a 1:1 vol ratio through homogenizing and stirring on an ice bath. On day 0, mice received a 100-150 μL subcutaneous injection of the freshly prepared emulsion at the tail base. And a booster immunization was administered on day 21 post-primary immunization through equivalent volume injection at the same site, following the same emulsification protocol.

To induce CIA model in Chip+/− mice (C57 background) and C57BL/6 WT mice, 6.67 mg/mL CFA was fully emulsified with chick type II collagen (2 mg/mL, Chondrex, USA) at a 1:1 vol ratio. On day 0, eight-week-old mice received primary immunization through injection of 100-150 μL emulsion at the base of the tail. Subsequent booster immunization was administered at the same anatomical site on day 14 using identical emulsion parameters. The calculation of CIA incidence was used the percentage of the mice with CIA phenotype and all immunized mice.

Isolation and culture of mouse primary FLS

Primary FLS were isolated from normal and CIA DBA/1 J mice according to the established enzymatic digestion protocol described by Rowan S Hardy et al. [28]. Briefly, the hindlimbs were aseptically dissected from CIA mice at day 35 post-induction. Following removal of periarticular soft tissues, specimens underwent 2-h enzymatic digestion at 37°C in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco, USA) containing 1 mg/mL collagenase IV and 0.1 mg/mL DNase I (Worthington Biochemical, USA). The resultant cell suspension was filtered through 70 μm strainer and washed with complete medium. Primary FLS cultures were maintained under standard conditions (37°C, 5% CO2), with medium renewal every 48 h. Reaching 80-90% confluence, FLS were subcultured. All experimental procedures utilized cells between passages 2-6 to ensure phenotypic stability.

Arthritis index and paw swelling

Paw swelling and arthritis index were quantitively assessed twice weekly following booster immunization. A standardized AI scoring protocol was implemented for systematic evaluation of arthritis severity, with classification criteria established as: 0 (no redness/swelling), 1 (mild tarsal/ankle swelling), 2 (erythema extending to metatarsophalangeal joints), 3 (generalized paw redness and swelling), and 4 (severe swelling/deformity). Individual animal arthritis index was calculated as the summation of all affected limb scores. All samples were graded according to the scoring standard by two blinded investigators.

Mechanical withdrawal threshold

Mechanical withdrawal threshold (MWT) were assessed via Chaplan's [29] up-down method using calibrated von Frey filaments (RSW, China). Following a 20-min habituation on elevated steel mesh platforms, standardized filaments were vertically applied through mesh apertures to hind paw midplantar regions of mice. Filaments were pressed until bending (2-3 s duration) with positive responses defined as abrupt withdrawal, flinching, or licking. The adjacent filament was subsequently applied following each positive or negative response, continuing until there was a change in response. The 50% MWT was calculated using Dixon's method. Baseline measurements were obtained 24 h prior to booster immunization.

Histopathological and immunofluorescence analysis

Mice knee joint samples were fixed in formalin, embedded in paraffin after decalcification, and sectioned for subsequent staining with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E), safranin O and Fast Green, tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining and immunofluorescence with corresponding antibodies (Table S1) to evaluate synovial inflammation and hyperplasia, the levels of proteoglycans of cartilage and the formation of osteoclasts. All histological parameters were separately evaluated using a semi-quantitative scoring system with scores ranging from 0 (normal) to 3 (severe) [27,30,31]. Two independent blinded observers performed the scoring and reached a consensus.

Synovial hyperplasia was scored based on the increasement in synovial membrane thickness compared with normal: 0 = normal thin synovial membrane with only 1-2 cell layers; 1 = mild hyperplasia, forming 3-5 stacked cell layers; 2 = moderate hyperplasia with multi-layered, synovial expansion; 3 = Severe maximal synovial hyperplasia, with invasive outgrowth toward cartilage/bone. Synovial inflammation was scored on a scale of 0 (no inflammatory infiltration) to 3 (severe inflammatory infiltration). Pannus formation score was assessed by the extent of neovascularization and invasive synovial tissue, ranging from 0 (no detectable pannus) to 3 (extensive pannus formation throughout the synovium, accompanied by erosion into the bone). Cartilage destruction was scored on a scale of 0-3 in Safranin O/Fast green staining sections, reflecting loss of proteoglycan and structural integrity, ranging from intact cartilage consisting of fully red (Safranin O) staining of the cartilage layers to almost complete loss of the articular cartilage. TRAP score was quantified by the presence and distribution of TRAP-positive cells in order to assess the bone erosion: ranging from 0 (no or only very small number of TRAP positive cells) to 3 (abundant positive cells occupying most of the bone resorption sites).

Immunoprecipitation, western blotting and q-PCR

For immunoprecipitation, cells were lysed with RIPA buffer with protease inhibitor (TargetMol, China) for 30 min on ice. Cell lysates were centrifuged for 15 min at 4°C. The supernatant was incubated with anti-CHIP antibody or the control igG with rotation overnight at 4°C and then 4-h incubation with Protein A + G Magnetic Beads (Beyotime, China). After that, washed the beads with PBS for three times, eluted with loading buffer and boiled for 10 min.

Western blotting and q-PCR analysis were performed in accordance with standard procedures. Detailed primer sequences and antibody specifications, have been provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Immunofluorescence (IF) analysis

All samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and permeabilized by 0.5% Triton X-100 for 10 min. After blocking with 3% Bovine serum albumin for 1 h, samples were incubated with primary antibodies targeting specific proteins (Listed in Table S1) overnight at 4°C. Samples were then incubated with fluorescent secondary antibodies at room temperature in the dark for 2 h. Nuclei were labeled with DAPI (Beyotime, China) for 5 min protected from light. Digital images were captured by a fluorescent microscope (Leica, SP8, Germany).

Cell transfection and in vitro functional analysis of CIA-FLS

Small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting mouse Chip and TRAF6 (Genomeditech, China) along with scrambled siRNA controls (siNC) were transfected into CIA-FLS using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) under serum-free conditions. To maintain inflammatory phenotypes, cells were supplemented with 10 ng/mL IL-1β for 24 h post-transfection for subsequent functional assessments. Experiments were repeated for at least three times independently. Cellular proliferation and migration were evaluated through EdU staining, cell cycle assay, and scratch wound healing assays, with experimental replicates performed in triplicate. Detailed methodologies are provided in Supplementary Materials.

Tranwell assay

Transwell assay was used to determine the migration and invasion of CIA-FLS. CIA-FLS were resuspended in free-serum medium and added into the upper chamber (8 μm, Nest, China) with a density of 1 × 104 cells, while the lower chamber was filled with 600 μL DMEM with 10% FBS. After incubation, carefully remove the cells remaining in the upper chamber. The migrated cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, stained with 1% crystal violet for 30 min, and three random filed view of each well were photographed under the microscope. For invasion, Matrigel was added into the upper chamber before cells seeding. The subsequent steps followed those of migration assay.

Molecular docking

The GRAMM software was employed for protein-protein interaction analysis using rigid-body docking methodology. The experimental protocol consisted of: (1) Searching target protein sequences from UniProtKB database; (2) Utilizing SWISS-MODEL homology modeling server to predict optimal three-dimensional structures; (3) Conducting molecular docking simulations with generated PDB files. Computational processing yielded 10 distinct docking conformations, with the top-ranked model demonstrating optimal binding affinity. Binding free energy values were calculated using empirical scoring functions to evaluate interaction stability.

AAV injection

The AAV5 vector carrying Chip overexpression construct (CMV-ZsGreen1-WPRE) was generated by Genomeditech. Intra-articular administration of AAV-Chip (1×10^13 vg/mL, 10 μL/joint) was performed two days post booster immunization, with AAV-GFP (named AAV-NC) serving as the control group. Knee samples were harvested four weeks post-injection for subsequent evaluation of Chip overexpression efficacy.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were repeated at least three times, with data presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, USA). Student's unpaired t-test was used to analyze the difference between two group. The analysis of more than two groups was conducted with One-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett's multiple comparisons test for comparing difference between each group and the control group. Two-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni's multiple comparisons test was adopted for time-matched analyses. A p-value <0.05 was established for statistical significance.

The results of IF show that CHIP expression in the joint synovium of CIA mice was downregulated compared with that of normal mice (Fig. 1A). Bioinformatic analysis of the NCBI's Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (GSE89408) also identified a significant downregulation of CHIP mRNA in RA synovial tissue compared to both healthy controls and osteoarthritis (OA) patients (Fig. 1B). Relative to primary FLS isolated from healthy mice (Normal-FLS), both mRNA and protein levels of CHIP were significantly reduced in FLS derived from CIA mice (CIA-FLS) (Fig. 1C and D). To determine the role of CHIP in the pathological progress of RA, a CIA model was established using Chip knockdown mice (Chip+/−) (Fig. 2A). Moreover, the Chip+/− mice exhibited exacerbated arthritis manifestations characterized by erythema, joint deformity, swelling of hind paw joints, and statistically significant elevations in both arthritis index and paw swelling relative to WT controls (Fig. 2C and D). Histological analysis of the knee joints also conducted in 8-week-old Chip+/− mice and littermate WT mice before CIA induction (Fig. S2). The results suggested that there are no signs of synovial hyperplasia, inflammatory cell infiltration or bone erosion before CIA induction. Histological analysis revealed that synovial hyperplasia, inflammatory cells infiltration and pannus formation of Chip+/− CIA mice were worse than those observed in WT CIA mice (Fig. 2E and H-J) (p < 0.05). In addition, Safranin O and Fast Green staining demonstrated that the layer of cartilage in Chip+/− CIA mice were clearly thinner than in WT controls, suggesting the severity of cartilage erosion was more significantly greater in Chip+/− CIA mice than in WT controls (Fig. 2F and K). TRAP staining revealed abundant osteoclasts in Chip+/− CIA mice, and Chip deletion could markedly enhanced arthritis-associated osteoclasts formation and bone destruction (Fig. 2G and L). IF staining of the joint synovium was further performed to examine the expression of proliferation cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) (a proliferation marker). The result revealed that the synovial proliferation was markedly exacerbated in the CIA of Chip+/− mice (Fig. 2M). CHIP depletion potentiates the proliferation and inflammatory phenotype of CIA-FLS

To further illustrate the effects of CHIP on FLS, which is the important cells of synovial membrane involved in the pathogenesis of RA, siRNA was used to knockdown the endogenous expression of CHIP in CIA-FLS in vitro. The knockdown efficiency of siRNAs for CHIP was confirmed at protein levels (Fig. 3A). The enhancing effect on cell proliferation was evidenced by EdU staining, where more EdU-positive cells in CHIP-depleted group than control group (Fig. 3B). In addition, siRNA-mediated knockdown of CHIP increased the proportion of cells in S and G2 phases, suggesting that loss of CHIP promoted cell cycle progression in CIA-FLS (Fig. S3). Next, we examined the mRNA expression of cell cycle regulatory factors such as Cdkn1a, Cdkn1c and Ccnd1 to further confirm the role of CHIP in cell cycle (Fig. 3C). Ccnd1 encodes Cyclin D1, while Cdkn1a, Cdkn1c are the critical cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors (CKI) [32]. Compared with the cells transfected with siNC, the expression levels of Cdkn1a and Cdkn1c were significantly decreased, while the expression of Ccnd1 was increased. We further characterized the role of CHIP on the migration and invasion in CIA-FLS. Transwell and wound-healing assays demonstrated that the migration and invasion rate of CIA-FLS was substantially enhance in CHIP knockdown CIA-FLS cells when compare to negative controls (Fig. 3D–G). CHIP overexpression inhibits the proliferation and inflammation in CIA-FLS

To determine whether CHIP is functionally involved in the pathogenic phenotype of RA FLS, we overexpressed CHIP by using lentivirus in CIA-FLS (Fig. 4A). The cell cycle assay showed that up-regulation of CHIP significantly reduced the proportion of cells in S and G2 phases in CIA-FLS (Fig. 4B and C). The expression of cell cycle regulatory factors (Cdkn1a, Cdkn2a, Cdkn1c and Ccnd1) was consistent with cell cycle analysis (Fig. 4D), indicating that CHIP is an essential player in the regulation of cell proliferation of CIA FLS. In wound healing migration assay, CHIP-overexpressed cells exhibited a lower migration rate than controls (p < 0.05) (Fig. 4E). Furthermore, qPCR analysis of inflammatory cytokine expression demonstrated a marked suppression of pro-inflammatory mediators (Il1b, Il6 and Il8). Conversely, a contrasting upregulation pattern was observed in anti-inflammatory cytokines (Il4 and Il13) (Fig. 4F). CHIP promotes the degradation of TRAF6 with a proteasome-dependent manner in CIA-FLS

To gain further insights into the role of CHIP in alleviating RA progression, we sought to identify potential substrates of CHIP. Our prior work demonstrated CHIP-mediated degradation of TRAF family protein suppresses NF-κB signaling during bone remodeling [16]. Notably, TRAF family genes are the critical RA susceptibility genes, with TRAF6 overexpression correlating with synovitis severity [33,34]. Given the inverse relationship between CHIP and TRAF6 expression, we hypothesized CHIP exerts RA-protective effects through TRAF6 suppression. To verify this hypothesis, we first confirmed the co-localization of CHIP and TRAF6 in the synovium of CIA mice by double immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 5A). Molecular docking result predicted the stable binding between CHIP and TRAF6, with a binding energy of −18.5 kcal/mol (Fig. 5B). Endogenous interaction of CHIP and TRAF6 also confirmed in CIA-FLS (Fig. 5C). Additionally, CHIP silencing markedly upregulated the protein levels of TRAF6 in CIA-FLS (Fig. 5D and E), but did not significantly affect other TRAF family proteins, such as TRAF2 and TRAF5 (Fig. S4). Conversely, overexpression studies demonstrated an inhibitory effect of CHIP on TRAF6 expression, with a marked reduction in TRAF6 protein levels compared to control lentivirus-infected CIA-FLS (Fig. 5D and E). These regulatory effects were further confirmed by IF analysis (Fig. 5F and G). The proteasome inhibitor rescue experiment indicated that CHIP overexpression promoted the degradation of TRAF6, and the expression levels of TRAF6 reversed with MG132 treatment (Fig. 5H and I). CHIP regulates the pro-inflammatory phenotypes of CIA-FLS by targeting TRAF6

To further investigate whether TRAF6 knockdown rescue the pro-inflammatory phenotypes of CHIP in CIA-FLS, we established double gene knockdown model in CIA-FLS through simultaneous transfection of CIA-FLS with both siChip and siTraf6. CHIP knockdown significantly enhanced the migratory and invasive capacity of CIA-FLS, while these effects were rescued by Chip and Traf6 double knockdown (Fig. 6A and B, and Fig. S5). In addition, the number of EdU-positive cells was significantly enhanced in the siChip group compared with siNC group. In contrast, the number of EdU-positive cells was significantly decreased in the Chip and Traf6 double knockdown group in comparison with siChip treated group (Fig. 6C and D). Our results revealed that TRAF6 inhibition could rescue CHIP depletion-mediated promotion of proliferation in CIA-FLS. Further, TRAF6 inhibition also normalized the higher expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including Il1b, Il8 and Tnfa in CIA-FLS transfected with siChip (Fig. 6E). Meanwhile, TRAF6 restoration also reversed the anti-inflammatory and proliferation-blocking effects of CHIP overexpression in CIA-FLS (Fig. S6). Moreover, TRAF6 inhibition effectively reversed the enhanced phosphorylation levels of NF-κB (p65) induced by CHIP deficiency in CIA-FLS (Fig. 6F and G). Discussion

CHIP is a critical E3 ubiquitin ligase and molecular chaperone that maintains proteins homeostasis through dynamic substrate regulation [11,35]. Emerging evidence highlights its multifaceted role in regulating autophagy/lysosomal function [36], bone remodeling [15], inflammation [12] and tumor immunity [37,38], yet its special function in RA and autoimmune diseases remains unclear. The synovial microenvironment in RA is characterized that FLS acquire tumor-like aggressive phenotypes, exhibiting hyperproliferation, invasion and sustained pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion [1,4]. Based on the important role of CHIP in inflammation modulation and tumor suppression, coupled with our preliminary data, we hypothesized its involvement in RA pathogenesis. Our present study revealed a novel protective role for CHIP, evidenced by the down-regulated expression of CHIP in CIA synovium and the more severe arthritis phenotype observed in Chip-knockdown mice. Mechanistically, CHIP promoted the degradation of TRAF6 in a proteasome dependent manner and suppressed CIA-FLS activation via modulating the TRAF6/p65 signaling pathway, thereby inhibiting synovial hyperplasia and inflammation in CIA mice. These results indicated that CHIP plays a critical role in CIA and potentially identified it as a promising therapeutic target for RA intervention.

CIA induction in Chip+/− mice differs from that in DBA/1 J mice in terms of CFA concentration, collagen type, and the interval of booster immunization to accommodate their inherent genetic differences in susceptibility. Chip+/− mice were used in the current study to verify the inhibitory effect of CHIP on arthritis at an in vivo level for the first time. Our findings demonstrate that CHIP deficiency exacerbates CIA pathogenesis, amplifying synovial hyperplasia, inflammatory responses, cartilage and bone erosion. Our results were similar to those previously observed on other inflammatory disease models in Chip knockdown mice. The absence of Chip aggravates the inflammatory response and increases the secretion of pro-inflammatory factors in mice [12,39]. A similar promoting effect of CHIP deletion on bone destruction has been reported in a previous study on bone metabolism [15]. These data collectively establish CHIP as a critical negative regulator in inflammatory arthritis.

Inflammation stands as a critical phenotype in RA, serving as a pivotal factor driving bone and cartilage erosion [3]. In RA, pro-inflammatory FLS are the main source of inflammatory cytokines, with these mediators amplifing the synovial inflammation, accelerate RA progression [40]. In our present study, CHIP silence upregulated the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and inhibited the expression of anti-inflammatory cytokines in CIA-FLS, whereas CHIP overexpression resulted in the opposite outcomes. These data demonstrated that CHIP represses the abnormal migration and inflammatory phenotypes of CIA-FLS.

TRAF6, a critical binding protein in TNF receptor-associated signaling, critically modulates Toll-like receptor (TLR)-mediated inflammatory and immune pathways [[41], [42], [43], [44]]. As a reported RA susceptibility gene [33], elevated TRAF6 expression of synovium correlates with synovitis severity in RA patients [34], while its inhibition ameliorates collagen-induced bone loss and matrix metalloproteinase dysregulation in RA models [25]. Consistently, we found that TRAF6 was upregulated in CIA murine synovium, in contrast to the low expression of CHIP, suggesting a potential regulatory relationship between them. Molecular docking and functional assays confirmed that CHIP interacts with TRAF6 and promotes the proteasome degradation of TRAF6. In vitro rescue experiments demonstrated that CHIP exerts protective roles by targeting TRAF6 in CIA-FLS. Furthermore, CHIP treatment attenuated CIA progression in vivo by suppressing synovial hyperplasia and inflammation through TRAF6 modulation.

Bone erosion represents a progressive feature of RA and is caused by a disruption of bone homeostasis, which is driven by inflammatory cytokine-mediated receptor activator of nuclear factor κ-B ligand (RANKL) overexpression on FLS [3,45]. RANKL initiates and transmits osteoclast differentiation signals through a variety of intracellular signaling pathways by recruiting downstream TRAF6 [22,46,47]. Inhibition of synovial inflammation has been demonstrated to prevent the progression of bone erosion [3,45]. Our findings suggested that the AAV-Chip therapy markedly suppressed osteoclastogenesis and ameliorated CIA-associated bone loss. This therapeutic effect may be attributed to its dual mechanism: firstly, CHIP treatment effectively inhibits the secretion of inflammatory cytokines from FLS, thereby suppressing the activation of inflammatory osteoclasts. Furthermore, TRAF6 is a crucial mediator of RANKL signaling pathway and plays a pivotal role in osteoclast differentiation [15,20,22,48]. Our data suggested that AAV-Chip treatment may also mitigate bone erosion in CIA mice through the enhancement of TRAF6 degradation and subsequent inhibition of TRAF6-mediated osteoclast differentiation.

Recent studies have demonstrated that E3 ubiquitin ligase involves in the pathogenesis of RA by precisely regulating the certain protein stability. For example, FBW7 regulates the function of B cells to affect the production of autoantibodies in RA [49], while Mid1 specifically drives the proliferation and migration of FLS by degrading DPP4 [6]. The substrates of these E3 ligases are usually certain functional protein. In our experiments, we found that CHIP targets and degrades TRAF6 in CIA FLS. It is well-known that TRAF6 serves as a critical upstream regulator of pro-inflammatory cascades, linking the inflammation and bone metabolism in RA [20,21]. TRAF6 serves as a critical upstream regulator of NF-κB activation, primarily through its E3 ubiquitin ligase activity. It promotes the phosphorylation and degradation of IκB through its own ubiquitination, subsequently leading the nuclear translocation of NF-κB and initiating the transcription of inflammatory genes [50,51]. CHIP is a more direct intervention target, which inhibits the inflammatory signaling driven by degrading the pro-inflammatory E3 ligase TRAF6. This may help explain the dual therapeutic effects of AAV-Chip treatment observed in CIA mice.

Finally, a few limitations of our study that need to be discussed, and it also points to our future research directions. Because we used Chip knockdown mice instead of FLS-specific Chip conditional knockout mice, it is hard to fully exclude the contributions of other cells in vivo. The synovial fibroblast specifically knockout mice have been established and we will utilize them to better evaluate the effect of CHIP on FLS in the pathogenesis of RA in the future. Additionally, our results depend on the animal model and public data analysis. The clinical validation data from RA patients and humanized RA models are the key of next step. Although we confirmed that AAV-Chip excreted therapeutic effect by targeting TRAF6, a systematic assessment of its impact on other TRAF family proteins or potential off-target effects is absent. Moreover, comprehensive safety assessment via other technologies like proteomics is the crucial step for future translation. Furthermore, given the challenges of gene therapy in clinical applications, small-molecule agonists of CHIP or inhibitors of TRAF6 ubiquitination could offer a more feasible pharmacological strategy. Computer-aided drug design approaches, such as virtual screening and molecular docking against the CHIP-TRAF6 interaction interface, may identify lead compounds capable of enhancing CHIP's E3 ligase activity or disrupting TRAF6/p65 signaling.

Conclusion

CHIP deficiency mice exhibit exacerbated joint inflammation and increased disease severity in CIA model. CHIP overexpression lowers inflammatory cytokine levels, inhibits RA-FLS proliferation and TRAF6/p65 signaling, ultimately alleviating synovial hyperplasia, bone loss and ameliorating the progression of CIA. Our present investigation provides mechanistic insight into the pathogenesis of RA while highlighting CHIP as a promising target for developing novel therapeutic strategies in RA.
