Radiographic outcomes and subsidence rate in hyperlordotic versus standard lordotic interbody spacers in patients undergoing anterior cervical discectomy and fusion
Authors: Rajkishen Narayanan, Nicholas B. Pohl, Jonathan Dalton, Yunsoo Lee, Alexa Tomlak, Anthony Labarbiera, Meryem Guler, Emilie Sawicki, Sebastian I. Fras, Mark F. Kurd, John J. Mangan, Ian David Kaye, Jose A. Canseco, Alan S. Hilibrand, Alexander R. Vaccaro, Christopher K. Kepler, Gregory D. Schroeder, Joseph K. Lee
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:Anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) is a common surgery for patients with degenerative cervical disease and current interbody spacers utilized vary based on material composition, structure, and angle of lordosis. Currently, there is a lack of literature comparing subsidence rates or long-term radiographic outcomes with hyperlordotic and standard lordotic spacers. This study compares long-term radiographic outcomes, subsidence rate, and rate of fusion in patients who underwent ACDF with hyperlordotic or standard interbody placement.Materials and Methods:Patients who underwent 1–3-level ACDF with either a standard lordosis or hyperlordotic interbody were included. Standard radiographs were evaluated for C2–7 lordosis (CL), sagittal vertical axis, C2 slope (C2S), T1 slope (T1S), subsidence rate, and fusion.Results:Forty-five patients underwent ACDF with hyperlordotic interbody placement and after a 1:1 propensity match with standard lordotic patients, 90 patients were included. 1-year postoperative radiographs demonstrated the hyperlordotic cohort achieved higher CL (15.3° ± 10.6° vs. 9.58° ± 8.88°; P = 0.007). The change in CL (8.42° ± 9.42° vs. 0.94° ± 8.67°; P < 0.001), change in C2S (−4.02° ± 6.68° vs. −1.11° ± 5.42°; P = 0.026), and change in T1S (3.49° ± 7.30° vs. 0.04° ± 6.86°, P = 0.008) between pre- and postoperative imaging were larger in the hyperlordotic cohort. There was no difference in overall subsidence (P = 0.183) and rate of fusion (P = 0.353) between the cohorts.Conclusion:Hyperlordotic spacer placement in ACDF can provide increased CL compared to standard lordosis spacers, which can be considered for patients requiring restoration or maintenance of CL following ACDF.

INTRODUCTION

Anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) remains a common surgical approach for treating patients with degenerative cervical disease who fail to respond to nonoperative management.[1] The procedure has been shown to restore cervical alignment, which leads to improvement in Neck Disability Index scores, neck pain, and health-related quality of life.[23] Furthermore, an improvement specifically in cervical lordosis following ACDF has previously been associated with Japanese Orthopaedic Association scores, pain improvement, and a decrease of disability.[45]

Among the existing methods designed to improve cervical lordosis, achieve appropriate alignment, and decrease the incidence of subsidence following ACDF, the utilization of interbody implants with variable biomechanical properties has become an area of interest among medical device companies and surgeons.[4] Current interbody spacers can vary based on material composition, structure, as well as angle of lordosis.[678] Previous research has evaluated the use of anatomic, lordotic, and hyperlordotic interbody spacers with variable results in cervical lordotic improvement.[3910] However, in the studies that evaluated hyperlordotic spacer placement in ACDF patients, the incidence of subsidence and long-term radiographic outcomes were not compared to standard lordosis spacers.

The purpose of this study is to compare pre- and 1-year postoperative radiographic outcomes in patients who undergo 1–3-level ACDF with hyperlordotic and standard lordotic interbody spacer placement. In addition, interbody placement positioning, overall fusion rate at all operative levels, and incidence of subsidence will be assessed on postoperative radiographic imaging.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This was a retrospective cohort study performed at a single academic tertiary care center. After institutional review board approval that permitted a waiver of informed consent, a structured query language search was performed using Current Procedural Terminology codes to identify adult patients who underwent primary 1–3-level ACDF for cervical radiculopathy and/or myelopathy from 2019 to 2022. Manual chart review of operative notes then further identified patients who received an anterior plate and placement of a 10° PROTI 360°™ hyperlordotic spacer (DePuy Spine, Inc., Raynham, MA), an interbody device with 10° of lordosis, a footprint of 16 mm × 15 mm, with varying degrees of height as determined by surgeon intraoperatively [Figure 1a]. Additional patients were identified through chart review who underwent ACDF using an anterior plate with a standard lordosis structural allograft spacer (7° lordosis) [Figure 1b]. The standard lordosis spacers were composed of cortical-cancellous allograft while the hyperlordotic cages were made of polyetheretherketone (PEEK) with an integrated layer of titanium. Patients under 18 years old, treated with an interbody spacer that did not have 10° or 7° lordosis, with incomplete medical records, or who underwent ACDF for revision surgery, traumatic injury, or infection were excluded.

Patient demographics and surgical characteristics were collected through the additional chart review of the electronic medical record. The demographics and surgical characteristics collected include age, sex, race, body mass index (BMI), smoking history, diabetes mellitus history, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), and number of vertebral levels fused.

Pre- and postoperative radiographic measurements were collected using our institution’s picture archiving and communication systems database (Sectra, AB, Linkoping, Sweden). Preoperative, initial postoperative, and 1-year postoperative neutral lateral cervical spine radiographs were obtained for each patient. C2–7 lordosis (CL), C2–7 sagittal vertical axis (SVA), C2 slope (C2S), and T1 slope (T1S) were measured on preoperative and 1-year postoperative radiographs. Measurements of pre- and 1-year postoperative radiographic parameters are listed in Table 1.[111213] Interbody positioning was measured on the initial postoperative radiograph utilizing the center point ratio. The center point ratio is the distance between the spacer midpoint to the midpoint of the vertebral body divided by the length of the superior endplate of the inferior vertebra.[1415] Anterior, middle, and posterior interbody positioning was defined as a center point ratio of >0.60, 0.40–0.59, and <0.39, respectively. Subsidence was evaluated by measurement of segmental vertebral body height on initial and 1-year postoperative radiographic images. Segmental vertebral body height was determined by measuring the distance between the midpoint of the cranial vertebral upper endplate and the midpoint of the caudal vertebra lower endplate at the fusion level. Subsidence was determined to be present if loss in segmental vertebral body height between initial and 1-year postoperative imaging measurements was ≥2 mm.[16] Postoperative cervical fusion was measured on 1–2-year postoperative flexion-extension lateral radiographs. Fusion was determined by measuring the interspinous motion for each operating level between the flexion and extension measurements. Patients were considered to demonstrate fusion if the interspinous motion was <2 mm.[17] Interrater reliability tests for all perioperative radiographic measurements are listed in Table 2.

After confirming pre- and postoperative imaging as well as spacer placement, 189 patients were initially screened. Due to statistical differences in demographics, we performed a 1:1 propensity score match for patients undergoing ACDF with hyperlordotic interbody spacer placement with standard lordotic spacer ACDF patients. The propensity score match controlled for age, sex, BMI, CCI, smoking history, and number of vertebral levels fused. A caliper of 0.1 was used to conduct the match and the number of levels fused was matched categorically and not continuously. There were no discrepancies in matching the number of levels fused. Statistical analysis was performed comparing demographics and radiographic metrics in patients with hyperlordotic and standard lordotic interbody placement. t-tests or Mann–Whitney U-tests were used to compare continuous data and Fisher’s exact tests were used for categorical data. All statistical analyses were completed using R studio (version 4.1.2, Vienna, Austria) and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

In total, 45 patients underwent ACDF and hyperlordotic interbody spacer placement with preoperative and 1-year postoperative imaging. After a 1:1 propensity match with standard lordotic patients, a total of 90 patients were included for final analysis. The average follow-up rate was 1.85 ± 0.86 years. When comparing the two cohorts, there were no significant differences in levels operated between hyperlordotic and standard lordotic groups [Table 3].

Radiographic parameter measurements taken at 1 year postoperatively demonstrated the hyperlordotic cohort achieved more lordosis in terms of CL (15.3° ±10.6° vs. 9.58° ±8.88°; P = 0.007). There were no other differences between 1-year of postoperative radiographic measurements assessed. When evaluating 1-year postoperative radiographic measurement change from preoperative measurements, the change in CL (ΔCL) (8.42° ±9.42° vs. 0.94° ±8.67°; P < 0.001), change in C2S (ΔC2S) (−4.02° ±6.68° vs. −1.11° ±5.42°; P = 0.026), and change in T1S (ΔT1S) (3.49° ±7.30° vs. 0.04° ±6.86°, P = 0.008) were significantly different between the hyperlordotic and standard lordotic cohorts. There were no differences in the change in SVA (ΔSVA) (P = 0.721) between the two cohorts [Table 4].

The incidence of subsidence at the most cranial/single-level spacer was higher in the standard lordotic cohort compared to the hyperlordotic cohort (28.9% vs. 6.67%; P = 0.013). However, there were no differences in subsidence incidence at the second most cranial spacer (P = 0.088), caudal spacer (P = 0.412), and overall subsidence at one or more levels (P = 0.183) between the cohorts. In patients with flexion-extension radiographs, both hyperlordotic and standard lordotic patients demonstrated similar rates of fusion at all operating levels on flexion-extension radiographs (86.1% vs. 73.2%, P = 0.353) [Table 4].

DISCUSSION

Appropriate cervical alignment following ACDF remains paramount to avoid complications and poor surgical outcomes. In this study, patients who underwent ACDF with hyperlordotic interbody placement demonstrated increased 1-year postoperative CL compared to those with a standard lordotic spacer. Hyperlordotic spacer patients also experienced increased postoperative ΔC2S, ΔCL, and ΔT1S. While the incidence of subsidence of the most cranial/single-level spacer was shown to be lower in patients treated with hyperlordotic spacer, the overall incidence of subsidence in at least one level was similar between the patient cohorts. Furthermore, the rate of fusion at all operative levels was similar in both hyperlordotic and standard lordotic patients. These findings demonstrate that when maintenance or restoration of cervical lordosis is of concern in patients undergoing ACDF, utilization of an interbody spacer with a higher lordotic angle does allow for a greater lordotic change than standard lordotic spacers with a similar rate of fusion and no increased risk of subsidence.

Previous research has established the need for appropriate postoperative CL; however, the current literature comparing interbody spacers designed with varying degrees of lordosis in ACDF is limited and inconclusive. In a study by Al-Adli et al. evaluating radiographic outcomes in 101 patients who underwent ACDF with either anatomic or lordotic interbody spacer placement, both implant types restored CL and T1S minus CL (T1S − CL), a previously established marker for cervical deformity.[918] However, there was no difference in ΔCL or ΔSVA between the cohorts.[9] A study by Shahrestani et al. evaluated a series of 46 patients who underwent ACDF with >10° hyperlordotic spacer placement and while not directly reported by the authors, ΔCL based on the difference between average last follow-up and preoperative CL was 7.9°, which was similar to our reported ΔCL which was 8.42° ±9.42°.[10] Finally, a study by Li et al. compared 80 patients who underwent ACDF with either hyperlordotic 15° spacers or standard lordotic spacers and determined that both patient cohorts demonstrated comparable ΔCL. However, the authors only evaluated radiographic measurements on immediate postoperative imaging and additionally did not evaluate subsidence rates in patients treated with hyperlordotic spacers.[3] Overall, our findings agree with previous radiographic studies of hyperlordotic spacers. We further demonstrate these spacers are associated with increased ΔCL and similar 1-year subsidence when compared with propensity-matched ACDF patients treated with standard lordotic spacers.

Prior research has focused on the importance of T1S in global spinal alignment as it pertains to the junction of the cervical and thoracic spine.[19] These studies have suggested the T1S may aid in sagittal balance evaluation and the mismatch of T1S and CL was associated with cervical deformity.[20] Furthermore, Liu et al. evaluated 215 patients who underwent whole-spine X-rays to identify factors associated with T1S alignment and determined CL had a linear relationship with T1S.[19] A study evaluating kinetic MRI images of 83 patients with symptomatic degenerative spine conditions determined individuals with a larger T1S required a larger CL to preserve physiologic cervical sagittal balance.[21] Overall, our findings corroborate this previously described association between CL and T1S. Therefore, the use of hyperlordotic spacers in ACDF patients must be carefully considered as a potential tool to address lordosis and overall cervicothoracic alignment. However, surgeons should be cognizant of the preoperative lordosis and T1S to prevent potential postoperative misalignment.

The incidence of subsidence on 1-year postoperative imaging was determined to be higher in standard lordotic spacers at the most cranial/single-level spacer, but the overall subsidence incidence at one or more levels was similar in both patient cohorts. Subsidence is known to be clinically relevant, as the incidence of interbody spacer subsidence has been previously shown to be associated with poor radiographic outcomes, incidence of pseudoarthrosis, recurrence of preoperative symptoms, and worse patient-reported outcomes.[22] In terms of cervical alignment and lordosis, Duey et al. evaluated 131 patients who underwent 1–3-level ACDF and determined subsidence was significantly associated with loss of lordosis.[23] Interestingly, a prior study by Nguyen et al. showed that hyperlordotic interbody placement in patients who underwent anterior lumbar interbody fusion was at greater risk of subsidence.[24] Our results show that hyperlordotic spacers in ACDF were not associated with an increased risk for subsidence and still allowed for greater ΔCL postoperatively. We postulate this decrease in subsidence incidence is due to the larger footprint of the hyperlordotic interbody utilized as an increased footprint size leads to reduced stress load into the endplate after surgery.[25] Hyperlordotic spacers had middle positioning compared to standard lordotic spacers which we further attribute to the larger footprint of the hyperlordotic interbodies utilized. The larger spacer footprint may contribute to more middle positioning and may contribute to the differences in radiographic alignment and subsidence rates demonstrated in this study. In addition, the fusion rates were similar in both patient cohorts. Fraser and Härtl previously reported the overall fusion rate in patients undergoing 1–3-level ACDF was 87.3%, which is similar to our patient cohorts.[26] This may indicate that the use of hyperlordotic interbody spacers in 1–3-level ACDF may provide larger lordotic changes in patients requiring more substantial lordotic restoration without additional risk for subsidence or pseudoarthrosis compared to commonly utilized standard lordotic spacers.

There are several limitations to this study. First, due to its retrospective nature, there is the potential for selection bias. Second, on average, SVA was <4 cm in both cohorts, therefore, this may not represent patients with true deformity. Future studies in patients with SVA >4 cm should be considered. Third, the interbody spacers evaluated were structural allograft and PROTI 360°™ hyperlordotic spacers, which are made of cortical-cancellous allograft material and PEEK with titanium, respectively.[2728] These differences in biomechanical materials could potentially have influenced patient cervical alignment and subsidence rates.[29] Fourth, spacer positioning was evaluated in this study; however, information regarding the spacer footprint was unavailable by manual chart review. Currently, there are several commercially available footprints for structural allografts ranging from 11.5 to 13 mm whereas the hyperlordotic PROTI spacers are 9 mm in length.[2728] It is likely that the demonstrated difference in spacer positioning may be due to differences in footprint size. Additional research should evaluate the association between spacer positioning, spacer lordosis, and radiographic measures. Finally, we did not evaluate patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), complications, or reoperations in this study. As global cervical alignment has previously been associated with postoperative PROMs,[13] further studies should evaluate radiographic outcomes following hyperlordotic interbody placement in conjunction with these surgical outcome measures.

CONCLUSION

Hyperlordotic spacer placement in patients undergoing 1–3-level ACDF led to larger changes in CL than in patients treated with standard lordotic interbody spacers. Furthermore, the fusion rate at all operative levels and the overall incidence of subsidence at one or more levels was found to be similar in the patient cohorts evaluated. Our results ultimately demonstrated hyperlordotic spacer placement can provide increased CL by design in comparison to standard lordotic spacers. This study provides information that may be utilized by surgeons when selecting an appropriate interbody spacer in patients requiring restoration or maintenance of CL following ACDF.
