Ononin-modified Chitosan/β-glycerophosphate hydrogel-loaded adipose-derived stem cells promote the repair of diabetic wounds in rats
Authors: Fang Xu, Jian-chao Peng, Yi-ling Li, Zhi-xin Huang, Meng-jun Teng, Cui-liu Gan, Ting Liao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the mechanistic synergy of an ononin-modified chitosan/β-glycerophosphate (CS/β-GP) hydrogel loaded with adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs) in repairing diabetic wounds (DWs). We specifically aimed to determine if ononin could biochemically remodel the hostile diabetic microenvironment to enhance ADSCs survival and potency.MethodsPrimary rat ADSCs were characterized by flow cytometry. A thermosensitive CS/β-GP hydrogel was fabricated, and its microstructure was characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). he biological response of ADSCs to ononin was assessed via CCK-8, scratch assays and Live/Dead staining to identify the optimal concentration. A diabetic wound model in rats was established, with all rats randomly assigned into five groups: Control, model, ononin (On), ADSCs, and ononin+ADSCs (On+ADSCs). Wound healing rates were recorded at days 0, 4, 8, and 12 post-treatment. At 12th day, the synergistic impact of ononin and ADSCs on the wound microenvironment was evaluated by measuring healing rates, inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6). Additionally, the cumulative release profiles of IGF-1, VEGF, and TGF-β3 from the hydrogel-loaded ADSCs were monitored over 96 h, fitting a Korsmeyer-Peppas kinetic model to characterize the sustained release behavior. Histopathological evaluation, immunohistochemistry for CD31, collagen I, and α-SMA, and Western blot analysis for VEGF, VEGFR2, and CD31 were performed for mechanistic exploration.ResultsSEM confirmed a three-dimensional interconnected porous microstructure of the CS/β-GP hydrogel, providing a biomimetic scaffold for cell retention. 20µM ononin significantly promoted ADSCs proliferation and migration without observable cytotoxicity (P < 0.05). The Ononin release profile from the hydrogel followed a sustained release pattern, in accordance with the Korsmeyer-Peppas model. The hydrogel demonstrated sustained release of both Ononin and ADSCs-derived growth factors. Notably, ononin acted as a biochemical modifier that enhanced the paracrine activity of ADSCs. Compared with the model group, the On+ADSCs group exhibited the most significant acceleration in wound closure, re-epithelialization, and collagen deposition. This was accompanied by a robust suppression of inflammatory cytokines and a marked upregulation of CD31, collagen I, and α-SMA. Mechanistically, the combination therapy potently activated the VEGF/VEGFR2 signaling axis, significantly outperforming monotherapies (P < 0.05).ConclusionOnonin-modified CS/β-GP hydrogel effectively promotes diabetic wound healing by optimizing the inflammatory microenvironment and amplifying ADSCs-mediated angiogenesis through the VEGF/VEGFR2 axis. Introduction

Diabetic wounds (DWs), affecting more than 20% of individuals with diabetes mellitus (DM), represent a formidable clinical challenge characterized by stagnant healing trajectories [1]. Defective neovascularization and diminished peripheral perfusion are the primary pathophysiological drivers of delayed healing in DWs [2]. Effective repair critically depends on angiogenesis—a coordinated process that restores oxygen and nutrient supply to the wound bed, thereby facilitating fibroblast activation, collagen deposition, and re-epithelialization [3, 4]. These regenerative events are principally orchestrated by the vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and its key downstream effector, VEGF receptor 2 (VEGFR2) [5–7]. Crucially, the persistent inflammatory microenvironment and high proteolytic activity within the DWs led to a dysregulated hypoxia response [8]. This biochemical dysregulation results in inadequate VEGF/VEGFR2 expression and impaired capillary formation, which not only thwarts endogenous repair mechanisms but also poses a significant barrier to the survival and therapeutic efficacy of exogenous cell-based therapies [9, 10].

Adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs) have emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for wound repair, owing to their potent pro-angiogenic capabilities mediated through the paracrine secretion of essential cytokines, including IGF-1, TGF-β3, and VEGF [11, 12]. Nevertheless, the clinical efficacy of direct ADSCs transplantation is frequently compromised by the hostile diabetic microenvironment (DME) [13]. The DME is characterized by an accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), high protease activity, and pro-inflammatory factors, which severely impair cell retention, survival, and bioactivity upon delivery [14]. To overcome these biological barriers, the development of an advanced protective delivery vehicle is essential [15, 16]. In this study, we utilized a Chitosan/β-glycerophosphate (CS/β-GP) hydrogel—a biocompatible, thermosensitive natural polymer capable of mimicking the biomimetic extracellular matrix (ECM) architecture [17]. Beyond providing structural support, this 3D scaffold acts as a “physical shield” that isolates encapsulated ADSCs from oxidative stress and enzymatic degradation. By creating a stable, protective sanctuary, the CS/β-GP hydrogel ensures superior cell retention and sustained therapeutic delivery, thereby addressing the physical limitations of cell therapy in chronic diabetic pathology [18, 19].

However, physical protection alone is often insufficient to overcome the deep-seated biochemical inhibitory signals characteristic of DWs. Beyond structural support, active modulation of the pathological microenvironment is critical to unlock the full therapeutic potential of cell therapies. Ononin, a prominent isoflavonoid derived from Astragalus membranaceus, has demonstrated potent antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and pro-proliferative activities across diverse disease models [20–22]. For instance, Icariin, as an isoflavonoid, has been shown to robustly induce angiogenesis by up-regulating the mRNA expression of VEGF, VEGFR1, and VEGFR2 [23].

Despite these individual merits, the potential of an integrated, ononin-modified CS/β-GP hydrogel loaded with ADSCs in orchestrating the complex stages of diabetic wound repair remains largely unexplored. Therefore, this study investigated the therapeutic efficacy of this composite biomaterial and its impact on the VEGF/VEGFR2 signaling axis in diabetic rats. By integrating physical protection with biochemical priming, we aim to provide a novel therapeutic strategy and establish a robust mechanistic foundation for the management of chronic, non-healing DWs.

Materials and methods

Animals

Eight-week-old male Sprague-Dawley rats (200–250 g) were procured from the Animal Experimentation Centre of Guangxi Medical University. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Liuzhou People’s Hospital (KY-2023-104) and conducted in compliance with NIH Guidelines for Laboratory Animal Care and Use.

Fabrication of CS/β-GP hydrogel

CS/β-GP hydrogel was synthesized using established protocols [16], A specific amount of chitosan (CS) powder was dissolved in 0.5% (w/v) acetic acid solution to prepare a 2% (w/v) CS solution and magnetically stirring for 3 h. After complete dissolution, the solution was autoclaved for 30 min. Separately, a specific amount of β-glycerophosphate (β-GP) was added culture medium to prepare a 56% (w/v) β-GP solution. After a 15-min ice bath, the 56% (w/v) β-GP solution was added dropwise to the 2% (w/v) CS solution at a ratio of 1:5, with gentle shaking of the mixture during the addition, and the pH of the solution was adjusted to 7.4 using a 0.1 mol/L NaOH solution to obtain the final CS/β-GP hydrogel solution. A stock solution of ononin (MedChemExpress, USA) was prepared at a concentration of 100 mg/mL in DMSO. This solution was subsequently diluted with culture medium to a final concentration of 2 mg/ml. A 56% (w/v) β-GP solution was then prepared using this diluted ononin solution. The ononin-modified CS/β-GP hydrogel was obtained by combining the ononin-containing β-GP solution with the 2% (w/v) CS solution, followed by pH adjustment to 7.4.

Scanning electron microscope

The CS/β-GP hydrogel prepared as described above was freeze-dried at − 80 °C for 3d and subsequently coated with a gold-palladium layer for 4–5 min using an MC1000 ion sputtering machine (HITACHI, JPN), and then analyzed using the SU8100 scanning electron microscope (SEM, HITACHI, JPN).

In vitro drug release studies from hydrogels

Firstly, ononin solutions at various concentrations (5, 10, 15, 20, 25, and 30 µg/mL) were prepared, and their absorbance was measured at 258 nm using a UV-visible (UV-Vis) spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, JPN). A standard calibration curve for ononin release was then established based on the Lambert–Beer law. Subsequently, 500 mg of ononin-modified CS/β-GP hydrogel was immersed in 40 mL of PBS (pH 7.4) and incubated at 37 °C. The absorbance of the collected supernatant was measured at 258 nm by UV-Vis, and the cumulative release profile was calculated by back-calculating the ononin concentration based on the established standard curve. Isolation, culture and identification of ADSCs

The method for isolating ADSCs is adapted from previous literature [24]. The rats were anaesthetized with 1% pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg, i.p., Sigma, USA), and then subjected to surgery. Adipose tissue was isolated from the medial groin of the rat thigh. After removing the adipose tissue membrane and blood vessels, the adipose tissue was digested with collagenase and then centrifugated to obtain the cell deposition, which was resuspended and seeded into culture flasks, followed by incubation at 37 °C in a 5%CO₂ atmosphere. Until the third-passage cells were collected for experiments. Third-passage ADSCs (approximately 1 × 10⁵ cells per tube) were incubated with antibodies against CD90, CD105, CD34, and CD45 (Abcam, USA) for 45 min at room temperature in the dark. The immune phenotyping of cells was analyzed using a flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA), and data were processed with FlowJo v10.9.0 software.

Live/dead assay

The cytocompatibility of the CS/β-GP hydrogel and its effects on ADSC viability were evaluated using a Live/Dead assay. ADSCs were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 3 × 105 cells per well and assigned to two experimental groups: the ADSCs group (cells encapsulated in CS/β-GP hydrogel) and the On + ADSCs group (cells encapsulated in CS/β-GP hydrogel modified with 20µM ononin). After the designated incubation period, the supernatant was aspirated, and the wells were washed multiple times with PBS. A Live/Dead working solution was prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Invitrogen, USA), added to the wells, and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. Following incubation, the working solution was removed, and a small volume of PBS was added to maintain hydration. The samples were immediately imaged using a fluorescence microscope (Evos FL Auto, USA). The proportion of dead cells was subsequently quantified by analyzing multiple fields of view per group.

Quantification of growth factors

To evaluate the effect of ononin on the secretion of growth factors by ADSCs. ADSCs were treated with 20µM ononin, and the supernatant was collected at various time points. Samples were added to 96-well plates and incubated at 37 °C for 45 min. The concentrations of growth factors were measured using the corresponding ELISA kits, and the absorbance at 450 nm was recorded using a microplate reader (BioTek Epoch, USA). The concentrations of IGF-1, VEGF, and TGF-β3 at different time points were calculated based on the corresponding standard curves.

Animal grouping, modelling and intervention experiments

All animal procedures were approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Liuzhou People’s Hospital (KY-2023-104). Male Sprague-Dawley rats (200–220 g) were randomly assigned into five groups (n = 10 per group): (1) Control group (non-diabetic); (2) Model group (diabetic, saline-treated); (3) On group (ononin-modified CS/β-GP hydrogel); (4) ADSCs group (ADSCs-loaded CS/β-GP hydrogel); and (5) On + ADSCs group (ononin-modified CS/β-GP hydrogel loaded with ADSCs). To induce diabetes, rats (except the control group) were intraperitoneal injection with 45 mg/kg streptozotocin (Sigma, USA) while the control group received the same volume of saline instead. After 3 days, the blood glucose of the rats was detected by a blood glucose measuring instrument. Rats were considered successfully modeled as diabetic if they exhibited fasting blood glucose (FBG) levels ≥ 280 mg/dL, accompanied by classic symptoms such as polydipsia, polyphagia, and dull, thinning fur [25].

For groups involving ADSCs, cells were encapsulated in the CS/β-GP hydrogel at a concentration of 1 × 106 cells/mL (2 mL of hydrogel solution per wound). In the ononin-modified groups, the concentration of ononin was maintained at 20µM, based on the optimal concentration determined from prior in vitro assays.

On Day 0, rats were anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of 1% pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg). After shaving and disinfection, a circular full-thickness skin wound (3.0 cm in diameter) was created on the dorsal midline using a surgical template. Hemostasis was achieved via compression with sterile gauze [26]. The control and model groups received saline-moistened dressings, while the intervention groups were treated with their respective hydrogel formulations. All wounds were covered with sterile gauze and secured with transparent film dressings.

Gross observation of wound healing

Wound healing observations and specimen collection were performed on days 0, 4, 8, and 12 after treatment. The skin wound for each group was recorded at each time point by an SLR camera (Canon, JPN) and analyzed ImageJ v2.3.0 software. Wound healing rate:

After 12th day of photographing, the wound scab was removed with tooth tweezers and scalpel to fully expose the wound. The wound tissue was completely removed at 2 mm outside the wound edge, and the blood stains were washed with normal saline. The removed wound tissue was stored in liquid nitrogen or 4% paraformaldehyde solution for subsequent experimental purposes.

Quantification of inflammatory cytokines

On Day 12 post-intervention, wound tissue samples were harvested from each group for cytokine quantification. The wound tissues were homogenized with PBS and centrifuged at 5000 × g for 10 min. The supernatant was then collected for subsequent analysis. The levels of inflammatory cytokines were measured using the corresponding ELISA kits (Thermo Fisher, USA). The OD value was measured at 450 nm of the microplate reader (BioTek Epoch, USA) to quantify the concentration of each cytokine.

Pathological examination

Sections of the wound tissues were prepared at a thickness of 4 μm. During Hematoxylin-Eosin (H&E) staining, hematoxylin staining solution was used to stain for 5 min, and 1% hydrochloric acid alcohol was used to differentiate for 5s after washing with running water. After dyeing with 1% eosin dye for 90s, gradient alcohol dehydration and xylene transparency, the neutral gum was sealed. During Masson staining, the sections were taken after dewaxing and hydration and fixed in Bouin’s solution overnight at room temperature for 8 h. After rinsing with running water, the tissues were immersed in 1% phosphomolybdic acid aqueous solution for 5 min, stained with aniline blue solution, soaked in 1% glacial acetic acid for 1 min, dehydrated and transparentized, and sealed with neutral gum. Histological changes, including capillary distribution, epithelialization, and collagen deposition, were observed under a microscope at 200 × magnification. The area of collagen fibers was quantified from captured images using ImageJ v2.3.0 software.

Immunohistochemistry

The wound tissues were routinely paraffin-embedded. The sections were incubated in 10mmol/L sodium citrate buffer for 15 min for antigen retrieval The sections were then incubated with primary antibodies against α-SMA (1:500, Abcam, UK), CD31 (1:2000, Abcam, UK), and Collagen I (1:500, Abcam, UK) overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, secondary antibody (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA) was added and incubated for 1 h. The coloration and distribution of α-SMA, CD31 and collagen I in wound cells of rats were detected under microscope. The positive staining of each index was determined by ImageJ v2.3.0 software, and the positive expression level was used to represent the protein expression level per unit area. The analysis results for Collagen I and α-SMA are expressed as the average density of positive staining areas (IOD/area). For CD31, the average number of positive microvessels was manually counted across five random fields per slide. All histological assessments were performed by two independent observers, blinded to the experimental groups, ensuring the objectivity of the results.

Western blot

The wound tissues were retrieved from liquid nitrogen, and lysate (RIPA: PSMF = 100:1) was added. Following homogenization and lysis on ice, the tissues was centrifuged at 5000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to collect the supernatant for subsequent analysis. Primary antibodies against VEGF (1:5000, Huabio, CN), VEGFR2 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), CD31 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), and β-actin (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA) were incubated on the samples and then used to incubate the samples with secondary antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology, USA) diluted 1:1000 for 2 h at 24 °C. Chemiluminescent substrate was then applied for signal development, and the ImageJ v2.3.0 software was used to quantify data.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism (v10.0). All quantitative data are expressed as the Mean ± Standard Deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments. Prior to inferential analysis, the normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and the homogeneity of variance was verified via Levene’s test. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test to identify significant differences between specific pairs. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001).

Results

Characterization of CS/β-GP hydrogel

The prepared CS/β-GP hydrogel was a colorless, transparent liquid that maintained a flowable state at 24 °C. Upon increasing the temperature to 37 °C, the hydrogel underwent a rapid sol-gel transition, becoming non-flowable and solidifying (Fig. 1A). This thermosensitive property is highly advantageous for diabetic wound dressings, as it allows for ease of application and subsequent formation of a protective microenvironment suitable for cell survival [27]. SEM analysis of the lyophilized hydrogel revealed an irregular, three-dimensional porous network structure (Fig. 1B). This interconnected porosity is essential for nutrient transport and gas exchange, providing an ideal scaffolding environment for encapsulated cells.

(B) SEM cross-section images of hydrogels (scale bar: 100 μm). (C) Chemical structure of ononin. (D) The cumulative release curve of ononin. Plots of different kinetic models, including: (E) Zero order, (F) first order, (G) Higuchi diffusion model, and (H) Korsmeyer–Peppas equation

The in vitro release profile of ononin from CS/β-GP hydrogel was evaluated under simulated physiological conditions (Fig. 1C). The ononin-loaded hydrogel exhibited an initial burst release of approximately 43.6% within the first 8 h, followed by a sustained release phase, reaching a cumulative release of 95.3% at 24 h (Fig. 1D).

To analyze the release kinetics, the data were fitted to zero-order, first-order, Higuchi, and Korsmeyer–Peppas models (Fig. 1E-H). Among these, the Higuchi model provided the best fit (R²=0.983), indicating that the release was primarily diffusion-controlled within the hydrogel matrix. The zero-order kinetic model also demonstrated a good fit (R²=0.967), suggesting that the release rate remained nearly constant during the sustained release phase, with an approximate release rate of 4.6% per hour. The Korsmeyer–Peppas model yielded a release exponent (n = 0.97), consistent with zero-order kinetics, suggesting that hydrogel swelling and matrix erosion may have contributed to the release mechanism. The first-order model also showed a reasonable fit, indicating that the release was partially concentration-dependent. Collectively, these findings confirm that ononin-loaded CS/β-GP hydrogel exhibit sustained and stable release properties suitable for wound healing applications, enabling a continuous supply of ononin to the wound site while reducing the frequency of administration.

Characterization of ADSCs and the effects of ononin on cellular function

The morphological evolution of ADSCs was monitored via inverted light microscopy (Fig. 2A). Primary ADSCs initially exhibited a heterogeneous population, including spherical, polygonal, and spindle-shaped cells. By the third passage (P3), the cells achieved a more homogeneous and stable phenotype, characterized by an elongated, fibroblast-like spindle morphology with characteristic parallel or vortex-like growth patterns. Flow cytometric analysis confirmed the identity of the P3 ADSCs, showing high expression of the MSC-specific surface markers CD90 and CD105, while being negative for the hematopoietic markers CD45 and CD34 (Fig. 2B).

The cytotoxic profile of ononin on ADSCs was evaluated across a concentration range (0.1~100µM) using the CCK-8 assay (Fig. 2C). Ononin demonstrated excellent cytocompatibility at concentrations up to 20µM, where cell viability remained above 100%, suggesting a potential pro-proliferative effect. However, significant cytotoxicity was observed at higher concentrations, with viability dropping to approximately 78% at 50µM and 43% at 100µM. The calculated half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) was approximately 59µM. Consequently, 20µM ononin was selected as the optimal, non-toxic concentration for all subsequent functional assays.

The influence of ononin on the migratory capacity of ADSCs was assessed using a scratch-wound assay. Compared to the control group, treatment with 20µM ononin significantly accelerated the wound closure rate at 24 h post-scratch (Fig. 2D, E), indicating that ononin promotes the lateral migration of ADSCs. To evaluate the suitability of the CS/β-GP hydrogel as a delivery vehicle, the viability of encapsulated ADSCs was examined. Live/Dead fluorescence staining (Fig. S1) revealed that after 24 h of in situ gelation, the vast majority of ADSCs in both the CS/β-GP and On + CS/β-GP groups remained viable (green fluorescence), with an overall viability exceeding 90%. These findings confirm that neither the hydrogel matrix nor the 20µM ononin modification adversely affects the survival or integration of the encapsulated stem cells.

Ononin enhances ADSCs secretion of IGF-1, TGF-β3, and VEGF

The influence of 20µM ononin on the paracrine activity of ADSCs was quantitatively assessed via ELISA across a temporal gradient from 6 to 96 h. IGF-1 secretion gradually increased, reaching a peak at 24 h, followed by a gradual decline by 96 h (Fig. 3A). In contrast, TGF-β3 secretion peaked earlier at 12 h and then decreased steadily, indicating a high level of early-stage release by ADSCs (Fig. 3B). VEGF secretion was significantly higher than that of IGF-1 and TGF-β3, also peaking at 12 h before gradually declining (Fig. 3C), suggesting that ADSCs sustain the release of growth factors over time.

The healing of DWs by ononin-modified CS/β-GP hydrogel loaded ADSCs

The wound closure process was photographically documented on days 0, 4, 8, and 12 (Fig. 4A). By day 4, the On + ADSCs group exhibited a significant reduction in wound area compared to the Model group (P < 0.05, Fig. 4B), outperforming both the On and ADSCs groups. Macroscopically, wounds in the control and On + ADSCs groups appeared relatively dry with robust scab formation and significant wound edge contraction, with a notable absence of purulent secretions. Conversely, the model and ADSCs groups showed signs of impaired healing, characterized by the presence of suspected bacterial biofilms and minimal marginal convergence.

On days 8 and 12, the wound healing rates in the On + ADSCs group remained significantly higher than those in either the On or ADSCs groups, suggesting a synergistic effect between the ononin-modified matrix and the encapsulated stem cells. Notably, by day 12, the degree of re-epithelialization in the On + ADSCs group approached that of the control group. These findings demonstrate that the ononin-modified CS/β-GP hydrogel effectively mitigates the persistent inflammatory microenvironment and accelerates the transition from the inflammatory phase to the proliferative phase, thereby promoting superior wound closure in diabetic rats.

To ensure the reliability of the therapeutic evaluation, FBG levels were monitored throughout the study. Following streptozotocin induction, all diabetic rats (Model, On, ADSCs, and On + ADSCs groups) consistently maintained FBG levels exceeding 280 mg/dL over the 12-day experimental period, confirming the successful establishment of a stable diabetic phenotype (Fig. 4C).

Anti-inflammatory effects of ononin-modified CS/β-GP hydrogel loaded ADSCs on DWs

To further validate the anti-inflammatory ability of ononin-modified CS/β-GP hydrogel loaded ADSCs anti-inflammatory ability in vivo, The concentrations of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 were determined in rat wound tissues by ELISA. Compared with the model group, levels of these inflammatory cytokines were lower in the On, ADSCs, and On + ADSCs groups (P < 0.05), with the On + ADSCs group revealing greater reduction than either the On or ADSCs groups alone (P < 0.05). Additionally, the On group had lower cytokine levels than the ADSCs group (P < 0.05, Fig. 5A–C). To sum up, the detection of inflammatory factors in DWs showed that ononin-modified CS/β-GP hydrogel-loaded ADSCs could inhibit pro-inflammatory response and promote anti-inflammatory response by down-regulating TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6, which in turn promoted the healing of DWs in the direction of the inflammatory process.

Morphological observations on wound healing of DWs by ononin-modified CS/β-GP hydrogel-loaded ADSCs

Re-epithelialization and the restoration of skin architecture are hallmarks of successful wound repair [28]. To evaluate the quality of tissue regeneration, H&E staining was performed on wound tissues harvested at day 12 (Fig. 6A). In the control group, the regenerated skin exhibited a well-organized, stratified structure with distinct tissue layers, mature granulation tissue, and a near-complete absence of inflammatory cell infiltration. Conversely, the model group showed poorly defined tissue layers, disorganized cellular morphology, and persistent inflammatory cell clusters. Notably, the On + ADSCs group displayed an intact and well-defined skin architecture, characterized by robust granulation tissue enriched with neovascularization and minimal inflammatory infiltration. Compared to the Model, On, and ADSCs groups, the On + ADSCs group demonstrated a superior epidermal and dermal structure. Quantitative analysis of epidermal thickness further confirmed these observations, showing that the On + ADSCs group possessed a significantly thicker and more mature epidermis than either the On or ADSCs groups alone (P < 0.05, Fig. 6B).

To assess extracellular matrix remodeling and collagen deposition, Masson’s trichrome staining was utilized (Fig. 6C). The On + ADSCs group exhibited significantly higher collagen density compared to the Model group (P < 0.05). Furthermore, the collagen fibers in the On + ADSCs group were more densely packed and regularly arranged in parallel patterns compared to the fragmented and sparse fibers observed in the On or ADSCs groups (P < 0.001). These data suggest ononin-modified CS/β-GP hydrogel-loaded ADSCs facilitates DWs healing by promoting collagen deposition.

The effects of ononin-modified CS/β-GP hydrogel-loaded ADSCs on the expression of CD31, Collagen I and α-SMA in DWs

To further elucidate the mechanisms underlying the accelerated healing, immunohistochemical staining was performed to evaluate the expression of CD31, Collagen I, and α-SMA within the wound tissues (Fig. 7A). Previous studies have shown that CD31 is predominantly expressed on neovascular endothelial cells [29], Collagen I constitutes the major ingredient of the ECM [30], and the expression of α-SMA increases the contractility of myofibroblasts [31]. Quantitative analysis revealed that the expression levels of CD31, Collagen I, and α-SMA in all three intervention groups were significantly upregulated compared to the Model group (P < 0.05). Notably, the On + ADSCs group demonstrated a superior capacity to stimulate the expression of these markers compared to either the On or ADSCs groups (P < 0.05, Fig. 7B–D). Specifically, the dense CD31-positive staining indicates that the ononin-modified hydrogel loaded with ADSCs effectively promoted angiogenesis, while the robust expression of collagen I and α-SMA suggests enhanced ECM deposition and myofibroblast-driven wound contraction, respectively. Collectively, these findings confirm that the ononin-modified CS/β-GP hydrogel loaded with ADSCs exerts a potent therapeutic effect by orchestrating vascular regeneration and structural remodeling during the diabetic wound healing process.

Ononin-modified CS/β-GP hydrogel loaded with ADSCs activated the VEGF/VEGFR2 signaling pathway in DWs

To further investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying accelerated wound healing, we examined the VEGF/VEGFR2 signaling pathway, a critical regulator of angiogenesis [32]. Western blot analysis of wound tissues harvested at day 12 post-surgery (Fig. 8A) revealed that the protein expression levels of VEGF, VEGFR2, and CD31 were significantly downregulated in the Model group compared to the control group (P < 0.01), reflecting the impaired angiogenic capacity inherent in the diabetic microenvironment.

Discussion

Diabetic complications pose a significant global burden, with 15 ~ 20% of patients developing chronic non-healing wounds [33, 34]. Notably, 40% of cases recur post-treatment, and 20% remain unhealed after one year, highlighting the clinical inadequacy of current interventions [35]. Unlike the coordinated physiological healing phases (hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling), DWs are typically sequestered in a prolonged pro-inflammatory state [36, 37]. This pathological stagnation, driven by hyperglycemia-induced oxidative stress and impaired vascularization, results in chronic tissue degradation. Current therapeutic strategies thus prioritize modulating the inflammatory milieu and restoring local blood supply to facilitate re-epithelialization [38].

ADSCs are pivotal in regenerative medicine due to their paracrine-mediated pro-angiogenic and re-epithelialization capabilities [39, 40]. However, their therapeutic potential in cell-only therapies is severely hindered by the hostile wound microenvironment. To overcome this, we employed a CS/β-GP hydrogel to provide a protective, biomimetic niche [41, 42]. Our SEM analysis revealed a highly interconnected 3D porous architecture, which not only facilitates nutrient exchange but also offers an expansive internal surface for ADSCs adhesion and proliferation, consistent with findings that hydrogel-encapsulated ADSCs significantly outperform carrier-free cells in promoting vascularization and collagen synthesis [43, 44].

To further augment the regenerative milieu, we incorporated ononin, an isoflavonoid from Astragalus membranaceus known for its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties [45, 46]. By integrating ononin into the CS/β-GP matrix, we created a synergistic platform for orchestrating diabetic wound repair. Critically, our kinetic modeling demonstrated that the ononin release fits the Higuchi and Korsmeyer-Peppas models, indicating a controlled, diffusion-dominated release complemented by matrix swelling. This sustained release profile ensures the prolonged bioavailability of ononin, providing a continuous stimulus for effective wound closure and tissue remodeling in the chronic diabetic environment.

In wound healing, while a transient inflammatory response initiates repair, persistent and excessive inflammation in DWs leads to chronic tissue damage and stagnated healing [47]. Elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, are clinical hallmarks of diabetic ulcers that correlate with disease severity [48, 49]. This hostile milieu further impairs the regenerative capacity of transplanted ADSCs. Extensive evidence indicates that pharmacological priming with isoflavonoids can bolster the angiogenesis and cellular resilience [23, 50]. Our in vitro results confirmed that ononin (20µM) significantly enhances ADSCs proliferation and migration while optimizing the paracrine secretion of essential growth factors (IGF-1, TGF-β3, and VEGF). Correspondingly, in vivo data demonstrated that the On+ADSCs group achieved accelerated wound closure and healing rates comparable to non-diabetic controls by day 12. Histological analysis further revealed that this treatment effectively attenuated neutrophil infiltration and pro-inflammatory cytokine levels. These findings suggest that the ononin-modified CS/β-GP hydrogel promotes repair by mitigating chronic inflammation, thereby facilitating the crucial transition from the inflammatory to the proliferative phase of healing.

In DWs, microcirculatory dysfunction fundamentally impedes granulation tissue formation by reducing neovascularization and inducing structural disorganization [51]. Furthermore, persistent hyperglycemia triggers endothelial dysfunction, characterized by compromised barrier integrity and increased vascular permeability, which ultimately restricts the delivery of essential nutrients to the wound site [52, 53]. Consequently, stimulating robust angiogenesis is a critical therapeutic priority for resolving impaired diabetic ulcer healing. While ADSCs are known to facilitate angiogenesis and re-epithelialization via paracrine mechanisms [54], our histological findings demonstrate that the On + ADSCs group significantly outperformed all other intervention groups in terms of epithelial maturity, capillary density, and collagen organization. Specifically, the elevated expression of CD31, Collagen I, and α-SMA suggests that the ononin-modified CS/β-GP hydrogel loaded with ADSCs effectively orchestrates a multifaceted repair process involving vascular sprouting, matrix deposition, and myofibroblast-mediated wound contraction.

At the molecular level, VEGF serves as a master regulator of vascular development [55]. Our data indicate that the delayed healing observed in the model group is closely associated with the suppression of the VEGF/VEGFR2 signaling axis. Conversely, treatment with the ononin-modified hydrogel system significantly upregulated the expression of VEGF, VEGFR2, and CD31.

Despite these promising findings, several limitations warrant consideration. Primarily, this study employed a non-splinted rodent model. While rodent wounds heal partially through contraction mediated by the panniculus carnosus, we mitigated this inherent bias by prioritizing histological regeneration markers—including granulation tissue maturation and neo-epithelial length. These parameters provide a more robust metric of biological repair than macroscopic wound closure alone. Nonetheless, subsequent research utilizing silicone-splinted or porcine models will be essential to validate these outcomes in a re-epithelialization-dominant healing context. Furthermore, our biological assessments were longitudinally restricted, focusing predominantly on day 12. Although this time point captures the successful resolution of chronic inflammation and the peak of stable neovascularization, it may not fully encapsulate the dynamic kinetic fluctuations of the initial cytokine surges. Additionally, optimizing the clinical dosage of both ononin and ADSCs will be a critical prerequisite for ensuring safe and effective translational application in human patients.

Conclusion

In summary, the ononin-modified CS/β-GP hydrogel loaded with ADSCs significantly accelerates diabetic wound healing in rats by orchestrating a tripartite synergy: the physical shielding of the hydrogel, the biochemical microenvironment remodeling by ononin, and the potent paracrine regenerative action of ADSCs. This composite system effectively resolves chronic inflammatory arrest, stimulates the VEGF/VEGFR2-mediated angiogenic response, and promotes high-quality tissue remodeling. While this study provides a robust theoretical foundation for the treatment of DWs, further longitudinal studies and clinical dosage optimization are required to fully realize its translational potential.
