Nationwide outcomes of cardiovascular surgery in patients with Parkinson disease
Authors: Jose L. Diz Ferre, Alexander Cabulong, Guangjin Zhou, Jean-Luc A. Maigrot, Siran M. Koroukian, Nicholas Schiltz, Eugene H. Blackstone, Edward G. Soltesz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNotably, movement disorders like Parkinson disease (PD) are not currently included in the Society of Thoracic Surgeons risk stratification models. This study aimed to provide large-scale contemporary data describing cardiovascular surgery outcomes in patients with PD.MethodsBetween 2016 and 2019, the Nationwide Inpatient Sample included 1,051,725 discharges for coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), valve, aortic, or other cardiovascular surgery. Among these were 4675 patients (0.44%) with a diagnostic code for PD. Balancing-score matching and logistic regression were used to evaluate the influence of PD on cardiovascular surgery outcomes.ResultsPatients with PD were more often male, white, age >65 years, and on Medicare and were more likely to undergo CABG. Among balancing score–matched pairs, patients with PD had similar in-hospital mortality (2.3% vs 2.4%; P = .8), but more often experienced complications (46% vs 39%; adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 1.30; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.13-1.50), including ischemic stroke, pneumonia, and delirium, and had more frequent nonhome discharges (54% vs 32%; aOR, 2.46; 95% CI, 2.13-2.84). They also had a longer median length of stay (9 days vs 8 days; P < .001) and a higher associated median cost, in 2019 USD ($46,826 vs $45,933; P = .005).ConclusionsCardiovascular surgery can be performed safely on patients with PD with no increased hospital mortality, but these procedures are associated with more frequent complications, more frequent nonhome discharges, longer hospital length of stay, and higher costs. Surgeons may consider involving neurologic disorder experts to optimize the care of this vulnerable population.

Central Message

Patients with Parkinson disease had an increased risk of in-hospital complications and nonhome discharge. Surgeons may consider involving a multidisciplinary team to optimize care for this population.

Perspective

Movement disorders like Parkinson disease are increasingly common in the aging surgical population, yet their impact on cardiac surgery outcomes has been overlooked because of their exclusion from standard risk models. The study reflects awareness that neurologic comorbidities may meaningfully influence recovery, resource use, and discharge disposition.

The complex relationship between Parkinson disease (PD) and cardiovascular disease, compounded by shared cardiometabolic risk factors and disease processes, may increase the need for cardiovascular surgeries in patients with PD while elevating surgical risks and worsening outcomes.1 Although movement disorders are not part of the Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) risk stratification, patients with PD often require cardiovascular surgery.

Cardiovascular and cardiometabolic diseases, including ischemic heart disease, heart failure,1,2 and arrhythmias,3,4 are frequently observed in patients with PD. Concurrent risk factors, such as advanced age, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and male sex, are independently associated with both cardiovascular disease and PD.5 Structural cardiovascular abnormalities, such as left ventricular hypertrophy and diastolic dysfunction, may be exacerbated by mechanisms involving atherosclerotic plaque formation—a shared pathway of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases.1,6, 7, 8 Moreover, common PD treatments, including levodopa, dopamine agonists, and anticholinergic agents, have been associated with cardiovascular adverse effects, such as hypotension, restrictive valvular heart disease, and ventricular tachyarrhythmias, respectively.9 Levodopa, the cornerstone of PD management, has been linked to increased aortic stiffness and hypertension, which may contribute to left ventricular diastolic dysfunction.5,10

Population-level data on cardiovascular surgical outcomes in patients with PD has remained unexplored until now. Here we used the National Inpatient Sample (NIS) to evaluate outcomes at admission, including postoperative mortality, complications, nonhome discharges, length of stay, and total costs of patients with PD undergoing cardiac surgery.

Materials and Methods

Ethics Statement

The Institutional Review Board at Case Western Reserve University deemed this study exempt (STUDY20201146) from review on April 11, 2020, owing to its use of publicly available, deidentified data. The need for informed written consent was also waived.

Data Source

The NIS (Healthcare Cost Utilization Project, Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality) is a US all-payer inpatient database containing discharge data in the form of International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) diagnosis and procedure codes from more than 35 million hospitalizations. Patients in this database represent a 20% stratified random sample of all discharges in any given year.11 Discharge weights from the NIS were used to create national estimates.

Patient Cohort

The 2016 to 2019 NIS was queried for patients who underwent coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), valve surgery, aortic surgery, or a combination of these procedures using ICD-10-CM (Clinical Modification) codes (Table E1). Exclusion criteria included age <18 years old or >100 years old, history of heart transplantation, isolated left ventricular assist device, percutaneous and other mechanical circulatory support, and those with missing data for age, sex, and length of stay (LOS). After exclusion criteria were applied (n = 146,045), there were 1,051,725 discharges for cardiovascular surgery; of which, 4675 (0.44%) had PD.

Independent Variables

The main independent variable of interest was PD, which was defined using the ICD-10-CM codes listed by the Chronic Condition Warehouse (Table E2).12 Admissions were stratified into cohorts of PD and non-PD groups for comparison. Patient-level variables and demographics were derived from administrative data. Age on admission date was categorized into 18-44, 45-54, 55-64, 65-74, 75-84, and 85+ years. Sex was labeled as male or female. Race was grouped as White, Black, Hispanic, or “Other” (ie, Native American, Asian or Pacific Islander, and other race or mixed race). Admission status was classified as elective, urgent, or missing. Median household income was ordered according to quartile or marked as missing. Insurance status was categorized as Medicaid, Medicare, private, uninsured, “other,” or missing. Hospital-level characteristics included hospital teaching status (ie, metropolitan nonteaching, metropolitan teaching, or nonmetropolitan hospital), urban/rural designation (ie, rural, urban nonteaching, and urban teaching), hospital region (ie, northeast, midwest, south, or west according to the US Census Bureau), hospital bed size (ie, small, medium, or large), and hospital ownership (ie, government, private, private investor owned). Standard patient and hospital characteristics from the NIS, as well as comorbidities defined by Elixhauser and colleagues’13 method for administrative databases, were reported.

Hospital Outcomes

The primary outcomes were in-hospital mortality, overall complications, and discharge destination. Complications were identified using ICD-10-CM codes (Table E3) and included stroke (ie, ischemic and hemorrhagic), renal complications, pneumonia, respiratory failure, pulmonary embolism (PE)/deep vein thrombosis (DVT), gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding, non-GI bleeding, delirium, and infection/sepsis.

Discharge destination categories included home, grouping routine discharges to home/self-care or home health care, or as nonhome, which included transfer to a skilled nursing facility (SNF), a short-term skilled facility, or other.

Additional outcomes included LOS and estimated total costs. Total costs were derived from total charges using a cost-to-charge ratio created by the Healthcare Cost Utilization Project based on accounting reported from the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, to provide an estimate of actual costs incurred. Total costs were adjusted for inflation using the Gross Domestic Product index, and reported in terms of 2019 USD, coinciding with the last year of study data.14

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were conducted using SAS System for Unix, v9.4 (SAS Institute). Non-normally distributed data were summarized as median with interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were reported as frequency and percentage. Descriptive statistics were calculated applying discharge weights to create national estimates. For all comparisons between patients with PD and without PD, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test and χ2 tests were used for continuous and categorical variables, respectively. The nonparametric Fisher exact test was used for small (n <30) and non-normally distributed samples.

To control for potential confounding factors, balancing score matching (ie, propensity score matching in the setting of a nonrandomizable exposure).15,16 was conducted between groups of patients with PD and without PD. A balancing score was calculated using logistic regression with PD as the dependent variable and surgery type, age, sex, race, admission status, median household income, insurance, location/teaching status of hospital, hospital bed size, region, and calendar year as the independent variables. A 1:10 greedy matching algorithm with a caliper of 0.2-fold the standard deviation of the linear balancing score was applied.15,17 Covariates balance before and after matching were assessed using standardized differences between those with and without PD (Figure E1). We also compared outcomes between cohorts using a logistic regression model, adjusting for confounders identified in a backward selection model. All P values were 2-tailed, with P < .05 considered statistically significant. However, given the large study population, we emphasized clinical significance over statistical significance. Results are presented with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Matched Outcomes

After balancing score matching (Table E6), patients with PD and those without PD had similar in-hospital mortality (2.3% vs 2.4%; P = .84), and thus logistic analyses were not performed for this outcome. Patients with PD had higher associated frequencies of overall complications (46% vs 39%; P < .001); more often experienced stroke, pneumonia, and delirium; and were more likely to have a nonhome discharge (54% vs 32%, P < .001) (Table 2). In the bivariate analysis, patients with PD had an associated increased risk of overall complications (unadjusted odds ratio [uOR], 1.33; 95% CI, 1.16-1.53; P <.001) and nonhome discharge (uOR, 2.52; 95% CI, 2.19-2.89; P <.001). Discussion

Principal Findings

In this large, nationally representative cohort, patients with PD who underwent cardiovascular surgery had higher comorbidity compared to patients without PD. After controlling for confounders with balancing score matching, complications and nonhome discharges were higher among patients with PD, whereas in-hospital mortality was similar in the 2 groups. Patients with PD had also a longer median LOS and higher total costs. In the multivariable analysis adjusting for comorbidities, patients with PD had an associated increased risk of complications and nonhome discharge.

Demographics and Comorbidity Burden

We found that patients with PD were more likely to be white and less likely to be black. This trend may be driven by racial disparities in PD diagnosis, as black patients are less likely to be diagnosed with PD and more likely to receive a missed or incorrect diagnosis compared to white patients.18,19 Underdiagnosis and delayed management of diseases including PD may be a contributing factor to the poorer cardiac surgery outcomes observed among black patients.20 Patients with PD also more often had neurologic and somatic comorbidities, such as arrythmias, depression, paralysis, psychoses, renal failure, and weight loss.

Our study also found that patients with PD had with lower prevalences of drug abuse and obesity. This aligns with previous literature describing patients with PD as having a very low incidence of substance use disorder, even though certain medications for PD, such as dopamine agonists, can predispose patients to behavioral addictions.21 Modifiable risk factors, such as obesity, alcohol consumption, and smoking, have been associated with poorer surgical outcomes.22

Mortality

Although previous studies have found that patients with PD undergoing cardiovascular surgery are at increased risk of mortality,23 we did not observe significant between-group differences in postoperative mortality. PD has been described as an independent predictor of mortality in other surgical indications, such as femoral24 and spinal fractures25; however, a small retrospective analysis did not find any differences in 30-day mortality following cardiovascular surgery among patients with PD compared to patients without PD.26 The underlying reasons why PD might not be a significant risk factor for postoperative mortality are not fully understood but may be explained by careful patient selection for those with increased physical and cognitive reserve who may be at lower operative risk.

Complications

Patients with PD more often experienced complications, including ischemic stroke and pneumonia. Stroke is the second-leading cause of death and sixth-leading cause of long-term impairment among patients with PD.27 The mechanism predisposing patients to ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke may involve elevated inflammatory markers, atherosclerotic plaque deposition,6 hypertension, arrhythmias, and aortic stiffness,10 driven by both structural and pharmacologic effects of PD and its treatment with medications like levodopa.10 The cardiovascular and cerebrovascular pathways associated with PD and its treatments should be considered for monitoring patients who may be at risk for cardiac surgery or predisposed to poorer outcomes postsurgery.

The increased frequency of pneumonia observed among patients with PD has been described previously.26 This outcome may be attributed to the progressive development of dysphagia in PD, which predisposes patients to aspiration pneumonia—the leading cause of mortality, accounting for approximately 25% of deaths in this vulnerable population.28,29 This population also may be at greater risk of postoperative pneumonia because of limited mobility and inspiratory muscle weakness.

Discharge Destination

Long-term skilled facilities provide care for patients with multimorbid conditions, including a large proportion of Medicare patients with PD.30 Our study found that patients with PD were significantly more likely to be discharged to a long-term facility than to home. Previous research has shown that many cardiac surgery patients discharged to care facilities do not return to their prior quality of life, with less than one-third surviving at home after 1 year postsurgery.31 Additionally, respiratory complications like pneumonia, which are more common in patients with PD, were found to be strong predictors of admission to a long-term acute care facility, which was associated with worse survival.32 Therefore, patients at risk for nonhome discharge should undergo a multidisciplinary team evaluation as part of preoperative decision making, especially for nonemergency surgery candidates.

Limitations

Several limitations of our study should be acknowledged. First, the NIS is an administrative database constructed around billing information and thus provides only discharge-level information without follow-up data. By defining PD as a binary indicator variable, we could not investigate whether the type of PD (ie, primary vs secondary Parkinsonism vs Parkinson-plus syndromes) was associated with cardiovascular surgery outcomes. Parkinsonian symptoms after the procedure were not considered. Additionally, data on patients with PD receiving treatment at the time of cardiovascular surgery or treatment management during hospitalization were not available to analyze its association with outcomes. As a retrospective cohort design, this study is subject to the possibility of selection bias. Although balancing score matching was used to control some baseline differences in patient cohorts, we cannot exclude the possibility of residual or unmeasured confounding in this observational study. Future studies with more robust, clinically granular data are needed to investigate the hypotheses supported in this study.

Conclusions

Cardiovascular surgery can be performed safely in patients with PD with no increased hospital mortality. However, patients with PD had a greater comorbidity burden and were at increased risk for complications, including stroke and pneumonia. These factors may have contributed to the increased frequency of nonhome discharges observed in this study, which could be a driver of their lower survival. Future efforts should focus on holistic preoperative decision making, especially for nonemergency surgery candidates, mitigating complication risks, and increasing the frequency of safe home discharges. Surgeons may consider involving neurologic disorder experts to optimize care of this vulnerable population.
