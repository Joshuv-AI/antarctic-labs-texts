Comparative study of 18F-labeled PET radiopharmaceuticals in an Alzheimer’s disease mouse model
Authors: Bok-Nam Park, Su-Min Kim, Young-Sil An
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Background

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder and the most prevalent cause of dementia. Clinically, it is characterized by symptoms such as memory loss, cognitive decline, behavioral changes, and functional impairments [1, 2]. These symptoms profoundly affect patients and their caregivers, often leading to considerable social and economic burdens [3, 4]. Despite extensive research, there is currently no definitive cure for Alzheimer’s disease. Existing treatments focus primarily on managing symptoms rather than halting or reversing disease progression [5, 6].

Ongoing research is exploring the underlying mechanisms, disease progression, and potential therapeutic strategies for AD. Given the ethical and practical challenges of conducting experimental studies involving human participants, animal models are extensively used in Alzheimer’s research. Various mouse models are commonly employed owing to their ability to replicate key pathological features of the disease, including amyloid-beta deposition and tau pathology [7–10]. One hallmark feature of AD is the accumulation of amyloid plaques, traditionally necessitating brain tissue biopsy for direct assessment. These plaques can be visualized noninvasively using positron emission tomography (PET). PET imaging allows for a convenient and less invasive evaluation of amyloid deposition, providing a significant advantage over traditional biopsy methods. In animal models, PET imaging of amyloid deposition offers valuable insights into disease progression and the effectiveness of therapeutic interventions, enabling researchers to monitor these processes dynamically without invasive procedures [11–14].

Several radiopharmaceuticals labeled with fluorine-18 (18F) are available for amyloid PET imaging, including 18F-florbetaben (FBB), 18F-flutemetamol (FMM), and 18F-florapronol (FPN) [15–17]. Although these three radiopharmaceuticals have been validated for imaging amyloid deposition in the human brain and are commercially available, questions remain regarding their ability to effectively reflect amyloid deposition in mouse brains. While animal models share similarities with humans, species-specific differences may affect the binding and imaging characteristics of these radiopharmaceuticals. A previous animal study showed that the comparison of imaging results among these radiopharmaceuticals is highly constrained, with significant variability in their imaging characteristics [18]. Consequently, it remains unclear which 18F-labeled radiopharmaceutical is most suitable for use in AD research utilizing mouse models. This uncertainty complicates the selection process, making it challenging for researchers to select the most effective imaging agent for their specific study objectives.

This study aimed to evaluate and determine which 18F-labeled amyloid PET radiopharmaceuticals are most suitable for use in AD mouse models. By identifying the optimal radiopharmaceuticals for amyloid imaging in these models, we seek to enhance the accuracy and effectiveness of preclinical studies, thereby contributing to advancements in AD research.

Methods

Mouse model

For this study, we utilized male FAD4T mice, commercially sourced from GemPharmatech (GemPharmatech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China). The FAD4T strain has been validated as an appropriate AD model in numerous published studies [19–21]. These mice carry human-derived amyloid beta precursor protein genes with Swedish and Indiana mutations, along with human presenilin 1 (PSEN1) mutations M146V and L286V, integrated into the C57BL/6JGpt mouse genome. The mice were maintained under a 12-h light/dark cycle with unrestricted access to food and water. We selected six-month-old male FAD4T mice for the study, as this age allows for the development of significant β-amyloid plaques. For the control group, six-week-old C57BL/6 N male mice were used. All experimental protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Ajou University School of Medicine, Suwon, Republic of Korea (IACUC No. 2022-0017). For euthanasia, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (maintained at 2–2.5% concentration) using a Summit Portable Anesthesia Machine [22]. The depth of anesthesia was confirmed by assessing pedal reflex responses with forceps and monitoring respiratory rate reduction. Once unconscious, euthanasia was performed by gradually introducing carbon dioxide (CO₂) until respiratory cessation, following institutional animal care guidelines.

Amyloid PET imaging

PET imaging studies were conducted on both control and AD mice using 18F-FBB (NeuraCeq, DuChemBio Co., Ltd., Seoul, South Korea), 18F-FMM (Vizamyl™, DuChemBio Co., Ltd., Seoul, South Korea), and 18F-FPN (Alzavue®, FutureChem Co., Ltd., Seoul, South Korea). Radiochemical purity was verified daily before use to ensure compliance with PET imaging standards. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (maintained at 2–2.5% concentration), and each of them was administered 18.5 MBq of the 18F-labeled amyloid PET tracer intravenously via the tail vein. Following a 90-min distribution period, brain imaging was performed using a high-resolution SimPET scanner (Brightonix Imaging Inc., Seoul, South Korea). Each mouse underwent a 20-min scan in a prone position, with the brain aligned centrally in the horizontal field of view. The PET images were reconstructed using a three-dimensional ordered subset expectation maximization algorithm incorporating corrections for decay, detector dead time, scatter, and random coincidences. The resulting image resolution in the central field of view was less than 1 mm full width at half maximum. Each mouse underwent PET imaging sequentially with all three radiotracers (18F-FBB, 18F-FMM, and 18F-FPN) using a within-subject design. To allow sufficient tracer washout and avoid cross-contamination, each imaging session was performed at least two days apart.

PET image analysis

PET data were analyzed using the PMOD Rodent Brain Tool (PNROD) software, version 4.301 (PMOD Technologies, Zurich, Switzerland). The β-amyloid PET images were spatially normalized to the Ma-Benveniste-Mirrione mouse brain volume of interest (VOI) atlas [23, 24] provided by PNROD, with a 0.6-mm filter applied for smoothing. The transformed PET images were then automatically coregistered with a T2-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) template specific to the Ma-Benveniste-Mirrione mouse model, and segmentation was used to define the VOIs. The delineated VOIs on MR images are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. Of the 19 VOIs available in the Ma-Benveniste-Mirrione atlas [23], the primary regions of interest included the cortex, known as the principal site of β-amyloid accumulation, and the cerebellum, which was used as the reference region. The standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR) was calculated by dividing the SUV of the cortex by that of the cerebellum.

Statistical analysis

All data are expressed as mean and standard deviation (SD). Welch’s t-test was used to determine differences in SUVRs between the control and AD groups, as this method does not assume equal variance between groups. If any radiopharmaceutical showed significant differences between the control and AD groups in the t-test, a Difference-in-Differences (DiD) analysis was conducted using R version 4.4.1. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) test was used to analyze the differences in SUVRs among the three radiopharmaceuticals. If significant differences were detected in the ANOVA test, the Tukey–Kramer post-hoc test was performed for pairwise comparisons between the subgroups. All statistical analyses, except for the DiD analysis, were performed using MedCalc® Statistical Software version 22.026 (MedCalc Software Ltd, Ostend, Belgium; https://www.medcalc.org; 2024). Statistical significance was set at values of p < 0.05.

Results

Comparison of SUVR between control and AD groups

In the brain images obtained using 18F-FBB, the SUVR of the AD mice was significantly higher (n = 14, mean ± SD, 1.06 ± 0.15) than that of the control group (n = 8, 0.81 ± 0.07; p < 0.001). A similar pattern was observed with 18F-FMM, where the SUVR of the AD mice was significantly higher (n = 17, 0.97 ± 0.02) than that of the control group (n = 10, 0.94 ± 0.04; p = 0.024). However, PET imaging using 18F-FPN did not show a statistically significant difference in cortical uptake between the control (n = 11, 1.05 ± 0.10) and AD groups (n = 14, 1.12 ± 0.09; p = 0.071). These findings are visually supported by representative PET images in Fig. 1, which demonstrate prominent differences in cortical tracer uptake between AD and control mice for 18F-FBB and 18F-FMM, but only minimal difference for 18F-FPN. The results of the DiD analysis implied that the difference in SUVR between the AD and control groups was significantly larger for 18F-FBB than for 18F-FMM (estimate = -0.212, p < 0.001, Fig. 2).

Comparison of SUVR among 18F-labeled amyloid PET radiopharmaceuticals

In the control group, the SUVR values varied significantly across the different 18F-labeled amyloid PET radiopharmaceuticals. The highest SUVR was observed with 18F-FPN (1.05 ± 0.10), followed by 18F-FMM (0.94 ± 0.04), and the lowest with 18F-FBB (0.81 ± 0.07). Statistically significant differences were found among the groups (p < 0.001). Post-hoc tests revealed that all pairwise comparisons between these groups were statistically significant (p < 0.05).

In the AD group, the SUVRs among the three radiopharmaceuticals also showed statistically significant differences (p < 0.001, ANOVA). The highest SUVR was observed with 18F-FPN (1.12 ± 0.09), followed by 18F-FBB (1.06 ± 0.15) and 18F-FMM (0.97 ± 0.02). Post-hoc test results revealed a significant difference between 18F-FPN and 18F-FMM (p < 0.05), whereas 18F-FBB did not show significant differences compared to the other radiopharmaceuticals. For a detailed comparison of the SUVRs between the normal and AD groups across different radiopharmaceuticals, please refer to Fig. 3.

Discussion

Our study aimed to evaluate the suitability of three 18F-labeled amyloid PET radiopharmaceuticals—18F-FBB, 18F-FMM, and 18F-FPN—for imaging amyloid deposition in a mouse model of AD. The findings from our PET imaging demonstrated significant differences in the performance of these tracers in differentiating between the AD and control groups.

Both 18F-FBB and 18F-FMM effectively distinguished AD mice from control mice, with 18F-FBB showing a particularly higher SUVR than that of 18F-FMM in the AD group. This indicates that 18F-FBB provides a more pronounced differentiation of amyloid burden, reinforcing its reliability as a tracer for amyloid imaging. 18F-FMM also showed significant differences in SUVR between the AD and control groups; however, the magnitude was less pronounced compared to 18F-FBB. This suggests that, while 18F-FMM is effective in reflecting amyloid accumulation, 18F-FBB is superior in highlighting amyloid plaque deposition in this AD model.

Conversely, 18F-FPN did not exhibit a statistically significant difference in cortical uptake between the AD and control groups. This may be attributed to differences in pharmacokinetics and binding affinity among 18F-FPN, 18F-FBB and 18F-FMM. One crucial factor could be the timing of imaging. Although 18F-FPN is typically imaged at 60 min post-injection in human studies, we conducted imaging at 90 min post-injection to standardize the conditions across all tracers. This delay may have led to reduced cortical retention of the tracer, due to washout effects, potentially resulting in an underestimation of the amyloid burden and consequently, non-significant SUVR differences. Previous rodent studies using 18F-FPN support the use of earlier imaging windows (30–60 min) as more optimal for capturing amyloid depositions [25]. However, due to experimental constraints and the need for cross-tracer standardization, we maintained a fixed imaging time-point. Future studies should evaluate tracer-specific imaging windows to more accurately assess the potential use of 18F-FPN for amyloid detection in preclinical models.

The DiD analysis provided deeper insights into the comparative effectiveness of these radiopharmaceuticals. Moreover, it indicated that the difference in SUVR between the AD and control groups was significantly larger for 18F-FBB than for 18F-FMM. This suggests that 18F-FBB is more effective than 18F-FMM in highlighting amyloid plaque accumulation, making it a more potent tool for distinguishing between AD and control mice. This finding aligns with that of Son et al. [18], who reported that 18F-FBB shows better correlation with neuropathological findings and more prominent cortical uptake than 18F-FMM. They attributed these differences to the higher binding affinity, more favorable pharmacokinetics of 18F-FBB, and better kinetic parameters, consistent with our findings. Our study supports and enhances their findings by demonstrating 18F-FBB’s superior performance through a comprehensive analysis that includes the DiD approach.

This study has several limitations. First, although the FAD4T mouse model is well validated for AD, verification with other AD animal models is needed to assess the generalizability of our findings. Different models exhibit varying patterns of amyloid pathology, which may influence radiotracer binding and uptake. Therefore, the tracer performance observed in this model may not fully represent its behavior in models with distinct plaque characteristics. Future comparative studies across multiple models are warranted to validate our results. Second, as discussed above, our imaging protocol involved conducting PET scans 90 min post-injection for all tracers to standardize conditions. However, this timing may not be optimal for 18F-FPN, which typically requires earlier imaging (< 60 min post-injection), potentially leading to an underestimation of its performance. Future studies should explore optimized imaging time-points for this tracer, particularly within the 30–60 min post-injection window supported by prior rodent studies using 18F-FPN [25]. In addition, while our study focused on static PET imaging to enable cross-tracer comparison, this single time-point approach may not fully capture the kinetic characteristics of tracers like 18F-FPN, which may reach peak uptake earlier. Although dynamic imaging was beyond the scope of this study, future research incorporating time-activity curve analysis and kinetic modeling may help optimize imaging windows and provide deeper insight into tracer behavior. We encourage future studies to incorporate dynamic protocols, particularly for 18F-FPN, to optimize imaging windows and improve tracer characterization. Third, this study did not include in vitro autoradiography to validate tracer binding at the tissue level. While autoradiography could provide spatially resolved confirmation of amyloid accumulation—particularly for 18F-FPN, where in vivo SUVR differences were not significant—its absence limits the interpretation of tracer-specific binding. Future studies incorporating autoradiographic validation are encouraged to complement in vivo PET findings and more accurately assess regional tracer distribution. Fourth, this study did not include immunohistochemical (IHC) staining to confirm amyloid pathology across groups. While SUVR-based PET quantification provides a non-invasive means to assess tracer performance, the absence of ex vivo pathological validation limits our ability to directly correlate in vivo imaging with regional amyloid burden. Future studies incorporating IHC data may help validate and complement SUVR measurements, particularly in evaluating inter-individual variability in amyloid depositions. Fifth, animal mortality led to variation in group sizes, as additional animals beyond the number approved by the Animal Ethics Committee could not be included. This inconsistency could impact the robustness of our findings, highlighting the need for more consistent group sizes in future studies to enhance reliability. Sixth, there was an age difference between the AD and control mice. Six-month-old AD mice were used to ensure sufficient amyloid plaque accumulation, which is essential for evaluating the performance of amyloid PET tracers. In contrast, 6-week-old C57BL/6 N mice were selected as controls due to their availability and prior validation in the literature as a standard non-AD comparator strain, without expected amyloid pathology [26]. Although this age mismatch could introduce potential confounding effects, SUVR quantification primarily reflects specific amyloid deposition rather than nonspecific age-related uptake. Therefore, we believe the impact on our primary findings is limited, and the comparative evaluation across tracers remains valid within the context of this study. Lastly, we could not include 18F-florbetapir, a well-known radiopharmaceutical [27], because it is not commercially available in our country. This limitation restricted our ability to conduct direct comparisons using this tracer. Future studies could address this gap through international collaborations or by evaluating alternative tracers with similar binding profiles that are locally accessible.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that 18F-FBB and 18F-FMM are effective radiopharmaceuticals for amyloid imaging in AD mouse models, with 18F-FBB showing the most significant differentiation between the AD and control groups. These findings contribute to the optimization of imaging agents used in preclinical Alzheimer’s research, aiding in the development of more accurate and sensitive diagnostic tools for the early detection and monitoring of AD.
