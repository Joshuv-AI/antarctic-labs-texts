A randomised controlled trial to compare the occurrence of postoperative nausea and vomiting in early versus conventional feeding in children undergoing daycare surgery under general anaesthesia
Authors: Ranju Singh, H Santoshkumar Huligeri, Pooja Singh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background and Aims:The time paediatric patients should resume oral intake after surgery is still ill-defined. No specific evidence suggests that the usual practice of postoperative fasting of 4–6 h to reduce postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) is beneficial. The primary objective of this study was to assess the occurrence of PONV with early oral feeding compared to conventional feeding in children undergoing daycare surgery under general anaesthesia.Methods:A randomised controlled trial was conducted in 300 children undergoing daycare surgery under general anaesthesia. Children were randomised into the early feeding group (Group EF, n = 150) or the conventional feeding group (Group CF, n = 150). Group EF received carbohydrate-containing oral fluids when the child demanded feed in the postoperative period. Group CF received oral fluids 4 h post-anaesthesia. All patients were monitored for occurrence of PONV, postoperative pain, duration of hospital stay and parental satisfaction. The incidence of PONV was compared using the Chi-squared test, while other continuous variables were compared using the Student’s t-test.Results:Both groups were comparable regarding PONV (12% in Group EF vs. 18.7% in Group CF, P = 0.109). The Face, Legs, Activity, Cry, Consolability scores were significantly lower in Group EF at 0 min (P = 0.011), 30 min (P = 0.001) and 1 h (P < 0.001). Patients in Group EF had a significantly shorter duration of hospital stay, that is, 6.31 [standard deviation (SD): 3.52] [95% confidence interval (CI): 1.45–12.24] h in EF versus 10.13 (SD: 2.99) (95% CI: 5.12–16.33) h in CF (P < 0.001). Parents of the children in Group EF had significantly better parental satisfaction scores (P < 0.001).Conclusion:Early postoperative feeding in children undergoing lower abdominal, non-gastrointestinal surgery under general anaesthesia does not increase the incidence of PONV.

INTRODUCTION

The Early Recovery After Surgery guidelines for perioperative care in neonatal intestinal surgery recommend early enteral feeds as early as possible.[1] The time paediatric patients should resume oral intake after surgery is still ill-defined. The protocol of postoperative fasting of 4–6 h is supported by some older studies, which suggested that withholding oral fluids reduces vomiting and duration of hospital stay.[23] Modern anaesthetic techniques have helped in reducing the incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) and postoperative gastrointestinal dysfunction in current anaesthetic practice.[45] Recent studies suggest that early postoperative feeding does not increase PONV and may be beneficial.[36] More data is required to define the timing of starting oral intake postoperatively.

Therefore, we planned this study with the primary objective of studying the occurrence of PONV with early oral feeding (EF) compared to conventional feeding (CF) in children undergoing daycare surgery under general anaesthesia (GA). The secondary objectives were to study the need for antiemetics, postoperative pain, postoperative analgesics, duration of hospital stay and parental satisfaction in EF compared to CF.

METHODS

The study was a randomised controlled trial conducted in a tertiary care hospital after obtaining approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee (vide approval number LHMC/IEC/thesis/2019/117R dated 25 November 2019). The study was registered at the Clinical Trials Registry-India (vide registration number CTRI No-CTRI/2020/01/022789, accessible from https://ctri.nic.in/). The study was carried out from January 2020 to October 2021 on all children of either gender, aged between 3 and 10 years, belonging to the American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status I and II, undergoing daycare surgery under GA with caudal block. Written informed consent was obtained from the parents of all the participants for participation in the study and the use of patient data for research and educational purposes. Patients with a history of motion sickness, difficult airway, airway anomalies, gastrointestinal disorders and patients with contraindications to caudal epidural anaesthesia were excluded from the study. The study procedures followed were in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975, as revised in 2013, and good clinical practice.

Patients fulfilling the inclusion criteria were randomised to receive EF (Group EF) or CF (Group CF). A pre-anaesthetic check-up and routine investigation were done for all patients. Patients were fasted preoperatively as per the institutional protocol- 8 h for solid foods, 4 h for milk and semisolids and 2 h for clear fluids. On arrival in the operating room, a pulse oximeter (SpO2) probe, electrocardiography (ECG) electrodes and non-invasive blood pressure (NIBP) cuff were attached. Before administering anaesthesia, patients were randomised into one of the two groups (Group EF or Group CF). Simple random sampling was employed, and a computer-generated random number sequence was used for randomisation. The allocation was written on a piece of paper and kept inside an opaque envelope. The envelopes were sequentially numbered and were sealed to ensure concealment. Two of the authors (RS and PS) were responsible for generating the random sequence and sealing the opaque envelopes. The other author (HSH) enroled the participants, opened the sealed opaque envelopes and assigned them to the respective intervention. Due to the obvious nature of the intervention, this study was not blinded.

Group EF (n = 150) – received carbohydrate-containing oral fluids as soon as the child demanded feed after anaesthesia.

Group CF (n = 150) – received carbohydrate-containing oral fluids after 4 h of anaesthesia.

The carbohydrate-containing fluids were any one of 5% dextrose, apple juice, mango drink or orange juice. Standardised GA was induced by intravenous (IV) fentanyl (2 µg/kg), propofol (1.5 mg/kg) and rocuronium (0.6 mg/kg) if an IV line was available; otherwise, inhalational anaesthesia was administered with sevoflurane (5%–8%) in oxygen–air mixture. After induction, a weight-appropriate supraglottic device (SGD) (i-gel; Intersurgicals, Berkshire, UK) was inserted, and the patient was put on assisted ventilation. All patients received a caudal epidural for postoperative pain relief in the lateral decubitus position using 0.75 ml/kg of 0.2% ropivacaine solution with clonidine 1 µg/kg. The patient’s ECG with heart rate (HR), NIBP, SpO2 and end-tidal carbon dioxide (EtCO2) and the inspiratory and expiratory concentrations of the anaesthetic agent were monitored throughout the anaesthesia and surgery. At the end of the surgery, anaesthesia was terminated, and residual neuromuscular blockade was reversed with IV glycopyrrolate (0.01 mg/kg) and neostigmine (0.05 mg/kg). The SGD was removed, and patients were shifted to the post-anaesthesia care unit (PACU). Intraoperatively, the fluid management was done using Holliday Segar Formula (4–2–1). Crystalloids such as Ringer’s lactate, plasmalyte and 0.9% normal saline were used.

After being fully awake as per the University of Michigan Sedation Scale (0), patients in Group EF were given carbohydrate-containing oral fluids as soon as the child demanded feeding.[7] Patients in Group CF received carbohydrate-containing oral fluids after 4 h of anaesthesia. Children in each group were offered as much fluid as they wanted to drink, up to 10 ml/kg body weight per hour. The time and volume of the first postoperative oral intake were recorded. Postoperatively, maintenance IV fluids were continued based on Holliday Segar Formula until the patient started taking oral feeds. Once the oral feeds were started, maintenance IV fluids were stopped. Standard monitoring for HR, NIBP, SpO2, and level of consciousness was continued during the postoperative period. Vomiting was assessed at intervals of half an hour for the first 2 h and then hourly till discharge, and the incidence was recorded. This assessment was done by the anaesthesia resident posted in PACU while in the ward; the nurse and doctor on duty assessed the child and were asked to telephonically contact one of the authors in case of nausea or vomiting. In addition, the child’s caregivers were educated and advised to telephonically communicate with one of the authors. The child was also visited as and when required. The scoring system used for nausea and vomiting was modified by Subramaniam et al.[8] This is a numeric scoring system for PONV, where 0 indicates no nausea or vomiting, 1 indicates nausea alone, 2 indicates vomiting once in 30 min or more and 3 indicates persistent nausea or vomiting two or more times within a 30-min period. Children with a PONV score of ≥1 were considered positive for PONV. They were given IV ondansetron at 100 µg/kg as the rescue medication. After feeding, patients’ pain was assessed using the Face, Legs, Activity, Cry, Consolability (FLACC) score by the anaesthesia resident on duty during the postoperative period.[9] The FLACC score was assessed after feeding and then at every 30 min for the first 2 h, followed by hourly for the next 3 h. If the score was ≥4, the child was given rescue analgesics as per the institutional protocol (paracetamol 20 mg/kg given IV). No eye contact and no awareness of surroundings were identified as emergence delirium, while abnormal facial expression, crying and inconsolability were interpreted as pain. Any postoperative complications such as desaturation, aspiration or reintubation were recorded during the observational period. Before discharging the patients, parents’ satisfaction was assessed using a 5-point satisfaction scale (1 = very satisfied, 2 = satisfied, 3 = neutral, 4 = dissatisfied, 5 = very dissatisfied). The parents were given clear verbal and written instructions about the child’s aftercare, such as instructions on recognising PONV and when to seek treatment or visit the hospital. They were also taught to recognise pain by noticing excessive cry, facial grimacing, restlessness, abnormal body posture and inform the authors telephonically. Telephone contact numbers were issued to parents to seek advice and report complications (such as recurrent nausea, vomiting, sedation, and cough) if any occurred. The parents were telephonically questioned regarding any episode of PONV till the first 24 h after surgery.

The sample size calculation for the study was based on the incidence of PONV in a previous study conducted by Chauvin et al.[10] Considering an expected incidence of PONV of 11.40% with early feeding and 23.93% with late feeding, as reported by Chauvin et al., assuming 80% power and 95% confidence interval (CI) for a two-tailed test, with a 5% dropout rate, the sample size required was 150 patients per arm. The data were compiled and subjected to statistical analysis using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences statistics software version 23.0 (International Business Machines Corporation Corp, Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables, including the FLACC score, duration of hospital stay and time to first analgesic requirement, were presented as mean (standard deviation) or median if the data was unevenly distributed. The incidence of PONV was expressed in frequencies and percentages. Comparison of continuous variables between the groups was performed using Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test as applicable. Categorical variables were compared between the groups by using the Chi-square test. A P value less than 0.05 was taken for all statistical tests to indicate a significant difference.

RESULTS

Data from 300 patients (Group EF, n = 150 and Group CF, n = 150) were included in the analysis [Figure 1]. The demographic characteristics, type and duration of surgery, and volume and timing of postsurgical feed are presented in Table 1. The occurrence of PONV in Group EF was (12%) less than that in Group CF (18.70%), though the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.109). The characteristics of PONV and antiemetic requirement are shown in Table 2. The FLACC score was significantly lower in the first hour in Group EF compared to Group CF [Table 2]. The number of patients requiring postoperative analgesics (P = 0.519) and the dose of rescue analgesics were also comparable between the two groups (P = 0.204). Time to first analgesic requirement after the first feed was 112.33 [standard deviation (SD): 109.31] (95% CI: 50.2, 164.2) min in Group EF and 101.41 (SD: 71.62) (95% CI: 36.5, 134.2) min in Group CF and was comparable between the two groups (P = 0.745). In either of the two groups, no patient developed any feeding-related complications, such as aspiration and need for reintubation or rehospitalisation (P > 0.05). Patients in Group EF had a significantly shorter duration of hospital stay, that is, 6.31 (SD: 3.52) (95% CI: 3.41, 8.33) h, compared to patients in Group CF, that is, 10.13 (SD: 2.99) (95% CI: 7.45, 13.33) h (P < 0.001). Parents of the children in Group EF [median (interquartile range {IQR}) 1 (1–2)] had significantly better satisfaction scores (P < 0.001) compared to the parents of children in Group CF [median (IQR) 2 (1–2)].

DISCUSSION

In this study, children undergoing daycare surgery under GA who were given EF had similar nausea and vomiting but had better FLACC scores, shorter duration of hospital stay, and better parental satisfaction than those who received CF.

The study by Kearney et al.[2] supported delayed postoperative feeding. They found that vomiting occurred in only 38% of patients whose fluid intake was withheld, compared to 56% in those who drank early. However, this study had several flaws, such as the use of opioids (which may have caused PONV) and the lack of standardisation of feeding for the control group. In the early postoperative period, oral fluid intake has shown benefits for children undergoing GA, including a rapid return to a normal diet, reduced thirst, early bowel movement, early ambulation and increased parental satisfaction.[11]

A study on the early postoperative period in children undergoing GA for tonsillectomy reported the PONV incidence to be 4.34% and 12.3% in the study and control groups, respectively.[11] The low incidence of PONV in this study compared to our results may be due to the very low volume of fluid administered to the patients (≤1.5 ml/kg) and the intraoperative use of dexamethasone and other antiemetic drugs in all patients.[11] Other authors have reported incidences of PONV similar to that seen in our study.[6101213] As seen in our study, a reduction in antiemetic requirement was observed in another study, which allowed patients to feed early.[13]

In children, it is very difficult to differentiate between the causes of postoperative distress, which can be due to many reasons such as thirst, hunger, pain, PONV and emergence delirium.[14] Hunger and thirst are the most common causes of crying in children after surgery. Thus, an oral feed may reduce the indiscriminate use of analgesics.[6] Intraoperatively, children in both groups received caudal epidural analgesia to manage their perioperative pain. Despite the caudal block, the FLACC scores were lower in the EF group. These lower FLACC scores indicated that feeding children early in the postoperative period makes them more comfortable and distress free. Results similar to those of our study have been observed before.[4]

None of the patients in our study had complications like aspiration, desaturation, or need for reintubation or rehospitalisation, probably due to fluid administration only on demand and when the child was fully awake and conscious and had intact swallowing reflexes. Our study results were comparable to those of other studies reported in the literature.[6101112]

While previous studies have shown the reduced duration of PACU stays with EF, the effect on the total duration of hospital stay has not been studied.[1012] To the best of our knowledge, no published literature compares the effect of early postoperative oral feeding with the duration of total hospital stay. Also, the parents of children in Group EF were more satisfied than the parents of the children in Group CF (P < 0.001) since the children in Group EF were less bothered by hunger and thirst and were thus crying less. These findings are similar to those of another study published earlier.[10] While most studies have assessed patient satisfaction related to early postoperative feeding, our study is probably one of the very few that have evaluated the effect of early postoperative feeding on parental satisfaction.[613] Parental satisfaction is critical as it is an important quality improvement parameter.[15]

The main limitation of our study design was the issue of blinding. Although both randomisation and allocation concealment from the investigators were ensured, the intervention is such that blinding is not feasible.

CONCLUSIONS

Our study showed that early postoperative feeding in children undergoing lower abdominal, non-gastrointestinal day care surgery under general anaesthesia does not increase the incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting compared to conventional (delayed feeding) regimens. Early feeding is associated with a shorter duration of hospital stay and improved parental satisfaction.
