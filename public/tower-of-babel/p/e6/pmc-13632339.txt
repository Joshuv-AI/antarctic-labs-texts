Mapping the landscape of allele-specific expression in porcine genomes
Authors: Wen-ye Yao, Marta Gòdia, Lingzhao Fang, Martien A. M. Groenen, Lijing Bai, Kui Li, Ole Madsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAllele-specific expression (ASE) is the imbalanced expression of two alleles of the same locus. It is quite pervasive among many species and is associated with health and economically relevant traits. ASE is often used to support the identification of variants related to gene expression (cis-eQTL). Thus, profiling allele-specific expression represents a significant step in elucidating the mechanism underlying gene expression regulation.ResultIn this study, we developed an ASE pipeline using publicly available RNA-seq data and open-source software. Using this pipeline, we are able to profile pervasive allelic imbalance across 42 tissues and 34 breeds from the Farm-GTEx-pig consortium at both SNP and gene levels without the need for parental genotypes or whole genome sequence data. We find that ASE is widely, but not evenly, spread across the genome. We also observe considerable variation in ASE profiles across various tissues, where the site fraction ranged from 1.3% to 54.1%. ASE tends to be highly tissue-specific, with limited overlap across tissues. The functional analysis of tissue-specific ASE sites indicates that they are involved in important biological functions of these tissues. Our ASE pipeline can be readily applied to other RNA-seq datasets for livestock and other species, thereby expanding its potential utility.ConclusionsThe wealth of available ASE resources provides a solid foundation for identifying regulatory elements within the genome that drive complex traits in livestock, making our pipeline and results valuable resources for researchers in this field.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13059-026-04174-z.

Background

Diploid organisms carry two alleles at each locus. For a long time, it was thought that both alleles were equally expressed and thereby equally contributing to an individual’s phenotype. Most of the time, the expression of two alleles is indeed equal, but for some genes, the expression of the two alleles is unequal, a phenomenon called allelic imbalance or allele-specific expression (ASE). ASE appears to be a relatively common phenomenon observed across a wide range of species, such as humans [1], mouse [2, 3], cattle [4, 5], pig [6], sheep [7], horse [8], chicken [9], and fish [10]. The most well-known ASE phenomena include Chromosome X-inactivation [11], in which the inactivated chromosome is suppressed as a result of epigenetic changes in the chromosomal structure, and imprinted genes [12], where epigenetic signatures govern the parent-of-origin expression of these imprinted genes [13]. Multiple genetic and epigenetic mechanisms can lead to ASE, and most ASE events are thought to be caused by genetic variation [14]. These genetic variants can impact the binding affinity of transcription factors [15], chromosomal states [16, 17], and mRNA splicing [18], leading to ASE. ASE can be identified and quantified by RNA sequencing, typically overlapping uniquely mapped RNA-sequence reads to heterozygous sites and counting the expression level of each allele. Genotype data can be retrieved from DNA sequences, SNPs called from RNA-sequencing data, imputation genotypes based on SNPs called from RNA-sequencing data or genotype arrays.

The prevalence and pattern of ASE have been widely studied across tissues in human genomic analyses. Data from the human Lymphoblastoid cell line (LCL) by the Geuvadis consortium [7] suggested that 6.5% of the LCL genes show ASE. The prevalence of ASE across the human GTEx samples showed that the distribution of significant ASE site fractions ranged from 1.7 to 3.7% across tissues [19]. This research indicated that brain regions had the lowest ASE site fraction (~ 2%), while blood showed the highest ASE site fraction (~ 3.6%). A later human GTEx data release (GTEx v8), comprised of a comprehensive ASE resource derived from 15,253 samples across 54 tissues, showed that 53% of protein-coding genes showed strong ASE in at least 50 individuals [1]. Another analysis explored the changes in ASE over time from the same individuals at ages 70 and 80 [20], revealing a 2.6% increase in ASE with age. Many genes that showed changes in ASE over time were associated with the immune response, suggesting that ASE could be involved in aging.

A relatively diverse ASE fraction has been found among tissues of different mammals, such as cattle, sheep, mice, and pigs. Around 20% of mouse transcripts showed ASE in tail-tip fibroblasts [2]. Several studies have investigated the prevalence of ASE in cattle [4, 5, 21–23]. These studies generally indicated that ASE is widely spread throughout cattle genomes across different breeds, while varying widely among various tissues, with the fraction of ASE sites ranging from 10% in the thymus to 65% in the lung [21]. In crossbred blackface sheep, the ASE discovery rate comprised, on average, 5.8% of the expressed heterozygous sites in each individual [7]. However, the shared ASE profile across the four sheep tissues and two cell types was limited, suggesting that ASE is highly tissue-specific.

Pigs (Sus scrofa) are key agricultural animals serving as the primary meat resource and biomedical model. DNA and RNA sequencing data of pigs have expanded rapidly in scale and volume over the past two decades. ASE analysis has become increasingly attainable. For example, an analysis of prenatal skeletal muscle in Tongcheng pig identified 11,300 ASE sites, representing about 10.6% of the SNPs tested, with 4 of these ASE variants reported to be associated with average daily gain in a Genome-Wide Association Study (GWAS) [24]. ASE analyses in peripheral blood identified 2,286 ASE sites in large white pigs, involving 763 genes with functional enrichment for RNA processing and immune function [25]. Furthermore, around 42% of all expressed genes showed ASE in the brain from a synthetic pig breed (a cross between seven breeds) [26]. Although these ASE analyses revealed the prevalence and importance of ASE in pigs, a substantial cohort study to explore ASE is still lacking despite thousands of RNA-seq data being available online.

In this study, we develop an ASE pipeline using publicly available RNA-seq data and open-source software (Additional file 2: Fig. S1) and profile a pervasive ASE resource from a comprehensive pig RNA-seq dataset containing 6,020 samples spanning 42 tissues (including 3 cell lines) and 34 breeds from the Farm-GTEx-pig project [27]. From this ASE profile, we provide insight into the distribution of ASE in the pig genome across tissues and breeds and generate tissue-shared and specific ASE profiles. This extensive ASE atlas data set provides a valuable foundation for further analyses to identify the molecular regulatory variants underlying complex traits in pigs.

Results

Genotype data comparison

Variants from whole genome sequences are considered the gold standard for ASE detection. In our study, the genotype data were imputed to the whole-genome level based on RNA-sequence called heterozygous SNPs from the Farm-GTEx-pig consortium (see Methods). To test the accuracy of the imputed SNPs in our project, we compared these imputed genotype data with actual DNA-based genotype calls and corresponding RNA-seq derived genotypes for 17 samples (derived from 7 tissues and 3 breeds; Additional file 1: Table S1). After filtering with the standard quality control criteria, the average variant number detected from DNA, RNA-seq, and RNA-imputed (RNAim) data was 8,011,460, 144,412, and 1,260,754, respectively (Additional file 1: Table S1). We observed higher genotype concordance (GC), including heterozygous genotype concordance (HetGC), for DNA-RNAim than for DNA-RNA (Additional file 1: Table S1 & Additional file 2: Fig. S2A). The GC and HetGC of DNA-RNAim revealed two outliers, namely for the liver sample in Pietrain and the muscle sample in Duroc. These two samples also displayed deviating numbers of RNAim SNPs (Additional file 1: Table S1). In the Farm-GTEx consortium database, it appears that these samples were incorrectly assigned to a breed and therefore, imputed from an incorrect reference population. This indicates the importance of correct population assignment for genotype imputation. Overall, DNA-RNAim genotype showed increased concordance (average GC%: 97.80% and HetGC: 97.31%, two outliers were excluded), compared to the DNA-RNA derived genotype (average GC%: 87.03% and HetGC: 74.33%). This pattern is also supported by previous studies, which have shown that genotype imputation from RNA-seq derived variants can improve concordance relative to raw RNA-derived genotypes [28, 29]. The high GC% and HetGC of DNA-RNAim indicates that most samples were correctly assigned to the breed, and the RNAim genotype data showed high accuracy.

Reference allele mapping bias remains a considerable issue in ASE analyses. We compared the effect of the mapping bias correction using the genotype data from DNA, RNA-seq, and RNAim resources. The effect of removing the reference mapping bias was assessed using the reference ratio (reference ratio = reference reads/total reads). The reference ratio decreased to around 0.5 using all genotype data after applying the filtering criteria described in Methods (Additional file 2: Fig. S2B). We then measured the effect of ASE detection using these three genotype data. In general, we observed similar ASE trends among breeds and tissues for these three groups (Additional file 2: Fig. S2C). With more SNP sites in the DNA genotype dataset, there are more ASE sites detected from the DNA genotypes (Additional file 1: Table S1). Meanwhile, the muscle sample in Duroc and the liver sample in Pietrain exhibited lower ASE numbers than other muscle and liver samples (Additional file 1: Table S1). This discrepancy is likely explained by the imputation error described above, thus, these two samples were excluded from all subsequent analyses. Besides these two outliers, a similarity trend in ASE across the three genotype data (Additional file 2: Fig. S2C) suggests that any potential bias from RNA-imputed genotyping is consistent across tissues and breeds, enabling the use of RNA-imputed genotype data for subsequent analysis.

Genome-wide ASE profiles

We constructed a genome-wide ASE profile across all samples using the workflow we developed (Additional file 2: Fig. S1). After applying the basic QC and filtering criteria (Methods), a total of 6,020 samples were retained for subsequent analyses, encompassing 39 tissues and 3 cell lines from 34 breeds (Additional file 1: Table S2). For the tissues, the median sample size was 78, ranging from 2 (cerebellum) to 1,313 (muscle) (Additional file 2: Fig. S3A). Two main breed categories were included, namely cross and pure breeds. For the pure breeds, there were a total of 1,845 samples, including the most common commercial breeds, such as Yorkshire and Duroc, and different local breeds, such as Wuzhishan, Meishan, and Tibetan breeds (Additional file 2: Fig. S3B). Most samples were from crossbreeds (N = 4,175), which included a variety of breed combinations (Additional file 1: Table S2 & Additional file 2: Fig. S3C).

The mapping bias was assessed by the global distribution of the reference ratio for each sample. The reference ratio across all samples was close to 0.5 (Fig. 1A & Additional file 2: Fig. S3A, B, C), with a minimal bias towards the reference allele. It should be noted that the allelic mapping bias cannot be entirely eliminated, despite applying various filtering steps. For the SNP-level ASE analysis, a mean of 11,318 tested sites (ranging from 268 to 92,052) was obtained from the RNA-imputed SNPs for ASE testing, accounting for 1.71% of the 660,261 RNA-imputed SNPs located in gene regions. On average, 850 sites showed significant ASE (Fig. 1B), accounting for 7.51% of the total tested sites. The ASE sites per sample ranged from 10 to 46,825, with the corresponding ASE site fraction ranging from 0.23% to 77.2% (Additional file 3: Table S3). The ASE sites were broadly but unevenly distributed across autosomal chromosomes (Fig. 1C). We assessed the relationship between ASE site density and the total gene number across the genome using 1-Mb windows. A moderate positive correlation was observed (adjusted R2 = 0.47, p ≤ 0.05; Additional file 2: Fig. S4B), indicating that ASE events are generally consistent with the gene density across the genome. The distribution of ASE sites showed that 91% of the ASE sites were located in annotated genic regions (Additional file 3: Table S4). In the genic regions, most ASE sites were observed in introns (48%), followed by 22.60% in the UTRs and 20.50% in CDS, respectively (Additional file 3: Table S4). The number of ASE sites per 1 Mb segment was, on average, 136. For 22.9% of these segments (521 of 2,272 total segments), no ASE sites were detected. The majority of these segments (485 of 521) had no tested sites either and were primarily located in intergenic or non-expressed regions. For the rest of the segments with ASE sites (1,751 segments), there were 7 segments with a high density of ASE sites (ASE site count ≥ 1,000), while 439 segments had a very low density of ASE sites (ASE site count ≤ 42). Thirty-seven segments showed a 100% ASE site fraction, with all tested sites being significant, but the number of ASE sites in these segments was low, ranging from 1 to 14. The ASE sites were not only unevenly distributed within chromosomes but also showed unequal enrichment across different chromosomes (Fig. 1D). The highest ASE site density was on chromosome 12, after normalization for chromosome length, with a density of 366 sites per 1 Mb. The lowest density was on chromosome 16, with 77 sites per 1 Mb. Similar patterns were observed when normalized by gene and exon lengths (Additional file 2: Fig. S4C, D). To further examine the ASE site occurrences along chromosomes, we captured the accumulated occurrence rate of 1 Mb segments with tested sites and ASE sites for each chromosome (Additional file 2: Fig. S4E). The accumulation of ASE and tested sites occurred at a similar pace for all autosomes, implying a uniform occurrence of ASE sites along the genome.

For gene-level ASE, a total of 13,684 genes were detected as significant ASE genes with at least 1 ASE site in at least one individual. For each individual, a median of 261 ASE genes was identified among 2,145 tested genes, ranging from 1 to 5,978 ASE genes (Additional file 3: Table S3). The distribution of the ASE genes along the genome showed a similar trend as ASE sites (Additional file 2: Fig S5A). Six segments presented an enrichment of ASE genes, with more than 50 ASE genes. As expected, the distribution of the ASE genes was highly correlated with ASE sites (R2 = 0.85, p-value < 2.2e-16, Additional file 1: Fig S4B). We also compared our ASE genes with published imprinted genes in the pig. The majority of published imprinted genes (21 of 30) were also identified as monoallelic expression genes (MAE gene, Additional file 3: Table S5) across tissues. Among these detected imprinted genes, 16 were observed as MAE genes in the same tissues as previously reported (Additional file 4: Table S6), but not all samples showed monoallelic expression. Meanwhile, most of the genes were also identified as ASE genes across tissues. These results suggest that our workflow can recover known imprinted signals, although the detection of MAE from RNA-imputed genotypes should be interpreted with caution.

Effect of covariates on ASE identification

In RNA-data analyses, the effects of technical covariates can be a considerable problem, especially in gene expression analyses, in which read counts across samples are compared. However, in ASE analyses, read counts from the different alleles at the same position within an individual are compared, which makes it less susceptible to technical covariates [30]. We explored the effects of different technical covariates on the ASE detection. First, we analyzed the correlation between the fraction of significant ASE sites and genes and various technical variates. A relatively high correlation was observed with the number of reads after wasp filtering (adjusted R2 = 0.379), which is expected as the statistical power of detecting ASE is related to the total read count. For the remaining technical covariates, the correlations were significant (p-value < 0.05) but not highly correlated (adjusted R2 < 0.2). We studied whether the high reads covered on specific sites could minimize the correlation of technical covariates, considering that higher read coverage per site or gene could ensure sufficient detection power. The correlation was decreased with > 30 reads covered but remained significant. Also, the correlation difference between the two settings was small (Table 1). To further investigate the effects of technical covariates on ASE patterns across samples, multiple-dimensional scale (MDS) clustering of ASE sites was performed based on the pairwise ASE distance between samples. Samples were labeled with different technical covariates. The clustering did not exhibit a specific pattern for the technical covariates across samples (Additional file 2: Fig. S6A-C), as contrasted with the tissue and breed clustering (Additional file 2: Fig. S6D), which is also consistent with the higher correlation with breeds (adjust R2 = 0.292) and tissues (adjust R2 = 0.475), indicating the ASE distribution pattern among samples was not driven by batch effect. ASE distribution among tissues and breeds

Considerable variation was observed in the ASE distribution across tissues and breeds. Among the 42 tissues (3 cell lines included), the ASE sites fraction ranged from 1.31% in the pituitary to 53.24% in the blastomere (Fig. 2A). The lowest ASE site fraction was found in the brain (2.56% ASE on average among brain tissues), consistent with the observation in human GTEx data [19]. Also, the cartilage and synovial membrane showed a relatively low ASE site fraction (~ 2.7%), indicating a relatively stable and differentiated nature, which typically exhibits less transcriptional variability between alleles. The intestine-related tissues exhibited a modest ASE sites fraction (mean 4.77%) compared with the overall tissue average (mean: 8.16%). A high ASE site fraction was observed in oocytes and embryo-related tissues, with the highest values occurring during the early embryo development. These inflated ASE fractions in these tissues should be interpreted with caution, as samples from different oocyte and embryonic stages may have been pooled, potentially inflating heterozygosity and ASE estimates. At the gene level, a total of 13,684 ASE genes were found in at least one sample, of which 11.51% were detected in only one sample. Similar trends were observed across tissues, as for ASE site fractions, albeit with a higher ASE gene fraction than the ASE site fraction (ranging from 4.46% in the pituitary to 66.37% in the blastomere). At the breed level, breeds with more than 10 samples were selected to explore ASE between breeds (cell lines were excluded). As shown in Fig. 2B, the ASE site and gene fraction varied in general between breeds. A relatively high ASE site fraction was found in the local Chinese breeds (~ 18.64% on average). There was no significant difference in the ASE site fraction between crossbreeds (average: 6.10%) and commercial breeds (Fig. 2B, commercial breeds including: Yorkshire, Landrace, Duroc; average: 5.23%). However, breed showed a significant, though relatively weak, correlation with ASE site fraction (adjusted R2 = 0.29, Table 1), and tissue composition explains a larger proportion of variation in ASE fraction (Additional file 2: Fig. S6D). Thus, the ASE fractions at the breed level are likely reflected by tissue type; for example, the Bama Xiang population showed high ASE site and gene fractions (47.78% for ASE site fraction and 36.15% for ASE gene fraction), of which 70 of 115 samples were from blastomere tissue. Considering the sample size distribution is quite uneven across breeds, only a few tissues were selected, and pure breeds were grouped into commercial and Chinese local breeds to explore the breed-level distribution of ASE site fractions. Only Tongcheng (a Chinese local breed) showed a high fraction of ASE sites (average: 42.1%) compared to other pure breeds in muscle tissue (Additional file 2: Fig. S7A). For the other tissues (blood, adipose, liver, uterus), there was no substantial difference between commercial breeds and Chinese local breeds (Additional file 2: Fig. S7B).

Tissue-specific ASE genes

Multiple studies have indicated that ASE genes likely originate from cis-regulatory genetic variation, supported by frequent overlap between ASE and cis-eQTLs [1, 22, 33] and variants in promoter or enhancer regions that may alter transcription factor binding affinity [6]. Thus, ASE will, therefore, depend on the segregation of these variants within and between populations. In addition, tissue-level or tissue-specific ASE, regulated by, e.g., epigenetic marks, may indicate variation in tissue-specific regulatory sites (i.e., enhancers). To explore whether tissue-level or tissue-specific ASE genes were present in our data, we searched for genes expressed in multiple tissues that showed ASE in only a single tissue or a subset of tissues (see Materials/Methods for details). In short, genes were defined as tissue-level ASE genes if at least 70% of samples within a specific tissue category expressed the gene, and among those, 70% exhibited significant ASE. If the tissue-level ASE gene was detected in only one tissue and was generally tested for ASE across other tissues, it was classified as a tissue-specific ASE gene (see Methods for details). A total of 165 tissue-level ASE genes were detected across 28 tissues (Fig. 3A, B, Additional file 4: Table S9), ranging from 1 to 63 genes per tissue (Additional file 3: Table S8). 142 tissue-level ASE genes were only detected in one tissue and 23 tissue-level ASE genes (e.g. SLA-3, ZNF622) were observed in more than one tissue. Among these tissue-level ASE genes, two genes showed strong tissue-specific ASE: ENSSSCG00000011237 (PDCD6IP) in Blastomere and ENSSSCG00000017089 (ANXA6) in Fibroblast. These were detected across 28 tissues but were only identified as ASE in these two tissues separately. PDCD6IP codes for the programmed cell death 6 interacting protein involved in cytokinesis after mitosis. It has been well studied in the mouse, showing ubiquitous expression [34], and is involved in multiple processes, such as membrane repair, cytokinesis, and cell proliferation [35]. ANXA6 belongs to a family of calcium-dependent membrane and phospholipid-binding proteins. The ENSSSCG00000001229 (SLA-3) gene showed highly tissue-shared ASE. It showed ASE across multiple tissues (in 10 of the 20 tissues tested). We grouped tissues by similar function to further explore tissue-level ASE signatures. Across the intestinal tissues (jejunum, duodenum, small intestine, colon, caecum), thirty-three tissue-level ASE genes were identified, each detected in at least one but most not uniformly across all tissues (Fig. 3C). There were 3 tissue-level ASE genes detected in the four intestine-related tissues: SLA-3, OAS2, ENSSSCG00000041364 (Fig. 3C). OAS2 codes for a 2'−5'-oligoadenylate synthetase 2 protein, which is involved in host anti-PRRSV response [37]. SLA-3 plays a critical role in immune response, while the ENSSSCG00000041364 gene’s function is to be explored. However, these three tissue-level ASE genes were widely expressed across most pig tissues and were also detected as tissue-level ASE genes in other tissues, suggesting they are not intestine-specific. For the rest of tissue-level ASE genes in intestinal tissues, the GO analysis revealed several important genes involved in different biological processes (Additional file 4: Table S10), such as lysozyme activity (LYZ, CPSF6), peptidoglycan muralytic activity (LYZ, CPSF6), defence response to bacteria (LYZ, S100A14, FCN2, CPSF6, OAS1), vitamin, mineral digestion and absorption (SLC46A1) and metabolism (SUCLG2). In the brain tissues, four brain-specific ASE genes were identified (ALDH5A1, GH1, DMP1, DSPP). ALDH5A1 is involved in the gamma-aminobutyric acid metabolic process; a mutation of this gene is related to neurological phenotypes [38]. GH1 is a member of the somatotropin prolactin family of hormones and is primarily synthesized and secreted in the anterior pituitary [39]. DSPP and DMP-1 are highly phosphorylated proteins and essential for properly developing hard tissues [40]. For other tissue categories, such as embryo-related tissues (embryo, blastomere, morula, blastocyst, placenta) and reproductive tissues (testis, uterus, ovary, oocyte), tissue-level ASE genes displayed strong tissue-specific patterns, and no tissue-level ASE genes were shared within each category. In the immune system category (bone marrow, blood, macrophages, lymph nodes), tissue-level ASE genes were only detected in the lymph nodes.

Discussion

This study is the first to investigate global ASE across pig tissues and breeds using RNA-seq data compiled by the Farm-GTEx consortium [27]. We adapted an ASE analysis workflow based on widely used and well-established methods. The large sample size allowed us to establish an extensive ASE resource covering most pig tissues and breeds. We explored the overall distribution of ASE across the genome, tissue-level, and breed-level ASE characteristics.

Genotype data accuracy is an important factor in identifying true ASE. Genotype data are ideally obtained from DNA sequencing, but RNA-seq data have also been used to identify heterozygous sites and detect ASE in many species, including humans [28], sheep [7], and chickens [41]. However, SNP detection and genotype calling from RNA-seq also present several challenges. For example, a heterozygous site may be incorrectly called as homozygous due to mono-allelic expression; variation detected at the RNA level may not be present at the DNA level; and differences in gene expression levels, which lead to variation in read depths, constitute major challenges for SNP detection at the individual level. However, few studies have used RNA-seq imputed (RNAim) genotype data in ASE analyses [28, 42]. RNAim genotype data were applied in this work for the following reasons. Imputation increases variant numbers (Additional file 1: Table S1), and population-level imputation benefits from the information accumulated by the reads across individuals. To evaluate the accuracy of RNAim genotype data, we compared the heterozygous genotype concordance (HetGC%) of RNA imputed genotypes and RNA genotypes to DNA derived genotypes. The RNAim genotype data showed a higher average HetGC% (97.31%) compared with DNA-RNA HetGC% (74.33%) (Additional file 2: Fig. S2A). RNAim genotype data also exhibited similar performance in removing mapping bias and capturing ASE trends as DNA and RNA derived genotype data (Additional file 2: Fig. S2B, C). Overall, based on the large dataset used here, we are convinced that the imputed genotype data are suitable for identifying the ASE trends at the population level, although caution is needed when interpreting individual or single-site ASE events.

Additionally, we compared published imprinted genes with our ASE results to evaluate the biological relevance of our results. More than half of the known imprinted genes (16 of 30) were identified as a MAE gene in the same tissues as previously reported. The tissue-level concordance between the imprinted genes and our MAE genes confirmed the ability of our analyses to capture known imprinting patterns. In addition, most imprinted genes displayed ASE rather than strict MAE across tissues or individuals. Such variation indicates that imprinting may not be strictly monoallelic across all tissues or individuals, and can be context-dependent, showing partial, incomplete, or tissue-specific monoallelic expression rather than a uniform pattern across the organism. However, new MAE genes in our study should be taken with some caution, as imputation errors may have converted homozygous loci into apparent heterozygous sites, thereby inflating the observed MAE gene number.

ASE sites were widely but unevenly distributed across the genome, with hotspots correlating with gene-rich regions on the chromosomes. In human LCL cell lines, ASE-rich genomic loci were identified that are particularly associated with immune gene clusters, such as the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) on chromosome 6, where a sharp increase in ASE cumulative occurrence rates was observed [43]. However, in our study, ASE sites exhibited relatively uniform occurrence rates, and no specific functional enrichment was detected within ASE hotspots. This discrepancy may be attributed to the mixed tissue types and the diverse breeds analysed in our study, potentially obscuring the cell-specific patterns observed in more homogeneous datasets.

We explored the variation in ASE numbers (sites and genes) across different tissues. Firstly, a distinct clustering pattern of ASE sites was observed on the MDS plot comparing pairwise ASE distances between individuals. Distinct tissue clusters strongly suggest that tissue type has the most considerable effect on ASE site fraction. Among the tissue-level ASE distributions, embryo-related tissues showed the highest ASE site fraction, with variation across developmental stages (highest in blastomeres and lower in morula and blastocyst). It is expected that the paternal genome is silent to avoid rejection by the maternal immune system during the early stages of embryo development [44]. Oocyte samples also showed relatively high ASE fractions. This may partly reflect the complex chromatin reorganization and allele-dosage changes that occur during oogenesis [45], as well as the extensive epigenetic reprogramming reported in oocytes and early embryos across mammals, including pigs [46]. In addition, the use of bulk RNA-seq may have introduced heterogeneity from multiple oocytes at different meiotic stages. Future studies using well-annotated single-embryo or single-cell datasets will be important to validate ASE patterns during early development. However, these elevated estimates should be interpreted with caution because metadata for some RNA-seq datasets were incomplete, and pooling cannot be excluded, which may inflate heterozygosity and ASE estimates. The lowest ASE site fraction was found in the brain-related tissues, a similar trend as observed in humans (2% ASE), supporting reduced gene diversity in the brain [47]. However, another study showed that the ASE genes in the porcine brain are estimated to be 42% of all genes [26] and around 14–26% in the cattle brain [21]. Also, in the thymus, the cattle study showed a 10% ASE site fraction (986 ASE sites out of 9,781 tested sites), whereas 2.95% ASE sites were observed in our study. A direct comparison, however, is not appropriate since the differences in the level of ASE may not only come from biological reasons, such as tissue type, species, development stage, genetic diversity, but also from technology confounders, such as different methods, filtering criteria, and statistical ways to detect ASE. Thus, a standard, well-accepted pipeline could help to reduce the technical bias and improve the comparability of results. The ASE profiles tended to be highly individual and tissue-specific, and ASE genes shared within and across tissues and cell types were limited. We still found several tissue-level and tissue-specific ASE genes, and their functions were consistent with the tissue type. These tissue-level ASE genes likely reflect variation in tissue-specific regulatory sites where SNPs in noncoding regions may affect transcription factor binding affinity or chromatin state between alleles. Previous studies have also shown that genetic and epigenetic effects can interact through allele-specific DNA methylation [48]. Further analysis, including haplotype-specific epigenetic data, such as DNA methylation or histone modifications, may provide clues on how these ASE genes are regulated.

We also explored the breed-level ASE. In general, Chinese local breeds exhibited relatively high ASE site fraction (mean ~ 18.64% for all tissues), while no significant difference in the ASE site fraction was observed between crossbreeds (average: 6.10%) and commercial breeds (Yorkshire, Landrace, Duroc, average: 5.23%) (Fig. 2B). However, because tissue type and breed are partly confounded in the dataset (Table 1), the observed correlations between ASE fraction and tissue or breed should be interpreted cautiously. We selected several tissues to compare the ASE fraction among breeds. Tongcheng showed a higher ASE fraction in the muscle than the other breeds. Tongcheng is a Chinese indigenous breed with slow-growing muscle and high meat quality [49], while commercial breeds such as Yorkshire show rapid muscle growth and high lean meat [50]. Previous RNA-seq studies have also reported distinct expression profiles between these indigenous and commercial breeds, suggesting underlying regulatory differences [51, 52]. ASE results may indicate breed-specific cis-regulatory variation related to muscle development, but further functional studies are needed to clarify the relationship. For the other tissues (blood, adipose, liver, and uterus, Additional file 2: Fig. S7B), there was no significant difference between commercial breeds and the Chinese local breed, despite their independent domestication histories and differences in genetic architecture [53]. Introgression from Chinese into European breeds has helped improve economically important traits in commercial breeds [53–55], suggesting that these introgression regions play a role in gene expression. Thus, future studies applying the origin of alleles (e.g., Asian versus European) for ASE genes may provide insight into the effect of historical introgression on gene expression. In our study, we didn’t consider the parent-of-origin effect due to a lack of trio-family samples. Furthermore, the direction of the cross will also affect the ASE expression profile. Studies using reciprocal crosses in chicken [9], mouse [54], and pig [6] have highlighted the complexity of ASE and parent-of-origin expression.

Knowledge of ASE may provide further insights into the molecular basis underlying complex phenotypes. With the vast ASE resource described in the current study, assessing the connection between economically relevant phenotypes and ASE sites/gene profiles could improve the power to find causal variants through GWAS [43]. Loci exhibiting ASE have been associated with production traits, including intramuscular fat content in pigs [55] as well as meat quality in cattle [56] and pigs [57]. Several further investigations can be conducted, e.g., combining ASE with eQTL to fine-map cis-regulatory functional variants [58] and exploring introgressed and deleterious regulatory variation [59]. Combining multi-omic data will provide further insight into the contribution of epigenetic variation to gene expression variation in a population [60, 61]. Our publicly available SNP-level and gene-level ASE data will provide a powerful resource complementing the available eQTL data established by the Farm-GTEX-pig consortium [22].

Conclusions

In this work, We used the data from the Pig-GTEx consortium to conduct a vast SNP- haplotype- and gene-level ASE resource consisting of 42 tissues and 34 pig breeds. At first, we observed that the ASE is broad but unevenly distributed across the genome. Then, we explored the diversified ASE distribution across tissues and breeds. The embryo and reproduction-related tissues showed a relatively high ASE site and gene fraction. This vast ASE resource has numerous uses for studying regulatory variation, such as studying the effects of rare regulatory variation, fine-mapping cis-regulatory functional variants with the eQTL, and exploring the introgressed regulatory variation. By making the vast SNP- and gene-level ASE data in the porcine genome for the first time, we assume that this resource will find similarly broad use as the human ASE resource [1, 33].

Methods

Sample collection, RNA-seq, and genotype data

In total, 7,008 samples’ RNA sequence data were collected from the porcine Farm-GTEx project [27]. A similar QC workflow was performed as the porcine Farm-GTEx project. Briefly, adaptors were trimmed, and poor-quality reads were discarded using Trimmomatic (v0.39) [62]. BAM files were produced by mapping RNA-seq fastq files to the Sscrofa11.1 reference genome (Ensembl v100) using STAR (v2.7.0) [63], with the options: -outFilterMismatch-Nmax 3, -outSAMmapqUnique 255. Then, the samples with a mapping rate lower than 85% were selected and tested for possible contamination or wrongly assigned species. 10,000 reads were randomly selected from the fastq files from these samples by seqtk (version:1.3-r106) and blasted against the NCBI’s nucleotide (NT) database (NCBI v5) [64]. The samples in which more than 3% of selected reads showed the highest similarity to at least one non-pig species within the top 5 NT-hit species were excluded from the analysis (77 samples removed). Reference mapping bias was eliminated by applying the WASP methodology [65] using STAR with options “-waspOutputMode SAMtag” combined with each sample’s genotype data. Finally, 6,927 samples were kept with QC quality ≥ 30 and mapping rates ≥ 65%. The unique (MAPQ = 225) and passed WASP filtered (with the tag “vW:i:1”) reads were kept by using Samtools (version: 1.14) [66] with the “view” function for the next steps. The GATK (v4.2.1.0) [67] MarkDuplicate mode with default parameters was used to remove PCR duplicate reads.

The imputed, phased genotype data used in this study were obtained from the Farm-GTEx consortium and contained 3,087,268 population-level variants across 7,008 samples [27]. In brief, these variants were called from RNA sequence data, and heterozygous SNPs were imputed to genome-wide SNPs based on the multibreed pig genomics reference panel (PGRP, Farm-GTEx). We use the term RNA-imputed (RNAim) genotype to refer to these genotype data. To get sample-level genotype data, Bcftools (Version: 1.9) [66] was used to select the individual genotype data from the RNA-imputed genotype data. The homozygous reference sites (with “0|0”) and clustered SNPs (≥ 3 SNPs in 10 bp windows) were removed to get the final individual genotype. Finally, each sample had an average of 1,400,235 SNPs.

DNA-seq variant detection

WGS data of 3 individuals from three breeds (Duroc, Pietrain, and Yorkshire) were included (PRJEB19268). The alignment was performed using BWA-MEM (Burrow-Wheeler Aligner) [68] with options “-M -R '@RG\tID:PigWUR150\tSM: PigWUR-150–1\tPL:ILLUMINA'”. Freebayes (v0.9.21) [69] was utilized to identify variants for each sample with the options “–min-base-quality 10”, “–min-mapping-quality 20”, “–min-alternate-fraction 0.2”, “–haplotype-length 0”, “–pooled-continuous –ploidy 2”, “–min-alternate-count 2”. Subsequently, VcfFilter (v0.2) [70] was employed to remove low-quality sites using the option “-f QUAL > 20”, while vcfkeepgeno [70] was used to select specific FORMAT fields: “GT DP AD” within the VCF file. Indels were excluded, and bi-allelic SNPs were chosen using VCFtools (0.1.16) [71]. Afterward, clustered SNPs (≥ 3 SNPs in 10 bp windows) were eliminated by using the tools “VariantFiltration” in GATK (v4.2.1.0) (-window 10 -cluster 3).

Mapping bias filtration and ASE detection

A two-step approach was employed to decrease reference mapping bias. Initially, the option “waspOutputMode” in STAR (v2.7.0) was used at the RNA-seq alignment stage, as described above. Despite the use of WASP a preference of outliers towards the reference genome was still observed. The following approach was therefore used to omit these biases: the mean of the reference ratio was calculated [Reference ratio = reference reads/total reads] for each sample. The reference ratio's Interquartile Range (IQR) across all samples was calculated (IQR = 0.0065). Thresholds for selecting non-biased samples were computed as [0.4915, 0.51915] ([Q1-1.5*IQR, Q3 + 1.5*IQR]). Samples falling outside these thresholds were considered potential outliers and removed for the next analysis.

SNP-level ASE was generated using the phASER (version 1.1.1) package [72] with filtered RNA alignment file and sample genotype file described above as input file with the following settings: -mapq 255 and -baseq 10. The read coverage of the reference or alternative allele was calculated using phASER. The raw allele sites refer to variants (heterozygous sites) covered by at least one RNA-seq read, as reported by the phASER output for each sample. Subsequently, the sites were selected by these filter criteria for further analysis: 1) total mapped reads ≥ 15 for each locus/sample; 2) minimum of 3 reads mapped to reference or alternative allele; 3) proportion of mapped reference or alternative reads accounting for > 2% of the total mapped reads. These filtering criteria removed the sites with potential sequencing errors and mono-allelic expression (MAE). MAE sites were excluded due to the inflated number of MAE sites caused by errors in imputed genotype calling (unpublished observation). For the remaining sites (termed as ‘tested sites’ hereafter), a binomial test was used to estimate significant deviation from the expected 0.5 allele ratio (with the binom.test() function in R) [73]. Raw p values were corrected for multiple tests via the Benjamini–Hochberg test [74]. The ASE sites with a false discovery rate (FDR) ≤ 0.05 were considered significant.

Haplotype-level ASE was generated using the phASER package. For each sample, the haplotype expression output file (haplotypic_counts.txt) contains the number of unique reads that map to each haplotype for all phased haplotype blocks. Then, haplotypic blocks with a total read count of more than 8 were selected for the ASE gene analysis. The ASE gene were identified using phASER Gene AE 1.2.0, employing –min_haplo_maf 0.01, and utilizing the Ensembl annotation Sscrofa11.1 v104 gene features bed file as well as the filtered haplotypic count file from phASER. The raw output for the ASE genes contained the unique read count and variant number for each gene per sample. For the raw gene-level ASE data, similar processes were used to select the ASE gene as for ASE sites: 1) the total number of allelic reads covering each gene is ≥ 15 reads; 2) the allelic reads mapped to each haplotype are ≥ 2; 3) the proportion of reads for each haplotype accounts for > 2% of the total mapped read. The remaining genes (termed ‘tested gene’) were tested for significant imbalanced expression using binomial tests, and p values were corrected using the Benjamini–Hochberg method, and ASE genes with FDR ≤ 0.05 were considered significantly imbalanced. To test the allelic expression of published imprinted genes, MAE genes were selected based on the following criteria: 1) the total number of allelic reads covering each gene is ≥ 15 reads; 2) the allelic reads mapped to each haplotype allele (reference or alternative) ≤ 1.

ASE sites fraction = (ASE sites/Tested sites) *100 for each individual.

ASE gene fraction = (ASE gene/Tested gene) *100 for each individual.

Lastly, only samples with more than 200 tested sites and 10 ASE sites were retained for further analysis, resulting in 6,020 samples (Additional file 1: Table S2). The workflow of the ASE analysis pipeline is detailed in Fig. S1(Additional file 2: Fig. S1).

ASE distribution

To investigate the distribution of SNP level and gene-level ASE along the pig genome, the chromosomes were segmented into 1 Mb windows, and ASE site numbers and ASE ratio (ASE ratio = ASE sites/tested sites per 1 Mb) were calculated accordingly. The ASE number on each chromosome is related to the chromosome length. To further investigate the ASE enrichment difference on each chromosome, the ASE density per 1 Mb was calculated per chromosome and normalized based on the length of each chromosome, total gene length and total exon length. Multidimensional scaling cluster by ASE data

The classical multidimensional scaling (MDS) was used to evaluate sample clustering. At first, the distance between the two samples was calculated as the proportion of non-shared ASE sites at the total shared tested sites [30]. Then, a pairwise distance matrix was built for all samples, and MDS was produced by the cmdscale() function in R (v4.2.3).

Tissue-level ASE gene identification

Gene ASE was calculated at the sample level. To identify the tissue-level and tissue-specific ASE gene, we performed the following steps. Firstly, the small sample size tissues with similar functional anatomy were merged into one category, such as cerebellum (n = 2) and cerebrum (n = 2), which were merged into the brain tissue category. Then, for each tissue category, genes were classified based on two criteria: first, a gene was considered a tissue-level tested gene if it was expressed (total read covered > 15) in at least 70% of samples within that tissue category. Second, among these tested samples, the gene was required to show significant ASE in at least 70% of cases. Genes meeting both criteria were defined as tissue-level ASE genes. Genes that met this criterion in only one tissue category, but were expressed (tested) in other tissues without significant ASE, were further classified as tissue-specific ASE genes. Cell lines were excluded from this analysis. GO analysis (ShinyGO 0.80 [75]) was performed for all tissue-level ASE genes per tissue to explore these ASE gene functions.
