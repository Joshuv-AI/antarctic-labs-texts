Expression of VISTA on peripheral blood mononuclear cells in Systemic lupus erythematosus: association with disease activity and treatment status
Authors: Afsaneh Enteshari-Moghaddam, Rozita Abolhasan, Meysam Motevasseli, Nasrin Fouladi, Majid Eterafi, Sahar Samemaleki, Hamed Kyanfar, Elham Safarzadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSystemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a heterogeneous autoimmune disease characterized by autoantibody production and multi-organ involvement due to dysregulated immune responses. Ongoing research aims to understand the underlying mechanisms of this immune dysregulation, with particular interest in immune checkpoint molecules such as VISTA (encoded by the VSIR gene), which exerts context-dependent immunomodulatory effects. We analyzed a large-scale single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) dataset (GSE174188) comprising 261 samples (162 SLE cases, 99 controls) to investigate VSIR expression patterns across peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) subsets in SLE versus controls, using bioinformatics tools. Additionally, fresh PBMCs were isolated from SLE patients and healthy controls, and VSIR mRNA levels were quantified by RT-qPCR in treatment-naïve cases and those receiving Prednisolone, Hydroxychloroquine, or supplementary medications. Associations between VSIR expression and clinical/demographic parameters, including disease activity index, were also evaluated. scRNA-seq analysis revealed significantly upregulated VSIR expression in monocytes from SLE patients compared to healthy controls. In contrast, pseudobulk differential expression analysis of the entire PBMC population, corroborated by RT-qPCR on fresh samples, demonstrated downregulation of VSIR in SLE cases overall (log₂ fold change = -1.23). Notably, VSIR expression differed across treatment groups, with the lowest levels observed in treatment-naïve patients and higher levels in those receiving first-line therapies (prednisolone and hydroxychloroquine). Furthermore, VSIR expression exhibited a significant negative correlation with SLE disease activity index. This study demonstrates decreased VSIR expression in the whole PBMC population in SLE patients, suggesting a potential role in disease pathogenesis processes possibly through altered immune checkpoint regulation. Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a heterogeneous systemic autoimmune disease characterized by a dysregulated immune system and production of the autoantibodies [9]. SLE often presents as a chronic disease with diverse clinical manifestations that can affect multiple organs, including the skin, kidneys, joints, and nervous system, and shows a higher prevalence among women [6]. The etiology of SLE remains complex and multifactorial, involving interactions between genetic predispositions, epigenetic alterations and environmental triggers that lead to aberrant immune responses [15]. This dysregulation results in the activation of both innate and adaptive immune pathways, culminating in the production of pathogenic autoantibodies by B cells and the formation of immune complexes that can deposit in various tissues, causing inflammation and damage [8]. Clinical manifestations of SLE are highly variable, ranging from mild symptoms to severe organ involvement, which complicates diagnosis and treatment [2]. Ongoing research aims to elucidate the underlying mechanisms driving this disease, with particular attention to the roles of immune cell subsets and cytokine networks in mediating tissue damage and disease progression [36].

V-domain immunoglobulin suppressor of T-cell activation (VISTA) encoded by VSIR gene, is a novel immunoregulatory molecule from the B7 family [25], expressed on both myeloid and lymphoid cells. VISTA functions by negatively regulating immune responses, maintaining peripheral tolerance, and controlling autoimmunity [34]. Previous studies have demonstrated that disrupting immune checkpoints can lead to severe autoimmune diseases [26]. Interestingly, combined inhibition of VISTA and PD-L1 has been proposed as a therapeutic strategy for T-cell-mediated conditions, including cancer [20]. On the other hand, activation of the VISTA pathway has been shown to suppress inflammation, highlighting its potential as a promising approach for treating autoimmune diseases [19]. Recent research indicates that VISTA plays a critical role in various autoimmune conditions, including SLE. In SLE models, VISTA deficiency has been associated with increased susceptibility to autoimmunity, suggesting its protective role in maintaining immune homeostasis [30]. Studies have also shown that VISTA functions as an immune checkpoint in multiple mouse models of autoimmune diseases, such as rheumatoid arthritis and experimental allergic encephalomyelitis (EAE), where its absence exacerbates disease severity [34]. In addition to its role in autoimmunity, VISTA’s dual function as both a ligand and receptor allows it to modulate T-cell responses effectively. It interacts with various binding partners and can influence immune cell behavior in different microenvironments [34]. The potential for VISTA-targeted therapies is being explored in clinical settings, with ongoing trials assessing the efficacy of targeting human VISTA using monoclonal antibodies and small molecules [32].

In this study, we evaluated the cell-type-specific expression patterns of VSIR within the heterogeneous peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) compartment of patients with SLE and healthy controls using a comprehensive single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) atlas. VSIR mRNA levels in PBMCs from newly diagnosed cases, those undergoing various treatment regimens, and healthy controls were also quantified. Our findings shed light on the potential roles of VSIR, particularly its expression in monocytes, in the pathogenesis and progression of SLE. Furthermore, this study demonstrates how VSIR expression responds to common medications used in the management of SLE, such as Prednisolone and Hydroxychloroquine.

Methods and materials

Data acquisition and preprocessing

The scRNA-seq used in this study was obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) under accession number GSE174188. This large-scale multiplexed scRNA-seq (mux-seq) dataset contains 1,263,676 PBMCs from 261 unique samples of 162 cases and 99 healthy controls [27]. According to the metadata and supplementary information from the original publication, the SLE cases exhibited varied treatment histories at the time of sampling: no therapy (n = 21), azathioprine (n = 15), mycophenolate mofetil (n = 54), hydroxychloroquine (n = 113), methotrexate (n = 13), and calcineurin inhibitors (n = 10). Donor-level treatment metadata were not available in the dataset. Disease activity was assessed using the SLE Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI) based on clinical manifestations. The dataset had already undergone preprocessing and annotation by the original authors [27]. For downstream analysis and visualization, we imported the pre-processed and annotated data into Scanpy; v1.9.3 [31]. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) embeddings were visualized using scanpy.pl.umap.

Differential expression and gene set enrichment analysis

Differential expression analysis on the generated pseudobulk count matrix was performed using pyDESeq2, a Python implementation of the DESeq2 R package [23]. DESeq2 employs a generalized linear model (GLM) framework with a negative binomial distribution to model count data and account for variability between samples. Genes were considered differentially expressed if they met the criteria of |log2FC|> 1 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.01. GSEA analysis for MSigDb Hallmark gene sets (v.2020) was performed using the EnirchR interface implemented in GSEApy (https://github.com/zqfang/GSEApy). Associated p-values were computed with Fisher’s exact test. Adjusted p-values (q-values) were calculated using the Benjamini–Hochberg method for correction for multiple hypothesis testing.

Single cell compositional analysis

Single-cell compositional changes in the scRNA-seq dataset were assessed using the scCODA framework [5] to test for differences in cell-type proportions between sample groups. We modeled the effects of the binary covariate’s disease status (162 SLE cases vs. 99 healthy controls) using cell-type annotations. For each donor, cell-type proportions were first calculated, after which scCODA’s automatic reference selection was applied. This procedure identifies the cell type with minimal dispersion in relative abundance that is present in at least 95% of samples; in this dataset, it selected NK cells as the reference. Parameter inference was performed via Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) sampling with the default chain length of 20,000 iterations. False discovery rates (FDRs) were set to 0.2 for the disease status analysis, following the original scCODA recommendations. Cell types for which the respective covariate showed a statistically credible effect are reported in the main figures.

Study design and ethics statement

Forty-five consecutive patients who fulfilled the 2019 ACR/EULAR classification criteria for systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) were recruited from the Rheumatology Clinic of Imam Khomeini Hospital, Ardabil, Iran. Based on their current treatment regimens, the SLE patients were categorized into five groups as follows: (1) treatment-naïve patients, (2) patients receiving prednisolone (5–7.5 mg/day) plus hydroxychloroquine (200 mg/day), (3) patients receiving prednisolone (5–7.5 mg/day) plus hydroxychloroquine (200 mg/day) plus methotrexate (7.5–15 mg/week), (4) patients receiving prednisolone (5–7.5 mg/day) plus hydroxychloroquine (200 mg/day) plus azathioprine (100–200 mg/day), and (5) patients receiving prednisolone (5–7.5 mg/day) plus hydroxychloroquine (200 mg/day) plus mycophenolate mofetil (1–2 g/day).

Disease activity/severity was assessed using the SLE Disease Activity Index 2000 (SLEDAI-2 K) based on the criterion defined in kelly’s reference which is the comprehensive and reliable score index to carefully assess the clinical symptoms of SLE including central nerve, vascular, musculoskeletal, and renal damage. Disease activity was classified as follows: SLEDAI = 0: no activity, SLEDAI = 1–5: mild activity, SLEDAI = 6–10: moderate activity, SLEDAI = 11–19: high activity, and SLEDAI ≥ 20: very high activity (Supplementary Table 1). Subject who had a history of other inflammatory or autoimmune conditions were excluded to minimize confounding factors. Ten age- and gender-matched healthy individuals with no history of autoimmune disorders served as the control group. This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Ardabil University of Medical Sciences, Ardabil, Iran (approval reference number: IR.ARUMS.REC.1401.066), in accordance with the institution’s guidelines and applicable ethical standards. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment.

Sample collection and PBMC isolation

Fresh peripheral blood samples were collected from SLE patients and healthy controls who attended Rheumatology Clinic. PBMCs were isolated using the Ficoll density gradient centrifugation method. Following isolation, cell viability was assessed using Trypan blue exclusion staining to ensure the integrity of the samples for subsequent analyses.

RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis, and real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from PBMCs using TRIzol Reagent (AnaCell, Iran) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The purity of RNA were examined using spectrophotometry (Nanodrop 2000, Thermo Scientific). cDNA was synthesized from total RNA by a commercial kit (YTA, Yekta Tajhiz Azma, Iran) based on the manufacturer’s instructions. RT-qPCR was done via AMPLIQON 2× qPCR Master Mix Green-No ROX on a Bio Rad CFX96 real-time PCR system, adhering to the manufacturer’s recommendations. The specific primer sequences for VSIR are as follows: (F: 5′- GCGGATGGACAGCAACATT ‐3′, R: 5′‐ TTGGAGAGTCAGGGACAGGG‐3’) and GAPDH (F: 5′‐AAGGTGAAGGTCGGAGTCAAC 3′, R: 5′‐GGGGTCATTGATGGCAACAA‐3′). The 2−ΔΔCt method was used to determine the relative gene expression levels, and normalized to GAPDH as the internal control.

Statistical analyses

Preprocessing and initial computations were conducted in Python (version 3.9) using pandas (1.4.2), SciPy (1.8.0), and NumPy (1.21.5). Statistical hypothesis testing, including Mann–Whitney U tests for two-group comparisons, Kruskal–Wallis tests followed by Dunn’s post-hoc test (with multiplicity correction), and Spearman’s rank correlations, were performed using SPSS (version 27) and/or R (with relevant packages). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Single-cell RNA-seq revealed the cell-type specific expression of VSIR in PBMCs of SLE and healthy individuals

We employed a publicly available scRNA-seq atlas of 1,263,676 PBMCs. The dataset was composed of 261 unique samples of 162 cases and 99 healthy controls obtained from the California Lupus Epidemiology Study (CLUES) [18] and the ImmVar Consortium [33] and profiled by multiplexed single-cell sequencing (mux-seq) technology (Fig. 1A). To assess alterations in cellular composition in SLE, we compared cell type frequencies between SLE cases and healthy controls using scCODA. Compared to controls, SLE cases showed a significant increase in classical monocytes (cMono) with log2 fold change (log2FC) of 0.5 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 (Fig. 1B).

To evaluate overall VSIR expression across the PBMC compartment, we performed a pseudobulk differential expression analysis, accounting for cell type composition. This approach aggregated single-cell expression data from whole PBMCs, thereby enhancing statistical power for robust gene expression comparisons. This revealed downregulation of VSIR in SLE cases compared to controls, with a log2FC of -1.23 (Fig. 1C).

Single-cell RNA-seq revealed the aberrant gene expression in SLE monocytes and VSIR gene expression in monocytes revealed distinct patterns across the clinical groups

To characterize the expression pattern of VSIR, we visualized its expression levels across annotated PBMCs. UMAP plot revealed that VSIR is predominantly expressed in monocyte clusters (Fig. 2A). Among the various cell types analyzed, cMono exhibited a marked upregulation of VSIR expression in SLE patients compared to healthy controls, with non-classical monocytes (ncMono) showing a modest increase. In contrast, the expression levels of VSIR in other cell types, including B cells, natural killer (NK) cells, CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and conventional dendritic cells (cDC), remained largely comparable between SLE patients and healthy individuals (Figure. 2B). Accordingly, we performed a targeted pseudobulk differential expression analysis exclusively within monocyte clusters, comparing SLE patients to healthy individuals (Fig. 2C). Among the most significantly upregulated genes in SLE monocytes were EPSTI1, IFI27, and OTOF. EPSTI1 promotes monocyte adhesion to endothelial cells, facilitating their recruitment to inflammatory sites [3], while IFI27 is an interferon-stimulated gene indicating a heightened type I interferon response, which is characteristic of SLE and contributes to chronic inflammation and autoimmunity [4]. This analysis confirmed a significant upregulation of VSIR in monocytes of SLE patients, with a log2FC of 1.6 relative to controls. The observed upregulation of VSIR in specific monocyte subsets suggests their potential role in the immunomodulatory dysregulation characteristic of SLE. To further explore the biological processes affected by the altered gene expression in SLE monocytes, we performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) using the MSigDB Hallmark gene sets. Among the pathways with most notable enrichment was complement pathway, interferon alpha and gamma response, cholesterol homeostasis, and coagulation (Fig. 2D).

In whole PBMCs, flare SLE patients demonstrated significantly higher VSIR expression than non-flare SLE patients, with no significant difference compared to healthy controls (Fig. 3A). Demographics and clinical profiles of study participants

Our study consisted of 55 participants, comprising 10 healthy controls and 45 patients diagnosed with SLE. The mean age of the patient group was 38.66 ± 10.57 years, which was comparable to the mean age of the healthy group (39.10 ± 7.03 years). Females predominated in both groups, accounting for 80% of the patient group (n = 36) and 70% of the healthy group (n = 7). There were no statistically significant differences in age (Mann–Whitney U test, p = 0.77) or sex distribution (Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.67) between the healthy control group and the SLE group, confirming appropriate demographic matching. Based on SLE Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI), patients were grouped into three categories: Mild & Moderate (31.1%), High (40%), and Very High (28.9%). (Table 1)

We further categorized the SLE patients into subgroups according to their prescribed therapies: new cases (n = 9), and those receiving Prednisolone plus Hydroxychloroquine (n = 9) as first-line treatment. Prednisolone is a corticosteroid that possesses potent anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive effects, effectively alleviating symptoms associated with inflammation. Hydroxychloroquine, an antimalarial with immunomodulatory properties, is recommended as foundational therapy for all SLE patients unless contraindicated [28]. For patients whose initial treatment proves insufficient, additional immunosuppressive agents are often employed, including Methotrexate (n = 7), Azathioprine (n = 11), and Mycophenolate Mofetil (n = 9). Methotrexate is an antimetabolite and antifolate agent commonly used for managing a range of disorders involving immune system dysfunction, SLE among them. It works by inhibiting the proliferation of cells, particularly lymphocytes, thereby reducing immune system activity. Methotrexate is often used when other treatments are ineffective or insufficient [17]. Azathioprine is an immunosuppressive medication and is frequently prescribed for moderate-to-severe SLE to manage symptoms and prevent flares. Azathioprine is particularly effective in treating lupus nephritis and has a well-established safety profile. Mycophenolate mofetil is another immunosuppressant that inhibits lymphocyte proliferation by blocking purine synthesis. It is increasingly utilized for managing lupus nephritis and other severe manifestations of SLE due to its efficacy and relatively favorable side effect profile compared to other immunosuppressants [10]. Female predominance persisted across subgroups ranging from 66.6% to 88.8%. The mean age of participants ranged from 37.22 to 42.27 years across the different groups (Table 1). Clinical symptoms of patients are summarized in supplementary Table 2.

Dynamic expression of VSIR in PBMCs samples

To corroborate the findings from scRNA-seq, we evaluated VSIR gene expression in PBMCs from SLE and healthy controls using RT-qPCR. Relative VSIR expression was significantly decreased in new SLE cases compared to healthy controls (Fig. 4A). Next, we performed an exploratory analysis comparing VSIR expression in newly diagnosed SLE patients with different treatment regimens; however, due to limited sample sizes, these comparisons were underpowered and should be interpreted as descriptive rather than conclusive. According to our findings, newly diagnosed cases exhibited the lowest relative expression of VSIR. Compared with the newly diagnosed group, all treatment groups, except Prednisolone + Hydroxychloroquine + Methotrexate, showed significantly higher VSIR expression (p < 0.05, p < 0.05, and p < 0.001, respectively), with the Prednisolone + Hydroxychloroquine + Mycophenolate Mofetil group demonstrating the highest expression. However, no statistically significant differences in VSIR expression were observed among the treatment subgroups. These findings suggest that the different treatment regimens evaluated in this study were not associated with detectable differences in VSIR expression (Fig. 4B).

Association of VSIR expression with demographic and clinical characteristics

We then evaluated the mean expression of VSIR among demographic and clinical variables. The SLEDAI-2 K was significantly associated with VSIR expression (p = 0.03), but no other significant associations were found between VSIR expression and demographic characteristics (Table 2). We also compared VSIR expression among clinical symptoms and found that cases with musculoskeletal features had significantly higher expression (p = 0.01). The mean VSIR expression was 0.32 ± 0.15 in cases with at least one musculoskeletal symptom, compared to 0.20 ± 0.07 in cases without (Table 3).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the single-cell expression pattern of the VSIR gene in PBMCs and found that it was predominantly expressed in monocyte clusters. Furthermore, VSIR expression was upregulated in monocytes from SLE patients compared to healthy controls. According to previous studies, SLE monocytes exhibit a distinct gene expression profile characterized by an interferon signature, which is associated with disease activity and inflammatory responses. Specifically, recent studies have found that cMono from SLE patients displayed enhanced expression of genes related to pro-inflammatory pathways, playing a role in immune regulation and inflammation [11, 14]. In SLE patients, there is an increase in the frequency and activation of monocytes, which correlates with higher levels of inflammatory cytokines and immune complex formation [13]. Upregulation of VSIR in these cells reveals an association that may reflect an immunomodulatory effect and contribution to SLE pathogenesis. This finding warrants functional studies to elucidate how monocytes, as key mediators of inflammatory responses, could influence T cell activation through interactions with other immune cells. Studies have shown that monocytes in SLE patients exhibit an activated phenotype characterized by increased expression of costimulatory molecules, such as CD80 and CD86, which are essential for T cell activation [35]. This activation may lead to enhanced production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, further perpetuating the inflammatory environment associated with SLE [21]. VISTA delivers inhibitory signals to myeloid cells, including neutrophils, plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs), and monocytes, thereby dampening their activation and overall function. In SLE, monocytes exhibit up‑regulated inflammatory pathways and heightened expression of antigen‑presentation genes. Genetic loss or disruption of VISTA signaling in mice triggers spontaneous or aggravated autoimmunity, characterized by elevated inflammatory cytokines and pronounced myeloid activation, underscoring VISTA’s critical role in peripheral tolerance [30]. Therefore, insufficient VISTA engagement on monocytes, as observed in this study, may amplify their antigen‑presenting capacity and boost pro‑inflammatory cytokine and chemokine production, thereby intensifying the autoimmune response in SLE [12].

We also assessed the relative expression of VSIR in newly diagnosed SLE cases and compared it across various treatment regimens. Our results showed that untreated newly diagnosed SLE cases exhibit the lowest expression of VSIR, consistent with findings that immune checkpoint molecules, are dysregulated in active SLE, reflecting the complex interplay between immune activation and pathogenesis of this disease [29]. Notably, higher VSIR expression was observed in all treatment groups compared to treatment-naïve cases, suggesting a potential association between treatment status and immune checkpoint activity. Despite the elevated VSIR levels in treated groups, our analysis did not find a statistically significant difference among the various treatment regimens. This included combinations with Methotrexate, Azathioprine, or Mycophenolate mofetil. This pattern may suggest that the association between VSIR expression and treatment status is primarily driven by Prednisolone and Hydroxychloroquine use, with limited additional variation attributable to other drugs. It is also possible that these additional immunosuppressants exert their therapeutic effects predominantly through pathways distinct from VSIR, such as inhibition of lymphocyte proliferation (Methotrexate, Mycophenolate mofetil) or purine synthesis (Azathioprine) [1, 16]. However, given the cross-sectional design of this study, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, as the observed differences may reflect underlying variation in disease severity, duration, or patient selection rather than a direct effect of therapeutic intervention. Longitudinal studies with paired pre- and post-treatment samples are needed to establish whether treatment causally modulates VSIR expression. Analysis of the association between VSIR expression and clinical variables demonstrated significantly higher expression levels in SLE patients presenting with musculoskeletal manifestations, suggesting that VISTA is preferentially upregulated within the inflammatory pathways driving joint involvement in SLE. Although VISTA generally restrains systemic and renal lupus manifestations [12], it supports immune-complex-driven arthritis in preclinical models [7], implying that the VISTA pathway functions in a tissue- and context-dependent manner: protective in certain SLE compartments but potentially permissive for synovial inflammation [24]. Clinically, these findings position VISTA as a potential biomarker for a joint-dominant SLE endotype and highlight the importance of accounting for musculoskeletal involvement in the design and interpretation of VISTA-targeted therapies. The elevated expression of VSIR in PBMCs from SLE patients during disease flares, compared with those in non-flare states, is also consistent with previous reports demonstrating the activation of key immune pathways during flares. These pathways include complement activation, enhanced immunoglobulin responses, leukocyte activation, increased cytokine production, interferon (IFN) signaling, and neutrophil-driven innate immune responses [22].

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the clinical cohort size was modest, and treatment subgroups were uneven and relatively small, which limits statistical power and precludes definitive conclusions regarding differential effects of individual immunosuppressive agents. Given the heterogeneity of SLE, larger multi-center cohorts will be required to validate these findings. Second, the subgroup analyses were exploratory and should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Specifically, information on individual patient treatment exposures was not publicly available for the GSE174188 dataset. This represents a major limitation, as it prevented treatment-stratified comparisons of cell composition or pseudobulk differential expression across treatment subgroups, thereby making it difficult to distinguish disease-associated effects from treatment-related differences Third, although we observed a strong inverse correlation between VSIR expression and disease activity, the cross-sectional design does not allow causal inference. Longitudinal studies and mechanistic experiments are required to determine whether VSIR directly contributes to immune regulation or reflects compensatory immune checkpoint activation. Finally, functional assays and protein analysis were not performed to directly assess the immunoregulatory role of VSIR in monocytes, which represents an important direction for future research.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study provides evidence of downregulated VSIR expression on PBMC of SLE patients, suggesting its potential contribution to disease pathogenesis. Our findings further demonstrate that VSIR expression differs across treatment groups, with higher levels observed in patients receiving first-line therapies compared to treatment-naïve cases, indicating an association between VSIR expression and treatment status. These observations highlight a potential link between VSIR expression and clinical treatment patterns in SLE.; however, longitudinal studies are needed to clarify whether treatment causally influences VSIR expression and its role in SLE management.
