Perspectives and Needs of Caregivers in Protecting Their Child With Asthma From Wildfire Smoke
Authors: Jason J Lau, Kate Tokareva, Jonathan D Cogen, Catherine J Karr, Mary E Crocker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesTo determine the caregiver‐reported barriers, communication needs, and potential interventions to reduce wildfire smoke (WFS) risk for children with asthma.Study DesignEnglish‐ and Spanish‐speaking caregivers of children 4–13 years old with asthma were recruited from pediatric pulmonology clinics in Western Washington. Demographics and WFS‐related practices were assessed via one survey and one interview, using interpreters when needed. Inductive and deductive thematic analysis was conducted using the Capability, Opportunity, and Motivation for Behavior and Source‐Message‐Channel‐Receiver frameworks.ResultsTwenty‐three caregivers participated. Interviews were conducted in English (n = 19, 83%) or Spanish (n = 4, 17%). Participant statewide social vulnerability index by 2022 census tract ranged from 0.026 to 0.997. Qualitative analysis revealed several themes including (1) significant anxiety about WFS, (2) uncertainty about reliability of the air quality index and when to take specific protective actions, (3) complex decision‐making regarding staying indoors, and (4) financial barriers to implementing recommendations. Caregivers preferred receiving personalized messages about WFS from trusted health sources. We mapped the identified barriers to potential strategies to facilitate caregivers’ ability to enact WFS recommendations.ConclusionsCaregivers experience a variety of barriers to WFS safety. The intervention strategies we identified represent opportunities for action and areas for future research aimed at improving health outcomes for children with asthma at risk for WFS exposure.Keywords: behavior‐change interventions, climate change, environmental health, reactive airways disease, vulnerable populations

Introduction

Mounting evidence suggests that exposure to wildfire smoke (WFS) is linked to increased symptoms, hospitalizations, and healthcare costs for children with asthma [1, 2, 3, 4]. Additionally, long‐term WFS exposure may increase the risk of development of new asthma and chronic reductions in lung function [5, 6, 7, 8]. Children with asthma are more susceptible to the adverse effects of WFS than their peers [2, 3, 9]. Exposure is higher among those of minoritized racial and ethnic groups, limited English proficiency, and lower educational attainment [10]. Climate change has increased the frequency and intensity of wildfires, leading to increased WFS exposure over time [9, 11].

The Pediatric Environmental Health Specialty Units, a collaborative effort of the Centers for Disease Control, Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), and American Academy of Pediatrics, recommend actions (Figure 1) that caregivers can take to reduce exposure to WFS for healthy children [12, 13]. An integral part of these recommendations is to monitor and act on the air quality index (AQI), which is reported by the EPA at www.airnow.gov. The EPA recommends protective actions at lower levels of AQI for those sensitive to WFS, including children and other high‐risk groups (people with heart or lung disease, older adults, pregnant people, minority populations, and outdoor workers), compared to the general population [14, 15]. Caregivers of children with asthma should also ensure availability of asthma medications and establish an asthma action plan with their healthcare provider [12, 16].

Public health and pediatric healthcare providers play a critical role in communicating the health risks of WFS exposure and educating caregivers on strategies to reduce their child's WFS exposure risk [17, 18]. However, previous studies have shown limited uptake of guidance to use the AQI to determine when to act on ambient air pollution [19, 20]. Additionally, several WFS interventions require resources such as HEPA air cleaners (commonly known as air purifiers using HEPA filters) and N95 masks or knowledge that may be difficult to access for under‐resourced groups.

This qualitative study aimed to explore perspectives of caregivers of children with asthma on the impact of WFS on their families, the barriers they face to implementing WFS protective behaviors, and the ideal communication strategies to provide WFS‐related information. We developed a multilevel set of potential interventions to address the barriers identified.

Methods

Participants

Caregivers were recruited from three ambulatory pediatric pulmonology specialty clinics in Western Washington from October 2023 to March 2024. Eligibility criteria included adult caregivers speaking English or Spanish with a child aged 4–13 years old with a diagnosis of asthma and prescribed a daily inhaled steroid. We focused on children 4 years and older and those on a daily inhaled steroid due to a higher certainty of a true asthma diagnosis; children older than 13 were excluded due to the desire to focus on children likely to be most dependent on their caregiver for protection from WFS. Caregivers were excluded if they resided outside of Washington State or their child had another significant chronic pulmonary or cardiac condition. We used the Social Vulnerability Index (SVI) [21] to ensure the inclusion of a range of socioeconomic status. Recruitment proceeded until thematic saturation was reached during data analysis, defined as the point at which successive interviews yield no new codes or themes and only reinforced the existing thematic framework. Each participant received a $50 gift card as compensation.

Data Collection

Surveys were collected in English or Spanish using REDCap [22, 23] and assessed demographic characteristics, recent impacts of WFS on their child's asthma, and habits regarding checking air quality and using air cleaners (E‐text 1—Survey). We conducted semi‐structured qualitative interviews focused on caregivers’ prior experience with WFS events, current use of and barriers to specific WFS protective actions, and preferred ways to receive information related to WFS and health. Interview questions drew upon the Capability, Opportunity, Motivation for Behavior (COM‐B) model [24] to understand driving factors that influence behavior change, and the Source, Message, Channel, Receiver (SMCR) model [25] to understand preferences for receiving WFS communication (E‐text 2—Interview Guide) Interviews were performed via telephone or video conference and interviews were transcribed. Interpreters were used and transcriptions were translated as needed.

Data Analysis

Surveys were analyzed descriptively. Interview transcripts were analyzed thematically with an initial codebook created deductively based on the COM‐B and SMCR frameworks, and then adjusted inductively after review of the first two transcripts. Subsequently, five other random interview transcripts were coded separately by two researchers (JJL, KT) and reviewed for consensus. Disagreements were discussed and the codebook refined until an intercoder reliability > 0.8 was achieved [26]. The remaining transcripts were coded by one researcher (JJL) and reviewed for accuracy by KT or MC. Themes were generated through summarization, comparison and contrast, identifying outliers and missing concepts, and assessing for most and least frequently represented ideas. Methods and results were reported according to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) Checklist [27].

Positionality: Our research team included four pediatric physicians (JJL, JDC, CJK, MEC), all of whom provide medical care for children with asthma, and a medical student (KT) who had engaged in prior research on climate change impacts on health. This study was motivated by the authors’ desire as healthcare professionals to learn how caregiver perspectives could inform clinical and community‐based WFS interventions.

This study was approved by the Seattle Children's Hospital Institutional Review board on 8/29/2023 (IRB # STUDY00004483). Informed consent was obtained by each participant prior to data collection.

Results

Thirty‐six caregivers were approached; nine declined participation and four consented but did not complete the interview. Twenty‐three caregivers completed interviews, of whom 22 completed the survey. Nineteen (86%) were female, 11 (47%) had an SVI between 0.71 and 1.0 (indicating greater social vulnerability), and four caregivers (17%) completed the interviews in Spanish. Additional participant demographics are shown in Table 1. Our COREQ checklist, which includes numbers of refusals and drop‐outs, can be found in Table 2. The majority of caregivers reported that their child's health had been impacted by WFS (Table 3). Over half of participants (54%) reported having an air cleaner at home, and 3 (13%) owned an air quality monitor. Fifteen (68%) preferred mobile app as a source of air quality information, and 19 (86%) reported checking once or more per day when aware of local WFS events, versus 3 (13%) when not aware of WFS.

Caregivers' General Experiences and Perspectives on Wildfire Smoke

General Experiences With WFS

The majority of participants were aware of WFS in the region in recent years, but described a wide range of experiences, from smoky days being a minor nuisance to significant disruption, including academic and social interruptions, cancellation of summer plans, and need for evacuation. The majority of caregivers reported seeing WFS‐attributed symptoms in their child (including eye or throat irritation, cough, wheezing, shortness of breath, respiratory distress, or headaches). Severity of symptoms ranged from mild to life‐threatening, including one episode requiring hospitalization. Caregivers also described that WFS had a negative impact on their child's mental health and social wellbeing, attributing these effects from prolonged indoor confinement and reduced social and physical activities. One caregiver expressed this as the loss of their summer “grace period” between cold virus seasons.

Caregivers Worry About WFS

Personal feelings of worry, helplessness, fear, and frustration were common. Most worried about the immediate effects of WFS on their child's breathing, such as increased asthma symptoms and need for emergency care. Several also had concerns about the impact of repeated exposures on their child's lung health later in life.

Some caregivers worried that their current efforts to mitigate exposure would not be enough to help their child avoid negative health outcomes. Feelings of helplessness were associated with not having effective ways to mitigate smoke exposure, and lack of control over the child's exposure at school. Some were fearful that even if they kept their child indoors, smoke could still affect them inside the home. One parent voiced the fear that poor air quality is not always visible, and can be relatively easy to overlook, with potentially serious health consequences.

Understanding of WFS and Asthma

While our study was not designed to test caregivers’ knowledge about WFS, a sizable proportion of participants (n = 7, 30%) reported not knowing much or needing to know more about how WFS impacts asthma.

Feasibility and Acceptability of WFS Protective Recommendations

Understanding and Trust of the AQI

Many participants were aware of the existence of the AQI and its purpose of describing the air quality. They recalled that higher numbers indicated worse air quality, though typically relied on colors to signal the need to change behavior. There was a lack of clarity on what thresholds corresponded to specific recommended actions for healthy and sensitive individuals.

Several participants expressed uncertainty around the accuracy, relevance, and timeliness of current AQI information, fueled by discrepancies between information from different sources. Participants described this issue for both websites and mobile device apps, but it is unclear which specific data sources were most problematic. Some caregivers also doubted the relevance of air quality data when sensors were not close to their home or information was infrequently updated. Distrust of AQI information was also pronounced when AQI data and sensory cues (such as visibility or odors in the air) disagreed. Some people described triangulating between multiple sources of AQI data plus sensory information to decide whether action to avoid WFS was needed. For example, one participant would “split the difference and take a sniff test outside to see how bad it is.”

Decisions to Check and Act on the AQI

Participants had different cues and habits for checking the AQI. Some participants, especially those with home air quality monitors, routinely checked the air quality daily; sometimes incidentally when checking the weather. Those who did not have a routine of checking tended to rely on external triggers, such as the news, social networks, and apps. Sensory cues and symptoms (heavy breathing, headaches, eye irritation) were also potent triggers. Once they were aware of WFS in their area, some participants reported checking more frequently for a period of time.

Staying Indoors

Participants reported a complex decision‐making process about whether to keep their child indoors due to WFS. It was clear they valued the physical, mental, and social benefits of outdoor activities for their child. Keeping their child indoors meant sacrificing these benefits, while adding the burden of keeping their child occupied and safe indoors. For some, this meant being unable to work due to needing to provide childcare. Other factors such as the current AQI, the child's current health status (including respiratory health, mental health, and social wellbeing), and the wellbeing of the rest of the family were also given weight.

Participants also expressed uncertainty around operationalizing the recommendation to stay indoors. They wondered about whether low‐grade exposure could have long‐term risks, how much time outdoors was considered safe, and how to balance the potential negative impacts of staying indoors.

Caregiver Perspectives on School Practices

Participants supported that their child's school monitored air quality and restricted outdoor activities for poor air quality. While some were satisfied with their school's policies around air quality, other participants wanted stricter precautions, and were frustrated by inadequate thresholds for keeping children indoors. Additionally, some participants raised concerns about lack of accommodations or staffing to keep children indoors when needed, and inadequate ability of schools to keep their indoor air clean.

Use and Effectiveness of Portable Air Cleaners

For those who had portable air cleaners, usage ranged from continuous operation to use only when air quality was poor. Common barriers included remembering to turn the device on or change the filters, with additional concerns about air dryness and potential electrical fire risk. The majority believed air cleaners improved the indoor air, citing reduced symptoms, visibly dirty filters, or improved on indoor air quality monitor readouts. Among caregivers who did not own air cleaners, reported barriers included the cost of both device and replacement filters, uncertainty of which type to purchase, large device size, and confusion about the differences between portable air cleaners and dehumidifiers.

Delivering Information About WFS

When seeking out information about WFS and health on their own, participants used search engines and selected reputable websites, including those of local healthcare institutions and public health agencies. Health care providers, especially physicians and nurses,1‐ were highly trusted sources of information, particularly regarding information specific to asthma or to their child. Several wanted to hear about WFS from their child's school, although not all agreed they would notice or trust this information. Caregivers expressed interest in a broad range of content, including current air quality and forecasting, how to respond to poor air quality, and how WFS specifically affects children with asthma (including how much exposure is too much, how to recognize and prevent exacerbations, and long‐term impacts of exposure). Caregivers requested information that was action‐oriented and tailored to their child.

The preferred timing of communication varied depending on the type of content. Caregivers preferred to receive information about current air quality during WFS events, and pairing local AQI information with weather forecasts was considered helpful. Caregivers preferred receiving reminders about general WFS preparedness during spring or early summer, to allow sufficient time to act, spread out expenses, and obtain supplies before they are sold out. Several suggested tying reminders to milestones (such as the end of the school year or well‐child visits). One described that a discussion with their healthcare provider in the spring would help them weigh the health risks of outdoor versus indoor summer camp.

Discussion

The findings from this study reveal unique perspectives of caregivers of children with asthma, their strategies to mitigate the impacts of WFS, and specific informational needs related to WFS to aid in timely decision‐making.

Similar to previous research conducted with adults, caregivers expressed concerns beyond physical health effects in their child, including mental health and overall family well‐being [28]. The mental health concerns and anxieties described are consistent with previous research showing the psychological burden experienced by caregivers managing children with chronic health conditions or disabilities [29].

A novel finding from our study was the complexity of the decisions caregivers faced regarding protective behaviors for their children. For example, they articulated difficulties in keeping their child indoors during WFS episodes due to competing social, mental health, financial, and logistical factors. This highlights the need to consider the child and family holistically when providing anticipatory guidance and designing policy around reducing WFS exposure.

These decisions are further complicated by mistrust of air quality data, with caregivers reporting discrepancies between mobile apps and websites. As a result, caregivers reported relying on other information, such as seeing or smelling smoke, or behaviors among their social networks, to determine if action was needed. Reliance on sensory cues has been reported elsewhere [30, 31] and is particularly concerning, as smell and haze in the air are not always accurate or timely indicators of poor air quality [32]. Variations in AQI information may be explained by differences in source data (e.g., Environmental Protection Agency [EPA] regulatory monitors vs. private sensors), frequency of data updates, and how data is visually portrayed (see Figure 2 for an example). It seems unlikely that these differences in data portrayal are common knowledge, and caregiver trust in AQI information might be improved through education about these issues.

An example of discrepancies in visual portrayal of AQI data. A comparison of the default air quality maps for Washington State taken simultaneously from AirNow.gov [33] (Panel A) and Purple Air [34] (Panel B), which both use EPA and private air quality data, demonstrates that the Purple Air map appears to report significantly worse air quality. Several adjustments to the default Purple Air map are required to harmonize it with AirNow.gov, including applying an EPA conversion factor, eliminating indoor sensors, and adjusting the averaging period and marker colors (Panel C). To address these challenges, communication must be timely, relevant, and context‐specific. Caregivers commonly preferred mobile device notifications from weather apps or regional alert systems, aligning with other research [35]. Guidance on WFS preparedness was preferred during non‐peak wildfire periods, while timely updates on AQI and specific safety recommendations were valuable during active wildfires. Therefore, communication strategies may be most effective when tailored to seasonal and situational contexts.

Proposed Interventions Based on Study Findings

We used the COM‐B framework to organize barriers identified in our study and devise targeted intervention strategies (Table 5). These strategies can be categorized as targeting caregivers, organizations (including healthcare and schools), and environmental and health policy with the goal of improving caregivers’ capacity to protect their child from WFS.

At the caregiver level, interventions can focus on improving knowledge, fostering routines to monitor air quality, and addressing competing priorities through decision‐making tools. Practical education materials and tools tailored to family needs can empower caregivers and enhance their ability to protect children during smoke events. To improve caregiver confidence in AQI data, interventions should explicitly address common misconceptions and sources of distrust. Linking asthma action plans directly to real‐time AQI monitoring [36] and the development of targeted messaging specific to the risks children face during smoke events [37] would further strengthen recommendations. Financial barriers can be addressed with supply drives, vouchers, and instructions on constructing low‐cost DIY filters.

At the organization level, healthcare systems and schools play a pivotal role in supporting caregiver decision‐making. Healthcare organizations can empower their providers to provide WFS education by supplying communication tools and encouraging their use. Integrating AQI data with the electronic health record presents an opportunity to alert providers to increased risks specific to certain patients. Finally, ensuring that caregiver‐facing communications are accessible in multiple languages is crucial to addressing disparities related to WFS exposure [38].

Policy initiatives can increase the effectiveness of public health messaging by ensuring that it is actionable, relevant to children, acknowledges the complex decisions that caregivers face, and addresses potentially conflicting sources of AQI information. Local or regional SMS alert systems can be enacted to increase awareness. HEPA air cleaners, HVAC upgrades, and masks can be subsidized, or insurance coverage mandated for children with asthma. Finally, policies mandating minimum air quality standards and outdoor activity policies in schools will support caregivers’ efforts to protect their child while at school.

Addressing the multi‐dimensional barriers identified in this study through targeted educational, economic, communication, and policy‐level interventions could substantially improve caregiver capacity to protect children during wildfire smoke events. It is important to recognize that these interventions do not address the root causes of increasing WFS exposure, and that legislation to slow the pace of climate change and improve forest management practices are critical to protect child respiratory health.

Limitations

Some limitations of our study should be noted. Our sample was geographically limited to Western Washington State, which is moderately impacted by WFS exposure; therefore, caution should be used when generalizing results to more severely impacted regions. While we enrolled English‐ and Spanish‐speaking participants, we did not reach sufficient enrollment of Spanish‐speakers to analyze results by language group. Thus, the perspectives of non‐English‐speaking caregivers require further exploration to ensure adequate representation. Finally, while some caregivers did talk about the usage of masks during their interviews, we did not pursue discussion about perceived effectiveness or barriers to using masks in this study.

Conclusions

This qualitative study highlights that caregivers of children with asthma face complex decisions during WFS events as well as a variety of barriers that limit protective actions. Addressing these obstacles requires a multilayered strategy through collaboration with clinicians, schools, community organizations, public health practitioners, and policymakers. Future research should evaluate the effectiveness of these potential interventions in lowering asthma morbidity, healthcare utilization, and caregiver stress as wildfire seasons intensify.
