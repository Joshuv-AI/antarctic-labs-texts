Adherence to Iron and Folic Acid (IFA) among Women Availing Antenatal Services at a Rural Maternity Hospital in Karnataka
Authors: Kadambari Nanmaran, Avita R. Johnson, Rhea James, Malavika Nadig, Lavanya Varier, Anju Mathew
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Antenatal Iron and Folic Acid (IFA) supplementation prevents anaemia and improves maternal and child outcomes. We conducted a hospital based cross sectional study in Ramanagara district to assess antenatal IFA adherence and its determinants in rural Karnataka. 200 pregnant women ≥16 weeks of gestation were recruited by consecutive sampling and administered a face validated, structured questionnaire. It included a ten item tool to assess anaemia awareness, Patient Health Questionnaire 2, Generalized Anxiety Disorder 2 item and Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support. Women consuming more than 60% of IFA in a week were considered adherent. Binary logistic regression was done to identify its determinants. We found antenatal IFA adherence to be 89%. Poor anaemia awareness was found among 46.5%, probable depression among 11.0%, probable anxiety among 13.5% and high perceived social support among 78.5% of the women. Adherence counselling by a healthcare worker [aOR 4.32 (95% CI: 1.01–18.37)] and reminder by family member [aOR 10.57 (95% CI: 2.35–47.51)] were found to be significant facilitators to IFA adherence. Significantly higher adherence was found among women not experiencing side effects [aOR 4.01 (95% CI: 1.10–14.64)] or forgetfulness [aOR 7.0 (95% CI: 1.73–28.43)] and in those consuming a single tablet [aOR 3.46 (95% CI: 1.07–11.19)] as compared to two or more. We concluded that antenatal IFA adherence in rural Karnataka was high. Significant facilitators were the adherence counselling by a healthcare worker and reminder by a family member. Significant barriers were experiencing side effects, forgetfulness and consumption of two or more tablets.

INTRODUCTION

Half of all pregnant women in India are anaemic.[1] Anaemia is associated with post-partum haemorrhage and other adverse maternal and perinatal outcomes, including maternal death.[23] The Anaemia Mukth Bharat Initiative under National Health Mission, GoI, recommends that all pregnant women are given prophylactic 60 mg of elemental iron and 500 mcg of folic acid daily, for 180 days during pregnancy, and continued for 6 months, post-partum.[4] In rural, settings, antenatal adherence to IFA becomes vital, considering the challenges faced in the provision of care due to complications of anaemia. Very few studies done in the rural context have explored antenatal adherence to IFA and its determinants, including the role played by awareness of anaemia, presence of depression or anxiety and perceived social support. Identifying these determinants would provide direction for future targeted interventions. This study was conducted, with the objective of assessing adherence to IFA, among women availing antenatal services at a rural hospital in Ramanagara District; and to identify the possible barriers and facilitators of adherence to IFA.

METHODOLOGY

Study setting

A rural maternity hospital in Magadi Taluk of Ramnagara District, south Karnataka. Study Period: October to November 2020. Study population: Women availing antenatal services at the hospital. Sample size: NFHS-4 states that 44.6% of rural women in Karnataka consumed at least 100 tablets of IFA during pregnancy.[5] With 7% margin of error and 95% confidence level, sample size was estimated to be 200. Sampling Technique: Subjects were enrolled consecutively as they registered for the antenatal clinic or as they were admitted to the antenatal ward. Inclusion criteria: Women aged ≥18 years, of gestational age ≥16 weeks Exclusion criteria: Women with conditions like hearing and speech impairment or mental illness that prevented them from comprehending or responding to the questions. Study tool: A structured questionnaire was used to collect socio-demographic details, obstetric details, adherence to IFA, possible facilitators and barriers to adherence and awareness of anaemia. This questionnaire was face-validated by two maternal health experts. Awareness of anaemia was assessed using ten questions, with each correct response being given a score of one. We used Patient Health Questionnaire 2 (PHQ-2) that is a validated two-item tool with a total score of six to screen for depression.[6] At a cut-off score of three or greater, PHQ-2 has been found to have 72% sensitivity and 85% specificity, while screening for major depression.[7] We used Generalized Anxiety Disorder 2 (GAD-2) that is a validated two-item tool with a total score of six to screen for anxiety.[8] At a cut-off score of three or greater, GAD-2 has been shown to have 69% sensitivity and 91% specificity when screening for generalised anxiety.[9] We used the 12-item Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS), which is a tool designed to evaluate and quantify the perceived social support. It has 7 possible responses to each statement (scored 1-7) giving a total score of 84, with higher scores indicating greater perceived social support. The three subscale scores indicate support from family, friends and partner.[1011] MSPSS has good internal reliability when used among South Asians.[12] Socio-economic class was determined using Modified BG Prasad socio-economic classification.[13] Haemoglobin levels of the subjects were taken from their outpatient and/or inpatient records.

Operational definitions

A woman was considered to be adherent to IFA if she consumed ≥60% of the prescribed tablets for the previous week (≥4 tablets per week if taking once a day or ≥8 if taking twice daily).[141516] A woman was considered anaemic during the present pregnancy if her haemoglobin level was <11 g% at any point during pregnancy.[17] The woman was considered to have poor awareness regarding anaemia if she scored ≤5 on the anaemia awareness questions; intermediate awareness for score 6–7 and good awareness for score 8–10. Women scoring ≥3 on PHQ-2 were considered as having probable depression.[6] Women scoring ≥3 on GAD-2 were considered as having probable anxiety.[8] Women scoring 12–35 on the MSPSS, were considered as low perceived social support, 36 to 60 as medium perceived social support and 61–84 as high perceived social support.[11]

Ethical considerations

Institutional Ethics approval was obtained. Written informed consent was taken from the subjects. At the end of the interview, those found to be non-adherent to IFA were counselled and suggestions to improve adherence were given. Those identified as having probable depression or anxiety were referred to the consultant obstetrician for further management.

Statistical analysis

The data was analysed using IBM SPSS for Windows, Version 20.0. (Armonk, NY: IBM Corp). Data was described using frequencies, proportions, mean, standard deviation, median and inter-quartile range. For factors associated with adherence to IFA, Chi-square test and Fischer’s exact test were used, as applicable. All variables with a P value < 0.2 were included into a binary logistic regression model. P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

RESULTS

Of the 200 women included in the study, one-third of them were below 25 years of age, with majority (70%) having received ten or more years of formal education. Most of them (88.5%) were homemakers by occupation. 28% of them belonged to nuclear families and 42% of them belonged to Class I socio-economic status [Table 1]. More than half the subjects (56.5%) had anaemia in the present pregnancy and 178 (89%) were found to be adherent to IFA.

DISCUSSION

Our study looked at adherence to IFA among pregnant women. While the acceptable cut-off for self-reported adherence is typically 80% or higher for medication for chronic disease like diabetes and hypertension[1819]; for prophylactic IFA during pregnancy, the most used cut-off is 4 tablets or more in the last week (60% or higher).[141516] Our study found a high proportion of women, who were adherent to IFA. This may be due to better educational status of the women in our study, which probably resulted in better awareness. We assessed “awareness,” rather than “knowledge” of anemia, as in the knowledge domain, general awareness sits at the lower end of the continuum, and knowledge which is considered detailed and specific sits at the higher end.[20] Although we found no association between awareness of anemia and adherence to IFA, we noted that only a small proportion of women were aware that anemic women can bleed more during delivery and that anemia is due to dietary deficiency of iron. These gaps in awareness, if addressed may further improve understanding among pregnant women regarding the importance of consuming IFA supplements.

We found a similar proportion of IFA adherence as a hospital-based study in Mysore, which showed 71% IFA adherence, with anemic mothers, those with poor awareness of anemia and those in nuclear families less likely to be adherent. Experiencing IFA side effects was a significant barrier, like our study.[21] In a hospital-based study in Mangalore, 65% of the pregnant women were adherent to IFA, and the adherence was higher, among the subjects from lower socioeconomic class and among older women with higher parity. Forgetfulness was found to be a significant barrier like in our study,[22] likewise in a study in Belgaum Karnataka.[23]

A study in rural Ethiopia found that 44% of pregnant women were adherent to IFA. The factors associated with adherence to IFA were having received counseling, better knowledge of anemia, early ANC attendance, pregnancy history, and current anemia diagnosis.[24] Variable access, insufficient IFA supplies and inadequate counselling to encourage consumption, are barriers to both coverage and adherence of IFA as seen in a systematic review of studies conducted in Afghanistan, Bangladesh, Indonesia, Ethiopia, Kenya, Nigeria, and Senegal.[25] In our study we too found that receiving counseling by health care worker regarding IFA was significantly associated with its adherence. This indicates the need for spending few extra minutes counselling women during their ANC visits on IFA to improve adherence.

The free IFA given to all pregnant women in India as a part of essential obstetric care for the last few decades has resulted in a cultural acceptance to IFA during pregnancy and a basic awareness of the need for IFA supplementation, which probably explains the high rates of adherence in our study. However, the NFHS-5 showed that only 40.9% of rural women in Karnataka were adherent to IFA.[26] There still exist ‘low-performing districts’ within Karnataka state, located primarily in the north of Karnataka where maternal and child health indicators are well below that of the ‘high performing districts’, possibly lowering the average for the state.[27] The hospital where the present study has been conducted is located 50km from Bangalore, and has patients with higher education and socio-economic status which may have contributed to higher levels of adherence to IFA supplements as compared to other remote rural areas. This proximity to the state capital may have played a role in the constant availability and access to IFA as well.

CONCLUSION

Adherence to IFA was found to be high at 89%. There was poor awareness about anaemia in nearly half of the women. Significant barriers to IFA adherence were experiencing side effects, forgetfulness and consumption of two or more tablets. Significant facilitators to IFA adherence were counselling by a health care worker and reminder by a family members to take supplements. Comprehensive IFA adherence counselling by ASHA/PHCO/Medical Officer during ANC visits and involving family members in the supervision of daily IFA consumption will go a long way in improving IFA adherence among pregnant women. The counselling should stress on the consequences of anemia, benefits of IFA, suggestions for being adherent and ways to evade side effects of IFA.

Limitation

In our study, we used self -reported adherence. Thus, social desirability and recall bias may have played a role and our adherence measure may be an overestimate.
