Rivaroxaban in Chinese children with giant coronary artery aneurysms after Kawasaki disease
Authors: Guangan Dai, Bijue Liu, Xuecun Liang, Yixiang Lin, Lan He, Feng Wang, Qingfeng He, Quming Zhao, Lu Zhao, Yuanzheng Zheng, Chen Chu, Shuna Sun, Leonardo R Brandão, Guoying Huang, Xiao Zhu, Fang Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundData on the use of rivaroxaban in children with giant coronary artery aneurysm (GCAA) after Kawasaki disease (KD) remain limited.ObjectivesThis study evaluated the feasibility of rivaroxaban in Chinese children with GCAA after KD.MethodsThis study was conducted at the Children’s Hospital of Fudan University. Children aged 1 month to 18 years with persistent GCAA (diameter ≥ 8 mm or Z-score ≥ 10) during the postacute period of KD were included and followed up for at least 6 months. The study consisted of 2 stages. During the first stage (January-December 2023), patients received rivaroxaban following the 20-mg-equivalent regimen. In the second stage (January-November 2024), a pharmacometric model–informed rivaroxaban regimen was implemented. The primary outcome was a composite of GCAA thrombosis and major adverse cardiovascular event within 6 months. The secondary outcome included major bleeding and clinically relevant nonmajor (CRNM) bleeding.ResultsIn the first stage, 6 patients were enrolled (median age, 14.6 months; range, 4.7-130.8 months; median weight, 9.9; range, 6.6-33 kg; Z-score, 14.9; range, 10.6-28.0). No primary outcome or major bleeding occurred. Four CRNM bleedings were documented, leading to 6 dose adjustments. Two patients discontinued rivaroxaban due to improved coronary status. In the second stage, 14 patients received the 15-mg-equivalent rivaroxaban regimen (median age, 69.7 months; range, 16.4-143.1 months; median weight, 19.5 kg; range, 12.5-57.5 kg; Z-score, 14.7; range, 10.6-39.1). No primary or secondary outcome occurred. Two patient important bleeding no intervention events were documented. All patients remained on rivaroxaban beyond 6 months. External validation supported the predictive performance of model extrapolation.ConclusionThe 15-mg-equivalent rivaroxaban regimen appeared feasible in Chinese children with GCAA after KD, with no GCAA thrombosis, major bleeding or CRNM bleeding observed within 6 months. Larger studies are required to confirm these preliminary findings.Keywords: rivaroxaban, Kawasaki disease, giant coronary artery aneurysm, model-informed study design, population pharmacokinetics

Introduction

Kawasaki disease (KD) has an annual incidence rate of 94.7 of 371 per 100,000 children under 4 years old in Asia [[1], [2], [3]]. Coronary artery abnormalities are observed in approximately 7.2% to 7.9% of patients during the acute period (<30 days from KD onset), while 2.2% to 3.2% are found to have persistent lesions during the postacute illness period (≥30 days after KD onset) [1,2,4,5]. Giant coronary artery aneurysm (GCAA) is a rare but severe cardiac complication of KD, with an incidence of 0.13% to 0.7% [1,3]. Patients with GCAA after KD are at high risk of coronary artery thrombosis and major adverse cardiovascular events. Accordingly, lifelong antiplatelet and anticoagulant therapy is generally recommended [6,7]. In Mainland China, warfarin remains the most widely used oral anticoagulant in pediatric patients. However, its long-term management poses challenges, especially in infants and toddlers, due to developmental hemostasis, age-related variability in dietary vitamin K intake, the need for frequent international normalized ratio monitoring, and limited formulation options [[7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12]].

Rivaroxaban, an activated factor (F)Xa inhibitor, exerts its anticoagulant effect independently from antithrombin [13]. In 2022, it was approved by international regulatory agencies for 2 specific pediatric indications, supported by pharmacometrics models [[14], [15], [16], [17]]. First, a dosing regimen matching exposures of 20 mg daily in adults (the 20-mg-equivalent dosing regimen) is indicated for children under 18 years with venous thromboembolism[14]. Second, a dosing regimen matching exposures of 10 mg daily in adults (the 10-mg-equivalent dosing regimen) is indicated for post-Fontan patients aged 2 years or older, as shown in the UNIVERSE study, which compared rivaroxaban with aspirin [15,18]. In addition, other FXa inhibitors, such as apixaban and edoxaban, have been compared with warfarin/low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) in children with congenital and acquired heart diseases, including those with single ventricle and GCAA after KD [19,20]. However, no trials have yet compared rivaroxaban with warfarin/LMWH in pediatric patients with GCAA after KD.

Population pharmacokinetic (PPK) analyses have suggested that Asian patients may require a lower dosage of rivaroxaban, consistent with the findings from the J-ROCKET trial in Japanese adults [[21], [22], [23]]. Accordingly, the maximum dosage for Japanese children with venous thromboembolism (≥50 kg) has been reduced from 20 to 15 mg once daily [24]. However, reports on rivaroxaban use in pediatric patients with GCAA after KD, especially in those from Asian countries, remain limited [25,26]. Its application is more challenging due to (i) increased bleeding risk from concomitant antiplatelet drug use; and (ii) the predominance of younger patients (typically < 3 years) with variability in hepatic and renal function, as well as growth and development [6]. Given the low incidence of GCAA, pharmacometrics is a highly promising approach for precision dosing of rivaroxaban in these patients [27].

This study aimed to assess the potential of rivaroxaban for thromboprophylaxis in pediatric patients with GCAA after KD. Specifically, this study seeks to (ⅰ) evaluate the feasibility, effectiveness, and safety of various rivaroxaban dosing regimens in Chinese pediatric patients with GCAA after KD; (ii) propose an optimized dosing regimen that achieves an appropriate exposure level; (iii) provide pharmacokinetic (PK)/pharmacodynamic (PD) data on rivaroxaban in Chinese children, and (iv) validate the reliability of the model-informed study design (MISD) approach.

Methods

Study design

This single-arm, prospective, exploratory interventional study (www.clinicaltrials.gov, NCT06978439) was conducted at the Pediatric Heart Center of Children’s Hospital of Fudan University (Shanghai, China) from January 2023 to November 2024.

The study was conducted in 2 consecutive stages (Figure 1A). Enrollment for the first stage began in January 2023, during which the off-label use of rivaroxaban was reviewed and summarized. From October to December 2023, a dose optimization study guided by the MISD strategy was conducted (Figure 2). The findings from both the initial clinical experience and the dose optimization study were submitted and reviewed by the ethics board. Enrollment for the second stage began in January 2024 (Figure 1A).

Workflow and flowchart of the study. A. Workflow of enrollment. B. Flowchart of enrollment. CAL: coronary artery lesion; GCAA, giant coronary artery aneurysm; KD, Kawasaki disease; PD, pharmacodynamic; PK, pharmacokinetic.

Workflow of model-informed study design. Step 1, the published rivaroxaban population pharmacokinetic (PPK) models were extrapolated into Chinese children via 2 distinct approaches. Step 2, dosing regimens equivalent to 10, 15, and 20 mg once daliy were proposed. Results of model simulation were crossvalidated. Step 3, calibration of dosing regimens was made, following comparison between models, clinical evidence from the first stage and real-world clinical experience.

Patients

Patients aged 1 month to 18 years with persistent GCAA beyond the acute phase of KD were included. GCAA was defined as any coronary artery inner diameter of ≥8 mm or Z-score of ≥10 (calculated by Dallaire Z-score algorithm), assessed by echocardiogram or coronary artery angiogram (CAG) [6,7,28]. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (i) within 30 days of KD onset, (ii) evidence of uncontrolled inflammation or progressive coronary artery lesions, or (iii) contraindications to rivaroxaban.

Screening and enrollment were conducted among hospitalized patients, either recovering after the acute phase of KD or admitted for scheduled cardiology and GCAA assessments [7,29]. All patients received standard anticoagulation therapy (warfarin or LMWH) previously. The switch to rivaroxaban (Bayer Healthcare) was conducted during hospitalization based on the prescribing information [30]. After switching, patients remained hospitalized for a minimum of 4 days to allow for clinical observation and drug-level monitoring at steady state.

This study was approved by the Ethics Board of Children’s Hospital of Fudan University (Approval No. 2022-399 and No.2022-416) in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians of all patients prior to enrollment. In addition, assent was obtained from patients aged over 8 years. All clinical data were collected from the electronic medical record system and deidentified. Reporting followed the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) reporting guideline.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the composite effectiveness endpoint within the 6-month study period, including coronary artery thrombosis and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs). A MACE was defined as unstable angina, acute myocardial infarction, hospitalization for heart failure, unplanned coronary revascularization, stroke, or cardiovascular death. The secondary outcome was the composite safety endpoint, including major bleeding and clinically relevant nonmajor (CRNM) bleeding events. In addition, assessments during the extended treatment period beyond 6 months were reported as exploratory outcomes, comprising effectiveness and safety endpoints as well as treatment duration.

Patient demographics, baseline GCAA characteristics (anatomic location, inner diameter, and Z-score), concomitant antiplatelet use, initial rivaroxaban dose, and any dose adjustments were documented. Outcome assessments were conducted by cardiologists before discharge and at each standard-of-care outpatient visit at 3 and 6 months, in accordance with the 2017 American Heart Association scientific statement [29]. These assessments included history and physical examination, clinical symptom evaluations, echocardiography, and electrocardiography. Evaluation of bleeding tendency and documentation of bleeding events were also performed. Telemedicine records were reviewed to identify outpatient bleeding events or adverse reactions reported by families. Echocardiograms were performed to identify GCAA thrombosis, vessel wall calcifications, ventricular wall motion, chamber size, and cardiac function. Bleeding events were classified as major bleeding, CRNM bleeding, patient important bleeding no intervention (PIBNI), and minor bleeding, according to the updated bleeding severity scale for pediatric trials by the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis [31].

Rivaroxaban dosing regimen

The initial rivaroxaban regimen in the first stage followed the 20-mg-equivalent dosing regimen [14]. Following the dose optimization study, a dosing regimen amendment was submitted and approved on January 2, 2024 (Figure 1A). The model-informed dosing regimen was then implemented as the initial regimen for the second stage. For patients enrolled in 2023, dosage adjustments were made in January 2024 through telephone or outpatient follow-up.

Dose adjustment and discontinuation

Cardiologists adjusted or discontinued rivaroxaban if necessary, and each adjustment was documented. Dose adjustments were informed by clinical assessment of bleeding and the severity of coronary artery lesions. Coagulation tests were conducted as supplementary tools to support the clinical assessment of anticoagulant intensity. During the first stage, LMWH-calibrated anti-FXa activity, prothrombin time, and activated partial thromboplastin time were performed. Although these assays are not specific to rivaroxaban, they are relatively sensitive to elevated anticoagulant levels and were used to assist clinical decision making [32]. During the second stage, rivaroxaban plasma concentrations and rivaroxaban-calibrated chromogenic anti-FXa activity were introduced as more specific monitoring bioassays. These biomarkers were measured both at 2 to 4 hours postdose (to capture peak exposure) and immediately before the dose (to assess trough levels) after at least 3 continuous doses [33]. The estimated reference ranges for plasma concentrations and anti-FXa activity were derived from population data of adult patients who received rivaroxaban 15 mg once daily after total hip replacement. The peak plasma concentrations (5th-95th) was 125.5-277.65 μg/L [34].

Discontinuation of rivaroxaban was considered under the following circumstances: (i) if GCAA thrombosis, major bleeding, or CRNM bleeding occurred and the pediatric cardiologists determined that the patient was no longer suitable for continued rivaroxaban therapy; or (ii) if follow-up imaging (echocardiography or CAG) demonstrated regression of the aneurysm, defined as a maximum Z-score <10 and an inner diameter <8 mm. Patients who discontinued rivaroxaban remained under observation and were still monitored through scheduled follow-up visits and telemedicine throughout the 6-month study period.

Rivaroxaban plasma concentration and anti-FXa activity measurement

Plasma concentration and anti-FXa activity were measured during the second stage. After the first dose of rivaroxaban, blood samples were collected at 2 timepoints: 20 minutes to 1 hour and 7 ± 1 hours postdose. On day 4, trough (Ctrough, predose) and peak (Cmax, 3 ± 1 hours postdose) samples were collected. During each outpatient visit, peak and trough samples were collected when feasible.

For plasma concentration measurement, 2 mL of blood was collected into EDTA-containing tubes, centrifuged at 3000g for 10 minutes at 4 °C within 2 hours, and then stored at −80 °C for analysis within 3 months. It was measured using a sensitive ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry method [35,36]. Separation was performed on a Kinetex C18 analytical column (50 × 2.1 mm, 2.6 μm). A precipitation extraction method was used with acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid. The calibration range was 0.5 to 500 ng/mL, and the lower limit of quantification was 0.5 ng/mL.

For anti-FXa activity assay, 2 mL blood samples were collected into trisodium citrate–containing tubes and analyzed within 2 hours. It was analyzed using a STA R Max (Diagnostica Stago) and calibrated by a STA-Rivaroxaban Calibrator kit (Diagnostica Stago) [37]. All reagents and instruments were used in accordance with instructions from manufacturers.

Dose optimization guided by model-informed study design

Two published rivaroxaban PPK models were extrapolated to Chinese children using distinct approaches (Figure 2). In the ETHNIC model, the difference in apparent clearance (31%-43%) between White and Chinese adults was incorporated into the pediatric PPK model (Supplementary Table 1) [38,39]. In contrast, the MATURATION model was developed based on a Chinese adult PPK model, where estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was identified as a major covariate for clearance (Supplementary Table 1) [22]. A renal function maturation model was integrated to characterize the maturation process in the pediatric population [40].

The range of the 5th to 95th percentiles of area under the plasma concentration-time curve over 24h at steady state (AUC0-24,ss) was set as the reference range for model-informed precision dosing [34,41]. Target exposures of AUC0-24,ss were set as 771.5 to 2118.2 μg·h/L for 10 mg once daily, 1068.7 to 3487.85 μg·h/L for 15 mg once daily, and 1365.9 to 4857.5 μg·h/L for 20 mg once daily. Corresponding population PK data are provided in Supplementary Method 1[34]. Monte Carlo simulations were conducted in a virtual pediatric population for dose optimization, using ETHNIC model and MATURATION model separately. Detailed dose selection criteria are provided in Supplementary Method 1, Supplementary Figure 1, and Supplementary Table 2.

Crossvalidation between the 2 models was conducted to evaluate consistency and robustness of model extrapolation. Specifically, model-informed dosing recommendations derived from the ETHNIC model and the MATURATION model were compared across the 10-mg-, 15-mg-, and 20-mg-equivalent regimens. For each regimen, the differences in recommended daily doses between the 2 models were summarized and visualized. Given that eGFR approaches adult levels by approximately 2 years of age and that the MATURATION model applies bodyweight-adjusted dosing beyond this age, the comparison was stratified by age groups (<2 vs ≥2 years) to assess developmental differences in dose recommendations. In addition, the steady daily doses in first-stage patients were also compared with the model-informed dosing recommendations. Based on calibration with both modeling results and clinical observations, an optimized regimen was proposed.

External evaluation

External validation of the model extrapolation was conducted using the rivaroxaban PK/PD data from patients in the second stage. Nonlinear mixed-effects modeling (NONMEM) software (version 7.5; ICON Development Solutions) was used with the MAXEVAL = 0. Outputs from NONMEM were assessed by Perl-speaks-NONMEM (version 5.2.6; Uppsala University). Pirana (version 23.1.2; Certara) was used for workflow management. Predictive performance of the PPK model was evaluated by visual predictive checks and diagnostic goodness-of-fit plots.

In total, 6 dose adjustments were made, including 2 temporary discontinuations. In case 1, rivaroxaban was initiated at 15 mg once daily 1 week after CAG. Two days later, bleeding occurred and aneurysm formation at the previously healed femoral artery puncture site, which required surgical treatment. The dose was reduced to 5 mg twice daily after discharge. In case 3, persistent epistaxis developed on day 6 while on rivaroxaban 2 mg every 8 hours. The dose was then reduced to 2 mg twice daily, during which subcutaneous hematomas occurred. It was further reduced to 1.67 mg twice daily (one-sixth of a 10-mg tablet). Rivaroxaban doses were lower than those of the 20-mg-equivalent regimen in all patients. Dose adjustment details were documented in Supplementary Result 1.

Model- and clinical evidence-informed dose optimization

The bodyweight-adjusted regimens were derived from the ETHNIC model, while the age- and bodyweight-adjusted regimens were derived from the MATURATION model (Supplementary Table 3-5; Supplementary Figure 2-4). As shown in Supplementary Figure 5, the differences in recommended daily doses between the 2 models were minimal (mostly <0.5 mg) for children aged ≥2 years, but exhibited greater variability in those aged <2 years. Therefore, for children aged ≥ 2 years, bodyweight-adjusted regimens from both models were merged. For children aged < 2 years, the age- and bodyweight-adjusted regimen from the MATURATION model was applied to account for interindividual differences in bodyweight and renal function.

To further support dosing selection, we compared model-informed dosing recommendations with the steady daily doses used in first-stage patients. The 15-mg-equivalent dosing regimen demonstrated greater alignment. Furthermore, based on real-world clinical experience with 15 mg rivaroxaban in Chinese adults and Japanese children over 50 kg, 15 mg once daily was selected as the final target exposure. Finally, we proposed a 15-mg-equivalent, age- and bodyweight-adjusted dosing regimen for thromboprophylaxis in Chinese pediatric patients with GCAA after KD (Table 2).

Rivaroxaban application in the second stage

A total of 14 patients were included during the second stage (Figure 1B), following the optimized, 15-mg-equivalent dosing regimen (<2 years: 3 [21%]; ≥2 years: 11 [79%]). The median (range) was 19.5 (12.5-57.5) kg for bodyweight, 69.7 (16.4-143.1) months for age, and 14.7 (10.6-39.1) for the Z-score of the largest GCAA. Demographic and clinical data were provided in Table 1.

No primary outcome or secondary outcome was observed within 6 months. Only 2 PIBNIs were documented, including epistaxis and subcutaneous hematoma, both of which resolved spontaneously without medical intervention (Figure 3B, case 3 and 11). All patients continued rivaroxaban therapy beyond the 6-month period, with a median treatment duration of 269.5 (193-324) days.

Among the 11 patients aged ≥ 2 years, 2 required dose adjustments due to weight gain (Figure 3B). Among the 3 patients aged < 2 years old, 2 underwent dose adjustments after comprehensive assessment (Figure 3B, cases 4 and 9). Dose adjustment details were documented in Supplementary Result 2.

External validation

A total of 47 blood samples were collected in the second stage. The median (10th-90th) was 136.5 (80.2-201.5) ng/mL for Cmax and 4.2 (1.8-10.0) ng/mL for Ctrough. The median was 105.5 (63.0-176.0) ng/mL for peak anti-FXa activity and 5.0 (0-11.8) ng/mL for trough anti-FXa activity. A strong linear correlation was observed between plasma concentration and anti-FXa activity (R2 = 0.9761; P < 0.0001) (Supplementary Figure 7).

The observed rivaroxaban concentrations (Cmax and Ctrough) in Chinese children showed minimal deviation from the predicted values and aligned well with expectations (Figure 4, Supplementary Method 1). The observed concentration-time curves were consistent with the estimated concentration-time curves from both models, which confirmed their population predictive performance (Figure 4).

Discussion

This is the first and largest exploratory study to investigate rivaroxaban in pediatric patients with GCAA after KD. These findings provide preliminary support for rivaroxaban as a feasible anticoagulation option in this high-risk pediatric population. However, dose optimization may be necessary for Chinese pediatric patients with GCAA after KD. Guided by the MISD strategy, we proposed a 15-mg-equivalent, age- and bodyweight-adjusted dosing regimen. This strategy appeared feasible and well-tolerated in patients requiring dual antithrombotic therapy in the second stage. External validation reinforced the robustness of this precision dosing strategy in pediatric practice.

Previous studies have shown that the concomitant use of oral anticoagulants with antiplatelet agents increases the risk of major bleeding [42,43]. In a meta-analysis by Dentali et al. [43], dual antithrombotic therapy was associated with a higher incidence of major bleeding than anticoagulant monotherapy (odds ratio, 1.43; 95% CI, 1.00-2.02) [43]. With respect to rivaroxaban, 2 randomized controlled trials (COMPASS and VOYAGER PAD) suggested that combining rivaroxaban (2.5 mg twice daily or 10 mg once daily) with aspirin increased the risk of major bleeding compared with that by rivaroxaban alone [44,45]. However, pediatric data evaluating the safety of such combination therapy remain limited [25,26]. In the first stage of our study, 4 CRNM bleedings were reported among 6 patients who received the 20-mg-equivalent rivaroxaban regimen with an antiplatelet agent (Figure 3A). This represents a markedly higher bleeding incidence than the rate (6.8% [5/73]) of major or CRNM bleeding events reported in the EINSTEIN-Jr study, where rivaroxaban was used as monotherapy [14]. Although none of the bleeding events were life-threatening, they were clinically significant given the young age, lower body weight, and limited blood volume of the patients. These bleeding episodes also imposed psychological burdens on caregivers, which may have impacted treatment adherence and confidence in long-term anticoagulant therapy. For this high-risk pediatric population, more stable and predictable anticoagulant effects are essential to ensure both safety and efficacy. In addition, during the extended treatment period, the remaining 4 patients were clinically stable on reduced doses of rivaroxaban. These findings suggested that a tailored dosing strategy may be necessary for these patients.

In contrast to the first stage, only 2 PIBNIs were reported in the second stage (Figure 3B), including epistaxis and subcutaneous hematoma, both of which resolved spontaneously. As the symptoms were mild and anti-FXa activity levels remained within an acceptable range, no dose adjustment was made in either case. Most patients maintained the recommended dosages after discharge, and no primary or secondary outcome occurred during the 6-month period. Median Cmax (136.5 ng/mL) and anti-FXa activity (105.5 ng/mL) remained within the estimated reference range [34]. However, rivaroxaban dose adjustment was required in 2 patients under 2 years of age (cases 4 and 9). This finding may indicate that age-related differences in clearance were underestimated by the rivaroxaban pediatric pharmacometrics model in low-bodyweight patients, consistent with previous PK/PD studies [39,46]. Willmann et al. [39] also noted the challenge of predicting rivaroxaban exposure in children under 2 years of age due to their complex physiological profiles. Additional factors, such as dosage manipulation and interindividual variation, may also influence drug exposure [47]. Since only 3 patients under the age of 2 years were included, further PK/PD analysis with larger cohorts are necessary to validate and refine dosing strategies for this subgroup. Until then, routine monitoring of rivaroxaban plasma concentrations and anti-FXa activity is recommended when used in children under 2 years of age.

To address the gaps in current global pediatric datasets and account for ethnic-specific pharmacokinetics, we applied an MISD approach to explore an optimized dosing strategy for Chinese pediatric patients. To ensure the robustness of model extrapolation, ethnicity- and maturation-related factors were incorporated into 2 separate pharmacometrics models [22,38,39]. On one hand, the ETHNIC model was extrapolated from the pediatric PPK model developed by Willmann et al. [39]. The ethnicity-related difference in apparent clearance (31%-43%) between Asian and White adults was incorporated into the model to account for population-specific pharmacokinetics [38]. On the other hand, the MATURATION model was extrapolated from a Chinese adult PPK model in which eGFR was identified as a significant covariate for rivaroxaban clearance [22]. Pediatric renal clearance is influenced by age-dependent physiological changes, including kidney size, number of glomeruli, nephron filtration capacity, renal blood flow, plasma protein binding, and transporter expression. Although only approximately 6% of rivaroxaban is eliminated via glomerular filtration, eGFR not only serves as an indicator of glomerular filtration function but also correlates with overall renal maturation. Thus, it provides a practical and quantifiable surrogate marker for developmental changes in renal elimination capacity [40,48,49]. Crossvalidation between the 2 models confirmed consistency in model-informed precision dosing (Supplementary Figure 2-5), and external validation supported that both models adequately described the observed PK profiles in Chinese pediatric patients with GCAA after KD. Simulation-based predictions of time-concentration curve for once-daily dosing aligned well with observed drug exposure levels (Figure 4).

Building upon the modeling framework, the age stratification in the optimized dosing regimen was informed by multiple factors. First, the combination of physiologically based pharmacokinetic and PPK modeling in the EINSTEIN-Jr study demonstrated reliable prediction of rivaroxaban dose-exposure relationships in children aged ≥2 years (approximately ≥12 kg) [39]. Second, according to the renal maturation model by Rhodin et al. [40], the eGFR reaches approximately 98% of adult levels by the age of 2 years. Third, we observed strong concordance between the ETHNIC and MATURATION models in patients aged ≥2 years (Supplementary Figure 5). Based on these considerations, a bodyweight-adjusted regimen was applied in this subgroup, with the lower bodyweight boundary extended to 9 kg to reflect growth characteristics in Chinese children. In contrast, greater interindividual variability in exposure was observed among patients aged <2 years. Moreover, Willmann et al. highlighted that the physiological processes underlying rivaroxaban metabolism and elimination remain insufficiently characterized in this age group [39]. To better account for developmental variability and maturation process, an age- and bodyweight-adjusted regimen derived from the MATURATION model was applied for patients aged <2 years.

The selection of 15 mg once daily as the target exposure was supported by several factors. First, model-informed dosing recommendations based on the 15-mg-equivalent regimen aligned best with the steady daily doses in the first-stage patients (Supplementary Figure 3 and 4). Second, during at least 6 months of follow-up in the second stage, no thrombotic events were reported, and bleeding events were markedly reduced. Third, dual antithrombotic therapy (rivaroxaban with an antiplatelet agent) may increase bleeding risk. Given that patients with GCAA after KD typically require dual antithrombotic therapy, the 15-mg-equivalent exposure was considered a more appropriate and balanced target to provide moderate anticoagulant intensity.

Limitations

This study was a single-arm, exploratory study. Although it provided limited information, our study demonstrated the preliminary feasibility of a 15-mg-equivalent rivaroxaban dosing regimen in Chinese pediatric patients with GCAA after KD. Future clinical trial will be conducted to assess the safety and efficacy profile of the optimized dosing regimen. Due to the lack of oral suspension formulation in Mainland China, the 10-mg rivaroxaban tablet had to be split or dissolved in water prior to administration. This approach may introduce variability in dosing accuracy and absorption. Additional PK studies are warranted to explore rivaroxaban PK in Chinese children and facilitate the standardization of tablet splitting. In addition, dose adjustment in this study was primarily based on clinical evaluation. Although rivaroxaban-specific bioassays were introduced to support anticoagulant managing, their ability remains limited in the absence of validated pharmacological reference ranges for pediatric populations. Lastly, the proposed 15-mg-equivalent dosing regimen was optimized based on characteristics of Chinese children and clinical practice in a dual antithrombotic therapy setting. This regimen should not be extrapolated to non-Asian pediatric populations without further validation.

Conclusion

Dose optimization of rivaroxaban may be necessary for Chinese pediatric patients with GCAA after KD. In this exploratory study, we evaluated a 15-mg-equivalent, age- and bodyweight-adjusted dosing regimen in 14 patients over a 6-month period, during which no coronary artery thrombosis, MACE, major bleeding, or CRNM bleeding events were observed. These preliminary findings support the feasibility of this regimen as a potential thromboprophylaxis strategy in this high-risk pediatric population. However, a prospective, randomized controlled trial is required to assess the safety and efficacy of this optimized regimen before clinical application.
