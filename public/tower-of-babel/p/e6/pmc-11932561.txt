Stage IV, grade B periodontitis in a patient with coinfection with the human immunodeficiency virus, hepatitis C, and oral candidiasis
Authors: Thaissa Cavalcante Vieira Bitu, Raphaela Canuto Silva, Lucas Muniz Pinto Bandeira, Diana Araújo Cunha, Paulo Goberlanio Barros Silva, Átila Vinícius Vitor Nobre
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Opportunistic infections are common in patients infected with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) in acquired immunodeficiency syndrome. This case report demonstrates a clinical case of an individual with periodontitis, HIV/hepatitis C coinfection, and candidiasis, using different drugs and under irregular antiretroviral therapy. The combination of several local and systemic conditions highlights the importance of the case and the need for health professionals to know the management of these combined pathologies in the context of HIV infection. We can conclude that it is extremely important for dentists to be aware of periodontal diseases in HIV-infected patients, due to the pathogenic mechanisms associated with these pathologies.

INTRODUCTION

Periodontitis is an oral inflammatory disease of multifactorial origin, initiated by the biofilm accumulation, and is responsible for the progressive destruction of the protective and supportive tissues of the teeth. This oral condition is known to generate low-grade systemic inflammation capable of exacerbating preexisting systemic conditions.[1] Periodontal medicine has emerged as a branch of periodontics that studies the interrelationship between the pathogenesis of periodontal diseases (PDs) and certain systemic diseases, and the associated mechanisms involved.[2]

The current classification of PDs includes diabetes mellitus (DM) and smoking as conditions that directly affect the degree of progression of PDs. In addition to DM and smoking, other diseases/conditions are being investigated for their association with periodontitis, including infection with the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV).[23]

HIV infection can cause acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS). This condition is manifested by the depletion of CD4+ T lymphocytes to a level ≤200 cells per cubic millimeter of blood (200 cells/mm≥) associated with a detectable viral load. This change culminates in severe immunosuppression, making the individual susceptible to opportunistic infections.[34] Studies have investigated the role of the oral and intestinal microbiota in chronic HIV infection, especially regarding bacterial translocation and its metastatic effects. In addition, another mechanism of association between these chronic diseases has gained prominence: the possibility of inflamed gums acting as an HIV reservoir. The presence of viral reservoirs results in the persistence of the disease.[5]

Among the opportunistic infections that can affect people with AIDS, candidiasis and hepatitis C infection are among the most common. Candidiasis is a fungal infection caused by Candida spp., which can appear in the oral, vaginal, and perianal regions. In different parts of the world, around 40% of HIV-infected individuals develop candidiasis during some stage of the disease.[6] Infection with the hepatitis C virus is one of the main causes of severe liver disease. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that 71 million people in the world live with hepatitis C, and at least 400,000 die from diseases related to this infection.[67] The aim of this case report is to report a clinical case of an HIV-infected patient with stage IV, grade B periodontitis with HIV/hepatitis C co-infection, and candidiasis.

CASE REPORT

A 51-year-old female patient came to the dentistry clinic for dental treatment. She had been infected with HIV type 1 (HIV-1) for 19 years and was undergoing irregular treatment, with chronic hepatitis C coinfection and oral and vaginal candidiasis. She provided informed consent for data use.

T CD4+ lymphocyte count of 93 cells/mm3 of blood and a detectable viral load (79 copies) were observed, confirming AIDS. Antiretroviral therapy (ART) in use consisted of a reverse transcriptase inhibitor (Zidovudine®), a nucleotide analog reverse transcriptase inhibitor (Tenofovir®), and a protease inhibitor (Lamivudine®). In addition to ART, the patient was taking Phenytoin® 100 mg, sulfamethoxazole 200 mg and trimethoprim 40 mg - Bactrim® (Roche, Santo Amaro, São Paulo, Brazil), and Levothyroxine® 50 mg, to treat anticonvulsant crises, opportunistic infections, and hypothyroidism, respectively. During treatment, the patient developed a vulvar neoplastic lesion (microinvasive squamous cell carcinoma-high-grade vulvar intraepithelial neoplasia VIN III).

On intraoral examination, the patient presented probing depths >5 mm in contiguous teeth, loss of clinical attachment, and tooth loss. The lower central and lateral incisors presented grade III mobility, indicating extraction. The initial visible plaque index[7] was 63%, and bleeding on probing was 48% [Figure 1]. Large accumulations of supragingival calculus and subgingival plaque and calculus were observed. The clinical diagnosis associated with the radiograph was stage IV periodontitis, grade B [Figure 2a–f].

After clinical and anamnestic examination and extra- and intraoral examinations, complete periodontal treatment was performed. Precautions taken in this case were the same biosafety measures as those provided for any patient. Masks, sterile aprons, gloves, and protective glasses were used. No antibiotic protocol was performed. This treatment consisted of supragingival scaling of the entire mouth, extraction of teeth with grade III mobility [Figure 3a], and removal of caries and temporary restorations with glass ionomer cement (Riva Light Cure SDI Holdings Pty, Itajaí, Santa Catarina, Brazil) to alter the oral microbiota. Then, scaling and root planing were performed in the periodontal pockets, by quadrant, in 1-h sessions, using Gracey curettes (Hu-Friedy, Chicago, Ill, USA) and ultrasonic instruments (Bob-Cat, Dentsply/Cavitron, Long Island City, NY, USA). A mouthwash containing 0.12% chlorhexidine digluconate was prescribed every 12 h during therapy. After 3 months of reevaluation and a significant reduction in the plaque index and bleeding on probing, the cavities were definitively restored with composite resin.

(a) After extraction of anteroposterior teeth due to grade III mobility. (b) Panoramic radiographic image after extractions and fabrication of temporary containment

Educational work was carried out over the first 3 months, including instructions on oral hygiene with toothbrushes, the use of interproximal brushes, and the use of dental floss for interdental hygiene. This motivation was reflected in the reduction of the visible plaque index from 63% to 0% and 3%, and bleeding on probing from 48% to 13% and 13% at the 3- and 6-month reevaluations, respectively [Figure 1a–c]. Still in the basic periodontal therapy phase, a retainer was installed using the disinfected dental crowns as a temporary device until the definitive prosthesis was made [Figure 3b]. For the treatment of oral candidiasis, a protocol of Daktarin® (Janssen, São José dos Campos, SP, Brazil) in gel form, four times a day and Fluconazole® 150 mg (Medley, Campinas, São Paulo, Brazil) for topical use applied directly to the lesions was prescribed [Figure 4b].

DISCUSSION

This study describes a clinical case of HIV/hepatitis C co-infection and candidiasis in a patient with stage IV, grade B periodontitis. The diversity of local, systemic, and opportunistic conditions in the context of HIV infection highlights the complexity of this case.

The relationship between HIV-1 infection and periodontitis has gained greater prominence in recent years, due to advances in research on HIV-1 latency and the presence of viral reservoirs. Even with the effectiveness of ART, HIV-1 latency represents the greatest obstacle to the functional cure of AIDS. Periodontitis and HIV infection share critical signaling pathways for both, including the nuclear kappa b transcription factor pathway and the role of periodontopathogens in systemic inflammation. The interference of periodontitis in HIV-1 reservoirs is under investigation.[58]

In Brazil, the most recent data from the Joint United Nations Programme on HIV/AIDS show 39 million people living with HIV and 29.2 million receiving ART.[9] In the United States, 42.2% of individuals aged 30–79 have periodontitis.[10] Periodontitis and HIV infection are global public health problems. It has been observed that HIV-infected individuals have a more diverse subgingival microbiota, especially Streptococcus spp. and Veillonella spp., followed by Fusobacterium spp. and Prevotella spp. These species act as ligands to aid the aggregation of other periodontopathogenic species and are associated with tissue destruction.[45]

In addition to bacterial infections, other fungal and viral infections can act as opportunists in the immunosuppression characteristic of AIDS. The patient in question had HIV/hepatitis C/candidiasis coinfection. It has been reported that Candida albicans isolated from the oral cavity of HIV-infected individuals show greater activity of the exoenzymes phospholipase and hemolysin, compared to C. albicans isolated from HIV-uninfected individuals. The marked activity of these exoenzymes confers greater virulence to these species in HIV infection, and this justifies the concern about candidiasis in this specific group of patients.[6]

Advances in research on HIV infection and periodontitis may provide new insights into the associated pathogenic mechanisms and a better understanding of viral reservoirs. This clinical case demonstrates the importance of attention directed at PDs in HIV infection, suggesting periodontal treatment as an indispensable step in antiretroviral treatment.
