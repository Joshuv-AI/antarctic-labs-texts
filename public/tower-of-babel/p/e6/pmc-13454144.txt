Discovery of 5’ NAD capped viral RNAs reveals evolutionary divergent 5’ metabolite capping across hepaciviruses

ABSTRACT
Hepatitis C virus (HCV), an important pathogen and prototype hepacivirus, 5’ caps its RNA with flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD); no other 5’ metabolite capped viral RNAs have been identified. Here, we establish NADzymeID, allowing specific identification of 5’ nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD). Using NADzymeID, we find that Norway rat hepacivirus 1 (NrHV-1) and related hepaciviruses use 5’ NAD capping, whereas the closest relatives of HCV use 5’ FAD, thereby revealing evolutionary divergence of 5’ capping. NrHV-1 replication is attenuated in absence of NAD, and NrHV-1 is cleared more rapidly in mice with reduced intrahepatic NAD levels. As observed for 5’ FAD, 5’ NAD protects viral RNA from RIG-I mediated innate immune sensing. Thus, despite evolutionary divergence, metabolite capping generally confers evasion of innate immune responses. These represent the first examples of 5’ NAD capping for viruses, providing tools also to investigate the elusive function of cellular 5’ metabolite caps.

Introduction

A hallmark of eukaryotic mRNA is the canonical 5’ m7GpppN cap, which plays vital roles for gene expression by facilitating splicing, nucleocytoplasmic transport, stabilization and translational initiation1,2. More recently, non-canonical 5’ caps have been discovered in bacteria, resulting from the use of common cellular metabolites, like nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD) and dephospho-coenzyme A (dpCoA), as non-canonical initiating nucleotides (NCINs) in RNA synthesis3–6. This finding expanded the repertoire of RNA modifications and changed the paradigm of 5’ capping being unique to eukaryotic mRNAs. To date, RNA capping with cellular metabolites has been described in bacteria, archaea and eukaryotes, including yeast, plants, mice, and humans, with 5’ NAD being the most studied5,7–11. The functional role of 5’ metabolite caps remains elusive and contradictory effects on RNA turnover and translation have been reported for 5’ NAD capping in different organisms12,13. In addition, studies of 5’ NAD capping are limited by the lack of specific methods for detection of 5’ NAD on RNA transcripts14, with detection methods either relying on broadly acting decapping enzymes like the RNA decapping hydrolase, NudC, or Rat1-interacting protein 1 (Rai1)15 or capture methods based on the use of ADP-ribosyl cyclase (ADPRC) for modification of NAD-capped and related RNAs5. In human cells, non-canonical caps constitute <1% of all 5’ caps and their relative abundance correlates with the concentration of the metabolites10,16. Furthermore, only few reports of specific RNA transcripts with high frequency of metabolite capping exist, indicating that initiation with NCINs largely is a stochastic event.

We recently described that hepatitis C virus (HCV) RNA is 5’ capped with the cellular metabolite flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) at high stoichiometry (>75%), thereby providing the first example of RNA 5’ FAD capping across all domains of life17. The HCV 5’ FAD cap represents a novel viral capping strategy based on strong preference of the HCV RNA dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp), NS5B, to use FAD rather than the more abundant ATP in replication initiation17. NS5B replicates the viral positive-sense RNA genome ((+)RNA) in a two-step process using a full-length negative-sense RNA strand intermediate ((–)RNA). In each step, NS5B binds the 3’ end of the viral RNA and initiates RNA synthesis de novo18. Using its adenosine (A) moiety, FAD can base-pair with the conserved HCV ( + )RNA 3’ terminal uridine (U) and serve as NCIN to produce 5’ FAD capped HCV (–)RNAs17. In the second step, most HCV genotypes use FAD for initiation, while others initiate with GTP, leading to 5’ triphosphorylated (5’ ppp) (+)RNAs17. The 5’ FAD cap protects HCV RNA from RIG-I mediated innate immune sensing17. RIG-I is the main cytoplasmic sensor of 5’ ppp RNA and its activation leads to expression of type I and III interferon (IFN) and of interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs)19,20. Consistent with a functional role of the 5’ FAD cap, depletion of FAD inhibits HCV replication in vitro17,21.

Worldwide, around 50 million are chronically infected with HCV22, which comprises six epidemiologically relevant genotypes23. No HCV vaccine is available and despite effective therapy24, chronic HCV infection remains a major cause of hepatic fibrosis, liver cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma, causing more than 200,000 annual deaths22. HCV naturally infects only humans, and while chimpanzees are permissive to experimental infection, they are no longer available for research. This lack of immunocompetent animal models for studying HCV immune responses and pathology limits vaccine development and has therefore sparked an interest in related hepaciviruses as models. Hepaciviruses are all hepatotropic, infect a wide range of species and accordingly can be of veterinary importance25,26. Equine hepacivirus (EqHV) is the phylogenetically closest known relative to HCV and causes liver infection and hepatitis in horses27. The more distantly related GB virus B (GBV-B) primarily causes acute infection after experimental infection in small New World monkeys28. Norway rat hepacivirus 1 (NrHV-1) on the other hand typically causes chronic infection in rats and have been adapted to laboratory mice permitting infection up to ~5 weeks29. Hence, NrHV-1 provides coveted small animal models for HCV and for studies of chronic RNA virus infection as such, including of immune responses, vaccine approaches and pathology30–37. Bovine hepacivirus (BoHV) and bat hepacivirus (BHV) are yet other examples of HCV-related viruses. Hepaciviruses share many features, including genome organization, predicted proteins, and interaction with the liver-specific micro-RNA, miR-122, suggesting that they rely on similar regulatory mechanisms and immune evasion strategies. However, it remains unknown whether other hepaciviruses, similarly to HCV, employ 5’ FAD capping of their RNAs.

In this study, we demonstrate that 5’ metabolite capping is used throughout the genus, but surprisingly observe divergent evolution, resulting in two distinct clades preferring either FAD or NAD as 5’ metabolite cap. To detect 5’ NAD capping, we established NADzymeID, allowing specific identification of 5’ capping with NAD, thereby overcoming the lack of specificity of previously published methods and providing an important tool for future studies of 5’ NAD capping of host and viral RNAs. We further explore the correlates between 5’ metabolite capping and the phylogenetic relationship among viruses, as well as correlates with predicted structural features and in vitro initiation preferences of the encoded polymerases. The results represent the first examples of 5’ NAD capping for any RNA virus and greatly expand the list of viruses that employ 5’ metabolite capping.

Results

NrHV-1 is 5’ metabolite capped in vitro and in vivo

We previously found that HCV is 5’ capped with FAD17 and therefore, hypothesized that related hepaciviruses also use non-canonical 5’ metabolite capping. Due to the availability of animal models and in vitro systems33,38, we initially focused on NrHV-1, which has conserved terminal 5’ A on both RNA strands, compatible with 5’ metabolite capping. We applied the CapZyme-seq method15,17, which combines enzymatic de-capping with 5’ p specific adaptor ligation and high-throughput sequencing (Fig. 1a), to RNA isolated from of NrHV-1 (RHV-rn1 strain) infected McA-RH7777.hi rat hepatoma cells38. Surprisingly, NrHV-1 RNA 5’ reads were not enriched after treatment with Arabidopsis thaliana nudix pyrophosphohydrolase 23 (AtNUDX23), which specifically cleaves 5’ FAD capped RNA17, or the E. coli lipopolysaccharide core heptose(II)-phosphate phosphatase (RNA 5’ polyphosphatase; Rpp), which specifically reduces 5’ ppp to 5’ p (Figs. 1d and S1a-c)39. Known endogenous 5’ ppp RNAs, like 5S ribosomal RNA (rRNA)40, were enriched in the Rpp treated library, whereas mRNA 5’ termini were not. To interrogate other cap structures, we applied CapZyme-seq with NudC and S. pombe Rai1 (SpRai1), both with broad de-capping specificity to non-canonical 5’ caps, including FAD, NAD, adenosine diphosphate ribose (ADPR), diadenosine polyphosphates (ApnA), and dpCoA (Fig. 1a-c). NrHV-1 ( + )RNA 5’ reads were enriched in both libraries (Fig. 1e, f), and since SpRai1 does not cleave m7GpppN (Fig. 1c), these data suggested that NrHV-1( + ) RNA is capped with a 5’ metabolite cap other than FAD. From these experiments, the NrHV-1 ( + )RNA capping frequency was estimated to be >75% (75%, 88%, 99% for the individual replicates); low coverage prevented estimations for the (–)RNA. To confirm 5’ metabolite capping, we applied our previously established reduction RT-qPCR assay, which combines RNA de-capping with digestion by the 5’ p specific exonuclease, Xrn1, and RT-qPCR under conditions where no Xrn1-specific degradation of NAD-capped RNA occurs (Figs. 1a and S1d)17. In agreement with the CapZyme-seq results, we observed significant reduction for NrHV-1 ( + )RNA isolated from NrHV-1 infected McA-RH7777.hi cells following NudC and SpRai1 treatment, but not AtNUDX23 or Rpp treatment (Fig. 1g). In addition, we applied the reduction RT-qPCR assay to NrHV-1 positive mouse serum- and liver-derived RNA confirming 5’ metabolite capping of NrHV-1 ( + )RNA in vivo (Fig. 1h). To substantiate this finding, we applied CapZyme-seq, using pre-treatment with calf intestine phosphatase (CIP) to reduce background signal, to liver-derived RNA from NrHV-1 infected mice. This further demonstrated NudC dependent enrichment of 5’ reads from both NrHV-1 ( + )RNA and (–)RNA as well as host m7GpppN capped mRNAs (Fig. 1i).

Lastly, to increase sensitivity, we replaced the library preparation and deep sequencing of CapZyme-seq with a target specific RT-qPCR (CapZyme RT-qPCR, Figs. 1a and S1e). Applying this to liver-derived RNA samples from an NrHV-1-infected mouse showed NudC dependent enrichment of both NrHV-1 ( + )RNA and (–)RNA, thereby further validating non-canonical 5’ metabolite capping of NrHV-1 (–)RNA in vivo (Fig. 1j). Together, these results demonstrated that NrHV-1 ( + )RNA and (–)RNA are 5’ metabolite capped in vitro and in vivo, surprisingly with a NudC and SpRai1 cleavage substrate that is not FAD.

NrHV-1 is 5’ capped with NAD

NAD was previously described as non-canonical 5’ metabolite cap on cellular RNAs8. We therefore implemented ADPRC mediated biotinylation of 5’ NAD capped RNA followed by streptavidin pull-down, based on the previously described NAD captureSeq and ONE-seq methods5,41, using RT-qPCR to quantify enrichment (Fig. S2a, b). Compared to a 5’ m7GpppN capped control (Rps11 mRNA), both the NrHV-1 ( + )RNA and (–)RNA were enriched (Fig. 2a). To avoid the previously reported background from 5’ m7GpppN41, we pre-treated RNA to remove canonical caps, however, background from circular ADPR (cADPR), and perhaps ADPR (Fig. S2c), may still challenge unambiguous identification of NAD capped RNA using these methods. To specifically identify and differentiate NAD and (c)ADPR RNA 5’ caps, we therefore established the NADzymeID method (Fig. 2b). This includes sequential treatment of RNA with ADPRC, converting 5’ NAD to 5’ ADPR (or the reaction intermediate cADPR)42, and the (c)ADPR specific pyrophosphatase, NUDT5, from Sulfolobus islandicus (sisNUDT5) that specifically hydrolyses 5’ ADPR and cADPR to 5’ p11, thereby facilitating 5’ adaptor ligation and downstream RT-qPCR or library prep. Despite a lower de-capping efficiency compared to NudC, NADzymeID allows specific identification of NAD and (c)ADPR RNA 5’ caps (Figs. 1c and S2d-f). As an independent validation, we applied liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) to detect NAD+ released after nuclease P1 (NP1) digestion of RNA (Fig. S2h). The ratio of NAD+ to ribonucleotides released by NP1 treatment was significantly higher for total RNA isolated from NrHV-1 replicon-selected cells as compared to mock cells (Figs. 2d and S3). Moreover, enrichment for viral particles by ultrafiltration of the supernatant from high-titer NrHV-1 infections further increased NAD+ levels (Figs. 2d and S3). In contrast, we did not observe NP1 dependent release of ADPR, cADPR or NADH from the NrHV-1 containing RNA samples (Fig. S2i). Together these results demonstrate that NrHV-1 RNA is 5’ capped with NAD.

NrHV-1 depends on NAD+ for replication

In mammalian cells, NAD+ can be synthesized from the essential amino acid L-tryptophane de novo, from nicotinic acid (NA) through the Preiss-Handler pathway, or from nicotinamide (NAM) through the salvage pathway (Fig. 2e). Whereas L-tryptophane depletion is incompatible with cell viability, depletion of NAM was tolerated for several days and yielded a significant decrease in intracellular NAD+ levels (Fig. S4a, d). We found that NrHV-1 replication, as measured by the reporter replicon NrHV-1-SGR-Feo38, was significantly reduced upon NAM depletion but rescued by exogenous supplementation of either NAM or NAD+ (Fig. 2f). Furthermore, depletion of the FAD precursor, riboflavin, or the dpCoA precursor, pantothenic acid, did not affect NrHV-1 replication (Fig. S4b, c, e, f). To validate NAD+ dependency of NrHV-1 infection, we treated cells with FK866 (Fig. 2e), a well-described inhibitor of NAMPT43,44, the rate-limiting enzyme of the NAD+ salvage pathway. FK866 inhibited NrHV-1 infection in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 2g), with no significant effect on cell viability for concentrations under 10 µM (Fig. S5a). This reduction of infection was rescued by supplying exogenous NAD+, but not by the NAD+-derived metabolites, ADPR or cADPR (Fig. 2h), consistent with a requirement of NAD+ for NrHV-1 infection. Infection of McA-RH7777.hi cells with the unrelated, m7GpppN-capped Sindbis virus (SINV) was unaffected by FK886, supporting that the reduced NrHV-1 replication was a direct effect of NAD+-depletion (Fig. S5b, c).

Finally, to explore the role of NAD+ for NrHV-1 infection in vivo, we infected hepatocyte-specific Nampt knockout (HNKO) mice45 with the mouse adapted NrHV-1-BSLIS32. Intrahepatic NAD+ levels in the HNKO mice were reduced by >60% (Fig. S5d). In the early course of infection, NrHV-1 RNA titers were slightly higher in HNKO compared to WT mice, possibly due to the involvement of NAMPT in antiviral innate immune responses46. However, accelerated clearance of infection was observed in HNKO mice independent of the inoculum dose (Figs. 2i and S5e). The reduced intrahepatic NAD+ levels did not change NrHV-1 5’ capping, which remained NudC sensitive (Fig. S5f). These results are consistent with a role for NAD+ in NrHV-1 infection also in vivo.

NrHV-1 NS5B initiates replication with NCINs, including NAD+

The observed 5’ NAD capping of the NrHV-1 RNAs suggested that the NrHV-1 NS5B RdRp can use NAD as NCIN during de novo initiation of replication. Therefore, we performed in vitro replication assays using recombinant NrHV-1 NS5B (Fig. S6a) and an RNA template based on the 3’ terminal sequence of NrHV-1(+) (Fig. 3a). We observed efficient initiation and elongation with NAD+, but also with other metabolites, especially the structurally related ADPR and Ap2A, and with ATP (Figs. 3b, c and S6b). Increasing the temperature from 30 °C to 37 °C significantly reduced the yield of ATP initiation and elongation products (Fig. S6c, d), and at 37 °C the NAD elongation product accumulated in a time-dependent manner, much faster than the ATP derived elongation product (Fig. 3d). Moreover, more than 28-fold excess of ATP was required to inhibit NAD+ initiation and elongation but even at this high ATP/NAD+ ratio no ATP initiated elongation products were observed (Fig. 3e). 5’ NAD protects NrHV-1 RNA from intrinsic innate immune sensing

To investigate the function of the 5’ NAD cap, we first assessed the effect of 5’ NAD on viral RNA stability. After electroporating in vitro transcribed, non-replicating NrHV-1-SGR-Feo pol(–)38 RNA into McA-RH7777.hi cells, we observed only a slight but non-significant increase in half-life for 5’ NAD capped RNA, compared to 5’ ppp RNA (Fig. 3f).

The 5’ FAD cap protects HCV RNA from RIG-I mediated innate immune sensing17 and to assess whether 5’ NAD played a similar role, we transfected in vitro transcribed 5’ ppp or 5’ NAD capped, non-replicating NrHV-SGR-Feo pol(–) RNA into A549 reporter cells expressing GFP under the control of an IRF3-dependent IFNB1 promoter47. We observed RIG-I-dependent induction by 5’ ppp RNA, but not by 5’ NAD capped RNA (Figs. 3g and S6e). Furthermore, transfection of 5’ ppp, but not 5’ NAD RNA, into McA-RH7777.hi cells induced the innate immune response genes Ifnb1, Ifnl3, Mx1 and Ifit1 in a RIG-I-dependent manner, as well as the expression of the RIG-I protein itself (Figs. 3h, i and S6f, g). These findings broadly support a role for 5’ metabolite caps, including FAD and NAD, in viral evasion of innate immunity during infection.

5’ NAD capping is conserved for GBV-B

Our findings for HCV17 and NrHV-1 suggested that other hepaciviruses may also depend on non-canonical 5’ metabolite capping. Therefore, we investigated potential 5’ metabolite capping of GBV-B which carries 5’ A on both strands, compatible with metabolite capping and for which replicon systems exist28,48,49. CapZyme-seq on CIP-treated total RNA isolated from Huh-7.5 human hepatoma cells selected for GBV-B_SGR-neo replication showed enrichment of 5’ reads with NudC treatment for both GBV-B ( + )RNA and (–)RNA, but with low read coverage (Fig. 4a). Results from reduction RT-qPCR showed significant reduction after treatment with NudC, but not Rpp, AtNUDX23 or sisNUDT5 (Figs. 4b and S7a), consistent with 5’ NAD, but not with 5’ FAD, capping of GBV-B ( + )RNA. Enrichment of GBV-B ( + )RNA in NADzymeID following treatment with the NAD-specific combination of ADPRC and sisNUDT5 further supported this finding (Figs. 4c and S7b). Furthermore, the GBV-B ( + )RNA was enriched compared to the RPS11 mRNA control after ADPRC mediated biotinylation and streptavidin pull-down (Fig. 4d). To investigate 5’ metabolite capping of GBV-B in vivo, we isolated liver-derived RNA from an experimentally infected tamarin and observed NudC-specific enrichment of GBV-B ( + )RNA by CapZyme RT-qPCR (Fig. 4e). Hepaciviral 5’ cap preference correlates with structure predictions for NS5Bs

We reasoned that the striking correlation between 5’ metabolite capping and NS5B NCIN preference reflects structural differences among hepaciviral NS5Bs. Since NS5B structures have only been experimentally determined for HCV, we performed AlphaFold3 predictions50 with 10 different seeds for NrHV-1 NS5B with two Mg2+ ions, a 4 nt RNA template corresponding to the NrHV-1 ( + )RNA 3’ end, NAD+ and GTP (Fig. S10a). Except for residues in the NS5B C-terminus (551-583) and two loops binding the RNA template (18-29 and 394-397), predictions, including ligands, were with high or very high confidence (Figs. S11 and S12). In all predictions, the adenosine monophosphate (AMP) moiety of NAD+ was consistently positioned in the active site together with the Mg2+ ions and the incoming GTP. The adenine base of NAD+ and the guanine base of the incoming GTP stacked on top of each other and formed base pairs with the two 3’ terminal template bases, in agreement with the arrangement seen in experimentally determined HCV NS5B structures (Figs. 6b, c and S10a)51. While the exact conformation of the nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) substructure of NAD+ varied between predictions, NMN consistently occupied the same binding pocket, thereby allowing the identification of potential NS5B contact points in seven distinct regions (Figs. 6b, c and S10b-d). By comparing the NS5B sequences observed in proximity to NMN, we found that specific amino acid residues, in particular position 442, 445, and 446 in the beta-loop (beta-2) and 542 and 546 in the C-terminal loop (loop-5; numbering according to NrHV-1 NS5B), correlated with hepaciviral 5’ NCIN preference (Figs. 6d and S10e-f). Both the beta-loop and C-terminal loop are important for de novo initiation51–53, and in a recently published initiation structure of the template loaded HCV NS5B with FAD base paired to the terminal template U and in a state ready to become activated for initiation, specific interactions between FAD and residues M447 and Y448 of the beta-loop were suggested54. Among the corresponding positions in NrHV-1 NS5B (442-443), a tryptophan at position 442 is conserved among NAD+ preferring NS5Bs but absent for FAD preferring NS5Bs. This tryptophan is orientated towards the predicted NAD+ binding pocket and could thus stabilize NAD+ binding through stacking interactions (Fig. 6c), thereby potentially affecting NCIN selection. For the FAD initiated HCV NS5B structure, the C-terminal loop was suggested to stabilize the early elongation complex with residue W550 (corresponding to 544 for NrHV-1) directly interacting with the 5’ FAD54. Interestingly, the tryptophan at position 544 is only found for FAD initiating hepaciviruses, which combined with the clade specific amino acid residues at positions 542 and 546 suggests a 5’ cap specific interaction of this region in stabilization of the elongation complex. Accordingly, these structural features of hepaciviral NS5B polymerases could explain differences in 5’ metabolite cap preference.

Discussion

The current study identifies the first examples of 5’ NAD capping for any RNA virus and greatly expands the number of viruses known to use RNA 5’ metabolite capping. We demonstrate that 5’ metabolite capping is broadly used throughout the Hepacivirus genus, but that divergent evolution resulted in two separate strategies for metabolite cap usage. Viruses in the clade containing HCV, EqHV and BHV-K use 5’ FAD capping17, whereas NrHV-1, GBV-B, BoHV and RHV-E from the other major clade use 5’ NAD capping (Fig. 6a). RNA 5’ metabolite capping correlates with structural features and NCIN preferences of the hepaciviral NS5B polymerases, which are consistent with their phylogenetic relationship.

Support for hepaciviral 5’ NAD capping came from several lines of evidence involving the investigation of NrHV-1 in vitro and in vivo: Firstly, CapZyme-seq, reduction RT-qPCR and CapZyme RT-qPCR experiments were consistent with a 5’ NAD cap on NrHV-1 RNA. NAD cleaving enzymes, such as NudC and spRai1, decapped the NrHV-1 RNA, whereas enzymes without NAD cleaving activity, such as Rpp, AtNUDX23 or sisNUDT5 did not. Moreover, NrHV-1 RNA was pulled down by streptavidin after ADPRC mediated biotinylation of the 5’ cap5,41. However, since this method lacks specificity for NAD and can give signal for other types of 5’ caps14, we developed a new NAD specific method, NADzymeID, which combines treatment with ADPRC and sisNUDT5, thereby avoiding cross reactivity with other 5’ caps, including m7GpppN, cADPR and ADPR. Using NADzymeID, we unambiguously demonstrate that NrHV-1 RNA is 5’ NAD capped. The utility of NADzymeID extends beyond the analysis of viral RNAs and will allow unambiguous identification of 5’ NAD capped RNA in eukaryotes without the need for m7GpppN cap depletion14,55.

Secondly, LC-MS/MS, a decapping enzyme independent method for 5’ NAD detection, identified elevated NAD+ levels in total intracellular RNA during NrHV-1 replication. Although we cannot rule out a contribution from increased 5’ NAD capping of host RNA, the additional enrichment of NAD+ identified from RNA isolated from up-concentrated NrHV-1 particles is highly suggestive of the NAD+ signal deriving from NrHV-1 RNA. Furthermore, RNA derived NADH, ADPR or cADPR was not detected in these samples. Since we observed oxidation of our NADH standard to NAD+, we however cannot exclude a certain fraction of NADH capping of the NrHV-1 RNA, and accordingly have described this simply as 5’ NAD capped throughout.

Thirdly, NrHV-1 replication was attenuated in the absence of NAD, and NrHV-1 infection was impaired by the NAMPT inhibitor FK866 and could be rescued by NAD+ but not by ADPR or cADPR. Unlike for HCV17, depletion of the FAD precursor, riboflavin, did not affect NrHV-1 replication, and neither did depletion of the dpCoA precursor, pantothenic acid. NrHV-1 infection was resolved more rapidly in mice with reduced intrahepatic NAD+ levels, all supporting not only that NrHV-1 is 5’ NAD capped, but also that NAD capping plays an important role during infection in vitro and in vivo.

Finally, NrHV-1 NS5B initiated and elongated with a broader range of metabolites, but the highest efficiency was in the presence of NAD+ or the structurally related ADPR and Ap2A. This was in contrast to HCV NS5B (genotype 2a, JFH1) that has a strong preference for initiation with FAD, consistent with the 5’ FAD capping observed for HCV17. Intracellular NAD+ concentrations are ~50X higher than for FAD16, and although the exact intracellular concentrations of metabolites, such as ADPR and Ap2A are not known, these are likely much lower than for NAD+, making NAD+ the most accessible NCIN for NrHV-1 NS5B. The intracellular NAD+ to ATP ratio is ~1:1016, but at this ratio ATP did not significantly reduce the formation of the NADpG initiation product, and this excess of ATP did not result in formation of the 5’ ppp elongation product by NS5B. These findings suggest that ATP at its intracellular concentration is not an effective competitor for 5’ NAD+ capping, thereby rationalizing the observed 5’ NAD capping of NrHV-1.

Evidence for 5’ NAD capping was further supported by similar sensitivity of cell culture or tamarin liver-derived GBV-B RNA and bovine serum derived BoHV (+)RNA to decapping enzymes. GBV-B replication likewise depended on the presence of intracellular NAD. Moreover, GBV-B, BoHV, and RHV-E NS5Bs, initiated and elongated with a broad range of metabolites, generally with the structurally related NAD+, ADPR and Ap2A being most efficient. In contrast, serum and liver derived EqHV (+)RNA was AtNUDX23 sensitive suggesting a 5’ FAD cap, and similarly to HCV, EqHV and BHV-K NS5Bs had strong preference for initiation and elongation with FAD. Intracellular availability of free FAD, NAD+, ATP and potentially other metabolites for initiation of replication might contribute to regulation of hepacivirus replication during infection and disease progression in vivo. Given that FAD and NAD both are redox active metabolites, one may further speculate that their redox state plays a role, e.g. in regulating NS5B initiation or elongation.

The different NCIN preference and 5’ metabolite capping observed among the two hepacivirus clades points towards structural differences in the respective NS5B RdRps. Structure modeling of NAD+ together with NrHV-1 NS5B, a viral RNA template, GTP and two Mg2+ ions predicted NAD+ binding in the active site, base-paired to the template terminal position with the incoming GTP correctly positioned to perform de novo initiation. These predictions consistently positioned NMN in a large binding pocket surrounded by seven NS5B regions, in which specific amino acid residues correlated with the observed capping preference, in particular in the beta-loop and the C-terminal loop. W442, which is specific for the NAD capping clade, projected into the binding pocket towards NMN and could be stabilizing the initiation complex for NAD initiating polymerases (Fig. 6b, c). In contrast, the FAD clade, as exemplified by HCV NS5B, has a methionine at this position and stacking of the FAD isoalloxazine with the template 3’ U could instead provide further stabilization54. The NMN binding pocket is large for the NAD clade and therefore likely can accommodate a variety of metabolites, consistent with the more promiscuous initiation observed for this clade. In addition, the strong correlation of C-terminal loop residues 542, 544 and 546 with NCIN preference suggests that the role of this region in stabilizing the early elongation stage through interaction with the 5’ metabolite cap54 dictated convergent evolution of NCIN preference and specific C-terminal loop residues. Based on these NS5B capping preference correlated features, we predict that BHV-M uses 5’ FAD capping and that guereza hepacivirus (GHV), NrHV-2, RHV-F, RHV-I and BHV-L use 5’ NAD capping (Fig. S10e). This assignment further matches their phylogenetic relationship (Fig. 6a). We speculate that a common hepaciviral ancestor encoded the more promiscuous NAD-initiating RdRp type, from which the FAD preferring polymerases then evolved. Alternatively, a 5’ ppp initiating common ancestor could be supported by the deeply rooted RHV-J, which does not initiate with 5’ A (Fig. 6a)56, and at defining positions encodes amino acid residues that do not group with either of the FAD or NAD clades (Figure S10e). That would also explain the presence of 5’ pppG on the (+)RNA of certain HCV genotypes (1, 2b and 6), which only use 5’ FAD on their (–)RNA17.

The use of 5’ metabolite capping across the hepaciviruses indicates functional importance. As observed for the HCV 5’ FAD cap17, 5’ NAD did not significantly stabilize transfected NrHV-1 ( + )RNA beyond that of 5’ ppp NrHV-1 RNA. This could be the result of cap-independent miR-122-mediated protection against 5’ de-capping enzymes57. The potential redundant roles of miR-122 and the 5’ cap should therefore be investigated. In addition, 5’ NAD and FAD caps may be removed by de-capping exonucleases like Xrn158,59, NUDT1260 and DXO/DOM3Z61,62, the latter of which has been shown to restrict HCV replication63.

We previously found that 5’ FAD capping protects viral RNA from RIG-I mediated innate immune sensing17, and we here show that the same is true for 5’ NAD. In contrast, it was recently reported that 5’ FAD and 5’ NAD capped short blunt-end dsRNAs can activate RIG-I64. In our study, we used long viral RNA to mirror conditions during infection, and it is likely that the different structural composition of the RNAs used in the two studies explains the contradictory results. Nevertheless, viral RNA capping with FAD or NAD appears to represent a conserved innate immune evasion strategy that could be important early in infection before hepaciviral NS3-4A protease-induced cleavage and inactivation of the downstream innate immune signaling protein, MAVS, takes effect65–67 and thereby constitutes an important complementary viral evasion strategy.

The widespread distribution of 5’ metabolite capping among hepaciviruses suggests that yet other viruses may also have evolved RNA 5’ capping through de novo replication initiation with NCINs. The related orthoflavivirus and pegivirus genomes terminate with 3′ U, making their (–)RNA initiation compatible with adenosine dinucleotide metabolite usage. Moreover, Orthomyxoviridae, Paramyxoviridae and Rhabdoviridae replicate through de novo initiation and have conserved genomic 5’ A and 3’ U residues.

The 5’ FAD and NAD capping frequencies of >75% observed for hepaciviral RNA are the highest observed for any non-canonical metabolite capped RNA transcript5,7–9,17,61,68 and provide another example of effective viral hijacking of cellular factors. The high ratio of viral compared to cellular 5’ metabolite RNA caps makes the NAD and FAD caps potential antiviral targets. Here, we demonstrated antiviral activity of the NAD+ synthesis inhibitor, FK866, against NrHV-1 infection. Given that related inhibitors of NAMPT, such as GMX1777, have already been tested in clinical trials for anti-tumor activity69, therapeutic targeting of metabolites could potentially become an antiviral strategy.

Understanding the mechanistic and functional differences of 5’ FAD and NAD capping of hepaciviral RNA will not only improve our knowledge of viral 5’ metabolite capping but could also serve as an important model system to understand implications of metabolite capping of cellular RNAs, and the new method NADzymeID could further accelerate this. Taken together, the current study on one hand demonstrates a conserved mechanism of RNA 5’ capping in evasion of cell intrinsic innate immune responses, but surprising also identifies evolutionary divergence of 5’ metabolite RNA capping across the hepaciviruses. This represents the first examples of 5’ NAD capping for any RNA virus, opening a new frontier in virology and RNA biology.

Methods

Animal samples (larger animals)

Tamarin experiments were performed in strict accordance with UK Home Office guidelines, under a license (2373) granted to the host establishment by the UK Secretary of State for the Home Office which approved the work. For the original study, purpose-bred, weaned red bellied tamarins (Saguinus labiatus) were group-housed in same-sex groups for the duration of the study, with daily feeding and access to water ad libitum. Regular modifications to the housing area and environmental enrichment were made by husbandry staff. The environmental temperature was appropriate for tamarins and rooms were subject to a 12 h light dark cycle. Animals were acclimatized to their environment and deemed to be healthy by the named veterinary surgeon prior to inclusion on the study. For analysis of GBV-B capping, RNA was extracted from archived in vivo liver samples taken post-termination of tamarin M6, which was inoculated intravenously with 107 genome equivalents (GE) of GBV-B stock. Viremia at termination (week 7 post inoculation) was 108 GE/mL. For EqHV, RNA was extracted from acute phase serum or liver samples from three horses experimentally infected with the NZP1 strain; viremia at time of collection (week 1–6) was 107–108 GE/mL70. For BoHV, RNA was extracted from the serum of three naturally infected cattle; viremia at time of collection was 107–108 GE/mL71,72.

Infection of laboratory mice

Rodent experiments were performed according to protocols approved by the Danish Experiments Inspectorate (protocol 2022-15-0201-01292). All experiments adhered to guidelines outlined in the Guide of Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the European Union (EU directive 2010/63/EU), and the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals73. All work was consistent with an affirmative response to the ARRIVE 10 questionnaire. All animals had access to food (SAFE D03, SAFE Complete Care Competence, Rosenheim, Germany) and water ad libitum and were housed by gender (except for breeders) in Innovive IVC caging (air exchange rate of 63–75 L per hour) containing wood chip bedding, shelters, nesting material and biting sticks on a 12 h light dark cycle. Mice were kept at room temperature 22˚C ( + /– 2˚C) and humidity of 55% (+/–10%) following the regulation nr. 2028 af 14/12/2020. All experimentation was conducted during light cycle. Hepatocyte-specific Nampt knockout (HNKO) mice45, and WT littermates age matched between 35–85 days were infected under isoflurane anaesthesia via tail vein or intra-splenically with either high dose 1000 GE or low dose 300 GE inoculum of mouse-adapted NrHV-BSLIS per animal, using a stock with a mouse infectious dose-50 of 138 GE32. Infection with NrHV-1 was evaluated by TaqMan Fast Virus 1-Step Master Mix (ThermoFisher Scientific) using primers previously described38 on viral RNA isolated from blood samples collected by temporal facial vein puncture and purified by Trizol LS reagent (Invitrogen). Mouse liver was collected following euthanasia at day 3 or day 30 and stored in RNA later (Sigma) until RNA extraction. RNA was extracted from up to 100 mg of mouse liver homogenized in Trizol reagent (Invitrogen) using Precelly homogenizer.

Cell culture

The human hepatoma cell line Huh-7.574, the adenocarcinoma human alveolar basal epithelial cell line A549-pr(IFNβ)-GFP47,75 and the rat hepatoma cell line McA-RH7777.hi38 were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/ml penicillin, and 100 µg/ml streptomycin (P/S) (Sigma-Aldrich). Cells were sub-cultured every 2–4 days and maintained at 37 °C, 5% CO2.

Virus infections

For NrHV-1 infection, McA-RH7777.hi cells were seeded one day prior to infection with a cell-culture adapted version of the NrHV isolate rn1 (RHVcc-1)33. Infected cells were sub-cultured every 2–4 days and monitored for viral spread by immunofluorescence with mouse anti-RHV IgG33. When 70–80% of the cells were positive for NrHV-rn1 antigen, the supernatant was collected and the cells washed with PBS prior lysis with TRIzol (Invitrogen).

RNA extraction

RNA was extracted by addition of 0.2 volumes chloroform (Sigma-Aldrich) followed by vortexing 10–15 s, incubation at room temperature 2–3 min and centrifugation at 4 °C, 12,000 × g for 15 min. RNA was then purified from the aqueous phase using RNA Clean and Concentrator-25 kit (Zymo Research) and on-column DNase I treated, following manufacturer’s instructions. An additional RNA purification was performed with the RNA Clean and Concentrator-25 kit (Zymo Research). RNA concentration and quality were assessed using NanoDrop (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Selection for viral replication

The bicistronic replicons NrHV-1-SGR-FEO WT (adFEO10), replication-deficient (pol(–)) NrHV-1-SGR-FEO GNN38, and GBV-B neo-RepB76 were previously described. Here, we generated a GBV-B-FEO-RepB and the corresponding GNN construct by replacing the firefly luciferase gene of GBV-B luc-RepB (or GNN)49 with a FEO cassette using In-Fusion Snap Assembly Master Mix (Takara) cloning. Although we were unsuccessful in selecting replication competent colonies using the FEO replicon, its GNN derivative was used as control in related experiments. The NrHV-SGR-FEO WT (adFEO10) and NrHV-1-SGR-FEO GNN plasmids were linearized with MluI (NEB), while GBV-B neo-RepB and GBV-FEO-RepB GNN were linearized with SapI (NEB). NrHV replicon RNA was synthesized using the T7 RiboMAX Express Large Scale RNA Production System (Promega) according to manufacturer’s instructions. GBV-B replicon RNA was generated with the T7 MEGAscript T7 Transcription Kit (Invitrogen) following manufacturer’s instructions. The in vitro transcribed (IVT) RNAs were treated with TURBO DNase (Invitrogen) 30 min on ice and purified twice with the RNA Clean and Concentrator-25 kit (Zymo Research) and on-column DNase I treated in the first purification. RNA was quantified using Qubit RNA High Sensitivity assay kit (ThermoFisher Scientific) according to manufacturer’s instructions.

For antibiotic-resistant selection of cells harboring NrHV-1-SGR-FEO WT replication, 1×105 McA-RH7777.hi cells were seeded into 6-well plates one day prior to transfection. For transfection, 1 µg of NrHV-1-SGR-FEO WT or pol(–) replicon IVT RNA and 1 µL of Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) were mixed in 250 µL OptiMEM (Gibco), incubated 20 min at room-temperature and diluted with 750 µL DMEM + 3% FBS. Cells were transfected 4 h at 37 °C, 5% CO2 after which transfection medium was replaced with DMEM + 10% FBS + 1% P/S and cells were further incubated at 37 °C, 5% CO2. Selection with 500 µg/mL of G418 sulfate (Gibco) was initiated 2 days post-transfection, and fresh selection media was added every 2-3 days until cells transfected with NrHV-1-SGR-FEO-GNN RNA died. G418-selected cells with NrHV-1-SGR-FEO WT were washed with PBS and lysed for RNA extraction using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen) as described above.

For antibiotic-resistant selection of cells with GBV-B neo-RepB WT replication, 6 × 106 Huh7.5 cells were electroporated with 5 µL 50 µM miR-122 (GE Dharmacon, Horizon) and 15 µg GBV-B neo-RepB WT or GBV-FEO-RepB GNN replicon IVT RNA. Electroporation was performed in 400 µL cytomix (120 mM KC1, 0.15 mM CaCl2, 10 mM K2HPO4/KH2PO4 (pH 7.6), 25 mM HEPES (pH 7.6), 2 mM EGTA (pH 7.6), 5 mM MgCl2, pH adjusted with KOH, 2 mM ATP, 5 mM glutathione) in 4 mm cuvettes at infinite resistance, 270 V, 950 µF using a BioRad GenePulser Xcell (BioRad). Electroporated cells were resuspended in 15 mL DMEM + 10% FBS + 1% P/S and seeded in 10 cm dishes. Selection with 250 µg/mL G418 sulfate (Gibco) was initiated 2 days post electroporation and cells were sub-cultured with fresh selection media every 2-3 days. Selection was continued for 4 weeks during which all cells electroporated with GBV-FEO-RepB GNN RNA died. GBV-B neo-RepB WT electroporated and G418-selected cells were washed with PBS and lysed for RNA extraction using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen) as described above.

Production of NrHV-1-enriched supernatants for LC-MS/MS

NrHV-1 enriched supernatant production was based on a previously published strategy for HCV77. McA-RH7777.hi cells were infected with RHVcc-1 at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.03. At 3 days post-infection, the culture medium was replaced with DMEM + 2% FBS + 100 µg/mL P/S. Virus-containing supernatant was collected between 7 to 18 days post-infection, filtered through a 0.45 μm pore-size filter, and stored at −80 °C. The supernatant was thawed overnight at 4 °C and further filtered using a 0.2 μm dead-end filter (Nalgene). The filtrate was concentrated using tangential flow filtration (TFF) in two sequential filtrations using hollow fiber filters (Repligen) with a 500 kDa molecular weight cut-off and a surface area of 2600 cm² and 790 cm², respectively. 6 mL of retentate was loaded onto a 3-step iodixanol gradient (1 mL of undiluted 60% Optiprep Density Gradient Medium (Sigma), 1 mL of 20% Optiprep diluted with PBS, and 1 mL of 10% Optiprep diluted with PBS). PBS was used to adjust the final volume to ~11 mL. Tubes were centrifuged at 40,000 rpm for 2 h at 4 °C using an SW-41 rotor (Beckman Coulter). Three fractions were collected from the top of the density gradient: fraction 1 (7.5 mL), fraction 2 (1.2 mL), and fraction 3 ( ~ 2.3 mL). Supernatant samples were further upconcentrated using Amicon Ultra Centrifugal Filters (Millipore) following manufacturer’s instructions. Upconcentrated supernatants of fraction 2 or combined fractions 1 and 3 were mixed with 3 volumes of TRIzol LS Reagent (Invitrogen) for RNA extractions, performed as described above.

Quantification of NAD+ and ADPR using LC-MS/MS

50 micrograms (total RNA samples) or the RNA output of ultrafiltration NrHV-1 supernatant preparation was digested to ribonucleotides by nuclease P1 (Sigma) in 10 mM ammonium acetate (pH 6.0) and 1 mM ZnCl2 at 40 °C for 1 h, then ammonium bicarbonate to 30 mM and calf intestinal alkaline phosphatase (Sigma) was added and incubated an additional 1 h at 37 °C. Undigested controls, where only buffers and calf intestinal alkaline phosphatase were added, were run in parallel to monitor possible co-isolated free metabolites. Samples were precipitated with 3 volumes of acetonitrile and centrifuged (16,000 × g, 20 min, 4 °C). Stable isotope labelled internal standards (I.S., see mass list) were added to supernatants, lyophilized, and dissolved in 75% acetonitrile for LC-MS/MS analysis. An Agilent 1290 Infinity II UHPLC system with an Acquity PRM BEHAmide 150 × 2.1 mm (1.8 μm) column (Waters) was used for chromatographic separation. The mobile phase gradient with 10 mM ammonium acetate pH 7.0 (A) and acetonitrile with 10 mM ammonium acetate pH 5.5 (B) started with 75% B for 2 min, then 3 min of 75–65% B, 5 min of 65–10% B, 2 min of 10% B, and 4 min of 75% B at 0.21 ml/min. MS/MS detection was performed using an Agilent 6495 Triple Quadrupole monitoring m/z 664.1-136.1 (NAD), 666.1-136.1 (NADH), 666.1-514.1 (NADH qualifier), 560.1-348.1 (ADPR qualifier), 560.1-136.1 (ADPR), 560.1-348.1 (ADPR qualifier), 542.1-136.1 (cyclic ADPR), 542.1-348.1 (cyclic ADPR qualifier), 268.1-136.1 (A), 284.1-152.1 (G), 244.1-112.1 (C), 245.1-113.1 (U), 669.1-136.1 (13C5-NAD, I.S.), 565.1-136.1 (13C5-ADPR, I.S.), 273.1- 141.1 (13C5-A, I.S.), 246.1-114.1 (d2-C, I.S.), and 247.1-115.1 (d2-U, I.S.).

Expression and purification of NudC, spRai1, and sisNUDT5

NudC was expressed and purified as previously described17. Codon-optimized protein coding sequences for spRai1 and sisNUDT5 were separately cloned into pET-26b(+) using Ndel and XhoI restriction enzymes. For each protein, the plasmid was transformed by heat shock into E. coli Rosetta (DE3) competent cells. The cell culture was grown at 37 °C to OD600 = 0.6 in LB medium supplemented with 50 µg/ml kanamycin and 25 µg/ml chloramphenicol before induction with 1 mM isopropyl β- D-1-thiogalactopyranoside at 18 °C overnight. Cells were collected and washed with ice-cold binding buffer (20 mM Tris HCl (pH 7.9), 500 mM NaCl) followed by lysis using French press in the ice-cold binding buffer supplemented with cOmplete, Mini, EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail (Merck). Cell lysate was centrifuged at 9000 × g for 40 min at 4 °C and the supernatant was passed through a 0.45-µm filter. The cleared supernatant was loaded onto a HisTrap TALON Crude, 1 mL purification column (Cytiva) on an ÄKTA Go purification system and washed with 5 column volumes of the binding buffer. Protein was eluted with 20 column volumes of 0-100% gradient elution buffer (20 mM Tris HCl (pH 7.9), 500 mM NaCl and 1 M imidazole). Protein fractions were pooled and buffer exchanged using PD10 Desalting Columns (Cytiva) into storage buffer (20 mM Tris HCl (pH 8.0), 300 mM NaCl, and 5% glycerol). Protein purity was assessed with SDS-PAGE and protein concentration was assessed with NanoDrop (ThermoFisher Scientific) and BCA protein assays (Pierce).

Gel-based RNA decapping assay

RNA cleavage specificity of NudC, spRai1 and sisNUDT5 was assessed with a gel-based RNA decapping activity assay that compares the ability of the enzymes to cleave RNA with different 5’ metabolites. Radioactively labelled 40-nt RNA 5’ capped with different metabolites was synthesized as previously described78. The DNA template was prepared by annealing two DNA oligonucleotides ((T7f2.5-40n_AG_F and T7f2.5-40n_AG_R; Table S1) that include the T7φ2.5 promoter followed by a transcribed region of 40 nt with a single adenosine at the transcription start site. Radioactively labeled 5’ capped RNA was incubated with 1 µM of enzyme for 30 min at 37 °C in 1xNUDX buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 5 mM MgCl2, and 1 mM DTT. Cleavage reactions were terminated by addition of equal volume of Gel Loading Buffer II (Ambion), resolved with a 7 M urea, 0.2 % 3′-acrylamidophenylboronic acid, 10 % PAGE, and visualized using a PhosphorImager.

CapZyme-seq

Total RNA (1.25 µg or 5 µg) supplemented with 2 µL of 1:100 diluted ERCC Spike-in mix (SIRV-Set 3, Lexogen) was treated with Terminator 5’ phosphate dependent Exonuclease (TEX; Lucigen, TER51020), as previously described79. For CIP-treated reactions, 15 µg of total RNA was treated with Quick CIP (NEB) in rCutSmart buffer for 30 min at 37 °C. After TEX or CIP treatments, the RNA was enzymatically digested with de-capping enzymes. For NudC reactions, RNA was incubated with 1 µM of purified NudC in 1x NUDX buffer at 37 °C for 30 min. For AtNUDX23, sisNUDT5, and SpRai1, RNA was treated with 1 µM enzyme in 1xNUDX buffer at 37 °C for 30 min. For Rpp reactions, RNA was treated with 1 U/µl of Rpp (Bioresearch Technologies) in 1xRpp buffer supplemented with 1 U/µl of RNase inhibitor for 30 min at 37 °C. For negative control reactions, RNA was incubated in 1xNUDX buffer at 37 °C for 30 min. Each de-capping reaction was purified with RNA Clean & Concentrator Kit-5 or -25 (Zymo Research), according to manufacturer’s instructions. CapZyme-seq libraries were generated and sequenced as previously described17.

CapZyme-seq analysis

CapZyme-seq sequencing data was trimmed for adapter sequences and reads shorter than 15 nt using cutadapt (cutadapt -a AGATCGGAAGAGCACACGTCT -q 20 -m 15 --nextseq-trim=20)80. The RNAs expressed in the McA-RH7777.hi cells were identified by pseudomapping of the trimmed reads to rat transcriptome (Ensembl Rnor6.0), or in case of in vivo samples, the mouse transcriptome (Ensembl GRCm39) using Kallisto (kallisto quant --single -l 200 -s 30)81. The transcripts with an expression level higher than 1 TPM were used to make a Bowtie2 index combined with sequences for rat or mouse ncRNAs. Subsequently, the sequencing reads were mapped against the index using bowtie2 (bowtie2 --norc --very-sensitive)82. The fasta files used for the analysis and the resulting bam files normalized to contain same number of mapped reads for display in the Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) desktop application83 is available at 10.17894/ucph.f86fb67e-efb2-47c2-95a5-737ee7dcf00a (NrHV-1) and 10.17894/ucph.8045c244-8696-4086-9302-565541bf8441 (GBV-B). The presented coverage plots are based on the IGV display of the normalized bam files exported as SVG and edited in Adobe Illustrator. The featureCounts function from the Subread package were used to obtain 5’ termini counts for 5’ end of reads mapping within the first 5 nts of each transcript present in the fasta file used for mapping. The input to featureCounts was the non-normalized sam files and a SAF file that defined the first 5 nts for each transcript (featureCounts --read2pos 5)84. For plotting and statistical analysis, featureCounts count files were imported into R and analysed using DESeq2 and Wald significance tests using a multi-factor design including the sample information as a term in the design formula (paired test)85. To estimate 5’NAD capping percentage of NrHV, the counts obtained in each library were normalized to the sum of the counts obtained for the mitochondrial and cytoplasmic large subunit (LSU) and small subunit (SSU) rRNAs and the normalized count of the corresponding control sample was subtracted. For each replicate of the NrHV-1 ( + )RNA, the normalized NudC counts were divided by the sum of normalized NudC and Rpp counts. Data were plotted using base-R86 and the final figures were assembled using Adobe Illustrator. Sequence fastq data files are available at the European Nucleotide Archive (ENA) under the accession PRJEB89091.

Hepacivirus NS5B nucleotide sequences with a deletion of the sequence coding for the C-terminal membrane-anchoring domain were cloned into a Champion pET303/CT-His plasmid with a C-terminal His6 tag (ThermoFischer Scientific) as follows: NS5B sequences were amplified from pRHV-rn1 (for NrHV WT)31, pRHV-rn-1-SGR-FEO-GNN (for NrHV-1 pol(–))38, GBV-B luc-RepB (for GBV-B)49, or pNZP1 (for EqHV)87, using Q5 Hot Start High-Fidelity DNA polymerase (New England Biolabs) and primers as in Table S2. RHV-E (RHV-pm-339)56, BoHV (463)71 and BHV-K (BHV-PDB-829)88, NS5B nucleotide sequences were synthesized as gBlocks Gene Fragments (IDT) using GenBank sequences NC_021153, NC_038432 and NC_038430 (Table S2). The pET303/CT-His plasmid along with the NrHV-1 WT, NrHV-1 GNN, and BHV-PDB-829 amplified sequences were digested with XhoI and XbaI restriction enzymes (NEB). The BoHV fragment was digested with SpeI-HF and SalI-HF (NEB). NrHV-1 WT, NrHV-1 GNN, BHV-PDB-829 and BoHV DNA fragments were ligated into pET303/CT-His using T4 DNA ligase (ThermoFischer Scientific). The GBV-B, EqHV and RHV-pm-339 DNA fragments were cloned into the pET303/CT-His plasmid using In-Fusion Snap Assembly Master Mix (Takara). Ligation and In-Fusion reactions were transformed by heat-shock into TOP10 (ThermoFischer Scientific) or Stellar (Takara) chemically competent E. coli, respectively. The sequences of the constructs were validated by Sanger sequencing (Macrogen Europe).

NS5B plasmids were transformed into Rosetta 2 (DE3) competent E. coli (Novagen/Sigma-Aldrich) by heat shock. For protein expression, starter cultures were grown at 37 °C in 30 mL 2X YT medium supplemented with ampicillin (50 µg/mL) and chloramphenicol (34 µg/mL) until reaching OD600 = 0.5-1.0. Starter cultures were diluted in 600 mL Overnight Express Instant TB autoinduction medium (Novagen) supplemented with ampicillin (50 µg/mL) and chloramphenicol (34 µg/mL) and incubated at 22 °C and 150 rpm shaking for 24 h before harvesting cells by centrifugation at 4000 × g and 4 °C for 20 min. The supernatant was loaded onto the column and washed with 50% binding buffer and 50% elution buffer (50 mM Na2HPO4 (pH 8.0), 500 mM NaCl, 0.05% n-octyl-β-D-glucoside, 250 mM imidazole) and the protein was eluted using 100% elution buffer. NS5B de novo replication initiation assay

10 µL of de novo replication initiation reactions contained 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 5 mM MgCl2, 5 mM DTT, 0.25 µg NS5B, 10 µM RNA template (IDT), 1 mM of indicated initiating nucleotide and 1 mM of GTP (Sigma Aldrich) supplemented with 0.3 or 0.5 µL of α32P-labeled nucleotide (3000 Ci mmol−1, Perkin Elmer or Revvity). Assembled reactions were incubated at 30 or 37 °C for 1 h and were terminated by addition of equal volume of Gel loading buffer II (Ambion). 14 nt RNA (IDT) was 5’ radioactively labelled with T4 polynucleotide kinase (ThermoFisher Scientific) following manufacturer’s instructions and used as a size marker. ATP-G, FAD-G and NAD-G size markers were synthesized as previously described78 with DNA template prepared by annealing two DNA oligonucleotides ((T7f2.5-40n_AG_F and T7f2.5-40n_AG_R; Table S1). Production of 5′ NCIN-capped in vitro transcribed replicon RNAs

To produce 5’ NCIN-capped RNA we engineered pNrHV-SGR-FEO GNN38 and pGBV-B luc-RepB GNN76 with a T7φ2.5 promoter, as described previously for JFH1-SGR-Feo-GNN constructs17. pNrHV-1-SGR-FEO GNN T7Aφ2.5 was linearized with MluI (NEB), pGBV-B luc-RepB GNN T7φ2.5 was linearized with SapI (NEB), and pJFH1-SGR-Feo-GNN T7φ2.5 was linearized with XbaI (NEB). 5’ metabolite capped RNA was produced by in vitro transcription using MEGAscript T7 transcription kit (Invitrogen) with reactions including 1 µg linearized plasmid, 7.5 mM CTP, UTP and GTP, 1.5 mM ATP and 6 mM of either FAD, NAD+ (ThermoFisher Scientific) or ADPR (Sigma–Aldrich). Control reactions generating 5’ ppp RNA were prepared in parallel with 7.5 mM ATP, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Reactions were incubated 2 h at 30 °C followed by treatment with TURBO DNase (Invitrogen) for 30 min on ice and purified twice with the RNA Clean and Concentrator-25 kit (Zymo Research). To control for co-purification of free metabolites, 6 mM FAD, NAD+ or ADPR was added prior to purification. IVT RNA was analyzed on a 1% agarose gel and quantified using the Qubit RNA High Sensitivity assay kit (ThermoFisher Scientific).

RT-qPCR reduction assay

1 µg of total RNA was subjected to enzymatic decapping followed by Xrn1-mediated 5’ monophosphate degradation. For in vivo samples with low RNA quantity, the extracted RNA was spiked into 1 µg total RNA from mock McA-RH7777.hi cells. For 5’ ppp dephosphorylation, RNA was treated with Rpp (Bioresearch Technologies) at 1 U/µL in 20 µl of 1×Rpp reaction buffer for 30 min at 37 °C. For 5’ FAD decapping, RNA was treated with AtNUDX23 at 1 µM in 50 µl of 1×Nudix reaction buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 5 mM MgCl2) supplemented with 1 mM DTT for 30 min at 37 °C. For decapping of a broader range of 5’ non-canonical caps, including 5’ NAD, RNA was treated with either NudC or SpRai1 at 1 µM in 50 µl of 1×Nudix reaction buffer supplemented with 1 mM DTT for 30 min at 37 °C. For the specific degradation of 5’ monophosphorylated RNA resulting from enzymatic decapping, RNA was treated with Xrn1 (NEB) at 1 U/µL in 20 µL of 1xNEB buffer 3 supplemented with Murine RNase Inhibitor (NEB) at 1 U/µL for 60 min at 37 °C. All enzymatic treatments were followed by RNA purification using RNA Clean & Concentrator Kit-5 (Zymo Research), according to manufacturer’s instructions. All samples in each assay were incubated in the reaction buffers in parallel, with or without addition of the specified enzyme.

Quantification of viral RNA and housekeeping gene RPS11 RNA was performed using RT-qPCR primers and probes listed in Table S1. RT-qPCR were performed in 20 µL reactions with 1xTaqMan Fast Virus 1-Step Master Mix (Applied Biosystems), 0.5 μM primers and probes and 2 µL of purified RNA from enzymatic reactions. Thermal cycling was performed as follows: (1) 10 min at 55 °C; (2) 95 °C for 3 min; (3) 45 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 58 °C for 30 s; (4) 40 °C for 1 min.

NADzymeID and CapZyme RT-qPCR

For the NADzymeID 5’ NAD decapping, RNA was first treated with ADPRC (Sigma-Aldrich) at 1 µM in 20 µL of 1x ADPRC reaction buffer (50 mM HEPES (pH7) and 5 mM MgCl2) supplemented with Murine RNase Inhibitor (NEB) at 1 U/µL for 45 min at 37 °C. Subsequentially, ADPRC-treated RNA was subjected to 1 µM of sisNUDT5 in 50 µL of 1×Nudix reaction buffer supplemented with 1 mM DTT for 30 min at 37 °C. RT-qPCR reaction setup and thermal cycling conditions were performed as described above for RT-qPCR reduction assay.

ADPRC-mediated biotinylation and streptavidin pull-down

Purification of 5’ NAD RNA was done as described previously41. Briefly, 1 µg of total RNA was treated with mRNA decapping enzyme (MDE; New England Biolabs), according to manufacturer’s instructions, to decrease non-specific enrichment of m7GpppN-capped RNAs. For control reactions, 0.1 ng of 5’ NCIN capped IVT RNA was spiked into 1 µg total RNA prior to MDE treatment. m7GpppN-cap depleted RNA was then incubated with 100 mM N-[2-(2-hydroxyethoxy)ethyl]biotinamide (HEEB) and ADPRC (800 µg/mL) in 10 µL of ADPRC reaction buffer (50 mM Na-HEPES pH 7.0, 5 mM MgCl2) at 37 °C for 1 h. RNA was extracted with TRIzol followed by purification with RNA Clean & Concentrator Kit-5 (Zymo Research, R1013). 100 µL of C1 streptavidin Dynabeads (ThermoFisher Scientific) was washed three times with 300 µL LiCl hybridization buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 1 mM EDTA, 500 mM LiCl, 1% triton X-100, 0.2% SDS, 0.1% sodium deoxycholate) and incubated with the purified RNA in 200 µL of LiCl hybridization buffer for 30 min at 37 °C, shaking 300 rpm. Magnetic beads were washed three times with 250 µL of low stringency buffer (1x SSPE, 0.1% SDS, 1% NP-40, 4 M urea) at 58 °C followed by three washes with high stringency buffer (0.1x SSPE, 0.1% SDS, 1% NP-40, 4 M urea) at 58 °C. For RNA elution, beads were washed twice with 100 µL of proteinase K buffer (100 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 50 mM NaCl, 10 mM EDTA) and incubated in proteinase K buffer supplemented with 40 µg of proteinase K (NEB) for 60 min at 37 °C. Eluted RNA was purified with RNA Clean & Concentrator Kit-5 (Zymo Research, R1013)

Quantification of viral RNA was performed using RT–qPCR with TS-O-01528, TS-O-01529 and TS-O-01530 oligos recognizing HCV RNA; TS-O-00176, TS-O-00177 and TS-O-00178 oligos recognizing RPS11 mRNA; TS-O-01544, TS-O-01545 and TS-O-01546 oligos recognizing NrHV RNA; or TS-O-01516, TS-O-01517 and TS-O-01518 recognizing rat RPS11 mRNA (Table S1). The RT–qPCR reactions were assembled using TaqMan Fast Virus 1-Step Master Mix (Applied Biosystems 4444432) and thermal cycling was performed with the following conditions: 55 °C for 10 min; 95 °C for 3 min; 45 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, and 58 °C for 30 s; and 40 °C for 1 min.

Strand-specific NrHV-1(–) RNA quantification was performed using two-step RT-qPCR. The RT reaction was assembled in 1x Tth DNA polymerase buffer (400 ng purified RNA, 1.25 mM MgCl2, 0.5 mM dNTPs, 1.25 mM MnSO4, 0.5 µM internally tagged TS-O-01794 primer) and was incubated for 2 min at 100 °C followed by primer annealing for 5 min at 65 °C. Temperature was increased to 70 °C, and 1U of MasterAmp Tth DNA polymerase (LCG Biosearch Technologies) was added to the mix. The reaction mix was incubated 20 min at 70 °C, and the RT activity was inhibited with 1 mM EGTA pre-heated at 70 °C. qPCR was performed with TS-O-01795, TS-O-01796 and TS-O-01797 recognizing NrHV-1 and TaqMan Fast Virus 1-Step Master Mix (Applied Biosystems 4444432) as described above.

Metabolite-precursor depletion replication assay

To prepare metabolite-precursor depleted cell culture medium (dDMEM), DMEM without glucose, cystine and vitamins powder (D9800-02D, US Biological) was dissolved in Milli-Q water and supplemented with 63 mg/L L-cystine-2HCl, 4 mg/L choline chloride, 4 mg/L folic acid, 7.2 mg/L, inositol, 4 mg/L pyridoxal-HCl, 4 mg/L, thiamine-HCl, 3.7 g/L sodium bicarbonate and 4.5 g/L D-glucose (Sigma–Aldrich). To prepare vitamin specific-depleted medium, dDMEM was supplemented with 4 mg/L pantothenic acid hemicalcium salt, 4 mg/L nicotinamide (Sigma–Aldrich) or 0.4 mg/L riboflavin (Sigma–Aldrich) as indicated. Vitamin-depleted FBS was prepared by dialysis of FBS (dFBS) against 1.2 M NaCl at 4 °C for 96 h in a 3.5 MWCO Slide-A-Lyzer dialysis cassette (ThermoFischer Scientific).

For NrHV-1 and HCV replication assays, 5×104 McA-RH7777.hi or Huh7.5 cells, respectively, were seeded into 6-well plates and incubated 24 h at 37 °C, 5% CO2. Cells were washed twice with PBS and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C, 5% CO2 in either vitamin-reconstituted dDMEM + 10% FBS, or riboflavin-, niacinamide- or pantothenic acid-depleted dDMEM + 10% dFBS. For transfection, 1 µg of specified IVT RNA and 1 µL Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) were mixed in 100 µL dDMEM, incubated for 20 min at room temperature and diluted with 900 µL dDMEM + 3% dFBS. Cells were transfected for 4 h at 37 °C, 5% CO2. Transfection media were replaced by either vitamin-reconstituted dDMEM + 10% FBS, or riboflavin-, niacinamide-, or pantothenic acid-depleted dDMEM + 10% dFBS. Riboflavin-, niacinamide-, and pantothenic acid-depleted cells were supplemented with 10 µM FAD, NAD+ or dpCoA, respectively. Transfected cells were incubated at 37 °C, 5% CO2 for 24, 48 or 72 h and then lysed for luciferase measurements with the Luciferase Assay System (Promega), following manufacturer’s instructions. Luciferase signal was measured on a CLARIOStar Plus microplate reader.

NAD+ cycling assay quantification

NAD+ levels were determined using an enzymatic cycling assay, as described previously89. In brief, NAD+ was extracted by lysing cell pellets or liver tissue (15–20 mg) in 0.6 M perchloric acid (PCA). Lysates, cleared by centrifugation (5 min 10,000 × g, 4 °C), were neutralized, diluted (700-fold for cells and 1000-fold for tissue extracts) in 100 mM Na2HPO4 buffer pH 8) and further used in the enzymatic reaction. A serial dilution of NAD+ standard was measured together with the samples, and the resulting calibration curve was used to determine NAD+ concentration in the samples. Protein precipitates from the PCA extracts were solubilized by heating in 0.2 M NaOH (95 °C, 10 min) and their protein content was measured with bicinchoninic acid assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific #23227).

Inhibitor assays

For NrHV-1 assays, cells were infected with a NrHV-1-HiBiT reporter virus from stock titrated for optimal luciferase quantification 48 h post infection. The NrHV-1-HiBiT reporter virus contains the HiBiT tag inserted into the NS5A region90. For Sindbis virus (SINV) assays, we generated infectious stock using the pToto1101/SINV-Luc plasmid91, which was linearized with ScaI and used as template to generate m7GpppN-capped RNA using the mMESSAGE mMACHINE SP6 Transcription kit (Invitrogen, AM1340). The resulting in vitro transcribed RNAs were treated with Turbo DNase I and purified with RNA Clean & Concentrator Kit-25 (Zymo Research, R1017). 3 × 105 Huh7.5 cells were seeded into 6-well plates and incubated 24 h at 37 °C, 5% CO2. Cells were transfected with a mix of 1 µg of purified pToto1101/SINV-Luc RNA and 1 µL of Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen 11668027) in DMEM + 10% FBS + 1% P/S. Supernatants were collected 24 h post transfection and this SINV-luc stock was titrated for optimal luciferase expression 48 h post infection.

For inhibitor assays, 6,750 McA-RH7777.hi cells were seeded per well into laminin-coated 96-well plates and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C, 5% CO2. Cells were washed with PBS and incubated 24 h at 37 °C, 5% CO2 with DMEM + 10% FBS + 1% P/S containing relevant dilutions of FK866 inhibitor (0.3125 nM to 20 nM) (Sigma–Aldrich)43,44 or DMSO, and with or without addition of 10 µM of NAD+, ADPR or cADPR. Cells were infected with either NrHV-1 or SINV-Luc stocks diluted in DMEM + 10% FBS + 1% P/S containing relevant dilutions of FK866 inhibitor (0.3125 nM to 20 nM) (Sigma–Aldrich)43,44 or DMSO, and with or without addition of 10 µM of NAD+, ADPR or cADPR. Cells were incubated at 37 °C, 5% CO2 for 48 h. For NrHV-1-HiBiT infected cells, luciferase expression was determined with Nano-Glo HiBiT Lytic Detection System on a CLARIOStar Plus microplate reader, following the manufacturer’s instructions. For SINV-Luc infected cells, luciferase expression in cell lysates was measured using the Luciferase Assay System (Promega E1500) on a CLARIOStar Plus microplate reader, the following manufacturer’s instructions.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was determined for FK866 treated and metabolite precursor-depleted cells using XTT reduction as a marker of mitochondrial function. For FK866 assays, 6,750 McA-RH7777.hi cells were seeded per well into laminin-coated 96-well plates and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C, 5% CO2. Cells were washed with PBS and incubated 24 h at 37 °C, 5% CO2 with DMEM + 10% FBS + 1% P/S containing relevant dilutions of FK866 inhibitor (0.3125 nM to 20 nM) (Sigma–Aldrich)43,44 or DMSO. Fresh media DMEM + 10% FBS + 1% P/S containing relevant dilutions of FK866 inhibitor (0.3125 nM to 20 nM) (Sigma–Aldrich)43,44 or DMSO were added and cells incubated at 37 °C, 5% CO2 for 48 h. For metabolite precursor-depletion assays, 104 McA-RH7777.hi or Huh7.5 cells were seeded per well into 96-well plates, coated with gelatine 0.1% for McA-RH7777.hi cells, and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C, 5% CO2. Cells were washed twice with PBS and incubated for 24, 48, 72 or 96 h at 37 °C, 5% CO2 in either vitamin-reconstituted dDMEM + 10% FBS, or riboflavin-, niacinamide- or pantothenic-depleted dDMEM + 10% dFBS with or without supplemented with 10 µM FAD, NAD+ or dpCoA, respectively.

At relevant time points, the medium was replaced with 100 µL of FluoroBrite DMEM (Gibco) containing 300 µg/mL XTT (Thermo Fisher) and 7.67 µg/mL PMS (Sigma–Aldrich). Cells were incubated with XTT solution 2 h at 37 °C, 5 % CO2, and absorbance measured at 670 and 450 nm using a plate reader.

RNA stability assays

To measure RNA stability, 6 × 106 McA-RH7777.hi cells in 400 µL cold cytomix (120 mM KCl, 0.15 mM CaCl2, 10 mM K2HPO4/KH2PO4 (pH 7.6), 25 mM HEPES (pH 7.6), 2 mM EGTA (pH 7.6), 5 mM MgCl2, pH adjusted with KOH, 2 mM ATP and 5 mM glutathione) were electroporated in triplicates with 1 µg of either 5′ NAD+, 5′ ppp- or 5′ p NrHV-1 IVT RNA. 5′ p NrHV-1 IVT RNA was derived from 5′ NAD+ and 5′-ppp treated with NudC or Rpp, respectively. Cells were electroporated using 4 mm cuvettes at infinite resistance, 270 V and 950 µF using a BioRad GenePulser Xcell (BioRad). Electroporated cells were resuspended in 15 mL DMEM + 10% FBS, and 2 mL per time point was plated in gelatin-coated 6-well plates for RNA extraction, as described in previous section. The extracted RNA was analyzed by RT–qPCR as described above.

Western blot

Total protein was collected in RIPA lysis buffer (Santa Cruz) and quantified using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific). The protein was resuspended in LDS sample buffer, subjected to denaturing electrophoresis, and transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane. The membrane was probed with rabbit anti-human RIG-I (EPR18629) (1:1,000; Abcam ab180675), mouse anti-rat RIG-I (3B10F5) (1:1,000; ThermoFisher Scientific 67556-1-IG), or the control rabbit anti-GAPDH (EPR16891) (1:10,000; Abcam ab181602). Subsequently, the blot was probed with the secondary antibody, anti-rabbit (1:10,000; Abcam ab97051) or anti-mouse (1:10,000; Cytiva NA931) conjugated with horseradish peroxidase antibody, visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ThermoFisher Scientific 34579) and imaged with the ChemiDoc Touch Imaging System (BioRad).

Intrinsic innate immune stimulation

For RIG-I knockdown, 8 × 106 McA-RH7777.hi or A549-pr(IFNβ)-GFP cells47 in 400 µl cold cytomix were first electroporated with 60 pmol of custom designed rat RIG-I siRNA (5’-GAAGCAGCAUUUGAUGAGATT-3´ sense and 5´-UCUCAUCAAAUGCUGCUUCGT-3´ antisense), or siGENOME Non-Targeting siRNA #5 (Horizon discovery D-001210-05-05), as described above. Electroporated cells were resuspended in 30 mL of DMEM + 10% FBS, plated in T-175 flasks and incubated at 37 °C, 5% CO2. Two days post first electroporation, 8 × 106 McA-RH7777.hi or A549-pr(IFNβ)-GFP cells were electroporated again with 60 pmol of the same siRNA, as described previously. Electroporated cells were resuspended in 40 mL DMEM + 10% FBS, and 2 mL plated in 6-well plates and incubated 24 h at 37 °C, 5% CO2. One day after the second siRNA electroporation, cells were transfected with indicated amounts of 5′ ppp or 5′ NAD+ NrHV-SGR-FEO GNN RNA and 1 µL of Lipofectamine 2000 in DMEM supplemented with 3% FBS for 4 h at 37 °C. Mock transfections were carried out in parallel. After removal of the transfection medium, McA-RH7777.hi or A549-pr(IFNβ)-GFP cells were incubated in DMEM + 10% FBS for 16 h or 24 h, respectively, at 37 °C, 5% CO2. McA-RH7777.hi were lysed with TRIzol for RNA extraction, followed by purification and on-column DNase I digestion using RNA Clean & Concentrator Kit-5.

To measure levels of rat innate response genes, RNA was subjected to the RT–qPCR with TS-O-2050 and TS-O-02051 primers recognizing IFNB1; TS-O-02052 and TS-O-02053 primers recognizing IFNL3; TS-O-02054 and TS-O-02055 primers recognizing IFIT1; TS-O-02024 and TS-O-02056 primers recognizing MX1, or TS-O-01516, TS-O-01517 primers recognizing RPS11 mRNA the latter primer pair used for normalization (Table S1). NS5B alignments and structure predictions

NS5B alignments (Fig. 6) were generated with Clustal Omega in Geneious Prime, and included (virus name, abbreviation, isolate, protein accession number): HCV genotype 1a, HCV 1a, H77, AAB66324; HCV genotype 2a, HCV 2a, JFH1, BAB32872; HCV genotype 3a, HCV 3a, DBN, AOW41317; HCV genotype 4a, HCV 4a, ED43, ADF97233; HCV genotype 5a, HCV 5a, SA13, AAC61696; HCV genotype 6a, HCV 6a, HK6a, AVH80664; Equine hepacivirus, EqHV, NZP1, AJF44501; Norway rat hepacivirus 1, NrHV-1, RHV-rn1, AQV09561; Norway rat hepacivirus 2, NrHV-2, E43, AIU36221; Rodent hepacivirus J, RHV-J, RMU10-3382, AGC52807; Rodent hepacivirus F, RHV-F, NLR07-oct70, AGC52814; Rodent hepacivirus E, RHV-E, RHV-339, AGJ71779; Rodent hepacivirus I, RHV-I, SAR-3, AGC52836; GB virus B, GBV-B, B, AAC54059; Bovine hepacivirus, BoHV, BovHepV_463, AKH10573; Bat hepacivirus K, BHV-K, PDB-829, AGK41004; Bat hepacivirus M, BHV-M, PDB-491, AGK41008; Bat hepacivirus L, BHV-L, PDB-112, AGK41007; Guereza hepacivirus, GHV, 1/BWC08, AGQ22075; Human pegivirus genotype 1, HPgV-1, EA, AAC55951. The phylogenetic tree was derived using PhyML 3.1 via the Phylogeny.fr platform92,93. Two Sample Logo94 was used to identify significant differences (p < 0.01) in amino acid sequence in the binding pocket regions between viruses belonging to the NAD and FAD clades based on the same multiple sequence alignment.

AlphaFold350 was used for predicting the de novo initiation structure of NrHV-1 NS5B (residues 1-583, GenBank KX905133) bound to the NrHV-1 ( + )RNA 3’ end template (5’-AACU), NAD+, two Mg2+ ions, and an incoming GTP. Ten predictions were made using the same input but with different seeds to assess the variability of predictions (Table S3). For the best model in each prediction, the per-residue measure of local confidence (pLDDT) values and predicted aligned error (PAE) plot were inspected (Figs. S11–12). PAE viewer was used for visualization of PAE plots95. In seven predictions, the models had very high confidence (pLDDT > 90%) in the whole adenosine part of NAD+ (Figs. S11–12), and these models were included in the further analysis, whereas the three predicted models 2, 3, and 8 with lower pLDDT values in parts of adenosine (70% <pLDDT <90%) were discarded. A structural alignment was carried out for the seven models, and NrHV-1 residues within 5 Å of NAD+ were selected for further analysis. The binding pocket, in which NAD+ was predicted to bind to NrHV-1 NS5B, was expanded from these residues to also include their neighboring residues within three positions leading to definition of ten regions surrounding the binding pocket. The seven regions predicted to interact with NMN are described in the text and Fig. S10b, d. These initiation site proximal regions were named according to the dominating secondary structure and classified as being in proximity (5 Å) to the AMP and/or the nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) part of NAD+. Structural analyses of AlphaFold3 models and visualization of protein structures were carried out using the PyMOL software package96. All structural NS5B alignments were based on backbone atoms and either NrHV-1 residues 62-347 or HCV genotype 2a residues 62-350 corresponding to the least flexible parts of the finger and palm domains. Geneious Prime was used for visualization of sequences and alignments.
