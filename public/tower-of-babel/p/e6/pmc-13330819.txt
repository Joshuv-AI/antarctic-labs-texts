S-acylation switches the fate of a plant peptide precursor in cell wall damage responses
Authors: Xiaoshi Liu, Min Li, Shihui Li, Xiaolin Lu, Zhiying Wang, Jiuer Liu, Yuewen Zheng, Weixian Ye, Chi Li, Chuanliang Liu, Panpan Li, Meiqi Yuan, Hongbo Li, Xuanang Zheng, Xibao Li, Jian-Feng Li, Caiji Gao, Chengwei Yang, Jianbin Lai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Under stress conditions, organisms produce damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) to activate defense signaling pathways. Thus, precise regulation of DAMPs is essential for organismal survival, yet the underlying molecular mechanisms remain poorly understood. Plant elicitor peptides (Peps), which are processed from their precursor proteins PROPEPs, constitute a major class of DAMPs in plants. Here, we report that PROPEP1 undergoes S-acylation, a reversible lipid modification critical for its targeting to the vacuolar membrane and for its function in mediating responses to cell wall damage. Mutations at the S-acylation sites alter the trafficking route of PROPEP1 and promote its delivery to the vacuolar lumen for degradation. Upon cell wall damage, expression of PROTEIN S-ACYL TRANSFERASE 10 (PAT10) is up-regulated, thereby promoting S-acylation of PROPEP1 and ensuring its correct subcellular localization and functional role. Collectively, this study uncovers a posttranslational mechanism governing the fate of the precursor of a plant damage-associated peptide, thereby advancing our mechanistic understanding of DAMP regulation across diverse biological systems.

INTRODUCTION

When organisms are exposed to stress, they internally produce damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) to activate their defense and repair systems (1). Because DAMPs exert a profound impact on cellular survival, these molecules must be carefully sequestered under normal physiological conditions (2). However, to enable rapid stress responses, the DAMP precursors are often constitutively expressed in a standby state. Therefore, tight regulation of DAMP activation represents a conserved strategy among different species (3). For instance, immunity-related small peptides are typically synthesized as inactive precursors, such as plant elicitor peptides (Peps), and are proteolytically processed into their active forms under stress conditions (4). Nevertheless, subtle fluctuations in intracellular signals, such as the changes in calcium ion concentrations, may trigger inadvertent DAMP activation. Thus, there likely exists an intracellular regulatory factor that precisely governs the fate of DAMPs and their precursor molecules, including small peptides; however, the underlying mechanisms remain largely elusive across all organisms.

The plant cell wall, a critical structural component serving as a defensive barrier, functions as a surveillance system for cellular damage. Under environmental stress, plant cell wall integrity is compromised, leading to the release of DAMPs, such as peptides or cell wall–derived glycans, which subsequently activate downstream defense signaling pathways to enhance stress tolerance (5). The family of Peps, which are embedded in the C termini of their precursors (PROPEP1-8) in Arabidopsis, has been recognized as an important type of DAMP in response to cell wall damage (6, 7). Upon damage resulting from stresses such as pathogen infection, the increased influx of calcium into the cytosol activates type-II metacaspases to cleave PROPEPs and produce mature Peps (8, 9). The released Peps are recognized by the extracellular domains of the receptor-like kinases PEPR1 and PEPR2 on the plasma membrane of adjacent cells (10–12). The activated receptor complex, which contains PEPRs and BAK1 (13), transduces signaling via cytoplasmic kinases (14), triggering immune and defense responses, such as calcium influx, reactive oxygen species burst, and defense hormone production (15, 16). The roles of Peps in the responses to multiple stresses have been well characterized in different types of plants (17, 18).

As cellular signals detect damage, DAMPs can be passively released following membrane rupture during various forms of cell death (19). It has been demonstrated that Peps can be directly released when the cell is disrupted (9). During this process, the trafficking of precursors into distinct subcellular compartments may enable precise regulation of signaling under normal physiological conditions and facilitate a rapid transition to an activation-steady state upon cellular damage (3). Several members of the PROPEP family, such as PROPEP1, have been demonstrated to localize on the vacuole membrane of plant cells (7, 9). The absence of a transmembrane domain in PROPEP1 raises a fundamental question regarding its targeting mechanism to the vacuolar membrane and whether this specific subcellular localization is essential for its release and function.

Through bioinformatics prediction based on CSS-Palm (20), we identified PROPEP1 as a potential substrate for S-acylation, an important form of posttranslational protein lipidation. S-acylation, also named S-palmitoylation, is the process of transferring long-chain fatty acids, primarily palmitate, onto the cysteine residues of target proteins to enhance the membrane affinity, possibly mediating their subcellular localization and trafficking (21). The status of protein S-acylation is reversibly regulated by protein acyl-transferases (PATs) and de–S-acylation enzymes (22). This modification plays a critical role in various biological processes during development and stress responses in plants (23). For instance, dynamic S-acylation has been documented to regulate endocytosis to maintain the balance of plant development and immunity (24); the PAT10-mediated S-acylation modulates plant growth and salt tolerance (25, 26). However, the role of this posttranslational modification in the regulation of peptide precursors remains unclear even in mammalian cells.

Here, we found that in response to cell wall damage, PAT10-mediated S-acylation of PROPEP1 is up-regulated in plant cells, redirecting its trafficking to the vacuolar membrane and protecting it from degradation in the vacuole, thereby enhancing resistance to cell wall damage. This study addresses the critical question of how DAMP components are precisely and rapidly regulated, providing an answer at the level of posttranslational modifications.

RESULTS

S-acylation mediates the localization of PROPEP1 to the vacuolar membrane

Previous studies have demonstrated that several members of the Arabidopsis PROPEP family, including the representative PROPEP1, are localized on the vacuolar membrane (7, 9). Therefore, understanding the mechanism behind the vacuolar membrane association of PROPEP1 may help establish a potential link between this localization and the precise control of Pep signaling. Through prediction using CSS-Palm software (20), we have identified PROPEP1 as a putative substrate of S-acylation, which typically mediates the membrane association and trafficking of target proteins. Consequently, we initially investigated the effect of 2-bromopalmitate (2-BP), an S-acylation inhibitor (27), on the subcellular localization of PROPEP1. Compared to the control samples, in which green fluorescent protein (GFP)–PROPEP1 was localized to the membrane of multiple vacuoles as previously described (7, 9), the treatment of 2-BP resulted in mislocalization of GFP-PROPEP1 in both protoplasts and the intact root cells of transgenic plants (Fig. 1, A and B). This observation supports the notion that S-acylation may play a role in regulating PROPEP1 localization.

Using a biotin-switch assay (28), it was demonstrated that GFP-PROPEP1 undergoes S-acylation in plant cells (Fig. 1C). According to the prediction, the C19 and C30 residues, which reside in the N terminus of PROPEP1, were identified as its potential S-acylation sites. Mutations of either of these two cysteine residues to serine residues resulted in a reduction of the S-acylation level of PROPEP1; when both sites were mutated, its S-acylation was almost undetectable (Fig. 1, C and D), indicating that these two cysteine residues are the primary S-acylation sites on PROPEP1. Most members of the Arabidopsis PROPEP family process cysteine residues in their N-terminal regions (fig. S1A). PROPEP6, situated on the vacuolar membrane (7), was also S-acylated, but PROPEP3, located in the cytoplasm (7), did not undergo S-acylation (fig. S1, B to D), supporting the connection between S-acylation and the localization of PROPEPs. In protoplasts, both single and double site mutations altered the distribution of GFP-PROPEP1 from the vacuolar membrane to cytosolic foci (Fig. 2A). Similarly, in intact root cells of seedlings, the C19S or C30S mutation had a partial impact on the localization of GFP-PROPEP1, while the double site mutation led to its complete spread into cytosolic regions (Fig. 2B).

The C19,30S variant of GFP-PROPEP1 exhibited colocalization with the endocytosis marker FM4-64 in root cells (Fig. 2C). To further elucidate the impact of S-acylation on the subcellular distribution of PROPEP1, its colocalization with organelle markers was analyzed. The wild-type (WT) PROPEP1-GFP was found to colocalize with the vacuolar membrane marker mCherry-VAMP711 (29); however, the C19,30S variant of PROPEP1 demonstrated colocalization with the trans-Golgi network/early endosome (TGN/EE) marker RFP-VHAa1 and the prevacuolar compartment (PVC) marker RFP-Rha1 (Fig. 2D and fig. S2A) (30). Furthermore, in contrast to the WT PROPEP1, the localization of the C19,30S variant exhibited sensitivity to brefeldin A (BFA), an inhibitor of protein trafficking between the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and the Golgi apparatus (Fig. 2E). There are several routes for protein trafficking from the ER to the vacuole in plants (31); to determine the transport pathway for PROPEP1 targeting to the vacuolar membrane, we analyzed the localization of PROPEP1-GFP in two specific mutants: in the δ-subunit of Adaptor Protein-3 (AP-3) mutant (pat4-2), which exhibits disrupted direct transport from the ER to the vacuole (32), and in zigzag mutant (zig-1), which displays defective trafficking between the TGN and the PVC/vacuoles (33). In comparison with WT and zig-1 mutant, the distribution of PROPEP1-GFP in the pat4-2 mutant was shifted from predominantly localizing at the vacuolar membrane to forming puncta (Fig. 2F). These findings indicate that S-acylation facilitates the direct targeting of PROPEP1 to the vacuolar membrane, and the impairment in this modification leads to its localization to other subcellular compartments.

S-acylation contributes to PROPEP1’s function in cell wall damage responses

Danger peptide signaling mediated by Peps has been reported to be responsive to cell wall damage (34), which occurs during pathogen attacks and other stress conditions. If S-acylation contributes to cell fate determination during cell wall damage, its modification level may dynamically adjust in response to this form of cellular stress. To simulate the disruption of cell wall damage, we treated the seedlings with the cellulose biosynthesis inhibitor isoxaben (ISX) (34). Further biotin-switch assay data indicated that the S-acylation level of PROPEP1 was significantly up-regulated upon ISX treatment relative to normal conditions (Fig. 3, A and B). Because ISX did not significantly alter total PROPEP1-GFP protein levels (fig. S3, A and B), these findings support the notion that cell wall damage specifically enhances PROPEP1 S-acylation. Because environmental stresses may lead to cell wall damage, and the Pep signaling has been demonstrated to play a role in salt tolerance (35, 36), we further examined the dynamics of PROPEP1 S-acylation under high-salinity stress. Consistently, the S-acylation level of PROPEP1 was also elevated in response to high-salt treatment (Fig. 3, C and D).

If the vacuolar membrane localization mediated by S-acylation is essential for the functional regulation of PROPEP1, any defect in this modification would disrupt the role of this Pep precursor in cell wall damage responses. With eight members in the Arabidopsis PROPEP family (7), overexpression is commonly used to study their functions and exclude functional redundancy (6, 16). Therefore, we generated transgenic Arabidopsis plants overexpressing either the WT or C19,30S version of untagged PROPEP1, and individual lines with similar levels of PROPEP1 transcripts were selected for further analysis (fig. S4A). Under control conditions, both WT and mutant versions of PROPEP1 overexpressing plants exhibited a similar phenotype to untransgenic control plants. However, when exposed to ISX, seedlings overexpressing the WT PROPEP1 showed greater resistance to ISX compared to untransgenic control seedlings and seedlings overexpressing the C19,30S variant of PROPEP1. The greening rates and Fv/Fm (variable fluorescence/maximum fluorescence) values supported the idea that overexpression of PROPEP1 enhances resistance to cell wall damage, and S-acylation is crucial for its function in this process (Fig. 4, A to C; the greening rates over time are presented in fig. S5).

Previous studies have demonstrated that Peps induces the expression of defense genes, such as Plant Defensin 1.2 (PDF1.2), in plant cells (6, 15, 16). Therefore, we conducted further analysis to investigate the effect of PROPEP1 and its S-acylation on these signaling components in response to ISX. The reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) data supported the finding that the overexpression of the WT PROPEP1, but not the C19,30S version, resulted in a significantly higher increase in PDF1.2 expression during ISX treatment (Fig. 4D). Furthermore, we investigated the role of S-acylation in modulating PROPEP1 function under salt stress. The results indicated that seedlings overexpressing WT PROPEP1 exhibited greater salt tolerance compared to those overexpressing the C19,30S variant of PROPEP1 and untransgenic control seedlings (Fig. 4E). These findings suggest that S-acylation is essential for the functionality of PROPEP1 in regulating downstream signaling following cell wall damage.

S-acylation redirects the targeting of PROPEP1 to modulate its protein stability

Given the data presented above, it is evident that S-acylation plays a crucial role in both the localization and function of PROPEP1; thus, it is important to elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying this process. As PROPEP1 undergoes cleavage by calcium-activated metacaspases to generate mature Pep1 for release into extracellular regions (8, 9), it is essential to investigate the impact of S-acylation on the cleavage of PROPEP1 and release of Pep1. First, we conducted a comparison of the cleavage rates between the WT and C19,30S versions of PROPEP1-GFP in protoplasts using the previously described approach (8). The results indicated that the mutation of S-acylation sites did not significantly affect the generation of Pep1-GFP, suggesting that this modification does not regulate the cleavage of the precursor (fig. S6, A and B). Further fractionation revealed a significantly higher Pep1/PROPEP1 ratio in the membrane fraction of PROPEP1(C19,30S)-GFP than in PROPEP1(WT)-GFP plants (fig. S7, A to C), suggesting that S-acylation–deficient PROPEP1 may be processed in intracellular vesicles, impairing Pep1 release into the extracellular space. Therefore, we examined the release of Pep1-GFP in transgenic seedlings expressing either of these two versions of PROPEP1-GFP with or without ISX treatment. In the absence of ISX, Pep1-GFP was not detectable in the medium. However, when seedlings expressing the WT PROPEP1-GFP were grown with ISX, it was detected that Pep1-GFP accumulated in the medium, but the C19,30S mutation significantly attenuated this accumulation (Fig. 5, A and B, and fig. S8, A and B). This evidence supports the idea that S-acylation enhances the release of Pep1 into extracellular regions.

Therefore, S-acylation may govern the subsequent fate of PROPEP1 prior to its proteolytic cleavage. Because WT PROPEP1 localizes to the vacuolar membrane, whereas the C19,30S variant is delivered to the TGN/EE and PVC, we questioned whether both proteins are associated with vacuolar targeting. Thus, we treated seedlings expressing either the WT or the C19,30S variant of PROPEP1-GFP with concanamycin A (Conc A), a specific inhibitor of vacuolar H+–adenosine triphosphatase (V-ATPase) known to suppress vacuolar protein degradation (29), under both normal and ISX treatment conditions. As a result, a weak GFP signal was detected in the vacuole of root cells expressing the WT PROPEP1-GFP, suggesting that a subset of WT PROPEP1 is delivered to the vacuolar lumen for degradation. Notably, treatment with Conc A induced a dramatic increase of GFP fluorescent puncta in the vacuoles of seedlings expressing the C19,30S variant of PROPEP1-GFP; this effect was further enhanced upon cotreatment with both Conc A and ISX (Fig. 5, C and D). The localization of these GFP puncta to the vacuolar lumen in root cells was further confirmed in seedlings coexpressing the C19,30S PROPEP1-GFP with mCherry-VAMP711 (29), a marker of the vacuolar membrane (Fig. 5E and fig. S2B). This finding supports the notion that un–S-acylated PROPEP1 is delivered into the vacuole for degradation.

Following this conclusion, S-acylation contributes to the stability of PROPEP1 protein, which is subject to vacuole-dependent degradation. Consistently, in transgenic lines exhibiting comparable PROPEP1 transcript levels (Fig. 6A), the steady-state protein level of WT PROPEP1-GFP was higher than that of its C19,30S mutant variant (Fig. 6B). Upon treatment with Conc A, the protein accumulation of the C19,30S PROPEP1-GFP variant increased significantly (Fig. 6, B and C). To further corroborate this observation, we selected a transgenic line expressing elevated levels of the C19,30S PROPEP1 transcript (Fig. 6D), thereby achieving comparable basal protein levels of WT and C19,30S versions of PROPEP1-GFP under normal conditions. Consistently, Conc A treatment again induced pronounced accumulation of the C19,30S variant, resulting in its higher accumulation than the WT version of PROPEP1 (Fig. 6, E and F). Collectively, these results demonstrate that the S-acylation deficient form of PROPEP1 is targeted to the vacuole lumen for sequential degradation, thereby reducing Pep1 release in downstream responses.

PAT10 catalyzes the S-acylation of PROPEP1 in response to cell wall damage

Given that S-acylation is catalyzed by PATs, characterizing the specific PAT(s) responsible for the PROPEP1 S-acylation would enhance our understanding of the regulatory mechanism involved in this process. Twenty-four PATs exist in Arabidopsis, and most of them are localized to the plasma membrane, Golgi apparatus, or ER (37), but PAT10 has been found on the membrane of vacuoles (25, 26), making it a potential candidate for facilitating the S-acylation of PROPEP1. Given that the above results indicate that ISX treatment enhances the S-acylation of PROPEP1, we further measured PAT10 transcript levels in seedlings following ISX treatment. RT-qPCR analysis revealed that PAT10 transcript levels were up-regulated under ISX treatment relative to normal growth conditions (Fig. 7A). The biotin-switch assay indicated that the S-acylation level of PROPEP1 was significantly lower in the pat10-1 mutant, a well-characterized transferred DNA insertion mutant of PAT10 in the previous study (25), compared to WT cells (Fig. 7B). Consistently, PAT10 expression was also increased under high-salinity treatment (fig. S9), supporting our conclusion that PROPEP1 S-acylation is elevated in response to salt stress (Fig. 3, C and D). Furthermore, coimmunoprecipitation (co-IP) assays revealed that PROPEP1 physically interacts with PAT10 in plant cells (Fig. 7C). In contrast, PROPEP3 did not interact with PAT10 (fig. S10), accounting for its absence of S-acylation in plant cells. These findings support a functional association between PAT10 and the S-acylation of PROPEP1 in planta.

When expressed in protoplasts derived from pat10-1, GFP-PROPEP1 was observed to be concentrated in cytoplasmic puncta (Fig. 7D), similar to the effect seen with the S-acylation site mutation of PROPEP1. In comparison, while PAT11 is also localized to the vacuolar membrane (37), our finding indicated that the localization of PROPEP1 was not altered in the cells from the PAT11 mutants (fig. S11, A to C), suggesting the functional specificity for PAT10 in this process. In addition, GFP-PROPEP1 was found to be distributed in the cytosolic region in intact cells of transgenic seedling roots with a pat10-1 background (Fig. 7E). To confirm that the subcellular location of GFP-PROPEP1 in pat10-1 is dependent on S-acylation, we coexpressed mCherry-PROPEP1(WT) and GFP-PROPEP1(C19,30S) in the WT or pat10-1 protoplasts. As a result, the mCherry and GFP signals nearly completely overlapped in the pat10-1 cells, but only minimal overlap was observed in the WT cells (Fig. 7F), supporting the conclusion that the localization alternation of PROPEP1 in pat10-1 is attributable to a defect in S-acylation. These findings provide support for PAT10’s role as the enzyme responsible for the S-acylation–mediated trafficking of PROPEP1.

To determine whether the protein stability of PROPEP1 in the pat10-1 mutant mirrors that observed for the S-acylation defective PROPEP1, we examined WT PROPEP1-GFP accumulation in WT and pat10-1 seedlings following treatment with ISX and Conc A. As a result, the accumulation of PROPEP1-GFP in the vacuolar lumen of root cells was markedly increased in the pat10-1 mutant (Fig. 8A), similar with the effect of S-acylation site mutations. Consistently, PROPEP1 protein levels were notably diminished in the pat10-1 mutant relative to WT, despite comparable PROPEP1 transcript abundance in both genetic backgrounds (Fig. 8, B and C). Given the essential role of S-acylation for the function of PROPEP1 in cell wall damage responses, we conducted a further investigation into the ISX sensitivity of pat10-1 plants. The result indicated that, under ISX treatment, pat10-1 seedlings exhibited a significantly lower greening rate compared to both WT and complementation plants (Fig. 8D and fig. S4B). Consistently, ISX-induced PDF1.2 expression was significantly higher in WT plants than in pat10-1 mutant plants (fig. S12). These results suggest that PAT10 is involved in regulating cell wall damage responses through the S-acylation of PROPEP1 in plant cells.

DISCUSSION

In eukaryotic cells, DAMPs potently activate defense responses but may also inadvertently trigger cell death. Consequently, their activity must be tightly controlled to prevent spurious activation under homeostatic conditions, yet rapidly mobilized upon perception of cellular damage signals (38). In this study, we uncover a regulatory mechanism based on posttranslational modification, which determines the fate of a plant DAMP precursor in cell wall damage responses, offering an elegant solution to this fundamental biological paradox.

To achieve precise control of DAMPs, these signals are subject to multilayered regulation. For instance, in human cells, the expression of genes involved in DAMP responses is usually induced by damage-associated stimuli (39). In plant cells, multiple PROPEP genes are rapidly up-regulated upon cell wall damage (34). Subsequent release of active Peps from their precursors requires proteolytic cleavage mediated by calcium-activated metacaspases (8, 9). However, because PROPEP expression and intracellular calcium levels can be modulated during numerous physiological processes unrelated to damage, to ensure signal fidelity and prevent inappropriate activation, plant cells likely use a sophisticated lock mechanism that introduces an additional checkpoint between transcriptional induction and proteolytic activation. Previous studies have demonstrated that multiple PROPEP members in Arabidopsis, including PROPEP1 and PROPEP6, localize to the vacuolar membrane (7). Vacuolar membrane–associated proteins are typically synthesized on the ER and subsequently undergo distinct trafficking pathways (40). Our findings demonstrate that the trafficking and stability of precursor proteins constitute a dynamic regulatory node for achieving such fine-tuned control.

S-acylation, a reversible posttranslational modification, may represent a plausible candidate modulation strategy for this purpose. The attached fatty acyl chains enhance substrate hydrophobicity and govern its preferential partitioning into specific membrane microdomains (41). For instance, the subcellular trafficking of human RAS protein is strictly dependent on its S-acylation status (42); in plants, dynamic S-acylation regulates the shuttling of BON1 between the plasma membrane and endosomes (24). Our results showed that S-acylation plays a crucial role in directing the trafficking of PROPEP1 to the vacuolar membrane through an AP-3–dependent pathway. Notably, mutation of the S-acylation sites redirects PROPEP1 to the TGN/EE and PVC, ultimately delivering it to the vacuolar lumen for degradation. These results support the notion that S-acylation governs the intracellular trafficking route and final destination of PROPEP1. The current observation aligns with the established degradation mechanisms for membrane proteins in the vacuoles and lysosomes of eukaryotic cells (43), but the regulation of peptides via this pathway remains poorly understood. Furthermore, mature Pep1, generated from S-acylated PROPEP1, is released from the vacuolar membrane into the cytosol. Upon cell wall damage, cellular disruption leads to the release of cytosolic Peps, which are subsequently recognized by neighboring cells to amplify defense signaling (9). In response to cell wall damage, PROPEP1 is cleaved to release Pep1, while enhanced S-acylation, which protects PROPEP1 from vacuolar degradation, may serve to maintain its homeostasis in plant cells.

When cell wall damage occurs, the expression of PAT10 is increased, resulting in the up-regulation of the S-acylation and consequential targeting to the vacuolar membrane. Phenotypic analyses support the function of PAT10-mediated S-acylation of PROPEP1 in the plant resistance to cell wall damage. The colocalization of the S-acylation defective PROPEP1 with the markers prior to vacuolar targeting aligns with previous studies regarding the determination of trafficking route of CBLs mediated by PAT10 (25, 44). Because unmodified PROPEP1 is further delivered to the vacuole, its subcellular destination appears distinct from that of unmodified CBLs. Notably, our results revealed that S-acylation of PROPEP1 contributes to the response to salt stress; because both PAT10 and Pep1 have been reported to be involved in salt tolerance (25, 35), the current study established a connection between these two factors in a signaling module. Because S-acylation of PROPEP1 was markedly reduced but not completely abolished in the pat10-1 mutant, it would be of interest to identify additional PAT(s) potentially involved in this modification. Given the reversible nature of S-acylation (22), it would be valuable to explore the de–S-acylation process of PROPEP1. In addition, PROPEP6 also undergoes S-acylation for its localization on the vacuole membrane (fig. S1, E and F); however, PROPEP3, which is not directed to vacuoles (7), does not exhibit S-acylation. Given that PROPEP3 does not interact with PAT10, the differential S-acylation patterns observed among PROPEP family members are likely governed by structural specificity. Consequently, it is imperative to investigate the conservation and variation mechanisms of S-acylation of members in Arabidopsis and other species. Because SUMOylation plays a role in regulating the cleavage of PROPEPs (45, 46), it is also interesting to analyze the interplay among various modifications.

As a model, unmodified PROPEP1 is delivered to the vacuole for degradation to limit Pep signaling, whereas S-acylation targets PROPEP1 to the vacuolar membrane to maintain its essential level under normal physiological conditions. In response to cell wall damage, expression of PAT10 is induced, leading to enhanced S-acylation of PROPEP1. This S-acylation directs PROPEP1 trafficking to the vacuolar membrane via an AP-3–dependent pathway. Upon cell wall damage, metacaspases are activated and cleave PROPEP1, releasing mature Pep1 into the cytosol. Subsequently, cytosolic Pep1 is released to extracellular regions upon cellular disruption and perceived by PEPRs in neighboring cells, thereby triggering defense signaling (Fig. 9). Given the well-established roles of DAMP peptides in mediating responses diverse stresses, this study holds substantial promise for improving crop resistance. Moreover, because the regulatory mechanisms of DAMP peptides remain poorly understood, even in mammalian systems, our findings provide valuable insights with implications for future research across diverse species.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant materials and growth conditions

The Arabidopsis thaliana (Columbia) seeds were sterilized and stratified on Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium with 1.5% (w/v) sucrose and 0.75% (w/v) agar in the dark for 2 days at 4°C, and then moved to be grown under a long-day condition (16-hour light/8-hour dark) at 22°C. After 7 days, the seedlings were transferred to soil for further growth. The pat10-1 (25), pat4-2 (32), and zig-1 (33) seeds have been previously characterized and described. The pat11-1 (SALK_022544) and pat11-2 (SALK_026092) seeds were obtained from The Nottingham Arabidopsis Stock Centre.

Plasmid construction and transgenic plant generation

For expressing GFP-PROPEP1 or PROPEP1-GFP in protoplasts and transgenic plants, PROPEP1 was clone into the pCambia-35S:GFP vector. For phenotypic analysis, PROPEP1 with its stop codon was inserted into the pCambia-based vector driven by a 35S promoter. Mutation variants of PROPEP1 were obtained using site-directed mutagenesis. For expressing GFP-PROPEP1 or PROPEP1-GFP in pat10-1 mutant plants, PROPEP1 was cloned into the pCambia-35S:GFP vector. For the complementation plants of PAT10, the genomic region containing its native promoter (2000 base pairs) was cloned into the pCambia1300-221 vector and subsequently transformed into pat10-1. The transgenic plants were generated through Agrobacterium-mediated floral dipping (47), and independent T3 homozygous lines were used for analysis. The information of primers used in this study is included in table S1.

The biotin-switch assay

The assay was conducted in accordance with the previous description (48), incorporating minor modifications. Protoplasts were harvested, and proteins were extracted using lysis buffer (50 mM tris-HCl, pH 7.5; 150 mM NaCl; 0.5% Triton X-100; and 1× protease inhibitor cocktail), through incubation at 4°C for 1 hour. Following centrifugation at 20,000g for 15 min at 4°C, the supernatant was treated with 50 mM N-ethylmaleimide and incubated overnight at 4°C. Proteins were then collected via methanol-chloroform precipitation and dissolved in 200 μl of reconstitution buffer [1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), pH 7.4; 8 M urea; and 2% SDS]. The resulting suspension was divided into two tubes (100 μl each); in each tube, the sample was incubated with 50 μl of 4 mM biotin-HPDP (APExBIO), 2 μl of 100 mM EDTA, and 1 μl of 100× protease inhibitor cocktail, along with 50 μl of 2 M hydroxylamine (pH 7.4) or 2 M tris-HCl (pH 7.4), for 1 hour at room temperature. After methanol-chloroform precipitation, the proteins in each tube were dissolved in 100 μl of reconstitution buffer. Twenty microliters was saved as the input samples, and the remaining solution was mixed with 720 μl of PBS containing 0.2% Triton X-100. This diluted sample was then incubated with streptavidin-agarose beads for 1.5 hours. Following the incubation, the beads were rinsed twice with wash buffer (500 mM NaCl; 0.1%, w/v, SDS; and 1× PBS, pH 7.4) and once with 1× PBS. The proteins were subsequently eluted in a solution of 70 μl of wash buffer containing 5% β-mercaptoethanol for 20 min. The eluted proteins were then subjected to SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and immunoblotting using an anti-GFP antibody (TransGen Biotech, HT801-01) for analysis.

Confocal microscopy

To analyze the subcellular localization of PROPEP1 in protoplasts, the designated plasmid was transfected into protoplasts derived from leaves of 3-week-old Arabidopsis plants (49). Twenty-four hours later, the fluorescent signals were visualized using confocal microscopy. To observe the GFP-tagged PROPEP1 in root cells, 6-day-old transgenic seedlings were used. For ISX and Conc A treatments, subcellular localization was examined in root cells of 6-day-old PROPEP1-GFP transgenic seedlings. GFP puncta were identified in confocal micrographs using ImageJ software with the Trainable Weka Segmentation plugin for pixel-level classification. Quantification of punctum number was subsequently performed using ImageJ’s Analyze Particles plugin (50). All analyses were conducted using a Zeiss LSM 800 laser-scanning confocal microscope.

Protein-level detection

Six-day-old seedlings were treated with 1 μM Conc A in MS medium for various durations. Total proteins were extracted in 2× protein sample buffer, separated by SDS-PAGE, and analyzed by immunoblotting using anti-GFP antibody (TransGen Biotech, HT801-01) and anti-Rubisco (Biodragon, B1485).

Gene expression analysis

RNA was extracted using the HiPure Plant RNA Mini Kit (Magen) and then reverse-transcribed into cDNA with the PrimeScript RT kit (Vazyme). qPCR was performed using SYBR qPCR Mix (Tsingke) in the CFX 96 C1000 Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad).

Pep1 release detection

The 6-day-old seedlings expressing the WT or C19,30S version of PROPEP1-GFP were treated in the MS medium containing protease inhibitor cocktails, with or without 300 nM ISX for 16 hours. Proteins from the intact seedlings were saved as input samples. The medium was collected and incubated with GFP agarose beads for 2 hours at 4°C. After being rinsed three times with wash buffer (20 mM tris-HCl, pH 7.4; 100 mM NaCl; 10% glycerol; and 0.02% NP-40), the beads were mixed with 2× protein sample buffer containing 2.5% β-mercaptoethanol to elute proteins for anti-GFP immunoblotting.

The ISX tolerance assay

Seedlings were cultivated in the MS medium containing the specified concentration of ISX (34). Photographs were captured at designated time points, and greening rates were calculated. Chlorophyll fluorescence (Fv/Fm) was assessed using a Chlorophyll Fluorescence Imaging System (Technological, Colchester, UK).

Co-IP

To analyze the interaction between PROPEP1 and PAT10, PAT10-MYC was coexpressed with GFP or GFP-PROPEP1 in protoplasts. Total proteins were extracted using lysis buffer (20 mM tris-HCl, pH 7.4; 100 mM NaCl; 10% glycerol; 0.5% NP-40; and 1× protease inhibitor cocktail) for 30 min at 4°C. After centrifugation at 13,000g for 15 min at 4°C, an aliquot of the supernatant was retained as the input sample, while the remaining solution was incubated with anti-GFP Nanobody Agarose Beads (AlpaLife, KTSM1301) for 2 hours at 4°C. The beads were rinsed three times with wash buffer (20 mM tris-HCl, pH 7.4; 100 mM NaCl; 10% glycerol; and 0.05% NP-40). Last, both input and IP samples were analyzed by SDS-PAGE followed with immunoblotting using anti-GFP antibody (TransGen Biotech, HT801-01) and anti-MYC antibody (TransGen Biotech, HT101-01).

The PROPEP1 processing assay

The measurement of PROPEP1 cleavage was performed as previously described (8). PROPEP1-GFP-HA2 was constructed into the pHBT vector. The plasmids were used for protoplast transformation to express the corresponding proteins. Protoplasts were harvested 12 hours posttransfection, lysed in protein sample buffer, and denatured at 95°C for 5 min prior to SDS-PAGE and anti-hemagglutinin (HA) immunoblotting.

Cell fractionation assay

Proteins were extracted at 4°C for 30 min using a homogenization buffer composed of 50 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, and 13% sucrose, supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail. Subsequently, the lysate was centrifuged sequentially at 1000g and 3000g, each for 10 min at 4°C to collect supernatants. Further centrifugation was performed at 80,000g for 60 min at 4°C to separate soluble and pellet fractions. The resulting pellet was resuspended in an equivalent volume of the homogenization buffer. Both input and pellet fractions were subjected to SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting analysis using an anti-GFP antibody (TransGen Biotech, HT801-01).

Statistical analysis

The statistical data represent the means ± SD from three biologically independent experiments. Significance was assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test or Student’s t test (unpaired, two-tailed). A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
