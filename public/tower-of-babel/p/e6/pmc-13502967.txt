A Cost Input Estimation for City-based Implementation of Tobacco Vendor Licensing Policy in India
Authors: Hansa Kundu, Shivam Kapoor, Amit Yadav, Morgan Kreher, Kathy Wright, Ashish K Pandey, Rana J Singh
Published: 2026

INTRODUCTION

The Framework Convention on Tobacco Control (FCTC), adopted by the World Health Organization (WHO) in 2003, marks two decades of global efforts to combat the tobacco epidemic.[1] India has made significant commitments to global tobacco control by ratifying the WHO FCTC on February 5, 2004, and further became a party to the Protocol to Eliminate Illicit Trade in Tobacco Products on June 5, 2018.[2] The Protocol aims to curb all forms of illegal trade in tobacco products, which undermines public health efforts and causes substantial revenue losses to the public exchequer. Article 15 (7) of the WHO FCTC and Article 6 of the Protocol mandate each party to adopt and implement measures, including licensing, to control or regulate the production and distribution of tobacco products.[3]

Licensing-based regulation has played a vital role in controlling liquor sales globally by defining who can sell alcohol, where and when it can be sold, and under what conditions. This helps restrict sales near sensitive areas such as schools, religious places, and hospitals, while also enabling local governments to manage the density of liquor outlets within cities. A similar regulatory approach is seen in the licensing of food products and pharmacies, which is crucial for protecting public health and consumer safety.[4]

NEED FOR TOBACCO VENDOR LICENSING

The challenges associated with the existing tobacco control law in India, the Cigarettes and Other Tobacco Products Act (COTPA), 2003, are manifold. Unregulated points of sale, sale near educational institutions, and tobacco advertisements at the point of sale are some gaps in the existing law. Tobacco vendor licensing (TVL) is a regulatory policy instrument that governs the sale and distribution of tobacco products by retailers, wholesalers, and other vendors, with compliance with tobacco control laws serving as a key eligibility criterion for licensing. After adoption of the TVL policy, a reduction in the number of tobacco outlets was observed in Finland (28%), Hungary (83%), Australia (23%), San Francisco (8%), Philadelphia (20%), and New York (45%), demonstrating the efficacy of TVL as a tool to limit the overall number of retailers.[567891011] TVL also protects public health, especially by limiting access of tobacco products to vulnerable populations.[5]

In line with the WHO FCTC and the Protocol, the Government of India issued advisories in 2017 and 2018 urging states/union territories (UTs) to implement TVL.[1213] Following the advisories, ten states/UTs and over 100 cities/urban local bodies (ULBs) have adopted some form of the TVL policy.

INDIA’S “BOTTOM-UP” APPROACH TO TVL IMPLEMENTATION

India’s experience shows that tobacco control succeeds through bottom-up implementation. Measures such as the ban on loose cigarettes, the e-cigarette ban, and the adoption of Article 5.3 guidelines at the state level were first advanced subnationally before scaling nationally.[141516] However, evidence suggests that enforcement of the loose cigarette ban remains inconsistent across states and municipalities, with vendors and consumers frequently reporting limited oversight and compliance.[1718] In contrast, TVL provides an administrative framework through which vendors can be formally identified, compliance conditions can be communicated, and penalties such as suspension or cancellation of licenses can be applied for violations. Therefore, while localized adoption remains essential, strengthening regulatory mechanisms such as TVL may enhance the enforceability of existing tobacco control provisions, including restrictions on loose cigarette sales, by linking legal requirements with ongoing oversight and accountability.

The objectives of the paper are to outline the key policy activities and associated cost inputs required for city-level implementation of TVL in India and to highlight the financial feasibility of TVL by juxtaposing implementation costs with potential municipal licensing revenues.

The analysis applies a policy process framework, drawing on the stages of public policy described by Thomas Dye (2004),[19] to systematically identify TVL-related activities that require financial inputs.

Using Thomas Dye’s[19] policy stages framework, TVL implementation is conceptualized across six stages. Problem identification includes baseline surveys, vendor mapping, stakeholder consultations, and legal gap analysis. Agenda setting focuses on advocacy, media engagement, and issue briefings. Policy formulation involves drafting the TVL policy or gazette, legal consultations, review of best practices, and inter-agency coordination. Policy legitimation is achieved through gazette notification, legislative presentations, stakeholder feedback, and establishment of enforcement mechanisms. Policy implementation includes SOP development, staff training, vendor registration, license issuance, and enforcement. Policy evaluation entails compliance monitoring, post-implementation surveys, and third-party assessments. TVL remains at an early stage nationally in India, though several states and municipalities are in advanced stages. In most states, the Urban Development Department or local municipalities serve as the primary implementing agencies, supported by state/district tobacco control cells, civil society, and academic partners.

COST INPUT ANALYSIS FOR IMPLEMENTING TVL

Due to the dearth of literature around the financial estimates for implementing TVL policy in a jurisdiction, a cost input analysis [Table 1] is presented highlighting that the financial outlay required for initial phases of TVL implementation is minimal compared with the potential health benefits. Cost estimates were developed for an average Indian city with a population below 10 lakh, reflecting the scale at which municipal TVL adoption is currently occurring. Data sources included municipal budget norms, government notification and printing rates, third-party survey cost estimates, and programmatic experience from cities that have initiated or implemented TVL. Only direct, incremental costs required for policy adoption and early implementation were included. Routine enforcement activities and existing municipal resources were excluded where this formed part of regular administrative functions.

DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION

The cost analysis reveals that TVL policy entails minimal direct input costs, estimated at ₹247,317–₹317,195 per year for each city-based jurisdiction, making it a highly cost-effective intervention. Indirect costs such as IT system upgrades and awareness generation through regular municipality vans remain manageable, particularly when compared with the revenue potential of licensing. Even at the lowest end, the estimated licensing fee revenue in a city far exceeds the highest input cost required for TVL implementation. For instance, the licensing fee is fixed at ₹500 for all vendors in Mangaluru, Karnataka, whereas it is ₹1,200 for temporary kiosks, ₹4,800 for permanent kiosks, and ₹25,000 for wholesalers in Sikar, Rajasthan. Depending on the number of vendors in a city (e.g., 1,500–2,000 in Mangaluru, and 2,000–2,500 in Sikar, estimated using extrapolation of results from baseline survey s conducted by the city municipalities), the annual licensing fee revenue can range from ₹500,000 to ₹3,000,000.[2021] Thus, even the most conservative revenue estimates cover the maximum projected implementation cost, making TVL a self-sustaining and cost-effective policy intervention.[2021]

While revenue projections assume near-ideal compliance, real-world implementation may face challenges such as incomplete vendor mapping, vendor resistance, limited enforcement capacity, coordination gaps, and variable political commitment, resulting in lower initial revenues. These challenges can be mitigated through phased implementation, beginning with vendor mapping and sensitization, integration within existing municipal trade licensing systems, and clear nodal leadership with inter-departmental coordination.[22] Effective TVL implementation depends on inter-departmental coordination, political commitment, robust enforcement mechanisms, and systematic vendor mapping, in addition to financial feasibility. Municipal bodies typically act as nodal agencies, supported by State and District Tobacco Control Cells, with coordination required across health, police, food safety, and legal departments. Civil society organizations and academic institutions can also support municipalities through technical assistance, vendor mapping, compliance surveys, capacity building, and independent evaluations.

TVL is a policy-relevant and scalable instrument for strengthening tobacco control enforcement in India and advancing progress toward a tobacco endgame. By regulating vendor density, improving compliance, and generating local revenue, TVL operationalizes existing mandates under COTPA and the WHO FCTC.[23] This commentary highlights the importance of transparent and standardized cost models to inform wider adoption and invites further policy and research discourse on positioning TVL as a sustainable, system-strengthening intervention within India’s tobacco control framework.
