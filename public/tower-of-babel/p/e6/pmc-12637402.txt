Discovery of a PDE4B and PDE4D selective bifunctional degrader for management of chronic inflammatory disorders
Authors: Arvind Shakya, Qiao Liu, Chon Lai, Sherry Baker, Steven Greene, Paul Erdman, Athri Rathnayake, Angela Schoolmeesters, Connor M Blair, Elka Kyurkchieva, Navin Rajapakse, Imelda Lam, Yuan Yan Sin, George S Baillie, Shenlin Huang, Aparajita H Chourasia, Leah Fung
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAtopic dermatitis (AD) is a chronic inflammatory skin disorder characterized by redness and itching, impacting over 15 million individuals in the US. The pathogenesis of AD involves T helper 2 (TH2) and 17 (TH17) cytokines, which contribute to increased inflammation, a compromised skin barrier, and heightened susceptibility to infections and allergen sensitization. PDE4, a specific enzyme that degrades cyclic 3′,5′-adenosine monophosphate (cAMP), shows elevated expression in AD patients, maintaining a highly inflammatory environment. Inhibiting PDE4 effectively reduces pro-inflammatory cytokines in several chronic inflammatory diseases. However, current PDE4 inhibitors are often associated with significant adverse effects due to off-target activities. Recent developments in targeted protein degradation techniques have prompted interest in alternative strategies to modulate PDE4 activity with improved selectivity. Consequently, we have developed potent and selective bifunctional degraders for PDE4B/D. These degraders significantly reduce pro-inflammatory cytokine release from activated T cells in vitro, demonstrating a potency which is a thousand times greater than traditional PDE4 inhibitors. In addition, the PDE4B/D degrader (BTX-AP02) efficiently degrades PDE4D in the spleens of treated mice in a dose-dependent manner, with no significant adverse effects at dose levels used in the study. We also demonstrate the efficacy of our lead oral PDE4B/D degrader (BTX-AP04) in a mouse model of graft-versus-host disease (GvHD). Notably, our PDE4D degraders exhibit minimal degradation of known neo-substrates of cereblon (CRBN), indicating high specificity. Taken together, we propose the introduction of a more potent, specific, and safe PDE4B/D degrader as a novel treatment for inflammatory skin conditions such as AD.Keywords: Phosphodiesterases, Bifunctional degraders, Atopic dermatitis, Autoimmune disorders, Cereblon

Introduction

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is a multifactorial disease characterized by intense pruritus, skin inflammation and immune dysregulation due to both genetic and environmental factors [1]. Activation of TH2 cells and release of TH2-mediated cytokines including interleukin-4 (IL-4), IL-13, IL-31 and IL-22 in the skin is the major pathway that lead to the pathogenesis of AD in the patients [2,3]. Additionally in patients with AD, the imbalance between TH2/TH1 immune response is observed, which has been confirmed in mouse studies by the expression of IL-4 and interferon gamma (IFNγ) in skin lesions [4,5].

Management of mild to moderate AD includes topical anti-inflammatory drugs, topical corticosteroids as well as antihistamines [6,7]. For severe and refractory cases, treatment options include the use of therapeutic biologics against targets including tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), IL-4Rα, IL-13, IL-31, IL-22, IL-33, IgE etc., as well as inhibitors of JAK1/2, Tyk2 and PDE4 [[8], [9], [10]]. While these biologics, including those against TNFα, are highly effective in reducing inflammation, they are not curative and pose a significant risk associated with their usage (such as reactivation of tuberculosis and malignancy) [8,11,12]. The orally administered PDE4 inhibitor, Apremilast, was approved for psoriatic arthritis which suppresses inflammation by reducing the production of TH1, TH2 and TH17 cytokines [13,14].

Phosphodiesterase (PDE) enzymes belong to a superfamily comprising of 11 members which hydrolyze and inactivate cyclic nucleotides [15]. Members of PDE4 family (PDE4A-PDE4D) are expressed in immune cells as well as in the brain and are responsible for hydrolysis of cAMP [16]. Inhibition of PDE4 leads to accumulation of intracellular second messenger cAMP, which activates of protein kinase A (PKA) and subsequent phosphorylation and activation of cAMP-response element binding protein (CREB) [17]. Activation of this pathway leads to suppression of TNFα production leading to inhibition of other pro-inflammatory pathways [18].

While currently approved PDE4 inhibitors lead to decreased expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, they also exhibit certain adverse effects. Owing to inhibition of PDE4 in the central nervous system (CNS), PDE4 inhibitors exhibit nausea and emesis as its most common adverse effects [19]. Other adverse effects including headache, diarrhea, fatigue, nasopharyngitis and gastroenteritis have also been reported [20]. Hence there is a need to develop therapeutic options that are orally bioavailable but also do not cross the blood-brain barrier to avoid CNS associated adverse effects.

Novel modalities known as targeted protein degraders have recently been described with the potential to degrade protein targets previously considered undruggable. These degraders can be either molecular glues or more complex bifunctional degraders which include AUTACs, LYTACS etc [21]. These degraders co-opt ubiquitously expressed E3 ligases including CRBN, von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) and others to ubiquitinate the targeted protein and degrade it via proteosome machinery. We have developed a series of CRBN-based bifunctional degraders which specifically degrade PDE4B and PDE4D, without affecting the stability of PDE4A and PDE4C sub-families. Lead PDE4 bifunctional degraders displayed potency much better than the warheads used as the binder, thus had excellent anti-inflammatory activity in ex vivo as well as in vivo models of AD.

We have developed a bifunctional degrader which specifically degrades B and D isoforms of PDE4, sparing other two isoforms. Employing a series of biological assays, we show that these degraders display robust degradation of PDE4B/D resulting in significant inhibition of inflammatory cytokine production by the T cells. We further show the efficacy of these degraders in mouse models of atopic dermatitis and human PBMC induced model of graft versus host disease. Taken together, we have developed a new modality which has the potential to be used for the treatment of atopic dermatitis and other chronic inflammatory disorders.

Materials and methods

Cell lines, cell culture, and other compounds

HeLa (#CCL-2), A549 (#CCL-185), BxPC-3 (#CRL-1687), PANC-1 (#CRL-1469), HCT 116 (#CCL-247), COLO 201 (#CCL-224) and MCF7 (#HTB-22) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) (Teddington, UK). Human malignant melanoma epithelial skin cell lines A375 (#88113005) and MM415 (#10092319), were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Dorset, UK). HeLa, A549, MCF7 and A375 cells were cultured in DMEM with 2 mM L-glutamine supplemented with 10 % FBS and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin, whereas BxPC-3, PANC-1, HCT 116, COLO201, MM415 were maintained in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10 % FBS and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5 % CO2. Cells were split at 80–90 % confluence and media was replaced every two-three day as required.

Molecular modeling and in silico docking methodology

The x-ray crystal structures of cereblon bound to CC-220 (5V3O.pdb) and the ultrashort isoform of PDE4D bound to papaverine (3IAK.pdb) were downloaded from the RCSB PDB. Each structure was imported into MOE (www.chemcomp.com, Molecular Operating Environment (MOE), 2024.06; Chemical Computing Group ULC, 1010 Sherbrooke St. West, Suite #910, Montreal, QC, Canada, H3A 2R7, 2024) and cleaned up using MOE's ‘Structure Preparation’ function. Using the bound ligands in the x-ray structures as templates and the solvent-parameterized potential function Amber10:EHT [22,23] and GB/VI ΔG scoring function [24], the cereblon binding warhead and the PDE4D binding warhead of BTX-AP02 were docked into the cereblon and PDE4D x-ray structures respectively. Molecular Mechanics (MM) minimization was subsequently performed on the best scoring pose for each complex.

Protein-protein docking studies of the warhead bound cereblon and PDE4D poses were then performed using default MOE settings and potentials resulting in 1000 cereblon/PDE4D bound poses. Poses that were not feasible for bifunctional degraders, as the warheads were too far apart, were eliminated. In silico docking and subsequent MM minimization was then performed for BTX-AP02 into each protein-protein complex using default MOE settings and potentials. The resulting ternary complexes were then clustered, using MOE's Cluster function, with a RMSD cutoff 10 Å and were manually inspected to remove unfeasible poses. This analysis resulted in 20 clusters containing a total of 175 ternary complexes. A representative ternary complex dock from the most prevalent cluster is shown in Fig. 1E.

Western blotting

Cellular lysates were prepared in lysis buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1 % NP-40, 1 mM EDTA, 5 % glycerol) with Complete™ EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail tablets (Roche). Protein concentration of lysates was determined using the Bradford assay and all samples were equalised for protein concentration. Proteins were separated by SDS/PAGE (4–12 % Bis-Tris gels) and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes for Western blotting. The membranes were blocked in Intercept (TBS) Blocking Buffer (LI-COR) for 1 h at room temperature, followed by incubation with primary antibody dilution in Intercept T20 (TBS) Antibody Diluent overnight (LI-COR) at 4 °C with gentle shaking. Primary antibodies included rabbit anti-PDE4D (Abcam #ab171749, 1:2500), mouse anti-β-actin (Cell Signaling Technology #3700, 1:25000), mouse anti-PDE4A, mouse anti-PDE4B and rabbit anti-PDE4C (In-house, 1:5000). Membranes were washed with TBST and then incubated with fluorescently labeled IRDye secondary antibodies (LI-COR, 1:10000) for 1 h at room temperature. The secondary antibodies used were IRDye 680RD goat anti-mouse IgG (#926–68070), IRDye 680RD goat anti-rabbit IgG (#926–68071) and IRDye 800CW goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (#926–32210). Membranes were scanned on the Odyssey® CLx imaging system and signals was detected at 700 and/or 800 nm channels. All densitometry analyses were done using Image Studio (LI-COR). Proteins were normalized to β-actin as loading control. Changes in signals were represented as % of DMSO by taking the signal ratio of treatment normalized signal to DMSO normalized signal. Where necessary, representative images were shown in grayscale.

HiBiT assay

HiBiT cell lines (Promega) were used for neo substrate degradation assays. Cells were plated in 384-well clear-bottom plates and incubated at 37 °C overnight. The next day, cells were treated with compounds using D330e dispenser (TECAN) for a 10-point linear titration from 10 mM to 0.00262 mM in duplicate. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for set dosage time (6 h for Aiolos, CK1a, GSPT1, and Ikaros tagged cells, 24 h for SALL4 tagged cells). HiBiT levels were determined by adding Nano-Glo® HiBiT Lytic Reagent. Plates were read for luminescence with CLARIOstar plus (BMG Labtech). Dose-response curves were represented as % of DMSO.

cAMP determination using MSD platform

cAMP levels were measured in the PBMCs treated with BTX degraders and control agents using an MSD kit (Mesoscale Discovery, #K150W5D) following manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, PBMCs were seeded in a 96-well dish and treated with PDE4 inhibitors, Forskolin and BTX-AP03 for 1hr followed by prostaglandin E2 (PgE2) for 1hr. At the end of treatment, PBMCs were harvested, lysed in assay buffer followed by cAMP determination using an MSD instrument.

cAMP determination using EPAC-based FRET sensors

The detailed FRET methodology has been previously described [25]. Briefly, A549 cells were seeded on 24 mm coverslips and allowed to adhere overnight. On the next day, cells were infected with an adenoviral vector containing the EPAC-SH187 cAMP sensor (Klarenbeek et al., 2015) and incubated for 24 h. On the day of the experiment, each coverslip was mounted in a measurement chamber and rinsed twice with extracellular buffer (140 mM NaCl, 3 mM KCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 2 mM CaCl2, 20 mM Glucose, 10 mM HEPES; pH 7.2). After the second rinse, cells were left in 900 μl extracellular buffer and placed under the Olympus IX71 inverted microscope for observation. The microscope was equipped with a charge-coupled device camera (coolSNAP HQ monochrome camera system, Photometrics) and a Dual-view simultaneous-imaging system (DV2 mag biosystem, Photometrics, ET-04-EM). A computer connected to the microscope and running the MetaFluor software (Molecular devices; http://www.moleculardevices.com) was used for image acquisition and data analysis. After a region with suitable fluorescent cells was selected, the image acquisition was started (500 s exposure time; 1× gain). Bifunctional protein degraders were added to the cells in the measurement chamber 5 min after the start and images were acquired for 30 min following stimulus addition. At the end of the experiment, the sensor was driven into saturation by applying a combination of Forskolin (FSK; 25 μM) and isobutyl-methyl-xanthin (IBMX, 100 μM), and images were acquired for a further 5 min before concluding each experiment. Pharmacokinetic studies in mice

C57Bl/6 female mice (Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co.) were given BTX PDE4B/D degraders either as a bolus intravenously or intraperitoneally (IP). Blood samples were obtained by retro-orbital bleed while the animals were anesthetized with isoflurane or by terminal cardiac puncture. Blood samples were collected at the indicated times in tubes containing K2EDTA as anticoagulant. Samples were centrifuged within 1 h of collection and plasma was collected, diluted 1:1 with 25 mM citrate buffer (pH 1.5) and stored at −80 °C until analysis. Total compound concentrations were determined by LC-MS/MS. Where tissue concentrations were determined, tissues were collected from 4 animals at each time point, weighed, and stored at −80 °C until analysis. Tissue homogenate concentrations were converted to tissue concentrations to calculate tumor-to- plasma ratios.

Oxazolone induced model of atopic dermatitis

Atopic dermatitis was induced in 6–7 weeks old female Balb/c mice using oxazolone (Sigma). Briefly, mice under isoflurane anesthesia were shaved in the abdomen. 100 μL of 1.5 % oxazolone solution (prepared in 4:1 acetone/olive oil) was painted on the shaved abdomen on day 0 for induction of AD. 20 μL of 1 % oxazolone solution was applied 6 more times 3 days apart starting on day 7 on both inner and outer surface of left ear as a challenge. Ear thickness was measured 24-hr post each challenge.

Vehicle (10 % DMSO+90 % Solutol in 10 % HP-β-CD in water) as well as test agent (BTX-AP02) were dosed intraperitoneally (IP), Apremilast and Ruxolitinib were dosed orally (PO) whereas Dexamethasone and Crisaborole were doses as topical ointments. Dosing started on day 6 post induction of AD at indicated dose levels. Ear thickness was measured before the start of study as well as 24-hr post every challenge. Transepidermal water loss (TEWL) as well as level of cytokines were measured in skin and plasma at the end of study. Plasma, spleen and ear samples were collected at the end of study for pharmacokinetic (PK) and pharmacodynamic (PD) analysis.

GvHD monitoring

6–8-week-old female NCG (NOD/ShiLtJGpt-Prkdcem26Cd52Il2rgem26Cd22/Gpt) mice (GemPharmtech) were irradiated at the dose of 1Gy, followed by inoculation of 5 million human PBMCs via intraperitoneal route for developing GvHD. Mice were randomized into different groups on day 10 post-inoculation. PO dosing with BTX-AP04 and other standard of care (SoC) and control agents begin on day 10 at indicated dose levels. Mice were individually scored for five different parameters (body weight, activity, posture, fur texture and skin integrity) on a scale from 0 to 2. GvHD phenotyping score was collected three times per week. Clinical GvHD score was determined by adding the individual scores for all the parameters. The mice were monitored every day for survival. Final blood collection for FACs analysis was collected on day 42 and study was terminated on day 44.

Blood collection and flow cytometry

Whole blood samples were collected through the retroorbital vein and were in a tube containing EDTA-K2 and processed for FACS analysis. For hematology analysis, 30 μL whole blood was transferred to another tube pre-coated with EDTA-K2 and diluted by adding 180 μL of pre-cooled normal saline. Samples of peripheral blood were stained for 30 min with BV711-conjugated anti-human CD3, allophycocyanin (APC)-conjugated anti-human CD4, BB515-conjugated anti-human CD8, and BUV395-conjugated anti-human CD45 (all from BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA). Stained peripheral blood samples were then lysed with red blood cell lysis buffer, and absolute cell counts were calculated using BD liquid counting beads (BD Biosciences). Flow cytometry was performed using a BD Fortessa II instrument (BD Biosciences) and analyzed with FlowJo software.

Statistical analysis

For analysis over time, repeated measures mixed model using SAS PROC MIXED (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC) with dependent variable of % protein remaining and fixed effects for time, group, and their interaction. Otherwise analyses used a two-sided analysis of variance (ANOVA). Survival data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.4.0.673; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA) by the Kaplan–Meier method, and statistical significance between survival curves was determined with the log-rank test. All data are presented as mean ± SD unless mentioned otherwise. Sample sizes (n) refer to independent biological replicates. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Results

Robust and selective degradation of PDE4 is achieved in human cells

Anti-inflammatory effects of the PDE4 inhibitors are due to their ability to reduce the expression of proinflammatory cytokines by immune cells. Since our bifunctional degraders use Apremilast as the target binding ligand (TBL) which is a pan-PDE4 inhibitor, we checked degradation of all four PDE4 subfamilies (PDE4A-D) in human PBMCs. When PBMCs were treated with our lead degrader BTX-AP04, we observed the degradation of both PDE4B and PDE4D sub-families was rapid and dose-dependent with maximum degradation of both PDE4B and PDE4D at 86 % (Fig. 1A, B and 1C). While BTX-AP04 efficiently degraded PDE4B and PDE4D, the degradation of PDE4A and PDE4C was less than 40 % (Supplementary Fig. 1A and 1B). Not only did BTX-AP04 exhibit deep degradation of PDE4D and PDE4B, but it also degraded these substrates as early as 4h in PBMCs (Fig. 1B and C).

To establish an in vitro model for PDE4D degradation, we screened a panel of 9 human cancer cell lines for expression of all the isoforms of PDE4D. While most of these cell lines had low expression of PDE4D, PDE4D (long and short isoform) expression was statistically significantly higher in A549 cells than all other cell lines tested (Fig. 1D and E). Since BTX-AP04 degrades PDE4B as well, we confirmed the expression as well as degradation of PDE4B in A549 cells in the presence of BTX-AP04 (Supplementary Fig. 1C and 1D). Between long and short isoforms of PDE4D, it has been reported that short isoforms are preferentially upregulated in activated T cells [26] and hence degradation of the short isoforms will have greater impact on inflammation. Indeed, we observed that BTX-AP04 degraded both PDE4B (Supplementary Fig. 1C and 1D) and PDE4D in A549 cells in a dose-dependent manner, achieving approximately 95 % maximal degradation of short PDE4D isoforms (Supplementary Fig. 1E and 1F).

The length of the linker and exit vector of the bifunctional degrader have major impact on the interaction between the POI and CRBN [27,28]. To rationalize the high degradation potency of bifunctional degraders, we developed a PROTAC modeling methodology. In the method, protein-protein docking was first performed using MOE with the x-ray structure of Cereblon (5V3O.pdb) and the X-ray structure of ultrashort isoform of PDE4D (3IAK.pdb), each with bound warhead. Poses that are not feasible for bifunctional degraders were eliminated, e.g., if the warheads are too far apart. Docking with BTX-AP02 was then performed along with molecular mechanics minimization. Docked CRBN-BTX-AP02-PDE4D complexes were then clustered with a 10 Å RMSD cutoff. A representative ternary complex dock from the most prevalent cluster is shown in Fig. 1F. In this complex, BTX-AP02 forms multiple interactions with both CRBN and PDE4D, including the two H-bonds between the linker -NH and Ser 227 and Asp 272 of CRBN. Based on the molecular modeling, we modified the linker region of the degraders to develop a series of PDE4B/D degraders which displayed efficient degradation potential (Supplementary Table 1). Furthermore, as observed in PBMCs (Fig. 1A), our PDE4 degraders are highly potent in degrading both the long as well as short isoforms of PDE4D as indicated by low nanomolar DC50 values (Table 1).

PDE4 degraders achieve significantly higher cAMP accumulation compared to PDE4 inhibitors, and their degradation activity is CRBN-dependent

PDE4 inhibitors such as Apremilast and Roflumilast are known to cause increased accumulation of intracellular levels of cAMP [29]. To test if the PDE4B/D degraders can also elicit similar accumulation of cAMP [17], we treated PBMCs with known PDE4 inhibitors including Apremilast, Roflumilast and Crisabarole along with BTX-AP03, which also displays approximately 95 % PDE4D degradation, as BTX-AP04 (Supplementary Fig. 1G and 1H). Forskolin was included in the assay as a positive control. To induce the accumulation of cAMP, we treated the PBMCs with prostaglandin E2 for 1h post treatment with compounds included in this study. We observed dose-dependent accumulation of cAMP when PBMCs were treated with PDE4 inhibitors as well as Forskolin. Interestingly, we observed significantly higher cAMP accumulation in the PBMCs treated with BTX-AP03 (from 1 μM to 0.001 μM), indicating not only PDE4B/D degrader activity is mechanistically similar to PDE4 inhibitors, PDE4B/D degraders are >2-fold more potent than PDE4 inhibitors in attenuating the activity of phosphodiesterases (Fig. 2A and Supplementary Table 2). Moreover, we determined the phosphorylation status of CREB (phospho ser133) following treatment with PDE4B/D degraders as well as control articles. While we observed moderate increases in the ratio of pCREB/CREB in cells treated with BTX-AP03, it was not significantly different than Apremilast and Roflumilast (data not shown). We further employed a more sensitive cAMP sensor Epac-SH187 in a FRET-based assay to determine intracellular cAMP levels in A549 cells [25]. A549 cells transduced with the sensor were used to test the activity of PDE4B/D degraders against 1000-fold higher concentration of non-selective PDE4 inhibition with Rolipram. The change in FRET signal with Rolipram was higher when compared with PDE4B/D degraders (BTX-AP09 and BTX-AP10), though PDE4B/D degraders were used at much lower concentrations in these assays (Fig. 2B).

The PDE4 binder, Apremilast used for these degraders can potentially inhibit the activity of PDE4. The binding affinity to CRBN is critical for the efficacy of bifunctional degraders, as it facilitates the recruitment of the ubiquitin-proteasome machinery to the target protein. We and others have previously shown that N-methylation of the CRBN binding moiety of the bifunctional degrader inhibits its binding to CRBN. This effect may arise from steric hindrance or altered electronic properties, which can interfere with the optimal fit within CRBN's binding pocket [30]. We synthesized an N-methylated version of BTX-AP04 (BTX-AP04me) to determine the effect of methylation on the degradation of PDE4D in PBMCs. Methylation of BTX-AP04 significantly decreased the degradation of PDE4D at pharmacologically relevant concentrations but showed some off-target activity at higher concentrations tested (Fig. 2C and D).

One key factor that differentiates bifunctional degraders from active-site inhibitors is their catalytic mechanism of action compared to occupancy-driven events [31,32]. Depending on the resynthesis rate of the protein of interest, bifunctional degraders can have long-term effects when compared with conventional inhibitors. To test this hypothesis, we treated A549 cells with another PDE4B/D degrader, BTX-AP08 for 24 h, followed by washing off all the compound and observed the reappearance of PDE4D. As shown before, we saw efficient degradation of all the PDE4D isoforms in a dose-dependent manner, but in this experiment expression of PDE4D isoforms remained low even after 48hrs post-washout, especially for cells treated with higher concentrations of BTX-AP08, suggesting a low resynthesis rate of PDE4D protein (Fig. 2E) and a long-lasting catalytic degradation of the protein. These results raise the possibility that due to the catalytic mechanism of action for PDE4 degraders, these compounds could be dosed at lower dose levels as well as less frequently to achieve improved efficacy and fewer adverse effects in the patients suffering from atopic dermatitis.

PDE4 degraders are more potent than PDE4 inhibitors to reduce expression of inflammatory cytokines

Pathogenesis of AD includes aberrant activation of immune cells which produce pro-inflammatory cytokines leading to development of clinical signs associated with AD. Expression of these pro-inflammatory cytokines is dependent on the nature of the stimulant which include allergic, parasitic or auto-immunity. PDE4 inhibitors including Apremilast, Roflumilast and Crisaborole exert their physiological effects by inhibiting the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNFα and IFNγ etc. To probe the activity of immune cells ex vivo, we developed an assay utilizing human PBMCs which were stimulated with a cocktail of antibodies including anti-CD3ε and anti-CD28 for 24 h in absence or presence of conventional PDE4 inhibitors and degraders. The potency of the multiple compounds was evaluated based on their ability to inhibit the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines in the culture media using an MSD based assay. Half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) analysis of the cytokines in the culture media revealed that our lead degraders (BTX-AP04 and BTX-AP05) inhibit the release of TNFα up to 1000-fold more effectively than the clinically utilized Apremilast. These PDE4 degraders displayed similar potencies to inhibit the release of other cytokines as well, as listed in Table 2 and Fig. 3A–D.

We next tested whether this decrease in the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines is dependent on decreases in the levels of PDE4D and if the degradation of PDE4D is CRBN dependent, we ran the ex vivo cytokine release assay using human PBMCs in the presence of at least 100X molar concentration of a CRBN competitor. This CRBN competitor binds to the cellular pool of CRBN making it unavailable to the bifunctional degrader to recruit CRBN for PDE4D degradation. We observed ∼200fold decrease in the potency of PDE4D degraders in the presence of CRBN competitor leading to significant decreases in the IC50 for inhibition of pro-inflammatory cytokines (Fig. 3E–H). The CRBN competitor brought the activity of our degraders down to comparable levels to Apremilast, which is being used as the warhead for these bifunctional degraders. Moreover, BTX-AP04me completely lost its ability to downregulate the expression of inflammatory cytokines (Fig. 3I–L).

Additionally, we did not observe any cytotoxicity due to the compounds used in these assays (Supplementary Fig. 2A). CRBN-dependent degradation of spalt like transcription factor 4 (Sall4) has been associated with teratogenicity [33]. We determined the degradation of Sall4 in cells treated with PDE4 degraders and found that except for early compounds (BTX-AP01 and BTX-AP02), our lead PDE4 degraders had no effect on the stability of Sall4 (Supplementary Fig. 2B). We further tested if PDE4 degrades other known neosubstrates of CRBN such as Aiolos and Ikaros. Degradation of these neosubstrates has been shown to upregulate expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines [34,35]. Again, our lead degraders did not affect the stability of these transcription factors (Supplementary Fig. 2C and 2D). These data clearly suggest degrading PDE4B/D could be a viable strategy to potently downregulate the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines compared to just inhibition achieved by Apremilast.

Taken together, these data indicate cereblon-dependent degradation of PDE4B and PDE4D isoforms can downregulate the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines by activated T cells and are up to a thousand times more potent than conventional PDE4 inhibitors that are currently used in the clinic.

PDE4 degrader achieves exposure and efficacy in mouse model of atopic dermatitis

Based on the linker length, our degraders fall into two categories, one that degrades both mouse and human PDE4B/D and the others that degrade just human PDE4B/D Table 3. We determined the pharmacokinetic (PK) profile of compounds from both categories that efficiently degrade most of the PDE4D isoforms. BTX-AP02 can degrade both mouse and human PDE4D, with many favorable PK properties. However, BTX-AP02 possesses low oral bioavailability, owing to its long linker and high molecular weight (Table 3). Another compound, BTX-AP04, which can degrade only human PDE4D displayed 14.6 % oral bioavailability as well as good exposure with AUC of 7030 h∗ng/mL.

To determine the degradation potential of PDE4B/D degraders in vivo, we determined the degradation of PDE4D in mouse tissues following intraperitoneal administration of BTX-AP02 in C57Bl/6 mice. The mice were dosed with either vehicle or BTX-AP02 at 5 and 10 mg/kg and tissues including spleens and joints were collected for pharmacodynamic (PD) analysis 6h, 12h and 24h post dosing. Degradation of PDE4D protein expression in mouse tissues was determined using western immunoblotting. We observed a dose-dependent degradation of PDE4D in both spleen as well as joint tissues (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Fig. 3A–3C). Moreover, known neosubstrates of CRBN including Aiolos, Ikaros and CK1α were not degraded in mouse spleens (Supplementary Fig. 3D–3F) at all the dose levels. The results of the PK and PD study encouraged us to test the efficacy of BTX-AP02 in a mouse model of atopic dermatitis. Atopic dermatitis was induced using 1.5 % Oxazolone. Mice were rechallenged with 1 % oxazolone followed by ear thickness measurement 24h post-rechallenge, every 3 days post randomization (Fig. 4C). Ear thickness was measured to monitor the inflammatory response during the study. Plasma, spleen and ear samples were collected to determine systemic and local exposure of BTX-AP02. The PK bioanalysis results reveal that exposure levels higher than in vitro DC50 values (dotted line) were achieved, both systemically as well as locally in the ear tissue (Fig. 4D).

All the mice exhibited ear inflammation as measured by ear thickness before the treatment was initiated. While vehicle treated mice displayed maximum inflammation in the ear, mice treated with dexamethasone as a positive control had minimal inflammation. Approved PDE4 inhibitors including Crisabarole and Apremilast, as well as JAK1/2 inhibitor Ruxolitinib, led to significant decrease in the ear thickness when compared with the vehicle control. Importantly, BTX-AP02 was equally efficacious as approved therapies in this model of atopic dermatitis (Fig. 4E).

Mouse spleens were collected at the end of the study to determine the degradation of PDE4D in immune cells. We observed a dose-dependent degradation of all the isoforms of PDE4D in mouse spleens with maximum degradation of 69 % observed at 10 mg/kg BID (Fig. 4F). To determine if the degradation of PDE4D in mouse cells will lead to decreased expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines in immune cells, we determined the expression of cytokines in the plasma and mouse ears given that ears are the primary site of inflammation. While we did not observe significant reduction of these cytokines in plasma (Supplementary Fig. 3G–3I), levels of proinflammatory cytokines including IL2, IL4 and IL6 were significantly reduced in ear tissue. Interestingly, BTX-AP02 was superior to Ruxolitinib in reducing the local expression of these cytokines (Fig. 4G–I).

Orally bioavailable PDE4 degrader display better efficacy in mouse model of graft versus host disease

While our earlier lead compounds were not orally bioavailable, we were successful in developing orally bioavailable compounds, BTX-AP04, BTX-AP06 and BTX-AP07. All these compounds displayed exposure when dosed per os (PO) with oral bioavailability (%F) ranging from 14.6 to 29 % (Table 3). Since a different CRBN binding moiety was used for the development of an oral PDE4 bifunctional degrader, we did not observe degradation of PDE4B and PDE4D in mouse cells (data not shown). Species differences in the degradation of target protein has been previously observed with small molecule degraders due to key amino acid differences among species [36]. Since BTX-AP04 did not degrade PDE4B/D in mouse cells, we employed a mouse model of graft versus host disease (GvHD) where human PBMCs were inoculated in immunocompromised mice. Since BTX-AP04 degrades PDE4B/D in PBMCs and is highly efficient in downregulating expression of proinflammatory cytokines in the ex vivo assays, we proceeded to probe the in vivo efficacy using a human PBMC induced model of GvHD in NCG mice. Mice inoculated with human PBMCs begin to display signs of GvHD such as posture, activity, body weight loss and coat texture. Blood samples from the mice were analyzed by flow cytometry on day 7 post-inoculation to determine the proportion of CD3+CD45+ T cells in peripheral blood. Mice displaying robust engraftment of hPBMCs with at least 70 % CD3+CD45+ T cells (Supplementary Fig. 4A) were randomized into different treatment groups. To rule out the possibility of drug induced toxicity on PBMCs, peripheral blood was analyzed again on day 14 (4 days post dosing) for circulating T cells. We observed that mice in all treatment groups maintained the frequency of T cells in the peripheral blood (Supplementary Fig. 4B).

Efficacy was measured on survival as well as GvHD score, which was based on body weight loss, activity, posture, coat texture and coat integrity on a scale of 0–2. Interestingly, mice dosed with BTX-AP04 had a statistically significant higher probability of survival than the PDE4 inhibitors as well as cyclosporine A, which was used as the positive control in this study. The JAK1/2 inhibitor (Ruxolitinib) performed equally well in this study (Fig. 5A). Inflammatory cytokines including TNFα, IFNγ and IL-1β have been described as major cytokines involved in the development of GvHD in this model [37]. We collected plasma samples on day 5 post dosing to evaluate the effectiveness of BTX-AP04 as well as other standard of care and control agents. While we did not observe significant inhibition of IFNγ and IL-1β (data not shown), however we observed significantly low levels of TNFα in the plasma of the mice treated with BTX-AP04 when compared to the vehicle treated mice. Moreover, BTX-AP04 treated mice had significantly lower levels of TNFα when compared to both the PDE4 inhibitors as well as standard of care agents and cyclosporine A (Fig. 5B). All the treatment groups had significantly lower GvHD scores between day 16 to day 42 when compared to the vehicle control (Fig. 5C). Mice engrafted with human PBMCs did not show any signs of GvHD during initial phase of engraftment (3 weeks) but displayed decrease in body weight in the terminal phase of disease (Fig. 5D). Taken together, these results indicate that PDE4 degradation is not only significantly better than PDE4 inhibitors and Ruxolitinib in downregulating the expression of TNFα, it also results in delay in onset of signs of GvHD compared to standard of care agents.

In conclusion, we present data that PDE4B/D degraders can robustly degrade PDE4B and PDE4D sub-families of phosphodiesterase through CRBN mediated ubiquitination of these proteins followed by degradation via 26S proteasome. Degradation of PDE4B/D leads to potent inhibition of pro-inflammatory cytokines when compared with approved PDE4 inhibitors (Fig. 5E).

Discussion

Since multiple etiologies underly the development of atopic dermatitis, there are multiple treatment options avalable for these patients. Traditionally AD is being managed using topical therapies including emollients and topical corticosteroids. Other treatment options include immune suppressors such as cyclosporine and methotrexate, as well as pototherapy and patient education. Recently, biologic therapies such as Duplimumab which targets IL4/13 pathways have shown great promise for the treatment of moderate to severe AD [[38], [39], [40]]. While effective, these therapies have limitations in terms of safety and long-term use.

Phosphodiesterase 4 (PDE4) has emerged as a promising therapeutic target for various diseases, including inflammatory disorders such as atopic dermatitis, neurodegenerative diseases, and psychiatric disorders. Traditionally, small molecule inhibitors have been the primary approach to modulate PDE4 activity. However, recent advancements in drug development have led to the exploration of an alternative strategy: targeted degradation of PDE4 enzymes. The introduction of PDE4 inhibitors for treatment of indications including atopic dermatitis and Hidradenitis suppurativa (HS), has added a new dimension for the clinical management of AD due to its specific mechanism of action targeting underlying inflammatory processes [41,42].

PDE4 inhibitors have been extensively studied and have demonstrated efficacy in preclinical and clinical settings for treating conditions such as asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and psoriasis [43,44]. By blocking the enzymatic activity of PDE4, inhibitors elevate intracellular levels of cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP), thereby modulating downstream signaling pathways involved in inflammation, immune response, and neuronal function. Moreover, several PDE4 inhibitors have been approved by regulatory agencies and are commercially available, underscoring their clinical relevance. While Roflumilast and Crisaborole are approved for topical application only for pateints with AD, Apremilast is an orally available PDE4 inhibitor for these patients.

Despite their therapeutic potential, PDE4 inhibitors often exhibit dose-limiting side effects, including nausea, emesis, and gastrointestinal disturbances, attributed to the broad distribution of PDE4 isoforms throughout the body. Additionally, off-target effects may arise due to the structural similarity between PDE4 and other phosphodiesterase isoforms [45,46]. These challenges underscore the need for developing novel strategies to enhance the selectivity and tolerability of PDE4 inhibitors.

Targeted protein degradation has gained considerable attention as a promising therapeutic approach, facilitated by the development of proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs or bifunctional degraders) and other small molecules capable of inducing protein degradation. Several bifunctional degraders are already in various stages of clinical trials and many more are in discovery phase [47]. In the context of PDE4, selective degradation offers several advantages over inhibition, including enhanced potency and specificity, reduced systemic exposure, and potentially improved safety profiles.

We report the discovery of a new modality which can effectively downregulate inflammation associated with the pathogenesis of AD. We show that our bifunctional degraders are superior to approved PDE4 inhibitors including Apremilast and Roflumilast in terms of anti-inflammatory response, both in vitro and in vivo models. By selectively targeting 2 PDE4 subfamilies (B and D only) for degradation, this approach may mitigate off-target effects and minimize adverse reactions associated with traditional inhibitors. Since our degraders do not cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB) (Supplementary Table 3), it will potentially display fewer CNS related adverse effects including emesis and nausea, as has been reported with all the approved PDE4 inhibitors. Still, there is a possibility of other reported adverse effects including gastrointestinal related adverse effects. Development of a topical formulation for our PDE4 degraders could potentially circumvent these issues. As we continue to explore these degraders and optimize their usage, we may further advance our ability to alleviate the burden of this chronic skin disorder. Furthermore, the ability to use PDE4 degraders over extended periods without significant safety concerns enhances their attractiveness as a long-term therapy for AD.

We explored PDE4 degradation as it presents exciting opportunities for therapeutic innovation. We have focussed on optimizing the pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic properties of PDE4 degraders, elucidating their mechanism(s) of action, and evaluating their efficacy in preclinical models. Furthermore, we performed comparative studies directly comparing the efficacy and safety profiles of PDE4 inhibitors versus degraders that are warranted to inform clinical decision-making and maximize patient outcomes.

Another class of compounds known as SNIPERs (Specific and Nongenetic IAP-dependent Protein Erasers) that recruit inhibitor of the apoptosis protein (IAP) ubiquitin ligases to specifically degrade targeted proteins have been developed to degrade proteins including ERα, RAR, AR, BCR-ABL etc. (reviewed in Ref. [48]). A SNIPER compound using LCL-161 derivative as the IAP ligand was reported which can efficiently degrade PDE4 in HT1080 cells in a proteasome dependent manner [49]. PDE6D (PDEδ) is another PDE superfamily member for which bifunctional degrader has been developed which display potent anti-proliferative effects in KRAS mutant and PDE6D-dependent cancer cells as well as increased expression of enzymes involved in lipid metabolism and regulation of sterol synthesis [50,51]. While other PDEs are gaining therapeutic importance, thus far only PDE4 is being targeted in immunological indications.

In conclusion, the balance between PDE4 inhibition and degradation represents a pivotal frontier in drug discovery and development. While PDE4 inhibitors have demonstrated clinical utility, targeted degradation offers a promising alternative with the potential to overcome existing limitations. By critically evaluating the advantages and challenges of each approach, this manuscript aims to stimulate further research and innovation in the quest for effective therapeutics targeting PDE4-mediated pathways in human disease. PDE SNIPER and PROTAC technology could revolutionize the drug development and treatment strategies in multiple disease contexts.
