MRI findings of intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma with sarcomatoid differentiation: a retrospective case series
Authors: Hao Wu, Chunfang Hu, Hongmei Zhang, Xinming Zhao, Zheng Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo describe MRI findings of intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma with sarcomatoid differentiation (s-iCCA), summarize associated clinical features, and explore imaging characteristics that may help distinguish it from conventional intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma.Materials and methodsThis retrospective single-center study reviewed surgically resected, pathology-proven s-iCCA between June 2018 and September 2025. Patients who underwent preoperative liver magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with dynamic contrast enhancement and diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) were included; tumors arising from the extrahepatic bile duct or gallbladder and those treated before MRI were excluded. Two radiologists evaluated lesion morphology, signal characteristics, enhancement pattern, and tumor spread independently. Clinical, laboratory, pathologic, treatment, and follow-up data were collected.ResultsFive patients (2 men, 3 women; mean age, 64 years) were included. Serum carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA 19-9) was elevated in all patients, whereas alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) were within reference range in tested patients. All tumors presented as a dominant mass-forming lesion (3.4–9.4 cm; segments V–VII (right), n = 4; segments III/IV (left), n = 1). On MRI, lesions were hypointense on T1-weighted imaging and heterogeneously hyperintense on T2-weighted imaging; intratumoral hemorrhage suggested by T1 hyperintensity was observed in two patients, and extensive nonenhancing necrosis/cystic degeneration in three. All tumors showed marked diffusion restriction on DWI. Dynamic imaging demonstrated arterial peripheral rim enhancement with progressive and persistent heterogeneous enhancement without washout in all cases. Segmental bile duct dilatation was present in two patients, suspected portal vein tumor thrombus in one, and regional lymphadenopathy in three. Extensive necrosis or cystic degeneration was common and contributed to marked imaging heterogeneity.ConclusionIn this small series, s-iCCA frequently appeared as a necrotic and heterogeneous mass-forming lesion with diffusion restriction and progressive enhancement. Although these findings substantially overlap with those of conventional iCCA, prominent necrosis and marked heterogeneity may raise suspicion for sarcomatoid differentiation in the appropriate clinical context.Keywords: Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, Sarcomatoid differentiation, Case series, Liver Neoplasms, Magnetic resonance imaging

Background

Sarcomatoid differentiation represents a rare but clinically important pathway of dedifferentiation in biliary tract malignancies. In intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (iCCA), the sarcomatoid variant is characterized histologically by malignant spindle or pleomorphic cells admixed with an epithelial component, with immunohistochemical evidence of epithelial lineage (e.g., cytokeratin positivity) in at least a subset of tumor cells [1]. Reported series suggest that sarcomatoid change is uncommon among iCCA specimens [2], but it is consistently associated with aggressive biology, early metastasis, and poor outcomes [3–6]. Only limited data regarding its clinical features, diagnosis, and treatment are available [7]. Preoperative recognition of intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma with sarcomatoid differentiation (s-iCCA) is challenging. Symptoms and laboratory tests are nonspecific [8]. Importantly, imaging features substantially overlap with those of conventional mass-forming iCCA, making preoperative recognition of sarcomatoid differentiation particularly difficult. In addition, overlap with hepatocellular carcinoma and hypervascular metastases further contributes to diagnostic uncertainty in clinical practice [9]. Prior imaging literature on s-iCCA is limited largely to isolated cases or small mixed cohorts, with most descriptions based on computed tomography (CT) [10]; magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), particularly diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) and multiphasic enhancement patterns, has been less systematically characterized. The purpose of this study was to describe the clinical characteristics and MRI features of surgically confirmed s-iCCA at our institution and to highlight imaging patterns that may raise suspicion for this rare diagnosis, particularly in comparison with conventional mass-forming intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma.

Materials and methods

This retrospective study was approved by the institutional ethics committee (approval date: 8 July 2025; approval number: 25/331–5277). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Given the retrospective nature of the study and the use of previously acquired imaging and pathological data, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the ethics committee.

Study design and patients

We queried the institutional pathology database for patients with pathologically confirmed s-iCCA who underwent hepatic resection between June 2018 and September 2025. Inclusion criteria were: (a) pathologically confirmed s-iCCA, (b) preoperative liver MRI including dynamic contrast-enhanced sequences and DWI, and (c) available clinical and laboratory data. Exclusion criteria were: (a) tumor arising from the extrahepatic bile duct or gallbladder, and (b) prior locoregional or systemic treatment before MRI. The patient selection process is summarized in Fig. 1. Ultimately, five patients met all eligibility criteria and were included in the final analysis.

Clinical and laboratory data

Demographic information, presenting symptoms, hepatitis status, cirrhosis, and laboratory tests including liver enzymes and tumor markers including alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) and carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA 19-9) were extracted from the electronic medical record. Follow-up information was obtained from clinic notes and imaging reports.

MRI acquisition

MRI examinations were performed using a 3.0-T scanner (GE Discovery MR 750, General Electric Medical Systems) with an 8-channel body phased-array coil. The imaging protocol included axial T1-weighted, T2-weighted, diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), and dynamic contrast-enhanced sequences.

DWI was performed with b-values of 0 and 800 s/mm², and apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) maps were automatically generated. ADC measurements were performed by manually placing regions of interest (ROIs) over the entire tumor on the slice showing the largest tumor diameter. The mean ADC value was recorded for each lesion.

Dynamic contrast-enhanced T1-weighted imaging was performed after intravenous administration of an extracellular gadolinium-based contrast agent (gadopentetate dimeglumine, Magnevist, Bayer, Leverkusen) at a dose of 0.2 ml/kg and an injection rate of 1.5 ml/s. Images were acquired during the arterial phase (~ 25–30 s), portal venous phase (~ 60–70 s), and delayed phase (~ 120–180 s).

Slice thickness ranged from 3 to 5 mm depending on the sequence.

Imaging analysis

Two abdominal radiologists (with 1 and 20 years of experience), blinded to clinical outcomes, independently reviewed the MR images. Any discrepancies were resolved by consensus. The following features were recorded: tumor location and size, number of lesions (dominant mass with or without satellite nodules), margin (well-defined vs. ill-defined), intratumoral hemorrhage (T1 hyperintensity), necrosis/cystic degeneration (T2 hyperintensity without enhancement, necrosis was considered significant when it involved more than 50% of the tumor area on the largest cross-sectional slice), diffusion restriction on DWI, enhancement pattern (peripheral rim enhancement, progressive enhancement, persistent enhancement, or washout), biliary dilatation, vascular invasion/tumor thrombus, regional lymphadenopathy (short-axis diameter ≥ 10 mm), and evidence of distant metastasis. Because of the very small sample size, formal interobserver agreement analysis was not performed. In addition, MRI findings were descriptively compared with the corresponding surgical pathology findings on a case-by-case basis to explore radiologic–pathologic correlations.

Pathologic analysis

All cases were confirmed by postoperative histopathologic examination. The diagnosis of intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma with sarcomatoid differentiation (s-iCCA) was established based on the presence of both conventional adenocarcinoma components and sarcomatoid components composed of spindle or pleomorphic tumor cells on histopathological examination. Immunohistochemical staining was used to support the diagnosis, demonstrating epithelial markers (e.g., CK7, CK19) in the adenocarcinoma component and mesenchymal markers (e.g., vimentin) in the sarcomatoid component [11]. All pathological diagnoses were reviewed by two experienced pathologists, and a consensus was reached in all cases.

Statistical analysis

Given the small cohort, only descriptive statistics are reported. Continuous variables are summarized as mean (range) and categorical variables as counts.

Three patients were asymptomatic at presentation, with lesions detected incidentally during routine health examinations or imaging surveillance. The remaining two patients presented with abdominal pain or discomfort. Regarding underlying liver disease, chronic hepatitis B infection was present in three patients and cirrhosis in one. No patient had a history of gallstones or intrahepatic lithiasis.

Serum CA 19-9 levels were elevated in all patients (range, 31.7–927 U/mL), whereas AFP and CEA levels remained within the reference range in all tested patients. Liver function was generally preserved, with only mild elevations of γ-glutamyl transpeptidase or alkaline phosphatase observed in a minority of cases.

All patients underwent surgical resection. Four subsequently received adjuvant chemotherapy, whereas one was managed with postoperative surveillance alone. Histopathologic examination demonstrated both adenocarcinoma and sarcomatoid components in all tumors. The extent of sarcomatoid differentiation varied among cases, ranging from focal involvement to a predominant sarcomatoid component (~ 98%) in one patient. During follow-up, two patients developed intrahepatic recurrence or distant metastases.

Preoperatively, four lesions were interpreted as conventional intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma on imaging, whereas one was reported as a nonspecific malignant hepatic lesion without a definitive radiologic diagnosis.

Central non-enhancing regions corresponding to tumor necrosis were present in three cases and persisted throughout all post-contrast phases. Radiologic–pathologic correlation

A case-by-case comparison between MRI findings and surgical pathology was performed to explore the relationship between tumor composition and imaging appearance (Table 4). In several cases, extensive non-enhancing regions on MRI corresponded to pathologically confirmed tumor necrosis. Case 4 demonstrated the greatest degree of intratumoral heterogeneity and internal complexity on MRI and was pathologically composed predominantly of sarcomatoid carcinoma (~98%), with only a minor conventional cholangiocarcinoma component (~2%). In contrast, tumors with focal or moderate sarcomatoid differentiation generally exhibited less conspicuous heterogeneity.

Discussion

In this retrospective MRI-based case series of five surgically resected patients with s-iCCA, the most consistent imaging findings were a dominant mass-forming lesion, T1 hypointensity, heterogeneous T2 hyperintensity, marked diffusion restriction, arterial peripheral rim enhancement, and progressive persistent enhancement without washout. These findings are generally consistent with the limited imaging observations previously reported for s-iCCA [10, 12] and substantially overlap with those of conventional mass-forming intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma. However, extensive necrosis or cystic degeneration, marked intratumoral heterogeneity, and peripheral-predominant diffusion restriction were repeatedly observed in our cohort. Although these features are not specific, their presence in combination may raise suspicion for sarcomatoid differentiation and prompt consideration of s-iCCA in selected cases.

The clinical presentation of s-iCCA in our cohort was nonspecific, which is consistent with previous reports. Three patients were asymptomatic and were incidentally diagnosed, whereas only two presented with abdominal pain or discomfort. Chronic hepatitis B virus infection was present in three patients, supporting earlier observations that viral hepatitis may be associated with sarcomatoid transformation. Previous studies have also suggested that prior anticancer treatments, including transarterial chemoembolization, radiofrequency ablation, and percutaneous ethanol injection, may contribute to sarcomatoid transformation through epithelial–mesenchymal transition [13, 14]. However, none of our patients had received locoregional or systemic anticancer treatment before MRI, and a causal relationship between prior therapy and sarcomatoid differentiation remains unproven [15].

Serum tumor markers were similarly nonspecific. Although CA 19 − 9 was elevated in all patients, AFP and CEA remained within normal limits in tested patients. These findings are broadly consistent with previous reports and suggest that conventional laboratory markers alone are unlikely to distinguish s-iCCA from conventional iCCA [16–18]. Consequently, imaging remains particularly important in raising suspicion for this rare and aggressive subtype. Given the limited MRI literature on s-iCCA, comparison with conventional intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma may provide useful context for interpreting the imaging findings observed in our cohort.

Existing imaging literature on s-iCCA has largely focused on ultrasonography and CT [9, 18, 19]. Reported CT findings include a hypoattenuating hepatic mass with peripheral or heterogeneous enhancement and frequent intratumoral necrosis, but these findings are nonspecific and may overlap with other primary or secondary liver malignancies [9, 15, 20, 21]. By contrast, systematic MRI descriptions of s-iCCA remain scarce. The limited available literature has reported several recurring imaging findings, including heterogeneous signal intensity, extensive necrosis or cystic degeneration, restricted diffusion, peripheral or rim-like arterial enhancement, and progressive delayed enhancement [1, 22]. However, most published studies have consisted of isolated case reports or small case series and have primarily focused on describing imaging appearances rather than systematically evaluating how these findings compare with those of conventional mass-forming iCCA. Consequently, the imaging features that may raise suspicion for sarcomatoid differentiation, beyond those shared with conventional iCCA, remain incompletely characterized. Building upon these previous observations, we further analyzed the MRI features of our cohort.

To better contextualize the imaging manifestations of this rare tumor subtype, we compared our findings with those reported for conventional mass-forming iCCA in the literature (Table 5). Conventional mass-forming iCCA typically appears as a T1-hypointense and heterogeneous T2-hyperintense mass with irregular peripheral/rim arterial enhancement followed by progressive central or delayed enhancement. Kovač et al. summarized these typical MRI features and emphasized that the imaging appearance of mass-forming iCCA reflects fibrous stroma, viable peripheral tumor cells, mucin, and necrosis [23]. Granata et al. also reported that T1/T2 signal intensity, diffusion restriction, and dynamic enhancement patterns are useful MRI features for characterizing mass-forming iCCA [24]. In addition, Promsorn et al. evaluated diffusion-weighted imaging in 73 patients with intrahepatic mass-forming cholangiocarcinoma and showed that the extent of diffusion restriction may have prognostic relevance [25]. These studies indicate that many MRI findings observed in our s-iCCA cases are not unique and overlap substantially with conventional mass-forming iCCA. However, the combination of extensive necrosis or cystic degeneration, marked intratumoral heterogeneity, and peripheral-predominant diffusion restriction appeared repeatedly in our series and may serve as ancillary clues for considering sarcomatoid differentiation, rather than as definitive diagnostic criteria.

Dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI revealed several notable features. Most tumors showed peripheral or rim-like enhancement in the arterial phase. Progressive and persistent enhancement was subsequently observed in the portal venous and delayed phases, without washout. This enhancement pattern is broadly similar to that of conventional mass-forming iCCA [26]. However, in our cases, enhancement was often more uneven and incomplete, with fragmented or irregular distribution. Central non-enhancing areas, which matched tumor necrosis, were present and persisted in later phases. In contrast to hepatocellular carcinoma [27, 28], none of the tumors exhibited arterial hyperenhancement followed by washout. In the appropriate clinical and imaging context, a progressive yet incomplete enhancement pattern, particularly when associated with extensive necrosis, may raise suspicion for s-iCCA. While prior studies have emphasized the lack of specificity of peripheral enhancement and necrosis on CT [29, 30], our MRI-based observations suggest that the combination of rim-like enhancement, progressive but incomplete centripetal filling, and persistent central nonenhancement may provide complementary diagnostic clues.

Extensive intratumoral necrosis was a common finding in our cases and was seen in three of five patients. On MRI, necrotic areas appeared as markedly hyperintense regions on T2-weighted images and remained nonenhancing throughout all dynamic phases. In some cases, necrotic areas were larger than the enhancing solid parts. Although necrosis can occur in conventional iCCA [31], the very large and repeated necrotic areas in our cases may reflect rapid tumor growth and insufficient vascular supply associated with sarcomatoid differentiation. Therefore, when MRI shows a bile duct–origin liver tumor with an unusually large amount of necrosis, sarcomatoid differentiation may be considered as a potential diagnostic possibility [32]. This observation is concordant with prior clinical and pathologic reports describing the aggressive and necrosis-prone nature of sarcomatoid carcinomas [21].

Diffusion-weighted imaging provided additional insights into tumor characteristics. All tumors showed strong hyperintensity on DWI with relatively low ADC values, ranging from 1.34 to 1.66 × 10⁻³ mm²/s, which suggests high cellularity. However, these values were not lower than those reported for conventional iCCA, which may partly be related to our use of whole-lesion ROI measurements without exclusion of necrotic areas. Compared with conventional mass-forming iCCA, in which diffusion restriction is often more diffusely distributed throughout the tumor [33], our cases showed uneven diffusion restriction that was mainly located at the tumor edge, with less restriction in the necrotic center. In several patients, the area of diffusion restriction was larger than the enhancing viable tissue on contrast images, which led to a peripheral diffusion–enhancement mismatch. This pattern may reflect active tumor growth at the invasive margin around central necrosis, which fits with the aggressive nature of sarcomatoid change. Therefore, strong peripheral diffusion restriction in a necrotic mass-forming lesion may raise suspicion for s-iCCA in the appropriate clinical context.

Despite their aggressive imaging appearance, two tumors in our cohort demonstrated segmental intrahepatic bile duct dilatation. Although bile duct dilatation is not uniformly present in conventional mass-forming iCCA, its presence may still support a cholangiocytic origin, particularly when imaging findings are otherwise highly heterogeneous. Therefore, bile duct involvement may represent a useful ancillary clue suggesting biliary rather than hepatocellular differentiation [34].

The radiologic–pathologic correlations observed in our cohort provide additional insight into the imaging manifestations of s-iCCA. The marked imaging heterogeneity may reflect its mixed histopathologic composition, which contains both epithelial adenocarcinoma and mesenchymal sarcomatoid components [35]. Compared with conventional mass-forming iCCA, the pronounced heterogeneity and extensive necrotic change observed in some s-iCCA cases may reflect the coexistence of epithelial and sarcomatoid elements. Although these observations require validation in larger cohorts, they suggest that tumor composition may contribute to some of the imaging differences between s-iCCA and conventional iCCA.

Although preoperative recognition of s-iCCA may not directly alter standard surgical management, it may have potential clinical implications. Given its aggressive biological behavior and poor prognosis, early suspicion may aid in risk stratification and closer postoperative surveillance.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size and single-center retrospective design limit the generalizability of our findings. Second, the rarity of s-iCCA precluded comparison with a matched cohort of conventional iCCA or other hepatic malignancies. Third, follow-up time was short in some patients, which limited analysis of long-term outcomes and imaging–prognosis relationships. Fourth, interobserver agreement was not formally assessed because of the very small sample size. Larger, multicenter studies are needed to validate these observations.

Conclusion

S-iCCA is rare but clinically aggressive. On MRI, it may present as a mass-forming lesion with prominent necrosis, marked diffusion restriction, and peripheral rim or progressive persistent enhancement. These findings may raise suspicion for s-iCCA, although histopathologic confirmation remains necessary.
