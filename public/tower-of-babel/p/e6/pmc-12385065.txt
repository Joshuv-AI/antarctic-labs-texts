Leukemia inhibitory factor promotes human cholangiopathies, and its inhibition improves cholestasis in Abcb4 −/− mice

ABSTRACT
Background:Primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC) and primary biliary cholangitis (PBC) are immune-mediated cholestatic disorders characterized by progressive biliary inflammation and fibrosis, for which treatment options remain limited, underscoring the need for novel therapeutic targets. The leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF) is an IL-6-related cytokine that dysregulates the communication between epithelial cells and extracellular matrices by binding a heterodimeric complex formed by LIF receptor (LIFR) and gp130. The role of the LIF/LIFR system in PSC and PBC and its potential as a therapeutic target remain unclear.Methods:We investigated LIF/LIFR system alteration in PSC and PBC and assessed the therapeutic potential of LIFR antagonism in a genetic mouse model of PSC (Abcb4-/- mice). Single-cell transcriptomics analyses were performed to evaluate LIF and LIFR expression in human liver samples. Whole liver RNA-seq and immunostaining were used to assess LIF/LIFR levels and correlation with fibrotic and immune markers. The effects of LIFR antagonism were evaluated in vitro using LRI-310, a steroidal LIFR antagonist, on human cholangiocytes, HSCs, endothelial cells, and macrophages. In vivo, LRI-310 was administered to Abcb4–/– mice, and effects on liver injury, cholestasis, fibrosis, leukocyte infiltration, and gene expression were assessed.Results:LIF expression s enriched in human cholangiocytes, while LIFR is predominantly expressed by HSCs, endothelial cells, and macrophages. Whole liver RNAseq analysis and liver sections immunostaining demonstarted that increased LIF expression correlates with expression of markers of hepatic fibrosis and immune activation in PSC and PBC patients. LRI-310, a steroidal LIFR antagonist, attenuated human cholangiocytes activation and expression of inflammatory mediators, as well as the activation of liver sinusoidal cells and hepatic fibroblasts. In Abcb4 −/− mice administration of LRI-310 mitigated liver injury, cholestasis, liver leukocytes infiltration and reduced the expression of biomarkers associated with fibrosis, inflammation, and bile acid dismetabolism.Conclusion:Cholangiocyte-derived LIF promotes the formation of a pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic niche centred on damaged cholangiocytes. LIFR antagonism reverses fibrosis and immune dysregulation in Abcb4 −/− mice, supporting the development of anti-LIFR therapies in human cholangiopathies.

INTRODUCTION

Primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC) and primary biliary cholangitis (PBC) are 2 chronic immune-mediated cholestatic liver disorders characterized by inflammation-driven destruction/proliferation of cholangiocytes leading to progressive cholestasis, biliary fibrosis, and cirrhosis.1 PBC typically involves exclusively intrahepatic bile ducts, while PSC primarily involves large bile ducts and carries an increased risk of developing a cholangiocarcinoma.23 Although several therapies are currently available for the treatment of PBC,45 including ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA), as first-line therapy, and obeticholic acid6 alone or in combination with fibrates,7 or peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPARs) agonists such as elafibranor8 and seladelpar59 as second-line therapies, treatment of PSC remains suboptimal,10 and none of the available treatments improve the overall patient survival or transplant-free survival.111213

While PBC and PSC are considered immune-mediated disorders, the mechanisms that govern the formation of a pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic niche centered on damaged cholangiocytes remain poorly defined.14 The leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF) is a member of the interleukin (IL)-6 cytokine family secreted by epithelial cells and monocytes.15 LIF exerts multiple regulatory functions, including differentiation, proliferation, and survival in embryo and adult cells,16 and it is involved in cancer growth and invasiveness, driving epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) in pancreatic,17 colon, and gastric adenocarcinomas.16 In target cells, LIF binds to a heterodimeric complex formed by the LIF receptor (LIFR) and the glycoprotein (gp) 130.18 Upon binding by LIF, the LIFR/gp130 heterodimer, that is also activated by oncostatin M (OSM), promotes the phosphorylation of the signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT)3 that is central in regulating the immune response and is activated in the majority of human cancers1920 LIFR may also participate to the formation of tripartite receptor complexes with gp130 and the ciliary neurotrophic factor receptor activated by cardiotrophin 1 (CT-1), cardiotrophin-like cytokine factor 1 (CLCF1), ciliary neurotrophic factor (CNTF), and neuropoietin (NP),19 highlighting the potential role of this system in a number of human disorders.21 We have recently shown that microbial-derived bile acids, lithocholic and deoxycholic (LCA and DCA) and their isoforms, might function as endogenous antagonists of LIFR, preventing its activation and reversing some of the effects exerted by this receptor.15

LIF and LIFR are both expressed in the liver, and previous studies have suggested a potential role for the LIF-LIFR/gp130 pathway in regulating cholangiocytes' proliferation, apoptosis, and fibrosis development in models of mechanically induced cholestasis.22 Adding to this concept, selective deletion of sortilin, a key component of the LIFR/gp130 receptor complex, in biliary epithelial cells (BECs), impairs bile duct morphogenesis and diminishes the development of fibrosis in models of cholestasis.23 Further highlighting the potential role of LIF/LIFR in fibrosis, LIF expression is upregulated in other fibrotic diseases, and silencing of LIFR reduced lung and kidney fibrosis.24

Based on this background, we have embarked on a detailed characterization of LIF/LIFR expression in PBC and PSC patients and developed a selective LIFR antagonist that effectively reverses cholestasis in Abcb4 − / − mice.

METHODS

Human data

Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis of healthy human livers

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) from healthy liver samples was downloaded from the GSE115469 dataset, which comprises the transcriptional profile of 8444 cells obtained from the fractionation of fresh hepatic tissue from 5 healthy human livers, and data processing was done as previously described in the original study.25 The classification of the main cells types was carried out as previously described26 using the following known cell markers: hepatocytes (HPX, LBP, HPR, and SERPINA10); cholangiocytes (KRT19, KRT7, and FXYD2); endothelial cells (CD34, JAM2, and VWF); HSCs (COL1A2, LUM, and DCN); macrophages (CD68, CD163, and MSR1); B cells (CD19, FCRL5, and CD79A); NK cells (KLRF1 and NCAM1); and T cells (CD3D, CD3E, and CD3G).

Gene expression analysis in PSC and PBC patients and controls

Total RNA from human liver samples was collected from patients with PSC and PBC at the Medical University of Warsaw in Warsaw (Poland), and control liver samples were collected from Donostia University Hospital in San Sebastian (Spain) (permit number KB-0012/129/13). Expression levels were analyzed by reverse transcription and quantitative real-time PCR (q-PCR), as previously described (35750136 and 35217064). cDNA was synthesized using the SuperScript VILO cDNA Synthesis Kit (Invitrogen), followed by reverse transcription PCR in a Veriti Thermal Cycler (Applied Biosystems), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The gene expression (mRNA) of specific primer sequences was determined by real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) using iQ SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad) in a CFX384 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection as previously described. Expression of ribosomal protein L22 (RPL22) served as an endogenous control.

LIF immunostaining

Immunofluorescence staining of LIF and CK19 (see Supplemental Materials and Methods, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C62) was carried out in liver biopsies from PBC and PSC patients who were collected at the Perugia University Hospital (Perugia, Italy), and informed written consent was obtained from all patients included in the study in accordance with permission no. 357803.

The GSE159676 series

The GSE159676 dataset comprises RNA expression data obtained from microarray experiments conducted on liver tissue samples. RNA was extracted from fresh frozen tissue obtained from explanted livers or diagnostic liver biopsies from 21 subjects. Specifically, the dataset includes normal human liver tissue (tumor-free tissue from livers with metastasis from colorectal cancer) (n=6) and liver tissue from patients with chronic liver diseases, including PSC (n=12) and PBC (n=3). These samples allow comparative transcriptomic analyses between normal and chronic liver disease conditions.

Mice models of cholestasis

Abcb4 −/− mice, along with their C57BL/6 congenic littermates, aged 2–8 months, were used in this study. The experimental protocols were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Perugia, the Italian Minister of Health and the Instituto Superiore di Sanità (Italy), and were in agreement with the European guidelines (Directive 2010/63/EU) for the use of experimental animals (permission no. 727/2023–PR). The general health of the animals was monitored daily by the veterinarian in the animal facility. The mice were maintained under controlled environmental conditions and provided with unrestricted access to food and water. Two experiments of cholestasis were carried out: in the first, naïve Abcb4 +/+ and Abcb4 −/− mice aged 2 months were followed up until 5 or 8 months. In a second experiment, 5-month-old Abcb4 −/− mice were treated with 30 mg/kg LRI-310 and followed for 2 months.

Immortalized human cholangiocytes (H69) and normal human cholangiocytes

SV40-immortalized human bile duct epithelial cell line (H69 cells) and primary culture of normal human cholangiocytes (NHCs) were provided by Prof Jesus M. Banales, as characterized as described.2728

Immortalized human HSCs (LX-2)

LX-2 cells were provided by Cytion (reference: 305039) and they were grown in DMEM high glucose medium (Euroclone, reference: ECB7501L) enriched with 2% fetal bovine serum, 1% L-glutamine, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin.

Human hepatic sinusoidal endothelial cells (L-SEC)

L-SEC cells were from Innoprot (reference: P10652) and they were grown in fibronectin-coated T75 flask (Gibco, reference: 33010018) with their recommended endothelial cell medium, supplied by 5% fetal bovine serum, 1% endothelial cell growth supplement (ECGS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin solution (Innoprot, reference: P60104).

Human monocytes

Monocytes were isolated from human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), obtained as described previously.29

RESULTS

Assessment of LIF/LIFR in human cholangiopathies

To investigate the role of LIF and its receptor (LIFR) in cholestatic liver disease, we first investigated the expression of this cytokine and its receptor using single-cell transcriptomic data from the GSE115469 repository, alongside 3 independent cohorts of PBC and PSC patients (Figure 1). Single-cell analysis revealed that LIF expression is robustly enriched in cholangiocytes and NK cells, whereas LIFR expression (Figure 1A) is enriched in non-parenchymal liver cells such as endothelial cells, HSCs and macrophages.

We have then assessed whether expression of LIF/LIFR is modulated in cholestasis. For this purpose, we have examined liver samples from 3 independent cohorts of PSC and PBC patients. The first cohort included 9 PSC and 9 PBC patients and 9 age and sex matched non-PSC/non-PBC subjects (Figure 1B). The second cohort, used for immunohistochemistry studies, was formed by 5 PBC patients and 3 PSC patients (Tables 1 and Figure 1C). Finally, a third validation cohort consisting of 12 PSC, 3 PBC patients and healthy controls from the GSE159676 repository was used (Figure 1D). As shown in Figure 1B, a significant increase in LIF gene expression (Figures 1B–D) was detected in the whole liver tissue from PSC and PBC patients compared to healthy controls. Consistent with single-cell analysis, LIF expression was elevated in KRT7-positive bile duct cells, as confirmed by immunofluorescence of PBC and PSC hepatic biopsies (Figure 1C). Given that results from single-cell analysis suggested a role for LIF/LIFR in the cross-talk between cholangiocytes and non-parenchymal hepatic cells, we then queried the GSE159676 dataset to determine whether LIF/LIFR expression correlates with the expression of specific markers for cholangiocytes, endothelial, stellate, and immune cells (Figure 1E). Our expression/correlation analysis revealed that: (a) the expression of cholangiocytes markers, EPCAM, KRT19, and KRT7, was increased in PSC livers along with the expression of markers of endothelial differentiation, CDXC3CL1, CXCL16, and PECAM1, and fibrotic markers such as COL1A1 and TGFB2 (Figure 1D); (b) the expression of markers of monocytes, NK cells, and T cell subsets was increased in PSC patients (Figure 1E); (c) the expression of LIF positively correlated with the expression of markers of cholangiocytes, endothelial cells, fibrotic markers, and among the immune cells, with macrophages, CD4+ T and nTregs (Figure 1F).

A similar pattern of expression was observed in Abcb4 −/− mice, a murine model of PSC.29 For these purposes, 2-month-old and 8-month-old Abcb4 −/− mice and congenic littermates were used (Figure 2). While no significant differences in body weight were detected between the groups (Figure 2A), Abcb4 −/− mice developed a significant leukocytosis (white blood cells count, WBC) in comparison to the same-age control mice (Figure 2B) along with biochemical features of cholestasis as shown by increased levels of AST (U/L), ALT (U/L), bilirubin (mg/mL), and ALP (King unit/100 mL) (Figure 2C), which worsened with age. These biochemical changes are associated with bile duct proliferation, a dense inflammatory infiltrate, hepatocyte degeneration (Figure 2D), and fibrosis, which worsened with age (Figure 2E). Staining for CK19, a cholangiocyte biomarker, was robustly increased in the liver of Abcb4 −/− mice, confirming the cholangiocyte proliferation (Figure 2F). Immunofluorescence analysis of LIF expression (red) demonstrated that this cytokine is enriched in bile epithelial cells (BECs) (Figure 2G) and in the proximity to CK19-positive intrahepatic bile ducts, as shown in Figure 2F. In contrast, the expression of LIFR (green) was not significantly modulated in intrahepatic BECs in either 5-month-old or 8-month-old Abcb4 −/− mice (Figure 2H and Supplemental Figure S1, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C62), thus confirming the immunofluorescence analysis carried out in PBC/PSC samples (Figure 1C). The transcriptome analysis (RNA-seq) conducted on liver tissue of Abcb4 +/+ (n=5), Abcb4 −/− at 5 months (n=4) and 8 months old (n=3) (Supplemental Figure S2, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C62) demonstrated major differences between the 2 strains in the PCA analysis (Supplemental Figure S2A, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C62). The Venn diagram analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) revealed that 1425 transcripts were differentially modulated in Abcb4 −/− 5-month-old mice compared to Abcb4 +/+ naïve mice, while 2457 were differentially modulated in Abcb4 −/− 8 months in comparison to Abcb4 +/+ naïve mice (Supplemental Figure S2B-F, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C62). Supporting histologic findings, fibrotic markers such as Tgfb2, Col1a1, and Α-Sma were upregulated in Abcb4 −/− in a time-dependent manner (Supplemental Figure S2G, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C62). The Lif gene expression (Log2) was significantly upregulated in Abcb4 −/− mice while the Lifr gene (Log2) remained largely unchanged during the course of the disease (Supplemental Figure S2, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C62), supporting the immunofluorescence results.

In addition, exposure of NHC and H69 cells to 100 ng/mL of LPS for 24 hours markedly increased the expression of LIF (red) in both cell lines (Figure 3F) while reducing LIFR (green) expression in NHC but not in H69 (Figure 3E). Furthermore, the exposure of NHC to LPS promoted a generalized inflammatory response, as shown by the upregulation of IL-8, IL-6, CCL2 and ICAM-1 mRNAs (Figure 3H).

LIFR antagonism attenuates cholangiocyte activation and leukocyte recruitment

Because these data suggest a potential role of LIF in recruiting endothelial cells, myofibroblasts, and macrophages at the biliary interface, we then queried whether the antagonism of LIFR suffices to disassemble the formation of this inflammatory niche. For this purpose, we developed a novel small-molecule LIFR antagonist, the LIFR-inhibitor (LRI)-310 (Figure 4A, Table 2), and tested it in vitro and in Abcb4 −/− mice. As shown in Figures 4B, C, LRI-310 effectively inhibits LIF/LIFR interaction with an IC50 of 0.4 µM in the AlphaScreen assay and reverses the LIF-induced STAT3 phosphorylation in a concentration-dependent manner, indicating that this agent is an LIFR antagonist and blunts the downstream signaling activated by LIF.

Exploiting LRI-310, then, we have investigated whether LIFR inhibition suffices to prevent recruitment of non-parenchymal liver cells by damaged cholangiocytes in vitro. Results of these studies demonstrated that exposure of LPS-primed NHC to 1 µM of LRI-310 effectively reversed cholangiocytes activation (Supplemental Figure S3A, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C62) and counteracted the development of a pro-inflammatory phenotype by reducing the expression of inflammatory biomarkers, including IL-8, IL-6, CCL2, ICAM-1, as well as its own expression (Supplemental Figure S3A, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C62). Given that, in addition to cholangiocytes, LIFR is expressed by macrophages,30 HSCs (hSteC, LX-2) and hepatic endotheliocytes (Figure 3B and Supplemental Figure 3B), and the assembly of a pro-inflammatory niche around damaged cholangiocytes, requires the recruitment of various non-parenchymal liver cells, we have then investigated whether inflamed cholangiocytes promote the activation of macrophages and stellate cells, and whether this effect could be reversed by LIFR antagonism.

As illustrated in Figures 4D–F, the exposure of CD14+ macrophages isolated from healthy donors’ buffy coats (Supplemental Figure S4, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C62), and LX-2 cells to conditioned media harvested from LPS-primed NHCs activated a pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic program as demonstrated by a robust increase in the expression of CD38, TNFα, IL1β, and IL-6 mRNAs in macrophages (Figure 4E) and COL1A1, TGFβ, αSMA, and TIMP1 in LX-2 cells (Figure 4F). This pattern was reversed by 1 µM of LRI-310 treatment, which induced a polarization of M2 macrophages through the upregulation of IL-10 as well as an increase in MMP9 in LX-2 cells (Figures 4E, F). Moreover, the downregulation of LIFR expression was reversed by LRI-310. Thus, LIFR antagonism suffices to mitigate cholangiocyte inflammation and cell recruitment at the cholangiocytes–macrophages–fibroblasts interface.

LIFR antagonism counteracts disease development in Abcb4 −/− mice

We have then investigated whether LIFR antagonism attenuates disease development in a mouse model of cholestasis. For this purpose, 5-month-old Abcb4 −/− mice were administered with 30 mg/kg LRI-310 (n=7) or left untreated (n=10) and monitored until the age of 7 months. Wild-type mice (n=8) were used as controls. As shown in Figure 5A, there were no significant differences in body weight changes among the 3 experimental groups over time. Biochemical analyses indicated that AST, ALT, ALP, and bilirubin plasma levels were significantly higher in naïve Abcb4 −/− mice compared to their wild-type counterparts (Figure 5B) and that treating Abcb4 −/− mice with LRI-310 effectively reduced AST, ALT, ALP, and bilirubin levels (Figure 5B). Although the number of WBC increased with treatment, the percentage of neutrophils was reduced by LIFR antagonism (Figure 5C). Untreated Abcb4 −/− mice developed PSC-like features at the histopathology examination, including accumulation of inflammatory cells in the proximity of bile ducts and extending into the hepatic parenchyma, liver fibrosis (Sirius red staining) and bile duct proliferation (Cytokeratin 19 immunostaining) (Figures 5D–F). Treatment with LRI-310 effectively attenuated these changes (Figures 5D–F). To gain further insights into the transcriptional profile promoted by the LIFR antagonism, we performed an AmpliSeq Transcriptome analysis (RNA-seq) on total livers of Abcb4 −/− mice and Abcb4 −/− mice treated with LRI-310, and compared them with wild-type littermates. The PCA analysis (Figure 6A) revealed major dissimilarities between all 3 experimental groups. Abcb4+/+ naïve (black circles) were spatially located on the left side of the PCA plot, distinctly separated from the Abcb4 −/− groups. Within the Abcb4 −/− mice, naïve animals (purple circles) were clustered at the bottom right, while those treated with the LIFR antagonist (light blue circles) were positioned at the top left. Venn diagram analysis of differentially expressed transcripts, revealed that 1896 transcripts were differentially modulated in Abcb4 −/− mice compared to Abcb4 +/+ naïve mice (blue circle), while only 491 transcripts were exclusively modulated by LRI-310 treatment (pink circle), the intersection between the 2 circles included 196 common transcripts that were modulated in Abcb4 −/− mice treated or not treated with LRI-310, highlighting the extent of gene expression changes due to LIFR antagonism (Figure 6B). In detail, in comparison to their wild-type littermates, RNA transcripts from Abcb4 −/− mice were robustly enriched for genes involved in fibrosis, inflammation, and metabolism. Consistent with previous studies,29 as illustrated in Supplemental Figure S2, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C62, the most upregulated genes in comparison to wild-type mice were Canexin (Canx), followed by Neurotrophic receptor tyrosine kinase 2 (Ntrk2) and Lymphocyte Antigen 6 Family Member D (Lyd6), suggesting an activated immune response, consistent with chronic inflammation and immune cell recruitment. Moreover, upregulation of matrix metalloproteinase 7 (Mmp7) and E74-like factor 3 (Elf3) indicates enhanced extracellular matrix remodeling and epithelial activation,29. Conversely, expression of genes such as peroxisomal biogenesis factor 16 (Pex16),31 sulfide quinone reductase-like protein (Sqrdl),32 and 3-beta-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 5 (Hsd3b5),33 reflecting impairments in metabolic and disruptions in peroxisomal and mitochondrial functions, likely contribute to oxidative damage and metabolic imbalance in cholestatic livers (Figure 6D, left). The upregulation and downregulation of these genes were lost in Abcbc4 −/− mice administered with LRI-310, and a distinct set of genes appeared modulated by LIFR antagonism. Specifically, the upregulation of DNA damage-inducible transcript 4 (Ddit4),34 insulin-like growth factor-binding protein 1 (Igfbp1),35 NIPA-like domain-containing protein 1 (Nipal1),36 indicated a better adaptive response to cellular stress and metabolic demands (Figure 6D, right). Furthermore, we observed a robust regulation of nuclear receptor subfamily 0, group B, member 2 (Nr0b2—also known as Shp), a critical downstream target of the primary bile acid sensor, the Farnesoid X receptor (FXR).37 Since Shp is critically involved in repressing Cyp7a1 (ie, the bile acid synthesis), this finding highlights the potential of targeting the LIF–LIFR axis as a therapeutic strategy to restore bile acid homeostasis in cholangiopathies.

The per pathway analysis of these differentially expressed genes was performed using the TAC software (Affimetrix) to further dissect the molecular pathways underlining disease development (Figure 6E, top) and the effect of LIFR antagonism (Figure 6E, bottom). The analysis demonstrated that LRI-310 downregulated several pathways in inflammation, fibrosis, and cellular proliferation, while upregulating amino acid metabolism. The heatmap shown in Figure 7F summarizes the fold changes in target genes involved in the LIF–LIFR axis. Treatment of Abcb4 −/− mice with LRI-310 led to the downregulation of key inflammatory mediators, including Lif, IL-6, Ccl2, and Cxcl2, highlighting the anti-inflammatory effects of LIFR antagonism. In addition, the proliferation markers Krt19 and Krt7, which are associated with bile duct epithelial activation, were also downregulated, suggesting a reduction in intrahepatic cholangiocyte proliferation (Supllemental Figure 5). The expression of markers of endothelial cell activation, such as Pecam1, and the fibrotic marker Col1a1, was decreased by LRI-310 treatment (Figure 7F). These findings collectively demonstrate the therapeutic potential of targeting the LIF–LIFR axis in modulating inflammation, proliferation, and fibrosis in cholestatic liver disease.

Of these, 8 populations were classified as LIFRhigh and 4 as LIFR+ immune cells. While the overall percentage of LIFR+ cells remained unchanged across the experimental groups when considering all 12 populations together (Figure 7E), a significant reduction in LIFRhigh cells was observed in Abcb4 −/− naïve mice compared to their wild-type counterparts (Figure 7F). LIFR antagonism remodels the bile acid pool in Abcb4 −/− mice

RNA-seq analysis of the liver transcriptome across the 3 experimental groups highlighted significant transcriptional adaptations in response to cholestasis in Abcb4 −/− mice. These alterations include a downregulation of Shp and an upregulation of Cyp7a1 and Cyp2c70, 2 key genes was protein products are responsible for bile acid synthesis and hydroxylation. These transcriptional shifts suggest an attempt to compensate for cholestatic stress by increasing bile acid synthesis along with the simultaneous downregulation of regulatory feedback mechanisms. In addition, an upregulation of efflux transporters Mrp4 38 and Mrp5, as well as the bile acid reabsorption transporter Asbt, occurred in cholestatic mice. Since these genes are positively (Shp) or negatively (Cyp7a1) regulated by FXR,37 these findings are consistent with the development of defective FXR signaling in Abcb4 −/− mice. These changes were mitigated by LRI-310, suggesting a partial restoration of bile acid homeostasis (Figure 8A). Quantitative LC–MS/MS analysis of bile acid composition in various compartments of Abcb4 −/− mice was consistent with the above-mentioned molecular changes. Abcb4 −/− mice were characterized by a significant accumulation of bile acids in the plasma and liver (Figures 8B, C). In contrast, fecal bile acid excretion was markedly reduced (Figure 8D). Further analysis demonstrated increased levels of primary bile acids (CA, CDCA, and MCAs) in both plasma and liver, while excretion of secondary bile acids was significantly diminished, resulting in an overall shift toward a higher primary-to-secondary bile acid ratio across different compartments. Treating mice with LRI-310 reversed these changes and increased fecal bile acid output. Plasma levels of tαMu, tβMu, and tCDCA were higher in naïve Abcb4 −/− mice compared to wild-type littermates. LIFR antagonism effectively reduced tCDCA levels (Supplemental Figure S8, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C62). Moreover, the hepatic content of tβMu, gCA, and tHCA was significantly elevated in untreated Abcb4 −/− mice compared to wild-type controls. The administration of LRI-310 reduced tβMu and gCA levels, suggesting a beneficial effect on bile acid metabolism (Supplemental Figure S9, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C62). The fecal excretion of both primary and secondary bile acids (LCA, 3-oxo-allo-LCA, and d5,6 LCA) was reduced in Abcb4 −/− mice, although LRI-310 administration restored the primary-to-secondary bile acid ratio in feces (Supplemental Figure S10, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C62). These findings indicate that while obstructive cholestasis in Abcb4 −/− mice leads to profound disturbances in bile acid metabolism, closely resembling the dysregulation observed in PSC patients, LIFR antagonism effectively reverses this pattern.

DISCUSSION

In the present study, we have identified LIF as a cholangiocyte-restricted cytokine and provided evidence that the LIF/LIFR system is modulated in PSC and PBC patients. In addition, we have demonstrated that inhibiting this system reverses fibrosis and cholestasis in Abcb4 −/− mice. These results establish a mechanistic role for LIF in the pathogenesis of human cholangiopathies, providing a strong rationale for the development of LIFR antagonists as a novel therapeutic strategy for PSC and PBC.1739

Previous studies have shown that the LIF/LIFR system mediates communication between extracellular matrix components, playing a key role in liver fibrosis development and progression.2340 In this study, we present evidence that LIF exhibits a distinctive expression pattern, being the first cytokine prevalently expressed by cholangiocytes. Furthermore, the LIF expression is robustly induced in the CK19-positive cholangiocytes and peribiliary areas in patients with PBC and PSC, suggesting a contributory role to biliary injury by fostering a pro-inflammatory niche centered around inflamed cholangiocytes.41 Several of our findings support this conclusion: (a) By single-cell analysis, we have shown that in the normal liver, LIF expression occurs almost exclusively in cholangiocytes and NK cells, while expression of LIFR is detected in various non-parenchymal liver cells, including endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and HSCs. (b) The same pattern of expression was confirmed in human cell lines, with NHC cells, normal human cholangiocytes,42 expressing the highest level of LIF, and, as a slight variation with the normal liver, also the highest expression of LIFR. (c) The LIF /LIFR immunostaining of liver sections from livers obtained from PSC and PBC patients shows that LIF expression strongly colocalizes with CK19-positive cells. (d) The immune profiling of the liver transcriptome of PSC patients demonstrated that LIF expression correlates with the liver expression of markers for monocytes, NK cells, and T cell subsets.43 (e) By in vitro studies we have shown that exposure of biliary epithelial cells to LPS promotes a pro-inflammatory response characterized by increased expression of LIF and other pro-inflammatory and chemoattractant factors, such as ICAM-1, IL-6, IL-8, and CCL2,29 whose expression is increased in the liver of PBC and PSC patients.44 (f) Exposing liver non-parenchymal cell lines to supernatants obtained from LPS-primed cholangiocytes (NHC), resulted in a LIFR-dependent activation of macrophages and LX-2 cells, confirming the unique role of the LIF/LIFR system in the assembly of pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic niche around damaged cholangiocytes.1 Together, these data provide morphological and biochemical evidence that LIF and its receptor might play a mechanistic role in cholangiopathies by modulating the biliary and peribiliary immune landscape, promoting the recruitment of inflammatory cells and fibrosis.24

In the present study, we have reported the description of a novel and potent LIFR antagonist.45 LRI-310 was discovered by building on a previous developed chemical strategy46 that has allowed us to identify several potential LIFR antagonists1545 Using this selective LIFR antagonist we have shown that LIF blockade in vitro suffices to revert macrophages and LX-2 activation induced cholangiocytes supernatants, providing a pharmacological evidence of the central role of LIF in fostering the immune niche at the bile ducts interface.44 Further on, in vivo treatment with LRI-310 reversed the development of pathological and biochemical features of cholestasis in Abcb4 −/− mice, a model of PSC.47 Treatment with LRI-310 significantly reduced the liver expression of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, including Lif, Il-6, Ccl2, and Cxcl2, confirming its anti-inflammatory and immune-modulatory potential. Histological and transcriptomic analyses further demonstrated that pharmacological antagonism to LIFR attenuates bile ducts (CK19) proliferation, hepatic fibrosis, and leukocyte infiltration, with notable reductions in key fibrotic markers such as Col1a1 and Tgfb2.17 The potential for immune-regulation of pharmacological inhibition of LIFR in Abcb4 −/− mice was confirmed by the t-SNE analysis, since in vivo administration of LRI-310 reshaped the liver immune landscape by reducing the number of pro-inflammatory macrophages while increasing the percentage of regulatory T cells.30

We have also demonstrated that LIFR is expressed by hepatocytes. This might be of clinical relevance, since LIF overexpression has been correlated with the development of cachexia in oncology patients.48 Since LIF reduces the de novo lipogenesis, its inhibition is deemed useful in oncology settings.

In addition to the above-reported molecular and immune effects, LRI-310 improved the biochemical features of cholestasis and reshaped the bile acid metabolism by reversing dysregulation of genes encoding for bile acid synthetic enzymes and transporters.38 By RNA-seq analysis, we have shown that disease development in Abcb4 −/− mice associates with remodeling of the expression of bile acid biosynthetic genes,49 characterized by the downregulation of Nr0b2(Shp) 37 and upregulation of Cyp7a1 and Cyp2c70,50 suggesting a compensatory response to cholestatic stress by increasing bile acid synthesis while dampening regulatory feedback.51 In addition, the upregulation of bile acid transporters (Mrp4, Mrp5, and Asbt) highlights an attempt to maintain bile acid homeostasis under cholestatic conditions. These molecular alterations are associated with a profound dysregulation of bile acid composition, characterized by an accumulation of primary bile acids in the liver and plasma, impaired fecal excretion, and a shift toward a higher primary-to-secondary bile acid ratio. Such alteration impacts microbial composition by modifying the proportion of BSH-expressing bacteria as previously demonstrated.29 These changes mirror disturbances observed in PSC patients. Notably, LRI-310 treatment mitigated these transcriptional and metabolic alterations, reducing bile acid accumulation, restoring the primary-to-secondary bile acid balance, enhancing fecal bile acid clearance, and reducing tCDCA, tβMu, and gCA levels.29 These changes might contribute to the beneficial effects exerted by LRI-310, since secondary bile acids are relatively robust LIFR antagonists.15

Despite the present results being promising, the study has some limitations. First, while LRI-310 effectively reversed fibrosis and immune dysregulation in Abcb4 −/− mice, its therapeutic potential and its long-term efficacy and safety must be confirmed in clinical trials. Moreover, while the present study provides a strong foundation for the development of small-molecule anti-LIFR, further research is needed to fully characterize the intersection of LIF/LIFR signaling with other pro-fibrotic and inflammatory pathways. Future studies should explore not only the benefits of LRI-310 in additional models of cholestasis and liver injury but also its potential applications in conjunction with other pharmacological approaches.3

In summary, this study provides morphology and molecular evidence to support a mechanistic role for LIF/LIFR in PSC and PBC. The ability of LRI-310 to attenuate the development of morphological, molecular, and biochemical features of cholestasis in Abcb4 −/− mice underscores the therapeutic potential of pharmacological inhibition of LIFR in human cholangiopathies.
