Cyclopropanation of unactivated alkenes with diazo compounds enables facile synthesis of challenging cyclopropanes and late-stage sesquiterpene modification
Authors: Le Zhang, Na Zhang, Dinggang Wang, Xiao Yuan, Xinyu Han, Miaomiao Zhuo, Guangyang Guo, Wei-Dong Zhang, Hao Zhang, Yu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cyclopropanes are prevalent motifs in pharmaceuticals and natural products; however, the construction of complex cyclopropanes remains a longstanding issue owing to low intrinsic reactivity and steric congestion. Herein, we report a visible-light-mediated cyclopropanation of unactivated alkenes with diazo compounds via an energy-transfer (EnT) pathway. This protocol provides direct access to a range of cyclopropanes, including spirocyclic, fused and those bearing quaternary carbon centers. Notably, a broad spectrum of unactivated alkenes—spanning endocyclic, bridged, polysubstituted, and terminal systems, along with diverse diazo reagents—are well tolerated, underscoring the generality of the method. The practicality is demonstrated by gram-scale synthesis and late-stage modification of natural products such as valencene, β-caryophyllene and flavanone. Moreover, in a preliminary in vitro assay, the caryophyllene oxide derivative exhibits promising anti-atopic dermatitis activity, showing nearly twice as high inhibition of CCL17 and CCL22 compared to its precursor, while exhibiting no detectable cytotoxicity. Finally, mechanistic studies support the involvement of triplet carbene species. These findings highlight the potential of this photochemical strategy for the rapid construction of challenging cyclopropanes and its utility in the development of bioactive natural product analogues.

Introduction

Cyclopropanes represent some of the most prevalent cyclic pharmacophores found in marketed drugs,1 where their incorporation has been demonstrated to enhance metabolic stability, increase potency, and modulate conformational properties.2 They are also widely encountered in natural products, such as cycloastragenol and ptaquiloside (Scheme 1a).3–6 Despite their significance, access to cyclopropanes with high structural complexity, including spirocyclic and fused frameworks as well as quaternary carbon centers, remains severely challenging. This difficulty arises from the combined effects of intrinsic ring strain, steric congestion, and rigid topological constraints.7–10

To address these limitations, a few approaches have been developed, among which [2 + 1] cyclization of alkenes with carbene species has emerged as a powerful method.11,12 However, these methods predominantly rely on activated alkenes in combination with diazo compounds or related carbene precursors.13–15 For example, our group has previously disclosed the synthesis of cyclopropanes bearing quaternary carbon centers via energy-transfer (EnT) activation of N-tosylhydrazones,16 which offered a practical route to highly congested EWG-free cyclopropanes. Nevertheless, its application is confined to donor/donor carbene precursors and features a limited range of compatible alkenes. Meanwhile, Shen and co-workers developed various carbene-based cyclopropanation protocols with broad substrate compatibility.17–20 In addition, the synthesis of spirocyclic and fused cyclopropanes has been reported by a few groups, including the groups of Nagib, Giri and Renaud.21–25 These studies mainly exploit cyclopropanation reactions involving alkenes with carbon-centered radicals or carbene intermediates. Collectively, these findings have substantially expanded the structural diversity of cyclopropanes. However, only a limited number of these methodologies have proven amenable to gram-scale synthesis, and their application to the late-stage functionalization of complex natural products remains scarce. Moreover, the biological evaluation of the resulting analogues has been rarely explored. Indeed, only sporadic examples of such late-stage functionalization have been reported to date,26 further highlighting the need for more general and versatile synthetic methodologies (Scheme 1b).

Unactivated alkenes are among the most common building blocks in organic synthesis, frequently serving as key structural motifs in natural products.27–29 Therefore, the direct construction of cyclopropanes via unactivated alkenes would mark a significant advance in cyclopropane synthesis. However, such transformations remain difficult, particularly with multisubstituted variants, due to their inherently low reactivity and pronounced steric congestion, which collectively impede effective interaction with external reagents.30 To overcome these longstanding limitations, several innovative strategies have recently been developed.31–34 For example, in 2023, an intermolecular organophotocatalytic cyclopropanation of unactivated alkenes with α-bromo-β-ketoesters and α-bromo monoesters was reported.35 More recently, Qiu and co-workers reported an electrochemical cyclopropanation of terminal unactivated alkenes with methylene compounds, further expanding the repertoire of activation modes.36 These methods have enriched approaches for the cyclopropanation of unactivated alkenes; however, most of them are applicable to terminal unactivated alkenes, and their application to structurally complex unactivated alkenes remains underexplored. Diazo compounds and N-tosylhydrazones represent an important and well-established class of carbene precursors for the cyclopropanation of alkenes; however, only a limited number of unactivated alkenes are compatible with these protocols (Scheme 1c).37–44 Therefore, the development of an efficient photochemical strategy for the cyclopropanation of unactivated alkenes with diazo compounds is highly desirable.

In light of these considerations and our ongoing interest in carbene chemistry,45–47 the synthesis of strained small rings,48,49 and drug discovery,50–54 we report a photocatalytic activation of diazo compounds via EnT with unactivated alkenes to access structurally demanding cyclopropanes. This mild protocol enables the efficient synthesis of spirocyclic and fused frameworks, all-carbon quaternary centers, and a diverse array of polysubstituted cyclopropanes. The synthetic utility of this method is further underscored by its scalability in gram-scale reactions and its broad applicability in the late-stage functionalization of complex natural products, including valencene, β-caryophyllene and flavanone. Notably, in vitro activity evaluation shows that compound 50c exhibits promising anti-atopic dermatitis activity with nearly twice as high activity as the natural product precursor (Scheme 1d). This work expands the frontiers of carbene chemistry and provides a proof of concept for its utility in generating bioactive natural product analogues.

Results and discussion

In line with our hypothesis, we initially explored the cyclopropanation of benzyl 2-diazoacetate 1a with tert-butyl 3-methyleneazetidine-1-carboxylate 1b as the model substrates (Table 1). Through screening of various photocatalysts, Ir[dF(CF3)ppy]2(dtbbpy)PF6 was identified as the optimal photocatalyst (Table 1, entries 1–4). Based on these results, the optimal conditions were established as 3 mol% Ir[dF(CF3)ppy]2(dtbbpy)PF6 in dichloromethane (DCM) under irradiation with 40 W, 456 nm Kessil lamps for 15 h, delivering the corresponding azaspiro[2.3]hexane 1c in 68% yield. Further solvent screening indicated that tetrahydrofuran (THF), acetonitrile (ACN), N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF), and 1,4-dioxane resulted in diminished reaction efficiency relative to DCM (Table 1, entries 5–8; see the SI for more optimization details). Control experiments established that both the photocatalyst and light were indispensable for productive cyclopropanation (Table 1, entries 9–10).

With the optimized conditions in hand, we subsequently investigated the substrate scope of the cyclopropanation of benzyl 2-diazoacetate 1a with a diverse array of unactivated alkenes (Scheme 2). We first explored unactivated alkenes leading to spirocyclic cyclopropane architectures. Boc-protected azaspiro[x.y]alkanes were successfully obtained, including azaspiro[2.3]hexane 1c and azaspiro[2.5]octane 2c in moderate yields. In parallel, the corresponding N-phenyl-substituted azaspiro[2.5]octane 3c was obtained in a lower yield, likely because the N-phenyl group diverted the reactive intermediate toward competitive C–H insertion at the expense of the targeted spirocyclization.55 Encouraged by these results, the scope was further extended to azadispiro substrates, affording azadispiro[2.1.35.13]nonane 4c in 62% yield. Subsequently, the method was also applicable to another heteroatom, enabling oxaspiro[2.5]octane 5c in 54% yield. Meanwhile, 6-phenylspiro[2.5]octane 6c and its derivative 7c were smoothly accessed via cyclopropanation in comparable yields. Next, the method was applied to five-to eight-membered endocyclic alkenes, providing a variety of fused bicyclic cyclopropanes in moderate to good yields. For bicyclo[3.1.0]hexane, the effect of C3 methyl ester substitution was investigated, affording 8c and 9c in acceptable yields. Similarly, both C3-methylated and non-methylated bicyclo[4.1.0]heptane were converted to 10c and 11c, yielding moderate results. The protocol was further applicable to bridged alkenes, delivering tricyclo[3.2.1.02,4]octane 12c, and tolerated various C6 substituents, including acetyl- (13c), triethoxysilyl-(14c) and tert-butoxycarbonyl-(15c). Epoxycyclopropa[b]naphthalene 16c was also obtained in 55% yield. Moreover, medium-sized endocyclic alkenes were likewise competent substrates, furnishing bicyclo[5.1.0]octane 17c and bicyclo[6.1.0]nonane 18c with moderate yields. Polysubstituted unactivated alkenes were further evaluated to access cyclopropanes bearing quaternary carbon centers: 1,1-disubstituted, trisubstituted and tetrasubstituted alkenes exhibited moderate compatibility under the standard conditions, affording cyclopropane products 19c–21c in 45–64% yields. Of particular interest, the cyclopropanation of dehydroamino esters proceeded efficiently (up to 85%), yielding novel unnatural amino acids containing a cyclopropane skeleton 22c. Meanwhile, a cyclopropane product bearing two quaternary carbon centers 23c was also obtained in moderate yield, demonstrating the potential of this method for the synthesis of highly congested cyclopropanes. The relatively lower yields are likely attributed to a combination of steric congestion and conformational constraints associated with the cyclic alkenes. Regarding the stereoselectivity of this class of substrates, we found that those with more rigid or conformationally constrained frameworks exhibit somewhat enhanced diastereoselectivity.

Building on these results, the substrate scope was expanded to monosubstituted terminal alkenes (Scheme 3). Allylbenzene and its derivatives bearing electron-donating substituents on the aromatic ring, such as methyl or methoxy groups, underwent smooth cyclopropanation, yielding 24c–26c in 52–68%. In addition, aryl halide-substituted alkenes (fluoro- and bromo-) were well tolerated, producing 27c and 28c in moderate to good yields. Beyond simple allylbenzene substrates, unactivated alkenes bearing other aromatic groups were likewise compatible; for example, a naphthyl-substituted terminal alkene reacted efficiently to give 29c. Moreover, terminal alkenes featuring an ester linkage underwent cyclopropanation to furnish 30c in 46% yield. Beyond simple aryl frameworks, allylic alkenes bearing heteroatom substituents: vinyl ethers and vinyl thioethers reacted efficiently, affording products 31c and 32c. Notably, an allylic silane within the α-alkene series was also successfully cyclopropanated, delivering product 33c. Furthermore, unactivated alkenes bearing electron-withdrawing substituents (acetoxy-, carboxyl-, and cyano-) were smoothly converted to cyclopropanes 34c–36c in 30–65% yields. Importantly, the scope was further extended to longer-chain unactivated alkenes bearing electron-withdrawing groups (hydroxy- and halo-), which underwent efficient cyclopropanation to afford 37c–40c in moderate yields. In addition, we found that most terminal alkenes exhibit low diastereoselectivity (typically d.r. = 1 : 1–1 : 2), suggesting generally weak stereocontrol in this transformation.

Subsequently, the scope of diazo reagents was explored to assess the generality of the cyclopropanation protocol using unactivated alkene 31b. A range of diazoacetates bearing cyclohexyl, ethyl, tert-butyl, as well as remotely substituted tert-butyl, reacted efficiently to afford the corresponding cyclopropane products 41c–44c. Notably, diazo substrates bearing aromatic motifs linked via longer-chain alkyl or heteroatom chains were also well tolerated; for example, 4-phenylbutyl- and 3-phenoxybenzyl- substituted reagents gave 45c and 46c. Encouraged by these findings, we observed that a diazo compound bearing a strongly electron-withdrawing cyano group also afforded the cyclopropane product 47c in moderate yield.

To demonstrate the practicality of this strategy, it was extended to the synthesis of cyclopropane-containing natural product analogues (Scheme 4a). Sesquiterpene natural products bearing inherently unactivated alkenes underwent late-stage diversification to furnish novel cyclopropane-containing derivatives 48c–52c. In addition, flavanones could be converted into the corresponding cyclopropane-fused natural product derivative 53cvia a sequential process involving Wittig olefination followed by cyclopropanation. To assess scalability, a gram-scale reaction of benzyl 2-diazoacetate 1a with norbornene 12b (10.0 mmol) delivered 12c in 48% isolated yield (1.17 g), highlighting the practicality of this protocol (Scheme 4b). Notably, previous studies demonstrated that sesquiterpene- and flavanone-derived compounds can serve as anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory agents, which are highly relevant to the treatment of AD.56–59 Inspired by these findings, we sought to evaluate the biological activities of both the parent compounds and the resulting derivatives in a TNF-α/IFN-γ-induced HaCaT cell model (Scheme 4c). qPCR results showed that, among the tested compounds, compound 50c at a dosage of 10 µM significantly reversed the aberrant overexpression of CCL17 and CCL22 mRNA induced by TNF-α/IFN-γ. Its inhibitory effect was not only nearly twice as high as its prototype compound but also showed a level of CCL17 downregulation comparable to the clinical positive drug tapinarof. After confirming the in vitro efficacy of compound 50c, its safety became another focus of our attention. Through CCK-8 cell viability assays, we found that although at a low concentration of 1 µM, all drugs (including the positive control tapinarof, the prototype caryophyllene oxide, and the derivative 50c) had no impact on cell survival, at the effective anti-inflammatory concentration of 10 µM, the cell viability in the tapinarof group significantly decreased (p < 0.001), suggesting strong in vitro cytotoxicity at this concentration. In contrast, neither the prototype caryophyllene oxide nor its derivative 50c showed any significant cytotoxicity at the 10 µM effective anti-inflammatory concentration, with cell viability showing no significant difference compared to the DMSO control group (Scheme 4d). While these in vitro results are encouraging, they are preliminary in nature. The AD activity of 50c has thus far only been evaluated in a single cell-based model, and its therapeutic relevance remains to be validated through further studies, including in vivo efficacy evaluation, pharmacokinetic profiling, and mechanistic investigations. Nonetheless, these findings serve as a proof of concept for late-stage cyclopropanation as a strategy to modulate the biological activity of natural products.

To gain insight into the reaction mechanism, a series of mechanistic experiments were performed (Scheme 5). An on–off light irradiation experiment showed that continuous irradiation was required for product formation (Scheme 5a). UV-vis absorption studies revealed that the diazo compound 1a exhibited only weak absorption in the near-UV region, whereas the photocatalyst PC showed significantly stronger absorption. Notably, the absorption spectrum of the mixture 1a + PC did not display any new red-shifted band, indicating the absence of a ground-state electron donor–acceptor (EDA) complex. These results suggested that photoexcitation may occur predominantly on the photocatalyst (Scheme 5b). Stern–Volmer analysis revealed that the excited state of the photocatalyst was selectively quenched by the diazo compound, whereas no significant quenching was observed with the alkene (Scheme 5c). Subsequently, we found that the [2 + 1] cyclization reaction was suppressed upon the addition of 5.0 equivalents of stable radicals (2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidin-1-yl)oxidanyl (TEMPO) or butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT), indicating that a radical intermediate was probably involved in the cyclization reaction (Scheme 5d). The addition of azulene or perylene, known triplet quenchers, markedly inhibited the reaction, which is consistent with the involvement of triplet carbene intermediates and in good agreement with previous reports (Scheme 5e).60,61 Based on the above observations and literature precedents, a plausible mechanism was proposed (Scheme 5f). Upon visible-light irradiation, the photocatalyst is excited and is proposed to transfer its energy to the diazo compound, giving rise to a putative triplet diazo intermediate (1). Subsequent extrusion of nitrogen is suggested to generate the corresponding triplet carbene species (2), which reacts with the unactivated alkene to form the triplet-state diradical species (3). Intersystem crossing of this diradical then furnishes the cyclopropane product 31c.

Conclusions

In summary, we have developed a photocatalytic cyclopropanation that overcomes the intrinsic reactivity limitations of unactivated alkenes, thereby enabling the synthesis of structurally challenging cyclopropanes. Notably, a broad range of unactivated alkenes—spanning endocyclic, bridged, polysubstituted and terminal systems—along with diverse diazo reagents, are well tolerated. The practicality of the method is further demonstrated by gram-scale synthesis and the late-stage functionalization of various natural products. Mechanistic investigations are consistent with an EnT-induced pathway involving triplet carbene intermediates. Furthermore, preliminary biological evaluation revealed that caryophyllene oxide analogue 50c suppressed inflammatory mediators (CCL17 and CCL22) in stimulated HaCaT keratinocytes without detectable cytotoxicity in this in vitro assay. Overall, this work provides a valuable platform for accessing challenging cyclopropanes and serves as a proof of concept for their potential in generating bioactive natural product analogues.
