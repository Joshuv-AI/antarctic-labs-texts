Management of Treatment Resistant EGFR Inhibitor‐Induced Papulopustular Exanthema With Isotretinoin and Dapsone
Authors: Ishana Dixit, Raquel Ruiz Araujo, Pablo Fernandez‐Peñas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground/ObjectivesEpidermal growth factor receptor inhibitors (EGFRi) commonly cause papulopustular exanthemas that may compromise the success of cancer therapy. While tetracyclines are first‐line treatment, data on alternative systemic agents for refractory cases remain limited. This study compares the effectiveness and tolerability of isotretinoin and dapsone for managing tetracycline‐resistant EGFRi–induced papulopustular eruptions in a tertiary dermatology service.MethodsA retrospective review was conducted of patients with EGFRi‐induced papulopustular eruptions treated with isotretinoin or dapsone between 2018 and 2024. Clinical data included demographics, malignancy type, EGFRi agent, eruption severity, prior therapies, Physician's Global Assessment, treatment outcomes, time to best response, adverse events, and EGFRi dose modification. Group comparisons used one‐way ANOVA and chi‐square/Fisher's exact tests.ResultsSixteen patients were identified (8 isotretinoin, 8 dapsone). Baseline severity was higher in the isotretinoin group. Time to best response was shorter with isotretinoin (mean 14.9 weeks) than dapsone (21.0 weeks). Complete resolution occurred in 62.5% of isotretinoin‐treated patients and 50% of dapsone‐treated patients. Both agents were well tolerated, with only mild mucocutaneous effects in the isotretinoin group and mild anemia in one dapsone patient. Survival at follow‐up was higher among patients who continued EGFRi (with or without dose reduction) than among those who ceased treatment. No significant differences were observed between isotretinoin and dapsone in EGFRi dose modification patterns or overall response rates.ConclusionsBoth isotretinoin and dapsone are effective, well‐tolerated options for managing tetracycline‐resistant EGFRi‐induced papulopustular eruptions. Isotretinoin showed a trend toward faster improvement, while ultimate response rates were similar. Maintaining EGFRi therapy, even at reduced doses, in conjunction with dermatology treatment was associated with superior dermatological outcomes and higher survival at follow‐up compared with cessation of EGFRi treatment, highlighting the importance of proactive dermatologic intervention to support oncologic treatment continuity.Keywords: dapsone, EGFR inhibitor, isotretinoin, papulopustular eruption

Introduction

Epidermal growth factor receptor inhibitors (EGFRi) are widely used in the treatment of various cancers, including non‐small cell lung cancer, gastrointestinal cancers, and head and neck cancers. Despite their efficacy, EGFRi are frequently associated with dermatologic adverse events, particularly papulopustular exanthemas (sometimes referred to as acneiform eruptions), which can significantly impact patients' quality of life and may necessitate dose reduction or discontinuation of therapy [1, 2].

The management of EGFRi induced papulopustular eruptions has traditionally relied on tetracycline‐class antibiotics; however, increasing interest has emerged in alternative systemic therapies, including retinoids and dapsone, particularly for patients who are refractory to conventional treatment. Isotretinoin, a systemic retinoid, has shown promise in several studies for its efficacy in reducing the severity of these eruptions [3]. A systematic review by Bierbrier et al. reported that 77% of patients treated with isotretinoin experienced moderate to significant improvement in their symptoms with minimal adverse events [3]. Dapsone, an anti‐inflammatory and antimicrobial agent, has also been explored for its potential benefits in managing EGFRi‐induced skin toxicities [4, 5, 6]. While there is less extensive data on dapsone compared to isotretinoin, its anti‐inflammatory properties suggest it could be a viable alternative, particularly in patients who may not tolerate retinoids well [7]. Despite promising individual reports, the most appropriate therapy between isotretinoin and dapsone for managing EGFRi‐induced papulopustular eruptions remains uncertain. Comparative data are scarce, particularly regarding the effect of these agents on both dermatologic outcomes and the ability to maintain cancer therapy. This study presents our institutional experience with isotretinoin and dapsone, aiming to provide practical insights into their role in clinical management.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective review of patients who developed EGFRi‐induced papulopustular eruptions that were resistant to tetracyclines and were subsequently treated with either isotretinoin or dapsone at our tertiary dermatology service between 2018 and 2024. Tetracycline‐resistant eruptions were defined as persistent moderate–severe disease despite ≥ 8 weeks of appropriately dosed oral tetracycline therapy with standard topical measures, requiring escalation to isotretinoin or dapsone. Demographic and clinical data were extracted from electronic medical records, including age, gender, malignancy type, EGFRi agent, time to onset of eruption, previous treatment for papulopustular exanthema, Physician's Global Assessment (PGA) at presentation, best response to therapy, time to best response, treatment completion status, adverse events, survival after eruption onset, and EGFRi dose modification. Comparisons between groups were performed using one‐way ANOVA for continuous variables and chi‐square/Fisher's exact tests for categorical variables. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients whose clinical images are included in this manuscript. All images were used with permission, and identifying features have been removed where possible.

Results

Sixteen patients were included, evenly divided between dapsone and isotretinoin. The mean age was similar between groups (55.5 years for dapsone vs. 54 years for isotretinoin, p = 0.65), with an unbalanced gender distribution with more males in the isotretinoin group (62.5% vs. 37.5%). Colorectal cancer was more common among isotretinoin patients and non‐small cell lung cancer predominated in the dapsone group (p = 0.13). EGFRi use varied significantly between groups (p = 0.0047), with cetuximab and panitumumab more frequently prescribed in the isotretinoin cohort, and erlotinib used only in the dapsone group. This highlights that isotretinoin and dapsone were used as second‐ or third‐line therapies.

Among patients who continued to experience papulopustular reactions, those continuing their original oncology dose ultimately achieved significant clinical improvement; however, their time to best response was substantially longer (mean, 26.8 weeks) than for those who underwent dose reduction (mean, 12.9 weeks). Patients who underwent dose reduction reached maximum improvement earlier, suggesting a trade‐off between maintaining oncologic dosing intensity and dermatologic symptom control. Clinical outcomes in those who ceased EGFRi were less favourable, both in terms of dermatologic response and overall survival. Notably, survival analysis showed a statistically significant association between continuation of EGFRi (with or without dose reduction) and higher likelihood of being alive at follow‐up compared with cessation (χ2 = 8.26, p = 0.016). The efficacy of isotretinoin and dapsone in managing eruptions remained comparable across oncologic treatment groups. Both agents were well tolerated. Isotretinoin‐related adverse effects included xerosis, cheilitis, and nasal mucosal dryness, while dapsone was associated with mild anaemia in one patient, not necessitating discontinuation. EGFRi therapy was continued without dose adjustment in 37.5% of patients, reduced in 50%, and ceased in 12.5%, with no significant difference in dose modification rates between treatment groups.

Discussion

Our findings extend the evidence on the importance of properly managing EGFRi‐induced papulopustular eruptions by showing that continuation of oncologic therapy, whether at full or reduced dose, leads to better outcomes. Additionally, we have demonstrated that dermatologic improvement can be achieved in tetracycline‐resistant patients by using systemic agents such as isotretinoin and dapsone. Standard first‐line therapy for EGFRi‐induced papulopustular eruptions comprises topical corticosteroids combined with oral tetracyclines, with systemic corticosteroids used briefly for severe flares. In this series, isotretinoin and dapsone were initiated after lack of response to doxycycline and prednisone, positioning them as second‐ or third‐line interventions. However, both agents may warrant earlier consideration in selected cases where tetracyclines are contraindicated or ineffective.

In our cohort, continued EGFRi therapy (with or without dose reduction) coincided with better dermatological control than EGFRi cessation. Survival at follow‐up was also higher among those who continued EGFRi than among those who ceased treatment. However, given the retrospective design, small sample size, and confounding by indication, these findings should be interpreted as associations rather than evidence that dermatological intervention or EGFRi continuation in this cohort improved survival.

EGFRi‐induced papulopustular eruptions remain a challenging adverse effect in oncology, with implications for patient comfort, treatment adherence, and overall quality of life. In this cohort, both isotretinoin and dapsone demonstrated clinical benefit in patients whose eruptions were refractory to tetracycline‐class antibiotics. Although statistical significance was not reached in most comparisons due to the small sample size, several clinically relevant trends emerged. Isotretinoin was associated with a shorter time to best response, while dapsone was often initiated in patients with earlier‐onset and more severe eruptions (Table 1). This pattern suggests a degree of selection bias, as treatment allocation was not randomised, and clinicians may have chosen dapsone for cases where retinoids were contraindicated or where rapid escalation of therapy was deemed necessary (as isotretinoin is theoretically slower in reaching efficacy, although our results suggest a faster effect in EGFRi treated patients).

The mechanisms of action for these drugs differ in ways that may be relevant to the clinical phenotype. EGFRi‐induced papulopustular eruptions are driven largely by follicular epithelial injury and sterile neutrophilic inflammation [8, 9]. Isotretinoin's keratinisation‐modulating and anti‐inflammatory effects may explain its tendency toward faster improvement [10]. Dapsone's inhibition of neutrophil chemotaxis and myeloperoxidase activity may be more relevant to pustular or neutrophil‐dominant patterns, although this effect may take longer to translate into visible resolution [11]. The similar ultimate response rates between the two groups indicate that both mechanisms can be effective in EGFRi‐associated inflammation, even if the time course differs.

Given the mechanistic rationale for systemic retinoids in EGFRi‐induced papulopustular eruptions, it is important to consider whether other agents within the retinoid class may have similar efficacy. Although isotretinoin and acitretin share class‐related anti‐inflammatory properties, their mechanisms are not identical. In EGFR‐inhibitor papulopustular eruptions, isotretinoin primarily modulates neutrophil‐driven inflammation and innate immune signalling through strong sebocyte‐immune interactions and TLR‐2 suppression [12, 13, 14], whereas acitretin exerts stronger effects on keratinocyte differentiation and cytokine regulation [15, 16]. These mechanistic differences may explain their variable clinical roles in managing EGFRi‐related cutaneous toxicity and isotretinoin remains the more biologically plausible retinoid in this setting.

In parallel, our findings support a role for neutrophil‐targeted anti‐inflammatory strategies in tetracycline‐refractory disease. Agents such as colchicine, which inhibit neutrophil chemotaxis and inflammasome‐mediated inflammation [17], share mechanistic overlap with dapsone and may have theoretical utility in neutrophil‐predominant EGFRi eruptions, but there is no published clinical data to support colchicine in this context.

Together, these observations highlight testable hypotheses for future studies, including stratifying refractory EGFRi‐induced eruptions by dominant pathogenic pathways (sebosuppression and follicular differentiation versus neutrophil‐driven inflammation) to guide selection of retinoid‐based or neutrophil‐modulating therapies.

Our results are consistent with published data supporting isotretinoin as an effective treatment for EGFRi‐induced eruptions, such as the systematic review by Bierbrier et al., which reported high response rates and favourable tolerability. In contrast, evidence for dapsone remains largely anecdotal, but our findings suggest that it is a well‐tolerated and potentially useful option when isotretinoin is unsuitable. The lack of significant differences in adverse events in this cohort is reassuring, although routine monitoring for anaemia with dapsone and for mucocutaneous and laboratory abnormalities with isotretinoin remains essential. Beyond its dermatologic utility, dapsone may also have anticancer relevance by suppressing interleukin‐8–mediated neutrophilic inflammation, thereby potentially reducing both cutaneous toxicity and IL‐8–driven tumour signalling [18]. Although this remains experimental, it provides an additional rationale for further evaluating dapsone in EGFR inhibitor–treated patients.

These findings reinforce the importance of individualising treatment choices, considering comorbidities, previous drug exposures, patient preferences, and clinical morphology. The decision to use isotretinoin or dapsone should also be informed by the urgency of symptom control given that more prolonged time to resolution may be clinically relevant in patients at risk of treatment interruption.

This study is limited by its retrospective, observational design and potential confounding by treatment selection. Notably, treatment groups were imbalanced with respect to EGFRi class: patients treated with isotretinoin were predominantly on monoclonal antibody therapy, whereas those treated with dapsone more commonly received tyrosine kinase inhibitors. These classes may have distinct cutaneous toxicity profiles, which may have influenced both eruption severity and response to adjunctive therapy. This imbalance likely reflects selection bias driven by clinical context and safety considerations rather than therapeutic equivalence.

In addition, differences in safety profiles and patient factors may have influenced treatment choice. Isotretinoin use is constrained by pre‐existing comorbidities, ocular or oral mucosa xerosis, lipid profile, while dapsone carries risks such as haemolysis and methaemoglobinaemia, which may limit their use in populations with G6PD deficiency or pre‐existing anaemia. The small sample size and lack of adjustment for baseline oncologic prognosis and disease severity further limit causal inference. Accordingly, comparisons between isotretinoin and dapsone should be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis‐generating only.

Conclusion

EGFRi papulopustular eruptions are a common toxicity that may threaten continuation of effective oncologic therapy. In this retrospective series, both isotretinoin and dapsone were well tolerated and associated with meaningful clinical improvement in tetracycline‐refractory cases. Isotretinoin demonstrated a trend toward faster response, while overall response rates were similar between agents. Continuation of EGFRi therapy with adjuvant use of these agents was associated with better dermatologic outcomes and higher survival at follow‐up compared with cessation; however, these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the observational design and limited sample size. Close collaboration between dermatologists and oncologists is essential to optimize cutaneous management while preserving continuity of oncologic treatment. Prospective studies are needed to define optimal treatment selection and timing in this high‐risk population.
