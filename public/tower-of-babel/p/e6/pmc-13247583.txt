DamSegment: A curriculum-structured image dataset for damage assessment of concrete dams
Authors: Vahidreza Gharehbaghi, Caroline Bennett, Rémy D. Lequesne, Hang Zhao, Jian Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Structural health monitoring (SHM) remains underutilized in dam engineering practice despite its potential to enhance asset management through earlier damage detection and risk-informed decision-making. To advance research in automated crack and spalling detection, we introduce DamSegment, a high-resolution image dataset curated to support development of deep learning-based damage assessment of large-scale concrete infrastructure. The dataset comprises 3500 image patches derived from images captured during an on-site visit of a concrete dam. Unlike existing public datasets, DamSegment includes dam-specific deterioration mechanisms, such as freeze-thaw-induced spalling and complex map-cracking patterns, which are often underrepresented in general concrete damage datasets. Images were acquired using multiple mobile devices to ensure diverse viewpoints and surface conditions. The collected images were divided into 512 × 512 or 640 × 640 - pixel patches and presented in a manner that supports damage classification, detection, and segmentation tasks. Images intended for damage segmentation are sorted by crack detection difficulty, enabling curriculum learning-based model training in which neural networks progressively learn from simple to complex damage patterns. Each image is annotated with pixel-level polygons and masks, making the dataset suitable for multiple computer vision tasks including damage classification, detection, and segmentation.

Background

High-quality data are essential for training deep learning models for structural health monitoring (SHM) [1]. For computer vision-based SHM focused on surface damage identification of civil infrastructure, the use of diverse data encompassing complex crack patterns, multiple damage types, and varied real-world environmental conditions facilitates the development of more generalizable models for damage detection.

Recent advances in deep learning technologies have significantly improved damage detection in civil infrastructure, offering powerful alternatives to conventional visual inspection methods. Despite these advancements, widespread adoption of deep learning for dam inspection remains limited by the scarcity of high-quality, well-annotated training datasets. Most publicly available concrete damage datasets are focused on general civil infrastructure such as pavements, bridges, and buildings, rather than capturing the unique surface characteristics of concrete dam surfaces. Furthermore, generating pixel-level segmentation masks which are required for training semantic segmentation models, is labor-intensive and time-consuming, further limiting the availability of such datasets.

Concrete dams experience deterioration over their service life due to environmental exposure, freeze-thaw cycling, and extreme loading events. These conditions can lead to multiple damage mechanisms, including cracking, spalling, delamination, erosion and abrasion, seepage-related leakage and staining, thermal distress, and chemically-driven degradation such as alkali-silica reaction (ASR) and carbonation. While these mechanisms are important for dam safety assessment, DamSegment is specifically designed for computer vision applications. Therefore, it provides pixel-level annotations for two primary surface-visible damage categories that can be consistently identified in RGB imagery: cracks and spalling.

To address the limitations of existing datasets, DamSegment provides pixel-level annotated images of concrete dam surfaces exhibiting diverse crack patterns and spalling conditions. The dataset is hierarchically organized from simple damage geometries to complex map-cracking patterns, enabling progressive or curriculum-based learning strategies for model training.

Annotation formats

DamSegment is designed to be compatible with widely used deep learning architectures, including the YOLO family and U-Net-based segmentation models. The available annotation formats within the dataset are summarized in the following (see Fig. 5).Fig. 5Annotation formats included in DamSegment.Fig. 5 dummy alt text

Annotation formats included in DamSegment.

Pascal VOC format (JSON)

This format supports object detection tasks by providing metadata such as class identifiers and bounding-box coordinates (top-left and bottom-right corners) [2]. It is compatible with common detection frameworks, including Faster R-CNN [3] and SSD (Single Shot Detector) [4], and can be extended to incorporate segmentation masks for pixel-level annotation.

YOLO format (TXT)

Widely adopted in recent YOLO architectures (e.g. YOLOv8 and later) [[5], [6], [7], [8]], this format stores object information in plain text. Each line represents one instance and includes the class identifier and normalized bounding-box parameters (center x, center y, width, height). For instance, segmentation polygon coordinates defining pixel-level boundaries are also included.

Mask format (PNG)

Used for semantic and instance segmentation tasks, this annotation format represents each pixel in a binary mask image with a corresponding class label or instance identifier. It facilitates precise pixel-level segmentation and supports evaluation metrics such as Intersection over Union (IoU). Segmentation networks such as DeepLab [9], U-Net [10], and Fully Convolutional Networks (FCNs) [11] are typically trained and evaluated using this format.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Data acquisition context and setup

The images in this dataset were collected during two inspection campaigns conducted under distinct environmental conditions. The first campaign took place in December 2022 under cloudy, post-rain conditions, producing wet or partially wet concrete surfaces. The second campaign was conducted in September 2024 under sunny weather, with dry concrete surfaces. This dual-condition acquisition strategy was intentionally designed to capture variability in surface moisture and illumination, reflecting realistic operational challenges encountered during field inspections.

All images were acquired using handheld smartphone cameras operated in automatic mode. No artificial lighting was employed; images were captured under ambient lighting conditions. Photographs were taken at a standoff distance of approximately 1–3 m, primarily from near-normal viewing angles, consistent with standard field inspection practice geometry.

To ensure privacy protection and compliance with data-sharing policies, personally identifying metadata (e.g., GPS coordinates contained in EXIF data) have been removed prior to public release. Patch generation

The raw images were divided into non-overlapping image patches, generating over 20,000 intermediate patches for potential annotation. A quality-control stage was subsequently applied to remove patches that were severely blurred, significantly over- or under-exposed, partially occluded by equipment or personnel, or otherwise non-informative for surface-damage analysis (see workflow in Fig. 2). Following this screening process, 3500 high-quality patches were retained for inclusion in the final dataset and subsequently partitioned into the three task-oriented subsets, as described in Section 4.4.

For damage classification, an initial deep learning model was trained using 400 manually labeled images across two classes. The trained model was then applied to classify candidate patches as Crack or Non-crack. All model predictions were manually verified and refined to ensure labeling accuracy, resulting in a set of 2000 annotated classification images. The crack class includes images containing visible surface damage such as cracks and spalling, while the non-crack class represents intact concrete surfaces. Fig. 3a shows example samples from each class.

For damage segmentation, the processed image patches were annotated at the pixel level for crack and spalling regions, as described in Section 4.5 (see Fig. 3c). To support curriculum-based training strategies, the annotated images were organized into three difficulty levels: Easy, Medium, and Hard [16]. For each level, images are provided with labels in three formats, as shown in Fig. 5. Difficulty categorization was determined primarily by damage-pattern characteristics rather than background or lighting conditions. Images were ranked according to crack density and width. The lower third, characterized by fewer and predominantly wider cracks, was classified as Easy. The middle third was labeled Medium. The top third, exhibiting a higher number of cracks with predominantly finer widths, was classified as Hard. This sorting process combined deep learning-assisted analysis and classical image-processing techniques under human supervision. Fig. 6 presents samples of each difficulty level. All segmentation masks and labels were visually inspected and confirmed by civil engineers.Fig. 6Damage sorted based on difficulty level.Fig. 6 dummy alt text

For damage detection, the same 1500 images used for segmentation were employed. The annotated polygons were converted into bounding boxes defined by their minimum and maximum coordinate values to support object detection tasks. Fig. 3b shows examples of crack and spalling annotations with corresponding bounding boxes.

To improve boundary precision and smoothness, an additional post-processing step was applied to refine the annotated polygons. This refinement combined the Segment Anything Model (SAM) [17] with classical image-processing techniques. The approach was applied to both model-generated pseudo-labels and human annotations, resulting in more accurate and smoother damage boundaries, as illustrated in Fig. 7. Annotation workflow

The image patches were annotated using the Roboflow web application for pixel-level labeling of cracks and spalling. An iterative annotation strategy, guided by curriculum-learning principles, was adopted. Specifically, images in the easy difficulty bins were annotated first, and a YOLOv11 model [5] trained on these initial labels was subsequently used to assist in labeling more complex samples. All annotations were reviewed and verified by a civil engineer, with corrections made as necessary to ensure consistency and accuracy. This human–AI collaborative workflow substantially reduced manual labeling effort while maintaining high annotation accuracy [18]. Calibration, validation, and quality control

The images in DamSegment were acquired using multiple smartphone cameras operating in automatic mode (auto-exposure, auto-focus, and auto-white balance). Because the dataset is designed for 2D appearance-based tasks, namely damage classification, detection, and segmentation on RGB patches, and does not involve geometric measurements or radiometric quantification, explicit geometric or radiometric calibration was not required. All publicly released images are standardized to a 512 × 512-pixel and 640 × 640-pixel RGB format, enabling direct integration into computer vision pipelines.

To ensure reliability, consistency, and annotation quality, the dataset underwent multiple validation and quality-control procedures:•Image-level quality control: Candidate patches generated from the raw images were screened to remove severely blurred, over- or under-exposed, partially occluded, or otherwise non-informative samples. As discussed, several patches without damage were retained.•Annotation validation: All crack and spalling annotations were created or reviewed by structural engineering graduate students under the supervision of licensed structural engineers. Ambiguous cases were discussed and resolved by consensus. Final segmentation masks and bounding boxes were visually inspected to ensure that damage boundaries accurately follow visible crack and spalling patterns.•Refinement of segmentation masks: A post-processing step combining the Segment Anything Model (SAM) with classical image-processing techniques was applied to refine boundary locations and remove small artifacts. The refined masks, which exhibit smoother and more accurate damage contours, are the labels included in the public release.

Limitations

Segmentation and classification of cracks were carried out by civil engineers to ensure strong domain-informed labeling. Nevertheless, as with any manually annotated dataset, subjective interpretation may introduce minor inconsistencies, particularly in regions where crack boundaries or severity levels are ambiguous. In addition, DamSegment focuses exclusively on two surface-visible damage types, cracking and spalling, that can be consistently identified in RGB imagery. Other deterioration mechanisms discussed in the Background section, such as erosion or abrasion and delamination are not explicitly included, except that cracks or spalls resulting from these mechanisms are labeled.
