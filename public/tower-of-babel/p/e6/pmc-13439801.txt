Background

Measles is a highly contagious viral infection that spreads through the air and contaminated surfaces [1]. Measles has been a vaccine-preventable disease since the introduction of the first measles vaccine in 1963 [2]. This first vaccine was followed by the MMR vaccine in 1971, which protects against Measles, Mumps, and Rubella [2]. Measles serves as a key indicator disease due to its importance in various fields, including virology, epidemiology, clinical research, statistics, and mathematical biology [3]. Mathematical modeling of measles outbreaks has a long history, beginning with Pyotr Dimitrievich Enko’s seminal work in 1889, who used a chain binomial process to develop the first model for measles spread decades before the widely known Reed-Frost model [3]. Since then, measles has played a major role in the development of mathematical epidemiology as a key tool for investigating the spread and control of infectious diseases. The pronounced regularity and high incidence of measles epidemics, particularly evident through major outbreak peaks in the pre-vaccination era (before 1964), make measles data especially suitable for testing and validating new epidemic model frameworks [3, 4].

In recent decades, measles cases have been reported in non-vaccinated and non-immune individuals in the United States, despite being declared eliminated in 2000. Although MMR vaccination is an effective measure to reduce the risk of measles outbreaks [2, 5] and prolong the inter-epidemic periods [6, 7], it has been subject to hesitation and refusal by some groups due to religious and ideological reasons, among others [8–10]. The 2025 measles outbreak in Texas and its spillover into other states are among many examples of a resurgence of measles in the United States.

In January 2025, laboratory-confirmed measles cases were reported in West Texas. These were the first measles cases in Texas since 2023. This case introduction in Texas progressed into an outbreak that started in Gaines County and spread to other 36 West, North and Central Texas counties, including Andrews, Atascosa, Bailey, Bexar, Borden, Brewster, Brown, Carson, Cochran, Collin, Dallam, Dawson, Eastland, Ector, El Paso, Erath, Fannin, Gaines, Garza, Hale, Hardeman, Hockley, Lamar, Lamb, Lubbock, Lynn, Martin, McLennan, Midland, Parmer, Potter, Randall, Reeves, Rockwall, Terry, Upshur, and Yoakum. In mid-August 2025, a total of 762 cases were reported (see Fig. 1), with approximately 38% among school-aged children.Fig. 1Map of the data of the number of measles cases in 37 Texas counties [11]

Map of the data of the number of measles cases in 37 Texas counties [11]

We developed an age-structured multi-stage mathematical model to describe the spread of measles across three age groups: 0–4 years (preschool-age children), 5–17 years (school-age children), and 18 years and older (adults). We fitted the model to reported measles outbreak cases in Texas through mid-June using Bayesian inference. We used the fitted model to estimate the epidemiological parameters of this outbreak, including age-specific initial immunity level and the effective reproduction number. We used Leave-One-Out (LOO) cross-validation to assess the model’s predictive performance. Finally, we used the model to conduct a counterfactual intervention analysis of the measles outbreak. We consider multiple scenarios of school closures and reactive, age-specific, targeted vaccination strategies.

Methods

Population and data sources

We used data on reported cumulative measles cases from January 23, 2025, to June 17, 2025 [11]. The measles case data are categorized by age groups (0–4 years, 5–17 years, and 18 years or older) and are shown in Table S1 in Supplementary Material S1.1. The data span 37 several counties in West, North, and Central Texas (see Fig. 1 and the Introduction). We also used county-level population size data from 2020 to 2024 [12]. Total population sizes in 2024 are 376,327, 1,096,513, and 4,659,604 for the age groups 0–4 years, 5–17 years, and 18+ years, respectively. We used MMR vaccination coverage data for those counties from 2019 to 2024 [13] to inform the prior distribution of immunity levels across the three age groups.

Estimation and simulation

Measles outbreak analyses

We conducted several counterfactual analyses to investigate the impact of various reactive interventions on the spread of measles in West, North, and Central Texas. The interventions could be classified into daycare & schools closures, reactive vaccinations, and a combination of both. The first type of intervention, daycare and school closures, affects age-specific contact rates, thereby reducing transmission rates.

Intervention 1: daycare and school closures

Discussion

As of November 26, 2025, the CDC reported 1,798 confirmed measles cases across 43 jurisdictions, resulting from 46 outbreaks [25]. Only 23 of whom involve international visitors. In all, 92% of the cases are unvaccinated or have unknown vaccination status. West, North, and Central Texas have accounted for almost 45% of those nationally confirmed measles cases [11], with an age pattern consistent with the nationally reported data [11, 25]. The measles outbreak in Texas resulted in the hospitalization of 13% of cases (comparable to 12% in the USA [25]). Two out of the three fatalities in the US occurred to unvaccinated school-aged children in Texas [11, 25].

Measles is a vaccine-preventable childhood disease that can be prevented with two doses of the measles, mumps, and rubella (MMR) vaccine, a very safe and effective vaccine [11, 25]. According to the CDC [25], MMR vaccine coverage among kindergartners in the USA dropped from 95.2% to 92.7% between 2019 and 20 and 2023–24. Typically, the herd immunity threshold for measles is about 95%. In Texas, there is a 3.72% drop in the MMR vaccination rate of kindergartners, from 96.96% in 2019–20 to 93.24% in 2024–2025 [13]. Among seventh graders, the drop in MMR vaccination is approximately 1.47%, but it remains above the herd immunity threshold at 97.25% coverage [13].

In the 2024–25 school year, 25 of the 37 focus counties had MMR vaccine coverage below 95% among kindergarten-age infants. The lowest rate is in Gaines County of 77.26% [13], where the outbreak originated, a clear case of health externality. See Figure S2 for more details about the changes in MMR vaccine coverage among kindergartners from the school year 2019–20 to 2024–2025. Only five of the 37 focus counties have less than 95% MMR vaccine coverage among seventh graders in the same school year, including Gaines at 84.78% [13]. See Figure S3 for more details about the changes in MMR vaccine coverage among seventh graders from the school year 2019–20 to 2024–2025.

In this paper, we employed an age-structured compartmental SEIR model, supported by a statistical model, and estimated using Bayesian inference to study counterfactual intervention scenarios during the first weeks of the outbreak. Those scenarios include daycare and school closures, reactive vaccination campaigns, and combinations of these strategies. The estimated effective reproduction number at the time of the first announcement suggests immunity below the level needed to mitigate outbreaks. Ideally, a preemptive vaccination would have mitigated the outbreak. In the current case, a more substantial curbing of the outbreak could have been achieved through a combination of school closures and reactive vaccination of children.

The marginal benefit of extending closure durations varies by intervention type and the level of transmission reduction. Reactive vaccination campaigns show no notable change in marginal benefit with higher rates. The benefit of daycare closure grows gradually with each additional week of closure, peaking around 9 weeks, then tapers off, preventing fewer than 8 additional cases. Longer school closures have a more significant impact, with the number of cases averted for each additional week plateauing after 9 weeks and then decreasing, reaching fewer than 25 cases. The combined closure provides the highest marginal benefit at week 7 across all studied reduction levels, with diminishing returns above that. The additional cases prevented by vaccinating infants and the combined infant and children vaccination campaign decline nonlinearly with each 0.1% increase in rates, whereas the children vaccination campaign decreases nearly linearly, showing small gains from additional vaccination efforts.

The current model did not explicitly incorporate the spatial structure of the outbreak; it assumed homogeneous mixing across affected counties. Additionally, it did not account for differences in contact patterns between rural and urban areas or for mobility-driven transmission between counties. These simplifications might introduce bias in estimates of the outbreak dynamics and intervention impacts. With future availability of county-level incidence data, a spatially explicit SEIR model could be developed, incorporating variable contact rates and mobility patterns across counties to better understand the geographic spread. However, since most cases were reported in and around Gaines County, with fewer cases in distant counties, the core findings on intervention effectiveness and diminishing returns are likely to stay valid.

Previous modeling studies have focused on estimating the growth rate of the 2025 measles outbreak in Texas and New Mexico [26], predicting the spatial spread of the outbreak [27], and simulating hypothetical county-level case introduction and potential outbreak size of measles outbreaks in Texas under different pre-outbreak MMR vaccination coverage [14]. However, these studies did not investigate the potential impact of reactive control strategies, such as temporary closure of schools and daycares and reactive vaccination, which have been essential for controlling previous measles outbreaks in the United States [28]. Our study builds on prior modeling studies by conducting a retrospective analysis of the 2025 measles outbreak in Texas to assess the potential impact of reactive strategies on its spread.

Conclusions

In conclusion, a comprehensive education campaign on the importance of vaccination and its safety is warranted to avoid the health and economic toll of measles. When routine/preemptive vaccination is suboptimal, other measures, such as reactive vaccination and preferably extended school/daycare closure, may help curb the spread of an outbreak. To maintain the USA measles elimination status, it is paramount to redouble efforts to address those topics/concerns that promote vaccine hesitancy in under-vaccinated communities across the country.
