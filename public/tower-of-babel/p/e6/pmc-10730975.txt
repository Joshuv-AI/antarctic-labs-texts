Virtual Visits for Developmental Screening in High-Risk Infants
Authors: Madhangi Jayaraman, Mandhir Gupta, Ninu Varghese, Rui Yan, Julie X Xu, Jeffrey Scott Mallin
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe High-Risk Infant Follow-Up program screens neonatal intensive care unit graduates for developmental delays and refers patients to physical, occupational, or speech therapy. At Kaiser Permanente Downey, the High-Risk Infant Follow-Up program assessed development via video visits during the COVID-19 pandemic. This study compares rates of enrollment in and referral to therapies and California Regional Center services between infants assessed by video visits and those assessed in person.MethodsStudy participants included infants in the High-Risk Infant Follow-Up program between March 2020 and March 2021 with developmental assessments at adjusted ages 6, 12, and 18 months. Rates of enrollment in services and referral to physical, occupational, and speech therapy and Regional Center services were compared between infants assessed virtually and in person.ResultsThere were no significant differences in rates of enrollment or referral to physical, occupational, or speech therapy services or Regional Center services. The rate of referral at any of the 3 visits was 8.6% at in-person visits and 10.9% at virtual visits (p = 0.49). Infants whose first visit was virtual were just as likely to complete all 3 developmental assessments as those whose first visit was in person.DiscussionThe COVID-19 pandemic stay-at-home orders provided a “natural experiment” demonstrating the effectiveness of virtual visits as compared to in-person visits. Although the developmental assessment tool used was not standardized to be used virtually, virtual visits were just as likely to result in referrals to services and did not lead to loss of follow-up.ConclusionVirtual visits may be useful and convenient for some families, but further study is required.

Introduction

Premature and low birth weight infants are at higher risk for developmental delays. 1,2 Hintz et al found that 50% of premature and very low birth weight infants exhibit neurodevelopmental impairment. 1 Early initiation of physical, occupational, and speech therapy (PT, OT, and ST, respectively) improves cognitive and motor development in infancy, with some benefits persisting in preschool. 3 Infants at risk for developmental delays depend on timely developmental assessments and referrals for services to PT, OT, and ST. All neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) accredited by California Children’s Services are required to have an associated High-Risk Infant Follow-Up (HRIF) program. The HRIF program provides 3 developmental assessments for infants born before 32 weeks gestational age, or whose birth weight is less than 1500 g or who have other predisposing conditions for developmental delays. 4 Based on the results of these developmental screenings, infants are offered referrals to or are encouraged to continue receiving PT, OT, and ST. Referrals are also offered for infant developmental therapies provided by the Regional Centers of California, a state-based program not affiliated with Kaiser Permanente. Regional Centers provide services to persons with developmental disabilities, including infants at high risk for disabilities such as preterm and low birth weight infants. These services include therapies aimed at improving all domains of development, such as cognitive, motor, and language development.

Prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, developmental assessments required in-person visits. However, during the COVID-19 pandemic and mandated stay-at-home measures, telehealth usage for developmental assessments and therapy expanded across the US and internationally. 5–9 Only a few studies have evaluated virtual and in-person visits have similar rates of referrals to PT, OT, and ST and developmental therapies. 10,11

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the Kaiser Permanente Downey HRIF program utilized both synchronous virtual visits and in-person visits to screen high-risk infants for developmental delays and to provide referrals, providing a unique opportunity to compare the effectiveness of virtual visits to in-person visits. This study compares rates of referral to and enrollment in PT, OT, and ST and Regional Center services between in-person and virtual assessments.

Demographic data were collected by the case manager at the time of NICU discharge. Parents were asked to identify their ethnicity and race. Data were sent to the California Perinatal Quality Care Collaborative. 12 Scores from developmental assessments and utilization of and referral to PT, OT, and ST and Regional Center services were also sent. The California Perinatal Quality Care Collaborative created a dataset and made it available for analysis.

Patients were assessed with 3 standard visits. Standard visit 1 (SV1) occurred around adjusted age 6 months, standard visit 2 (SV2) occurred at approximately adjusted age 12 months, and standard visit 3 (SV3) was conducted at adjusted age 18 months. In March 2020, due to the COVID-19 pandemic and subsequent stay-at-home orders, in-person visits were converted to virtual visits. During the latter part of the year, some developmental assessments were performed in person.

Infants were retrospectively analyzed. Infants were assessed at SV1, SV2, and SV3 with BINS. 13 The BINS consists of 11 to 13 items and is designed to assess risk of developmental delay, particularly in high-risk infants. Items are selected from the Bayley Scales of Infant Development-—Second Edition, in addition to assessments of muscle tone and quality of movement. The BINS is scored using a numeric scale and covers cognitive, language and motor skills, and neurological function. The score is then translated to an overall rating of low, moderate, or high risk for developmental delay. 13 The BINS was used for both virtual and in-person visits.

Clinicians sometimes elected to use a more detailed assessment such as the Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development, third edition (Bayley-III). 14 Prior to the pandemic, the Bayley-III was used for SV3. However, the Bayley-III cannot be performed virtually. In addition, the BINS can be completed in 15 to 30 minutes, while the Bayley-III requires 1 hour to complete. To avoid prolonged contact and potential COVID exposure to patients and parents, the BINS was used for both virtual and in-person visits for all but 2 patients during the study period. These 2 patients were assessed with the Bayley-III. As there were no comparable virtual assessments utilizing the Bayley-III, the 2 visits of patients who were seen in person and assessed with the Bayley-III were excluded. No virtual visits were excluded.

All assessments were conducted by 1 of 3 physicians. In-person assessments were conducted in clinic with parental assistance, using toys and materials provided by the publisher of the assessment.

In contrast, virtual visits were conducted via a telehealth platform known as HealthCare Anywhere that is compliant with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act. Clinicians used videoconferencing via HealthCare Anywhere to call parents. Parents were asked to provide comparable toys or non-toy substitutes and were provided instructions via video to have the infants perform developmental tasks. Clinicians observed the infants via video and scored their development. As parents sometimes had difficulty convincing the child to perform the task, parental report was accepted to confirm completion of a developmental task.

Statistical analysis

Infant demographic and clinical characteristics were described using percentages and compared between in-person and virtual visits using the chi-squared test. Results of the developmental screenings at each of the first, second, and third visits were presented as a number and a percentage, assessed as low, medium, and high risk, and compared between infants assessed in person and infants assessed virtually. Rates of referral to and utilization of PT, OT, and ST and Regional Center services were compared between infants seen in person and those assessed virtually. Statistical significance was determined using the chi-squared test. Odds ratios for referral were calculated using logistic regression analyses. Adjustment for further covariates was not possible due to the small sample size.

Results

During the period from March 2020 to March 2021, 428 visits for developmental assessment were completed using the BINS. Of these, 178 were first visits, usually around adjusted age 6 months. Another 147 were second visits, usually at adjusted age 12 months, and 103 were third and final visits at adjusted age 18 months.

There were no significant differences between gestational age, birth weight, or gender between infants whose first visit was virtual as opposed to in person (Table 1). No significant differences were found between the 2 groups for any other qualifying condition such as seizure activity or length of time on oxygen (Table 1). Similarly, infants who had a second virtual or in-person visit were not statistically significantly different when comparing gestational age, birth weight, gender, or other qualifying conditions (Table 1). At SV3, the in-person group had a higher proportion of patients with birth weight more than 1500 g compared to the virtual group (46.4% vs 34.7%, p = 0.04, Table 1). There were no differences for gestational age or gender. No differences were found between both groups at SV3 for other qualifying conditions.

Overall, rates of referral at any of the 3 standard visits were 8.6% (10/116) at in-person visits and 10.9% (34/312) at virtual visits (p = 0.49).

At SV1, a similar proportion of patients seen in person and virtually scored at low risk for developmental delay (57.7% vs 60.3%, p = 0.82, Table 2). Similarly, at SV2, patients assessed in person and virtually also had a similar proportion with scores of low risk for developmental delay (66.7% vs 65.8%, p = 0.17, Table 2). Finally, at SV3, 50% of those evaluated in person scored at low risk for developmental delay in contrast to 61.3% evaluated virtually, a statistically insignificant result (p = 0.40, Table 2).

At SV2, 25% of patients in the in-person visit group were receiving PT compared to 22.5% of patients in the virtual visit group (p = 0.68, Table 3). Hardly any were referred to PT: 0% in the in-person group and 3.6% in the virtual visit group (p = 0.68, Table 3). Similar proportions of patients were receiving OT (22.2% in the person group vs 27.9% in the virtual visit group, p = 0.66, Table 3) and were referred to OT (2.8% vs 2.7%, p = 0.66, Table 3). Roughly the same proportion of patients in the in-person group as in the virtual visit group were receiving Regional Center services (72.2% vs 66.7%, p = 0.14, Table 3).

Similar proportions of patients at their third and final visit received any referral (21.4% in-person vs 14.7% virtual, p = 0.41, Table 3). In the in-person visit group, 32.1% of patients were receiving PT, and 3.6% were referred at SV3; in the virtual visit group, 25.3% were receiving PT, and 2.7% were referred (p = 0.75, Table 3). In the in-person visit group, 32.1% of patients were receiving OT, and 0% were referred to OT; in the virtual visit group, 30.7% were receiving OT, and 1.3% were referred (p = 0.82, Table 3). In the in-person visit group at SV3, 25% were receiving ST, and 17.9% were referred to ST at the time of the visit. In contrast, 12% of the virtual visit group were receiving ST, and 12% were referred, but these results were not statistically different (p = 0.26, Table 3). Finally, 82.1% of patients in the in-person visit group and 65% in the virtual visit group were receiving Regional Center services (p = 0.39, Table 3). None of the in-person visits and only 2.7% of the virtual visits resulted in Regional Center service referrals at SV3 (p = 0.39, Table 3). There was no significant difference in referral to PT in patients who had a virtual visit compared to an in-person visit after adjusting for birth weight (OR 1.76, 95% CI [0.59-5.27], p = 0.31). This was also true for OT (OR 1.72, 95% CI [0.57-5.15], p = 0.33) and Regional Center services (OR 0.66, 95% CI [0.24-1.83], p = 0.42).

Similar proportions of patients who had in-person SV1 and virtual SV1 went on to complete all 3 visits (61.5% in-person SV1 vs 65.9% virtual SV1, p = 0.58).

Discussion

The COVID-19 pandemic created unique challenges and opportunities for health care practioners and staff. To avoid delaying critical developmental assessments while protecting patients, parents, clinicians, and staff from exposure to COVID-19, the Kaiser Permanente Downey HRIF program adopted a creative approach for high-risk infants. The COVID-19 pandemic provided an opportunity for a “natural experiment” to determine if virtual visits would compromise care delivery.

Using synchronous virtual video visits with the developmental tools already available, infants were screened for developmental delay. Infants screened virtually were receiving and referred to developmental therapies at similar rates as those screened by in-person assessments. There were no significant differences in referrals to PT, OT, ST, and Regional Center services at the first, second, or third visit. There were also no significant differences in the proportion of infants in both groups who were receiving these services at each of the 3 visits. Interestingly, infants screened virtually at the first visit were equally likely to complete all 3 visits as those screened in person. The sample size was small and limits the degree to which a difference could be detected. However, these results suggest that virtual assessments and interactions with clinicians helped families to access crucial developmental assessments and therapies while still in isolation.

Of note, more parents declined Regional Center services in the virtual visit group compared to the in-person group. It is possible that clinicians may have been more effective in convincing parents to utilize Regional Center services in person rather than virtually. Anecdotally, parents reported that the Regional Center was also offering virtual therapy, which some parents did not feel was helpful and may have deterred parents from using the Regional Center’s services. However, a majority of parents in both groups utilized Regional Center services when offered.

There are several potential limitations to this study. First, although the BINS was used virtually, it is important to note that the BINS was neither designed nor validated to be used as a virtual assessment tool. It is also intended to be used with the proprietary toys sold by the publisher. With virtual visits, parents were allowed to use toys and non-toy substitutes that they had at home. The BINS assessment was also designed as an observational assessment that does not include parental report. Including parental report and nonstandardized toys could potentially lower or increase scores, affecting rates of referral. Even though the BINS was not used in the way that it was intended, significant differences were not found in assessment outcomes between infants in the in-person and virtual visit groups. Second, since the time of this study, the BINS has not been widely usead it is derived from an older version (the second edition) of the Bayley Scales of Infant Development—Second Edition. Currently, the fourth edition is in use. Although the BINS continues to be commercially available, the lack of widespread use limits the generalizability of this study. Third, this was not a prospective study, and study groups were not assigned at the time when infants entered the cohort.

Nevertheless, this “natural experiment” suggests that virtual visits can play a useful role in developmental assessments. Patients were able to continue to have developmental follow-up in the comfort of their homes without any loss of follow-up or therapy. For parents, virtual visits can save time in travel. 11,15 Virtual visits may be more convenient for parents of infants who are medically fragile and require additional equipment such as oxygen. 16 For premature infants at increased risk of infection, having a virtual visit may help to limit their exposure to viral illnesses. 16 Importantly, provision of valuable therapies to high-risk infants was not delayed.

Future large-scale studies with standardized assessments are warranted. One standardized assessment tool that could be used in such a study is the Developmental Assessment of Young Children, second edition (DAYC-2). The DAYC-2 also does not require the use of proprietary toys, allowing families to use materials that they have at home. As a result, the DAYC-2 lends itself to both synchronized virtual video visits and in-person clinic visits.

Conclusion

During the COVID-19 pandemic and subsequent stay-at-home orders, use of virtual visits for developmental assessments resulted in comparable rates of utilization of and referral to infant development therapies as in-person visits. This suggests that virtual visits do not lead to a loss of services and may benefit some families.
