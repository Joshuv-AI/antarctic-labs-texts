Supporting Evidence-based Responses to Emotional Needs in Emphysema (SERENE): protocol for a randomized, open-label mechanistic trial comparing Coping Skills Training and disease-specific education for depressive symptoms conducted in United States health systems
Authors: Joanna L. Hart, Daniel Carter, Chloe Hinton, James Grilli, Lily A. Brown, Nora Brier, Yingying Lu, Casey Whitman, Michael O. Harhay, Michael Sims, Carmen Alvarez, Lisa R. Miller-Matero, C. Virginia O’Hayer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDepressive symptoms and anxiety are highly prevalent among people with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), strongly associated with poor outcomes, and rarely recognized or treated. Integrating families into interventions may amplify supportive care treatment effects and overcome common challenges, yet this strategy is understudied. The Supporting Evidence-based Responses to Emotional Needs in Emphysema (SERENE) trial’s main objective is to identify the mechanisms through which a family-partnered Coping Skills Training (CST) reduces depressive symptoms among patients with COPD, testing five putative mechanisms: family relationship quality, patient and caregiver self-efficacy, patient loneliness, and caregiver psychological distress.MethodsSERENE will enroll 375 patient-support person (i.e., family caregiver) dyads from two academic health systems. Eligible patients have documented COPD, elevated levels of depressive symptoms (i.e. PHQ-8 scores ≥ 8), and age ≥ 18 years old. Ineligible participants are those with new or changing behavioral health treatments or behavioral health emergencies. After enrollment by research staff, we randomize dyads in a 2:1 ratio to receive either a 12-week CST program or a 12-week COPD education program, respectively. Both are delivered to the dyads via phone or videoconferencing sessions and, therefore, arm assignment is not blinded to staff nor participants. We will test whether randomization to receipt of CST leads to improvements in patients’ depressive symptoms and test mechanisms of efficacy. The primary efficacy outcome is PHQ-9 scores 14 weeks following enrollment. Our five putative mechanisms, corresponding to those previously specified, are measured with the Family Emotional Involvement and Criticism Scale, the General Self-efficacy Scale, the UCLA Loneliness Scale, the PHQ-9, and the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7. We will measure secondary outcomes through 12 months. Those performing data analyses will remain blinded to group assignments at the individual level until completion of the primary analyses. The study team identifies and reports serious adverse events (i.e., suicidal behaviors or psychiatric hospitalizations).DiscussionSERENE will determine how scalable supportive care interventions that strengthen existing social networks, including the crucial support of family caregivers, improve outcomes in COPD. The results will lay the foundation for paradigm-shifting approaches to managing COPD and similar illnesses through family-directed supportive care interventions.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov Identifier NCT06600126. Introduction

Background and rationale {6a}

Patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) have high levels of psychological distress that are both strongly associated with poor health outcomes and rarely treated [1]. COPD is the third leading cause of death globally, affecting over 300 million people worldwide [2], and is expected to affect up to 600 million people by 2050 [3]. Psychological distress develops in COPD as patients experience dyspnea, functional limitations, dependency, isolation, and threats of mortality. Psychological distress co-occurring with COPD has a profound negative impact on patient well-being and clinical outcomes, exceeding even the influence of objective pulmonary function decline [4]. Indeed, the presence of depressive symptoms has been associated with worse quality of life; increased dyspnea; lower medication adherence; more frequent and more severe COPD exacerbations and hospitalizations; and a four times greater risk of needing rehospitalization after an exacerbation [1, 5–7].

Worldwide, the presence of both depressive symptoms and a diagnosis of COPD are both highly associated with worse health care access. Both individual and community financial wealth are associated with a lower risk of developing COPD [8, 9] and, once developed, COPD outcomes are worse among patients living in poverty [8]. Individuals in these groups also have limited access to behavioral health resources [10]. Such structural stressors diminish individuals’ and families’ physical, social, and psychological resources and reduce access to health-enhancing services such as behavioral health care and tobacco treatment (Fig. 1). Interventions that improve patients’ and families’ abilities to cope with the associated chronic stress and disease-specific functional losses, relationship strain, and symptoms may mitigate the burden of COPD and improve both patient- and family-centered outcomes as well as traditional clinical outcomes.Fig. 1Conceptual model of mechanisms through which Coping Skills Training (CST) improves patient outcomes. Treatments for psychological distress co-occurring with COPD, particularly non-pharmacologic interventions, are underused despite improving patients’ clinical and functional outcomes and reducing their disability and healthcare utilization [1, 5]. Our prior work revealed that pulmonologists lack evidence, self-efficacy, and resources to identify and manage these symptoms, although they recognize the relevance and importance in COPD management [11]. Therefore, multidisciplinary resources tailored to COPD are necessary. Interventions that leverage patients’ existing social environments are a promising part of that multidisciplinary approach, as high-quality family relationships (i.e., families with a sense of cohesion, role fulfillment, problem solving, and communication) are associated with better outcomes for patients with serious illnesses [12]. In COPD, less supportive family relationships are associated with harmful smoking behaviors, increased dyspnea, and lower QOL via higher psychological distress [13–15]. In addition, engaging with the family caregivers who already provide support to patients enhances the scalability of such programs.

Coping Skills Training (CST) is a scalable intervention that teaches cognitive-behavioral skills to patient-family caregiver (i.e., support person) dyads, enabling them to better respond to the many emotional, physical, functional, and psychosocial consequences of COPD [16]. In a prior trial, CST improved patients’ psychological and health-related quality of life more than an educational control [16]. Yet, the mechanisms of the intervention’s success remain unknown and the prior work did not adequately represent patient groups in which COPD is highly prevalent [16].

Objectives {7}

The Supporting Evidence-based Responses to Emotional Needs in Emphysema (SERENE) Trial now seeks to understand how patients’ social environments can be leveraged as a critical pathway to reduce their depressive symptoms. Our central objective is to identify the mechanisms through which Coping Skills Training (CST) reduces patients’ depressive symptoms. SERENE specifically aims to (1) identify mechanisms through which a cognitive-behavioral skills program delivered to patients and their support persons reduces patient distress, (2) elucidate causes of heterogeneity in the efficacy of CST, and (3) identify barriers and facilitators to CST uptake and delivery.

Trial design {8}

SERENE is a two-arm randomized mechanistic clinical trial. Patients with COPD enroll with an identified support person (i.e., family caregiver) as a dyad. Each dyad is assigned to one of two 12-week interventions in a 2:1 ratio: CST or COPD education, respectively (Fig. 2). Most patients receive CST given the trial is designed to identify mechanisms of CST’s action. COPD education is the attention control comparison. We will collect patient-reported data in three phases: at 5 and 7, 12 and 14, and 24 and 26 weeks after program initiation, with primary measures at 12 and 14 weeks. Clinical outcomes will be observed via electronic medical records for up to 12 months after enrollment.Fig. 2Study process and participant flow

Methods: Participants, interventions and outcomes

Study setting {9}

SERENE will enroll 375 patients with COPD who are experiencing depressive symptoms at University of Pennsylvania Health System (Penn) and Henry Ford Health (HFH). We selected Penn and HFH to reach and represent populations most affected by COPD in the United States, including patients often experiencing limited access to health care resources [8, 17]. Penn is headquartered in Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, where the 25% poverty rate is the highest of the ten most populous US cities. HFH is headquartered in Detroit, Michigan. Detroit’s 35% poverty rate is the highest among all large US cities. Both cities also have two of the highest rates of deep poverty (i.e., households earning < 50% of the federal poverty limit). Both health systems have networks of clinics located in rural areas. Recruitment began September 2024 at Penn and March 2025 at HFH.

Eligibility criteria {10}

Eligible patients have a confirmed diagnosis of COPD of any severity and are experiencing high levels of depressive symptoms (i.e., Patient Health Questionnaire-8 score ≥ 8) [18]. Such scores indicate elevated levels of depressive symptoms over the prior two weeks, with higher scores indicating more depressive symptoms and the selected cut-off being highly sensitive and specific for major depression [19]. Patients undergo psychological risk assessment administered by trained research staff (Supplement). Those with active suicidality, mania, or psychosis receive immediate referral to specialized behavioral health management, as these patients’ care needs exceed the scope of the intervention. Participants must have access to either telephone or videoconferencing equipment for 30 min once per week for 12 weekly sessions given the remote delivery of the intervention. We exclude patients who do not have an identifiable support person willing to participate because this trial specifically tests mechanisms reliant on patients’ existing social structures. Interventions

Explanation for the choice of comparators {6b}

We compare a socio-behavioral educational with an attention control arm that augments usual care for depressive symptoms in COPD. Currently, patients with COPD rarely receive care for psychological distress associated with their disease. We selected CST based on preliminary work, in which patients, families, and clinicians identified important characteristics of interventions to reduce psychological distress in COPD (i.e., remote delivery, telephone-based options that do not require advanced technological resources or literacy, involvement of family members, do not rely on behavioral health clinicians given resource limitations) [11]. We selected our comparison arm as patients COPD often experience social isolation that may be affected by increased interpersonal contact regardless of the content [20, 21]. Therefore, we selected a comparator that provided routine, brief interpersonal contact without any specific content or techniques used in CST.

Intervention description {11a}

Coping Skills Training

CST is a socio-behavioral educational intervention previously shown to reduce depressive symptoms in patients with COPD and co-occurring psychological distress [16]. CST focuses on modifying the cognitive factors (i.e., expectations and attitudes) and behaviors (i.e., relationship functioning, physical activity, health promotion) that affect physical symptoms and disability in chronic illness. This CST program was specifically developed for use with patients with COPD and for remote delivery to dyads (i.e., by telephone) [16]. We further adapted the CST content based on this previous testing and for our target populations [16]. CST focuses on improving participants’ responses to stress, imbalances in the patient-caregiver relationship, and communication, and provides support persons with skills to coach patients in using positive coping strategies [16]. The program includes 12 30-min weekly sessions (Table 2) delivered to the dyad. Specialized research staff lead each CST session after intensive training conducted by a clinical psychologist experienced in CST (CVOH). Prior to delivering CST sessions to participants, research staff demonstrate mastery through mock sessions recorded and reviewed by the trainer. The sessions occur via telephone or videoconferencing with ongoing oversight from clinical psychologists (NB, LB, CVOH) to promote fidelity and provide additional coaching. Participants receive a mailed workbook of CST materials used during and between the sessions and have access to audio recordings of key exercises. The dyad’s experiences completing the exercises are reviewed in subsequent sessions, along with the dyad’s perceived progress, setbacks, and evolving relationship. Intervention components

We measure fidelity to CST as designed using quantitative and qualitative data. Research staff administering the sessions record each session’s duration in minutes and document field notes of discussions during meetings with the clinical psychologists, including the details of and rationale for any adaptations to the intended content [22, 23]. Consistent with recommendations from the NIH Behavior Change Consortium, we audio-record a random sample of 10% of sessions. Trained assessors rate fidelity of CST sessions based on whether pre-defined, key elements occur.

Attention control (COPD Education)

The control arm provides COPD Education sessions to augment usual care and provide an attention control. Dyads receive 12 10-min weekly sessions conducted by telephone or videoconferencing during which trained research staff provide scripted support focused on general information about COPD [21]. Participants also receive a mailed workbook of educational materials originally developed by The COPD Foundation [24], portions of which were selected to correspond to each session without overlapping with CST content (Table 2). Research staff delivering the COPD Education sessions are blinded to the CST content to prevent contamination.

Criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated interventions {11b}

Participation in CST or COPD Education sessions is suspended if a participant requires acute behavioral health care interventions such as psychiatric hospitalization. This may occur due to study staff recognizing acute needs and referring for evaluation and/or through evaluations external to the study. Participants may return and continue the interventions with approval from a treating behavioral health clinician. Participants may withdraw at any time or when advanced illness, prolonged hospitalization, or death precludes ongoing participation of the dyad.

Strategies to improve adherence to interventions {11c}

Participants may access the intervention sessions via telephone or videoconferencing and on flexible schedules (i.e., evening and weekend times) that maximize accessibility. Prior to each session, participants receive customized reminders via telephone or electronic message. In addition, participants may complete the 12 planned weekly sessions over no more than 16 weeks to provide flexibility and maximize adherence to the intervention. We also use strategies such as rapport building with staff, study branding, and welcome packets to improve adherence.

Relevant concomitant care permitted or prohibited during the trial {11d}

Participation in SERENE does not prohibit or prevent any required treatment for COPD or behavioral health. Staff refer respiratory symptoms or other physical health concerns to participants’ treating pulmonary or primary care clinicians. When a participant is hospitalized for reasons other than behavioral health, the dyad may elect to continue the sessions during the hospitalization or defer until discharge. During a behavioral health crisis, participation in SERENE interventions is suspended until the individual has clearance from a treating clinician to resume sessions, to prevent conflict with the individual’s primary behavioral health treatments.

Provisions for post-trial care {30}

Following the 12-week intervention period, participants provide data through 26 weeks following enrollment. Research staff and clinicians continue to respond to participants’ reports of suicidal ideation or behavior, or who experience physical COPD symptoms (i.e., COPD exacerbations) throughout their participation in the trial. No specific post-trial care is provided. The tools dyads receive during the trial, such as the workbook, remain available to them.

Outcomes {12}

Our primary efficacy outcome is patient-reported depressive symptoms, as measured by the PHQ-9. Setting this as the primary outcome allows us to test mechanisms leading to its improvement. The PHQ-9 is a 9-item, self-report questionnaire with a 4-point rating for the burden of each symptom over the prior 2 weeks (i.e., score range of 0–28), with higher scores indicating more depressive symptoms. The PHQ-9 is specific for major depression and sensitive to change [25]. We will measure the PHQ-9 at baseline and at 7, 14, and 26 weeks after enrollment (Fig. 3). The primary efficacy outcome will be measured at 14 weeks, or two weeks after completion of the assigned program. Our five potential mediators include (a) family relationship quality, as reported by patients and caregivers using the Family Emotional Involvement and Criticism Scale (FEICS) [26]; (b) patient and (c) caregiver self-efficacy, measured with the General Self-efficacy Scale (GSE) [27]; (d) patient loneliness, measured by the UCLA Loneliness Scale [28]; and (e) caregiver psychological distress, measured by the PHQ-9 and Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) [29]. We will measure these constructs at baseline and at 5, 12, and 24 weeks to facilitate causal testing (Fig. 3). We selected these mediators drawing on existing evidence and our conceptual model of CST’s theoretical mechanisms of action. The FEICS a 14-item instrument in which the participant rates each statement using a 5-point Likert scale. The items include seven to assess perceived criticism and seven to assess emotional involvement, producing two scales ranging from 0–28 with higher numbers indicating higher levels of the respective construct. The GSE is a 10-item instrument in which the participant rates each statement using a 4-point Likert scale, resulting in a score ranging from 0–30 with higher numbers indicating higher levels of self-efficacy. The UCLA Loneliness Scale is a 20-item instrument in which the participant rates each statement using a 4-point Likert scale, resulting in a score ranging from 0–60 with higher numbers indicating greater loneliness. The GAD-7 is a 7-item instrument in which the participant rates each item on 4-point Likert scale, resulting in a score ranging from 0–28 with higher numbers indicating greater anxiety. Schedule of outcome measures

Participant timeline {13}

The total study duration for participants is 26 weeks. After completing the eligibility assessment, dyads complete a baseline survey and participate in one session per week for the next twelve weeks. Dyads have up to sixteen weeks to complete the program. Surveys are administered in weeks 5, 7, 12, 14, 24, and 26. Electronic medical record data will be collected passively for 12 months. Further timeline detail can be found in Fig. 3.

Sample size {14}

In a simulation using 250 iterations, we confirmed that a sample size of 300 dyads would result in at least 80% power to detect mediators that account for a PHQ-9 score difference of at least 2.75 points and a direct effect of the intervention on PHQ-9 scores of at least 3.25 points. Combined, these differences exceed the clinically significant difference for PHQ-9 [25]. We simulated a two-arm study (n = 300 dyads, 2:1 allocation) with PHQ-9 outcomes on the 0–27 scale and modest clustering intraclass correlation coefficient. The intervention was specified to yield a 3.25-point reduction via the direct effect and a 2.75-point reduction via the indirect (mediated) effect. Lower scores indicate improvement. Each simulated dataset was analyzed with a mediation model, and power was the proportion of 250 replicates with p < 0.05 for the total, indirect, and direct effects. Enrolling 375 dyads allows for up to 20% attrition prior to measurement of the primary outcome.

Recruitment {15}

Our recruitment procedures promote access to research for all eligible patients and include multiple avenues aligned with scalable clinical implementation. We actively recruit patients from primary care and pulmonary outpatient clinics. Patients with COPD presenting to selected clinical sites are uniformly screened for depressive symptoms to promote improved detection, then offered information about the study if potentially eligible. In addition, we disseminate information about SERENE broadly across the health systems and promote pathways for direct referrals from clinicians, caregivers, and patients themselves. Additional details appear in the Supplement. Study-related activities are offered in English and Spanish based on language prevalence in the communities served by Penn and HFH. Both the patient and the enrolling support person undergo a detailed informed consent process. Participants receive up to 100 USD for completion of study surveys in addition to receiving in-kind program services at no cost.

Assignment of interventions: allocation

Sequence generation {16a}

Dyads are randomized in a 2:1 ratio, stratified by health system, into CST or COPD Education, respectively. More dyads are randomized to CST to facilitate interrogation of mechanisms underlying its known efficacy. Staff initiates assignment using a randomization program Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) for each dyad, programmed to use randomization blocks of six (i.e., four CST dyads and two COPD Education dyads) and stratified by health system. Within each block, the order of the six assignments are randomized by sampling without replacement.

Concealment mechanism {16b}

The REDCap program provides concealment prior to randomization.

Implementation {16c}

Enrolling staff learn the dyad’s arm assignment following consent and baseline survey completion. The research staff members then subsequently connect the dyad with the appropriate intervention-administering staff member, who reveals the arm assignment to the dyad. Participants and the study team are aware of the randomized treatment given the nature of the interventions in this open-label trial.

Assignment of interventions: Blinding

Who will be blinded {17a}

Investigators and staff directly performing the data analysis will be blinded to group assignments until completion of the primary analyses, including mediation analyses. Staff providing COPD Education sessions are blinded to the CST content to avoid contamination of the attention control intervention.

Procedure for unblinding if needed {17b}

Not applicable.

Data collection and management

Plans for assessment and collection of outcomes {18a}

Participants respond to follow-up instruments directly using individualized REDCap survey links or, if preferred, by telephone with staff collecting their responses using the standardized survey instruments. All staff completed training and confirmation of instrument use prior to engaging in data collection. Staff confirm completeness of data collection when participants asynchronously provide responses to the surveys. REDCap is hosted at Penn and is a secure, web-based software platform designed to support data collection for research studies [30]. Participants receive a personalized electronic link to complete the instruments at each of the scheduled follow-up time points.

Plans to promote participant retention and complete follow-up {18b}

We engage in best practices for retention of participants. First, we reduce barriers to participation through offering multiple modalities and flexible timing for survey completion, minimizing the length of follow-up surveys, and prioritize key outcome measures. Second, we build community through study branding and building rapport between participants and study staff, who are trained in relational communication skills. Third, we provide compensation for time associated with completion of the surveys. Our participant-facing materials were reviewed by investigators with expertise in enrollment and retention of diverse participants and by community members and patients to maximize accessibility and relevance. Participants who miss surveys are still eligible to participate in the remaining study tasks to minimize missing data and promote maximal participation.

Data management {19}

All identifiable study data will be stored behind firewalls on secure servers. Each participant will be assigned a participant code, which will be used exclusively in all analytical files. Qualitative data from participant interviews will be transcribed and maintained in an Atlas.ti (Lumivero, Denver, CO) database on the secure server, along with the original audio files in the event primary data verification is requested or required. Electronic data that must be transferred between sites will be shared via institutionally-approved, privacy-compliant secure file transfer protocols using the REDCap secure sharing platform or other similar platform.

Confidentiality {27}

All identifiable data will be stored behind firewalls on secure servers. Individual identifiers will not be used when collecting data, as each participant will be assigned a participant code for all study data purposes. All datasets and files with participant codes will be secured using multiple, redundant protective measures to guarantee the privacy and safety of the participant data. REDCap, which is our primary electronic data capture platform used in the trial, is a secure, web-based application for direct data entry. REDCap is privacy-compliant and hosted at Penn’s highly secured data center. Participants provide informed consent for disclosure of acute symptoms (e.g., suicidal ideation or symptoms of a COPD exacerbation) with clinicians outside the study team for the purposes of clinical care. Otherwise, no information collected during the study is shared with the clinical team caring for the patient.

Statistical methods

Statistical methods for primary and secondary outcomes {20a}

We will first describe all quantitative data using appropriate summary statistics, with variance estimates, and graphical representations of the distributions. We will compare distributions of the enrolled patients’ characteristics to those of all the patients identified as potentially eligible for the study to quantify the trial’s external validity. In all quantitative analyses, we will model the health system from which the patient was recruited as a fixed effect, thereby accounting for confounding by health system and adjusting variance estimates for clustering of participants within health systems.

Our primary analytic approach will be to conduct intention-to-treat (ITT) analyses using truncated regression. This approach will allow for more accurate estimation of effects by effectively dealing with the left-censored nature of the PHQ-9 data given the inclusion cut-off (Table 1). With patients’ baseline PHQ-9 score included in the model, this approach will facilitate a comparison of the efficacy of the intervention in reducing distress among all randomized participants, modeling the health system from which the patient was recruited as a fixed effect. To further investigate the mechanisms underlying the observed effects, we will perform a mediation analysis. Specifically, we will evaluate whether differences in the five pre-specified outcomes mediate the observed differences in PHQ-9 scores based on treatment arm. We will estimate the causal pathway from a single exposure (i.e., treatment arm), to a temporally subsequent mediator (i.e., the five mediator outcome variables), and ultimately to an outcome (i.e., psychological distress). Receipt of the CST program will be modeled as a binary variable and mediating and psychological distress outcome scores as count variables. The 12-week measurement of the mediation variable corresponding to the 14-week PHQ-9 score will be entered into the primary model. These models will allow us to differentiate and test the CST program’s direct and indirect (i.e., mediated) effect on patients’ psychological distress. We will examine and account for covariance among the mediators. In a final model, we will retain only those mediators significantly associated with randomization to the CST program or improvement in patients’ PHQ-9 scores. Additional analytic details are in the Supplement.

Methods for additional analyses (e.g. subgroup analyses) {20b}

We will repeat the primary analyses for the mediator outcomes measured at 24 weeks, corresponding to the patients’ PHQ-9 scores at 26 weeks to determine the longer-term effect of the CST program mediated by our hypothesized factors. We will repeat these primary and secondary analyses using a per-protocol analysis that includes only patients who participate in ≥ 50% (i.e., six) of the CST program sessions. This analysis complements the ITT analyses by offering a clearer understanding of the intervention's potential for practical effectiveness given conditions of optimal uptake. For treatment heterogeneity, we will test statistical interactions between pre-selected patient characteristics and the two interventions in truncated regression. The selected characteristics hypothesized to influence CST’s efficacy based on existing disparities in COPD outcomes include race, income, gender, and rurality of residence.

We will also use mixed methods to characterize CST’s (1) utilization, (2) acceptability and feasibility, and (3) fidelity among our expected participants (Fig. 1) [22, 31]. Our approach will include post-session and post-program CST evaluations by participants; semi-structured interviews with a subset of CST participants, potentially eligible but non-participating patients, and clinicians; analyses of administering staff’s self-reported feasibility and field notes; and the intervention fidelity assessments. Together, these approaches will provide external validity to our results, identify mechanisms of poor uptake, and identify strategies to surmount barriers in future implementation. Additional methodological details appear in the Supplement.

Methods in analysis to handle protocol non-adherence and any statistical methods to handle missing data {20c}

We have chosen the primary endpoint at 14 weeks after enrollment and designed the study based on prior retention success to minimize missingness of our key outcomes. However, missing data for the primary or mediator outcomes can occur due to non-response or patient death, as these outcomes are patient-reported. We will fully describe all data that are missing at each follow-up time point (i.e., at 12, 14, 24, and 26 weeks). Our secondary analyses will use multiple imputation methods that assume data are missing at random by including all baseline characteristics, treatment assignment, and reasons for missingness in the imputation model. This approach allows us to use baseline data and any available follow-up data (e.g., at 12 and 14 weeks) to inform the generation of values for subsequent missing data points (e.g., at 24 and 26 weeks), and properly adjusts for the uncertainty in the resulting imputed values [32]. We will implement the multiple imputation using chain regression method.

Oversight and monitoring

Composition of the coordinating centre and trial steering committee {5d}

The Palliative and Advanced Illness Research Center is the coordinating center. To ensure trial progress, discuss challenges, and ensure consistency across the two sites, research teams across the sites meet at least weekly.

Patients, caregivers, clinicians, and patient and health equity advocates also collaborated from its earliest stages of the research, including guiding the aims, intervention selection, and study design. Prior to trial launch, study advisors (a) co-developed a plan to identify and triage concerns for acute exacerbations of COPD; (b) provided input on drafted recruitment materials and trial branding; (c) reviewed and refined retention strategies for enrolled participants (e.g., addition of a certificate of completion); and (d) participated in focus groups and teleconferences to finalize patient-facing materials. Study advisors, both longitudinal and one-time, will remain involved to review trial progress at key stages to provide input on trial progress and trial results. Advisors will also participate in dissemination activities, including strategizing for clinical implementation of relevant trial results.

Composition of the data monitoring committee, its role and reporting structure {21a}

An Independent Study Monitor (ISM), an objective faculty member who is not involved with the design and conduct of the study and who has expertise in behavioral health research, reviewed our data safety plan and study protocol before the enrollment of the first participant and will do so on a semi-annual basis. Two statisticians are involved in the analysis, one providing supervision and oversight review. There is no separate data coordinating center.

Adverse event reporting and harms {22}

During study progress, we monitor suicide risk identified on patient-reported outcomes and complete suicide risk assessment and referral for urgent behavioral health care when necessary by our safety plans and as detailed in our informed consent process. The study investigators are responsible for promptly identifying and reporting all serious adverse events, protocol deviations or violations, and unanticipated events. Research staff who identify such events through review of hospitalization alerts or participant outreach record the available details and provide information to the investigators for further review. The ISM will also review all such events. Reportable events will include participant death due to suicide, participant psychiatric hospitalization or suicide attempt, or other unexpected serious adverse event possibly related to study involvement. Such events will be reported to the Penn IRB and the National Institutes of Health.

Frequency and plans for auditing trial conduct {23}

As described above, fidelity to the CST intervention is incorporated into data collection.

Plans for communicating important protocol amendments to relevant parties (e.g. trial participants, ethical committees) {25}

Protocol changes that may affect participant safety or decision to consent to enroll will be approved by the IRB prior to implementation. As needed, the informed consent script and form will be updated and approved prior to use. For analytic or other scientific changes, the protocol will be updated and such changes will be reported in the planned manuscript or other reporting of trial results.

The trial results will be disseminated through printed materials mailed to participants and supporting clinicians; via ClinicalTrials.gov; in scientific publications and presentations; and to selected community organizations, health system contacts, and media- and website-based forums.

Discussion

Patients exist within socioecological environments that influence their health behaviors, care quality, outcomes, and psychological functioning [13, 14, 33–36]. Family relationship quality is a modifiable risk factor for poor health including psychological distress [14]. Despite this, chronic illness care models have rarely capitalized on these well-established principles, as most focus interventions on the patient alone. Using a novel paradigm, SERENE focuses on chronic illness as a stressor threatening family functioning. By testing a family-focused intervention, we will identify the mechanisms through which CST improves patients’, support persons’, and their relational outcomes to decrease patients’ depressive symptoms. This approach applies social and behavioral science to facilitate refinement and scaling of effective and efficient interventions that addressing a critical yet under-addressed aspect of COPD care.

Such novel approaches to chronic illness care are necessary due to poor penetration of this care into routine clinical practice. Integration of care for patients’ psychological health into COPD care holds the potential to improve outcomes [37]. Patients with COPD, their family members, and clinicians identify coping and emotional symptoms as top priorities in COPD care [34]. Despite these key facilitators, a minority of patients with COPD experiencing psychological distress ever receive available treatments [19, 38, 39]. Clinicians often fail to recognize patients’ needs, lack sufficient resources to respond to needs when identified, and lack self-efficacy to explore and manage patients’ psychological distress [1, 11, 37, 40]. The barriers patients face to accessing health care (e.g., barriers to in-person care in advanced COPD or limited technological literacy for telehealth) and stigmatization of both tobacco-associated lung disease and psychological distress also contribute to this care gap [1, 40]. Paradoxically, the demographic groups most affected by COPD [8, 9, 41, 42] are the same groups who experience more psychological distress yet lack access to behavioral health resources [10, 38]. Therefore, unrecognized and/or untreated psychological distress in COPD compounds and contributes to poor COPD outcomes in these patient subgroups [8, 42].

More broadly, a chronic illness care model integrating supportive, holistic, or “generalist” palliative care benefits patients yet is rarely delivered [34]. Patients’ needs dramatically outstrip the available supply of resources for behavioral health, palliative care, and social support services [43]. This is particularly true in areas with fewer socioeconomic and healthcare resources [44]. Leveraging patients’ existing resources by effectively skill-building within their social environments acknowledges the critical importance of family in chronic illness care and provides new opportunities to scale supportive care interventions. This mechanistic trial of a social-behavioral educational intervention directed at patients experiencing psychological distress in the setting of COPD and their support persons will provide new insight into how scalable improvements to patients’ social environments may lead to improved patient- and family-centered outcomes.
