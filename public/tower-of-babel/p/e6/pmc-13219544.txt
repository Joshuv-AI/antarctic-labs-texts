Brain Health in Former Professional Motorsport Drivers: Case Series in a Population Exposed to Sport-Related Concussions and Repetitive Head Impacts
Authors: Kévin Polet, Yanis Aeschlimann, Samuel Deslauriers-Gauthier, Solange Hesse, Adeline Morisot, Alain Pesce, Stéphane Chanalet, Mathieu Liberatore, Marc Faraggi, Robert Scarlot, Sandrine Louchart De La Chapelle, Benoît Paulmier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProfessional motorsport drivers face a high risk of concussions and repeated subconcussive head impacts due to their exposure to significant g-forces. Concussion symptoms may persist in some individuals, and repeated head impacts are thought to contribute to a gradual accumulation of axonal damage over time. However, little is known about the long-term neurological consequences of practicing professional motorsports. This case series aimed to present the neurological health status of former professional motorsports drivers and to explore potential relationships between cumulative head impacts exposure, resulting from professional motorsport practice over time, and long-term brain health.MethodsRecruitment began following a concussion awareness campaign led by the Automobile Club de Monaco targeting former professional motorsport drivers. Neurological health status was examined through clinical assessments (SCAT5 symptom inventory, neuropsychological assessment, video-oculography) and through neuroimaging (MRI with SWIp sequences and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI); 18 FDG-PET). We correlated data with cumulative head impact exposure, encompassing total motorsport exposure duration, professional practice duration, and self-reported concussion numbers.Results12 former professional motorsport drivers (all male, age 55.8 ± 12.1) were recruited, including 58% with cognitive complaints and 50% with motorsport-related concerns. They had a mean motorsport practice duration of 31.3 ± 5.8 years, with a mean professional career of 26.3 ± 6.5 years, and a mean retirement age of 40.4 ± 5.6 years. While all participants had engaged in multiple motorsport disciplines, 83% had competed in Open-wheel single-seater racing and/or Sport car racing. 75% had at least one symptom on SCAT5 (2.5 ± 2.8), 33% at least one cognitive impairment (0.4 ± 0.7), 75% at least one impaired eye movement (1.5 ± 1.1), 58% at least one MRI abnormality (0.9 ± 1), 92% at least one DTI abnormality (1.8 ± 1.4) and 33% at least one 18 FDG-PET abnormality (0.7 ± 1.2). Attention (25%), processing speed (58%), visual episodic (58%), and working (50%) memory skills were above average. Only eye movement abnormalities were correlated to total exposure duration and to professional practice duration.ConclusionThis case series revealed that former professional motorsport drivers presenting with brain health concerns frequently exhibited mild, nonspecific neurological abnormalities. Two findings were particularly noteworthy: first, eye movement abnormalities correlated with cumulative motorsport exposure duration, and second, participants showed an atypical cognitive profile with above-average visuospatial and processing speed performances. This unique profile may make standard clinical assessments less sensitive for detecting subtle pathological processes—whether during their active career or later in life. While no causal links can be established, the observed findings may stem from cumulative head impact exposure and a high cognitive reserve, both of which might be inherent to professional motorsport practice. These observations highlight the need for large-scale longitudinal studies that integrate pre-season baselines with video-oculography and DTI, along with prolonged post-career follow-up, to specifically investigate the effects of repeated head impacts on this population during their career and throughout aging.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40798-026-01036-7.Keywords: Professional motorsport driver, Sport-related concussion, Repetitive head impacts, Aging, Video-oculography, Diffusion tensor imaging, Cognition

Introduction

Although significant improvements have been made in driver safety [1], the incidence of sport-related concussions (SRC) and repetitive head impacts likely remains high in motorsports [2–5], even in comparison to other high-risk sports like equestrian activities or rugby [6]. For instance, one study found a significantly elevated risk of concussion among drivers, estimating it at 181 per 100,000 participants when adjusted for national sports participation figures [6]. This particular Australian cohort showed an incidence more than three times higher than that reported for rugby players. The annual concussion rate among professional motorsport athletes varies, ranging from 0.67 for saloon or single-seater cars to 1.64 for NASCAR, and from 1.57 for MotoGP to 1.86 for Enduro [1]. Furthermore, a significant proportion of motorsport athletes, between 30 and 50% of drivers, report experiencing concussions [2, 3], with nearly half of motocross riders self-reporting at least one concussion symptom during an entire racing season [4]. However, establishing a precise incidence of concussion risk in motorsports is challenging due to several factors. These include the limited number of studies, the specific differences between various motorsport disciplines, methodological biases such as reliance on self-reported concussion numbers, and the probable underreporting of concussions in these sports. Some studies, for instance, only count concussions that required hospitalization [6], which is not necessary in the majority of cases. Despite these challenges, the reported incidence of motorsport concussion in the literature varies from 6.3 to 25% [3], based on highly heterogeneous studies ranging from single events to over a decade of follow-up.

Head kinematics studies in motorsport highlight that athletes are exposed to significant vibrations, high acceleration, and deceleration g-forces, generating both linear and rotational acceleration forces, even in the absence of crashes [5, 7–9]. The magnitude of force exposure varies depending on the type of motorsport, the racing conditions, and the frequency and severity of crashes [10]. For example, during racing laps, a NASCAR study reported linear acceleration magnitudes ranging from 2.46 to 7.48 g, and rotational velocities from 1.25 to 3.35 rad/s [11]. In grassroots dirt track racing, median peak linear acceleration was approximately 5.5 g, and rotational velocity was 3.0 rad/s, with maximum magnitudes reaching 26.8 g and 19.0 rad/s respectively, often with several perturbations per second [12, 13]. During crashes, head kinematics reveal significantly higher forces: median peak linear accelerations have been reported at 19.0 g in NASCAR [10], 38.15 g in IndyCar [9], and from 7.30 to 13.4 g in grassroots dirt track racing (with rotational velocity between 6.94 and 9.67 rad/s) [12–14]. The forces and the vibrations drivers are exposed to can have both physical and cognitive effects [7]. These include repetitive subconcussive head impacts (rSHI), defined as impacts that do not cause immediate symptoms but their repetition can lead to negative consequences [15]. Indeed, these specific impacts involve linear acceleration of less than 10 g—a threshold from which potential clinical changes can occur [16, 17]—and may be related to SRC. Moreover, a study on the Indy Racing League identified that a peak linear acceleration exceeding 50 g at the cockpit level during an accident was associated with the development of traumatic head injuries [18].

SRC, as defined in the Amsterdam 2022 consensus statement [19], “is a traumatic brain injury caused by a direct blow to the head, neck or body resulting in an impulsive force being transmitted to the brain that occurs in sports and exercise-related activities. (…) SRC results in a range of clinical symptoms and signs that may or may not involve loss of consciousness”. Diagnosing SRC relies on both self-reported symptoms and clinical assessments, covering psychiatric, physical, ocular, and cognitive signs [20]. Physical manifestations include motor signs such as vertigo, balance problems, or gait difficulties, as well as symptoms, such as sleep disturbance, fatigue, and headache. Ocular disturbances are also common [21] and primarily involve oculomotor abnormalities. These can include impairments in saccades (increased latency and reduced accuracy in voluntary saccades [22–35]), smooth pursuit, fixation (nystagmus and square-wave jerks) and anti-saccades tasks [23–28, 30, 31, 35–37]. Additionally, abnormalities in accommodation, convergence, and pupillary light reflex may be present [31, 37], alongside visual symptoms like eye fatigue, blurred vision or difficulty focusing [31]. Cognitive signs can vary, but executive functions, attention, working and long-term memory impairments are frequently reported, with deficits in social cognition also being observed [38–42].

While SRC symptoms are often benign and resolve within days, there is significant individual variability in both symptomatology and recovery [19, 30, 39, 43, 44]. The majority of concussed individuals recover within 3 months [39, 45, 46]. However, in 10–20% of cases [46], persistent symptoms, defined as those present after 4 weeks [19], particularly cognitive impairment [42, 47], are observed. Chronic symptoms, alongside potential neuropathological changes, can persist for years [44].

Clinical symptoms and recovery may not correlate with radiological findings. Computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrate poor utility in detecting SRC [48, 49], with standard structural neuroimaging studies frequently appearing normal [19, 50, 51]. However, advanced MRI techniques, such as susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI) and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) metrics computed from diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), have revealed frequent white matter abnormalities, even in asymptomatic individuals [52]. SWI can detect diffuse vascular lesions due to SRC [52–54], which are often linked to greater cognitive impairment [55]. DTI enables the detection of diffuse axonal injuries, the most common lesion associated with concussion symptoms and repetitive head impact exposure [50, 56–69]. Fractional anisotropy (FA), an anisotropy index influenced by local microstructure, is the most altered indicator depending on the level of repeated head impact exposure [57]. Patterns of FA abnormalities in white matter tracts have been identified depending on the type of SRC, including rSHI, acute or chronic [60, 61]. Even when standard MRI is inconclusive, functional neuroimaging studies highlighted abnormalities in the fronto-temporal lobes [62], known to be sensitive to head impact mechanisms [63], with decreased cerebral blood flow on arterial spin labeling MRI sequences [64], or hypometabolism in 2-deoxy-2-(18F) fluoro-D-glucose positron emission tomography (18 FDG-PET) [65].

Evidence suggests a dose–response relationship between the cumulative number of repetitive rSHI and white matter abnormalities [66]. These abnormalities are primarily characterized by a reduction in FA [67–71]. This consistent FA reduction, driven by cumulative exposure rather than single impact events, highlights an accumulative risk of structural axonal damage with prolonged exposure [67, 69, 71–73]. These signs of brain injury are also reported in former concussed athletes [58, 59], suggesting a common structural injury mechanism for both SRC and rSHI exposure in collision and contact sports [66]. Consequently, both repeated SRC and rSHI may be linked to severe long-term complications. These include chronic traumatic encephalopathy, a neurodegenerative tauopathy [74–77], dementia [78, 79], and other neurodegenerative diseases, particularly motor neuron disease [80–85], which is associated with the duration of impact exposure [81, 83]. Therefore, the risks of these long-term neurological complications appear to primarily concern retired professional athletes rather than amateur athletes [86]. The existing literature is, however, heterogeneous with most studies demonstrating a high risk of bias and failing to control for potential confounding factors (e.g., genetic, demographic, health-related, or environmental) [86].

A recent 3-year longitudinal study [9] involving 13 professional IndyCar drivers, who experienced at least one accident across two of the three seasons investigated, reported no significant decrease in oculomotor performance, despite exposure to significant acceleration (median of 38.15 g). This finding suggested an absence of short-term neurological consequences from cumulative impact exposure. In contrast, a systematic review in amateur athletes exposed to rSHI after a single season [66] revealed structural brain alterations, specifically decreased FA, but heterogeneous findings regarding cognitive performance decrements. Furthermore, another systematic review [86] highlighted that former contact, collision, and combat athletes, on average, exhibit poorer performance on cognitive tests compared to control participants. Consistent with the dose–response hypothesis [66], it appears that structural damage may only become sufficiently severe to induce noticeable functional changes above a certain threshold of sustained impacts. Consequently, several seasons of head impact exposure in active athletes might not be severe enough to produce clearly detectable functional changes. Therefore, functional symptoms may manifest gradually over time, particularly with longer athletic careers due to increased exposure to rSHI [87], especially in professional athletes who experience a higher number of rSHI impacts compared to amateur athletes [66]. Given all these considerations, although there is increasing concern regarding exposure to head accelerations/vibrations [87, 88], the long-term neurological status of former professional motorsport drivers (including neurophysiological and functional data) remains largely unknown.

The objectives of this case series were: (1) to assess neurological health status through data from clinical evaluations (clinical scales, video-oculography, neuropsychological assessment) and neuroimaging (MRI, DTI, 18 FDG-PET), and (2) to explore potential relationships between cumulative head impact exposure (corresponding to years of participation in motorsports) and long-term neurological consequences in a population of former professional motorsport drivers. Aligned with the dose–response hypothesis, we propose that the cumulative head impact exposure from recurrent high-acceleration events in motorsport may lead to long-term neurological issues following a professional career.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

The Monaco Initiative for concussion in Motorsport Pilots study (MIMP) is an observational, case series, registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04730167) and written according to Fleischmann et al.'s guidelines [89].

The case series was conducted at Princess Grace Hospital, Monaco. Recruitment began in November 2021, following a concussion awareness campaign led by the Automobile Club de Monaco. Recruitment ended in June 2023, with data collection completed in August 2023 and data analysis finalized in March 2024.

Inclusion criteria stipulated that subjects be over 18 years old, retired from professional motorsport practice (e.g., Formula 1, Formula 2, enduro, rally, etc), and have undergone a comprehensive memory assessment at Princess Grace Hospital (prompted by a cognitive, somatic or emotional complaint, sleep disorders, or a motorsport-related concern). A motorsport-related concern was defined as an apprehension regarding potential long-term effects of significant exposure to a high-risk sport, characterizing individuals seeking a neurological assessment primarily due to their professional history of repetitive head impacts. Professional motorsport status was defined as an individual competing at a high level for whom motorsport was the primary source of income, derived from a salary and/or substantial sponsorship contracts. This definition excludes individuals who participated in motorsport on an amateur basis. All data were collected from Princess Grace Hospital medical files, as memory assessments were performed as part of routine care. Data were anonymized and recorded in a database hosted at the Clinical Research Unit-Memory Clinic of the hospital. DTI data were transmitted to the Inria Centre at Université Côte d’Azur for analyses.

As a single center, considering the Memory Clinic’s experience with populations exposed to mild traumatic brain injury due to sports practice and the allocated time frame, the estimated sample size was between 10 and 15 subjects.

This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Monaco Principality and the Princess Grace Hospital Clinical Research steering committee. It was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the 1964 Helsinki declaration and its later amendments. Written informed consent for data collection, analysis, and use was obtained from all participants. The Monegasque authorities for the protection of personal data (CCIN) issued a favorable opinion for the implementation of data processing within this study.

Measures

Participant Characteristics and Clinical Assessment

Data collected included demographics (age, educational level) as well as medical history (including psychiatric, neurological, and cardiovascular history potentially interfering with the multimodal assessments) and history of sports practice (exposure duration corresponding to the time interval between the start and the retirement of motorsport practice, type of motorsport practiced, and duration of professional career). This information as well as history of concussions was collected from personal and medical history interviews. A concussion was counted if the event and its consecutive symptoms, as described by the participants, were deemed significantly evocative of a concussion by the experienced clinician who conducted the medical history interview (S.LDLC). This was based on the subjects' responses to the question “How many concussions do you think you already had? Please describe the concussion and physical consequences”. Current concussion symptoms were screened using the Sport Concussion Assessment Tool 5TH Edition (SCAT5) symptom inventory [90]. This subscale yields two outcome measures: the "total number of symptoms (/22)" and the "symptom severity score (/132)". Cumulative head impact exposure was determined by three factors: exposure duration, defined as years of motorsport participation; professional practice duration; and the number of reported concussions.

Neuropsychological Outcomes

We collected results from a comprehensive neuropsychological examination, that assessed global cognitive function (using the total MoCA [91] score) and seven cognitive domains: episodic memory, working memory, executive functions, social cognition, attention, constructional praxis and language. For each test, neuropsychological performance was evaluated against normative data specific to each participant's age, sex, and educational level. Each performance was classified as significantly impaired, normal, or above average (performance > 1 SD). Finally, global cognitive function and each cognitive domain were categorized as normal or impaired. The sum of impaired cognitive domains was calculated for each participant.

Video-oculography Outcomes

Eye movements were recorded with a Mobile EyeBrainT2® (SURICOG), a CE-marked (conforming to European health and safety directives) head-mounted infrared eye-tracking device (300 Hz, 0.25° accuracy at 60 cm from the screen).

For each participant, eye latency, mean and peak velocity, and gain and percentage of error were compared to age-matched normative data (provided by SURICOG) and were judged abnormal if values differed by > 1.65 SD. This concerns horizontal saccades (measured with a lateral angle of 20° using a gap paradigm for reactive saccades and an overlap paradigm for voluntary saccades), vertical saccades (measured with a vertical angle of 12° using the step paradigm), and antisaccades task (a paradigm assessing inhibition ability). Abducting/adducting ratio was calculated to detect internuclear ophthalmoplegia. Horizontal and vertical smooth pursuits, as well as fixation (square-wave jerks and nystagmus), were qualitatively judged as normal or impaired by an experienced clinician (K.P.). This assessment corresponds to a standard oculomotor analysis routinely performed in our memory clinic of the Princess Grace Hospital. Finally, the sum of the observed abnormalities was calculated for each participant.

MRI and DTI Protocols

MRI scans were acquired using a 3T Philips Ingenia Elition scanner with a 32-channel brain antenna. Axial, coronal and sagittal high-resolution sequences were obtained, including T1-weighted 3D, T2-weighted 3D FLAIR, DWI, and axial SWI with phase enhancement (SWIp).

To minimize data collection bias, MRI scans were analyzed by three experienced raters (K.P., S.LDLC, B.P.) using consensus reading. White matter hyperintensity was assessed using the Fazekas scale [108] to determine the cerebral microvascular lesion load. Additionally, the number of lacunes and microbleeds was counted. Neurodegeneration (atrophy) was subjectively estimated by the raters in the frontal, parietal, occipital, temporal lobes, and cerebellum in each hemisphere as standardly practiced in routine clinical care. Hippocampal atrophy was assessed using the Scheltens’ scale of Medial Temporal Atrophy [109]. Brain MRI abnormalities were defined as a Fazekas’ or Scheltens’ scale score exceeding 1, the presence of at least one lacune and/or microbleed, or atrophy in at least one of the listed brain regions.

Diffusion data were acquired with anterior–posterior phase encoding polarity (TR = 1923 ms, TE = 96 ms, voxel size = 2.5-mm isotropic) with 32 diffusion directions (b = 800). Each volume consisted of 90 × 90 × 60 voxels. Preprocessing for eddy current-induced distortion and motion correction was performed using FSL [110]. FA was calculated using MRtrix3 [111] and registered to the MNI template with a non-linear transformation via the Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs) python library [112]. Finally, average FA within white matter regions from the JHU DTI-based white matter atlas (available at https://identifiers.org/neurovault.collection:264) was computed. Eleven regions of interest (ROIs) were selected based on frequent reporting in the mild brain traumatic injury-DTI literature [113, 114]:

Bilateral white matter tracts were considered whenever possible. FA was analyzed for a total of 15 ROIs, including the average across all white matter.

The OpenNeuro Dataset ds004215 [115] (The NIMH Healthy Research Volunteer Dataset) served as a DTI reference sample because the subjects' age range (20–72) largely overlapped that of the drivers (33–77), despite the age distributions not being matched. The DTI reference sample was selected to maximize the sample size for the linear regression analysis rather than being strictly sex-matched to the clinical cohort. Consequently, women were included in the DTI reference sample, even though all participants in this case series were men. Same preprocessing, DTI measurement extraction, and MNI spatial registration pipeline used for the driver data were applied to the DTI reference sample data. Additionally, the same regions were considered for the DTI reference sample. These data were acquired using a 3T General Electric DISCOVERY MR750 scanner with spin-echo echo-planar imaging for anterior–posterior phase encoding (TR = 7800 ms, TE = 60 ms, voxel size = 1.8125 × 1.8125 × 2 mm) and 24 diffusion directions with a b value of 1000. Each volume consisted of 128 × 128 × 80 voxels. While we acknowledge this resolution differs from the clinical protocol, this dataset was selected due to the scarcity of large-scale, open-source diffusion MRI data specifically covering the older adult demographic required for this study. Mean FA values of each subject of the DTI reference sample were collected for each white matter ROI. A linear regression with a 95% confidence interval was performed to normalize mean FA values according to age for each ROI. FA values below this 95% confidence interval were considered abnormal.

18 FDG-PET Protocol

All patients fasted for at least 4 h, and their blood glucose levels were monitored (< 200 mg/dL). The patients were then injected with 3.7 MBq/kg 18F-FDG and left to rest with their eyes closed in a dark and quiet environment for a 30-min uptake period. PET data were acquired on a clinical PET/CT system (SIEMENS Biograph128 Vision 600 Edge, Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany) with a head holder, using an 8-min bed position and three-dimensional whole-head acquisition. All patients underwent unenhanced low-dose CT (120 kV, Caredose 4D, slice thickness of 2 mm) for attenuation correction and anatomical reference. PET images were reconstructed into coronal–transaxial–sagittal orientation slices (440 × 440 matrix) with TOF and TrueX (high-definition PET) with 8 iterations and 5 subsets, Gaussian filter (FWHM 2 mm), zoom of 2. The PET images were also reconstructed using TOF and OSEM (8 iterations and 5 subsets), Gaussian filter (FWHM 3 mm), zoom of 2 and matrix of 440 × 440. Images were visualized on PETsyngo VG80C workstation using Siemens Scenium software VE40B.

18 FDG-PET scans were analyzed quantitatively and visually.

Quantitative analysis: Mean of the uptake values of 18 FDG-PET tracer in the frontal, parietal, occipital, temporal, and medial temporal lobes of each hemisphere were compared with an age-matched normative data (Scenium software VB30A from Siemens Medical Solutions USA®). Values differing by > 2 SD were considered abnormal, consistent with standardized clinical protocols for brain 18 FDG-PET quantitative assessment.

Statistical Analysis

The characteristics of the participants were described according to their nature by mean and standard deviation or sample size and percentages. To investigate correlations between exposure duration/number of concussions and clinical/neuroimaging variables, the sums of concussion symptoms (SCAT5), cognitive impairments, eye movement abnormalities, and MRI, DTI, and 18 FDG-PET findings were used as quantitative measures, and Pearson's correlation test was used. Results with P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. No adjustments for multiple comparisons were performed to optimize the identification of all possible significant results, accepting the risk of type 1 error. Statistical analysis was performed using R version 4.4.1 software.

All video-oculography, MRI, DWI, and 18 FDG-PET scan data were collected for all participants. In contrast, for the neuropsychological assessment, missing data concerned: 5/12 (42%) participants for the PASAT, 3/12 (25%) participants for the Stroop, 1/12 (8%) participant for verbal episodic memory. Missing data were excluded from the analysis.

Results

Sample Characteristics

A total of 12 male subjects were included, aged 33–77 years (mean ± SD, 55.8 ± 12.1 years), who began practicing motorsport at the age of 16.3 ± 6.7 years. All participants had competed professionally in various motorsports (detailed information is available beneath Table 2). The mean duration of motorsport practice was 31.3 ± 5.8 years, with a mean professional career of 26.3 ± 6.5 years, and mean retirement age of 40.4 ± 5.6 years.

Description of Clinical, Neuropsychological, and Oculomotor Outcomes

The SCAT5 symptom inventory showed that 9/12 (75%) of participants reported at least one symptom. They reported a mean of 2.5 ± 2.8 symptoms [range: 0–10] with a mean severity of 2.9 ± 3.3 [range: 0–11]. In descending order of frequency, the reported symptoms were: “difficulty in concentrating” and “difficulty in remembering” 5/12 (42%), “neck pain” 4/12 (33%), “trouble falling asleep” and “nervous or anxious” 3/12 (25%), “balance problems” and “irritability” 2/12 (17%), “headache”, “sensitivity to noise”, “don’t feel right”, “fatigue or low energy”, “drowsiness” and “more emotional” 1/12 (8%), all were reported at a low intensity level (score ≤ 2).

Although no participant presented with significant global cognitive impairment on the MoCA test, 4/12 (33%) exhibited impairment in at least one cognitive domain, with a mean of 0.4 ± 0.7 [range: 0–2] impaired domains. The most frequently impaired cognitive domain was working memory 3/12 (25%), primarily verbal working memory updating, affecting 3/7 (43%) of participants who completed the PASAT test. Episodic memory was the second most frequently impaired cognitive domain 2/12 (17%), verbal modality only, with deficits in encoding 1/12 (8%) and retrieval of information 2/12 (17%). Attention was the third most impaired cognitive domain 1/12 (8%) with a deficit in selective and sustained attention observed for 1/12 participant (8%) respectively. Executive functions were not significantly impaired overall, but deficits in mental flexibility, planning, and inhibition were observed in 2/12 (17%), 2/12 (17%) and 1/12 (8%) of participants, respectively. No deficits were observed in conceptualization or verbal fluency. No deficits were observed for other cognitive domains (social cognition, constructional praxis, language) nor for visual episodic memory, visual working memory, processing speed or divided attention.

Notably, several cognitive performances were above average. In descending order of frequency, these included: visual episodic memory: 7/12 (58%) (encoding, retrieval, storage and consolidation), processing speed: 7/12 (58%), visual working memory: 6/12 (50%), verbal working memory updating: 2/7 (29%), verbal episodic memory: 3/12 (25%) (encoding, retrieval and consolidation), divided and selective attention: 3/12 (25%), sustained attention: 2/12 (17%), and verbal working memory: 1/12 (8%).

Nine of the 12 participants (75%) had at least one impaired eye movement with an average of 1.5 ± 1.1 [range: 0–3] abnormalities. For horizontal saccades, 1/12 (8%) had increased latency (reactive and voluntary), 1/12 (8%) had slowed velocity (mean and peak), and 3/12 (25%) had dysmetria (hypometria in 2/12 (17%), hypermetria and centripetal hypermetria in 1/12 (8%)). For vertical saccades, 1/12 (8%) had increased latency, 2/12 (17%) had slowed velocity (mean and peak), and 5/12 (42%) had dysmetria (hypometria in 5/12 (42%), hypermetria and centripetal hypermetria in 1/12 (8%)). Fixation impairment was present in 3/12 (25%) of participants and only consisted of significant production of square-wave jerks. Smooth pursuit (vertical) and anti-saccades (increased latency) were impaired in 1/12 (8%) respectively. No internuclear ophthalmoplegia was observed.

Discussion

Main Findings

The primary aim of this case series was to examine the neurological health status of former professional motorsport drivers and the correlations between observed neurological abnormalities and cumulative head impacts exposure from sport practice. The results showed that clinical and neuroimaging abnormalities were common but mild, with eye movement abnormalities positively correlated with exposure duration and professional career length.

None of the participants received a diagnosis of chronic traumatic encephalopathy, neurodegenerative disease, dementia, or a psychiatric disorder after the comprehensive examinations. Cardiovascular investigations were requested for one participant whose cerebral MRI showed evidence of leukoaraiosis, lacunes, and microbleeds. Regarding the incidentally discovered micronodular vacuolating neuronal tumor, following established clinical protocols for such benign lesions, the participant is currently under regular radiological surveillance. The lesion has remained stable to date, and no intervention was required, reflecting the appropriate management of incidental findings within this case series. For the remaining participants, the observed abnormalities were considered nonspecific and mild, with no significant impact on their daily lives, and no changes to their treatment or management were indicated. All participants were advised to return for a follow-up at the memory clinic should their cognitive complaints persist.

Despite the nonspecific nature of these symptoms for our case series, it is noteworthy that 75% of participants exhibited at least one subjective concussion symptom on the SCAT5 symptom inventory, averaging 2.5 symptoms per participant. To our knowledge, there is a lack of normative data for a healthy non-athletic adult population approximately 50 years of age, making direct comparisons challenging. However, the frequency of participants reporting at least one subjective symptom was higher than the 48% observed in a cohort of young active motocross drivers [4], and the average number of symptoms was double that reported in pre-season baseline normative data from professional men's rugby league players [116]. The low symptom reporting in this athletic group may be influenced by "sandbagging", a strategy to intentionally underreport symptoms at baseline [117]. A large study of healthy high school and college participants [118] reported a comparable mean symptom severity score of 3.27, similar to our study's score of 2.9. However, the symptom profiles differed. In our cohort, 54% of participants reported a cognitive-related symptom, with 42% experiencing difficulties concentrating or remembering. This is in stark contrast to the healthy young population, of whom 14.1% reported difficulty in concentrating and 12.2% reported difficulty in remembering, and to the professional rugby players, where these numbers were only 8.2% and 8.9% respectively [116]. While our cohort was characterized by a high prevalence of cognitive-related symptoms, the most frequent symptoms in the young healthy population were "fatigue or low energy" (22.3%) and "trouble falling asleep" (15.1%) [118]. Among professional rugby players, "fatigue or low energy" (17.1%) and "neck pain" (12.6%) were most common [116]. Additionally, several symptoms were completely absent in our cohort, including "pressure in the head", "feeling slowed down", "feeling in a fog", "dizziness", "sensitivity to light", "sadness", "blurred vision", "confusion", and "nausea or vomiting". All of these were reported, albeit rarely, in both the healthy young population and the professional rugby players [116, 118]. The varying symptom profiles across different populations, along with the finding that concussion symptoms are nonspecific and influenced by socio-demographic and health factors [119], highlight the complexity of interpreting these self-reported symptoms.

Video-oculography revealed the highest incidence of clinical abnormalities, with 75% of participants exhibiting at least one eye movement abnormality (mean of 1.5 abnormalities per participant). While nystagmus and internuclear ophthalmoplegia were not observed, abnormalities were heterogeneous, primarily involving vertical saccades (hypometria and decreased velocity) and increased square-wave jerks’ production. Although the overall profile resembled that of a control population more closely (see [120] for example) than a population with acute concussion [24–27, 29, 33, 34, 36, 37] or persistent postconcussive symptoms [22, 28, 32, 34, 35, 37], significant decreases in vertical peak velocity and increased square-wave jerks production indicated potential posterior fossa dysfunction [121, 122], which could be a long-term consequence of repeated head impacts (see [36] for square-wave jerks). Furthermore, these abnormalities may have emerged over time, as repeated head impacts do not appear to have short-term consequences on eye movements in active professional motorsport drivers [9]. While this study [9] did measure vergence smooth pursuit and optokinetic nystagmus (eye movements often impaired after a concussion), its evaluation of saccades was partial, limited to predictive saccades using a low-frequency acquisition device (100 Hz). Consequently, it did not assess reflexive and voluntary saccades (including measures of latency, peak velocity, and gain), anti-saccades, or fixation, whereas the most common eye movement abnormalities observed in our own cohort were saccadic dysmetria and a high frequency of square-wave jerks. Thus, a more comprehensive examination of oculomotor capabilities, using a higher acquisition frequency, could be beneficial. This would allow for the investigation of possible short- and medium-term consequences of repeated head impacts in active motorsport drivers and enable a comparison with our longer-term findings.

The frequency of objective cognitive impairments was 33%, with participants showing at least one significantly impaired cognitive domain. This is lower than the 54% of subjective cognitive complaints observed. These mild difficulties affected working memory, attention and executive functions, consistent with the long-term consequences of mild traumatic brain injuries observed in others studies of the general population [47] and in retired athletes [123–125]. In our population, cognitive impairment was more than twice as frequent as reported in individuals with persistent postconcussion symptoms [46], but aligns with other studies suggesting underestimation of such impairments [42]. Notably, this 33% prevalence is higher than the 5–22% reported for mild cognitive impairment in the general population aged 60 and over [126, 127]. This discrepancy, despite our cohort being younger, suggests a potentially elevated rate of cognitive difficulties. Although there was a high frequency of clinical abnormalities, we paradoxically also observed a high frequency of above-average abilities. Cognitively, it primarily involved visual episodic memory, processing speed, and visual working memory. This atypical cognitive profile could reflect the cognitive expertise acquired through intense sport practice [128], particularly motorsport, which leads to decreased motor [129] and oculomotor [130] reaction times and improved visuospatial abilities [131], associated with greater recruitment of brain areas dedicated to motor control and spatial navigation [131].

18 FDG-PET was the neuroimaging technique that revealed the fewest abnormalities, with 33% of participants exhibiting an abnormal examination. Decreased metabolism primarily affected the frontal and temporal lobes, brain regions commonly impacted by concussion [62]. Additionally, in individuals with persistent postconcussive symptoms, frontal lobe hypometabolism is associated with decreased cognitive performance [132], consistent with our findings of cognitive impairment in 33% of participants. However, the hypometabolic patterns following traumatic brain injury are often heterogeneous, and the link between these patterns and long-term cognitive impairment in SRC is questionable. This is because metabolic patterns can change over time and with age within individuals, making 18 FDG-PET an unreliable tool for longitudinally determining whether a brain injury is improving or declining [133]. Notably, patients with persistent postconcussion symptoms often present with a metabolic profile characterized by both hyper- and hypometabolism in multiple brain regions, which may reflect a combination of pathological changes and compensatory mechanisms [134] that likely evolve over time. This topic warrants further investigation due to the limited research available, and longitudinal follow-up of 18 FDG-PET patterns in a population of athletes exposed to repetitive head impacts would be particularly valuable.

MRI revealed more abnormalities, with 58% of participants exhibiting at least one abnormality. This finding is substantially higher than the 9.4% of incidental findings reported in a cohort of young healthy volunteers (mean age 21.9 years) [135]. Notably, the most common abnormalities in that healthy cohort were benign, such as pineal cysts and widened bifrontal subarachnoid spaces, and all findings were clinically silent. In our case series, brain atrophy was the most common observation (42%), primarily affecting the frontal lobe, a common finding in athletes with a history of concussion [136]. While a previous study of 117 former professional American Football players reported a similar reduction in frontal and temporal lobe volumes, the study specifically noted atrophy of the medial temporal regions (hippocampus, amygdala, entorhinal cortex, and parahippocampal gyrus) compared to unexposed asymptomatic controls [137]. No significant medial temporal atrophy was observed in our cohort of former professional motorsport drivers, with all participants having a Scheltens' score ≤ 1. Despite a similar mean age (retired American Football players: 59 years; former motorsport drivers: 56 years) and a longer mean career duration in our motorsport driver cohort (18 years vs. 26 years), the absence of medial temporal atrophy in our group suggests that the nature of head impacts in American Football may lead to greater susceptibility in these regions. Furthermore, diffuse vascular lesions were less frequent, with 25% of participants exhibiting significant microvascular lesion load and 17% having lacunes and microbleeds. This is higher than observed in a cohort of active elite rugby players, where microbleeds were noted in only 7% [52].

DTI was the technique that revealed the most abnormalities in our population, consistent with the literature [57]. 11 of 12 (92%) of former professional motorsport drivers presented abnormalities in mean FA, either in the whole white matter (67%, 8/12) or in at least one sampled tract (67%, 8/12), a higher than observed in an active elite rugby players cohort (17%, 7/42) [52]. It is important to note that our DTI reference sample, derived from the OpenNeuro Dataset ds004215 [115], differed from the motorsport drivers' data in terms of site and acquisition parameters. However, the observed decrease in mean FA aligns with other sport-related repetitive head impacts studies highlighting that decreased FA is a long-term consequence of SRCs and rSHI due to repeated head impact exposure [57, 60], suggesting that white matter microstructure alterations may be frequent in our cohort of former professional motorsport drivers. Similar to another study [60], the pattern of white matter alteration was asymmetric, predominantly affecting the left hemisphere in our study. The highest prevalence of FA abnormalities was observed in the left anterior corona radiata (58%), consistent with other studies in populations with acute SRC or exposed to rSHI [64, 114], as this bundle is thought to be highly susceptible to axonal injury due to its configuration [138]. Conversely, with the exception of the internal capsule in one participant, no decreased mean FA was observed in the corpus callosum or uncinate fasciculus, regions often affected in SRC (at both the acute and persistent stages) or in cases of rSHI [60, 61, 114]. Finally, a systematic review suggests that FA is either unchanged or increased after SRC while rSHI generally led to a decrease in FA [114]. This could indicate that the patterns of decreased FA observed in our cohort may be more closely associated with rSHI than with repeated SRCs.

Decreased mean FA is associated with greater exposure to sport-related repeated head impact [57] and is also observed in the chronic SRC phase [60], interpreted as a direct mechanical injury to axons and a sign of neurodegenerative processes [57, 60]. Moreover, while robust studies are still needed to confirm this link, former athletes in contact and collision sports appear to have an elevated risk of dementia [82]. This is primarily linked to conditions, such as chronic traumatic encephalopathy [76] and motor neuron disease [83, 84], likely due to the cumulative effect of repetitive SRCs and rSHI sustained throughout their careers [79]. The degenerative mechanism associated with decreased mean FA may be consistent with the high frequency of brain atrophy observed in our sample. However, none of our participants received a clinical diagnosis of neurodegenerative disease or dementia following the examinations conducted. Furthermore, DTI is known to be highly sensitive but nonspecific [57], and the link between reduced mean FA and cognitive impairment remains debated [57], as some studies have not found a link between cognitive performance and decreased mean FA [52] or number of concussions [139–141]. One possible explanation is that professional athletes may still benefit from the beneficial effects of sports on cognition [142, 143], likely enabling them to develop significant cognitive reserve through intense sporting practice, and the brain’s ability to compensate for structural alterations [144, 145], nullifying potential cognitive declines due to aging and repeated SRC and rSHI. This is consistent with the atypical cognitive performances observed in our sample, characterized by high visuospatial and processing speed abilities. However, heterogeneity in performances was observed, with some mild cognitive deficits also present in our cohort, similar to findings in studies of retired professional athletes [86]. Given that our cohort is still young (mean age of 55.8 years), it would be interesting to follow this cohort over time to determine whether these probable compensatory mechanisms are maintained and if, ultimately, a correlation with DTI abnormalities might appear later. The frequency of FA abnormalities appears more closely related to the frequency of abnormalities observed in video-oculography. The control, computation and generation of eye movements involve numerous brain areas, such as cortex, brainstem, and cerebellum, interconnected within widely distributed networks that also include brain regions involved in other functions like cognition [61, 146]. Therefore, in many cases, eye-tracking proves to be more sensitive than neuropsychological assessment in detecting cognitive disorders [147], making eye-tracking technology a sensitive tool for SRC and rSHI assessment. This tool also appears promising for studying long-term consequences as eye movement abnormalities were correlated with cumulative head impact exposure duration (exposure duration and professional practice duration) in our case series. Unexpectedly, this correlation was not found for the number of FA abnormalities. This finding seems to contradict the dose–response relationship suggested by the literature [66]. However, this discrepancy could be a methodological issue as we correlated the number of observed abnormalities in different ROIs rather than the mean FA value. Given the consistently low FA values found in almost all of our retired drivers, it is likely that this methodological approach was not sensitive enough to detect such a relationship. It would have been interesting to also correlate the FA values with the other clinical factors (eye movement and cognition abnormalities, number and severity of symptoms on the SCAT5 symptom inventory), as well as with the imaging factors (number of abnormalities observed on MRI and 18 FDG-PET). Qualitatively, there was a strong link between FA and eye movement abnormalities, particularly for the anterior corona radiata, consistent with the literature [61]. The corona radiata is a large white matter bundle connecting the thalamus to the cerebral cortex and the fronto-parietal cortex to the basal ganglia and brainstem [147]. Its anterior part is involved in eye movement [61], cognition and motivation [148]. Due to its anatomical configuration, the corona radiata is highly susceptible to axonal injury [138], making it one of the regions most frequently affected by head impacts [61, 64, 114]. For these reasons, the study of the long-term consequences of repeated head impacts should include an assessment of eye movements and cognition using eye-tracking in conjunction with an assessment of the FA value of the anterior corona radiata in DWI scans.

No correlation was observed between number of declared concussions and any clinical or neuroimaging factors, consistent with other studies [10, 138–140]. However, we observed trends suggesting negative correlations between the number of reported concussions and cognitive impairment and FA abnormalities. Furthermore, participants reported an average of 3.5 concussions over a mean professional practice career of 26.3 years, or 0.13 concussions per year, which appears to be an underestimation based on the literature [1, 4]. Several studies have shown that concussions are underreported or unrecognized by motorsports drivers (and athletes in general) even with concussion awareness programs [149–151]. Therefore, the number of self-reported concussions may not appear to be a reliable factor for studying the long-term consequences of repeated head impacts in former athletes. While not indicating the specific number of SRCs or rSHI, factors, such as career and sport practice duration, appear to be more interesting, albeit approximate and indirect, measures.

Strengths, Limitations, and Future Directions

To our knowledge, this case series is the first to comprehensively examine the neurological health of a sample of former professional motorsport drivers and to explore potential relationships between cumulative head impacts exposure and long-term neurological outcomes in this concussion-prone population.

Although a case series design is useful for generating hypotheses, its descriptive nature and the absence of a formal control group do not allow for the establishment of a causal relationship. Furthermore, our case series has several limitations.

First, the small sample size means the results should be interpreted with caution. Moreover, the sample was exclusively male: including female drivers in future studies would provide a more comprehensive dataset for this specific population.

Second, the lack of previous clinical data for each participant (including pre-season baseline, in-season medical follow-ups, or post-accident evaluations) precludes establishment of a causal link between cumulative head impacts exposure and the observed long-term clinical and neuroradiological findings.

Third, establishing a control group of professional drivers may represent a challenge due to the unique nature of each motorsport discipline and the inherent exposure to repeated head impacts. Therefore, it would be necessary to compare these observations with a control group comprising former professional athletes who experienced significantly less exposure to repeated head impacts. While individual clinical and neuroimaging data for each driver were compared against their age-matched normative data (consistent with standard clinical practice), it would have been more robust to perform a point-by-point comparison with a matched control group not exposed to repeated head impacts. Specifically for the DTI data, a control group with data acquired at the same site and using the same parameters would allow for a quantitative analysis of white matter alterations in motorsport drivers. The DTI reference sample constituted from the OpenNeuro Dataset ds004215 [115] did not have strictly identical acquisition parameters to the group of retired drivers. First, participants in this case series and the DTI reference sample were not perfectly sex-matched as we prioritized a larger sample size to improve the stability of our linear regression models over strict demographic matching. Second, there was a discrepancy in spatial resolution between the clinical and research scans. This was unavoidable due to the lack of available large-scale DTI datasets for this specific age group that closely mirrored our clinical acquisition parameters. It is also important to note that DTI data contain site-specific variations [152], but due to the small number of participants in our study, we opted against harmonization. While head acceleration event exposure was not specifically recorded for DTI reference sample participants, the rigorous exclusion criteria suggest a health profile comparable to or more conservative than that of the general population. Our comparisons with the DTI reference sample should be interpreted as potential trends, to be supported by further data, rather than definitive evidence.

These results should be interpreted with caution because, as shown by known neuropsychological base rates, isolated low scores can occur in healthy individuals, particularly as the number of tests increases [153, 154]. Indeed, the average number of significant impairments per participant remained low (0.4 neuropsychological, 1.5 oculomotor, and 0.9 DTI anomalies), and an isolated deficit should not be viewed as definitive evidence of pathology. However, specific qualitative patterns—such as high prevalence of heterogeneous cognitive profiles, vertical dysmetria (42%), square-wave jerks (25%), and whole-brain FA decrease (67%)—appear atypical for the general population and justify longitudinal follow-up in larger cohorts.

Another limitation of the study is our recruitment method. Based on routine care and a public concussion awareness campaign led by the Automobile Club de Monaco, a potential bias was introduced. Indeed, this campaign may have attracted a subgroup of drivers who are more vigilant about their health, which is not representative of all former professional drivers. As a result, the participants may have been more inclined to consult a Memory Center due to pre-existing concerns about their neurological health. About 58% of participants reported a cognitive complaint, while 50% had concerns specifically related to motorsports. Qualitatively, we observed that abnormalities appeared to be slightly less frequent and less severe in participants without cognitive complaints although none of them exhibited a strictly normal clinical profile across all modalities. It is important to note that at the initial medical consultation, nearly half of the participants presented solely for a “motorsport-related concern” without active symptoms. Among those reporting a cognitive complaint (58%), the symptoms were described as mild and nonspecific, with no participants initially reporting somatic, emotional, or sleep-related disturbances. Future studies should include retired drivers without such complaints to get a more objective understanding of long-term neurological consequences.

Consistent with other studies, we assessed cumulative head impact exposure using sport practice duration. However, even though the drivers followed a concussion awareness campaign, we couldn't precisely determine the number of concussions (as self-reported data were unreliable) or the specific repeated head impacts professional drivers experienced. Obtaining such precise, career-long measurements is currently impractical. Furthermore, it should be noted that the reported data reflect an estimated incidence of head impacts with symptoms, rather than formal, medically documented concussion diagnoses over the course of the drivers' careers. Therefore, sport practice duration should be interpreted with caution as a proxy for head impact exposure.

Nevertheless, the descriptive findings from this population of retired professional drivers suggest a risk of atypical cerebral aging. This warrants more rigorous, large-scale studies to evaluate these hypotheses and explore the identified causal factors. The effect sizes observed for cognitive and neuroradiological parameters suggest that future prospective studies would require a sample size of approximately 40 to 60 participants per group to achieve sufficient statistical power (0.80). Although these figures may appear modest, they would represent a substantial proportion of this highly specific professional population, thereby ensuring high scientific credibility and representativeness.

Our case series provides initial recommendations for monitoring the neurological health of active and former motorsport drivers. If future, large-scale studies confirm the atypical cognitive and oculomotor abilities observed in this population, likely resulting from their sports practice, establishing pre-season baselines for active professionals should then be considered. These should include a neuropsychological assessment (executive and attentional abilities including processing speed, working memory, and verbal and visual episodic memory, which would be more in-depth than the standardized assessment of concussion from the SCAT5), and an eye-tracking examination (including video-oculography and cognitive tasks such as anti-saccades). An advanced brain MRI, with SWI and DWI sequences (including whole white matter and ROIs analysis) is also recommended.

Concurrently, it seems necessary to increase athletes' awareness of concussion and promote the implementation of concussion awareness programs. The combination of baseline assessments and information programs would increase the sensitivity of SRC detection in this population and improve long-term monitoring of professional practice. The high frequency of DTI abnormalities observed in our sample, consistent with findings in others impacts and collision sports [57, 60], raises questions about the long-term brain health of this population and its potential vulnerability to neurodegenerative processes. However, this population appears to compensate for the observed brain abnormalities, likely due to a significant cognitive reserve. A longitudinal study would be essential to investigate the complex links between axonal injuries, cognitive reserve, and the type of brain aging following professional motorsports practice.

Conclusions

To our knowledge, this is the first in-depth study of neurological health status within a population of former professional motorsport drivers. In this case series, numerous mild, nonspecific neurological abnormalities were observed in a sample of former professional drivers, a population exposed to repeated head impacts from motorsport participation. As discussed, the discrepancy between our findings and the drivers' self-reported history aligns with existing literature suggesting that head impacts and concussions are frequently underreported or unrecognized in this sporting context [149–151]. MRI-DTI and video-oculography were more often associated with abnormalities in the study population, with oculomotor abnormalities being positively correlated with exposure duration. The preliminary findings from this case series justify a more robust prospective investigation with a larger sample. This new study should incorporate the collection of individual pre-season baseline data and include multiple control groups, such as matched individuals without a history of repeated head impacts and retired professional athletes from sports without significant head impact exposure. A larger sample would also enable detailed comparisons between former drivers from different motorsport disciplines and between amateur and professional cohorts, allowing for the use of robust statistical analysis that was not possible in the current study.

The combination of video-oculography and MRI-DTI appears promising for studying the consequences of SRC and rSHI throughout the career, by combining both a quantification of FA in the whole-brain and ROIs analysis (specifically including the corona radiata, which, due to its anatomical configuration, is both susceptible to diffuse axonal injury mechanisms and strongly interacts with networks involved in oculomotor function).

Our case series also suggests that this population of former professional motorsport drivers may be characterized by exceptional cognitive skills, including increased processing speed and excellent visuospatial abilities. It is plausible that these abilities are closely linked to their professional motorsport practice, contributing to the development of a significant cognitive reserve. Thus, it appears that commonly used clinical tests may not be adequate for this population without sport-specific validation. That is why we suggest that the data from pre-season baseline assessments —which are already standard for acute concussion management— should be preserved for long-term clinical use. Establishing an individualized lifelong profile for each professional driver would not only aid in the detection of subsequent concussions during their career but would also provide a valuable reference for monitoring brain health and detecting subtle cognitive changes decades later, during retirement. Finally, given the apparent high frequency of white matter microstructure alterations observed in this population, longitudinal studies are crucial for monitoring their aging process.
