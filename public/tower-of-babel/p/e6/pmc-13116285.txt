The Relationship Between Academic Stress and Student Suicide: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Manish Tyagi, Vinod Kumar Singh, Hare Krishna, Ajay Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveAcademic stress has been increasingly identified as a possible risk factor for suicidal behavior among students at all levels of education and in international settings. This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted to synthesize the existing evidence for the association between academic stress and suicidal ideation and behavior.MethodA systematic search was performed across six databases, viz., PubMed, Scopus, PsycINFO, Web of Science, CINAHL, and the Cochrane Library, from inception until May 2025. The criteria for study inclusion necessitated them to quantitatively measure academic stress and suicide-related outcomes, viz., suicidal ideation and suicide attempts, among adolescent or young adult student groups. Data extraction involved demographic details, measurement tools for stress and suicidality, statistical findings, and covariate adjustments. The AXIS tool was applied to evaluate the risk of bias. Meta-analysis was carried out using RevMan 5.4.1, employing a random-effects model to estimate pooled mean differences (MD) for continuous outcomes and odds ratios (OR) for dichotomous outcomes.Results10 cross-sectional studies were included in the review. Academic stress was measured with a range of instruments, mostly self-report questionnaires. Most studies reported suicidal ideation as the main outcome, and fewer used suicide attempts or probability estimates. There were distinct methodological variations in stress severity classification, psychologic or academic confounder adjustment, and psychometric properties reporting. Pooled mean difference between females and males in academic stress was nonsignificant (MD=10.38; 95% CI: -6.34 to 27.09; p=0.22), as was between suicidal ideation (MD=14.43; 95% CI: -22.16 to 51.02; p=0.44). Overall continuous outcomes were also nonsignificant (MD=12.38; 95% CI: -3.70 to 28.47; p=0.13). The pooled odds ratios for suicidal ideation and suicide attempts, however, were statistically significant (OR=0.01; 95% CI: 0.01 to 0.02 and OR=0.00; 95% CI: 0.00 to 0.02 respectively; both p<0.0001) indicating a correlation with increased academic stress. Additionally, there was extremely large heterogeneity in most of the pooled analyses (I2> 98%).ConclusionsThe prevalence of academic stress had a strong association with suicidal ideation and suicide attempts in dichotomous outcome models. However, continuous outcomes did not yield significant differences in the two genders. Associations that were found were confounded by measurement instrument variability, not making proper adjustments for psychiatric and academic confounders, and methodological differences in studies.

Introduction

Academic stress diffusion has emerged as an important psychosocial issue in education and public health discourse as a result of its intricate interaction with mental health risk factors in youth and young adults. Academic stress, a particular type of psychological distress, refers to chronic psychological distress brought about by academic pressures such as exams, performance reviews, competitive ranking, workload, and self-expectations or other expectations (Deb et al., 2015). The construct is multi-dimensional in nature, including sociocultural expectations, institutional settings, and sociocognitive, affective, and behavioral correlations, and is shaped by both sociocultural expectations and psychological resilience at both the institutional and individual levels (Pascoe et al., 2020). The expansion of competitive educational practices, especially in transitional educational settings and high-stakes testing settings, has increased the prevalence of academic stress at all secondary and post-secondary education levels on an international level (OECD, 2017).

Empirical research has increasingly illuminated the connection between academic stress and an array of psychological outcomes, including internalizing symptoms such as depression and anxiety and externalizing behaviors such as substance use and aggression (Pascoe et al., 2020). One of the new areas of research is the intersection between academic stress and suicidality, including suicidal ideation and suicide attempts. Suicidality in students is a complex construct that is typically mediated by the additive effect of psychosocial stressors, ineffective coping strategies, academic underachievement, and mental illness (World Health Organization, 2021). Research has established that significant academic stress can serve as a standalone risk factor or as a mediator that interacts with previous psychopathologies in order to increase suicidal outcomes (Liu & Tein, 2005).

Teenagers and young adults are also exposed to increased vulnerability based on the stage of development with identity formation, peer susceptibility, and the formation of educational aspirations; all of these aspects can be negatively impacted by persistent academic pressure (Arnett, 2014). Neurobiologically, stress at this stage of development can have the potential to affect prefrontal–limbic circuitry, thus impairing emotion and impulse control, having the potential to raise the risk of suicidal behavior (Romeo, 2017). Sociocultural processes such as stigma over failure, parental expectations, and collectivist ideology also have the potential to enhance negative beliefs about academic achievement and self-esteem, thus enhancing distress in case of non-fulfillment of performance expectations (Hesketh & Ding, 2005). These processes have been reported in various educational systems, most notably in South and East Asian countries, where academic achievement is highly associated with honor and socioeconomic status for the family (Liu & Tein, 2005).

In spite of the apparent correlation between academic stress and suicide-related outcomes, empirical literature is highly heterogeneous. Assessment of academic stress and suicidality is widely heterogeneous, varying from standardized multi-domain psychometric tests to single-item categorical ratings (Deb et al., 2015). In addition, studies vary substantially in terms of study design, study samples, control for confounding variables, and outcome period definition, making comparison and synthesis across studies difficult. In addition, whereas some studies control for psychological mediators like depression, resilience, and coping styles, many studies do not uniformly control for co-morbid mental health disorders, thus limiting the inferential generalizability of the observed associations (Pascoe et al., 2020; Romeo, 2017). As global concern for student mental health increases and the possibility of suicide being prevented by early detection of modifiable risk factors like academic stress, there is a critical need to critically evaluate the evidence that links academic stress to suicide-related outcomes. Accordingly, the present systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted to synthesize the current evidence that investigates the association between academic stress and suicidal ideation or behavior among students.

Materials and methods

Eligibility Criteria

The PECOS framework was used to systematically develop the research question and guide the eligibility criteria in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 reporting guidelines (Page et al., 2021). The Population (P) were young adults and adolescents who attended secondary or tertiary schools. The Exposure (E) was academic stress, defined as distress caused by academic pressures, demands, or exams. The Comparator (C), if applicable, were students with lower levels of academic stress or no exposure to academic stress. The Outcomes (O) were suicidal ideation, suicide attempts, and suicide risk probabilities, assessed using validated measures or self-report questionnaires. The Study design (S) was limited to cross-sectional studies.

Cross-sectional studies were considered for inclusion in this review due to their capacity to study the relationships between academic stress and suicide-related outcomes in large and heterogeneous student populations at one point in time. This design permitted the simultaneous gathering of self-report information on both exposure to stress and suicidal ideation or behavior, as necessary for derivation of initial correlations without compromising ethical and practical demands of longitudinal follow-up studies. Because of the sensitive and confidential nature of suicidality, as well as reliance on institutional settings for data gathering, the cross-sectional study offered an accessible and prevalent methodological strategy throughout the literature examined.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Inclusion criteria for studies were: (1) peer-reviewed observational studies (cross-sectional, cohort, or case-control) of original work published in English; (2) studies of student populations in schools, colleges, or universities; (3) quantitative measurement of academic stress using any self-report or validated measurement instruments; (4) quantitative measurement of suicide-related outcomes, i.e., suicidal ideation, suicidal attempts, or suicide risk; and (5) reporting of data in a format amenable to the extraction of effect size estimates (e.g., OR, β, r) or continuous/dichotomous data for comparisons. Research articles were not included if they satisfied the following: (1) were editorials, case reports, conference abstracts, meta-analyses, or reviews; (2) did not measure general psychological stress specifically in academic settings; (3) did not include a direct measure of suicide outcome; (4) were clinical psychiatric samples or included institutionalized samples; or (5) did not provide extractable quantitative data or effect sizes. Geographical location and year of publication exclusions were not applied.

Database Search Protocol

An exhaustive search was conducted in electronic databases on six databases: PubMed, Scopus, PsycINFO, Web of Science, CINAHL, and the Cochrane Library. Each database was optimized individually with Boolean operators, truncation, proximity operators, and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) to ensure maximal sensitivity and specificity in the retrieval process. The search strategy framework included three broad conceptual fields: academic stress, suicidality, and the student population (table 1). The MeSH terms and keywords used in the search were "academic stress," "educational stress," "suicidal ideation," "suicide attempt," "students," "adolescents," and their respective synonyms. The search results were then exported to EndNote to enable removal of duplicates, followed by an evaluation of titles, abstracts, and full texts based on predetermined eligibility criteria. The search was updated in April 2025.

Data Extraction Protocol

Data extraction was conducted separately by two reviewers on a pre-tested structured extraction form in Microsoft Excel. The items extracted were: (1) bibliographic information (author, year, country); (2) study information (design, sample size, age, gender split); (3) measurement instruments for academic stress and suicidality (name of instrument, domains, psychometric properties); (4) outcomes (prevalence, severity, binary/ continuous scoring); (5) statistical tests (approach, effect size, confidence interval, p-values); (6) covariate adjustments (academic, demographic, psychological); and (7) raw means, standard deviations, and event rates where applicable. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus.

Risk of Bias Assessment Protocol

The bias assessment was carried out using the AXIS tool (Downes et al., 2016), which is specifically designed for cross-sectional studies. Each study was evaluated across different areas, such as clarity of purpose, suitability of study design, justification of sample size, validity of measurements, management of non-responders, and performance of statistical analysis. Two reviewers separately evaluated each area as "Yes," "No," or "Unclear." Differences were resolved and discussed. Studies with more than five "No" ratings were deemed to be at moderate-to-high risk of bias.

Meta-Analysis Protocol

Meta-analytical synthesis was performed using Review Manager software (RevMan 5.4.1) with a random-effects model to adjust for heterogeneity between studies. For continuous outcomes (e.g., suicidal ideation by sex and academic stress), mean differences (MD) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated. For dichotomous outcomes (e.g., suicide attempt yes/ no and suicidal ideation yes/no), odds ratios (OR) with 95% CI were calculated. Heterogeneity was assessed by I2statistic, Chi2test, and Tau2. Subgroup analyses were performed to examine discrepancies between genders and between suicidal ideation and suicide attempts. Forest plots were used to graphically display effect sizes, and significance was tested using Z-tests. Publication bias was not tested since there were few studies in each meta-analysis category.

Results

The study selection process was performed across six databases (figure 1), i.e., PubMed (n = 62), Scopus (n = 71), PsycINFO (n = 58), Web of Science (n = 63), CINAHL (n = 66), and the Cochrane Library (n = 68), a total of 388 records were identified. After removal of 36 duplicate records, there were 352 unique records for screening. No records were excluded through automation tools or any other reasons. All 352 records were screened, and none of them were excluded during title and abstract screening. After full-text article retrieval attempts, 29 records were found to be inaccessible. Among 323 full-text reports screened for eligibility, 87 were excluded as case reports, 76 as literature reviews, 51 due to not meeting PECOS criteria, 65 as editorials, and 34 as thesis articles. In total, 10 studies were included in qualitative and quantitative synthesis, and no other records were found through other sources.

Study selection process for this review

Bias assessment observations

Selection bias was identified as high in three studies (Ang & Huan, 2006; Kim et al., 2024; Waseem, 2023), moderate in several studies (Arun et al., 2017; Khan et al., 2016; Okechukwu et al., 2022; Sahlabadi et al., 2022), and low in a smaller number of studies (Banerjee & Chatterjee, 2016; Uğurlu & Ona, 2009; Zhang et al., 2012) (see figure 2). Performance bias demonstrated greater variability across the included studies, with high risk reported in some investigations (Ang & Huan, 2006; Khan et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2012), moderate risk in others (Banerjee & Chatterjee, 2016; Uğurlu & Ona, 2009; Okechukwu et al., 2022; Waseem, 2023), and low risk in the remaining studies (Arun et al., 2017; Kim et al., 2024; Sahlabadi et al., 2022).

Bias assessment observations for this review

Detection bias was most frequently categorized as low risk in several studies (Ang & Huan, 2006; Banerjee & Chatterjee, 2016; Uğurlu & Ona, 2009; Sahlabadi et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2012), while a moderate risk was observed in some studies (Arun et al., 2017; Kim et al., 2024), and a high risk was identified in others (Khan et al., 2016; Okechukwu et al., 2022; Waseem, 2023). Attrition bias was generally low across the included studies, with a high risk reported in only one study (Okechukwu et al., 2022). Reporting bias was highest in several studies (Arun et al., 2017; Uğurlu & Ona, 2009; Sahlabadi et al., 2022), moderate in others (Khan et al., 2016; Kim et al., 2024; Waseem, 2023), and low in the remaining studies. Finally, other potential sources of bias were rated as moderate to high in approximately half of the included studies (Arun et al., 2017; Banerjee & Chatterjee, 2016; Uğurlu & Ona, 2009; Kim et al., 2024; Sahlabadi et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2012), while the remaining studies were assessed as having a low risk of bias (Ang & Huan, 2006; Khan et al., 2016; Okechukwu et al., 2022; Waseem, 2023).

Demographic characteristics assessed

The demographic characteristics of the included studies demonstrated considerable heterogeneity (see table 2). The studies represented diverse geographical contexts, including Singapore (Ang & Huan, 2006), India (Arun et al., 2017; Banerjee & Chatterjee, 2016; Waseem, 2023), Malaysia (Khan et al., 2016), South Korea (Kim et al., 2024), Nigeria (Okechukwu et al., 2022), Iran (Sahlabadi et al., 2022), Turkey (Uğurlu & Ona, 2009), and China (Zhang et al., 2012). These studies collectively cover several global regions, including South Asia, East Asia, Southeast Asia, West Asia, and Africa.

Demographic characteristics of included studies

Temporally, the included studies span nearly two decades, ranging from earlier research conducted in 2006 (Ang & Huan, 2006) to more recent investigations published in 2024 (Kim et al., 2024). Notably, the majority of studies were conducted within the past decade, reflecting the sustained international relevance of research examining academic stress and its psychological consequences among students.

Sample sizes varied substantially across studies. The largest sample consisted of a nationally representative South Korean cohort of 77,363 participants (Kim et al., 2024). Other studies reported moderate sample sizes, including a Chinese sample of 671 students (Zhang et al., 2012) and a Malaysian–Indian comparative sample comprising 600 students, with 300 participants from each country (Khan et al., 2016). Several studies relied on smaller, localized samples, such as a study of 232 Indian students (Arun et al., 2017) and approximately 270 Indian adolescents enrolled in coaching institutions (Waseem, 2023).

Across the included studies, the mean age of participants generally ranged from adolescence to young adulthood. Secondary school samples reported mean ages of approximately 15.4 years (Ang & Huan, 2006) and 15.7 years (Kim et al., 2024), whereas university-based samples included young adults with mean ages of approximately 21.5 years (Sahlabadi et al., 2022) and 25.0 years (Okechukwu et al., 2022). Some studies did not explicitly report mean age but described participants as early adolescents (Arun et al., 2017) or college-aged students (Khan et al., 2016; Uğurlu & Ona, 2009).

Gender distribution was explicitly reported in only a limited number of studies. Approximately equal gender representation was observed among Singaporean secondary school students (approximately 50% male; Ang & Huan, 2006) and within the large South Korean sample (approximately 49% male; Kim et al., 2024). One study conducted in Iran included a female-only cohort (Sahlabadi et al., 2022), whereas the Nigerian sample consisted predominantly of male participants (approximately 65%) (Okechukwu et al., 2022). In several other studies, gender composition was not clearly reported; however, mixed-gender samples were generally assumed (Arun et al., 2017; Banerjee & Chatterjee, 2016; Waseem, 2023; Uğurlu & Ona, 2009; Zhang et al., 2012).

The measurement of academic stress across the included studies demonstrated considerable variability, reflecting differences in the conceptualization and operationalization of stress (see table 3). Several standardized instruments were used to assess academic stress. These included the Student Life Stress Inventory (SLSI) (Banerjee & Chatterjee, 2016), the Lakaev Academic Stress Response Scale (LASRS) (Okechukwu et al., 2022), the Osipow Occupational Stress Questionnaire, which was adapted for student populations (Sahlabadi et al., 2022), the General Health Questionnaire (GHQ-12) and the Mooney Problem Checklist (MPC) (Arun et al., 2017), and the Life Stress Inventory (Zhang et al., 2012). In contrast, several studies employed study-specific or unspecified stress measures (Ang & Huan, 2006; Khan et al., 2016; Waseem, 2023). Additionally, a large-scale study conducted in South Korea used a single-item measure that categorized stressors into predefined domains, including academic achievement (Kim et al., 2024). The diversity of measurement approaches highlights the complexity of academic stress as a construct and reflects methodological differences across geographical and cultural contexts.

Academic stress assessment characteristics

Substantial heterogeneity was also observed in the domains of academic stress assessed across studies. Commonly reported domains included academic workload, examination pressure, and peer competition (Ang & Huan, 2006; Waseem, 2023), as well as family and societal expectations (Khan et al., 2016; Kim et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2012). Other studies assessed multidimensional responses to stress, including cognitive, behavioral, physiological, and emotional reactions (Okechukwu et al., 2022), while some focused on professional or academic role-related responsibilities (Sahlabadi et al., 2022). In several studies, individual stress domains were not explicitly defined; instead, broader conceptual frameworks or composite stress scores were used (Ang & Huan, 2006; Khan et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2012).

Reporting of psychometric properties was limited across the included studies. When reported, internal consistency reliability was high, with Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.83 (Okechukwu et al., 2022) and 0.89 (Sahlabadi et al., 2022). However, most studies did not provide clear evidence of psychometric validation for the measurement instruments used (Ang & Huan, 2006; Banerjee & Chatterjee, 2016; Khan et al., 2016; Uğurlu & Ona, 2009; Waseem, 2023; Zhang et al., 2012).

Most measurement strategies relied on self-administered questionnaires completed at a single time point, typically within classroom or institutional settings (Ang & Huan, 2006; Khan et al., 2016; Okechukwu et al., 2022; Sahlabadi et al., 2022; Uğurlu & Ona, 2009; Waseem, 2023; Zhang et al., 2012). Large-scale surveys occasionally employed online questionnaire methods (Kim et al., 2024). Interviewer-administered assessments were uncommon and were reported in only one study (Arun et al., 2017). Data collection generally occurred during regular academic periods, although some studies conducted assessments during specific academic phases, such as examination periods or the beginning of academic terms (Ang & Huan, 2006; Waseem, 2023).

In most studies, academic stress was measured using continuous rating scales rather than categorical classifications (Ang & Huan, 2006; Khan et al., 2016; Okechukwu et al., 2022; Waseem, 2023; Zhang et al., 2012). However, some studies applied categorical thresholds based on established cut-off scores. For example, a score greater than three on the GHQ-12 was used to indicate psychological distress (Arun et al., 2017), while Osipow stress scores exceeding 150 were interpreted as indicative of high stress (Sahlabadi et al., 2022).

Control for academic confounding variables, such as grade point average (GPA) or academic ranking, was limited across the included studies. Only a small number of studies explicitly controlled for academic confounders through stratified analyses or multivariable statistical models (Banerjee & Chatterjee, 2016; Kim et al., 2024). One study indirectly accounted for academic factors through predefined group classifications based on academic achievement (Arun et al., 2017). However, most studies did not systematically control for academic covariates, suggesting limited consideration of academic performance or attendance as potential confounding variables in the relationship between academic stress and suicidal outcomes (Ang & Huan, 2006; Khan et al., 2016; Okechukwu et al., 2022; Uğurlu & Ona, 2009; Waseem, 2023; Zhang et al., 2012).

A large majority of research studies focused on suicidal ideation mainly, if not exclusively, as their sole measure of outcome (Ang & Huan, 2006; Khan et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2012; Okechukwu et al., 2022; Sahlabadi et al., 2022; Waseem, 2023; Arun et al., 2017; Banerjee & Chatterjee, 2016), whereas fewer focused on actual attempts at suicide (Kim et al., 2024) or probability-based estimates of suicide risk (Uğurlu & Ona, 2009) (table 4). A range of standardized and psychometrically assessed measures was applied to the evaluation of suicidal ideation, e.g., the Beck Scale for Suicide Ideation (SSI) (Okechukwu et al., 2022), Adult Suicidal Ideation Questionnaire (ASIQ) (Arun et al., 2017), Suicide Behaviors Questionnaire (SBQ) (Sahlabadi et al., 2022), Suicidal Ideation Questionnaire (SIQ) (Ang & Huan, 2006; Khan et al., 2016), Suicide Risk Eleven (SRE) scale (Banerjee & Chatterjee, 2016), and the Suicide Probability Scale (SPS) (Uğurlu & Ona, 2009). Two studies additionally employed less-complex assessment tools, including self-report single items (Kim et al., 2024; Waseem, 2023). In spite of such diversity of measurement tools successfully evoking suicidal behavior-related phenomena, differences in measurement rigor, psychometric validation, and specificity across the tools revealed an inherent variation in sensitivity and specificity in suicide risk identification in different demographic populations.

Suicide-Related Outcome Characteristics

The period of time over which suicidal consequences were investigated was significant in variability. Some investigations had point-in-time measures of current or recent suicidal thinking (Khan et al., 2016; Okechukwu et al., 2022; Uğurlu & Ona, 2009; Waseem, 2023), while others used longer retrospective reporting periods, e.g., “past month” (Arun et al., 2017), “past few weeks” (Ang & Huan, 2006), “past 12 months” (Kim et al., 2024; Sahlabadi et al., 2022; Waseem, 2023), or combined recent thinking with lifetime history of attempts (Sahlabadi et al., 2022). Confirmation of outcomes was consistently self-reported and collected with anonymous questionnaires or classroom surveys and may represent a common methodological limitation in terms of social desirability bias or recall error (Ang & Huan, 2006; Khan et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2012; Arun et al., 2017; Banerjee & Chatterjee, 2016; Kim et al., 2024; Okechukwu et al., 2022; Sahlabadi et al., 2022; Uğurlu & Ona, 2009; Waseem, 2023).

Few studies had controlled or measured co-occurring mental health variables systematically. One study in Singapore measured depression and used it as a mediator (Ang & Huan, 2006). Another study in India used the GHQ-12 for measuring general psychological distress and studied some learning disorders (Banerjee & Chatterjee, 2016). Although coping styles and psychological resilience were frequently measured, specific psychiatric illnesses like depression or anxiety were rarely measured in conjunction with suicide outcomes (Okechukwu et al., 2022; Uğurlu & Ona, 2009; Waseem, 2023). Most studies did not include specific mental health measures or excluded participants on psychotropic drugs specifically to prevent psychiatric confounding (Sahlabadi et al., 2022). Most studies therefore did not control for well-established psychiatric risk factors for suicidality, restricting the potential to make full etiological inferences.

Few studies utilized formal severity grading scales for suicidal behavior. The majority of analyses utilized continuous suicidal ideation scores based on standardized measures without explicit categorical risk stratification (Ang & Huan, 2006; Khan et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2012; Okechukwu et al., 2022). Some studies dichotomized scores into explicit risk categories (e.g., SBQ score > 7 as a marker for high ideation risk) (Sahlabadi et al., 2022) or employed simple binary measures of suicidal ideation presence/ absence (Kim et al., 2024; Waseem, 2023). The Suicide Probability Scale (SPS) was utilized to measure overall suicide risk probability continuously but without explicit grading categories (Uğurlu & Ona, 2009). Accordingly, although studies utilized predominantly continuous scoring, methodological inconsistency in risk categorization created inter-study comparability and interpretability challenges.

Psychological covariate adjustment was inconsistent and, in some studies, inadequate. While one study specifically controlled for adolescent depression as a mediating variable (Ang & Huan, 2006), other studies did not consistently control for psychiatric or psychological covariates (Arun et al., 2017; Banerjee & Chatterjee, 2016; Khan et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2012; Okechukwu et al., 2022; Sahlabadi et al., 2022; Waseem, 2023). A very large Korean study (Kim et al., 2024) controlled extensively for temporal and demographic covariates but did not explicitly include specific confounding mental health variables. While coping styles, resilience, and social support processes were typically explored as mediators or moderators, analysis as covariates was not systematically conducted, reflecting methodological differences and possible confounding variables in differing findings.

Different analytical techniques were used across studies (see table 5). Hierarchical multiple regression analysis was used to examine mediation effects around depressive symptoms as a mediator (Ang & Huan, 2006). Similarly, multivariate regression models based on Baron and Kenny’s model of mediation were used to examine mediation through coping styles and social support systems (Khan et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2012). Logistic regression models were used to estimate associations between binary outcomes of suicidal ideation and categorical levels of stress (Kim et al., 2024; Sahlabadi et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2012). Relatively less complex analytical techniques such as ANOVA and Pearson correlations were used by some studies to establish significant differences and correlations between stress and suicidal ideation scores (Arun et al., 2017; Banerjee & Chatterjee, 2016; Uğurlu & Ona, 2009; Waseem, 2023). Moderation analysis with hierarchical linear regression, specifically using the PROCESS macro, examined the interaction of coping styles and psychological resilience on the relationship between stress and suicide (Okechukwu et al., 2022). Methodological design therefore reflected different levels of analytical complexity and rigor in examining the direct, indirect, and interactive effects relevant to the research question.

Statistical and methodological characteristics

Effect measures demonstrated widespread variability, including standardized regression coefficients (β), odds ratios (OR), correlation coefficients (r), and F-statistics, and therefore represent various analytical approaches. Regression analysis studies primarily reported standardized β coefficients, which quantify the strength and directional quality of predictive associations (Ang & Huan, 2006; Khan et al., 2016; Okechukwu et al., 2022). Logistic regression analyses documented several odds ratios, which demonstrated significant increases in suicidal ideation risk at high levels of stress (OR = 11.83, 95% CI ≈ 5.3–26) in Iran (Sahlabadi et al., 2022), and similarly increased odds for ideation and attempts (OR ≈ 2.9–3.8) identified in South Korea (Kim et al., 2024). While some studies presented confidence intervals explicitly (Kim et al., 2024; Okechukwu et al., 2022; Sahlabadi et al., 2022), most did not, which could potentially limit interpretability regarding the precision of effect estimates (Ang & Huan, 2006; Arun et al., 2017; Banerjee & Chatterjee, 2016; Khan et al., 2016; Uğurlu & Ona, 2009; Waseem, 2023; Zhang et al., 2012).

All studies employed a p-value cutoff of p < .05 to denote statistical significance (Ang & Huan, 2006; Arun et al., 2017; Banerjee & Chatterjee, 2016; Khan et al., 2016; Kim et al., 2024; Okechukwu et al., 2022; Sahlabadi et al., 2022; Uğurlu & Ona, 2009; Waseem, 2023; Zhang et al., 2012). Missing data management was generally performed using complete-case analysis, resulting in exclusion of cases with missing data and potentially introducing missing data bias (Ang & Huan, 2006; Arun et al., 2017; Banerjee & Chatterjee, 2016; Khan et al., 2016; Kim et al., 2024; Okechukwu et al., 2022; Sahlabadi et al., 2022; Uğurlu & Ona, 2009; Waseem, 2023; Zhang et al., 2012). Only one large survey employed weighted analyses to maintain representativeness (Kim et al., 2024). More sophisticated approaches such as multiple imputation were not used, reflecting a methodological limitation that may influence the external validity of the results.

Multiple testing correction adjustments were infrequently applied across the included studies and may represent a shared methodological limitation. One study made adjustments for multiple outcomes by conducting analyses separately by year and by sex (Kim et al., 2024). Most studies reported that no multiple testing corrections were applied, which could increase the risk of Type I error (Ang & Huan, 2006; Arun et al., 2017; Banerjee & Chatterjee, 2016; Khan et al., 2016; Okechukwu et al., 2022; Sahlabadi et al., 2022; Uğurlu & Ona, 2009; Waseem, 2023; Zhang et al., 2012). However, most studies tested a limited number of hypotheses, which may have implicitly reduced excessive multiple comparison risks.

Key covariates examined included depression (Ang & Huan, 2006), coping styles and resilience factors (Khan et al., 2016; Okechukwu et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2012), and demographic factors such as sex, age, academic semester, year of study, academic field, socioeconomic status, and nationality (Khan et al., 2016; Kim et al., 2024; Sahlabadi et al., 2022; Uğurlu & Ona, 2009). Notably, depression fully mediated the relationship between academic stress and suicidal ideation (Ang & Huan, 2006), whereas coping styles and social support were commonly tested as mediators or moderators, often reducing the direct effect of stress on suicidal ideation (Khan et al., 2016; Okechukwu et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2012). Age and academic stage also appeared to influence outcomes, with younger students showing greater vulnerability (Sahlabadi et al., 2022). Demographic subgroup analyses identified differential stress–suicide associations across populations, including differences between Indian and Malaysian students (Khan et al., 2016) and between male and female students (Kim et al., 2024).

For continuous outcomes (figure 3), subgroup analysis of suicidal ideation scores for male vs. female participants identified vast heterogeneity (I2=100%; Chi2=281.63, p<0.00001) with a mean difference of 14.43 (95% CI: -22.16 to 51.02) that was statistically nonsignificant (Z=0.77, p=0.44). Furthermore, academic stress also identified vast heterogeneity (I2=98%; Chi2=55.99, p<0.00001) and a mean difference of 10.38 (95% CI: -6.34 to 27.09; Z=1.22, p=0.22) which was also nonsignificant. Overall, the continuous outcomes identified vast statistical heterogeneity (I2=99%; Chi2=343.13, p<0.00001) and identified a mean difference of 12.38 (95% CI: -3.70 to 28.47; Z=1.51, p=0.13) which was overall nonsignificant. The observed difference between suicidal ideation and academic stress between subgroups was also nonsignificant (Chi2=0.04, df=1, p=0.84; I2=0%).

Discussion

Growing concern about academic pressure and its association with suicidality in student populations must be interpreted within a broader psychosocial, institutional, and developmental framework. Academic stress is not a uniform or isolated phenomenon; rather, it is shaped by educational systems, sociocultural expectations, institutional competitiveness, and individual psychological vulnerability. Evidence from high-stress professional training environments, particularly within medicine and other health-related disciplines, demonstrates that sustained academic pressure, burnout, emotional exhaustion, and performance-driven cultures can substantially increase the risk of depressive symptoms and suicidal ideation. These parallels suggest that academic stress among students may function through mechanisms similar to those observed in other high-demand educational and professional contexts, underscoring its relevance as a significant mental health risk factor (Dyrbye et al., 2008).

An important observation arising from this review is that all studies meeting inclusion criteria were conducted in non-Western countries, primarily in South and East Asia, the Middle East, and parts of Africa. This pattern does not reflect a deliberate selection bias, as no geographical restrictions were applied in the search strategy. Rather, it may reflect substantive differences in research focus and conceptual frameworks across regions. In Western contexts, academic stress is frequently examined under broader constructs such as general psychological distress, burnout, or mental health disorders, rather than being operationalized as a distinct exposure. Additionally, Western studies on suicidality often prioritize psychiatric diagnoses (e.g., depression, anxiety, substance use disorders) as primary explanatory variables, with academic stress treated as a contextual or secondary factor. Ethical, legal, and institutional barriers surrounding the direct assessment of suicidal ideation among students may further limit the availability of eligible studies from the United States and Europe. Consequently, while the present findings demonstrate consistent associations between academic stress and suicidality, their generalizability to Western student populations should be interpreted with caution.

Personality organizations, psychopathological vulnerability, and identity consolidation stages of adolescence and early adulthood are of utmost importance in establishing the susceptibility of an individual to stress-related psychopathology. University students, especially in transitional or competitive education environments, have been found to exhibit higher psychological vulnerability, expressed in the form of predisposition to mood disorders, drug consumption, and identity issues—traits usually mediated through core personality factors and developmental instability (Arnett, 2014). In some students, especially those deeply involved in professional studies like medicine, nursing, or midwifery, these vulnerabilities are augmented by moral distress, secondary traumatic stress, and idealistic expectations regarding professional identity, which, if not addressed, may appear as maladaptive coping or drug consumption behaviors (Dyrbye et al., 2014).

When all the included studies were pooled to draw overall inferences, differences and similarities were observed. Ang and Huan (2006), Khan et al. (2016), and Okechukwu et al. (2022) all concurred with the general observation that the relationship between academic stress and suicidal ideation was moderated by psychological factors like depression, coping, and resilience. These three studies demonstrated methodological strength by using regression-based methods and indirect effect estimation and consistently exhibited suppression of significant direct effects of stress when mediating variables were controlled. Zhang et al. (2012) also corroborated this observation by identifying coping as a partial mediator and thus supporting the psychological buffering hypothesis proposed in Ang and Huan (2006), Khan et al. (2016), and Okechukwu et al. (2022).

In contrast to this, the studies by Banerjee and Chatterjee (2016), Arun et al. (2017), Waseem (2023), and Uğurlu and Ona (2009) were based mostly on correlational analysis or group comparisons among ANOVA-based groups with no controls over psychological covariates; their interpretations were hence mostly descriptive in nature. Even so, the results achieved by Banerjee and Chatterjee (2016) and Waseem (2023) were qualitatively similar to those reported within the studies by Ang and Huan (2006) and Khan et al. (2016), confirming suicidal ideation and academic stress to bear high positive correlation. Even so, however, methodological faults found within the studies by Banerjee and Chatterjee (2016) and Waseem (2023), such as failure in psychometric validation and multivariable adjustment, compromised the integrity of their comparability to studies by Ang and Huan (2006) and Khan et al. (2016). In contrast to this, however, Kim et al. (2024) stood alone given its wide scope and quality methodology, utilizing logistic regression and a national sample with extensive covariate adjustment. Although clinical indicators of mental health were not available within the present study, findings achieved were congruent with those reported by Ang and Huan (2006), Khan et al. (2016), and Okechukwu et al. (2022), particularly regarding the increased susceptibility of suicidality to both academic and family stress.

Likewise, Sahlabadi et al. (2022) adopted a similar approach to Kim et al. (2024) by utilizing logistic regression with categorical thresholds but differed in the further illumination brought on the moderator roles of age and semester and the exclusion of psychological entities like depression or coping. Even so, however, these two studies, Kim et al. (2024) and Sahlabadi et al. (2022), demonstrated large effect sizes alongside statistically significant associations, in contrast to the reporting of non-significant or borderline effects in the studies by Arun et al. (2017) and Waseem (2023).

The study by Uğurlu and Ona (2009) differed from the others considerably in that it measured the outcome as suicide probability rather than suicidal ideation or attempts alone and failed to control for psychiatric or academic variables when examining coping styles. Although partially congruent with the research of Khan et al. (2016) and Zhang et al. (2012) in examining the mediating role of coping, the lack of control limited the inferential conclusions relative to the other studies. Likewise, Arun et al. (2017) presented a reversed outcome, in which academically average students were found to have increased levels of stress and ideation, differing from the pattern predicted in the majority of studies. Although this reversal was interesting, it was not accompanied by further covariate analysis and could result from sampling biases or uncontrolled confounding variables.

India and other low- and middle-income countries represent a unique instance of compounding intersecting psychosocial stressors including socioeconomic inequality, competitive schooling systems, limited mental health facilities, and sustained stigma about mental illness. Intersecting educational stress with such social determinants produces amplified psychological distress among marginalized subpopulations of students such as first-generation students, girls, and science, technology, engineering, and mathematics students (Patel et al., 2018). Cross-national analyses have also documented that middle school onset of psychological stress often leads to chronic stress or affective disorders in late adolescence and early adulthood, where academic pressures act as a uniform stressor across developmental stages (Compas et al., 2017).

The stress-evoked phenomenon of psychological morbidity is not peculiar to specific disciplines or geographic regions, as witnessed by its universal occurrence in international datasets. Studies in nursing students have revealed alarming incidences of depressive, anxiety, and somatic symptoms—a condition exacerbated by self-negligent behaviors, institutional limitations, and emotional fatigue associated with clinical training (Ríos-Risquez et al., 2016). To alleviate this growing concern, psychological interventions such as mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT), acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT), and dialectical behavior therapy (DBT) have been evaluated for their effectiveness among high-risk groups such as medical and nursing students. Systematic reviews have indicated significant improvements including stress regulation, reduction of rumination, and improved mood stability (Galante et al., 2018).

Meta-analyses report that approximately one-third of medical students exhibit severe psychological and behavioral problems, including suicidal thoughts, sleep disorders, substance abuse, and reduced academic functioning, suggesting that the educational environment may act both as a precipitating and sustaining factor in psychological deterioration (Rotenstein et al., 2016; Dyrbye et al., 2006; Ishak et al., 2013; Quek et al., 2019). Emotional intelligence, often absent in conventional educational systems, has also been proposed as a protective factor; its absence has been associated with ineffective stress appraisal, emotional instability, and impaired social interactions, all of which may increase suicide risk among students in high-pressure professions (Brackett et al., 2011).

The COVID-19 pandemic further accelerated deterioration in students’ mental health through widespread social isolation, disruption of academic functioning, and breakdown of peer support systems—factors closely linked with psychological distress and suicidality. Systematic reviews have shown that pandemic-related stressors amplified existing vulnerabilities and created new risks for suicidal ideation and suicide attempts among students (Son et al., 2020). Minority student groups, including female Arab students in Israel, have experienced additional intersectional stress related to acculturation conflict and identity negotiation challenges, which have been associated with non-suicidal self-injury and suicidal ideation (Khoury-Kassabri et al., 2021).

Another important but understudied area of research in the Indian context is how student suicide is portrayed in mainstream media, which can influence public perception and institutional responses. Analyses of newspaper reports have identified recurring themes of academic failure, family pressure, and institutional neglect, suggesting systemic gaps in early detection and preventive interventions (Armstrong et al., 2018). Post-pandemic educational transitions, particularly the shift from online learning back to face-to-face evaluation systems, have also created additional adjustment difficulties for students. Those exposed to sudden academic transitions reported higher anxiety and anticipatory stress, highlighting the need for carefully designed reintegration strategies within educational institutions (Aristovnik et al., 2020).

More recent observational studies from South Asia have also shown that allied health and rehabilitation students—groups often underrepresented in mental health research—report high levels of depression, anxiety, and suicidal behavior. These findings suggest that the mental health risk associated with academic stress extends beyond traditionally high-pressure disciplines such as medicine or engineering (Khan et al., 2022). In addition, longitudinal studies from Western countries have documented increasing trends in student suicides during the pandemic era, with suicide risk strongly associated with disruptions in academic engagement, social support, and accessibility of mental health services (Gunnell et al., 2020).

Limitations

This review was faced with several methodological inconsistencies in the studies involved. Firstly, there was significant heterogeneity in measurement and conceptualization of academic stress. Several of the studies used non-standard or single-item measures without demonstrating psychometric validity and thus reducing construct comparability. Additionally, suicidality outcome measurement was highly inconsistent and varied from validated multi-item scales to simple yes/no self-report items and thus reducing the reliability of outcome verification. Also, most of the studies used cross-sectional designs without temporal resolution and thus reducing the ability to make causal inferences. Psychiatric covariates like depression and anxiety were rarely measured or controlled for despite having been established as confounding variables of suicidality.

Clinical recommendations

The findings included in the current review advance the development of an integrated corpus of clinical and public health recommendations. Ongoing screening of factors of suicide risk and stress for school achievement should be included in school health programs, especially in times of increased academic stress, e.g., examination periods or periods of transitional criticality. Schools must develop evidence-based evaluation procedures attuned to the cultural context to allow for earlier identification of students presenting high levels of stress and suicidal ideation. Psychological resilience and coping must be strengthened through sound mental health education curricula, stress management strategies, and exposure to qualified counseling professionals. Extra care must be taken with young students and freshmen, as they are most vulnerable to the psychological effects of stress. Institutional policy must ensure academic expectations, testing practices, and competitive environments are not disproportionately disadvantageous to the mental health of students.

Empirically, longitudinal designs will need to be used in future studies to further elucidate the temporal dynamics and causal linkages between academic stress and suicidality. Standardized measures of academic stress and suicide outcomes will need to be employed to maximize comparability between groups. Psychiatric comorbidities such as depression and anxiety will need to be controlled for in studies so that the independent effect of academic stress can be estimated. Lastly, future studies will need to investigate differential stress effects on suicidal behaviors by demographic subgroups since this would form the basis for more targeted prevention interventions.

Conclusion

The existence of academic distress was strongly correlated with increased risks of suicidal ideation and attempts, particularly when examined with respect to binary classifications. In contrast, continuous outcome analyses did not show gender differences. More methodologically rigorous studies are required with respect to the heterogeneity of measures of stress and suicide, differential covariate adjustment, and lack of adequate psychometric validation. Results should be interpreted in the context of high statistical heterogeneity and minimal control for psychiatric comorbidities.
