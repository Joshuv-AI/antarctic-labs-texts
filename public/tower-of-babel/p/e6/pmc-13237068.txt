Astrocyte fatty acid metabolism as a driver of risk for major depressive disorder
Authors: Eamon Fitzgerald, Nicholas O’Toole, Irina Pokhvisneva, Eric J. Nestler, Gustavo Turecki, Corina Nagy, Michael J. Meaney
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have successfully identified genetic loci associated with major depressive disorder (MDD), yet the complex gene networks underpinning this polygenic risk remain largely uncharacterised. Here, we elucidate the neurobiological mechanisms of MDD by analyzing co-expression networks of 94 risk genes in the human prefrontal cortex. By linking these networks to individual symptoms, we identify the FADS1 (fatty acid desaturase 1) network as a central integrator across symptom clusters. We find that the FADS1 network functions primarily in astrocytes to regulate fatty acid metabolism and influence oligodendrocyte-related cell states. Furthermore, we identify FGF2 as a synaptic effector of this pathway and highlight PPARα (peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha) as a putative therapeutic target. These results establish astrocyte fatty acid metabolism as a critical mechanistic contributor to MDD and a promising avenue for treatment.

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a leading contributor to the global burden of disease and is associated with immense socio-economic consequences1,2. Pharmacotherapy is typically the primary treatment strategy for MDD but large clinical trials show high relapse rates with common anti-depressant drugs3. Identifying novel therapies is a priority but is hampered by our currently incomplete mechanistic understanding of MDD.

Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) show conceptual promise to identify MDD mechanisms in an unbiased fashion. Indeed, analysis on GWAS summary statistics have nominated many hundreds of genes that contribute to MDD risk. However, the genetic architecture of MDD is highly polygenic, and as such, risk is not driven by individual genes, but through complex gene networks4,5. Genetically identified risk genes are likely important components of these networks, but in isolation have a minimal contribution to overall risk5,6.

Co-expression networks are one method to identify the broad networks through which genes act to influence phenotypic variation. They are typically built in an unbiased fashion using RNA-sequencing datasets7, but an alternative method is to construct seeded co-expression networks, where networks are built around candidate genes such as those identified from post-GWAS analyses. Seeded co-expression networks have been used successfully to interrogate the mechanistic underpinnings of psychiatric disorders8,9 and previous work has shown this approach can accurately recapitulate CRISPR loss of function experiments10. We first identified MDD risk genes by annotating MDD GWAS summary statistics to the gene level using chromatin interaction data from the human dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC). The DLPFC is among the most well-studied brain regions in MDD, with evidence from post-mortem, model systems, and imaging studies as well as clinical trials pointing towards an important role of the DLPFC in MDD11–14. We created seeded co-expression networks for these genes using RNA-seq data from the DLPFC of the GTEx dataset. We validated these networks in internal and external datasets and then associated the most robust networks with the nine individual symptoms of MDD assessed by the patient health questionnaire 9 (PHQ9).

These networks clustered into distinct patterns of association with the individual MDD symptoms and gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis showing that the clustered networks represented distinct functional mechanisms. The FADS1positive (fatty acid desaturase 1) network (i.e. genes positively correlated with FADS1 expression in the DLPFC) was the network most densely connected with other network clusters and emerged as an important candidate for further study. The FADS1positive network was associated with fatty acid signalling and primarily expressed in astrocytes. Integration of the FADS1 network with snRNA-seq data from control and MDD samples showed that the FADS1positive network could define an astrocyte population depleted in MDD. This depleted astrocyte population was associated with the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and the oligodendrocyte lineage. Further analyses suggested the FADS1positive network drove transcriptomic changes in excitatory neurons through changes in astrocyte-excitatory neuron communication, putatively driven by FGF2 (fibroblast growth factor 2). Finally, we identified PPARα (peroxisome proliferator activated receptor alpha) as a promising therapeutic candidate for further investigation in MDD.

Results

Generating seeded co-expression networks to characterise the mechanisms of MDD risk genes

We first identified MDD risk genes by annotating an MDD GWAS15 to the gene level using Hi-C data from the DLPFC16–25 (Supplementary Fig. 1). We identified 153 genes with significant associations following correction for multiple comparisons (hereafter referred to as MDD risk genes, Fig. 2A; Supplementary Data 1). The MDD risk genes from HMAGMA were not enriched in any DLPFC co-expression networks identified from any of the eight previous studies we assessed6–13, all of which used a WGCNA (weighted correlation network analysis) approach (Supplementary Fig. 2). Although WGCNA, as shown by many studies, is a powerful approach for mechanistic inference6–15, considering the broad distribution of MDD risk genes across modules we reasoned a seeded co-expression approach may be more informative for mechanistic inference in this instance. We tested each MDD risk gene for co-expression with each of the 23,326 genes in the RNA-seq dataset (following filtering, normalisation and regression of covariates; see methods section). Any gene with a correlation coefficient >0.5 or <−0.5 and an FDR P < 0.05 was considered to be positively or negatively co-expressed, respectively. Together all genes positively or negatively co-expressed with a particular seed were considered to form positive or negative co-expression networks. Therefore, we use the word “network” to refer to the set of correlations with an MDD risk gene, and as such does not capture correlations between genes that are not the MDD risk gene (as is done in WGCNA networks for instance).

A positive co-expression network can be considered as the genes with direct functional associations with the MDD risk gene, while the negative co-expression network can be conceptualised as the processes downstream of those positively correlated genes. There was a strong correlation (r = 0.89; Pearson correlation) between the number of genes positively and negatively correlated with a given MDD risk gene (Fig. 2B; Supplementary Data 2). Remarkably, 55.4% of all genes tested for co-expression were co-expressed with at least one MDD risk gene (Fig. 2C), illustrating a striking degree of complexity. Processes related to neuronal activity were among the most common gene ontology (GO) terms enriched across all networks, with similar terms enriched in both positive and negative networks, suggesting oppositional regulation of processes by MDD risk gene networks (Fig. 2D, E).

Of all genes co-expressed genes with an MDD risk gene, 78.9% were co-expressed with more than one seed (Supplementary Fig. 3A; Supplementary Data 3). There was also a dose-dependent inverse relation between the number of networks a particular gene was present within and their loss-of-function constraint (LOUEF score) in the gnomAD database26 (Fig. 2F; Kruskal-Wallis p < 2.2e-16). We observed a similar pattern for missense mutation Z-score (Supplementary Fig. 3B; Kruskal-Wallis p < 2.2e-16), but a more modest association with synonymous mutation Z-scores that was also statistically significant (Supplementary Fig. 3C; Kruskal-Wallis p = 0.027). Psychiatric risk genes are typically highly intolerant to mutations, prompting us to ask if they were present in more networks than the background set of brain-expressed genes. Indeed, MDD risk genes (as defined in this study), SFARI high-confidence autism genes, and schizophrenia risk genes (those associated with rare variants27) were present in more networks than expected (Fig. 2G; Kruskal-Wallis p < 2.2e-16).

We next performed further enrichment analyses using genes divided into three categories: (i) those not co-expressed with an MDD risk gene, (ii) those present in <=5 networks, and (iii) those present in >5 networks. Genes present in >5 networks and thus highly implicated in MDD-related pathways were enriched in genes differentially expressed in rodent models of chronic stress28 (chronic variable stress- nucleus accumbens p = 1.3e-6; chronic variable stress- prefrontal cortex p = 1.8e-4; social isolation- nucleus accumbens p = 6.6e-17; social isolation- prefrontal cortex p = 3.3e-5; Fischer’s Exact Test; Fig. 2H). Genes present in >5 networks were also enriched in GWAS of common variants for bipolar disorder29 (p = 2.2e-7; MAGMA) and schizophrenia30 (p = 1.5e-5; MAGMA; Fig. 2H). Genes present in <= 5 networks were enriched in the schizophrenia GWAS (p = 9.2e-5; MAGMA), but not for other psychiatric disorders31–35. We found no enrichment using genes not co-expressed with MDD risk genes, suggesting seeded co-expression networks may be an efficient way to investigate pleiotropic mechanisms across psychiatric disorders.

Integration of networks with PHQ9 GWAS identifies symptom-specific MDD molecular associations

We validated the reproducibility of our co-expression networks using internal (adjacent tissue from the GTEx consortium with largely overlapping individuals; Supplementary Fig. 4A and Supplementary Fig. 5C) and external samples (using data from phase 1 of the CommonMind consortium20,23; Supplementary Fig. 4B and Supplementary Fig. 5B). In both validations, we assessed the correlation between each of our primary co-expression networks and the replication dataset. For both validation cohorts, we observed variable replications (internal r: range −0.06–0.9, mean = 0.72; external r: range −0.25–0.8, mean = 0.43). The replication for individual networks was highly correlated with the number of genes co-expressed with the MDD risk gene (Supplementary Fig. 4C; Supplementary Data 5; GTEx internal rho = 0.85 and p < 2.2e-16; CommonMind external rho = 0.55, P = 1.7e-8; Spearman correlation), which plateaued at 1000 co-expressed genes.

We also note substantial overlap of the genes within these co-expression networks, with 90% and 82% of the internal and external datasets, respectively enriched for genes in their corresponding co-expression networks from the original dataset (Supplementary Fig. 5). These data demonstrate networks with a larger number of genes are more reproducible. We therefore constrained our subsequent analyses to those networks with more than 1000 co-expressed genes.

To examine mechanisms proximal to each MDD risk gene, we used GWAS enrichment analysis with our positive co-expression networks in responses to the PHQ-9 included as part of the UK Biobank. We note that our initial characterisation of MDD risk genes using the MDD GWAS from Als et al.15, was performed using summary statistics that excluded the UK Biobank sample. This approach avoided the reuse of the UK Biobank in our PHQ-9 symptom-specific analyses. As guided by our previous validations, we used positive co-expression networks with more than 1000 co-expressed genes in this analysis (16 networks). Hierarchical clustering of the standardised betas from the GWAS enrichment analyses suggested three groups of networks (Fig. 3A). Cluster 1 (11 networks) was enriched for GO terms primarily related to neuronal function (Fig. 3B). Cluster 2 (2 networks) was enriched for GO terms related to metabolic processes such as lipid metabolism (Fig. 2C), and enrichment analyses (Fig. 3A) suggested a preferential association with suicidal ideation and feelings of inadequacy. We next investigated the inter-network interactions using network analysis, which allowed us to identify densely connected networks (Fig. 3E). We generated several network-based metrics including degree, betweenness, closeness, eigenvector centrality and pagerank (Fig. 3F; Supplementary Fig. 6). These metrics largely showed similar patterns across networks, with the notable exception of the FADS1positive network in betweenness, which had by far the highest betweenness of any network (Fig. 3F), with dense connections to several networks from the other clusters. We also observed a similar pattern in the internal GTEx validation dataset, with the FADS1positive network having the largest betweenness of any network. However, in the external CMC validation dataset, the FADS1positive network did not have the highest betweenness (Supplementary Fig. 7). Nevertheless, we considered the FADS1positive network as an attractive candidate for further investigation, as FADS1 has also been associated with MDD in several other analyses including Mendelian randomisation3, transcriptome-wide association studies4 and differential expression in human post-mortem tissue5. The FADS1positive network was also primarily associated with suicidal ideation, making it the most tractable to investigate through integration with post-mortem MDD datasets. Furthermore, the FADS1positive network was among the most well-replicated networks in our internal (r = 0.89; enrichment P < 2.22-16) and CMC external validations (r = 0.61; enrichment P < 2.22-16). Furthermore, we were able to validate the FADS1 network in two other external datasets using DLPFC tissue from control samples (Labonté et al.36 r = 0.59; enrichment P < 2.22-16 and Chang et al.23, r = 0.52; enrichment P < 2.22-16).

The FADS1positive network is astrocyte-specific and altered in MDD

We next aimed to further characterise the functional, cell-specific and MDD-associated characteristics of the FADS1positive network. Predictably, GO analysis of the network revealed enrichment for processes related to the metabolism of fatty acids (Fig. 4A). Encouragingly, the FADS1positive network was enriched for protein-protein interactions in STRINGdb37 (Supplementary Fig. 8; p < 1.0e-16), suggesting our RNA co-expression is also related to protein function. A down sampling and bootstrapping approach to estimate the number of genes in the FADS1positive network across the 11 distinct GTEx regions showed a large number of co-expressed genes across all regions, with the largest network in the DLPFC (Supplementary Fig. 9A). After the DLPFC, the anterior cingulate cortex, hippocampus and nucleus accumbens held the next largest FADS1positive networks. Each of these regions is strongly implicated in the neurobiology of MDD38. The FADS1positive network was enriched in several WGCNA modules from the human DLPFC across numerous studies (Supplementary Fig. 9B), and we found a signature highly overlapping to the FADS1positive network when we used sparse partial least squares to derive a latent variable of DLPFC gene expression correlated with FADS1 expression (909 of 949 genes overlapped with the FADS1positive network; Supplementary Fig. 9C). Together these data suggest the seeded coexpression approach is reproducible with other distinct methods. We then used snRNA-seq data from the DLPFC of control samples13 to determine the cell specificity of the FADS1positive network, which showed striking specificity for astrocytes (Fig. 4C). We also, however, saw enrichment of the FADS1positive network in astrocyte genes differentially expressed between non-human primates (gorilla and chimpanzee) and humans (Fig. 4D). Together these data suggest the FADS1positive network is associated with depression-relevant behaviours across species, but there are also likely important human-specific features of the network.

We next asked whether the expression of FADS1positive network genes was different in human post-mortem MDD samples compared to controls. We thus used snRNA-seq data from the DLPFC of control and MDD samples13 to investigate astrocyte-specific expression of the FADS1positive network. We generated a module score and aggregated it to the sample level before comparing across groups, which showed a significant decrease in expression of the FADS1positive network in MDD (Fig. 4E; p = 0.018; Wilcoxon test). We next used a bootstrapping approach with 10,000 iterations to assess the composition of the FADS1positive network in bulk RNA-seq data from the DLPFC of control and MDD samples36. We found a striking decrease in the number of co-expressed genes in MDD compared to a sample size-matched control group (Fig. 4F; p < 2.22-16; Wilcoxon test). We could, reassuringly, reproduce this finding using a microarray dataset41 (Fig. 4G; p < 2.22-16; Wilcoxon test). FADS1 expression did not change between control and MDD samples, suggesting a complex relation between FADS1 expression and the number of genes co-expressed with FADS1 (Supplementary Fig. 9D, E).

We then used strict criteria (present in >95% of bootstrapped networks), to define co-expression networks across control and MDD groups, with a high degree of confidence. This approach allowed us to compare FADS1positive network composition across control and MDD samples. The MDD group had 54 genes co-expressed with FADS1 in more than 95% of bootstrapped networks (hereafter high-confidence co-expressed genes), with only 3 genes common with the control group (Fig. 4H). Meanwhile, the control group had 215 high-confidence co-expressed genes, again with only 3 being common with the MDD group (Fig. 4I; Supplementary Data 6). The MDD-specific co-expressed genes were enriched for GO terms related to lipoprotein function (Fig. 4J; Supplementary Data 7) while, in contrast, the control-specific genes were enriched for GO terms related to myelination and the extracellular matrix (Fig. 4K). Astrocyte fatty acid metabolism is essential for correct myelination42, and decreased myelination in the PFC of patients with MDD has previously been observed43. Together these data demonstrate the number of genes co-expressed with FADS1 decreases in MDD, which may result in a decreased capacity for myelination.

The FADS1positive network can define astrocyte states altered in MDD

Astrocytes are a heterogenous cell type with intricate communication patterns with all brain cell types. We aimed to further dissect the impact of the FADS1positive network on astrocyte function and cell interactions in MDD using a DLPFC snRNA-seq dataset from control and MDD individuals13. We extracted astrocytes from the dataset using previously defined broad cell type classes13 and clustered the astrocytes using only expression of the FADS1positive network, rather than the most variable genes as done in a typical analysis pipeline. This approach allowed us to ask whether the FADS1positive network could define astrocyte clusters and then ask if these clusters were altered in MDD. Indeed, we could identify three clusters of astrocytes using the FADS1positive network (Fig. 5A–D; Supplementary Fig. 10A, B; Supplementary Data 8). All three clusters expressed canonical astrocyte genes (Fig. 5E). Cluster 0 was enriched for GABRB1 (Gamma-Aminobutyric Acid Type A Receptor Subunit Beta 1) and the extracellular matrix regulator HPSE2 (heparanase 2; Fig. 5E). Interestingly the GABA-A receptor, a sub-unit of which is encoded by GABRB1, is the target for the recently approved anti-depressant brexnanolone44. Cluster 1 was primarily enriched for genes related to blood-brain barrier (BBB) function and GO terms related to cell adhesion, functions which have previously been associated with MDD45,46 (Fig. 5E; Supplementary Fig. 9C). We then assessed the spatial localisation of these astrocyte clusters using the human DLPFC spatial transcriptomic dataset from Maynard et al.47. Genes enriched (positive logFC and FDR p < 0.05) within each of the three astrocyte clusters (Fig. 5A), as well as genes enriched in broad classes of other canonical cell types, were used to build module scores in the spatial transcriptomic data (representative scores of the astrocyte clusters can be seen in Fig. 5F). We then correlated the spatial localisations of these scores across 12 distinct sections and calculated the mean spatial correlation between cell type scores (Fig. 5G). In line with our previous analysis, there was a strong colocalization between the Cluster 2 score and a score from excitatory neurons, suggesting a role for this subset at excitatory synapses. Cluster 1 was strongly expressed in layer I and the white matter and was highly colocalized with scores from the oligodendrocyte lineage and endothelial cells, once more suggesting a role for this subtype at the BBB. The Cluster 0 score mirrored the correlations of Cluster 1 but with weaker associations suggesting it was more functionally diffuse (Fig. 5G).

We next used Milo48 to identify differentially abundant neighbourhoods using our FADS1positive defined dimensional reductions. We observed depletion of several neighbourhoods in MDD, which were predominantly localised to Cluster 1. Differential expression analysis between the neighbourhoods depleted in MDD and non-depleted neighbourhoods showed a strong signature of BBB genes in the MDD-depleted neighbourhoods (Fig. 5I; Supplementary Fig. 10D; Supplementary Data 9). We used GSEA to test the enrichment of the FADS1positive network in these differential-expression statistics and found it highly enriched (Fig. 5J). In this analysis there was a polarisation of the genes in the FADS1positive network to both ends of the differential expression statistics, but particularly those genes enriched in MDD-depleted neighbourhoods. GO term enrichment for the genes differentially expressed between depleted and non-depleted neighbourhoods showed enrichment for synapse-related terms in the non-depleted neighbourhoods (Fig. 5K), with enrichment for GO terms related to BBB function within the markers of depleted neighbourhoods (Fig. 5L).

We replicated key aspects of these findings in an independent MDD snRNA-seq dataset from Maitra et al.14. For instance, we again identified three clusters of astrocytes using the FADS1positive network, with enrichment for similar GO terms (Supplementary Fig. 11). We also identified MDD-depleted astrocytes using Milo and these depleted neighbourhoods were also enriched for BBB-related genes (Supplementary Fig. 12). Correlation of the differential expression statistics for the astrocyte-depleted and non-depleted signatures between the two datasets showed a strong correlation (Supplementary Fig. 13; R = 0.6 and p < 2.2e-16; Pearson correlation), suggesting we are capturing a reproducible pattern of astrocyte depletion that is defined by the FADS1positive network.

Taken together, these data demonstrate the FADS1positive network is involved in diverse astrocyte functions within distinct astrocyte clusters. Further, there is a reproducible depletion of an astrocyte population associated with the BBB and the oligodendrocyte lineage in MDD.

FGF2 is a putative regulator of these altered astrocyte states on downstream synaptic function

We next asked what mechanisms might act downstream of the FADS1positive network. We reasoned that a parsimonious extension of our analysis, which has focused on genes positively correlated with FADS1, would be to investigate genes negatively correlated with FADS1 as the genes likely downstream of the FADS1positive network. The FADS1negative network numbered 388 genes, which were primarily expressed in excitatory neurons (Fig. 6A), enriched in GO terms related to synaptic function (Fig. 6B) and enriched at an uncorrected P-value threshold in several psychiatric-related GWAS (including MDD and suicide attempt; Fig. 6C). We did not observe preferential expression of the FADS1negative network in excitatory neuron subtypes, suggesting the FADS1negative network represents a general pattern of expression common to DLPFC excitatory neurons (Supplementary Fig. 14). Astrocyte and neuronal functions are intimately linked, therefore, our characterisation of the FADS1negative network led us to hypothesise the FADS1positive and FADS1negative networks may be linked through cell-cell communication. To test this, we leveraged multiple published datasets to define the subcellular enrichment of the FADS1positive and FADS1negative networks. We first used data from a mouse study that characterised the sub-cellular localisation of astrocyte proteins using an in vivo proximity labelling approach49. The FADS1positive network was enriched for several subcellular astrocyte compartments, with the strongest enrichment seen in proteins proximate to GLT1, which is localised to the astrocyte portion of the tripartite synapse and is involved in glutamate uptake (Fig. 6D).

We then used synapse-annotated genes from the synGO database50 and proteins present at the human post-synaptic density51 to determine if the FADS1negative was also enriched at the synapse. Indeed, we saw a marked enrichment of the FADS1negative network in both synaptic gene sets (Fig. 6E). Together these data suggest the FADS1positive network acts within astrocytes and affects the expression of synapse-related genes in excitatory neurons.

We then used NicheNet52 to identify putative astrocyte ligands (that were in the FADS1positive network) with the regulatory potential to affect excitatory neuron genes in the FADS1negative network. This analysis nominated FGF2 as a putative candidate to drive astrocyte-excitatory neuronal signalling in the context of FADS1 co-expressed genes (Fig. 6F). In rodents, astrocyte FGF2 is differentially modulated by both stress and anti-depressants, and FGF2 has been associated with astrocyte-neuron communication53–55. This finding also agrees with a large body of literature using both human and animal models to describe a role for FGF signalling in MDD56–58.

We then wondered if genes within the global FGF signalling pathway were differentially expressed in astrocytes of MDD samples. We used the GO term “FGF signalling pathway” to define genes involved in FGF signalling and a pseudobulk analysis with the voom-limma method to test for differential expression within our previously defined astrocyte clusters (from Fig. 5A). We observed a striking downregulation of FGF pathway genes in astrocyte Cluster 1 (which was characterised by a BBB signature and neighbourhoods depleted in MDD; Supplementary Fig. 14D; Supplementary Data 10). Out of 79 genes tested for differential expression in Cluster 1, 40 passed correction for multiple comparisons and 75 had a negative fold change. These data show profound differential expression of FGF signalling components within astrocytes associated with the BBB and the oligodendrocyte lineage in MDD and suggest a role for FGF2 in driving downstream changes in synapse-related gene expression in excitatory neurons.

PPARα as a putative therapeutic modulator in MDD

We finally sought to identify druggable therapeutic targets from our analyses (Fig. 7A). Our analyses identified two prominent and reproducible signatures that were disrupted in MDD, which represented promising signatures to therapeutically target; the 212 genes that were only co-expressed in control samples (and not MDD; Figs 4I, K), and the genes that were enriched in MDD-depleted astrocyte neighbourhoods (Fig. 5I). The DrugMatrix database is among the largest databases assaying the transcriptomic effects of various drugs and therefore, represented an ideal database in which to test for enrichment of our gene signatures59,60. This analysis revealed 622 and 481 compounds associated with co-expressed genes lost in MDD and the markers of MDD-depleted astrocytes, respectively (Fig. 7B and Supplementary Data 11 and 12). We note these analyses define agents that modulate our signatures and may therefore be pro- or anti-depressant. However, we observed enrichment for several drugs used to treat MDD (e.g. paroxetine, fluoxetine and citalopram), other psychiatric disorders (e.g. quetiapine, fluphenazine, olanzapine, clozapine, lorazepam and diazepam) and drugs that target previously postulated MDD-related mechanisms, such as inflammation (e.g. mefenamic acid), glucocorticoid signalling (e.g. hydrocortisone and dexamethasone) and sex-hormone signalling (e.g. diethylstilbestrol). We also saw enrichment for multiple agonists of PPARα (including bezafibrate, clofibrate, fenofibrate and gemfibrozil), several of which appeared among the most enriched drugs. The DrugMatrix database contains signatures across multiple cell lines and concentrations. We therefore reasoned that an association across multiple conditions would provide stronger evidence for an association. Among the most frequently associated drugs were two that target PPARα (Fenofibrate and Bezafibrate). Moreover, each of the two drugs were associated with both gene signatures (Fig. 7C). PPARα is a nuclear receptor that regulates the expression of a range of lipid-modulating genes61 and so represented an intriguing candidate. Encouragingly PPARA was also a member of the FADS1positive network (Supplementary Fig. 15A, r = 0.69; Pearson correlation), but other PPAR family members PPARD and PPARG were not co-expressed with FADS1 (Supplementary Fig. 14A, C; r = 0.02 and −0.12; Pearson correlation).

We finally used the Open Targets platform to search for genetic associations with PPARA. Surprisingly there was an MDD GWAS significant locus 88Kb from PPARA (Fig. 7D; Supplementary Data 13)62, making it the second closest gene to the locus (WNT7B was the closest at 84Kb). We could not identify any brain expression QTLs for PPARA, despite its robust expression, which negated the possibility of confirming the association using colocalization or Mendelian randomisation. There was however also evidence for PPARA as the causal gene in multiple other GWAS, including several pertaining to co-morbidities or risk factors for MDD including addiction, inflammation and body weight (Fig. 7D). Together these data support the further study of PPARα as a potential therapeutic target in MDD.

Discussion

We used multiple complementary and orthogonal analyses of genomic data, the results of which converged on a therapeutically actionable MDD mechanism. We identified a co-expression network associated with MDD that associated with fatty acid metabolism within astrocytes of the DLPFC. Integrating the FADS1positive network with snRNA-seq data identified a subpopulation of astrocytes associated with the BBB and the oligodendrocyte lineage that were decreased in MDD and defined only by variation in expression of the FADS1positive network. We then provided converging evidence from multiple, distinct approaches using bulk RNA-seq and snRNA-seq MDD data suggesting that PPARα agonists may act as modulators of MDD-relevant pathways suggesting they are putative anti-depressant agents. The diffuse definitions of MDD used in GWAS63 complicate the identification of associated mechanisms64 and new approaches are needed to confidently delineate therapeutic targets. Here, we illustrate the genomic approach of generating, characterising and integrating seeded co-expression networks across modalities is a viable strategy for the delineation of therapeutically actionable mechanisms.

Several streams of research link astrocyte alterations to MDD in humans65–67 or depressive-like behaviours in animal models45,46,68. One notable finding from these studies has been a specific disruption of astrocyte function at the BBB45,46,69,70. We observed complementary findings by clustering astrocytes based on the FADS1positive network, suggesting a role for fatty acid metabolism in astrocyte dysregulation at the BBB. Notably chronic stress in mice produces alterations in brain fatty acid metabolism71. Many fatty acids, particularly polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), are supplied to the brain via the periphery72. PUFAs are metabolised by FADS1 into various active metabolites, such as eicosapentaenoic acid, which play an important role in the regulation of myelination73. Importantly, a meta-analysis of clinical studies revealed that eicosapentaenoic acid shows anti-depressant effects74. We did not observe differential expression of FADS1 itself but instead observed downregulation of the whole FADS1positive gene network, which aligns with the systems biology perspective of considering the network itself (rather than an individual gene or protein) as the unit in which disease risk manifests4,5. Together with the previously published literature, this data implies that astrocyte fatty acid substrate availability, may mediate the effect of FADS1 on the FADS1positive network and downstream MDD phenotypes. The capacity for diet-supplied fatty acids to modulate these pathways and disease states is an outstanding question and one that is of central importance.

Our data suggest that PPARα represents a possible avenue for future therapeutic investigation in MDD, with the literature suggesting PPARα agonism as the likely desired therapeutic action. For instance, administration of palmitoylethanolamide (PEA; an endogenous PPARα ligand), or the inhibition of PEA degradation, produces anti-depressant-like effects in mice75–77. Furthermore, in a small clinical trial PEA improved the anti-depressant effect of citalopram when used as an adjuvant78. Non-endogenous PPARα agonists such as fenofibrate, WY14643 and aspirin also have a range of PPARA-dependent effects within the rodent brain including anti-depressant-like76,79,80 and anxiolytic-like effects76,81, modulation of memory81–83, altered astrocyte function81,84 and increased synaptic plasticity82,83. Furthermore, knocking out PPARA in mice results in reduced cognitive flexibility and altered synaptic gene expression85–87. Together with these data, our results provide a strong basis for the further investigation of PPARα agonists as anti-depressant agents.

The current primary clinical use of PPARα agonists is for dyslipidaemia88 and interestingly peripheral lipid alterations are commonly observed in MDD89–91. The FADS locus (includes FADS1, FADS2 and FADS3) is the most pleiotropic regulator of peripheral metabolite levels, with significant genetic associations for 79 metabolites, including 75 lipid species92. Furthermore, there is evidence to suggest that peripheral lipid dysregulation caused by the FADS locus is associated with MDD93,94. FADS1 was also nominated as an MDD risk gene within the brain in our study as well as those using a transcriptome-wide association study framework4, and we also describe MDD alterations in FADS1 co-expression networks in the DLPFC. Disentangling the peripheral and central effects of FADS1 lipid metabolism in MDD is an important future question. To fully address this question, larger cohorts and methods with improved throughput and resolution for lipid analysis that are suitable for use in post-mortem tissue will be essential.

Our study suggests a model under which the FADS1positive network has multiple functions relating to distinct astrocyte states. In MDD, there is a specific disruption of FADS1positive network function related to the BBB and the oligodendrocyte lineage, suggesting a decreased abundance of fatty acids for myelination. During MDD the predominant FADS1positive network-related function, therefore, shifts to astrocyte-neuronal communication via FGF2. The current study also comes with limitations. First, the generation of robust co-expression networks requires large sample sizes and study designs, studies such as ours, will benefit from increasing MDD cohort sizes. Second, we also relied on transcriptomic methods throughout this study. However, these methods are limited in their ability to infer events that may not be well represented at the transcriptome level, such as lipid metabolism. Emerging single-cell metabolomic methods95 promise to build upon our results. Third, there are many methods to annotate GWAS to the gene level with no accepted best approach. A more holistic understanding of the genetic architecture of disease will allow for more accurate identification of causal genes from GWAS loci and facilitate their interpretation through studies such as ours. Fourth, our findings are largely derived from observational studies and, despite grounding in genetics, unmeasured variables that correlate with MDD status could influence our findings. Finally, the findings emerging from these analyses are intended to identify plausible novel mechanisms and therapeutic targets but require validation. Furthermore, we note the network analysis we conducted did not replicate in the CMC dataset and thus must be interpreted with caution. Although we use seeded co-expression networks throughout our study, alternative approaches using WGCNA networks, like the elegant approach from Borcuk et al.96,97, are also powerful tools for mechanistic inference. However, a strength of our approach is the ability to mobilise converging evidence from multiple human data sources, in this instance to implicate fatty acid metabolism in astrocytes as a mechanism for MDD and identify PPARα as a putative therapeutic target to modulate this pathway.

Methods

The research published within this manuscript was conducted on previously collected data and therefore not subject to ethical approval related to data collection. All relevant ethical guidelines were stringently adhered to through the analysis process and access to protected data in the case of GTEx were through approval (accession number phs000424.v9.p2).

Annotation of MDD GWAS using Hi-C data with H-MAGMA

For the MDD GWAS annotation we used the largest publicly available summary statistics (Als et al.), excluding the UK Biobank and the 23 and me sample. We excluded the UK Biobank sample to eliminate the potential for bias introduced by the reuse of datasets (we use GWAS summary statistics from the UK Biobank PHQ-9 in Fig. 3 to associate co-expression networks with specific MDD symptoms). The 23 and me sample was not made publicly available and as such could not be included15. The MDD summary statistics were downloaded from iPSYCH as described in the original publication15 (https://ipsych.dk/en/research/downloads/). We then annotated these summary statistics to the gene level using Hi-C data from the DLPFC16, which was obtained from https://zenodo.org/record/6382668#.Y9PSG3bMJPY, as previously described through the H-MAGMA framework17,98,99. We used SNP annotations from the 1000 genomes European dataset and gene annotations from the NCBI website build 38. We chose H-MAGMA over MAGMA because the region-specific approach to annotating the GWAS summary statistics aligned nicely with our region-specific approach to seeded co-expression networks.

Bulk RNA sequencing quality control and normalisation

GTEx v8 data were used because of their well validated RNA extraction and sequencing methodology, large sample size and well documented technical phenotype data. Raw gene-level read counts, transcript-level expected counts, sample attributes, and sequencing metrics were downloaded from the GTEx portal and dbGaP (accession number phs000424.v9.p2). Only samples and genes passing GTEx QC were retained. Analyses were restricted to Brain – Frontal Cortex (BA9). Samples were excluded if marked ineligible or if the donor had the following: amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, Alzheimer’s or other dementia, encephalitis, influenza, multiple sclerosis, Parkinson’s disease, stroke or cerebrovascular disorder, systemic lupus, bacterial or viral sepsis, active cancer, schizophrenia, major depressive disorder, substance use (heroin or prescription drug abuse), pneumonia, unexplained seizures, or positive blood cultures. After filtering, 111 samples remained. Genes with >0.1 counts per million (CPM) in >25% of samples were retained (23,326 genes). Counts were normalised using the TMM method in edgeR v3.38.1, log₂-transformed, and filtered for low expression. Outlier samples were identified by connectivity Z-scores from biweight midcorrelations (|Z| > 3) and removed (n = 4). Thirty-two sequencing metrics (e.g., mapping rate, read quality, duplication rate, insert size) were standardised and summarised by PCA; the first ten components were used as covariates. Additional covariates included RNA integrity number (RIN), Hardy scale, refrigeration interval, post-mortem interval category, sex, and age. Expression residuals were obtained by ordinary least squares regression of normalised counts on all covariates. The resulting residualized expression matrix was used for all downstream co-expression analyses.

Labonté et al. : Data from Labonté et al.36 were downloaded from GSE102556 in FPKM form. Genes were filtered to those with an FPKM (Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads) >0.5 in 25% of samples, leaving 22,893 genes in the dataset. The effects of age, age2, pH, sex, post-mortem interval, RIN (RNA integrity number) and RIN2 were then regressed from the data.

Chang et al.: Microarray data were used as in a previous meta-analysis23. Data were normalised using the “rma” function in the affy v1.72.0 package100. Outlying samples were identified by a connectivity Z-score greater than three standard deviations and were removed. The following covariates were then regressed from the data using the “ComBat” function in the sva v3.42.0 package101; age, race, sex, post-mortem interval, pH, RIN, RNA degradation (quantified with the “AffyRNAdeg” function) and batch. Probe IDs were converted to gene names using the biomaRt package in R.

Generation of seeded co-expression networks

Co-expression networks were generated using a Pearson correlation between the MDD risk gene and all genes expressed in the dataset. As per previous studies9, a gene was said to be positively co-expressed with an R > 0.5 and an FDR P-value < 0.05, a gene was said to be negatively co-expressed with an R < −0.5 and an FDR < 0.05. The FDR was calculated for each network independently and based on the 23,326 correlations conducted. Bootstrapping was carried out using 10,000 iterations while down sampling to the smallest sample size among compared groups.

Mutational intolerance analysis

Mutation metrics were downloaded from the gnomAD database v2.1.126. We used the LOEUF (loss-of-function observed/expected upper bound fraction) score as a conservative measure of mutational intolerance, along with missense Z-score and synonymous Z-score as provided. All genes expressed in the GTEx dataset that were tested for co-expression were used as background in the analysis.

Enrichment analyses

Fisher’s Exact Test was conducted to test enrichment between various gene sets of interest as implemented in the GeneOverlap v1.30.0 package102. The number of genes from which the test gene set was generated, were used as the background in each analysis.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

GSEA was conducted using the R package fgsea v1.20.0103 using differential gene expression statistics ranked by log fold change. Where necessary rodent gene lists were converted to their human orthologues using the R biomaRt package v2.50.3104. The normalisation function of the fgsea package has been reported as unstable with gene sets larger than 500, therefore, we used the 500 genes with the strongest correlation co-efficient with FADS1 in the analysis. Of note, a similar analysis with the full complement of FADS1 correlated genes produced similar results.

Internal and external validation

Internal validation was conducted using the GTEx “Brain_cortex” data, which is considered a technical replicate of the “Brain_frontal_cortex_BA9” dataset that forms the basis of the majority of our analyses. The “Brain_cortex” data underwent dissection and processing using a different protocol and at a different site to the “Brain_frontal_cortex_BA9” dataset, but both datasets contain overlapping individuals. These data were downloaded and analysed in an identical manner using the same covariates as the “Brain_frontal_cortex_BA9” analysis.

External validation was done using the phase 1 data from the DLPFC of the common mind consortium as described previously23. Data were filtered and normalised in an identical way to the GTEx data, leaving 26,599 genes expressed at a CPM > 0.1 in more than 25% of samples. These genes were normalised using the TMM method and log normalisation. Outliers with a connectivity score more than 3 standard deviations were removed. The effect of covariates was regressed from the data (covariates: Age, Age2, Batch, pH, Sex, PMI_hrs, RIN, RIN2 and the first 10 sequencing principal components). Co-expression networks were then generated in an identical way to the GTEx data.

GWAS enrichment

We conducted GWAS enrichment using MAGMA v1.1098, with GWAS annotated to the gene level using H-MAGMA17. We used SNP annotations from the 1000 genomes European dataset and gene annotations from the NCBI website build 38. We annotated GWAS using Hi-C data from the DLPFC16, which was obtained from https://zenodo.org/record/6382668#.Y9PSG3bMJPY.

For the PHQ9 analysis, the beta coefficients from the regression (produced by the gene-set analysis function in MAGMA) were scaled for each network and hierarchically clustered using the pheatmap v1.0.12 package105. The dendrograms were then cut to form three clusters of networks.

For details of GWAS summary statistics used and their sources see Supplementary Data 14.

Gene ontology (GO) analysis

We conducted GO analysis using the clusterProfiler v4.2.2 package106, with all genes expressed in the relevant dataset used as background in the analysis. We ran enrichment for biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions in a single analysis. For Fig. 3B-3D, significant terms were imported into Cytoscape v3.8.0107, where similar terms were clustered and a word cloud was used to suggest words common to all terms within a given cluster. We then manually refined these terms for brevity and clarity.

Network analysis

Network analysis on co-expression networks was conducted with igraph v1.5.1108. To generate the adjacency matrix, we first identified the co-expression networks with more than 1000 positively co-expressed genes (defined as the number of genes with an FDR < 0.05 and R > 0.5). Then for these networks we correlated the coefficients of all genes, with those from the other networks. Specifically, the correlation matrix was generated from an input matrix containing 23,326 rows (the number of genes against which co-expression was tested) and 14 columns (the number of networks with more than 1000 genes positively co-expressed). The adjacency matrix was then constructed from this correlation matrix by thresholding the presence or absence of a correlation. Edge thickness was set to represent the weight between nodes. The layout was automatically determined using the Fruchterman-Reingold layout algorithm. The betweenness was then calculated on this adjacency network using the “betweenness” function within igraph.

Protein-protein interactions

Estimates of protein-protein interactions were made using STRINGdb37 using standard settings. Protein-protein interaction networks were then exported to Cytoscape v3.8.0107 for plotting.

Sparse partial least squares (sPLS)

A sparse partial least squares (sPLS) analysis was performed using the mixOmics package (v6.18.1) to derive a latent variable capturing the expression pattern of FADS1 in the GTEx Brain Frontal Cortex (BA9) dataset. Expression data were preprocessed as previously described. The predictor matrix (X) included all genes except FADS1, and the response vector (y) contained normalised FADS1 expression values. An sPLS model with one component and 1183 selected genes (keepX = 1183) was fit to mirror the number of genes coexpressed with FADS1 (FDR < 0.05, R > 0.5) identified in our seeded coexpression network. The latent variable was sign-adjusted to ensure a positive correlation with FADS1 expression. The latent was highly correlated with FADS1 expression r = 0.86 and p < 2.2e-16.

Single cell expression of networks

We downloaded the Nagy et al. raw data from GSE144136 and used the cell type assignations from the original study, but grouped them to form broad cell classes. We excluded nuclei expressing fewer than 700 and greater than 7000 genes, before normalisation with the “NormalizeData” function. Data were then split and integrated by batch using Seurat v4.3.0109 and the standard Seurat pipeline before using the “AddModuleScore” function to identify cell type specific expression patterns.

To compare module scores between control and MDD, module scores were aggregated by sample and condition before comparison with a Wilcoxon test.

Analysis of snRNA-seq data

We used the Nagy et al. and Maitra et al. data as they are the largest published snRNA-seq datasets for MDD. To cluster astrocytes based on variation in expression of genes within the FADS1positive network we started with the Nagy et al. raw data. We subset this data to nuclei annotated as astrocytes and removed two batches as they had too few nuclei to perform integration (batches 1M and 2M). We then subset the count matrix to genes within the FADS1positive network and ran the standard Seurat integration and analysis pipeline as previously described. We used the first 10 principal components and a resolution of 0.2 to generate clusters and a UMAP dimensional reduction of the nuclei. We then extracted the metadata with the FADS1positive based clusters and combined it with the full astrocyte count matrix to perform downstream analysis, such as identification of cluster marker genes, differential expression and differential abundance.

Maitra et al. dataset: We downloaded the Maitra et al.14 data from GSE213982 and analysed the data in an identical pipeline to the Nagy et al. dataset. We used the first 10 principal components and a resolution of 0.1 to generate clusters and a UMAP dimensional reduction of the nuclei.

Differential abundance and gene expression testing with miloR

We conducted differential abundance testing between control and MDD cells using the miloR v1.2.0 package48. We started with our FADS1positive clustered object containing the full count matrix and converted it to a Single Cell Experiment object and subsequently to a Milo object. We used the following parameters for the analysis; k = 20, d = 20 and prop = 0.2. We then tested neighbourhoods for differential abundance using batch and age as covariates. Differentially abundant neighbourhoods were defined as those with a spatial FDR < 0.1. The differentially abundant neighbourhoods with a negative log fold change (i.e. decreased in MDD), were tested for marker genes by comparison with all other neighbourhoods using log normalised counts with the “findNhoodGroupMarkers” function in miloR.

Spatial analysis of module scores

We downloaded 10X Visium data from Maynard et al. (samples: Br2743, Br2720, Br3942, Br6432, Br6471, Br6522, Br8325, Br8492, Br8667)47. We normalised data using the Seurat pipeline and the “SCTransform” function. We created a module score using the “AddModuleScore” function. This score was created using all genes enriched in broad cell types or the FADS1positive defined astrocyte clusters. Marker genes were defined as those with a positive logFC and an FDR P-value < 0.05. Correlations between cell types were generated independently for all slices. We then averaged the correlation coefficients of all slices to obtain the final correlation matrix for plotting.

Differential gene expression in the FGF signalling pathway

To test for differential gene expression within the FGF pathway we used genes annotated to the GO term “fibroblast growth factor receptor signalling pathway” (GO:0008543). The astrocyte count matrix were subset to these genes and differential analysis run using pseudobulk counts with the limma-voom method implemented within the Libra R package, which has been shown to be among the best performers in single cell differential expression testing110.

Drug analysis

We conducted drug enrichment analysis using the DrugMatrix databases and the iLINCS software60. We used the online interface provided and standard settings to identify patterns of association (Supplementary Data 11 and 12). Results were returned in the form of absolute Z-score. We aggregated the significant results by perturbagen to identify targets associated with multiple concentrations or cell lines.

Open targets

We used the Open Targets Genetics platform111 to search for GWAS hits associated with PPARA. We conducted our search in December 2022. We manually annotated the results into broad groups to facilitate interpretation. We provide the data using the Open Targets L2G (locus-to-gene) score, which is analogous to a probability score based on a machine learning pipeline trained to identify likely causal genes from significant GWAS hits. The L2G score can be interpreted as probability the gene is the target of the causal locus under the assumption the GWAS is similar to those included in the training set.

Statistical analysis

All analysis unless otherwise stated were carried out using R v4.1.1112 and Rstudio v1.4.1717113. GWAS enrichment was conducted using the MAGMA package. All package versions are specified in their relevant methods section. Plots were generated using either the packages mentioned throughout the methods section or in ggplot2 v3.4.025,113,114. Plots were combined into figures using Inkscape v1.0. The Benjamini-Hochberg method (False Discovery Rate; FDR) was used to correct for multiple comparisons where appropriate, except for annotation of the MDD GWAS where a Bonferroni correction was applied. The relevant statistical tests used are described throughout the results section and the figure legends.
