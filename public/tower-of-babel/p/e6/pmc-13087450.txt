Refining the Pathophysiology of Access-Related Hand Dysfunction: Key Methodological Directions
Authors: Guirong Hu, Sirui Xiong, Xiaoyu Yu
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

We commend Kim et al.1 for their insightful study linking access-related hand dysfunction to skeletal muscle atrophy following hemodialysis access placement. Although their findings advance our understanding of access-related hand dysfunction, we wish to highlight 3 methodological limitations that may refine the interpretation of their mechanistic conclusions.

First, the study design overlooks the systemic hemodynamic impact of access type. With 46.2% of patients receiving brachiocephalic arteriovenous fistulae at the upper arm, which carry a significant risk for developing high-flow states (commonly defined as > 1500–2000 ml/min), the potential for inducing cardiac overload and systemic hypoperfusion is substantial. This is well-established, because high access flow can lead to increased cardiac output demand and a “steal” phenomenon, diverting blood flow from systemic and cerebral circulation.2 Consequently, the observed grip strength decline and maladaptive mitochondrial response may reflect a systemic compromise rather than local muscle pathology. This critical omission may steer clinical management away from essential diagnostic assessments (e.g., access flow surveillance) and effective interventions like flow reduction surgery.

Second, the mitochondrial analysis is incomplete. The finding of increased citrate synthase activity without improved respiration was interpreted as insufficient biogenesis. However, in chronic kidney disease or end-stage kidney disease, impaired mitophagy and defective quality control are well-documented.3 The reported “increased mitochondrial content” likely represents accumulated dysfunctional mitochondria (not de novo biogenesis), which could explain the lack of functional improvement. Targeting biogenesis could worsen outcomes, whereas strategies aimed at enhancing mitophagy might be more appropriate.

Third, the reliance on bulk RNA-sequencing limits cell-type resolution. This technique provides only average gene-expression profiles, inherently obscuring tissue heterogeneity and the contribution of individual cell types.4 The reported upregulation of myogenic genes is attributed to muscle fibers; however, postsurgical muscle infiltration by macrophages could confound this signal.5 Consequently, the derived molecular mechanisms may misidentify the effector cell population, and any resulting myofiber-targeted therapeutic strategies risk being misdirected, potentially overlooking key immune-modulatory pathways in the muscle microenvironment.

Taken together, future studies should incorporate access flow measurements, assess mitochondrial quality control, and employ spatial or single-cell transcriptomics. These approaches are essential to accurately elucidate the pathophysiology of access-related hand dysfunction and developing effective, targeted therapeutic strategies.
