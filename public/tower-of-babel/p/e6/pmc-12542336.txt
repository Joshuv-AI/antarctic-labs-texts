Factors influencing heart rate variability in nurses following night shifts: a prospective observational clinical study
Authors: Taihe Zhan, Ziying Zhang, Zhimin Shi, Hongyan Xie, Daogang Zha, Xiumei Wei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHeart rate variability (HRV) has been established as a key indicator of autonomic nervous system activity, reflecting both sympathetic and parasympathetic modulation. This study was aimed at investigating the factors that influence HRV in nurses following night shifts.MethodsA prospective, observational, single-center study was conducted in the office corridor of a tertiary hospital in southern China. Thirty-five female nurses (median age: 27.0 years) working ward shifts were enrolled; all the participants were free of severe cardiopulmonary diseases and mobility limitations. Nurses completed their routine night shifts, and electrocardiograms (ECGs) were recorded for six minutes during a standing break on the mornings of the first and second days after their shifts. The study sought to identify the factors that influenced the primary outcome, i.e., the HRV after night shifts. The study also assessed the factors that affected a secondary outcome, i.e., the HRV after a one-day recovery period.ResultsAge and the frequency of awakenings in the 24 h preceding a night shift were inversely associated with low-frequency (LF) power after the night shift. Additionally, age was an independent predictor of LF power on the first morning after the night shift. On the other hand, the total catch-up sleep duration within 24 h after a night shift was negatively correlated with the HRV the second morning following the night shift.ConclusionsOlder nurses and those experiencing more frequent awakenings before their night shifts exhibited lower LF power on the first morning after night duty, indicating reduced autonomic activity under the measured conditions. A longer duration of catch-up sleep within 24 h after a night shift was associated with lower HRV values on the second morning, reflecting decreased autonomic activity. These findings highlight potential relationships between pre- and post-shift sleep adjustments and post-shift HRV.Trial registrationThis study was retrospectively registered with Clinicaltrials.gov. Registration Date: August 1, 2024. Clinicaltrials.gov ID: NCT06542510.Keywords: Heart rate variability, Night shift, Sleep, Autonomic nervous system, Nurses

Introduction

Shift work, particularly that involving night-shift schedules, disrupts circadian rhythms and sleep–wake patterns, leading to a range of adverse health outcomes. Night-shift-induced sleep deprivation has been reported to increase the risk of various health conditions, including gastrointestinal dysfunction, metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular disease, mental health disorders, and cancer [1, 2]. Additionally, nurses working night shifts often experience fatigue and excessive sleepiness, which not only impair their performance but also increase the risk of medical errors [3, 4]. Owing to the nature of hospital operations, nurses must provide continuous medical care around the clock, making shift work unavoidable. To mitigate the adverse effects of night shifts and optimize their nocturnal performance, nurses commonly adopt strategies such as relaxation techniques, physical activity, healthy dietary habits, and pre- and post-shift sleep adjustments [5].

In addition to the long-term consequences of chronic night-shift exposure, such as diabetes, obesity, and hypertension [6], and its reported inverse association with long-term cardiovascular health [7], there are also short-term physiological effects. These include low-grade systemic inflammation, with higher levels of biomarkers such as serum interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-10 (IL-10), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), hypersensitive C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), and lipopolysaccharide-binding protein and higher leukocyte counts than those in daytime workers [8, 9]. Short-term autonomic changes have also been documented; for example, Min-Huey Chung et al. (2009) reported greater sympathetic activity during sleep in night-shift nurses than in their day-shift counterparts [10].

Heart rate variability (HRV), which is a noninvasive physiological marker that reflects autonomic nervous system (ANS) regulation, specifically the dynamic balance between sympathetic and parasympathetic (vagal) influences on cardiac function, has been widely used in clinical research on mental health disorders, sleep disturbances, hypertension, coronary heart disease and other diseases [11–13]. Lower HRV generally indicates reduced parasympathetic (vagal) activity or heightened sympathetic dominance, both of which have been linked to increased risks of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality [14, 15]. Vagal inhibition—a reduction in parasympathetic outflow—can occur in response to physiological or psychological stressors, including circadian disruption due to night-shift work [16, 17]. HRV analysis allows researchers to assess these shifts in autonomic balance quantitatively, providing insights into the physiological cost of night-shift work.

Previous research, including our own, has used HRV to examine autonomic responses in night-shift nurses [16]. Our previous study revealed increased cardiac autonomic nervous system activity in nurses on the first morning after a night shift, as reflected by their elevated HRV indices [16]. Furthermore, Our previous studies also employed HRV as an investigative tool, revealing stronger vagal inhibition in nurses performing a six-minute walk test on the first morning after a night shift [16]. Other studies have similarly used HRV to assess interventions, such as the sympathetic modulation effects of Saam acupuncture in night-shift nurses [18].

Our previous study showed that nurses had significantly lower HRV in the morning before a night shift than on the first morning after the shift, as detailed in the supplementary material associated with our previous publication [16]. Despite these findings, the relationship between pre- and post-shift behavioral adjustments—particularly sleep patterns—and HRV changes after night shifts remains underexplored. In a subsequent investigation, we collected HRV data from night-shift nurses who stood in a fasting state for six minutes on the first and second mornings after a night shift. Additionally, we recorded their pre- and post-shift adjustments, including sleep patterns.

The present study was aimed at exploring the influence of nurses’ pre- and post-shift adjustments—particularly their sleep patterns—on their HRV after night shifts, thereby contributing to a better understanding of strategies that may mitigate the physiological strain of night-shift work.

Methods

Study design and participants

This prospective observational clinical study included 35 female nurses (median age: 27.0 years) working ward shifts at Nanfang Hospital. Demographic data, pre-night-shift sleep patterns within the 24 h preceding a night shift (including sleep duration in hours, the frequency of awakenings, and sleep quality rated on a 0–10 visual analogue scale), workload intensity (rated on a 0–10 visual analogue scale), and post-night-shift recovery sleep within the 24 h following a night shift (including sleep duration in hours, frequency of awakenings, and sleep quality on a 0–10 visual analogue scale) were collected. Additionally, HRV was assessed during a quiet standing period after night shifts. Correlation analyses were conducted to explore the factors associated with HRV after night shifts.

Eligible participants included healthy female nurses aged ≥ 18 years who were free of severe cardiopulmonary disease and mobility limitations and were willing to participate in this study. The exclusion criteria were as follows.

Participation in activities known to influence HRV (e.g., sexual activity) within 24 h before or after a scheduled night shift;

Participation in activities known to influence HRV (e.g., sexual activity) within 24 h before or after a scheduled night shift;

Presence of diagnosed health conditions affecting cardiac autonomic function, such as thyroid disorders, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, or coronary heart disease;

Presence of diagnosed health conditions affecting cardiac autonomic function, such as thyroid disorders, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, or coronary heart disease;

Recent onset (within one week) of acute illnesses, including upper respiratory tract infection, fever, cough, diarrhea, or similar conditions;

Recent onset (within one week) of acute illnesses, including upper respiratory tract infection, fever, cough, diarrhea, or similar conditions;

Intake of caffeine-containing beverages (e.g., coffee, strong tea), tobacco, alcohol, or other stimulants, as well as use of β-blockers, sedatives, psychotropic drugs, or medications known to affect heart rate, within three days prior to study participation;

Intake of caffeine-containing beverages (e.g., coffee, strong tea), tobacco, alcohol, or other stimulants, as well as use of β-blockers, sedatives, psychotropic drugs, or medications known to affect heart rate, within three days prior to study participation;

Current pregnancy or having dependent children at home requiring night-time care; and.

Current pregnancy or having dependent children at home requiring night-time care; and.

Judgement by the research team that the individual was unsuitable for participation.

Nurses who were interested in participating were initially informed about the eligibility criteria through a recruitment group. Research physicians then conducted a detailed screening process to confirm their eligibility. The study procedures were thoroughly explained, and the participants were scheduled accordingly. Written informed consent was obtained before initiating the study.

Data collection and analysis

During the CRF data collection process, the following information was recorded: the age, body mass index (BMI), years of night shift experience, night-shift frequency, pre-night-shift sleep patterns, night-shift workload intensity, and post-night-shift recovery sleep of each participant.

ECG recordings were obtained using the Diagnostic Monitoring Software (DMS) of CardioScan Holter System 300–4 A recorders. An ECG segment was extracted from the last 6 min of the 10-minute quiet standing period. The dynamic ECG data were analyzed using the DMS of CardioScan 12.0 (DM Software, Stateline, NV, USA). The ECG analyst manually reviewed R-wave markers to correct misidentified or missed R-wave events, after which the software automatically calculated HRV indices. These indices included the following.

SDNN (the standard deviation of normal-to-normal (R–R) intervals).

RMSSD (the root mean square of successive differences).

pNN50 (the percentage of adjacent intervals differing by > 50 ms).

TP (total power, 0.000 to 0.500 Hz).

VLF (very low frequency, 0.003 to 0.040 Hz).

LF (low frequency, 0.040 to 0.150 Hz).

HF (high frequency, 0.150 to 0.401 Hz).

LF/HF ratio.

One researcher recorded the HRV data on a CRF, while a second researcher independently verified their accuracy.

Clinical study outcomes

HRV data were collected from the final 6 min of the 10-minute quiet standing period on both the first and second mornings after the night shift. The HRV parameters were then correlated with the relevant adjustment factors before and after the night shift. Pre-shift adjustments referred to modifications in total sleep time, including daytime sleep on the day preceding the night shift; evaluated total sleep duration; subjective sleep quality; and frequency of awakenings. Post-shift adjustments referred to modifications in total sleep time, especially daytime recovery sleep on the day following the night shift, evaluated using the same parameters. In accordance with institutional policy, nurses were off duty during the daytime preceding and following a night shift.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables are reported as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs). Given the nonnormal distributions of most HRV indices, Spearman’s correlation analysis was used to assess the relationships among pre-night-shift adjustments, post-night-shift sleep, and HRV. A multiple regression analysis was subsequently conducted to identify the factors that influenced the HRV parameters. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement

No patients or members of the public were involved in the design, recruitment, or conduct of this research.

Correlation analysis between the clinical characteristics and HRV parameters

The results of a correlation analysis between the clinical variables—such as sleep patterns—and HRV parameters after the night shifts are presented in Tables 2 and 3, with Spearman correlation scatter plots illustrated in Fig. 1. Our analysis revealed that age and the number of sleep awakenings within 24 h before a night shift were weakly negatively correlated with the LF power on the first morning after the night shift (r = -0.341, p = 0.045 and r = -0.359, p = 0.034, respectively). Additionally, weak negative correlations were observed between the catch-up sleep duration within 24 h after a night shift and HRV indices—including the SDNN, the RMSSD, pNN50, TP, VLF, LF, and HF—on the second morning after the night shift. Furthermore, our results suggested a weak positive correlation between the night-shift frequency and the LF/HF ratio on the first morning after the night shift, although this association did not reach statistical significance (r = 0.328, p = 0.055).

Therefore, a multiple linear regression analysis was performed by using the LF power on the first morning after a night shift as the dependent variable and age, BMI, pre-night-shift sleep characteristics, and night-shift workload intensity as independent variables.

This study identified age as a significant predictor of the LF power on the first morning after a night shift. Additionally, the number of pre-night-shift awakenings showed a nearly significant negative effect (p = 0.068). Discussion

This real-world study involved investigating the impacts of nurses’ pre- and post-night shift adjustments on HRV. Our findings reveal that both age and the number of sleep awakenings before a night shift are inversely correlated with the LF power on the first morning after night duty. Notably, age emerged as a significant negative predictor of the LF on the first morning after the night shift. Additionally, the interval between successive night shifts exhibited a weak positive correlation with the LF/HF ratio on the first morning after night duty. Furthermore, an inverse relationship was observed between the total duration of recovery sleep within 24 h after a night shift and the HRV the following morning.

HRV, which is a measure of the beat-to-beat variability exhibited by the heart rate, reflects the dynamic interplay between the sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous systems [19]. Higher HRV indicates efficient autonomic function and good adaptation, whereas lower HRV often reflects inadequate autonomic adaptation and poor physiological functioning [20]. A previous study suggested that the SDNN mainly reflects sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous system activity, which is highly correlated with VLF, LF, and TP, whereas the RMSSD and pNN50 are closely related to parasympathetic activity [21]. HF has been reported to reflect parasympathetic and vagus nerve activity [22]. While LF power is generally considered an indicator of both sympathetic and vagal activity, the LF/HF ratio represents sympathovagal balance [21]. Given that the HRV recordings in our study were obtained while participants were standing quietly, LF power predominantly reflected vagal modulation, as reported by Yokobori et al. [23].

Our previous study demonstrated that nurses exhibited heightened autonomic nervous activity immediately after completing a night shift. By the following morning, their autonomic activity had returned to a relatively lower level, approaching the baseline period. On the morning prior to the subsequent night shift (baseline period), autonomic activity remained at this relatively low level [16]. These findings suggest that older nurses or those experiencing more frequent sleep disturbances in the 24 h preceding a night shift exhibit lower autonomic nervous system (ANS) activity on the first morning after completing their night shift. Conversely, a longer night-shift interval may facilitate autonomic adaptation. Moreover, with an increase in the recovery sleep duration within 24 h after the shift, the ANS activity gradually returned to the baseline from the heightened state induced by night-shift work.

In our cohort, the LF (a heart rate variability metric) on the first morning after a night shift was inversely associated with age. This finding is consistent with those of previous studies reporting an age-related decline in HRV [24, 25], indicating reduced autonomic regulation in older nurses. However, our findings indicate that the number of years engaged in night-shift work is not significantly associated with the HRV observed during the first morning after night duty. This aligns with the results of Barckhan et al., who reported that long-term night-shift work may not exert a substantial effect on HRV [26]. Similarly, our study revealed no significant association between BMI and HRV, which appears to contradict the findings of Wang et al., who suggested that a higher BMI is linked to a lower HRV [27]. This discrepancy may be attributed to the fact that the majority of our study population had normal BMIs and were generally healthy. Additionally, Skornyakov et al. demonstrated that HRV is influenced by sleep, physical activity, and other lifestyle factors, whereas endogenous circadian rhythms exert only a relatively weak effect [28]. This may explain why our study did not identify a significant relationship between night-shift intensity and HRV on the first morning after a night shift, as the subjects who experienced different work intensities were all awake throughout the night.

Our previous study revealed that ANS activity increased after a night shift in comparison with pre-night-shift levels and gradually returned to the baseline after a 24-hour recovery period [16]. A previous study revealed no difference in the 24-hour total autonomic activity levels between night-shift nurses and day-shift nurses [29]. In line with our findings, Guo et al. reported a weak association between sleep disturbances and waking LF power [30]. Sleep disturbances may lead to fragmented sleep and excessive autonomic demand, resulting in diminished ANS activity after night shifts. Moreover, prior research has suggested that while the sleep duration does not directly impact cardiac autonomic regulation, sleep quality plays a significant role, as reflected in HRV [31]. This finding is consistent with our results, which indicate no clear association between the sleep duration before night shifts and the HRV after night shifts. Thus, an extended sleep duration may not be essential for night-shift preparation, whereas the minimization of sleep interruptions before night shifts could be crucial for maintaining ANS functioning.

A certain amount of catch-up sleep appears to be necessary after a night shift. Some studies have shown that after a period of sleep deprivation, HRV indicators such as HF, the RMSSD, and pNN50 decrease during recovery sleep [32–34]. Our study revealed that as the catch-up sleep duration increased, the HRV on the second morning after the night shift decreased compared with that on the previous morning, suggesting a transition from a heightened autonomic state toward a more regulated balance. However, no significant relationship was detected between sleep quality or the number of awakenings during catch-up sleep and the HRV on the second morning after the night shift.

Older ages or higher pre-night-shift sleep awakening frequencies in nurses are associated with poor adaptation to night-shift stress, which is characterized by decreased autonomic activity after the shift. Post-shift recovery sleep can improve this hyperstress state, as evidenced by the decreased autonomic activity on the second morning after the night shift. From the perspective of HRV regulation after night shifts, our findings suggest that older age may be a limiting factor for night-shift work. Reducing sleep disturbances before night shifts and ensuring adequate catch-up sleep afterward may be beneficial for achieving autonomic function recovery. While a correlation analysis does not establish causality, the temporal relationships observed in our study suggest potential causal links that warrant further investigation. These findings provide valuable insights for developing shift management strategies, particularly regarding pre- and post-night shift interventions. From an occupational health perspective, HRV monitoring may serve as a non-invasive tool for the early identification of shift workers at elevated cardiovascular risk, particularly those exhibiting persistently low HRV following night duty. Integrating routine HRV assessment into workplace health surveillance programs could enable targeted interventions, such as individualized shift scheduling or tailored recovery strategies, to mitigate adverse cardiovascular outcomes in vulnerable staff. However, whether HRV can be used to screen for long-term cardiovascular risk in shift workers remains uncertain. Our study did not include longitudinal follow-up to assess cardiovascular outcomes, and the HRV measurements were obtained at a specific time point—on the morning after a night shift—rather than across multiple contexts. Therefore, while our findings provide insight into short-term autonomic changes related to night-shift work, further prospective studies with extended follow-up and repeated HRV assessments are needed to clarify its predictive value for cardiovascular risk.

This study has inherent limitations that are typical of observational research, including its exploratory nature and relatively low level of evidence. In addition, sleep-related variables were assessed solely through self-reported questionnaires, without the use of objective measures such as actigraphy, which may reduce measurement precision. The timing of HRV measurement could not be standardized to an exact clock time relative to the night shift, potentially introducing additional variability. Moreover, certain potentially confounding factors, such as menstrual cycle phase and individual fitness level, were not collected, and these variables may have influenced the HRV outcomes. The comparator HRV data, including pre-night-shift HRV values, were reported in the supplementary materials of our previously published study [16]. To prevent redundancy and ensure compliance with publication ethics, these baseline data are not reproduced here; readers are referred to the cited reference for the full details [16]. Nevertheless, our results offer informative perspectives on the importance of optimizing sleep conditions before and after night shifts. Ensuring a sleep-friendly environment that minimizes disruptions before shifts and promotes sufficient catch-up sleep after shifts may be crucial for maintaining ANS stability. In practical terms, optimizing pre- and post-night-shift sleep conditions could be incorporated into institutional fatigue management protocols. For example, scheduling patterns that allow longer intervals between night shifts—especially for older nurses or those prone to fragmented pre-shift sleep—may facilitate autonomic recovery. Post-shift interventions, such as providing dedicated quiet rest areas and ensuring sufficient time for supplementary sleep, could further support autonomic balance and help reduce long-term health risks. Our study offers insights into the factors that influence HRV before and after night shifts and provides a foundation for further research aimed at mitigating the adverse effects of night-shift work.

Conclusions

Our findings indicate that, under the specific measurement conditions in this study, older nurses and those with more frequent pre-shift awakenings tend to exhibit lower LF power on the morning following a night shift. Longer catch-up sleep duration after night duty was associated with lower HRV on the second morning, which may reflect a return toward baseline autonomic activity. However, the optimal duration of catch-up sleep remains uncertain and warrants further investigation.
