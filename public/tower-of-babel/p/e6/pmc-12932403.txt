Determining patient-oncologist cancer treatment intent concordance using systematic screening in a supportive care center
Authors: Kayley M. Ancy, Sujin Ann-Yi, Yi Huang, Clark R. Andersen, Yvonne Heung, Grecia Aldana Singleton, Eduardo Bruera, David Hui, Donna S. Zhukovsky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeAccurate illness understanding is a necessary component of goal-concordant care. Few prior studies have investigated systematic screening for illness understanding. The objectives of this study were to determine the extent of agreement between patient-reported and oncologist-documented cancer treatment intent using systematic screening and to explore the association between such agreement and patient characteristics.MethodsWe implemented illness understanding screening for patients who presented to our supportive care center (SCC). We compared patient responses regarding chance of cure and primary treatment goal on a survey administered at their initial consultation visit with the oncologist-documented treatment intent in the electronic health record.ResultsThe analysis included 300 patients (mean age, 59 years); most were women (56%), were White (80.3%), and had advanced disease (78%). Regarding concordance, 152 (50.7%) patients had concordant treatment intent (cure, n = 64; non-cure, n = 88), and 148 (49.3%) patients had discordant treatment intent, with 98.6% (n = 146) of patients with discordant treatment intent inaccurately reporting a treatment intent of cure. Patients with discordant treatment intent were more likely to be Black (OR = 3.13, p = 0.02), have a primary cancer site of “other” (mostly melanoma and sarcoma) (OR = 2.30, p = 0.04), have advanced disease (OR = 6.25, p < 0.001), and report lower spiritual pain (OR = 0.88, p = 0.04).ConclusionThe rate of inaccuracy in patient-reported curability perception and primary treatment goal at our SCC is high, supporting the need for systematic screening and communication interventions to improve illness understanding and provide goal-concordant care.

Introduction

Accurate illness understanding is important for patients with cancer to make informed decisions and appropriately plan for their future. Such understanding is linked to patient preferences regarding cancer-directed therapies and less aggressive care near the end of life [1, 2]. However, cancer patients and their oncologists often have discordant perceptions of the intent of treatment. For example, studies have consistently shown that large proportions of patients with advanced cancer report that their disease is possibly curable, even when their oncologists have a non-curative treatment intent [3–10]. Smaller rates of such discordance have been reported for patients whose oncologists have a curative treatment intent, but at least two studies have shown that, under certain circumstances, considerable proportions of such patients may report an erroneous understanding of their oncologists’ intent, with discordance rates of 4.9% and 33%, respectively [11, 12]. There are many proposed reasons for high levels of discordance, including unclear or inaccurate provider communication, limited discussion of this sensitive topic, and inadequate time during encounters for consistent dialogue [13]. Prompting discussions of illness understanding and goals of care can result in improved clinical outcomes [14–16], but misunderstanding often persists [17].

Screening may be a unique approach that allows for the identification of patients with the greatest need for understanding of their illness who may benefit from more support. Screening has been used to improve other outcomes; for example, screening with the Edmonton Symptom Assessment System (ESAS) has improved symptom burden and is now routinely used in oncology settings to provide higher quality patient care [18]. Our team implemented systematic screening of two components of illness understanding in our supportive care center (SCC) using the validated Prognosis and Treatment Perception Questionnaire [4]. To our knowledge, there has been only one group that has previously investigated illness understanding screening in the setting of cancer care. These studies found that illness understanding screening had a high completion rate and was well-received by both patients and providers [19, 20].

The primary objective of this study was to determine the agreement between patient-reported and oncologist-documented cancer treatment intent using systematic screening of two components of patients’ illness understanding. The secondary objective was to explore the association of this agreement with patient characteristics. If results from illness understanding screening are consistent with previously published non-screening studies, this will provide the basis for future studies utilizing screening as an intervention.

Methods

Study design

We completed a retrospective chart review of patients who presented to the SCC at The University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center between July 1, 2023, and October 1, 2023. This period was chosen to allow adequate run-in time after the initial implementation of screening in April 2022. This study was approved by the institutional review board at MD Anderson, and a waiver of informed consent was granted. Consecutive patients who presented to the SCC for an initial consultation visit with a physician during the study period were screened for eligibility. Eligible patients were at least 18 years old, had completed a virtual initial consultation at the SCC, had a diagnosis of malignancy, and had received active treatment within the preceding month (as reported by question 1 of the screening). Ineligible patients had no evidence of active cancer (defined as having no evidence of disease for at least the last year), had incomplete illness understanding screening (defined as not completing either question 2 or 3 of the screening), and had no oncologist-documented treatment intent available at the time of SCC consultation.

Systematic screening

All patients who present to the SCC are prompted to complete an electronic three-question screening survey prior to a visit no more often than every 2 months. Approximately 80% of SCC visits are completed virtually, and all patients are prompted to complete the screening survey and other routine measures, including ESAS.

Question 1 of the screening asks about the patient’s current cancer treatment: “What cancer treatment(s) is/are you currently receiving (check all that apply)?” Answer choices are “Drug therapy (e.g., chemotherapy, targeted therapy or immunotherapy),” “Radiation,” “Surgery,” and “No treatment in the past month.” The remaining two questions are taken from the Prognosis and Treatment Perception Questionnaire [4]. Question 2 asks about the patient’s primary treatment goal: “If you had to choose one, what would you say is your primary goal of your current cancer treatment? Many of these goals may be important to you, but please check the one goal that you feel is most important to you right now.” Answer choices are “To lessen my suffering as much as possible,” “For me and/or my family to be able to keep hoping,” “To make sure I have done everything,” “To extend my life as long as possible,” “To cure my cancer,” “To help cancer research,” and “Other,” which includes the opportunity to provide a free-text answer. Question 3 asks about perception of curability: “How likely do you think it is that you will be cured of cancer? Please check one.” Answer choices are “No chance (0% chance of cure),” “Very unlikely (less than 10% chance of cure),” “Unlikely (10–24% chance of cure),” “Somewhat likely (25–49% chance of cure),” “Moderately likely (50–74% chance of cure),” “Very likely (75–90% chance of cure),” or “Extremely likely (more than 90% chance of cure).”

Data collection

Patient demographic variables, including age, gender, race, ethnicity, marital status, religion, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status, primary cancer site, disease status, and presence of a second primary cancer, were extracted from the electronic health record. The electronic health record includes “White or Caucasian” and “Black or African American” as options for a patient’s race; in this study, we refer to these options as “White” and “Black,” respectively, for ease of reporting. Disease status 6 months before the SCC visit was extracted from advance care planning (ACP) notes, when available. Disease status was defined as advanced if “metastatic,” “2 + line [of therapy],” or “no SOC [standard of care] options” was selected in the ACP notes. Disease status was defined as not advanced if either “localized/regional” or “NED [no evidence of disease]” was selected in the ACP notes. When not discernible in the ACP notes, disease status was determined by chart review; here, disease status was defined as advanced if a metastatic, locally advanced (unresectable), or recurrent solid tumor, or a hematologic disease in at least first relapse was documented, and disease status was defined as not advanced if a localized or locoregional solid tumor or a hematologic disease in first-line treatment or first remission was documented. When it was unclear how disease status should be defined, three physicians with backgrounds in oncology, internal medicine, and palliative medicine discussed the case until they reached consensus. Patient responses to the ESAS, as well as treatment intent documented in either treatment plan order (TPO) or ACP note on the date of the initial consultation to the SCC, were also extracted.

Measures

Patient-reported treatment intent was considered cure if patients answered either question 2 with “To cure my cancer” or question 3 with “Somewhat likely (25–49% chance of cure),” “Moderately likely (50–74% chance of cure),” “Very likely (75–90% chance of cure),” or “Extremely likely (more than 90% chance of cure).” Patient-reported treatment intent was considered non-cure if they answered both question 2 with anything except “To cure my cancer” and question 3 with “No chance (0% chance of cure),” “Very unlikely (less than 10% chance of cure),” or “Unlikely (10–24% chance of cure).” This method of classifying question 3 dichotomously into curable or incurable was used previously [4, 21, 22]. For question 2, free-text responses to the “Other” answer choice were reviewed by the co-investigators and interpreted as “cure” when appropriate.

Oncologist-documented treatment intent was determined from one of two sources. We first attempted to determine oncologist-documented treatment intent through TPO documented in the electronic health record. Options for TPO treatment intent include “cure,” “life prolongation,” “phase I research,” “supportive care,” and “symptom control.” For each patient, we considered, in the following order of priority and as available, a TPO that was active at the time of the SCC visit, a TPO that was entered but not yet active at the time of the SCC visit, and a TPO that was entered within 6 months before the SCC visit. If a TPO meeting those criteria was not available, we considered ACP notes completed by either a medical or surgical oncology physician or advanced practice provider within 180 days before the SCC visit. Templates are often used for ACP notes, allowing a provider to select a cancer treatment goal of “cure,” “life prolongation,” or “best supportive care.” For both TPOs and ACP notes, oncologist-documented treatment intent was categorized as cure if “cure” was selected as the treatment goal and as non-cure if any other selection was made.

Statistical methods

An a priori power analysis for a goal sample size of 300 estimated that the 95% confidence interval would not exceed ± 0.11 for any continuous variable or ± 0.06 for any binary outcome. Additionally, with a sample size of 150 patients per primary outcome group, our study had an estimated power of 80% to detect an effect size of 0.33 for the association between the binary primary outcome (i.e., concordance or discordance) and continuous characteristics, based on a two-sample t-test with a two-sided alpha of 0.05. Our study was similarly powered to detect differences between binary primary outcomes in categorical characteristic proportions of 0.5 and 0.66, based on a two-group chi-square test.

Demographic and clinical characteristics were summarized as medians with quartiles or frequencies with percentages by status of patient-oncologist treatment intent agreement. Differences between agreement statuses were assessed using a two-sample t-test or chi-square test. Variables with p-values less than 0.1 on this univariable analysis were included in a multivariable analysis. Despite having a p-value of less than 0.1 in the univariable analysis, oncologist-documented treatment intent was omitted from the multivariable analysis, as modeling revealed that it lacked independence from disease status. A significance level of 5% was chosen, and all statistical analyses were performed using the software R (version 4.4.1).

Results

Patient population characteristics

To reach our goal enrollment of 300 patients, we screened 357 consecutive patients who presented to the SCC for virtual initial consultation visits. Of the 57 patients that were excluded, 40 (70%) did not have an eligible oncologist-documented treatment intent in their TPOs or ACP notes, 8 (14%) completed the pre-visit screening but did not complete their initial SCC visit, 7 (12%) did not have an active malignancy, 1 (2%) did not complete either question 2 or 3 of illness understanding screening, and 1 (2%) was not receiving active treatment. Patient-reported and oncologist-documented treatment intent

Oncologist-documented treatment intent was non-cure for 234 (78%) patients. Of these patients, 88 (37.6%) reported a concordant treatment intent (i.e., non-cure) and 146 (62.4%) reported a discordant treatment intent (i.e., cure) (Table 2). Overall, of the 300 patient-oncologist dyads, 152 (50.7%) had concordant treatment intent for cure (n = 64) or non-cure (n = 88), whereas 148 (49.3%) had discordant treatment intent, including 146 (98.6%) in which the patient had a treatment intent of cure.

Associations between patient characteristics and treatment intent concordance

The findings of the univariable analysis of demographic and baseline clinical characteristics are summarized by concordant and discordant treatment intent in Table 4. Patients with breast, head and neck, and hematological malignancies were more likely to be in the concordant group, and patients with “other” malignancy types, including melanoma and sarcoma, were more likely to be in the discordant group (p = 0.02). Patients in the discordant group had higher ECOG performance scores (p = 0.015). The only ESAS-measured symptom burden component that differed between the groups was spiritual pain, which had a larger range of scores in the concordant group (p = 0.05). Patients in the discordant group were more likely to have advanced disease (p < 0.001) and have a TPO-documented goal of life prolongation or phase I research (p < 0.001). Discussion

Using systematic screening, we found that most patients whose cancer is being treated with curative intent report an accurate curability perception and primary treatment goal, whereas most patients whose cancer is being treated with non-curative intent report either an inaccurate curability perception or primary treatment goal. Three-fourths of patients receiving treatment with curative intent identified their primary treatment goal as cure, whereas about one-third of patients receiving treatment with non-curative intent reported their primary treatment goal as cure. Overall, about half of the patients reported a treatment intent in accordance with their oncologists’ treatment intent. Patients that reported treatment intent discordant from that of their oncologist were more likely to be Black, have a malignancy classified as “other” (most of which were melanoma and sarcoma), have advanced disease, and report lower spiritual pain.

In the present study, most patients receiving treatment with oncologist-documented curative intent accurately reported at least a 75% chance of cure or a primary treatment goal of curing their cancer. Only two patients (0.7% of the total study population and 3% of the patients receiving treatment with curative intent) reported a pessimistic, inaccurate view of these two components. These percentages are considerably lower than those demonstrated in two previous studies. In a small prospective study of 125 patients with all stages of solid tumors, 9.6% of patients who received treatment with curative intent reported pessimistic treatment intent [11]. In a larger prospective study of 599 patients with cancer of all disease stages, 4.9% of patients reported a pessimistic treatment goal and 6% of patients reported a pessimistic chance of cure [12].

We also found that most patients receiving treatment with non-curative intent reported either an inaccurate understanding of their curability or a curative primary treatment goal, with over half of these patients reporting at least a 25% chance of cure. This is largely consistent with previously published non-screening studies. A secondary analysis of a randomized trial in patients with advanced cancer that used the same question regarding curability perception as the one in our study found that most patients (70.7% at week 12 and 66.1% at their last survey before their death) reported that their cancer was likely curable [22]. A systematic review that looked at different assessments of illness understanding found that studies of perception of curability had varied response options, with reported assessment accuracy ranging from 35 to 84% [10].

Three-fourths of patients with curable disease and one-third of patients with incurable disease reported “cure” as their primary treatment goal. Two studies that included patients with advanced, incurable solid tumors and used the same question regarding primary treatment goal as the one used in our study respectively reported that 33.3% and 36% of patients reported their primary treatment goal was curative [3, 23]. Loucka et al. found a slightly higher rate (67%) of patients with advanced solid tumors reporting a non-curative primary treatment goal and a lower rate (22%) of patients who accurately reported a chance of cure of less than 10% [6]. These findings mirror those of our study, which found that patients receiving treatment with non-curative intent are more likely to accurately identify their primary treatment goal as non-cure than they are to accurately identify their disease as incurable.

Reported rates of concordance for cancer treatment intent vary, but the reported rates of discordance are consistently high. One study that enrolled patients with solid tumors of all stages found a high overall concordance rate of 76%; however, this study included mostly patients with earlier-stage cancer, and only 1% of patients with concordant treatment intent were receiving treatment with non-curative intent [11]. A secondary analysis including patients with only advanced solid tumors found a treatment intent concordance rate of 39.9% [24]. A large prospective cohort study of patients with advanced solid tumors found that when asked to dichotomously report their disease’s curability over multiple time points, over half of patients (54.5%) either inaccurately reported their disease’s curability or were uncertain at their last assessment [25]. Our findings are consistent with these previously published studies, with an overall treatment intent concordance rate of 50.7% and a lower treatment intent concordance rate of 37.6% for patients receiving treatment with non-curative intent.

In the present study, treatment intent discordance was more likely to involve patients who were Black, had certain malignancies, and had advanced disease. Discordance was also associated with lower spiritual pain. Prior studies have demonstrated that non-White race was associated with discordant illness understanding [24, 26–28]. Our study only found a significant association between discordance and Black race, with discordance rates in patients of Asian and other descent being statistically insignificant. In some studies, lower spiritual well-being scores have been associated with accurate prognostic awareness [25, 29]; however, these studies did not use the ESAS. The minimal difference in psychological distress noted between our concordant and discordant groups may be due to the inclusion of patients with concordant curative treatment intent, which likely made it less likely to detect the distress more often seen in patients who are prognostically aware of incurability [3–5, 7, 24]. Certain malignancies have been associated with accurate or inaccurate illness understanding with mixed results [27]. Our study found an association between treatment intent discordance and malignancies included in the “other” category, which comprised primarily melanoma and sarcoma. This finding potentially warrants future study among these populations.

There are limitations inherent to studying illness understanding. It has many components, with our study including only perception of curability and treatment goals. Even for specific components, studies measure outcomes and interpret results with significant variation, making comparison difficult. Patients may report what they hope for rather than what they have been told or may not be willing to acknowledge their disease’s incurability on a survey. Similarly, reported lack of knowledge may not prevent appropriate end-of-life preparation [30]. Our study has some unique, individualized limitations. It is a retrospective study in a single center, which is a comprehensive cancer center, and many of our patients may self-select for more aggressive care and may be more likely to have an optimistic outlook regarding their outcomes. We also cannot be certain that the oncologists appropriately disclosed treatment intent, and similarly, lack of documented discussions of illness understanding does not mean that they did not occur.

Despite increased recognition of illness understanding as an integral component of high-quality care, illness misunderstanding remains prevalent. Such illness misunderstanding was reflected in the systematic screening completed in our SCC, where most patients with advanced, incurable disease reported either at least a 25% chance of cure or that the primary goal of their cancer treatment was cure. Although some associations between characteristics and discordance between a patient’s understood treatment intent and their oncologist’s treatment intent are consistent with those found in the literature, the implications of these similarities are unclear given the heterogeneity demonstrated in prior studies.

Future research should focus on identifying the subgroups of patients who would most benefit from addressing possibly inaccurate illness understanding, especially given that other studies have demonstrated associations between accurate illness understanding and higher emotional distress. We are currently piloting a screening tool that aims to capture patients’ information needs and preferences, as well as their values and coping concerns, to identify those needing the greatest support. Our hope is that by screening for illness understanding and adequately addressing their needs, we can improve clinical and patient-reported outcomes. Consideration of patient-identified meaningful outcomes should be explored as goals for future research as well.
