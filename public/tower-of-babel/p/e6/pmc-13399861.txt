Gluteal Undersurface Concealed Crescent Injury Endoscopic Deep Surface Repair
Authors: Bryant P. Elrick, Judas Z. Kelley, Eric Welder, Cody Ramirez, Benjamin L. Judkins, James W. Genuario
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPartial‐thickness articular‐sided gluteus medius and minimus tears—referred to as gluteal undersurface concealed crescent injury lesions—can cause debilitating pain. Persistent symptoms despite nonoperative care indicate operative management. Conventional endoscopic repair techniques for partial‐thickness gluteal tears have commonly involved a transtendinous approach, which involves tendon splitting, anchor placement, and knot tying. Despite satisfactory outcomes, several characteristics of conventional techniques may perpetuate pain and irritation postoperatively and result in prolonged operative and recovery times. This technical note describes a tissue‐sparing gluteal undersurface concealed crescent injury tear repair using all‐suture knotless anchors in an “X‐linked” compressive configuration (GUCCI‐X).

The hip abductors, previously termed the “rotator cuff” of the hip, 1 are essential for stabilization of the hip and pelvic girdle during locomotion. Tendinopathy or tearing of the abductor tendons can result in greater trochanteric pain syndrome, which is a debilitating condition with lateral hip pain, weakness, and gait instability. 2 Nonoperative treatment including nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory medication, physical therapy, and bursal injections (i.e., corticosteroid and platelet‐rich‐plasma) are first‐line therapies for greater trochanteric pain syndrome; however, a subset of patients may continue to have debilitating pain and weakness that interferes with activities of daily living, necessitating operative treatment. 2 , 3

Hip abductor tears, specifically tears of the gluteus medius or minimus tendons, can be subclassified into intrasubstance (longitudinal), partial‐thickness (PT), or full‐thickness. 4 , 5 Over two‐thirds of surgically treated gluteal tendon tears are PT. 3 Deep gluteal PT undersurface tears often occur in a crescent‐shaped pattern. The senior author (J.W.G) describes these PT tears as gluteal undersurface concealed crescent injuries (GUCCIs). These lesions can be challenging to diagnose as they cannot be directly visualized either open or endoscopic. They are characterized by deep disruption of articular‐sided fibers and discontinuity with the underlying bone (Figure 1). These lesions are considered the “hip‐equivalent” of a partial articular‐sided supraspinatus tendon avulsion injury in the shoulder. 6

The purpose of this technical note is to describe the senior author's preferred technique for endoscopic deep gluteal undersurface compression repair using knotless all‐suture anchors.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Anesthesia and Patient Positioning

After induction of general anesthesia, the patient is placed in the supine position on the Stryker Guardian hip table (Guardian Hip Distraction System; Stryker Endoscopy, Kalamazoo, MI). The patient is positioned at the edge of the bed, toward the operative side. The surgical leg is internally rotated and abducted 30° with neutral flexion to take tension off the iliotibial (IT) band and enlarge the trochanteric space. The operating table is gently airplaned away 10° to 20°, and the hip is prepped and draped in standard sterile fashion (Figure 2).

Portal Placement and Diagnostic Endoscopy

The complete surgical technique is shown in Video 1 and is performed using a 30° arthroscope. Two standard hip endoscopy portals, distal lateral accessory portal and proximal lateral portal, are utilized (Figure 3A). The distal lateral accessory portal is established using placement of a spinal needle under fluoroscopic view. The needle is placed at the level of the vastus ridge and is directed superoposterior to enter the gluteal space in the posterior bursal facet (Figure 3B). This portal functions primarily as the gluteal viewing portal. The proximal lateral portal is then established as the gluteal working portal under direct visualization using a spinal needle. Trochanteric bursectomy is carried out using a combination of an endoscopic shaver and radiofrequency ablation wand. It is improtant to ensure debridement is sufficiently carried anteriorly in the trochanteric space to access the entire footprint (Table 1). This exposes the vastus lateralis distally, the gluteus medius muscle (without violating the perimysium) proximally, and the gluteus maximus posteriorly. Avoid violation of the IT band to prevent swelling and poor visualization. If IT band lengthening is anticipated in the setting of tightness, it should be performed after the gluteal repair. Inspection of the superficial fibers of the gluteal tendons reveals intact superficial fibers with the presence of a “wave” sign (Figure 4). This finding is consistent with a GUCCI tear and confirms the patient is indicated for deep gluteal undersurface compression repair. All 4 corners of the wave (also known as bubble) sign are marked with a coablation wand and c‐arm fluoroscopy.

A 0.9 mm diameter drill (Phoenix Microfracture System; Pivot Medical, Sunnyvale, CA) is used in a trans‐tendinous manner to perform microfracture of the greater trochanter to facilitate vascular channeling and promote deep surface bone‐to‐tendon healing. The drill is typically passed 10 to 15 times, depending on the size of the GUCCI tear, to prepare the footprint. Four 2.3 mm knotless all‐suture anchors (Iconix Knotless, Stryker Sports Medicine, Greenwood Village, CO) are consecutively drilled and placed in a square‐shaped configuration along the periphery of the footprint. The authors prefer the 2.3 mm suture anchors given a propensity for failure during tensioning that was previously experienced using smaller anchors (Table 1). Suture limbs are then shuttled sequentially in an “X‐linked” configuration (GUCCI‐X). In order to aid in suture management, the authors recommend alternating suture colors and prefer a single color for posterior (i.e., blue) and another for anterior (i.e., party braid) (Table 1). The first 2 anchors are drilled and placed, proximal posterior followed by proximal anterior (Figure 5A). Then, the repair suture (blue) of the proximal posterior anchor is shuttled through the proximal anterior anchor and preliminarily tightened (Figure 5B,C). The third anchor is drilled and placed distal posterior (Figure 5D). The repair suture of the proximal anterior anchor (party braid) is then shuttled through the distal posterior anchor and preliminarily tightened (Figure 5E,F). The final fourth anchor is drilled and placed (Figure 5G). Notably, the authors find it easier to drill and place both distal anchors from the distal lateral accessory portal while viewing from proximal to lateral. Consecutive shuttling ensues from distal posterior to distal anterior and then from distal anterior back to the first proximal posterior anchor (Figure 5H‐J). Following suture shuttling, the 2 posterior limbs will now be party braid (“party [braid] in the back”), and the 2 anterior limbs will now be blue (Figure 5K). Anchor tension is set and the remaining suture limbs can now be cut; however, it is the authors’ preference to take the 2 posterior and anterior suture limbs and tie them together to achieve the GUCCI‐X repair construct which promotes enhanced compression anteriorly and posteriorly (Figure 6).

In the setting of poor tissue quality, particularly if the tear involves greater than 50% of tendon thickness, dermal‐derived allograft can be used to augment the repair. Based on footprint size, 1 to 4, 15 × 4.5 × 3 mm human dermis allografts (Dermis on Demand Allograft, DePuy Synthes Sports Medicine, Raynham, MA) can be incorporated onto the shuttling sutures between anchors to provide further tissue augmentation (Figure 7).

Endoscopic view of a left hip from the distal lateral accessory portal using a 30° scope with two 15 × 4.5 × 3 mm human dermis allografts (blue stars) incorporated onto the shuttling sutures between anchors to augment the repair.

The hip is then ranged through full motion to confirm stability of the repair and resolution of the “wave” sign. All excess fluid is removed and the portals are closed in standard fashion.

Postoperative Rehabilitation

Rehabilitation begins immediately postoperatively. The patient is placed in a hip abduction orthosis with flat foot 20% partial weight‐bearing for 4 to 6 weeks with hip flexion limited to 30° to 75°. Progression to full weight‐bearing and range of motion is initiated at 4 weeks using assistive walking devices. Strengthening and gait retraining begins once full range of motion is achieved. Full activity typically resumes in 4 to 6 months.

DISCUSSION

This technical note describes an endoscopic 2‐portal technique for repairing PT GUCCI tears with knotless all‐suture anchors. Endoscopic techniques commonly use “transtendinous” approaches that involve creating a longitudinal split in the midsubstance of the tendon, followed by side‐to‐side repair of the split and reduction to the insertional footprint using 1 or more suture anchors to repair tendon leaflets. 5 , 6 Despite satisfactory outcomes, challenges of the transtendinous approach exist, including access, symptomatic knots, and risk of damage to healthy tissue during debridement. 5 , 7 , 8

The author's described endoscopic technique preserves integrity of the gluteal tendons by obviating the need for a tendon split and using the nanofracture drilling to create bony bleeding while minimizing damage to the overlying tendon, thereby maintaining native tissue architecture (Table 2). The use of soft, all‐suture knotless anchors eliminates knot burden and reduces the potential for postoperative IT band irritation and associated symptoms. Central to the construct is the cross‐linked (“X”‐shaped) suture configuration, which theoretically enhances biologic healing and improves biomechanical strength of the repair.

Despite its advantages, this technique has limitations. Most notably, its application has been limited to the repair of PT GUCCI lesions. In cases of full‐thickness gluteal tears, more conventional endoscopic techniques remain preferable. Substantial tendon retraction may require open conversion for adequate fixation. Additionally, this endoscopic repair strategy precludes direct visualization and formal decortication of the abductor footprint potentially limiting biologic healing.

In summary, this endoscopic technique for the repair of GUCCI tears utilizing all‐suture knotless anchors offers a reproducible, minimally invasive technique that preserves native tissue and optimizes biomechanical healing through its cross‐linked, compressive design. While offering advantages over conventional methods, its limitations should be carefully considered. Future clinical studies are indicated to evaluate outcomes and durability.
