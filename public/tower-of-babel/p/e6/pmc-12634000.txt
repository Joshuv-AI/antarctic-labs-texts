Barriers and motivators experienced by South Asian primary care physicians in conducting research
Authors: Waseem N. Ahmed, Marriam Sheraz, Sankha Randenikumara, Hina Jawaid, Nusrat Jahan, Md Ferdous Rahman, Ramakrishna Prasad, Swaathi Balasubramanian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction:Research in primary healthcare is vital for improving clinical practices, health outcomes, and policy effectiveness. Despite recognizing its importance and willingness to conduct it many general practitioners face barriers in doing it.Objectives:Our study aimed to understand the challenges in conducting research and identify factors that motivate research culture among primary care physicians in South Asia.Methods:Descriptive cross-sectional study is conducted among practicing primary care physicians in several South Asian countries. The online survey forms were emailed and shared on professional social media platforms. Data analysis was done using SPSS software version 23.0. Descriptive analysis such as frequency and percentages were calculated. For categorical data chai square was applied.Results:Out of the 155 participants, 62% had participated in research training sessions. However, less than one-fifth of the participants had published research articles at least once in the past two years. The main obstacles were lack of mentorship (72%), insufficient training (66%), lack of technical or statistical support (65%), and financial constraints (59%). The primary motivators were to update knowledge (63%) and to improve the quality of care (61%). We did observe a significant negative correlation (p=0.02) between the number of years in practice and the motivation to conduct research.Conclusion:Most primary care physicians recognize the value of research. However, obstacles such as a lack of mentorship, formal training, and financial support hinder their research efforts. The motivating factors we identified could be used to inspire more primary care physicians to engage in research.

Introduction

Research, along with the evidence it generates, plays a crucial role in improving global health, health equity, and economic development. Every healthcare provider, whether wittingly or unwittingly, contributes to this research by producing data, identifying trends, and implementing and testing solutions until an effective one is discovered. Therefore, understanding the ecosystem of medical research is essential to strengthen it.[1] Primary healthcare is the first point of contact for most individuals with healthcare providers.[2] Research in this field is crucial as it continuously evaluates and improves the effectiveness of clinical practices and health policies. Research plays a vital role in better understanding of the diseases, their prevention and treatment, and overall health promotion. The translation of evidence into practice requires critical appraisal and research activities.[3] The utmost importance of research in primary care was brought to the forefront by Barbara Starfelid’s work, which highlighted the effectiveness and impact of primary care on health outcomes through her various studies.[2] Unfortunately, only 10% of the world’s research resources are dedicated to 90% of the health problems that people present at primary care centers, a disparity often referred to as a 10/90 gap.[4] Most of the previous studies reveal that the majority of general practitioners (GPs) have a positive attitude and understand the importance of research. However, they also face many barriers, such as a lack of adequate time and financial support.[5] Concerns about the involvement of patients in research and a reluctance to share their records have been identified as a major barrier in a study conducted among GPs in Germany.[6] The inability to collaborate and network with like-minded people has been also recognized as a hindrance to research activities.[67] Another study in Europe indicates that GPs lack sufficient influence to attract local and national funding for research. This study also highlights the lack of postgraduate training as a main barrier.[7] Some doctors also have identified interruption in patient flow, a lack of capacity, and a lack of understanding of research as the main inhibitory factors.[8] The absence of a dedicated research team and reliable internet access have also been limiting factors for research work among clinicians in Africa.[9] A global problem in primary care centers is a workforce shortage, which leaves no additional time to engage in research.[10] A lack of research culture, along with greater inclination towards patient care, is common in general practice.[711] A Generalist perspective on a focused research topic is also a barrier, as in Reductionist Biomedical Model.[711] A lack of mentorship and opportunities are also limiting factors.[79]

Completing a research project early in one’s career has been shown to have positive effects on future research participation.[12] A study conducted in Bahrain found that senior and board-certified physicians exhibited promising attitudes toward research.[2] GPs may face barriers and limitations at various levels, including individual, economic, professional, institutional, scientific, and facility-based.[13] However, there is no universally accepted tool to assess attitudes towards research.[14] While there have been few studies conducted in various countries, data are scarce specifically in South Asia. Doctors collectively are a valuable source of information. Family practice is unique in its recognition of the patient’s context and the interplay of family dynamics, social relationships, cultural background, and economics in the causation and presentation of any illness, as well as the response to any given treatment.[15]

The primary objectives of our study were to explore and assess the challenges faced by primary care doctors in conducting research, to describe the factors that either enable or hinder research in clinical practice and to identify the motivating factors that could aid in the development of strategies to promote research.

Methods

We conducted a descriptive cross-sectional study among primary care physicians (PCPs) practicing in South Asian countries, including Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, Nepal, Pakistan, and Sri Lanka. This group comprised trained family physicians and GPs as well as doctors with basic qualifications who provide patient care at first contact settings. We piloted and revised the questionnaire accordingly. The study was conducted from January 2022 to June 2022. We obtained clearance from the Institutional Research Committee and the Ethical Committee.

We emailed the online survey to the PCPs and professional forums of PCPs on social platforms. We obtained informed consent from all participants and did not collect identification details from any participant. We entered the collected data into an Excel sheet and removed duplicates and incomplete survey submissions. Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS software for windows, version 23 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). We employed descriptive methods such as frequency analysis and percentage analysis. We used the Chi-square test to find significance in categorical data, considering a probability value (P) <0.05 as statistically significant with a precision of 95% and a 0.05 confidence interval.

Discussion

Our study confirmed that the majority of the participants understand the importance of research in improving the quality of care. However, the main barrier to conducting the research is the lack of mentorship. This finding aligns with the experiences of European GPs, who also reported a lack of support from trained primary care researchers.[7] Another main obstacle found in our study was the lack of training, both in conducting research and performing statistical analysis. A study conducted in Saudi Arabia also identified a lack of mentorship and training as major barriers to conducting research.[16] Financial constraints are also stated as crucial barriers, a finding that is consistent with the experiences of European GPs.[7] Other common barriers identified globally include time constraints and increased workload.

Our results show that South Asian PCPs are motivated to conduct research regardless of their age, gender, country, and qualification. This contrasts with a study conducted in Oman, which found that young male doctors are more motivated to conduct research.[5] Interestingly, we observed a significant negative correlation (P = 0.02) between the number of years in practice had the motivation to conduct research. One possible explanation could be that as physicians gain more experience, they are subjected to high workloads and tight deadlines. This result could show that burnout, which negatively impacts on the motivation of researching.[17]

Our study revealed that, despite having advanced education and extensive clinical experience, only a small number of PCPs regularly publish articles. The majority of them do recognize research as a crucial factor for enhancing their knowledge and improving the quality of care they provide. However, they find the process of conducting research to be challenging. Addressing these issues could be a key to maintaining and engaging research motivation among experienced physicians.

According to the responses received, most of our respondents (62%) have actively participated in some research training sessions. In addition, a significant number of participants have been involved in research at some point in their lives: About 45% reported involvement in research within the last year, and 40% have published research as a part of their degree completion. However, despite these experiences, 66% of participants identified ‘lack of research training’ as a main barrier to conducting research. This discrepancy indicates that many trainees may be conducting research in a mechanical way to fulfill a compulsory component of their courses, rather than gaining a comprehensive understanding of how to conduct independent research.

Our research highlights a common issue in primary care across South Asian countries. Despite facing similar conditions and problems, these countries often rely on evidence from Western countries for disease management due to a lack of local research. This reliance could potentially lead to less effective treatments and interventions, as the context and conditions in Western countries can be quite different from those in South Asia.[18] Similar sentiments were shared by Canadian Family Physicians also, who reported that practice guidelines and recommendations in primary care should be developed in the local context, which could lead to more relevant and effective healthcare strategies and interventions.[12]

While the barriers faced by PCPs have clear similarities, the healthcare system and disease conditions are quite different in Western countries from those in South Asian countries. Due to a lack of local research, South Asian health systems mainly rely on evidence from Western countries for disease management.[18] There is a pressing need for local research to better patient management and study the disease patterns unique to their own countries. PCPs, who are often the first point of contact for patients and follow the disease at every stage, should actively participate in research for effective data collection and study of disease patterns.

In most South Asian countries, research methodology is not part of the undergraduate medical curriculum.[19] A study conducted in India found that about 91% of students have no research experience at the undergraduate level.[20] This lack of early exposure to research methodology is a major reason why, despite their higher clinical degrees and experience, the PCPs find research challenging. To overcome this barrier, it is suggested that research should be made a compulsory part of undergraduate medical education. Students should be encouraged and guided by experienced researchers to conduct research and publish in peer-reviewed international journals. Evidence shows that research mentorship leads to successful student outcomes. For instance, 28% of undergraduate students at a German medical institute had authored a publication and 7.8% of them had been first authors.[21] Having a well-defined research infrastructure in medical schools is crucial to promoting research and fostering a culture of research among future physicians.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study reveals that South Asian PCPs recognize the importance of research for advancing their knowledge and skills and improving the quality of care. However, they face significant barriers such as lack of mentorship, inadequate formal training in research, lack of financial support, and work-related commitments. These barriers align with those identified among PCPs in other regions. The motivating factors identified in our study could be leveraged to actively promote research among the PCPs in South Asia.

Future research should focus on exploring effective strategies for overcoming the barriers reported in this study. Further studies could also investigate how to best utilize the identified motivating factors to encourage research among PCPs. This could lead to a more robust research culture in primary care settings in South Asia, ultimately leading to improved healthcare outcomes.
