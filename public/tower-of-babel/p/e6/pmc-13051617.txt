Helping people navigate and make sense of cancer information: challenges and perspectives
Authors: Lisa Rezende, Gissoo DeCotiis, Eric K Singhi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPeople living with cancer, and their caregivers and families, have access to many sources of health information. This brings both positives and challenges. Some information is not easy to understand and it can be difficult to know what is relevant to an individual’s situation. It can also be difficult to know what sources are trustworthy, how to check accuracy, and what might be false information (misinformation or disinformation). This podcast brings together the perspectives of a medical oncologist, a patient advocate, and a recipient of risk-mitigating cancer care who also brings experience of caregiving and volunteering for an organization that provides information to support those at risk of developing cancer. They discuss various approaches to help people find relevant and trustworthy health information, and consider how healthcare providers can support this. An accompanying infographic provides a patient’s guide to cancer-focused medical journals, which can be shared with people with cancer and their caregivers to help open the door to the patient-friendly content that is sometimes published by these journals. Overall, people are encouraged to seek support from their healthcare providers to find and interpret cancer information, with consideration of relevance to their own situation and the validity of the source.KEYWORDS: Cancer, health information, health literacy, misinformation, patients, social media

Script

Dr Singhi: : Hello, my name is Dr Eric Singhi. I am a Thoracic Medical Oncologist and Assistant Professor at the University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center, with a personal research focus in patient education and young patients with lung cancer. In this podcast, we will explore the challenges and opportunities that people with cancer may face when navigating health information. So this is a podcast for people living with cancer, their families and caregivers, patient advocates and healthcare providers in the oncology field, and anyone with an interest in cancer information.

As part of our discussions, we will attempt to open the door to the world of cancer-focused medical journals as an available information source. Once you’ve listened to the podcast, take a look at the accompanying infographic for more information about patient-friendly content that can sometimes be found in these journals. This infographic, as well as further details, including funding and disclosure information related to this podcast, are available on the article homepage at Taylor & Francis Online (www.tandfonline.com).

So why are we discussing how people navigate health information? Well, people living with cancer have access to a huge amount of health information beyond what is offered by their healthcare providers. They may be driven to seek out this information as a result of fragmented care, brief consultations, and limited personalized information. There are different approaches to finding health information, depending on the individual and their personal cancer journey, and this is shaped by varying levels of health and digital literacy and by the unique emotional burden, uncertainty, and stigma associated with cancer. It may be that some people with cancer have friends or family members who are able to share relevant knowledge. And then some people turn to the internet, with research suggesting that this is particularly true for younger people and those with higher levels of education and income [1]. Having said that, the internet is also used by many older people, and seemingly more so among those with a recent cancer diagnosis than among those with other chronic illnesses [2]. Overall, the internet can be a valued source of information for people living with cancer and is often used to find information about cancer and health in general, to search for information about treatment options, and to learn about the experiences and coping strategies of others [1].

Access to such volumes of information can bring many positives, including knowledge, empowerment, and a sense of community with others who share similar lived experiences. However, sometimes news stories or social media posts can be sensationalized, and even incorrect or misleading, potentially causing unnecessary distress and perhaps even impacting the health-related attitudes and behaviors of people living with cancer [3]. It can be difficult for people to understand what is credible and what is relevant to their own situation.

So, there are certainly challenges, and in this podcast we are going to dig deeper into this topic and consider ways to support people with navigating health information.

Let’s start by thinking about what information people are looking for and where they might go to find it. In my experience, people living with cancer generally want to understand their diagnosis and treatment options, or “what they’re dealing with and how to deal with it.” My coauthor, Gissoo De Cotiis, previously headed up the patient advocacy function for Daiichi Sankyo in Oncology for many years and has worked closely with people who are living with cancer. In Gissoo’s experience, many people start with online search engines like Google, which these days also offer responses generated by artificial intelligence, or AI. Then people move onto social media groups, typically on Facebook for older people, or Instagram, X, or even TikTok for younger people. Some people may also look at content on YouTube and some may identify relevant patient organizations. Gissoo also notes that some “scientifically savvy” people, who perhaps have a scientific or medical background, may find their way to physician associations or cancer-focused medical journals.

From my perspective as a Medical Oncologist, I also find that people often turn to digital tools, including AI platforms and social media, and this seems to be an increasing trend. Social media can offer a lot of information, but not all of it is reliable; some comes from influencers with limited credibility and can fuel the spread of false information. False information can include misinformation, which is shared by people in good faith because they believe it, and disinformation, which is shared with the deliberate intention of misleading others [4,5]. Interestingly, research suggests that cancer-related misinformation and harmful content on social media typically generates more engagement than factual and safe content [6], so the number of “likes” is not necessarily an indicator of trustworthy content. Similarly, AI can bring advantages, offering readable and patient-oriented information, but again there are dangers associated with the potential for false information [7]. False information is a real challenge, and it can be difficult for people to know how to validate the accuracy and credibility of information.

At this point I’d like to hand over to my coauthor, Lisa Rezende, who has some insightful first-hand experiences with seeking out credible cancer information and supporting others to do the same. Lisa, perhaps you could introduce yourself and tell us about your perspective?

Lisa: : Thank you Eric. My name is Lisa Rezende and I am a Professor of Practice in the Department of Molecular and Cellular Biology at the University of Arizona. As well as being a scientist, I’ve had many years of risk-mitigating cancer care, reconstructive surgery, and surgical complications, and I’ve also had experience of caregiving and volunteering for an organization that provides information to support those at risk of developing cancer. Let me tell you a little bit about my story and the perspective I bring.

Working in science, I have a scientist’s instinct to question everything and meticulously critique the validity of information sources, rather than taking things at face value. So, when my mother had a recurrence of ovarian cancer in 2006, I had concerns about her seeking information via online search engines and was keen to find credible sources of information. One of the sources I came across was a group called FORCE, which stands for Facing Our Risk of Cancer Empowered. As a scientist, it struck me that this group cited references; this means they provided evidence sources that backed up what they were saying, which is a standard approach in the science world and a sign of robust, credible information.

Two years later, I tested positive for a BRCA1 mutation, just as my mother had. BRCA1 mutations are known to be associated with increased risk of breast and ovarian cancer. To reduce my risk of breast cancer, I underwent a risk-reducing mastectomy. Unfortunately, I experienced several complications after the surgery. Again, I looked to FORCE for information and support while I managed the complications, using their message boards, and even attending one of their conferences. I gained so much value from FORCE that, ultimately, I went on to volunteer for them and held a post as their Director of Education for 3 years. I still volunteer with FORCE today and continue to receive follow-up medical care in relation to my increased cancer risk.

Dr Singhi: : So, Lisa, where do people go to look for information about cancer?

Lisa: : In my experience, people living with cancer tend to identify media sources or health sites that they feel they can trust. A survey by FORCE has indicated that young women use a variety of media sources to seek information about breast cancer and inform their medical decisions [8]. Similarly, another survey has indicated that people living with non-small cell lung cancer also refer to a range of sources, including social media, cancer support groups, and cancer websites from academic centers or national societies [9]. And a survey of adult patients at a Canadian cancer center has also indicated that social media is often referred to, for example, to learn about cancer, distract from cancer, and connect with others [10].

Dr Singhi: : Great, so it sounds like there are plenty of different information sources that people are using, but what challenges do you see?

Lisa: : I believe a key challenge is equity in ensuring that everyone has access to high-quality health information, given variations in health literacy, reading literacy, native languages, internet access, and digital accessibility needs. Also, increasingly relevant, is the variation in comfort levels and competence with using AI to actively search for and summarize information. Evidence suggests that digital technologies are likely to increase certain health inequities [11]. This challenge is compounded by the complexity and inconsistency of the terminology that is used. More patient-friendly language is needed, but without “dumbing things down,” and also pitching things appropriately for people with higher health literacy who are seeking more detail. Some people want a quick summary and others want depth, so different formats may be helpful for different people.

Another key challenge for people is understanding what is relevant to their personal situation. For example, breast cancer has many different subtypes, but news stories don’t always specify what subtype the research refers to, and people don’t always know their own subtype if they haven’t asked their healthcare provider for this information.

In addition, there is language used by physicians and scientists that can be easily misinterpreted by people outside the field. For example, when people hear about a clinical trial where the treatment effects were “statistically significant,” they can assume this means the treatment would be beneficial to them. But statistical significance doesn’t necessarily mean the treatment is one that they can or should switch to, and single efficacy findings need to be viewed in a wider context (for example, with consideration of side effects or impact on quality of life).

Dr Singhi: : We mentioned earlier that some people may use AI to search for health information. What do you think of AI being used in this way, and do the same challenges apply?

Lisa: : AI may be a valuable tool for people seeking health information, but currently I have two main concerns around its use. Firstly, an important limitation is that AI can only reflect the information that it is trained on, and that information may vary in how reliable and relevant it is. For example, it could be based on well established scientific evidence, or it could be based on personal opinions from people with ulterior motives. Or a more specific example is that models may not be trained on information around rare cancers, so AI-generated content could be misleading or confusing for people living with rare cancer types.

And that relates to my second concern around AI: as with information sources, the challenge remains that information may not be directly relevant to the individual and their specific tumor type. It’s crucial that people understand how to create appropriate prompts in order to generate precise and relevant outputs that apply to them. People should also be mindful of the security if using prompts that include details of their own health conditions in an open AI system.

In addition, AI can generate over-simplified and/or inaccurate content, and the original sources of information are not always transparent [7]. References cited by AI tools can be what are known as “AI hallucinations” rather than real information sources. I’d suggest that people don’t always appreciate the need to verify AI-generated information or know how to verify it. I’d also suggest that careful fact checking may be a lot to expect from patients who are already incredibly busy navigating appointments, treatment, and insurance, alongside regular work and family commitments – it may simply not be realistic for many people, regardless of their level of health literacy.

There are many considerations for patients to bear in mind when using AI, but there are organizations that are trying to safeguard patients from the risks. For example, the mission of the Light Collective is to advance the collective rights, interests, and voices of patient communities in health technology [12]. In particular, they highlighted the risks of patients using AI to solve their own health problems, calling for more accountability from technology companies [13].

A recommended approach from the UK Patient Information Forum is to prompt AI to search specified trusted sources (such as a specific patient or health organization) and then fact check the response by asking the AI tool where it found the information and cross-checking the original sources [14].

I’ve touched on a few challenges here, but overall there is a growing need to better support people living with cancer in understanding, critiquing, and appropriately interpreting the wealth of information they see online and in the media.

Dr Singhi: : There are certainly a lot of challenges to address! Do you have any other thoughts on how to support people in understanding and appropriately interpreting information?

Lisa: : The FORCE XRAY program offers a positive example of how it can work in practice. FORCE was finding that some news stories were raising questions via their helpline and message board services, and that there was an appetite for blog posts to provide clarity. For example, people were deeply concerned by new stories relating to research that suggested an increased risk of salivary gland cancer in people with BRCA mutations [15]. It was a solid study but it required interpretation to clarify that salivary gland cancer is rare, so the overall risk for those people with BRCA mutations is still minimal. These sorts of news stories led FORCE to create the XRAY program, which offers interpretation around cancer research that is picked up by the media, packaging the information in a way that isn’t overwhelming and offering different levels of detail depending on what the user is looking for. XRAY has evolved, improved, and now has been supporting people living with cancer for more than 10 years [16].

Overall, my parting advice would be to encourage people to seek out credible sources of information based on recommendations from healthcare providers and patient organizations, carefully consider the relevance to their own personal situation, and check that research stories are backed by robust evidence.

Dr Singhi: : Thanks Lisa. I completely agree that people living with cancer should feel supported by their healthcare providers in their search for credible information. I see healthcare providers as being uniquely positioned to provide this support, for example by using their own online presence to share reliable information and address false information [17]. As a Medical Oncologist myself, I share my own posts on social media, which naturally leads to conversations with my patients, fostering transparency and openness. Healthcare providers can also seek to understand false information, correcting understanding through education and advising on future online searches [18]. Rather than being dismissive or discouraging people from looking for information, I personally advocate that healthcare providers re-direct people toward established, credible sources, such as patient organizations. For example, in the lung cancer field, patient organizations include LUNGevity [19], GO2 For Lung Cancer [20], and EGFR Resisters [21].

In the scientific community, the most credible “gold standard” information about cancer research efforts comes from articles that are written by the researchers themselves and published in medical journals. This is because they are written by the people directly involved in the research, offer sufficient detail to appropriately interpret the research, and are subjected to certain quality control steps that aim to ensure accuracy and minimize bias. One such quality control step is peer review – during peer review, an article is independently assessed by experts in the field who provide feedback around the quality, appropriateness, and accuracy of the work, which must be addressed before the article can be published [22].

While journal articles and other resources targeted to a scientific audience are typically detailed and complex, there are resources available to help people make sense of the technical jargon and approaches to clinical trials in the cancer field [23–26], and some people may feel equipped and motivated to read these articles. In addition, some articles may be accompanied by so-called “plain-language summaries” or PLS, which are peer-reviewed summaries that aim to describe the key points using easy-to-understand language. PLS are sometimes short pieces developed and published alongside the main article. In this case they potentially have limited value as they can only provide a top-level summary. Alternatively, they can be more lengthy standalone publications that are published separately from the original main article – these are known as plain-language summaries of publications, or PLSPs, and typically cover the majority of the content of the original article, with a link to the original article for any readers seeking additional detail [27]. The researchers who choose to write up their research as a PLS or PLSP are not necessarily experts in the required writing style, but there is general guidance available on writing in plain language [28,29], and some publishers also share helpful recommendations. For example, the publishers of the PLSPs provide writing tips and encourage researchers to use readability checkers and seek feedback, for example from editors or patients, to help ensure that content is pitched at an appropriate level [27].

Some cancer-focused medical journals have broader patient-focused initiatives that are a positive mechanism for supporting patients with credible peer-reviewed content using patient-friendly language that they can understand. Such initiatives help to bridge the gap in bringing the science to patients. For example, a journal called JAMA Oncology offers a collection of health information specifically written for patients, with easy-to-understand explanations of breast, lung, and prostate cancer treatments [30]. I coauthored a patient-friendly article in this collection to help explain the language of clinical trials [26]. Other examples of patient-friendly content in journals include a podcast series in the Journal of Clinical Oncology that shares experiences from healthcare providers, caregivers, and people living with cancer [31], and “Perspectives” articles in the journal Oncology and Therapy that aim to share true patient experiences as well as new developments in the field that may impact patient care [32].

Notably, there seems to be an appetite for this sort of patient-friendly content. I have been involved in designing and running a survey to explore patient perspectives around the need for patient-friendly, easy-to-understand resources in lung cancer care. In our survey, when 114 patients with non-small cell lung cancer were shown a patient-friendly infographic summary of a clinical trial, 95% of the patients thought that that format was helpful, and 92% felt it helped them understand the rationale and data behind their recommended treatment plan [9]. I also often treat patients who are participating in clinical trials and find that many of these patients refer to summaries of clinical trials, especially if they are presented visually, shared via social media, and re-shared frequently to raise awareness. For example, the New England Journal of Medicine has published one-page summaries as part of various clinical trial articles. The CheckMate 816 lung cancer trial is an example of a trial that has been reported in the New England Journal of Medicine together with a one-page summary [33], and it has also been described in a PLSP [34], with both the original article and the PLSP receiving attention on social media.

However, while these sorts of summaries are credible sources of information, they are not a universal approach across journals [35–37] and can still be difficult to read and understand [38,39]. Moreover, not everyone will know to look for them or where to find them, so there is a need to bring them to the audience, with healthcare providers and patient organizations being in a good position to help with dissemination. Ideally, for a more tailored approach, relevant PLS and PLSPs could be recommended to patients by their healthcare providers, based on their individual circumstances and health literacy levels. Where physician consultations do not allow for sufficient time, nurses may be well placed to support with this. This can be a two-way process, with patients also bringing up any information sources they have found themselves. I understand from Lisa that FORCE and other patient advocacy groups encourage patients to bring information they have found to appointments for discussion, although this is not always possible, for example due to time limitations and higher-priority discussion topics. Or sometimes that stress that the patient is under during the appointment can make it difficult for them to remember everything that they had planned to talk about. But, overall, the ideal scenario would be for patients and healthcare providers to work together to find and discuss relevant PLS and other patient-friendly information sources.

As mentioned at the start of this podcast, we have created an infographic guide to support people who may be interested in looking for patient-friendly content in cancer-focused medical journals. This guide is available alongside this podcast, on the article homepage at Taylor & Francis Online. We call on healthcare providers and patient organizations to share it with those who might find it helpful.

Beyond the plain-language summaries published in some journals, other types of easy-to-understand summaries may be of interest to people living with cancer. Notably, in the EU, there is a requirement to develop summaries of clinical trials and their findings for inclusion in an online database [40], with recommended guidance available to help ensure that they can be read easily by a lay audience [41]. Such summaries are not currently mandated in the US.

One point I would like to add is that many journals refer to “plain-language summaries” and “PLS,” but I find that some people react negatively to the term “plain-language,” perceiving it to mean that information needs to be simplified and “dumbed down” before people are able to understand it. For this reason, I advocate for the use of the term “patient-friendly.” At a broader level, it is important to explore appropriate terminology that resonates with patient audiences. The language guide from the International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer, or IASLC, is an example of efforts to establish language that is respectful, free from stigma, inclusive, and equitable [42].

Language also needs to be pitched to an appropriate level of literacy. Some recommendations suggest that health information should be written for a reading age of around 9–11 years to support understanding by all [43], while others emphasize the need to understand and tailor to the target audience [44]. From my perspective, easy-to-read information is great for some people, but we also need to remember that others will prefer information targeted to a higher literacy level. To this point, there has been a survey that asked people what they thought of different versions of a PLS: a low-complexity text-only version, a medium-complexity text-only version, a high-complexity text-only version, and a visual graphical version [45]. It is interesting to see the different versions side by side and how content can be adjusted to different literacy levels. Overall, the most popular PLS was the graphical PLS, followed by the medium-complexity PLS that was written for a reading age of 14–17 years. Meanwhile, some people felt that the low-complexity PLS was over-simplified [45]. Of course, preferences vary and can be influenced by many factors, so perhaps there is no such thing as the “perfect” PLS. Nonetheless, in my view, the survey further highlights the need to understand the audience and recognize that needs and preferences vary.
