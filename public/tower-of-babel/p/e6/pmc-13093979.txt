Integrated single marker scanning and sparse Bayesian learning improves performance of detection for GWAS
Authors: Jin Zhang, Zhenghan Wu, Mingzhi Cai, Ke Liu, Xiaoyu Han, Chang Liu, Gang Han, Yangjun Wen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Single-locus and multi-locus models provide a statistical framework for conducting genome-wide association studies (GWAS). However, mainstream GWAS algorithms face challenges such as low detection power, high false positive rates, and slow computational efficiency as the dimensionality of genomic data continues to grow. We introduce KinLmSBL, a two-stage approach that integrates single-locus scanning with polygenic background control and multi-locus sparse Bayesian learning. We validate the approach through a series of simulations and demonstrate that KinLmSBL outperforms four other single- and multi-locus approaches in detecting low heritability variants, controlling the false positive rate, and increasing efficiency. We apply KinLmSBL to maize, rice, and human datasets, further investigating its performance in identifying more known genes with lower computational cost. Overall, KinLmSBL provides an efficient GWAS tool for mining genes in high-dimensional biological data.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13007-026-01516-7.

Background

Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have revolutionized the study of complex traits by systematically mapping genetic variants to phenotypic outcomes. Leveraging high-throughput genotyping and sequencing technologies, GWAS analyzes single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) across entire genomes to identify associations with traits ranging from crop yield to human diseases [1].

However, the exponential growth of genetic markers (often millions per study) introduces the “curse of dimensionality”, complicating the simultaneous analysis of all variants. Classical single-locus models, such as linear regression implemented in PLINK [2], address this issue by testing each SNP individually: a genome-scanning approach that avoids computational overload. Nevertheless, the identification of numerous false positives following initial GWAS analysis, coupled with false negatives resulting from Bonferroni or false discovery rate (FDR) corrections, remains a persistent challenge in the field.​

Single-locus linear mixed models (LMM), also referred to as mixed linear models (MLM), enhance the power to detect quantitative trait nucleotides (QTNs) by efficiently controlling false positive rates [3]. They achieve this by incorporating random polygenic effects and excluding the tested marker to correct for confounding factors such as population stratification and cryptic relatedness. The high computational intensity of MLM has spurred the development of rapid and efficient algorithms to reduce computational burden, enabling MLM to become widely used and powerful tools in GWAS. Notable examples includes EMMA [4], FaST-LMM [5], GEMMA [6], BOLT-LMM [7], and fastGWA [8]. GEMMA is recognized as a benchmark method for single-locus MLM scanning GWAS, it is an efficient and exact method that is faster than EMMA, capable of yielding accurate P-values even in the presence of strong population structure and remaining suitable for studies with large association panels [6]. fastGWA robustly controls false positive associations under population stratification and relatedness through a sparse genetic relationship matrix, and it outperforms the most efficient existing MLM-based GWAS tools in terms of speed [8]. However, the above single-locus methods inherently ignore the numerous polygenes with minor effects and require stringent multiple testing corrections, leading to reduced power for detecting low heritability variants [9].

Besides single-locus analysis, multi-locus models represent a significant methodological advance by simultaneously estimating marker effects across the genome. Approaches such as LASSO [10] and empirical Bayes [11] have shown promise in improving detection power. Particularly, sparse Bayesian learning (SBL) offers a probabilistic framework for variable selection by estimating per-marker prior variances [12]. Theoretically, this method aligns with empirical Bayes [11], especially in its approach to variable selection where markers with estimated prior variances below a certain threshold are excluded from the model. The L2 penalty in SBL induces a continuous shrinkage effect, which effectively addresses collinearity while maintaining sparsity. However, the above multi-locus methods still fail to account for polygenic backgrounds, resulting in elevated false positive rate.

Multi-locus mixed model approaches, such as MLMM [13] and rMVP [14, 15], have been proposed to address the limitations of single-locus or multi-locus models. MLMM uses the heritable variance estimate as a criterion to terminate forward inclusion. Subsequently, backward elimination is performed starting from the final forward model to allow for more thorough exploration of the model space, and it demonstrates strong performance in terms of FDR control and statistical power [13]. rMVP integrates both fixed effect and random effect models in its analysis, improving statistical power while reducing computing time. It decomposes the MLM into a fixed effect model and a random effect model, which are applied iteratively to enhance the power of detecting candidate genes associated with population structure [14], and leverages block matrix multiplication and parallel computation computing to accelerate association tests [15]. Nevertheless, due to its relatively high false positive rate, rMVP still employs Bonferroni correction.

To address the aforementioned limitations, we propose a two-stage GWAS algorithm that combines linear model fitting with kinship and sparse Bayesian learning, referred to as KinLmSBL. The first stage of KinLmSBL involves constructing a single-locus mixed linear model and strategically implementing a matrix transformation for polygenic background correction. Next, general linear regression is performed to scan high-throughput markers, identifying a small number of potentially associated SNPs for the second stage. Finally, a multi-locus model is constructed, and SBL is performed to identify the significant loci with FDR correction. Through extensive simulations, we demonstrate that KinLmSBL is fast, robust, and effectively controls for false positive associations in the presence of a polygenic background while also detecting low heritability variants, compared to four other methods. We further illustrate the utility of KinLmSBL by analyzing real data from three populations.

Results

A comparative analysis of the results was conducted using KinLmSBL, SBL, rMVP, fastGWA, and GEMMA, each evaluated separately on the simulation experiments and real datasets, including maize [16], rice [17], and the 16th QTL-MAS Workshop [18]. We adopted several evaluation criteria to ensure a standard for assessing these comparative results.

Simulation experiments

We first performed eighteen simulation experiments to validate KinLmSBL, the number of QTNs was set at 20 and 50, while the total heritability was set at 0.3, 0.5, and 0.8. Combinations were made among three sample sizes: 1000, 2000, and 4000, resulting in a total of 18 different combinations. Then, three simulation studies with sample sizes of 1000, 2000, and 4000 were conducted to assess the robustness of the method against model misspecification which means the simulation involves assessing the impact of unobserved SNPs. Each of these combinations was repeated 100 times to ensure the reliability and statistical significance of the experimental results.

Statistical power

In the experiments described above, the statistical power of each QTN was defined as the ratio of the number of samples in which the QTN was detected to the total number of samples, under the condition that the P-value was lower than the specified threshold.

Despite varying heritability levels, KinLmSBL showed remarkable stability. This characteristic was particularly prominent when compared with other methods. For instance, in the case of 2000 samples and 20 QTNs, when the heritability decreased from 0.8 to 0.3, the power of KinLmSBL only fell from 100% to 84.6%, a decrease of 15.4%. In contrast, the power of SBL decreased from 100% to 85.5%, a decline of 14.5%; rMVP fell from 95.9% to 69.5%, a decrease of 26.4%; fastGWA decreased from 77.7% to 42.5%, a reduction of 35.2%; and GEMMA fell from 97.5% to 48%, a decrease of 49.5%. This smaller magnitude of change suggested that the KinLmSBL method could maintain a relatively stable and higher power performance, even when faced with fluctuations in heritability (Fig. 1a, b, c, and Table S1).

KinLmSBL demonstrated strong advantages across different samples. For example, with a heritability of 0.5 and 20 QTNs, when the sample size decreased from 4,000 to 1,000, the power of KinLmSBL only dropped from 100 to 84.8%, almost reaching full-score power. In contrast, the power of SBL decreased from 100% to 82.9%, a reduction of 17.1%; rMVP descended from 91.2 to 63.8%, a decrease of 27.4%; fastGWA decreased from 91.2% to 25.7%, a drop of 65.5%; and GEMMA decreased from 97.2 to 37.5%, a reduction of 59.7% (Fig. 1b and Table S1). This showed KinLmSBL can efficiently use samples, quickly reaching high power.

In responding to changes in the number of QTNs, KinLmSBL demonstrated excellent robustness. Taking a heritability of 0.8 and a sample size of 4,000 as an example, when the number of QTNs changed from 20 to 50, the power of KinLmSBL only dropped from 100 to 98.4%, a decrease of 1.6%. In contrast, the power of SBL dropped significantly from 100% to 96.5%, a reduction of 3.5%; rMVP increased from 94.9 to 95.7%, an increase of 0.8%; fastGWA fell from 95.2 to 56.4%, a decline of 38.8%; and GEMMA dropped from 99.9 to 79.3%, a decrease of 20.6% (Fig. 2c, I, and Table S1). When the total heritability remained constant and the number of QTNs increased, the heritability of each QTN, on average, decreased. Consequently, it was natural for statistical power to reduce. However, the power of KinLmSBL decreased little compared to other methods.

In the simulation experiments involving 20 QTNs, KinLmSBL demonstrated excellent detection power for identifying a single QTN with lower heritability or minor allele frequency (MAF).

In situations where the heritability of a single QTN was less than 0.05, referred to as low heritability, such as in a sample size of 2000 with a total heritability of 0.3, five QTNs ranging from the 8th to the 12th had heritability values of different levels. The corresponding powers of KinLmSBL were from 45% to 95%. For these five QTNs, the variation range of SBL was from 39% to 93%, rMVP was from 1% to 92%, fastGWA was from 0% to 52%, and GEMMA was from 1% to 52% (Table S2). All these methods were less powerful than KinLmSBL. This trend remained consistent when the total heritability increased to 0.5. For a single QTN with a heritability greater than 5%, referred to as high heritability, such as QTN 2 (r²=5.21%) and QTN 12 (r²=5.07%), all five methods showed nearly identical statistical power in the scenario of a sample size of 4000 and a total heritability of 0.8, with the power of each method reaching 100% (Table S2).

When the MAF was less than 0.05, as in the case of QTN 6 (MAF = 0.04) with 2000 samples and a total heritability of 0.3, the power of KinLmSBL was 100%, while the detection powers of the other four methods were from 86% to 100%. In this scenario, KinLmSBL ranked the same as SBL (Table S2).

The results showed that KinLmSBL exhibited a strong detection ability in cases of relatively small sample sizes, low heritability, and low MAF.

False positive rate

All the false QTNs detected by the five methods in eighteen simulation experiments were used to calculate the empirical false positive rates (FPR) of these methods. The presentation and analysis of these results are provided below.

With the number of QTNs fixed at 20 and heritability set at 0.8, the results clearly showed the excellent performance of KinLmSBL compared to other methods across various heritability levels and sample sizes (Fig. 1f and Table S1). Among the three multi-locus methods, which include KinLmSBL, SBL, and rMVP, KinLmSBL demonstrated an exceptionally low FPR. As the sample size increased from 1,000 to 4,000, the FPR of KinLmSBL decreased from 0.05% to an impressively low 0.02%. In contrast, SBL had relatively high and stable FPR values, ranging from 0.4% to 0.79%. rMVP showed a sharp upward trend in FPR with the growth of the sample size, reaching as high as 9% at a sample size of 4,000 (Fig. 1f and Table S1). When compared with two single-locus methods, which include fastGWA and GEMMA, KinLmSBL still maintained its superiority in terms of FPR. For example, at the sample size of 4000, KinLmSBL had a much lower FPR of 0.02%, while fastGWA had an FPR of 0.04%, and GEMMA had an FPR of 0.03% (Fig. 1f and Table S1).

When the sample size and QTNs number were fixed, among the three multi-locus methods, KinLmSBL consistently had the lowest FPR. For example, at a sample size of 1,000 and with 20 QTNs, KinLmSBL showed an FPR decreasing from 0.09% to 0.05%, while SBL had an FPR ranging from 0.3% to 0.4%, and rMVP had an extremely high FPR increasing from 0.02% to 0.3% (Fig. 1d, e, f, and Table S1). When compared with two single-locus methods, in the case of heritability of 0.5, KinLmSBL had an FPR of 0.09%, fastGWA had an FPR of 0.00%, and GEMMA’s FPR was < 0.01% (Fig. 1e and Table S1). Although the FPR of the single-locus methods may have seemed relatively low, their statistical power was insufficient (26% and 38%). Meanwhile, the power of KinLmSBL achieved 85% (Fig. 1b and Table S1).

When the heritability was fixed at 0.8 with a sample size of 4,000, among the multi-locus methods, KinLmSBL stood out with its minimal FPR, particularly when there were 20 QTNs (Fig. 1f and Table S1). When the number of QTNs increased to 50, although the FPR of KinLmSBL rose, it remained far lower than that of SBL and rMVP (Fig. 1l and Table S1). For instance, the FPR of KinLmSBL was 0.04%, while rMVP had a relatively high FPR of almost 18%, and SBL had an FPR of 0.84% (Fig. 1l and Table S1). In comparison with single-locus methods, KinLmSBL’s low FPR gave it a significant advantage. For example, when there were 20 QTNs, KinLmSBL’s FPR was 0.02%, while the FPR of fastGWA was 0.04%, and the FPR of GEMMA was 0.03% (Fig. 1f and Table S1).

Overall, among the five methods, KinLmSBL had the lowest FPR when compared to the other two multi-locus methods. Although the FPR of KinLmSBL may have been slightly higher in some cases when compared to single-locus methods, its statistical power was significantly greater than that of single-locus methods, and these minor drawbacks did not overshadow its overall advantages.

F1 score

We used the F1 score to evaluate the five methods, which performed better as it increased, and Table S1 showed KinLmSBL’s consistent superiority across varying QTN counts, sample sizes, and heritability levels: with 50 QTNs and 0.3 heritability, increasing the sample size from 1,000 to 4,000 raised KinLmSBL’s F1 score from 0.14 to 0.48, SBL’s from 0.08 to 0.17, fastGWA’s from 0 to 0.28, and GEMMA’s from 0.01 to 0.31, while rMVP’s decreased from 0.08 to 0.03; with 50 QTNs and 1,000 samples, raising heritability from 0.3 to 0.8 boosted KinLmSBL’s from 0.14 to 0.53, SBL’s from 0.08 to 0.23, and GEMMA’s from 0.01 to 0.28, with rMVP’s dropping from 0.08 to 0.04 and fastGWA’s fluctuating near 0. In summary, KinLmSBL outperformed the other four methods with the most robust F1 scores and significant, stable improvements across all tested scenarios.

Robustness of model misspecification

The impact of model misspecification on FPR and computational time can be observed in these three scenarios: 1000, 2000, and 4000 sample sizes. Compared with the multi-locus methods SBL and rMVP, KinLmSBL’s FPR of 0.01% was only slightly higher than rMVP’s 0.00% with 4000 sample sizes. Meanwhile, its speed was second only to rMVP across all sample sizes. Overall, KinLmSBL was robust; even if the model was somewhat misspecified, it could still better control the FPR and maintain a high computational efficiency (Table S3).

Accuracy of estimated QTN effects

In what follows, we used the mean absolute error (MAE) of QTN effect estimates as a measure of accuracy, where a lower MAE indicated a better method. As listed in Table S2, KinLmSBL consistently demonstrated its advantages across various sample sizes and levels of heritability. When the sample size was 1,000 and heritability was 0.3, for QTN 7, KinLmSBL’s MAE of 0.37 was among the best of the evaluated methods and lower than SBL’s 0.38; for QTN 3, KinLmSBL, with an MAE of 0.42, outperformed SBL’s 0.52. When heritability increased to 0.5 at the same sample size, in QTN 4, KinLmSBL’s MAE of 0.38 was lower than SBL’s MAE of 0.41; in QTN 8, its MAE of 0.39 was better than SBL’s MAE of 0.57. In the case with a sample size of 2,000 and heritability of 0.3 for QTN 8, KinLmSBL’s MAE of 0.24 was lower than SBL’s 0.33; for QTN 11, its MAE of 0.21 was lower than SBL’s 0.26. When heritability was 0.5 for 2,000 samples, in QTN 2, KinLmSBL’s MAE of 0.32 was lower than rMVP’s 0.42; in QTN 8, its MAE of 0.27 was better than SBL’s 0.60. Overall, the accuracy of estimating QTN effects using KinLmSBL remained relatively high compared to the other methods.

Regarding the impact of varying heritability levels on computational time, KinLmSBL stood out prominently for its exceptional efficiency and stability. For instance, when dealing with 20 QTNs and 1,000 samples, as heritability increased from 0.3 to 0.8, the running time of KinLmSBL remained 3 min. In stark contrast, the time-consuming SBL method increased from 101 to 143 min, an increment of 42 min. Meanwhile, rMVP’s running time maintained 1 min. fastGWA’s running time ranged from 72 to 137 min, an increase of 65 min (Fig. 4a, b, c and Table S1). GEMMA’s running time maintained 65 min. At a heritability of 0.3, KinLmSBL was approximately 34 times faster than SBL, roughly 24 times faster than fastGWA, and around 22 times faster than GEMMA (Fig. 4a and Table S1). At a heritability of 0.8, KinLmSBL was roughly 48 times faster than SBL, approximately 46 times faster than fastGWA, and around 22 times faster than GEMMA (Fig. 4c and Table S1).

KinLmSBL showed remarkable adaptability to different sample sizes while maintaining a relatively low increase in running time compared to other methods. When considering the influence of sample size changes with a fixed heritability of 0.5 and 20 QTNs, as the sample size increased from 1000 to 4000, the running time of KinLmSBL gradually rose from 3 to 21 min, resulting in an increase of 18 min. In comparison, SBL’s running time ascended from 114 to 260 min, a massive increase of 146 min. rMVP’s running time increased from 1 to 6 min, an escalation of 5 min. fastGWA’s running time climbed from 1 to a staggering 121 h, an increase of almost 120 h. GEMMA’s running time increased from 65 to 497 min, an ascent of 6 h. At a sample size of 1000, KinLmSBL was about 40 times faster than SBL, 25 times faster than fastGWA, 22 times faster than GEMMA and 0.3 times slower than rMVP. At a sample size of 4000, it was around 12 times faster than SBL, 300 times faster than fastGWA, and 24 times faster than GEMMA (Fig. 4b and Table S1). For computational efficiency, a method that stated the larger the sample size, the smaller the magnitude of the increase in time would be, was a good one. Examples of such methods included KinLmSBL and rMVP.

Even when confronted with changes in the number of QTNs, KinLmSBL displayed outstanding stability in its running time, significantly outperforming other methods. With a fixed heritability of 0.8 and 4000 samples, as the number of QTNs increased from 20 to 50, the running time of KinLmSBL maintained 21 min. In contrast, SBL’s running time surged from 379 to 441 min, an increase of 1 h. rMVP’s running time grew from 6 to 8 min, an increment of 2 min. GEMMA’s running time was about 8 h (Fig. 4c, f and Table S1). When there were 20 QTNs, KinLmSBL was approximately 14 times faster than SBL, 390 times faster than fastGWA, and 25 times faster than GEMMA (Fig. 4c and Table S1). When the number of QTNs increased to 50, KinLmSBL was about 21 times faster than SBL, 400 times faster than fastGWA, and 25 times faster than GEMMA (Fig. 4f and Table S1).

In conclusion, a comprehensive analysis of the running time data under diverse conditions of heritability, sample sizes, and the number of QTNs showed that KinLmSBL is only slightly slower than rMVP. Nevertheless, KinLmSBL has indisputably demonstrated remarkable efficiency and stability.

Discussion

Proposal of the method

In recent years, GWAS has emerged as a pivotal approach for analyzing high-dimensional genomic data. Numerous studies have demonstrated that controlling for population structure and polygenic background significantly improves the detection power while reducing false positive rates [1]. The multi-locus method SBL, proposed by Wang et al. [12], enabling efficient processing of high-dimensional genomic data and maintaining high power for detecting loci with low heritability. However, when solving the hierarchical linear model (7), SBL only accounts for covariates and fails to incorporate polygenic backgrounds, leading to elevated false positive rates (Table S1, Fig. 1d, e, f, j, k, l). Consequently, Bonferroni correction is still required when setting significance thresholds for SBL. To address this limitation, we fully integrated polygenic background correction into a mixed linear model and proposed a two-stage multi-locus method based on SBL, termed KinLmSBL. This method leverages model transformation [9], the GLM scanning function [15], and FDR correction [29], which collectively enable effective control of false positive rates while achieving higher statistical power compared to the original SBL method.​

Performance of the method

The selection of a threshold in the first stage is extremely important, as it may impact the different outcomes produced by the two-stage method. Consequently, we consider three thresholds: 0.005, 0.01, and 0.05. Through the analysis of simulated data, as shown in Table S9, the results indicate that when the threshold is set at 0.01, the power is relatively high, the false positive rate is low, and the speed is fast. Therefore, 0.01 is selected as the criterion for entering the second stage, which was confirmed in the previous study [30].

KinLmSBL exhibited the fastest computation speed among the five methods in real data analyses (Table 1) and ranked second only to rMVP in simulation experiments (Tables S1 and S3, and Fig. 4), with computational efficiency attributed to three key optimizations. First, KinLmSBL implements block matrix multiplication and parallel-accelerated association tests for markers via the MVP.GLM function from the rMVP package. In addition, we compiled SBL’s pure R code into Rcpp code, resulting in a substantial speed improvement. Finally, the model transformation process involved the multiplication of two matrices, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathbf{C}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\boldsymbol{G}$$\end{document}, in our program (see model (5)). When the number of markers reached a substantial magnitude, the transformation process consumed a significant amount of time. We use the following strategy: since \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\boldsymbol{G}$$\end{document} is a genotype matrix that contains a considerable number of 0-valued elements, it can first be converted into a sparse matrix, denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{\boldsymbol{G}}$$\end{document}. Then, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbf{C}\tilde{\boldsymbol{G}}$$\end{document} will save more time than \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathbf{C}\boldsymbol{G}$$\end{document}. The Matrix package in R supports fast sparse matrix conversion. Generally, the lower the MAF of SNPs, the sparser the matrix and the faster the conversion process.​

Notably, we included population structure matrices as covariates in model (1) for maize and rice datasets. For computational efficiency in GWAS, covariate effects estimated from the null model can be projected out prior to fitting for specific SNPs. This allows the fitting of SNP-specific models with only a few parameters, thereby streamlining the analysis [31, 32]. We adopted this strategy.

An interesting aspect emerging from the real data analysis is that KinLmSBL maintains high detection proficiency even for variants with low MAF. For example, in the analysis of the FTS trait in maize, we detected the GRMZM2G069525 gene near 16,873,544 bp on chromosome 2 (Table S4) for low-frequency variants (0.01 < MAF < 0.05). For the PH trait in rice, we detected three low-frequency variants: the genes LOC_Os02g52230, LOC_Os02g52340, and LOC_Os02g52480 near 32,079,869 bp on chromosome 2, accounting for 10.7% of the total (Table 2 and S5).

Prospects of the method

Although the simulated phenotypic data were generated from real genotypic data of maize, the simulation experiments comprehensively verified the broad applicability of KinLmSBL. Firstly, the simulated phenotypic sample size was set to 1000, 2000, and 4000; the number of SNPs was 50,000, the number of QTNs was set to 20 and 50, along with total heritability of 0.3, 0.5, and 0.8. A total of 18 simulation scenarios were designed, and situations of low MAF and low heritability were also considered (Tables S7 and S8). Three special simulation studies were conducted with sample sizes of 1000, 2000, and 4000 to assess the method’s robustness against model misspecification, particularly evaluating the impact of unobserved SNPs. Secondly, four real datasets were selected from maize, rice, and humans (Table 1; Fig. 5). Collectively, these experimental designs and data selections demonstrate the broad application potential of KinLmSBL.

Compared to single-locus methods such as fastGWA and GEMMA, KinLmSBL eliminates the need for strict multiple testing corrections and identifies more trait-associated loci. In contrast to multi-locus methods like SBL, which is also a Bayesian approach, KinLmSBL accounts for the polygenic background and employs matrix transformation and rapid GLM scanning to reduce false positives while maintaining high computational efficiency. Notably, rMVP and SBL may miss potential associated loci which strict Bonferroni correction.

KinLmSBL provides a novel two-stage GWAS method for high-dimensional genomic data, integrating GLM scanning and SBL. Despite its significant advantages in GWAS detection power and computational efficiency, the application scope of KinLmSBL can be further expanded. Future optimizations may focus on three key directions: incorporating gene-gene and gene-environment interaction modules into the sparse Bayesian framework, where synchronous identification of main effects and interaction effects is achieved via dedicated prior distributions to enhance the interpretability of heritability; constructing a hierarchical model of ‘genome–intermediate molecular phenotype–target trait’ and introducing multi-omics weight coefficients to clarify the functional pathways of associated loci; and adopting LD block-based sparse learning combined with parallel computing to reduce memory usage and iteration time for large-sample, high-dimensional data, thus enabling adaptation to larger-scale GWAS analyses. These extensions will further enrich KinLmSBL’s application scenarios and provide more comprehensive support for dissecting the genetic mechanisms of complex traits.

Methods

Overview of KinLmSBL

In this section, we will give a detailed description of KinLmSBL. It is a two-stage GWAS algorithm. In the first stage, it constructs a mixed linear model and corrects for the polygenic background using FASTmrEMMA’s matrix transformation [9]. It then employs rMVP’s MVP.GLM function to scan high-throughput SNPs [15], screening for a small number of potentially associated SNPs for the next stage. In the second stage, it constructs a hierarchical linear model, applies the SBL algorithm to estimate marker effects and construct the Wald test statistic [12], and implements FDR correction [29]. Eventually, significant loci related to the trait are identified.

Linear regression scan

To further reduce a large number of markers based on the transformed model (5) described above, we conducted regression analysis on the high-throughput SNPs using a linear model fitting function called MVP.GLM [15] from the rMVP package in R to expedite the single-locus scan. Benefiting from the block matrix multiplication and parallel computation strategies, the MVP.GLM function significantly reduces computation time for the least square estimation of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\beta\:$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma^{2}$$\end{document} and runs dozens of times faster than the lm function in R.

Approaches for comparisons

When verifying the advantages of KinLmSBL, we selected four comprehensive benchmark methods. These included two prevalent multi-locus methods, rMVP and SBL, the latter of which is also a GWAS method under the Bayesian framework. Four methods also included two typical single-locus methods, fastGWA and GEMMA. We conducted comparisons and verification using both simulated and real data.

SBL

SBL is a multi-locus method that can quickly process high-dimensional genomic data [12]. It also maintains a relatively high detection ability even for loci with low heritability. However, when solving model (7), it considers only covariates and fails to account for the pairwise genetic relatedness among individuals. As a result, it has a relatively high false positive rate. Therefore, the Bonferroni correction is still adopted when setting the significance threshold. SBL can be run using the sbl package in R software.

rMVP

rMVP is a memory-efficient and parallel-accelerated R package that integrates the FarmCPU method, which is a multi-locus mixed model divided into two parts: the fixed effect model (FEM) and the random effect model (REM), which are used iteratively until a stage of convergence is reached [14, 15]. The FEM includes the samples to be tested, single-marker scanning, and the associated loci (pseudo-QTNs), which are used as covariates to control the false positive rate. To avoid overfitting of the FEM, the REM is used to estimate the pseudo-QTNs, and the kinship matrix is defined using these pseudo-QTNs. The iteration terminates when the pseudo-QTNs remain unchanged. However, the Bonferroni correction is still adopted in rMVP due to its relatively high false positive rate. rMVP can be implemented using the rMVP package in R software.

fastGWA

fastGWA is a single-locus MLM method that controls for population stratification using principal components and for relatedness through a sparse genetic relationship matrix for GWAS [8]. It uses the Cholesky decomposition of sparse matrices to propose an algorithm based on lattice search to solve restricted maximum likelihood functions, replacing matrix determinant and inverse operations. fastGWA utilizes sparse matrix technology to further reduce the memory and computational burdens. However, its computational efficiency depends on the density of the sparse genetic related matrix. fastGWA runs on the GCTA program.

GEMMA

GEMMA is a benchmark method of single-locus MLM scanning GWAS[6], an accelerated version of EMMA, which is several times faster than the EMMA method. Zhou et al.[6] pointed out in their article that the objective functions of each round of optimization iteration can be estimated by using triple recursion, and all the iterative processes only involve scalar multiplication, and the calculation does not depend on the number of samples. GEMMA runs on the gemma software.Regarding the significance threshold, KinLmSBL uses FDR correction, while other comparative methods employ the Bonferroni correction.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Simulations

The simulation is based on genotype data sourced from maize [16] and includes 10 chromosomes, 4476 individuals, and 681,257 markers. Before conducting GWAS, genetic quality control was required for the maize data. Firstly, we used PLINK software [2] with a recall rate of 0.95. Then, we used Beagle 5.1 software [33] to conduct genotype imputation and phasing of genotypes for comparison between individuals.

At a MAF greater than 0.05, referred to as common variants [34], 4500 markers were selected for each of chromosomes 1 to 10. The starting position was chosen randomly. Once the starting position was fixed, 4500 consecutive markers were selected. To assess the detection performance of low-frequency variants, defined as having a MAF between 0.01 and 0.05 [34], a subset of SNPs was selected, with 500 markers chosen for each chromosome from 1 to 10. The starting position was the same as that used for the common variants. After determining the starting position, 500 consecutive markers were selected. Additionally, 4000 individuals were randomly selected from a pool of 4476. A large population for simulation was then generated, consisting of these 4000 individuals and 50,000 SNPs, with the linkage disequilibrium structure being approximately like that of the original population.

The phenotype was generated using the linear model \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{y} = \boldsymbol{\mu} + \sum_{i=1}^{s} \boldsymbol{x}_{i}\beta_{i} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}$$\end{document} is the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:n\times\:1$$\end{document} genotype vector from the population consisting of 4000 individuals and 50,000 SNPs described above. The number of QTNs (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:s$$\end{document}) was set to 20 and 50, and their total heritability (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{h}^{2}$$\end{document}) were set to 0.3, 0.5, and 0.8, with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta_{i}$$\end{document} representing the simulated effect size of each QTN. The term \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol\varepsilon \, \sim \mathrm{MVN}(\boldsymbol 0,10 \times \,\mathbf I)$$\end{document}, while the total mean \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\boldsymbol{\mu\:}$$\end{document} was set to 10. Each sample was analyzed using the KinLmSBL, SBL, rMVP, fastGWA, and GEMMA methods for comparison. FDR was defined as the proportion of false positives among all detected markers. A detected QTN within 10 kb of the simulated QTN was considered a true QTN. The Type I error was calculated as the ratio of the number of false positive effects to the total number of zero effects (FPR) considered in the full model. F1 score is defined as the harmonic meaning of the precision and recall. To measure the bias of QTN effect estimate, the mean absolute error (MAE) was defined as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{MAE} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=1}^{N} |\hat{\beta}_{j} - \beta_{j}|$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\beta}_{j}$$\end{document} is the estimate of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\beta\:$$\end{document} for each QTN in the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:j$$\end{document}th sample.

Real datasets in maize, rice, and QTL-MAS workshop

To illustrate the application of KinLmSBL in genetic research, we downloaded three real data sets. One data set is from maize [16], which we used in the simulation experiments, and we also used it directly for analysis. The other two data sets are from rice [17] and the 16th QTL-MAS Workshop [18]. In total, we analyzed four traits: flowering time measured as days to silk (FTS) of maize, plant height (PH) of rice, flowering time (FT) of rice, and T2 of the 16th QTL-MAS Workshop data. Specifically, we applied an inverse normal transformation to the original phenotypic data of maize.

In the maize dataset, the original data includes 4476 samples and 681,257 SNPs, after genetic quality control [2] and imputation [33], we obtained 2279 samples and 239,322 SNPs for the FTS trait. We used Admixture software [35] to compute its population structure and selected the optimal Q-matrix based on cross-validation error. In Figure S1a, we examined its LD decay pattern [36] and searched for genes located within 10 kb of significant SNPs associated with the FTS trait on the website https://maizegdb.org/.

In the rice dataset, the total sample size was 413, and the number of markers was 44 K [17]. The first four principal components were selected to correct for population structure, and genes associated with the PH and FT traits were identified at significant sites within 200 kb, according to the LD decay plot of the rice data (Figure S1b). Then, search for relevant references about the mentioned traits on the website www.ricedata.cn to verify the associated genes.

The 16th QTL-MAS Workshop consists of 4000 individuals and 10,000 SNPs [18], with 3000 individuals having complete phenotypes. For the process of mining genes in the 16th QTL-MAS Workshop: Check whether the set QTNs exist among all the significant loci, and these QTNs are the genes related to the T2 trait.

All real and simulated data analyses were conducted under the Linux system. The CPU configuration was as follows: 314.361 GB of running memory, 96 cores, and the model was Intel Xeon Platinum 8168 CPU @ 2.70 GHz. KinLmSBL employed the rMVP package and Rcpp code from the sblgwas function, which was compiled from the sbl package; SBL utilized the sbl package; rMVP used the rMVP package; GEMMA employed the gemma software package; and fastGWA utilized the GCTA software package.
