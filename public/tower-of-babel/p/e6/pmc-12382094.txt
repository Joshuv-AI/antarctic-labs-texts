Preparation and characterization of a iRGD-modified recombinant spider silk particles for antitumor polypeptide drug delivery into cancer cells
Authors: Ben Wang, Hongbo Li, Ying Chen, Zhi Chen, Pingping Li, Xi Zhang, Xiaoji Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpider silk is highly attractive material because of its superior mechanical properties and excellent biocompatibility, enabling it to self-assemble into a wide range of morphological structures for drug delivery system. However, most spider silk particles developed as drug carriers are based on complex repetitive domains of spider silk proteins and exhibit relatively large particle sizes (> 300 nm), which limits their biomedical applications. In this study, we engineered a novel recombinant spider silk protein (NC-iRGD) by integrating terminal domains derived from major ampullate silk and the tumor-penetrating peptide iRGD. The silk particles were generated by mixing with a high-concentration potassium phosphate buffer and exhibited an average particle size of approximately 170 nm, which is smaller than that of other reported spider silk particles. Under incubation of silk particles in the drug solution, a 90% loading efficiency for the peptide drug (ChMAP-28) were determined. The cytotoxicity result showed that NC-iRGD particles displayed excellent biocompatibility and high drug loading efficiency in the neutral pH and low ionic strength. The release of ChMAP-28 was shown to be dependent on the ionic strength and pH of the release buffer. Additionally, NC-iRGD demonstrated enhanced tumor penetration and greater cytotoxicity against cancer cells compared to NC particles due to its iRGD sequence. Overall, the high drug loading capacity, controlled-release capability, and improved tumor penetration of NC-iRGD particles make them a promising novel drug delivery system for targeting polypeptide therapeutics to tumor microenvironments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12896-025-01023-y.Keywords: Spider silk particle, iRGD, Antitumor polypeptide, Drug delivery, Controlled release

Introduction

In recent years, particulate drug carriers have gained significant attention in cancer therapy, largely owing to their benefits in regulating particle size, structural design, biodegradation speed, drug encapsulation efficiency, and release behavior [1–3]. However, a key challenge in using particulate carriers for cancer chemotherapy is the insufficient intracellular release of drugs, particularly within target organelles such as lysosomes [4]. To address this issue, researchers have developed intelligent delivery systems that can modulate carrier behavior in response to specific stimuli [5]. Among these strategies, pH responsiveness is widely employed because the pH value varies significantly within the intracellular microenvironment [6–8].

Particulate carriers are internalized into cells through endocytic mechanisms, and their subsequent intracellular trafficking is closely determined by the particular endocytic route involved [9]. Although the mechanisms of endocytosis are not fully understood, drug carriers typically traffic to lysosomes where they undergo acidification and enzymatic degradation. Interestingly, polyhistidine (an affinity tag commonly used for protein purification) has been employed to modify polymer carriers, conferring pH sensitivity [10]. However, the limited biodegradability of these polymers may lead to side effects such as lysosomal storage disorders. In fact, many natural biomolecules, such as spider silk and silkworm silk, exhibit pH responsiveness near their isoelectric point (pI), enabling drug loading and release through changes in net or surface charge [11–13]. A model based on electrostatic interactions has been proposed to explain the pH-dependent release of positively charged drugs. Therefore, selecting an appropriate pI to optimize electrostatic attraction or repulsion in drug delivery systems is a viable strategy.

Spider silks, formed through the assembly of various protein combinations, allow spiders to carry out a range of functions such as prey capture, locomotion, and protection of offspring [14]. The unique mechanical advantages of silk-based biomaterials, combined with their inherent biocompatibility and biodegradability, have garnered significant attention for drug delivery applications [15]. In contrast to regenerated silk fibroin, the use of genetic engineering has been widely explored to achieve precise, bottom-up regulation of recombinant spidroins, encompassing their composition, sequence, structure, and functional properties [16–19]. By modifying gene codons, positively or negatively charged amino acid residues can be precisely regulated to achieve the desired isoelectric point (pI) [20, 21]. Although silk particles hold great potential as drug carriers, most reported silk particles are derived from the repetitive domains of spider silk proteins. Due to the absence of terminal domains, these recombinant silk proteins tend to form inhomogeneous particles with relatively large sizes as a result of their uncontrollable self-assembly properties. In contrast, the terminal domains of spider silk proteins, which function as molecular switches controlling molecular assembly, have been rarely explored for drug delivery applications. On the other hand, functionalization of silk particles through the incorporation of functional peptides, such as cell-penetrating peptides, offers the potential for targeted drug delivery to deeper tumor regions. Among these cell-penetrating peptides, iRGD, a novel nine-amino-acid peptide containing the Arg-Gly-Asp (RGD) motif, demonstrates high binding affinity to αvβ integrins, which are enriched in tumor vasculature, and offers notable advantages in drug delivery systems, including low toxicity to normal cells and targeted release [22]. The tumor-targeting capability of iRGD is significantly enhanced because it specifically binds to the neuropilin-1 (NRP-1) receptor, thereby triggering NRP-1-dependent endocytosis and promoting improved tumor penetration [23].

In this study, we present a novel recombinant spider silk particle system designed for positively charged drug delivery, including applications involving polypeptide drugs. The recombinant spider silk protein, abbreviated as NC, is only composed of the N-terminal (NT) and C-terminal (CT) domains of the major ampullate silk protein 1 (MaSp1) from the orb-weaving spider Araneus ventricosus. We further engineered a silk variant (NC-iRGD) to enhance its penetration efficiency through the incorporation of iRGD at the C-terminus. The NC and NC-iRGD particles were investigated in terms of their morphology, size, zeta potential, and toxicity against cells. Furthermore, the loading and release of positively charged antitumor peptide (ChMAP-28) were investigated.

Materials and methods

Construction of recombinant plasmids

For NC construct, the primers used for NT were NT-F (5’- GGT CAG TCA CCA TGG CAA AAC-3’) and NT-R (5’- CAC GGC ATT TGC CTG AGC-3’), while primers for CT were CT-F (5’- CAG GCA AAT GCC GTG TCA GCA TCA TCT GCT GCT GCA G-3’) and CT-R (5’- TTA AGA AAG AGC TTG GTA AAC AG-3’). To generate NC, the NT and CT were used for overlap PCR, and the products were gel extracted, purified, ligated into pET-32a vector and transformed into Eecherichia coli strain DH5α. In terms of NC-iRGD, the NC-iRGD gene was synthesized by Beijing Tsingke Biotech Co., Ltd. The correct recombinant plasmids were confirmed by nucleotide sequencing.

Protein expression and purification

The plasmids were transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3) for expression study. The cells were grown in LB medium with ampicillin (0.1 mg/mL) at 37℃ until OD600 was 0.6–0.8. Next, protein expression was induced with 0.3 mM isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactoside (IPTG) (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd), and the cells were grown at 37℃ for further 3 h. Following induction, the cells were collected at 6000 r.p.m, resuspended in lysis buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 300 mM NaCl, pH 8.0), and then lysed using a Pressure Cell Press JN-3000 Plus (JNBIO, China). As both proteins were found in the insoluble fraction, the pellets obtained by centrifugation were washed twice with 4 M urea and 1% Triton X-100, and then resuspended in lysis buffer containing 8 M urea sonicated at 250 W on ice for 20 min to ensure complete solubilization, and subsequently centrifuged again to collect the supernatant. The protein solution was dialyzed in lysis buffer and then centrifuged to remove precipitated protein. Finally, the purified protein was dialyzed against lysis buffer at 4℃ and then analyzed by SDS-PAGE to determine the purity.

Particle preparation

The purified protein NC was dialyzed against 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0) at room temperature using dialysis membranes with a molecular weight cut-off of 3 kDa. Subsequently, the concentration of the resulting NC solution was determined by UV-Vis spectroscopy at 276 nm, and then was used for particle preparation. Brifely, 50 µL of NC solution (~ 0.1 mg/mL) was rapidly mixed by piptte with 1 mL of 2 M potassium phosphate buffer (pH 8.0) and incubated for 1 h at 25℃. Next, the particles suspension was dialyzed against water overnight, centrifuged at 12,000 r.p.m for 30 min, and suspended in ultrapure water. The particle concentration in mg/mL was determined gravimetrically.

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) and zeta-potential analysis

The samples were diluted to a concentration of 0.01 mg/mL in the same buffer and analyzed by dynamic light scattering (DLS) using Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Instruments, UK) to determine the particle size and zeta potential of NC particles. All measurements were conducted at 25 °C in triplicate.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The samples were pipetted onto a conductive silicon wafer and allowed to dry in the air, followed by coating with platinum for 120 s with an ion sputter. Next the surface morphology of silk particle was analyzed using a Hitachi SU8010 instrument (Japan) at a voltage of 3 kV at room temperature.

Stability of recombinant silk particles

The stability of NC and NC-iRGD particles was analyzed at different pH (5.5 and 7.4) and at different ionic strength (50, 100, and 154 mM NaCl). Furthermore, samples were analyzed directly after redispersion as well as after 1 h and 24 h for particle size and zeta-potential using the Zetasizer Nano ZS. To study the long-time stability of NC and NC-iRGD particles, the NC and NC-iRGD stock dispersion in ultrapure water was stored at 4 °C over a period of 6 months.

Cytotoxicity study

Immortalized human skin fibroblasts (Wuxi Newgain Biotechnology Co.Ltd) were cultured in DMEM medium with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (JYK-FBS-300, Inner Mongolia Jinyuankang Biotechnology Co., Ltd) and 80 µg/mL gentamycin, and grown at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Subsequently, the cells were seeded into 96-well plates at 2 × 104 per well and incubated overnight. Next, varying concentrations of silk particles were added to the cultures and then incubated for 72 h. Meanwhile, cells without particles were used as a negative control. MTT and CCK-8 assays were employed to evaluate the in vitro cytotoxicity. For MTT assay, 50 µL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL) was added to each well. After a 4 h incubation period, the medium was carefully aspirated, and the insoluble purple formazan crystals were dissolved in 200 µL of DMSO. Then, the absorbance of the solution was measured using a ELX808IV (Bio-Tek Instruments, USA) microplate reader. For the CCK-8 assay, a medium supplemented with 10% CCK-8 solution was added to each well, followed by incubation for 2 h. Subsequently, the absorbance of each well was measured at 450 nm using an ELX808IV microplate reader (Bio-Tek Instruments, USA). The cell viability (%) was calculated as follows:

Drug loading and release

To investigate the applicability of NC particles as drug carrier for peptide drugs, the antitumor peptide ChMAP-28 was chosen as a model peptide to examine the loading behavior and release behavior in the release buffer. Loading with ChMAP-28 was performed in 10 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.4). The silk particles suspension was mixed with drugs, treated by gentle agitation for 1 h at room temperature to obtained the desired w/w-ratio (%) of drugs to silk particles (1 mg/mL). Next, the drugs-loaded silk particles were collected by centrifugation. The release buffer was removed after centrifugation at predetermined time points and replaced with fresh release buffer. The supernatant collected by centrifugation was analyzed for total protein content using the BCA protein assay kit. The experiment was performed at least in triplicate over a period of 30 days. All release experiments were conducted with a total payload of 20% (w/w) ChMAP-28. To determine whether the protein detected in the release media originated from ChMAP-28 or the degradation products of silk particles, NC and NC-iRGD particles without loaded ChMAP-28 were subjected to the same experimental procedures and analysis as described above.

In vitro cellular uptake of ChMAP-28 loaded silk particles

The cellular uptake of silk particles into A549 cells was observed using confocal laser scanning microscope (Nikon, Japan). The silk particles were prepared and then loaded with FITC-labeled ChMAP-28. In brief, 200 mg of ChMAP-28 and 10 mg of FITC (Shanghai Acmec Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd) were dissolved in 50 mL of 20 mM borate buffer (pH 8.5). The fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) solution was then added to the ChMAP-28 solution, and the mixture was stirred for an additional 16 h at room temperature. Afterwards, the solution was dialyzed against highly purified water to remove unbound FITC. The resulting FITC-labeled ChMAP-28 solution was freeze-dried and subsequently used in the loading procedure. The loading experiments were performed with a total payload of 20% (w/w) ChMAP-28. The A549 cells were cultured in a confocal dish at a density of 2 × 105 cells per dish and maintained at 37℃ for 12 h. Subsequently, the cells were washed three times with PBS, incubated with FITC-ChMAP-28 loaded silk particles for an additional 4–6 h, and then fixed with 4% (w/v) glutaraldehyde for 15 min. After another three washes with PBS, the nuclei of the cells were stained with DAPI for 15 min. Excess DAPI was removed by washing with PBS, and subsequently, 1 mL of PBS was added to maintain the cell morphology.

Flow cytometry

A total of 2 × 105 A549 and SKBR-3 cells were seeded into a 6-well plate and incubated with silk particles without drugs, NC-ChMAP-28 particles, and NC-iRGD-ChMAP-28 particles (1 µM) in DMEM containing 10% FBS for 24 h. The silk particles loaded with ChMAP-28 were prepared with a total payload of 20% (w/w) relative to the weight of the particles. Subsequently, the culture medium was replaced with DMEM supplemented with 2% FBS, and the cells were further cultured for 3 h. Fluorescently labeled probes were then added, and the cells were incubated for an additional 1 h. Cell death analysis using Annexin V-FITC/propidium iodide (PI) double staining (KeyGEN BioTECH) and quantification of dead cells by flow cytometry were performed 2 h later. All flow cytometry experiments were repeated at least three independent biological replicates.

Western-blotting analysis

Cells from the aforementioned treatment groups were lysed using RIPA buffer supplemented with a 1% protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma, USA). Protein concentrations were determined by a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal amounts of heat-denatured proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were blocked for 2 h before being incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (1:1,000), followed by a 2 h incubation at 37 °C with an appropriate secondary antibody.

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were collected, dissociated using 0.25% trypsin, and then counted before being seeded into glass-bottom dishes at a density of 2 × 10⁴ cells per dish. Following 24 and 48 h of culture, the cells underwent immunofluorescence staining. The stained samples were then visualized and imaged using a laser scanning confocal microscope.

Mitochondrial membrane potential assay

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well and incubated overnight. Following treatment with different experimental groups, the cells were harvested via trypsinization and centrifugation, and subsequently washed with PBS. The untreated cells and CCCP (Carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenyl hydrazone)-treated cells were set as negative and positive controls, respectively. The JC-1 dye was added to resuspend cell pellets, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. The samples were then centrifuged for 5 minutes, washed twice with assay buffer, and resuspended. The results were presented as the ratio of red to green fluorescence.

Comet assay

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 4 × 105 cell/well and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Subsequently, cells treated with different groups were collected by trypsinization and centrifugation. An 80 µL aliquot of 1% normal-melting-point agarose (NMA) was prepared, pipetted onto microscope slides, covered with a coverslip, and allowed to solidify at 4 °C. Next, the cell suspension was mixed with 0.7% low-melting-point agarose (LMA) at a ratio of 1:10 (v/v), and the mixture was immediately pipetted onto pre-coated microscope slides, covered with a coverslip, and allowed to solidify at 4 °C for 10 min. After complete lysis at 4 °C for 2.5 h, the coverslip was washed twice with ddH2O and subsequently incubated in alkaline electrophoresis buffer for DNA denaturation. Following electrophoresis, the coverslip was neutralized in neutralization buffer for 20 min, washed with cold PBS, stained with ethidium bromide (EB) for 10 s, and washed again with ddH2O twice before being analyzed using a Nikon Eclipse 90i fluorescence microscope.

Statistics

Statistical analysis was conducted by Prism 9.4 using one-way ANOVA. Statistical significance between groups was considered if the P-value was < 0.05. An asterisk denotes statistical significance as follows: **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001. All data are indicated by mean values ± standard deviation (SD).

Results

Production and purification of both NC and NC-iRGD

The recombinant silk proteins NC and NC-iRGD were successfully produced by E. coli and purified as soluble protein using low concentration of urea and 1% Triton X-100. NC-iRGD was constructed by the fusion of N- and C-terminal domains of A. ventricosus MaSp1 and the iRGD peptide (Fig. 1A). The amino acid sequence of NC-iRGD are shown in Fig. 1B. Due to the high GC content of the MaSp1 repeat unit, recombinant proteins containing these repeats typically exhibit low expression yields [21, 24]. In this study, NC and NC-iRGD exhibited high yields, with 80 mg per liter of shake flask culture for NC and 75 mg for NC-iRGD. Furthermore, SDS-PAGE analysis showed both NC and NC-iRGD were purified to homogeneity (Fig. 1C).

The N- and C-terminal sequences are highlighted in orange and blue, respectively. The iRGD sequence is indicated in italic. (C) 12% SDS-PAGE gel analysis of purified NC and NC-iRGD proteins

Preparation and colloidal stability of silk particles

NC and NC-iRGD particles were successfully produced in 2 M patassium phosphate buffer (pH 8) (Fig. 2). The NC and NC-iRGD particles exhibited similar sizes of 176 ± 3.5 nm and 180 ± 6 nm, respectively, which are smaller than those of previously reported silk particles derived from the repetitive domains of spider silk (Fig. 2).

Morphology of NC and NC-iRGD particles

The colloidal stability of NC and NC-iRGD particles at different pH and ionic strength was analyzed. We found that both NC and NC-iRGD particles were stable in ultrapure water and at pH 7.4 over a period of 48 h, whereas a decrease in pH towards or below their isoelectric point resulted in obvious particle agglomeration (Fig. 3A). Furthermore, their particle size remained almost constant at pH 7.4 when the ionic strength increased to 154 mM. In contrast, both NC and NC-iRGD particles exhibited poor colloidal stability at pH 5.5 across all tested ionic strengths (Fig. 3B). For NC particles, the zeta-potential value decreased from − 28 mV at pH 7.4 and 50 mM NaCl to -10 mV at pH 5.5 and 154 mM NaCl (Fig. 4A). In the case of NC-iRGD particles, the zeta-potential value decreased from − 27 mV at pH 7.4 and 50 mM NaCl to -6 mV at pH 5.5 and 154 mM NaCl (Fig. 4B). Taken together, these results are consistent with the measured sizes of the two silk particles under varying pH and ionic strength conditions. In addition, the zeta-potential adopted positive values when the pH (4) was below the isoelectric point (pH 4.6) of both two silk proteins (Fig. 4A and B). The long-term stability of NC and NC-iRGD particles in ultrapure water was analyzed after storage at 4 °C for 6 months, during which the particles maintained a stable size (Figure S1).

Cytotoxicity

The cytotoxicity of NC and NC-iRGD particles was analyzed using Immortalized human skin fibroblasts at pH 7.4. The biocompatibility of both NC and NC-iRGD particles showed no significant differences, with cell viability remaining above 80% at all tested particle concentrations, including the highest one (Fig. 5A and B). Meanwhile, both silk particles showed no toxicity toward normal cells as assessed by the CCK-8 assay, which is consistent with the MTT results (Fig. 5C and D).

Drug loading

These two silk particles exhibited similar loading and loading efficiency. The results showed that it was possible to load more than 55% (w/w) by simple incubation of these two particles in a peptide containing phosphate buffer solution, and its loading efficiencies remained at least 50% for w/w-ratios ranging from 2 to 60%, indicating a very effective loading of ChMAP-28 and a strong interaction between silk particles and ChMAP-28 (Fig. 6A and B). However, the loading efficiency decreased significantly when the drug-to-silk particle weight ratio exceeded 40%. Previous studies showed that the charge of silk particles loaded with drugs can be regulated from the values of w/w-ratio, which can influence the loading process. Thus, we hypothesize that the decrease in loading efficiency may be attributed to the reduced surface charge density of the silk particles. On the other hand, considering the relatively high loading efficiency of both silk particle types at a weight ratio of 20% (w/w), subsequent experiments were conducted using this ratio.

To test our hypothesis, the zeta-potential of silk particles loaded with drugs at different w/w-ratio was analyzed. The results showed that loading both two particles with ChMAP-28 results in only a slight decrease in the zeta potential of the silk particles when the w/w-ratio was below 40%, while the charge of particles exhibit a significant change in the zeta-potential of particles exceed to a loading of 40% (Fig. 6C and D). The influence of ionic strength on the loading and loading efficiencies was also investigated. The result showed that an increase in ionic strength from 50 to 154 mM led to a similar decrease in loading and loading efficiencies for both particles (Fig. 6E, F and G, and 6H). Based on these results, both silk particles exhibit similar material properties, indicating that the addition of the iRGD sequence at the C-terminus did not significantly alter the physicochemical properties of NC.

Cumulative drug release

The in vitro release of drugs from both silk particles was investigated. The pH and ionic strength of the release buffer was varied to evaluate their impact on the release profile of ChMAP-28 from 20% (w/w) NC and NC-iRGD particles. At pH 5.5 and pH 6.5 at least 70% of ChMAP-28 was released from NC particles and 80% from NC-iRGD particles within the first 24 h (Fig. 7A and B). The release profiles reached the plateau by 5 days, with nearly 95% cumulative release of ChMAP-28 from both silk particles (Fig. 7A and B). The release behavior of NC and NC-iRGD particles within 24 h was quite similar to that reported for protein-loaded silk particles. However, no significant amounts of ChMAP-28 were detected at pH 7.4 even after 30 days, indicating that electrostatic interactions remain very strong between silk particles and ChMAP-28 within this pH range (Fig. 7A and B). Given the change of zeta-potential of NC and NC-iRGD particles at different pH levels, the pH-dependent release of ChMAP-28 may be attributed to the weakening of electrostatic interactions. The acidic extracellular tumor microenvironment (pH ∼6.5-7.0) provides a key targeting cue distinct from physiological conditions (pH ∼7.4), enabling selective drug release from pH-responsive NC/NC-iRGD particles upon tumor accumulation. Besides from pH, the release results showed that the change of ionic strength of the release buffer had a very strong influence on the resulting release profile (Fig. 7C and D). On the other hand, no protein content was detected in the supernatant from the release medium incubated with these two silk particles in the absence of loaded ChMAP-28, indicating that the measured signal corresponds specifically to released ChMAP-28. Both NC and NC-iRGD particles loaded with ChMAP-28 exhibited enhanced drug release at 154 mM NaCl compared to those at 50 mM NaCl (Fig. 7C and D). For NC-ChMAP-28 particles, increasing the ionic strength from 50 mM to 154 mM resulted in a significant increase of the released ChMAP-28 from no detectable amounts to around 60% after 30 days of incubation (Fig. 7C and D).

To determine whether both two particles could enter cancer cells, the 20% (w/w) NC and NC-iRGD particles loaded with FITC-ChMAP-28 were incubated with A549 cells and analyzed by confocal microscopy. The results showed that intense green fluorescence was detected in A549 cells incubated with both two silk particles, indicating efficient uptake of these two silk particles by cancer cells (Figure 8). Cell apoptosis study

To investigate whether drug-loaded NC and NC-iRGD particles could induce apoptosis in tumor cells, A549 and SKBR-3 cells were treated with NC-ChMAP-28 and NC-iRGD-ChMAP-28 particles (20% (w/w)), respectively. The apoptosis results of A549 cells are presented in Figure 9A. The apoptosis rate of the NC particle group was nearly identical to that of the control group, indicating its excellent biocompatibility. (Figure 9A and 9B). For A549 cells, the apoptosis rates in the NC-ChMAP-28 and NC-iRGD-ChMAP-28 treatment groups were approximately 60% and 80%, respectively, which are higher than that in the free ChMAP-28 group. In the case of SKBR-3 cells, the apoptosis rates in the NC-ChMAP-28 and NC-iRGD-ChMAP-28 treatment groups were approximately 50% and 80%, respectively, which are consistent with the trends observed in A549 cells (Figure 9C). This indicates that the drug-loaded NC and NC-iRGD particles can effectively induce tumor cell apoptosis (Figure 9B and 9C). In addition, Western blot and immunofluorescence analyses of cleaved caspase-3 (c-caspase-3) were also employed to assess apoptosis in tumor cells. Similar to the flow cytometry results, the expression level of cleaved caspase-3 was elevated to some extent in the ChMAP-28-loaded silk particle-treated groups, indicating that both types of silk particles loaded with ChMAP-28 can induce apoptosis in tumor cells (Figure S3A, S3B, and S3C). Furthermore, the mitochondrial membrane potential and Comet assay results demonstrated apoptosis and DNA damage in tumor cells treated with ChMAP-28-loaded NC and NC-iRGD particles, further supporting the induction of tumor cell apoptosis by ChMAP-28-loaded silk particles (Figure S4A, S4B, and S4C).

Discussion

In this study, we designed and produced two recombiannt spider silk proteins to prepare nanoparticles for drug delivery. By comparing the migration of these two bands with that of the molecular weight marker, the apparent molecular weights of NC and NC-iRGD were found to be slightly higher than their calculated values. This phenomenon has been previously observed for proteins rich in positively charged residues, as well as for other recombinant spider silk proteins with high hydrophobicity [25, 26]. Given the hydrophobic nature of NC and NC-iRGD, the slower-than-expected migration may be attributed to their increased tendency to aggregate.

Previous studies shown that spider silk particles can be formed by protein precipitation leaded by rapid mixing with high concentration of potassium phosphate buffer [27–29]. The salting method used for the production of silk particles is based on the hydrophobic interactions between silk molecules. When mixed with a silk protein solution, potassium phosphate enhances these hydrophobic interactions, promoting the assembly of silk molecules into nanoparticles through hydrogen bond formation [30, 31]. Compared to other silk-based particles, our NC and NC-iRGD particles demonstrated a significantly smaller particle size, which may be attributed to the lower protein concentration used in our preparation. It was demonstrated that process parameters, such as protein and potassium phosphate concentrations, significantly influenced the size and morphology of the particles. Generally, particles prepared at lower protein concentrations exhibited a smaller average size compared to those prepared at higher protein concentrations [32, 33]. Therefore, our NC and NC-iRGD particles were synthesized at a low protein concentration to achieve smaller silk particle sizes.

When the pH approaches the protein’s pI, the effect of NaCl becomes more pronounced, a phenomenon that can be attributed to enhanced electrostatic shielding. Previous study indicated that near the protein’s isoelectric point (pI), where net surface charge is minimal and electrostatic repulsion is weakest, increased ionic strength (e.g., NaCl) compresses the electrical double layer (Debye screening), drastically reducing the range of any residual repulsive forces. This enhanced electrostatic shielding allows short-range attractive forces (like van der Waals interactions) to dominate, significantly accelerating particle aggregation kinetics and consequently destabilizing the colloidal system [34]. The zeta potential serves as a crucial parameter for assessing the stability of colloidal systems. Typically, a zeta potential magnitude of at least ± 15 mV is required to ensure stable dispersion of nanoparticles [35]. Therefore, the significant particle agglomeration observed at pH 5.5 and under high ionic strength (154 mM NaCl) may be attributed to the reduced zeta potential.

Due to the overall negative charge of NC and NC-iRGD particles at a pH of 5.5 or higher, the positively charged ChMAP-28, a peptide with anti-tumor activity and an isoelectric point of 12 [36], was selected to evaluate the feasibility of loading NC and NC-iRGD particles with peptide drugs. Although it remains unclear whether the diminished surface charge of silk particles caused by these high drug loadings compromises their colloidal stability, previous studies suggest that the observed reduction in zeta potential (to approximately − 10 mV) at high drug loading ratios may have a potential impact on overall colloidal stability [35]. For spider silk-based particles, protein drugs not only adsorbed to the particles’ surface, but also can diffusing into the matrix of silk particles [32]. Taken together, our results suggest that peptide drugs may initially diffuse into the matrix of silk particles at low drug-to-silk particle weight ratios and subsequently adsorb onto the particle surface via electrostatic interactions. Although several silk-based delivery systems have been developed, studies have demonstrated that electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions are the primary factors influencing drug loading [37–40]. Previous studies have demonstrated that electrostatic interactions have the greatest influence on the loading of ChMAP-28 into silk particles [41]. In the case of MS1 and MS2 silk particles, hydrophobic interaction played a major role in the loading of this drug, whereas the loading TuSp1 particles with drugs is mainly attributed to the electrostatic interplay [28, 42, 43]. Elevated ionic strength increases the concentration of counterions in solution, which electrostatically shield the charged groups on the negatively charged silk particles and the positively charged ChMAP-28 peptide. This shielding compresses the electrical double layer and weakens the long-range electrostatic attraction between them, thereby decreasing the loading efficiency driven by electrostatic interactions [44, 45]. As ionic strength strongly influences the zeta-potential of charged particles, the reduced loading observed at higher ionic strengths suggests that the loading of both particles with ChMAP-28 is primarily driven by electrostatic interactions.

The pH-responsive drug release mechanism represents a key characteristic of both NC and NC-iRGD particles. This differential pH triggers protonation of ionizable functional groups within the carrier structure, leading to destabilization, swelling, or cleavage, and consequent payload release specifically within the tumor tissue [46, 47]. Additionally, no detectable amount of ChMAP-28 was released over the entire 30-day period at 50 mM NaCl, suggesting that the release of ChMAP-28 from silk particles is primarily driven by the degree of electrostatic interactions [48]. Judging from the similar loading and release profiles between the two silk particles, NC and NC-iRGD particles exhibited no obvious difference in their capacity for drug affinity and controlled release. Given the strong penetrating capacity of the iRGD peptide, NC-iRGD exhibit higher cellular uptake efficiency than NC, which is also an important aspect in drug delivery [49].

The induction of tumor cell apoptosis by drug-loaded silk particles is an important criterion for evaluating the suitability of silk particles as drug carriers, as demonstrated by Annexin V-FITC/propidium iodide (PI) double staining, as well as Western blotting and immunofluorescence analyses of key apoptotic markers. Distinct staining patterns distinguish apoptotic stages: Annexin V-FITC single-positive cells represent early apoptosis, characterized by phosphatidylserine exposure while plasma membrane integrity remains intact. Annexin V-FITC and PI double-positive cells indicate late apoptosis or necrosis, signifying both phosphatidylserine externalization and subsequent loss of membrane integrity. PI single-positive cells typically represent necrotic cells or late-stage apoptotic cells with compromised membranes, where phosphatidylserine exposure may be obscured [50, 51]. Based on our tumor cell apoptosis results, NC-iRGD particles demonstrated a higher drug delivery capacity compared to NC particles.

Conclusion

In this study, we designed a novel recombinant silk protein named NC and its functionalized derivative (NC-iRGD) as a penetrating enhancer for drug delivery. The NC protein was constructed based on the terminal sequences of A. ventricosus MaSp1, and NC-iRGD was generated by the addition of iRGD at its C-terminus. As a result, NC and NC-iRGD can be easily prepared by the salt out method and their colloidal stability is excellent in solutions at neutral condition and low ionic strength. NC and NC-iRGD particles were seccessfully evaluated as promising drug carriers for the delivery of peptide drugs. Following preparation, large quantities of ChMAP-28 were loaded onto two particles with up to 90% loading efficiency due to strong electrostatic interactions between positively charged peptide (ChMAP-28) and negatively charged silk particles. Similar to the other silk particles, both particles showed good biocompatibility [52–55]. Given the low release rate at physiological conditions (pH 7.4, 154 mM NaCl), the NC and NC-iRGD particles are suitable for carrying the drug through blood plasma. In addition, a burst release of this drug at pH 6.5 was observed, making them a promising material for drug delivery in acid condition, such as tumor microenvironment. Although the two silk-based particles exhibited similar drug loading and release profiles, NC-iRGD demonstrated enhanced tumor penetration ability and greater cytotoxicity against cancer cells compared to NC particles due to its iRGD sequence. In conclusion, the facile fabrication process, the subtle employ of pI as a pH switch, the strong tumor penetration ability, and the reasonable loading efficiency along with favorable release rate render the NC and NC-iRGD particles a promising protein-based material for specific drug delivery system.
