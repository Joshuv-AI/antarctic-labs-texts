Ileal J-Pouch interposition as a salvage technique for failed coloanal anastomosis: a case report
Authors: Saeed Derakhshani, Milad Karimian Ghadim, Sara Ashtari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSurgical management of low rectal cancer can be challenging, particularly when postoperative complications such as anastomotic leakage and ischemia arise. This case report highlights the use of an ileal J-pouch interposition as an innovative solution in a patient with compromised colonic length and perfusion.Case presentationWe present the case of a 50-year-old female patient with diabetes who underwent an intersphincteric resection, coloanal anastomosis, and appendicostomy, followed by an Antegrade Colonic Enema (ACE) for T2N0 rectal adenocarcinoma in November 2020. Postoperatively, she developed anastomotic leakage and a rectovaginal fistula due to ischemia, requiring a second surgery. Intraoperatively, the left and transverse colon were ischemic, leaving only the right colon viable, perfused by the ileocolic artery. Due to insufficient length for coloanal anastomosis, an ileal J-pouch was created and anastomosed to the anal canal, with the right colon connected to the pouch. The patient has achieved favorable long-term oncologic and functional outcomes, maintaining good bowel control with loperamide and ACE.ConclusionsThis case underscores the complexities of surgical management in low rectal cancer and the potential use of ileal J-pouch interposition as an effective solution for complications arising from primary surgical efforts. Further long-term studies are warranted to evaluate the sustainability of this approach in similar patient cohorts.

Background

Rectal cancer, particularly in its lower segment, poses significant surgical challenges in achieving oncologic control while preserving bowel continuity and minimizing postoperative complications [1, 2]. Low rectal cancer, defined as a tumor located less than 5 cm from the anal verge, often necessitates complex surgical techniques to ensure adequate resection margins and functional outcomes [3, 4]. The standard surgical approach involves low anterior resection with total mesorectal excision (TME) followed by straight coloanal anastomosis [5, 6]. However, the excision of the sigmoid colon during surgery reduces stool storage capacity, leading to a cluster of symptoms known as low anterior resection syndrome (LARS) [7, 8]. These symptoms, which include increased bowel frequency, urgency, incomplete evacuation, and fecal incontinence, significantly impair patients’ quality of life, particularly in those with mid-to-low rectal cancer [7, 8]. LARS can profoundly affect social activities, mental well-being, and overall quality of life [9, 10]. To address these challenges, alternative surgical techniques such as colonic J-pouch, transverse coloplasty, and side-to-end anastomosis have been developed as alternatives to the standard straight coloanal anastomosis [11, 12].

The colonic J-pouch anastomosis was first introduced by Lazorthes et al. [13] and Parc et al. [14] in 1986. Studies have shown that a colonic J-pouch measuring 5–8 cm in length provides optimal functional outcomes [15]. The ileal J-pouch, in particular, has been well-documented in the management of inflammatory bowel disease. It demonstrates superior functional outcomes, including improved continence and defecatory function, compared to straight ileoanal anastomosis [16–18]. While this technique has been widely used in patients with ulcerative colitis and familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP) undergoing restorative proctocolectomy, its application in rectal cancer surgery is less common and remains understudied [19–21]. Emerging evidence suggests that ileal J-pouch interposition may serve as a viable alternative in select patients undergoing rectal cancer resection or in cases where the colon is unsuitable for anastomosis due to ischemia or insufficient length [22, 23]. This innovative approach addresses complications such as anastomotic leakage or ischemia that may arise from primary surgical efforts. By preserving bowel continuity and improving functional outcomes, ileal J-pouch interposition offers potential benefits for patients with compromised colonic perfusion or anatomical limitations.

Despite its potential advantages, the use of ileal J-pouch interposition in rectal cancer surgery remains exceedingly rare. To date, only isolated experimental reports in animal models have been published [22] and, to our knowledge, there are no documented human clinical cases outside of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) settings. This underscores the novelty of the present case and highlights its contribution to the limited literature on salvage options in complex low rectal cancer. At the same time, it emphasizes the need for further clinical studies to evaluate the safety, functional outcomes, and long-term efficacy of this technique before wider adoption can be recommended.

Case presentation

Patient history

A 50-year-old female with a history of diabetes underwent surgery for low rectal cancer on November 30, 2020. The procedure included intersphincteric resection, coloanal anastomosis, and appendicostomy, followed by an antegrade colonic enema (ACE). Pathology confirmed adenocarcinoma, staged as T2N0. Informed consent has been obtained from the patient included in this study.

Clinical course

Postoperatively, the patient developed ischemia at the anastomotic site, resulting in a leak and rectovaginal fistula. She was managed non-operatively with intravenous antibiotics, parenteral nutrition, transvaginal drainage, and strict glycemic control. Serial CT imaging confirmed gradual resolution of pelvic fluid collections and absence of ongoing infection. On March 15, 2021, approximately 15 weeks after the initial surgery, she underwent a second operation to manage rectovaginal fistula. Intraoperatively, the left and transverse colon were mobilized but found to be ischemic. Only the right colon, perfused by the ileocolic artery, was viable on visual inspection.

As the right colon was too short for a coloanal anastomosis, the surgical team opted for an ileal J-pouch interposition as an alternative to permanent colostomy. A 12-cm ileal J-pouch was constructed; the right colon was anastomosed to the pouch, which was then anastomosed to the anal canal. An appendicostomy was created to facilitate ACE administration.

Surgical technique

Following induction of general anesthesia and lithotomy positioning, a midline laparotomy was performed. The ischemic left and transverse colon were resected, preserving the right colon with an intact ileocolic artery. A 24-cm segment of ileum was measured proximal to the ileocecal valve and divided with adequate mesenteric length for a tension-free reach into the pelvis.

The ileal J-pouch was fashioned by folding the segment back on itself to form two parallel 12-cm limbs, which were joined along the antimesenteric border with two linear cutting staplers, creating the pouch reservoir.

The right colon was anastomosed to the pouch using hand-sewn technique: a continuous layer of 3 − 0 absorbable sutures for seromuscular closure. The pouch was then brought into the pelvis without tension and hand-sewn to the anal canal with interrupted 2 − 0 absorbable sutures.

An appendicostomy was created in the right lower quadrant using the Malone antegrade continence enema technique. Total operative time was 280 min; estimated blood loss was 450 mL. No intraoperative complications occurred.

Perioperative management

Prophylactic intravenous ceftriaxone and metronidazole were administered at induction. The patient underwent mechanical bowel preparation the day prior and received postoperative low-molecular-weight heparin. Postoperatively, she was managed in the surgical ward, commenced clear fluids on day 2, and advanced to a soft diet by day 4. Analgesia consisted of intravenous acetaminophen and patient-controlled morphine for the first 48 h. ACE irrigation was initiated two weeks after surgery.

Notably, a diverting stoma was not created during the salvage procedure. This decision was based on several factors: the patient’s strong preference to avoid a permanent stoma, the resolution of pelvic sepsis prior to surgery (confirmed clinically and radiologically), and the presence of a functional appendicostomy, which allowed for postoperative decompression and irrigation. Intraoperatively, the ileal J-pouch was well-perfused, and the anastomosis to the anal canal was tension-free and meticulously constructed, further supporting the decision to proceed without a protective stoma.

Prior to this surgery, the patient had developed a rectovaginal fistula and contained pelvic sepsis following the initial anastomotic leak. This was managed non-operatively with intravenous antibiotics, parenteral nutrition, transvaginal drainage, and strict glycemic control due to her diabetic status. Serial CT imaging confirmed resolution of fluid collections and absence of ongoing infection, allowing for definitive reconstruction in a clean setting.

Postoperative follow-up and outcomes

The patient has been followed annually for five years with colonoscopy, CT imaging, and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) testing; Colonoscopy annually: no anastomotic strictures, pouch complications, or recurrent lesions detected (Fig. 1: Follow-up colonoscopy at 5 years post-surgery). The pouch mucosa is healthy with no evidence of stricture, recurrence, or pouch-related complications, confirming long-term functional and oncologic success of the ileal J-pouch interposition). CT scans of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis annually: no local recurrence or distant metastases. Serum CEA levels measured every 6 months for the first 3 years and annually thereafter, consistently within normal range (1.2–1.8 ng/mL).

Discussion

The surgical management of low rectal cancer requires balancing oncologic clearance with preservation of bowel function, a challenge heightened in patients with comorbidities such as diabetes, which increase the risk of ischemia and anastomotic leakage [27–29]. The present case illustrates not only the technical complexity of salvage surgery following failed coloanal anastomosis but also the rationale for selecting ileal J-pouch interposition among the available salvage strategies.

When such complications occur, salvage options include redo coloanal anastomosis, creation of a colonic J-pouch, transverse coloplasty, side-to-end anastomosis, the Turnbull–Cutait delayed pull-through, or permanent colostomy [13, 14, 16–18, 27, 28]. Redo coloanal anastomosis is often considered the first option if colonic length and perfusion can be restored; however, in this patient, the left and transverse colon were ischemic, and the risk of recurrent leakage was judged high. Severe pelvic scarring and vascular compromise further increased the technical difficulty of a redo procedure [27, 28]. Colonic J-pouch and coloplasty procedures can optimize postoperative function when colonic tissue is viable [13, 14, 16–18], but they were not feasible here due to inadequate colonic length. The Turnbull–Cutait technique offers a staged reconstruction, but it prolongs recovery, requires an additional operation, and carries a risk of pelvic sepsis in the interim period. Permanent colostomy, while safe and reliable, would have significantly affected the patient’s quality of life, and she was strongly motivated to avoid this outcome.

In this context, ileal J-pouch interposition was chosen as it offered a vascularly reliable, tension-free reconstruction without the need for splenic flexure mobilization, thereby reducing the risk of further ischemic compromise. The physiological advantages of the ileum, including greater compliance and reservoir capacity, are well documented in ileal pouch–anal anastomosis (IPAA) for ulcerative colitis and familial adenomatous polyposis, where acceptable continence and quality-of-life outcomes are consistently reported [30–32]. These benefits were borne out in our case, with the patient achieving a Wexner continence score of 3, a LARS score of 15, and an EORTC QLQ-CR29 score of 83/100 after five years of follow-up. These results compare favorably with outcomes reported for colonic J-pouch and coloplasty in rectal cancer surgery [13, 14, 16–18]. In addition, annual colonoscopies over 5 years revealed no pouch complications or recurrence, CT scans confirmed the absence of metastatic disease, and CEA levels remained stable. These findings reinforce the feasibility of ileal J-pouch interposition in carefully selected cancer patients.

Nonetheless, risks unique to this approach must be acknowledged. Pouchitis, although uncommon outside inflammatory bowel disease, remains a possible complication [30, 31]. Mechanical issues such as twisting, obstruction, or anastomotic stricture can occur, and metabolic disturbances from ileal resection, including bile salt malabsorption and vitamin B12 deficiency, are theoretical concerns. Compared with redo anastomosis, which carries a higher risk of repeat leakage, and permanent diversion, which compromises quality of life, ileal J-pouch interposition offers an intermediate solution that balances functional preservation with acceptable safety [31].

To date, only a few case reports and small series have explored ileal pouch use in rectal cancer salvage [19–21, 33]. Ghahramani et al. [22], demonstrated in canine models that ileal J-pouches significantly increase reservoir capacity compared with straight coloanal anastomosis or coloplasty. Hida et al. [34] confirmed superior stool storage and quality of life with J-pouches in rectal cancer patients when the anastomosis was close to the anal verge. While promising, these outcomes must be interpreted cautiously, as long-term functional deterioration has been described in IPAA cohorts [31], underscoring the need for careful surveillance.

This case demonstrates that ileal J-pouch interposition can be considered a feasible and functionally favorable salvage option in highly selected patients with failed coloanal anastomosis due to colonic ischemia or inadequate length. It fills a critical niche when traditional colonic reconstruction is not possible and the patient wishes to avoid permanent stoma. However, its adoption should be tempered by awareness of its technical demands, potential complications, and lack of long-term outcome data in oncologic populations. Future studies preferably multicenter, prospective comparisons with other salvage modalities, are needed to define optimal patient selection criteria, functional durability, and oncologic safety.

Limitations and future directions

This report describes a single patient and cannot establish comparative efficacy or superiority over other reconstructive techniques. Functional assessment lacked preoperative scores and physiologic testing (anorectal manometry, defecography) due to patient preference. Postoperative evaluation relied on patient-reported outcomes supplemented by validated scoring tools. Nevertheless, the combination of long-term oncologic surveillance (annual colonoscopy, annual CT, CEA monitoring for 5 years), and standardized functional measures provides meaningful insight. Despite these encouraging results, larger prospective studies are needed to compare ileal J-pouch interposition with other salvage options, evaluate long-term pouch function and oncologic safety, and develop standardized patient selection criteria based on colonic viability and comorbidity profiles.

Conclusion

Ileal J-pouch interposition can serve as a salvage technique to restore bowel continuity in patients with failed coloanal anastomosis due to ischemic or insufficient colon. This single-case report demonstrates the technical feasibility and acceptable functional outcomes of this approach, offering a potential alternative to permanent stoma in highly selected patients. However, the current evidence remains limited to this isolated case, and broader application requires validation through future studies.
