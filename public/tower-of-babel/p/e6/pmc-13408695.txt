A comparative ML approach to classify Lupinus species using VIS-NIR spectral data from whole seeds and various data transformation techniques and resampling methods
Authors: Josefa Díaz-Álvarez, Francisco A Galea-Gragera, Francisco Chávez de la O, Pedro A Salguero-López, Fernando Llera Cid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe increasing interest in the cultivation and utilization of Lupinus species is driven by their nutritional value and potential for sustainable agriculture. However, the non-destructive taxonomic classification of Lupinus species from whole seeds spectral data remains scarcely explored. In this study, five machine learning classifiers were comparatively evaluated for discriminating seven Lupinus species using visible and near-infrared (VIS-NIR) spectral data, in both reflectance and absorbance modes, acquired from seeds of the official active collection of the CICYTEX Germplasm Bank. The dataset was characterized by marked class imbalance. Model performance was assessed using raw spectra and four preprocessing strategies (including three hybrid combinations) combined with six resampling methods and a no-resampling baseline. Two validation approaches were applied: an 80/20 train-test split and stratified 5-fold cross-validation. Across both spectral domains and validation schemes, Random Forest and Support Vector Classification achieved the best overall performance, with F1 scores above 94%, and AUC, accuracy and precision values above 97%. Logistic Regression also showed substantial improvements when hybrid preprocessing techniques were applied. Cross-validation confirmed the robustness and generalization ability of the best-performing models. These findings support the use of VIS-NIR spectroscopy combined with machine learning as a rapid, objective, and non-destructive framework for Lupinus species discrimination in imbalanced germplasm datasets. This approach may assist taxonomic identification, germplasm management, and the preliminary screening of promising materials for breeding programs, although further validation under independent environmental and acquisition conditions is still required.Keywords: VIS-NIR spectroscopy, Machine learning, Multi-class classification, Data preprocessing, Germplasm identification, Legume seedsSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Ecology, Ecology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Lupin (Lupinus genus, Leguminosae family) comprises more than 200 species (Fig.1). Originating from the Mediterranean region, these species are now distributed across the Americas, North Africa, the Middle East, and Europe1. The increasing cultivation and use of lupin are driven by its low production costs, remarkable adaptability to diverse climates and soils, and exceptional nutritional and health-promoting properties. Additionally, lupin cultivation supports sustainability goals by contributing to soil improvement through biological nitrogen fixation2.

Multiplication trial of ecotypes from the official active collection of lupins (Lupinus spp.) of the CICYTEX Germplasm Bank, conducted at the La Orden-Valdesequera Agricultural Research Institute, part of the Extremadura Scientific and Technological Research Centre (CICYTEX). The figure shows the different Lupinus species studied in this work, Lupinus plants (a), seeds (b).

Traditionally, lupin seeds have served as food for both humans and livestock, especially during periods of scarcity. They are currently recognized for their outstanding nutritional and functional value3. Lupin seeds contain high-quality plant protein (36–44%), comparable to soybean (38–44%)4, though this content varies by species, environments, and genotype. Agronomic management and environmental factors directly influence both nutritional quality and alkaloid content, highlighting the need for rapid tools to select lines with improved profiles and lower toxicity2,5.

As a result, lupin and its derivatives provide an excellent plant-based protein source to meet demand for healthier diets that reduce reliance on animal protein. Lupin is primarily used as animal feed () and, to a lesser extent, as a snack for human consumption ()6. It is important to highlight the strategic significance of lupin cultivation in Mediterranean regions, especially in Extremadura (Spain), the fourth-largest producer of Iberian pig worldwide. Recently, studies are underway to promote the use of Lupinus albus as an alternative to soybean in animal feed, with promising results in both meat quality and environmental sustainability7.

In recent years, dietary habits have shifted towards healthier patterns, motivated by health, ethical, and environmental concerns8. Lupin seeds are gluten-free and suitable for people with celiac disease or food allergies. Their high fiber content (17–30%) supports gut health, and their ability to reduce blood glucose levels benefits people with diabetes. Lupin consumption is also associated with reduced fat accumulation, increased satiety, lower cardiovascular risk (by reducing cholesterol absorption and blood pressure), improved nervous system function, and stronger bone health9.

The growing threat of antibiotic resistance and the potential of lupin as an antibacterial and antifungal agent have sparked scientific interest. These properties are linked to the presence of secondary metabolites in lupin, including antioxidants (lutein, zeaxanthin, and beta-carotene), flavonoids, saponins, phytosterols, tannins, polyphenols, carotenoids, tocopherols, and peptides, which contribute antimicrobial, antifungal, anticancer, and anti-inflammatory activities10. However, lupin plants and seeds also contain alkaloids—natural toxic substances that serve as defensive mechanisms against herbivores and insects. These alkaloids impart a characteristic bitter taste and pose health risks for humans and animals, causing vomiting and diarrhea if not properly processed6. Alkaloid content varies by species, genotype, environmental conditions, and agronomic management1,2.

The wide diversity of Lupinus species and their outstanding nutritional and pharmacological properties, makes precise species identification a relevant research goal. Non-destructive techniques, particularly visible and near-infrared spectroscopy (VIS-NIR), have become important tools for identifying and classifying agricultural products due to their speed, precision, and minimal sample preparation.

This work focuses on seven Lupinus species adapted to the Mediterranean area, including some wild types and the three most widely cultivated species: white lupin (L. albus), yellow lupin (L. luteus), and narrow-leaf lupin (L. angustifolius). VIS-NIR reflectance and absorbance data were obtained directly from whole seeds, providing valuable information for analysis by advanced machine learning (ML) algorithms. By adopting a data-driven approach, which is highly applicable in agronomy, the information provided by VIS-NIR spectroscopy is especially useful for the classification tasks of agro-food products11–13.

In this context, the present study addresses the limited development of non-destructive methodologies for the taxonomic classification of Lupinus species from whole seeds. Specifically, we propose a comparative framework based on VIS-NIR reflectance and absorbance spectra, combined with different ML algorithms, preprocessing strategies, and resampling methods, in order to optimize the discrimination of seven Lupinus species represented in an official active germplasm collection. In this way, the study not only aims to provide a rapid and non-destructive approach for species identification, but also a robust methodological evaluation in an imbalanced dataset with relevance for the management and characterization of plant genetic resources.

Background

In the last decade, research on lupin plants has increased considerably due to their nutritional properties, health benefits, and potential for pharmacological applications9. In general, most studies have focused primarily on the analysis of their chemical components and nutritional attributes. Traditionally, invasive and destructive techniques such as chromatographic and analytical methods have been employed. Chromatographic techniques include high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)14,15, gas chromatography (GC)10, liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS)16, and thin-layer chromatography (TLC)17. Traditional analytical methods, such as Kjeldahl and Soxhlet, are robust for food chemical analysis but chemically alter the samples18,19.

Recent comparative studies on Mediterranean species (L. albus, L. luteus, and L. pilosus) have shown considerable morphological and phytochemical variability, highlighting L. albus for its higher protein content ( ) and L. pilosus for its elevated antioxidant activity5. These results underscore the need for non-destructive screening techniques that allow for the capture of such diversity and the selection of optimal genotypes for breeding.

Among the non-invasive and non-destructive techniques, VIS-NIR and NIR, combined with chemometric methods, have become established as a rapid, cost-effective, and reliable approach to assess the quality and composition of seeds11,12. These approaches extract relevant information using mathematical and statistical algorithms20; Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) provides high-resolution data to identify functional groups21, and hyperspectral imaging (HSI) adds the essential spatial component for constituent mapping22.

The most common chemometric methods—though not the only ones—include principal component analysis (PCA), partial least squares regression (PLS), multiple linear regression (MLR), artificial neural networks (ANN), and support vector machines (SVM). These methods have been used in various research studies. For example, in 23, the authors analysed the effects of ultrasound on the structural, physicochemical, and antioxidant properties of lupin seeds. PCA revealed that a two-principal component model explained of the total variation in the data. In 24, the feasibility of developing healthy and sustainable meat alternatives using lupin flour was assessed, with MLR and PLSR applied to evaluate the chemical composition compared to real meat.

Within the legume domain25, established an important precedent by evaluating NIR reflectance spectra from intact seeds of Lupinus angustifolius and L. luteus to predict seed coat proportion, thereby demonstrating the early potential of non-destructive spectroscopy in lupin breeding. More recently26, developed a controlled VIS–NIR–SWIR hyperspectral imaging benchmark for grain-legume seed accession identification from intact seeds, showing that visible-domain optical contrasts provide highly informative cues for material discrimination.

The effectiveness of these technologies can be further enhanced when they are integrated with advanced ML pipelines. In other agricultural and food matrices, the combination of spectral augmentation, feature selection, and ML has enabled accurate amylose-content detection in rice, whereas stacking generalization architectures have achieved very high classification performance in varietal identification and adulteration detection in fruits, seeds, and edible oils27,28. Taken together, these studies support the methodological feasibility of combining spectral data with advanced algorithms to resolve fine-grained classification boundaries in complex spectral datasets.

Artificial intelligence (AI) and ML models are increasingly used to extract value from spectral data. The application of HSI combined with ML techniques is rapid, non-invasive, and non-destructive, presenting multiple possibilities for food quality evaluation29. Due to the high dimensionality of HSI data, ML algorithms outperform traditional statistical methods, automatically identifying patterns and relationships that enable tasks such as varietal classification, component quantification, and early anomaly detection30. In31, computer vision was applied to classify Lupinus mutabilis Sweet to determine if seeds were suitable for human consumption, achieving an accuracy of . The literature contains numerous references to the use of HSI and ML for the analysis of hyperspectral and satellite images. With respect to research specifically focused on the genus Lupinus, previous studies have mainly centered on the identification of invasive species, analysis of chemical components, nutrients, and pesticides, among other topics32–34.

Their versatility is evident in recent studies, such as the discrimination of organic and conventional brown rice using benchtop, portable, and HSI NIR devices35, or in 13, where these techniques are applied to 835 lines of Phaseolus vulgaris, Lupinus albus, and L. mutabilis. The combination of NIR spectroscopy with the MARVIN ProLine physical seed analyser enabled the non-destructive determination of internal composition (protein and moisture) and key morphological traits (seed length, width, and weight) in a single measurement, accelerating the screening of promising lines. Several recent reviews have highlighted that the integration of NIR/VIS-NIR spectroscopy with ML algorithms enables precise classification and prediction of compositional and varietal parameters in seeds and foods, revolutionizing quality control and authenticity in agricultural and food matrices, and emphasizing that appropriate spectral preprocessing, selection of relevant variables, and choice of ML algorithm are crucial for robust models12.

Recently, a research work addressed the binary classification between sweet and bitter Lupinus ecotypes using the same datasets. The results with F1 scores and AUC values above 92% demonstrated the ability to this discrimination target36.

A review of the recent literature suggests that studies directly addressing the taxonomic classification of Lupinus species using non-destructive techniques, such as VIS-NIR spectroscopy applied to whole seeds in combination with advanced ML algorithms, remain scarce. This gap justifies the need for rapid and non-destructive methodologies aimed at the taxonomic identification of Lupinus species in the context of conservation, characterization, and management of official active germplasm collections.

Beyond mere taxonomic classification, VIS-NIR spectroscopy combined with ML offers promising tools for seed traceability, genetic improvement, and the sustainable management of plant genetic and agricultural resources. For instance, it has been demonstrated that this technology enables rapid and non-destructive characterization of germplasm, accelerates the selection of superior genotypes, and facilitates varietal traceability both in germplasm banks and seed supply chains. In legumes and related crops, its validity has been confirmed as efficient support for breeding programs and the optimized management of diversity and resource sustainability 37. These techniques also contribute to maximizing ecosystem services, such as biological nitrogen fixation, by ensuring precise identification and selection of species used in cover crops and sustainable rotations.

Materials

This investigation focused on seven annual species of lupin, including the three most widely cultivated species—Lupinus albus L. (white lupin), Lupinus angustifolius L. (blue lupin), Lupinus luteus L. (yellow lupin)—and four wild species: Lupinus hispanicus Bioss & Reut, Lupinus gredensis Gand, Lupinus micranthus Geus, and Lupinus cosentinii Guss. The seeds used in this research work were selected from the official active collection of the genus Lupinus at the CICYTEX Germplasm Bank, located at the “Finca La Orden-Valdesequera” Agricultural Research Institute (, ) (Fig. 1). All ecotypes were maintained at a controlled temperature between 0 and and relative humidity, ensuring their viability and traceability38. Prior to analysis, seeds were equilibrated to room temperature to homogenize their moisture content and ensure representativeness in the spectral measurements.

Methods

Capturing spectral data

VIS-NIR and HSI are widely used techniques for the non-destructive analysis of seeds. In this work, VIS-NIR spectral data were captured using a VIS-NIR spectroradiometer in interactance-reflectance mode, covering the range nm, which provides information related to color as well as various physiological and biochemical characteristics. A total of 2, 151 spectral variables were obtained, corresponding to the wavelengths registered by the instrument, with a spectral resolution of 3 nm (maximum half-width) at 700 nm and 10 nm (maximum full half-width) at 1, 400 nm and 2, 100 nm.

Before each measurement series, a reference spectrum was recorded using a ceramic plate (white reference); this procedure was repeated every five samples. Both the reference spectra and those of the Lupinus species samples were recorded using a spectral acquisition software and a motorized tool (150mm, 22 rpm), equipped with a halogen light source. This tool rotates samples during spectral analysis to improve measurement accuracy.

Measurements were performed in the spectroscopy room of the Radiometry and Remote Sensing Laboratory, Forage Crops and Pastures Area, at the “Finca La Orden-Valdesequera” Agricultural Research Institute, under controlled environmental conditions (temperature and relative humidity) and in complete darkness to avoid interference from ambient light. Figure 3 shows the spectroradiometer and the motorized tool used in this study.

Equipment used to capture and record spectral data included a VIS-NIR spectroradiometer and a motorized rotating platform. The image on the right shows examples of lupin seeds.

To minimize the effect of particle size and improve the representativeness of spectral measurements, each sample was analysed in quadruplicate, and each recorded spectrum corresponds to the average of 25 consecutive scans over the sample. This procedure reduces both instrumental and sample variability, increasing the reproducibility and reliability of the data obtained39.

To obtain absorbance spectra, the reflectance data (R) collected from the spectroradiometer were transformed according to the standard formula: . This mathematical conversion was performed for all spectral measurements, resulting in two independent datasets for analysis: one for reflectance and one for absorbance. Reflectance and absorbance describe different processes in the interaction between materials and electromagnetic radiation, and both were recorded for comparative analysis. In this study, both types of spectra were organized in separate datasets for subsequent analysis.

Preprocessing data: Transformations

In spectroscopy, the quality of raw data can be compromised by instrumental or environmental noise and sample-inherent effects, such as light scattering and particle size variations. These sources of error generate optical heterogeneity that may mask or distort patterns relevant to the analysis. Therefore, spectral preprocessing is a critical stage for enabling ML algorithms to identify reliable patterns and maximize model performance. The effectiveness of ML techniques depends not only on data quantity but, especially, on their quality, making an exhaustive preprocessing phase essential40.

In this study, both reflectance and absorbance data underwent a mandatory common pretreatment: the spectrum considered for each sample is the result of averaging four independent measurements, each itself the mean of 25 consecutive scans over the sample. This strategy reduces the contribution of instrumental and sample noise, improving the representativeness and robustness of the spectra used in the analysis41. Consequently, when the term ”raw data” is used in subsequent sections, it always refers to the spectra obtained after this common averaging pretreatment, prior to the application of any further specific transformation technique.

Before applying transformation techniques, the presence of missing values—derived from potential instrumental limitations, sensor failures, or acquisition and transmission errors—was checked42. After data curation, the final dataset comprised 871 spectra, each the result of averaging four individual spectra, corresponding to the ecotypes of the seven studied species.

Spectral preprocessing is essential for correcting artifacts, improving signal-to-noise ratio, minimizing scattering and background effects, and facilitating calibration and comparison between spectra43. In this study, various data transformation strategies were applied, all of which are listed in Table 1. The choice and combination of these pretreatments was based on the scientific literature and empirical optimization performed on the dataset, aiming to maximize robustness and analytical accuracy. Although the number of combinations evaluated was considerable, previous studies have provided useful guidelines for their selection44, and recent works have demonstrated that combining different techniques improves the quality of AI models45.

To avoid over-processing, spectral transformations were not applied indiscriminately, but were selected based on both the literature and an empirical evaluation of their behaviour on the dataset. In addition, the combination of an independent train-test split and stratified cross-validation helped verify that the observed improvements did not rely on excessive processing alone, but on consistent patterns present in the data.

After applying the described pretreatments, the final dataset was organized in Excel format, with an independent sheet for each transformation technique and an additional one for the raw data. This structure facilitated systematic comparison and analysis of the impact of each pretreatment on the performance of ML algorithms for the classification of Lupinus species. Regarding the data analysis, this study was conducted using the Python framework (3.8.10). Pandas (2.0.3) and NumPy (1.24.4) were used for data preprocessing and numerical operations, Matplotlib (3.7.5) for visualization, SciPy (1.10.1) for scientific computing, Scikit-learn (1.3.2) for ML modelling and hyperparameter optimization, and imbalanced-learn (0.11.0) for implementing resampling techniques.

To evaluate model performance, two validation strategies were employed with both datasets (reflectance and absorbance): a simple split into a training set () and a test set (), and stratified K-fold cross-validation (CV) (K=5). This dual approach allows comparison of algorithm performance under different partitioning scenarios and helps minimize the risk of overfitting, especially in the context of class imbalance.

Preprocessing data: Dataset balance

Class imbalance is a relevant challenge in classification problems, especially in real-world applications where misidentifying minority classes can incur a high cost. In this study, focused on the classification of seven Lupinus species using spectral information, a non-uniform distribution of samples among classes was observed, as shown in Fig. 4. Specifically, L. micranthus, L. cosentinii, and L. hispanicus were underrepresented in both the reflectance and absorbance datasets.

To quantify this imbalance, the imbalance ratio (IR) was calculated as the ratio of the number of instances in the majority class to the minority class (See Eq. 1), yielding in both datasets48. Under these conditions, any accuracy metric would be unreliable. Therefore, various resampling methods, essential in both statistics and ML, were evaluated to adjust the representation of minority classes and provide greater robustness to the models. Resampling methods allow the generation of multiple subsets from the training set and adjustment of the models to each sample, providing additional information about their behavior and variability.

Table 2 summarizes the resampling techniques applied and the IR obtained after their application. The choice of SMOTE and ADASYN was based on their ability to improve minority class representation while refining decision boundaries. Hybrid methods such as SMOTE-ENN and SMOTE-Tomek further reduce noise and inter-class overlap, whereas Borderline-SMOTE and SVM-SMOTE enhance discrimination in critical boundary regions. Particularly, ADASYN increases sampling in harder-to-learn areas. RandomOverSampler preserves original feature distributions in a simple and efficient way. Overall, these techniques were selected to provide a robust methodological comparison for imbalanced spectral datasets and to improve the reliability of non-destructive species classification in seed germplasm collections.

The performance of ML algorithms depends largely on the configuration of their hyperparameters. To ensure a homogeneous tuning procedure and a rigorous comparison among the different ML algorithms, hyperparameter selection was carried out systematically through grid search, implemented using the GridSearchCV function from the Scikit-learn library in Python. A specific search space containing multiple hyperparameter combinations was defined for each model. The evaluation of all possible combinations can greatly improve the results, but increases computational cost, especially when considering the 350 combinations generated by the different preprocessing, resampling, and classifier methods. The optimal configuration was selected through stratified K-fold cross-validation (K=5), using F1 score metric as the main optimization criterion due to the imbalanced nature of the dataset. This procedure allowed the identification of the most appropriate configurations for each classifier and supported a consistent methodological comparison across the evaluated models.

To streamline the analysis, after confirming that overall performance was similar across many combinations, the final selection focused on the raw data and four transformation techniques, thus optimizing accuracy, robustness, and computational efficiency. Spectral interpretability analysis

To improve model interpretability and identify the wavelengths contributing most strongly to species discrimination, an additional post hoc explainability analysis was performed on selected RF configurations for reflectance and absorbance data. The primary analysis focused on spectra pretreated with SNV+1D (2-3-3) without resampling, as this configuration yielded one of the best classification performances in the main experiments and allowed a direct interpretation of wavelength relevance without the added influence of synthetic minority-class generation. SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values were calculated on the test set to estimate the contribution of each wavelength variable to the model output53. Global wavelength importance was summarized using the mean absolute SHAP value across samples, whereas class-level plots were used as complementary visualizations. In addition, supplementary SHAP analyses were obtained under SVM-SMOTE resampling, as this method showed competitive classification performance while specifically targeting minority-class boundary regions, thus providing a useful complementary scenario for assessing the consistency of the main discriminative spectral regions under class-balanced conditions.

In addition to the analyses based on spectral pretreatments and resampling methods, the ML classifiers were also evaluated using the raw data, both with and without resampling. Model performance was initially assessed using the F1 score and AUC-ROC metrics. The F1 score represents the harmonic mean of precision and recall, as defined in Equation (2). In turn, the AUC-ROC measures the rate of true positives versus false positives at different decision thresholds, providing insight into the model’s ability to discriminate between classes. In addition, accuracy, precision, and recall were incorporated to provide a more complete evaluation of classifier performance. Taken together, these metrics allow a more robust interpretation of model behaviour in the context of class-imbalanced data. The complementary values of accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score for the best-performing configurations are summarized in the Supplementary Material, using RF as an example of model performance (Tables S9 and S10).

In this study, two validation strategies were implemented for both datasets (reflectance and absorbance): (i) data were split into two sets, allocating for training and for testing (hold-out validation); and (ii) a stratified K-fold cross-validation (K=5) was performed. Experimental result using K-fold cross validation

In accordance with the previous results, the problem was addressed using stratified K-fold cross-validation with K=5. Stratification is a crucial decision for imbalanced datasets, as it enables the data to be divided into five disjoint subsets while preserving the class proportions. The model is trained and validated K times; in each iteration, a different fold is selected as the test set, and the remaining folds are used for training. This partitioning method makes the model more reliable, accurate, and robust. Furthermore, this procedure mitigates overfitting, particularly identified in MLP, DTR, and minority classes52.

Resampling methods are applied exclusively to the training set; the test set is not involved in this process, so the number of instances belonging to minority classes in the test set may be lower than that of the majority classes.

Statistical tests

This study compared five ML algorithms using 5-fold CV, evaluating their performance through six resampling methods together a no-resampling baseline and five data transformation techniques applied to reflectance and absorbance spectral data. Statistical analysis was based on the F1 score metric. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and homoscedasticity using the Levene test. When both assumptions were satisfied, a parametric test (ANOVA) was applied; otherwise, a non-parametric test (Kruskal–Wallis) was used to determine whether significant differences existed among the ML models under each resampling and spectral transformation.

Given the large number of combinations evaluated, these statistical tests were provided in the Supplementary material (Tables S2-S8 and Figures S2-S4). Overall, the results obtained with reflectance and absorbance data were similar, although absorbance tended to provide slightly better performance in several optimized configurations. A direct statistical comparison between both spectral domains for each resampling and transformation combination is also provided, together with the corresponding graphical comparison. Finally, the results of the statistical tests indicated significant differences among the methods evaluated. In addition, the statistical analysis allows the most robust data processing strategy to be determined.

Discussion

This study evaluated the classification of seven Lupinus species using VIS–NIR reflectance and absorbance spectra acquired from whole seeds. Five classifiers were analysed under different preprocessing and resampling strategies to address class imbalance. The initial 80/20 train–test split showed high discriminative performance but also revealed signs of overfitting in minority classes, which motivated further validation using 5-fold CV.

Overall, the results showed that RF and SVC achieved the most robust and consistent performance under both validation approaches. Hybrid preprocessing strategies further improved classifier behaviour, especially in reflectance, where several configurations reached values close to 99%. LGR also became highly competitive when hybrid transformations were applied, approaching the performance of RF and SVC under CV. These findings suggest the importance of preprocessing for reducing non-informative variance, improving scaling, and enhancing class-related spectral differences, particularly in models that benefit from lower collinearity and clearer linear separability. Similar observations have been reported in other agricultural matrices, where preprocessing proved important for resolving fine-grained spectral overlaps and improving model stability12,54.

When contextualized within the broader literature, the present work contributes to extending previous non-destructive seed classification studies to an imbalanced dataset derived from an official active germplasm collection26,55. Although direct studies focused on intact-seed taxonomic classification within the genus Lupinus remain scarce, the present results support the feasibility of using VIS–NIR spectroscopy and ML as as a promising rapid and non-destructive approach for species-level discrimination in germplasm management25,56.

The SHAP-based interpretability analysis provided additional support for the physicochemical plausibility of the classification models. Rather than relying on isolated wavelengths, the models appeared to rely on recurrent spectral regions particularly around 846–862 nm, 1106–1119 nm, 1172–1184 nm, 1263–1276 nm, 1338 nm, and 1721–1729 nm. Several of these regions fall within wavelength domains commonly associated with overtone and combination bands involving C–H, O–H, and N–H bonds. In particular, the interval around 1170–1275 nm is compatible with C–H-rich compounds such as carbohydrates and lipids, whereas the region around 1435–1500 nm may involve N–H- and O–H-related absorptions associated with proteins, carbohydrates, alcohols, or phenolic-rich matrices. Likewise, the range around 1610–1760 nm, including the recurrent bands observed near 1720–1730 nm, is widely associated with first-overtone C–H vibrations frequently linked to lipid-rich materials56,57. These findings suggest that the models may have captured a combination of compositional information from the seed matrix together with optical variation related to seed-coat and surface properties, which is also compatible with previous NIR studies in lupin seeds25.

The complementary SHAP visualizations obtained under SVM-SMOTE resampling (Fig. S5 in Supplementary Material) suggested that the main discriminative spectral regions remained broadly consistent, although the relative contribution of some wavelengths changed. This suggests that the classification framework remains robust even when minority-class representation is reinforced. These assignments should nevertheless be interpreted with caution. NIR bands are broad and highly overlapping, and spectra acquired from intact seeds integrate multiple sources of variation, including seed-coat properties, internal reserves, moisture-related contributions, and storage-associated effects56,58. Therefore, the SHAP results should not be interpreted as direct proof of specific compounds, but rather as the identification of wavelength regions that are spectrally informative and chemically plausible for species discrimination.

This study has several limitations. The number of samples was limited, the dataset was highly dimensional, and several classes were underrepresented, which may increase the risk of overfitting and reduce transferability. In addition, the material analysed came from one official active germplasm collection, and spectral behaviour may be influenced by environmental and seed-related factors such as growing season, production environment, agronomic management, physiological state, residual seed moisture, seed-coat characteristics, and storage history. Because the analysed material represents ecotypes of different origins from an official active germplasm collection, this diversity is valuable but may also introduce additional spectral heterogeneity2,56,58,59. Moreover, the SHAP analysis was performed on selected RF configurations and should therefore be regarded as model-dependent, while the lack of species-specific wet-chemistry measurements means that the physicochemical interpretation remains indicative rather than confirmatory. Future work should therefore include external validation using independent accessions, contrasting environments, additional seasons, and, where possible, targeted compositional assays to strengthen the biological interpretation and generalizability of this framework.

Conclusions

This study evaluated the classification of seven Lupinus species using VIS–NIR reflectance and absorbance data from whole seeds. Five classifiers, four data transformations, and six resampling methods were evaluated. An 80/20 train–test split showed high classification performance but signs of overfitting in minority classes, leading to the use of 5-fold CV. LGR with hybrid transformations achieved highly competitive performance (), approaching that of RF and SVC, and may therefore represent a robust and computationally efficient alternative.

5-fold CV results confirmed the strong performance of RF and SVC (F1 score > 97%) and improved the performance estimates of the remaining classifiers compared to the initial experiments, particularly when using reflectance data. For absorbance data, improvement was especially evident when resampling methods were applied.

Regarding resampling techniques, ADASYN, SMOTE-ENN, and SMOTE-Tomek helped reduce the effect of class imbalance and contributed to more balanced classification across species when using reflectance data. In the case of absorbance, balanced performance was more consistently obtained with SMOTE-ENN, SVM-SMOTE, and ADASYN. Furthermore, the multilayer perceptron showed clear improvement with absorbance data than with reflectance data.

The results highlighted the importance of employing hybrid data transformation techniques, which generally improved classifier performance for both VIS-NIR reflectance and absorbance data, a result further corroborated by cross-validation. Statistical analyses were applied to compare model performance across the evaluated experimental combinations. First, the normality and homoscedasticity of the data were analysed with Shapiro-Wilk and Levene tests, respectively. Depending on whether the assumptions for parametric testing were met, either ANOVA or the Kruskal-Wallis test was applied, indicating significant differences among ML models in several experimental combinations Overall, RF and SVC showed the strongest and most consistent performance, while hybrid data transformation techniques contributed to improved performance in several classifiers. In this context, LGR showed performance close to RF and SVC when these techniques were applied.

In addition, this approach should be externally validated using independent accessions, additional production seasons, and seed material obtained from different environments or germplasm collections in order to confirm its robustness and transferability under realistic application scenarios.

Moreover, the selected VIS–NIR models identified interpretable spectral regions associated with species discrimination, particularly in wavelength domains commonly related to C–H, O–H, and N–H containing compounds. The complementary SHAP analysis performed under SVM-SMOTE resampling suggested that these main discriminative regions remained broadly consistent, even when minority-class representation was reinforced. This finding provides support for the physicochemical plausibility and spectral robustness of the proposed classification approach, although future studies including species-specific compositional analyses will be necessary to confirm the biological meaning of the most influential wavelengths. Altogether, the results highlight the potential utility of VIS-NIR spectroscopy combined with ML in agronomic applications, supporting germplasm characterization and preliminary screening in seed collections, while further validation will be required before broader agronomic or breeding applications can be recommended.
