Exploring sex differences in myocardial fibrosis in patients with structurally normal hearts
Authors: Jan Mazur, Katarzyna E Gil, Katarzyna Mikrut, Ann Lowery Black, Suzanne Smart, Vien T Truong, Karolina M Zareba
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMyocardial fibrosis as evaluated by cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) plays a central role in pathophysiology of cardiovascular diseases and exhibits sex-specific differences. The goal of our study was to assess sex-related differences in fibrosis in patients with structurally normal hearts. We retrospectively studied patients undergoing clinical CMR at 1.5T with preserved cardiac function and no structural abnormalities. Standardized CMR protocols included cine imaging, T1 mapping, and late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) imaging. LGE presence, extent, and pattern, and extracellular volume fraction (ECV) were evaluated according to sex. The primary outcomes were all cause mortality and new-onset heart failure. Of 525 patients studied (43.1 ± 14.2 years, 69.5% female), 258 (49.1%) exhibited nonischemic LGE. Nonischemic LGE was more common in males (61.3% vs. 43.8%, p < 0.001), and remained so in multivariable analysis after adjustment for clinical covariates (OR 2.2, CI 1.17–4.13, p = 0.015). Women had higher ECV values (26.5 ± 4.3% vs. 23.2 ± 3.7, p < 0.001), and these sex differences remained significant in multivariable analysis (β coefficient = 1.29, p = 0.02). During 5.8 years follow-up there was no association between primary outcomes and nonischemic LGE. There was a trend towards association between ECV increase and all-cause mortality. The optimal ECV cutoff for all-cause mortality was 31.3% for females and 27.5% for males. Among patients with structurally normal hearts with preserved systolic function referred for clinical CMR exams, nonischemic LGE is more frequently observed in males, whereas ECV values are higher in females. Introduction

Sex-based distinctions in cardiac physiology contribute to variations in prevalence, progression, and treatment of cardiovascular diseases1,2. Myocardial fibrosis (MF), which is the remodeling of the extracellular matrix in response to stress and injury, is a process that plays a central role in pathophysiology of cardiovascular diseases and is known to exhibit sex specific differences1,3,4. Exploring sex-specific patterns of fibrotic cardiac remodeling has the potential to result in tailored treatment2. Since persistent MF and excessive extracellular matrix remodeling may result in cardiac hypertrophy and ventricular dilation, prevention of MF development is a potential strategy for preventing heart failure (HF) of nonischemic etiology4,5. Existing guidelines fail to account for the intricate sex-specific differentials inherent to MF, potentially hindering the identification of optimal treatment modalities6. Underrepresentation of women in studied cohorts is creating another obstacle in improving and individualizing treatment7.

Myocardial fibrosis is characterized by excessive deposition of extracellular matrix and matricellular proteins in the interstitial and perivascular compartments of the heart8. Cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) can detect and quantify MF at the macroscopic level (focal replacement MF) using late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) imaging, or at the microscopic level (interstitial MF) by extracellular volume fraction (ECV)3,9. Interstitial microscopic MF is potentially reversible, whereas macroscopic MF is predominantly thought to be permanent10. There is a scarcity of comprehensive data regarding sex-related distinctions in MF among both healthy cohorts and patients with cardiovascular diseases, and their association with prognosis1,3,11,12. Macroscopic MF is more significant in males with myocarditis, aortic stenosis and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy6,8,11–13. Females, on the other hand, both healthy and with coexisting hypertension, diabetes or aortic regurgitation, have been shown to have higher native myocardial T1 with conflicting data regarding ECV1,14–18.

The presence of MF has been associated with adverse outcomes in various cardiomyopathies, raising concern that its adverse prognostic value may apply to other patient populations19–28. It has been recently demonstrated in two large patient cohorts with structurally normal hearts that macroscopic MF, as evaluated by LGE, does not correlate with all cause-mortality, new onset HF, or arrhythmic outcomes28,29. However, diffuse interstitial MF is associated with all-cause mortality in a relatively low risk cohort of patients with structurally normal hearts and preserved ejection fraction, and this may serve as a marker of adverse outcomes28. Sex-based differences in the prognostic value of macroscopic and microscopic MF have not been investigated.

The overall goal of our study was to assess sex-related differences in MF in patients with structurally normal hearts.

Materials and methods

Patient population

We retrospectively studied consecutive patients undergoing clinical CMR exams at 1.5T (Magnetom Avanto and Espree, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) between 2010 and 2017 with preserved cardiac function and no structural cardiac abnormalities. The data time period was chosen to provide long-term follow-up of at least 5 years. Inclusion criteria were LVEF ≥ 50%, age ≥ 18 years, and the presence of LGE imaging. Although contrast administration with LGE imaging was a requirement for inclusion, T1 mapping was not a requirement as it was not routinely performed on all patients during the study period, particularly prior to 2014. The electronic medical record was reviewed for demographic and clinical data, and cardiac testing (electrocardiography, Holter monitoring, stress testing, echocardiography, coronary computed tomography angiography, single photon emission computed tomography, and left heart catheterization). Exclusion criteria were aimed at creating a low-risk cohort without structural heart disease and without findings or disease processes known to be associated with worse clinical outcomes. Ventricular chamber dimensions were deemed normal based on previously published reference ranges as noted in societal guidelines (SCMR); papillary muscles were included in the ventricular volume. The exclusion criteria were as follows: significant coronary artery disease (infarct LGE pattern, positive stress testing, obstructive lesions on left heart catheterization > 70%, or prior coronary intervention); LV hypertrophy > 12 mm; acute myocarditis; moderate or severe valvular disease; atrial fibrillation; congenital heart disease; sarcoidosis, amyloidosis; hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, hemochromatosis; cardiac channelopathies; lamin A/C mutation; abnormal right ventricular structure/function; pulmonary hypertension; Friedreich ataxia; dystrophies; constrictive pericarditis; anti-neoplastic therapy; and sickle cell disease. Additionally, patients with pacemakers and defibrillators were excluded28. To ensure comprehensive outcomes data, only patients who were followed within The Ohio State University health system for at least one year after the CMR exam were included. The study was approved by The Ohio State University’s Institutional Review Board. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, The Ohio State University’s Institutional Review Board waived the need of obtaining informed consent. All methods and protocols for this study were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

CMR image acquisition & analysis

Standardized CMR protocols were utilized including cine imaging, pre-contrast and post-contrast T1 mapping, and LGE imaging30. Postprocessing was performed utilizing cvi42 software (Circle Cardiovascular Imaging Inc. Calgary, Alberta, Canada). LV volumes and LVEF were measured from short-axis (SAX) stacks of cine frames that covered the LV. The left atrial volume index (LAVI) was calculated using the biplane area-length method31. Native and post-contrast myocardial T1 values (Modified Look-Locker Inversion Recovery) were measured within the septum on the mid SAX maps and ECV was calculated using the standard formula32. The septal region of interest was chosen based on prior data supporting its use and included regions of LGE if present. Regions of interest encompassing > 20 pixels were drawn in the septum on greyscale T1 maps as recommended by societal guidelines. Hematocrit (within 30 days) was utilized for ECV calculation. The presence, pattern, and extent of LGE was assessed by two level 3 CMR readers blinded to outcomes data. LGE presence was assessed per societal guidelines – demonstrating high signal intensity brighter than nulled myocardium in at least two consecutive short-axis slices or in two orthogonal imaging planes. LGE patterns included midwall and subepicardial fibrosis. Isolated right ventricular insertion point fibrosis was evaluated but excluded from analysis. Extent of LGE was reported using the 17 segment American Heart Association [AHA] classification33. CMR sequence details were previously published28.

Follow-up & outcomes

Follow-up was performed by review of the electronic medical record and vital status with duration calculated from the date of CMR. The primary outcomes were all-cause mortality and new-onset HF. The electronic medical record was reviewed in detail to ascertain cause of death. Cardiovascular death was defined as sudden cardiac death, HF, stroke, or thromboembolism. The lack of precise cause of death was not an exclusion criterion. New-onset HF was defined as new diagnosis of HF documented by a physician requiring diuretic therapy. A secondary arrhythmic outcome was defined as ventricular tachycardia (VT), ventricular fibrillation, VT ablation, or appropriate implantable cardioverter defibrillator (ICD) intervention.

Statistical analysis

Categorical data are expressed as the frequency with percentage, and comparison between groups was performed using the chi-square test or Fisher exact test. The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using skewness, kurtosis, visual inspection of the histogram, and QQ plot. Continuous variables are displayed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normal distribution or presented as median (interquartile range) for non-normal distribution. T-test or Mann–Whitney U test was performed to compare differences amongst two groups for normally and non-normally distributed variables, respectively. To assess sex-based differences in ECV and LGE presence, logistic and linear regression analyses were conducted, respectively. Regression diagnostics were performed to test model assumptions including linearity of the data, lack of influential values, normality of residuals, and homoscedasticity. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were drawn to assess differences between the two groups for time-to-event data. The date of the CMR study served as the starting point (time zero). Cox regression models were used to assess the association between clinical outcomes and both ECV and LGE presence in the entire cohort, as well as in sex-stratified subgroups. Furthermore, the optimal cutoff value for ECV and outcomes of mortality was determined using maximally selected rank statistics, which take time-to-event into account. Proportionality assumptions of the Cox regression models were assessed by using Schoenfeld residuals. The deviance residuals and the dfbeta values were used to examine influential observations. Martingale residuals against continuous predictors were applied to assess nonlinearity and the functional form of predictors. Hazard ratios are presented as mean and 95% confidence intervals. For all statistical tests, two-sided p values of < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA) and R software, version 4.0.3 (The R Foundation, Vienna, Austria) with the “readxl”, “tidyverse”, “table1”, “compareGroups”, “naniar”, “survival”, and “survminer” packages.”

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 525 patients were included in the analysis, mean age 43.1 ± 14.2 years, 69.5% female (Table 1). Female patients had worse cardiovascular risk profiles with more history of hypertension and HF, and thus higher diuretic use. As expected, indexed LV volumes were smaller and LVEF was higher in females vs. males. There were no other significant differences in age, comorbidities, pharmacotherapy, scan indications, and CMR parameters between genders.

Sex differences in myocardial fibrosis

A total of 258 (49.1%) patients exhibited nonischemic LGE (Table 1). Data to calculate ECV was available in 276 (52.6%) patients. Nonischemic LGE (excluding isolated right ventricular insertion point fibrosis) was more common in males than females (61.3% vs. 43.8%, p < 0.001, Fig. 1), with a higher rate of both midwall and subepicardial LGE patterns. The median number of involved AHA segments was higher in men than women. The most frequently involved AHA segments included the basal to mid inferoseptal and basal inferolateral walls (Fig. 2). Women were more likely to have higher ECV values (26.5 ± 4.3% vs. 23.2 ± 3.7, p < 0.001, Fig. 1). Pre-contrast myocardial T1 was significantly higher whereas post-contrast myocardial T1 and hematocrit were significantly lower in the female cohort. Association of fibrosis and outcomes

During a median follow-up of 5.8 years (IQR: 3.9–7.6), 13 (2.5%) patients died (9 women, 4 men), and 18 (3.4%) developed new-onset HF (11 women, 7 men). A total of 31 (5.9%) patients met the composite endpoint of all-cause mortality and new-onset HF (21 women, 10 men). Among the ECV cohort, 6 (2.2%) patients died (5 women, 1 man) and 13 (4.7%) developed new-onset HF (8 women, 5 men), yielding 19 (6.9%) patients who met the composite endpoint (13 women, 6 men).

There were no significant differences in all-cause mortality (HR: 0.5, 95% CI: 0.06–4.28, p = 0.53), new-onset HF (HR: 1.47, 95% CI: 0.48–4.49, p = 0.5), or composite endpoint of all-cause mortality and new-onset HF (HR: 1.11, 95% CI: 0.42–2.91, p = 0.84) in male vs. female patients. Within both the male and female cohorts, there was no association between primary outcomes and nonischemic LGE. There was a trend towards association between ECV increase (assessed as a continuous variable per 1% increase) and all-cause mortality both in female (HR 1.16, 95% CI 0.99–1.35, p = 0.07) and male cohorts (HR 1.33, 95% CI 0.99–1.8, p = 0.06). The optimal ECV cutoff for all-cause mortality using maximally selected rank statistics was 31.3% for females and 27.5% for males. These findings indicate that females with an ECV of 31.3% or higher and males with an ECV of 27.5% or higher have a significantly increased risk of mortality compared to those with lower ECV values (Fig. 4).

The arrhythmic outcome (VT or ventricular fibrillation, VT ablation, appropriate ICD intervention) was met by 27 (5.1%) patients including 25 (9.1%) patients from the ECV analysis subgroup. There were no significant differences in arrhythmic outcomes (HR: 0.78, 95% CI: 0.31–1.95, p = 0.84) in male vs. female patients. Within both male and female cohorts, there was no association between arrhythmic outcomes and LGE presence or ECV elevation (per 1% increase).

Discussion

We investigated sex-related differences in myocardial fibrosis in patients with structurally normal hearts. Our data suggest that, among patients with structurally normal hearts with preserved systolic function referred for clinical CMR exams, nonischemic LGE is more frequently observed in males, whereas ECV values are higher in females. We note that there was a trend towards an increase in all-cause mortality associated with ECV increase, both in female and male cohorts over a 5-year follow-up. LGE presence and ECV elevation were not associated with new-onset HF or arrhythmic events in this cohort.

In a relatively lower risk cohort without any structural heart abnormalities, we demonstrate that nonischemic LGE is more common in males than in females. Sex-related differences in MF arise from an intricate interplay of sex chromosomes, epigenetics and hormones2,6,34. Genome-wide association studies have revealed differential responses to pressure overload between sexes: men display amplified pro-fibrotic and inflammatory reactions, whereas extracellular matrix remodeling and inflammatory gene pathways are attenuated in women4,13,35. Cardioprotective influence in females may be partially attributed to sex differences in beta-adrenergic responsiveness36. Pronounced significant MF in response to beta-adrenergic stimulation has been demonstrated in male but not female mice, likely due to a higher activation of cardiac fibroblasts into myofibroblasts36. Females show lower level of transforming growth factor beta and angiotensinogen, both involved in synthesis and deposition of MF6. Interestingly, women after menopause retain protection against MF, despite decrease in level of antifibrotic 17 beta-estradiol5,12,36. The mechanism of this phenomenon is unclear. Our findings of more frequent nonischemic LGE in men with structurally normal hearts are corroborated by the observation of more pronounced macroscopic replacement MF in men with other cardiac pathologies including myocarditis, dilated cardiomyopathy, and aortic stenosis, all confirmed by histology6,8,11–13. This may suggest a potential variation with women exhibiting a reduced propensity for progression towards apoptosis and irreversible myocyte injury.

Extracellular volume fraction, a measure of interstitial fibrosis, was higher in females than in males in our cohort of patients with structurally normal hearts. This is in alignment with findings demonstrated in another small cohort of healthy volunteers1. Higher ECV values could be partially explained by the fact that the interstitium as well as the number and size of myocytes remains constant throughout life in females, whereas males experience significant loss of myocytes with the subsequent increase of size of the remaining myocytes through fusion and loss of interstitium1,37. Recent observation of no significant increase in ECV with age in females further supports this hypothesis35. Lower hematocrit in women could also potentially contribute to higher ECV13,17. In our study, however, females had lower hematocrit values but had higher pre-contrast as well as lower post-contrast myocardial T1 values. This is in agreement with the prior analysis of the Multiethnic Study of Atherosclerosis, which revealed higher pre-contrast T1 values and ECV along with lower post-contrast T1 values in females2,17. Native myocardial T1 has been demonstrated to be higher in healthy females even after adjustment for blood T1 values and heart rate14,15. Partial volume effect in thinner female hearts might be a factor14,16. Since ECV is believed to serve as the outcome from downstream impact of comorbidities and clinical risk, higher prevalence of hypertension and history of HF within our female cohort, mirroring trends observed in the general population, could potentially have an influence12,27,38,39. However, our further analysis showed higher ECV values in females even after adjustment for those comorbidities (Table 2). Further studies with histological correlation are needed to determine if higher ECV values in females represent more significant interstitial MF or sex specific reference values for native myocardial T1 and ECV should be applied for recognition of increased interstitial MF.

Presence of nonischemic LGE in patients with structurally normal hearts—regardless of sex—may not require further testing or tailored therapy in the absence of other risk factors28. Although stratification by gender was limited by the low number of events, our analysis suggests that the association between ECV elevation and all-cause mortality may hold true both for males and females with different ECV thresholds resulting from biological differences between the two sexes. If confirmed, ECV elevation could be a potential therapeutic target for both sexes. Since limited data on ECV decrease with renin-angiotensin-aldosterone blockers is already available, existing agents could be investigated24.

Limitations

This is a single center study which should be validated in large multicenter cohorts with longer follow-up. T1 mapping was not routinely performed at our institution in all patients prior to 2014 which limits our sample size for this portion of the analysis. Whole heart T1 mapping coverage was not obtained due to the retrospective nature of this clinical CMR cohort with protocols focusing on mid SAX maps. Given our relatively young and lower risk cohort, our event rate was low thus limiting outcomes analysis, including accuracy of identifying optimal ECV cutoff for predicting mortality in the male and female cohorts. Although arrhythmias were not the primary outcome evaluated, our study did not find a significant association between MF and arrhythmic outcomes. This may be explained by several reasons including our lower risk cohort (as evidenced by age, mean ECV, and median LGE segments involved), as well as more heterogeneous “softer” outcomes (VT ablation, appropriate ICD discharge). Lastly, LGE quantification was not performed in this cohort which is a limitation. However, LGE assessment was performed according to societal guidelines utilizing location and extent via the AHA segment model.

Conclusions

Sex specific differences in myocardial fibrosis are evident even in patients with structurally normal hearts with preserved systolic function. Nonischemic LGE appears more common in males, whereas ECV values are higher in females. Application of sex specific reference values for native myocardial T1 and ECV could potentially improve detection of diffuse interstitial MF. ECV elevation could potentially serve as a risk stratification tool; however, its association with adverse outcomes needs further studies in larger populations with a greater number of events and longer follow-up.
