Global prevalence of metabolic syndrome among individuals with sarcopenia: a systematic review and meta-analysis of 72,129 patients
Authors: Fei Zhang, Hang Xu, Ziwen Wang, Dishan Wu, Jinyun Wang, Zhong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSarcopenia is an age-related geriatric syndrome associated with an increased risk of metabolic syndrome (MS). However, the prevalence of MS among individuals with sarcopenia remains uncertain. This meta-analysis aimed to estimate the global pooled prevalence of MS in patients with sarcopenia.MethodsWe conducted the first global systematic review to quantify the prevalence of MS among people with sarcopenia. A systematic search of PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, Embase, and CINAHL Complete databases was conducted on 28 May 2025. Pooled prevalence was calculated with a random effects model. Sensitivity and subgroup analyses were performed to identify sources of heterogeneity. This meta-analysis was prospectively registered in the PROSPERO database (Number: CRD420251115631).Results15 studies involving 72,129 individuals with sarcopenia were ultimately included and sample sizes ranged from 47 to 61,438. The pooled prevalence of MS among individuals with sarcopenia was 30%(95% CI 22%–40%); with substantial between-study heterogeneity (I² = 99.3%, p < 0.001). Diagnostic criteria markedly influenced the estimates: studies using the National Cholesterol Education Program Adult Treatment Panel III (NCEP ATP III) definition yielded 29% (95% CI 22%–37%), whereas those employing the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) definition reported 47% (95% CI 46%–48%). Substantial geographic variation was observed, with relatively higher prevalence estimates reported in South Korea (39%, 95% CI 20%–59%).ConclusionsThis study highlights the global prevalence of MS in individuals with sarcopenia, with comparatively higher pooled prevalence estimates observed in South Korea, the United States, and Australia. Standardized diagnostic criteria and unified management strategies for this comorbidity warrant urgent investigation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-026-02092-5.

Introduction

Sarcopenia is a syndrome characterized by a progressive, generalized loss of skeletal muscle mass and strength [1]. Over 50 million adults worldwide had sarcopenia in 2000, and estimates suggest the number will exceed 200 million by 2040 [2]. A systematic review and meta-analysis found that sarcopenia affects 25–73.7% of residents in nursing facilities and 5.2–62.7% of older adults living in the community [3]. Although traditionally viewed as an age-related process, it can also arise secondary to diverse disease states, including cancer [4], chronic hepatic failure [5], and numerous other conditions. The European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People (EWGSOP), the International Working Group on Sarcopenia(IWGS), the Foundation for the National Institutes of Health (FNIH) and the Asia Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) all advocate that sarcopenia be diagnosed only when both low muscle mass and impaired muscle function are present; nevertheless, their phenotypic criteria and specific cut-off values are not fully consistent [6].

A universally accepted definition of metabolic syndrome (MS) has not yet been agreed upon, just like sarcopenia. MS is characterized by a combination of abdominal adiposity, atherogenic dyslipidemia, elevated fasting glucose, and raised blood pressure. This cluster of interrelated risk factors collectively increases the risk of cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, and stroke [7, 8]. MS has been defined by several organizations, most notably the National Cholesterol Education Program Adult Treatment Panel III (NCEP ATP III) and the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) [9]. The estimated global prevalence of MS varies significantly depending on the diagnostic criteria, ranging from 12.5% to 31.4% worldwide [10]. For instance, using the IDF criteria, the prevalence is estimated to be approximately 25%, with further variations based on race, age, and sex [11, 12]. Insulin resistance and inflammatory reactions are pivotal drivers in the pathogenesis of both sarcopenia and MS. Insulin resistance can drive muscle atrophy by blunting anabolic signaling through the canonical insulin cascade, such as phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase, 3-phosphoinositidedependent protein kinase-1, Akt, mammalian target of rapamycin, and p70S6 kinase [13, 14]. Skeletal muscle is the principal site of glucose disposal, mediating roughly 80% of systemic glucose clearance. Accordingly, any appreciable decline in muscle mass attenuates this uptake, fostering insulin resistance and disrupting metabolic homeostasis [15]. Obesity, hypertension, and diabetes are core components of MS. Notably, these conditions are also established and modifiable factors that trigger sarcopenia [16]. Zhang et al. [17] reported a clear positive link between sarcopenia and MS (OR 2.01, 95% CI 1.63–2.47), whereas Longitudinal analyses by Li et al. [18] observed no significant association between sarcopenic obesity and the subsequent development of MS. Such contradictory evidence in the existing literature necessitates a systematic synthesis of available data to more reliably estimate the burden of MS among individuals with sarcopenia. Although recent study has shown that among middle-aged and older non-obese adults with sarcopenia, the pooled prevalence of MS is 36.45% (95% CI 28.28–45.48%) [17], no meta-analysis has yet synthesized the prevalence of MS across the entire sarcopenic population. Therefore, the aim of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to estimate the pooled prevalence of MS among individuals with sarcopenia at a global level. By providing a quantitative summary of current evidence, this study seeks to clarify the epidemiological burden of this comorbidity and to inform future research directions.

Methods

This systematic review was prospectively registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under registration number CRD420251115631 and is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement [19]. The eligibility criteria were defined using the PEOS framework (population, exposure, outcomes, and study design), which is appropriate for observational studies focusing on disease prevalence without a comparator group. Data pooling

A comprehensive search was conducted across five databases including PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, Embase, and CINAHL Complete to gather relevant observational and retrospective studies. The complete exclusion criteria are depicted in Fig. 1.

PRISMA flow diagram of the selection process of studies on MS prevalence among sarcopenia patients

Identification

A systematic search of PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, Embase, and CINAHL Complete databases was conducted on 28 May 2025. The search strategy was developed to ensure comprehensive and appropriate coverage of published studies addressing the relationship between sarcopenia and MS [1, 20]. The following key terms were employed: sarcopenia, muscle loss, prevalence, occurrence, MS, and MetS. Full search syntax appears in Supplementary Table 1. Reference lists were manually screened for additional relevant studies. Corresponding authors were not contacted for supplementary data. No language restrictions were imposed during the search; however, only English and Chinese language publications were included in the screening phase.

Screening

Title and abstract screening were performed by two independent reviewers with cross-verification, while full-text screening and data extraction were conducted by one reviewer and verified by a second reviewer. A data extraction form based on PEOS criteria was developed, extracting the following data: First author, year, number of individuals with sarcopenia, number of individuals with MS (cases among sarcopenia group), time setting, country, population, study design, age, gender, diagnostic criteria for sarcopenia, and diagnostic criteria for MS.

Eligibility

Risk of bias assessment was performed using the JBI (Joanna Briggs Institute) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Prevalence Studies [21]. Scores were assigned for each criterion based on whether the study adequately addressed it: Yes = 1, Not Applicable = 0, Unclear = 0, No = 0. The overall methodological quality of each study was determined by its total score. Studies achieving a total score ≥ 7 were considered of good quality [22]. Studies with a total score ≥ 5 were included in this meta-analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data were extracted directly from each publication, capturing sample sizes and the reported counts of individuals with sarcopenia and MS. I² values exceeding 50% signal substantial heterogeneity [23]. All meta-analyses were conducted in R 4.4.2 using the “meta”, “metafor”, “dplyr”, and “ggplot2” packages. Subgroup meta-analyses were conducted according to MS definition (NCEP-ATP III, IDF, and other criteria) and by country, with pooled prevalence for each outcome derived via a random-effects model.

Results

Based on the search strategy, 2,743 studies were identified, of which 101 were selected for full-text review. A total of 15 studies meeting the inclusion criteria were ultimately included in this review [18, 24–37] (Fig. 1).

Quality of studies critical appraisal

Study quality was evaluated with the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Studies Reporting Prevalence. Scores ranged from 5 to 9 out of 9. 11 studies met the criterion for high quality (≥ 7 points), while the remaining 4 were classified as moderate quality (≥ 5 points) because of limitations in sampling strategy, sample-size justification, and clarity of the diagnostic approach. Across the cohort, the most common limitations were failure to justify sample size (n = 9) and inadequate handling of non-response (n = 11). Conversely, all studies provided clear descriptions of participants and settings, employed valid diagnostic criteria for MS and applied appropriate statistical techniques. The complete JBI appraisal table is provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Characteristics of the included studies

Table 2 summarizes the baseline characteristics of 15 eligible studies, comprising 72,129 participants (sample sizes ranged from 47 to 61,438). All studies reported either the mean age or the age range. Gender was not reported in 3 studies; among the remaining 12 studies, which included 70,027 participants, 36,858 were men (52.6%) and 33,169 were women (47.4%). The majority of studies (n = 9) employed a cross-sectional design, while 6 studies utilized a cohort design. Only baseline data from these 6 cohort studies were included. Publication years ranged from 2004 to 2025, with the majority published within the last decade (n = 9). Most of the included studies were conducted in Asia (n = 9). Across the six countries included, China, South Korea, Japan, and the United States supplied the greatest share of studies (n = 3). At present, no universal consensus has been reached for the diagnostic criteria of either MS or sarcopenia. Regarding sarcopenia, six of the included studies applied internationally recognized definitions, with three adopting EWGSOP criteria, two employing AWGS criteria, and one utilizing FNIH criteria. In contrast, nine studies diagnosed MS according to the NCEP ATP III definition, whereas three studies relied on the IDF criteria.

Meta-Analytic findings

Figure 2 synthesizes data from 15 studies comprising 72,129 participants with sarcopenia. The pooled prevalence of MS derived from a random-effects model was 30% (95% CI 22%–40%). Substantial heterogeneity was observed (I² = 99.3%, τ² = 0.0256, p < 0.001).

Sensitivity checks and publication bias

To evaluate the robustness of the pooled prevalence for MS among individuals with sarcopenia, we performed a sensitivity analyses. Systematic removal of each individual study did not materially alter the pooled prevalence, which remained stable within a narrow range of 29–33% (Fig. 3). The largest deviation was observed after excluding Sanada 2012, yielding a prevalence of 33% (95% CI 24–46%). Publication bias among the included studies was assessed using funnel plots. Visual inspection of the funnel plot (Fig. 4) showed an approximately symmetrical distribution of the Freeman–Tukey double-arcsine transformed proportions around the pooled effect, with most studies clustering toward the top of the plot and only minor asymmetry at the lower left quadrant. The trim-and-fill method was applied to explore the potential impact of funnel plot asymmetry, resulting in the imputation of two hypothetical studies. After adjustment, the pooled prevalence decreased from 30% (95% CI: 22%–40%) to 27% (95% CI: 19%–36%). Although the pooled estimate was slightly lower, the confidence intervals before and after adjustment showed substantial overlap, and the overall interpretation of the findings remained materially unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 1). Egger’s linear regression test yielded t = 1.67 (df = 13, p = 0.118), providing no statistically significant evidence of publication bias.

Subgroup analysis

To explore country heterogeneity, we stratified the 15 included studies by the nation in which they were conducted. As shown in Fig. 5, country-specific pooled prevalence estimates showed considerable variability, with relatively higher estimates observed in South Korea (39%, 95% CI 20%–59%), the United States (33%, 95% CI 18%–51%), and Australia (31%, 95% CI 15%–50%), while China showed a prevalence of 29% (95% CI 19%–41%). Japan exhibited a comparatively lower estimate (25%, 95% CI 7%–50%) and Brazil showed a comparatively lower pooled prevalence (13%, 95% CI 6%–26%).

Fig. 5Subgroup analysis based on country

Subgroup analysis based on country

To determine whether the diagnostic criteria influenced the pooled prevalence, we stratified the 15 studies according to the MS definition employed (Supplementary Fig. 2). NCEP-ATP III (9 studies, 65,207 participants) produced a pooled prevalence of 29% (95% CI 22–37%), with marked between-study heterogeneity (I² = 98.3%). IDF criteria (3 studies, 6,559 participants) yielded a higher pooled prevalence of 47% (95% CI 46–48%), accompanied by minimal heterogeneity (I² = 0%). “Other” definitions (3 studies, 363 participants) resulted in the lowest pooled estimate of 18% (95% CI 1–45%), with extreme heterogeneity (I² = 96.6%).

Discussion

As far as we are aware, this systematic review is the first to quantify the prevalence of MS in the individuals with sarcopenia. Our findings indicate that the pooled prevalence of MS among individuals with sarcopenia is 30% (95% CI 22%–40%). The robustness of our main finding is supported by a sensitivity analysis, which demonstrated that the overall estimate was not excessively influenced by any individual study. Nevertheless, a key feature of our analysis was the high level of heterogeneity (I² = 99.3%), and our subgroup analyses offer crucial insights into this variation. Our findings indicate that the reported prevalence of MS changed markedly when different diagnostic criteria were applied. Under the NCEP-ATP III criteria, the prevalence of MS in individuals with sarcopenia was 29% (95% CI 22%–37%), whereas the IDF definition yielded a prevalence of 47% (95% CI 46%–48%). In addition to inconsistencies in the diagnostic criteria for MS, substantial heterogeneity was also observed in the definitions of sarcopenia across the included studies, which were based on several international consensus statements, including EWGSOP 2010/2019, AWGS 2019, and the FNIH criteria. These definitions differ with respect to the components they emphasize, which include muscle mass, muscle strength, and physical performance, as well as in the cutoff values applied for classification. Recently, the AWGS released its 2025 consensus update, which proposes a conceptual shift from the traditional diagnosis of “sarcopenia” toward a more continuous and comprehensive framework of “muscle health.” [38] This paradigm update partially reflects the current heterogeneous landscape of sarcopenia definitions. Future research would benefit from the adoption of more harmonized frameworks for evaluating sarcopenia, which may facilitate improved reproducibility and generalizability of research findings. The prevalence of MS also varies considerably across different patient populations. One review that included 11 eligible studies involving 2,501 patients reported a pooled prevalence of 19.4% (95% CI 15.1% to 23.8%) among individuals with inflammatory bowel disease [39]. Another meta-analysis conducted by Janani et al. estimated the pooled prevalence of MS in patients with IBD to be 21.8% (95% CI 14.3–31.6%) [40]. A meta-analysis involving 54,278 patients with COPD found an overall prevalence of MS of 37% (95% CI 30.6–43.8%; I² = 99.03%, p < 0.001) [41]. The prevalence of MS varies by underlying condition: 41.3% (95% CI 28.8–55.1) in patients with schizophrenia [42], and 46.8% (95% CI: 38.6–55.2%) in those with gout [43]. Meta-analytic evidence indicates that the pooled prevalence of MS among pregnant women is 16.3% [44].

Across different study populations, the prevalence of MS also varies. Country-specific pooled prevalence estimates showed considerable variability, with relatively higher estimates observed in South Korea (39%, 95% CI 20%–59%), the United States (33%, 95% CI 18%–51%), and Australia (31%, 95% CI 15%–50%), while China showed a prevalence of 29% (95% CI 19%–41%). Japan exhibited a comparatively lower estimate (25%, 95% CI 7%–50%), and Brazil showed a comparatively lower pooled prevalence (13%, 95% CI 6%–26%). However, the wide confidence intervals and substantial within-country heterogeneity suggest that differences in population characteristics strongly influence prevalence estimates. Moroccan adults demonstrate a substantially higher prevalence of 31% [45].In children and adolescents with obesity, the prevalence of MS is 29.4% [46]. These results imply that ethnic diversity and age are key determinants of the global variation in prevalence.

Recent evidence consistently demonstrates an inverse relationship between sarcopenia and MS. Lower skeletal muscle mass is associated with higher MS prevalence, whereas gains in muscle mass confer protection [47–49]. Despite divergent views on the association between individual metabolic-syndrome components and sarcopenia prevalence, most studies have found that MS is linked to a higher prevalence of sarcopenia [37, 50]. The biological mechanisms linking sarcopenia to MS are multifaceted. Insulin resistance acts as a key mediator linking MS to sarcopenia [37]. Systemic insulin resistance in MS disrupts insulin-mediated anabolic signaling, which is vital for muscle protein synthesis [51]. Moreover, the chronic inflammation characteristic of MS, driven by pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6, not only exacerbates insulin resistance but also directly impairs myogenesis and promotes muscle atrophy [52, 53]. Genetic factors also contribute: Park et al. [54] examined polygenic risk scores for sarcopenia and their interplay with exercise, suggesting that genetically susceptible individuals of sarcopenia may carry a height risk of MS. Consequently, Middle-aged and older adults with elevated genetic liability for sarcopenia might therefore benefit from regular physical activity as a preventive strategy against MS. Sex-stratified analyses reveal gender-specific sarcopenia thresholds and their differential association with MS, underscoring how demographic factors modulate this link [48]. Physical inactivity, inflammation, and myostatin are likewise recognized as contributors to the association between sarcopenia and MS [47]. Moreover, our latest research proposes a novel mechanism involving exosomes in the comorbidity of sarcopenia and MS. Exosomal miRNAs derived from senescent skeletal muscle drive systemic metabolic dysregulation by targeting critical signaling pathways in insulin sensitivity and lipid metabolism [55].

Our findings have several limitations. First, the prevalence of MS among sarcopenic individuals exhibited pronounced heterogeneity (I² = 99.3%), underscoring substantial inter-study disparity. Stratifying by diagnostic criteria or by country failed to diminish it, and even stepwise exclusion sensitivity analyses left the variability largely intact. The persistence of high heterogeneity despite these analytical approaches indicates that additional, unexamined sources of variability are likely present. The lack of uniform diagnostic criteria for sarcopenia and MS represents an additional limitation. Only some studies adopted internationally recognized definitions, and variation in diagnostic approaches may have contributed to the substantial heterogeneity observed across studies. Future investigations should converge on harmonized diagnostic criteria and standardized methodologies to curb heterogeneity and enhance the comparability of findings. Moreover, residual heterogeneity may be partly attributable to differences in population characteristics that could not be fully explored in the present analysis, including age distribution, sex composition, genetic background, and regional lifestyle or environmental factors. The limited number of available studies and the inconsistent reporting of key covariates precluded meta-regression to formally assess these factors, which should be addressed in future well-characterized studies. Second, another limitation of this meta-analysis is the restriction to English and Chinese language publications, which may have resulted in language bias. Studies published in other languages were not included and could have contributed additional evidence, particularly from regions underrepresented in the current analysis. Third, the pooled prevalence estimates for several countries relied on a limited number of studies. Consequently, these country-level findings warrant cautious interpretation, particularly due to their wide confidence intervals and substantial within-country heterogeneity.

Conclusions

The high prevalence of MS as a comorbidity in sarcopenia, especially in countries like South Korea, the United States, and Australia, underscores the need for routine metabolic screening in sarcopenia management. To further improve clinical outcomes, it is also crucial to standardise the diagnostic criteria for MS specifically for the sarcopenic population.
