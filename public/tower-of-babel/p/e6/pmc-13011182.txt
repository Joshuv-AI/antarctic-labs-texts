Customized ultra-flexible and adhesive spinal cord stimulation electrode for chronic pain management
Authors: Miao Tang, Zili Li, Liting Sun, Quansheng He, Haoyi Yang, Jia-Jia Wang, Yousheng Shu, Juan Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Epidural spinal cord stimulation (SCS) has emerged as an effective therapy for chronic pain. However, its long-term efficacy is limited by the poor biocompatibility and instability at the electrode-tissue interface. Herein, we developed an innovative electrode fabrication strategy to address these limitations and validated it in mouse chronic pain models. We designed and synthesized a modified polydimethylsiloxane substrate carrying hydroxyl groups and sulfur anchor points. The hydroxyl groups enhance interfacial adhesion with surrounding tissue, improving mechanical stability and preventing electrode displacement, while the thiol groups form strong chemical bonds with the metal circuit layer, effectively avoiding delamination. The fabricated electrode with this innovative material demonstrated exceptional stability and maintained functional integrity for over 6 weeks with minimal inflammatory responses. SCS with our electrode showed robust analgesic effects in spinal cord injury, sciatic nerve injury, and diabetic neuropathic pain models. Specifically, the analgesic effects persisted for over 1.5 h after stimulation, unveiling its sustained efficacy. Stable spinal local field potential recordings further confirmed reliable neural interfacing of the electrode. Our research introduces a promising electrode revealing long-term stability, exceptional biocompatibility, and validated efficacy across diverse pain models, which paves the foundation of electrode fabrication for advanced neuromodulation technologies in treating chronic pain management.

Introduction

Chronic pain profoundly diminishes the life quality of patients, and often leads to psychological comorbidities such as anxiety and depression [1]. Pharmacological treatments serve as the mainstay for managing neuropathic pain. However, they frequently fail to provide adequate pain relief [2,3], and patients often face the risk of recurrence [4,5]. These clinical limitations highlight the urgent need for effective non-pharmacological therapeutic strategies for drug-refractory pain. In this context, neuromodulation has emerged as a compelling alternative, with epidural spinal cord stimulation (SCS) standing out as a highly promising modality due to its ability to effectively relieve drug-refractory pain and reduce reliance on opioid medications [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. Unfortunately, the long-term efficacy of current devices for neuromodulation is hindered by technical fabrication challenges and instability.

Most existing implantable SCS electrodes utilize rigid, high elastic modulus circuit systems, which are mechanically mismatched with the surrounding soft spinal tissue. This issue could induce differential strain and micromotion at the electrode-tissue interface, deteriorating the long-term durability. For example, the widely used substrates like polyimide (PI) suffer from electrode migration during spinal movements due to the mechanical mismatch with soft spinal cord tissue [[10], [11], [12], [13]]. This migration will provoke acute and chronic tissue responses, leading to chronic inflammation, tissue damage, poor signal stability, and reduced long-term device performance. To mitigate this mechanical mismatch, recent advances have explored flexible neural interfaces [14,15]. Thinner PI substrates were designed to minimize implantation injury and improve compatibility with spinal movements [7,[16], [17], [18]]. However, thinner PI substrates are prone to tearing and mechanical failure.

It is important to note that reducing mechanical mismatch primarily improves stress distribution and conformal contact at the tissue interface, but does not inherently guarantee stable positional fixation of the electrode. In addition to bulk substrate mechanical compliance, another critical yet distinct challenge is controlling stable fixation at the electrode–tissue interface under dynamic spinal conditions. Specifically, circumferential electrodes such as 360-degree SCS electrodes were fabricated through complex lithography techniques and processes [19]. The complex shapes of circumferential electrodes pose challenges for surgical implantation and increase clinical risks. Thereafter, a minimally invasive fixation strategy was developed for fixing electrodes by incorporating shape actuation, during which the rolled needle-like electrode is implanted on the site of interest and unfurled into its paddle-type conformation with air pressure through the fluidic tube [7]. Nevertheless, leakages of the tube could induce pressure losses and lower the efficiency. Moreover, it is also difficult to expand the electrode to reach accurate positioning in vivo through pressure control [20]. As a result, current SCS electrode designs often address either mechanical compliance or fixation stability in isolation, but rarely integrate both in a unified material and device architecture, thereby limiting their long-term reliability and broader clinical applicability.

Moreover, the SCS electrodes also suffer from adhesive performance issues, which could impede the practical application of flexible material to chronic pain management [6,[21], [22], [23]]. Commercial or synthetic PI materials also suffer from significant challenges in biocompatibility assessment, primarily due to the complex interplay of multiple variables inherent in their composite nature [24]. Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) has emerged as a promising alternative substrate for flexible electrodes due to its flexibility, biocompatibility, and chemical stability [[25], [26], [27]]. However, its poor hydrophilicity and weak adhesion to biological tissues and conductive metals remain significant barriers to long-term service. The weak adhesive issue could also give rise to poor stability at the electrode-tissue interface and compromise therapeutic outcomes. For instance, clinically implantable flexible PDMS or polyurethane lacks sufficient adhesion to the tissue, resulting in internal displacement that prevents precise stimulation for targeted therapy [28]. A critical yet underexplored strategy to address these limitations is improving adhesion stability at the electrode-tissue [29,30] and electrode-metal interfaces [[31], [32], [33], [34]]. Though micro-nano surface structuring [34] and plasma treatments [33] have been used to enhance adhesion in other contexts, their transient effects of recovery of hydrophobicity and incompatibility with the super-soft spinal cord make them unsuitable for SCS applications.

To overcome these challenges, we developed a one-pot chemical strategy to engineer ultra-conformal dural patch electrodes. The multiscale interfacial adhesion mechanism that synergistically enhanced electrode-spinal cord integration was established by leveraging click chemistry-mediated hydroxyl groups grafted onto the PDMS backbones, coupling with dynamic boron ester and dative bonding networks. This design paradigm not only addresses the chronic inflammation induced by traditional rigid interfaces but also enables stable electrophysiological signal acquisition across dynamic spinal movements. We further conducted a systematic evaluation of the electrode across representative neuropathic pain etiologies, encompassing peripheral, central, and metabolic pathologies in mice. The electrodes demonstrated robust biocompatibility, consistent signal fidelity, and significant analgesic efficacy across all tested pain models. These results underscore the potential of this system as a versatile neuromodulation interface for diverse chronic pain conditions. Our methodology provides a versatile toolbox for next-generation bioelectronic interfaces, effectively bridging material innovation with unmet clinical needs in neuromodulation therapeutics.

Results and discussion

Preparation of substrate materials for the SCS electrode

Hydroxyl groups represent the most dominating factor in governing the adhesion of electrode materials [35,36]. Previous PDMS-based adhesive elastomers carrying hydroxyl groups have been synthesized through either two-step or simplified block strategies [26,27,37], and have demonstrated favorable adhesion performance. The block PDMS was first synthesized through a click reaction between vinyl-terminated PDMS and dithiothreitol (DTT). Subsequently, the block polyborosilane (PBS) was obtained after a condensation reaction between boric acid (BA) and the separated block PDMS. However, these systems were not specifically designed for neural electrode interfacing, where simultaneous requirements on mechanical stability, tissue adhesion, and metal–polymer bonding must be satisfied. In this work, we implement a one-pot synthesis strategy to deliberately integrate multiple functional motifs within one PDMS-based elastomer (denoted as one-pot PBS), achieving fabrication of tissue-interfacing bioelectronics (Fig. 1A). More importantly, this one-pot process streamlines fabrication and avoids intermediate separation or transfer steps required in previous strategies, which is favorable for large-scale fabrication. The key innovation lies in forming a crosslinked elastomer via pairwise reactions between functional groups on backbones, and the BA serves as a crosslinker. It should be noted that the block PBS has a longer flexible backbone and a lower crosslinking density. Meanwhile, the crosslinking and chain growth occur simultaneously in the one-pot system. The dynamically crosslinked network hinders the chain mobility, leading to a shorter flexible backbone and higher crosslinking density (Fig. 1B). The chemical structure of the resulting one-pot PBS was characterized through Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy. The results demonstrate the formation of the PBS elastomers as shown in Fig. S1. The C=C stretching vibration peak at 1610 cm−1 arising from vinyl-terminated PDMS disappeared after reaction, indicating the successful thiol–ene reaction between PDMS and dithiothreitol (DTT). Meanwhile, a new peak appeared at 1370 cm−1, which is attributed to the asymmetric B-O stretching from the crosslinking points [38]. The stretching vibration of thiol groups was still observed at 2500 cm−1 due to the thiol-capped PBS backbone. The broad peak located at 3500 cm−1 represents the hydroxyl groups. Moreover, the X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurement was employed to characterize the chemical structure of one-pot PBS. The B 1s spectrum demonstrates binding energies at 190.2, 192.4, and 194.1 eV (Fig. 1C), which are attributed to the bonding of B–O–C and B–OHx species [26,39]. Furthermore, the 1H NMR spectrum of PBS (partially dissolved) demonstrates a chemical shift at 0.76 ppm, which is ascribed to the methylene groups adjacent to Si-O bonds after click reaction, whereas the peaks at 5.23 and 4.6 ppm are attributed to the hydroxyl groups in the elastomer (Fig. S2) [39]. It should be noted that the hydroxyl groups are active species, and it is difficult to quantitatively characterize the content in the system. The dangling hydroxyl groups within the network contribute to robust tissue adhesion [35,36,40]. While sulfur species act as anchoring points for the gold electrode through Au–S interactions [41]. Notably, these structural distinctions translate directly into mechanical performance differences between block PDMS and block PBS (Fig. 1D and E). As schematically illustrated in Fig. 1D, block PBS consists of longer effective network chains with extensive entanglements and limited dynamic crosslinking, whereas one-pot PBS forms a dense network of shorter effective network chains. Rheological analysis reveals that one-pot PBS exhibits solid-like behavior across the entire tested frequency range, with storage modulus (G′) consistently exceeding loss modulus (G″). Whereas, block PBS displays liquid-like characteristics in the low-frequency regime due to chain relaxation and entanglement effects. In addition, one-pot PBS possesses a significantly lower elastic modulus than block PBS due to the dynamic nature of borate crosslinks and the reduced effective chain length.

The mechanical adaptability of electrode substrates is a critical requirement for spinal cord applications. The solid-like rheological response, reduced modulus, and smoother stress-strain behavior of one-pot PBS collectively suppress creep, relaxation, and deformation under physiological motion, ensuring stable electrode conformation in the dynamic spinal environment. To contextualize the novelty of our hydroxyl- and thiol-functionalized PDMS-based SCS electrode, we compared it with state-of-the-art flexible/implantable SCS technologies (Table S1). Our design uniquely integrates tissue-matching mechanics (1–100 kPa), robust interfacial adhesion (Au–S coordination and hydroxyl-rich interface), and superior long-term in vivo impedance stability, while enabling simplified one-pot, lithography-compatible fabrication. These properties distinguish one-pot PBS from previously reported PDMS-based adhesive systems and make it particularly suitable for stable and minimally invasive spinal cord electrode implantation and chronic neuromodulation.

Adhesion properties and electrochemical stability of PBS-based SCS electrodes

The obtained PBS serves as a substrate for the SCS electrode. The interactions between the electrode and tissue are shown in Fig. 2A. The adhesion between the electrode and the skin surface mostly relies on the abundant hydroxyl groups in PBS, which form hydrogen bonds with the skin. This strong yet reversible adhesion ensures stable contact with the skin while allowing easy detachment without damage. Simultaneously, the excess sulfur species and free thiol end-groups in PBS interact with the gold surface at the nanoscale [42], forming robust dynamic bonds that enhance the adhesion of the gold patterns and prevent surface degradation. These dual adhesion mechanisms provide a stable and biocompatible interface for in vivo applications. To fabricate the flexible electrode with gold patterns, a multilayer design and preparation process were employed to enhance both adhesion and mechanical stability (Fig. 2B and S3). From a device architecture perspective, a fully monolithic PBS-based electrode could, in principle, further minimize mechanical mismatch with spinal tissue. However, the extremely low modulus and high compliance of PBS currently impose challenges for reliable metal patterning and device handling. Therefore, a compromise hybrid architecture was proposed and fabricated, in which fabrication robustness is provided by a thin PDMS support layer, while the mechanical and adhesive interactions with tissue are dominated by PBS. A commercial silicone elastomer was first spin-coated to form the base layer, followed by curing at 100 °C. A prepolymer mixture of PBS was then applied as an adhesive layer and cured under UV light to achieve strong binding between the layers due to their similar chemical structures. Subsequently, gold electrode patterns were deposited using magnetic sputtering technology (Experimental Section), and the final structure was encapsulated to ensure durability (Fig. 2C).Fig. 2Structural design and interfacial performance of the PBS-based flexible electrode. This fabrication process allows the electrode to conform to the intricate geometry of the spinal cord and minimizes tissue damage during implantation. To evaluate the reliable adhesion and electrochemical functionality of the flexible electrode, we focused on the interaction mechanisms underlying chemical structure design. Fig. S4 illustrates the flexibility of the electrode when attached to a spinal cord, demonstrating its compatibility with soft tissue. The stimulation sites were strategically positioned at thoracic vertebra 13 (T13), where sciatic nerve afferents enter the spinal cord, ensuring precise neural modulation [43]. These features collectively ensure the electrode's long-term stability and functionality in a humid in vivo condition.

To address the challenge of surface circuit detachment in humid environments, we designed a substrate with sulfur species and thiol groups capable of forming stable physical bonds with noble metals such as gold and platinum [44]. The XPS analysis provided detailed insights into these interactions. As shown in Fig. 2D, the PBS substrate demonstrated a peak shift to 83.5 eV in the Au4f binding energy compared with that of the neat characteristic of Au0 and the appearance of a broad S2p peak at 162 eV, indicating the formation of a thiolate-gold bond [41]. Detailed peak analysis of the XPS spectra is presented in Fig. S5. Meanwhile, there are no sulfur signals in the PDMS substrate, indicating no surface functionalization of the gold. The analysis of the S2p core level spectrum provides strong proof of interaction between the gold surface and the PBS substrate. This interaction is expected to enhance the long-term stability of the electrode, as the thiolate-gold bonds help prevent the detachment of the conductive material in humid or physiological conditions.

To assess the electrochemical performance of the PBS electrodes, we performed cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements under simulated physiological conditions. As shown in Fig. 2E, the CV curves of PBS electrodes remained highly consistent over different immersion times, demonstrating stable electrochemical activity even after prolonged immersion in PBS buffer. In contrast, the CV curves of PDMS electrodes (Fig. S6A) exhibited significant degradation within six weeks (Fig. S7A). These results correlate with the XPS results, which contribute to the stable electrochemical performance of the PBS electrode and prevent surface degradation. Moreover, the enhanced interface interaction ensures the reliable long-term operation of the electrode in physiological environments. Furthermore, the charge storage capacity (CSC) (Fig. S7A), calculated from the CV curves, remained stable across different weeks, further validating the reliability of the PBS electrode for long-term neural stimulation.

To determine the safe current injection limit of the flexible electrode, we measured voltage transients under biphasic current injection at amplitudes ranging from 10 to 80 μA (Fig. S8). The result demonstrated that the electrode could reliably deliver therapeutic currents without inducing adverse electrochemical effects. Notably, transient voltage waveforms recorded after repeated stimulation remained highly consistent with baseline measurements (Fig. S8B), indicating that the charge injection capability was preserved without polarization drift. The electrochemical stability of the flexible electrode under chronic active stimulation is shown in Fig. 2F, with PDMS controls in Fig. S6B, and the corresponding CSC results are shown in Fig. S7B. These results indicated that the electrode–electrolyte interface remained structurally and electrochemically intact even after repeated active stimulation. This stability directly reflects the robust interfacial bonding enabled by thiol–Au functionalization, which prevents delamination and surface degradation under dynamic electrical stress. The stable electrochemical activity is favorable for SCS electrodes in reliable long-term neuromodulation. This consistent electrochemical behavior underscores the robust nature of the PBS electrode in vivo applications, where long-term performance is an essential aspect.

The PBS substrate also demonstrated superior hydrophilicity and adhesion compared to the conventional PDMS substrate, as confirmed by water contact angle measurements (Fig. 2G). Adhesion strength tests (Fig. 2H) revealed that the PBS adhesive layer maintained a strong attachment to the freshly dissected spinal cord and skin tissues of mice under dry and simulated physiological conditions. Notably, even in phosphate buffer solutions, the adhesive strength remained consistent. To provide further validation, dynamic video recordings were taken to visually confirm the electrode's flexibility and adhesion to the spinal cord in a 3D dynamic environment. These dynamic recordings were captured using high-resolution imaging, demonstrating how the electrode conformed to the spinal cord's surface while remaining securely in place despite continuous movement (Supplementary Video 1), making it particularly suited for long-term in vivo applications. The video demonstrated that the electrode maintained stable attachment during movement, further supporting its excellent adaptability to the soft and dynamic spinal tissue. This evidence underscores the robustness of the PBS electrode's adhesive properties in conditions that closely mimic the in vivo environment. This enhanced adhesion contributed to the stability of the electrode impedance (Fig. 2I), which stabilized at 200 kΩ for one week after implantation. In comparison, traditional electrodes demonstrated a gradual increase in impedance over time [45,46], indicating deteriorative functionality of the electrode. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) results demonstrated that the impedance remained stable at approximately 50 kΩ, with no significant increase or abrupt changes over the 3-day stimulation period compared to the PDMS-based electrode (Fig. 2J and S9), indicating the long-term stability of the thiol-functionalized PDMS electrode–electrolyte interface under repeated active stimulation. To further evaluate the thermal safety under prolonged active stimulation, in vivo temperature monitoring was conducted during 2 h of continuous spinal cord stimulation (20 Hz, 50 μA, 0.2 ms biphasic pulses) as shown in Fig. 2K. The maximum temperature difference (ΔT) between the electrode and tissue was <0.5 °C, and the gradual increase in temperature (from ∼30 °C to ∼32 °C) was attributed to the mouse's physiological recovery from anesthesia, rather than Joule heating generated by the electrode. These results confirm that the stimulation parameters used in this study do not induce significant thermal stress at the electrode–tissue interface. To address mechanical fatigue concerns, we performed cyclic bending tests up to 5000 cycles (Fig. S10). Optical microscopy revealed that the PBS electrode maintained structural integrity throughout, while the PDMS electrode showed progressive cracking and delamination, confirming the superior mechanical robustness of our design for chronic neuromodulation.

Biocompatibility and stability of SCS electrodes after in vivo implantation

The design of the electrode materials and structure directly influenced the surgical approach, enabling a minimally invasive procedure to implant the device. Conventional spinal electrodes, typically fabricated with rigid PI, necessitate extensive surgical exposure to ensure stable fixation. These procedures often require the removal of one or more vertebral laminae, increasing surgical complexity and risk [[47], [48], [49]]. In contrast, our PBS-based electrode, engineered with strong interfacial adhesion, tunable mechanical compliance, and proven biocompatibility, conforms closely to the spinal surface without sutures or adhesives. This reduces the need for invasive anchoring, simplifies implantation, and minimizes local immune responses, as confirmed by long-term histological analysis. These features position our flexible electrode as a minimally invasive, patient-friendly alternative to conventional SCS systems, with enhanced potential for chronic stability and reduced surgical burden.

As illustrated in Fig. 3A, the surgery involves drilling a small hole into the vertebra of the mouse subjected to unilateral spinal cord injury (SCI), followed by electrode implantation into the epidural space. In this model, the spinal dura may be partially disrupted due to the injury procedure, so the electrode is positioned rostral to the lesion site without compressing the spinal cord. This approach minimizes damage to the spinal cord while maintaining the electrode's secure placement. The electrode's wings conform naturally to the surrounding muscle tissue on both sides of the spine (Fig. 3B), ensuring stability without the need for additional anchoring mechanisms. Furthermore, the timeline for testing the safety and effectiveness of the electrode is outlined in Fig. 3C. Von Frey tests and local field potential (LFP) recordings were conducted pre- and post-SCS to assess the SCS impact on the mechanical pain threshold of mice and spinal neural activity. Immunohistological analysis was performed 2 months after the electrode implantation to evaluate potential tissue damage and inflammatory responses. This minimally invasive surgical design, combined with the exceptional flexibility and adhesion properties of the PBS electrodes, addresses the key limitations of existing spinal cord electrode technologies. Compared to traditional paddle electrode implantation, our method enables a minimally invasive procedure with reduced bone removal and tissue disturbance. To assess the long-term biocompatibility of our electrode, we evaluated neuroinflammation via immunofluorescence staining of macrophage marker protein CD68 and inflammatory cytokine TNFα in the spinal dorsal horn [50,51]. Our results demonstrated that very low-level expression of CD68 and TNFα in the spinal dorsal horn 2 months after electrode implantation, indicating the limited infiltration of macrophages and neuroinflammation caused by electrode implantation (Fig. S11). To comprehensively assess the immune and inflammatory responses elicited by the electrode implantation, we also examined the activation of the resident immune cells of the central nervous system microglia in the spinal dorsal horn. Specifically, the cell density of Iba-1+ microglia in spinal cord sections was low (Fig. 3D). Taken together, our data indicate that the PBS-based electrode induces a minimal inflammatory response upon implantation, suggesting that the material is biocompatible and well-tolerated by spinal tissues. Interestingly, further analysis displays that SCS with the PBS electrode led to a relative reduction in the number of SCI-induced microglia in the affected area (Fig. 3E), suggesting SCS could reduce SCI-induced neuroinflammation. Microglia are directly implicated in neuropathic pain mechanisms, as their activation promotes central sensitization through the release of pro-inflammatory mediators and BDNF [50,52,53], and inhibition of microglial activation alleviates neuropathic pain in animal models [54]. These results provide preliminary evidence for the therapeutic potential of PBS-based electrodes in treating SCI-induced neuropathic pain.

To visually demonstrate the conformability of the flexible electrode, we placed it onto the spinal cord surface, where it closely adhered without noticeable detachment (Fig. 3F). This result is consistent with the mechanical and adhesive properties quantified in Fig. 1E. To evaluate electrode stability, we performed MRI scans over two months, consistently observing secure attachment of the electrode to the dorsal horn of the spinal cord without detectable movement (Fig. 3G). This provides additional, critical evidence of the electrode's long-term in vivo stability. Due to the limited resolution of MRI, post-mortem dissection was performed two months after implantation to further confirm the accuracy and long-term stability of electrode placement. The dissection images showed that the electrode remained structurally intact and closely adhered to the left side of the spinal cord (Fig. 3G), consistent with its original position. These findings demonstrate the excellent fixation and positional stability of the electrode over time, with no noticeable migration. They further support the use of PBS-based electrodes for long-term neural stimulation applications and demonstrate the potential for clinical application in safely and reliably delivering electrical stimulation over extended periods.

In vivo electrical stimulation and LFP recording on the spinal cord of chronic pain mouse models with the PBS-based electrode

To evaluate the efficacy of SCS delivered by PBS-based electrodes in chronic pain management, we assessed the mechanical pain threshold in the hindpaw (i.e. paw withdrawal threshold, PWT) via the von Frey test in three different chronic pain models: spared nerve injury (SNI), spinal cord injury (SCI), and diabetic neuropathy (DNP). As shown in Fig. 4A–C, von Frey tests were performed before and on day 14 (SCI and SNI models) after the injury, as shown in Fig. 4A and B. Meanwhile, the paw withdrawal thresholds before and after SCS were measured on week 6 after the STZ injection (DNP model, aligning the observation time points with the pathophysiology and progression of SCI and SNI models [55]) as shown in Fig. 4C. In the SNI and SCI models, SCS was associated with a significant increase in ipsilateral hindpaw withdrawal thresholds, with values partially returning toward baseline over the course of approximately 1 h. These findings support a localized analgesic effect of PBS electrode-mediated SCS under the tested conditions. The underlying mechanisms of SCS-induced long-term analgesic effects are complex, extending beyond neuroglial modulation as observed in this study, which involves the reduction of microglial activation. Additionally, SCS enhances GABAergic inhibition [56], activates descending pain-modulatory pathways [57], and suppresses aberrant firing of thalamus neurons that receive inputs from the spinal cord [58]. These combined actions target both neuronal and glial components of pain signaling, contributing to sustained pain relief. Interestingly, in the DNP model, where both hindpaws are affected by diabetic neuropathy, we observed that left-side stimulation not only relieved pain in the left hindpaw (Fig. 4C) but also significantly alleviated pain in the right hindpaw (Fig. S12). This cross-side analgesic effect is particularly intriguing, as SCS could modulate pain-processing circuits across the bilateral spinal cord, even when the stimulation is unilateral. It has been reported that local field potential (LFP) measurement in the lumbosacral spinal cord could reflect transcranial focused ultrasound-induced analgesia in an inflammatory pain animal model [59], neurovascular coupling in the spinal cord during electrical stimulation of the sciatic nerve [60], or to decode intravesical pressure [61]. To gain deeper insights into the underlying mechanisms of chronic pain and the effects of SCS, we recorded LFP signals from the dorsal epidural region over the lumbar spinal cord before and after SCS. As shown in Fig. 4D, we observed a clear difference in the LFPs recorded before and after SCS. It is worth mentioning that when we recorded LFP, the mice were free-moving (Fig. 4E), which greatly reduced the stability of the recording. Representative raw power spectrum density (PSD) traces from an individual mouse are provided in Fig. S13. The LFP signal from the spinal cord exhibited changes of increased spectral power, particularly in the low-frequency range (0-100 Hz), which was reported to be associated with pain processing in the spinal cord. However, SCS did not affect the LFP in sham mice (with no SCI surgery) (Fig. 4G). To further dissect the frequency-specific electrophysiological effects of SCS, we quantified log10 power in canonical neural oscillation bands (beta: 13–30 Hz, theta: 4–8 Hz, alpha: 8–12 Hz, gamma: 30–100 Hz) before and after stimulation (Fig. S14). The band-specific analysis demonstrates that SCS induces coordinated modulation across multiple oscillatory bands. Such broadband spectral alterations suggest that SCS reshapes network-level oscillatory dynamics implicated in pain processing and sensory integration. The specific shift in SCI-induced LFP power correlates well with the behavioral data obtained from the von Frey test, where electrical stimulation resulted in a significant increase in pain threshold. These results further support the notion that electrical stimulation modulates pain-processing circuits in the spinal cord.

This modulation of neural circuits by SCS provides valuable insight into the mechanisms of pain and the role of spinal cord stimulation in manipulating nociceptive pathways. The ability to monitor and correlate LFP signals with behavioral outcomes offers a creative approach to understanding the neurophysiological basis of pain and optimizing SCS therapies. LFP-based signatures have shown utility in guiding adaptive stimulation paradigms in deep-brain stimulation and transcranial electrical stimulation. In this context, our results suggest the potential of spinal LFPs as a candidate signal for future closed-loop SCS systems. Moreover, the combined electrophysiological and behavioral results highlight the translational promise of this platform for next-generation, feedback-driven neuromodulation therapies. Further studies incorporating quantitative decoding, biomarker stability analysis, and long-term validation under freely moving conditions will be required to fully establish closed-loop functionality, where biocompatibility, stability, and signal fidelity are paramount.

Conclusion

In this study, we engineered a modified PBS-based electrode specifically designed for SCS, which exhibited substantial therapeutic efficacy across various chronic pain models, including SNI, SCI, and DNP. The results demonstrate that the electrode not only offers excellent biocompatibility and adhesion but also produces long-lasting analgesic effects, making it favorable for chronic pain management. These findings underscore the potential of PBS-based electrodes for targeting multiple chronic pain conditions and provide a more effective and minimally invasive alternative to current therapies. Our PBS-based flexible electrode demonstrated excellent in vivo stability and biocompatibility in rodent models for at least two months, supporting its potential for chronic neuromodulation. The combination of strong tissue adhesion, mechanical compliance, and low-impedance performance positions this platform as a promising candidate for clinical spinal cord stimulation. These attributes suggest that, with further optimization of electrode configuration and fabrication processes, the design could be translated to human applications, enabling long-term, minimally invasive, and stable neuromodulation in clinical settings. The PBS-based electrode developed in this study represents a promising tool for the treatment of chronic pain. Its ability to modulate pain in multiple models, along with its biocompatibility and minimal inflammatory response, makes it an attractive candidate for future clinical applications. However, further research is essential to enhance the capabilities, particularly through the development of multi-channel configurations. This advancement is crucial to address the challenges associated with complex neuromodulation and to ensure long-term stability in clinical applications.

Experimental Section

Materials

Vinyl-terminated polydimethylsiloxanes (v-PDMS, MW: 8199 g/mol) were purchased from J&K Scientific. Dithiothreitol and α,α-dimethoxy-α-phenylacetophenone were obtained from Aladdin Company. Boric acid was sourced from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., ground, sieved through a 100-mesh aluminum screen, and dried for 24 h before use. Sylgard 184 PDMS elastomer was purchased from Dow Corning. All other chemicals were analytical grade and used without further purification.

Synthesis of block PBS elastomers

The PBS elastomer was synthesized using a one-pot method. The v-PDMS (ene-group) was mixed with DTT (thiol-group) at a stoichiometric ratio of 1:1 and stirred at room temperature under a nitrogen atmosphere for 2 h to form a homogeneous precursor. BA was dissolved in 0.5 mL of tetrahydrofuran and added to the PDMS-DTT mixture, followed by stirring for 2 h. After the addition of 2,2-bis(hydroxymethyl)propionic acid, the mixture was cured under UV light (365 nm) for 1 h to produce the final elastomer.

Fabrication of SCS electrodes

The SCS electrode was fabricated using a multi-layered structure to provide sufficient dimensional stability and handling robustness during metal deposition. A 50-μm-thick PDMS base layer was spin-coated onto a clean silicon wafer at 3000 rpm and cured at 100 °C for 2 h. A PBS prepolymer layer was spin-coated onto the PDMS base layer and cured under UV light for 1 h in a nitrogen atmosphere to form an adhesive interface. Metallic conduction was achieved by depositing a 50-nm-thick Au layer followed by a 50-nm Pt layer using magnetron sputtering. Conductive wires were attached to the electrode pad using conductive silver glue to ensure stable electrical connections. For the final PBS encapsulation, polyimide film was custom-cut and covered on the specific electrical contact regions (electrode pads and wire connection sites) as a mask. PBS prepolymer was then manually coated on the electrode surface, cured under UV light for 1 h in a nitrogen atmosphere, and the PI mask was peeled off to expose the above functional regions selectively. All non-functional areas were fully encapsulated by PBS. The final structure was encapsulated with PBS, leaving specific regions exposed to electrical contact. This design ensured high flexibility, adhesion, and biocompatibility for implantation in spinal cord tissues. The detailed geometric structure of the as-fabricated mouse spinal cord electrode is shown in Fig. S15. The device has a total thickness of 150 μm and features two circular functional active sites (electrical contact regions) with a diameter of 200 μm each, yielding an active surface area of ∼0.0314 mm2 per site. The total length of the electrode from the base pads to the tip of the active sites is 5.5 mm, and the maximum width of the device at the base is 4 mm.

Characterization

Dynamic mechanical properties of the PBS elastomer were measured using a HAAKE MARS III rheometer at 25 °C over a frequency range spanning from 0.1 to 500 rad/s. Tensile tests were conducted using a CMT-4304-QY universal testing machine at a strain rate of 50 mm/min. Adhesion strength was evaluated through a 90-degree peeling test (ASTM D6862) under dry and PBS-buffered conditions, with a dwell time of 5 min. The electrochemical performance of electrodes in vitro was carried out in a three-electrode system with electrode, Pt wire, and commercial Ag/AgCl electrode as a working, a counter, and a reference electrode, respectively. The 0.01 M phosphate buffer saline was used as the electrolyte. Cyclic voltammograms were obtained with voltages from −0.6 to 0.8 V at a scan rate of 0.05 V s−1. A minimum of five cycles was performed for each measurement to allow the recording to settle, and only the final recorded cycle was analyzed. Impedance was assessed using a Keysight E980AL Precision LCR Meter. FTIR spectroscopy was recorded in the spectral range from 4000 to 500 cm−1 with Thermo Nicolet 6700. Surface chemistry and bonding interactions were analyzed using XPS (Thermo Scientific K-Alpha). Water contact angle measurement was performed with Dataphysics. 1H NMR measurements were performed on a Bruker DRX-500 spectrometer with DMSO as a solvent. Temperature evolution of the SCS electrodes under electrical stimulation was quantified using a FLIR A308 infrared (IR) thermal imaging camera (FLIR Systems, Inc., Wilsonville, OR, USA)

Animal models, surgery, and stimulation parameters

Three chronic pain models were established to evaluate the therapeutic potential of SCS delivered by PBS electrodes: spared nerve injury of the sciatic nerve, diabetic neuropathy, and spinal cord injury.

SNI model

SNI was induced in male mice (C57BL/6J, 6–8 weeks old, 20–25 g) after being anesthetized with Zoletil (Tiletamine hydrochloride: 12.5 mg/kg) and Xylazine hydrochloride (0.08 mg/kg) cocktail. The left sciatic nerve was exposed via a blunt dissection, and the common peroneal and tibial branches were transected and ligated using 7-0 silk sutures (Ethicon). Approximately 1-2 mm of nerve tissue was excised to ensure complete transection and prevent nerve regeneration. Sham surgeries were performed by exposing the nerve without transection. The skin of animals was sutured, and they were monitored during recovery. Analgesics (tolfenamic acid, 0.04 mL/kg, i.p.) and anti-inflammatory drugs (benzylpenicillin sodium, 477.6 mg/kg, i.p.) were administered daily for three consecutive days post-surgery to manage pain and inflammation.

DNP model

DNP was induced in male mice (C57BL/6J, 3 weeks old, 13–15 g) by administering streptozotocin (STZ, Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# S0130) to impair pancreatic β-cell function. STZ was freshly prepared daily in sodium citrate buffer (10 mM, pH 4.5, 10 mg/mL). Mice were fasted overnight before the first injection and received intraperitoneal injections of STZ at 55 mg/kg daily after 6 h of food deprivation for five consecutive days. Fasting glucose levels and paw withdrawal thresholds (von Frey test) were recorded before injection. On Day 28, mice with fasting blood glucose >13 mmol/L and reduced paw withdrawal thresholds (0.4–0.6 g) were deemed successfully modeled. DNP induces bilateral pain, and both left and right hind paws were analyzed during stimulation experiments.

SCI model

SCI was induced in male mice (C57BL/6J, 6–8 weeks old, 20–25 g) using a unilateral dorsal horn lesion protocol previously described with modification [62]. After being anesthetized with Zoletil (Tiletamine hydrochloride: 12.5 mg/kg) and Xylazine hydrochloride (0.08 mg/kg) cocktail, a laminectomy was performed at the T13 vertebral level to expose the spinal cord. A needle (length 5 cm, tip diameter 150 μm) was inserted 0.2 mm lateral to the midline, advanced to a depth of 0.2 mm, held for 3 min, and then retracted. Sham surgery was performed by exposing the spinal cord under the T13 without injury. The wound was covered by a small piece of absorbable gelatin sponge, and the skin was closed by suture. Post-surgical care was the same as the SNI model.

Mechanical pain test

Mechanical allodynia was assessed by using von Frey filament stimuli to the ipsilateral hindpaw as previously described [57]. Mice were adapted in Plexiglass cages on a metal grid for 30 min before the test. Von Frey filaments (Touch TestTM monofilaments kit, North Coast Medical, USA) with a range of forces from 0.008 to 1.0 g were vertically applied to the plantar surface of the hind paw when it was bent for 1 s per stimulus. A positive response was defined as an abrupt paw withdrawal or licking behaviors. Each filament was tested five times in ascending order (lowest force first) with 10-sec inter-stimulus intervals. The withdrawal threshold was calculated as the force eliciting ≥3 positive responses out of 5 applications.

PBS electrode implantation

For electrode implantation, a 1-mm wide hole was drilled into the left T13 vertebra under anesthesia. A PBS electrode was inserted into the epidural space and secured with biological glue, ensuring stability without compressing the spinal cord. Post-surgical care was the same as the SNI model.

SCS parameters

Electrical stimulation was applied using a Model 2100 stimulator (A-M Systems) with the following parameters: pulse duration of 0.2 ms at 20 Hz, current of 50 μA, delivered for 2 h daily. Stimulation began two weeks post-surgery for SCI/SNI models and 6 weeks for the DNP model to evaluate analgesic effects.

In vivo electrophysiological recordings

Local field potentials (LFPs) were recorded using the Zeus acquisition system (Bio-Signal Technologies). Signals were acquired at 30 kHz with hardware filtering (1–1000 Hz bandwidth). For LFP analysis, signals were digitally low-pass filtered at 500 Hz and down-sampled to 1000 Hz. In the present study, only 2 channels of a custom flexible neural electrode were used for LFP recording. Grounding was established to a screw positioned above the cerebellum. One to two contacts placed over the cerebellum served as reference and ground, respectively, and the spinal LFP was obtained by computing the differential signal between the two spinal channels. This configuration primarily reflects locally shared spinal activity and is expected to reduce common-mode signals across channels. LFP signals were recorded from the dorsal epidural region over the lumbar spinal cord. The power spectrum was calculated following procedures similar to a previous study [63]. First, the LFP signal was segmented into 10-s non-overlapping epochs. Epochs containing significant motion artifacts were subsequently excluded. Motion-contaminated epochs were identified based on visual inspection for large-amplitude fluctuations and abrupt baseline shifts, and only artifact-free epochs were included in subsequent analyses. For each of the remaining segments, the power spectrum was calculated using the mtspectrumc function from the Chronux toolbox [64] with parameters TW = 3 and K = 5. These individual segment power spectra were then averaged. Finally, the averaged power spectrum was log10-transformed to facilitate visualization across the entire spectrum.

Immunofluorescence staining

To evaluate inflammatory responses and biocompatibility, spinal cord tissues were collected after electrode implantation. Mice were anesthetized with a cocktail of Zoletil 50 (Virbac) and Xylazine Hydrochloride (Huamu Animal Health Products) at a ratio of 1:1.3:14.3 (55 μL/10 g). Perfusion was performed with saline, followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, Biosharp, Cat# BL539A). Spinal cord tissues were post-fixed in 4% PFA at 4 °C overnight and cryoprotected in 30% sucrose for 48 h. Cryostat sections (15 μm) were prepared using a Leica CM1950 cryostat and mounted on glass slides. For immunofluorescence, sections were blocked with 3% normal donkey serum (Jackson ImmunoResearch, Cat# 017-000-121) in PBST (0.3% Triton X-100) for 30 min at room temperature. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies including rabbit anti-CD68 IgG (Proteintech, Cat# 83014-5-RR, 1:200), mouse anti-TNFα IgG (Proteintech, Cat# 60291-1-Ig, 1:100), or rabbit anti-Iba-1 IgG (FUJIFILM Wako, Cat# 019-19741, 1:500) and subsequently with donkey anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody conjugated with Alexa Fluor 488 (Jackson ImmunoResearch, Cat# 711-545-152, 1:500) or Donkey Anti-Rabbit IgG secondary antibody conjugated with Cy3 (Jackson ImmunoResearch, Cat# 711-165-152, 1:500) and DAPI (Beyotime, Cat# C1002, 1:10,000) for 2 h. Fluorescence imaging was conducted using an Olympus VS200 confocal microscope to assess microglial activation.

MRI assessment

The spatial positioning and long-term stability of the implanted electrodes were monitored using a 9.4T MRI system. Mice were scanned at multiple time points (4 and 8 weeks post-implantation) to assess electrode adherence and the presence of any spinal cord inflammation or structural abnormalities. Imaging confirmed stable attachment of the electrodes to the dorsal spinal cord and minimal inflammatory response.

Randomization, blinding, and inclusion criteria

Animals were randomly assigned to experimental groups using a computer-generated randomization schedule. All behavioral assessments were performed by an experimenter blinded to treatment allocation. For the SNI and SCI models, only animals exhibiting significant mechanical hypersensitivity (paw withdrawal threshold <0.4 g) were included for stimulation experiments. For the DNP model, inclusion criteria are described in the “DNP Model” section.

Statistical analyses

Data were represented as mean ± standard error of the mean (mean ± SEM). Normality was assessed for all datasets before conducting statistical analysis. Detailed statistical tests and post hoc analyses employed in each experiment were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software).
