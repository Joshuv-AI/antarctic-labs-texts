GhSTH‐2 Integrates Host Redox Signalling and Effector Recognition and Confers Resistance Against Verticillium dahliae in Cotton
Authors: Siyu Zhang, Jinglong Zhou, Zili Feng, Feng Wei, Lihong Zhao, Yalin Zhang, Heqin Zhu, Hongjie Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPR10/Bet v1‐like proteins are key mediators of plant defence responses against microbial pathogens. Verticillium wilt, caused by the soil‐borne fungus Verticillium dahliae, represents a major threat to global cotton production, yet the functions of PR10/Bet v1‐like proteins in cotton resistance to this disease remain poorly understood. In this study, we characterise GhSTH‐2, a PR10/Bet v1‐like protein family in cotton, which is strongly upregulated at early stages of V. dahliae infection. Our results demonstrated that GhSTH‐2 acts as a positive regulator of Verticillium wilt resistance and directly interacts with both the V. dahliae effector protein VdEPG1 and the redox‐related protein GhTXNDC9. Silencing of GhSTH‐2 in cotton compromised disease resistance, reduced reactive oxygen species (ROS) bursts, impaired lignin deposition, and downregulated the expression of ROS‐producing genes. Conversely, overexpression of GhSTH‐2 enhanced ROS production and disease resistance in Arabidopsis thaliana . Notably, ROS production was reduced in ΔVdEPG1‐infected plants, accompanied by downregulated expression of GhSTH‐2 and GhTXNDC9, and VdEPG1 was found to suppress GhSTH‐2‐triggered programmed cell death (PCD) and ROS accumulation. Collectively, these results suggest that GhSTH‐2 contributes to cotton immunity by modulating ROS signalling through molecular associations with GhTXNDC9 and VdEPG1, highlighting its potential as a genetic target for enhancing Verticillium wilt resistance.Keywords: cotton, PCD, PR10/Bet v1, ROS, VdEPG1, Verticillium wilt

Introduction

Cotton (Gossypium spp.) is the most widely cultivated natural‐fibre crop and a key agricultural commodity worldwide (Yang et al. 2020; Abdelraheem et al. 2019). However, cotton production is threatened by Verticillium wilt, a destructive vascular disease caused by the soil‐borne fungal pathogen Verticillium dahliae, which induces xylem blockage, wilting, plant death, yield loss and fibre deterioration (Depotter et al. 2016; Fradin and Thomma 2006; Zhou et al. 2021; Song et al. 2020; Chang et al. 2023). The pathogen can persist in the soil for extended periods in the form of microsclerotia, which germinate upon encountering a suitable host to ensure sustained virulence (Wang et al. 2022; Ma et al. 2021). In addition to the microsclerotial survival strategy, fungal plant pathogens secrete effectors to establish infection. These effectors can disrupt the immune responses triggered by host perception of the pathogen and compromise plant defence mechanisms, thereby facilitating pathogen colonisation (Cook et al. 2015; Jones and Dangl 2006; Rovenich et al. 2014). V. dahliae employs a diverse array of effector proteins to suppress host immunity and promote infection (Gao et al. 2019; Ding and Redkar 2018). Certain phytopathogens secrete cell wall‐degrading enzymes (CWDEs) that act as virulence factors to depolymerise plant cell walls and facilitate host cell invasion (Klockner et al. 2018; Kikot et al. 2009). Endopolygalacturonases, a key class of CWDEs within the glycoside hydrolase 28 (GH28) family, exhibit pectin‐degrading capability (Reignault et al. 2008; Henrissat 1991). VdEPG1, a member of the GH28 family, acts as a virulence factor in V. dahliae; it not only facilitates the colonisation by V. dahliae within plant tissues but also promotes successful infection by disrupting the biosynthesis of jasmonic acid (JA) (Liu et al. 2023). Limited resistant germplasm and the pathogen's evolving virulence exacerbate this threat (Xu et al. 2011). The identification of novel resistance genes and development of resistant cultivars remain the most fundamental, effective, and economically sustainable approaches for disease management.

Plants employ a two‐layered innate immune system comprising pattern‐triggered immunity (PTI) and effector‐triggered immunity (ETI) to detect and respond to pathogens (Ngou et al. 2021). During plant immune responses, the perception of microbe‐/pathogen‐associated molecular patterns (MAMPs or PAMPs) constitutes the first layer of innate immunity (Zipfel 2008). PAMPs/MAMPs are recognised by pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) at the plant cell surface, activating PTI to restrict further pathogen colonisation (Zipfel 2008; Jones and Dangl 2006). The second layer of immune perception involves intracellular recognition of pathogen effectors by specialised receptors, which activate ETI (Dodds and Rathjen 2010). Studies show that combined PTI and ETI elicit a stronger immune response through mutual potentiation of both immune signalling pathways (Ngou et al. 2022; Ngou et al. 2021). PTI and ETI can trigger a series of immune responses, including the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) signalling, activation of mitogen‐activated protein kinases (MAPKs), callose deposition, defence‐associated gene expression and cell wall lignification (DeFalco and Zipfel 2021; Yuan et al. 2021). ROS production plays a central role in plant immune responses (Lamb and Dixon 1997; Qi et al. 2017). ROS act as key signalling molecules that enable cells to rapidly respond to different stimuli (Mittler et al. 2022). Beyond being by‐products of biotic and abiotic stress, ROS play an essential role in integrating environmental signals and activating stress‐response networks, thereby rapidly establishing defence mechanisms (Mittler et al. 2022).

The major forms of ROS in cells include hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), superoxide (O2 −), singlet oxygen (1O2), the hydroxyl radical (•OH), and various organic and inorganic peroxides (Waszczak et al. 2018; Smirnoff and Arnaud 2019; Sies and Jones 2020). Most cells maintain ROS homeostasis by balancing the production, clearance, and transport of ROS (Mittler et al. 2022). The response to pathogen infection after being recognised by the pathogen is usually initiated by a transient oxidative burst mediated by respiratory burst oxidase homologues (RBOHs) or exosome peroxidase (Daudi et al. 2012). Related studies have showed that under pathogen challenge, the plant defence signalling molecule salicylic acid (SA) increases and alters the cellular redox state. This leads to a reduction in the disulphide bonds within NPR1, triggering the reductive release of monomers from its oligomeric form (Tada et al. 2008). The monomers then translocate to the nucleus, where they activate the expression of a set of pathogenesis‐related (PR) genes (Despres et al. 2003; Lindermayr et al. 2010; Kinkema et al. 2000). The SA‐induced NPR1 oligomer‐to‐monomer reaction is catalysed by thioredoxins (TRXs) (Tada et al. 2008), TRXs are small polypeptides of 12–14 kDa with reduction‐redox properties, featuring characteristic folding, known as a TRX‐fold, and an active site formed by the signature WCG/PPC, which is widely present in various organisms (Xinastle‐Castillo and Landa 2022; Holmgren 1995). Redox regulation is a universal regulatory mechanism. The exchange of thiol‐disulphide bonds constitutes the basis of redox regulation (Balsera and Buchanan 2019). TRXs play a central role in this process by catalysing disulphide bond reduction, thereby maintaining intracellular redox homeostasis and modulating the activity of target proteins involved in immune signalling (Balsera and Buchanan 2019; Cejudo et al. 2021; Jiménez et al. 2025). Based on this established framework, we hypothesise that the GhTXNDC9 may function through a similar reductive mechanism, potentially collaborating with the GhSTH‐2 to target RBOHs or their upstream regulators, thereby playing a crucial role in ROS‐mediated signalling pathways during stress responses in cotton.

Upon pathogen recognition, plants initiate rapid defence responses, including the activation of genes involved in different defence‐related signalling pathways, with pathogenesis‐related (PR) proteins playing a crucial role in immunity (Novacky and Goodman 1994; Fritig et al. 1998; Durrant and Dong 2004). PR proteins constitutea broad class of plant proteins, inhibit pathogen growth and spore germination, and also function as antibacterial agents, hydrolases and protease inhibitors (Kumar et al. 2025; Morris et al. 2021; Zribi et al. 2021). These proteins are classified into 17 families based on protein structure, amino acid sequence and biological activity (van Loon, Geraats, and Linthorst 2006; van Loon, Rep, and Pieterse 2006; van Loon and van Kammen 1970). The Bet v1 family is further divided into 11 subclasses, with major latex proteins (MLPs) representing a distinct subclass of the PR10/Bet v1‐like family (Radauer et al. 2008; Morris et al. 2021; Fujimoto et al. 1998; Samanani and Facchini 2002; Samanani and Facchini 2001). PR10/Bet v1‐like proteins play crucial roles in plant defence and growth processes. Early expression of PR‐10 enhances cotton resistance to Verticillium wilt (Chen et al. 2013; Morris et al. 2021), whereas silencing of the cotton gene GbPR10.5D1 reduces resistance against V. dahliae (Guo et al. 2022). Similarly, OsPR10A in rice is involved in growth and reproduction, and its overexpression results in reductions in plant height and seed set rate (Zhang et al. 2019). PR10/Bet v1‐like proteins are also involved in phenolic compound biosynthesis, contributing to plant structure and growth regulation (Morris et al. 2021). To further explore the role of PR10/Bet v1‐like family members in cotton resistance to V. dahliae, we systematically analysed key genes belonging to this family. This analysis revealed that GhSTH‐2 and its homologues showed a marked early response upon fungal challenge, ultimately confirming the disease resistance function of GhSTH‐2. The findings reveal that GhSTH‐2 enhances cotton resistance to Verticillium wilt by modulating ROS signalling, thereby providing a potential target for improving cotton resistance against this disease.

Results

GhSTH‐2 Gene Cloning and Bioinformatics Analysis

In a previous transcriptomic study of cotton response to V. dahliae infection (Sun et al. 2013) (Accession codes, SRA: PRJNA89721, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bioproject/PRJNA89721), global gene expression profiles were systematically characterised following challenge with the fungal strains Vd991 and Vd070. Subsequent analysis of this dataset revealed that both GhSTH‐2 (GH_A04G1252.1) and its homologue (GH_A04G1587.1) were significantly upregulated upon infection. Their transcript levels peaked during early infection stages and declined by 24 h post‐inoculation (hpi), indicating a strong early response to fungal challenge (Figure S1b). Further analysis identified GhSTH‐2 as a member of the PR10/Bet v1‐like protein family. To investigate the evolutionary relationships, we performed phylogenetic analysis using MEGA XI software, which demonstrated that STH‐2 is closely related to members of the MLP subfamily (Figure S1c). Multiple sequence alignment analysis revealed conserved structural features among the proteins. The sequences, including AtMLP423, GaSTH‐2, GrSTH‐2, GbSTH‐2 and GhSTH‐2, exhibited a shared glycine‐rich loop region, which is potentially crucial for maintaining protein conformation related to ligand binding. Notably, the Bet_v_1 domain was identified as a highly conserved segment across these sequences. This conservation suggests that the Bet_v_1 domain probably undertakes fundamental biological functions, such as participating in molecular interactions or stress responses, which are evolutionarily retained in these plant proteins (Figure S1a).

Silencing GhSTH‐2 weakens Cotton Defence Response to V. dahliae

To explore the function of GhSTH‐2 in cotton resistance to V. dahliae, we employed the virus‐induced gene silencing (VIGS) technology to silence GhSTH‐2 expression in cotton plants. The positive control TRV::GhPDS showed a photobleaching phenotype, confirming the feasibility of the VIGS system (Figure 1b). Reverse transcription‐quantitative PCR (RT‐qPCR) analysis revealed that GhSTH‐2 transcript levels were significantly reduced in silenced plants compared with controls (Figure 1c). At 21 days post‐inoculation (dpi) with Vd080, we observed the clear phenotypic differences. Compared with controls, TRV::GhSTH‐2 seedlings exhibited more severe wilting and leaf yellowing, higher leaf drop rates and reduced plant height (Figure 1a). Disease index (DI) analysis further confirmed these findings, as silenced plants exhibited a higher disease incidence rate (Figure 1d). Lignin deposition in the cell walls constitutes a critical barrier limiting vascular pathogen colonisation (Ninkuu et al. 2022). Longitudinal section analysis revealed that inoculated plants exhibited significantly enhanced vascular bundle browning. Compared with the control plants, TRV::GhSTH‐2 exhibited increased vascular browning (Figure 1f). Phloroglucinol staining showed that V. dahliae inoculation induced xylem thickening in both control and silenced plants, with less thickening in silenced plants (Figure 1g). Lignin content was also lower in silenced plants (Figure 1h). qPCR analysis revealed a significant increase in fungal biomass in the silenced plants compared with the controls (Figure 1e). Leaf samples were taken from plants at different time periods after inoculation with Vd080. RT‐qPCR analysis revealed significantly downregulated expression levels of ROS‐related genes, GhRBOHA and GhRBOHD in TRV::GhSTH‐2 plants compared with TRV::00 controls (Figure 1k). Furthermore, defence‐related genes GhNOA, GhJAZ1 and GhPPO showed markedly decreased expression in GhSTH‐2‐silenced plants (Figure S2a). In the lignin biosynthesis pathway, key genes including GhC4H1, GhPAL, GhCOMT1, GhCCoAOMT1, GhCAD1 and GhCCR1 showed significantly lower transcript levels in silenced plants (Figure S2b).

Based on this result, 3,3′‐diaminobenzidine (DAB) and nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) staining were performed to detect the accumulation of ROS. The TRV::00 controls exhibited more intense DAB staining (dark brown precipitates) and NBT staining (blue formazan) compared with the silenced plants (Figure 1i). Quantification of H2O2 content revealed significantly lower levels in the GhSTH‐2‐silenced plants compared with the TRV::00 plants following Vd080 infection (Figure 1j). These results suggest that silencing GhSTH‐2 compromises cotton defence against V. dahliae, particularly by ROS production, as silenced plants exhibited a markedly diminished ROS burst capacity.

Subcellular Localization

To investigate the subcellular localization of GhSTH‐2, we constructed a GhSTH‐2‐GFP fusion vector and transiently expressed it in 4‐week‐old Nicotiana benthamiana leaves by Agrobacterium‐mediated injection. CBF1‐RFP, a nuclear marker, and H2B‐RFP, a cytomembrane marker, were co‐expressed with GhSTH‐2–GFP in N. benthamiana. Fluorescence signals were observed after co‐expression, indicating that GhSTH‐2 localised to both the cell membrane and nucleus (Figure 2). GFP protein was detected by immunoblot assay and its size was determined to be 42.6 kDa (Figure S3).

GhSTH‐2 Interacts With VdEPG1

Previous studies have found that VdEPG1 functions as a virulence factor suppressing plant immune pathways during infection (Liu et al. 2023). A yeast two‐hybrid (Y2H) assay identified a direct interaction between GhSTH‐2 and VdEPG1 (Figure 3a). This protein–protein interaction was further confirmed in N. benthamiana leaves through bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) (Figure 3c) and luciferase complementation imaging (LCI) assays (Figure 3b). The LCI assay specifically demonstrated that luciferase signals were only observed at infiltration sites co‐expressing GhSTH‐2‐nLUC and VdEPG1‐cLUC, while no signal was detected in control infiltrations (Figure 3b). Similarly, BiFC analysis showed YFP fluorescence signals in N. benthamiana leaves co‐expressing GhSTH‐2‐nYFP and VdEPG1‐cYFP, but not in control treatments (Figure 3c). VdEPG1 is a member of the glycoside hydrolase family 28 (GH28). The key interaction regions between GhSTH‐2 and VdEPG1 were determined by constructing truncated mutants. Experiments demonstrated that amino acids 235–271 within the GH28 domain constitute the critical region mediating the GhSTH‐2–VdEPG1 interaction (Figure 3a).

GhSTH‐2 Interacts With GhTXNDC9

To explore the molecular mechanism of GhSTH‐2 in the resistance of cotton to V. dahliae, we used GhSTH‐2 as a bait protein, cloned it into the pGBKT7 vector, and conducted Y2H assays to screen the cDNA libraries prepared from cotton plants. Y2H assays confirmed that the protein GhTXNDC9 (GH_D02G2259) interacted with GhSTH‐2 (Figure 4a). To validate these interactions, LCI and BiFC assays were likewise conducted. The GhTXNDC9 was cloned into cLuc, while the GhSTH‐2 was fused to nLuc, and the constructs were co‐expressed in the leaves of N. benthamiana through Agrobacterium‐mediated infiltration. Strong luminescence signals were detected in leaves co‐expressing GhSTH‐2‐nLuc/GhTXNDC9‐cLuc, whereas no signal was observed in control combinations (Figure 4b). BiFC assays revealed yellow fluorescent signals in N. benthamiana leaves co‐expressing GHSTH‐2‐nYFP and GhTXNDC9‐cYFP, while control treatments showed no detectable fluorescence (Figure 4c). GhTXNDC9 contains a thioredoxin domain, which has been reported to be involved in cellular redox homeostasis. Therefore, GhSTH‐2 may participate in intracellular redox reaction through its interaction with GhTXNDC9, thereby contributing to ROS‐associated signalling during plant resistance to V. dahliae.

∆ VdEPG1 Infection Attenuates ROS Production and Downregulates GhSTH‐2 and GhTXNDC9 Expression

In previous studies, to investigate the function of VdEPG1 in the pathogenicity of V. dahliae, a VdEPG1 knockout mutant (ΔVdEPG1) and a complemented strain were constructed using the homologous recombination method. Pathogenicity assays revealed that the complemented strain restored virulence to wild‐type (WT) levels, whereas the ΔVdEPG1 mutant exhibited significantly attenuated virulence. These results demonstrate that VdEPG1 is a crucial virulence factor for V. dahliae (Liu et al. 2023). Building upon the observed interaction between GhSTH‐2 and VdEPG1 proteins, we employed VIGS to suppress GhSTH‐2 expression, followed by pathogenicity assays using ΔVdEPG1 strains to elucidate the functional significance of this interaction in cotton resistance to Verticillium wilt. The positive control displayed a photobleaching phenotype (Figure 5b) with RT‐qPCR confirmation of GhSTH‐2 gene silencing (Figure 5c).

After inoculation by conidial suspensions of ΔVdEPG1, GhSTH‐2‐silenced plants exhibited more severe chlorosis and wilting symptoms compared with the control plants (Figure 5a). Their DI was significantly higher than that of the control plants (Figure 5d). Longitudinal stem section analysis revealed more pronounced vascular discolouration in the silenced plants (Figure 5e). Furthermore, qPCR analysis indicated a higher fungal biomass in TRV::GhSTH‐2 plants (Figure 5f). However, disease severity in ΔVdEPG1‐inoculated plants was markedly reduced compared to WT‐inoculated plants (Figures 1a and 5a).

RT‐qPCR analysis revealed that the expression levels of GhSTH‐2 and GhTXNDC9 were significantly downregulated in TRV::00 plants inoculated with the ΔVdEPG1 strain compared to those inoculated with the WT strain (Figure 5i,j). A similar expression pattern was observed in GhSTH‐2‐silenced plants, where GhTXNDC9 expression was markedly reduced following ∆VdEPG1 inoculation (Figure 5j). To further assess ROS accumulation, DAB and NBT staining were performed on cotton leaves inoculated with the ΔVdEPG1 strain. The staining results demonstrated that GhSTH‐2‐silenced plants exhibited weaker DAB staining and reduced NBT staining compared with TRV::00 plants (Figure 5h). Quantification of H2O2 analysis confirmed that H2O2 levels were consistently lower in TRV::GhSTH‐2 plants than in TRV::00 controls (Figure 5g). Simultaneously, to investigate the impact of the VdEPG1 deletion strain on H2O2 production in WT plants, we compared the H2O2 levels in WT plants inoculated with the two strains. The results showed a significant reduction in H2O2 content in plants inoculated with the ΔVdEPG1 compared to those inoculated with the WT strain (Figure S4b). RT‐qPCR analysis revealed significant downregulation of ROS‐associated genes in TRV::GhSTH‐2 plants, including GhRBOHA and GhRBOHD, compared with TRV::00 control plants (Figure 5k). Furthermore, the expression levels of defence‐related genes—GhNOA, GhJAZ1 and GhPPO—were markedly reduced in GhSTH‐2‐silenced plants (Figure S4a).

Overexpression GhSTH‐2 Enhances Arabidopsis thaliana Resistance, ΔVdEPG1 Infection Attenuates ROS Response

To further investigate the role of GhSTH‐2 in Verticillium wilt resistance, we generated transgenic A. thaliana overexpressing GhSTH‐2 (OE:GhSTH‐2). T3 generation plants were obtained through three consecutive screenings on 1/2×MS medium supplemented with 0.1% kanamycin. Protein expression in three T3 generation transgenic lines was confirmed by western blot analysis using GFP‐tag antibodies (Figure S5a). Both transgenic (OE:GhSTH‐2) and wild‐type (Col‐0) plants were inoculated with conidial suspensions of either WT or ΔVdEPG1 strains. At 15 dpi, OE:GhSTH‐2 plants exhibited larger leaves and reduced chlorosis and wilting compared with Col‐0 controls (Figure 6a). Following inoculation with the ΔVdEPG1 strain, all A. thaliana lines exhibited reduced disease symptoms compared with WT strains' infection, with OE:GhSTH‐2 transgenic plants demonstrating significantly enhanced resistance (Figure 6a). DI values corroborated these phenotypic observations (Figure 6c). Quantification of fungal biomass revealed a significant reduction in fungal colonisation in OE:GhSTH‐2 plants compared with the Col‐0 control. Although ΔVdEPG1‐inoculated plants showed reduced fungal biomass relative to WT V. dahliae‐infected plants, the fungal load in the OE:GhSTH‐2 lines remained significantly lower compared to Col‐0 plants under the same inoculation conditions (Figure 6d). Concurrently, to investigate the impact of pathogen infection on the expression of homologous genes, we examined the transcript levels of AtMLP423 (the homologue of GhSTH‐2) (AT1G24020) and AtTXND9 (the homologue of GhTXNDC9) (AT2G18990) following inoculation with different fungal strains. The results revealed that inoculation with the ΔVdEPG1 mutant significantly reduced the expression of AtMLP423 in A. thaliana Col‐0 (Figure S6a). Furthermore, inoculation with the ΔVdEPG1 strain downregulated the expression of AtTXND9 in both OE:GhSTH‐2 and Col‐0 plants (Figure S6b). These expression patterns are consistent with those observed in cotton.

ROS staining revealed significantly enhanced ROS accumulation in OE:GhSTH‐2 plants relative to Col‐0 plants following inoculation (Figure 6b). Intriguingly, ΔVdEPG1‐inoculated plants of both genotypes exhibited markedly reduced ROS deposition, as evidenced by weaker DAB and NBT staining intensities compared with WT strain‐inoculated plants (Figure 6b). Simultaneous detection of H2O2 in A. thaliana leaves revealed that the H2O2 content in OE:GhSTH‐2 plants was significantly higher than in Col‐0 plants, and A. thaliana inoculated with WT strains exhibited markedly greater H2O2 accumulation compared with those inoculated with ΔVdEPG1 strains (Figure S5b). RT‐qPCR analysis was performed to determine the expression levels of defence‐related and ROS‐associated genes. The results showed that after inoculation with the WT strain, the expression of ROS‐related genes (AtRBOHA and AtRBOHD) was higher in OE:GhSTH‐2 plants compared with the control plants (Figure S7a). Concurrently, the expression of defence‐related genes (AtNOA, AtJAZ1 and AtC4H1) was also significantly elevated in the overexpression lines (Figure S7b). A similar expression pattern was observed following inoculation with the ΔVdEPG1 strain, wherein the transcript levels of both the ROS‐related and defence‐related genes were significantly higher in OE:GhSTH‐2 plants than in control plants (Figure S7c,d). These collective results demonstrate that overexpression of GhSTH‐2 significantly enhances resistance against V. dahliae through multiple defence mechanisms. The attenuated ROS response in ΔVdEPG1‐inoculated plants suggests the GhSTH‐2–VdEPG1 protein interaction may modulate host immunity.

The Effector VdEPG1 Inhibits GhSTH‐2—Induced PCD

The GhSTH‐2 protein was expressed in a prokaryotic expression system, purified, and vacuum‐infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves. Programmed cell death (PCD) was observed at the infiltration sites (Figure 7a). Previous studies demonstrated that VdEPG1 suppresses NLP1‐induced cell death (Liu et al. 2023). To further examine whether VdEPG1 affects GhSTH‐2‐induced PCD, we purified recombinant VdEPG1 protein and co‐infiltrated equimolar mixtures of GhSTH‐2 and VdEPG1 into N. benthamiana leaves. GhSTH‐2 and VdEPG1 proteins were verified by western blot analysis using an anti‐His tag antibody (Figure S8). Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay kit, and equal amounts of each protein were infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves. Co‐infiltration of GhSTH‐2 and VdEPG1 significantly reduced necrosis compared with GhSTH‐2 alone, while VdEPG1 alone caused no visible symptoms. These results indicate that VdEPG1 suppresses GhSTH‐2‐induced PCD. DAB staining analysis revealed distinct patterns of H2O2 accumulation in infiltrated leaf tissues (Figure 7b). Comparative examination revealed that GhSTH‐2‐infiltrated sites exhibited markedly darker brown deposits and stronger H2O2 accumulation, whereas co‐infiltration with VdEPG1 resulted in lighter staining and reduced H2O2 levels (Figure 7b). Collectively, our findings demonstrate that VdEPG1 functions to suppress the PCD and concomitant H2O2 burst triggered by GhSTH‐2 in N. benthamiana.

Discussion

Verticillium wilt, a devastating soil‐borne disease, causes significant yield losses in cotton and various other economically important crops (Rhouma et al. 2024; Fradin and Thomma 2006). Although progress has been made in breeding partially resistant cultivars, the scarcity of genetically stable, highly resistant germplasm continues to pose a major challenge (Zhu et al. 2023). Thus, molecular characterisation of host defence pathways and identification of key resistance‐associated genes are essential for developing durable resistance. Plants employ multilayered immune strategies against V. dahliae, encompassing cell wall reinforcement via callose and lignin deposition, accumulation of antimicrobial secondary metabolites (e.g., phytoalexins) and activation of defence genes encoding PR proteins and hormone signalling components (Shaban et al. 2018; Zhou and Zhang 2020; Dhar et al. 2020; Ngou et al. 2022). Transgenic sugar beets overexpressing BvMLP1 and BvMLP3 (Bet v1/MLP subfamily) exhibited a reduction in severity of Rhizoctonia root rot (Holmquist et al. 2021). In A. thaliana , overexpression of the maize PR‐10 type I gene ZmPR10.1 significantly attenuated disease symptoms caused by Pseudomonas syringae compared with wild‐type plants (Xie et al. 2010). Interestingly, it was found that GhMLP28 interacts with cotton ethylene response factor 6 (GhERF6), which contributes to plant resistance against V. dahliae (Yang et al. 2015). Collectively, this evidence suggests that PR10/Bet v1‐like proteins play a pivotal role in plant defence against pathogen infection through multiple mechanisms, although their underlying molecular basis remains largely elusive (Liu and Ekramoddoullah 2006). In this study, we identified the PR10/Bet v1‐like protein family member GhSTH‐2 (Gh_A04G0895) as a positive regulator of Verticillium wilt resistance in cotton. Transcriptomic profiling revealed a significant upregulation of GhSTH‐2 expression upon V. dahliae infection, suggesting its potential involvement in early defence responses. The bioinformatic analysis indicated that GhSTH‐2 is a conserved PR10/Bet v1‐like protein in cotton, sharing high sequence similarity with its homologues. Phylogenetic analysis confirmed its close evolutionary relationship with MLP family homologues (Figure S1). Functional characterisation demonstrated that GhSTH‐2 silencing compromised cotton's defence against V. dahliae (Figure 1), while heterologous overexpression in A. thaliana enhanced resistance to this pathogen (Figure 6). Together, there findings collectively indicate that GhSTH‐2 contributes to plant immunity in both cotton and heterologous systems.

ROS play a crucial role in plant immune responses against pathogen invasion, functioning as signalling molecules that trigger various defence reactions following pathogen recognition (Lamb and Dixon 1997; Qi et al. 2017; DeYoung and Innes 2006; van Loon, Geraats, and Linthorst 2006; van Loon, Rep, and Pieterse 2006; Suzuki and Katano 2018). The generation of immune‐related ROS in plants is primarily mediated by respiratory burst oxidase homologues (RBOHs). Among these, RBOHD serves as a key component of PTI and is responsible for producing the majority of extracellular ROS during pathogen infection (Torres et al. 2002; Li et al. 2021). Consistent with this, GhSTH‐2‐silenced plants exhibited reduced ROS accumulation, diminished H2O2 levels and lower expression of ROS‐producing genes such as GhRBOHD, while overexpression lines displayed stronger ROS bursts. Notably, GhSTH‐2 was found to directly interact with GhTXNDC9, a redox‐related protein containing a functional thioredoxin domain that has been previously implicated in ROS homeostasis (Despres et al. 2003; Lindermayr et al. 2010; Tada et al. 2008). This interaction suggests that the association between GhSTH‐2 and GhTXNDC9 may play a significant role in modulating intracellular redox signalling, potentially cooperating to promote ROS‐dependent defence pathways upon external pathogen challenge.

However, pathogens have evolved sophisticated effector strategies to suppress host immunity (Dangl and Jones 2001; Zipfel 2014). Endopolygalacturonase (EPG), a member of the GH28 family, facilitates the infection of V. dahliae by degrading pectin, a key component of the plant cell wall (Henrissat 1991). Previous studies have demonstrated that VdEPG1 may regulate the ability of V. dahliae to respond to soil osmotic stress, while also suppressing the host immune response induced by VdNLP1. Furthermore, it is involved in disrupting the JA signalling pathway mediated by GhOPR9, thereby weakening host resistance and promoting colonisation by V. dahliae (Liu et al. 2023). Our investigation revealed a direct interaction between GhSTH‐2 and VdEPG1. In cotton, inoculation with the ΔVdEPG1 strain led to significant downregulation of GhSTH‐2 and GhTXNDC9 expression, which was not observed with the complementation strain (Figure S4c), implicating VdEPG1 in the regulation of these genes during infection (Figure 5). Strikingly, the ROS burst was substantially weaker in both ΔVdEPG1‐inoculated cotton and A. thaliana compared to WT‐inoculated controls, pointing to a critical function for the VdEPG1 effector in regulating the GhSTH‐2‐mediated oxidative stress response (Figures 5 and 6).

Further evidence came from protein infiltration assays. Recombinant GhSTH‐2 triggered PCD and ROS accumulation in N. benthamiana leaves, while co‐infiltration with VdEPG1 substantially attenuated these defence responses (Figure 7). The correlation between diminished H2O2 accumulation and reduced necrosis at co‐infiltration sites further implicates ROS signalling in GhSTH‐2‐mediated PCD (Figure 7). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that VdEPG1 probably targets GhSTH‐2‐regulated ROS pathways to suppress host immunity, revealing a sophisticated mechanism of effector‐mediated immune suppression during V. dahliae disease progression. Subcellular localization provides additional insights into this regulatory network. GhSTH‐2 localised to both the plasma membrane and the nucleus (Figure 2), while VdEPG1 exhibited exclusive localization to the plasma membrane (Liu et al. 2023). This spatial distribution suggests that GhSTH‐2 may coordinate defence signalling across cellular compartments—potentially mediating early pathogen recognition and ROS burst at the plasma membrane, while simultaneously regulating defence gene expression in the nucleus (Figure 8).

Schematic of molecular interactions among GhSTH‐2, VdEPG1, and GhTXNDC9. Verticillium dahliae secretes the virulence effector VdEPG1, which localises to the plant plasma membrane and interacts with GhSTH‐2. Pathogen infection induces GhSTH‐2 expression, leading to the activation of disease resistance‐related genes in the nucleus. The GhSTH‐2–GhTXNDC9 complex is postulated to trigger the reactive oxygen species (ROS) burst by stimulating the activity or expression of NADPH oxidases, such as GhRBOHD, thereby conferring resistance to V. dahliae nvasion. Conversely, VdEPG1 suppresses GhSTH‐2‐induced immune responses by inhibiting ROS accumulation, thereby facilitating pathogen infection.

Collectively, our findings establish GhSTH‐2 as a positive regulator of cotton resistance against V. dahliae through ROS‐dependent defence pathways involving GhTXNDC9. Notably, the pathogen effector VdEPG1 antagonises GhSTH‐2 activity, suppressing ROS signalling and PCD to facilitate pathogen colonisation. This work reveals a complex host–pathogen interface in which GhSTH‐2 enhances cotton immunity, while VdEPG1 acts as a counter defence effector, highlighting a potential molecular target for breeding or engineering cotton varieties with improved Verticillium wilt resistance (Figure 8).

Experimental Procedures

Plant Materials, Fungal Strain and Growth Conditions

The cotton materials selected for this study were all the upland cotton G. hirsutum 'Zhongzhi Cotton No. 2’ and were cultivated in a greenhouse with the environmental parameters of 28°C (light, 16 h)/22°C (dark, 8 h) and a relative humidity of 60%. The highly virulent Verticillium dahliae strain Vd080 (previously maintained in our laboratory collection) was initially cultured on potato dextrose agar (PDA) at 25°C for 7 days. Fungal mycelia were then transferred to Czapek‐Dox liquid medium and incubated at 25°C with continuous shaking at 150 rpm for 7 days to facilitate spore production (Zhu et al. 2013). The resulting microconidial suspension was filtered and adjusted to a final concentration of 1 × 106–107 CFU/mL using sterile distilled water. For control experiments, N. benthamiana plants were grown under identical greenhouse conditions (25°C/22°C, 16‐h photoperiod, 60% RH), while wild‐type A. thaliana (Col‐0 ecotype) was maintained at a constant 22°C with 16‐h light/8‐h dark cycles throughout the study period.

VIGS

The coding sequence (CDS) of GhSTH‐2 was amplified from cotton cDNA and cloned into the tobacco rattle virus (TRV) vector. TRV::GhPDS was used as a positive control for monitoring VIGS efficiency. All TRV constructs were transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101 and subsequently infiltrated into the cotyledons of 10‐day‐old cotton seedlings using syringe injection. When the positive control TRV::GhPDS displayed a photobleaching phenotype in newly developed true leaves, successful gene silencing in TRV::GhSTH‐2 plants was confirmed by RT‐qPCR analysis, with each repeat containing three leaves. Approximately 7 days later, after confirming successful silencing of GhSTH‐2, the plants were inoculated with Vd080 to assess disease resistance. The leaf samples were collected at 0, 1, 3, 6, 9, 12, 24 and 48 h post‐inoculation (hpi) for subsequent analysis.

Disease Index Calculation

Disease severity in cotton seedlings was evaluated on a 0–4 scale: 0, no symptoms; 1, withering/loss of one or two cotyledons; 2, withering/loss of one true leaf; 3, withering/loss of two true leaves; and 4, complete defoliation or plant death. Disease index was assessed at 21 dpi with the pathogen. The disease index (DI) was calculated as DI = [Σ(Disease grade × Number of diseased plants)/(Total number of assessed plants × 4)] × 100, with each experiment comprising ≥ 80 plants per replicate across three biological replicates (Wei et al. 2019).

ROS

The cotton true leaves were collected at 0, 1, 3, 6, 9, and 12 hpi with V. dahliae spore suspensions (107 CFU/mL). Samples were gently rinsed with distilled water and immediately immersed in DAB or NBT solution for histological detection of ROS production.

The DAB staining solution was prepared by dissolving 100 mg of DAB in 100 mL of double‐distilled water with thorough mixing. The NBT staining solution was prepared by dissolving 100 mg of NBT in 100 mL of phosphate buffer (pH 7.8). The working solution was stored at 4°C protected from light. For histochemical staining, leaf samples were completely immersed in the DAB or NBT solution and incubated in darkness at room temperature (22°C–25°C) for 8–12 h, until regions developed a characteristic dark brown colouration or a typical deep blue colouration. Following staining completion, samples were decolourised in 95% ethanol at 80°C for 20 min. Photographic documentation was performed immediately after decolourisation.

Stem Segment Disease Observation and Fungal Recovery Culture

After inoculation with V. dahliae, at 21 dpi stem segments (2–3 cm in length) were excised from the first nodal position of cotton plants. Stem samples were longitudinally sectioned to observe the damaged degree. For quantitative analysis of fungal biomass, V. dahliae‐specific primers were used for qPCR detection of genomic DNA (Hu et al. 2018).

RNA Extraction, Reverse Transcription and qPCR

Total RNA was extracted by using the FastPure Plant Total RNA Isolation Kit (Polysaccharides& Polyphenolics—rich; Vazyme) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Total DNA was extracted using the Fungal DNA Kit (Omega Bio‐tek) according to the manufacturer's instructions. First‐strand cDNA was synthesised using the HiScript III All‐in‐one RT SuperMix Perfect for qPCR (Vazyme). RT‐PCR and qPCR were performed according to a procedure described previously (Liu et al. 2023). Three independent biological and technical repeats were performed. The primers used are listed in Table S1.

Construction of Overexpression Vectors and A. thaliana Plant Transformation

The open reading frame (ORF) of GhSTH‐2 was cloned into pCAMBIA2300 vector under the control of the CaMV 35S promoter. The recombinant plasmid was transferred into A. tumefaciens GV3101. Successfully transformed colonies were cultured in Luria Bertani medium at 28°C with 180 rpm shaking until reaching OD600 = 2.0, collected by centrifugation, and resuspended with solution (Murashige and Skoog [MS] medium 4.3 g/L, sucrose 50 g/L, Silwet 77 200 μL/L). Transformation of A. thaliana was performed using the floral‐dip method (Clough and Bent 1998). Transgenic A. thaliana seeds were sown on MS medium supplemented with 50 mM kanamycin for selection of positive transformants. Homozygous lines were established through three consecutive generations of antibiotic selection (T1–T3). The T3 generation homozygous plants were used for subsequent experimental analyses.

Y2H Assay

The CDS of GhSTH‐2 was cloned into the pGBKT7 vector by homologous recombination, and potential interacting proteins were subsequently screened from a yeast cDNA library constructed from V. dahliae‐inoculated cotton roots using a Y2H system. The candidate interacting proteins obtained from the screening were sequenced to further validate their interactions with GhSTH‐2. The candidate interaction genes were cloned into the pGADT7 vector and co‐transformed with the bait vectors into Y2HGold yeast cells. Transformed colonies were selected on SD/−Trp/−Leu medium. Subsequently, positive clones were transferred to SD/−Ade/−Trp/−Leu/−His medium for interaction screening. All primer sequences used for vector construction are provided in Table S1.

Subcellular Localization

The GFP‐tagged construct (GhSTH‐2‐GFP) was transformed into A. tumefaciens GV3103 and cultured at 28°C to reaching OD600 = 1.0. The bacteria were pelleted by centrifugation and resuspended to OD600 = 0.8. Similarly, Agrobacterium harbouring the CBF1‐RFP and H2B‐RFP plasmids was cultured and adjusted to the same concentration. The bacterial suspensions were mixed and co‐infiltrated into 4‐week‐old N. benthamiana leaves using a needleless syringe. After 24 h of dark incubation followed by 24 h in greenhouse conditions, GFP and RFP fluorescence signals were examined by confocal microscopy at 48 h post‐infiltration. All primer sequences used for vector construction are provided in Table S1.

LCI

Protein–protein interactions were analysed in N. benthamiana leaves using the LCI assay (Chen et al. 2008). The coding sequence of GhSTH‐2 was cloned into the pUC‐nLUC vector to generate nLUC‐GhSTH‐2. Similarly, GhTXNDC9 and VdEPG1 were inserted into pUC‐cLUC to produce cLUC‐GhTXNDC9 and cLUC‐VdEPG1. All constructs were transformed into GV3101 and co‐infiltrated into the leaves of N. benthamiana. At 48 hpi, 1 mM D‐luciferin potassium salt was sprayed evenly onto the infiltrated leaf areas. The luciferase signals were captured using a 5200 Multi chemiluminescent imaging system (Tanon). All primer sequences used for vector construction are provided in Table S1.

BiFC

To detect the interactions between proteins in vivo, BiFC analysis was performed as described previously (Yu et al. 2008). For BiFC assays, the GhSTH‐2 sequence was cloned into the pXY106‐nYFP vector, while the coding sequences of GhTXNDC9 and VdEPG1 were inserted into pXY104‐cYFP. All constructs were independently transformed into A. tumefaciens GV3101. The cultures of GhSTH‐2‐nYFP and VdEPG1‐cYFP were mixed 1:1 and co‐infiltrated into the N. benthamiana leaves and the cultures of GhSTH‐2‐nYFP and GhTXNDC9‐cYFP were also mixed and then co‐infiltrated into the N. benthamiana leaves. Fluorescence signals were detected 48 hpi using a confocal laser scanning microscope at 488 nm. All primer sequences used for vector construction are provided in Table S1.

Protein Expression and Purification

Protein expression and purification were performed as described previously (Zhou et al. 2021). The coding sequences of GhSTH‐2 and VdEPG1 were cloned into the pET28a expression vector using restriction sites EcoRI and HindIII. The recombinant constructs were transformed into Escherichia coli Rosetta (DE3) competent cells. Protein expression was induced by adding 0.5 mM isopropyl β‐D‐1‐thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG), followed by incubation at 16°C for 20 h with shaking at 120 rpm. His‐tagged recombinant proteins were purified by using His‐tag Protein Purification Kit (Beyotime). The primers used are listed in Table S1.

Protein Extraction and Western Blotting

Protein was extracted from the leaves of N. benthamiana after treatment (Liu et al. 2021). The leaves were ground in liquid nitrogen and then protein extraction buffer was added. The mixture was centrifuged for 15 min at 12,000 g at 4°C; then the supernatant was boiled in protein sample buffer for 5 min. Protein samples were separated by 12% SDS‐PAGE and then transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes using a wet transfer system in transfer buffer (25 mM Tris, 192 mM glycine, 20% v/v methanol).

Lignin Content Determination

Lignin content was determined using the lignin content test kit (Solibao). Stem tissues of cotton plants were oven‐dried at 80°C to constant weight and ground into powder. Approximately 3 mg of the powder was weighed and transferred into a 2 mL centrifuge tube. Reagents supplied with the kit were sequentially added to the tube according to the manufacturer's instructions. The tube was sealed with sealing film, thoroughly mixed, and incubated in a water bath at 80°C for 40 min to proceed with acetylation, with vortexing every 10 min. After cooling to room temperature, the supernatant was collected, mixed with glacial acetic acid, and its absorbance was measured at 280 nm using a spectrophotometer. The lignin content was then calculated according to the manufacturer's protocol.
