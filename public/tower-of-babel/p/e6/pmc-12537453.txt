Vaccine hesitancy and bone health: Musculoskeletal sequelae of vaccine-preventable diseases
Authors: Roban Shabbir, Zubair Shabbir, Paulo EL Parente, Ali Azad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractVaccine hesitancy threatens global health by diminishing herd immunity and permitting the return of vaccine-preventable diseases (VPDs) including poliomyelitis, measles and coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19). These infections carry short-term and long-term musculoskeletal consequences, such as paralysis, limb deformities, septic or reactive arthritis, osteomyelitis and chronic pain, that place an ever increasing burden on orthopaedic services. This narrative review synthesizes clinical and epidemiological evidence linking sub-optimal immunization to orthopaedic morbidity caused by major VPDs.Outbreak data show that low vaccination coverage precipitates spikes in invasive bacterial disease after varicella, post-polio deformities, tetanus-related fractures, rubella-associated arthritis and COVID-19-related avascular necrosis (AVN). This impact is amplified in rapidly ageing regions such as Asia, where the population ≥ 60 years will nearly double by 2050, significantly increasing the prevalence of sarcopenia and osteoporosis that exacerbate VPD complications. Although orthopaedic surgeons are not primary vaccinators, they routinely manage these downstream effects and hold a trusted position in society from which they are able to strongly advocate for immunization. Integrating brief vaccine counselling, perioperative vaccination checks, and public health initiatives into orthopaedic practice can improve uptake and reduce preventable disability. Strengthening immunization programs and countering hesitancy are essentially critical not only for infection control but also for safeguarding a rapidly declining global musculoskeletal health. A coordinated strategy involving public-health authorities, primary-care providers, and specialists, including orthopaedic surgeons, is essential to mitigate vaccine hesitancy and its orthopaedic sequelae.Keywords: Vaccine hesitancy, Musculoskeletal complications, Communicable diseases, Public-health advocacy, Elder frailty

Introduction

Vaccines have dramatically decreased the incidence of several infectious diseases that resulted in a wide array of disability, including musculoskeletal (MSK) complications. In recent years, however, vaccine hesitancy has threatened these successes by contributing to declines in immunization coverage and subsequent outbreaks of Vaccine-preventable diseases (VPDs) [1]. Vaccine hesitancy is the delay in the acceptance or refusal of vaccines despite their availability. This is a phenomenon typically fueled by distrust and misinformation [2]. The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic amplified this sentiment, as routine childhood vaccination rates fell [3] and misinformation about vaccines spread into mainstream discourse [4]. In 2020, routine pediatric vaccinations in the United States dropped by over 25% due to reduced healthcare visits, parental concerns during lockdowns, and rising sentiment of vaccine hesitancy [3]. Furthermore, in a cross-sectional questionnaire during COVID-19, Pakistan, where polio remains endemic, was found to have the highest rate of vaccine hesitancy [5]. Such gaps in immunity have real-world consequences: previously controlled diseases, such as measles and poliomyelitis, have re-emerged in communities because of low vaccination rates and this has placed individuals at risk of severe complications, including those affecting the bones, joints, and muscles [6,7].

Orthopedic complications can arise both during the acute infection and as a long-term sequela of many VPDs. Historically, poliovirus infection led to permanent paralysis, limb deformities, and growth impairments; Haemophilus influenzae type B (Hib) caused septic arthritis in infants; and tetanus induced muscle spasms so violent they could fracture bones [8,9]. More recently, the COVID-19 pandemic has demonstrated the MSK toll of a pandemic, with survivors experiencing persistent myalgias [10], arthralgias, muscle weakness, and in some cases, avascular necrosis (AVN) due to prolonged illness and treatments [11]. Even common viral infections like influenza can exacerbate frailty in older adults, leading to falls, functional decline, or reactive MSK pain [12]. To this end, inadequate vaccination on a population level increases both infection incidence and morbidity, which strains healthcare systems and significantly worsens the quality of life for survivors. Asia, in particular, is experiencing a demographic transition, projecting that the elderly population aged ≥ 60 will double by 2050 [13]. This projection will substantially amplify the region's burden of osteoporosis, sarcopenia, and other MSK sequelae of VPDs [14].

Orthopedic surgeons, although not often involved in primary care and prevention, frequently treat the consequences of VPDs. This may range from limb reconstruction subsequent to polio to debridements due to osteomyelitis. As such, orthopedists have a unique position to witness the indirect effects of vaccination lapses. There is a growing sentiment that every physician, including specialists and surgeons, shares the responsibility to advocate for public health measures, including promoting immunizations and combating misinformation [15]. This review examines key VPDs and their MSK manifestations, highlighting how these outbreaks can impact orthopedic practice as a whole. Relevant information was obtained from peer-reviewed studies and global health data on diseases, including poliomyelitis, tetanus, measles, rubella, varicella, Hib, pneumococcal disease, influenza, COVID-19, and Tuberculosis (TB), emphasizing their orthopedic relevance. Additionally, the roles of orthopedic surgeons in advocacy for vaccination and integrating preventive health into their daily practice are discussed, while underscoring the ethical dilemma to protect not only an individual patient but also global health [15]. We aim to provide an academic and practical perspective on vaccine hesitancy as a factor contributing to the orthopedic disease burden around the globe, and to encourage multidisciplinary collaboration in addressing this public health challenge.

Methods

This narrative review was performed using a non-systematic search strategy across PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and authoritative public health sources such as the World Health Organization (WHO) and Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), without restriction by publication date. Search terms included combinations of “vaccine-preventable diseases,” “vaccine hesitancy,” “musculoskeletal complications,” and “orthopedic outcomes.” Articles were chosen based on clinical relevance to orthopedic and MSK complications associated with vaccine-preventable infections, prioritizing original research, clinical studies, epidemiological reports, and review articles. Older literature and historical studies were purposefully included to provide comprehensive background on orthopedic sequelae and the evolution of vaccine interventions.

Results

Vaccine-preventable diseases and musculoskeletal complications

Poliomyelitis and post-polio syndrome

Poliomyelitis (polio) is the classic example of how inadequate vaccination can result in severe orthopedic sequelae. Nearly 1 in 200 cases can invade the central nervous system and cause irreversible acute flaccid paralysis. As it typically affects children under 5, paralytic polio often presents with asymmetric limb weakness and muscle wasting. Among paralyzed patients, nearly 10% die due to respiratory muscle paralysis [16]. Prior to the use of vaccines, polio was the leading cause of permanent MSK disability, survivors often had devastating muscle atrophy, joint contractures, and limb-length discrepancies that required orthotics for bracing or surgical management. For example, paralytic polio can result in several conditions such as neurological foot drop, scoliosis from asymmetric muscle paralysis of the trunk, or hip deformities caused by imbalanced muscle forces. Orthopedic interventions such as tendon transfers, osteotomies, and arthrodeses are often utilized to increase function and mobility in polio survivors [17].

Widespread use of the polio vaccines (both inactivated and oral polio vaccines) since the late 1950s has nearly eradicated polio globally and drastically reduced these downstream complications. However, vaccine hesitancy and uneven immunization coverage have allowed polio to persist, despite advances in medicine and technology.

Case-link: In recent years, polio has remained endemic in only two countries, Afghanistan and Pakistan. Meanwhile, vaccine-derived poliovirus outbreaks have occurred in a few under-immunized communities utilizing the oral polio vaccines [18]. A stark reminder of these outbreaks came in 2022 when an unvaccinated young adult in New York [19] developed paralytic polio, the first U.S. polio case in nearly a decade, resulting in flaccid paralysis of the lower limbs [1]. This case was linked to a circulating vaccine-derived strain, and it underscored that all regions are at risk if herd immunity dips below critical thresholds. From an orthopedics standpoint, this patient faces long-term rehabilitation and potential surgery to correct the paralysis-related deformities. These deformities have also been linked to a higher incidence of osteoporosis and fragility fractures, especially subsequent to falls, as observed in a Korean survey [6,7]. Persistently low immunization coverage created the conditions for a 2019 resurgence of paralytic polio in the Philippines, where IPV vaccination rates had plummeted to 23%. This gap in herd immunity allowed a vaccine-derived poliovirus to circulate, resulting in confirmed cases of paralysis [,[19], [20], [21]. Additionally, polio survivors may develop a condition referred to as post-polio syndrome decades later. It is characterized by new-onset muscle weakness, joint pain, and fatigue in previously affected limbs, even if permanent deformities did not present earlier [17]. Post-polio syndrome can impact between 15% and 80% of polio survivors and further contributes to MSK disability, typically requiring orthopedic support for the progressive weakness [6,7]. Each of these cases represents a long-term orthopedic challenge, as survivors require extensive management for neuromuscular sequelae, including orthotics, reconstructive procedures, and rehabilitative therapy.

In summary, strong polio vaccination programs are a necessity to prevent paralytic cases that may result in lifelong neuromuscular impairment. As vaccine hesitancy undermines polio immunization, the orthopedic burdens can range from acute paralysis management (such as splinting for contracture prevention) to providing fitted orthotics for post-polio sequelae. The resurgence of polio serves as a warning that the devastating effects of this disease remain a threat when immunization gaps arise [1]. Orthopedic surgeons may thus increasingly encounter polio-related disabilities in their practice, especially in regions or populations with low vaccination rates. As a result, orthopedic surgeons play a crucial role in patient education. From their firsthand experience, these surgeons have the opportunity to urge unvaccinated patients to receive the polio vaccine for themselves and for their children and recommend booster shots for anyone living in or traveling to regions where polio is endemic.

Tetanus

The toxins produced by Clostridium tetani result in tetanus and lead to severe neuromuscular dysfunction. As it is acquired via wound contamination, tetanus is not infectious but is preventable through vaccination. Tetanus is most prevalent in communities with inadequate tetanus immunization or booster coverage and traumatic injuries (through a variety of mechanisms, including puncture wounds and open fractures) [22]. Risk factors for severe infection include elderly age, diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, and ulceration [23]. Tetanus causes sustained muscle contractions and forceful spasms. These prolonged muscle spasms have direct consequences, as depending on the severity, they can cause tendon avulsions, joint dislocations, and even fractures of long bones or the spine. Termed opisthotonus, these severe contractions of the spine can cause compression fractures of the vertebrae [9]. In neonatal tetanus, muscle spasms can cause epiphyseal separations or skeletal muscle tears [22].

Commonly reported are spinal fractures or long-bone fractures from severe convulsions. Additionally, immobilization and muscle rigidity for prolonged periods can result in long-term joint stiffness and pressure ulcers, which may later require surgical management and rehabilitative management [24]. The systemic effects of tetanus compound the MSK sequelae. Even after recovery from the initial infection, patients may need orthopedic intervention to correct fractures and manage joint contractures or deformities resulting from the prolonged spasms.

Case-link: The hallmark of tetanus is violent, repetitive muscle spasms, and this drives the subsequent orthopedic burden. Historically, when tetanus incidence was high, vertebral fractures were very common (up to 57.5% of generalized tetanus cases) [25]. More contemporarily, vaccination rates have risen but literature still shows breakthrough cases especially in rural populations, with up to 80,000 global deaths and mortality up to 70% of cases in 2015 [26]. While generally more prevalent in males, neonatal tetanus has been found to occur more in areas of low sociodemographic index (SDI), while elderly tetanus occurs in areas of higher SDI [27]. Other unique complications subsequent to Tetanus infection seen in Asian case reports include spinal fracture, iliopsoas hematoma, and rhabdomyolysis precipitating acute renal failure [28,29]. Depending on the time a patient arrives in an intensive care unit (ICU) setting, sedation and muscle relaxants can be used to decrease the intensity of tetanic spasms effectively [30]. In under-resourced settings, however, these injuries persist due to limited resources. A study of adult tetanus managed in the ICU setting observed that 9.6% of cases still suffered fractures secondary to muscle spasms, despite otherwise receiving hospital care [9].

Tetanus incidence has declined in countries with high vaccine coverage and routine wound care prophylaxis, using toxoid boosters and immunoglobulin for high-risk wounds [31]. Still, where vaccine hesitancy or low vaccine/booster access result in unvaccinated individuals, sporadic tetanus cases continue to persist. From an orthopedic perspective, prevention is preferable to managing the aftermath of tetanus. Orthopedists can encourage patients to keep their tetanus immunizations up to date, emphasizing that tetanus is a deadly disease with severe MSK effects that are completely preventable through vaccination each decade. Recommending a tetanus booster in the context of injury care or during routine visits is simple, yet effective in supporting these public health measures.

Measles and rubella

Measles and rubella are viral exanthems that are preventable by the combined MMR (measles, mumps, rubella) vaccine. Both diseases have experienced a resurgence as a result of vaccine hesitancy, causing outbreaks in communities with low MMR coverage. While primarily known for their acute systemic manifestations like fever and rash, other complications such as encephalitis or congenital defects may have consequential MSK effects [32].

Measles (Rubeola): Measles remains one of the most contagious diseases, and outbreaks can occur rapidly, particularly when herd immunity status is not achieved. Although neurological or respiratory complications are more common, MSK complications can occur as well. During the acute phase of measles, patients often experience severe myalgias due to the high fever caused by bodily responses attempting to halt viral proliferation. Adult measles can also lead to transient reactive arthritis during the early recovery stages. This typically presents as self-limited arthritis affecting the large joints like the knees for up to a few weeks after the rash [33]. Arthritis has been noted as a complication of measles in outbreaks; however, it tends to be underreported because it resolves without chronic damage and has a low severity compared to the other symptoms of measles. More importantly, however, a measles infection can lead to an immunosuppressive state that can last for months after recovery, referred to as “immune amnesia” [34]. This immunosuppression can predispose measles survivors to secondary infections, some of which may involve the MSK system. For instance, a child recovering from measles may be more susceptible to other viral or bacterial infections, such as osteomyelitis or septic arthritis from Staphylococcus aureus. Measles can precipitate a decline in overall health and nutritional status in malnourished children [35]. This may worsen underlying rickets or bone irregularities. In rare instances, measles has been associated with osteomyelitis through hematogenous spread in immunocompromised patients. Another complication of measles is subacute sclerosing panencephalitis (SSPE), a neurodegenerative condition that presents decades after the initial infection. SSPE primarily affects the brain, with progression leading to severe motor impairment, spasticity, and contractures. This requires surgical procedures to relieve contractures and rehabilitative efforts to position and prevent the formation of pressure ulcers or sores [36]. As a result, each measles case prevented by vaccination also prevents the downstream cascade of effects, including those on the MSK system.

Rubella (German Measles): Rubella typically causes a milder illness in children than measles. However, Rubella has unique MSK complications, generally in adults, pregnant women, and perinatal populations. Postnatal rubella infections are notorious for causing acute arthritis, particularly in post-pubertal females. Over 50% of adult women with natural rubella infection experience joint symptoms, ranging from mild arthralgia to arthritis; this typically involves the small joints of the hands, wrists, and knees [37]. This arthritis usually begins as the rash fades and can last up to four weeks. For most patients, rubella-associated arthritis is self-limiting, but the intermittent acute joint pain can be debilitating and may require symptomatic treatment. Although the rubella vaccine can induce a transient arthralgia in women (up to 15%), these reactions are mild and resolve shortly after [38]. The small potential for this mild vaccination reaction does not outweigh the serious consequences of natural rubella infection.

The most consequential impact of rubella is Congenital Rubella Syndrome (CRS), which spreads transplacentally. CRS can include a spectrum of birth defects. Infants with CRS are often born with low birth weight and failure to thrive. Some have radiographic bony changes known as “celery stalk” metaphyses (transient demineralization bands in long bones), and this indicates a disturbance in bone development during the gestational period. Furthermore, CRS can result in developmental delays and motor disabilities [39]. These CRS-related motor impairments often require orthopedic intervention in the form of orthotic devices or surgery for contractures.

Case-link: Both measles and rubella have become hypoendemic in various regions because of strong vaccination efforts. However, vaccine hesitancy has led to several outbreaks in these hypoendemic regions. Globally, measles cases surged in 2018–2019 to the highest levels in decades, with large outbreaks in regions of Africa, Europe, and Asia where immunization gaps have persisted. In 2019, the United States experienced over 1300 cases of measles (the most in 25 years), centered in communities with low MMR vaccination rates [33]. Rubella outbreaks have also occurred in communities with low vaccination rates and can threaten pregnant women and result in preventable CRS cases. Every outbreak causes acute illness but also carries the risk of MSK issues. Japan for example, experienced two large epidemics between 2012 and 2019, driven by low vaccination rates among adult men. The 2012–2013 epidemic alone resulted in over 12,000 cases and at least 45 cases of devastating CRS [40,41]. Beyond this outcome, the outbreaks produced a considerable number of adult infections featuring the characteristic MSK morbidity of rubella, including debilitating arthralgia and acute arthritis, directly illustrating how vaccination gaps increase the burden of MSK symptoms in a population.

For orthopedists, measles or rubella outbreaks increase physician burden by requiring increased diligence in diagnosing sequelae like reactive arthritis or seeing an influx of children with developmental orthopedic needs secondary to congenital infections. Advocating for the MMR vaccine is critical. Orthopedists treating women of childbearing age or immunocompromised patients (eg, those on biologics for rheumatologic disease) should be mindful in coordinating with primary care to ensure these patients are protected against these VPDs, as they are especially vulnerable to complications. Additionally, during community outbreaks, orthopedic clinics can collaborate with public health messages. This can remind patients that, beyond the rash, these VPDs can have systemic effects that can be easily prevented. Through reinforcing the importance of MMR vaccination, a surgeon's voice can prevent a variety of neurological, fetal, and MSK manifestations.

Varicella (Chickenpox) and invasive bacterial superinfections

Before the introduction of vaccines, Varicella zoster virus (VZV) caused chickenpox in the vast majority of children. Primary varicella infection typically causes fever and a generalized rash of vesicles. Although chickenpox itself is self-limited in healthy children, a serious complication was secondary bacterial infection of the skin lesions (before the advent of vaccines). Open vesicles served as entry points for bacteria, particularly Group A Streptococcus (GAS) and Staphylococcus aureus. These secondary infections sometimes spread beyond the skin, causing invasive diseases with significant MSK implications, including septic arthritis, osteomyelitis, and necrotizing fasciitis [42].

Varicella infection has been established as a significant risk factor for invasive GAS infection in children. Epidemiological studies in the 1990s revealed that nearly 20% of invasive GAS cases in pediatrics had a recent history of varicella infection. Having chickenpox can increase a child's risk of invasive GAS disease and Staphylococcus aureus by an estimated 40- to 60-fold during the two weeks following the onset of the varicella rash [[43], [44], [45]]. The MSK manifestations of invasive GAS include osteomyelitis, septic arthritis, and necrotizing soft tissue infections involving muscle compartments. For example, a child with chickenpox might develop a high fever and refusal to bear weight and be diagnosed with GAS osteomyelitis of the femur or a septic knee joint requiring urgent orthopedic intervention. Invasive GAS can also cause streptococcal toxic shock syndrome and necrotizing fasciitis, wherein deep soft tissues, including muscle, are rapidly destroyed [43]. These scenarios demand emergent surgical debridement and can result in significant tissue loss or limb amputation. The fatality rate for such complications was substantial, even with aggressive care. This can be problematic and result in growth disturbances or limb-length discrepancies if the infection affects the growth plate.

Introduction of the varicella vaccine in the 1990s in the U.S. has dramatically reduced not only chickenpox cases but also these secondary MSK complications. A study in Canada and France observed a 70% reduction [45] in the rate of varicella-associated invasive GAS infections and Staphylococcus aureus after the implementation of universal varicella vaccination [46]. Similarly, hospitalizations for varicella-related osteomyelitis and septic arthritis have declined in the vaccine era [44]. This is a striking example of how a vaccination program can indirectly prevent serious orthopedic infections. Before the vaccine, an orthopedic surgeon in pediatrics may have treated a few cases of post-varicella septic arthritis or osteomyelitis annually. In contrast, such cases are now rare in regions with high vaccine coverage and herd immunity. The varicella vaccine prevents this cascade of events. However, when vaccination rates drop, these complications can resurface. For instance, in communities opposed to varicella vaccination, outbreaks of chickenpox have been followed by reports of invasive GAS cases.

Case-link: A notable surge in pediatric invasive GAS was observed in some regions in 2022, much attributed to concurrent viral infections, including varicella in under-vaccinated children [47]. Orthopedic surgeons should be aware of a child's vaccination history when presenting with MSK infections. If an unvaccinated child has had chickenpox in the past week or two and now presents with bone or joint pain, a high index of suspicion for invasive bacterial infection is warranted. Maintaining high varicella vaccination coverage has proven to be an effective strategy for reducing the pediatric MSK surgical burden in Asia. For instance, after Japan implemented routine vaccination in 2014, incidence fell by approximately 80% [48,49]. Similarly, after Taiwan introduced free varicella vaccine nationally in 2004, coverage in one-year-olds rose rapidly and varicella-related hospitalizations (with explicit skeletal diagnoses including arthritis and osteomyelitis) fell significantly in children under 6 years [50]. By preventing varicella outbreaks, these programs drastically reduce the risk of secondary osteomyelitis and septic arthritis. Early orthopedic consultation for drainage or debridement can be lifesaving in these cases.

Vaccine hesitancy around varicella (sometimes misperceived as a benign rite-of-passage) can lead to outbreaks with avoidable cases of bone/joint infections [2]. Orthopedists, particularly in pediatrics, have witnessed the near elimination of varicella-related osteomyelitis and septic arthritis in their vaccinated patient populations. This public health success underscores the interconnectedness of vaccination and orthopedic health. They can contribute by educating parents that the varicella vaccine not only prevents a rash but also potentially life-threatening MSK infections in their children.

Haemophilus influenzae type B and other bacterial infections

Before the development of the Haemophilus influenzae type B (Hib) conjugate vaccine in the late 1980s, Hib was the leading cause of bacterial meningitis in young children and also a common cause of septic arthritis and osteomyelitis in infants and toddlers. Hib was a prevalent invasive disease among children between 6 months and 2 years, as maternal antibodies decline. If unvaccinated at this point, children are highly susceptible to Hib. Prior to vaccination, an infant presenting with fever and an acutely swollen joint often had Hib septic arthritis until proven otherwise. The hip joint was frequently involved, and delayed diagnosis or treatment could result in osteonecrosis of the femoral head or damage to the growth plate. This can lead to permanent and irreversible hip deformity. In a multicenter study in Finland from 1982 to 1988, Hib was responsible for 36% of septic arthritis cases in children, second only to Staphylococcus aureus [8]. Hib septic arthritis in infants typically requires urgent surgical irrigation and IV antibiotics. Even with appropriate care, some infants suffered growth disturbances of the affected limb or chronic joint stiffness [51].

With widespread Hib immunization, invasive Hib disease incident rates plummeted by more than 95%. Finland, for example, introduced the Hib vaccine in 1986, and the proportion of pediatric septic arthritis cases caused by Hib dropped from one-third to zero within a few years [8]. By the 1990s, Hib essentially disappeared as a cause of bone and joint infection in vaccinated populations. This enabled pediatricians and orthopedists to narrow initial antibiotic coverage, more so to gram-positive organisms, while treating septic arthritis, creating a safer and more effective treatment while practicing better antibiotic stewardship [8]. Conversely, regions or populations that remain unvaccinated for Hib still report cases of osteoarticular Hib infections. In low-income countries before vaccine introduction, Hib was a notable cause of osteomyelitis in young children, sometimes in atypical locations (eg, multiple bone involvement). Chronic complications like limb length discrepancy or joint dysfunction were the orthopedic result for some survivors [51].

Other encapsulated bacteria that can seed bones and joints include Streptococcus pneumoniae and Neisseria meningitidis. Pneumococcal septic arthritis or osteomyelitis historically affected young children (especially those with conditions such as sickle cell disease or immunodeficiencies) and the elderly. The heptavalent pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV7), introduced in 2000, and subsequent higher-valent PCVs have significantly reduced invasive pneumococcal disease, and its MSK manifestations in children. A study on arthritis patients noted that pneumococcal vaccination was associated with a significant reduction in serious pneumococcal infections (including septic arthritis) over the following decade [52].

Meningococcal disease can cause meningococcal arthritis or purpura fulminans, leading to limb ischemia and amputations. Vaccines against meningococcal strains have decreased these severe outcomes in many countries [53]. However, outbreaks can still occur where vaccine uptake is low (as seen with serogroup C or W outbreaks in certain communities).

Case-link: A Japanese center's 2008–2020 bone/joint infection series found Hib and pneumococcus disappeared as a leading cause of osteoarticular infections in children under 5, once Hib and pneumococcal vaccines were widely available [54]. In South Korea, as Hib vaccination rose to 96%, the proportion of Haemophilus influenzae among invasive bacterial infections in children fell from 20.1% in 2000 to 1.7% in 2019, resulting in fewer chances for hematogenous seeding to bone [55].

From an orthopedic lens, the outcomes following Hib vaccination demonstrate how a vaccine can virtually eliminate several surgical emergencies, such as infant septic arthritis and its sequelae. When vaccination rates drop, emergency medicine physicians and orthopedic surgeons must be that much more vigilant in suspecting diseases that have become obscure. During the early stages of the COVID-19 pandemic, pediatric vaccination rates declined, and some hospitals observed an uptick in severe bacterial infections [3]. As such, parents and physicians alike must be diligent in preventing such disease, ideally through vaccination and advocating for their community to complete vaccine schedules. Preventing Hib meningitis and arthritis through vaccination is significantly more preferable to treating the potential lifelong complications that may arise from it. In communities where vaccine hesitancy is on the rise, orthopedists can share their first-hand experiences of seeing unvaccinated children with devastating bone infections, adding a compelling clinical voice to the advocacy of vaccine benefits.

Influenza and pneumococcal disease in older adults

Seasonal influenza and pneumococcal infections (primarily pneumonia) are VPDs that disproportionately affect older adults and those with chronic health conditions. While uncommon, these respiratory infections can indeed impact MSK health.

Influenza: The influenza virus commonly causes systemic symptoms, including fever, myalgias, and fatigue. In healthy adults, the myalgias are self-limited, but in the elderly, influenza can precipitate a rapid decline in functional status. Older patients with influenza often become bedridden or significantly weakened during and after the illness. This immobility and deconditioning can contribute to sarcopenia and subsequently osteopenia. This increase in frailty, in turn, can increase the risk of falls and fractures. Influenza-related acute respiratory failure can lead to ICU stays, during which patients may develop ICU-acquired weakness or critical illness myopathy, both of which cause profound muscle weakness requiring lengthy rehabilitation [56]. There have even been reports of influenza-associated rhabdomyolysis and subsequent acute compartment syndrome in rare cases, necessitating surgical intervention [57]. In children, influenza can cause benign acute myositis, characterized by calf muscle pain and difficulty walking for several days; while this is typically a self-limiting condition, it illustrates influenza's potential to affect muscle function [58]. A severe influenza season can indirectly strain orthopedics due to a surge in fall-related injuries and strain physiotherapists due to post-ICU MSK rehabilitation.

Case-link: At the patient level, severe influenza is followed by more falls and fractures. For example, in a national cohort of adults over the age of 65 hospitalized with laboratory-confirmed influenza, the adjusted hazard ratio (HR) of a fracture in the year following discharge was 1.24, hip fracture 1.21, and fall injury 1.20 versus matched controls [59]. With fewer hospitalizations and less deconditioning, influenza's tie to fewer acute fall-related admissions can be especially significant in the elderly that have a lower metabolic reserve.

Vaccination against influenza has been shown to both reduce the incidence of flu infection and mitigate the severity of breakthrough cases. One study emphasized that influenza (and pneumococcal) vaccines can help preserve functional ability and prevent the cascade of decline in individuals with MSK conditions [12]. This is particularly relevant for patients with osteoporosis or prior fractures. Orthopedic clinics, especially those managing geriatric fractures or arthritis, can collaborate with primary care or directly offer influenza vaccination to their patients as part of comprehensive care.

Pneumococcal Infections: Streptococcus pneumoniae can cause pneumonia, sepsis, and, less commonly, septic arthritis or vertebral osteomyelitis. In older adults or the immunocompromised, such as those on immunosuppressive medications or those who are asplenic, invasive pneumococcal disease is a life-threatening disease. A pneumococcal pneumonia can lead to prolonged hospitalization, during which muscle deconditioning and bone loss (resulting from inactivity or steroid treatment) can occur. Pneumococcal pneumonia can develop hematogenously and seed to bone, leading to bacterial arthritis of various joints. Septic arthritis requires urgent arthrocentesis and can result in permanent joint damage if not promptly treated. Patients with rheumatoid arthritis have an elevated risk of pneumococcal arthritis due to both their disease and immunosuppressive therapies; vaccination in this group significantly reduces serious pneumococcal infections [52]. Additionally, severe pneumonia can precipitate vertebral compression fractures in those with osteoporosis [60]. There is also evidence that patients who are infected with influenza or pneumonia in the perioperative period have higher rates of complications and delayed recovery [61]. This may result in poor participation in physical therapy or prosthetic joint infection in extreme cases due to transient bacteremia.

Pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCV13, PCV15) and polysaccharide vaccines (PPSV23) are recommended for older adults and high-risk groups to prevent these infections. Studies have shown notable declines in invasive pneumococcal disease in vaccinated populations, which, by extension, results in fewer cases of pneumococcal septic arthritis and hospitalizations [12]. A decade-long follow-up of arthritis patients receiving PCV7 found a significant reduction in pneumonia and other invasive pneumococcal events, supporting vaccination as a protective measure [52]. Encouraging vaccination for surgical candidates, well in advance of surgery, can also be viewed as part of optimizing surgical care.

While internists and pulmonologists primarily manage influenza and pneumococcal disease, their impact on orthopedic health is tangible. Vaccine hesitancy in older adults, whether due to misconceptions about vaccine efficacy or fear of side effects, can lead to lower uptake of flu and pneumococcal vaccines, resulting in more preventable illnesses [2]. A collaborative stance, where the orthopedic team reinforces messages about annual influenza vaccination and age-indicated pneumococcal vaccination, can help reduce the direct burden of these infections and the indirect orthopedic sequelae.

COVID-19 and musculoskeletal sequelae

The COVID-19 pandemic, caused by SARS-CoV-2, offered an insight into the MSK impact of severe viral illness. The multi-system involvement and global scale presented unique challenges for orthopedic and rehabilitation medicine, as acute infections often caused MSK symptoms. During the acute phase, myalgia was a common complaint reported in up to 50% of patients. Severe cases led to viral myositis and even rhabdomyolysis. While reactive arthritis occurred, generalized weakness and arthralgia were more frequent, with many patients experiencing a prolonged convalescence marked by slow recovery of muscle strength [11].

For patients with severe COVID-19, MSK complications were highly prevalent, especially following ICU stays. ICU-acquired weakness was common and sometimes worsened by corticosteroid therapy. Prolonged prone positioning for ventilation was also implicated in nerve compression injuries, such as brachial plexus palsies, adding a neuropathic dimension to recovery [62]. Furthermore, a devastating complication seen in some survivors was AVN, likely from a combination of high-dose steroids and coagulopathy, which has led to total hip arthroplasties in younger patients [63].

The most widespread MSK issue resulting from SARS-CoV-2 is termed “Long COVID,” where symptoms persist for months to years. Fatigue, muscle weakness, and joint pain are among the most frequently reported complaints, often limiting daily activities [64]. These symptomatic profiles and treatment overlap with fibromyalgia, requiring tailored rehabilitation. One study found that 25% of individuals reported new MSK pain that persisted after the acute infection, increasing the burden on orthopedic and physiotherapist services [65]. COVID-19 vaccines were critical in reducing severe disease and post-infectious sequelae. Vaccinated individuals with breakthrough infections typically had milder illness and a significantly lower risk of developing long COVID [66]. Conversely, communities with low rates of vaccination faced repeated surges, overwhelmed healthcare systems, and produced more patients with severe COVID-19 sequelae, including MSK morbidity [65].

Case-link: In Hong Kong's territory-wide matched cohort with over 400,000 COVID cases and median follow-up time of one year, prior SARS-CoV-2 infection increased major osteoporotic fractures (HR 1.22), hip fractures (HR 1.33), and falls (HR 1.28), with even larger effects in the acute-phase of COVID (hip fracture HR 2.69; fall HR 3.83) [67]. This example of infection-driven MSK harm is especially harmful in the growing, elderly Asian population. Improving booster uptake in older adults is therefore a practical orthopedic prevention strategy.

Orthopedists, physiatrists, and sports medicine physicians now manage various COVID-related conditions, from athletes with exercise intolerance to ICU survivors with sarcopenia, requiring surgery for nerve damage or joint contractures. The pandemic forced orthopedic practices to adapt new COVID related protocols. It reinforced their role in public health crises, with many surgeons becoming advocates for vaccination to facilitate a return to routine orthopedic care. Overall, the COVID-19 pandemic demonstrated how insufficient vaccination can result in widespread MSK consequences, ranging from acute symptoms to chronic disability. The pandemic underscored the protective effect of vaccines; it solidified the medical community's role in advocating for their use to prevent not only disease, but the morbidity and mortality associated with it.

Tuberculosis and Bacille Calmette–Guérin (BCG) vaccination

TB reclaimed its position as the world's leading infectious-disease killer in 2023, with an estimated 11 million new cases and 1.25 million deaths [14]. The BCG vaccine, invented over a century ago, remains the only licensed TB vaccine, with a landmark meta-analysis showing its efficacy in decreasing TB deaths by nearly 70%, and overall disease severity by 50% [68]. These benefits, however, have not translated nearly as well into MSK spheres: osteo-articular TB still accounts for up to 5% of all cases and causes lifelong disability through vertebral collapse, kyphotic deformity, and secondary osteoporosis. Spinal involvement is highly prevalent among TB survivors. Rajasekaran et al. estimates around 150,000 new spinal-TB cases each year, noting that chronic inflammation and prolonged immobilization accelerate bone loss and fracture risk [69].

Case-link: Asia in particular shoulders about 64% of the global TB burden, with India, China, Indonesia, and Pakistan leading the counts [69]. A 2023 audit from a central region of India reported that of 208 osteo-articular TB cases, 72% resulted in spinal lesions. This underscores the orthopedic burden of such disease in area [70]. As BCG coverage across most Asian countries exceeds 90% in infants, its efficacy varies among demographics. In Bangkok, BCG vaccines were found to have about 74% protection, with more robust suppression of disseminated and bone/joint forms of TB, although a rare complication of the vaccine itself is BCG-osteitis [71,72]. Because of geographic region, genetics, environmental factors, and bacterial variability, adult spinal-TB remains persistent despite high infant coverage [68].

As such, sustaining high neonatal BCG uptake, and keeping patients informed about adult boosters, and integrating TB chemoprophylaxis into fragility-fracture prevention programs could meaningfully reduce the region's orthopedic TB burden, especially among spine surgeons.

Approaches for integration of orthopedists in promoting vaccination

Traditionally, advocacy of preventative measures, including vaccines, has been managed primarily by infectious disease doctors and primary care providers. Orthopedic surgeons have focused on the direct management of MSK issues, without needing much discussion about the benefits of vaccination and its ability to prevent severe disease. However, the evolving landscape of public health and increasing public health outbreaks have prompted a reevaluation of a surgeon's role in vaccination advocacy. Orthopedic surgeons often develop long-term, trusted relationships with a subset of patients. They directly see the impact of VPDs on patient outcomes and should advocate for such. There is an ethical and professional argument that all physicians, regardless of specialty, share responsibility for public health measures when such measures substantially affect patient well-being [15].

The American Medical Association's Code of Medical Ethics states that physicians should participate in activities that contribute to the health of the community and respond to public health crises. During the COVID-19 crisis, many orthopedic surgeons stepped into expanded roles, assisting in ICUs or staffing emergency departments, which illustrated their flexibility and commitment as physicians [73]. Ethically, the pandemic reaffirmed that surgeons are physicians first, with a duty to promote the health of their communities. Discussing vaccination with patients became integral, especially in caring for high-risk surgical candidates [15]. This aligns with the broader role of all physicians acting as stewards of public health and patient care. Orthopedic surgeons have great authority and respect within a community after years of healing a variety of communities. A recommendation from a specialist can reinforce the importance of vaccines to patients who might undervalue advice from primary care physicians.

The patient demographic in fracture or arthroplasty clinics is disproportionately older adults, the group with exceptionally low baseline vaccine uptake especially in countries with large disparities. For example, India's nationally representative survey indicated that among adults over 45 years of age, several vaccination rates were suboptimal in 2018, including TdAP (2.7%), Typhoid (1.8%) Influenza (1.5%), and Pneumococcal (0.7%). Reasons for this include differences in education, funding, demographics, community health programs and workers, vaccine roll outs, misinformation, and linguistic and cultural diversity across states [74]. Orthopedists work under tight time constraints. Accordingly, we recommend initially trying to first implement system-level nudges that do not depend on surgeon time: one-click EMR prompts (to remind staff of pertinent vaccination for corresponding age groups), nurse-delivered or pharmacy-routed vaccination, and low-friction education queued in the waiting room. The aim is to reduce orthopedic workload (fewer MSK infections and post-viral falls/fractures) while keeping the clinic fast and predictable. A consideration prior to integrating specialists like orthopedic surgeons can be to provide local vouchers to increase turnout, a strategy that has been successful in Japan [76]. Furthermore, waiting room screens can be used to improve patient knowledge and occasionally change behavior, as posters and pamphlets alone have previously been negligible [77]. For example, short, looping clips can be played, tying an increase in falls or fractures to influenza or COVID-19, with a message at the end informing patients of same-day vaccination at the pharmacy or nurse station. This can help patients initiate the conversation instead of a one-sided conversation from a healthcare worker.

Bernstein outlines a framework for how orthopedic surgeons (and other non-primary care specialists) can support vaccinations if time allows [15]. They propose three tiers of involvement:

Minimalist approach (discussion): With this approach, orthopedists can include a brief discussion or disclaimer of a patient's vaccine status during a visit. For instance, a pediatric orthopedic surgeon might ask parents if the child has had all the recommended vaccines, as prompting the topic displays its importance. Surgeons can provide educational materials to direct patients to resources if they show signs of hesitancy. This takes minimal time but can lay the seed to clear any misconceptions. This aligns with how surgeons already address other health maintenance topics when relevant, such as advising smoking cessation for better fracture healing.

Moderate approach (referral/recommendation): In addition to discussion, surgeons can actively facilitate vaccination by referring patients to their primary care physician or local pharmacy for necessary vaccines, or by placing electronic orders or reminders. For example, an orthopedic clinic could implement protocols to vaccinate a patient with a recent fragility fracture after their medical record alerts to a lapse in vaccination status, resulting in automatic referral to a nearby vaccine clinic. Some orthopedic departments have collaborated with pediatricians to ensure that injured children who missed routine care during the pandemic can catch up on their vaccinations. This approach requires modest effort—coordination with other providers and EMR alerts—but can significantly increase vaccine uptake.

Maximalist approach (vaccination): The most involved approach is for orthopedic practices to offer vaccinations in-clinic. This could mean stocking certain vaccines (like flu shots during the season) and administering them to willing patients on the spot. One such pediatric and hand surgery orthopedic clinic offered the influenza vaccine, resulting in a notable increase in vaccination rates by 42% among their patients [78]. In addition, pneumococcal or zoster vaccines can be offered to further protect the elderly population. While this approach may have logistical and cost considerations, it represents a very well-integrated model of care. In times of crisis, orthopedic staff could be mobilized to vaccinate if needed. In some healthcare systems, surgeons and anesthesiologists participated in administering the COVID-19 vaccine to employees to expedite coverage.

Orthopedic surgeons can leverage their platform as trusted specialists to promote public health equity. By participating in media talks or educational outreach, they can highlight how infectious disease prevention eases hospital strain and protects elective surgery schedules. Those in leadership can also advocate for institutional policies, such as employee mandates or community outreach programs. An important ethical consideration is balancing vaccination encouragement with respect for patient autonomy [79]. Surgeons can apply their skills in discussing surgical risks and benefits to address patient concerns about vaccines in an empathetic manner. While a public health crisis may warrant more rigid and paternalistic institutional policies, individualized practice should focus on understanding a patient's perspective and unique history to guide them toward evidence-based choices.

Surgeons may also encounter rare vaccine-related injuries, such as shoulder injury related to vaccine administration (SIRVA) [80]. Managing these cases appropriately is crucial for maintaining public confidence in vaccines. Additionally, surgeons treating immunocompromised patients must collaborate with specialists like rheumatologists and infectious disease experts to ensure MSK treatments are aligned with a safe and effective vaccine plan [12].

Finally, the idea of vaccination as orthopedic prevention can be promoted with creative campaigns. Programs such as “Own the Bone,” a national quality improvement initiative by the American Orthopaedic Association, have significantly increased screening for osteoporosis to prevent fractures and provide healthy ways for patients to prevent or mitigate osteoporosis [81]. Similarly, educational efforts can be made as a collective to show how VPDs can lead to MSK complications, such as osteomyelitis from chickenpox. Beyond any individual VPD, a patient's overall wellness is integral to successful orthopedic outcomes.

Conclusions

Vaccine hesitancy is a global health challenge with direct consequences for MSK health. Lapses in vaccination against diseases like polio, tetanus, measles, COVID-19, and others can lead to a host of orthopedic complications, from septic arthritis to paralysis. The evidence is unequivocal: high vaccination rates correlate with a dramatic reduction in these sequelae [8,12]. Conversely, where hesitancy allows preventable diseases to resurge, orthopedic practitioners witness a return of debilitating conditions that had become rare [65].

Orthopedic surgeons have a vested interest and an ethical duty to support vaccination. Reinforcing the principle that “surgeons are physicians first,” they can play a pivotal advocacy role [15]. This can range from providing support for helpful hospital implementations, educating patients on how vaccines prevent crippling complications, integrating vaccine assessments into routine care, and using their respected voices to combat misinformation by sharing powerful narratives about the real-world damage caused by VPDs.

Ultimately, vaccine hesitancy is a risk factor that can be attenuated to decrease the burden of MSK disease. Public health messaging must highlight that vaccines preserve quality of life by preventing long-term morbidity, not just mortality. By adopting prevention as a core strategy and collaborating with the public health community, the field of orthopedics can uphold its equitable commitment to patient welfare and better outcomes. This unified effort is essential to VPDs and all the downstream complications, and decreasing MSK complications to contribute to a healthier, more mobile society.
