Prolonged Postnatal Hypoxia Impairs Lung Development and Causes Severe Pulmonary Hypertension in Mice
Authors: Luca Zazzeron, Kakeru Shimoda, Paul Lichtenegger, Elizabeth Moore, Helena Tattersfield, Alexandra K. Marenco, Jiayi Kang, Haobo Li, Eizo Marutani, Donald B. Bloch, Fumito Ichinose
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn children born at high altitude, lung development is affected by hypoxia, which can lead to pulmonary hypertension and right ventricular failure. Animal models of postnatal, transient, hypoxia failed to reproduce these conditions seen in children living at high altitude. The aim of this study was to establish a mouse model of pediatric pulmonary hypertension associated with abnormal lung development.MethodsC57bl/6J mice were exposed to normobaric hypoxia at 11% inspired fraction of oxygen starting on postnatal‐days 1 to 4 (“11%‐birth”). At age 8 weeks, pulmonary arterial pressure and right ventricular function were assessed using echocardiography and invasive hemodynamics. The physiology and the structure of lung parenchyma and vasculature were assessed through evaluations of gas exchange, respiratory mechanics, and histopathology. Results were compared with control mice breathing 21% inspired fraction of oxygen for 8 weeks (21%‐birth), and to adult mice breathing 11% inspired fraction of oxygen for 8 weeks (11%‐adult).ResultsSeventy percent of 11%‐birth mice survived until age 8 weeks; the mice subsequently experienced a steady decline in survival, with a median lifespan of 150 days. Mice that survived for 8 weeks had evidence of impaired lung development, such as alveolar and vascular simplification with muscularization of small pulmonary vessels, impaired gas exchange, and altered respiratory mechanics. These changes were associated with reduced exercise capacity, severe pulmonary hypertension, and right ventricular failure. Adult mice subjected to chronic hypoxia had mild changes in the pulmonary vasculature resulting in mild pulmonary hypertension without right ventricular dysfunction.ConclusionsThis mouse model of prolonged hypoxia beginning early after birth offers a novel approach to explore treatments for pediatric pulmonary hypertension linked to abnormalities in lung development.

More than 80 million people live at altitudes exceeding 2500 m above sea level. 1 Chronic exposure to low oxygen levels is strongly associated with the development of pulmonary hypertension (PH). The reported prevalence of PH in adults living at high altitude ranges from 6% to 25%, with higher rates typically observed in men. 2 , 3 , 4 Although precise epidemiological data in children are limited, a seminal article by Penaloza demonstrated that pulmonary artery pressure is significantly elevated in children born and raised at high altitude, averaging 45±16 mm Hg (mean±SD) in children aged 1 to 5 years, and 28±10 mm Hg in those aged 6 to 14 years—compared with normal values <20 mm Hg in children residing at low altitude. 5 , 6 , 7 These findings indicate that a substantial proportion of children at high altitude fall within a pathological range. Although hypoxia exposure in adulthood typically leads to vascular remodeling, chronic hypoxia during early life can disrupt normal airway and pulmonary vascular development. 8

In children with PH, disease progression is more rapid than in adults. Survival rates vary widely depending on the underlying cause of PH and access to appropriate treatment. If left untreated, PH leads to right ventricular (RV) failure with a median survival of 10 months. 9 Specific vasodilator therapies, such as endothelin receptor antagonists, prostacyclin analogs, and phosphodiesterase type 5 inhibitors have been used in children, based on results obtained in studies in the adult population. However, treatment of pediatric PH remains challenging, and despite new drug therapies, long‐term outcomes for children with severe PH remains poor. 8 , 10

Several animal models have been developed to study adult PH, with the traditional approach involving subjecting adult mice to an inspired fraction of oxygen (FiO2) of 10%. This hypoxia‐induced model of PH induces mild proliferation of the smooth muscle cells in pulmonary vessels that have a diameter <50 μm. The resulting PH is mild and fully reversible once the FiO2 is returned to normal. This model lacks key features of severe adult human PH, which, in addition to remodeling of the vascular medial layer, is often characterized by endothelial cell proliferation (including plexiform lesions) and marked intima fibrosis. 11 To better replicate human PH, additional animal models have been developed incorporating antiangiogenic agents (eg, Sugen) or genetic modifications. 12 , 13 , 14

There are no animal models that reliably mimic pediatric PH resulting from impaired lung development. Although hyperoxia has traditionally been used to model bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD), previous studies have also investigated the effects of transient postnatal hypoxia in mice and its adverse impacts on cardiopulmonary development. Subjecting mouse pups to 2 weeks of hypoxia at 12% FiO2 produces some lung changes that resemble those seen in children with BPD, such as alveolar and vascular dysplasia. However, these changes resolve after mice are no longer exposed to decreased FiO2 and no signs of PH are subsequently detected in adult mice. 15

The objective of this study was to investigate the effects of prolonged hypoxia, initiated either pre‐ or postnatally and sustained throughout life, on lung and vascular development, PH, and RV function in mice. Additionally, we sought to compare the impact of prolonged hypoxia during lung development with that of chronic hypoxia in adulthood and to explore potential differences in the mechanisms underlying the development of PH. We found that prolonged hypoxia caused abnormal lung development with alveolar and vascular simplification, intense pulmonary vasculature muscularization, and severe PH leading to RV failure. We propose that vascular simplification is a key factor driving the severity of PH in this context. Our results suggest that future therapies targeting lung microvascular maturation and angiogenesis may prove more effective for children living at high altitudes.

METHODS

All data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. Materials and analytic methods used are also available to other researchers for the purpose of reproducing the results or replicating the procedures.

Animals

All animal work was approved by the Subcommittee on Research Animal Care and the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Massachusetts General Hospital. We studied C57bl/6J mice of both sexes. Groups were balanced for sex, age, and starting weight. Because no differences between sexes were found, data for male and female mice are presented together. Six groups of mice were studied (Figure 1A). Control mice breathed 21% FiO2 throughout their life (21%‐birth). Mice subjected to normobaric hypoxia beginning at fertilization (11%‐E0) were generated by breeding adult mice in 11% FiO2. A third group of mice underwent breeding in air and pregnant female mice were subsequently exposed to 11% FiO2 starting on embryonic age 15 to 18 (11%‐E15–18). A fourth group of pregnant female mice delivered pups in room air and were moved to 11% FiO2 with their pups on postnatal day 1 to 4 (P1–4, 11%‐birth). A fifth group of adult mice were exposed to 11% FiO2 starting at age 8 weeks for 8 weeks (11%‐adult). The final group of mice was exposed to 11% FiO2 from P3 to age 8 weeks and then moved to air for 8 weeks (11%–21%).

Exposure of Mice to Various Chronic Breathing Regimens

Mice were housed in acrylic chambers. A concentration of 11% O2 was obtained by mixing medical air with pure nitrogen from a liquid nitrogen tank or by direct separation of nitrogen and oxygen present in room air by a nitrogen generator. CO2 was scavenged with soda lime. Temperature and humidity were continuously monitored (Data S1).

Transthoracic Echocardiography

Transthoracic echocardiography was performed in anesthetized mice to assess the pulmonary artery acceleration time (PAAT) and its ratio to the pulmonary ejection time, which are well‐described surrogates of PA pressure. 16 The free wall thickness of the right ventricle was measured to assess RV hypertrophy. RV systolic function was estimated by measuring the RV fractional area change and the tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion.

Invasive Hemodynamic Measurements

The RV systolic pressure (RVSP) was measured using a 1.4F pressure‐volume catheter inserted in the RV of anesthetized and mechanically ventilated mice through a thoracotomy.

Fulton's Ratio Measurement

After euthanasia, mouse hearts were excised from the thoracic cavities, the atria removed, and the free wall of the RV was separated from the left ventricle and the septum. The ratio of the weight of the RV to the weight of the left ventricle and the septum was calculated (Fulton's ratio).

Lung and Heart Histology

After mice were euthanized, the airway was distended with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS at a pressure of 20 cmH2O. Some lungs were distended with 4% paraformaldehyde through the pulmonary artery to remove blood. The lungs and the heart were stored in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 hours and then dehydrated and embedded in paraffin wax. Lung sections were stained with hematoxylin–eosin to evaluate lung parenchyma. For evaluation of the lung vasculature lung sections were stained with antibodies against α‐SMA (alpha‐smooth muscle cells monoclonal antibody), nuclei (DAPI) and endothelial cells (von Willebrand factor). Midpapillary heart sections were stained with hematoxylin–eosin to evaluate heart morphology and with wheat germ agglutinin, a lectin that binds to glycoproteins on the cell membrane, producing detailed visualization of the cardiomyocytes that allows quantitative analysis of cardiomyocytes cross sectional area. An automated software (CellProfiler, v 4.2.6, Broad Institute) 17 was used to automatically analyze wheat germ agglutinin‐stained heart sections (Data S1).

Treadmill Endurance Test

Eight‐week‐old mice were subjected to a treadmill endurance test to evaluate their exercise capacity. After a 5‐minute warm up period on the treadmill (Columbus instruments) at a 10‐degree incline, during which the speed increased from 5 m/min to 15 m/min, mice underwent a run phase in which the speed was increased at a pace of 2 m/min per min until exhaustion was reached. Mice drifting toward the back of the treadmill were encouraged to run with gentle prodding. Exhaustion was defined as their inability to maintain pace despite gentle encouragement.

Heart Failure Markers

To assess markers of right heart failure, the free wall of the right ventricle was collected for quantitative polymerase chain reaction analysis of mRNA encoding BNP (B‐type natriuretic peptide), ANP (A‐type natriuretic peptide), and α‐MHC and β‐MHC (alpha‐ and beta‐myosin heavy chain). Plasma samples were collected for BNP quantification using a commercially available ELISA kit (Raybiotech Inc).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.3.1. Comparisons between 2 groups were conducted using the unpaired or paired Student's t test, as appropriate. For comparisons among multiple groups, 1‐way ANOVA with post hoc Bonferroni correction was used for normally distributed data; otherwise, the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied. To assess differences in pulmonary vessels muscularization among experimental groups, we analyzed the percentage of vessels classified as nonmuscularized, partially muscularized, or fully muscularized in each lung section. As the outcome is ordinal categorical, data were analyzed using a 2‐way mixed‐effects model followed by post hoc Bonferroni correction. Survival curves were analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared with the log‐rank test. Data are presented as mean±SD unless otherwise indicated. Statistical significance was defined as P<0.05.

RESULTS

Development of a Mouse Model of High‐Altitude Pediatric Pulmonary Hypertension

To develop a model of pediatric PH that replicates features observed in children living at high altitude, breeding of adult mice in normobaric hypoxic conditions at an FiO2 of 11% was established, allowing pups to be born under conditions that are similar to children born at high altitude. Although the pregnancy success rate was 100%, the delivery success rate was 50% due to fetal resorption on E12–15. At birth, 90% of the pups were found dead, and the remaining 10% did not survive beyond P3. To improve survival, exposure to 11% FiO2 was delayed until E15 to 18. Under these conditions, approximately 30% of the pups were found dead on P0, and another 30% died between P14 and P21. The remaining 40% survived until 2 months of age but exhibited a gradual decline in survival, with no mice living beyond 100 days.

Delaying hypoxia to the postnatal period (P1–P4) significantly improved survival, with 30% of the mice dying between P14 and P21, and the remaining 70% of the pups reaching 2 months of age. Beyond this point, survival declined, with a median lifespan of 150 days. No statistically significant difference in survival was observed between male and female mice in the 11%‐birth group. However, a trend toward higher mortality in male mice was noted (Figure S1). Notably, mice transferred from 11% to 21% FiO2 at age 8 weeks and mice exposed to 11% FiO2 starting at age 8 weeks showed no mortality until age 300 days (Figure 1B).

These findings demonstrate that early prenatal hypoxia results in high mortality, whereas postnatal hypoxia leads to a progressive decline in survival but with longer median lifespans.

Prolonged Postnatal Hypoxia Causes Severe Pulmonary Hypertension and Right Ventricular Hypertrophy

Given the high mortality rates associated with prenatal hypoxia, we focused our studies on 11%‐birth mice. To assess the impact of chronic hypoxia throughout development on pulmonary pressure and RV remodeling, transthoracic echocardiography and invasive hemodynamic measurements were conducted at age 8 weeks. Additionally, the Fulton's ratio during development (ages 1, 2, and 3 weeks) and at age 8 weeks was assessed to monitor RV hypertrophy over time. Results were compared with control mice breathing 21% FiO2 (21%‐birth) and to adult mice exposed to 11% FiO2 for 8 weeks (11%‐adult). After 8 weeks of hypoxia, the PAAT and its ratio to the ejection time were reduced in both 11%‐birth and 11%‐adult mice compared with controls. The decrease in a PAAT and PAAT/ejection time was greater in 11%‐birth mice, suggesting more severe PH (Figure 2A and 2B). Invasive measurement of the RV systolic pressure also showed significantly more severe PH in 11%‐birth mice compared with 11%‐adult mice (21%‐birth versus 11%‐adult versus 11%‐birth: 27±3 versus 42±6 versus 67±9 mm Hg, P<0.0001 for each comparison, Figure 2C). Mice subjected to hypoxia beginning shortly after birth developed evidence of RV hypertrophy as early as 1 week of age (Figure 2D), to a degree significantly more severe than hypoxic adult mice (Fulton's ratio 21%‐birth versus 11%‐adult versus 11%‐birth: 23±2 versus 36±4 versus 56±6%, P<0.0001 for each comparison, Figure 2E). Using an unbiased automated histological analysis, a greater increase in the RV cardiomyocytes cross‐sectional area was observed in 11%‐birth mice when compared with 11%‐adult and to 21%‐birth mice (Figure 2F through 2H). No significant sex differences were found in RVSP or Fulton index within the 11%‐birth group. Nonetheless, female mice exhibited slightly higher mean values for both parameters compared with males (Figure S2).

These results demonstrate that mice subjected to prolonged hypoxia, beginning shortly after birth, develop PH and RV hypertrophy to a significantly greater degree than similar treatment of mice after lung development is complete.

Prolonged Postnatal Hypoxia Causes Pathological Right Ventricular Hypertrophy and Failure

Because mice exposed to 11% FiO2 from birth developed severe PH by 8 weeks of age and exhibited a progressive decline in survival, we conducted experiments to determine whether severe PH led to RV dysfunction and failure, potentially accounting for the increased mortality.

In 8‐week‐old 11%‐birth mice, TTE showed severely dilated RV, reduced tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion, and reduced RV fractional area change compared with 21%‐birth and 11%‐adult mice (Figure 3A through 3D), suggesting RV dysfunction. To further investigate the significance of increased right‐sided pressures, the weight of the liver was measured, and its ratio to the body weight was found to be increased in 11%‐birth mice compared with 21%‐birth and 11%‐adult mice, suggesting venous congestion (4.6±0.5 versus 4.5±0.5 versus 5.3±5%, P<0.001, Figure 3E). Postmortem evaluation of the hearts showed severe RV hypertrophy and dilation with flattening of the septum and deformation of the left ventricle shape in 11%‐birth mice, whereas 11%‐adult mice showed mild RV free wall thickening without RV dilation or septal flattening (Figure 3F through 3G and Figure S3).

To further investigate the effect of postnatal hypoxia on RV function, we measured RV and plasma markers of heart failure. The levels of mRNA encoding ANP, BNP, β‐MHC, and the ratio of β‐ and α‐MHC mRNA levels were increased in the RV of 11%‐birth and 11%‐adult mice compared with controls. The increase was greatest in 11%‐birth mice (Figure 4A through 4E). The plasma level of BNP was increased in 11%‐birth mice compared with 21%‐birth mice (10.5±2.5 versus 4.2±1.3 pg/mL, P<0.0001, Figure 4F).

To investigate the effect of exposure to 11% FiO2 beginning at birth on cardiopulmonary function, 21%‐birth and 11%‐birth mice were subjected to an exercise endurance treadmill test in room air. The exercise capacity of 11%‐birth mice was significantly decreased compared with that of 21%‐birth mice, as evidenced by a reduced work (0.56±0.17 versus 0.87±0.2 Joules/g, P<0.01), shorter distance traveled (169±51 versus 259±55 m, P<0.01) and reduced time (700±170 versus 968±139 s, P<0.01) (Figure 4G through 4J).

These results suggest that PH in mice exposed to 11% FiO2 beginning shortly after birth leads to severe RV remodeling, pathological hypertrophy, impaired RV function, and reduced exercise capacity.

Lifelong Hypoxia Causes Abnormal Alveolar Development, Pulmonary Vasculature Simplification, and Intense Muscularization

Given the severe phenotype observed in mice exposed to hypoxia beginning at birth, we investigated whether significant changes in the lung parenchyma and pulmonary vasculature could account for these differences. Lung histology showed significant alveolar simplification in 11%‐birth mice compared with 21%‐birth and 11%‐adult mice, as demonstrated by an increased mean linear intercept (21%‐birth versus 11%‐adult versus 11%‐birth: 28±3 versus 28±2 versus 40±5 μm, P<0.0001, Figure 5A and 5B and Figure S4). Assessment of lung respiratory mechanics and gas exchange properties in 11%‐birth mice showed an increase in lung compliance compared with 21%‐birth mice (51±10 versus 33±3 μL/cmH2O, P<0.001, Figure 5C and 5D). There was impaired gas exchange in 11% birth compared with 21% birth mice, as demonstrated by lower arterial oxygen partial pressure in mice acutely ventilated with 11% FiO2 (42±5 versus 52±1 mm Hg, P<0.01, Figure 5E). No differences in arterial CO2 partial pressure, pH, bicarbonate levels or base deficit were observed between groups (Figure 5F through 5H). To rule out pulmonary edema as a cause of decreased lower arterial oxygen partial pressure, we assessed the wet‐to‐dry ratio of the left lung, which was similar in 21%‐birth, 11%‐birth, and 11%‐adult mice (Figure 5I).

To investigate how chronic hypoxia, beginning early after birth affected pulmonary vasculature development and remodeling, the percentage of nonmuscularized, partially muscularized (defined as 0%–50%), or fully muscularized (50%–100%) peripheral vessels with an external diameter less than 50 μm was measured. Vessel density was assessed by counting the number of vessels within a predefined area of the lung section and normalizing this count to the area analyzed. Control mice had significantly more nonmuscularized vessels than 11%‐birth and 11%‐adult mice, and 11%‐birth mice had significantly more fully muscularized vessels than controls and 11%‐adult mice (Figure 6A and 6B). The number of vessels per unit area in 11%‐birth mice was significantly reduced as compared with 21%‐birth and 11%‐adult mice (Figure 6C).

To further illustrate these findings, we examined hematoxylin and eosin‐stained nonperfused lung sections. These histological images demonstrate full muscularization in small pulmonary vessels in 11%‐birth mice. Although the smooth muscle layer often extends circumferentially in fully muscularized vessels, the associated medial wall thickening is relatively modest compared with other experimental models of PH. Importantly, we did not observe any plexiform or inflammatory lesions in this model (Figure 6D).

These results show that exposure to low FiO2 beginning early after birth interferes with alveolar development and causes marked vascular remodeling with significantly reduced vessel density and severe vascular muscularization. In contrast, in adult mice exposed to hypoxia, vessel density is not significantly reduced and vessel wall muscularization is modestly increased. These differences may explain the contrasting phenotypes observed between control mice and those exposed to low FiO2 either from birth or in adulthood.

Pulmonary Hypertension Persists After the Discontinuation of Hypoxia

In adult mice, hypoxia‐induced PH and RV hypertrophy are reversible after discontinuation of hypoxia. 12 We hypothesized that in this model, PH may not be reversible after discontinuation of hypoxia due to irreversible structural changes occurring during lung development. Mice that were exposed to hypoxia beginning on P3 were moved to air at age 8 weeks and assessed again at age 16 weeks (11% to 21% group). Although echocardiography showed an increase in PAAT, PAAT/ejection time, and tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion over time, denoting a decrease in PH and improved RV function (Figure 7A through 7C), invasive hemodynamic assessment showed only partial reversal of PH. The RVSP in the 11% to 21% group was lower than 11%‐birth mice but higher than 21%‐birth mice (26±2 versus 67±9 versus 50±5 mm Hg, P<0.0001 for each comparison, Figure 7D). As a second indication of persistent RV hypertrophy, the Fulton's ratio in 11% to 21% mice was lower than in 11%‐birth mice but higher than controls (23±2 versus 56±6 versus 34±3%, P<0.0001 for each comparison, Figure 7E). These results show that in this model of severe PH associated with BPD, both PH and RV hypertrophy persist after discontinuation of hypoxia.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we developed a new mouse model of severe PH with RV failure by exposing mouse pups to hypoxia beginning soon after birth. This treatment was associated with abnormal lung development and analysis of the lung parenchyma and pulmonary vasculature in adult mice that underwent lifelong exposure to hypoxia revealed features similar to those seen in models of BPD, including alveolar and vascular simplification.

In our experience and according to the available literature, 18 exposure of adult mice to hypoxia alone induces mild proliferation of the smooth muscle cells in the pulmonary vessels with diameter less than 50 μm, which leads to mild PH (RVSP 35–40 mm Hg) and mild RV hypertrophy (with Fulton's ratio 30%–35%), without signs of RV dysfunction. These changes are generally reversible, and PH completely resolves after increasing FiO2 to room air. 12 In this study, however, exposure to 11% FiO2 beginning shortly after birth interfered with vascular development in the lung and caused not only an intense proliferation of smooth muscle cells in small pulmonary vessels but also a reduction in the number of vessels. These changes lead to the development of severe PH (RVSP ~70 mm Hg), RV hypertrophy (Fulton's ratio ~55%), and RV dysfunction, changes that were not reversible after mice were switched to air.

In our study, we were able to establish a model of PH associated with impaired lung development by using prolonged postnatal exposure to 11% FiO2. We selected 11% FiO2 for studies in adult mice because we wanted to compare the effect of the same degree and duration of hypoxia in adult mice and mice exposed during their development. We acknowledge that this exposure level differs from the 10% FiO2 typically used to induce mild, reversible PH in adult mice. However, we repeated measurements in adult mice exposed to 11% FiO2 and ensured that this degree of hypoxia resulted in a comparable level of PH to what has been previously reported with 10% FiO2 exposure.

Our model of hypoxia was induced under normobaric conditions, which differs from high‐altitude (hypobaric) hypoxia, where both barometric pressure and inspired oxygen levels are reduced. The 11% FiO2 used in our study approximates the partial pressure of oxygen at an altitude of ~5000 m. Although few humans reside permanently at such elevations, this degree of hypoxia is commonly used in experimental models to study PH. Prenatal exposure to 11% FiO2 in our model resulted in approximately 40% survival, highlighting the severity of hypoxic stress at this level. Although direct comparisons to human populations are limited by species‐specific differences in hypoxia tolerance and adaptation, it is noteworthy that many pregnant individuals living at high altitudes often relocate to lower elevations for delivery to reduce the risk of prenatal and perinatal hypoxic complications. However, mothers and infants frequently return to high‐altitude environments shortly after birth, exposing neonates to chronic hypoxia during a critical window of postnatal lung development. Thus, our postnatal hypoxia model remains clinically relevant for investigating the consequences of early‐life hypoxia in high‐altitude human populations.

Lung development progresses through several stages, 19 , 20 and although the timing differs between mice and humans, key similarities and distinctions exist. Humans are born during the alveolarization phase, whereas mice are born in the saccular stage (E17.5‐P4). Preterm birth in humans often leads to BPD because infants delivered during the saccular stage require mechanical ventilation and oxygen therapy, which can cause lung injury. Although hyperoxia has been more traditionally associated with models of BPD and pulmonary vascular disease, emerging evidence, including our findings, suggests that hypoxia can similarly impair alveolarization and vascular development. The mechanisms underlying these effects may differ: hyperoxia is known to induce oxidative stress and inflammation, whereas hypoxia may impair angiogenesis and cellular differentiation. Subjecting mice to short‐term (1–2 weeks) postnatal hypoxia has successfully reproduced some features of BPD, such as alveolar and vascular simplification. 21 , 22 , 23 Ramani et al. demonstrated that exposing mouse pups to 12% or 85% FiO2 from postnatal day 2 to 14 resulted in alveolar simplification, as evidenced by increased mean linear intercept, total lung volume, total lung capacity, and lung compliance. Although both hypoxia and hyperoxia can disrupt lung development, the underlying mechanisms affecting the airways and pulmonary vasculature are likely distinct. Hyperoxia has been extensively studied in this context, but the impact of hypoxia on lung development remains relatively underexplored. This gap in knowledge is particularly relevant for understanding the long‐term respiratory outcomes in children born and raised at high altitudes. Moreover, lung development, continues beyond the saccular stage, progressing through alveolarization and microvascular maturation, which extend into young adulthood—approximately postnatal day 36 in mice and 17 to 21 years in humans. At high altitudes, children experience chronic hypoxia throughout these developmental stages, potentially affecting alveolar and vascular maturation more profoundly than transient hypoxic exposure at birth.

Our study demonstrates that prolonged hypoxia, throughout the saccular, alveolarization, and microvasculature maturation phases, results in alveolar diffusion impairment, impaired angiogenesis with vascular simplification, and vascular remodeling, ultimately leading to severe PH and RV failure, recapitulating some features that can complicate BPD such as PH and RV failure. 5 Other studies that focused on shorter exposure times, despite showing changes in lung vascular development, failed to reproduce PH and RV failure in adulthood. Although brief exposures may be logistically simpler and yield faster results, our study reproduced a strong pathological phenotype within 8 weeks of hypoxic exposure. This provides a powerful platform for studying therapeutic interventions targeting PH, RV function, exercise capacity, and survival—key outcomes relevant to clinical practice.

Our findings indicate that significant cardiopulmonary abnormalities persist despite an 8‐week recovery period in normoxia, suggesting that early‐life hypoxia induces structural and functional changes that are not fully reversible. Vitali et al. investigated the effects of Sugen combined with hypoxia, a model that induces PH and RV hypertrophy persisting even after 10 weeks of normoxic recovery. Notably, in the same study, the vehicle group exposed to hypoxia alone showed full normalization of RVSP, PAAT, and Fulton's ratio after 10 weeks recovery in room air. In contrast, our model, demonstrating only partial resolution of PH following developmental hypoxia, more closely parallels the persistent phenotype seen in the Sugen‐hypoxia model. Althoughit is possible that a longer recovery period could result in further improvement, the enduring abnormalities we observed suggest that early‐life hypoxia disrupts critical developmental processes such as angiogenesis and pulmonary vascular maturation. We believe that both early‐life hypoxia and Sugen exposure in adulthood may cause more permanent alterations to vascular architecture, distinct from the more transient smooth muscle cells remodeling typically associated with adult‐onset hypoxia alone.

This study has some limitations. First, the lifelong hypoxic exposure used in our model to reproduce PH associated with abnormal lung development in children living at high altitude may not fully reflect the clinical scenario of children born at low altitude with BPD and PH. In low‐altitude settings, hyperoxic exposure is a primary driver of BPD, often occurring in conjunction with other contributing factors such as prematurity, mechanical ventilation, and infection, whereas hypoxia is typically intermittent. Second, although the model successfully induces severe PH and RV dysfunction, further studies are needed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms. A key limitation of our study is the inability to definitively distinguish small nonmuscularized vessels as arteries or veins based on morphology alone, a challenge shared by previous studies that have similarly included 0% muscularized vessels in their analyses without definitive classification. Determining whether hypoxia exerts its influence on vascular development at a distinct developmental stage and characterizing the spatial aspects of this effect by identifying the specific vessel sizes and branching levels within the pulmonary vasculature will provide critical insights into how hypoxia contributes to the development of severe PH and RV dysfunction. Another limitation is that mice exposed to hypoxia beginning shortly after birth or at 8 weeks of age were evaluated at different ages (8‐week‐old versus 16‐week‐old) and this could potentially introduce some differences due to the age difference and affect some of the findings of this study. Finally, the mortality observed in our study introduces a potential bias by limiting analysis to surviving animals, which may not fully represent the broader effects of hypoxia. Addressing these limitations in future studies could improve our understanding of the pathophysiology and therapeutic potential of this model.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we developed a novel mouse model of severe PH and RV failure associated with impaired lung development by exposing newborn mice to decreased FiO2, beginning shortly after birth. This model replicates some pathological features of BPD, including alveolar and vascular simplification, severe pulmonary vascular remodeling, and RV dysfunction, which are only partially reversible after return to air. The findings highlight the critical role of disrupted vascular development and angiogenesis in the progression of PH and provide a robust platform to study disease mechanisms and evaluate potential therapeutic interventions aimed at improving outcomes in children with PH linked to abnormal lung development.
