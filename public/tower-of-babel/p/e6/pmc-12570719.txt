MRI study on hippocampal subfield volume loss and abnormal functional connectivity in patients with diabetic retinopathy
Authors: Yaqi Song, Zhijun Zhou, Weiqi Ji, Ning Wang, Liang He, Ji Zhang, Xiujuan Chen, Jianguo Xia, Weizhong Tian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to investigate the characteristics of gray matter volume loss in hippocampal subfields and alterations in whole-brain functional connectivity among patients with diabetic retinopathy (DR), and to examine the correlations between these neural changes and neuropsychological scale scores as well as clinical indicators.MethodsStructural and functional magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data, along with neurocognitive assessments, were acquired from 32 patients with diabetic retinopathy (DR) and 38 age- and sex-matched healthy controls using a Siemens 3.0T MRI scanner. Hippocampal subfield volumes were segmented and extracted using the Anatomy toolbox and Restplus (v1.25) within the MATLAB R2022a environment. Subregions demonstrating significant volumetric alterations were identified through subsequent between-group comparisons and defined as regions of interest (ROIs) for seed-based whole-brain functional connectivity (FC) analysis. The relationships between gray matter volume, functional connectivity values, neuropsychological test scores, and clinical indices were investigated using partial correlation analysis.ResultsCompared to healthy controls, patients with diabetic retinopathy (DR) exhibited significantly reduced bilateral entorhinal cortex (EC) volumes and increased volume in bilateral dentate gyrus (DG) (P < 0.05, FDR-corrected). Using these volume-altered subregions as regions of interest (ROIs), seed-based functional connectivity (FC) analysis revealed significantly increased FC strength between the left EC and the left caudate nucleus, alongside significantly decreased FC strength between the right DG and the right middle occipital gyrus (GRF correction: voxel-level p < 0.001, cluster-level p < 0.05, two-tailed). Furthermore, partial correlation analyses demonstrated significant positive correlations between the FC strength of the right hippocampal DG subfield and the right middle occipital gyrus, and scores on both the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) (r = 0.560, p = 0.001) and the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) (r = 0.541, p = 0.002).ConclusionsPatients with diabetic retinopathy (DR) demonstrate structural alterations in hippocampal subfields and widespread functional dysconnectivity across the brain. Notably, the strength of functional connectivity between the hippocampus and the visual cortex was significantly and positively correlated with cognitive function. This study provides neuroimaging evidence supporting the mechanisms underlying central nervous system complications in DR, suggesting that hippocampal subfield volumes and specific functional connectivity patterns hold potential as neuroimaging biomarkers for early intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12880-025-01979-5.

Introduction

Diabetic retinopathy (DR), as one of the most typical microvascular complications of diabetes, is a leading cause of unpreventable blindness in adults [1]. In recent years, growing evidence has indicated that DR not only affects vision but is also closely associated with an increased risk of cognitive dysfunction [2–4]. A meta-analysis by Wu et al. further confirmed this association and suggested that the underlying mechanisms may involve multiple pathways [5].

First, the retina and the brain share high similarities in embryonic origin, anatomical structure, and physiological function [6]. The blood-retinal barrier and the blood-brain barrier (BBB) are closely related in both structure and function. Increased permeability of the blood-retinal barrier in DR patients may reflect similar impairment of the BBB, which could lead to neuroinflammation, microcirculatory disturbances, and cognitive decline [7]. Second, hyperglycemia and insulin resistance can promote microvascular damage by inducing endothelial dysfunction. This not only accelerates the progression of DR but may also exacerbate cerebral small vessel disease, thereby contributing to cognitive impairment [8]. Furthermore, DR and cognitive decline share common risk factors, such as hypertension, which may act synergistically to further increase the risk of cognitive deficits [9].

It is noteworthy that diabetic retinopathy and Alzheimer’s disease (AD) exhibit shared pathological features in terms of neurovascular unit impairment, including neuroinflammation, vascular degeneration, and aberrant activation of glial cells [3, 10]. Metabolic dysregulation serves as a critical pathological link between the two conditions—dyslipidemia and insulin resistance not only exacerbate retinopathy but may also promote cognitive decline and AD progression by facilitating Aβ deposition and tau protein phosphorylation [11–14]. In addition to classical neuronal degeneration, AD patients often exhibit retinal alterations, such as optic neuropathy and ganglion cell damage [15], further supporting the interplay between retinal and cerebral pathways in the disease process.

The hippocampus, a key brain region responsible for encoding and retrieving episodic memories, is highly vulnerable to metabolic disturbances [16, 17]. Its subfields exhibit differential sensitivity to metabolic stress induced by hyperglycemia, due to variations in the distribution of glucose transporters (GLUTs). Subregions with abundant GLUT expression, such as CA3 and the dentate gyrus (DG), demonstrate greater susceptibility to metabolic alterations and are thus more prone to neuronal atrophy and dysfunction [18–20]. Studies have indicated that quantifying the volumes of hippocampal subfields can detect early cognitive decline more sensitively than assessing total hippocampal volume [21]. Therefore, investigating structural alterations in hippocampal subfields holds potential value for understanding DR-related cognitive impairment.

This study employs multimodal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to systematically characterize cerebral structural changes in DR patients through fine-grained segmentation of hippocampal subfields, and further explores the relationship between these structural changes and whole-brain functional network reorganization. By adopting a multidimensional analytical approach, we aim to elucidate the neural mechanisms and progression of cognitive dysfunction in DR patients, thereby providing imaging evidence for early identification and intervention.

Materials and methods

The data for this study were derived from the “DR Prospective Cohort Study.” A portion of this dataset was previously utilized in an analysis of differences in gray matter volume and cortical metrics between patient and healthy control groups, which has been published (Song Y et al., 2024; doi:10.1007/s11682-024-00905-7). In contrast, the present study focuses specifically on the hippocampus, a distinct gray matter nucleus, to investigate its microstructural alterations and their relationship with whole-brain functional connectivity. This work aims to provide neuroimaging data elucidating the neural correlates of cognitive impairment in DR patients.

Subjects

This prospective study recruited a cohort of 32 diabetic patients with retinopathy, alongside 38 age-, sex-, and education-matched healthy volunteers (Table 1).

Inclusion criteria for the patient group were as follows: (1) all patients diagnosed with diabetic retinopathy (DR) were admitted as inpatients to the Department of Ophthalmology, Affiliated Taizhou People’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University, during the period from March 2022 to October 2023; (2)No history of mental illness; (3) met the diagnostic criteria of the American Academy of Ophthalmology (AAO) for diabetes and DR; (4) Absence of speech, auditory, or other functional impairments; (5) All participants were right-handed.

Inclusion criteria for the control group were as follows: (1) Cognitively normal healthy middle-aged and elderly individuals matched to the DR group for age, sex, and education level; (2) No history of hypoglycemic medication use, fasting blood glucose < 6.1 mmol/L, postprandial blood glucose < 7.8 mmol/L.

Exclusion criteria for both the DR group and the healthy control group included: (1) Mental disorders or neurodevelopmental disorders; (2) History of other eye diseases; (3)History of cardiovascular disease or other endocrine disorders; (4) History of psychoactive substance abuse; (5)History of cerebrovascular disease, brain tumors, or tumors in other locations.

MRI acquisition

MRI data acquisition were acquired using a Siemens Skyra 3.0T scanner with a 16-channel head coil. All the participants were instructed to keep their eyes closed and remain in an awake state while keeping their heads still. Initial conventional sequences, including T1-weighted imaging (T1WI), T2-weighted imaging (T2WI), and T2-FLAIR, were acquired first. Two experienced radiologists independently reviewed these images to screen for and exclude subjects with organic brain lesions (e.g., tumors, stroke, cerebrovascular malformations, intracranial trauma, congenital developmental anomalies, or neurodegenerative pathologies). Subsequently, high-resolution 3D-T1 structural images were obtained using an MPRAGE sequence with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) = 2300 ms, echo time (TE) = 2.98 ms, flip angle (FA) = 9°, 176 contiguous slices, slice thickness = 1 mm, field of view (FOV) = 256 × 256 mm², and isotropic voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm³. Resting-state functional MRI data were then acquired using a gradient-echo planar imaging (EPI) sequence with these parameters: TR = 2160 ms, TE = 30.0 ms, FA = 90°, 40 axial slices, slice thickness = 3 mm, FOV = 256 × 256 mm², voxel size = 4 × 4 × 3 mm, and 240 volumes.

Neuropsychological testing

Prior to MRI scanning, a trained neurologist with 8 years of specialized experience in neuropsychological assessment conducted comprehensive evaluations of all participants. Neurocognitive assessment was performed using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) and Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), evaluating multiple cognitive domains including attention/concentration, executive function, memory, language abilities, visuospatial skills, and orientation. Psychological assessment incorporated the Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS) and Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS). Additionally, we systematically collected demographic characteristics and clinically relevant data, including metabolic parameters (fasting/postprandial blood glucose, glycated hemoglobin [HbA1c], and lipid profile).

Data processing

Hippocampal subfield volume analysis

Using the Anatomy toolbox based on the MATLAB platform and SPM software, the hippocampus was segmented into five subregions: the cornu ammonis (CA), dentate gyrus (DG), entorhinal cortex (EC), subiculum (Sub), and hippocampus-amygdala transition area (HATA) (Fig. 1). The whole hippocampus and its subregions were defined as regions of interest (ROIs). Structural images were preprocessed and analyzed using the CAT12 software package (http://dbm.neuro.uni-jena.de/cat/). Subsequently, the RESTplus V1.25 fMRI data analysis toolkit was utilized to extract gray matter volume (GMV) from each ROI for further analysis.

The processing of resting-state fMRI data

All functional MRI preprocessing was conducted using RESTplus V1.25 on MATLAB R2013b. The pipeline began with the conversion of DICOM images to NIFTI format, followed by the removal of the first 10 time points to allow for magnetic field stabilization. Subsequent steps included slice timing correction and motion realignment, which resulted in exclusion of three healthy controls exhibiting > 3 mm head displacement. Functional images underwent spatial normalization to MNI space, Gaussian smoothing (6-mm FWHM kernel), and linear detrending. Nuisance covariate regression removed contributions from 24 motion parameters, white matter, cerebrospinal fluid, and global mean signals. Mean time courses were then extracted from each hippocampal subregion. Whole-brain functional connectivity mapping was created by computing Pearson correlation coefficients between each subregion’s time series and all cerebral voxels, with subsequent Fisher’s r-to-z transformation yielding standardized zFC maps. Overlaid functional images of the hippocampal subfields are provided in the supplementary materials (Figure S1).

Statistical analysis

Between-group analysis of demographic and clinical data

Demographic characteristics, clinical parameters, and neuropsychological assessments were compared between the two groups using SPSS 26.0. Continuous variables were analyzed using Student’s t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate, while categorical variables were assessed with the chi-square test. A significance threshold of p < 0.05 was applied.

Analysis of gray matter volume differences

Group differences in the gray matter volume (GMV) of hippocampal subfields were assessed using an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA). The model included the group as a fixed factor, with age, sex, education level, and total intracranial volume (TIV) as covariates. Multiple comparisons were corrected using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method, with an FDR-corrected p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Functional connectivity analysis

The hippocampal subregions showing significant GMV differences were used as seed points for functional connectivity (FC) analysis. The FC between each seed point and all other brain voxels was compared between groups using a two-sample t-test in SPM, with age, gender, and education level included as covariates. Multiple comparisons were corrected at the cluster level using Gaussian Random Field (GRF) theory (voxel-level p < 0.001, cluster-level p < 0.05, two-tailed).

Correlation analysis

The GMV values of the altered hippocampal subregions and the FC values of significant connections were extracted using RESTplus. Partial correlation analyses were then performed in SPSS 26.0 to examine the relationships between these neuroimaging metrics (GMV and FC) and neuropsychological test scores, controlling for age, gender, and education level. A pp-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussions

Compared with healthy controls, patients with diabetic retinopathy (DR) exhibit significant hippocampal atrophy and cognitive impairment, accompanied by altered functional connectivity across multiple brain regions. These neural alterations likely contribute to the observed cognitive dysfunction in DR. The hippocampus is a structurally heterogeneous region supporting diverse functions, wherein synaptic transmission between hippocampal neurons critically underpins the acquisition and retention of learning and memory capacities [22]. Crucially, the hippocampus contains abundant insulin receptors and demonstrates high sensitivity to glycemic fluctuations. Elevated blood glucose levels cause endothelial injury, thereby compromising hippocampal function and promoting atrophy [23]. The characteristic cognitive deficits in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) patients—including impaired memory and executive function—may stem from multifaceted mechanisms such as insulin resistance, amyloid deposition, oxidative stress, and inflammatory mediators, which collectively disrupt microvascular integrity over time [24, 25].

The entorhinal cortex (Ent) is a crucial node connecting the hippocampus with cortical and subcortical regions. The deep neuronal structure of the Ent is important for spatial cognition, memory storage, consolidation, and retrieval [26]. Damage to the Ent can disrupt hippocampal projections to distant brain regions, leading to varying degrees of cognitive impairment. The hippocampus represents a crucial target of secondary central injury in individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). Impairments in hippocampal synaptic transmission have been observed across various rodent models of T2DM [27]. Given that factors such as hyperglycemia can impact cognitive status and are closely linked to hippocampal structure and function [28], it is plausible to suggest that the reduced cognitive level observed in DR patients in this study may be attributed to their microvascular damage and aberrant insulin signaling pathways.The trisynaptic hippocampal circuit (DG - CA3 - CA1) establishes spatial neural representations that mediate spatial navigation and episodic memory [29, 30]. While the CA1 subfield plays a critical role in memory encoding and consolidation, the CA2/CA3 and DG subregions support adaptive learning and social memory processing [31, 32]. Notably, our study observed increased volume in the DG subfield of diabetic retinopathy (DR) patients, potentially representing a neuroplastic compensatory mechanism for memory impairment associated with this condition.

This study identified altered functional connectivity between hippocampal subregions and specific brain areas, such as the occipital lobe and basal ganglia. The visual network (VN) located in the occipital lobe primarily processes and integrates visual information [33]. Upon receiving visual input, the visual cortex transmits information through the ventral occipitotemporal pathway to temporal regions, establishing a critical link with memory function [34, 35]. Previous research has demonstrated visual pathway abnormalities in diabetic retinopathy (DR) patients: Qi et al. reported disrupted functional connectivity between the primary visual cortex (V1) and higher-tier visual areas during resting state [36], suggesting impaired information transfer within V1 due to retinopathy; Li et al. further documented significantly reduced modular connectivity between the frontoparietal network (FPN) and visual network (VN) in DR patients [37], indicating compromised information transmission efficiency within the visual system. Crucially, our findings reveal weakened functional connectivity between the right hippocampal dentate gyrus (DG) subregion and the right middle occipital gyrus – also part of the VN – which may be directly related to vision impairment-induced dysfunction in these specific neural circuits.

The basal ganglia are critically engaged in sensorimotor integration, with the caudate nucleus functioning as a multisensory processing hub [38]. Neurophysiological studies have identified visual-responsive neurons in the dorsolateral caudate exhibiting broad receptive fields and heightened sensitivity to dynamic visual stimuli [39]. Our findings reveal significant correlations between progressive retinal pathophysiology in diabetic retinopathy (DR) patients and altered functional connectivity between hippocampal subfields and visual cortical regions. Specifically, we observed: (1) attenuated connectivity between the right dentate gyrus (DG) and right middle occipital gyrus, and (2) enhanced connectivity between the left entorhinal cortex (EC) and left caudate nucleus. Given prior evidence of right-hemispheric predominance in hippocampal atrophy patterns in Alzheimer’s disease [40], we postulate that visual pathway disruption in DR disproportionately compromises right hippocampal processing of environmental inputs. Conversely, increased left hippocampal EC connectivity may represent a neuroplastic adaptation, potentially compensating for visual degradation through unilateral network reinforcement mechanisms.

Patients with diabetic retinopathy (DR) exhibit cerebral microvascular dysfunction and neural pathway impairment. These pathophysiological alterations differentially compromise hippocampal subfield integrity, neuronal activity, and functional coordination, ultimately manifesting as deficits in memory encoding, spatial navigation, and related cognitive domains [41]. Critically, correlation analyses revealed a significant positive association between functional connectivity of the right dentate gyrus (DG)-right middle occipital gyrus circuit and cognitive performance measured by both the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) and Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE). This relationship indicates that weakened functional connectivity between these regions is associated with poorer cognitive function. These findings align with the hippocampus’s established role in information processing and synaptic integration within distributed cognitive networks [42].

Conclusions

This study revealed significant structural alterations and functional connectivity dysregulation in hippocampal subregions of diabetic retinopathy (DR) patients. Specifically, the observed entorhinal cortex atrophy and dentate gyrus (DG) volume enlargement may serve as potential neuroimaging markers associated with DR-related neural dysfunction. Future investigations will integrate multimodal MRI techniques to quantitatively analyze retinopathy-associated structural and functional changes, aiming to establish sensitive neurobiological markers that provide neuroimaging evidence for clinical diagnosis and treatment.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged in this study. Firstly, the sample size was relatively small, which might have had an impact on the statistical power of the results to some extent. Additionally, it is important to note that this study solely adopted a cross-sectional design without dynamic observation of the disease process in patients with diabetic retinopathy (DR) and longitudinal analysis. Therefore, future research endeavors should aim to conduct meticulous classification of DR patients and strive for comprehensive analysis of alterations in brain structure and function at different stages of DR.
