Designing Complex Care Management Programs to Support Patients With Substance Use Disorder: An Essential and Overlooked Opportunity
Authors: Kelly M. Schuering, Daniel P. Martin, Deborah Goldfarb, Erin Nahrgang, Alison A. Galbraith, Christine A. Pace
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Individuals with substance use disorders (SUDs) have high rates of medical and mental health comorbidities, health care utilization, and costs. Improving the capacity of care management programs to meet the needs of those with SUDs could benefit Medicaid accountable care organizations, but there are few reports of strategies to do so.Program Approach:The Boston Medical Center Health System Complex Care Management (CCM) program aims to build trusting relationships with the highest-risk patients across five Massachusetts Medicaid accountable care organizations and work with those individuals to achieve their health-related goals. Given that more than half of CCM-enrolled patients have been diagnosed with a SUD, CCM has adjusted its staffing models, workforce recruitment and development, patient identification and engagement, approach to health-related social needs, and community partnerships to better meet the needs of patients with an SUD. We have developed strategies to recruit diverse staff; train them on SUDs, harm reduction, motivational interviewing, and trauma-informed care; embed staff members in inpatient and community settings; and ensure teams have access to behavioral health staff for additional support as needed. We have also built partnerships with community, governmental, and health care organizations to ensure patients receive appropriately comprehensive care despite complex social, medical, and behavioral health needs.Outcomes:Although most patients with a SUD agree to enroll in CCM when outreached, our data indicate room to improve the rates at which we reach these individuals and sustain engagement. Preliminary pre–post data showed that CCM-enrolled patients had lower rates of overdose and higher rates of engagement with outpatient care for SUDs, mental health, and medications for opioid use disorder.Conclusion:Our experience demonstrates that with appropriate training and tools, care management staff perform vital work to improve outcomes for individuals with SUDs, although more detailed research is needed to assess the effectiveness of specific intervention components.

BACKGROUND

Substance use disorders (SUDs) are major contributors to rising health care costs. Individuals with a SUD are more than twice as likely to be hospitalized than those without a SUD (Gryczynski et al., 2016), and SUDs and associated medical conditions are among the leading causes of readmissions among Medicaid patients (Weiss, & Jiang, 2021). Moreover, the burden of SUDs has increased since the COVID-19 pandemic, with rising rates of alcohol use disorder (Schmidt et al., 2021) and opioid overdose (Gomes et al., 2023), particularly among underserved populations (Chacon et al., 2021).

Accountable care organizations (ACOs) aim to reduce the total cost of care while improving care quality. Given the burden of SUDs among their populations, Medicaid ACOs in particular must continually adapt their core functions such as behavioral health network development (D’Aunno et al., 2015; McDowell et al., 2018), reimbursement, utilization management, quality improvement initiatives (Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, 2024), and care management programs to meet the needs of individuals with a SUD.

Effective care management for patients with SUDs remains understudied. Although case management has been shown to help patients stay in SUD treatment and recovery (Vanderplasschen et al., 2019) and is recommended as a part of comprehensive SUD care by the Substance Use and Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA, 2021), there is minimal discussion in the literature on the rationale for, approach to, or effectiveness of SUD-focused support as a part of general care management programs for high-risk individuals. In this article, we share details about how one complex care management (CCM) program serving Medicaid patients has approached supporting complex patients with a SUD via its staffing model, workforce development, patient identification and engagement, approach to health-related social needs, and partnership development, as well as highlighting where opportunities remain.

Context

The Boston Medical Center Health System (BMCHS) is an integrated health system that includes a Medicaid-focused health plan and eight Medicaid ACOs. The BMCHS CCM program serves patients in five of these ACOs. CCM teams of nurses, community health workers, pharmacists, pharmacy technicians, and behavioral health clinicians are centrally hired and supervised, though embedded within a variety of clinical sites, including community health centers, academic medical centers, independent primary care practices, and hospitals. The program enrolls patients at high risk for future health care costs, identified either through clinician referrals or an internally validated claims-based risk algorithm.

The program began in 2016 as a pilot program within a single primary care practice; a multidisciplinary team developed the staffing and engagement model based on literature reviews, review of clinical data on high-utilizing patients within the practice, and consultations with experts in CCM, including the Camden Coalition. When Massachusetts Medicaid launched its ACO program in 2018, BMCHS scaled the program across practices within 2 of our first ACOs. The program has undergone iteration over time based on clinical data, performance on key process indicators, and feedback from patients, staff, and providers, as well as the health care environment during the COVID-19 pandemic. For example, behavioral health clinicians were not on the original pilot team, but were added in 2019 once the populations’ significant behavioral health needs became clearer. Additionally, in 2025, we launched a patient-reported outcomes assessment process, which will more systematically consider patients’ experiences to guide future iterations.

CCM teams focus on building trusting relationships with enrolled patients to work together towards their goals. After patients consent to the program, CCM staff complete a standardized assessment and work with the patient, their providers, and caregivers to develop a care plan. Care plans address patient-directed goals such as chronic disease self-management, health behavior change, engagement with outpatient medical and behavioral care, and health-related social needs, such as food security, transportation, and stable housing. Staff work with patients through telephonic outreach and in-person encounters in homes, shelters, clinics, hospitals, subacute nursing facilities, and community-based settings. Staff coordinate with primary care and specialty providers, home health agencies, and community agencies through placing, obtaining, and following up referrals to ensure patients can access the specific services they need. When patients are admitted, staff collaborate with inpatient care teams on discharge planning and follow up with the patient within 72 hours of discharge via a home visit whenever possible. As the program is intensive and intended to be time limited (average duration: 10 months), teams aim to increase patients’ self-efficacy and outside support as they progress.

Patients’ work with the program can end either in graduation or disenrollment. Patients graduate from the program when they have made significant progress toward their stated goals and shown continued engagement in their care and addressing needs with decreasing team support. A patient is disenrolled when they no longer wish to participate, the team and patient have failed to make progress toward their goals despite multiple efforts to address barriers over time, or the team can no longer reach the patient despite multiple attempts at both telephonic and in-person outreach. We also try to reach the patient through any additional contacts, community partners, or street outreach as appropriate. Disenrolling a patient due to lack of progress or disengagement is a team decision that is often made in conjunction with managers when there are limited, if any, additional ideas on how to reach the patient, and only after attempting to communicate this to the patient. Patients can be reenrolled after graduation if they have new needs or have significantly increased utilization or after disenrollment if they show an increased desire and ability to engage.

The importance of addressing SUD in our CCM program

Although the prevalence of SUDs among the general ACO population is 13.0%, the prevalence among the population identified as eligible for CCM based on our risk algorithm is 70.9% and among the CCM-enrolled population is 52.8% (Table 1). Within the CCM-enrolled population, patients with a SUD experience comparable rates of most comorbid chronic medical conditions and higher rates of mental health conditions and homelessness. They also have a higher total cost of care, inpatient utilization, and emergency department visits (Figure 1). Thus, ensuring that CCM can address the unique needs of individuals with SUD is of paramount importance.

PROGRAM APPROACH TO SUPPORTING INDIVIDUALS WITH SUD

Staffing models

CCM has adjusted its staffing models to better meet the needs of the most complex patients, including those with SUDs. In most settings, the “core” CCM team dyad is a community health worker and a nurse. Behavioral health clinicians, clinical pharmacists, and pharmacy technicians work across several dyads to support patients as needed. Over time, we identified the need for teams with condition-specific expertise (e.g., HIV-AIDS, chronic kidney disease, and SUD). We have added a recovery coach to an SUD-focused team and a dedicated homeless outreach coordinator who partners with shelters and street-based services to locate and build relationships with unhoused patients, many of whom have SUDs, in our largest, urban ACO. In addition, CCM staff are embedded in hospitals with a high volume of ACO admissions to enroll eligible inpatients and support enrolled patients while they are admitted. These CCM staff members continue to build rapport over multiple admissions, use motivational interviewing to promote harm reduction and/or treatment, and partner with medical teams, SUD clinicians, and outpatient CCM teams to help bridge inpatient and ambulatory settings. Finally, our leadership team includes addiction-trained physicians and behavioral health clinicians with addiction treatment experience to ensure that our program design appropriately considers the needs of those with a SUD.

Recruitment and workforce development

We aim to hire staff who demonstrate traits effective in engaging complex patients with a SUD and further develop SUD-specific knowledge and skills through ongoing education and training. As has been shown to be effective, we try to recruit staff who are patient-centered, nonjudgmental, flexible, able to collaborate well in interdisciplinary settings, have experience working with patients with behavioral health complexity, and, whenever possible, have lived experience and reflect the communities we serve (Komaromy et al., 2021; Madden et al., 2020). We assess these key attributes by consistently incorporating case scenarios with SUD-related challenges in our individual interviews and, when possible, conducting group interviews to observe interpersonal communication. To address staff members’ variable SUD knowledge and previous experience when hired, we incorporate foundational training for all new staff and conduct regular training on SUDs and addiction guidelines. Examples include building rapport and discussing SUDs, harm reduction, trauma-informed care, motivational interviewing, navigating SUD resources and levels of care, diagnosis and treatment of co-occurring mental health conditions, and additional topics as requested by staff. Staff knowledge and capacity are further developed through partnering with CCM’s behavioral health staff for expert consultative support, crisis intervention, and assistance with finding treatment placement. Finally, more tailored coaching is often included in regular case discussions with managers and clinician leaders.

Patient identification, enrollment, and engagement

CCM has incorporated SUDs directly into our risk algorithms and adjusted our patient engagement techniques to more successfully partner with patients with a SUD. Internal validation work has found that many off-the-shelf risk scores underestimate the costs of members with SUDs or behavioral health conditions. Moreover, individuals with a SUD may have less engagement in outpatient care and thus lower odds of being referred to CCM due to geographic transience, homelessness, and prior experiences of stigma in medical settings. Thus, we directly incorporated SUDs in our patient risk algorithm to better identify individuals at risk for future high costs.

After a patient is identified as CCM-eligible, staff prioritize meeting patients “where they are” both geographically and in terms of their substance use. We emphasize in-person engagement for enrollment and have found that many patients who were unable to engage in our program initially will enroll in the future after seeing our staff consistently, particularly during hospital admissions. Once enrolled, we continually assess what a patient’s goals are regarding their substance use. When a patient does not view their substance use as a problem and/or does not identify reducing substance use as a current goal, staff focus on other patient priorities and harm reduction rather than treatment and abstinence. To help patients stay connected to their care team, we provide temporary access to a cell phone supplied by our program while working to connect them to longer-term options like the federal Lifeline program.

Health-related social needs

To build trust and rapport, our teams often start their work with patients by addressing health-related social needs, such as food, clothing, or transportation. Food insecurity is highly prevalent in our population, and our teams work to address this through linkage to hot meals, food pantries, medically tailored meals, and nutritional subsidy programs. Given that almost one-third of our patients with a SUD are unhoused, we have developed additional supports to help patients find the housing that is right for them. Our program has built partnerships to provide housing navigation, connections with low-threshold housing programs, legal services to address barriers to housing (such as a criminal offender record), and support with accommodation needs for patients with disabilities; we also partner with philanthropic programs that can provide furniture for the newly housed. Fifty-five CCM patients (65% of whom have a SUD) have been housed through this partnership to date. We follow newly housed individuals for at least a year, with a focus on mental health support, recovery support, and harm reduction during what can be a vulnerable and difficult transition. We also link patients to partnerships funded by the state’s ACO program and other community organizations to support patients with obtaining food, nonemergent medical transport, and housing navigation that can continue even after graduation from our program.

External clinical partnerships

Given our patients’ significant comorbid physical and behavioral health needs, we often face challenges in obtaining post–acute medical services/placements due to concerns about their behavioral health needs and in psychiatric or SUD placements due to their high medical needs. Over time, we have built partnerships with SUD agencies, the Department of Mental Health, subacute nursing facilities, respites, and visiting nurse agencies to better support patients with SUDs. By setting up regular meetings to review shared cases and referrals and demonstrating our ability to adequately coordinate care with patients’ medical and psychiatric providers, we have built trust with these agencies to ensure that our patients receive appropriately comprehensive care. We also train staff to coordinate between multiple systems and levels of care and regularly review escalation pathways for particularly challenging cases in which leadership input may be needed.

PRELIMINARY OUTCOMES AND REMAINING CHALLENGES

While a comprehensive analysis of the impact of CCM on utilization across all enrolled patients is in progress, preliminary analysis of process and outcome metrics indicates promising results and potential areas for improvement. Evaluation and data review of our CCM program impact have been approved by the Boston University Medical Center Institutional Review Board #H-38229. Note that all analyses were conducted with R version 4.4.1 (R Core Team, 2024).

Our enrollment data show high rates of initial program consent among patients with SUDs, although we still have room to improve our outreach efforts. The fact that 52.8% of CCM-enrolled patients have a SUD while 70.9% of eligible patients have a SUD indicates that we are not reaching all those with SUDs who may benefit from the program (Table 1). Interestingly, the rates of consenting to the program once outreached are higher among patients with a SUD (72.7% [95% confidence interval (CI): 70.7%, 74.7%]) compared with the general population (27.5% [95% CI: 26.2%, 28.7%]) with similar rates of care plan completion (56.5% [95% CI: 53.9%, 59.1%] among those with a SUD compared with 59.7% [95% CI: 57.0%, 62.3%] for those without) (Figure 2). This shows that initially connecting with patients with SUDs may remain the biggest barrier to program enrollment.

CCM program engagement and progression over time.

Our data show we also have room for improvement in sustaining program engagement with our patients with SUDs over time. Overall, patients with a SUD are more likely to be disenrolled (as opposed to graduating) from the program compared with those without a SUD. At 18 months after CCM enrollment, the rate of graduation is significantly lower in patients with a SUD (54.9% [95% CI: 49.3%, 60.1%]) compared with those without a SUD diagnosis (64.9% [60.1%, 69.4%], p < .001), estimated via Gray’s test (Gray, 1988). Correspondingly, rates of disenrollment at 18 months are significantly higher for those with a SUD (27.9% [95% CI: 24.6%, 31.4%] than for members without a SUD diagnosis 20.1% [17.1%, 23.3%], p = .001). It takes more time for patients with an SUD to be ready for graduation compared with those without an SUD. The median time to graduate is 4.1 months longer for patients with an SUD relative to those without an SUD (SUD: 15.3 months vs. no SUD: 11.2 months, p < .001). This may be because patients with a SUD may have more intermittent engagement. Although disenrollment rates were somewhat higher for patients with SUD compared with those without, the trajectory over time, especially after 12 months, was similar between the groups due to continued engagement efforts by the CCM team.

To assess the impact of the program on connection to outpatient mental health care, SUD treatment, and medications for opioid use disorder (MOUD), we conducted a 3-month pre–post analysis of claims for 717 patients with a SUD using our insurance product for at least three continuous months before and after enrollment in CCM between July 2023 and May 2024. Most notably, this pre–post analysis showed that the rates of overdose after CCM enrollment decreased by over 40%, showing the potential impact of our teams’ efforts in harm reduction and/or linkages to care (Table 2). Outpatient SUD care, MOUD adherence (defined as at least 80% of included days covered), and outpatient mental health care increased in the 3 months after CCM enrollment as well. Although these data show improved utilization of outpatient behavioral health care services and harm reduction after CCM enrollment, findings are subject to limitations such as regression to the mean and potential confounding factors, and findings need to be confirmed through additional approaches, particularly further studies that include a matched comparison group. It is also clear that overall utilization of outpatient behavioral health services and MOUD remains low; thus, supporting patients’ access to behavioral health care, MOUD, and harm reduction remains an important area of continued focus for program improvement.

In summary, the data show promise in terms of initial enrollment and harm reduction, although there remains ongoing room for improvement in sustaining engagement and connection to care to bring patients closer to their identified goals. We will continue to reflect on and formally assess what is most impactful in initiating and sustaining patient engagement with CCM to help patients achieve their SUD-related goals and graduate from our program. Based on our current understanding, we should continue to build partnerships with emergency departments, primary care practices, shelters, behavioral health clinics, and housing agencies to improve our ability to connect with patients, strengthen staff expertise in patient engagement and SUD navigation, and advocate for more low-threshold and supportive housing and appropriate longitudinal and intensive outpatient behavioral health care throughout the state.

CONCLUSIONS

CCM programs must develop strategies to engage and support patients with SUDs to effectively reduce avoidable medical expenses and improve the health outcomes of high-risk patients. We have designed our staffing models, recruitment and workforce development, patient identification and engagement, approach to health-related social needs, and partnership development with the needs of individuals with SUDs in mind. Ongoing detailed research, particularly with control groups, is needed to assess the effectiveness of specific intervention components and overall impact on medical costs, overdoses, and patient outcomes. However, our work indicates that with appropriate training and tools, diverse and interdisciplinary care management staff can and do perform vital work to improve outcomes and experiences of care for individuals with SUDs across the health care system.
