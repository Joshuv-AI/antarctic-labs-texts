Cellular and molecular mechanisms of thrombosis and thrombus-targeted thrombolytic strategies
Authors: Xuehao Yu, Jinlong Zhang, Yidan Zhang, Jiahua Wang, Yao Zhao, Mei Wang, Yuanyuan Guo, Yuehua Li, Yu Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The formation of thrombus is intricately linked to the development of severe cardiovascular diseases, including myocardial infarction, stroke, deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, and peripheral vascular diseases. Despite advancements in thrombolytic therapy, challenges still exist regarding thrombus targeting and treatment safety, as excessive doses of thrombolytic agents often lead to complications such as bleeding and neurotoxicity. A thorough understanding of the cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying thrombosis is critical for the development of safer and more effective thrombolytic strategies. It is widely acknowledged that various cells and key molecules play significant roles in thrombus formation. Building on this knowledge, a series of thrombus-targeted thrombolytic strategies have been developed, focusing on the targeted delivery and localized release of thrombolytic agents at the site of the thrombus. This review first summarizes the roles of cells and molecules in thrombus formation and provides a comprehensive overview of the classical cellular and molecular mechanisms of thrombosis. Furthermore, we explore the latest advancements in thrombus-targeted thrombolytic strategies, which are grounded in the critical cells and molecules associated with thrombosis. Finally, we conclude with a brief discussion of potential future directions in this emerging field.

Introduction

Thrombosis refers to the formation of a blood clot within a blood vessel, potentially blocking blood flow and leading to serious vascular diseases such as myocardial infarction, stroke, deep venous thrombosis (DVT), pulmonary embolism, and peripheral arterial diseases[[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]]. Thrombosis can be classified into arterial thrombosis and venous thrombosis based on its location [6]. Arterial thrombosis is typically triggered by rupture of the atherosclerotic plaques. Under high shear forces, the thrombi formed in the arteries contain a higher proportion of platelets and more compact fibrin[[7], [8], [9]]. In contrast, venous thrombosis is a multicausal disease occurring as the result of interacting genetic, environmental, and behavioural risk factors[[10], [11], [12]]. Thrombolytic agents (TAs), such as tissue plasminogen activators (tPA), streptokinase (SK), and urokinase (UK), are enzymes that catalyze plasminogen into plasmin, resulting in dissolution of the fibrin matrix of thrombus and finally restoration of the blood flow [13,14]. However, thrombolytic treatment by TAs still has many challenges, including excessive bleeding complications, rapid blood clearance, and central nervous system (CNS) toxicity[[15], [16], [17], [18], [19]]. To address these issues, efforts have focused on using targeted synthetic nanocarriers to enhance thrombolytic therapy by chemical conjugation or encapsulation which can protect the TAs from fast clearance in the bloodstream and deliver TAs to the thrombus site specifically [20,21]. Thrombus formation is a complex process involving multiple cells and molecules. Therefore, understanding the functions of cells and molecules in thrombosis and clarifying the mechanism of thrombosis are the basics of thrombolytic therapy. In turn, these cells and molecules associated with thrombosis also provide ample opportunities for the design of thrombus-targeted thrombolytic strategies for the treatment of thrombus. To our best of knowledge, there is no relevant detailed review focused on the cellular and molecular mechanisms of thrombosis and thrombus-targeted thrombolytic strategies as far. In this present review, we first describe and summarize the roles of cells (i.e., erythrocytes, platelets, and leukocytes) and functions of molecules at different stages of thrombus formation, thus providing a comprehensive overview of the classical cellular and molecular mechanisms of thrombosis. Furthermore, based on the cells and molecules associated with thrombosis, we have exemplified the most recent thrombus-targeted delivery systems including cell-targeted and molecule-targeted systems for thrombolytic therapy. Finally, the possible future directions and perspectives in this emerging area are briefly discussed.

Cellular and molecular mechanisms of thrombosis

The formation of thrombus can be preliminarily divided into primary hemostasis and secondary hemostasis [22,23]. Single-layered endothelial cells protect the underlying collagen and prevent the formation of thrombus. Under normal physiological conditions, endothelial cells create a surface that safeguard against platelet activation and triggering of coagulation cascade. Their cell surface is endowed with various fibrinolytic and antithrombotic properties, which contributes to preserving blood fluidity. These protective mechanisms encompass platelet inhibition, the heparin-antithrombin III system for inhibiting coagulation, the tissue factor pathway inhibition to hinder thrombus formation, the thrombomodulin/protein C system for promoting fibrinolysis, and inherent fibrinolytic qualities[[24], [25], [26], [27], [28]].

Once the endothelial cells have been damaged, as shown in Scheme 1, the subendothelial matrix is exposed to the blood flow. Under conditions of high shear stress, von Willebrand factor (vWF) from plasma binds to the exposed collagen through its A1 domain[[29], [30], [31], [32]]. Subsequently, platelets adhere to the collagen-bound vWF via GPIbα within their GPIb/V/IX complex (①)[[33], [34], [35]]. This initial anchoring process results in a slowing of blood flow and occurs rapidly as platelets become tethered to the endothelial matrix. Platelets then firmly adhere to the subendothelial matrix through the engagement of collagen receptors GPVI. Unlike GPIb/V/IX complex, the stimulation of GPVI directly binds to collagen and induces platelet activation, which leads to secretion and inside-out signalling of the integrins α2β1 and αIIbβ3 (②) [36,37]. GPVI works together with α2β1 to facilitate platelet adhesion and spreading on the site of vascular injury as well as the release of intracellular substances from platelets [38,39]. The activated integrin αIIbβ3 binds to a tetrapeptide (RGD sequence) or a dodecapeptide (HHLGGAKQAGDV sequence) present in fibrinogen, to vWF, to collagen, or to other adhesion substrates in the complex matrix [40,41]. Subsequently, the binding of αIIbβ3 and fibrinogen results in firm adhesion and stable aggregation of activated platelets [42,43]. After adhesion and aggregation, platelets produce and release various molecules from dense bodies and α-granules[[44], [45], [46]] (③). These molecules not only reinforce platelet-mediated thrombus formation but also contribute to coagulation and subsequent inflammatory effects (primary hemostasis). As signals for platelet activation increase, phosphatidylserine (PS) is activated and exposed, serving as a second messenger to enhance the action of plasma thrombin [47,48] (④). In addition to converting fibrinogen into fibrin, activated thrombin cleaves the amino terminus of the 7-transmembrane-domain protease-activated receptor (PAR) and triggers a signalling cascade to enhance platelet activation [49] (⑤). During fibrin formation process, a relatively dense meshwork is formed to entrap many coagulation factors and red blood cells, ultimately leading to the formation of a compact blood clot (secondary hemostasis) [50,51]. Through the release of chemokines and adhesion receptors such as P-selectin from α-granules, platelets recruit leukocytes, especially neutrophils and monocytes, into the growing thrombus [52]. P-selectin can combine with P-selectin glycoprotein ligand-1 (PSGL-1) to provide an efficient scaffold for leukocyte recruitment, linking the phase of platelet activation to pro-inflammatory function [53,54] (⑥). Monocytes express higher levels of tissue factor (TF) compared to neutrophils, initiating the extrinsic coagulation cascade and driving thrombin catalysis of fibrin formation[[55], [56], [57]] (⑦). This reveals the close connection between the inflammatory response and coagulation. After the formation of a relatively stable blood clot, platelets begin to exert their contractile function. Platelets with intact cytoskeletons rely on the contraction of their filopodia to pull and contract the attached fibrin[[58], [59], [60]]. This process is crucial for enabling the thrombus to effectively seal the wound. Cell-targeted thrombolytic strategies

The formation of thrombus involves various cells that are crucial for maintaining smooth blood flow and are actively involved in hemostasis and coagulation. During thrombosis, platelets serve as the first responders to exposed collagen. Platelets adhere to and aggregate at the injury site within the vessel, releasing active substances that further initiate the coagulation cascade. This process also recruits both red blood cells and white blood cells with their ligand-receptor interactions, playing a critical role in thrombosis. Strategies that target or are derived from these cells is a promising design trend with highly significant practical implications. In this section, considering that red blood cells, platelets, and white blood cells play crucial roles during the process of thrombus formation, we first discuss the specific functions of these cells in thrombosis, thereby revealing the cell-targeted thrombolytic strategies for the application of thrombus therapy.

Red blood cell-targeted systems

There is a growing consensus on the crucial role of red blood cells (RBCs) in thrombus formation [61,62]. The function of RBCs in thrombosis is contingent upon their intricate interactions with other clot components. With a concentration of approximately 5 × 109 mL−1 of blood, these anucleate cells are present in significant quantities and possess both biocompatibility and plasticity. Several mechanisms have been investigated regarding how RBCs may contribute to thrombosis [71,72]. Firstly, the degree of clot contraction is diminished when RBCs are present in clots, which is a critical step to promote blood flow. Aleman et al. have reported that the activity of factor XIII (FXIII) is crucial for the retention of RBCs within blood clots and directly influences the size of the thrombus, which is mediated by fibrin α-chain crosslinking [73]. Secondly, RBCs facilitate platelets adhesion toward vessel wall, which increases the deposition of platelets at thrombi [74,75]. Meanwhile, activated platelets can lead to the exposure of platelet FasL, which will activate FasR on RBCs, responsible for the externalization of phosphatidylserine (PS) to the membrane of RBCs. This process not only leads to programmed cell death of RBCs but also results in the formation of prothrombinase complex and ultimately the generation of thrombin [76]. As natural long-circulating delivery vehicles, RBCs can be modified into nanoparticles to implement targeted therapy [77]. To date, the membranes of RBCs have been caught significant attention for the design of targeted drug delivery systems in thrombolytic therapy. For example, Zhao et al. developed a type of RBC membrane-coated dextran-tirofiban conjugate nanoparticle (T-RBC-DTC NPs) to specifically target to the thrombus site (Fig. 1A) [78]. Tirofiban is a clinical antiplatelet agent, which effectively reduces platelets aggregation by antagonizing platelet receptors. The T-RBC-DTC NPs are coated within RBC membranes conjugated with the fibrin-targeting peptide Cys-Arg-Glu-Lys-Ala (CREKA) to enhance the accumulation of the nanoparticles at the fibrin-rich thrombus site. Dextran is modified with an H2O2-responsive linker that can be oxidized by reactive oxygen species (ROS). This design not only achieves thrombus-targeted delivery but also mitigates inflammatory responses at the thrombus site to a certain extent. In the model of mouse carotid artery thrombosis, it showed a sufficient accumulation of T-RBC-DTC NPs at the thrombus site, resulting in advanced antithrombotic activity compared to free tirofiban (Fig. 1B). As shown in Fig. 1C, the hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was observed that the content of thrombus is significantly reduced. Platelet-targeted systems

Platelets, as the initial components to adhere to the thrombus site, play a crucial role in the thrombotic process. The diverse integrins present on their surface provide valuable insights for researchers in developing targeted strategies and enhancing biocompatibility in the field of thrombolysis. In this section, we will explore the roles of various platelet components and their implications for thrombolytic therapy. Targeted therapies based on platelets are diverse and versatile. One approach involves encapsulating drugs within platelet membranes, leveraging the abundant receptors present on the membrane for active targeting[[63], [64], [65]]. Additionally, researchers can simulate the interactions between platelets and biomolecules during thrombus formation, utilizing the integrins on platelets, such as αIIbβ3 and the GPIb-IX-V complex, to achieve thrombus targeting. Furthermore, attention has been drawn to P-selectin present on platelets, which can be targeted with small molecules to facilitate thrombus tracking. Thus, platelet-based systems could be primarily divided in four types: platelet membranes-, platelet αIIbβ3-, platelet GPIb-IX-V complex-, or platelet P-selectin-targeted systems, which are well-organized and discussed in the sections from 3.2.1 to 3.2.4.

Platelet membranes-targeted systems

Platelets are tiny cells exist in the blood, produced by large cells called megakaryocytes through complicated mechanisms involving cell membrane protrusions, cytoskeleton rearrangement, and proplatelet formation [[81], [82], [83]]. Like mature RBCs, platelets do not have nucleus. Platelets have various functions including adhesion, aggregation, granule release, and contraction, which contribute to the process of thrombus formation [84]. Similar to the application of RBC membranes, the use of platelet membranes can also reduce the occurrence of haemolysis. Based on this, researchers designed platelet membranes coated nanoparticles (PM@NPs) to enhance biocompatibility of the drug delivery systems as well as to reduce adverse drug reactions. For example, Hua et al. developed a platelet membrane-coated recombinant staphylokinase (r-SAK) (denoted as PM-r-SAK) for thrombus targeting [65]. The PM-r-SAK was prepared by encapsulating r-SAK into platelet membranes using the membrane extrusion method (Fig. 4A). In the near-infrared fluorescence imaging study, it was found that the PM-r-SAK exhibited a good thrombus targeting in a rabbit blood clot model (Fig. 4B and C). Compared to the sole use of r-SAK, this PM-r-SAK has been proven to be four times more effective thrombus-targeting activity than that of conventional drugs both in vitro (Fig. 4D) and in vivo models (Fig. 4E).Fig. 4(A) Schematic diagram of the preparation of the PM-r-SAK by the extrusion method. Platelet αIIbβ3-targeted systems

In addition to using platelet membranes, the receptors present on platelets are also the hot spots for target designs. These receptors are located on the platelet membrane as well as intracellular granules. Once platelets are activated by the binding of initial agonists, these receptors undergo conformational changes, revealing high-affinity binding sites for fibrinogen. The most used receptor in platelets as a target is αIIbβ3, also known as the GPIIb-IIIa complex. Fibrinogen binds to αIIbβ3 to induce platelet aggregation via three putative specific recognition sites, namely, a tetrapeptide containing RGD sequences such as RGDF and RGDS at two α chain segments of α95-98 and α572-575, and a dodecapeptide (HHLGGAKQAGDV, H12) at a γ chain segment of γ400-411, thus culminating in the formation of a fibrin-platelet complex. Therefore, it is feasible to modify drug delivery systems with fibrinogen or fibrinogen-derived peptides to develop platelet-based systems for targeted thrombolysis. For example, Huang et al. designed a cRGD-coated, polyethylene glycol (PEG)-modified nanoliposome for tPA encapsulation (denoted as tPA-PEG-cRGD-lip) [85] (Fig. 5A). As shown in Fig. 5B, the selective binding ability of tPA-PEG-cRGD-lip to activated platelets was directly observed with a strong green fluorescence by using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM). However, the use of the RGD sequence alone presents challenges due to its limited stability and potential immunogenicity, compounded by the fact that the RGD sequence is present in various cells, resulting in a lack of specificity in targeting [86,87]. Researchers tend to use the HHLGGAKQAGDV sequence in γ chains of fibrinogen with better specificity in recent designs. For example, Absar et al. designed a fibrinogen-derived peptide to deliver albumin-camouflaged tPA to the site of thrombus [88]. In this system, albumin was first conjugated with thrombus homing peptide of CQQHHLGGAKQAGDV, which is a C-terminal γ-chain peptide sequence of fibrinogen that could bind with the receptor of αIIbβ3. On the other side of the albumin, it directly conjugates to tPA via a thrombin-cleavable peptide linker of GFPRGFPAGGC. Using albumin as an intermediate bridge, the fibrinogen-related peptides and tPA are connected (Fig. 6A and B). The efficient targeting ability of the system was confirmed through observation of the construct's binding with activated platelets under a fluorescence microscope (Fig. 6C). Platelet GPIb-IX-V-targeted systems

GPIb-IX-V complex is the second largest receptor on the platelet surface, approximately 25,000–35,000 copies/platelet [89]. It is composed of one GPIbα subunit disulfide-linked to two molecules of GPIbβ, and non-covalently linked with GPIX and GPV in a 2:4:2:1 ratio [90]. Its main function is to mediate the first critical step of platelet tethering by promoting binding to vWF on damaged subendothelial cells under high fluid shear stress [91]. Furthermore, GPIb-IX-V complex has an affinity for thrombin [92]. It is currently unknown, but it is reasonable to consider that targeting both vWF and thrombin binding sites of GPIb-IX-V might provide potentials for the treatment of thrombosis. For example, some antibodies have been designed to achieve targeted therapy for thrombosis by influencing this binding process. Among them, the antibodies targeting GPIbα including h6B4-Fab, GPG-290, and the monoclonal antibody SZ2[[93], [94], [95]]. Although targeted thrombus delivery strategies based on GPIb-IX-V complex are not yet well-developed, it is believed that GPIb-IX-V complex may become a new research hot spot in the targeted design for thrombolytic therapy.

Platelet P-selectin-targeted systems

P-selectin is a member of the C-type lectin family. When platelets are stimulated by agonists such as adenosine diphosphate (ADP), membrane flipping occurs and promotes the release of α-granules on the surface of activated platelets and endothelial [96,97]. P-selectin glycoprotein ligand-1 (PSGL-1) is broadly expressed on most white blood cells (WBCs), which interacts with P-selectin present in activated platelets and mediates leukocyte rolling, adhesion, and transmigration [98,99]. In the beginning, PSGL-1 is only regarded as an adhesion molecule that binds P-selectin, but now, a growing consensus has recognized it as an important regulator of many facets of immune responses by myeloid cells and T cells both in settings of homeostasis and inflammation [100]. It is hypothesized that targeting thrombolytic agents to P-selectin would enhance local thrombolysis and reduce bleeding risk. Fucoidan, known as one of the ligands of P-selectin, has played a role in the design of targeted thrombus therapy. Cedric et al. developed Fucoidan functionalized core-shell polymer microcapsules (Fuco-MCs) and confirmed that Fuco-MCs could bind to activated platelet aggregates even at high shear rates [101]. Furthermore, Zhang et al. used the thrombin-responsive peptide (GGLVPRGFGG, pep) as the bridge to connect MnO2 NPs and Fucoidan. uPA was chosen as the model drug to construct a smart drug delivery system of MnO2/uPA@pep-Fuco to identify the thrombus accurately [102]. Besides, Liu et al. have developed the self-assembled nanoparticles of DIP-FU-PPy NPs, which consist of polypyrrole (PPy), dipyridamole (DIP), and P-selectin-targeted Fucoidan (FU) through optimization of a co-assembly engineering process, which were designed to be delivered directly to thrombi (Fig. 7A) [103]. As shown in Fig. 7B, the fluorescence intensity of DIP-FU-PPy NPs in activated platelets was higher than those of DIP in non-activated and activated platelets. The fluorescence intensity indicated that FU affected the specific binding of DIP-FU-PPy NPs to activated platelets, further verifying the ability of Fucoidan-decorated NPs to precisely deliver therapeutics to the thrombus site. Leukocytes-targeted systems

Many studies have demonstrated the close relationship between thrombosis and immune cells. In 2013, Engelmann et al. coined the term “immunothrombosis” and described the mechanisms underlying the interaction between coagulation activation and the innate immune system [104]. The binding of fibrinogen to integrin αMβ2 (Macrophage-1 antigen, Mac-1) is crucial for leukocyte function and innate immunity in vivo, leading to changes in cell migration, phagocytosis, NF-κB-mediated transcription, production of chemokines and cytokines, degranulation, and other processes [[66], [67], [68]]. Furthermore, P-selectin on platelets induces inflammation by binding to P-selectin receptors (i.e., PSGL-1) and integrins on leukocytes as mentioned. The inflammation, localized at the thrombus, further leads to platelet aggregation and activation, as well as a cascade of coagulation factor reactions [105]. Main leukocytes involved in thrombus formation are neutrophils and macrophages [106]. Upon activation, neutrophils release granular proteins and chromatin, which together form extracellular fibres that bind to both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, known as neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) that are mainly composed of double-stranded DNA (dsDNA), histone, myeloperoxidase (MPO), cathepsin G, and neutrophil elastase (NE) [107,108]. In acute inflammation, NETs are abundant in the body and serve to degrade virulence factors and kill bacteria [109]. Main microbicidal effectors of neutrophils have been identified to serine proteases, including neutrophil elastase and cathepsin G [110]. The externalization of nucleosomes can also effectively promote coagulation and intravascular thrombus growth in vivo [111]. Monocytes, as well as microparticles and platelets express tissue factor (TF). These TF remain silent in the absence of stimulation. However, upon exposure to various inflammatory stimuli, they become activated, interact with factor VII, and initiate the coagulation process[[112], [113], [114]]. There are diverse strategies for targeted thrombosis based on leukocytes. For example, Sun et al. developed a liposomal nanomedicine to achieve targeted ischemic lesions and stimulus-responsive drug release (Fig. 8A) [115]. In the system, a peptidylarginine deiminase 4 (PAD4) inhibitor, Cl-amidine, was encapsulated into a liposomal nanocarrier (named as C-Lipo/CA), which was modified with reactive oxygen species (ROS)-responsive polymers and fibrin-binding peptides. In vitro experiments have demonstrated its excellent thrombus targeting capability (Fig. 8B), as well as its promising therapeutic effect in stroke treatment (Fig. 8C). In a mouse model of middle cerebral artery occlusion reperfusion (MCAO), C-Lipo/CA was able to inhibit the formation of NETs, thereby inhibiting the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase-stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS-STING) pathway in ischemic brain tissue. In addition, Burnouf et al. developed a targeted thrombolytic technology inspired by phagocytes. This technology involves the phagocytosis of polypyrrole-polyethyleneimine nanocomplexes (Ppy-PEI NCs) by macrophages, which then functions at the thrombus site upon exposure to near-infrared radiation (NIR) [116]. Molecule-targeted thrombolytic strategies

In addition to the receptor molecules present on various cells, there are also some key molecules that are free in the plasma, which play an important role in thrombosis. Recent studies have investigated molecule-based thrombolysis strategies using vWF, collagen, factor XIIIa, reactive oxygen species (ROS), pH (i. e., H+), thrombin, and fibrin, which are discussed in the sections from 4.1 to 4.7.

vWF-targeted systems

vWF, a multimeric glycoprotein in plasma, is primarily synthesized by vascular endothelial cells and megakaryocytes. vWF is transcribed as a single-chain prepropolypetide of 2813 amino acids [69,70]. It binds to exposed subendothelial collagen, offering a thrombogenic platform for platelets via the interaction with GPIb on platelets at the site of damaged vessel [117]. At vascular injury sites, vWF multimers anchor to collagen fibrils via A3 domains, enabling platelet adhesion through GPIb-V-IX interaction with the vWF A1 domain. As a fundamental component of thrombus, some studies have proved that vWF-targeted systems could mediate the accumulation of nanoparticles to the thrombus. For example, Kawata et al. constructed a tPA-loaded nanoparticle composed of basic gelatin and zinc ions for thrombolysis (Fig. 9A) [118]. In order to evaluate targeting ability of nanoparticles in vivo, they use radiolabeled 125I to label tPA. Free 125I-labeled tPA or nanoparticles containing the same dose were injected into the tail vein of a mouse with thrombotic occlusion in the left femoral artery (FA). The ratio of radioactivity of the thrombi in the left FA to the control right FA was approximately 3-fold higher in the mice treated with the nanoparticles than that in the mice treated with free 125I-labeled tPA, indicating the nanoparticles have a higher affinity for thrombus than free tPA. It was found that tPA activity was reduced by approximately 50 % by the nanoparticle formulation, which could be fully recovered by ultrasound application in vitro. In vitro vWF binding assay revealed that the nanoparticles exhibited an equivalent binding capability to vWF as the antihuman vWF antibody, owing to the inclusion of basic gelatin and heat-denatured collagen, both of which possess a high affinity to vWF. Collagen-targeted systems

Collagen is located within the vascular wall and accounts for 40 % of the total protein in the wall. As an insoluble scaffold, collagen is crucial for tissue integrity and provides a surface for the attachment of other matrix components and the adhesion of vascular cells [120]. Due to its unique properties, it has been widely employed as a biomimetic material in biological engineering applications, particularly in the field of tissue engineering and biomaterials science[[121], [122], [123]]. After injury, collagens are exposed to blood flows, active platelets, and initiate hemostasis. Thus, the local administration of anti-collagen antibodies was discovered to apply to the site of vascular injury as an efficient tool for thrombus treatment [124]. While the collagen-targeted delivery systems are not widely used in the field of thrombolysis compared with other molecules, insights can be gained from their application in the treatment of atherosclerotic plaques. Atherosclerosis is pathologically defined by lipid deposition, chronic inflammatory responses, and collagen remodelling in the arterial wall [24,27,52]. Therefore, targeted therapeutic strategies aimed at collagen have shown promising potential in managing atherosclerosis. Translating these atherosclerosis strategies to thrombosis treatment, a potential role for collagen-targeted systems in thrombus formation and dissolution is envisioned. The formation of a thrombus often accompanies vascular wall injury and collagen exposure. Therefore, developing targeted drug delivery systems that focus on collagen could enable precise treatment of thrombi. By employing collagen-targeted nanocarriers, the concentration of antithrombotic drugs at the thrombus site can be enhanced, while the properties of collagen can be leveraged to facilitate drug penetration and release, thereby improving therapeutic efficacy. For example, Wang et al. developed a collagen-homing T-peptide (TLTYTWS) nano system with a matrix metalloproteinases-cleavable activatable cell penetrating peptide (ACPP) to selectively attach and break down collagen structures in plaque rupture, which might provide us with inspiration for the collagen-targeted delivery to thrombus [125].Moreover, drawing from the treatment experiences in atherosclerosis, we might consider combining collagen-targeted systems with other therapeutic agents to achieve a multi-targeted treatment approach. For example, a collagen-targeted delivery system that incorporates antiplatelet or anticoagulant drugs may exert synergistic effects at different stages of thrombus formation, leading to a more comprehensive treatment outcome. This multi-target strategy could enhance the effectiveness of the treatment while also mitigating drug resistance and side effects. In summary, although collagen-targeted strategies in thrombosis drug delivery are still in their infancy, future research can explore more innovative targeted treatment methods by leveraging successful experiences from atherosclerosis, providing new insights and solutions for thrombus treatment.

Factor XIIIa-targeted systems

Crosslinking of fibrin fibres is the crucial aspect of clot network stabilization, which is catalyzed by activated factor XIIIa (FXIIIa). After activation by the thrombin, factor XIII (FXIII) transfers to FXIIIa and forms covalent bonds between the α-chains and γ-chains of fibrin. This promotes crosslinking within protofibrils and stabilizes the fibrin network [126,127].Thus, as a crucial component of the fibrin network, FXIIIa could inspire the design of a factor XIIIa-targeted system for the treatment of thromboembolism. For example, Wange et al. designed a nanoparticle named IR780/FPHM/LK [128] (Fig. 11A). FPHM has emerged as a promising nanotheranostic agent for the treatment of obstructive thrombosis through the straightforward self-assembly of polycaprolactone-polyethylene glycol (PCL-PEG) and polycaprolactone-polyethyleneimine (PCL-PEI) in conjunction with FXIII-conjugated polymers. The IR780/PHM NPs were synthesized through the self-assembly of PCL-PEI and PCL-PEG via a solvent exchange method, yielding various formulations. A solution of PCL-PEI and IR780 was first prepared in THF, which was then gradually introduced into 20 mL of double-distilled water while stirring at room temperature. The organic solvents were subsequently removed by evaporating the mixture under vacuum at 37 °C. Following this, lumbrokinase (LK) was added dropwise to the IR780/PHM solution, and the mixture was gently vortexed before being incubated for 30 min to promote the formation of IR780/PHM/LK nanocomplexes. In a mouse model of carotid thrombosis induced by FeCl3, these nanoparticles demonstrated precise targeting of the thrombus, which is observed by multispectral optoacoustic tomography (MSOT) scanning to investigate the clot binding capability in vivo (Fig. 11B). ROS-targeted systems

In the thrombus formation process, the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) is intricately linked to changes in the local microenvironment. Initially, vascular injury or impaired blood flow results in a hypoxic environment, which subsequently promotes ROS production [129,130]. It is a well-established fact that ROS levels significantly increase at the site of thrombosis, which promotes inflammatory reactions and consequently leads to thrombus deterioration. There are two major ROS-responsive mechanisms for the commonly used ROS-responsive systems, namely ROS-induced non-cleavable hydrophobic–hydrophilic transition and ROS-induced structural cleavage [131]. On one hand, the oxidation of ROS-reactive ester bonds aids in scavenging ROS, thereby reducing ROS-induced cytotoxicity. On the other hand, at the site of thrombosis, ROS-reactive ester bonds can be oxidized by ROS, leading to the rupture of targeted nanoparticles and subsequent drug release at the thrombus site. Based on these characteristics, Zhang et al. designed a thioketones (TK) chemical bond linked target deliver system for thrombus delivery. As a ROS responsive linker, TK bond could be cut and release drugs at low level of H2O2 [132]. In addition, Zhao et al. developed a ROS-responsive platelet membrane-coated argatroban-loaded polymeric nanoparticle (PNP Arg) (Fig. 12A) [133]. The poly vanillyl alcohol-cooxalate (PVAX) is composed of vanillyl alcohol and oxalate ester bonds. The anticoagulant argatroban was encapsulated within PVAX nanoparticles. When ROS levels in the blood increase, the oxalate ester bonds are oxidized, leading to the release of anticoagulant from the carrier. In the in vivo mouse model, fluorescence imaging study showed a high targeting capability of PNP Arg (Fig. 12B). With injection of PNP Arg to the mouse, the H&E staining demonstrated excellent thrombolytic efficiency, which was evidenced by the less remained blood clot in the carotid arteries (Fig. 12C). Besides, Li et al. designed a biomimetic MnO2 nanoparticles loaded with fingolimod which possessed the ability to react with H2O2 to decompose into divalent manganese ions (Mn2+) while generating O2 [134](Fig. 13A). pH-targeted systems

Alterations in local pH (i.e., H+) to a more acidic level impact cellular metabolism and function, further exacerbating inflammatory responses. Due to the low oxygen content in the environment of the blood clot, the acidic substances accumulate within the clot, which results in a low pH environment. This characteristic of thrombosis provides possibilities for targeted drug release. Additionally, the therapeutics release at ischemia could effectively target an acidic environment. For example, Li et al. created a uPA-Oxd conjugate, in which uPA and Oxd are linked through a pH-sensitive bond (Fig. 14A) [135]. The entire conjugate is modified with RGD, which enables the substance not only to target the platelet GPIIb/IIIa receptor but also release in a low pH environment. Blood clots stained with RGD modified conjugates showed higher fluorescent intensity compared to the non-RGD formulations. Interestingly, the amount of FITC-uPA-Oxd-RGD binding to thrombus was apparently higher than that of FITC-uPA and FITC-uPA-Oxd owing to the specific binding of RGD to activated platelets in the thrombus (Fig. 14B). Thrombin-targeted systems

Thrombin plays a pivotal role in thrombosis by catalysing the conversion of fibrinogen into fibrin [136]. Due to the high expression of thrombin at the site of thrombosis, thrombin-sensitive systems have been developed to achieve targeted release specifically at the thrombus site. For example, in order to solve the concerns of the direct thrombin inhibitors deleterious side effects such as bleeding, Palekar et al. described an antithrombotic agent formed through the conjugation of direct thrombin inhibitors to the surface of unilamellar liposomes. This strategy uses a short chain peptide, d-phenylalanyl-l-prolyl-l-arginyl-chloromethyl ketone (PPACK), which inhibits thrombin through alkylation of a histidine residue at the active site [137]. Gunawan et al. reported a type of B-PEtOxAlk capsule loaded with uPA. This carrier targets the active form of the GPIIb/IIIa integrin receptor on the surface of activated platelets [138]. It can be cleaved by the serine protease thrombin, selectively triggering the release of the uPA for fibrin degradation (Fig. 15A and B). To investigate the targeting ability of GPIIb/IIIa, the unloaded and non-degradable capsules coated with scFv were fabricated. These capsules were divided into non-targeted (scFv -ve) and targeted (scFv + ve) groups, in which the scFv antibody was coupled with an azide group and conjugated to the B-PEtOxAlk capsule surface. Subsequently, the targeting performance of these capsules was verified using flow cytometry. Fibrin-targeted systems

Blood coagulation is thought as a series of amplifying enzymatic reactions, which finally lead to stable fibrin formation [139]. Human fibrinogen, circulating at concentrations of 2–4 g/L, is a 340 kDa glycoprotein composed of 3 pairs of polypeptide chains: Aα, Bβ, and γ [140]. Binding to integrin αIIbβ3 on activated platelets, fibrinogen turned into crosslinked fibrin fibres which can be stretched 2.5 times as their original length like spider silk [141,142]. Among the different fibrin-related ligands, the CREKA peptide (Cys-Arg-Glu-Lys-Ala) is a desirable targeting ligand and has a good targeting ability for overexpressed fibrin in different models [143]. For example, Chen et al. developed a nanoplatform which was prepared by attaching the liposomes with fibrin homing peptide CREKA (CREKA-Lipos) to stem cells (such as mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs)) membranes, named as CREKA-MSCs [144]. It was found that CREKA-MSCs had a better therapeutic effect than MSCs without fibrin-target ligands, indicating that CREKA modification enhanced the distribution and stem cell therapy in myocardial ischemia-reperfusion (MI/R) rat models.

Conclusions and future perspective

It is crucial to emphasize that the thrombus-targeted thrombolytic strategies discussed above do not act through a single cell type or molecule alone. In the management of thrombosis, conventional therapeutic strategies frequently focus on singular targets—for example, platelets or specific coagulation factors [1,3]. Thrombogenesis, however, is a complex process involving diverse cellular components and interconnected signalling cascades, which limits the effectiveness of single-target interventions. To overcome this limitation, multi-targeted therapeutic approaches have been developed, designed to act on several pathways concurrently. Such strategies can improve treatment efficacy while lowering the potential for resistance development. One illustrative method involves simultaneously targeting red blood cells (RBCs) and platelets; red blood cells not only affect hemodynamic properties but also promote platelet activation via cytokine signalling. By addressing multiple mechanisms, this approach holds potential for both improved clinical outcomes and greater personalization of treatment, adapting to individual variations in thrombosis pathophysiology. For instance, Xie et al. developed a dual RBC/platelet targeted drug delivery system using cationic chitosan (CS) grafted with zwitterionic 2-(N-oxide-N, N-diethylamino) ethylmethacrylate (ODE)-modified RGD peptide and anionic heparin (HS), encapsulating uPA (uPA-CS/HS@RGD-ODE) [145]. In this system, the ODE ligands bind to RBCs, reducing nonspecific protein deposition and extending uPA's half-life to 120 min. RGD ligands enhance targeting to activated platelets, while the CS matrix protonates in the acidic thrombotic environment to release uPA. This study presents a novel multi-targeted therapeutic approach for drug delivery that enhances thrombus tracking, prolongs uPA's half-life, and facilitates targeted release in thrombotic conditions. In addition to thrombolysis, hemostasis plays a crucial role in clinical diagnosis and treatment[146,147]. Hemostasis is a key physiological process that prevents excessive bleeding and involves the complex interplay of vascular constriction, platelet activation, and the coagulation cascade. Understanding the molecular mechanisms of thrombus formation is not only essential for thrombolytic therapy but also for effectively managing bleeding events. Firstly, the various stages of hemostasis, including vascular constriction, platelet aggregation, and coagulation formation, ensure that a stable blood clot can be rapidly formed at the site of vascular injury, thereby reducing blood loss. Secondly, there exists a delicate balance between hemostasis and thrombus formation; an excessive hemostatic response may lead to unnecessary thrombus formation, which can result in serious conditions such as deep vein thrombosis or myocardial infarction. Therefore, in-depth research into the mechanisms of hemostasis can provide important insights for developing therapeutic strategies targeting both bleeding and thrombotic disorders, helping clinicians find the optimal balance between controlling bleeding and preventing thrombus formation. Furthermore, understanding the hemostatic process also aids in the development of new therapeutic tools for rapid and effective hemostasis at bleeding sites[[148], [149], [150]]. To full elucidate the mechanisms of action for each thrombolytic and hemostatic system, a comprehensive understanding of the diverse roles played by various cells and molecules in thrombus formation is essential. This article begins by presenting an overview of the classical cellar and molecular mechanisms of thrombosis, which include platelet adhesion, activation, and aggregation, as well as the subsequent procoagulant and pro-inflammatory pathways. Additionally, the distinct functions of key cells and molecules involved in thrombus formation are detailed.

Moreover, this article highlights thrombus-targeted thrombolytic strategies associated with specific cells or molecules, demonstrating promising antithrombotic effects in preclinical in vitro and in vivo models. A summary of these thrombolytic strategies is presented in Table 2. However, several limitations still exist. For instance, many validations of thrombolytic efficacy are confined to in vitro static conditions and animal models, which do not effectively translate to human applications. This raises concerns regarding the clinical thrombolytic effectiveness of these strategies. Furthermore, the thrombus models employed are often singular and simplistic. Due to the considerable variability in thrombus composition across different anatomical sites, the dissolution of thrombi at a single location cannot adequately demonstrate the true thrombolytic efficacy of targeted systems. Besides preclinical limitations, the effective use of thrombus-targeting nanomaterials in clinical settings faces several significant obstacles, notably regulatory challenges, manufacturing limitations, and gaps in translation [[151], [152], [153]]. Regulatory bodies such as U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) enforce restrict approval processes that demand extensive data on safety and efficacy, including detailed evaluations of biocompatibility and toxicity. Furthermore, the intricate and costly nature of synthesizing these nanomaterials restricts their scalability, as conventional production techniques often fail to maintain the necessary consistency and purity. To overcome these barriers, it is essential for researchers to investigate useful manufacturing methods, such as microfluidics and 3D printing, alongside the development of standardized production protocols and sufficient quality control measures. Additionally, the existing gap between basic research and clinical implementation highlights the critical need for enhanced interdisciplinary collaboration among researchers, healthcare professionals, and industry partners. Such cooperation is vital to ensure that research findings effectively meet clinical demands. Consequently, we anticipate the development of more refined thrombolytic strategies that enable precise targeting of thrombi and better validation of materials across various thrombus components or formation sites. In the future, the integration of artificial intelligence (AI) technologies could play a pivotal role in the design of thrombus-targeting nanomaterials, potentially accelerating their clinical translation and improving their effectiveness. AI and machine learning can streamline computational drug design by predicting interactions between nanomaterials and biological targets, identifying candidates with optimal binding affinity to thrombus components. AI models facilitate the examination of extensive datasets, which assists in uncovering novel mechanistic insights and identifying promising drug targets, while also enhancing the efficiency of drug evaluation. This approach not only enables in silico assessments of compounds but also paves the way for subsequent synthesis and in vitro testing. For example, Rovenchak et al. employed deep learning autoencoders to explore the chemical space of known inhibitors to generate potential protein C inhibitor candidates [154]. Huang et al. utilized AI to design dabigatran derivatives, synthesizing three compounds that showed bioactivity similar to dabigatran [155]. Xie et al. used a computational biology algorithm to identify recurrent risk modules (RRMs) linked to high-risk molecular targets for recurrent VTE [156]. Moreover, AI technology has the capability to identify clots across different imaging modalities. For example, it can detect deep venous thrombosis (DVT) through computed tomography (CT) angiography, as well as DVT via ultrasound [157,158]. Furthermore, AI can facilitate the analysis of clinical trial data, uncovering patterns that inform trial designs and improve patient outcomes. By leveraging these technologies, the future of thrombus-targeting nanomaterials holds great promise for enhanced clinical application and patient care.
