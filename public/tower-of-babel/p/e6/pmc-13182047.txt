Chemoradiotherapy-induced Th17/IL-17 signaling correlates with therapeutic tolerance and early recurrence in cervical carcinoma
Authors: Fanglei Cao, Na Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the role of Th17 cells and IL-17 signaling in mediating therapeutic resistance and early recurrence in cervical carcinoma. Th17 cells were generated in vitro, and cervical carcinoma cells were subsequently exposed to cisplatin in the presence of Th17-conditioned media or recombinant IL-17. To elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms, synthetic siRNAs targeting Akt1 (si-Akt1) and Akt2 (si-Akt2) were employed. Gene expression levels were quantified using ABIVII7 qRT-PCR, and phosphorylation of AKT at Thr308 and Ser473 was assessed. Cervical carcinoma cell lines (HeLa and SW756) were cultured and subjected to siRNA-mediated knockdown of AKT1 and AKT2. Cytotoxicity assays were conducted to evaluate cell viability under various treatment conditions. Cells were stimulated with either standard medium or conditioned medium from in vitro–differentiated Th17 cells. Th17 cell–induced resistance to cisplatin and radiation co-treatment was found to be mediated via the AKT signaling pathway. Moreover, Th17 cells promoted chemoradiotherapy tolerance in cervical cancer cells, with a potential contribution from IL-17 signaling. Although chemoradiotherapy significantly decreased cell viability across all cell lines, preconditioning with recombinant IL-17 notably mitigated this effect, resulting in increased cellular survival. These findings suggest a critical role for Th17 cells in modulating the therapeutic response and recurrence risk in cervical carcinoma through AKT pathway activation.Keywords: Th17 cell, Cervical carcinoma, Chemoradiotherapy, AKT signaling

Introduction

Sustained exposure to high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV) infections significantly contributes to the development of cervical carcinoma, making it the fourth most common carcinoma among females worldwide [1]. The standard approach to managing individuals with cervical carcinoma involves phase-dependent therapeutic interventions, typically including adjuvant platinum-based synchronous chemoradiotherapy (CRT) alongside surgery for late-stage cervical carcinoma with prognostic risk factors for locally relapsed disease. Platinum-based chemotherapies are administered to patients with late-stage, invasive, or relapsed cervical carcinoma [2]. However, drug tolerance remains a significant clinical challenge in a proportion of cases.

According to oncological staging, 8% to 26% of patients with cervical carcinoma experience disease relapse, with recurrences most frequently occurring within the first two years following completion of primary therapies [3]. While CRT effectively targets neoplastic cells, it also compromises immune cell function [4]. Different T cell subpopulations exhibit different responses to diverse treatment regimens, displaying differential sensitivity or viability [5–6]. This may lead to the development of a therapy-induced microenvironment in the bloodstream and neoplastic tissues of patients, potentially influencing disease progression and prognostic outcomes.

Invasive cervical neoplasms are often associated with intense inflammatory infiltration within the matrix [7–10]. Numerous studies have shown that HPV-transformed keratinocytes play a crucial role in establishing an inflammatory microenvironment during cervical oncogenesis by producing interleukin cytokines [7, 9, 11]. This inflammatory milieu exhibits pro-inflammatory and pro-tumor profiles across various forms of cervical carcinoma [12, 13], with elevated levels observed in the circulation of cervical carcinoma patients [14]. Moreover, it infiltrates the tissues of cervical carcinoma patients [15] and correlates with adverse outcomes in patient prognosis [16].

Previous findings suggest that high-risk HPV initiates local Th2 inflammation in the early stages, involving antibody-forming cells and promoting an immunosuppressive microenvironment that facilitates neoplastic progression [8, 9]. Long-term Th2 inflammation often develops during persistent HPV infections and contributes to neoplastic advancement [17]. However, increasing evidence indicates that inflammatory CD4⁺ T-cell subsets, particularly IL-17–producing T helper (Th17) cells, play a central role in sustaining chronic inflammation, shaping the tumor microenvironment, and promoting resistance to anticancer therapies [12, 14–16]. Importantly, these inflammatory Th17 responses may coexist with or emerge alongside Th2-skewed immunity during disease progression, contributing to therapy resistance at later stages.

A recent study demonstrated that immunologic signatures could be utilized to predict the remission of CIN2-3 lesions [18]. Previous research has investigated the expression of peripheral cytokines at various stages of cervical disease and their relationship with the presence or absence of high-risk HPV. Patients with CIN III and cervical carcinoma exhibited significantly higher white blood cell counts compared to controls. Plasma concentrations of IFNγ were significantly lower in CIN III and carcinoma patients than in women with CIN II or controls. Plasma concentrations of IL-12, IL-2, IL-4, and TNFα did not differ significantly between groups. However, plasma concentrations of TNFRII were significantly lower in CIN III and carcinoma patients compared to those with CIN II. IL-10 was detected with increased frequency in the plasma of CIN III and cancer patients. These findings suggest a shift towards a Th2-type cytokine pattern in women with CIN III lesions during the carcinogenesis of cervical cancer [9].

The lymphocytes were screened for the production of FoxP3, a hallmark of regulatory T cells [19], and CD20, a marker of B cells [20], which is expressed in plasma cells in general. A hallmark predominantly produced by antigen-presenting cells includes dendritic cells (DCs) and B cells, which upon exposure, can down modulate the inflammatory response after uptake of immune complexes [21].

The environments of HPV-related neoplasms play a crucial role in oncogenesis, as demonstrated by multiple studies [21–25]. The presence of M1 and M2 macrophages has been strongly associated with clinical outcomes and lymph node metastasis [26, 27], contributing to an immunosuppressive and cancer-promoting environment [28]. The impact on leukocyte/lymphocyte subpopulations has garnered widespread interest, as treatment-induced alterations in the homeostatic profile of immune cells may disrupt antitumor activities and promote therapeutic resistance. Research has demonstrated an increase in immune-suppressive monocytes following CRT in the circulation of cervical carcinoma patients [29]. Th17 cells have been characterized as elongated effector cells with a high proliferative capacity and an anti-apoptotic phenotype in response to various cell death-inducing agents [30].

Mechanistically, IL-17 exerts its pro-tumorigenic effects through engagement of IL-17 receptors expressed on cervical carcinoma cells, leading to activation of downstream PI3K/AKT signaling pathways. Activation of AKT promotes tumor cell survival, anti-apoptotic signaling, and resistance to cytotoxic stress induced by chemotherapy and radiotherapy. This Th17/IL-17–AKT axis therefore represents a biologically plausible mechanism linking immune-mediated inflammation to therapeutic tolerance in cervical carcinoma [30].

Although Th2 and Th17 cells represent distinct CD4⁺ T-helper subsets, accumulating evidence indicates that IL-17–producing Th17 cells play a dominant role in shaping inflammatory tumor microenvironments and promoting therapeutic resistance. In the present study, we specifically investigated the contribution of Th17-derived IL-17 signaling to chemoradiotherapy tolerance in cervical carcinoma and its downstream activation of the AKT pathway.

Materials and methods

Production and cultivation of Th17 cells in vitro

Naïve CD4⁺ T cells were isolated by negative selection from freshly obtained peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) purchased from the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (Beijing, China) using the Human Naïve CD4⁺ T Cell Isolation Kit II (Miltenyi Biotech). For Th17 differentiation, naïve CD4⁺ T cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with anti-CD3/CD28 stimulation in the presence of transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β, 5 ng/mL), interleukin-6 (IL-6, 20 ng/mL), interleukin-1β (IL-1β, 10 ng/mL), and interleukin-23 (IL-23, 10 ng/mL), together with neutralizing antibodies against IFN-γ and IL-4, for 5–7 days, as previously described [22].

Th17 differentiation was confirmed by detection of IL-17 A secretion in culture supernatants using ELISA. Conditioned media (CM) from differentiated Th17 cultures were collected and used for subsequent experiments.

Cell culture

SiHa, CaSki, and SW756 cell lines were also obtained from the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (Beijing, China). For cytotoxicity assays, culture medium at a dilution of 1:20 with 100 ng/mL IL-17 A or Th17 cells was supplemented, stimulating 5 × 103 cells per well in 96-well dishes for 24 h, or by using the culture buffer as the controls. Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All experiments were performed in at least three independent biological replicates, and data represent averaged values. The Th17-to–cervical cancer cell ratio (1:20) and the IL-17 concentration (100 ng/mL) were selected based on preliminary optimization experiments and previously published studies demonstrating biologically relevant IL-17 signaling in tumor cells. Neutralizing anti-IL-17 antibody at a concentration of 1 µg/mL, or its corresponding control antibody, was added to the CM 2 h prior to administration. For chemotherapeutic trials, cells were exposed to sequential concentrations of the chemotherapy agent cisplatin overnight. For CRT trials, cells were treated with cisplatin at a concentration of 1.56 µg/mL for 2 h as a radiosensitizer. Cells were irradiated with a single dose of 6 Gy using a calibrated X-ray irradiator at room temperature. For all experiments, cells treated with culture medium alone or control conditioned medium served as negative controls, and negative-control siRNA was used in all gene-silencing experiments. All cell lines were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination using PCR-based assays and were confirmed to be mycoplasma-negative. Cell line authentication was verified by the supplier prior to distribution.

siRNA transfections

We elucidated the molecular mechanisms using synthetic small interfering RNA (siRNA) specific for Akt1 (si-Akt1) and Akt2 (si-Akt2), procured from GenePharma (Shanghai, China). Cells were seeded at a density of 3.0 × 105 cells in 6-well culture dishes and silenced using Lipofectamine RNAi Max (Shanghai, China) with either 10nM si-Akt1 or 10nM si-Akt2. The sequences of the synthetic siRNAs were as follows:

siAkt1: 5′-GAG CGG GAG GAG UGG ACA ATT-3′ (sense) and 5′-UUG UCC ACU CCU CCC GCU CTT-3′ (antisense).

siAkt1: 5′-GAG CGG GAG GAG UGG ACA ATT-3′ (sense) and 5′-UUG UCC ACU CCU CCC GCU CTT-3′ (antisense).

si-Akt2: 5′-GAT CCG ATA GCC CGC ATC CAC TCT TCC CTC TCA TTC ATG AGA GGG AAG AGT GCT TGC GGG CTA TCT TTT TTG GAAA-3′ (sense) and 5′-AGC TTT TCC AAA AAA GAT AGC CCG CAA GCA CTC TTC CCT CTC ATG AAT GAG AGG GAA GAG TGG ATG CGG GCT ATCG-3′ (antisense).

si-Akt2: 5′-GAT CCG ATA GCC CGC ATC CAC TCT TCC CTC TCA TTC ATG AGA GGG AAG AGT GCT TGC GGG CTA TCT TTT TTG GAAA-3′ (sense) and 5′-AGC TTT TCC AAA AAA GAT AGC CCG CAA GCA CTC TTC CCT CTC ATG AAT GAG AGG GAA GAG TGG ATG CGG GCT ATCG-3′ (antisense).

A negative control (NC) sequence was used: 5′-GGA ACC CTG TTT CCG TGA AGA-3′ (sense) and 5′-TTC TCC GAA CGT GTC ACG T-3′ (antisense). Validated knockdown was confirmed by quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR). Following transfection, the cells were stimulated and utilized for cytotoxicity assays as described above.

qRT-PCR

The protocol and primer designs for the current assays were followed as previously described [8]. Twenty-four hours after plasmid transfection, total RNA was extracted from breast cancer tissue or cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitro-gen company), and further purified with RNaseR reagent (Epicentre company). Subsequently, total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the Prime Script RT Reagent Kit (Takara company). The relative expression levels of RNA were determined using ABIVII7 qRT-PCR (Bio-Rad company). Specific primer sequences used in this study were as previously published by Wei et al., with GAPDH serving as the internal reference gene. The relative expression levels of RNA were analyzed using the 2-Δ Ct method.

ELISA assays of pThr308- and pSer473- AKT

Phospho-Akt (pSer473) and pThr308-Akt ELISA kits (purchased from ThermoFisher) were utilized to measure the levels of pThr308- and pSer473-AKT in cells. Cells were inoculated at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well overnight in a 96-well plate. All reagents and samples were prepared according to the instructions provided in the manufacturer’s manual.

Statistics

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 24.0. All experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates (n ≥ 3). Data are presented as mean ± SD. Comparisons between two groups were performed using Student’s t-test. Comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison post-hoc test. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Th17 cells induce tolerance to cisplatin and co-culture in cervical carcinoma cells in relation to IL-17

We investigated whether Th17 cells influence the response of cervical carcinoma cells to chemotherapeutic drugs or irradiation, given their infiltration of cervical carcinoma tissues and their association with progressive disease [8, 22]. Initially, we examined the effect of cisplatin, the most frequently administered agent in cervical chemotherapy. HeLa cells were co-cultured with in vitro-produced Th17 cells at a ratio of 1:20 overnight. Following removal of Th17 cells, cervical carcinoma cells were exposed to sequential concentrations of cisplatin. The co-culture significantly reduced the sensitivity of HeLa cells to cisplatin-induced apoptosis (Fig. 1A), leading to improved cell viability. Consistent responses were examined across all cervical carcinoma cell lines, with pre-stimulation of SiHa and SW756 cells with rhIL-17 resulting in marked enhancement of cell viability following cisplatin treatment, particularly at the highest dose of cisplatin (6.25 µg/mL) (Fig. 1B). HeLa, SiHa, and SW756 cell lines represent cervical carcinoma models with distinct HPV backgrounds, enabling evaluation of IL-17–mediated effects across heterogeneous cellular contexts. CaSki cells were initially included; however, repeated experiments demonstrated high variability in baseline growth and inconsistent responses to cisplatin and irradiation, preventing reliable interpretation. Therefore, CaSki cells were excluded from further mechanistic analyses.

(A) Cell viability was assessed after co-culturing HeLa and Th17 cells followed by cisplatin treatment for 24 h; (B) Cell viability was evaluated after pre-stimulating SiHa and SW756 cells with rhIL-17 at the highest dose of 6.25 µg/ml of cisplatin. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01

Th17 cells induce tolerance to irradiation and cisplatin in cervical carcinoma cells

Consistent results were obtained in exposure experiments using a dose of 6 Gy for 48 h, which maximized cell mortality sensitization in earlier assays (Fig. 2A). Treatment with 6 Gy reduced cell survival among all three tested cell lines, while pre-stimulation with rhIL-17 significantly enhanced cell survival following irradiation (Fig. 2B). As concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT) is the gold standard for the management of cervical carcinoma > FIGO IIB [2], we performed a combination of cisplatin administration and irradiation to mimic CRT in vitro. CRT significantly decreased cellular viabilities in all three cell lines (Fig. 2C). Moreover, preconditioning with recombinant IL-17 markedly reduced cellular reactivity to CRT, resulting in enhanced cell viability.

Th17 cells induce tolerance in an IL-17 dependent manner

We investigated whether Th17 cells influenced the responsiveness of cervical carcinoma cells to chemotherapeutic drugs or irradiation. Initially, we tested cisplatin, the most frequently prescribed candidate in cervical carcinoma chemotherapy. HeLa cells were co-cultured with in vitro Th17 cells at a 1:20 ratio for 24 h, followed by removal of Th17 cells and treatment with cisplatin. Co-culture significantly attenuated the sensitivity of HeLa cells to cisplatin-induced cell death, leading to improved cell survival (Fig. 3A).

(A) HeLa cells were co-cultured with in vitro Th17 cells (1:20); (B) HeLa (left), SiHa (middle), and SW756 cells (right) were treated with medium (darker bars) or rhIL-17 (lighter bars); (C) HeLa (left), SiHa (middle), and SW756 cells (right) were stimulated with 6 Gy or rhIL-17; (D) Cells were treated with cisplatin for 2 h and irradiated with 6 Gy; (E) SiHa and SW756 cells were subjected to stimulation with culture medium or CM of Th17 cells produced in vitro. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01

It is well-documented that cisplatin therapy leads to reduced cell viability in single cell lines SiHa, HeLa, or SW756. Subsequently, we observed uniform responses across all tested cancer cell lines. Pre-stimulation of SiHa, HeLa, and SW756 cells with rhIL-17 A resulted in a marked increase in cell viability at the highest application of cisplatin (6.25 µg/mL) (Fig. 3B). Similar findings were observed in irradiation assays using a dose of 6 Gy, which resulted in the highest sensitization to cell death in previous experiments (Fig. 2). Treatment with 6 Gy reduced the viability of all three tested cell lines (Fig. 3C; dotted bars), while pre-stimulation with rhIL-17 (red bars) dramatically enhanced the post-irradiation increase in cell viability.

Chemo-irradiation administration significantly further decreased cell viability in all three cell lines (Fig. 3D, striped bars). Moreover, preconditioning with recombinant IL-17 (blue bars) markedly diminished cell reactivity to CRT, resulting in greater cell viability. Additionally, pre-stimulation of SiHa and SW756 cells with CM (pink bars) decreased reactivity to CRT-induced cell death and increased cell viability (Fig. 3E). Neutralization of Th17 cells with IL-17 in CM completely abolished Th17-induced resistance (Fig. 3E, brown bars). Overall, our results indicate that IL-17 plays a crucial role in Th17 cell-mediated chemo-resistance of cervical cancer cells.

The expression of AKT in cervical cancer cells

To demonstrate the activation of the AKT signaling pathway, we examined the expression of pThr308- and pSer473-AKT by stimulating SiHa and HeLa cells with CM derived from Th17 cells in vitro (Fig. 4A and B). Stimulation resulted in a significant elevation of pThr308- and pSer473-AKT expression in both SiHa and HeLa cell lines tested. AKT comprises two main isoforms, AKT1 and AKT2 (Fig. 4C).

The expression of pThr308- and pSer473-AKT in (A) SiHa and (B) HeLa cells; (C) Knockdown of AKT1 and AKT2 in cells. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01

Th17-induced tolerance of cervical tumors is AKT-dependent

Both AKT1 and AKT2 knockdown markedly reduced IL-17 or CM cell dying tolerance in both cell lines. Under normal conditions, the growth of SiHa and HeLa cells could be inhibited by treatment with cisplatin and 6 Gy (Control vs. Cisplatin+6 Gy, **P < 0.01). A complete reversal was achieved following concomitant elimination of AKT1 and AKT2. While chemotherapy only marginally decreased cell viability, preconditioning with rhIL-17 and CM of Th17 cells fully restored their viability (Fig. 5). These results provide evidence that Th17-induced resistance to combined therapy is determined by the AKT pathways.

Discussion

Th17 cells, characterized by IL-17 expression, infiltrate cervical carcinoma tissues and are associated with advanced neoplastic stages and disease progression [23–27]. Understanding the role of Th17 cells in the stage-dependent management of cervical carcinoma is crucial. Our findings highlight the critical function of Th17-induced AKT signaling in conferring resistance to CRT in cervical cancer cells. The significant correlation between Th17 cells and activated AKT could have important implications for personalized treatment strategies, particularly by targeting the Th17 signaling pathways or AKT activation directly. Specifically, therapies that inhibit Th17 differentiation or function could mitigate their contribution to chemoresistance in cervical carcinoma. Furthermore, targeting the AKT pathway, either through small molecule inhibitors or monoclonal antibodies, could restore the sensitivity of cancer cells to chemoradiotherapy, improving treatment efficacy. This approach could be particularly beneficial for patients whose tumors are characterized by Th17 cell infiltration and AKT pathway activation, offering a more tailored and effective treatment regimen. Despite differences in HPV status among cervical cancer cell lines, IL-17–mediated AKT activation produced consistent trends in therapeutic tolerance, suggesting that this mechanism is not restricted to a single HPV subtype.

In contrast to other tumor types, IL-17-producing carcinoma tissues are primarily detected in the stroma [28–31]. We discovered that Th17-induced AKT signaling serves as a responsive mechanism of resistance. Specifically, we observed a decline in activated STAT3 in patient situ bio-sections as carcinoma progressed from invasive to infiltrative stages, with low or absent expression of pSTAT3 (Tyr705) [32, 33]. Complete catalytic activities require phosphorylation of threonine and serine residues [34]. Knockdown of AKT1 and AKT2 fully reversed Th17-induced resistance in SiHa and HeLa cells, indicating that the presence of predominantly expressed heterodimers AKT1 and AKT2 is sufficient for Th17 resistance [35]. PIK3CA mutations in cervical carcinoma patients lead to aberrantly activated AKT, which is associated with worsened post-chemotherapy prognosis [36]. In our study, all cervical cancer cells demonstrated maximal resistance to therapy under the influence of Th17 cells. Induction of AKT signaling by neoplastic carcinoma has been implicated in other malignancies [37], making the PI3K/AKT pathway an attractive target for clinical studies [38]. Th17-induced AKT signaling not only contributes to resistance to chemotherapy and radiotherapy but also may play a pivotal role in immune evasion and tumor progression [33]. By activating key survival and proliferation pathways in cervical carcinoma cells, Th17 cells may enhance tumor growth and metastasis [33]. The activation of AKT signaling could lead to immune suppression within the tumor microenvironment, further facilitating immune evasion and limiting the effectiveness of immune-based therapies [33]. This suggests that targeting Th17-mediated AKT signaling may hold therapeutic potential not only for overcoming chemoresistance but also for modulating immune responses in cervical cancer.

However, this study has some limitations. Although the observed changes in cell viability were modest, they were consistent and reproducible across multiple cervical cancer cell lines and independent experimental replicates, supporting a biologically relevant effect of IL-17–mediated signaling on therapeutic tolerance. Firstly, the small sample size limits the generalizability of our findings.

Conclusion

Our research has uncovered a novel role for Th17 cells in the management of cervical carcinoma. We have demonstrated that Th17 cells mediate AKT-dependent cancer cell resistance during CRT. These findings shed light on drug tolerance and personalized therapeutic responsiveness following CRT in cervical cancer patients. Moreover, they support the concept of a Th17-based immunotherapeutic regimen in cancer management. Given the observed Th17-induced resistance, future studies should explore targeted strategies to modulate Th17 activity and enhance the efficacy of CRT. Integrating immune-based interventions alongside conventional therapies may provide a promising approach to overcoming therapeutic resistance and reducing early recurrence in cervical carcinoma. Ultimately, this work paves the way for the development of novel therapeutic strategies that combine immunotherapy with chemoradiation, with the potential to improve patient outcomes and reduce relapse rates in cervical carcinoma.
