Outcome of cholecystectomy in octogenarian with concurrent cholecystitis and cholangitis receiving percutaneous cholecystostomy and subsequent interventive endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography
Authors: Chih-Yu Li, Yu-Ling Pan, Pei-Shan Wu, Hsin-Hua Lai, Wen-Liang Fang, Liang-Yu Chen, Jiing-Chyuan Luo, Kuei-Chuan Lee, Ming-Chih Hou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute cholecystitis (AC) with concurrent cholangitis is a life-threatening condition in elderly patients. We aimed to investigate the outcome of cholecystectomy in patients aged ≥ 80 years with moderate to severe AC who underwent percutaneous cholecystostomy and subsequent interventional endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP).MethodsBetween January 2008 and February 2021, we retrospectively enrolled 174 patients who underwent percutaneous cholecystostomy and subsequent ERCP at Taipei Veterans General Hospital. The patients were divided into cholecystectomy and non-cholecystectomy groups after discharge. Clinical outcomes including overall survival rate, recurrence rate of biliary-related events, and complications of ERCP and cholecystectomy, were analyzed. The Kaplan–Meier model was used to interpret the overall survival and cumulative recurrence rates.ResultsThere were 34 patients receiving cholecystectomy (cholecystectomy group), and 98 patients received conservative treatment (non-cholecystectomy group). The overall mortality rate and biliary-related mortality did not differ between the cholecystectomy and non-cholecystectomy groups (20.5% vs. 35.7%, p = 0.082; and 0% vs. 3%, p = 0.083, respectively). The 1-year recurrent rate, 3-year recurrent rate and 5-year recurrence rates of biliary-related events were significantly lower in the cholecystectomy group than in the non-cholecystectomy group (2.9% vs. 18.4%, p = 0.002; 5.9% vs. 20.4%, p = 0.014; 8.8% vs. 24.5%, p = 0.02, respectively). There was no significant difference in the 1-year recurrence rates of cholangitis (2,9% vs. 6.1%, p = 0.407), and pancreatitis (0% vs. 1.0%, p = 0.320). The overall ERCP-related complication rate in the elderly population was low (4%). The overall rate of surgical complications was 11.8% in the cholecystectomy group.ConclusionsCholecystectomy lowers the recurrence rate of biliary-related events in octogenarians with moderate-to-severe cholecystitis and concurrent cholangitis after percutaneous cholecystostomy and subsequent interventional ERCP.

Background

Acute cholecystitis (AC) concurrent with cholangitis is a life-threatening disease, especially in patients aged ≥ 80 years old. The 2018 Tokyo guidelines interpreted the principles of management for both acute cholecystitis and cholangitis [1]. Laparoscopic cholecystectomy (LC) has been considered the gold standard of treatment for cholelithiasis [2, 3]. Approximately, 10% of patients with symptomatic gallstones [4] have concurrent common bile duct stones, and endoscopic retrograde cholangio-pancreatography (ERCP) is the prior treatment of choice before receiving cholecystectomy [5]. However, only a small number of octogenarians undergo cholecystectomy after ERCP due to the high risk of anaesthesia associated with multimorbidities. In such high-risk surgical populations, percutaneous cholecystostomy tube drainage has played a vital role in the past few decades [6]. According to a previous study, early percutaneous cholecystostomy reduced hospital stay and procedure-related bleeding without increasing the mortality rate [7]. Although percutaneous cholecystostomy mostly serves as a bridge to surgical treatment, there is little evidence or consensus on whether elderly patients require additional cholecystectomy [8, 9]. Our previous study indicated that early cholecystectomy (≤ 6 weeks after cholecystostomy) had a lower recurrence rate of AC than delayed cholecystectomy (> 6 weeks after cholecystostomy) among octogenarians with AC receiving cholecystostomy [10]. However, the decision to perform cholecystectomy in octogenarians remains controversial. In clinical practice, we could still observe patients with age ≥ 80 years old suffering from recurrent AC and choledocholithiasis, especially those who did not receive cholecystectomy after ERCP and percutaneous cholecystostomy. The proportion of patients who did not undergo additional cholecystectomy varied between studies, ranging from 43 to 94%, as did the outcomes [11, 12].

This study aimed to investigate the outcome of cholecystectomy among patients aged ≥ 80 years old with concurrent moderate-to-severe acute cholecystitis and cholangitis after receiving percutaneous cholecystostomy and subsequent ERCP.

Methods

Study design and patient selection

Hospitalized patients aged ≥ 80 years who underwent percutaneous cholecystostomy and subsequently underwent successful ERCP for concurrent AC and cholangitis at Taipei Veterans General Hospital between January 2008 and February 2021 were included. Percutaneous cholecystostomy was performed as an alternative to cholecystectomy in these patients because of the high risk associated with anaesthesia. ERCP was subsequently performed for concurrent acute cholangitis with suspicion of common bile duct stones. Patients were excluded if they had a history of malignancy, were diagnosed with cancer-related obstructive jaundice, failed large CBD stone removal via ERCP, failed ampulla vatar approach, underwent interventional ERCP before percutaneous cholecystostomy, underwent cholecystectomy before ERCP, or were followed up for less than 30 days. All patients were followed for five years or until death. Patients who underwent percutaneous cholecystostomy followed by interventional ERCP were categorized into cholecystectomy or non-cholecystectomy groups. The study design is illustrated in Fig. 1. Definition

Concurrent acute cholecystitis (AC) and acute cholangitis were diagnosed based on clinical criteria, including Charcot’s triad and characteristic symptoms (right-upper-quadrant abdominal pain, nausea or vomiting, fever, and jaundice in cholangitis), physical examination (positive Murphy’s sign), laboratory findings, and imaging studies (abdominal ultrasound or computed tomography) [13, 14]. AC severity was graded according to the Tokyo guidelines and classified as mild (grade I), moderate (grade II), or severe (grade III) [15]. Percutaneous cholecystostomy was performed in patients diagnosed with moderate to severe AC who presented with severe sepsis, septic shock, suspected gallbladder perforation, or inadequate clinical improvement after 48 h of medical therapy. ERCP was performed following confirmation of choledocholithiasis complicated by obstructive jaundice. The patients who underwent interventional ERCP underwent either endoscopic papillary balloon dilation or endoscopic sphincterotomy.

After the resolution of concurrent AC and acute cholangitis following percutaneous cholecystostomy and interventional ERCP, the preoperative anaesthetic risks were evaluated for all patients using the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification system. [16].

The interval from percutaneous cholecystostomy to tube removal was defined as the duration between the date of gallbladder pigtail catheter insertion and the date of catheter removal. Decisions regarding catheter removal were made by the attending physicians based on the patients’ clinical status. The interval from percutaneous cholecystostomy to ERCP was defined as the duration between gallbladder pigtail catheter insertion and the performance of ERCP. For patients undergoing surgery, the interval between ERCP and cholecystectomy was defined as the duration between the date of ERCP performance and the date of cholecystectomy surgery.

The ERCP-related complications included post-ERCP pancreatitis, bleeding, and bowel perforation. The perioperative complications included bile leakage, biliary sepsis, postoperative ileus, and wound infection. Both procedure and operation-related mortalities during the same hospitalization period were defined as in-hospital mortality. Biliary-related mortality was defined as death due to serious episodes of AC, cholangitis, or pancreatitis.

Biliary-related events included AC, cholangitis, and pancreatitis. In contrast, biliary tract infections defined as acute cholecystitis, acute cholangitis or both. Recurrent biliary-related events were defined as new episodes that occurred after cessation of therapy and discharge. Previous biliary-related events were defined as biliary-related events that occurred before enrollment.

Data collection

Medical records were reviewed for data collection, which included age, sex, underlying diseases, ASA scores, abdominal computed tomography or sonography findings, procedure or operation-related complications, recurrence of biliary-related events, and mortality.

Percutaneous cholecystostomy

The condition of the gallbladder was initially assessed using abdominal ultrasonography before injecting a local anaesthetic (2% xylocaine). Cholangiography was performed by inserting a 22-gauge needle through the right intercostal approach under ultrasound and fluoroscopic guidance. Subsequently, an 8–10-French pigtail catheter (Bioteque Corp., Ilan, Taiwan) was inserted into the gallbladder. The position of the catheter was rechecked using ultrasound, as previously described [10, 17].

ERCP

All ERCP procedures were performed by experienced endoscopists (> 500 cases) at Taipei Veterans General Hospital. Olympus JF-260 V and TJF-260 V side-view duodenoscopes were used. The procedure was performed under conscious sedation using midazolam and fentanyl. After CBD stone extraction, the bile duct was routinely irrigated with approximately 100 mL of saline to ensure that no residual stones remained [18–20].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 24.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to assess continuous variables, whereas the chi-squared test was used to analyze categorical data. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. The multivariable Cox regression was used to adjust the confounding. Variables with p < 0.2 in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariable model [21]. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. The Kaplan–Meier model was used to interpret overall survival and cumulative recurrence rates.

Results

This study included 132 octogenarians who were diagnosed with concurrent moderate-to-severe AC and cholangitis. The average follow-up period was 44.1 ± 37.2 months. The patients had a mean age of 85.7 ± 4.0 years, with a predominance of males (68.9%). Among the participants, only 34 underwent cholecystectomy after AC resolution, whereas the remaining 98 were managed conservatively without subsequent surgery.

Patients who underwent cholecystectomy had significantly lower rates of recurrent biliary-related events (Fig. 3). The overall recurrence rate was lower in the cholecystectomy group (8.8% vs. 26.5%, p = 0.009), with similar trends at 1 year (2.9% vs. 18.4%, p = 0.002), 3 years (5.9% vs. 20.4%, p = 0.014), and 5 years (8.8% vs. 24.5%, p = 0.02) (Table 2). Notably, patients in the non-cholecystectomy group experienced a high rate of recurrent AC following initial resolution, with recurrence rates of 17.3%, 20.4%, and 23.5% at 1, 3, and 5 years, respectively. Nevertheless, the 1-year recurrence rates of acute cholangitis and acute pancreatitis did not differ between the groups (2.9% vs. 6.1%, p = 0.407, and 0.0% vs. 1.0%, p = 0.320, respectively). For recurrent acute cholecystitis, we had observed 16 cases of Grade I (16.3%), 3 cases of Grade II (3.1%), and 4 cases of Grade III (4.1%) in the non-cholecystectomy group (Table 2). The grade II recurrent cholangitis was significantly lower in the cholecystectomy group (0% vs. 5.1%, p = 0.025), but there were no difference in grade I and grade III recurrent cholangitis (8.8% vs. 6.1%, p = 0.594, and 0% vs. 1.0%, p = 0.558, respectively). We only observed mild degree recurrent pancreatitis with no significant difference between groups (2.9% vs. 2.0%, p = 0.764).

The overall complication rate of ERCP was 4.1%, with specific complications including bleeding (1.0%), hollow organ perforation (1.0%), and post-ERCP pancreatitis (2.0%). The overall complication rate of cholecystectomy was 11.8%, including two (5.9%) cases of severe biliary sepsis, one (2.9%) case of wound infection, and one (2.9%) case of post-operative ileus. Among the 34 patients in the cholecystectomy group, 18 underwent laparoscopic cholecystectomy and 16 underwent open cholecystectomy. Notably, 4 patients initially scheduled for laparoscopic surgery required conversion to open cholecystectomy, corresponding to a conversion rate of 22.2% (4/18). Two (5.9%) patients had postoperative infection, but none of them died during hospitalization. One patient died postoperatively after laparoscopic cholecystectomy; however, the cause was attributed to multiple chronic comorbidities rather than biliary-related events. The operation-related complications and post-operation overall biliary-related recurrent rates did not differ among the types of surgery (p = 0.263 and p = 0.491). The average hospital stay during the cholecystectomy was 9.6 ± 12.4 days. In contrast, in-hospital mortality of biliary-related events occurred in two patients (2.04%) in the non-cholecystectomy group, both of whom died during the same admission after undergoing percutaneous cholecystostomy and interventional ERCP.

In Taipei Veterans General Hospital, activities of daily living (ADL) were categorized into three levels: Grade 1 (independent). Grade 2 (partial dependent), and grade 3 (total dependent). The mean ADL grade before cholecystectomy was 0.8 ± 0.7, and the mean ADL grade at discharge was 0.8 ± 0.6. The average pain score, ranging from 1 to 10, before cholecystectomy was 2.8 ± 2.0 points, while the average pain score on postoperative day 3 had decreased to 2.0 ± 1.5 points.

Discussion

In this study, patients with concurrent acute cholecystitis and cholangitis receiving percutaneous cholecystostomy and subsequent ERCP still benefit from the cholecystectomy, even for patients aged ≥ 80 years. The patients who underwent cholecystectomy had lower rates of recurrent biliary-related events than those who did not undergo cholecystectomy throughout the follow-up period, indicating a lower frequency of life-threatening episodes of biliary tract infections and hospital readmissions.

Previous studies have reported high recurrence rates after the resolution of AC following percutaneous cholecystostomy in patients aged ≥ 70 years old, 22–42% during the 2.2–5 years follow-up duration [22–26]. Consistently, in our study, patients aged ≥ 80 years experienced a progressively increasing cumulative incidence of recurrent AC, reaching 23.5% over a five-year follow-up period after initial resolution. Notably, patients who underwent cholecystectomy had lower overall recurrence rates of biliary-related events during the five-year follow-up compared to those who did not; however, this benefit was specific to recurrent AC, with no significant difference in the recurrence rates of other types of biliary-related events. The 1-year recurrence rate of acute cholangitis was slightly higher in the non-cholecystectomy group than that in the cholecystectomy group; however, this difference was not statistically significant (2,9% vs. 6.1%, p = 0.407). Similarly, the 1-year recurrence rate of acute pancreatitis remained low in both groups (0.0% vs. 1.0%, p = 0.320), with comparable findings at 3 and 5 years for cholangitis and pancreatitis. These findings suggest that cholecystectomy primarily serves to prevent recurrent acute cholecystitis, but does not significantly reduce the recurrence of acute cholangitis or pancreatitis. However, the overall low recurrence rates of cholangitis and pancreatitis in both groups may be attributable to the endoscopic papillary balloon dilation and endoscopic papillotomy performed during ERCP.

Although the overall mortality and biliary-related mortality rates were similar between patients who underwent cholecystectomy and those who did not, it is noteworthy that 12% (3/25) of the deceased patients in the non-cholecystectomy group died from biliary-related events within the five-year follow-up period. This finding highlights the importance of preventing recurrent acute cholecystitis to improve long-term survival. Based on these results, we suggest that octogenarians with stable cardiopulmonary function may still benefit from cholecystectomy after percutaneous cholecystostomy and interventional ERCP.

The preventive effect of cholecystectomy was mainly limited to recurrent acute cholecystitis, while no significant reduction was observed in the recurrence of acute cholangitis or acute pancreatitis. As shown in Table 2, patients who did not undergo cholecystectomy had a cumulative incidence of recurrent acute cholecystitis as high as 23.5% over the five-year follow-up period, a risk that can be completely eliminated through definitive cholecystectomy. In contrast, there was no statistically significant difference in the recurrence rates of acute cholangitis and pancreatitis between the two groups. This can be explained by the fact that most patients underwent endoscopic sphincterotomy or balloon dilation during ERCP, which effectively reduced the risk of recurrent cholangitis and pancreatitis regardless of subsequent cholecystectomy. Thus, the principal benefit of surgery in this elderly population lies in preventing recurrent acute cholecystitis.

The surgical risk was not as high as expected. In the general population, the operation-related morbidity and mortality rates for cholecystectomy were 1.6–5.3% and 0.08–0.7%, respectively [27–29]. Our operation-related morbidity rate (11.8%) was slightly higher than that reported in a previous study, but the mortality rate (0%) was non-inferior. Surgical risk tended to be an individual factor, owing to the small number of morbidities (4/34). ERCP is also a relatively safe treatment in patients with concurrent advanced cholecystitis and cholangitis because of its low complication rate (4.1%) and mortality rate (0.0%). Our ERCP-related complication rate was similar to those reported in previous studies [30–32], suggesting a non-inferior procedure-related risk in our study. In addition, the two patients who died during the same hospitalization after receiving percutaneous cholecystostomy and interventional ERCP was caused by critical conditions rather than from treatment-related complications.

Our study focused on more serious and complicated clinical situations, such as concurrent choledocholithiasis, and enrolled a larger number of patients than the previous studies. With the development of technology and the formation of an aging society, we will encounter more patients over 80 years of age, along with clinical challenges and dilemmas, such as the conditions in this study. Further investigations of this dilemma among the elderly population, especially octogenarians, are needed.

A limitation of this retrospective observational study lies in its inherent clinical selection bias. The decision to undergo cholecystectomy was influenced by both physician judgment and patient or family preference. As shown in Table 1, patients in the non-cholecystectomy group were older and had a higher prevalence of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and coronary artery disease. This baseline heterogeneity was not a coincidence but the result of careful clinical decision-making. These patients were preferentially chosen for conservative management because their anesthesia and surgical risks were considered higher. In order to more accurately assess the treatment effect, we performed a multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analysis. As shown in Table 3, the difference in recurrence risk between groups was primarily driven by cholecystectomy, rather than the baseline age or comorbidities. We had considered using propensity score matching to control selection bias. However, the use of propensity score matching would substantially reduce the available sample size for analysis. Even though we collected data over a 13-year period, there were only 34 octogenarians who underwent cholecystectomy. Further reduction in sample size due to propensity score matching would lead to insufficient statistical power for subsequent data analysis. Furthermore, the over-idealized sample derived from matching may not accurately represent the real-world clinical population, which potentially reducing the generalizability of our findings. Additionally, propensity score matching is limited in its ability to account for time-dependent biases, which could lead to results that differ from clinical reality. Therefore, we chose to utilize the Cox proportional hazards model to mitigate selection bias. Despite this adjustment, potential physician- or patient-related bias may still have contributed to the observed differences in outcomes between the two groups, and residual confounding cannot be fully excluded. This limitation highlights the need for larger, potentially multicenter trials in the future to more effectively control for such confounding variables.

In conclusion, cholecystectomy can reduce the recurrence rate of acute cholecystitis without increasing the overall mortality rate in octogenarians with concurrent moderate-to-severe AC and cholangitis. Thus, cholecystectomy is recommended for patients aged ≥ 80 years old with concurrent AC and cholangitis receiving percutaneous cholecystostomy and subsequent ERCP.
