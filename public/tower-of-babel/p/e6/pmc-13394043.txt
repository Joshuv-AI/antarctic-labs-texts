KYNU in Gastric Cancer Cells Promotes Tumor Progression by Influencing Macrophage Polarization Via PF4
Authors: Xilun Cui, Changfeng Li, Yuanda Liu, Chang Liu, Wei Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGastric cancer (GC) progression is driven by an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME). Kynureninase (KYNU) is upregulated in GC and linked to poor prognosis, while platelet factor 4 (PF4)‐induced nonclassical macrophages promote immune evasion. This study investigates whether KYNU regulates macrophage polarization via PF4 in GC. KYNU expression was modulated in GC cells to assess PF4 regulation and nonclassical macrophage polarization. Functional consequences were evaluated by proliferation, migration, invasion, and phagocytosis assays. Mechanistic studies included transcriptomics, PI3K/Akt manipulation, and rescue experiments. In vivo validation used a syngeneic mouse model, and clinical relevance was confirmed by multimarker tissue analysis. High KYNU expression in GC cells specifically promoted macrophage polarization toward a nonclassical phenotype characterized by S100A8 and MMP7 expression. These non‐classical polarized macrophages subsequently enhanced GC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion while suppressing phagocytic activity. Mechanistically, KYNU upregulated PF4 expression with the involvement of the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway, which in turn drove nonclassical macrophage polarization. In vivo studies demonstrated that KYNU promoted tumor growth through this pathway, whereas PI3K/Akt inhibition suppressed both tumor progression and nonclassical macrophage infiltration. Clinical analysis further confirmed the coordinated upregulation of KYNU, p‐Akt, PF4, and S100A8 in human GC tissues. KYNU acts as a signaling transducer in GC, involving PI3K/Akt to upregulate PF4, driving nonclassical macrophage polarization and tumor progression. This mechanism represents a novel pathogenic driver and a promising therapeutic target for reprogramming the immunosuppressive TME.

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is a highly aggressive malignancy with persistently high global incidence and mortality [1, 2]. Despite advances in targeted therapies (e.g., against HER2) and immune checkpoint inhibitors (e.g., anti‐PD‐1/L1 antibodies), their overall efficacy remains limited and responsive populations are narrow [3, 4]. The evolution of cancer treatment has progressively shifted from broadly cytotoxic agents to targeted and immune‐based strategies, yet therapeutic resistance remains a major challenge. In this context, understanding the interplay between tumor metabolism, immune microenvironment remodeling, and treatment resistance has emerged as a critical frontier [5]. A deeper understanding of GC pathogenesis, particularly the role of the tumor microenvironment (TME) in driving tumor progression and immune evasion, is therefore essential for developing new therapeutic strategies. The TME, a complex ecosystem comprising tumor cells, immune cells, extracellular matrix, and signaling molecules, plays a pivotal role in GC development [6]. Within the TME, tumor‐associated macrophages (TAMs) represent a major immune cell population with remarkable functional plasticity [7]. Although macrophages are commonly categorized into classically activated M1 (anti‐tumor) and alternatively activated M2 (pro‐tumor) phenotypes, emerging evidence supports the existence of diverse macrophage states beyond this binary classification [8, 9].

There exists a distinct macrophage polarization state specifically driven by platelet factor 4 (PF4/CXCL4) [10]. This factor inhibits monocyte apoptosis and promotes their differentiation toward a specific phenotype with defective clearance of apoptotic cells [11, 12]. Beyond its classical roles in hemostasis and inflammation, PF4 has been detected in the TME of malignancies including pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma and hepatocellular carcinoma [13, 14]. Intriguingly, PF4 displays context‐dependent expression and function, and can exert tumor‐promoting effects in specific cancer settings [15, 16]. However, whether tumor cells themselves can produce PF4 to reprogram macrophages within the gastric cancer TME remains unknown [17]. This gap leads to a compelling hypothesis: tumor‐derived PF4 could be an intrinsic mechanism through which GC cells remodel the local immune microenvironment by polarizing macrophages, a possibility yet to be explored in GC.

Tryptophan metabolism is a key pathway enabling tumor immune evasion. While the IDO1/TDO axis is well‐studied, recent evidence highlights the importance of the downstream enzyme kynureninase (KYNU) in this cascade [18]. KYNU converts kynurenine to metabolites like anthranilic acid and promotes tumor progression and immunosuppression in cancers including gastric and lung cancer [19, 20]. Our previous work confirmed KYNU's contribution to gastric cancer malignancy, and single‐sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) further linked high KYNU expression to increased macrophage infiltration in the TME [19]. However, the precise mechanism by which KYNU modulates immune cells—especially its interaction with macrophages in gastric cancer—remains unclear.

The pathogenesis of GC is complex and heterogeneous, involving dysregulation of multiple signaling pathways such as MAPK/ERK, HER2, PI3K/Akt, and p53 [21]. Among these, the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway—a central regulator of cell proliferation, survival, metabolism, and inflammation—plays a critical role in both the initiation and progression of GC, and its dysregulation contributes to resistance against chemotherapy and targeted therapies [21]. This pathway integrates diverse upstream signals and regulates downstream gene expression, including chemokines [22, 23]. Based on this, we hypothesize that PI3K/Akt may serve as a key signaling node, potentially participating in the regulation of downstream effectors such as PF4 by KYNU.

Thus, we propose that in gastric cancer, tumor‐derived KYNU may upregulate PF4 expression, a process in which the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway is involved. PF4 may subsequently influence macrophage polarization toward a non‐classical subtype, ultimately promoting malignant progression of GC. This study aims to elucidate the molecular mechanism by which KYNU regulates PF4 in gastric cancer cells and to investigate the impact of this non‐classical macrophage subtype on tumor cell behavior, with the goal of providing new potential targets and a theoretical foundation for immunotherapy in gastric cancer.

Methods

Cell Lines, Reagents, and Expression Vectors

All human GC cell lines (NUGC3 (RRID: CVCL_1612), MKN74 (RRID: CVCL_2791)), mouse GC cell lines (MFC (RRID: CVCL_5J48)), human monocytic cell lines (THP‐1 (RRID: CVCL_0006)), and mouse monocyte–macrophage cell lines (RAW264.7 (RRID: CVCL_0493)) used in this study were obtained from the cell bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. Cells were cultured in RPMI‐1640 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) at 37°C under a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Pictilisib (PI3K inhibitor) and 740 Y‐P (PI3K activator) were provided by Selleck. Recombinant PF4 protein and phorbol 12‐myristate 13‐acetate (PMA) were purchased from MedChemExpress. Lentiviral vectors for KYNU knockout and overexpression were acquired from Heyuan Biotechnology (China). PF4 overexpression plasmids and siRNAs were obtained from IBSBIO (China).

RNA Interference, Lentiviral Infection, and Transfection

To modulate KYNU expression in GC cell lines, cells were infected with lentiviruses carrying KYNU overexpression or shRNA constructs or corresponding control vectors. Following infection, stable cell lines were established by selection with puromycin (2 μg/mL). To regulate PF4 expression, GC cells were transfected with PF4 overexpression plasmid, siRNA, or respective control vectors. The siRNA targeting mouse PF4 was designed with the sense sequence 5′‐CGAAGAAAGCGAUGGAGAUCU‐3′, and the siRNA targeting human PF4 was designed with the sense sequence 5′‐CCAACUGAUAGCCACGCUGAA‐3′. Transfection efficiency was assessed via qPCR at 24 h and Western blotting at 48 h posttransfection.

Induction of Macrophage Polarization

To differentiate THP‐1 cells into M0 macrophages, cells were seeded at 2 × 106 cells per well in six‐well plates and stimulated with 20 ng/mL PMA for 24 h. After replacing the medium with fresh complete medium, cells were cultured for an additional 24 h to obtain adherent, quiescent M0 macrophages. For the nonclassical macrophage polarization, RAW264.7 or THP‐1‐derived M0 macrophages were incubated with medium containing 1 μM mouse or human recombinant PF4 protein for 48 h. Control groups received an equal volume of PBS. The polarization outcome was validated by Western blotting analysis of the M4‐associated markers S100A8 and MMP7. When inducing polarization with tumor‐conditioned medium (CM), GC cells were first cultured in basal medium for 24 h. The supernatant was then collected and used to culture RAW264.7 or THP‐1‐derived M0 macrophages for an additional 48 h.

Quantitative PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Lablead, China), and cDNA was synthesized with the SuperScript III First‐Strand Synthesis System (Lablead, China). Quantitative PCR was performed with Power SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Lablead, China). The relative expression levels of target genes were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are listed in Table S1.

Cell Proliferation Assay

GC cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded into 96‐well plates at a density of 3000 cells/well. The cells were then treated with CM harvested from M0 or the nonclassical macrophages following a 24‐h culture period. At 0, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h, 10 μL of Cell Counting Kit‐8 (CCK‐8) reagent (Lablead, China) was added to each well, followed by incubation at 37°C for 1 h. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Cell proliferation rates were calculated according to the formula: [(OD experimental − OD blank) ÷ (OD control − OD blank)] × 100%.

Transwell Assay

To simulate cell invasion, Matrigel (Lablead, China) was added to Transwell chambers (Labelect, China); for the migration assay, no Matrigel was used. Matrigel was diluted 1:7 with RPMI‐1640 medium, and 50 μL of the diluted solution was evenly coated onto the upper chamber. Then, 5 × 104 cells suspended in 200 μL of serum‐free RPMI‐1640 medium were seeded into the upper chamber, while 600 μL of CM was added to the lower chamber. After 24 h of incubation, nonmigrated cells on the upper surface were gently removed with a cotton swab. Cells that migrated to the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 15 min, and washed with PBS. Images were captured under a microscope, and the number of migrated or invaded cells was quantified using Image J software.

Phagocytosis Assay

Gastric cancer cells were trypsinized, counted, washed with PBS, and resuspended to a density of 1 × 107 cells/mL. The cell suspension was then incubated with CFSE (MedChemExpress, China) under light‐protected conditions at 37°C for 10 min. Following incubation, complete medium was added, and the cells were centrifuged to remove the supernatant. The pellet was washed with complete medium to eliminate unbound dye. CFSE‐labeled gastric cancer cells were co‐cultured with unstained macrophages in six‐well plates at an effector‐to‐target ratio of 5:1 for 2 h at 37°C under 5% CO2. After co‐culture, cells were harvested and stained with antibodies against CD11b (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) or F4/80 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) to identify monocytes/macrophages. The proportion of CFSE‐positive macrophages among the total macrophage population was calculated as the phagocytic rate.

Flow Cytometry

Culture medium was aspirated from six‐well plates, and cells were detached using EDTA‐free trypsin. Subsequently, 1 mL of PBS was added to each well, and cells were gently resuspended and collected by centrifugation. After discarding the supernatant, cell pellets were resuspended in 100 μL of staining buffer and incubated with 1 μL of Fc‐receptor blocking reagent on ice for 10 min. Following blocking, cells were washed with 1 mL of PBS and centrifuged. The pellet was resuspended in 100 μL of staining buffer containing appropriately titrated fluorochrome‐conjugated antibodies, vortexed gently, and incubated on ice in the dark for 20 min. Cells were then washed with 1 mL of PBS and resuspended in an appropriate volume of buffer for acquisition on a flow cytometer (NovoCyte 3080, USA). Live cells were first gated, followed by identification of CD11b‐ or F4/80‐positive cells as the monocyte–macrophage population. Within this gate, CFSE‐positive cells were quantified as macrophages that had phagocytosed gastric cancer cells.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

Cells grown on coverslips or tissue sections were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. After fixation, cells were permeabilized in PBS containing 0.1% Triton X‐100 (Lablead, China) for 15 min. Subsequently, samples were blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS at room temperature for 1 h. Next, samples were incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies: CD68 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), F4/80 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), Pan Cytokeratin (MedChemExpress, China), KYNU (Proteintech, China), PF4 (Proteintech, China), Phospho‐Akt (Ser473) (Cell Signaling Technology, USA), S100A8 (Proteintech, China), and MMP7 (Proteintech, China). Appropriate secondary antibodies—including Goat Anti‐Mouse IgG (AbFluor 488) (Immunoway, China), Goat Anti‐Rat IgG (DyLight 488) (Immunoway, China), Goat Anti‐Rabbit IgG (AbFluor 488) (Immunoway, China), and Goat Anti‐Rabbit IgG (AbFluor 594) (Immunoway, China)—were applied for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Finally, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Immunoway, China), and coverslips were mounted with anti‐fade medium. Images were acquired using a confocal microscope (Nikon, Japan).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded tumor tissues were sectioned at 4 μm thickness. After deparaffinization, antigen retrieval was performed using citrate buffer (pH 6.0, Sigma‐Aldrich, USA) under high‐pressure heating. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked by incubation with 3% H2O2 for 15 min at room temperature. Sections were then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against F4/80, Pan Cytokeratin, KYNU, PF4, Phospho‐Akt (Ser473), and S100A8. All slides were detected using an HRP‐labeled polymer‐based secondary antibody system, followed by color development with DAB or AEC and counterstaining with hematoxylin. Immunohistochemical staining results were independently evaluated by two pathologists and semi‐quantitatively scored based on staining intensity and the percentage of positive cells.

Western Blot

Proteins were extracted from cells, and protein concentrations were determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Lablead, China). Samples were mixed with 5× loading buffer and denatured at 100°C for 10 min, followed by separation on SDS‐PAGE gels (Lablead, China). Subsequently, proteins were transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (Millipore, USA). The membrane was incubated overnight (18 h) with primary antibodies against the following proteins: KYNU, PF4, PI3 Kinase p85 alpha (Abmart, China), Akt (pan) (Cell Signaling Technology, USA), Phospho‐Akt (Ser473), S100A8, MMP7, and GAPDH (Proteintech, China). After washing, the membrane was incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies for 1 h. Protein bands were visualized using ECL reagents (MedChemExpress, China) and imaged with a chemiluminescence imaging system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). GAPDH was used as an internal loading control. The optical density of each target band was normalized to that of the corresponding GAPDH band within the same sample to obtain comparable relative expression levels across replicates.

ELISA

The concentration of PF4 in cell culture supernatants was quantified using a PF4 ELISA Kit (FineTest, China). Clarified culture supernatants were collected, and both standards and samples were added to a 96‐well plate precoated with capture antibody and blocked. After incubation for 90 min, the plate was washed, followed by sequential addition of biotinylated detection antibody and HRP‐conjugated streptavidin, with thorough washing after each step. Color was developed using TMB substrate, and the reaction was stopped before absorbance was measured at 450 nm. Sample concentrations were calculated based on a standard curve.

RNA‐Sequencing (RNA‐Seq)

RNA‐seq was performed on stably transfected GC cells (n = 3 per group). Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Reads were aligned to GRCh38/hg38, and DEGs were identified using DESeq2 (|log2FC| ≥ 1, FDR < 0.05). GO and KEGG enrichment were analyzed with clusterProfiler (FDR < 0.05).

Bioinformatics Analysis

The mRNA expression levels in patients with GC in TCGA and GTEx were acquired from the UCSC Xena project (http://xena.ucsc.edu). RNA expression data in tissues and cell lines were retrieved from the Human Protein Atlas (proteinatlas.org). All statistical analyzes were performed using R software.

Animal Studies

The syngeneic 615 mouse strain, used as hosts for the mouse gastric cancer cell line MFC, was purchased from Qizhen Laboratory Animal Technology Co. Ltd. (Hangzhou, China). All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Basic Medical College, Jilin University (2025697) and conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines. Mice were housed under specific pathogen‐free (SPF) barrier conditions at 22°C ± 1°C on a 12‐h light/dark cycle with free access to food and water. Male 615 mice, 6–8 weeks old, were acclimatized for one week before the experiment. For each group, five mice were used. In the syngeneic tumor model, 2 × 106 MFC cells were subcutaneously injected into each mouse to establish solid tumors. In some studies, mice received intraperitoneal injections of Pictilisib or DMSO vehicle control seven times over a 2‐week period. Body weight was recorded, and tumor growth was monitored every other day by measuring tumor dimensions with digital calipers. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula ½ × L × W2. At the experimental endpoint, mice were euthanized and tumor specimens were collected for further immunohistochemical analysis.

Clinical Specimens

All GC tissue samples and matched adjacent normal tissues were obtained from the biobank of China‐Japan Union Hospital of Jilin University. The use of these specimens and associated de‐identified clinicopathological data was approved by the Ethics Committee of the same hospital (2025100902).

Statistical Analysis

All experiments were performed at least three times independently. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism software. Normality of data distribution was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test. If the data followed a normal distribution and homoscedasticity was satisfied, parametric tests were applied. Differences between two groups were analyzed using Student's t‐test, and comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Correlation analyzes were performed using Spearman's rank correlation and linear regression. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (*p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; ****p < 0.0001; ns p > 0.05).

Results

High Expression of KYNU in GC Cells Influences Macrophage Polarization

Our previous study suggested that elevated KYNU expression in gastric cancer cells may contribute to an immunosuppressive microenvironment and is closely associated with tumor‐associated macrophages [19]. To select appropriate cell lines for KYNU functional studies, we first examined KYNU expression levels in human gastric cancer cell lines using the Human Protein Atlas database (Figure S1A). NUGC3 cells exhibited relatively high KYNU expression (nTPM = 45.5), whereas MKN74 cells showed very low expression (nTPM = 0.3). Therefore, both cell lines were subjected to knockdown and overexpression experiments. Notably, NUGC3 cells with higher endogenous KYNU were more suitable for evaluating the effect of KYNU knockdown, while MKN74 cells with very low basal KYNU expression were particularly suitable for overexpression studies. For mouse gastric cancer studies, the widely used and commercially available MFC cell line was selected for both knockdown and overexpression experiments due to its suitability for the syngeneic 615 mouse model. To exclude potential off‐target effects, we designed three independent shRNA sequences targeting KYNU. Each construct was transfected into gastric cancer cells, and knockdown efficiency was evaluated by qPCR and Western blot. All three shRNAs effectively reduced KYNU mRNA and protein levels, and the one with the highest knockdown efficiency was selected for all subsequent loss‐of‐function experiments (Figure S1B–C). Based on this, we established stable KYNU knockdown and overexpression NUGC3, MKN74, and MFC cell lines, including the following groups: NC1 (empty vector control for overexpression), OE (KYNU overexpression), NC2 (scrambled shRNA control for knockdown), and SH (KYNU knockdown). To investigate whether and which type of macrophages are polarized by gastric cancer cells via KYNU, conditioned media from these stable gastric cancer cell lines were used to culture THP‐1 and RAW264.7 macrophage lines. The resulting macrophages were harvested, and qPCR was performed to evaluate the expression of 12 polarization‐related markers: M1 (CD86, NOS2), M2 (CD163, MRC1), M2a (ARG1, FIZZ1), M2b (IL10), M2c (SPHK1, SOCS3), M2d (VEGFA), and nonclassical polarization subtype (MMP7, S100A8). Heatmap analysis revealed that high KYNU expression in gastric cancer cells significantly upregulated the expression of S100A8 and MMP7 in macrophages, while only slightly affecting M1 and M2 polarization markers (Figure 1A). Furthermore, immunofluorescence confirmed that the conditional medium from overexpression or knockdown of KYNU in gastric cancer cells correspondingly increased or decreased the expression of these two markers in macrophages (Figure 1B). To assess the effect on macrophage recruitment, we performed Transwell migration assays. The results demonstrated enhanced migration ability of macrophages cultured in CM from KYNU‐overexpressing cells (OE‐KYNU‐CM), whereas migration was markedly suppressed in macrophages exposed to CM from KYNU‐knockdown cells (SH‐KYNU‐CM) (Figure 1C,D). Together, these data indicate that GC cells with high KYNU expression can influence macrophage polarization by producing some uncertain factors, and this promoting effect on macrophages with high expression of S100A8 and MMP7 is accompanied by macrophage migration.

(B) IF detection of target proteins in macrophages after culture with CM. Scale bars: 10 μm. (C, D) Representative images and quantitative analysis of macrophage migration assessed by Transwell assay following CM treatment.

Given that S100A8 and MMP7 may mark a nonclassical macrophage polarization state, and that PF4 has been reported as a specific inducer of this state [10, 24, 25], we performed PF4 neutralization experiments to investigate whether PF4 influences macrophage polarization in the tumor microenvironment. Specifically, PF4 neutralizing antibody (5 μg/mL) or isotype control IgG was added to the conditioned medium of KYNU‐overexpressing NUGC gastric cancer cells, and its effects on macrophage marker expression and migration were examined. Western blot analysis revealed that neutralization of PF4 in the conditioned medium significantly reduced the expression of S100A8 and MMP7 in macrophages (Figure S2A). Transwell migration assays demonstrated that the migratory capacity of macrophages was markedly attenuated when cultured in conditioned medium preincubated with the neutralizing antibody (Figure S2B). Collectively, these functional perturbation experiments suggest that PF4 may serve as a key factor mediating KYNU‐induced alterations in macrophage phenotype and function. Although PF4 has been reported to promote the growth and metastasis of certain tumor cells, such as hepatocellular carcinoma [14, 15], whether it exerts similar functions in gastric cancer remains unclear.

CM From PF4‐Induced Macrophages Promotes the Proliferation and Migration of GC Cells

To investigate the impact of these PF4‐induced nonclassical polarized macrophages (abbreviated as NCM) on the malignant phenotype of gastric cancer cells, we used recombinant PF4 protein to generate the macrophages with high expression of S100A8 and MMP7 (Figure 2A), and then examined their phagocytic function as well as the malignant phenotype of gastric cancer cells cultured in the obtained CM [26]. CCK‐8 proliferation assays showed that NCM‐CM significantly promoted the proliferation of gastric cancer cells compared with M0‐CM (Figure 2B). Transwell assays demonstrated that regardless of Matrigel coating, the number of GC cells migrating through the membrane was markedly increased in the presence of NCM‐CM, indicating that NCM enhance the invasive and migratory capabilities of gastric cancer cells (Figure 2C–F). Furthermore, flow cytometry analysis revealed that compared with M0, NCM exhibited reduced phagocytic activity toward gastric cancer cells (NUGC3, MFC) (Figure 2G,H). Collectively, these results suggest that NCM‐CM exerts a tumor‐promoting effect in gastric cancer.

(B) Proliferative activity of gastric cancer cells cultured in CM (CCK‐8 assay). KYNU Upregulates PF4 Expression in GC Cells

To investigate the relationship between KYNU and PF4 in gastric cancer, we first assessed PF4 mRNA levels following KYNU overexpression or knockdown in gastric cancer cells using qPCR. The results revealed a significant positive correlation between PF4 and KYNU expression (Figure 3A). To further validate the clinical relevance of this connection, we analyzed public mRNA expression datasets. Using the TCGA and GTEx databases, we compared PF4 expression between digestive system tumors and normal tissues. The results showed that PF4 was significantly upregulated in stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD) compared with normal tissues (Figure S3A). Moreover, analysis of the TCGA‐STAD cohort revealed that PF4 expression was significantly higher in high‐grade (G3) tumors than in low‐grade (G1&G2) tumors, suggesting that PF4 may be associated with tumor malignancy in gastric cancer (Figure S3B). Furthermore, a significant positive linear correlation between PF4 and KYNU expression was observed in gastric cancer tissues from the TCGA dataset (Figure S3C). Collectively, these public database analyzes support a potential clinical relevance of the connection between KYNU and PF4 in gastric cancer progression, although its biological significance remains to be further validated. This correlation was further confirmed at both protein and secretory levels by Western blot and ELISA analyzes (Figure 3B,C). Notably, these initial experiments were performed using all three cell lines. For subsequent rescue experiments, NUGC3 was retained as the representative human cell line and used alongside the mouse MFC cell line for functional assays. MKN74, which showed very low endogenous KYNU expression, was primarily used for the initial correlation analysis. Subsequently, to perform rescue experiments, PF4 overexpression plasmids and siRNAs were constructed, and PF4 was knocked down in KYNU‐overexpressing cells and their controls, while PF4 was overexpressed in KYNU‐knockdown cells and their controls. Among the three designed siRNAs, the one with the highest knockdown efficiency was selected for subsequent experiments (Figure S3D). Western blot analysis showed that knocking down PF4 in KYNU‐overexpressing gastric cancer cells reduced PF4 protein levels without affecting KYNU expression. Conversely, overexpressing PF4 in KYNU‐knockdown cells increased PF4 protein levels while leaving KYNU expression unchanged (Figure 3D,E). To determine whether KYNU‐mediated PF4 regulation in gastric cancer cells functionally impacts macrophage polarization, we collected conditioned media from the above cells and used them to treat macrophages. Western blot analysis revealed that knockdown of PF4 in KYNU‐overexpressing cells markedly reduced the expression of S100A8 and MMP7 in macrophages, whereas PF4 overexpression in KYNU‐knockdown cells significantly increased their expression (Figure 3F). Together, these findings demonstrate that PF4 is a required downstream effector of KYNU in gastric cancer cells, and that KYNU may play an important role in influencing macrophage polarization through PF4.

KYNU Upregulates PF4 With the Involvement of the PI3K/Akt Signaling Pathway

To further investigate the mechanism by which KYNU regulates macrophage polarization via PF4 in gastric cancer, we performed RNA‐sequencing on KYNU‐stable gastric cancer cell lines. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed genes revealed that the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway was significantly enriched in both KYNU‐overexpression and KYNU‐knockdown groups, ranking among the top three enriched pathways in each case—whereas the tryptophan metabolism pathway was not prominently enriched (Figure 4A). Although the tryptophan metabolism pathway showed enrichment in the KYNU‐overexpression group, it ranked lower, and KYNU was the only differentially expressed gene within this pathway [27]. Other upregulated genes in the KYNU‐overexpression group were predominantly enriched in pathways such as oxidative phosphorylation and arachidonic acid metabolism. These results suggest that the functional mechanism of KYNU in gastric cancer may be independent of its canonical metabolic role and is closely associated with the activation status of the PI3K/Akt pathway.

KYNU regulates PF4 involving the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway. (A) KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of KYNU‐stable gastric cancer cell lines. (B) Western blot detection of pathway‐related proteins in gastric cancer cells. (C, D) Western blot results of gastric cancer cells treated with pathway inhibitors or activators. (E) IF detection of target proteins in macrophages cultured with CM. Scale bars: 10 μm.

Subsequently, we validated the effect of KYNU on PI3K/Akt activation by Western blot. The expression levels of PI3K and the p‐Akt/Akt ratio were positively correlated with KYNU expression (Figure 4B). Rescue experiments using the PI3K activator 740 Y‐P and the inhibitor Pictilisib demonstrated that in KYNU‐overexpressing cells, treatment with Pictilisib markedly reduced the p‐Akt/Akt ratio and PF4 expression. Conversely, in KYNU‐knockdown cells, activation with 740 Y‐P significantly increased p‐Akt/Akt and PF4 levels, while KYNU expression remained unaffected by either activator or inhibitor (Figure 4C,D). Moreover, immunofluorescence microscopy showed that inhibiting PI3K/Akt signaling in gastric cancer cells reduced the expression of S100A8 and MMP7 in macrophages cultured with CM, whereas activating PI3K/Akt promoted this non‐classical macrophage polarization (Figure 4E). Collectively, these findings indicate that the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway is involved in the effect of KYNU on PF4 expression and subsequent non‐classical macrophage polarization, as pharmacological modulation of this pathway consistently altered PF4 expression and macrophage phenotype.

KYNU Promotes Gastric Tumor Growth and Upregulates p‐Akt, PF4, and S100A8 Expression In Vivo

Building on the finding that KYNU upregulates PF4 and influences macrophage polarization with the involvement of the PI3K/Akt pathway in vitro, we next investigated whether this pathway is involved in tumor progression in vivo. We established a syngeneic subcutaneous tumor model using MFC gastric cancer cells with stable KYNU overexpression or knockdown (Figure 5A). No significant difference in body weight was observed among the experimental groups. KYNU overexpression promoted subcutaneous tumor growth, whereas KYNU knockdown reduced tumor volume and weight, confirming that KYNU facilitates gastric cancer progression in vivo (Figure 5B–E). Given that PI3K and total Akt expression showed no significant differences in our previous in vitro assays, we focused our IHC analysis on the active, phosphorylated form of Akt (p‐Akt) in the collected tumor samples. IHC analysis further revealed that elevated KYNU expression in tumors correlated with increased levels of p‐Akt, PF4, and the M4 macrophage marker S100A8 (Figure 5F). These experimental results suggest that KYNU may promote gastric tumorigenesis and influence macrophage polarization in vivo through a PF4‐dependent mechanism involving the PI3K/Akt pathway. To test this, we next employed a PI3K inhibitor in vivo.

PI3K Inhibitor Suppresses KYNU‐Induced Gastric Tumor Growth and Reduces PF4 Expression and Infiltration of Macrophages with High S100A8 Expression In Vivo

To investigate the therapeutic relevance of targeting KYNU‐activated signaling, we assessed the in vivo efficacy of the PI3K inhibitor Pictilisib on gastric tumor progression and associated macrophage polarization. We treated mice bearing subcutaneous tumors derived from MFC NC1 or OE‐KYNU cells with the PI3K inhibitor Pictilisib (75 mg/kg) or DMSO vehicle every other day for two weeks (Figure 6A) [28]. No significant difference in body weight was noted among the treatment groups. Inhibition of PI3K signaling significantly reduced tumor volume and weight in both NC1 and OE‐KYNU groups, regardless of basal KYNU expression, indicating that the PI3K/Akt pathway is involved in KYNU‐promoted tumor growth (Figure 6B–E). Subsequent immunohistochemistry of tumor tissues revealed that Pictilisib treatment downregulated p‐Akt, PF4, and S100A8 expression compared with the DMSO control, while KYNU levels remained unchanged (Figure 6F). These results demonstrate that the PI3K inhibitor suppresses tumor growth and concurrently decreases the expression of PF4 in tumor tissues and S100A8 in macrophages. In summary, pharmacological blockade of PI3K/Akt signaling effectively suppresses KYNU‐induced gastric tumor progression and attenuates nonclassical macrophage polarization influenced by downstream PF4 in the tumor microenvironment.

Clinical Validation That KYNU‐Associated PI3K/Akt Pathway Involvement Drives PF4‐Mediated Polarization of Macrophages with High S100A8 Expression

To further evaluate the clinical relevance of our findings, using an IF system validated by positive controls (human liver for KYNU/p‐Akt; bone marrow for PF4/S100A8) and negative controls (primary antibody omission) (Figure S4A,B), we first performed IF analysis on six paired GC tissues and adjacent normal samples. The results demonstrated that KYNU was significantly overexpressed in GC tissues, accompanied by concordant upregulation of p‐Akt, PF4, and S100A8 (Figure 7A,B). We then expanded the cohort to 20 GC specimens and conducted quantitative correlation analysis based on immunofluorescence staining. The expression levels of p‐Akt, PF4, and S100A8 showed significant positive correlations with KYNU expression (Figure 7C). Furthermore, when samples were stratified into high‐ and low‐KYNU groups according to KYNU expression, the high‐KYNU group exhibited significantly elevated levels of p‐Akt, PF4, and S100A8 compared to the low‐KYNU group (Figure 7D). These clinical findings demonstrate that KYNU‐associated PI3K/Akt pathway involvement and the resulting PF4‐mediated polarization of macrophages with high S100A8 expression are functionally active in human gastric cancer and associated with disease progression.

Discussion

GC remains one of the leading causes of cancer‐related mortality worldwide, and its complex immunosuppressive TME plays a crucial role in promoting tumor progression and therapeutic resistance [29]. Within the TME, TAMs act as key regulators of tumor immunity, and their functional plasticity profoundly influences clinical outcomes [30]. Although the classical M1/M2 polarization paradigm has been extensively studied, certain nonclassical macrophage subpopulations may play distinct and critical roles in immune regulation within solid tumors [31]. Meanwhile, tryptophan metabolic reprogramming represents a hallmark of cancer and serves as an important pathway for tumor immune evasion. KYNU, a key enzyme in this pathway, has been implicated in gastric cancer progression, yet its precise mechanisms of action, particularly in regulating macrophage polarization, remain incompletely understood [19, 32]. Our study integrates these two pivotal mechanisms by demonstrating that KYNU influences the PI3K/Akt pathway to promote PF4‐mediated non‐classical macrophage polarization, thereby advancing gastric cancer progression.

Our study first established the precise role of KYNU in regulating macrophage polarization. By systematically analyzing multiple polarization markers, we found that gastric cancer cells overexpressing KYNU specifically induced macrophages to highly express S100A8 and MMP7, rather than broadly promoting classical M2‐type polarization. Of note, the macrophage subpopulation characterized by S100A8 and MMP7 expression was initially identified in atherosclerosis for its pro‐inflammatory role and designated as M4 [26]. These two markers have been validated as specific to CXCL4‐induced M4 macrophages, with transcriptomic and protein‐level evidence confirming that their expression pattern is distinctly different from M1 or M2 subtypes [24, 26]. Although M4 macrophages share some markers with other polarization states, the combination of S100A8 and MMP7 has been widely adopted as a reliable signature for M4 identification in both human and experimental settings [24, 25]. Our experiments further demonstrated that PF4 neutralization reversed the expression of these two markers, suggesting that this nonclassical macrophage polarization is dependent on PF4, consistent with the defining features of M4. However, studies on M4 in tumors remain scarce. Since our current identification relies primarily on marker panels, we cannot definitively conclude that these cells are strictly bona fide M4 subtype. They may represent a partial activation state or an M4‐like population with distinct characteristics in the gastric cancer microenvironment. The non‐classical macrophages generated in this study exhibited a unique pro‐tumor phenotype. On the one hand, their phagocytic capacity was significantly reduced, impairing immune surveillance; on the other hand, they strongly enhanced the malignant behavior of gastric cancer cells in the microenvironment. These functional features parallel the reported role of M4 macrophages in promoting plaque instability in atherosclerosis, suggesting that M4 may represent a disease‐transcendent myeloid effector subset that disrupts tissue homeostasis and mediates matrix remodeling [10, 33]. Future studies employing high‐resolution technologies such as single‐cell RNA sequencing are warranted to comprehensively compare the transcriptomic profiles of the macrophages identified in this study with those of classical M1, M2, M4, and other non‐classical subtypes, thereby clarifying their precise identity.

We further identified PF4 as the critical mediator through which tumor‐derived KYNU influences macrophage polarization, establishing gastric cancer cells themselves as a noncanonical cellular source of this chemokine [34]. The expression of PF4 is subject to intricate, multilayered regulation involving diverse signaling pathways. Existing evidence indicates that PF4 is modulated by transcription factors such as GATA1 and multiple signaling cascades, forming a complex regulatory network that varies across physiological and pathological contexts [35, 36]. Kasper B et al. demonstrated that PF4‐stimulated immediate and delayed monocyte functions are closely associated with MAPK, Syk, and PI3K [37]. In hepatocellular carcinoma, Wang KT et al. reported that PF4 expression may be regulated by the β‐catenin pathway, whereas in vascular endothelioma, PF4 is modulated by the PI3K/Akt signaling axis [14, 38]. Notably, our transcriptomic analysis revealed a robust activation of the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway upon altered KYNU expression, whereas no significant enrichment was observed in other reported pathways or the canonical tryptophan metabolic pathway. Furthermore, accumulating evidence from single‐cell analyzes and cellular experiments has demonstrated that KYNU can regulate the PI3K/Akt pathway in tumors [39, 40]. KYNU has been suggested as a potential transcriptional target of CD44 signaling, which is known to activate PI3K/AKT through receptor tyrosine kinases such as HER2 [41]. Thus, it is plausible that in gastric cancer cells, KYNU may participate in a similar receptor‐coupled signaling axis upstream of PI3K/AKT. This notion is further supported by recent findings in glioblastoma, where TRIM29 was shown to activate the PI3K/AKT pathway by promoting NEFL ubiquitination and degradation, illustrating a paradigm in which upstream regulators converge on this central signaling axis to drive tumor progression [42]. Collectively, these observations suggest that the primary role of KYNU in gastric cancer cells may have shifted from that of a metabolic enzyme to a signaling transducer, with its function being closely linked to the PI3K/Akt pathway. Through a series of experiments, we established that KYNU is involved in the PI3K/Akt pathway to promote PF4 upregulation. Critically, this pathway may operate independently of KYNU's metabolic products. Additionally, it is possible that the PI3K/Akt pathway represents only one of several signaling routes influencing PF4 expression and macrophage polarization. This “metabolism‐signaling” coupling mechanism illustrates how tumor cells exploit the same molecular tool to simultaneously cope with metabolic and immune stress—a highly efficient strategy for immune editing and malignant progression [43]. This finding holds significant importance as it reveals a novel mechanism by which tumor cells actively shape a favorable immune microenvironment through the expression of PF4, which modulates adjacent immune cells. It also provides a fresh perspective for understanding the dynamic interactions between tumor cells and the TME.

Furthermore, we systematically validated the pathogenic involvement of KYNU‐associated PI3K/Akt pathway and its downstream PF4‐mediated nonclassical macrophage polarization through integrated in vivo and clinical investigations. In vivo mouse experiments confirmed that KYNU promotes tumor growth, whereas PI3K/Akt inhibition reversed this effect and downregulated PF4 and S100A8 in tumor tissues, providing strong causal evidence. More clinically relevant, in human gastric cancer tissue samples, the expression of KYNU, p‐Akt, PF4, and S100A8 showed significant positive correlations and coordinated upregulation. This signaling pathway was closely associated with disease progression, highlighting its potential translational value. Given the limited success of clinical trials targeting the upstream rate‐limiting enzymes IDO1/2, future therapeutic strategies may consider directly intervening in KYNU or its non‐canonical signaling pathway, which could simultaneously block the immunosuppressive metabolites derived from its classical metabolic activity and interrupt the nonclassical macrophage‐prone, pro‐metastatic microenvironment driven by its noncanonical function [44, 45]. Our in vivo efficacy data preliminarily support the effectiveness of PI3K/Akt inhibitors such as Pictilisib, although its clinical translation has been limited by insufficient efficacy and dose‐limiting toxicities [46]. Future efforts should focus on developing inhibitors that specifically target the signaling function of KYNU, or combining such approaches with existing immune checkpoint blockade, which may open new avenues for overcoming treatment resistance in advanced gastric cancer.

Although our data demonstrate a functional link, the precise mechanism by which KYNU is involved in the PI3K/Akt pathway remains to be elucidated—whether through direct interaction or via modulation of upstream receptors or adaptor proteins. To address this open question, we propose two testable hypotheses as future research directions. First, KYNU may be functionally linked to receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) signaling. It is well established that RTKs such as HER2 activate the PI3K/Akt pathway [41]. Given that KYNU can be regulated by upstream signals, we hypothesize that KYNU might participate in an RTK‐coupled signaling axis. To test this, future studies could employ RTK inhibitors (e.g., HER2 inhibitor trastuzumab or lapatinib) or RTK‐specific siRNAs in KYNU‐overexpressing gastric cancer cells. If KYNU‐induced PI3K/Akt activation and PF4 upregulation are abrogated by RTK blockade, this would position KYNU within an RTK‐dependent pathway. Conversely, stimulation with recombinant ligands (e.g., EGF or heregulin) in KYNU‐knockdown cells could determine whether RTK activation is sufficient to bypass KYNU loss. Second, unbiased omics approaches could identify unknown molecular intermediaries. Co‐immunoprecipitation coupled with mass spectrometry could systematically profile KYNU‐interacting proteins, which may reveal previously unrecognized scaffold or adaptor proteins involved in its signaling function. Phosphoproteomic or ubiquitinomic analyzes might also identify critical posttranslational modifications of KYNU or its interacting partners. Collectively, these strategies provide a framework for future investigations into the molecular link between KYNU and PI3K/Akt activation. Elucidating this mechanism may further our understanding of KYNU's non‐canonical function and could offer new opportunities for therapeutic intervention in gastric cancer.

Our study also has several limitations. First, the specific effector molecules through which the non‐classical macrophages enhance the invasion and migration of gastric cancer cells in CM need to be systematically identified using proteomic or other high‐throughput approaches. Second, whether PF4‐induced macrophage polarization is mediated by its canonical receptor CXCR3, and whether this pathway may cross‐talk with other KYNU‐driven pro‐survival mechanisms (such as recently reported resistance to cuproptosis) warrants further investigation. Finally, whether targeting this pathway can enhance the efficacy of existing chemotherapy or immune checkpoint inhibitors in gastric cancer represents a promising translational direction for future research.

In summary, this study systematically reveals and validates a novel immunomodulatory pathway driven by the gastric cancer metabolic enzyme KYNU, which operates independently of its canonical enzymatic activity. KYNU upregulates PF4 expression and secretion through its involvement in the regulation of the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway, specifically polarizing macrophages toward a pro‐tumorigenic non‐classical phenotype, thereby remodeling the immunosuppressive microenvironment and accelerating gastric cancer progression. These findings expand our understanding of the tumor‐biological functions of KYNU and deepen insights into the complexity of the gastric cancer TME. Moreover, they provide a solid theoretical foundation and a set of promising intervention targets for the development of novel therapeutic strategies aimed at specific remodeling of the TME in gastric cancer.
