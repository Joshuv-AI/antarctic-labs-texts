Fractional CO2 Laser Treatment for Female Vaginal Relaxation Syndrome: A Prospective Study
Authors: Qiao Li, Jian Meng, Yajing Wang, Yueyue Chen, Xiaoyu Niu, Dongmei Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundVaginal relaxation syndrome (VRS) is a common condition that adversely affects women's quality of life. Conventional non‐surgical therapies provide limited benefit for patients with mild to moderate disease. Fractional CO2 laser (FxCO2) therapy has emerged as a minimally invasive treatment option; however, its long‐term efficacy and safety remain to be fully elucidated.ObjectiveTo prospectively evaluate the efficacy and safety of FxCO2 in women with mild to moderate VRS.MethodsThis prospective self‐controlled study enrolled 101 women diagnosed with mild to moderate VRS. All participants underwent three sessions of FxCO2 treatment at 4‐week intervals. Clinical outcomes were assessed at baseline and at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months after the final treatment using the Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI), Vaginal Laxity Questionnaire (VLQ), and Vaginal Health Index (VHI). Adverse events were recorded throughout the follow‐up period.ResultsAll primary outcome measures improved following treatment. The mean FSFI total score increased from 21.4 to 25.9, the VLQ score improved from 2.68 to approximately 3.0, and the VHI score increased from 17.0 to 19.25 (all p < 0.001). Subgroup analyses indicated that treatment efficacy was not significantly influenced by parity, age, or the presence of concomitant urinary incontinence. Only mild and transient adverse events were reported, all of which resolved spontaneously.ConclusionsFxCO2 therapy effectively improves sexual function, subjective vaginal tightness, and vaginal mucosal health in women with vaginal relaxation syndrome. The short‐term efficacy is favorable, with no significant safety concerns observed.Keywords: fractional CO2 laser, prospective study, safety, sexual function, treatment efficacy, vaginal relaxation syndrome

Introduction

Vaginal relaxation syndrome (VRS) refers to a reduction in vaginal contractility resulting from injury, degeneration, or weakening of the connective tissues supporting the vaginal wall and pelvic structures. Clinical manifestations may include widening of the vaginal introitus and a sensation of vaginal looseness or air passage. In more severe cases, VRS can lead to diminished sexual sensation and reduced quality of sexual life. VRS is commonly associated with the physiological aging process; pregnancy, multiple vaginal deliveries, menopausal hormonal changes, and obesity may further exacerbate its symptoms [1, 2]. With increasing societal attention to female reproductive health and quality of life, the negative psychological, physical, and social impacts of VRS have gained growing recognition. Previous international studies have reported that approximately 24%–38% of women experience symptoms related to vaginal laxity, highlighting VRS as a common gynecologic condition that may negatively influence aspects of women's quality of life [3, 4].

Current non‐surgical treatments for VRS include pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT) and electromagnetic biofeedback therapy. These modalities are primarily indicated for women with mild to moderate VRS or reduced pelvic floor muscle strength and are also used as adjunctive therapies following vaginal tightening surgery. Among them, PFMT is the most widely adopted; however, its effectiveness largely depends on patient compliance and long‐term adherence, and overall response rates remain suboptimal [5]. In recent years, energy‐based therapies—including laser, radiofrequency, and focused ultrasound—have demonstrated therapeutic potential in the management of female pelvic floor disorders. Fractional CO2 laser (FxCO2) therapy, in particular, has attracted increasing interest. Its proposed mechanism involves photothermal effects that enhance cellular metabolism, induce overexpression of heat shock proteins, and trigger tissue repair responses, thereby promoting collagen remodeling, neovascularization, and micro‐neural regeneration, ultimately improving the elasticity of peri‐vaginal connective tissues [6]. Although preliminary studies suggest that laser therapy may offer short‐term benefits for VRS, most available evidence is limited by small sample sizes, short follow‐up durations, and a lack of systematic evaluation across different patient subgroups. Consequently, uncertainties remain regarding the durability of treatment effects and long‐term safety.

Based on this background, we conducted a prospective self‐controlled study to comprehensively evaluate the efficacy and safety of FxCO2 therapy for VRS. Additionally, subgroup analyses were performed to explore the influence of key clinical factors on treatment outcomes, aiming to provide more robust evidence to support individualized therapeutic strategies.

Materials and Methods

Participants

This prospective self‐controlled study enrolled 101 women with mild to moderate VRS who presented to the gynecology outpatient clinic of our hospital between February 2024 and August 2024. VRS was assessed by the same experienced physician using a standardized digital examination. With the patient in the lithotomy position, at rest and under adequate lubrication, fingers were gently inserted into the vagina to a degree that caused no pain or discomfort. Vaginal tone was graded as follows: insertion of two fingers was considered normal, two fingers with looseness indicated mild laxity, three fingers indicated moderate laxity, and four or more fingers indicated severe laxity [7]. In addition, all participants completed the Vaginal Laxity Questionnaire (VLQ). The VLQ categorizes patients' subjective perception of vaginal tightness into seven levels (very loose, moderately loose, slightly loose, neither loose nor tight, slightly tight, moderately tight, and very tight), corresponding to scores from 1 to 7. A VLQ score ≤ 3 was considered diagnostic of VRS [8].

Inclusion criteria:

① Diagnosis of mild to moderate VRS according to established criteria;

② Women aged ≥ 18 years with an active sexual life, a Pelvic Organ Prolapse Quantification (POP‐Q) stage < 2, and willingness to participate with written informed consent;

③ Subjective complaint of reduced sexual sensitivity;

④ VLQ score ≤ 3.

Exclusion criteria:

① History of psychiatric disorders, cerebrovascular disease, or other conditions causing communication difficulties;

② Active or recurrent genital or urinary tract infections;

③ Malignancy;

④ Severe urinary incontinence(UI), anterior or posterior vaginal wall prolapse, or POP‐Q stage ≥ 2;

⑤ Use of vaginal estrogen therapy or physical therapy for pelvic floor disorders within the preceding 6 months;

⑥ History of pelvic reconstructive surgery, including procedures for POP, UI, or fecal incontinence.

All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of West China Second University Hospital, Sichuan University (Approval No. 2024213).

Intervention

All FxCO2 treatments were performed using the Femilift CO2 laser system (Alma Lasers, Israel). For premenopausal patients, treatments were scheduled ~1 week after menstruation, and patients were confirmed to have no active vaginal bleeding or infection before each session. During treatment, patients were placed in the lithotomy position. After routine disinfection of the vulva and vagina, a 360° intravaginal probe was lubricated with a sterile water‐based gel and inserted.

Treatments were delivered using the system's preset “aesthetic mode” with the following parameters: laser power = 60 W; spot size ≈ 1 cm² composed of a 9 × 9 matrix (81 microbeams); each microbeam diameter ≈ 75–100 μm; energy = 60–80 mJ/pixel; pulse duration (ON time) = 54–162 ms. During each session, the probe was rotated 6 full turns from the posterior vaginal fornix to the vaginal introitus, generating an average of 8 laser spots per rotation. Each treatment session consisted of 3–4 passes, resulting in an estimated total cumulative energy of approximately 1000 J per session.

Each patient underwent a total of three treatment sessions, with a 4‐week interval between sessions.

Outcomes

Clinical outcomes were systematically assessed using multiple instruments over a follow‐up period of 12 months. Evaluations were conducted at baseline (prior to treatment) and at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months after the final laser session. Outcomes were categorized as primary, secondary, and safety endpoints.

Primary Outcomes

Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI): The FSFI is a 19‐item validated questionnaire assessing sexual function over the preceding 4 weeks across six domains: sexual desire, subjective arousal, vaginal lubrication, orgasm, sexual satisfaction, and dyspareunia. The Chinese version of the FSFI has been validated, with a total score < 23.45 considered the optimal cutoff for diagnosing female sexual dysfunction in Chinese women [9].

Vaginal Laxity Questionnaire (VLQ): The VLQ evaluates patients' subjective perception of vaginal tightness on a 7‐point scale, ranging from very loose to very tight, with corresponding scores from 1 to 7. A VLQ score ≤ 3 was used to define vaginal relaxation syndrome. For assessment of treatment response, a score ≥ 5 was considered indicative of a clinically effective outcome [10].

Vaginal Health Index (VHI): The VHI is a clinician‐assessed measure based on vaginal examination, evaluating five parameters: elasticity, vaginal fluid volume, pH, epithelial integrity, and moisture. Each parameter is scored from 1 to 5, and the sum of all scores represents the overall vaginal health status, with higher scores indicating better vaginal health [11].

Secondary Outcomes

Pelvic Organ Prolapse/Urinary Incontinence Sexual Questionnaire–12 (PISQ‐12): The PISQ‐12 is a validated instrument designed to assess sexual function in women with pelvic floor disorders. It consists of 12 items, with total scores ranging from 0 to 48. Higher scores indicate better sexual function and a lower negative impact of pelvic floor symptoms on sexual activity [12].

Pelvic floor muscle(PFM) strength: Pelvic floor muscle strength was evaluated using the Modified Oxford Scale (MOS) [13]. Participants were placed in the lithotomy position. The examiner, wearing a sterile glove, inserted the index finger approximately 4–6 cm into the vaginal canal and palpated the pelvic floor muscles at the 4 and 8 o'clock positions. Participants were instructed to perform PFM contractions under two conditions: rapid contraction and continuous contraction. Rapid contraction: Participants were instructed to contract and immediately relax the PFM as quickly as possible. Continuous contraction: Participants were instructed to contract the PFM, maintain the contraction for 10 s, then fully relax. Muscle strength during both contraction types was graded using the MOS scoring system (0–5): 0 = no contraction, 1 = flicker, 2 = weak, 3 = medium, 4 = strong, 5 = strong with lift.

Sexual Satisfaction Questionnaire (SSQ): Sexual satisfaction during vaginal intercourse was assessed using a subjective questionnaire with five response categories (none, poor, moderate, good, very good), with one option selected to reflect overall sexual satisfaction [14].

Patient Global Impression of Improvement (PGI‐I): The PGI‐I scale was used to assess patient‐reported satisfaction with improvement in VRS. A 5‐point Likert scale was applied: 1 = very dissatisfied, 2 = dissatisfied, 3 = neutral, 4 = satisfied, and 5 = very satisfied [15].

Overall Treatment Satisfaction: At the final follow‐up visit, overall satisfaction with the treatment process was recorded using a 4‐point scale: 0 = dissatisfied, 1 = somewhat satisfied, 2 = satisfied, and 3 = very satisfied.

Safety Outcomes

Adverse Events: All adverse events observed by investigators or spontaneously reported by patients throughout the study period were documented in detail, including event type, time of onset, severity, relationship to treatment, and clinical outcome, to comprehensively evaluate treatment safety.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.0). The Shapiro–Wilk test was applied to assess normality of continuous variables. Questionnaire scores and sexual activity frequency data for both primary and secondary outcomes were found to be non‐normally distributed (all p < 0.05); therefore, nonparametric statistical methods were employed. Pelvic floor muscle strength grades, as ordinal variables, were also analyzed using nonparametric tests. Descriptive statistics are presented as median (interquartile range [IQR]) or mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables, and as frequency (percentage) for categorical variables.

Within‐group comparisons:

The Wilcoxon signed‐rank test was used to compare outcome measures at each follow‐up time point (1, 3, 6, and 12 months) with baseline values to evaluate the temporal effects of laser treatment. To control for inflation of type I error due to multiple comparisons, Bonferroni correction was applied, with a corrected significance level of α = 0.05. For pelvic floor muscle strength across multiple time points, the Friedman test was first used to assess overall differences, followed by post hoc pairwise comparisons when appropriate.

Subgroup analyses:

Subgroup analyses were conducted according to parity (< 2 vs. ≥ 2 deliveries), age (< 35 vs. ≥ 35 years), and status. Between‐group comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test, with the same multiple‐comparison correction applied. To address potential confounding in comparisons between UI subgroups, inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was performed based on propensity scores. Overlap weights were calculated, and weighted between‐group comparisons were conducted using the survey package (svyranktest) to approximate randomized balance.

All statistical tests were two‐sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Primary Outcomes

FSFI

As shown in Figure 1A, the mean baseline FSFI score was 21.4, consistent with the range indicative of sexual dysfunction. Following FxCO2 treatment, FSFI scores increased significantly, reaching 25.9 at 12 months after the final session. All follow‐up scores were significantly higher than baseline (p < 0.001).

Subgroup analyses (Figure 1A1‐3) demonstrated significant improvements in FSFI scores across all follow‐up visits regardless of parity, age, or UI status (all p < 0.001). No significant differences in FSFI scores were observed between subgroups at any follow‐up time point.

VLQ

As illustrated in Figure 1B, the mean baseline VLQ score was 2.68, consistent with the diagnostic threshold for VRS. At 1 month post‐treatment, the score increased to 3.15, followed by a slight decline but remaining stable at approximately 3.0 throughout subsequent follow‐up. VLQ scores at all follow‐up time points were significantly higher than baseline (all p < 0.001).

Subgroup analyses (Figure 1B1‐3) showed significant improvements in VLQ scores from baseline across all follow‐up visits, irrespective of parity, age, or UI status (all p < 0.001). Treatment response at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months post‐treatment was not significantly influenced by parity, age, or the presence of UI (p > 0.05).

VHI

As shown in Figure 1C, mean VHI scores increased from 17 at baseline to 19.25 at 12 months post‐treatment, representing a statistically significant improvement (p < 0.001).

Subgroup analyses (Figure 1C1‐3) indicated consistent improvements in VHI scores across all follow‐up time points in patients stratified by parity, age, and UI status (all p < 0.001). No significant differences were identified between subgroups at any follow‐up visit (p > 0.05).

Secondary Outcomes

PISQ‐12

As shown in Figure 2, the mean PISQ‐12 total score increased significantly from 36.0 at baseline to 39.7 following treatment (p < 0.001). Subgroup analyses demonstrated significant improvements in PISQ‐12 scores at all follow‐up visits across parity, age, and UI strata (all p < 0.001). Between‐group comparisons revealed that patients with UI had significantly lower PISQ‐12 scores than those without UI at all follow‐up time points (p < 0.001), whereas no significant differences were observed between parity or age subgroups (p > 0.05).

Overall Treatment Satisfaction

Overall treatment satisfaction assessed using a 4‐point scale is shown in Figure 5B. Scores demonstrated a skewed distribution, with a mean of 1.25 ± 0.68 and a median of 1.0 (IQR, 1.0–2.0). Adverse Events

No serious adverse events related to FxCO2 treatment were reported during the treatment period or throughout the 12‐month follow‐up. Mild and transient treatment‐related discomfort was reported by 4 patients (4/101, 3.96%), including pelvic soreness in one patient and vaginal dryness or burning sensation in 3 patients. All symptoms resolved spontaneously or with minimal symptomatic management, without the need for additional medical intervention. No vaginal or urinary tract infections, persistent pain, scarring, or other long‐term complications were observed.

Discussion

Main Findings

This 12‐month prospective, self‐controlled study systematically evaluated the efficacy of FxCO2 treatment for VRS. Overall, favorable outcomes were observed across all primary endpoints. Improvements in FSFI, PISQ‐12, and SSQ scores indicate that laser treatment was associated with clinically meaningful enhancements in sexual function and sexual quality of life. Meanwhile, the observed increase in VHI scores suggests improvement in vaginal mucosal elasticity and hydration. These therapeutic effects are likely attributable to the photothermal mechanism of FxCO2. By creating an array of microscopic thermal zones (MTZs), the laser induces controlled microinjury and subsequent tissue repair responses. This process has been shown to stimulate fibroblast activation and proliferation, promoting neocollagenesis and elastin remodeling, which together may contribute to enhanced connective tissue integrity and elasticity of the vaginal wall at the microscopic level [16].

Secondary outcomes further supported the overall effectiveness of the intervention. PFM strength demonstrated significant post‐treatment improvement, suggesting reinforcement of pelvic supportive structures. Consistent increases in PISQ‐12 scores further indicate improvements in sexual quality of life. In addition, patient‐reported outcomes, including PGI‐I and overall treatment satisfaction, reflected meaningful subjective benefits, with most participants reporting satisfaction levels ranging from “somewhat satisfied” to “satisfied”.

Subgroup analyses showed that treatment efficacy was generally consistent across parity, age, and UI status. However, patients with concomitant UI exhibited relatively smaller improvements in sexual function, as measured by PISQ‐12, compared with those with isolated VRS. This finding may be related to greater baseline pelvic floor dysfunction and the independent negative impact of UI on sexual experience. As tissue remodeling and neuromuscular recovery progressed, these differences diminished over time, with improvements at 3 months becoming comparable to those observed in patients without UI.

Comparison With Other Studies

Two representative studies have previously evaluated the efficacy of FxCO2 treatment for VRS. Lauterbach et al. reported that this therapy significantly improved FSFI and VHI scores in premenopausal women and doubled sexual activity frequency within 3 months post‐treatment. However, the FSFI pain domain was unaffected, and the improvements declined by 6 months [1]. Gao et al. similarly demonstrated improvements in FSFI and VHI scores and applied vaginal tactile imaging (VTI) to assess vaginal wall biomechanical properties [16]. However, this study had a small sample size (n = 29), limited long‐term follow‐up, and only 16 patients completed VTI assessment.

Compared with these studies, the present findings demonstrate both consistency and meaningful extension. With respect to treatment durability, sustained improvements across multiple outcome domains, including FSFI and VHI, were observed up to 12 months post‐treatment, without the decline reported at 6 months by Lauterbach et al. This discrepancy may plausibly be explained by several factors. First, the study population was younger (mean age 35 vs. 47.7 years), potentially conferring greater regenerative capacity of vaginal connective tissue. Second, although different laser systems were used, the energy parameters applied in this study were higher (60–80 mJ/pixel, cumulative energy approximately 1000 J per session vs. 7.5–12.5 mJ/pixel, with total energy not reported), which may have induced a more robust and durable tissue remodeling response.

In addition, this study expands the existing literature by systematically demonstrating, through predefined subgroup analyses, that improvements in sexual function (FSFI), subjective vaginal tightness (VLQ), vaginal health (VHI), and pelvic floor muscle strength were generally consistent regardless of parity, age, or UI status—an aspect not comprehensively addressed in prior investigations.

Implications for Practice

The findings of this study provide clinically relevant insights into the application of FxCO2 therapy for VRS. The observed improvements in sexual function, vaginal health, and subjective vaginal tightness support its role as a non‐surgical therapeutic option for women seeking enhancement of sexual quality of life, particularly those with poor adherence to PFMT or those desiring a relatively rapid functional response.

Importantly, subgroup analyses indicated that favorable outcomes were achieved across different parity groups, age categories, and in patients with mild‐to‐moderate UI. These findings suggest that patient selection for this therapy need not be restricted based on parity, age, or the presence of concomitant mild pelvic floor disorders (such as UI).

Furthermore, FxCO2 therapy was generally well tolerated, with treatment‐related adverse events being mild and transient. Nevertheless, as an energy‐based modality, its clinical use warrants caution. Safety communications issued by the American Urogynecologic Society (AUGS) [17] in 2020 highlighted the current lack of sufficient evidence supporting the use of laser and other energy‐based devices for genitourinary or sexual symptoms, with long‐term safety and efficacy yet to be fully established. Accordingly, treatment should be performed by trained professionals using standardized treatment parameters and structured follow‐up protocols.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as a prospective self‐controlled study, it lacks randomization and blinding. Second, efficacy assessments primarily relied on patient‐reported outcomes, which are inherently subject to subjective bias. Third, certain subjective instruments used in this study, such as the VLQ and SSQ, have not been formally validated specifically for the VRS population, although they are widely applied in existing clinical research [7, 8]. Future studies should prioritize multicenter, large‐scale randomized controlled trials with longer follow‐up periods. In addition, incorporating objective assessments—such as vaginal pressure measurements and imaging‐based evaluation of vaginal wall morphology, thickness, and tissue elasticity—may provide more robust evidence for treatment efficacy and safety. Recent advances in imaging technologies for assessing vaginal wall structural changes may offer promising tools to improve the objectivity of outcome evaluation in studies of energy‐based vaginal therapies [18].

Conclusions

FxCO2 therapy effectively improves sexual function, subjective vaginal tightness, and vaginal health in patients with VRS, with treatment benefits sustained for up to 12 months. These effects were consistently observed across subgroups stratified by parity, age, and UI status. Patient‐reported global improvement and treatment satisfaction were favorable, and adverse events were mild and transient. Further large‐scale, multicenter randomized controlled trials are warranted to confirm the long‐term efficacy and safety of this therapeutic approach.
