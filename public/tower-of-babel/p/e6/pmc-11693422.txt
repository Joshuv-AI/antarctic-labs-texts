Clinically meaningful outcomes in refractory metastatic colorectal cancer: a decade of defining and raising the bar
Authors: M. Fakih, G.W. Prager, J. Tabernero, N. Amellal, E. Calleja, J. Taieb
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Currently, there is no consensus definition for clinically meaningful outcomes in randomized clinical trials (RCTs) designed to evaluate new treatments for patients with refractory metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC). Since 2014, recommended targets for improvements in overall survival and progression-free survival have been published by several societies, including those from the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) Clinically Meaningful Outcomes Working Group in 2014, the European Society for Medical Oncology-Magnitude of Clinical Benefit Scale (ESMO-MCBS) in 2015, and Colorectal Cancer Canada (CCC) consensus statements in 2019. However, evidence from several systematic reviews suggests that in a substantial proportion of RCTs that led to oncology drug approvals, the recommended thresholds of ASCO and ESMO-MCBS were not met. In addition to efficacy and safety, quality of life (QoL) is important to patients with mCRC, especially for those who are receiving later-line therapy or end-of-life care. As such, both ESMO-MCBS and CCC recommend the inclusion of QoL assessments in the design of mCRC clinical trials. Since the publication of the ASCO recommendations in 2014, there has been significant progress in the development of treatment options for patients with refractory mCRC; these include the approvals of trifluridine/tipiracil (FTD/TPI) as a single agent and in combination with bevacizumab, and the approval of fruquintinib. Among the phase III RCTs in third-line mCRC, only the SUNLIGHT trial of FTD/TPI plus bevacizumab met all recommended thresholds for clinically meaningful improvements, while also demonstrating a manageable safety profile and slower deterioration in multiple measures of QoL compared with FTD/TPI alone. The results from the SUNLIGHT study show that incremental gains in several clinically meaningful endpoints are achievable, thus raising the bar in defining clinically meaningful outcomes for emerging therapies in refractory mCRC.

Introduction

When investigating potential new treatment regimens in clinical trials, the assessment of clinical benefit relative to current standards of care (SOCs) can be highly subjective, and there is no consensus definition to confirm when clinical significance has been achieved.1 Previous efforts to define clinically meaningful outcomes in oncology have frequently identified overall survival (OS) as the most appropriate and reliable measure of efficacy1, 2, 3; other outcomes, including progression-free survival (PFS), disease control rate (DCR), and quality of life (QoL), have increasingly been used to help guide clinical decision making.4 In the age of value-based medicine, however, it is imperative that clinical trials are designed not only to demonstrate statistically significant benefits over SOC, but also to demonstrate a sufficient magnitude of benefit to be considered clinically meaningful to both patients and providers.5

In this review, we will discuss consensus metrics for clinically meaningful outcomes in metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC), with a focus on patients with refractory disease, that is, those who have progressed through, or are not candidates for, standard treatment with fluoropyrimidine, oxaliplatin, irinotecan, bevacizumab, and—if indicated—epidermal growth factor receptor inhibitors, and are seeking further treatment to extend the life and maintain symptom control. These metrics include recommended targets from the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) Clinically Meaningful Outcomes Working Group, the European Society for Medical Oncology-Magnitude of Clinical Benefit Scale (ESMO-MCBS), and the Colorectal Cancer Canada (CCC) consensus statements. In addition, we will discuss tools for evaluating health-related QoL (HRQoL) in patients with mCRC and appraise clinical outcomes associated with therapies that have been approved for the treatment of patients with refractory mCRC within the past 10 years to assess how they perform against evolving measures of clinical meaningfulness.

Treatment considerations in colorectal cancer

The choice of treatment for patients with mCRC is generally influenced by tumor and patient characteristics, line of treatment, treatment efficacy and tolerability, QoL, and consideration of individual treatment goals.6

Different stakeholders, such as patients, clinicians, and healthcare regulators, may have different perspectives on what treatment outcomes are of greatest clinical importance. Substantial discrepancies can exist between patient and clinician views on the aims, priorities, and expected life extension of treatments. For example, a therapy that shows a small OS benefit but meaningful improvements in QoL is likely to be accepted by patients, whereas a therapy that prolongs OS compared with SOC but is associated with major toxicities would be less desirable.5

Considering that treatment landscapes are continually evolving and treatment outcomes are improving, it is important to consider what is meant by a clinically meaningful outcome.

Consensus metrics for clinically meaningful outcomes in refractory mCRC

ASCO Clinically Meaningful Outcomes Working Group

In 2014, the ASCO Clinically Meaningful Outcomes Working Group established recommended targets for clinically meaningful outcomes in clinical trials for pancreatic, lung, breast, and colon cancers.3 For clinical trials in patients with refractory colon cancer, the clinically meaningful threshold for improvement in OS as a primary endpoint was defined as 3-5 months, with a target hazard ratio (HR) of ≤0.67 (Table 1). It is important to note that, for patients with mCRC, symptom burdens and treatment goals vary during the continuum of care. In the 10 years since the recommendations of the ASCO Clinically Meaningful Outcomes Working Group were published, many more first- and second-line treatment options have become available, each with the aim of reducing tumor size and delaying progression, thus allowing patients to benefit from later lines of therapy. In these later-line settings, the goals of treatment are more focused on maintaining disease control and improvements in survival and QoL in the absence of significant toxicity.7,8

ESMO-MCBS

The ESMO-MCBS is a validated tool that was developed in 2015 (version 1) and revised in 2017 (version 1.1) for assessing the magnitude of clinical benefit for cancer treatments through the use of a grading system that considers OS, PFS, disease-free survival, HR, response rate, QoL, prognosis, and toxicity (Table 1).2,9 Grading is from A to C in the curative setting and 1 to 5 in the noncurative setting; grades of A and B, or 4 and 5, indicate a substantial magnitude of benefit.2,9 The ESMO-MCBS QoL checklist was also designed to evaluate any QoL benefits measured in clinical trials.10

CCC consensus statements

In 2019, the CCC group developed five consensus statements for assessing the clinical benefit of any drug under consideration for use in chemorefractory mCRC.1 Similar to the ASCO recommendations, the first two CCC consensus statements identified OS as the most important measure of clinically meaningful outcome; the target improvement in OS was defined as ≥2 months and/or an HR of ≤0.75 (Table 1).1 In the third consensus statement, QoL was identified as an important factor, and, in the absence of QoL endpoints, the CCC group recommended that metrics such as quality-adjusted time without symptoms or toxicity (Q-TWiST) or time to deterioration in Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS) are incorporated into the design of randomized clinical trials (RCTs). The fourth statement defined treatment-related toxicities as an important factor and identified asymptomatic laboratory abnormalities as being of lower clinical relevance. In the fifth statement, efforts to incorporate patient and caregiver values and preferences into assessments of clinical benefit were highlighted as an unmet need.1

Thresholds for clinically meaningful outcomes in refractory mCRC clinical trials

According to the World Health Organization Model List of Essential Medicines, treatment with an oncology drug should increase OS by 4-6 months versus SOC in phase III RCTs; however, the magnitude of increase in OS that is considered clinically meaningful is likely to differ between cultures, age groups, and countries.4

It is notable, therefore, that many oncology drug approvals have been based on RCTs in which improvements in median OS were ≤3 months.11 Furthermore, evidence from several systematic reviews suggests that current ASCO and ESMO recommendations regarding clinically meaningful outcomes are not met. In a review of 559 articles published between 2014 and 2015, 53 claims of ‘meaningful benefit’ (of which 49 were in the advanced or metastatic setting) were identified in RCTs.12 Out of 14 of the 49 claims based on median OS, 6 (43%) and 4 (29%) met ASCO and ESMO recommendations, respectively, and among 25 claims that were based on PFS, 17 (68%) met ASCO and ESMO recommendations.12 In a systematic review of 277 RCTs in breast, pancreatic, lung, and colorectal cancers between 2011 and 2015, the median ASCO Value Framework score was 25 (a score of ≥45 is considered substantial benefit) and 38% met the ESMO-MCBS clinical benefit threshold.13 In a further study evaluating RCTs between 2011 and 2015 that had reported statistically significant superiority in the experimental arm in breast cancer, non-small-cell lung cancer, CRC, and pancreatic cancer, 31% met the ESMO-MCBS clinical benefit threshold.14 Together, these data highlight the need for better-defined thresholds of clinically meaningful benefit when designing RCTs.

Efficacy endpoints

OS is considered the gold standard measure of efficacy in most clinical trials in oncology, and expert groups from ASCO, ESMO, and CCC have suggested that an OS improvement of ≥2-3 months is clinically meaningful for patients with mCRC.1, 2, 3 However, in a systematic review of 150 phase III clinical trials that enrolled patients with mCRC between 1986 and 2016, fewer than one-third of the trials reported OS improvements of ≥2 months, with third- and later-line treatments and funding by pharmaceutical companies being negatively associated with such improvements compared with first-line therapies and funding by nonprofit organizations.15 An OS improvement of ≥2 months was less likely to be observed in trials spanning 1997-2016 compared with 1986-1996. Relative to earlier decades, 2007-2016 saw increases in second- and third-line trials, a decrease in first-line trials, increased inclusion of targeted therapies either alone or in combination with cytotoxic therapies, a decrease in novel agents being investigated, a greater likelihood of trials being funded solely by a pharmaceutical company, and a shift from overall response rate to PFS as the most common primary endpoint.15

A separate review of 298 RCTs of systemic treatment in breast cancer, CRC, and non-small-cell lung cancer also showed the increasing use of PFS as a primary endpoint (from 0% in 1995-2004 to 42% in 2010-2020).16 PFS offers a more rapid endpoint assessment than OS, and there is evidence that prolonged PFS is associated with enduring QoL; however, the correlation between treatment effects on OS and PFS is modest.17 Conversely, both duration of disease control and time to treatment failure have been shown to correlate with OS in clinical trials in patients with mCRC,18 although there is some concern that using DCR as an endpoint has the potential to exaggerate the anticancer effect of a treatment.19 Although not reliable surrogates for OS, outcomes such as PFS, DCR, and time to progression provide valuable information about disease activity and are indicative of benefit in cancers for which progressive disease is associated with an increased symptom burden.4

QoL endpoints

Patients with CRC report more comorbidities and poorer QoL compared with patients with other common cancers.20 Problems experienced by patients with CRC include lack of energy, poor sexual functioning, bowel problems, poor body image, emotional problems, and pain or discomfort, all of which have an impact on usual daily activities.21, 22, 23, 24 The economic burden of mCRC is also high, and lost productivity represents a substantial component of this burden, particularly among younger patients.25, 26, 27, 28, 29

Although it is important to integrate QoL from the time of diagnosis and throughout the patient care journey, QoL is particularly important in the management of patients receiving later-line treatment and end-of-life care for mCRC, owing to their limited life expectancy and the balance needed between the benefits and harms of treatment.30 Indeed, an analysis of 31 clinical trials in mCRC showed that in 50% of trials with no significant differences in survival between treatment arms, there were significant changes in HRQoL scores, suggesting HRQoL should be included as a co-primary or composite endpoint alongside tumor-based parameters.31 In addition, patient advocacy groups should be engaged alongside clinicians when developing and validating consensus guidelines for the inclusion of patient-reported outcomes in RCTs.

Safety

Treatment benefits, such as prolonged survival and improved QoL, must always be considered in the context of treatment harms; therefore toxicities are also relevant when assessing clinical benefit, especially when there are no significant differences in efficacy between treatments. The toxicities associated with third- and later-line therapies mean that patient monitoring is needed to minimize the impact on patient QoL as well as maximize the benefit of treatment.41 In this regard, patient education is of vital importance to ensure patients are aware of the advantages and disadvantages of the different treatment options and are included in treatment decisions.

Efficacy

To date, treatment with the combination of FTD/TPI plus bevacizumab in the phase III SUNLIGHT trial has provided the largest magnitude of OS benefit in the refractory setting, reporting a 3.3-month improvement in OS versus FTD/TPI alone [median 10.8 versus 7.5 months; HR 0.61, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.49-0.77; P < 0.001].42 Of note, SUNLIGHT was the first phase III study in the setting of refractory mCRC to demonstrate superior efficacy of a regimen to an existing therapy with proven efficacy in the same patient population. Had FTD/TPI plus bevacizumab been compared against the best supportive care, the magnitude of improvement would likely have been greater.

In terms of clinically meaningful benefit, the ESMO-MCBS scores for regorafenib, FTD/TPI monotherapy, and fruquintinib are 1, 3, and 3, respectively, which do not meet the threshold for substantial benefit. This trend is corroborated by the results from a systematic review of RCTs, which found that only 11% of studies evaluating treatments with noncurative intent for gastrointestinal cancers (CRC and pancreatic cancer) met the ESMO-MCBS meaningful clinical benefit threshold.13

By contrast, outcomes from the SUNLIGHT trial meet the ESMO-MCBS- and ASCO-recommended targets.2,3,9 The combination of FTD/TPI plus bevacizumab has an ESMO-MCBS score of 4, indicating substantial benefit, based on an OS HR of ≤0.65 and OS gain of ≥3 months (control OS ≤12 months), and it also meets the recommended targets proposed by ASCO of a median OS improvement (primary endpoint) of 3-5 months and HR of ≤0.67 and a PFS improvement of 3-5 months.

QoL

In an exploratory subgroup analysis of data from the CORRECT trial, QoL scores based on the EORTC QLQ-C30 and EuroQoL-5 Dimension (EQ-5D) questionnaires were similar between patients in the regorafenib group and the placebo group, both at baseline and at the end of the treatment.48 Similarly, results from a post hoc analysis of the CONCUR trial showed that HRQoL was similar between the regorafenib and placebo groups based on the EORTC QLQ-C30 questionnaire.49

In a post hoc analysis of data from the FRESCO-2 study, HRQoL was not negatively impacted by fruquintinib compared with placebo based on the EORTC QLQ-C30 and EQ-5D-5 Level questionnaires. The median time to deterioration in health utility, as measured by EQ-5D, was improved in the fruquintinib group relative to the placebo group (2.6 versus 1.9 months; HR 0.8, 95% CI 0.6-0.9; P = 0.001).50 Results from a separate post hoc analysis using Q-TWiST showed that fruquintinib increased quality-adjusted survival time by 2.04 months compared with placebo (6.25 versus 4.21 months; 95% CI 1.51-2.57) and had a relative Q-TWiST gain of 34%, thus demonstrating a clinically meaningful quality-adjusted survival benefit.51

In the RECOURSE trial, treatment with FTD/TPI significantly prolonged the median time to deterioration of ECOG PS from 0 or 1 to ≥2 compared with placebo (5.7 versus 4.0 months; HR 0.66, 95% CI 0.56-0.78; P < 0.001)52 and increased quality-adjusted survival time by 1.5 months (5.48 versus 3.98 months; 95% CI 1.49-1.52) using Q-TWiST analysis.53 In the SUNLIGHT trial, patients treated with FTD/TPI with or without bevacizumab showed no increase in symptom burden, as assessed by EORTC QLQ-C30; however, the time from randomization to worsening of ECOG PS from 0 or 1 to ≥2 was longer with FTD/TPI plus bevacizumab than with FTD/TPI alone (9.3 versus 6.3 months; HR 0.54, 95% CI 0.43-0.67).54 A longer preservation of performance status is important because prolonging physical performance and controlling symptoms may allow patients to maintain their physical function and further benefit from subsequent therapy.42 The combination of FTD/TPI plus bevacizumab also resulted in slower deterioration in multiple measures of QoL compared with FTD/TPI alone, including time to deterioration in global health status (median 8.5 versus 4.7 months; HR 0.50, 95% CI 0.38-0.65; P < 0.001).54 As with survival, it is reasonable to hypothesize that the QoL benefit from treatment with FTD/TPI plus bevacizumab may have been greater if the comparator arm had been best supportive care rather than FTD/TPI alone.

Safety

In clinical studies, the most commonly observed regorafenib-related adverse events (AEs) include hand–foot skin reaction, fatigue, diarrhea, and hypertension; dose-limiting toxicities include hand–foot skin reaction, hepatotoxicity, and infections.44,45,55 The most common fruquintinib-related AEs are hypertension, hand–foot skin reaction, asthenia, proteinuria, and diarrhea; potential dose-limiting toxicities include hypertension, hemorrhagic events, hepatotoxicity, proteinuria, and hand–foot skin reaction.46,47,56 The most common FTD/TPI-related AEs are hematologic (neutropenia, leukopenia) or gastrointestinal (nausea/vomiting, diarrhea, decreased appetite) events and fatigue; myelosuppression is a noted dose-limiting toxicity.43,57 The addition of bevacizumab to FTD/TPI is associated with an increased incidence of grade ≥3 neutropenia but not with an increase in the incidence of febrile neutropenia. The safety profile of FTD/TPI plus bevacizumab is consistent with the individual safety profiles of each therapy.42

Although there are guidelines or standardized protocols for the management of some of these AEs, such as the use of granulocyte colony-stimulating factors for neutropenia, management approaches for other AEs may involve a combination of approaches, including prevention, patient education, symptom amelioration, dose intensity management, or switching to better-tolerated regimens.58,59

Conclusions

Since the publication of the ASCO Clinically Meaningful Outcomes Working Group recommendations in 2014, the ESMO-MCBS in 2015, and the CCC consensus statements in 2019, significant progress has been made in the development of treatment options for patients with refractory mCRC, including the approval of single-agent FTD/TPI in 2015 and both fruquintinib and FTD/TPI plus bevacizumab in 2023. In the SUNLIGHT trial, the combination of FTD/TPI plus bevacizumab delivered both quantitative and qualitative benefits to patients, providing clinically meaningful improvements in survival per existing recommendations, as well as additional clinically meaningful outcomes, including prolonged physical performance and symptom control, along with a predictable and manageable safety profile.

The results of the SUNLIGHT study show that incremental improvements in multiple clinically meaningful endpoints are achievable and serve to both raise and set the benchmark for defining clinically meaningful outcomes in the treatment of refractory mCRC.

Future perspectives

The scope of this review is limited to discussion of clinical endpoints, such as OS, included in the pivotal phase III clinical trials for refractory mCRC in the third-line setting that led to drug approvals in this patient population. This review does not take into account outcomes from the real-world setting, as these data were not available. Results from real-world studies are of great value in providing a robust assessment of outcomes among various patient subgroups, including those not meeting RCT eligibility criteria. Furthermore, results from real-world studies are instrumental in providing data for the real-world effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of approved treatments. Additional analyses could support a more robust definition of clinical meaningfulness by including economic and patient-centered outcomes alongside real-world clinical data.
