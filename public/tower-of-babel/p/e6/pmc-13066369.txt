Study on the high-temperature static and dynamic constitutive model of silicon carbide-modified concrete
Authors: Jinhua Wang, Qingwei Chen, He Huang, Tao Chen, Biao Ren, Guogang Wang, Yixing Liu, Erlei Bai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Based on experimental investigations of concrete subjected to silicon carbide (SIC) modification, high-temperature exposure, and high-strain-rate loading, this study introduces the concept of a concrete damage factor. A probabilistic damage constitutive model for silicon carbide-modified concrete under elevated temperatures was developed, formulated according to the Weibull distribution within the framework of damage probability distribution theory. The study first examines the modification mechanism of silicon carbide under coupled high-temperature and high-strain-rate damage conditions. Seven statistical parameters were identified to characterize the individual and coupled influences of silicon carbide content, temperature, and strain rate. Subsequently, a general constitutive model for concrete was formulated. Following this foundation, modified constitutive relations were systematically established. The Type I modified models account for the individual effects of each factor. The Type II modified models address the coupled effects of factor pairs, including specifically the high-temperature constitutive model and the dynamic constitutive model for silicon carbide-modified concrete. Finally, the Type III modified model was developed, incorporating the simultaneous coupling of all three factors—representing the high-temperature dynamic constitutive model for silicon carbide-modified concrete.

Introduction

Research findings from scholars indicated that as temperature increased, calcium silicate hydrate, ettringite, and calcium hydroxide in concrete progressively decomposed1,2. The extent of decomposition in the cement paste was found to intensify with rising temperature. Upon reaching 800 °C, the cement paste had almost completely decomposed and no longer contributed to strength, while the strength of the coarse aggregate gradually declined3. To address this issue, modified concrete emerged as a significant research focus. Among the materials investigated, silicon carbide , which was characterized by its high-temperature stability, excellent corrosion resistance, and low thermal expansion coefficient, was demonstrated to substantially enhance the overall performance of concrete.

The constitutive relationship describes the quantitative relationship between externally applied loads and the corresponding internal responses in materials, encompassing stress–strain behavior and the correlation between external forces and failure criteria4. As a complex structural material, concrete demonstrates both stochastic characteristics and statistically predictable patterns in its mechanical properties, which are governed by its microstructural characteristics and strength architecture. The multi-scale nature of the "coarse aggregate-fine aggregate-cement matrix" system leads to the simultaneous manifestation of anisotropic and isotropic behaviors at different observational scales. Furthermore, manufacturing processes and cement hydration mechanisms determine the material’s complex "solid–liquid-gas" tri-phase composition, heterogeneity resulting from multiple chemical compounds with distinct properties, and nonlinear behavior arising from spatial discontinuities such as pores and microcracks. The methodologies for establishing constitutive relationships of impact-resistant concrete could be broadly categorized into three types: empirical fitting functions to derive constitutive models, constitutive models requiring numerical simulation software for solution, and constitutive models based on the fundamental principles of damage mechanics. Commonly employed models included the Maxwell model, the Holmquist–Johnson–Cook (HJC) model、Zhu–Wang–Tang (ZWT) model, among others5–8. Ezeldin9 systematically investigated the compressive behavior of plain concrete and steel fiber-reinforced concrete, proposing a single-parameter phenomenological constitutive model through finite element analysis. Holmquist and Johnson10 summarized four single or composite constitutive models, including the Holmquist-Johnson–Cook model they developed, compiled fixed parameters for various constitutive models, unified the formulations of several different models, and compared them with experimental results from cylindrical specimens. Bazant11 established a novel creep constitutive model, which was continuously refined and adjusted to incorporate variables such as temperature and strain rate. Subsequent improvements led to the development of a microplane constitutive model for concrete12, which calculated the projection of the strain tensor onto a microplane based on the principle of virtual work. A seventh updated version of the microplane model for plain concrete was later presented13. Hu Shisheng14 proposed a damage evolution equation and a damage-based linear viscoelastic constitutive model for concrete based on compiled experimental data. Gan15 studied the macroscopic mechanical properties and evolution of microscopic pore structure of plain fiber, single-admixture, and fiber-reinforced concrete under the combined action of 5% sulfate solution and freeze–thaw cycles, establishing and validating a macro-meso damage model for fiber-reinforced concrete. Lai16 introduced a new universal stress–strain model for post-fire concrete. Kong17 enhanced a constitutive model by refining the yield surface, incorporating tensile damage, introducing Lode angle dependency, and improving the strain rate effect. The modified HJC model was subsequently implemented in the finite element software LS-DYNA via a user-defined material model. Chen18 summarized existing dynamic material test data for geopolymer concrete (GPC) and compared it with widely used dynamic constitutive models for ordinary Portland cement concrete (OPC), discussing the applicability of these models for modeling GPC structures. New material constants for GPC strength models, equations of state, and dynamic increase factors were derived based on available GPC material test data, followed by numerical modeling. Li19 discussed the effects of steel fibers and polypropylene fibers on the damage evolution laws of fiber-reinforced concrete (FRC) and their underlying mechanisms. The study provided a comprehensive review of existing empirical and theoretical damage constitutive models for plain concrete and FRC, with a focus on meso-scale damage constitutive models for FRC based on composite homogenization and mixture theory.

Although modified HJC models and probabilistic damage models have been successfully applied to concrete under dynamic or thermal loading, they typically introduce temperature effects, strain-rate effects, and material modification through separate empirical correction terms. Such formulations may not naturally capture the interaction among these mechanisms within a unified damage evolution framework. Building upon experimental findings from concrete specimens subjected to silicon carbide modification, elevated temperatures, and high-strain-rate loading, this chapter introduces the conceptual framework of a concrete damage factor. Subsequently, we develop a damage constitutive model for silicon carbide-modified concrete that captures both static and dynamic mechanical behavior at high temperatures, formulated within the theoretical framework of damage probability distribution and obeying the Weibull distribution.

Experiment

Experimental equipments and procedures

The experimental materials included cement, crushed stone, sand, water, superplasticizer, and silicon carbide. Ordinary Portland cement (P.O 42.5R early-strength grade) was used as the cementitious material. The coarse aggregates were continuously graded crushed limestone with a maximum nominal particle size of 20 mm, blended at a ratio of 8:2 for large to small stone sizes. Natural sand from the Bahe River, with a fineness modulus between 2.3 and 3.0, was used as the fine aggregate. Tap water from Baqiao District of Xi’an, was employed for mixing. A polycarboxylate-based high-range water-reducing agent produced by Shanghai Chenqi Chemical Co., Ltd., with a water reduction rate of 28%, was utilized. The silicon carbide additive had a density of 3.2 g/cm3 and a purity of over 99%. Compared with the dynamic test results at room temperature, the peak stress of the specimens generally increases at 200 °C and 400 °C. This trend is consistent with findings from high-temperature static mechanical tests, suggesting that within a certain temperature range, elevated temperatures can accelerate the cement hydration process, enhance the hardened strength of the cement paste, and improve the pore structure of the concrete. In contrast, at 600 °C, the peak stress generally decreases compared to the dynamic mechanical results at room temperature, which aligns with the conclusions drawn from high-temperature static tests. This indicates that the concrete’s mechanical properties are degraded at this temperature. It is worth noting, however, that when the fineness of silicon carbide is 400 mesh, the peak stress at 600 °C shows only a minor reduction or even a slight increase. This phenomenon diverges from the observations in high-temperature static tests and implies that the coupled effects of high temperature and high strain rate may enhance the modifying efficiency of coarser silicon carbide.

Research on dynamic constitutive modeling

Failure mechanisms

Concrete damage and failure theory

The fundamental concept of concrete damage and failure theory treats concrete as a composite material comprising numerous simple microstructural elements combined geometrically. The strength of these microstructural elements was assumed to follow a specific probability distribution, such as the Weibull or normal distribution21.

On one hand, from the perspective of damage and failure at the microstructural level, when an external load was applied to the concrete, the entire material was considered to respond in a uniform mechanical state. The failure or survival of each microstructural element depended on its individual load-bearing capacity. That is, the number of damaged elements corresponded to those whose strength was lower than the current stress response required by the applied load. Since the strength of the microstructural elements followed a probabilistic distribution, the extent of damage in the concrete under a given mechanical state could be described using the corresponding cumulative distribution function.

On the other hand, from a macroscopic viewpoint based on the stress–strain relationship, the degree of damage in concrete could be incorporated into the correction of macroscopic mechanical properties through the definition of damage variables and the concept of effective stress. The corresponding correction formula is expressed as follows:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varepsilon = \frac{\sigma }{{E^{\prime}}} = \frac{{\sigma^{\prime}}}{E} = \frac{\sigma }{(1 - D)E}$$\end{document}where ε is strain, σ is nominal stress, σ’ is effective stress, E’ is the elastic modulus of the damaged material, E is the elastic modulus of the undamaged material, D is the damage variable and the ratio of the number of damaged microstructural elements to the total number of microstructural elements, which can be described using a specific probability distribution function.

The constitutive model for concrete was constructed by integrating both the microscopic and macroscopic perspectives described above. In this study, the model was developed based on damage and failure theory. A key contribution of the approach was the incorporation of a damage factor that linked the microstructural characteristics of concrete to its macroscopic mechanical response. The resulting model effectively captured the influence of internal micro-damage mechanisms on the macroscopic behavior of concrete. In selecting the probability distribution for micro-element strength, the Weibull distribution was adopted. The Weibull probability density function is a differentiable function of higher order, is expressed in a simple analytical form, and its cumulative distribution shape resembles the progressive nature of damage distribution in quasi-brittle materials7,22,23. By incorporating the probabilistic distribution of damage, different types of constitutive models could be distinguished, with various influencing factors reflected in the parameters of the probability density function. This approach effectively bridged macroscopic mechanical behavior with underlying microscopic mechanisms, offering a method that was both conceptually straightforward and practically applicable. In comparison to constitutive models derived from extensive microstructural analysis, the proposed method was simpler and computationally more efficient. Furthermore, unlike purely empirical parameter fitting, the incorporation of microstructural modification mechanisms into the definition of the damage probability rendered the present approach more physically meaningful.

Silicon carbide damage and modification mechanism

SEM observations indicate that SiC incorporation reduces pore connectivity, refines the interfacial transition zone, and alters microcrack propagation paths. Fine SiC particles primarily act as pore-filling and matrix-densifying agents, whereas coarser particles promote crack deflection and crack-bridging effects. These microstructural features provide direct experimental support for the assumed modification mechanisms adopted in the damage constitutive model.

Model statistics

The constitutive relationship examined in this study primarily addressed the stress–strain behavior and strength of concrete. The independent variables incorporated three factors: the content and fineness of SiC, temperature, and strain rate. Given the complexity of their interactions, it was essential to elucidate the statistical behavior of the model under both isolated and combined effects of these variables prior to establishing the constitutive model. Specifically, the analysis focused on concrete strength and the interrelationships among these influencing factors.

(1) σ₀: static compressive strength of plain concrete at ambient temperature.

This parameter represented the static compressive strength of plain concrete under ambient temperature conditions. To account for variations in the specimen casting process, a certain margin of error was permitted for σ₀ at a 95% assurance rate.

(2) σ₁: static compressive strength of silicon carbide-modified concrete at ambient temperature.

This parameter denoted the static compressive strength at ambient temperature of a concrete specimen, which was originally characterized by a static compressive strength of σ₀, after being modified with silicon carbide at specified combinations of content and fineness.11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma_{1} = SEF \times \sigma_{0}$$\end{document}

(3) σ₂: high-temperature static compressive strength of plain concrete.

This parameter represented the high-temperature static compressive strength of a plain concrete specimen, which initially possessed an ambient-temperature static compressive strength of σ₀, after exposure to specified thermal loading conditions.12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma_{2} = TIF \times \sigma_{0}$$\end{document}

(4) σ₃: dynamic peak stress of plain concrete at ambient temperature.

This parameter represented the dynamic compressive strength at ambient temperature of a plain concrete specimen, which initially possessed a static compressive strength of σ₀, after being subjected to various strain rate loadings.13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma_{3} = DIF \times \sigma_{0}$$\end{document}

The three aforementioned statistical measures were obtained by multiplying σ₀ by a corresponding coefficient. The values of these coefficients were related to the content and fineness of silicon carbide, temperature, and strain rate, respectively, and could be determined using empirical formulas and experimental data. This modeling approach was designated as the Type I constitutive model.

(5) σ₄: high-temperature static compressive strength of silicon carbide-modified concrete.

This parameter represented the high-temperature static compressive strength of silicon carbide-modified concrete, which initially possessed an ambient-temperature static compressive strength of σ₁, after exposure to specified thermal loadings. The value of σ₄ was derived from the combined effect of σ₁ and σ₂, incorporating a correction through the silicon carbide temperature enhancement factor. This parameter represented the dynamic compressive strength at ambient temperature of silicon carbide-modified concrete, which initially possessed a static compressive strength of σ₁ under ambient conditions, after being subjected to dynamic loading at various strain rates. The value of σ₅ was derived from the combined effects of σ₁ and σ₃, with corrections applied using the silicon carbide dynamic enhancement factor. The corresponding correction coefficient was calculated as follows:15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$DIF_{S} = \frac{{\sigma_{5} (\dot{\varepsilon },a,b) - (SEF \times \sigma_{0} )DIF}}{{(SEF \times \sigma_{0} )DIF}}$$\end{document}

The two aforementioned statistical measures were formulated by simultaneously considering two specific parameters from the Type I constitutive model, and were therefore designated as the Type II constitutive model.

(7) σ₆: high-temperature dynamic compressive strength of silicon carbide-modified concrete.

This parameter represented the high-temperature dynamic compressive strength of silicon carbide-modified concrete—which initially possessed an ambient-temperature static compressive strength of σ₁—under the combined effects of elevated temperatures and dynamic strain rate loadings. The value of σ₆ could be obtained through any of the following three approaches: first, by directly coupling σ₁, σ₂, and σ₃; second, by correcting σ₄ using the silicon carbide dynamic enhancement factor; third, by correcting σ₅ using the silicon carbide high-temperature enhancement factor. The corresponding correction coefficient was termed the Silicon Carbide High-Temperature Dynamic Enhancement Factor (TDF). As this coefficient embodied the coupling of the three aforementioned Type I constitutive models, the resulting formulation was classified as the Type III constitutive model. In this context, a TDF value of zero indicated that the strain rate had no effect on the modification of the high-temperature mechanical properties of silicon carbide-modified concrete. Conversely, a negative TDF value implied that the strain rate diminished the modifying effect of silicon carbide under high-temperature conditions.

Third-type constitutive model

The Type III constitutive model was developed by integrating all three factors discussed previously, resulting in the high-temperature dynamic constitutive model for SiC-modified concrete. Consequently, this model incorporated the most variables and exhibited the highest complexity in its formulation. The constitutive model did not account for potential alterations in the modifying effects of SiC under simultaneous high-temperature and high-strain-rate conditions. Nevertheless, it maintained considerable predictive accuracy by explicitly incorporating the individual modifying effects under isolated high-temperature and high-strain-rate loading regimes. In this framework, the DIFS value quantified the modifying effect of SiC on concrete under high strain rates, while the TIFS value characterized the corresponding effect under elevated temperatures. The interaction between temperature-induced microcracking and strain-rate strengthening is captured through their joint influence on damage evolution within the Weibull probability framework, rather than by direct empirical fitting of macroscopic stress–strain curves.

Conclusions

This study investigated the evolution of dynamic mechanical properties in silicon carbide-modified concrete under high-temperature conditions, elucidated the underlying strength modification mechanisms, and analyzed the development of stress–strain relationships under combined high-temperature and high-strain-rate loading. A damage probability constitutive model based on damage mechanics and following the Weibull distribution was established. The main conclusions are summarized as follows:By introducing the silicon carbide high-temperature enhancement coefficient and silicon carbide dynamic enhancement coefficient to replace the conventional high-temperature and dynamic strength growth factors, respectively, decoupling of the initial strength contribution of silicon carbide to concrete’s fundamental mechanical properties was achieved. This approach facilitated the formulation of a high-temperature dynamic constitutive model for silicon carbide-modified concrete.Purely empirical curve-fitting methods demonstrated limitations in capturing the internal structural modification mechanisms induced by silicon carbide, while purely theoretical derivations proved highly complex and struggled to establish clear micro–macro relationships. The damage model effectively reconciled these limitations by incorporating various influencing factors into its probability distribution function, establishing a rational constitutive framework through the relationship between damage factor and macroscopic mechanical responses.The developed Weibull-based damage probability constitutive model separately accounted for the effects of silicon carbide content, temperature, and strain rate, generating specific constitutive relations under corresponding environmental conditions. Results demonstrated that the model accurately simulated the constitutive behavior of silicon carbide-modified concrete under coupled high-temperature and high-strain-rate conditions.Both Type I constitutive model, which considering individual factor effects, and Type II model, which incorporating coupled effects of factor pairs, successfully decoupled the three influencing variables during model construction. For the Type III model addressing simultaneous coupling of all three factors under high-temperature dynamic conditions, despite its complex formulation with numerous variables, direct solution of coupled equations derived from the Type II model still yielded results showing good agreement with experimental data.
