Demography and Environment Shapes Genetic Variation: Spatiotemporal Genetic Dynamics in Cyclic Voles at Low Latitudes
Authors: Julio C. Domínguez, Javier Viñuela, Pedro P. Olea, María Calero‐Riestra, Jesús T. García
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTUnderstanding how genetic diversity persists in populations subject to extreme demographic fluctuations and recurrent bottlenecks is a central question in evolutionary and population ecology. Cyclic species provide ideal models for investigating this ecological puzzle, as they allow testing how demographic phases shape genetic dynamics. Here, we examine spatial and temporal variation in neutral genetic diversity in two populations of the common vole ( Microtus arvalis ) inhabiting agricultural landscapes in northern Spain. Using microsatellite data collected over 5 years and eight sampling events spanning different phases of the cycle, we show that genetic diversity remained consistently high despite > 200‐fold fluctuations in abundance, although trajectories differed between sites and cycle phases. Demographic crashes caused transient reductions—particularly in allelic richness—but genetic diversity was restored within one to two generations. Our results indicate that the short duration of bottlenecks limited the opportunity for drift, while immigration was the predominant driver of post‐collapse recovery. Gains of novel alleles during recovery phases, rather than vole reproduction, best explained increases in genetic diversity. Our results demonstrate that genetic structure is highly dynamic, varying predictably with cyclical temporal transitions rather than simple spatial heterogeneity. While demographic cycles drive regionally consistent patterns of genetic diversity and gene flow dynamics across sites, local spatiotemporal contingencies cause deviations during specific phases. These patterns reflect site‐specific variation in genetic connectivity, movement dynamics and ecological pressures, including predation. Gene flow and genetic relatedness also varied across scales and phases: local movements between field margins and into crops peaked during population increase, reducing relatedness and enhancing genetic mixing, whereas long‐distance immigration ceased during decline, allowing relatedness to recover. Together, these dynamics reflect a combination of short‐distance redistribution and long‐distance immigration, and point to the increase phase as the key period when voles engage in effective dispersal within agroecosystems. Finally, our findings provide partial empirical support for the Charnov‐Finerty model of density‐dependent dispersal and gene flow, while underscoring the limitations of both the Charnov‐Finerty and Lambin‐Krebs models in accounting for spatial heterogeneity and asynchronous dynamics. Overall, we identify immigration as a critical buffer against genetic erosion in cyclic populations.

Introduction

Classical population genetics theory typically assumes demographical stability, whereby neutral genetic variation reaches equilibrium through a balance between drift and gene flow (Slatkin 1993; Wright 1931). In reality, however, wild populations frequently experience substantial size fluctuations driven by environmental variability or anthropogenic pressures (Haddad et al. 2015; Pasinelli et al. 2011). Such fluctuations intensify the effects of drift in small populations (Nei et al. 1975; Slatkin and Excoffier 2012) and can promote divergence when gene flow is restricted (Ascensão et al. 2016; Vandewoestijne et al. 2008).

Cyclic populations, with their natural oscillating demography, provide a natural test of these dynamics. While theory predicts loss of genetic diversity during bottlenecks and increased differentiation due to intensified drift (Motro and Thomson 1982; Nei et al. 1975), empirical studies often reveal otherwise: many cyclic species persist with high genetic diversity (Berthier et al. 2005, 2006; Ehrich and Stenseth 2001). This pattern suggests that recurrent migration during population outbreaks buffers genetic losses and rescues local populations, mitigating classical bottleneck effects (Burton et al. 2002; Stringer et al. 2024).

Moreover, increasing evidence shows minimal genetic differentiation among local populations of cyclic species at landscape or regional scales (Domínguez et al. 2021, 2023; James et al. 2015), reinforcing the crucial role of migration not only for preserving genetic diversity, but also for synchronizing population dynamics across landscapes (Jangjoo et al. 2016; Myers 2018). However, empirical support for this remains limited, largely due to methodological constraints: most studies rely on single‐site or single‐time genetic samplings (Basto et al. 2016; Bergamaschi and Lama 2015), which risks overlooking spatial and temporal variability. Notably, the ecological and evolutionary processes shaping genetic patterns may vary with the phase of the population cycle, adding further complexity. Even modest shifts in sampling scale over kilometres or weeks can profoundly alter estimates of genetic composition and structure in outbreaking populations (Gauffre et al. 2014; Larroque et al. 2019). Approaches that integrate spatial and temporal replication are therefore essential for capturing the full dynamics of systems subject to rapid and recurrent demographic change.

Among vertebrates, voles (Microtus spp.) have emerged as model organisms for studying population cycles since Elton's seminal work in 1924 (Elton 1924). Since then, extensive research has highlighted their highly cyclical dynamics, typically showing 3–5‐year periodicity with sharp (commonly around 100‐fold) fluctuations in density between low and peak phases (Krebs and Myers 1974), with exceptional cases above 400‐fold (Krebs 2013). The high fecundity and short generation times of voles (Martínková et al. 2013; García et al. 2020) allow for the study of several full cycles within short time frames (Myers 2018). Moreover, their ecological significance particularly in agroecosystems where they are common pests—adds applied relevance (Jacob et al. 2014; Rodríguez‐Pastor et al. 2016; Giraudoux et al. 2019). Despite extensive research on demographic drivers of vole cycles, relatively little is known about their genetic consequences under fluctuating density regimes (Norén and Angerbjörn 2014; Row et al. 2016).

Two conceptual models have proposed divergent predictions regarding migration and genetic diversity in cyclic systems. The Charnov–Finerty model (C–F; Charnov and Finerty 1980) posits density‐dependent migration, with dispersal increasing with population size and reducing relatedness at high densities, whereas reduced dispersal at low densities is expected to increase relatedness and promote genetic erosion. Conversely, the Lambin–Krebs model (L–K; Lambin and Krebs 1991) suggests that immigration decreases with density while relatedness increases due to female philopatry, dynamics that may facilitate genetic rescue under low‐density conditions, when increased immigration enhances gene flow (Brown and Kodric‐Brown 1977; Rikalainen et al. 2012). Both models highlight migration as a key driver of spatial population dynamics and its potential to buffer the effects of genetic drift, thereby enhancing evolutionary potential (Ebert et al. 2002). These dynamics may ultimately help explain not only genetic patterns but also the mechanisms underlying population cycles themselves (Liebhold et al. 2004; Myers and Cory 2013).

Notably, most empirical studies examining genetic patterns under changing spatiotemporal and demographic conditions have focused on northern latitudes (García‐Navas et al. 2015; Norén and Angerbjörn 2014; Oli 2019; Pilot et al. 2010), characterized by highly seasonal climates and predator‐driven dynamics. However, recent evidence suggests that recruitment rather than predator‐induced mortality may be the primary driver of population cycles in some northern systems, highlighting the importance of bottom‐up processes in shaping demographic fluctuations (e.g., Lambin et al. 2025). Southern‐latitudes, however, offer a complementary perspective, as they combine milder climates and different seasonality with different predator communities and often more heterogeneous landscapes, all of which can shape both the demography and the evolutionary consequences of population cycles in unique ways (Lambin et al. 2006). In particular, less constrained breeding seasons and more variable environmental conditions may lead to different recruitment dynamics compared to northern systems, potentially altering the timing and magnitude of population growth. Such demographic differences can translate into contrasting dispersal regimes. In high‐seasonal systems, long‐distance movements are often constrained to narrow temporal windows (e.g., spring snowmelt), creating pulses of dispersal that strongly influence population connectivity (Lambin et al. 2006). In contrast, conditions in southern regions may favour more continuous or opportunistic dispersal, thereby altering both the timing and magnitude of gene flow, as observed in southwestern water vole ( Arvicola sapidus ) in southern Spain, which maintain surprisingly high genetic connectivity despite fragmented habitat (Centeno‐Cuadros et al. 2011).

In the Iberian Peninsula, the common vole Microtus arvalis asturianus represents an endemic clade (García et al. 2020) which has recently developed cyclical demography under distinctive climatic and ecological conditions (Luque‐Larena et al. 2013; Mougeot et al. 2019; Olea et al. 2024) but is highly variable at the regional level (Caminero‐Saldaña et al. 2024), thus contrasting with the more regular amplitude and periodicity of long‐established cycles in northern conspecifics (Hansson and Henttonen 1985). Previous genetic research indicates no overall loss of genetic diversity in highly heterogeneous landscapes, suggesting that high genetic connectivity, recurrent migration during peak phases and large N e may act as compensatory mechanisms maintaining genetic diversity despite habitat fragmentation (Domínguez et al. 2021, 2023). This system therefore provides an opportunity to test whether relationships between demographic phases, migration patterns and genetic parameters observed in the north persist—or diverge—when key ecological drivers are modified.

Here, we combine longitudinal demographic and genetic monitoring across two spatial replicates to uncover the mechanisms underpinning genetic variation in this system. Across 6 years, vole populations experienced three marked demographic bottlenecks (2012, 2015 and 2017; Figure 1). We focused on the cycle of greatest amplitude (2013–2015), which encompassed phases of post‐crash recovery, peak abundance and subsequent decline, and contrasted it with post‐recovery genetic metrics in 2016 (second cycle), whose reduced amplitude likely reflected a drought‐induced suppression of population growth (Olea et al. 2024). We tested whether changes in genetic diversity across the cycle aligned more closely with proxies of genetic drift (population size) or gene flow (arrival of novel alleles). We also evaluated relatedness shifts to assess which theoretical model—C–F or L–K—best matched empirical data. By integrating fine‐scale temporal data with spatial replication, our study fills a key empirical gap in understanding how demographic phases and migration interact to shape genetic patterns in a cyclic system under southern‐latitude conditions.

Materials and Methods

Organism Model and Study Sites

We conducted this study in the province of Palencia, located in the centre of the Duero basin—a predominantly agricultural region within the Castilla y León plateau in northwestern Spain. We sampled two local populations of the common voles separated by 14 km: Boada de Campos (Site 1; 41°59′ N, 4°54′ W) and Revilla de Campos (Site 2; 41°60′ N, 4°42′ W). These locations are representative of the region's agricultural landscape where vole outbreaks frequently reach regional maxima with demographic traits similar to northern cycles (Caminero‐Saldaña et al. 2024; Mougeot et al. 2019; Santamaría et al. 2019; Vidal et al. 2009). The distance between sites ensures demographic independence at the study scale, while maintaining climatic and habitat homogeneity given the species' typical dispersal range of 100–500 m and occasional movements in favourable conditions up to 1 km (Boyce and Boyce 1988; Kauer et al. 2024). The ecological characteristics of these areas, described in detail elsewhere (Planillo et al. 2023; Santamaría et al. 2019), are briefly summarized here: Site 1 covered an area of 6.23 km2 and Site 2, 5.25 km2, both dominated by agricultural land under moderate‐to‐high agricultural intensification. Traditionally, land use followed an extensive 2‐year fallow–cereal rotation system, primarily wheat (Triticum spp.) and barley ( Hordeum vulgare ). However, recent irrigation developments and reduced tillage practices (MAGRAMA 2013) have led to a more heterogeneous landscape. This includes irrigated crops such as alfalfa ( Medicago sativa ), sugar beet ( Beta vulgaris var. saccharifera), maize ( Zea mays ) and winter cereals such as rye ( Secale cereale ) and oat ( Avena sativa ), while fallows have become increasingly scarce. The region experienced a continental Mediterranean climate characterized by cold winters and hot, dry summers. The study area is included in the experimental biological control programmes of common vole pests, based on provisioning of next boxes for common kestrels ( Falco tinnunculus ) and barn owls ( Tyto Alba ). Site 1 is an experimental area holding 100 nest‐boxes and Site 2 a control area without nest‐boxes (see Jareño et al. 2023 for additional details).

Demographic Monitoring and Genetic Sampling

We conducted longitudinal demographic and genetic monitoring from 2013 to 2017, covering two successive population cycles (Figure 1a). We live‐trapped voles using Sherman traps deployed in both field margins and alfalfa fields, following Planillo et al. (2023). Sampling occurred 1–3 times per year, typically in late October‐early November, late March and late June‐early July, to coincide with agricultural phenology (crop sowing, growing and harvesting, respectively). Because our interest in this study was to assess the link between population size and genetic parameters, we spaced the sampling during periods of stationary (low) population (e.g., extended periods of low population density). Overall, we sampled voles eight times (cross‐sectional samplings) over the 5‐year period: autumn 2013, spring, summer and autumn 2014, spring 2015, summer and autumn 2016 and autumn 2017 (Figure 1a). Due to extremely low abundance in spring 2015 (only 14 individuals captured across both sites), sampling was temporarily halted until summer 2016. During this interval, we continued periodic field surveys and collected environmental and habitat data, confirming that vole densities remained too low for genetic sampling. Sampling resumed once extensive field signs of activity (such as active runways and fresh burrow systems) consistently indicated a post‐bottleneck population recovery across the agrarian matrix. This empirical threshold was operationally essential to ensure that our intensive trapping effort would yield sufficient spatial replication across landscape units to satisfy our repeated‐measures statistical framework (see below).

We optimized the sampling design to maximize captures by targeting stable substrates with suitable habitat. Therefore, we focused trapping on field margins—permanent strips of land that serve as refuges for voles and potential dispersal corridors—as well as within alfalfa fields (multi‐annual permanent crops; Santamaría et al. 2019). In total, 98 different field margins (54 in Site 1 and 44 in Site 2) and 11 alfalfa fields (6 in Site 1 and 5 in Site 2) were sampled repeatedly (Figure 1b). Within each field and along each margin, we used five trapping points, each consisting of 10 Sherman live traps (H.B. Sherman Traps Inc., Florida, USA), for a total of 50 traps per field or margin. To maximize trapping effectiveness, each trapping point (a group of 10 traps) was placed at active burrow systems (vole colonies) showing signs of recent activity (Santamaría et al. 2019). When no active burrows were visible, traps were placed at inactive burrow systems or randomly distributed within the field or margin. To identify fine‐scale temporal trends in genetic parameters, we aimed to sample the same units (fields and margins) at each time point. However, due to local extinctions or near‐zero abundances, not all units could be sampled during every event. After filtering out units with insufficient temporal replication, our analyses included 65 margins (32 in Site 1, 33 in Site 2) and 9 alfalfa fields (5 in Site 1, 4 in Site 2) (Figure 1b). Of these, 57% were sampled in at least five trapping sessions.

Because vole abundance can vary over very short time periods, we implemented an intensive sampling scheme to ensure data comparability between the two study sites and across sampling events. Each sampling session lasted four consecutive days—2 days per site—with traps set at dusk and checked the following morning (~12 h active). Capture rate (voles captured per 100 trap‐nights) served as a proxy for local abundance (Planillo et al. 2023). Sampling effort was consistent across sites, with 3200–3700 traps set per night during each sampling session. All trapping locations were georeferenced using GPS devices, and tissue samples were collected (ear clipping) and stored at −20°C for genetic analyses.

Microsatellite Genotyping and Statistical Analyses

We extracted genomic DNA from 3351 ear samples and genotyped individuals at nine polymorphic microsatellite loci following Domínguez et al. (2021). The size of the PCR products was determined at the Unidad de Genómica (Parque Científico de Madrid‐Universidad Complutense de Madrid, Spain). Electropherograms were scored using Geneious v10.2.3 (https://www.geneious.com), and 10% of all genotypes were re‐amplified and re‐scored to assess genotyping accuracy. We screened for allele dropouts and scoring errors using Micro‐Checker (Oosterhout et al. 2004), and assessed null alleles following Brookfield (1996) using PopGenReport v3.0.4 (Adamack and Gruber 2014).

Defining Population Cycle Phases

Accurately determining demographic phases in cyclic populations is inherently challenging, particularly in the absence of continuous high‐resolution monitoring (e.g., weekly or fortnightly trapping). For instance, our data indicate that the summer 2014 sampling coincided with a population peak (Figure 1a; Olea et al. 2024). However, it remains unclear whether this peak had already occurred prior to sampling (i.e., the population was already in decline) or occurred shortly afterward (i.e., the population was still increasing at the time of sampling, as the next sampling point was in November when the population abundance was significantly lower). Similarly, we cannot confirm the precise timing of the population peak in 2016, although independent surveys in the same region placed it in November (Jacob et al. 2020; Mougeot et al. 2019). Given that each genetic sampling acts as a time‐stamped record—capturing demographic and genetic changes occurring between two consecutive time points—we inferred two broader demographic phases based on changes in capture rate: an increase phase (autumn 2013–summer 2014) and a decrease phase (summer 2014–spring 2015) (Figure 1a). Within these phases, we defined four finer‐grained periods: P1 (early increase—autumn 2013 to spring 2014); P2 (late increase—spring to summer 2014); P3 (early decrease—summer to autumn 2014) and P4 (late decrease—autumn 2014 to spring 2015). These periods formed the basis of our main analyses, although period P4 was excluded from some analyses due to insufficient sample size. We focused on the first population cycle for the main analyses, as sampling was complete and regularly spaced, making the observed patterns less susceptible to biases outside our control. Additionally, we included a fifth period (summer–autumn 2016) for specific comparisons (see private alleles analyses), capturing the recovery of the population after the demographic bottleneck at the end of the first cycle (see Figure 1a).

Spatio‐Temporal Genetic Diversity and Relatedness Metrics in Relation to Population Size and Immigration

For each cross‐sectional sampling, we calculated unbiased expected heterozygosity (uHe) and allelic richness rarefied to a standard sample size (Ar) using the ‘diveRsity’ v1.1.9 package (Keenan et al. 2013). These diversity metrics are corrected for sample size, making them comparable across sites and sampling events. All metrics were calculated at the field margin scale. For visualization purposes, values were averaged across margins to obtain site‐level metrics per sampling event, and plotted with ggplot2 (Wickham 2011). To assess trends, we computed the temporal change in diversity (ΔuHe, ΔAr) at the margin level as the difference in metric values between each sampling and the previous one. We modelled these changes as response variables via linear mixed models (LMMs), with Period (P1–P3) and Site as fixed effects and Period nested within Phase and margin identity nested within Site as random effects. Period P4 was excluded from the calculation of increments due to limited sample size, which precluded reliable estimation of diversity changes. Post hoc Tukey tests were then used to assess pairwise differences between sites within each period.

We further explored the roles of drift and gene flow on temporal genetic variation. Capture rate (CR; captures per 100 trap‐nights) was used as a proxy for local vole abundance, which we employed as an indirect indicator of demographic conditions expected to influence the strength of genetic drift, given the well‐established inverse relationship between population size and the magnitude of genetic drift (Ellstrand and Elam 1993; Frankham 1996; Willi and Määttänen 2011). Temporal change in abundance between consecutive sampling events was calculated as ΔCR = CRn−CRn−1. To quantify gene flow, we used the ‘poppr’ v2.9.1 package (Kamvar et al. 2014) to determine the number of new alleles (i.e., immigration) in each field margin that were absent in the previous sampling event, interpreted as allelic gain (hereafter ALL G ). We used uncorrected diversity measures, since this analysis explicitly modelled vole abundance: number of alleles (Na), observed heterozygosity (Ho) and relatedness estimated using the Queller and Goodnight (1989) estimator (QGM) at the level of each field margin (i.e., local spatial units). This estimator takes allele frequencies into account, eliminates downward bias in small sample sizes and allows estimation of relatedness for subsets of population samples. QGM values represent the mean across all pairwise comparisons among individuals within each sampling event. Diversity metrics were treated as point estimates, and their associated uncertainty was not explicitly incorporated into subsequent analyses. We evaluated temporal increments in these metrics (ΔNa, ΔHo, ΔQGM), calculated as differences between consecutive sampling events, which served as response variables in LMMs. Predictor variables included Period, ΔCR, ALL G , as well as their interactions with Period (ΔCR × Period and ALL G × Period) to test for period‐specific effects. Predictor variables were selected a priori based on their ecological relevance and the specific hypotheses tested, rather than through a model selection procedure. We conducted pairwise post hoc comparisons of slopes using Estimated Marginal Means with Tukey contrasts (function ‘emtrends’ from the ‘emmeans’ package; Lenth 2025). These analyses were restricted to the first population cycle (P1, P2 and P3), which provided a complete and balanced sampling design.

To complement these models, we quantified gene flow dynamics at the population (i.e., Site) scale by computing the cumulative proportion of new alleles per sampling event. Specifically, for each sampling, allele counts were pooled across all field margins within each site, and we calculated the ratio between the number of novel alleles detected and the total number of alleles recorded up to that time point. This measure provides a direct temporal indicator of immigration's contribution to genetic composition. We visualized these dynamics using ggplot2 (Figure 4a), and applied paired t‐tests to assess differences in immigration rates between the two study sites.

Finally, to examine fine‐scale gene exchange within the agrarian matrix, we conducted a focused private allele analysis targeting voles from alfalfa field and their adjacent margins (see Figure 1b). For each alfalfa field, we counted alleles present exclusively in the voles of that field and absent in the four adjacent margins (hereafter AlfalfaPA), serving as a proxy for restricted gene flow. We modelled AlfalfaPA using LMMs, with Phase and Site as fixed effects and Field ID as a random effect. Analysis was restricted to the summer 2014 (peak of the increase phase) and autumn 2014 (early decline phase) sampling events, as these represent contrasting demographic conditions and had sufficient sample sizes from at least four out of five alfalfa fields. This approach allowed us to test whether gene exchange between fields and margins differed between phases of positive and negative population growth.

All analyses were conducted in R 3.6.1 (R Core Team 2019), with data visualization using the ggplot2 package (Wickham 2011). LMMs were run using ‘lme4’ package (Bates et al. 2015), and significance for fixed effects assessed using the ‘Anova’ function from the ‘car’ package (Fox and Weisberg 2019). We standardized continuous predictors to facilitate effect‐size interpretation and improve model convergence. Marginal and conditional R 2 values were estimated using the ‘r.squaredGLMM’ function from the ‘MuMIn’ package (Barton 2025). Model residuals were visually inspected to verify assumptions of normality and homoscedasticity. All models were formally validated against baseline null and simpler structures using Akaike's Information Criterion (AIC) and Likelihood Ratio Tests (LRT) fitted via Maximum Likelihood (ML, REML = FALSE; detailed in Table S1). The full model architecture was strictly retained across all parameters to guarantee analytical consistency, transparently report non‐significant biological interactions, and preserve the underlying spatial structure required for downstream site‐specific post hoc comparisons.

Results

We captured a total of 3351 voles across the eight sampling events over the 5‐year study period, with 3330 individuals successfully genotyped at nine microsatellite loci: 1724 from Site 1 and 1606 from Site 2. Capture rates exhibited pronounced temporal variation, ranging from 0 in autumn 2017 to a maximum of 45.18 in summer 2014 at Site 1, and between 0.22 and 43.15 at Site 2 (Figure 1b).

Analysis with Micro‐Checker revealed no consistent evidence of allele dropout, and null allele frequencies remained under 0.25 in all sampling events. Accordingly, all nine loci were retained for further analyses. Two sampling events were excluded from most statistical analyses due to insufficient sample sizes: spring 2015 (n = 0 for Site 1; n = 9 for Site 2), and autumn 2017 (n = 0 for both sites).

Genetic Diversity Variation Over Space and Time

Genetic diversity, measured as allelic richness (A R ) and heterozygosity (uHe), showed parallel increases at both sites during the population growth phase, representing a consistent temporal trend across space. In the early decline phase, however, patterns diverged: Site 1 showed a sharper reduction in A R and a modest decrease in uHe, whereas Site 2 maintained higher A R and even exhibited a moderate increase in uHe (see observed values in Figure 2). Differences in genetic dynamics between sites also emerged during the samplings of the second cycle, with marked reductions in Site 1 from summer to autumn contrasting with increases in Site 2 (Figure 2). LMMs indicated significant temporal variation in both ΔAr (F 2,246.86 = 4.389, p = 0.013) and ΔuHe (F 2,81.9 = 4.91, p = 0.009). Post hoc Tukey comparisons revealed that differences between periods P1 and P3 at Site 1 accounted for much of the observed variation (Table S2). There were no significant differences between sites in either ΔAr (F 1,48 = 0.07, p = 0.79) or ΔuHe (F 1,48 = 0.19, p = 0.66; Table S3), and the Site x Period interaction was also non‐significant (ΔAr: F 2,81.7 = 1.95, p = 0.15; ΔuHe: F 2,81.7 = 0.71, p = 0.49). The marginal R 2 values were 0.16 for ΔAr and 0.09 for ΔuHe, indicating that only a modest proportion of temporal variation was explained by fixed effects.

Local Genetic Responses to Abundance Fluctuations and Gene Flow

The increase in the number of alleles (ΔNa) was positively and significantly associated with allelic gain (ALL G ) at both sites (Site 1: F 1,49.52 = 104.34, p < 0.001; Site 2: F 1,45.86 = 44.85, p < 0.001). These associations varied significantly across periods (ALL G × Period interaction: F 2,49.56 = 8.69, p < 0.001 for Site 1, and F 2,47.46 = 5.83, p < 0.005 for Site 2; Table S3). Simple‐slopes tests confirmed that within each individual period, the slope between ALL G and ΔNa was statistically greater than zero (Table S3), demonstrating a consistent temporal effect. Pairwise slope contrasts showed that the positive influence of ALL G on ΔNa was significantly stronger during early decline (P3) compared to previous periods at both sites, particularly when contrasted with the late increase (Figure 3, Table S4).

There were no significant associations between ΔNa and changes in capture rate (ΔCR), nor significant period‐specific interactions in either site (Table S3). Similarly, no significant effects were detected for changes in heterozygosity (ΔHo; all p > 0.20).

Regarding changes in relatedness (ΔQGM), we observed a negative association with ALL G , which was statistically significant in Site 2 (F 1,47.9 = 8.97, p = 0.004), and marginally significant in Site 1 (F 1,49.8 = 3.51, p = 0.067; Table S4). The interaction ALL G x Period was not statistically significant in Site 1 (p = 0.141) and approached significance in Site 2 (p = 0.061), suggesting a potential trend where the relationships between ALL G and ΔQGM might differ across periods. However, post hoc analyses revealed no significant difference in the slopes between periods (Table S6), indicating that any observed variation is likely due to sampling variability rather than a consistent pattern.

ΔQGM was positively associated with changes in capture rate in Site 1 (ΔCR: F 1,41.3 = 12.29, p = 0.001; Table S5), with the strength of this relationship differing across periods (interaction: F 2,46.3 = 3.42, p = 0.041; Figure 3). Simple‐slope analysis indicated that this positive effect is concentrated in P1 (Table S5), and pairwise slope contrasts revealed a significantly steeper slope in P1 compared to P2 (estimate > 0, p = 0.032; Table S6), while contrasts involving P3 were not significant. No significant ΔQGM‐ΔCR relationships were observed in Site 2 (Table S5).

The explanatory power of the linear mixed models fitted for temporal changes in genetic metrics varied across response variables. Models for ΔNa showed high explanatory power (marginal R 2: Site 1 = 0.92; Site 2 = 0.90), whereas models for ΔQGM showed a moderate fit (marginal R 2: Site 1 = 0.37; Site 2 = 0.29).

Temporal Dynamics of Allele Incorporation at the Population Scale

To complement the local‐scale (field margin) analyses described above, we examined temporal trends in allele incorporation at the population level across both study sites, offering insights into immigration dynamics during the vole population cycle.

Both sites showed a synchronized and marked influx of new alleles during the increase phase, consistent with substantial gene flow during demographic expansion. In contrast, the early decline phase was characterized by a generalized slowdown in immigration: Site 2 exhibited no new alleles, and Site 1 only minimal additions (Figure 4a), suggesting a sharp reduction in immigration following the population peak.

Allelic input resumed after the bottleneck (spring 2015), with a pronounced pulse of new alleles detected in summer 2016, more substantial in Site 1 (20.6% of accumulated new alleles) than in Site 2 (14.5%). These values exceeded values recorded during the bottleneck (spring 2015: 15.0% and 13.7%, Sites 1 and 2 respectively), indicating intensified genetic influx during population recovery. Modest further gains occurred between summer and autumn 2016 (1.0% in Site 1 and 3.0% in Site 2), with a slight increase in Site 2 again in autumn 2017 (although based on a limited sample, n = 5). Throughout, Site 1 consistently showed higher levels of allele incorporation than Site 2, especially during the post‐bottleneck recovery. This trend approached statistical significance (paired t‐test: t 5 = 2.47, p = 0.056, 95% CI [−0.089, 4.569]), supporting spatial heterogeneity in post‐bottleneck immigration dynamics (Figure 4a).

Fine‐Scale Gene Exchange Within the Agrarian Matrix

We quantified fine‐scale gene exchange by calculating the proportion of private alleles found in each alfalfa field but absent from their adjacent margins (AlfalfaPA). These values are summarized in Table S7 and illustrated in Figure 4b. AlfalfaPA values ranged from 0.86% (summer 2014, Site 2) to 100% (spring 2015, same site and field). On average, AlfalfaPA exhibited a highly consistent temporal trajectory across both study sites, declining steadily during the increase phase and reaching minima at the population peak (summer 2014). It then rose during the decline and peaked at spring 2015, when vole densities were lowest. During the second, less pronounced cycle in 2016, AlfalfaPA remained low—comparable to levels near the peak of the first cycle—but inter‐site differences became more marked (Figure 4b). Statistical support indicated that the private allele was significantly higher during the decline compared to the increase phase (F 1,5.32 = 24.74, p = 0.004), with no significant effect of Site (F 1,6.95 = 0.02, p = 0.87) or Phase × Site interaction (F 1,5.32 = 1.53, p = 0.27). The model's fixed effects captured a moderate proportion of variance (marginal R 2 = 0.34), while inclusion of random effects raised explained variance (conditional R 2 = 0.82), indicating that variation among field margins was a major contributor to overall variability.

Discussion

Despite pronounced fluctuations in vole abundance, with capture rates varying roughly 45‐fold at Site 1 and 200‐fold at Site 2, among the highest reported in cyclic rodents (Krebs 2013), genetic diversity remained relatively high across two independent study sites. Our findings provide novel insight into how genetic diversity is lost and recovered in cyclic populations inhabiting highly‐dynamic landscapes, and the role of immigration in buffering genetic erosion in populations facing frequent demographic fluctuations.

Genetic diversity showed clear temporal variation across different phases of the cycle, yet its magnitude and timing were modulated by site‐specific environmental or demographic contingencies, leading to trajectories that differed markedly between sites. While both populations showed parallel increases in allelic richness and heterozygosity during the growth phase of the first cycle, subsequent declines and second‐cycle dynamics revealed contrasting site‐specific responses. This aligns with theoretical expectations that allelic richness is more sensitive to demographic reductions and declines more rapidly than heterozygosity. This occurs because demographic contractions preferentially eliminate rare alleles, disproportionately affecting richness, while heterozygosity is relatively retained (Leberg 1992; Nei et al. 1975). Additionally, these site‐specific differences reflect that populations responded differently to similar demographic phases. However, interpretations of diversity loss are constrained by the low number of captures during the bottleneck (14 captures across both sites), a known challenge in studies of cyclic species (Norén and Angerbjörn 2014). The 14 samples collected during the bottleneck in Spring 2015 yielded uHe values of 0.179 (Site 1) and 0.175 (Site 2). Assuming these samples were representative of the population, they indicate ~75% decline in heterozygosity relative to pre‐bottleneck levels.

Genetic diversity was restored within one to two generations post‐bottleneck, consistent with previous studies in cyclic rodents (Berthier et al. 2005, 2006; Ehrich and Jorde 2005). Thus, the strong demographic bottlenecks observed during the study period did not lead to a substantial reduction in population genetic diversity. Theoretical predictions suggest that the loss of genetic diversity depends on both the minimum population size and the subsequent growth rate following the decline (Nei et al. 1975), as well as the time (in generations) of the experienced bottleneck. We suggest that the short duration of the bottleneck—equivalent to 1–2 generations—limited the opportunity for genetic drift to act and buffered against genetic erosion.

Critically, the recovery after bottlenecks in 2013 and 2016 was driven by immigration‐mediated allele input, rather than by intrinsic demographic increases driven by local reproduction. This highlights gene flow as a predominant force over drift in the restoration of genetic diversity after valleys. Furthermore, the magnitude of changes in genetic diversity was proportional to the influx of new alleles at local scale, with field margins experiencing higher immigration rates showing a greater increase in genetic diversity. Therefore, voles inhabiting locations where gene flow was higher, at both population and local scales, recovered faster through the incorporation of new alleles compared to locations with lower gene flow. Although we did not measure landscape permeability directly, the observed patterns of allele exchange provide clear evidence that some sites are more effectively connected than others. This emphasizes the importance of spatial processes—such as migration and connectivity (i.e., the effective exchange of genes among populations)—in buffering the genetic consequences of demographic bottlenecks. Similar patterns have been observed in butterflies of fragmented landscapes, where populations in more connected landscapes exhibited less loss of allelic diversity and a faster genetic recovery following demographic declines (Jangjoo et al. 2016, 2020; Keyghobadi et al. 2005). These patterns suggest that landscape permeability and dispersal dynamics are critical factors in sustaining genetic resilience in taxa exposed to recurrent demographic fluctuations.

We also found scale‐dependent patterns of gene flow. At the population level, novel alleles nearly doubled during the increase phase, whereas they were either absent (Site 2) or very limited (Site 1) during the decline phase (Figure 4a). In contrast, at the local scale (field margins), allelic gain (ALL G ) continued to influence genetic diversity (ΔNa) during the decrease phase (Table S3), despite the lack of immigration at the population level (Figure 4).

The scale‐dependent divergence highlights two key aspects of the cycles. First, migration into the population primarily occurs during the growth phase, but the pattern exhibits spatial variation among populations. While some populations exhibited no immigration during the decline phase, others, even those in close proximity, continued to experience some level of immigration during that phase. This population‐specific variation is likely influenced by the degree of genetic connectivity with nearby populations. Classic experimental work in voles has shown that dispersal is most pronounced during population increase phases, with genetic and behavioural selectivity among dispersers (Myers and Krebs 1971), consistent with the patterns of density‐dependent gene flow we observed in our study. Within a metapopulation framework, such dynamics are particularly important, as the movement of individuals between subpopulations plays a central role in maintaining genetic diversity and mitigating the risk of local extinction (Hanski 1998; Roland et al. 2000). An alternative, non‐exclusive explanation is that the two sites were not situated at precisely the same point within their respective population cycles. For instance, one site may already be approaching peak density, while the other could be already starting the decline. This is plausible given the substantial intraspecific variation in cycle dynamics observed among small mammals (Hansson 1999, and see Caminero‐Saldaña et al. 2024 for our study species and area), and the fact that spatiotemporal synchrony is scale‐dependent (Ranta et al. 1998). Consequently, incorrect assumptions about the phase of the cycle may lead to biassed interpretations of underlying processes (Norén and Angerbjörn 2014). However, our sampling revealed similar abundances across the cycle at simultaneous sampling times in the two nearby areas (Figure 1), suggesting highly synchronized dynamics. This is consistent with expectations for regions characterized by high‐amplitude, regular 3‐year cycles, where peak regional densities have been reported (Caminero‐Saldaña et al. 2024; Mougeot et al. 2019; Sánchez et al. 2024).

Second, local movements within populations vary depending on landscape elements, likely reflecting localized behavioural responses to immediate ecological pressures such as reproduction, foraging opportunities and predation risk (Andreassen et al. 2002; Bujalska and Hansson 2000; Ims and Andreassen 2000). Movements between margins, although most frequent during the growth phase, persisted into the decline, as evidenced by allele gains during P3 (see Figure 3 and Figure S1). In the absence of immigration from external populations (no incorporation of new alleles into the population during this period; Figure 4a), these gains likely result from short‐distance movement of individuals (and genes) between margins, suggesting a re‐distribution of resident individuals across the landscape. Genetic evidence suggested that movements between alfalfa fields and neighbouring margins were also more pronounced during the increase phase (i.e., a reduction in private alleles), reaching a maximum near peak population density (Figure 4b). This pattern is consistent with previous studies reporting high synchrony along the cycle in vole abundance between alfalfas and margins (Sánchez et al. 2024) and density‐dependent spill‐over of voles from field margins into adjacent crop fields with suboptimal habitats (mainly cereal), driven by habitat saturation as population densities approach the peak (Rodríguez‐Pastor et al. 2016). The subsequent increase in genetic differences between fields and their adjacent margins during the population decline—reaching its maximum at the demographic valley—may reflect the effects of genetic drift driven by reduced population size and limited gene flow between adjacent habitat elements. As population size contracts, stochastic fluctuations in allele frequencies can lead to enhanced genetic divergence between nearby habitat patches (Nei et al. 1975). This pattern aligns with findings in the California vole ( Microtus californicus ), where marked genetic differentiation (as measured by Wright's F ST) was observed between populations separated by only 50–200 m (Bowen 1982). In that system, genetic differentiation weakened markedly from the early growth phase to peak abundance, followed by a rapid recovery of differentiation during the subsequent decline and valley phases, mirroring the temporal trajectory we observed (Bowen 1982; Lidicker 2015).

Such movement dynamics are likely modulated by ecological characteristics specific to each habitat type, such as vegetation cover, predation risk, food availability and agricultural practices like soil tillage (Fischer et al. 2011; Jacob and Hempel 2003). Moreover, short‐range gene flow may also be influenced by kin structure, as related individuals may disperse away from natal sites during the increase phase to reduce the likelihood of inbreeding (Berthier et al. 2006; Galliard et al. 2003). Consistent with these localized drivers, local relatedness at the margin scale exhibited different dynamics between the two study populations. In Site 1, the increase in relatedness appears to be primarily driven by the increase in vole abundance during the increasing phase, consistent with a positive density‐dependent pattern. Higher population growth during this reproductive phase was positively associated with increased local relatedness (Table S5, Figure 3c), suggesting that favourable demographic conditions during reproduction may facilitate the retention of kin groups and thereby enhance fine‐scale spatial genetic structuring by limiting dispersal. However, as the population neared its peak density, this relationship weakened and eventually became non‐significant, implying that beyond a certain density threshold, relatedness becomes independent from local vole abundance. This decoupling likely reflects the increasing influence of density‐independent factors, such as immigration from outside the population, which can dilute kin structure. In contrast, at Site 2, local relatedness patterns were not related to vole abundance at any point. Instead, the arrival of novel alleles through short‐distance dispersal played a more prominent role (see Table S5). This suggests that local movements of unrelated individuals, likely combined with long‐distance immigration, disrupted kin structure by increasing genetic mixing.

We documented four key inter‐site differences: (i) Site 1 suffered a markedly higher loss of genetic diversity during the decline phase (first cycle), and during the second cycle, than Site 2 (Figure 2); (ii) it exhibited greater genetic instability, with higher turnover of alleles across cycles; (iii) it nonetheless showed stronger diversity recovery driven by immigration; and (iv) retained more (local) kin structure during the increasing phase, indicative of limited local dispersal. Importantly, these patterns may be partly mediated by predation risk. In our study, Site 1 experienced greater predation pressure due to a biological control experiment involving nest‐boxes for raptors, which has demonstrably reduced local vole abundance (Jareño et al. 2023 and references therein). Predation is an ecological driver known to cause diversity loss (Gasc et al. 2010) and to influence dispersal behaviour, either reducing movements in elevated‐risk scenarios or inducing longer‐distance dispersal to evade danger (Gaines and McClenaghan 1980; Le Galliard et al. 2012; Vibe‐Petersen et al. 2006). In such contexts, dispersal may sometimes be maladaptive, as intense or spatially heterogeneous predation can elevate the costs of movement relative to benefits, thereby reducing settlement success and inhibiting internal redistributions (Barraquand and Murrell 2012). Our nest‐box occupancy data indicate higher use in years with elevated vole abundance (see also Viñuela et al. 2026), but seasonal variation in occupancy does not align neatly with our sampling periods, so we cannot directly associate predation intensity with relatedness in specific periods. Nevertheless, predation‐mediated dispersal constraints combined with external immigration likely shape the observed genetic trajectories, highlighting the importance of spatial connectivity beyond high‐risk areas. Under this scenario, dispersers in Site 1 may have faced elevated mortality or reduced settlement success, thereby inhibiting internal redistributions and accentuating the loss of genetic diversity during population collapse. Yet, the substantial allele influx during recovery (Figure 4a) suggests that immigration from external populations—outside the high‐risk zone—remained effective, underscoring the importance of spatial connectivity beyond the impacted area in facilitating genetic replenishment. Thus, predation‐mediated dispersal constraints and external immigration would operate synergistically, shaping the observed genetic trajectories. Future studies should integrate habitat structure, predation risk and dispersal success, thereby clarifying how ecological drivers interact to influence genetic resilience in cyclic systems.

Insights on Theoretical Models

The models proposed by Charnov and Finerty (1980) (C–F) and Lambin and Krebs (1991) (L–K) diverge in their predictions concerning the relationship between population density and relatedness, as well as in the underlying mechanisms thought to drive temporal changes in genetic structuring. While a direct comparison with our data is constrained by the fact that both models primarily focus on local female‐relatedness, and our genetic data are not sex‐disaggregated, the population‐level predictions of these models remain testable. In this regard, our results allow for meaningful evaluation of their broader demographic and genetic expectations, even if sex‐specific dynamics cannot be fully resolved.

The C–F model predicts decreasing local relatedness (among neighbours) and increasing gene flow with increasing density. Here, relatedness refers to local kin structure, which in our study is estimated at the field‐margin level using genetic data. According to this model, during the low‐density phase of the cycle, dispersal is limited, kin groups are retained locally, and relatedness among neighbours is high. As density increases, intensified competition promotes greater dispersal, resulting in a decrease in local relatedness and a concomitant increase in gene flow. Thus, the model predicts a decline in relatedness and enhanced genetic mixing from valley to peak phases of population growth. In contrast, the L‐K model predicts the opposite pattern: local relatedness is expected to increase during the growth phase due to kin retention during favourable demographic conditions, while gene flow is predicted to decrease as individuals remain in or near their natal areas.

Neither model fully captures the spatial contingencies and multi‐scale complexities observed in our study. First, we observed that changes in genetic diversity, relatedness and gene flow were strongly modulated by site‐specific environmental conditions. Differences between sites appear to reflect underlying variation in landscape structure, predation pressure, demographic history and connectivity with neighbouring populations. As a result, the temporal dynamics of genetic parameters and their underlying mechanisms are not spatially uniform or predictable, indicating that generalized eco‐evolutionary models must incorporate local ecological filters to remain predictive in highly dynamic or human‐modified landscapes.

Second, we found that movements between margins and alfalfa fields increased from valley to peak and declined thereafter. This pattern was accompanied by a marked decline in local relatedness (QGM) as density rose, consistent with positive density‐related dispersal and local gene flow. These short‐distance movements likely contributed to the observed breakdown of kin structure (i.e., local relatedness patterns), in line with the predictions of the C–F model. However, the magnitude of this decline in relatedness could not be fully explained by local movements alone. Instead, our data suggest that long‐distance immigration during the increase phase played a substantial role in eroding fine‐scale genetic structure (i.e., spatial patterns of genetic differentiation and relatedness at local scale). This also contradicts the L–K model, which posits increased relatedness at high population densities as a result of limited immigration and kin retention.

Further support for the role of long‐distance dispersal comes from the temporal pattern of novel allele introductions into the population's gene pool, which peaked during the demographic growth phase and dropped sharply after the population peak. The absence of new alleles during the decline phase indicates a cessation of (effective) immigration (i.e., individuals that successfully contribute genes to the population), allowing relatedness to recover as the population contracts. This dynamic more closely aligns with the C‐F model, and is consistent with previous empirical findings (e.g., Berthier et al. 2006; Xu et al. 2013), which report limited dispersal and gene flow during population decline.

Altogether, our findings show that genetic dynamics in cyclic systems are strongly modulated by local environmental and anthropogenic characteristics, with distinct eco‐evolutionary patterns emerging even between nearby study sites. Therefore, we caution against oversimplified assumptions about how such cycles operate and emphasise the necessity of integrating fine‐scale spatial and ecological features into predictive population genetics frameworks.
