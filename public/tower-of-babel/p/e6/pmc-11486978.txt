LSTM-based framework for predicting point defect percentage in semiconductor materials using simulated XRD patterns
Authors: Mehran Motamedi, Reza Shidpour, Mehdi Ezoji
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this paper, we present a machine learning-based approach that leverages Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks combined with a sliding window technique for feature extraction, aimed at accurately predicting point defect percentages in semiconductor materials based on simulated X-ray Diffraction (XRD) data. The model was initially trained on silicon-simulated XRD data with defect percentages ranging from 1 to 5%, enabling it to predict defect percentages from 0 to 10% in silicon and other semiconductor materials, including AlAs, CdS, GaAs, Ge, and ZnS. Through extensive experimentation, we explored different sequence lengths and LSTM units, identifying the optimal configuration as a sequence length of 3501 and 4500 units, which yielded the best results. The model’s mean absolute error at 4500 units was 0.021, the lowest among the LSTM configurations tested. The sliding window technique plays a crucial role in capturing temporal dependencies within the XRD data, allowing the model to generalize to other semiconductor materials. Additionally, we observed that increasing defect percentages consistently led to a rise in background intensity. We further examined the relationship between crystal structure and defect precentage predictions, uncovering consistent trends for materials with Diamond Cubic and Zinc Blende structures. This LSTM-based method offers a novel approach to predicting defect percentages using simulated XRD patterns of materials.Keywords: Long short-term memory, Sliding window technique, Sequence length, Number of units, Nuance effectSubject terms: Materials science, Theory and computation

Introduction

Machine learning and materials science synergy heralds a transformative era, offering novel avenues to decode intricate material properties. As machine learning algorithms analyze vast datasets from experiments like X-ray Diffraction (XRD), they unveil hidden patterns, accelerating the discovery and optimization of materials with unprecedented efficiency. The collaboration between machine learning and materials science marks a paradigm shift, offering innovative pathways to unravel intricate material properties.

X-ray Diffraction (XRD) is a powerful analytical technique widely employed in materials science to investigate the crystalline structure of substances. By exposing a material to X-rays and analyzing the resulting diffraction pattern, researchers can glean valuable insights into the arrangement of atoms within the material. In materials characterization, the integration of innovative machine learning techniques, such as Parameter Quantification Network 1, GTDL framework2, transfer learning3 autonomous XRD systems4, and self-supervised learning for phase transition identification transform real-time X-ray diffraction analysis 5, offering rapid and accurate phase identification for enhanced scientific processes. Automated X-ray diffraction pattern analysis, facilitated by computerized systems and software algorithms, expedites sample handling, data acquisition, and interpretation, enabling swift and accurate characterization of materials’ crystalline properties. In automated X-ray diffraction pattern analysis, pioneering deep learning methods showcase remarkable proficiency in accurately classifying crystal systems and space groups6,7, identification of crystal systems8, symmetry prediction9, and Predicting crystal dislocations in XRD for defect classification 10. In defect analysis, XRD becomes essential as deviations from the ideal crystal lattice, such as vacancies or impurities, become observable in the diffraction pattern. Leveraging machine learning on XRD data enables the precise prediction of defect percentages, revolutionizing defect analysis in semiconductor materials and paving the way for enhanced quality control and material optimization. Deep learning, specifically Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM)11, a type of recurrent neural network (RNN)12, has become a powerful tool for addressing complex material-related challenges.

LSTM networks perform exceptionally well in processing sequential data, making it particularly effective for time-dependent data and patterns. LSTM networks, crucial in Machine learning, demonstrate excellence across various applications. In natural language processing, they capture intricate language structures13. For time-series analysis, LSTMs predict future values, which are vital in stock market predictions14 and weather forecasting15. In genomics, they unravel sequential DNA patterns16. Their versatility in handling sequential data makes LSTMs indispensable for tasks ranging from language understanding to predicting dynamic patterns in various domains.

Quantifying the exact defect percentage in a material using X-ray diffraction (XRD) presents challenges due to the complex nature of defects and their influence on diffraction patterns. While XRD provides valuable information about the crystalline structure of materials, it may not yield precise defect percentages directly. Instead, interpreting XRD data about defects requires supplementary techniques and methodologies to estimate defect concentrations accurately. However, specific analyses and methods associated with XRD can provide insights into the crystal structure, crystallite size, and lattice strain, which can indirectly suggest the presence of defects. Some methods of XRD analysis that may provide indirect information related to defects are Williamson-Hall17, Rietveld Refinement18, Line Profile Analysis19, Warren–Averbach Fourier Analysis20, Scherrer Equation21, and Comparison with Reference Patterns. Each method offers valuable insights into characterizing defects within materials, yet limitations hinder precise quantification. For example, the Williamson-Hall method, although widely used for analyzing peak broadening, oversimplifies the complex contributions of defects, potentially leading to inaccurate assessments. Similarly, while Rietveld refinement excels in determining crystal structures, it struggles to adequately capture the intricate distributions of defects, resulting in a lack of robustness in defect quantification. Despite their potential for detecting subtle deviations indicative of defects, line profile analysis and Warren–Averbach Fourier analysis are susceptible to false positives caused by experimental noise. Their reliance on peak shapes and Fourier transforms may overlook genuine defect signatures, compromising defect quantification accuracy. Additionally, the Scherrer equation, commonly employed to estimate crystallite size from peak broadening, may misinterpret broadening effects caused by defects as merely indicative of grain size variations. This oversimplification can lead to inaccuracies in defect quantification and hinder a comprehensive understanding of defect characteristics. Lastly, comparing experimental XRD patterns with reference patterns, while helpful in identifying impurities and secondary phases, may fail to capture the nuanced variations caused by defects. This method often relies on qualitative assessments, potentially overlooking subtle defect-related features and complicating the accurate quantification of defects.

The primary aim of this investigation is to predict defect percentages accurately using simulated X-ray Diffraction (XRD) data. The cornerstone of our approach lies in training on silicon data with 1 to 5% defects, utilizing a sliding window technique and varied sequence lengths to extract statistical features. The results showcase the model’s performance and applicability across various semiconductor materials, including AlAs, CdS, GaAs, Ge, and ZnS. A key revelation from our investigation is the significant impact of sequence length and the number of units on the accuracy of defect percentage predictions, achieved through meticulous exploration of different sequence lengths and incorporation of the sliding window technique for feature extraction. This nuanced understanding contributes significantly to refining defect prediction methodologies in XRD data analysis, emphasizing the importance of fine-tuning these parameters for precise and reliable predictions. Additionally, XRD patterns exhibit a significant correlation between defect percentage and background intensity.

Results

Investigating the influence of window size on predicted point defect percentage

The sliding window technique, widely used in signal processing and time series analysis, has been applied in various fields, including machine learning and materials science. It is designed to analyze data by moving a fixed-size window across the dataset, extracting features at each step to capture temporal dependencies and patterns. In our investigation, this technique is employed to extract temporal features from simulated X-ray Diffraction (XRD) data. This approach enables the extraction of statistical features such as peak intensities, peak positions, and other relevant characteristics within each sequence, providing a comprehensive representation of the material’s diffraction profile. This methodology contributes to refining defect prediction methodologies in XRD data analysis, facilitating the model’s adaptation to the variability in different materials and defect concentrations.

Figure 1 depicts the segmentation process of X-ray Diffraction (XRD) patterns, where 17,000 data points are divided into sequences, each containing 3,501 data points. The segmentation process allows us to analyze the impact of sequence length on predicted defect percentages in silicon crystals. Each segment represents a distinct portion of the XRD pattern. This segmentation strategy is essential for capturing detailed structural features contributing to accurate defect percentage predictions.

Segmenting X-ray Diffraction (XRD) patterns from 17,000 data points into 3501-point sequences.

A comprehensive dataset was compiled, comprising X-ray diffraction (XRD) files representing 90 distinct defect configurations for each concentration, with each file containing 17,000 data points, providing a robust dataset for analysis. Each configuration represents a unique structural variation, distinguished by the specific indices of deleted atoms. For example, in a silicon lattice with a 1% defect concentration, 90 different sets of atom deletions were generated. Each set is defined by the indices of the removed atoms, resulting in distinct structural configurations. To systematically explore the relationship between sequence length and defect percentages prediction accuracy, sequence lengths ranging from 501 to 9501 data points were evaluated. These evaluations were initially conducted using a fixed number of 2500 LSTM units, as detailed in Figure S1 in the supplementary materials. The corresponding defect percentage was calculated for each sequence length, allowing for a detailed examination of the influence of sequence length on defect percentage prediction performance.

After evaluating the results at the fixed unit of 2500 LSTM units, further evaluations were conducted using 4,500 LSTM units to ensure that the sequence length of 3,501 data points consistently provided the best results across different model configurations. These additional evaluations confirmed that the sequence length of 3,501 data points remained the most effective, even when the number of LSTM units was increased.

Table 1 shows the effectiveness of the chosen sequence length. This evaluation used the predicted defect percentages across different sequence lengths and units, comparing models with sequence lengths of 501, 3501, and 9501 and unit counts of 2500 and 4500. Based on evaluation and analysis, the LSTM model with a sequence length of 3501, potentially with overlapping sequences corresponding to approximately one-fifth of the total data points in each XRD file, has provided the best results for predicting defect percentages in simulated data. This finding is crucial, as it demonstrates that the sequence length and number of units in the LSTM model significantly impact its ability to generalize and predict defect percentages accurately. The consistent performance of the model with a sequence length of 3501 across various defect classes, including the 0% defect class, underscores the importance of selecting the appropriate sequence length in the design of LSTM models for predicting point defect percentage. This sequence length appears to balance capturing sufficient temporal dependencies within the XRD data and maintaining the model’s ability to generalize effectively.

In machine learning, particularly when working with sequential data using models like LSTM, a “time step” refers to the interval at which the data is fed into the model. Each time step represents a snapshot of the data, which the model uses to learn and make predictions. Traditionally, multiple time steps capture temporal relationships and dependencies across different points in time, allowing the model to forecast future values based on past data. In this study, however, we utilized an LSTM model with a time step of 1. This configuration treats each sequence, containing 36 statistical features, as an independent entity rather than part of a continuous time series. By setting the time step to 1, the model processes each sequence of features separately without assuming any inherent temporal dependencies between them. The LSTM is used to learn from the individual sequences and the complex relationships between the 36 features within each sequence. This setup leverages the LSTM’s strength in capturing dependencies and patterns, even when the data is not organized as a traditional time series. As a result, the model effectively learns from and predicts outcomes based on the intrinsic properties of each sequence in the dataset.

Effect of LSTM units on predicting defects percentage

The number of units in an LSTM network plays a crucial role in determining the network’s capacity to capture and learn from temporal dependencies within data. Each unit in an LSTM represents a memory cell capable of retaining information over time through its internal mechanisms, such as input, forget, and output gates. These gates regulate the flow of information, allowing the network to selectively remember or forget information based on its relevance to the current task. Choosing the appropriate number of units involves balancing model complexity and computational efficiency. Furthermore, the number of units influences the LSTM’s ability to generalize effectively to unseen data. In our work, we investigated the impact of varying the number of units in a single LSTM model on predicting point defect percentage for a sequence length of 3501 data points. Table 2 provides a comparative analysis of simulated and predicted defect percentages for silicon at different units. The table highlights the significant improvement in accuracy achieved by increasing the number of units from 250 to 4500 due to computational resources.

For lower defect percentages (0 to 3%), the prediction errors across all three models are minimal, indicating that increasing the number of units from 250 to 4500 does not drastically improve accuracy in simpler scenarios. In the mid-range defect percentages (4 to 7%), the model with 4500 units shows slightly higher error for the 4% defect but generally performs better for defects from 5 to 7% compared to models with fewer units. A model with only 250 units has limited capacity to learn intricate patterns in the XRD data, making it struggle to capture subtle variations and nuanced features in defect percentages across the sequence length. It may also have limitations in retaining contextual information across the sequence, neglecting critical dependencies and relationships between data points.

In contrast, increasing the number of units allows the LSTM model to maintain better and utilize memory, capturing dependencies within the each sequence. This is crucial for XRD data, where the cumulative effect of multiple data points may influence defects. Moreover, regarding non-linear mapping, a model with only 250 units might struggle to create sufficiently complex non-linear mappings between input features and defect percentage predictions. On the other hand, an increased number of units provides more parameters for the model to develop intricate non-linear mappings, enabling it to adapt to the non-linear relationships inherent in XRD data.

We chose 4500 units for predicting point defect percentages in silicon because the LSTM model with 4500 units ensures more robust feature extraction. This enables the model to discern and emphasize relevant features in the XRD data.

XRD background intensity variation with point defect percentage

Traditional X-ray diffraction (XRD) methods primarily focus on analyzing diffraction peaks to deduce structural information about crystalline materials, often overlooking the background intensity in XRD patterns. While diffraction peaks provide valuable insights into the arrangement of atoms within the crystal lattice, the background intensity contains additional information about structural irregularities and defects present in the material.

Our study emphasizes the importance of background intensity in XRD patterns and its correlation with defect concentration. By systematically analyzing background intensity changes with varying defect percentages, we elucidate the influence of defects on the diffraction pattern. Analysis of the causes of errors in LSTM models

Understanding the relationship between model complexity and performance on simulated XRD data is crucial for analyzing the sources of error in our Long-Short-Term Memory (LSTM) models. Errors in our LSTM models typically stem from issues related to overfitting and underfitting, which are directly influenced by the choice of hyperparameters, such as the number of units and sequence lengths.

Underfitting is observed when our models lack the necessary complexity to discern the underlying patterns within the data. This issue is particularly apparent in models with shorter sequence lengths. For instance, a sequence length of 501 though computationally intensive, fails to capture essential temporal patterns, as evidenced by increased prediction errors. This is extensively illustrated in Supplementary Figure S4, where prediction errors across various sequence lengths are analyzed.

Conversely, overfitting occurs when models are too complex, hampering their generalization capabilities to new data. This often manifests in models with longer sequence lengths, where the models might pick up irrelevant patterns, decreasing their predictive accuracy on unseen data. The errors observed, particularly at larger defect densities, often reflect limitations in the model’s capacity to adapt to high variations within the simulated datasets. This phenomenon is highlighted in the comparative analyses where prediction errors increase with the complexity of the defect densities being modeled. These findings suggest that the model struggles more with accurately simulating scenarios as defect density increases because of inherent challenges in modeling complex interactions within the data.

Furthermore, selecting appropriate hyperparameters is pivotal, as demonstrated in Figures S5 and S6. These figures illustrate the impact of different sequence lengths and number of units on the sum of prediction errors, guiding us toward optimal model settings. The graphs clearly show how variations in these hyperparameters can significantly affect the model’s error rates, providing insights into the best configurations that balance complexity and accuracy.

Effective feature selection also plays a vital role in this context. The LSTM model can focus on the most relevant patterns by transforming raw input data into informative features, which is crucial for making accurate predictions. This process enhances the model’s ability to interpret the data more effectively and reduces the impact of variations within the data that can mislead the learning process. Our LSTM model and its training approach was refined by examining these aspects to enhance accuracy and reliability in predicting defect percentages. The comprehensive analyses in the supplementary figures support our conclusions and provide a deeper understanding of how different settings impact model performance.

Discussion

This investigation highlights the Effectiveness of the proposed approach in predicting material point defect percentages using simulated X-ray diffraction data. The model’s generalization to previously unseen data, demonstrated by predictions on the unknown test set, underscores its potential in material science applications. Comparing t-SNE plots before and after LSTM training assesses the model’s ability to learn meaningful representations of input data. Changes in the distribution and separation of materials in the feature space may indicate the model’s capacity to capture relevant patterns and relationships. A clear visual distinction in the post-training t-SNE plot suggests that the LSTM model has learned features enhancing discrimination of materials based on defect percentage. Training on silicon data was foundational, enabling the model to extract universal features applicable to various semiconductor materials.

Another highlight is that our LSTM model predictions for defect percentages in silicon and germanium, both with a diamond cubic structure, exhibit a coherent trend despite differences in lattice parameters. The systematic increase in defect percentages with simulated defect concentrations suggests a consistent pattern influenced by crystal structure. Materials with zinc blende structures (CdS, GaAs, ZnS, AlAs), also belonging to the FCC lattice type, demonstrate comparable trends in predicted defect percentages, implying a potential correlation between crystal structure and defect prediction.

A noteworthy limitation in our model is the low accuracy for crystals that do not share similar lattice parameters with trained materials. This issue can be addressed by introducing data with the intended element and using it to predict point defect percentages for test data with crystals having similar lattice parameters to the training data.

This research focuses solely on simulated data, and predictions based on experimental data are not considered in this paper. The scope is limited to proposing a novel machine learning method, and we do not expect the current models to perform well on real XRD data at this stage. For application to real-world data, the machine learning model would need to account for challenges such as peak overlap and instrumental broadening under practical measurement conditions, which we have not addressed here. Future work will focus on updating the models and validating them using experimental data.

Methods

Dataset preparation through XRD pattern simulations

This study employed computational simulations using Materials Studio software packages to investigate the effect of vacancy defects in silicon supercells. A Python script utilizing the Pymatgen library23 systematically introduced defects, generating modified supercells with different atomic arrangements for concentrations ranging from 1 to 5%. X-ray diffraction simulations were conducted using the Reflex module of Materials Studio with carefully configured parameters. Key parameters in the X-ray diffraction simulations included a Bragg–Brentano geometry, copper (Cu Kα1) radiation with a wavelength (λ) of 1.540562 Å, and a pseudo-Voigt profile function. The simulation spanned a 2θ range from 5° to 90° with a step size of 0.005°. Additional parameters, such as the zero point, anomalous dispersion, temperature factor, Monochromator, polarization, and scale factor, were meticulously configured to accurately represent the simulated X-ray diffraction patterns. These parameters are crucial for obtaining detailed and reliable diffraction patterns, enabling the characterization of structural variations induced by vacancy defects in the silicon supercell.

Each of the 90 distinct defect configurations underwent XRD pattern simulations, resulting in a dataset of 17,000 data points per configuration, covering the 5° to 90° range due to the 0.005° step size. This extensive dataset enabled the training of our LSTM model, ensuring robust and generalized defect density predictions across various configurations.

For further analysis, these patterns were segmented into sequences of 3501 data points each. The segmentation approach began from the first file of each defect concentration category, covering the XRD range from 5° to 22.5°. Subsequent sequences overlapped consecutively across subsequent files until reaching the last file of each defect concentration. This method ensured comprehensive coverage of the XRD spectrum from 5° to 90° across all sequences, providing a rich dataset for in-depth analysis.

Feature extraction in xrd data analysis

After data preparation, various feature extraction methods were considered to analyze XRD datasets representing defect concentrations ranging from 1 to 5%. Feature extraction methods, including Wavelet Transform24, Fast Fourier Transform (FFT)25, and Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT)26, have been widely employed in the literature for analyzing XRD data. While these methods are well-established in the literature, our study found that the sliding window technique with different sequence lengths stood out, enabling systematic analysis of datasets representing defect concentrations ranging from 1 to 5%. The combination of statistical features derived from the sliding window technique encompasses a comprehensive set including mean, standard deviation, median, minimum, maximum, variance, percentiles (10th, 25th, 50th, 75th, and 90th), range, interquartile range, coefficient of variation, median absolute deviation, entropy, and autocorrelation. These features are computed for both 2θ and intensity, resulting in 36 statistical descriptors extracted to provide a nuanced representation of the crystalline structures at different concentration levels. Class labels were assigned to facilitate supervised learning, aligning each concentration class with target labels. The dataset was strategically partitioned into training, validation, and test sets in the subsequent analysis phase. The specified proportions for this partitioning were 80% for training, 10% for testing, and 10% for validation. This distribution aimed to strike a balance between leveraging a substantial portion of the data for training the model, validating its performance on a dedicated subset, and evaluating its generalization on an independent test set. The machine learning algorithms exhibit sensitivity to input data to enhance the training process. A StandardScaler27 uses a standardized approach to normalize the features across the training, validation, and test sets. This standardization ensures that the features exhibit a mean of zero and a unit variance across the training dataset, ensuring consistency and coherence in the feature space.

Long short-term memory model

Our LSTM model is a Sequential neural network with two layers: an LSTM layer and a fully connected dense layer. The LSTM layer has 4500 units and contains 81,670,501 parameters, contributing to its ability to learn complex patterns. The second layer is a dense layer with one unit and a linear activation function, serving as the output layer. This layer produces a one-dimensional output for predicting point defect percentages and has 4,501 parameters, including weights and biases. Training over 400 epochs with mean squared error as the loss function provided insights into the model’s learning dynamics, monitored through metrics such as mean absolute error and root mean squared error. In the final phase, the trained model was applied to predict defect percentages in an unknown test dataset. The comprehensive analysis and prediction process provided valuable insights into the intricate relationships between X-ray diffraction features and material defect density in the context of materials science research.

The LSTM model, with a time step of 1, treats each sequence separately, updating its internal hidden states based on the current sequence without retaining information from previous sequences in the traditional sense. This approach focuses on learning from the immediate input only, making it particularly suitable for cases where the temporal sequence of data points within a larger dataset does not represent a continuous timeline.

In this configuration, labels guide the learning process, helping the LSTM associate each input sequence with the correct output value during training. This enables the model to generalize from the learned sequences to effectively predict outcomes for unseen data. The hidden states of the LSTM, updated at each step, capture crucial information from the current input sequence, allowing the model to discern underlying patterns and dependencies within that specific input.

For each input sequence, the model performs a forward pass. It processes the input sequence through the LSTM layers and generates predictions. The model’s predictions are compared to the actual target values (labels) using a loss function. The loss function quantifies the difference between predicted and true values. The model then performs a backward pass through the network to calculate the gradients of the loss concerning its parameters (weights and biases). This is known as backpropagation28. Using the computed gradients, the model updates its internal parameters using an optimization algorithm (Adam optimizer) to minimize the loss. The goal is to adjust the parameters to make the model’s predictions more accurate. These steps are repeated for multiple epochs until the model has seen the entire training dataset numerous times. The LSTM learns to make accurate defect percentage predictions throughout this process, capturing dependencies and patterns across the sequence.

The research focused on fine-tuning the models by experimenting with different hyperparameters, such as the number of units and sequence length. Through this extensive testing process, the study aimed to enhance the models’ predictive capabilities, specifically for predicting defect percentages on semiconductor materials. The results highlighted the importance of hyperparameters selection in achieving optimal model performance in this context.
