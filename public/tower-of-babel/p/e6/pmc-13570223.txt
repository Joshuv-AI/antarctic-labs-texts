A real‐world study on different initiation times and treatment durations of letermovir for cytomegalovirus prophylaxis after allogeneic haematopoietic stem cell transplantation
Authors: Shiyu Chen, Zhihang Su, Xiaohan Zhang, Xiaoqing Li, Xin Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryCytomegalovirus (CMV) reactivation is a frequent complication after allogeneic haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo‐HSCT), but the timing and duration of letermovir prophylaxis in routine practice remain uncertain. We retrospectively reviewed 97 allo‐HSCT recipients who received letermovir as primary prophylaxis, focusing on CMV‐related outcomes, transplant complications, survival and safety. Letermovir was initiated on day 0 in 31 patients, on day +7 in 44 patients, and on day +14 in 22 patients. The cohort was also stratified by prophylaxis duration, with 51 patients receiving 100‐day prophylaxis and 46 patients receiving 200‐day prophylaxis. Earlier initiation was associated with a lower 100‐day cumulative incidence (CIR) of CMV DNAemia/csCMVi (6.5% vs. 18.2% vs. 36.4%; Gray's test p = 0.008), later first CMV deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) detection (median 78 vs. 54 vs. 36 days; p = 0.005) and a shorter duration of DNAemia. Compared with the 200‐day group, the 100‐day group had more late CMV reactivation after discontinuation during follow‐up (21.6% vs. 0%; p < 0.001) and numerically lower 1‐year overall survival (80.4% [95% confidence interval (CI), 66.6–88.9] vs. 93.5% [95% CI, 81.1–97.8]; log‐rank p = 0.103), without more severe chronic graft‐versus‐host disease or grade ≥3 drug‐related adverse events. These real‐world findings suggest that both earlier initiation and longer letermovir prophylaxis deserve further evaluation after allo‐HSCT.Keywords: allogeneic haematopoietic stem cell transplantation, CMV reactivation, cytomegalovirus, letermovir, prophylaxis duration

INTRODUCTION

CMV infection is a familiar problem and remains difficult to predict in individual patients after allo‐HSCT. It may appear only as asymptomatic DNAemia, but it can also progress to clinically significant infection or end‐organ disease; CMV has been linked with graft‐versus‐host disease (GVHD), secondary infection, delayed immune recovery and higher transplant morbidity. 1 Despite the widespread adoption of newer prophylactic strategies, late CMV events and long‐term post‐transplant complications remain common. 2 , 3 Letermovir targets the pUL56 subunit of the CMV terminase complex. Because it lacks the myelosuppressive effects of conventional deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) polymerase inhibitors, letermovir has become a preferred option for CMV prophylaxis after transplantation. 4 , 5 Now the remaining questions are mainly about how to use it in daily treatment. Considerable variation exists across centres in both the initiation time and the duration of prophylaxis. 6 Those differences are not trivial: breakthrough DNAemia, late CMV after discontinuation, non‐CMV viral events and resistance still occur. 7 , 8 , 9 Clinical trials have established the value of letermovir, but many decisions at the bedside are more operational than theoretical. We reviewed 97 allo‐HSCT recipients treated at our centre to compare letermovir initiation on day 0, day +7 or day +14 and to examine 100‐day versus 200‐day prophylaxis. STUDY DESIGN AND PARTICIPANTS

This single‐centre retrospective real‐world study was conducted at Shenzhen Second People's Hospital. We included patients with haematological diseases who underwent first allo‐HSCT between January 2023 and June 2025 and received letermovir primary prophylaxis after transplantation. Clinical, transplant‐related, laboratory, virologic and follow‐up data were obtained from the electronic medical record system and laboratory. Written informed consent was obtained from patients or legal guardians. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Shenzhen Second People's Hospital/The First Affiliated Hospital of Shenzhen University and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligible patients were aged ≥14 years, had donor and/or recipient CMV‐IgG seropositivity before transplantation, received letermovir as primary prophylaxis after allo‐HSCT and had complete clinical and follow‐up data. Patients were excluded for active CMV infection within 1 week before transplantation, death or loss to follow‐up within 2 weeks after transplantation, concomitant use of another anti‐CMV drug for primary prophylaxis or severe hepatic or renal impairment that precluded letermovir use. Patients who developed transient, unconfirmed low‐level CMV positivity without treatment were retained but handled according to the end‐point definitions below.

Medication regimen and grouping

Letermovir was given orally at 480 mg once daily, or 240 mg once daily when co‐administered with ciclosporin. Patients were grouped by prophylaxis start date as day 0, day +7 or day +14. The same cohort was also analysed by planned prophylaxis duration as a 100‐day group or a 200‐day group. Group allocation reflected routine clinical practice during the study period rather than formal randomization.

Monitoring and definitions

Whole‐blood CMV DNA was monitored weekly after transplantation through at least day +200. Viral load <500 copies/mL was considered negative, and CMV DNAemia was defined as CMV DNA ≥500 copies/ml. A CMV DNA blip was a single low‐level positive result of 500–999 copies/mL that was not confirmed on repeat testing and did not require pre‐emptive treatment; these isolated blips were excluded from the primary CMV end‐point because they were transient and clinically untreated. Clinically significant CMV infection (csCMVi) was defined as CMV DNAemia requiring pre‐emptive anti‐CMV therapy or CMV end‐organ disease, consistent with prior letermovir trials and consensus definitions. In practice, pre‐emptive therapy was usually started for persistent or rising DNAemia, particularly at ≥1000 copies/mL, or when CMV disease was suspected. Letermovir primary prophylaxis meant administration after allo‐HSCT in the absence of active CMV infection requiring anti‐CMV treatment at initiation. GVHD was graded using the Glucksberg criteria. 10

Conditioning regimens

Haploidentical transplantation followed an anti‐human thymocyte globulin (ATG)‐based protocol broadly consistent with the Beijing haploidentical platform, using granulocyte colony‐stimulating factor (G‐CSF)‐mobilized donor stem‐cell collection and intensified immunosuppression. 11 The fludarabine, busulfan, cyclophosphamide and rabbit ATG conditioning regimen (FABC) included fludarabine 50 mg (30 mg/m2) on days −11 to −7, busulfan 0.8 mg/kg every 6 h on days −10 to −7, cyclophosphamide 60 mg/kg on days −6 and −5 and rabbit ATG 2.5 mg/kg/day on days −4 to −2. Matched transplantation used a busulfan and cyclophosphamide conditioning regimen (BUCY): busulfan 0.8 mg/kg every 6 h on days −10 to −7, cyclophosphamide 60 mg/kg on days −6 and −5 and rabbit ATG 2.5 mg/kg/day on days −3 and −2.

Complication prophylaxis

GVHD prophylaxis consisted of ciclosporin (from day −1, target trough concentration of 150–250 ng/mL, tapered from 3 months if no GVHD occurred), mycophenolate mofetil 0.5 g twice daily from day −12 and methotrexate 15 mg/m2 on day +2 and 10 mg/m2 on days +4 and +7. Infection prophylaxis included 2 weeks of pre‐HSCT intestinal decontamination with piperacillin‐sulbactam, levofloxacin, trimethoprim‐sulfamethoxazole, ganciclovir and posaconazole. Haemorrhagic cystitis (HC) prevention used urinary catheterization before cyclophosphamide, mesna at 0, 4 and 8 h after cyclophosphamide, urine alkalization with sodium bicarbonate, hydration at 2000 mL/m2/day and furosemide as needed. Relapse surveillance included bone marrow minimal residual disease, STR/chimerism and genetic monitoring on day 0, day +30, and at 3, 6, 9, 12, 18 and 24 months after transplantation, consistent with risk‐adapted monitoring after allo‐HSCT. 12 Alprostadil was administered until day +28 to prevent veno‐occlusive disease (VOD).

Study end‐points

The primary end‐point for the initiation time analysis was 100‐day CMV DNAemia/csCMVi. The duration analysis focused on late CMV reactivation after letermovir discontinuation during follow‐up. Secondary end‐points were CMV infection duration, peak CMV DNA level, overall survival (OS), acute GVHD, HC, haematopoietic reconstitution, relapse CIR, non‐relapse mortality (NRM), causes of death and safety.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0. Categorical variables are presented as n (%) and were compared using Pearson's chi‐squared or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables are shown as median interquartile range (IQR) and were compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Time‐to‐event outcomes, including CMV DNAemia/csCMVi, CIR and NRM, were analysed using competing‐risk methods and compared with Gray's test, with appropriate competing events considered. OS was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared by the log‐rank test. Outcomes without consistent event dates, including aGVHD, haemorrhagic cystitis and cGVHD, were summarized as proportions. If no events occurred in either group, statistical comparison was recorded as not applicable. Two‐sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Patient enrolment and follow‐up

Surviving patients were followed until 31 December 2025. Median follow‐up was 17.5 months (range, 1.2–25.5 months). Figure 1 shows the grouping process. Of 101 initially eligible patients, two were excluded for severe pretransplant liver and kidney impairment, defined as alanine aminotransferase (ALT) or aspartate aminotransferase (AST) >3 times the upper limit of normal or creatinine clearance <30 mL/min. One died from a non‐transplant‐related cause before day +15 and one was lost to follow up. The final cohort comprised 97 allo‐HSCT recipients receiving letermovir primary prophylaxis and with evaluable CMV monitoring and survival follow‐up. By initiation time, 31 patients started letermovir on day 0, 44 on day +7 and 22 on day +14. By duration, 51 received a 100‐day course and 46 received a 200‐day course.

Study flow diagram of allogeneic haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo‐HSCT) recipients receiving letermovir prophylaxis. The diagram shows patient screening, exclusions, final inclusion and grouping according to letermovir initiation time.

Baseline characteristics

Baseline characteristics were generally balanced across the three initiation groups. Median age was 37 years (range, 15–66), and 64.9% of patients were male. The main underlying diseases were acute leukaemia (AL), aplastic anaemia (AA), non‐Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) and myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS). Donor types included matched sibling, matched unrelated and haploidentical donor (HID). Age, sex, disease composition, donor type, conditioning regimen and donor/recipient CMV serostatus did not differ significantly across initiation groups (all p > 0.10), supporting comparability for the timing analysis (Table 1).

In the duration analysis, age, sex, disease status, disease type, ABO compatibility, pretransplant CMV DNA, pretransplant Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) DNA and conditioning regimen were comparable between the 100‐day and 200‐day groups (all p > 0.05). Donor type differed (p = 0.0004): HID transplantation was more frequent in the 100‐day group (92.2% vs. 63.0%), while matched unrelated donor transplantation occurred only in the 200‐day group (0.0% vs. 17.4%). This imbalance was considered when interpreting duration‐related outcomes, because the transplant platform may influence immune reconstitution, GVHD, infection risk and the chance of remaining on immunosuppression after day 100 (Table S1).

CMV outcomes by letermovir initiation time

The 100‐day competing‐risk CIR of CMV DNAemia/csCMVi increased with later letermovir initiation: 6.5% in the day 0 group, 18.2% in the day +7 group and 36.4% in the day +14 group (Gray's test p = 0.008). Earlier prophylaxis also delayed first CMV DNA detection, with median times of 78 days (IQR, 78–92), 54 days (IQR, 41–70) and 36 days (IQR, 28–49) respectively (p = 0.005). DNAemia duration followed the same direction and was shortest with day‐0 initiation: 7 days (IQR, 5–12), 14 days (IQR, 9–21) and 21 days (IQR, 14–32) respectively (p = 0.010). Among patients with confirmed CMV DNAemia/csCMVi, peak CMV DNA levels increased numerically with delayed initiation: 2840 copies/mL (IQR, 2300–3380), 5185 copies/mL (IQR, 3,787.5–11,142.5), and 13 745 copies/mL (IQR, 6,505–29100) in the day 0, day +7 and day +14 groups. This comparison was not significant (p = 0.090) (Table 2), but Figure S1 showed lower and less dispersed peak values in the day 0 group than in the day +14 group.

Transplantation‐related complications and haematopoietic reconstitution

GVHD outcomes did not differ significantly by initiation time. The day 0, day +7 and day +14 groups had similar rates of cGVHD (25.8%, 29.5% and 22.7%; p = 0.875), moderate‐to‐severe cGVHD (9.7%, 2.3%, and 9.1%; p = 0.352), grade I–II aGVHD (12.9%, 9.1% and 13.6%; p = 0.771) and grade III–IV aGVHD within 100‐day (22.6%, 9.1% and 13.6%; p = 0.258). Grade III–IV HC was also comparable (3.2%, 4.5% and 4.5%; p = 1.000). No post‐transplant lymphoproliferative disorder (PTLD) events occurred. EBV reactivation was uncommon, limiting meaningful between‐group analysis. Haematopoietic recovery was similar across groups: median neutrophil engraftment occurred at 17, 18 and 17 days (p = 0.693) and platelet engraftment at 23, 24 and 25 days (p = 0.890) (Table 2).

Survival outcomes by initiation time

One‐year OS was 96.8% (95% confidence interval (CI), 79.2–99.5) in the day 0 group, 86.4% (95% CI, 72.1–93.6) in the day +7 group and 72.7% (95% CI, 49.1–86.7) in the day +14 group. The log‐rank comparison showed a trend but did not reach significance (p = 0.065) (Table 2, Figure 2A). One‐year CIR of relapse was 16.1%, 11.5% and 0.0% respectively (Gray's test p = 0.163), whereas 1‐year NRM increased with delayed letermovir start (3.2%, 9.1% and 27.3%; Gray's test p = 0.027) (Table 2).

Overall mortality differed across initiation groups (6.5%, 13.6% and 31.8%; p = 0.049), but the distribution of specific causes of death did not (Fisher–Freeman–Halton exact test, p = 0.214). Deaths were mainly related to infection, GVHD, haemorrhage or multiple organ complications rather than relapse (Table 3). Baseline characteristics were not significantly different across letermovir initiation groups or conditioning‐strategy groups in the overall cohort, suggesting that donor type and transplant protocol were unlikely to be dominant sources of imbalance in the timing comparison. Because HID transplantation predominated, we also performed an exploratory HID subgroup analysis. The directions of CMV DNAemia/csCMVi, CIR, NRM, viral‐load kinetics and OS were broadly consistent with the full cohort (Table 4).

Outcomes by prophylaxis duration (100‐day vs. 200‐day)

During follow‐up, CMV reactivation occurred in 11 patients in the 100‐day group (21.6%) and in none in the 200‐day group (0%; p < 0.001). One‐year OS was numerically higher with 200‐day of prophylaxis than with 100‐day (93.5% [95% CI, 81.1–97.8] vs. 80.4% [95% CI, 66.6–88.9]), but the difference was not significant (log‐rank p = 0.103) (Table 5, Figure 2B). GVHD outcomes were similar between duration groups, including cGVHD (27.5% vs. 26.1%; p = 1.000), moderate‐to‐severe cGVHD (9.8% vs. 2.2%; p = 0.208), grade I–II aGVHD (11.8% vs. 10.9%; p = 1.000) and grade III–IV aGVHD (13.7% vs. 15.2%; p = 1.000) (Table 5). Because aGVHD usually occurs within 100 days, it was interpreted mainly as an early comparability measure; cGVHD was more relevant to duration. The 100‐day group had lower 1‐year CIR of relapse (3.9% vs. 17.6%; Gray's test p = 0.033) but higher 1‐year NRM (17.6% vs. 4.3%; Gray's test p = 0.044) (Table 5). Mortality was numerically higher in the 100‐day group (21.6% vs. 8.7%; p = 0.097), while causes of death were similarly distributed (p = 0.632) (Table 3). Relapse‐related death was uncommon in both groups; infection‐related mortality and transplant complications accounted for most deaths.

Safety

No grade ≥3 drug‐related adverse reactions were recorded in either duration group (Table 5). No safety signal emerged that would argue against earlier initiation or extension in appropriately selected patients, although this retrospective dataset was not powered to detect uncommon toxicities, subtle drug interactions or rare resistance events. Liver and kidney function results should still be reviewed when prophylaxis is prolonged.

DISCUSSION

In our cohort, earlier initiation of letermovir was associated with more favourable CMV‐related outcomes after allo‐HSCT. Patients who started letermovir on the day of transplantation experienced fewer early CMV DNAemia and csCMVi events, a later first positive CMV DNA result and a shorter DNAemia period than those who started on day +7 or day +14. Early inhibition of the CMV terminase complex may suppress viral replication. 5 As this was a retrospective real‐world study, our results should be interpreted as associations rather than evidence of causality. The peak–load comparison was not statistically significant, but the overall trend remained clinically relevant. In the first few weeks after transplantation, patients are exposed to profound immunosuppression; mucosal damage is also common, and CMV‐specific immune control has not yet fully recovered. Early administration of letermovir before sustained viral replication is established may help maintain a lower early viral burden. At the same time, we found that patients who started letermovir later had worse OS. Although this difference did not reach statistical significance, the increase in NRM was more prominent than CIR. This suggests that infection, GVHD, bleeding and multiple organ complications may contribute more substantially to inferior overall survival than relapse itself. Prior studies also have linked higher CMV viral load to increased transplant‐related and non‐relapse mortality 13 , 14 and early CMV reactivation to inferior survival. 15 , 16 However, we do not interpret these data as direct evidence that early letermovir initiation improves survival. A more reasonable explanation is that early CMV suppression may be associated with a lower burden of transplant‐related complications. In allo‐HSCT, these factors rarely act alone; CMV control, immune recovery, GVHD treatment and concomitant anti‐infective prophylaxis are closely related.

The duration analysis raises another issue that clinicians often face after discharge: the optimal time to discontinue prophylaxis remains uncertain. In our cohort, no CMV reactivation was seen in the 200‐day group, whereas 21.6% of patients in the 100‐day group developed reactivation. This agrees with previous reports showing that CMV events often occur after early discontinuation of letermovir, especially in patients with persistent immunosuppression, delayed immune recovery, severe neutropenia or post‐transplant cyclophosphamide (PTCy)‐based GVHD prophylaxis. 8 , 17 , 18 , 19 There was no late CMV reactivation after discontinuation in the 200‐day group. This finding is consistent with a randomized phase 3 trial showing that extended letermovir prophylaxis from 100 to 200 days reduced late‐onset csCMVi with an acceptable safety profile, 20 which supports previous studies suggesting that prolonged prevention may be beneficial for patients with delayed immune recovery or persistent immune suppression. 21 , 22 , 23 There is an important limitation in the duration comparison in our study. As shown in Table S1, the 100‐day group included more patients receiving HID transplantation, while MUD transplantation appeared only in the 200‐day group. This imbalance is important, because different donor types can influence immune reconstitution, GVHD, infection risk and NRM. Therefore, we analysed the HID subgroup separately. The direction of the results was generally similar to that of the whole cohort, although the number of patients was small and the analysis should be considered exploratory. For this reason, the duration results are better viewed as a basis for local risk assessment, not as a fixed rule for every patient.

Unlike prior studies mainly assessing letermovir versus no prophylaxis, 24 , 25 our study directly compared initiation at day 0, day +7 and day +14. Earlier initiation was associated with lower CMV incidence and reduced virologic exposure, providing practical evidence for optimizing prophylaxis timing. This is generally consistent with the suggestions of European Conference on Infections in Leukaemia (ECIL) and American Society for Transplantation and Cellular Therapy (ASTCT), which both emphasize stratification and continuous risk‐adapted monitoring and emphasize institution‐specific viral load thresholds set by their respective medical centres. 26 , 27 , 28 With earlier and longer letermovir prophylaxis, breakthrough DNAemia, resistance and broader viral effects require attention. Low‐level DNAemia and rare UL56‐associated resistance mutations highlight the need for standardized viral‐load thresholds, prompt repeat testing and access to resistance testing. 9 In haploidentical transplantation, possible associations with PTLD also support enhanced surveillance for other opportunistic viruses, including EBV, and careful immunosuppression management. 29

This study adds two practical points to the existing evidence. First, it directly compares initiation on day 0, day +7 and day +14; second, it evaluates the clinical decision between 100‐day and 200‐day prophylaxis. No serious drug‐related toxicity was observed in this study; however, liver and kidney function, drug resistance and breakthrough infection should still be monitored. Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Because it is a single‐centre study, and the sample size is not large, it limits our ability to perform more detailed analyses and evaluation of some subgroups, such as patients who received PTCy‐based GVHD prophylaxis, or umbilical cord blood transplantation, and the classification of CMV serum status of donors and recipients. We also lack the data regarding CMV‐specific T‐cell recovery and other indicators of immune reconstitution, so we cannot make a stopping rule based on the immune situation. Because prolonged prophylaxis may increase treatment costs, cost‐effectiveness should also be considered when selecting patients for extended letermovir prophylaxis. 30 In the future, it is necessary to carry out prospective multicentre research, use propensity score methods or randomization, plus immune monitoring, so as to determine who can benefit most from early initiation and prolonged prophylaxis.

In conclusion, initiation of letermovir on day 0 with extension of prophylaxis to 200 days was associated with improved CMV control and reduced late CMV reactivation after transplantation, and no clinically significant drug‐related adverse events were found. These findings require validation in larger, multicentre cohorts encompassing diverse transplant platforms. Future research should also evaluate the immune‐based stopping criteria, cost‐effectiveness and the broader virologic consequences of prolonged CMV prophylaxis after transplantation.
