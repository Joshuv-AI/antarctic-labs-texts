Immune checkpoint inhibitors in pediatric patients with melanoma: a systematic literature review
Authors: Charmy Vyas, Andriy Moshyk, Gina Fusaro, Stergios Zacharoulis, Mir Sohail Fazeli, Nishu Gaind, Shirin Behyan, Pratik Thakkar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Aim: This study summarized the existing evidence on the outcomes and safety of anti-PD-1s, anti-PD-L1s and anti-CTLA-4s in pediatric patients with melanoma.Materials & methods: MEDLINE® and Embase were searched from database inception to 01-12-2023.Results: Of 1537 records identified, 27 studies (k) of 64 patients were included. Most studies were case reports (k = 16). All studies used anti-PD-1s (nivolumab, pembrolizumab) alone or anti-CTLA-4s (ipilimumab). Survival outcomes (k = 7), response outcomes (k = 15) and adverse events (k = 16) varied. Safety profiles of anti-PD-1s and anti-CTLA-4s were broadly similar to that seen in adults.Conclusion: Despite scarce, heterogenous data, this review can be a reference for clinicians. Future clinical trials should include adolescents to grow the evidence base on immune checkpoint inhibitors in pediatric melanoma.

Background

Melanoma is a cancer of the melanocytes, which are pigment-producing cells that can be found throughout the body including in the skin [1]. In 2021, it was estimated that there were 106,110 new cases of melanoma in the United States and 7180 patient deaths from the disease in the same year [2]. However, pediatric melanoma – defined as melanoma occurring during childhood or adolescence before the age of 18 or 21 years [3] – is much less common. Pediatric melanoma accounts for less than 1% of all new cases of melanoma and about 1 to 3% of all pediatric cancers [4,5]. Additionally, rates of melanoma in the pre-pubescent population are significantly lower (1.1 cases per million persons) than those in the post-pubescent population (18.0 cases per million persons) [6].

There are currently a lack of clinical trials evaluating treatments for melanoma in pediatric patients. As such, current recommendations and treatments for pediatric patients with advanced melanoma are extrapolated from large, randomized, Phase III clinical trials on adults [7,8]. The clinical behavior of pediatric melanoma typically resembles that of adults [9]; however, key differences between pediatric and adult melanoma have not been fully characterized [3].

According to consensus [7,10], the current standard of care for pediatric patients under the age of 19 with localized melanoma consists of surgery as first-line treatment at various margins of resection depending on the thickness of the melanoma. In advanced stages of the disease, two classes of drugs including immunotherapy or targeted therapies may also be considered. Therapies targeting the MAPK pathway include BRAF (vemurafenib, dabrafenib, encorafenib) and MEK (cobimetinib, trametinib, binimetinib) inhibitors. Immune checkpoint inhibitors, on the other hand, target the CTLA4 (ipilimumab) or the PD1 (nivolumab and pembrolizumab) and can be used regardless of BRAF status [7].

Based on the results of studies in adult patients, the use of immune checkpoint inhibitors as adjuvant treatment have been extrapolated to pediatric patients with high-risk cutaneous melanoma, such as those with regional lymph node involvement [10]. Currently, for pediatric patients with resected stage III melanoma, the European Cooperative Study Group for Pediatric Rare Tumors (EXPeRT) within the European Union-funded Paediatric Rare Tumours Network – European Registry (PARTNER) project recommends adjuvant therapy with an anti-PD 1 agent (nivolumab or pembrolizumab) regardless of BRAF mutation, and dabrafenib plus trametinib for patients with BRAF V600 variant [7]. Moreover, nivolumab, pembrolizumab, or ipilimumab plus nivolumab are recommended in patients with unresectable stage III/IV melanoma regardless of BRAF mutation; however, in patients with BRAF V600 mutation, BRAF/MEK combination therapies including dabrafenib plus trametinib, vemurafenib plus cobimetinib, or binimetinib plus encorafenib are also recommended.

To inform treatment strategies, as well as to guide the design of future observational studies or clinical trials, there is a need to better understand the available evidence on the use of immune checkpoint inhibitors for the treatment of pediatric melanoma. This systematic literature review summarized the available evidence on the use of immune checkpoint inhibitors including PD-1 inhibitors (cemiplimab, dostarlimab, nivolumab and pembrolizumab), PD-L1 inhibitors (atezolizumab, avelumab and durvalumab) and anti-CTLA-4s (ipilimumab and tremelimumab) in pediatric patients with melanoma.

Methods

Standard methodologies for conducting systematic reviews as recommended by the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [11] were followed. Results for the review were reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [12].

Data sources & search strategies

Studies reporting immune checkpoint inhibitors (PD-1, PD-L1 or CTLA-4 inhibitors) were identified by searching MEDLINE® and Embase via OvidSP using predefined search strategies from database inception to 12 January 2023 (Supplementary Tables S1–S4). As part of the gray literature search, key oncology conferences were searched between 2021 and 2022 via Embase, including the American Association for Cancer Research, American Society of Clinical Oncology, European Society for Medical Oncology, and Society for Melanoma Research. Searches of United States and European clinical trial registry databases were also conducted to capture articles with reported results, as were manual searches of the reference lists of previously published literature reviews on similar topics.

Study selection

Study eligibility criteria for the literature review were defined using the PICOS framework (Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcome, Study design). Studies were included if they reported on pediatric patients (aged 0–21 years) with melanoma who received an anti-PD1, anti-PD-L1 or anti-CTLA-4, and reported on an efficacy/effectiveness or safety outcome. Studies were excluded if they were not published in English or were a literature review (not a primary study). Additionally, studies reporting on rare subtypes of melanoma (such as primary diffuse leptomeningeal melanomatosis or primary intracranial malignant melanoma) were excluded due to underlying differences in their biology and disease management.

Two independent senior reviewers were responsible for reviewing all abstracts according to the PICOS criteria. Abstracts considered eligible for inclusion proceeded to a full-text screening phase, where they were screened by the same reviewers. All records deemed eligible after full-text screening were included in the literature review. At each stage of the screening process, any discrepancies between reviewers in the decision to include or exclude an article were resolved by a third reviewer to reach a consensus.

Data extraction & quality assessment

Relevant data available from the publications were extracted independently by two reviewers; discrepancies were resolved in consensus with a third reviewer. Data extraction variables included study, intervention and patient characteristics, as well as efficacy and safety outcomes. Baseline characteristics of interest were age, sex, disease stage, performance status, treatment status, type of prior treatments and comorbidities. Efficacy outcomes of interest included overall survival, progression-free survival, disease-free survival, recurrence-free survival, as well as objective response, complete response, partial response, stable disease, progressive disease and mortality. Safety outcomes included total adverse events (AEs), treatment-related AEs, serious AEs, treatment-related serious AEs and treatment-related withdrawals.

Finally, two independent reviewers assessed the quality of the included studies. Study quality was assessed using Cochrane's tool for assessing risk of bias in randomized trials (version 2) [13], the Newcastle-Ottawa scale for cohort studies [14] and a modified version of the Newcastle-Ottawa scale for case reports and case series [15]. If necessary, conflicts were resolved via consensus with a third investigator.

Summary of evidence

Evidence was summarized as reported in the various publications. Overall survival was defined as the length of time from either the date of diagnosis or the start of treatment for a disease, during which the patient was still alive.

A meta-analysis was not considered appropriate for this review after assessing heterogeneity in patient characteristics, treatment characteristics and outcome definitions. Thus, a descriptive summary of the evidence was carried out.

Results

Study selection

The literature search identified a total of 1537 records. During the abstract screening phase, most records were excluded by population (not reporting on pediatric melanoma) or study design (e.g., literature reviews). Following full-text screening, a total of 28 publications reporting on 27 unique studies were included in the review (Figure 1); the study conducted by Que et al. (2021) was captured as both a conference abstract [16] and a full journal article [17].

The total sample size of pediatric melanoma patients across all studies was 64 patients ranging from 1 [18–35,42,43] to 12 [38,40]. Study country or region was reported for 25 studies and included Europe (number of unique studies [k] = 9) [20,26,29–32,35,36,43], North America (the United States and Canada; k = 7) [18,21,24,28,33,40,42], South America (k = 2) [19,25], Asia (k = 1) [16,17] and the Middle East (k = 1) [34]. Two single-arm trials were multi-national, and recruited patients from the United States, United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, Europe and Asia [38,39], while one retrospective cohort was multi-national, and recruited patients from Australia and New Zealand [37]. Study follow-up ranged from 3.5 months [31] to 69.6 months [37] across 24 studies (median: 12 months); follow-up length often corresponded to patient survival.

Among interventions, ipilimumab was the most frequently reported (k = 9; number of patients [n] = 32) [26,30,32,34–36,38,40,43], followed by nivolumab (k = 8; n = 11) [19,21,23,24,28,31,33,41], a combination of nivolumab and ipilimumab or ipilimumab and pembrolizumab (k = 6; n = 7) [18,25,27,37,41,42], pembrolizumab (k = 4; n = 12) [17,22,29,39] and a combination of nivolumab and cetuximab (k = 1; n = 1) [20].

Three studies reported on treatment switching [18,25,41]; Aljundi et al. (2022) reported a patient switching from nivolumab monotherapy to nivolumab plus ipilimumab (k = 1, n = 1) [18], Marjanska et al. (2020) reported that two patients switched from nivolumab plus ipilimumab to nivolumab monotherapy (k = 1, n = 2) [41], and one study reported a patient switching from pembrolizumab monotherapy to nivolumab plus ipilimumab (k = 1, n = 1) [25]. In two studies, the reason for treatment switching was described as disease progression after the first treatment [18,25]; no reason was provided for the third study [41].

Patient characteristics

Of the 23 studies reporting sex [16–35,38,41,42,43], half were female patients (k = 12, n = 20) [18–20,22,26,27,31,33,35,38,41,42], and half were male (k = 13, n = 20) [16,17,21,23–25,28–30,32,34,38,41,43]. The age of pediatric patients with melanoma who were treated with an immune checkpoint inhibitor was reported in 24 studies for 42 patients [16–28,30–36,38,41–43]. All patients were younger than 18 years; age ranged from newborn [33] to 17.8 years [41] (median: 12 years). Twelve patients were younger than 12 years old [17,20,22,23,28,32,33,41,42] and 30 patients were 12 years or older [18,19,21,24–27,29–31,34–36,38,41,43]. The remaining three studies only reported mean or median age for their total enrolled populations which were broader than our inclusion criteria [37,39,40]; however, reported mean or median ages of patients (13, 13.4 and 10 years) were consistent with the overall median we observed amongst all studies. Comorbidities were reported in only three studies [20,25,29].

Where stage of disease was recorded, 18 patients had stage III [17,19–22,26,29,33,35,36,38,41] and 15 patients had stage IV [27,30,35,38,41,43]. One patient had stage IB melanoma initially; however, the patient developed metastases three years after diagnosis and received treatment with an immune checkpoint inhibitor when axillary metastasis occurred [24]. TNM staging was also reported across eight studies, although stages varied [17,21,22,26,27,29,30,36].

Patients typically had melanoma as their primary cancer type; however, the patient described by Chambon et al. (2018) was described to present initially with a sarcomatoid carcinoma of the scalp. Nivolumab was initiated, and while receiving nivolumab therapy, the patient developed two large skin melanomas and several squamous cell carcinomas. Nivolumab therapy was continued to treat the melanoma, and the melanomas were eventually excised [20]. Complete details on patient characteristics per study are provided in Supplementary Table S5.

Efficacy outcomes

Survival outcomes including progression-free survival, disease-free survival, recurrence-free survival or overall survival, were reported across seven studies (Table 2) [17,23,28,35,38,41,43]. Overall survival ranged from 6.8 to 22.0 months; both patients were receiving adjuvant nivolumab monotherapy [41]. Overall survival in patients receiving ipilimumab monotherapy ranged from 11 months for a single patient in a metastatic setting [43] to 18.2 months in the advanced setting for four patients [38]. Progression-free survival ranged from 2.0 months for a patient in the advanced setting [17] to 9.9 months for a patient in the metastatic setting; both patients were receiving pembrolizumab [17]. For ipilimumab monotherapy, progression-free survival ranged from 2.6 [38] to 9.0 [43] months in an advanced and metastatic setting, respectively.

Most studies reported some form of response including partial disease (k = 8, n = 16) [17,19,25,26,32,34,38,41], stable disease (k = 6, n = 6) [17,29,33,38,40,43], complete response (k = 2, n = 4) [22,41] and partial response (k = 2, n = 3) [29,38]. Two studies noted that no response was achieved [21,39].

Of the included studies, 11 reported on the proportion of patients who died at some point during follow-up (time of death was not reported) [17,19,20,25,34,36–38,41–43]. Out of 29 total patients, 20 patients died; among patients receiving nivolumab and pembrolizumab monotherapy, all died [17,19,41]. In patients who were treated with ipilimumab monotherapy, half of them died during follow-up. Of note, none of the publications indicated whether death was attributed to the treatment under study. Furthermore, among the 29 patients who died during follow-up, 11 of them died following metastasis of disease [17,19,25,34,38,41,43]. These patients also varied with respect to age (range: 3.1–17 years), sex and disease type and stage. A summary of the characteristics of the patients who died following metastasis is provided in Supplementary Table S6.

Safety outcomes

Reporting of AEs varied across studies. The most commonly reported AEs included hepatic AEs, diarrhea, vomiting and nausea. The median length of follow-up for reporting of AEs was 10 months (k = 16; range: 0.5 [18,33,40] to 22.7 months [41]) [18,19,22–24,26–33,38,40,41].

Hepatic AEs were reported by nine patients (k = 4) treated with either nivolumab monotherapy (adjuvant setting, n = 1) [31], nivolumab plus ipilimumab followed by nivolumab monotherapy (adjuvant setting, n = 1) [41], ipilimumab monotherapy (advanced setting, n = 6) [38], or nivolumab plus ipilimumab (metastatic setting, n = 1) [27]. Diarrhea was reported by three patients (k = 2) [33,41] treated with either nivolumab (adjuvant setting, n = 1 [41]; metastatic setting, n = 1) [33], or nivolumab plus ipilimumab followed by nivolumab monotherapy (adjuvant setting, n = 1) [41]. Nausea was reported by two patients (k = 2) treated with nivolumab monotherapy (adjuvant setting, n = 1) [31] or ipilimumab monotherapy (advanced setting; n = 1) [38].

Immune-related AEs were explicitly reported in three studies for patients receiving nivolumab plus ipilimumab (metastatic setting) [27] and ipilimumab (unspecified treatment setting [40] and advanced settings [38]). Two of the three studies noted grade 3 or 4 immune-related AEs in a total of 15 patients (n = 6, 50% [38]; n = 9, 27% [40]); Merchant et al. (2016) further reported that 2 (6%) patients experienced more than one immune-related AE [40]. An additional single grade 2 immune-related AE was reported in a case study (n = 1) [27].

Only one study reported treatment-related discontinuation: 6 of 12 patients discontinued ipilimumab in the advanced setting (n = 1 of 4 at 3 mg/kg; n = 5 of 8 at 10 mg/kg); the authors further noted there were no treatment-related deaths [38]. Treatment-related AEs and grading of the AEs were scarcely reported. Complete details on reported AEs per study are provided in Supplementary Table S7.

Study quality assessment

All three interventional studies included in our review were single-arm, open-label studies with a high overall risk of bias due to lack of blinding and lack of a comparator group [38–40]. Among the 24 non-interventional studies (case reports, case series, retrospective and prospective cohorts), overall quality was fair (score of ≥4 out of 9 possible points in cohort studies [k = 6] [17,34–37,43] and score of ≥4 out of 8 possible points in case reports/series [k = 18]; Supplementary Tables S8–S9). Fair quality for cohort studies was attributed to use of secure database records to assess outcomes and generally adequate follow-up length, but use of patient samples that were typically small and not representative of the target population and lack of a comparator group. For case reports, studies were considered fair quality due to sufficient description of the case to allow other investigators to replicate the research or to allow practitioners to make inferences related to their own practice, and adequate ascertainment of the exposure and outcomes, but inadequate descriptions of dose-response effects or whether other alternative causes that may have explained the observation were ruled out.

Discussion

This literature review identified 27 unique studies evaluating anti-PD-1s and anti-CTLA-4s for the treatment of pediatric melanoma, reporting on a total of 64 patients. All included studies reported on anti-PD-1s (nivolumab or pembrolizumab) or anti-CTLA-4s (ipilimumab) alone or as combination therapy; no evidence on the use of anti-PD-L1s in pediatric populations was identified. The majority of studies were case reports, and study quality was generally considered fair to poor due to study biases in methodology, indicating an overall low-quality of evidence.

Approved checkpoint inhibitors for the treatment of melanoma in pediatric patients 12 years of age and older include nivolumab [44,45], ipilimumab [45,46], pembrolizumab [47], nivolumab in combination with ipilimumab [44,45], and nivolumab in combination with relatlimab [48]. With the exception of the label expansion of ipilimumab by the United States Food and Drug Administration for patients aged 12–17 years, which was based on the safety and efficacy outcomes of a phase II clinical trial in a pediatric population [38,46], there remains a lack of clinical trial evidence in pediatric patients with melanoma. Furthermore, high-quality observational evidence is also severely limited. Treatment strategies for melanoma in pediatric patients are often extrapolated from evidence of adult cases of melanoma, which was explicitly described in six studies included in this review [19,20,23,34,38,39]. Further, the weight-based dosing reported across included studies generally aligns with that of weight-based dosing in adult patients. Recent evidence suggests that there is no immune-mediated safety risk when adolescents are treated with approved adult dosing regimens for nivolumab with or without ipilimumab; future studies should explore dosing in pediatric non-adolescent patients [49]. Moreover, given the paucity of information on survival, as well as the heterogeneity of data in terms of dose, duration and follow-up period, drawing clear trends on the comparative efficacy of treatments was not possible. Thus, future clinical trials in adults should strongly consider including pediatric or adolescent patients to broaden the evidence base in this population.

In terms of safety, while most studies reported AEs, the types of AEs observed varied. In fact, most types of AEs were unique to a single patient. Thus, no conclusive trends on the comparative safety of immune checkpoint inhibitors for this population could be inferred. Nevertheless, observation of the types of AEs reported across three studies suggests that the safety profile of anti-PD-1s and anti-CTLA-4s is similar to that seen in adults [38,44,47]. However, it remains difficult to conclude on the safety of immune checkpoint inhibitors in the pediatric population as deaths due to treatment were not reported. Additionally, it is apparent from the relatively short follow up of the identified studies that long-term safety data are lacking in children treated with checkpoint inhibitors.

This is, to the best of our knowledge, the first systematic literature review to investigate the use of immune checkpoint inhibitors to treat pediatric melanoma patients. This review contributes to the body of evidence on pediatric melanoma as a cohesive repository of all current studies reporting efficacy and safety outcomes of immune checkpoint inhibitors in pediatric patients. Further, it may be used by clinicians as a reference when treating patients with similar profiles to those gathered in this review. However, limitations associated with the findings of this review should be noted. Most of the included studies were case reports, with limited evidence from cohort studies and clinical trials. Additionally, patient characteristics, namely disease type, disease stage, and performance status, as well as treatment type, dose and duration varied across studies, which hinders our ability to identify trends across the evidence base. For example, it was not possible to detect differences in efficacy or safety outcomes among adolescents aged 12–21 years (who are more likely to be comparable to adults) versus children under 12 years (in whom the disease may considered to be different). Lastly, the findings from our review should be interpreted with caution considering the few data points available and differences in patient characteristics.

Conclusion

Pediatric melanoma is a rare disease that is associated with high mortality and poor prognosis at advanced stages. While therapeutic options for late-stage disease include immune checkpoint inhibitors, pediatric patient treatment strategies are often based on evidence from adult populations. Data on the efficacy and safety of treatments are scarce, heterogeneous and are of lower quality. Future clinical studies should consider the inclusion of adolescents in adult clinical trials to allow further progress and knowledge in the treatment of childhood melanoma.

Future perspective

Given the substantial disease burden of pediatric melanoma, immune checkpoint inhibitors are likely to remain as pivotal treatment strategies. However, current reliance on adult patient data underscores the urgent need for dedicated research involving pediatric patients. Future clinical studies may include adolescents to gather robust, age-specific data on the efficacy and safety of immune checkpoint inhibitors. Additionally, the advancement of personalized medicine, incorporating genetic and molecular profiling, may offer new avenues for tailored treatments in this vulnerable population.
