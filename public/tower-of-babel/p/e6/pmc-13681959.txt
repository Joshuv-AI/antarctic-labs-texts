SFPQ/S100A8 complex induces filaggrin deficiency via selective autophagy and JNK phosphorylation in atopic dermatitis
Authors: Geunyeong Kim, Yujin Hong, Tae Young Han, Byeol Han, Beom Seok Park, Eun Ju Yang, Ayesha Kashif, Young Joon Jun, Hongchan An, Juram Lee, Ji Sook Lee, In Sik Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough atopic dermatitis (AD) is associated with a decrease of filaggrin levels, the underlying mechanisms are not clearly understood. Here, we aimed to investigate the pathogenic roles of the splicing factor proline- and glutamine-rich (SFPQ)/S100 calcium-binding protein A8 (S100A8) complex, a critical regulator inducing filaggrin deficiency in AD.MethodsThe formation of SFPQ/S100A8 complex was analyzed in the keratinocytes and skin tissues from patients with AD and 2,4-dinitrochlorobenzene (DNCB)-induced AD-like mice. The effects of leptomycin B, a CRM1 inhibitor, and rapamycin, a mTOR inhibitor were determined in DNCB-induced AD-like mice and the non-lesional and lesional primary keratinocytes of AD patients.ResultsThe relocation of SFPQ from the nucleus to the cytoplasm, elevated S100A8 levels, and SFPQ/S100A8 complex formation were proportional to filaggrin deficiency and AD severity in the keratinocytes of AD patients and AD-like mice. Interaction of the complex with filaggrin and c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) accelerated filaggrin degradation via selective autophagy and filaggrin downregulation via JNK phosphorylation, respectively. The mechanistic target of rapamycin kinase (mTOR) and chromosomal region maintenance 1 (CRM1) facilitated the cytosolic export of SFPQ to form the complex in human keratinocytes. Administration of leptomycin B and rapamycin suppressed the decrease in filaggrin levels by inhibiting SFPQ/S100A8 complex formation in AD-like mice.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates that the formation of SFPQ/S100A8 complex is a critical process in AD progression, which provides key insights into the pathogenic mechanisms of AD. The elucidation of the SFPQ/S100A8 complex axis in AD pathogenesis can be leveraged to develop therapeutic drugs for AD treatment.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12964-026-02776-5.

Introduction

Atopic dermatitis (AD), a common chronic skin disorder characterized by pruritis, irritation, redness, and inflammation, is closely associated with allergic asthma and allergic rhinitis [1–3]. AD is influenced by genetic components, skin barrier abnormalities, and hypersensitive immune responses. Particularly, the skin barrier plays vital roles in immune surveillance and homeostasis maintenance [4, 5]. Activation of c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) in AD suppresses FLG gene expression and upregulates cytokine secretion [6]. Autophagy also plays a crucial role in various aspects of AD. Inflammatory cytokines, chemical substances, and pathogens are degraded via autophagy, contributing to skin regeneration and protection [7]. Autophagy is generally suppressed by the mechanistic target of rapamycin kinase (mTOR) signaling pathway [8, 9]. Th2 cytokines, such as IL-4 and IL-13, activate the mTOR signaling pathway [10, 11]. Rapamycin, an mTOR inhibitor, serves as a potential therapeutic agent for AD by activating autophagy [12].

S100 calcium-binding protein A8 (S100A8) is a member of the S100 protein family consisting of calcium binding molecules exerting pro-inflammatory effects [13, 14]. The formation of S100A8 complexes, including homodimers (S100A8/S100A8) and heterodimers (S100A8/S100A9), enhances the inflammatory potential of S100A8 [15]. During the progression of AD, S100A8 levels are increased in the epidermal cells [16–18]. Elevated levels of S100A8 impair filaggrin expression in keratinocytes via the activation of toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), MAPKs, and NF-κB [19, 20]. Moreover, high levels of S100A8 in the skin correlate with the severity of AD by serving as an activator of inflammatory signals and a chemoattractant for neutrophils [21]. Notably, formation of S100A8/S100A9 complex in the cytoplasm is closely associated with the intracellular functions of S100A8, such as reactive oxygen species formation [22].

Splicing factor proline- and glutamine-rich (SFPQ) is a nuclear protein modulating transcriptional regulation, DNA damage repair, and speckle formation upon interaction with other nuclear RNA-binding proteins, such as the non-POU domain-containing octamer binding protein (NONO), paraspeckle component 1, and NeuN [23–25]. SFPQ plays an essential role in neuronal cell differentiation by binding with JNK and NONO [26]. Upon relocation to the cytoplasm, SFPQ forms complexes with abnormal Tau proteins within neurons and induces the formation of chronic pathological stress granules, serving as a trigger for neurodegenerative diseases, such as the Alzheimer’s disease [27, 28].

Chromosomal region maintenance 1 (CRM1) is a primary nuclear export receptor for proteins that regulates their movement from the nucleus to the cytoplasm upon interaction with Ran-GTP. This process maintains the normal cellular functions under physiological conditions [29]. Abnormal activation of CRM1 may be a key factor for the progression of various diseases. In viral infections, CRM1 is involved in the intracellular movement of hepatitis B virus or human immunodeficiency virus-1, facilitating the proper localization of these viruses in the cell [30–32]. Dysregulation of intracellular protein transport affects tumor growth and metastasis, particularly in breast cancer [33, 34].

In this study, we aimed to understand the pathogenic mechanisms of AD by focusing on SFPQ/S100A8 complex formation. Specifically, we investigated (1) the mTOR-dependent cytoplasmic relocation of SFPQ due to CRM1 activation in AD to determine the correlation between AD severity and SFPQ/S100A8 complex formation, (2) explored the mechanisms by which the SFPQ/S100A8 complex downregulates filaggrin, and (3) assessed the impact of impaired SFPQ relocation via mTOR and CRM1 inhibition.

Materials and methods

Materials

Hanks Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) (14025-076) was purchased from Life Technologies Inc. TNF-α (210-TA) and IFN-γ (285-IF) were purchased from R&D systems. 2,4-dinitrochlorobenzene (DNCB) (237329) and chloroquine (C6628) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. leptomycin B (S7580) and rapamycin (S1039) were obtained from Selleckchem (Houston, TX, USA). A JNK inhibitor (SP600125) was obtained from Calbiochem.

Antibodies

The following antibodies were used for western blotting: PSF (sc-374502, 1:1000), calgranulin A (sc-48352, 1:1000), specific to human filaggrin (sc-66192, 1:1000), specific to mouse filaggrin (sc-30230, 1:1000), and ERK2 (sc-1647, 1:1000) were obtained from Santa Cruz Biotechnology; p-JNK (9251, 1:1000), JNK (9252, 1:1000), p-mTOR (2971, 1:1000), mTOR (2972, 1:1000), K63 (5621, 1:1000), CRM1 (46249, 1:1000), p-c-Jun (3270, 1:1000), c-Jun (9165, 1:1000), p-p70S6K (9234, 1:1000), p70S6K (34475, 1:1000), anti-mouse IgG-HRP (7076 S, 1:3000), and anti-rabbit IgG-HRP (7074 S, 1:3000) were obtained from Cell Signaling Technology; S100A8 (PA5-47439, 1:1000) was obtained from Invitrogen. The following antibodies were used for immunocytochemistry: calgranulin A (sc-48352, 1:200), specific to human filaggrin (sc-66192, 1:200), specific to mouse filaggrin (sc-30230, 1:200), PSF (sc-374502, 1:200), SQSTM1/p62 (sc-48402, 1:200), and ubiquitin (sc-166553, 1:200) were purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology; SFPQ (ab38148, 1:300) was obtained from Abcam; CRM1/Exportin-1 (46249, 1:200) was purchased from Cell Signaling Technology; JNK1/2 (BS-2900R, 1:200) was purchased from Bioss; S100A8 (SAB2502065, 1:200) and ubiquitin K63 (05-1308, 1:200) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich; S100A8 (PA5-47439, 1:200), p-JNK1/2 (700031, 1:200), filaggrin (PA5-115235, 1:200), ubiquitin (PA1-10023, 1:200), filaggrin (MA5-13440, 1:200), and Ub-K63 (14-6077-82, 1:200) were purchased from Invitrogen. Secondary antibodies Alexa Fluor plus 488 donkey anti-rabbit IgG (A32790, 1:1000), Alexa Fluor plus 488 donkey anti-mouse IgG (A32766, 1:1000), Alexa Fluor plus 488 donkey anti-goat IgG (A32714, 1:1000), Alexa Fluor 568 donkey anti-rabbit IgG (A10042, 1:1000), Alexa Fluor 568 donkey anti-mouse IgG (A10037, 1:1000), Alexa Fluor 568 donkey anti-goat IgG (A11057, 1:1000), Alexa Fluor plus 647 donkey anti-rabbit IgG (A32795, 1:1000), Alexa Fluor plus 647 donkey anti-mouse IgG (A32787, 1:1000), and Alexa Fluor plus 647 donkey anti-goat IgG (A21447, 1:1000) were purchased from Invitrogen. The following antibodies were used for immunohistochemical staining: SQSTM1/p62 (sc-48402, 1:200), and calgranulin A (sc-48352, 1:200) were purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology; SFPQ (ab38148, 1:200) was purchased from Abcam; S100A8 (PA5-79948, 1:200) was purchased from Invitrogen; filaggrin (GTX37695, 1:200) was purchased from GeneTex; Exportin-1/CRM1 (46249, 1:200) was purchased from Cell Signaling Technology.

Cell culture and isolation of primary keratinocytes

The human keratinocyte cell line (HaCaT) was cultured in DMEM high glucose (12100-046, Gibco) supplemented with 10% inactivated FBS (S001-07, Welgene) and penicillin-streptomycin (10378016, Gibco). The cells were passaged by harvesting with trypsin-EDTA solution (15400054, Gibco) when they reached 80–90% confluence. Skin biopsies were performed in Nowon Eulji Medical Center (Seoul, Korea) after IRB approval by Eulji University IRB committee on Feb 11 2022 with approval number of EMCS 2021-12-022. All skin samples were washed with DPBS (14190144, Gibco) without calcium and magnesium containing 20 µg/mL gentamicin (15750, Gibco). The tissues were submerged in 2.4 U/mL Dispase II (4942078001, Roche) and incubated for overnight at 4℃. Epidermal layer were separated from the dermis and placed into 1X TrypLE (12563, Gibco) and incubated at 37℃ for 15 min. After centrifugation at 180 x g for 5 min, isolated primary keratinocytes were resuspended and incubated with Keratinocyte-SFM media (17005-042, Gibco). All cells were maintained at 37℃ and 5% CO2.

Animal experiments

All 8-week-old female C57BL/6J mice were cared in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility. All animal experiments were produced in accordance with the protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and User Committee of Eulji University (EUIACU22-19). The mice were allowed to adapt for 1 week before experimental procedures. To induce AD-like skin lesions, the mice backs were shaved with an electric razor. DNCB (0.1, 1, and 2%) was added to a 3 × 3 cm patch of sterile gauze, which were applied to the skins of mice once daily for a week. Leptomycin B (200 µg/kg) (S7580, Selleckchem), rapamycin (4 mg/kg) (S1039, Selleckchem) and dexamethasone (10 mg/kg) (D1756, Sigma) were dissolved in ethanol and were diluted in filter-sterilized PBS. Each inhibitor was intraperitoneally injected once per day for 1 week after administration of DNCB.

Confocal microscopy

For immunofluorescence experiments, human keratinocytes were grown on confocal dish (101350, SPL). Fixation and permeabilization steps were performed using with Intracellular Fixation & permeabilization buffer set (88-8824-00, Thermo scientific). Immunostainings were proceeded upon dilution of primary antibodies and overnight incubation at 4℃. The cells were washed three times in PBS with 0.2% Tween 20 and incubated for 1 h with diluted secondary antibodies at room temperature in a dark room. After additional three washes, the cells were counterstained with DAPI for 10 min at room temperature and analyzed using confocal microscopy (LSM 700, Carl Zeiss) with a Plan-Apochromat x63/1.4NA M27 oil immersion oil at room temperature. The microscopes were operated on the ZEN 2 software platform (Carl Zeiss).

Histological analysis

The skin tissues from mice, normal and AD patients were fixed with 10% formalin, embedded in paraffin, and cut into 3 μm sections. The sections were stained with hematoxylin-eosin solution (Sigma). For immunohistochemical staining for SFPQ, S100A8, filaggrin, and p62, the sections were deparaffinized by xylene and antigen retrieval step was proceeded using proteinase K and 0.3% H2O2. After blocking step, the sections were incubated overnight at 4℃ with the primary antibodies and thereafter incubated for 90 min at room temperature with secondary antibodies. The specimens were counterstained with hematoxylin and analyzed with light microscopy for histological evaluation (Leica Microsystems).

Cell lysis and western blotting

Harvested cells were lysed in CETi lysis buffer (TLP-121CETi, Trans Lab). The supernatant was isolated as total lysate. Sample concentration was measured by a protein assay kit (23227, Thermo scientific). Protein samples (40 µg/lane) were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to nitrocellulose membrane. Prepared membrane was incubated with primary antibodies (1:1000) for 1 h and secondary antibodies (1:3000) for 30 min at room temperature. After washing three times, the membrane was developed using the West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate (34580, Thermo scientific) and detected using iBright CL750 Imaging System (A44116, Thermo scientific). The same blot was stripped and reprobed with internal control antibodies such as anti-ERK2 antibodies.

Treatment with inhibitors and siRNA transfection

Human keratinocytes were stimulated with TNF-α (20 ng/mL) and IFN-γ (20 ng/mL). The cells were pre-incubated for 1 h with JNK inhibitor SP600125 (20 µM), leptomycin B (5 nM) or rapamycin (25 nM) before phosphorylation time (30 min, 9 h) after treatment with TNF-α and IFN-γ. Chloroquine (10 µM) were incubated for 1 h prior to treatment with TNF-α and IFN-γ. For siRNA-based experiments, HaCaT cells were transfected using DharmaFECT transfection reagent (T-2001-02) with the On-Target plus human S100A8 (L-011770-00-0005), SFPQ (L-006455-00-0010) and non-targeting siRNA pool (D-0018-10-05) from Dharmacon. Also, the cells were transfected with siRNA against CRM1 (sc-35116) or mTOR (sc-35409) from Santa Cruz Biotechnology and were analyzed for expressions of target proteins after 72 h.

Kinase assay

To detect the capability of S100A8, SFPQ, and SFPQ/S100A8 complex on JNK phosphorylation, these proteins were respectively added at a concentration of 1 µg in each tube, in which JNK were included at concentrations of 10, 20, 50, and 100 ng. The phosphorylation of JNK due to the respective recombinant protein was evaluated using the JNK1 kinase assay kit (V4071, Promega) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Production of recombinant proteins

The resulting PCR fragments of partial SFPQ fragments (214–598) were cloned into the pETDuet-1 vector. All constructs were confirmed by DNA sequencing (Bioneer, Daejeon, South Korea). Each clone vector was transformed into Rosetta2 (DE3) (Novagen) and grown in supplemented LB media (35 µg/mL chloramphenicol, 100 µg/mL ampicillin, and 0.5% glucose). pET carrying the cDNA encoding the JNK fused to His-tag were obtained from Vector Builder, and transformed into BL21 (DE3, Merck Millipore) for protein expression. The cells were grown in supplemented LB growth medium (100 µg/mL ampicillin). The cells were incubated after induction with 0.5 mM IPTG. His-bind purification kit (Merck Millipore) was used for protein purification. Protein concentration was determined by the BCA assay. Purified proteins were stored as 1 mg/mL at -70 °C. The cDNA fragments encoding S100A8 was obtained using standard molecular cloning techniques and recombinant S100A8 protein was produced according to our previous paper [35].

Surface plasmon resonance

SFPQ protein was immobilized on a chip and a surface plasmon resonance device (iMSPR-ProX, iCLUBIO) was used to evaluate the interaction of proteins. After binding S100A8 to SFPQ, filaggrin or JNK proteins were injected in increasing concentrations. The procedure was performed in a sole cycle sequence excluding the regeneration step. Kd, the dissociation constant, was computed using the instrument`s inbuilt software. The calculation was done using the formula RC= (C*Rmax)/(Kd + C), here Rc represents the binding level at the specified concentration and Rmax denotes the projected highest binding capacity.

Pull down assay

Nickle affinity pull-down assay was executed rendering to the manufacturer`s instruction using Pierce™ His protein interaction pull-down kit (Thermofisher Scientific). Cobalt chelate agarose resin was incubated with Buffer A (25 mM Tris-HCl, 0.15 M NaCl, 10 mM imidazole; pH 7.2) alone or with His-tagged protein samples (SFPQ or S100A8) for 1 h with gentle mixing. After centrifugation, the resin was washed five times with a wash-buffer. One mg of HaCaT cell lysate in buffer A was added to all of the columns and allowed to settle down for at least 2 h. The samples were centrifuged and washed extensively to remove unbound or loosely bounded proteins. All the steps were performed at 4℃ to ensure protein integrity. Finally, elution buffer was added to each column. The collected elution was loaded on an SDS-PAGE gel. S100A8 and SFPQ were detected using the western blotting.

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as the means ± SD. Statistical intergroup differences were analyzed using a paired t-test for a two-group comparison and one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test for more than two groups. The SPSS statistical software package (IBM) was used for statistical evaluation. A p value < 0.05 is considered statistically significant.

Results

SFPQ/S100A8 complex formed by SFPQ relocation and increased S100A8 expression interacts with filaggrin and JNK in AD patients

S100A8 levels are increased in allergic conditions, including AD. It is overexpressed in house dust mite-induced AD-like mice and human AD patients [20, 36]. S100A8 accelerates inflammatory responses via the formation of the complexes, such as S100A8/S100A9, in the cytoplasm [22]. Here, we aimed to identify the critical molecule binding to S100A8 in the cytoplasm as well as the complex triggering the progression and aggravation of AD. SFPQ was identified as a novel protein interacting with S100A8 using MALDI/TOF (Fig. S1A). His-S100A8 bound to SFPQ in the nucleus of control cells and TNF-α and IFN-γ-treated cells, and interacted with SFPQ in the cytoplasm of the cells treated with TNF-α and IFN-γ. As shown in Fig. S1B, His-S100A8 bound to SFPQ of the whole lysates after treatment with or without TNF-α and IFN-γ, whereas His-SFPQ interacted with S100A8 of the whole lysates after stimulation with TNF-α and IFN-γ, whereas His-SFPQ interacted with S100A8 in whole-cell lysates following stimulation with TNF-α and IFN-γ, as demonstrated by co-immunoprecipitation analysis. To further investigate the interaction between SFPQ and S100A8, SPR was performed using recombinant SFPQ and S100A8 proteins. SFPQ exhibited a binding affinity for S100A8 filaggrin (KD = 25.2 nM) (Fig. S1C). These results indicate that S100A8 is absent in the control but overexpressed in the cells treated with TNF-α and IFN-γ, and some SFPQ proteins move from nucleus to cytoplasm after stimulation with TNF-α and IFN-γ. Persistent production of abnormal complexes via dislocation of SFPQ is a key process in the pathogenesis of Alzheimer disease [28]. First, we performed immunohistochemistry of the skin tissues of AD patients to assess the protein expression alteration. The infiltration of inflammatory cells into the skin and epidermis thickness increased depending on the severity of AD (Fig. 1A, B). The overall expression of SFPQ in AD patients increased as the epidermis of AD patients was thicker than that of healthy subjects. The level of S100A8 also was increased, whereas filaggrin were decreased in the epidermis of AD patients. Levels of total IgE and cytokines, including IL-4, IL-13, IL-1β, and TNF-α, were increased in mild and severe AD patients (Fig. 1C, D) and the increase was positively correlated with the eczema area and severity index (EASI) score (Fig. S1D). Next, we investigated whether SFPQ interacted with S100A8 in the skin of AD patients. Immunocytochemical staining revealed SFPQ translocation to the cytoplasm, elevated S100A8 expression in the cytoplasm, and SFPQ/S100A8 complex formation in the keratinocytes of AD patients, but not in those of healthy subjects (Fig. 1E). Severe AD patients exhibited higher complex formation than mild AD patients. These results indicate the strong association between SFPQ movement and SFPQ/S100A8 complex formation in AD progression. Next, we explored the association between complex formation and filaggrin deficiency, a major manifestation of AD. Direct interaction between the SFPQ/S100A8 complex and filaggrin was observed, and SFPQ/S100A8/Filaggrin complex proportionally increased depending on the severity of AD (Fig. 1F). Filaggrin levels decreased, whereas SFPQ/S100A8/Filaggrin complex formation increased in AD patients. To validate our hypothesis that the complex affects filaggrin deficiency, and autophagy is the most predominant degradation pathway, we investigated whether the SFPQ/S100A8 complex induces filaggrin degradation via the autophagy pathway. SFPQ/S100A8/Filaggrin complex was associated with p62 in the cytoplasm (Fig. 1G). We previously reported that filaggrin expression is downregulated by JNK activation in AD [37]. Here, we further examined whether the SFPQ/S100A8 complex reduces filaggrin levels via JNK activation. Compared to the healthy controls, JNK phosphorylation increased without alteration of its expression in mild and severe AD patients (Fig. 1H). Although not conclusive, the findings suggest the possibility that the SFPQ/S100A8 complex influences JNK activity.

SFPQ/S100A8 complex induces filaggrin degradation via ubiquitin-dependent selective autophagy

To verify the role of the SFPQ/S100A8 complex in filaggrin degradation, we transfected HaCaT cells with siRNAs against SFPQ and S100A8. SFPQ and S100A8 siRNA transfection did not affect the basal expression of filaggrin but suppressed filaggrin reduction induced by TNF-α and IFN-γ (Fig. 2A and Fig. S2A). We further analyzed SFPQ/S100A8 complex formation in HaCaT cells treated with TNF-α and IFN-γ. S100A8 expression and SFPQ translocation from the nucleus to the cytoplasm increased at 9 h and peaked at 48 h, without any alteration in total SFPQ expression (Fig. S2B, C). Thus, the formation of the SFPQ/S100A8 complex began at 9 h and peaked at 48 h. To assess the binding of filaggrin with the SFPQ/S100A8 complex, HaCaT cells were treated with TNF-α and IFN-γ in a time-dependent manner. The formation of SFPQ/S100A8/Filaggrin complex was peaked at 48 h (Fig. 2B and Fig. S2D). To confirm the interaction of filaggrin with the SFPQ/S100A8 complex, surface plasmon resonance was performed using recombinant SFPQ, S100A8, and filaggrin proteins. SFPQ/S100A8 complex exhibited binding affinity for filaggrin (KD = 175 nM) (Fig. S2E).

As the SFPQ/S100A8/Filaggrin complex binds with p62 in the keratinocytes of AD patients (Fig. 1G) and filaggrin degradation by the complex can be associated with selective autophagy, we investigated whether this complex interacted with p62 and ubiquitin. Indeed, SFPQ/S100A8/Filaggrin complex interacted with both p62 and ubiquitin (Fig. 2C). K63-linked ubiquitination was associated with both p62 and the SFPQ/S100A8/Filaggrin complex (Fig. 2D). These results indicate that the filaggrin degradation due to SFPQ/S100A8 is closely involved in ubiquitin-dependent aggrephagy. As shown in Fig. 2E, chloroquine, an inhibitor of autophagy, blocked filaggrin reduction induced by TNF-α and IFN-γ. mTOR signaling pathway suppresses autophagy, and mTORC1/2 is involved in epidermis formation and keratinocyte differentiation [38]. However, no direct association between the mTOR signaling pathway and filaggrin degradation has been reported in AD pathogenesis. Therefore, we analyzed the effect of rapamycin on filaggrin reduction. Notably, rapamycin restored the filaggrin expression decreased by TNF-α and IFN-γ to a basal level (Fig. 2E). Next, we assessed the association between activation of the mTOR signaling pathway and SFPQ/S100A8 complex. TNF-α and IFN-γ induced the phosphorylation of mTOR and p70S6K in a time-dependent manner, and rapamycin inhibited the activation of both proteins (Fig. 2F, G). Interestingly, rapamycin inhibited the cytoplasmic movement of SFPQ and increase of p62 and K63 levels (Fig. 2H, I). Additionally, rapamycin partially suppressed S100A8 expression (Fig. 2J). Taken together, our results suggest the mTOR-mediated pathway as a key pathway in the formation of the SFPQ/S100A8 complex, which triggers filaggrin degradation.

SFPQ/S100A8 complex downregulates filaggrin expression via JNK activation

To assess the interactions of the SFPQ/S100A8 complex with JNK and p-JNK (Fig. 1I), we performed time-dependent western blotting analysis of HaCaT cells. After treatment with TNF-α and IFN-γ, JNK phosphorylation was induced at 15 min and peaked at 30 min (Fig. 3A). However, p-JNK was observed at 9 h, after complex formation (Fig. S2B), and JNK activation was maintained until 48 h (Fig. 3A). Activated JNK induced the phosphorylation of c-Jun, a downstream molecule. To confirm JNK activation, the cells were pretreated with SP600125, a JNK inhibitor, 30 min before stimulation with TNF-α and IFN-γ. SP600125 suppressed JNK activation at 30 min and 9 h (Fig. 3B). The cells were further treated with SP600125 8.5 h after stimulation with TNF-α and IFN-γ, and JNK phosphorylation was blocked at 9 h (Fig. 3C). When the cells were pre-stimulated with SP600125 30 min before treatment with TNF-α and IFN-γ and then treated with it 8.5 h after stimulation with TNF-α and IFN-γ, filaggrin downregulation was blocked at 48 h (Fig. 3D). Notably, siRNAs against SFPQ and S100A8 had no effect on the basal expression and activation of JNK (Fig. S3A). Target siRNAs reduced the phosphorylation of JNK at 9 h, without altering the basal JNK expression and they have no effect on JNK activation at 30 min (Fig. 3E and Fig. S3B). The SFPQ/S100A8 complex interacted with p-JNK and JNK from 9 to 48 h after treatment with TNF-α and IFN-γ but to a slightly different extent (Fig. 3F). To confirm the direct interaction between the SFPQ/S100A8 complex and JNK, we conducted surface plasmon resonance analysis, which showed the binding affinity of the SFPQ/S100A8 complex for JNK (KD = 132 nM) (Fig. S3C). The direct involvement of SFPQ/S100A8 in JNK activation was validated through a kinase assay (Fig. 3G). Neither SFPQ nor S100A8 induced the phosphorylation of JNK. As rapamycin inhibits SFPQ/S100A8 complex formation (Fig. 2H), we investigated whether mTOR is related to JNK activation. Rapamycin inhibited the phosphorylation of JNK at 9 h but not at 30 min (Fig. 3H and Fig. S3D). Therefore, TNF-α and IFN-γ induced the activation of mTOR, which subsequently phosphorylated JNK through the SFPQ/S100A8 complex. Therefore, SFPQ/S100A8 complex mediated by mTOR plays a crucial role in filaggrin reduction by inducing the degradation and downregulation of filaggrin via selective autophagy and JNK activation, respectively.

mTOR-mediated CRM1 activation is essential for SFPQ relocation to cytoplasm

As rapamycin blocked SFPQ/S100A8 complex formation (Fig. 2H) and cytoplasmic movement of SFPQ is an important step in this complex formation, we selected CRM1 as a key transporter for SFPQ activated by mTOR. CRM1 is a crucial protein for nucleocytoplasmic transport, and abnormal CRM1 movement leads to solid and hematologic tumor formation [39]. However, research on the translocation mechanisms of specific proteins in AD is scarce. As shown in Fig. 4A, cytoplasmic movement of SFPQ and CRM1 was increased and both proteins were colocalized in HaCaT cells stimulated with TNF-α and IFN-γ. This suggests that CRM1 plays a role in facilitating SFPQ movement and SFPQ/S100A8 complex formation, without alteration in CRM1 expression levels (Fig. 4B). These results were also observed in the keratinocytes of AD patients (Fig. S4). As mTOR inhibition suppresses SFPQ relocation and SFPQ/S100A8 formation, mTOR may be an upstream regulatory molecule of CRM1 (Fig. 2H). To evaluate the relationship of CRM1 with the mTOR-mediated pathway, we analyzed the effects of leptomycin B and rapamycin on CRM1. Leptomycin B and rapamycin inhibited the cytoplasmic movement of CRM1 and SFPQ, consequently suppressing SFPQ/S100A8 complex formation (Fig. 4C). The SFPQ/S100A8 complex formation was suppressed by siRNAs against CRM1 and mTOR (Fig. 4D). Leptomycin B blocked filaggrin reduction by inhibiting the major functions of the SFPQ/S100A8 complex, selective autophagy and JNK activation (Fig. 4E-G). To clarify the region involved in the interaction of SFPQ with CRM1, we predicted NOPS as an nuclear export signal (NES) candidate region using computational tools, such as NESdb, and constructed partially deleted recombinant proteins of SFPQ (Fig. S5A, B). In pull-down assays using recombinant proteins, recombinant SFPQ proteins with deletion of NOPS region did not bind to CRM1 (Fig. S5C). Additionally, S100A8 interacted with the recombinant SFPQ proteins having RRM1 and NOPS (Fig. S5D). These results imply that the NOPS region is involved in cytoplasmic translocation through CRM1 and interaction with S100A8 for complex formation. Taken together, SFPQ cytoplasmic movement is mediated by CRM1 regulated by the mTOR signaling pathway, and mTOR and CRM1 play key roles in SFPQ/S100A8 complex formation and AD pathogenesis.

SFPQ/S100A8 complex formation increases depending on the disease severity in DNCB- and DP-induced AD-like mice

Our results suggested a proportional relationship between SFPQ/S100A8 complex formation and severity of AD in human keratinocytes (Fig. 1E, J). Accordingly, we validated the association of SFPQ/S100A8 complex with AD severity in AD-like mouse model induced by DNCB and DP in a concentration-dependent manner. In histological analysis, higher concentration of DNCB resulted in a higher skin severity score and epidermis thickness in the mice (Fig. 5A, B). Skin severity score is positively correlated to epidermis thickness (Fig. S6A). DNCB treatment reduced filaggrin expression in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 5C, D). SFPQ levels in the DNCB-treated groups were similar to those in the control group. S100A8 levels increased depending on the concentration of DNCB. Total IgE and Th1 and Th2 cytokine levels in mice sera increased with DNCB levels in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 5E, F). To clarify the difference in SFPQ/S100A8 complex formation according to DNCB concentrations, we isolated keratinocytes from the skin tissues of mice. SFPQ/S100A8 complex formation proportionally increased with DNCB concentration in the cells (Fig. 5G). Increase in SFPQ/S100A8 complex formation significantly correlated with the skin severity score and inflammatory marker levels in AD (Fig. S6B). As shown in Fig. 5H, CRM1 was localized with SFPQ in the cytoplasm, and the colocalization was increased depending on the DNCB concentration, indicating that CRM1 induces the translocation of SFPQ for SFPQ/S100A8 complex formation. mTOR and p70S6K are the upstream molecules of CRM1 activation, which was suppressed by rapamycin and siRNA against mTOR (Fig. 4C, D). Next, we determined whether mTOR and p70S6K are phosphorylated in the skin of mice. The phosphorylation of mTOR and p70S6K was observed in the lysates of skin tissues using western blotting (Fig. S6C). Immunocytochemistry exhibited the SFPQ/S100A8 complexes bound to p-JNK, which proportionally increased with AD severity (Fig. 5I and Fig. S6D). As the direct binding of SFPQ/S100A8 to filaggrin increased, the expression of filaggrin decreased with increased severity (Fig. 5C, J). The SFPQ/S100A8/Filaggrin complex elevated after treatment with TNF-α and IFN-γ in a time-dependent manner compared to the untreated cells (Fig. S6E). Subsequently, to clarify selective autophagy-mediated degradation of filaggrin due to SFPQ/S100A8, we confirmed the binding of the complex to p62 and K63 (Fig. 5K and Fig. S6F). In AD-like mice induced by DP as an external allergen, it induced AD-like manifestations including the increase of skin severity score and epidermis thickness, the infiltration of inflammatory cells, and filaggrin deficiency in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. S7A-D). The increase of total IgE and inflammatory cytokines was accompanied by dose-dependent DP administration (Fig. S7E, F). As shown supplementary Fig. 7G, CRM1 was localized with SFPQ in the cytoplasm, and the colocalization elevated depending on administration of increased DP concentration. The SFPQ/S100A8 complex also increased and directly interacted with filaggrin, p62, K63, and p-JNK in concert with filaggrin reduction (Fig. S7H-J). These results further solidify that the formation of the SFPQ/S100A8 complex is involved in the development of AD.

Blockade of SFPQ translocation attenuates filaggrin deficiency and inflammation in AD-like mice

To determine whether SFPQ/S100A8 complex formation by SFPQ movement plays a crucial role in AD pathogenesis, we explored the inhibitory effects of rapamycin and leptomycin B on AD-like manifestations via immunohistochemical staining. The skin severity score and epidermis thickness were increased by DNCB administration and decreased by approximately 50–70% after leptomycin B and rapamycin treatment (Fig. 6A, B). Moreover, SFPQ levels were not altered in the experimental groups (Fig. 6C, D). DNCB administration enhanced S100A8 levels, which were reduced in the inhibitor-treated groups. Leptomycin B and rapamycin recovered filaggrin expression decreased by DNCB treatment. Total IgE and Th1 and Th2 cytokine levels in the sera were increased by DNCB, but this effect was reversed by leptomycin B and rapamycin (Fig. 6E, F). Dexamethasone was used as a positive suppressor of AD. To examine the relationship of inhibitory effects of leptomycin B and rapamycin with SFPQ/S100A8 complex formation, we isolated keratinocytes from the skin tissues of AD patients. SFPQ/S100A8 complex formation due to CRM1 activation was observed in the DNCB-treated group, but not in the inhibitor-treated group (Fig. 6G, H). Colocalization of p-JNK with the complex was observed in DNCB-treated group, but leptomycin B and rapamycin suppressed JNK activation (Fig. 6I). Next, we analyzed filaggrin degradation by the SFPQ/S100A8 complex. The complex interacted with filaggrin in the DNCB-treated group, and its formation was inhibited by leptomycin B and rapamycin (Fig. 6J). Binding of p62 and K63 with the complex was increased in the DNCB-treated group but diminished in the inhibitor-treated group (Fig. 6K).

Leptomycin B and rapamycin block filaggrin reduction by inhibiting SFPQ/S100A8 complex formation in the non-lesional and lesional keratinocytes of AD patients

As filaggrin downregulation and degradation by the SFPQ/S100A8 complex were suppressed by leptomycin B and rapamycin in HaCaT cells and AD-like mice (Figs. 4 and 6), we further analyzed the suppressive effects of both inhibitors using keratinocytes from the non-lesional and lesional skin of AD patients. TNF-α and IFN-γ increased the cytoplasmic movement of CRM1 and SFPQ, and SFPQ/S100A8 formation (Fig. 7A, B). SFPQ/S100A8 complex formation stimulated by TNF-α and IFN-γ in the presence of both inhibitors was accompanied by a decrease in CRM1 and SFPQ relocation compared to that in control cells. SFPQ/S100A8 complex formation was higher in lesional keratinocytes than in non-lesional keratinocytes. Pre-treatment with leptomycin B and rapamycin suppressed the interactions of the complex with filaggrin, K63, and p-JNK eventually inhibiting JNK phosphorylation and filaggrin reduction stimulated by TNF-α and IFN-γ (Fig. 7C-E). Furthermore, cytokine production was significantly decreased in the cells pre-treated with leptomycin B and rapamycin (Fig. 7F). Lesional keratinocytes exhibited higher secretion of cytokines than the non-lesional keratinocytes, but no significant differences were observed between the non-lesional and lesional keratinocytes. In conclusion, mTOR induces SFPQ movement via CRM1. Translocated SFPQ in the cytoplasm forms a complex with S100A8, and this complex plays an overarching role in AD by inducing filaggrin downregulation through JNK activation and filaggrin degradation via selective autophagy. These processes contribute to skin barrier defects in AD (Fig. 7G).

Discussion

AD is characterized by the disruption of epidermal barrier function. Filaggrin dysregulation in the epidermal barrier is a key process in AD pathogenesis [2, 40]. Loss of function (LOF) mutations in FLG are strong risk factors for AD [41, 42]. Immunological alterations by Th2 cytokines induce a vicious cycle in AD. Although these reports provide insights on the pathogenesis of AD, they have clear limitations. Therefore, we investigated the cause of filaggrin deficiency in AD in this study. Here, we found that the SFPQ/S100A8 complex was the overarching factor for filaggrin deficiency; it downregulated and degraded filaggrin via JNK activation and selective autophagy. SFPQ/S100A8 complex is also a critical hallmark as it exacerbates the manifestations of AD by severely reducing filaggrin expression.

Two essential steps are involved in the formation of the SFPQ/S100A8 complex: relocation of SFPQ and increase of S100A8 expression in the cytoplasm. Nucleocytoplasmic shuttling proteins are closely associated with cancer and neurodegenerative diseases. Aberrant cytoplasmic translocation of survivin and nucleophosmin promotes tumorigenesis [43, 44]. These proteins undergo mutation and hyperexpression in the target tissue, and their cytoplasmic transport mainly relies on CRM1. Mutation and overexpression of CRM1 alter the nucleocytoplasmic distribution of many proteins, resulting in oncogenesis [45, 46]. However, verifying their roles as pathogenic factors in AD remains difficult.

S100A8 levels are elevated in allergic conditions, including AD, where it primarily functions as a damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) [47]. Once secreted from cells, S100A8 binds to receptors such as TLR4, RAGE (receptor for advanced glycation end-products), or EMMPRIN, initiating inflammatory responses, including the release of inflammatory cytokines as external signals [48, 49]. In this study, we demonstrated that S100A8 plays a central intracellular role in AD by interacting with SFPQ in the cytoplasm (Figs. 1E, 5G and 6G and Fig. S2B). The interaction of SFPQ with S100A8 functions as a signal complex inducing filaggrin degradation and downregulation, in contrast to protein–protein aggregation in neurodegenerative diseases [50]. The SFPQ–CRM1 axis is critical for SFPQ/S100A8 complex formation in the cytoplasm. AD is a complex disease characterized by different subtypes (extrinsic and intrinsic subtypes) and endotypes with different molecular mechanisms [51]. Further investigations are needed to clarify the relationship of the subtypes and endotypes of AD with SFPQ/S100A8 complex formation.

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to demonstrate the significance of mTOR-mediated CRM1 activation in AD (Figs. 2E-G, 6 and 7). TFEB nuclear export is mediated by CRM1 and dependent on mTOR activation, which induces the phosphorylation of sites proximate to NES of TFEB [52]. Although the target nuclear protein is different from SFPQ, this report is a fundamental evidence of the association between mTOR and CRM1. Additionally, our study revealed the role of mTOR in the pathogenesis of AD. Increased expression of RAPTOR, a critical mTORC1 protein, is correlated with decreased filaggrin expression via damaged filaggrin processing in AD patients. The detrimental effect of overexpressed RAPTOR is mediated by blocking the activation of AKT1 and cathepsin H [53]. Although this study revealed the association between mTOR and filaggrin deficiency, the precise mechanism of mTOR activation and its association with AD severity remain unknown. mTOR is a representative modulator of autophagy that acts as a double-edged sword, exerting both cytoprotective and harmful effects. In this study, we evaluated the effect of the SFPQ/S100A8 complex formed via mTOR-mediated CRM1 activation on filaggrin deficiency. Although the regulatory mechanism of filaggrin expression involves various pathways, including cytokine regulation [53, 54], rapamycin-mediated upregulation of filaggrin expression suggests the role of mTOR-linked complex in filaggrin degradation (Figs. 2E and 6C and D). mTOR inhibits autophagy through the phosphorylation of ULK1, and under certain conditions, it dynamically alters the composition of the Atg1/ULK complex to induce selective autophagy [55]. Deletion of JunB/AP-1 upregulates p62 levels in AD patients and AD-like mice. However, knockout of p62 has no effect on filaggrin expression in mice [56]. These reports do not provide clear evidence of the relationship between deficiency and degradation of filaggrin. Here, we found that the complex directly binds to filaggrin and is subsequently degraded via selective autophagy involving p62 (Fig. 2H). This result suggests that SFPQ/S100A8/Filaggrin triggers the selective autophagy of filaggrin by recruiting ubiquitin, ULK1, and p62, subsequently degrading filaggrin; however, this warrants further investigation.

We also focused on filaggrin deficiency, which is exacerbated by the SFPQ/S100A8 complex through the downregulation of filaggrin expression mediated by JNK activation. (Fig. 3). JNK activation due to TNF-α and IFN-γ causes filaggrin downregulation [6]. AP1 binding to FLG promoter is altered from c-Jun/c-fos to c-Jun/Fra-1 after treatment with TNF-α and IFN-γ depending on the stage [57]. c-Jun/Fra-1 interacts with histone deacetylase and blocks filaggrin expression. Late activation of JNK (9 h) was abrogated by siRNAs against SFPQ and S100A8 (Fig. 3E). Despite differences between our results and those of previous studies, all findings are consistent regarding the importance of JNK activation in AD. JNK activation is closely related to the severity of AD in AD-like mice and AD patients (Figs. 1D and F and 5I and Fig. S6D).

Here, the effects of inhibitors (leptomycin B and rapamycin) to assess their potential as therapeutic agents for AD. Administration of leptomycin B and rapamycin significantly alleviated the manifestations of AD, including skin severity score, epidermis thickness, filaggrin deficiency, and inflammation, in DNCB-induced AD mice (Fig. 6A-F). SFPQ/S100A8 complex formation decreased as the relocation of SFPQ was blocked by leptomycin B and rapamycin (Fig. 6G). Leptomycin B, an inhibitor of CRM1, has been extensively studied as a cancer therapeutic drug [58, 59]. Rapamycin, an mTOR inhibitor, prevents immune rejection by suppressing immune hyperreaction [60].

This study has three major findings. First, SFPQ translocates to the cytoplasm and forms a complex with S100A8. This complex formation proportionally increases with AD severity. Second, SFPQ movement is mediated by CRM1, which is regulated by the mTOR signaling pathway. Third, SFPQ/S100A8 complex downregulates filaggrin via JNK activation and selective autophagy. These findings suggest the SFPQ/S100A8 complex formation as a common step in various allergic conditions, including AD, thus providing a new strategy for their treatment [61, 62].
