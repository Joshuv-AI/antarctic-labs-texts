Preparation of ketonyl C-glycosides from designed glycosyl sulfides and styrenes by a radical pathway
Authors: Weidong Shang, Yanan Hu, Yang He, Dawen Niu, Weimin Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Ketonyl C-glycosides, a vital subclass of alkyl C-glycosides, play essential roles in drug discovery, biochemistry, and materials sciences. However, a practical strategy that merges bench-stable glycosyl donors with styrenes—a ubiquitous class of synthetic building blocks—remains elusive. Herein, we report a simple and general approach for synthesizing ketonyl C-glycosides. The transformation uses designed glycosyl sulfides as air- and moisture-stable precursors to glycosyl radicals, styrenes as aglycone sources, and dimethyl sulfoxide as both an oxidant and solvent, proceeding under photoredox catalysis. Glycosyl radicals are generated from glycosyl sulfides via efficient unimolecular homolytic substitution. This approach obviates harsh conditions for donor activation and the use of activated aglycone precursors, accommodating extensive functional groups and heterocycles. The reaction also exhibits high stereoselectivity across various glycosyl units. Demonstrating synthetic versatility, this method enables efficient access to diverse drug-sugar conjugates and complex C-glycopeptidomimetics. Mechanistic studies reveal key intermediates, suggesting a radical-polar crossover pathway. This study offers a broadly applicable approach to valuable sugar-containing structures.

INTRODUCTION

Alkyl C-glycosides represent a fundamental class of carbohydrate derivatives, in which an alkyl unit (aglycone) is connected to the anomeric carbon of a sugar molecule via a C(sp3)─C(sp3) bond (see Fig. 1A for selected examples) (1). These compounds are ubiquitous and exhibit diverse biological activities, including antimalarial, antibacterial, and antitumor effects (2–7). In drug discovery, replacing the hydrolyzable O-glycosidic bond in O-glycosides with a robust C(sp3)-glycosidic bond to prepare alkyl C-glycoside mimetics has proven effective for improving pharmacological properties and bioavailability (8–10). Notably, this strategy has been shown to improve the activity of certain mimetics beyond that of their parent compounds (11–14). Studies in biochemistry have harnessed these compounds as affinity labels to identify acid/base catalytic residues in glycosidases (15). In addition to their biological applications, certain artificially synthesized ketonyl C-glycosides have been used in the development of semiconducting materials (16). Owing to their tremendous utility in diverse fields, the development of rapid and robust methods to prepare libraries of alkyl C-glycosides is highly sought after.

Ketonyl C-glycosides (Fig. 1A), an essential subfamily of alkyl C-glycosides, have gained particular attention because of their dual roles as components of pharmaceutical candidates (17) and their carbonyl groups, which enable straightforward modification into diverse glycoconjugate surrogates (18–20). Classical routes to ketonyl C-glycosides rely on the nucleophilic attack of carbon nucleophiles toward glycosyl electrophiles (Fig. 1B) (21). These methods include Knoevenagel condensation (22–25) and the Horner-Wadsworth-Emmons reaction (Fig. 1B, top) (26, 27), where enolates react with the open-chain forms of cyclic hemiacetals as intermediates. While effective, these methods are restricted to substrates with specific structural features, predominantly yielding β-configured products. Moreover, direct alkylation of glycosyl oxocarbenium ions or their synthetic equivalents with carbon nucleophiles (21), such as silyl enol ethers, enolates, and enamines, has also been well established (Fig. 1B, middle). However, these protocols often require either reactive but labile glycosyl donors or harsh activation conditions, posing challenges for functional group compatibility and practical implementation. As a result, developing general methods to prepare ketonyl C-glycosides under mild conditions from stable starting materials remains a formidable challenge. Meanwhile, methods that exhibit broad substrate tolerance, show high stereoselectivities, and are applicable to the synthesis of complex ketonyl C-glycoconjugates are still limited.

Reactions based on the glycosyl radical (28–31) offer a promising solution to the above challenge for two key reasons: First, radical-based reactions generally exhibit excellent tolerance of polar functional groups (32). Second, the stereoelectronic effects of glycosyl radicals provide unique control over stereoselectivity, often producing axially configured glycosides (33), particularly in C─C bond–forming reactions. In past decades, glycosyl radical–mediated reactions have enabled notable advancements in the C-glycosylation of diverse alkyl aglycone precursors, such as electron-deficient olefins (34–41), electrophilic carbonyl (42–46) or imine (47, 48) substrates, and redox-active alkyl electrophiles (49, 50). However, the applicability of these methods to the synthesis of ketonyl C-glycosides has yet to be experimentally demonstrated, and at present, styrenes remain underused for the construction of C(sp3)-glycosidic bonds (34, 35, 37, 51–53). Recently, silyl enol ether has emerged as a useful reagent for ketonyl C-glycoside synthesis under photoredox-catalyzed conditions (Fig. 1B), although its application is limited by stability concerns (54–57). In contrast, styrenes provide a stable and readily accessible alternative, making them attractive alkyl aglycone precursors but remaining underused in the preparation of ketonyl C-glycosides. However, their low reactivity toward glycosyl radicals, which are generally regarded as nucleophilic (58–60) and therefore preferentially react with electron-deficient alkenes, such as Michael acceptors, presents a persistent challenge for their use.

In this study, we report a reaction that produces ketonyl C-glycosides from bench-stable designed glycosyl sulfide and widely available styrenes in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) under photoredox-catalyzed conditions (1 + 2 + 3 to 4; Fig. 1C). A key design element is simplifying the task of generating a glycosyl radical (6; Fig. 1C) to the production of readily accessible iminyl radicals (5; Fig. 1C), achieved through a unimolecular homolytic substitution. Although homolytic substitution at heteroatoms (e.g., S or P) (61–63) is a well-established process, its use for generating glycosyl radicals remains relatively limited in carbohydrate chemistry (62). Notably, Crich pioneered the use of carbon-centered radicals to generate glycosyl radicals from thioglycosides via C─S bond homolysis (62). Enabled by this strategy, our method demonstrates the following advantages: First, a diverse range of styrenes can be efficiently glycosylated in DMSO to furnish ketonyl C-glycosides in good-to-excellent yields and diastereoselectivities. Second, we establish glycosyl sulfides as a useful class of precursors to glycosyl radicals. Third, this reaction demonstrates excellent functional group tolerance. Last, the reaction proceeds with good-to-excellent stereoselectivities across a broad array of glycosyl donors. Furthermore, this method has been successfully applied in the late-stage modification of diverse drug molecules and the preparation of ketonyl C-linked glycopeptidomimetics—two areas that remain challenging for traditional methods. Experimental studies were performed to provide insights into the mechanism of this process, which suggest a radical-polar crossover pathway.

RESULTS

Reaction validation and condition optimization

We commenced our study using 7a to 7c as the model substrates (Fig. 2). Compounds 7a to 7c could be readily prepared from peracetylated mannose in three to four steps, with overall yields of 36, 22, and 28%, respectively (see the Supplementary Materials). After extensive optimization, we found that the reaction among 7a, 8, and 3 occurred smoothly under visible light (7 W, 455 nm) irradiation in the presence of tris[2-(2-pyridinyl)phenyl]iridium [fac-Ir(ppy)3] as the photocatalyst to afford ketonyl C-glycoside 9 in high yield and with excellent axial selectivity (entry 1). Isothiazole 12a was formed as a byproduct. Under the optimized conditions, the potentially competing intramolecular 1,6-hydrogen atom transfer between the iminyl radical and anomeric hydrogen within 11 (dashed double-headed arrow in Fig. 2, middle) was not observed (64). A plausible reaction pathway is proposed (Fig. 2, middle). Iminyl radical 11, generated from oxime ester 7a via single-electron transfer (SET) reduction (65–67), initiates a unimolecular homolytic substitution at the anomeric sulfur atom to produce glycosyl radical 13. This radical reacts with 8 to form benzylic radical 14, which is oxidized to form benzylic carbocation 15. 15 is then captured by DMSO, forming alkoxysulfonium salt 16 (68), which, in the presence of 10 as a base, yields the desired ketonyl C-glycosides 9.

Control experiments listed in Fig. 2 revealed some of the key factors that affect the performance of this reaction. First, the valence state of the sulfur atom in glycosyl donors plays a crucial role in the generation of glycosyl radicals. Both sulfide 7a (entry 1) and sulfoxide 7b (entry 2) could serve as effective radical precursors. However, when the latter was used, product 9 was furnished in lower yield, accompanied by a substantial amount of olefin 18 and alcohol 19 as byproducts. We attribute the formation of 18 to β-elimination and the production of 19 to the hydration of benzylic carbocation 15. These results indicate that the iminyl radical could attack the divalent sulfur in the sulfide or the tetravalent sulfur in the sulfoxide group. The use of sulfone 7c failed to deliver any of the desired products, and only sulfone 20 and bihydrazone 21 were formed (entry 3). The former was likely generated via intramolecular 1,6-hydrogen atom transfer, and the latter was probably formed through iminyl radical dimerization. Of all the photocatalysts attempted, fac-Ir(ppy)3 ( E1/2IV/III∗ = −1.73 V versus saturated calomel electrode) (69) outperformed others (entries 4 to 6), presumably because of its redox potential matching those of the species (70) involved in the photoredox catalytic cycle. Switching iridium-mediated photoredox catalysis to copper catalysis with heating afforded only trace of 9 (entry 7). The main product in this case was identified as 2,3,4,6-tetra-O-acetyl-d-mannopyranose (22). We attribute the formation of 22 to the hydration of oxocarbenium ions generated by copper-mediated oxidation of glycosyl radicals. Under air atmosphere, the reaction still afforded a comparable yield of the product (entry 8). The addition of an extra base unexpectedly reduced the yield of the product (entry 9). DMSO was essential for this reaction, serving both as a solvent and oxidant. Replacing DMSO with another solvent failed to furnish any products, along with most of the starting materials remaining unreacted (entry 10). A screening of oxidants revealed that DMSO was optimal (see details in the Supplementary Materials). The concentration of DMSO markedly influenced the performance of this reaction. The yield of product 9 gradually increased with higher concentrations of DMSO (entries 11 to 13). Delightedly, the reaction performed unexpectedly well in aqueous DMSO, delivering the desired product in moderate yield (entry 14). Similarly, the standard reaction proceeded smoothly even in the presence of methyl α-d-glucopyranoside 23, a polyhydroxy compound added as an external additive, affording product 9 in 47% yield (entry 15). These results suggest the potential of using minimally protected or even unprotected donors in future studies.

Substrate scope regarding styrene acceptors

Having established optimal reaction conditions, we evaluated the scope of this reaction (Fig. 3). We found that a diverse range of styrenes participated in this reaction smoothly (Fig. 3, top). For instance, both electron-rich (25b, 25d, 25n, and 25o) and electron-deficient (25m and 25q) styrene rings were accommodated. Styrenes bearing phenoxy (25f), acetoxy (25g), amide (25h), pyrazole (25s), and triazole (25t) groups were all suitable substrates. Functional groups such as halogens (25i to 25k and 25r), boronic acid pinacol ester (25l), and formyl (25m) were well tolerated, offering convenient handles for subsequent derivatizations. A free primary alcohol group (25c) was compatible. Certain functional groups mentioned above might not be compatible with the conditions of classical routes to ketonyl C-glycosides. Radical-sensitive groups, including alkenes (25e and 25u), thioether (25p), and weak C─H bonds, were accommodated. Expanding the scope from simple styrenes to fused-bicyclic systems was viable, as exemplified by the formation of 25v, 25w, 25x, and 25y. Vinylated heterocycles also proved to be competent substrates, yielding the desired products (25z to 25ae) with high efficiencies. Notably, 1,3-dienyl aryl systems (25af and 25ag) participated smoothly in this reaction, affording unique alkyl C-glycosides with an α,β-unsaturated ketone moiety—a structural feature with potential antimalarial activity (as illustrated in Fig. 1A) (71). These products might be challenging to obtain using previously reported methods.

Synthetic applications in drug-sugar conjugates

Sugar conjugation has emerged as a valuable strategy to improve the pharmacokinetic profiles of drug candidates (72, 73). To explore the potential applicability of our method in this domain, we applied it in the preparation of structurally complex drug-sugar conjugates (Fig. 3, bottom). Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, such as Oxaprozin, Indomethacin, Naproxen, Ibuprofen, Dichlofenac, and Isoxepac, are prescribed to treat pain and inflammation. Through amide formation followed by our glycosylation method, these compounds were efficiently converted into their drug-sugar conjugates (25ah to 25am). Using a similar sequence, lipid-lowering agents such as Bezafibrate and Ciprofibrate, along with Febuxostat, a gout medication that reduces uric acid levels, were transformed into the corresponding drug-mannose conjugates (25ao, 25ap, and 25as). Ester formation followed by our glycosylation method proved equally effective for generating desired conjugates from Ambrisentan, a drug for pulmonary arterial hypertension, and Sulbactam, a β-lactamase inhibitor (25aq and 25au). In addition, vinylation of aryl halide in drug molecules, such as Fenofibrate, a lipid-lowering agent, and Loratadine, an antihistamine used for allergy treatment, followed by our method, efficiently produced the desired products (25an and 25at). Similarly, Estrone, a natural hormone, could be uneventfully glycosylated (25ar). Last, we demonstrated the synthesis of a ketonyl C-glycoside analog of Canagliflozin, an inhibitor for type II diabetes (25av). These results further underscore the broad functional group tolerance of our method and its practical applicability for the late-stage modification of pharmaceuticals.

Substrate scope regarding glycosyl donors

We next examined the generality of our method with respect to glycosyl donors using four styrene-derived drugs (27a to 27d) as acceptors (Fig. 4). Glycosyl donors encompassing diverse electronic and steric properties could undergo C-glycosylation with high efficiencies and selectivities. These include pyranosyl donors derived from glucose (28a), galactose (28b), rhamnose (28e), and fucose (28f), which provided the corresponding products in moderate-to-excellent yields. Other pyranosyl donors, such as those derived from glucuronate (28c) and 2-deoxyglucosamine (28d), also reacted smoothly under the optimized conditions. Despite the proposed formation of reactive carbocation intermediates in our reaction, a minimally protected donor (28h), with three free hydroxyl groups, exhibited a notably impressive performance. Alkyl C-furanosides were also conveniently prepared using our method (28i to 28l). For instance, a mannofuranose-derived donor delivered the desired alkyl C-glycoside in good yield and with exclusive 1,2-trans stereoselectivity (28i). We found that the stereoselectivity of ribose-derived donors was critically influenced by the choice of protecting groups. For example, when cis-hydroxyl groups in d-ribose donors were protected as a ketal, the reaction exclusively yielded 1,2-trans configured products (28k). In contrast, acetyl protection markedly reduced the stereoselectivity, affording the desired products with a 1,2-trans/cis ratio of 7/1 (28j). As expected, the more flexible radical leading to 28l, compared to others, resulted in lower stereoselectivity. All donors in this figure are bench-stable and readily prepared. Together, these results highlight the generality of our method.

Synthetic applications in C-linked glycopeptidomimetics

Synthetic C-linked glycopeptidomimetics have demonstrated a notable value in drug discovery (21). To showcase the utility of our method, we used it for the synthesis of ketonyl C-linked glycopeptidomimetics (Fig. 5). We found that a wide range of amino acid/peptide–derived styrenes could be efficiently glycosylated using sulfide 7a as glycosyl donors under modified conditions, which used peptide substrates as limiting agents. From these examples, we noted that protecting groups like trityl (Trt), fluorenylmethyloxycarbonyl (Fmoc), and tert-butyloxylcarbonyl (Boc), commonly used in peptide synthesis, were well tolerated. Notably, substrates bearing alcohol (30i and 30k), phenol (30j), primary amide (30t to 30w), and heterocycle such as imidazole (30d and 30q) and indole (30p) were fully compatible. These compounds mentioned above might be challenging for reported approaches using enolates or silyl enol ethers as aglycone precursors. To further demonstrate the generality of this method, we showed that glycosyl sulfide donors derived from galactose (30t), rhamnose (30u), ribose (30v), and mannofuranose (30w) coupled efficiently with a tripeptide derivative. The corresponding glycopeptidomimetics were obtained in good yields and with excellent stereoselectivities. These results further underscore the applicability and practical utility of our method in constructing structurally diverse C-linked glycoconjugates.

Mechanistic studies

To gain insights into the reaction mechanism, we conducted mechanistic experiments to probe key intermediates involved in this reaction (Fig. 6). First, we performed standard reactions in the presence of 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine-N-oxyl (TEMPO) using 7a and 32 as donors (Fig. 6A). Both reactions were completely suppressed: In the first case, the TEMPO-adduct 31 was detected by high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS), and in the second, the TEMPO-adduct 33 was isolated in 73% yield. These results indicate the intermediacy of glycosyl radicals in our method. To confirm the involvement of the benzyl radical intermediate, we conducted a clock experiment with radical probe molecule 36, producing conjugated olefin product 37 in 13% yield along with appreciable amounts of unidentified materials (Fig. 6B). This observation could be rationalized as follows (Fig. 6B, bottom): Addition of glycosyl radical Int-I to the terminal double bond of 36 generates benzylic radical Int-II, which undergoes rapid ring opening to form another benzylic radical Int-III. Oxidation of Int-III to benzylic carbocation Int-IV is followed by β-elimination, yielding 37. To probe the intermediacy of benzylic carbocation, we replaced DMSO with methanol (MeOH) and used non-nucleophilic ethyl acetate as the solvent under standard conditions (Fig. 6C, top), which afforded methyl ether product 38 in 69% yield. In addition, a series of reactions was carried out in DMSO using different equivalents of 18O-labeled water (H218O) to act as competing nucleophiles (Fig. 6C, bottom). The yield of hydration product 40 gradually increased with higher equivalents of 18O-labeled water, accompanied by a corresponding decrease in the yield of ketonyl C-glycoside 9. These results could be attributed to the preferential nucleophilic attack of stronger nucleophiles (e.g., MeOH and H218O) at the benzylic carbocation (39; Fig. 6C). Collectively, these findings support the mechanism suggested (Fig. 2, middle).

DISCUSSION

In this work, we have established a general and robust reaction to prepare ketonyl C-glycosides. This reaction uses bench-stable designed glycosyl sulfides, widely available styrenes, and DMSO as starting materials. We demonstrate that glycosyl radicals could be efficiently generated from glycosyl sulfides appended with an oxime ester moiety through a unimolecular homolytic substitution at the sulfur atom, initiated by an iminyl radical. A diverse range of styrenes, bearing an extensive variety of functional groups (including free hydroxyl, alkene, boronic acid pinacol ester, formyl, and thioether) and heteroaryls, is glycosylated by our method with high efficiency. In addition, various glycosyl units, including both pyranosyl and furanosyl types, can be smoothly incorporated. The method was successfully applied in the late-stage modification of diverse pharmaceutical agents to yield drug-sugar conjugates and enable the rapid preparation of C-linked glycopeptidomimetics. Mechanistic studies provide critical insights into the intermediates involved in this reaction, suggesting a radical-polar crossover reaction mechanism. We anticipate that this reaction will be rapidly adopted for the preparation of libraries of alkyl C-glycosides, which are frequently explored in drug development and biochemistry studies. Further investigations along these lines are currently ongoing in our laboratory.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Typical procedure for the photoredox-catalyzed reaction to prepare ketonyl C-glycosides

Under a N2 atmosphere, glycosyl sulfide 7a (133.8 mg, 0.20 mmol, 1.0 equiv), styrene 8 (108.4 mg, 0.60 mmol, 3.0 equiv), fac-Ir(ppy)3 (2.7 mg, 0.004 mmol, 0.02 equiv), and DMSO 3 (4 ml, 0.05 M) were weighed into an 8-ml screw-capped vial containing a magnetic stir bar. The vial was tightly sealed with a Teflon-lined cap and stirred at room temperature for 6 hours under irradiation from a 7-W, 455-nm blue light-emitting diode (LED). The reaction mixture was then diluted with EtOAc (100 ml) and washed sequentially with saturated aqueous NaHCO3 (100 ml) and water (2 × 100 ml). The combined organic layers were dried over anhydrous MgSO4, filtered, and concentrated under reduced pressure. The residue was purified by silica gel chromatography to afford product 9 as a white solid (α/β > 19:1, 76.5 mg, 73%).
