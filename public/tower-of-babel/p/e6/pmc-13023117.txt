Multilevel barriers to dog rabies vaccination uptake in Kilosa District, Tanzania
Authors: Tumaini Nyamhanga, Veronica Masawe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBarriers to dog vaccination in Tanzania in general, and in Kilosa District in particular, are not well understood. Therefore, this study sought to address the following research question: What are the multilevel barriers to the vaccination of dogs against rabies in Kilosa District? Guided by a socioecological model, the study explored the barriers to dog vaccination at multiple levels within the Kilosa District context. A case study design employing a qualitative research approach was used. Data were collected through focus group discussions (FGDs) and in-depth interviews to investigate contextual barriers to dog vaccination. The collected data were analyzed using a thematic analysis approach. The results are organized according to the levels of the socioecological model. At the individual level, barriers include limited literacy about rabies and dog rabies vaccination; low perceived risk of rabies transmission; low prioritization of dog vaccination in household financial decisions; and uncertainty regarding the cost of dog vaccination. At the organizational/health system level, barriers include fragmented provision of rabies-related health education, reactive rather than proactive sensitization efforts, and limited communication channels, primarily relying on loudspeaker announcements. At the community level, barriers include misconceptions about protection against rabies and mistrust in political leadership. In conclusion, Rabies vaccination uptake in Kilosa District is constrained by multilevel barriers. Individually, limited knowledge, low risk perception, and financial uncertainty reduce prioritization of dog vaccination. Organizational barriers include fragmented health education, weak cross-sector coordination, and reactive communication. At the community level, logistical challenges, absence of By-Laws, persistent misconceptions, and political mistrust further limit vaccination coverage.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s42522-026-00205-8.

Introduction

Rabies is a deadly zoonotic disease that affects the central nervous system [1–3]. Each year, more than 59,000 people die from rabies worldwide [4, 5], with approximately 99% of these deaths occurring in Asia and Africa, where canine rabies remains endemic [6–10]. The true burden of human rabies in low-income countries is likely underestimated due to underreporting of clinical cases, weak epidemiological surveillance systems, and limited laboratory confirmation [8–10]. In Africa alone, an estimated 21,000–25,000 people die from rabies annually [11, 12]. In Tanzania, rabies is estimated to cause approximately 1,500 human deaths each year [13].

Across much of Africa, canine rabies is the predominant form of the disease, with domestic dogs serving as both the primary reservoir and the main source of human infection [14]. In addition to classical rabies virus, several other lyssaviruses - often referred to as rabies-related viruses—have been identified in African countries. Due in part to biosurveillance challenges, most of these lyssaviruses have been associated with atypical hosts, including certain bat species and shrews.

Mass dog vaccination has led to the successful elimination of dog-mediated rabies in Europe, North and South America, Japan, and several other countries, where sustained vaccination campaigns form a core component of national rabies elimination strategies [15–17]. In contrast, exposure to unvaccinated dogs—predominantly in Asia and Africa—accounts for nearly 99% of all reported human rabies cases worldwide [18].

The prompt administration of post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) following exposure to rabid animals can effectively prevent human rabies [19, 20]. However, in low-income countries such as Tanzania, timely access to these life-saving treatments is often limited. Furthermore, many countries in sub-Saharan Africa struggle to consistently achieve the recommended dog vaccination coverage of 70% or higher required to interrupt rabies transmission [21–23].

A review of dog vaccination coverage across African countries indicates that Tanzania has an estimated dog rabies vaccination coverage of 37.24% [1, 2]. This level of coverage suggests that Tanzania remains far from achieving the global target of eliminating dog-mediated rabies by 2030. The global rabies elimination framework classifies countries into three phases: Phase I (preparation; dog vaccination coverage < 18%), Phase II (dog vaccination; coverage > 18% and < 70%), and Phase III (maintenance; coverage ≥ 70% with sustained vaccination efforts). Tanzania currently falls within Phase II. Similar to many countries in sub-Saharan Africa, Tanzania continues to face challenges in reaching the recommended dog vaccination coverage of 70% or higher [21–23]. However, the specific barriers to achieving adequate dog vaccination coverage in Tanzania remain poorly understood. Therefore, this study attempted to fill this gap by exploring multilevel barriers to the vaccination of dogs in the Kilosa district, using the socioecological model. That is, this study aimed to identify multilevel barriers to achieving effective dog vaccination coverage against rabies in Kilosa District, Tanzania.

Kilosa District was selected because it is a predominantly rural area where human–dog interactions are frequent and dog ownership is common, increasing the risk of rabies transmission. The district recorded a total of 14 rabid dog-bite cases between 2024 and January 2026 (personal communication with the District Veterinary Officer). The most recent incident of the rabid dog-bite involved a three-year-old boy who died as a result. These recurrent rabid dog-bite cases underscore ongoing gaps in prevention efforts. The district has reported persistent challenges in achieving adequate dog vaccination coverage, despite the availability of rabies control programs [24]. Furthermore, Kilosa reflects the typical socio-economic, cultural, and service delivery contexts found in many rural districts in Tanzania, making it an appropriate setting for examining multilevel barriers to dog vaccination.

The socio-ecological model posits that barriers to dog vaccination operate across multiple, interrelated levels. In this study, the model guided the exploration of barriers at the individual, community, and institutional/One Health system levels. By adopting this framework, the study aimed to generate evidence to improve understanding of the factors that hinder vaccination coverage. It is worth noting that the socioecological model usually includes an outermost layer of “policy and law”. The examples of rabies frameworks or policies in Tanzania include: the Animal Diseases Act of 2003; National Livestock Policy 2006; National Rabies Control Strategy 2019.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a case study design with a qualitative approach to data collection. Through focus group discussions (FGDs) and in-depth interviews, the researchers explored contextual barriers to dog vaccination. The case study design’s flexibility and depth enabled a comprehensive examination of the diverse factors limiting canine rabies vaccination.

Study area and context

This study was conducted in Kilosa District, located in the Morogoro Region of Tanzania, from 6th April to 30th June, 2026. In Kilosa, many households engage in pastoralism and small-scale farming, with livestock such as cattle, goats, and sheep forming a central component of household income and food security. Within this context, dogs are commonly kept as guards to protect livestock from predators and other threats, particularly in rural and peri-urban settings where herding activities are widespread.

Beyond their role in livestock protection, dogs are also valued for safeguarding household property. Their presence serves as a deterrent to theft and intrusion, reflecting community concerns about security and safety. These functional roles mean that dogs are often regarded primarily as working animals rather than companion animals, which may influence the level of investment in their health care, including preventive measures such as vaccination.

Despite the widespread presence and importance of dogs in Kilosa District, the area has experienced recurrent rabies outbreaks, indicating suboptimal dog vaccination coverage. This epidemiological context makes Kilosa a relevant setting for examining barriers to dog vaccination, as the interaction between livelihood practices, perceptions of dogs, and access to veterinary and public health services is likely to shape vaccination uptake.

Sampling strategy with gender consideration

With the assistance of local community leaders, a purposive sampling approach was used to identify and recruit participants for both Focus Group Discussions (FGDs) and key informant interviews [18]. Community leaders were first oriented on the study objectives and provided with clear inclusion criteria, including residency in the study area, experience with dog ownership or rabies control activities, and willingness to participate. They then assisted in identifying eligible individuals who met these criteria. Recruitment was structured to ensure gender balance, with equal numbers of female and male participants included in the key informant interviews. In addition, separate FGDs were conducted for women and men to facilitate open discussion and capture gender-specific perspectives. Participants were approached individually, informed about the study, and enrolled after providing voluntary consent.

Recruitment and discussion with respondents continued sequentially until data saturation was reached - the point in data collection and analysis when new information produced little or no change to the progression of the specified theme. A total of 12 FGDs were conducted, with equal sessions for men and women, involving a total of 78 participants: 40 men and 38 women.

Data collection methods

Data were collected through key informant interviews (KIIs) and focus group discussions (FGDs), with each FGD comprising 6–12 participants. To ensure data security and completeness, interviews were audio-recorded in addition to taking handwritten notes. The data collection process was facilitated by two researchers: a moderator and a note-taker. Prior to participation, respondents completed a socio-demographic survey.

Interview and FGD guides, structured around open-ended questions, were used to explore barriers to dog vaccination against rabies (see Table 1). In total, twelve FGDs and seven KIIs were conducted.

Data analysis

The audio recordings of the FGDs and interviews were transcribed verbatim and translated from Kiswahili into English. The transcripts were analyzed manually using an inductive thematic analysis approach [24, 25], following three stages: (1) line-by-line coding of the transcripts and field notes to develop initial concepts and segment the data for underlying ideas and meanings; (2) interpreting the codes to identify descriptive themes; and (3) synthesizing the descriptive themes into higher-order analytical themes.

Trustworthiness

Trustworthiness of the study was ensured through four methodological strategies. Credibility was enhanced by the use of well-facilitated FGDs and KIIs, prolonged engagement during data collection, and careful probing to capture participants’ authentic perspectives. Dependability was strengthened through the use of standardized, pilot-tested data collection tools and a transparent documentation of the research process. Confirmability was supported by independent coding by multiple researchers and team discussions to validate emerging themes and reduce individual bias. Transferability was addressed by providing a clear description of the study context, participants, and data collection procedures, enabling readers to assess the applicability of the findings to similar settings.

Reflexivity statement

The research team comprised investigators with training in public health, social sciences, and veterinary-related fields, with prior experience conducting qualitative research in community health settings in Tanzania. Both authors are Tanzanian nationals with familiarity with the local language (Kiswahili) and sociocultural context of the study area, which facilitated rapport building with participants and supported open discussion during FGDs. However, this familiarity may also have introduced assumptions about community knowledge and practices related to rabies and dog vaccination.

The study minimized researcher bias through collaboratively developed and pilot-tested tools, neutral facilitation of FGDs using semi-structured guides, and cautious probing. Data analysis involved independent reviews by both researchers, collective discussion of themes, and ongoing reflexive discussions to strengthen rigor and reduce subjective influence.

Barriers to vaccination of dogs against rabies

Barriers to dog rabies vaccination were explored using thematic analysis, guided by the socioecological model. Accordingly, the identified barriers are organized across three levels of influence represented in the model: the individual level, the organizational/health system level, and the community level.

Individual-level barriers

Four themes emerged under this section: limited literacy about rabies and dog rabies vaccination; low perceived risk of rabies transmission; low prioritization of dog vaccination in household financial decisions; and uncertainty regarding the cost of dog vaccination.

Limited literacy about rabies and dog rabies vaccination

Participants reported having limited and fragmented literacy about rabies, particularly regarding its transmission from dogs to humans and the preventive role of vaccinating dogs. While community members were familiar with the appearance of rabid dogs, they demonstrated inadequate understanding of the consequences of rabies infection and the importance of preventing the disease through dog vaccination. The absence of personally witnessed human rabies cases appeared to reduce the perceived seriousness of the disease and, consequently, the urgency to vaccinate dogs.

One participant explained:

We hear that once a dog with rabies bites a human being, he starts barking like a dog and later dies. But we have not seen a human rabies case, so people tend to ignore warnings about the fatality of rabies (FGD participant, Male).

Another participant similarly added:

There are many perceptions in the community about what happens to a person after being bitten by a rabid dog, but since no one here has seen someone die from rabies, people assume the risk is exaggerated and do not take the disease seriously. (FGD participant, Female).

Moreover, some participants appeared uncertain about the appropriate timing of dog vaccination, including whether vaccination should occur before or after a dog becomes infected. As one participant explained:

People know that rabies exists, but they think it is rare and only becomes a problem when there is an outbreak. As long as the dog looks healthy, vaccinating it is not seen as important” (FGD participant, Male).

Low perceived risk of rabies transmission

It was found that community members had a low perception of rabies risk, attributable to limited awareness of the disease and a tendency to delay dog vaccination until an outbreak occurs, as reflected in the following statement: “Our dogs are not sick, so there is no need for vaccination.”

Low prioritization of dog vaccination in household financial decisions

It was reported that since vaccination is not administered free of charge, some people do not see it as a need worth spending meagre money.

People do not see that it is worth spending money to vaccinate a dog. If vaccination were free, many people would vaccinate their dogs (FGD participants, Male).

Uncertainty regarding the cost of dog vaccination

It was reported that some people are not informed about the cost involved in vaccination. They just guess that vaccinating a dog may not be affordable.

People do not know the costs of getting a dog vaccinated, they guess it could probably be a lot of money (FGD participant, Female).

In contrast, some participants were of the view that some community members are aware that vaccination may be affordable or free, yet hesitation persists due to misconceptions. Together, these perspectives illustrate a spectrum of understanding within the community, revealing how both misinformation and partial knowledge influence vaccination decisions.

Some of us are actually aware that vaccinating a dog does not cost much, and in some cases it is even offered for free during campaigns, but many people still assume it is expensive and hesitate to take their dogs. (FGD participant, Female)

Organizational/ health system barriers

Four themes emerged under this section

fragmented provision of health education; reactive sensitization practices; reliance on loudspeaker announcements as the primary means of communication; and the absence of village-level By-Laws governing dog ownership and vaccination. These themes are described in detail in the sections that follow.

Fragmented provision of health education

It was learned that the provision of rabies-related health education to communities is fragmented. A One Health approach is largely absent, as health education activities are led exclusively by Veterinary Field Officers, with minimal or no involvement of human health personnel. This fragmentation limits the effectiveness of risk communication; in the absence of human health professionals, community members fail to fully appreciate the fatal and public health risks that unvaccinated dogs pose to their family members and the wider community. This concern was underscored by a senior medical staff member at a local health facility, who stated:

“We, on the human health side, our involvement begins only when a person bitten by a dog comes to the health facility for treatment. We do not participate in promoting dog vaccination; that responsibility is left to the veterinary staff” (Senior medical staff, local health facility).

Reactive rather than proactive sensitization

It was learned that active sensitization of dog owners to vaccinate their dogs is not conducted on a routine basis. Instead, sensitization efforts are intensified primarily in response to rabies outbreaks. Consequently, prior to the occurrence of an outbreak, dog vaccination activities are implemented to a very limited extent. This study was conducted in a ward that had experienced a rabies outbreak beginning in January 2024, approximately six months before data collection. The majority of participants reported that they only became aware of calls for dog vaccination in the current year, after the outbreak had already occurred. One of the participants thus remarked:

We only started hearing about rabies vaccination at the beginning of this year, after the outbreak had already occurred (FGD participant, Male).

Means of communication limited to loudspeaker announcement

It was further learnt through observation that means of communication about the urgency and importance of rabies vaccinations are limited to one method - notably loudspeaker announcement. That is, during an outbreak one energetic person with a bicycle is hired and tasked to go around the entire village, announcing using a loudspeaker calling dog owners to take them for vaccination. Other channels of communication - such as posters or leaflets about rabies - were not seen. To show the limited effectiveness of the loudspeaker approach, participants in one of the FGDs expressed limited clarity of messages conveyed through the loudspeaker particularly how one would take the dogs to the service [vaccination] delivery point. One of the participants thus said:

We hear announcements through loudspeakers that we are required to take our dogs to the Ward Government Offices so they get vaccinated, but we do not know how to take them there. FGD participant, Female).

Dog handling difficulty

Participants described challenges related to physically handling and transporting dogs to designated vaccination points as a significant barrier to vaccination uptake. Many dogs were reported to be unrestrained or unaccustomed to being handled, making it difficult for owners to move them safely over long distances. Unlike smaller or more manageable animals, dogs require specific handling skills or equipment, which many owners lack. As one participant explained, “Dogs are not chickens, how can we carry them and get them there?” (FGD participant, Female). It was asserted that these difficulties discouraged owners from attempting to bring their dogs for vaccination, contributing to low turnout at vaccination sites.

Lack of village By-laws on keeping and vaccination of dogs

Although Tanzania has a legal framework for animal disease control under the Animal Diseases Act of 2003, findings from this study indicate that the absence of locally enacted and enforced By-Laws was perceived as a key barrier to dog vaccination. Participants reported that, in the absence of clear village- or ward-level bylaws mandating vaccination or specifying penalties for non-compliance, dog vaccination was largely viewed as optional. One of the leaders commented:.

The village does not have By-Laws on keeping and vaccinating dogs. So, we rely on awareness creation so people can see the importance of keeping their dogs well. (Village Leader, Mandela).

Community-level barriers

Two themes emerged under this section, namely: misconception about protection against rabies; and political mistrust. They are described in detail in the sections that follow:

Misconception about protection against rabies

It was reported that there is a misconception among dog owners that a dog can be protected against rabies by cutting its ear and letting blood ooze out. One of the participants pointed out that:

People here believe that cutting a dog’s ear is protective against rabies (FGD participant, Male).

Political mistrust

It was reported that political mistrust may constrain access to dog vaccination. Some community members believe that the government has a responsibility to vaccinate dogs free of charge. The requirement to pay a token fee of TZS 3,000 per dog has led to resistance, with some accusing the responsible government department of corruption. One key informant explained:

People complain that being required to vaccinate a dog for a fee is a conspiracy between the local veterinary staff and the government department to make money and exploit the citizens (KI, Male).

They added that people perceive that, the vaccination of dogs against rabies is somebody’s business (chanjo ya kichaa cha Mbwa ni biashara mtu). This perspective of non-compliance is corroborated by the reality that was found in the field, that although the service provider is a civil servant, the government does not procure vaccines for her to administer to the dogs, even if at a fee. Instead, she uses her own money to procure the vaccines which she recovers by asking dog owners to pay TZS 3000 per a single dog.

Discussion

This study has revealed barriers at individual, organizational, and community levels that constrain the uptake of vaccination against rabies. At the individual level, limited literacy and the resulting low perception of risk emerged as major barriers to dog vaccination. Limited literacy regarding vaccination reflects restricted access to accurate and reliable information about rabies, a challenge that is particularly pronounced in rural settings. Similar findings have been documented in other studies, which show that inadequate knowledge about rabies undermines preventive practices such as routine dog vaccination [26, 27]. When individuals lack a clear understanding of rabies transmission and prevention, vaccination is often viewed as unnecessary rather than as a critical public health measure. From a One Health perspective, inadequate community understanding of rabies transmission and prevention not only puts humans at risk but also affects animal health and ecosystem safety; therefore, strengthening awareness and education is essential to promote dog vaccination as a critical measure for safeguarding both human and animal health.

Low-risk perception further compounds this barrier, as it is often rooted in the absence of visible or recently reported rabies cases within the community. In settings where rabies outbreaks are rare or not openly discussed, dog owners may underestimate their susceptibility to infection and the potential consequences of non-vaccination. In addition, misconceptions about rabies transmission may lead to an underestimation of how easily the disease can spread through bites or contact with saliva from infected animals. Consequently, the severe and almost invariably fatal nature of rabies may not be fully appreciated, fostering complacency toward preventive measures such as dog vaccination [28, 29]. Moreover, limited literacy and low-risk perception appear to reflect broader weaknesses in the provision of anti-rabies health education [30, 31].

Other individual-level barriers identified in this study include the low prioritization of dog vaccination within household financial expenditures and uncertainty about the cost of vaccination. The finding that households fail to prioritize dog vaccination within their financial expenditures indicates that rabies vaccination is not widely perceived as a budgetary priority. In many resource-constrained households, competing demands such as food, school fees, and healthcare for family members take precedence, leaving little room for preventive animal health measures. As a result, even though rabies vaccination is a highly effective and life-saving intervention, it may be regarded as non-urgent or dispensable, particularly among households facing persistent poverty. This pattern suggests not only economic constraints but also limited recognition of rabies as a serious public health threat. These findings are consistent with recent research from Nigeria, which similarly reported financial constraints and low prioritization of dog vaccination as key barriers to rabies control [32].

Uncertainty about the cost of vaccination further exacerbates this challenge as it reflectslack of clear and reliable information on the price of rabies vaccination. When dog owners are unsure about how much they are expected to pay, they may delay or avoid vaccinating their dogs altogether due to fear of unexpected or unaffordable expenses [33]. Such uncertainty may stem from variations in pricing across service providers, limited communication from veterinary authorities, or confusion about whether additional charges, such as consultation or registration fees, apply.

This lack of cost transparency can undermine trust in vaccination services and discourage timely uptake, particularly among low-income households. Even when vaccination services are affordable or offered at reduced cost, poor dissemination of this information may lead owners to assume that vaccination is expensive. Addressing this barrier requires clearer and more consistent communication about vaccination costs, as well as efforts to standardize fees where possible. In addition, the expansion of free or subsidized mass vaccination campaigns could reduce financial uncertainty and improve overall vaccination coverage.

Furthermore, this study identified five key organizational and health system barriers: fragmented health education, predominantly reactive rather than proactive sensitization, reliance on loudspeaker announcements as the main communication channel, dog handling difficulty, and the absence of village By-Laws regulating dog keeping and vaccination. These barriers are discussed in detail in the sections that follow.

The finding of fragmented health education points to inadequate collaboration between veterinary and human health professionals in the delivery of rabies-related information. Given that rabies is a zoonotic disease transmitted between animals and humans, effective prevention requires a coordinated, cross-sectoral approach. When veterinary and human health efforts operate in isolation, critical gaps can emerge in community awareness, access to vaccination, and understanding of prevention strategies, resulting in missed opportunities for effective rabies control [34].

Veterinary professionals are primarily responsible for vaccinating dogs, while human health professionals focus on managing human exposure through post-exposure prophylaxis. In the absence of coordinated messaging and joint community engagement, the public may receive incomplete or inconsistent information about the importance of dog vaccination and the risks of rabies transmission. Such fragmentation can weaken health education efforts and reduce trust in prevention messages. Limited collaboration between veterinary and human health sectors has been widely documented, including by Eussen et al. [34], highlighting the need for integrated, One Health–oriented approaches to rabies prevention and control. That is, to enhance rabies control, there is a critical need for integrated One Health strategies that foster coordinated action between human, animal, and environmental health sectors. This approach calls for policies that support joint planning and implementation of community education programs, ensuring that messages about rabies risks and prevention are consistent, clear, and widely understood. At the same time, strengthening communication and data sharing across sectors is essential, including linking human bite cases to dog vaccination records through robust surveillance systems. It is worth noting that cross-sector communication and data sharing are feasible in Tanzania, as demonstrated by a project that piloted Integrated Bite Case Management (IBCM) to strengthen collaboration between human health and veterinary workers. The IBCM approach was implemented in four regions—Mtwara, Mara, Lindi, and Morogoro. The assessment showed that IBCM dramatically improved case detection and enhanced communication between the two sectors [35].

The finding that awareness creation around dog rabies vaccination is predominantly reactive rather than proactive highlights a critical weakness in current public health efforts. Reactive approaches typically involve sensitization activities only after rabies cases or outbreaks have occurred, which limits their preventive impact and allows transmission risks to persist during periods of apparent calm. Such delayed responses often result in missed opportunities to promote routine vaccination and to sustain community vigilance against rabies.

In contrast, proactive awareness creation would entail continuous, structured education and sensitization campaigns regardless of outbreak status, reinforcing the importance of regular dog vaccination as a preventive measure. Evidence from Tanzania supports this observation, with Bardosh et al. [36] documenting similar operational barriers to dog vaccination in Kilombero and Ulanga districts. Strengthening proactive, routine communication strategies could therefore enhance risk perception, encourage timely vaccination, and contribute to more effective and sustained rabies control.

Furthermore, reliance on loudspeakers as the primary means of disseminating information about dog vaccination campaigns presents several limitations. Loudspeaker announcements may fail to reach all community members due to factors such as distance from the announcement point, competing background noise, or poor sound quality. As a result, some dog owners may remain unaware of vaccination schedules, locations, or the importance of participating in the campaigns, thereby limiting community-wide coverage. Poor communication has also been identified as a barrier to dog vaccination coverage in Uganda [37].

Moreover, dog handling difficulty emerged as an important health system–related barrier, as it reflects limitations in how vaccination services are organized and delivered rather than solely individual reluctance. Participants reported that many dogs were unrestrained and unaccustomed to close handling, making it physically challenging and sometimes risky for owners to transport them to designated vaccination points. This challenge was exacerbated when vaccination sites were located far from households, requiring owners to move dogs over long distances without appropriate leashes, cages, or other handling equipment. Similar challenges have been documented in other rabies-endemic settings, where poor dog manageability significantly reduces participation in mass vaccination campaigns [38].

From a health system perspective, these difficulties highlight gaps in vaccination delivery models that assume owners have the capacity, skills, and resources to restrain and transport dogs. In many rural contexts, such assumptions are unrealistic, given prevailing free-roaming dog populations and limited access to handling tools. The absence of supportive measures—such as trained dog handlers, mobile or door-to-door vaccination teams, and community-level demonstrations on safe dog handling - effectively shifts responsibility to owners and constrains equitable access to vaccination services. Evidence from low- and middle-income countries shows that adapting vaccination strategies to include mobile teams and trained personnel substantially improves coverage and community participation [37]. Addressing dog handling difficulty therefore requires health system adaptations that align service delivery with local dog ownership and management practices.

In addition, the present study found an absence of village By-Laws regulating dog ownership and mandatory vaccination. This finding highlights a critical gap between the existence of national-level legislation and its practical translation into local action. Although the Animal Diseases Act of 2003 [39] provides a legal foundation for the control of animal diseases in Tanzania, including provisions relevant to rabies prevention, the study findings suggest that this framework alone is insufficient to drive dog vaccination at the community level in the absence of operational local By-Laws.

Participants’ perception that dog vaccination is “optional” reflects the limited visibility and enforceability of the national Act at village and ward levels. In many rural settings, community members interact more directly with local governance structures than with national legislation. Where village or ward By-Laws are absent, unclear, or not enforced, there is little social or legal pressure to comply with preventive measures such as routine dog vaccination. This weakens accountability and allows vaccination decisions to be guided by individual priorities, risk perceptions, or financial considerations rather than public health obligations. Similar findings have been reported in studies conducted in Chad, where the absence of enforceable local regulations was associated with poor dog vaccination uptake [40].

With regard to community-level barriers, the present study identified two key issues: misconceptions surrounding rabies prevention and political mistrust. Notably, some dog owners in the study setting believed that rabies could be prevented by cutting a dog’s ear to allow blood to ooze out. This finding is particularly concerning, as it reflects the persistence of local myths or misinterpretations of traditional practices related to disease prevention. Such misconceptions may undermine the perceived necessity of scientifically proven measures such as vaccination. Addressing these beliefs through targeted, culturally sensitive health education is therefore essential prior to, and alongside, dog vaccination campaigns. Similar myths hindering effective rabies prevention and control have been documented in studies conducted in India [41, 42].

In addition, political mistrust emerged as an important barrier influencing participation in dog vaccination programmes. When community members perceive government institutions as untrustworthy or corrupt, they may resist or disengage from public health interventions, including vaccination campaigns, even when such programmes are intended to benefit them. In the present study, political mistrust was reported to constrain access to dog vaccination because some dog owners believed that the government is obligated to provide dog vaccination services free of charge. In practice, however, dog owners are required to contribute a small fee. This discrepancy may foster suspicion that veterinary officials are imposing charges corruptly, leading some dog owners to refuse participation altogether. Furthermore, the expectation that dog vaccination should be free may be influenced by broader experiences with human health services, where most vaccinations are provided at no cost [43]. Such expectations highlight the need for transparent communication regarding vaccination policies, costs, and the roles of both the state and dog owners in rabies prevention efforts.

Study limitations

Despite providing valuable insights into barriers to dog vaccination, this study has several limitations. First, it focuses primarily on obstacles and does not examine enabling factors or potential facilitators, which could have provided a more balanced understanding of vaccination uptake. Second, the study lacks triangulation with quantitative data or veterinary perspectives, which could have strengthened the validity of the findings. Additionally, while low literacy is identified as a barrier, it is unclear whether this reflects disinterest or limited exposure to information due to infrequent outreach; incorporating survey data on the frequency of educational campaigns could clarify this distinction.

Notwithstanding these limitations, this paper provides useful information with important implications for the planning and implementation of effective rabies control strategies in Kilosa District.

Conclusion and recommendations

This study found that low uptake of rabies vaccination is driven by interconnected barriers at individual, organizational, and community levels. At the individual level, limited literacy, low perception of rabies risk, financial constraints, and uncertainty about vaccination costs reduce awareness, trust, and prioritization of dog vaccination. These challenges are compounded by inadequate and fragmented health education, particularly in rural settings.

At the organizational and community levels, weak collaboration between veterinary and human health sectors, reactive and limited communication strategies, difficulties in handling and transporting dogs, absence of village By-Laws, persistent misconceptions about rabies prevention, and political mistrust further hinder vaccination uptake.

To address these challenges, the study recommends several actionable interventions. First, rabies health education should be institutionalized as a routine village-level activity rather than being triggered only by outbreaks. This education should explicitly adopt a One Health approach, with joint participation of veterinary officers and human health clinicians to deliver harmonized messages on rabies transmission, fatality, prevention, and the public health benefits of dog vaccination. Second, cost-related barriers should be addressed through transparent communication campaigns that clearly state vaccination fees, clarify when services are free or subsidized, and standardize pricing across providers where feasible. Third, to specifically address difficulties in handling and transporting dogs, vaccination strategies should be adapted to local contexts by introducing mobile or door-to-door vaccination teams, deploying trained dog handlers during campaigns, and providing community demonstrations on safe dog restraint techniques using locally available materials. Fourth, to address communication gaps, a multi-channel approach is recommended. This should include posters and leaflets placed in high-traffic areas, door-to-door sensitization by community workers, repeated local radio announcements, and mobile phone messaging (SMS/WhatsApp) where feasible.

Fifth, local governments at ward and village levels should develop and enforce By-Laws to support responsible dog ownership, including mandatory dog registration, restrictions on free-roaming dogs, and compulsory annual rabies vaccination. Enforcement mechanisms could include modest fines for non-compliance, coupled with community sensitization to ensure understanding and acceptance of the regulations. Implemented together, these measures could strengthen accountability, improve risk awareness, and substantially increase dog vaccination coverage, thereby contributing to more effective and sustainable rabies control.
