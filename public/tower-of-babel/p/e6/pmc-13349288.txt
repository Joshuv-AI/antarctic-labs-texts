Side-locked Headache Associated with Habitual Neck–Shoulder Mobile Phone Clamping: A Case Series
Authors: Sanjay Prakash, Varoon Vadodaria, Manju Yadav
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Side-locked headache is commonly regarded as a warning sign for secondary intracranial pathology; however, its musculoskeletal causes may be underrecognized. We report a series of seven patients with strictly unilateral headache consistently and temporally associated with the habitual clamping of a mobile phone between the neck and shoulder during hands-free multitasking. The headache was nonthrobbing, lacked migrainous or cranial autonomic features, and was reproducible with cervical palpation or sustained neck movements. Neurological examination and neuroimaging were normal in all cases. Cervical muscle tenderness, spasm, and trigger point–mediated referred pain were prominent findings. Conservative management focusing on avoidance of the implicated posture, posture correction, and physiotherapy was associated with substantial improvement or complete resolution in all patients. This series highlights neck–shoulder mobile phone clamping as a common posture-related trigger for cervicogenic or myofascial headache and underscores the importance of assessing smartphone-handling behaviors in patients with unilateral headache. These findings are hypothesis-generating and do not establish causality.

Introduction

Side-locked headache is considered a red flag for secondary headache disorders. Cervicogenic and musculoskeletal headaches represent an important, often overlooked, subset of unilateral headaches.[1] These headaches originate from cervical structures, including muscles, ligaments, joints, and nerves, with referred pain to the head via the trigeminocervical complex. Poor posture, sustained neck flexion or lateral flexion, and repetitive strain are well-established triggers.[23]

In recent years, smartphone use has dramatically altered cervical biomechanics.[4] One particularly harmful yet common posture is clamping the phone between the neck and shoulder during prolonged conversations, leading to sustained lateral neck flexion and shoulder elevation [Figure 1]. Despite its prevalence, this posture-related headache mechanism is underreported in the literature.

Methods

Between January 2024 and July 2025, a total of 1037 patients presented with headache, of whom 177 (17.3%) had side-locked unilateral headache. Among these, seven patients were identified with strictly side-locked headache associated with habitual neck–shoulder mobile phone clamping and were included in the present study. The Institutional Ethics Committee approved the study (SVIEC/ON/MEDI/RP/May/25165). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from the patients for publication of this report.

Cases were identified retrospectively from the clinical database based on the following criteria: (1) strictly unilateral (side-locked) headache without side shift; (2) a consistent temporal association between headache onset or exacerbation and habitual neck–shoulder mobile phone clamping; 3) reproducibility of symptoms with cervical posture or muscle palpation; and (4) absence of alternative primary or secondary headache diagnoses following detailed clinical evaluation and neuroimaging.

Case report-1

A 38-year-old man employed as a sales manager presented to the neurology outpatient clinic with complaints of unilateral headache for the past six months. The patient described a dull, aching pain strictly confined to the right side of the head and upper neck. The pain was localized to the temporal, parietal, and upper cervical regions and never shifted sides. It typically began in the upper cervical region and radiated to the right temporal and parietal areas. The headache occurred almost daily, with an average duration of 2–6 hours. Intensity was moderate (rated 5–6/10 on a visual analog scale), occasionally increasing toward evening. There was no throbbing quality. There were no associated migrainous features such as nausea, vomiting, photophobia, phonophobia, or aura. There were no autonomic symptoms such as lacrimation, nasal congestion, or ptosis. Sleep was not interrupted by headaches. Headache severity and impact were also assessed using the visual analog scale (VAS) and the headache impact test (HIT-6). The baseline VAS score was 6/10 and the HIT-6 score was 58, indicating substantial impact.

The patient reported 4–6 hours of daily mobile phone use, frequently clamping the handset between his right neck and shoulder to facilitate multitasking (e.g., typing or writing). These episodes of sustained postural strain lasted 20–40 minutes several times daily. He identified a direct temporal link between these prolonged “hands-free” conversations and aggravation of his side-locked pain.

Physical examination demonstrated marked tenderness over the right upper trapezius, levator scapulae, and paraspinal muscles. Palpable muscle spasm was noted on the right side. Sustained right lateral neck flexion reproduced the patient’s typical pain. Cervical range of motion was reduced in right lateral flexion and rotation. Trigger-point palpation over the right trapezius reliably reproduced the patient’s headache.

Given the strictly side-locked nature of the headache, a comprehensive differential diagnosis was undertaken.[1] The absence of migrainous features such as throbbing quality, nausea, vomiting, photophobia, phonophobia, and aura made migraine unlikely. The absence of cranial autonomic symptoms and restlessness during severe attacks excluded trigeminal autonomic cephalalgias, whereas the lack of neuralgiform pain excluded neuralgias. Secondary causes of unilateral headache were excluded through normal neurological examination and neuroimaging.

A diagnosis of posture-induced cervicogenic headache associated with habitual neck–shoulder mobile phone clamping was made. The patient was advised not to hold the phone between the neck and shoulder while talking. Physiotherapy was initiated, focusing on cervical stretching and strengthening exercises. Specific relaxation techniques targeting the trapezius and levator scapulae muscles were incorporated, along with structured postural retraining to reduce cervical muscle strain during daily activities. At three weeks, the patient reported an approximately 70% reduction in headache frequency and intensity. At two months, the headaches had nearly resolved. Following intervention, the VAS score decreased to 1/10 and the HIT-6 score to 42 at two months, reflecting marked clinical improvement. At the six-month follow-up, the patient remained largely asymptomatic with adherence to postural correction.

Across the seven cases (F:M, 1:6), all patients reported strictly right side-locked headache without side shift, most commonly involving the temporal, parietal, or occipitocervical regions. The headache was nonthrobbing and was not associated with migrainous features or cranial autonomic symptoms. In all patients, headache was consistently aggravated by prolonged phone conversations, especially when calls involved hands-free multitasking such as typing, writing, computer use, or other activities, leading to sustained clamping of the mobile phone between the right side of the neck and shoulder.

Cervical examination revealed ipsilateral muscle tenderness, palpable spasm, and trigger point–mediated referred pain, with reproduction of symptoms during sustained neck movements. Neurological examination and neuroimaging were normal in all cases. The diagnosis in this series was made after systematic exclusion of other primary and secondary unilateral headache disorders, reinforcing the likelihood of a posture-related cervicogenic or myofascial mechanism.[1]

Following conservative management centered on avoiding holding the mobile phone between the neck and shoulder during conversations, posture correction, ergonomic modification, physiotherapy, and trigger-point–directed interventions, all patients demonstrated substantial clinical improvement. Headache frequency and intensity were markedly reduced in most cases, with complete resolution observed in several patients during follow-up.

Discussion

The observed clinical features, including reproducible pain on palpation, a clear and consistent postural trigger, improvement following posture correction, and absence of migrainous features or cranial autonomic symptoms, were compatible with a musculoskeletal etiology. Discontinuation of the habitual “phone-clamping” posture, alongside targeted physiotherapy, was vital to the patient’s clinical improvement and the mitigation of headache frequency. This indicates that abnormal posture may have contributed to the generation of the side-locked headache. All patients met the International Classification of Headache Disorders (ICHD)-3 criteria for cervicogenic headache.[5] ICHD-3 recognizes that disorders of the cervical soft tissue elements may also cause cervicogenic headache. So, a diagnosis of posture-induced cervicogenic headache associated with habitual neck–shoulder mobile phone clamping was made. The headache phenotype observed in this case series also showed substantial overlap with headache attributed to cervical myofascial pain, as described in the appendix section of ICHD-3 (A11.2).[5] Several core features of the present cases closely align with headache attributed to cervical myofascial pain. All patients demonstrated ipsilateral cervical muscle tenderness, palpable spasm, and trigger point–mediated reproduction of their typical head pain, which are central characteristics of myofascial pain syndromes.[35]

We acknowledge that the observational nature of this case series and the absence of objective biomechanical or ergonomic assessment limit the ability to establish causality. The relationship between neck–shoulder mobile phone clamping and headache in this series should therefore be interpreted as associative and hypothesis-generating. However, the consistent temporal relationship, reproducibility of symptoms with posture, strict side-locking corresponding to the side of use, and improvement following avoidance of the implicated posture suggest that this association is unlikely to be entirely coincidental.

Postural abnormalities of the cervical spine, particularly forward head posture, have been implicated in the development and activation of myofascial trigger points in several head and neck muscles in patients with chronic tension-type headache and cervicogenic headache.[23] Such postural abnormalities are common among individuals who use smartphones extensively and may contribute to a range of musculoskeletal symptoms involving the cervical and shoulder regions. In a cross-sectional study by Kim and Kim (2015),[4] 292 university students were surveyed regarding subjective musculoskeletal symptoms associated with smartphone use. In this cohort, 55.8% reported neck pain and 54.8% reported shoulder pain after smartphone use, making these the most commonly affected regions among participants. Other body regions with reported discomfort included the eyes (42.1%), wrists (27.1%), arms and hands (19.2%), and waist (29.8%), whereas pain in the legs and feet was reported least frequently (9.6%). Although this study did not specifically assess headache as a primary outcome, the high prevalence of neck and shoulder symptoms associated with smartphone use supports the notion that prolonged and nonergonomic device use is correlated with musculoskeletal strain in the cervical-shoulder region. However, the authors did not assess specific phone-holding behaviors, such as clamping the phone between the neck and shoulder. They did not record lateral neck flexion, asymmetric postures, or hands-free multitasking behaviors. Posture was discussed only in general terms (e.g., sustained neck flexion during smartphone use), rather than as discrete, observable postural patterns.

Pathophysiology: Sustained neck–shoulder mobile phone clamping results in prolonged lateral cervical flexion combined with ipsilateral shoulder elevation, leading to continuous activation of the upper trapezius, levator scapulae, and cervical paraspinal muscles.[6] This static muscular loading, or sustained isometric contraction as occurs in prolonged asymmetric postures, predisposes individuals to local ischemia, metabolic stress, and trigger-point formation or activation. Experimental and clinical studies have demonstrated that nociceptive input from upper cervical muscles and joints converges with trigeminal afferents within the trigeminocervical complex, facilitating referred pain to the temporal, parietal, and occipital regions.[7] Ergonomic studies further demonstrate increased trapezius activation and asymmetric muscle loading during sustained lateral neck flexion and shoulder elevation, postures known to increase mechanical strain on the cervical musculature.[89] Such asymmetric loading may lead to the development of myofascial trigger points and pain. Repeated exposure to this posture may promote peripheral and central sensitization, resulting in persistent, strictly unilateral headache.[5]

The increasing prevalence of smartphone use has introduced new patterns of cervical strain. While forward flexion-related symptoms are commonly discussed, lateral flexion postures deserve equal attention. Clinicians should routinely inquire about phone-handling habits when evaluating patients with unilateral or cervicogenic headache. Recognition of these features is crucial to avoid unnecessary investigations and inappropriate long-term pharmacotherapy.

This case series has several limitations. The small sample size and descriptive design limit the ability to draw causal inferences. The small sample size also limits generalizability and precludes any inference regarding prevalence or causation. All patients were evaluated at a single tertiary-care neurology center. We acknowledge the possibility of referral and selection bias in this observational case series. Headache triggers and posture-related behaviors were identified clinically, without objective quantification of cervical posture, muscle activity, or phone-use duration. As this study was based on retrospective clinical observations, key exposure variables such as cumulative daily duration of phone clamping, exact posture angles, and occupational ergonomic factors were not objectively quantified. Follow-up duration was variable, limiting conclusions regarding long-term outcomes and recurrence.

Despite these limitations, the highly consistent phenotype observed across this series may represent a distinct clinical pattern within the spectrum of cervicogenic or myofascial headache. Although these cases overlap with the criteria for cervicogenic headache and headache attributed to cervical myofascial pain, the specific mechanical trigger suggests a unique etiology. These findings support the hypothesis that sustained postural strain from mobile device use warrants consideration as a discrete secondary headache subtype. Future prospective studies employing objective biomechanical assessments and standardized ergonomic interventions are essential to validate this hypothesis. Such research may determine whether these posture-related phenotypes warrant formal recognition or specific diagnostic criteria in subsequent revisions of international headache classification systems.

Conclusions

Habitual neck–shoulder mobile phone clamping may represent an underrecognized postural trigger for side-locked cervicogenic or cervical myofascial headache. The consistent unilateral pain pattern, reproducibility with cervical movements, and improvement after posture correction support a musculoskeletal mechanism. Awareness of smartphone-handling behaviours during clinical evaluation may help identify this reversible cause of unilateral headache and prevent unnecessary investigations. Further prospective studies are needed to validate these observations.
