Impact of processing techniques on chemical compositions and sensory attributes across five types of Yunnan large-leaf teas
Authors: Xueqing Dang, Yunfang Li, Zixian Lv, Fengmei Lu, Wen Li, Yi Zhang, Xiaoyu Shi, Guimei Yi, Hongxing Duan, Yan Hou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDistinct processing techniques form five types of Yunnan large-leaf teas. Besides sensory evaluation, HPLC, UHPLC–QTrap–MS/MS and HS-SPME-GC–MS were performed to explore the overall impact of processing techniques through comparisons among these five types of teas. Principal component analysis basically divided these teas into three groups, confirming that pile-fermentation and anaerobic fermentation have more profound impacts on organic acids, flavonoids and non-volatile metabolites than other typical processes. Different from phenolics oxidative polymerization in enzymatic oxidation and pile-fermentation to formulate thearubigins and theabrownins, anaerobic fermentation of pickled tea (PT) transformed ester catechins and rutin into four non-ester catechins and three simple flavonols (i.e. quercetin, kaempferol and myricetin), respectively. Despite similar volatile profile to raw Pu-erh tea, characteristic organic acids such as succinic acid, lactic acid and 3-phenyllactic acid were formed in anaerobic fermentation, and contributed to sour flavor of PT. This study would promote standardization and optimization for Yunnan tea production.Keywords: Pickled tea, Flavonols, Organic acids, Post-fermentation, Multi-omics, Sensory attributes

Introduction

Owing to its peculiar flavor and potential health benefits, such as antioxidant, antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, anti-hyperglycemic and anti-obesity effects, tea has become one of the most widely consumed beverage with abundant phenolics (Huang et al., 2021; Guo et al., 2021; Yao et al., 2026). Generally, Chinese teas can be classified into five major categories, namely non-fermented tea (i.e. green tea), lightly-fermented tea (i.e. white tea and yellow tea), semi-fermented tea (i.e. oolong tea), fully-fermented tea (i.e. black tea) and post-fermented tea (i.e. dark tea) (Luo et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2018). Yunnan Province has been verified as one of original centers of tea plants (Sun et al., 2024), and is suitable for phylogenetic development of various tea trees, with the second most tea annual output in China. Particularly, Chinese Assam tea (Camellia sinensis var. assamica), a distinctive tea resource in Yunnan Province, can be processed into green tea, white tea (WT), black tea (BT), ripened Pu-erh tea (RIT), raw Pu-erh tea (RAT) and pickled tea (PT) through distinct techniques. Similar to Pu-erh teas, PT also belongs to post-fermented tea due to its anaerobic fermentation after fixation (de-enzyming), which is mainly produced by ethnic groups such as the De'ang, Bulang and Jingpo in southwestern Yunnan region (Hou et al., 2023; Mao et al., 2024). As the critical process for PT production, the anaerobic fermentation primarily driven by lactic acid bacteria (LAB) and yeast, shapes its unique flavor—a mildly sour and refreshing taste with a sweet aftertaste, as well as excellent health benefits (Hou et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2024). Even so, characteristic flavor components of PT and its differences from other Yunnan teas have not been elucidated yet.

Raw materials and processing techniques together form characteristic tea flavor mainly evaluated by various taste attributes (e.g., bitterness, astringency, umami, mellow and sweet aftertaste) and aroma attributes (e.g., floral, fruity, green, woody and stale) (Hua et al., 2024). Catechins, flavonols, anthocyanins, phenolic acids, amino acids, nucleotides, and alkaloids such as caffeine are major non-volatile flavor compounds in tea infusion for bitterness, astringency and umami tastes (Ma et al., 2024; Yao et al., 2026). Additionally, organic acids and phenolic acids such as gallic acid might contribute to the sour note of teas (Ye et al., 2022). As key precursors of volatile flavor compounds such as alcohols, esters, aldehydes, ketones and terpenes, lipids, carotenoids, flavonoids, and amino acids show profound changes during tea processing through non-enzymatic, enzymatic or Maillard reactions, contributing to the formation of typical aroma (Feng et al., 2019).

Apart from environmental factor like altitude, typical processes lead to significant differences in the composition and concentration of intrinsic chemical constituents among various categories of teas (He et al., 2023; Tang et al., 2026). For instance, the fixation reserves abundant phenolic components such as ester and non-ester catechins in green tea due to the complete inhibition and destruction of intracellular enzyme activity, thus resulting in relatively higher in vitro antioxidant capacity and bitterness-astringency intensity (Wang et al., 2018; Zhou et al., 2022). In contrast, enzymatic oxidation (fermentation) and pile-fermentation promote the conversion of non-volatile and volatile metabolites, and the generation of tea pigments in BT and RIT, through intracellular or extracellular enzyme activity and microbial metabolism (Hashiguchi et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2024). Both the pile-fermentation of RIT and the anaerobic fermentation of PT involve extensive participation of various microbes, such as filamentous fungi, yeast and LAB. However, Aspergillus, Thermomyces and Bacillus dominate in the pile-fermentation (Ma et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2022), which are completely different from those in the anaerobic fermentation (Mao et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024).

BT, WT, RIT, RAT and PT are main types in Yunnan tea market. To explore the overall impact of processing techniques on flavor components of Chinese Assam tea, in this study, these five types of Yunnan large-leaf teas were all made of an identical cultivar of fresh tea-leaves harvested from same geographical origin. Besides sensory evaluation and major quality components determination, high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) and ultra high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem triple quadrupole mass spectrometry (UHPLC–QTrap–MS/MS) were performed for the quantitative determination of catechins, flavonols, phenolic acids, caffeine and organic acids. Additionally, widely targeted metabolomics and headspace solid-phase microextraction-gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (HS-SPME-GC–MS) were employed for the semi-quantitative analysis of non-volatile metabolites and volatile compounds. Through integrated multivariate statistical analysis and bio-informatics approaches, this study illustrated the overall impact of processing techniques on flavor components and identified characteristic compounds related to typical processes. These findings provide crucial theoretical foundations for standardization and optimization of Yunnan large-leaf tea production, thereby contributing significantly to the sustainable development of Yunnan tea industry.

Materials and methods

Tea samples preparation and collection

PT, WT, BT, RIT and RAT all were produced at Defeng Tea Industry Co., Ltd. of Dehong Dai and Jingpo Autonomous Prefecture, Yunnan Province, China, and made of consistent fresh tea-leaves, i.e., identical cultivar (Camellia sinensis var. assamica cv. Mengku dayezhong) with one bud and two leaves harvested in spring season of 2024. These five Yunnan large-leaf teas were processed by local existing processes with slight modifications as follows. The WT was made of fresh tea-leaves through two procedures, i.e., withering for 36–48 h at indoor temperature, and sun-drying for 4–8 h under natural condition. The BT was made through withering for 8–12 h at indoor temperature, rolling for 20–30 min, enzymatic oxidation for 4–5 h at 30 °C, and drying for 0.5–1.0 h at 80–100 °C. Both RAT and RIT were produced from sun-dried green tea that had been made of fresh tea-leaves through uniform primary processing, such as spreading out for 6 h, fixation for 6–10 min at 100 °C, rolling for 20–30 min and sun-drying for 4–8 h). The sun-dried green tea was regarded as the RAT without additional processing. The RIT was made of sun-dried green tea through pile-fermentation for about 35–40 days with an initial moisture content about 45% (6–8 times of turnings) and natural aging for 30 days. The PT was made of fresh tea-leaves through spreading out for 6 h, fixation for 6–10 min at 100 °C, rolling for 20–30 min, anaerobic fermentation for about 9 months and sun-drying for 4–8 h. Three biological duplicate samples were collected. After vacuum sealing, these tea samples were maintained at −20 °C for sensory evaluation, quality components and flavor compounds determination.

Chemicals and reagents

Caffeine, catechin (C), epicatechin (EC), epigallocatechin (EGC), gallocatechin (GC), epicatechin-3-gallate (ECG), epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG), catechin-3-gallate (CG), gallocatechin 3-gallate (GCG), gallic acid, ellagic acid, rutin, myricetin, quercetin, kaempferol, luteolin and taxifolin standards (all with a purity ≥98.0%) for HPLC determination were purchased from Must Bio-technology Co., Ltd. (Chengdu, Sichuan, China). The 65 organic acid standards, such as lactic acid, 3,4-dihydroxyphenylacetic acid, glutaric acid and succinic acid (all with a purity ≥99.0%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) for quantitative determination as standards via UHPLC–QTrap–MS/MS. 2-Octanol (GC–MS grade, purity ≥99.5%) and 2-chloro-L-phenylalanine (LC-MS grade, purity ≥98.5%) were purchased from TCI Development Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). LC-MS grades of methanol, formic acid and acetonitrile were purchased from CNW Technologies GmbH (Bielefeld, North Rhine-Westphalia, Germany). The analytical grade of Folin & Ciocalteu's phenol reagent, chloroform, acetic acid, ninhydrin, β-mercaptoethanol, o-phthalaldehyde, ethyl alcohol, sulfuric acid, ethyl acetate, and oxalic acid were obtained from Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). GC–MS grade of C7-C40 n-alkane solution was purchased from ANPEL Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Sensory assessment

According to ‘the Methodology for sensory evaluation of tea’ (GB/T 23776–2018) established by China National Institute of Standardization (CNIS), sensory assessment was conducted by a panel of five tea professionals with extensive experience in tea sensory evaluation. Five key indicators, including appearance, infusion color, aroma, taste, and infused leaf, were assessed professionally. After appearance evaluation, 3 g tea samples were soaked in 150 mL of boiling water twice for 2 min and 5 min each time. About 5 mL of tea infusion was collected at the second brew for taste and aroma evaluations with three times by a hundred-mark system. All evaluations were performed in a standardized sensory laboratory using a double-blind protocol to minimize subjective bias. Their descriptive terms were referenced from ‘Tea vocabulary for sensory evaluation’ (GB/T 14487–2017), ‘Product of geographical indication–Pu-erh tea’ (GB/T 22111–2008), ‘Black tea–part 2: Congou black tea’ (GB/T 13738.2–2017), ‘Compressed white tea’ (GB/T 31751–2015) and ‘Methodology for sensory evaluation of dried De'ang sour tea’ (DB5331/T 39–2023).

Detection of major biochemical components in teas

Water extracts content in five types of Yunnan large-leaf teas was determined by CNIS established method, namely ‘Tea–determination of water extracts content’ (GB/T 8305–2013). The content of tea polyphenols was determined using Folin & Ciocalteu's phenol reagent with gallic acid regression curve in CNIS method ‘Method for determination of tea polyphenols and catechins content in tea’ (GB/T 8313–2018). The content of free amino acid was analyzed in accordance with the CNIS established method ‘Determination of total free amino acids in tea’ (GB/T 8314–2013) using ninhydrin assay with L-glutamic acid regression curve. The total content of soluble sugars was measured by a UV-8000S ultraviolet-visible (UV–Vis) spectrophotometer (Yuanxi Instrument, Shanghai, China) at 620 nm via the anthrone‑sulfuric acid assay (Wang et al., 2021). The total contents of theaflavins, thearubigins and theabrownins were systematically analyzed at 380 nm using the UV–Vis spectrophotometer after being separated by various organic solvents (Wang et al., 2021).

Quantitative determination of catechins, flavonols, phenolic acids and caffeine by HPLC

Tea sample was mixed with 70% (v/v) methanol-water solution for tea extraction at 4 °C for 24 h. After centrifugation at 13,800 ×g (4 °C) for 10 min, the supernatant was collected for HPLC determination. Through filtration with a 0.45 μm nylon filter, the 2 μL of tea extract was injected into an Agilent 1200 series HPLC system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) comprising Poroshell 120 EC-C18 chromatogram column (100 × 4.6 mm, 2.7 μm; Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and a Phenomenex C18 guard column (10 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm; Phenomenex, Torrance, CA, USA) to determine eight catechins (i.e. C, EC, EGC, GC, CG, GCG, EGCG and ECG), four flavonols (i.e. rutin, myricetin, quercetin and kaempferol), two phenolic acids (i.e. gallic acid and ellagic acid), luteolin, taxifolin and caffeine. Solvent A (50 mL/L acetonitrile and 2.61 mL/L ortho-phosphoric acid solution) and solvent B (800 mL/L methanol solution) were prepared for HPLC separation by the gradient elution as follows: 0–16.0 min, 10–45% B; 16.0–22.0 min, 45–65% B; 25.0–25.9 min, 65–100% B; 25.9–29.0 min, 100% B; 29.0–30.0 min, 100–10% B (Mao et al., 2024). The whole flow rate was maintained at 0.8 mL/min, and its column temperature was set at 35 °C. The detection wavelengths were 280 nm (0–16 min) and 360 nm (16–30 min) (Ma et al., 2021). Quantification was conducted by using analytical calibration curves (R2 > 0.990).

Quantitative determination of organic acids by UHPLC–QTrap–MS/MS

Tea sample (about 50 mg) was extracted with 500 μL of 70% (v/v) methanol-water solution. After vortex oscillation for 3 min, the mixture was centrifuged at 13,800 ×g (4 °C) for 10 min to collect the supernatant for organic acids determination by UHPLC–QTrap–MS/MS. ExionLC™ AD liquid chromatography (SCIEX, Framingham, MA, USA) was united to QTRAP® 6500+ tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) (SCIEX, Framingham, MA, USA) equipped with IonDrive Turbo V electrospray ionization (ESI) source for the quantitative determination of organic acids.

UHPLC condition: an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm; Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA) was used for LC separation with a column temperature of 40 °C and an injection volume of 2 μL tea extract. The mobile phase consisted of 0.05% (v/v) formic acid water solution (A) and acetonitrile with 0.05% (v/v) formic acid (B). The gradient elution was applied as follows: 0–8 min, 5% B; 8–9.5 min, 5–95% B; 9.5–9.6 min, 95–5% B; 9.6–12 min, 5% B. The flow rate was set at 0.35 mL/min.

MS condition: Data acquisition was performed at −4500 V/5500 V in negative/positive ion modes with ESI source. QTrap–MS/MS was operated in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode by monitoring the ion pairs. Standard solutions ranging from 0.001 μg/mL to 1 μg/mL, and from 1 μg/mL to 100 μg/mL were prepared for calibration curve establishment, as well as linearity, limit of detection (LOD) and quantification (LOQ) evaluations.

Widely targeted metabolomics of non-volatile metabolites

Tea sample (10 mg) was extracted with 500 μL of extraction solvent (methanol: water, 3:1, pre-chilled to −40 °C, containing 2-chlorophenylalanine as internal standards). After three repetitions of homogenization at 35 Hz for 4 min and sonication in ice-water bath for 5 min, the samples were extracted for 24 h. The liquid supernatant was collected through centrifugation at 13,800 ×g for 15 min at 4 °C (Hou et al., 2023). The resulting supernatant was diluted 10 times after filtration through 0.22 μm microporous membrane.

In this work, 2 μL of tea extract was injected into an ExionLC AD System (Sciex Technologies, Framingham, MA, USA) with an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm; Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA). The 0.1% (v/v) formic acid solution (A) and acetonitrile (B) were as mobile phases for LC separation by the following gradient eluted program: 0–0.5 min, 2% B; 0.5–10.0 min, 2–50% B; 10.0–11.0 min, 50–95% B; 11.0–13.0 min, 95% B; 13.0–13.1 min, 95–2% B; 13.1–15.0 min, 2% B (Hou et al., 2023). The whole flow rate was maintained at 0.4 mL/min with a column temperature of 40 °C.

The QTrap 6500+ system Triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Sciex Technologies, Framingham, MA, USA) equipped with IonDrive Turbo V ESI source was applied for metabolite assay using MRM mode (Mao et al., 2024). The ESI source operation parameters were as follows: ion source, turbo spray; −4500 V/5500 V in negative/positive ion modes; curtain gas, 35 psi; source temperature, 400 °C; ion source gas I, 60 psi; gas II, 60 psi; declustering potential (DP), −100 V/100 V in negative/positive modes (Hou et al., 2023).

HS-SPME-GC–MS of volatile compounds

The tea sample (about 1000 mg) was placed into 20 mL headspace bottle with 10 μL 2-octanol (10 mg/L stock in dH2O) as internal standard for volatile compounds extraction through SPME cycle of PAL rail system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The program was as follows: Incubate temperature, 60 °C; Preheat time, 15 min; Incubate time, 30 min; Desorption time, 4 min. GC–MS analysis was performed by using an Agilent 7890B series gas chromatograph system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) coupled with an Agilent 5977B series mass spectrometer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA).

The GC separation was performed using a DB-Wax capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm, 0.25 μm, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) with a flow rate of carried gas (high-purity helium over 99.999%) at 1.0 mL/min via splitless injection mode. The front inlet purge flow was 3 mL/min. The oven temperature was programmed at 40 °C for 5 min, then increased at 5 °C/min to 245 °C, and kept for 5 min (Ma et al., 2025). The injection, transfer line, ion source and quad temperatures were 250 °C, 250 °C, 230 °C and 150 °C, respectively. The energy was −70 eV in electron impact mode. The MS data were acquired in scan mode with the m/z range of 20–400, solvent delay of 0 min. Chroma TOF 4.3× software of LECO Corporation and National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) database were used for raw peaks exacting, the data baselines filtering and calibration of the baseline, peak alignment, deconvolution analysis, peak identification, integration and spectrum match of the peak area.

Statistical analysis

Triplicate detection of each sample was executed to obtain reliable data. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to evaluate the significant differences of major biochemicals and phenolic compounds at P < 0.05 level. Principal component analysis (PCA) and orthonormal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) of organic acids, non-volatile metabolites and volatile compounds were performed using SIMCA software (v. 16.0.2, Umetrics, Umeå, Sweden). Differential metabolites were selected based on the criteria of P < 0.05, variable importance in projection (VIP) ≥ 1.0, and fold change (FC) ≥ 2.0 or ≤ 0.5. Data visualization, including circular plots, pie charts, and bar graphs, was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.5.0. Bar chart, radar chart, Venn diagrams, volcano plots, and heatmaps were generated using the R package (v. 4.3.3).

Results and discussion

Tea sensory and flavor difference across five types of Yunnan large-leaf teas

Numerous studies using the single Camellia sinensis cultivar have confirmed the leading role of typical processes on flavor formation and chemical variation of various teas, thus further affecting in vitro antioxidant activity and health benefits (Zhou et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2018). Sensory evaluation was conducted to evaluate the influence of processing techniques on tea sensory and flavor (Fig. 1A), and confirmed the significant differences in infusion color, taste, aroma, and appearance among these five tea types (Table 1). Because of complete inactivation of intracellular enzyme activity after the fixation, the RAT was similar to sun-dried green tea in flavor, such as bright yellow-green liquor color, strong bitterness-astringency, and lasting faint scent. Other than the fixation, the prolonged withering along with slight enzymatic oxidation ensured sweetness and mellow taste with lower intensity of bitterness and astringency, as well as obvious floral and fruity aroma of WT. The endogenous enzymes, such as polyphenol oxidase, peroxidase, β-glucosidase and lipoxygenase, catalyze flavor formation of BT in the enzymatic oxidation process ever since the rolling (Hashiguchi et al., 2023). Therefore, strong mellow taste, and the fragrance of floral and sweet/honey formed in the enzymatic oxidation were characteristic flavors of BT.

Different from BT and WT processing mediated by various intracellular enzymes, the PT and RIT processing both involve a diverse microbial community and their released extracellular enzymes under different fermentation conditions. Specifically, the pile-fermentation and the anaerobic fermentation are typical processes of RIT and PT, respectively, for their flavor formation. Generally, the moist-heat effect, microbial activity and extracellular enzyme catalysis jointly form bright deep-red liquor color, thick mellow taste and pure stale aroma of RIT (Li et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2022). However, the deficiency of moist-heat effect in the anaerobic environment prevented color transformation of PT. The PT still retained greenish-brown appearance and full yellow-green liquor color, which was consistent with RAT (Fig. 1A). Differently, the sourness and refreshing taste, and lasting sour aroma were regarded as peculiar flavor of PT, which should be attributed to its anaerobic fermentation process. LAB such as Lactobacillus plantarum, Lactobacillus paracollinoides and Lactobacillus vaccinostercus, and yeast including Candida metapsilosis, Cyberlindnera fabianii, have been identified as dominant microbes in the anaerobic fermentation (Mao et al., 2024). Among 26 isolated strains of LAB from De'ang PT, 24 belong to Lactobacillus plantarum, one belongs to Enterococcus casseliflavus, and one belongs to Lactobacillus acidophilus (Cao et al., 2019). In spite of its simplified processing with natural transformation of multiple non-volatile metabolites and volatile compounds, the WT exhibited superior performance in appearance, taste, aroma and infusion color. Comparatively, post-fermentation processes facilitated the development of distinctive flavor profiles. These findings provide a theoretical foundation for quality control and enhancement in tea production through process optimization.

Major quality components difference and their association with typical processing

Major biochemical components, such as tea polyphenols, free amino acids, soluble sugars, theabrownins, thearubigins and theaflavins, determine tea sensory characteristics (Wang et al., 2018). In this study, these quality components were systematically analyzed to explore their association with typical processing. Overall, these quality components showed significant (P < 0.05) differences among five types of Yunnan large-leaf teas (Fig. 1B). Their moisture contents all were below 10%, in accordance with national requirement. The RAT was observed with the highest content of water extracts, which is consistent with the study of Wang et al. (2018) that non-fermented teas such as green tea contain significantly higher levels of water extracts than post-fermented teas. The enzymatic oxidation, and post-fermentation such as pile-fermentation and anaerobic fermentation, both reduced water extracts content in teas. Comparatively, the BT possessed significantly (P < 0.05) higher content of free amino acids than other types, from the decomposition of proteins in tea processing. Amino acids served as nutrient substance for microbial growth, which significantly (P < 0.05) decreased the total free amino acid contents in the pile-fermentation and anaerobic fermentation. Additionally, the enzymatic oxidation process significantly (P < 0.05) reduced soluble sugar content of BT, for its oxidative condensation and subsequent polymerization with relevant tea pigments.

Theaflavins, thearubigins and theabrownins are derived from oxidative polymerization of catechins in tea processing (Chen et al., 2024; Hashiguchi et al., 2023). Through comparisons, RIT had the highest theabrownins content approximately 13% (w/w), followed by BT with a theabrownins content approximately 3.5% (w/w) and PT with a theabrownins content about 1.5% (w/w). Generally, the intracellular enzyme-mediated oxidation of BT promotes the oxidative polymerization of catechins to formulate theaflavins and thearubigins in sequence (Dong et al., 2021; Hashiguchi et al., 2023). Only part of thearubigins can be further condensed into theabrownins with other bio-active components during BT processing. Differently, moist-heat effect, the microbial activity and their released extracellular enzymes largely facilitated the accumulation of theabrownins in the pile-fermentation of RIT. Several strains such as Aspergillus niger, Aspergillus tubingensis and Aspergillus fumigatus, are conducive to theabrownins formation (Wang et al., 2021). However, due to anaerobic environment and room temperature, anaerobic fermentation prevented the oxidative polymerization of catechins and theaflavins, and further condensation with polysaccharides, proteins, amino acids and nucleic acids to generate theabrownins. Although a representative water-soluble pigment and bioactive macromolecule in dark tea (Huang et al., 2019; Li et al., 2023), the relatively lower accumulation of theabrownins in the anaerobic fermentation ensured bright yellow infusion of PT. The dominated mould such as Aspergillus and thermophilic fungi in the pile-fermentation can not be found in the anaerobic fermentation, which indicated the potential connection between microbial fermentation style and theabrownins generation.

Polyphenolic composition comparison among five types of Yunnan large-leaf teas

As critical chemical constituents in tea, (poly)phenolic compounds including catechins, flavonoids and phenolic acids, constitute approximately 18–36% of the dry weight in fresh tea-leaves and show profound variations during tea processing (Yan et al., 2020). Phenolic compounds detected by HPLC (Fig. S1), confirmed significant (P < 0.05) differences of catechins, flavonols and phenolic acids among WT, BT, PT, RIT and RAT (Table 2). Among these five types of Yunnan teas, the RAT contained highest levels of catechins, particularly ester catechins (i.e. CG, GCG, ECG and EGCG). Despite the prolonged withering with slight natural oxidation, the WT still possessed relatively higher levels of total catechins including ester catechins and non-ester catechins, which was over 10 times that in the BT and the RIT. Compared with the RAT, the WT contained significantly (P < 0.05) higher contents of GCG and CG, but significantly (P < 0.05) lower levels of EGCG and ECG, attributed to the existence of epimerization in the prolonged withering of WT. Confirming with previous studies (Ito & Yanase, 2022; Ma et al., 2024), both enzymatic oxidation and pile-fermentation drastically reduce catechins content over 90% to formulate complex oxidative products such as thearubigins and theabrownins.

Correspondingly, the PT was rich in non-ester catechins including EGC, GC, EC, and C that were significantly (P < 0.05) higher than other four types of Yunnan teas. In consequence, the anaerobic fermentation promoted the hydrolysis of ester catechins into relevant non-ester catechins and gallic acid, rather than their continuous oxidative polymerization. Horie et al. (2019) has revealed that LAB such as Lactobacillus plantarum in anaerobic fermentation releases tannase catalyzing the degradation of EGCG into EGC. Similarly, Japanese Ishizuchi-kurocha and Awa-bancha are also observed with highest level of EGC (Horie et al., 2019; Yamamoto et al., 2019). Compared to other catechins, EC exhibits greater stability in acidic (low pH) environments, which shows high retention in PT. The distribution differences of catechins lead to their different bitterness and astringency intensity (Dong et al., 2021). The rich non-ester catechins and gallic acid contributed to relatively high bitterness and sourness tastes of PT.

Flavonols dominated by rutin, kaempferol, myricetin and quercetin, are the main phenolic components in tea only second to catechins (Ke et al., 2025; Ma et al., 2021). Comparatively, the PT contained highest levels of three non-glycosylated flavonols (i.e. kaempferol, myricetin and quercetin), but relatively lower rutin content than that in RAT, indicating that the anaerobic fermentation promoted rutin into non-glycosylated flavonols through hydrolysis, hydroxylation and dehydroxylation. Concretely, kaempferol, myricetin and quercetin contents in PT reached about 0.52 mg/g, 0.47 mg/g and 1.47 mg/g as characteristic flavonols, respectively, which might endow its excellent health benefits (Barreca et al., 2021). Distinctively, the prolonged withering significantly enhanced rutin content from the glycosylation of quercetin and isoquercitrin, which was completely different from the enzymatic oxidation of BT and the pile-fermentation of RIT with dramatic decreases. Besides the degradation of ester catechins including EGCG and ECG under microbial esterase catalysis (Ge et al., 2019), the C-ring fission of flavonols and flavones also conduced to gallic acid accumulation in RIT with the highest content over 10 mg/g.

Abundant organic acids in the PT from anaerobic fermentation

Organic acids, despite constituting only 3% of dry weight, significantly influence tea quality (Yu et al., 2022). Quantitative analysis of 65 organic acids by UHPLC–QTrap–MS/MS (Fig. S2) were carried out to explore the distribution of organic acids across five types of Yunnan large-leaf teas (Fig. 2). A total of 52 organic acids were identified in these Yunnan teas. The PT contained the highest total content and the largest number of organic acids among these five types of Yunnan large-leaf teas, due to its typical anaerobic fermentation. Concretely, 48, 45, 41, 40 and 38 organic acids were found in PT, RIT, WT, BT and RAT, respectively. Among them, lactic acid and benzenepropanoic acid were specific organic acids only detected in PT with relatively high contents, which might contribute to its distinctive sour note.

PCA and OPLS-DA have been widely applied for tea classification (Ma et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2018). Based on 52 detected organic acids, the first two principal components (PC1 = 42.4% and PC2 = 37.0%) in the PCA (Fig. 2A) with a total variation of 79.4% completely divided these teas into PT, RIT and WBRT (i.e. the cluster of WT, BT and RAT) groups. Through OPLS-DA, 26, 33 and 29 differential (VIP ≥ 1.0, P < 0.05, and FC ≥ 2.0 or ≤ 0.5) organic acids were identified in RIT vs. WBRT, PT vs. RIT, and PT vs. WBRT, respectively (Fig. 2B–D). A Venn diagram (Fig. 2E) revealed 43 total and 10 common differential organic acids across all comparisons. The heatmap (Fig. 2F) exhibited the differential organic acids among five types of Yunnan teas, which should be attributed to microbial activity in pile-fermentation and anaerobic fermentation. Citraconic acid, 2-methylsuccinic acid and 4-hydroxybenzoic acid were regarded as characteristic organic acids in RIT, that were mainly derived from the degradation of amino acids such as valine, leucine and phenylalanine. Furthermore, succinic acid (about 173.0 μg/g), lactic acid (about 79.6 μg/g), 3-phenyllactic acid (about 9.1 μg/g), hydroxyphenyllactic acid (about 2.47 μg/g), indolelactic acid (about 1.23 μg/g) and 2-hydroxyisovaleric acid (about 3.21 μg/g) formed via anaerobic fermentation were characteristic organic acids for PT identification.

As major organic acids in PT, succinic acid and lactic acid were mainly derived from glycolytic pathway, pentose phosphate pathway and reducing tricarboxylic acid cycle in the anaerobic fermentation under the effect of LAB such as Lactobacillus plantarum. The LAB have high acid-producing capacity, lowering the pH value during the anaerobic fermentation (Xiao, Huang, Yang, Zhang, & Quan, 2015). LAB can transform glucose and sucrose into pyruvic acid via glycolytic pathway, which is further transformed into lactic acid by lactate dehydrogenase. Similarly, Lactobacillus plantarum and other functional bacteria in the anaerobic fermentation promoted glucose, fructose and xylose composition into succinic acid. The sensory threshold of succinic acid and lactic acid was about 23 μg/g and 50 μg/g, respectively, that both contributed to the sour flavor of PT. Their dynamic changes and specific formation pathways in anaerobic fermentation deserved further investigation.

Non-volatile metabolites profiles of five types of Yunnan large-leaf teas

A total of 300 non-volatile metabolites were identified by UHPLC–QTrap–MS/MS-based widely targeted metabolomics across five types of Yunnan large-leaf teas (Fig. S3), dominated by flavonoids, terpenoids, phenolics and alkaloids, with 49, 36, 30 and 30 compounds, respectively (Fig. 3A). Consistent with the analysis of organic acids, the PCA of non-volatile metabolites (Fig. 3B) also divided these teas into three groups, namely RIT, PT and WBRT, by the first two principal components (PC1 = 67.3% and PC2 = 10.9%). BT was clustered together with WT and RAT as WBRT group, indicating that the post-fermentation process, including pile-fermentation and anaerobic fermentation, had a more profound impact on non-volatile metabolites than other typical processes, such as the enzymatic oxidation and the withering. Furthermore, the anaerobic fermentation of PT was significantly difference from the pile-fermentation of RIT in non-volatile metabolites, due to their distinct fermentation temperatures, oxygen content, fermentation cycle, dominant microbes, and extracellular enzymes. Therefore, the extensive participation of LAB, yeast or filamentous fungi can significantly alter non-volatile metabolites in tea processing.

Through comparisons with RIT and WBRT, 121 (55 up-regulated and 66 down-regulated) and 167 (56 up-regulated and 111 down-regulated) DNMs were found in PT, respectively. In addition, 83 up-regulated and 18 down-regulated non-volatile metabolites were identified between RIT and WBRT. Due to the post-fermentation process, the most DNMs were identified between PT and RIT, particularly their 44 unique ones. Overall, the post-fermentation totally generated 219 DNMs, such as 38 flavonoids, 26 terpenoids, 22 phenolic acids, and 22 alkaloids (Fig. 3D). The PT also possessed higher levels of non-ester catechins including EC and EGC, but lower levels of ester catechins such as CG, ECG, GCG and EGCG, which was consistent with determination results by HPLC. The accumulation of 2-picolinic acid from the degradation of tryptophan via anaerobic fermentation also enhanced the sour flavor of PT.

Post-fermentation profoundly affected non-volatile metabolites in tea processing

Changes and differences in catechins during tea processing are important indicator for tea classification (Ma et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2018). Most ester catechins were retained in WT and RAT, but showed rapid decreases in BT, PT and RIT through enzymatic oxidation or post-fermentation. Different from their dramatic oxidative polymerization to form thearubigins and theabrownins in the pile-fermentation and enzymatic oxidation (Hu et al., 2021), the anaerobic fermentation mainly transformed ester catechins into non-ester catechins, due to limited oxidative polymerization. Compared with relatively stable flavones, flavonols and flavanones in enzymatic oxidation of BT and other typical processes, the post-fermentation obviously increased flavones (e.g., luteolin, luteolin-6-C-glucoside, diosmetin, scutellarein, and vicenin 2), flavonols (e.g., myricetin, quercetin and narcissoside) and flavanones (e.g., blumeatin, naringenin and taxifolin) in RIT or PT.

Among 22 differential phenolic compounds (Fig. 4B), 16 exhibited higher levels in RIT, which should be attributed to pile-fermentation that has been reported to generate various phenolic acids, such as pyrogallol, catechol, methyl gallate, and protocatechuic acid (Ge et al., 2019; Ma et al., 2021). The absence of oxygen in the anaerobic fermentation limited oxidative reactions, thus leading to relatively lower levels of phenolics in PT. Comparatively, the pile-fermentation facilitated the accumulation of relevant phenolics. The majority of differential alkaloids exhibited higher levels in RIT and PT, due to profound microbial activity in their post-fermentation. Additionally, the enzymatic oxidation also enhanced alkaloids level in BT than those in WT and RAT, which was consistent with the report of Wang et al. (2022). Despite the stability of caffeine during tea processing, such as fixation, withering and drying, the microbe-mediated post-fermentation could notably enhance the contents of purine alkaloids, such as theophylline, xanthine, hypoxanthine and guanine.

Nucleotides and amino acids are major umami substances in tea and show significant variation during tea processing (Hua et al., 2024). Generally, compared with BT or RIT, green tea and RAT contain relatively lower contents of xanthosine, uridine, guanosine, cytidine, and thymidine (Wang et al., 2022). The condensation of ribose or deoxyribose with pyrimidine or purine alkaloids, along with enzymatic oxidation and post-fermentation generated 13 differential nucleotides with higher levels in BT, RIT or PT (Fig. 4C). Different from pile-fermentation, which is accompanied by a dramatic decrease of amino acids, the enzymatic oxidation and the anaerobic fermentation both contributed to accumulation of several differential amino acids in BT and PT, respectively. L-norleucine, L-isoleucine, 5-aminovaleric acid and pyrrole-2-carboxylic acid were regarded as characteristic amino acids in the PT. Additionally, the pile-fermentation and the anaerobic fermentation also profoundly affected lipids and saccharides metabolism (Fig. 4D). Spearman correlation analysis further confirmed potential associations between differential compounds and sensory attributes (Fig. 4E). Notably, several catechins (e.g., EGC and EC), theaflavin and salicylic acid showed significant (P < 0.05) correlations with taste, aroma and infusion color in Yunnan large-leaf teas.

Volatile compounds difference caused by typical processing

Aroma-active compounds are mainly derived from carotenoids, glycosides, lipids, amino acids, phenolics and carbohydrates through non-enzymatic degradation, enzymatic reactions or Maillard reactions in tea processing (An et al., 2023). The withering, enzymatic oxidation, pile-fermentation, fixation and sun-drying as critical processes of WT, BT, RIT and RAT, formed their characteristic volatile profiles (Xu et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2022). To investigate the impact of typical processing, particularly anaerobic fermentation, on volatile compounds, combined with HS-SPME, GC–MS approach was carried out for volatile compounds determination. A total of 696 volatile compounds were identified across five types of Yunnan large-leaf teas (Fig. S4). PCA (Fig. 5A) divided these tea samples into four distinct groups by the first two principal components (PC1 = 35.2% and PC2 = 16.4%). The PT and RAT were clustered together as the RPT group, which were distinguished from RIT, WT and BT in volatile profiles. Compared with the significant influence of withering, enzymatic oxidation and pile-fermentation that caused obvious differences in volatile profiles among WT, BT, RIT and RPT, the impact of anaerobic fermentation on volatile compounds was relatively limited. Therefore, PT showed a similar volatile profile to RAT.

Volatile compounds difference among five types of Yunnan large-leaf tea. PCA of volatile compounds across the five types of tea. (B) Distribution of differential volatile compounds in different comparison groups. (C) Heat map showing the levels of 38 mutual differential volatile compounds across five tea types.

Venn diagram (Fig. 5B) showed that, 335 differential (P < 0.05, VIP ≥ 1.0, and FC ≥ 2.0 or ≤ 0.5) volatile compounds were totally identified in the comparisons of WT vs RPT, BT vs RPT and RIT vs RPT. Among them, these typical processes generated 38 common differential volatile compounds in Yunnan large-leaf teas. These volatile compounds were basically divided into two modules through hierarchical clustering analysis (Fig. 5C), which should be attributed to strong oxidative pathways in enzymatic oxidation, pile-fermentation or prolonged withering. Despite its profound impact on catechins, flavonols, organic acids and other non-volatile metabolites through hydrolysis, decomposition, hydroxylation and O-methylation, the anaerobic fermentation did not completely transform the volatile profile of the raw material. The PT and RAT retained higher contents of butanoic acid, 3-methyl-butanoic acid, 2-isopentyl-1,3-cyclopentanedione, and (−)-benihinal. Furthermore, 28 volatile compounds predominantly accumulated in WT, BT and RIT. In accordance with previous studies (Aaqil et al., 2024; Sasaki et al., 2017), benzaldehyde, 2 − ethyl−6methyl-pyrazine, methy-pyrazine and 3-acetyl-methylpyrrole were dominant volatile compounds in BT for its sweet and roasty aroma. Combined with original volatile components, the characteristic organic acids (e.g., lactic acid, succinic acid, phenylpropionic acid and indolelactic acid) formed in the anaerobic fermentation, jointly constructed the sour flavor of PT, which warrants further investigation. Acetic acid, acetoin, 4-ethylphenol, 2-furanmethanol, and phenylethyl alcohol with higher relative abundances also participated in the formation of the unique flavor of PT.

Conclusion

In this study, five Yunnan large-leaf teas were made from an identical cultivar fresh tea-leaves of Chinese Assam tea to investigate the overall impact of processing techniques on flavor components. The typical processes formed unique flavors and generated differential flavor components among these five types of Yunnan large-leaf teas. For instance, the anaerobic fermentation ensured peculiar sourness taste and sour aroma of PT, which were completely different from other four types. The enzymatic oxidation and the pile-fermentation enriched thearubigins and theabrownins in BT and RIT, respectively, through the oxidative polymerization of catechins. Different from catechins retention and epimerization in RAT and WT processing, the PT contained the highest levels of non-ester catechins and simple flavonols (e.g., kaempferol, quercetin and myricetin) from hydrolysis of ester catechins and flavonol glycosides (e.g., rutin) in the anaerobic fermentation. PCA of organic acids and non-volatile metabolites both identified three groups, i.e., PT, RIT and WBRT, confirming more profound impacts of pile-fermentation and anaerobic fermentation on taste compounds than other processing methods. However, PT and RAT were clustered together as RPT group with significant differences from other Yunnan teas in volatile profiles.

PT contained the most abundant organic acids. A total of 43 differential organic acids were identified in Yunnan teas, from degradation and decomposition of amino acids and sugars during the post-fermentation process. Succinic acid, lactic acid, 3-phenyllactic acid, hydroxyphenyllactic acid, indolelactic acid and 2-hydroxyisovaleric acid were characteristic organic acids for PT identification. With the synergy of original volatile compounds, the accumulation of characteristic organic acids such as lactic acid and succinic acid in the anaerobic fermentation, contributed to the formation of sour flavor of PT. The anaerobic fermentation also produced several characteristic flavonoids, alkaloids, nucleotides, amino acids and volatile compounds. This study, elucidating differential flavor compounds in Yunnan large-leaf tea caused by typical processing techniques, provides theoretical knowledge for the optimization of Yunnan tea production, particularly the flavor formation of PT.
