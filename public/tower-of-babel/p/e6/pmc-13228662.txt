Perspective: Genicular artery embolization for knee osteoarthritis - When the hype doesn’t match the evidence
Authors: Tijmen A van Zadelhoff, Rianne A van der Heijden, Sita MA Bierma-Zeinstra, P Koen Bos, Edwin HG Oei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper summarizes the current evidence on genicular artery embolization (GAE) for the treatment of knee osteoarthritis (OA). The goal of this minimally invasive treatment is to reduce synovitis by embolizing peri‑genicular neovascularization and subsequently alleviate pain. Multiple uncontrolled case series have generally demonstrated a promising efficacy of GAE with substantial, lasting pain reduction and improved function. However, these studies lack control groups, making it difficult to distinguish between treatment effect and placebo effect. Three sham-controlled randomized clinical trials (RCTs) have been published to date, with varying methodologies. While the first reported positive results, the most recent two RCTs reported no significant effect of GAE compared to a sham treatment. Moreover, systematic reviews have generally supported the efficacy of GAE but did not include the two most recent RCTs. Given the lack of robust evidence from updated systematic reviews including the most recent published studies, the growing clinical adoption of GAE is not justified and, in our opinion, premature.Keywords: Osteoarthritis, Knee, Embolization, Genicular artery

Introduction

Knee osteoarthritis OA is a common disease with pain as the most prominent symptom. OA in younger patient groups or at earlier stages is typically managed with conservative treatment strategies, including lifestyle changes, physiotherapy, pain medication, and intra-articular injections. In advanced-stage OA, treatment typically consists of joint replacement surgery. However, a substantial group of OA patients do not respond to conservative treatment, yet they are not eligible for joint replacement, for instance due to age considerations and comorbidities. For this patient group, a suitable therapy is lacking.

Neovascularization in and around the joint related to inflammation has recently been proposed as a new treatment target for knee OA [1]. This has led to the hypothesis that, if neovascularization could be decreased, this would lead to pain reduction. One way to diminish increased synovial perfusion and excess blood vessels is by transarterial embolization, i.e. the blocking of vessels using temporary or permanent embolic agents administered through microcatheters inserted via the groin (Fig. 1). Embolization has been around for decades in the field of interventional radiology to treat various disorders such as acute bleedings and benign and malignant tumors. Yet, the application of transarterial embolization to treat inflammatory musculoskeletal disorders was not reported until 2015 when Okuno et al. published a first report on the application of transarterial embolization in patients with knee osteoarthritis [2]. This report introduced the concept of genicular artery embolization (GAE) to treat knee OA, by embolizing neovessels around the knee with imipenem/cilastatin sodium as temporary embolic agent. Since then, there has been a rapidly increasing interest in GAE across the world and in various clinical communities including particularly the field of interventional radiology. This interest not only applies to evaluating the efficacy and risks of GAE in research settings, but, based on early scientific reports, also involves the implementation of GAE in regular patient care, with initiatives towards reimbursement by health care insurers being taken in several countries.

In this Perspective paper, we review the current evidence on GAE for the treatment of knee OA, with an emphasis on the few randomized controlled clinicals (RCTs) that have been published to date. We also discuss the published systematic reviews that have attempted to synthesize the evidence on the efficacy of GAE for knee OA.

Clinical case series

Since the initial reports on GAE for treatment of knee OA by Okuno et al., many clinical studies have been published on this therapy by various groups around the world [[3], [4], [5]]. Virtually all of these clinical studies are limited by the fact that they do not compare the treated group of knee OA patients against an untreated control group [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]]. Hence, these studies should be characterized as uncontrolled, observational, case series, in which the placebo effect is not, and cannot be, taken into consideration.

This Perspective paper is not comprehensive in reviewing all published clinical case series, but a common trend is noticeable across most papers reporting on this category of studies [[3], [4], [5],7,8]. Almost all studies reported a marked reduction in pain following GAE, with visual analogue scale (VAS) or total Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) score reduction ranging from 20.9 to 53.6 and 18 to 32 points decrease, respectively [3,5,[7], [8], [9], [10]]. These studies comprise patient groups from Japan, the USA, UK and China. Pain reduction was recorded for as long as 4 years after treatment. The embolization material used in these studies was either temporary (e.g. Imipenem/cilastatin) or permanent (e.g. spherical particles or polyvinyl alcohol particles). Commonly reported adverse events were transient peri‑genicular skin discoloration, post-procedural knee pain and groin hematomas. However, in none of these studies clinical results were tested against a control group. Therefore, despite the positive results concerning efficacy across all these observational studies, it must be noted that they are suboptimal in terms or study design, and caution must be taken when interpreting the results of such studies. Particularly, we have learned from previous sham-controlled studies in the surgical literature that effects measured in cohort studies and controlled studies with non-operative controls can be partially, and sometimes entirely, explained by the placebo effect of the intervention [11].

Randomized controlled clinical trials

Three RCTs on GAE have been published to date [[12], [13], [14]] (Table 1). In all RCTs a sham procedure was performed in the control group in order to mitigate the placebo effect, but the level of invasiveness of the sham procedure differed across the RCTs.

Published RCTs on GAE for knee OA.

The first RCT published by Bagla et al. was a relatively small RCT, conducted between June 2018 and May 2019, and was designed as a multicenter, single-blinded study in the United States [12]. 21 patients aged above 40 years with Kellgren and Lawrence (KL) grade 1 to 3 radiographic knee OA and a VAS pain score above 50/100, refractory to 3 months of conservative therapies (medication, physical therapy, or intra-articular injection) were included. They were randomized to undergo either GAE or a sham procedure in a 2:1 ratio, but as early as one month after the single blind period, all seven patients in the sham study arm crossed over to undergo GAE treatment. GAE was performed using 100–300-micron absorbable particles (OptiSphere; Teleflex, Minneapolis, Minnesota). Each genicular artery perfusing the knee region where pain was reported most severe, was embolized. The sham procedure involved digital subtraction angiography (DSA) to evaluate the origins and courses of the genicular arteries, as well as superselective DSA of the genicular arteries. The authors concluded that in patients with mild to moderate knee OA, GAE resulted in symptomatic improvement greater than the sham procedure with clinically significant reduction in pain and disability. In the follow-up and analyses, non-responders were excluded. This was accounted for with sensitivity analyses. The authors discuss that therefore the results should be interpreted as what can be expected for responders only and not for the entire population undergoing GAE. Moreover, the fact that at one month follow-up VAS pain did not change (Baseline: 78.9; 1 month: 78.4) in the sham group is worth mentioning because it has been demonstrated that novel, invasive procedures in OA are typically associated with a clinically relevant and lasting placebo effect [15].

The second published RCT published by Landers et al. was conducted in Australia between July 2017 and November 2019 [13]. This was a triple-blinded RCT of patients aged 18–75 years with radiographic OA severity of KL grade 2. Patients had moderate to severe unilateral knee pain resistant to at least six months of conservative treatment. 59 patients were randomized evenly between GAE (n = 29) and sham (n = 30). GAE was performed under light sedation using imipenem and cilastatin sodium (IPM-CS, Primaxin; Merck & Co, USA) as the embolic agent. Importantly, in the first four patients, only one genicular artery, the primary contributor to neovascularization, was embolized, while in the remaining 25 participants all genicular arteries with collateral regional supply were sequentially assessed and embolized if neovessels were visible and accessible. The sham procedure involved the same light sedation, local anaesthetic injection and a small incision in the groin, as in the GAE group. The interventionalist pretended to complete the same procedure as in the intervention group. Pre-recorded video images of an angiogram and embolization procedure were displayed on monitors that the participant could see. The sham procedure lasted 30 to 60 min to match the duration of the real procedure. In the overall analysis, the authors concluded that both in the GAE and the sham group, subjects improved substantially in pain and function at 12 months follow-up.

The most recently published RCT by Van Zadelhoff et al. involved a double-blinded sham-controlled clinical trial conducted in the Netherlands [14]. 58 patients were enrolled between June 2019 and December 2021 and were randomized evenly (29 patients in each group) between GAE and the sham procedure. In the GAE group, eight genicular arteries were systematically depicted with selective DSA, and embolization was performed of those arteries that demonstrated a hyperemic blush indicating angiogenesis. As the embolic agent, permanent 75/100 µm microspheres (Embozene, Varian, Palo Alto, CA, USA) were used. Patients in the sham group underwent a simulated GAE procedure comparable to the study by Landers et al., but without sedation. Unlike the other RCTs, in this study the success of blinding of the participants was confirmed by a formal analysis using the James Blinding Index. The results of this RCT indicated that one and four months after treatment or sham procedure, there was a similar level of pain reduction observed in both groups, indicating that a substantial placebo effect plays a role in the symptom reduction after GAE.

Apart from these published RCTs, at least one more sham-controlled RCT [16] and other RCTs comparing GAE against alternative treatments (corticosteroid injections and genicular neurolysis) [17,18] and evaluating different embolic materials [19] are currently still on-going.

Systematic reviews

Several systematic reviews have been published on the efficacy of GAE for knee OA. While all systematic reviews included the RCT by Bagla et al., none have yet included the most recent RCTs by Landers et al. and Van Zadelhoff et al. Generally, all systematic reviews concluded that GAE is an effective, safe and durable treatment for knee OA. However, many systematic reviews also highlighted the need for more RCTs.

At least two systematic reviews included a meta-analysis of pooled data from multiple studies. Both these reviews, however, did not yet include the most recent RCTs by Landers et al. and van Zadelhoff et al., whereas they included the RCT by Bagla et al. Epelboym et al. pooled 6 studies conducted between 2017 and 2022 and reported an overall estimated VAS reduction of 40 points (95 % CI: 29.9–51.0) 6 months after GAE. After 12 month the reduction was 37 points (95 % CI: 18.0 − 55.0), which was calculated from five studies [20]. Taslakian et al. pooled six studies and calculated a weighted mean difference of 39 points (95 % CI: 30−48) reduction for knee pain VAS score six months after GAE. After 12 months this was 36 (95 % CI: 22−51), also based on five studies [21]. These meta-analyses concluded that GAE is an effective, safe and durable treatment for knee OA but also that data from sham controlled trials is lacking.

A recent systematic review and cost analysis from Spain concluded that the effectiveness of GAE over standard treatment remains unclear, but that it costs an additional 3432.37 Euros per patient. For it to be cost-effective in Spain, GAE would have to add 0.137 quality-adjusted life years per patient [22].

Outlook

With the amount of attention GAE for the treatment of knee OA is getting across the world and in various scientific and clinical communities, we deem it fair to state that this new treatment is currently a hype. In our opinion, critical appraisal of the current evidence is particularly essential when a hype exists around novel medical developments. We believe we have identified cardinal gaps in the body of literature that is considered to represent the highest level of evidence – systematic reviews – as they have thus far not included recent high-quality RCTs that suggest a marked placebo effect associated with GAE. Therefore, we believe that an updated systematic review including these RCTs should be performed to offer a more accurate synthesis on the efficacy of GAE for knee OA. We also recommend that future research on GAE is directed towards studying subgroups that may be more amenable to GAE in line with its proposed working mechanism. With this we mean specific patients with symptoms attributable to overt synovitis, possibly confirmed by MRI or ultrasound, instead of the more general population with OA included in the previous RCTs, which may be a too heterogeneous population to demonstrate a real beneficial effect of GAE. Whether future research should focus on different embolic agents, especially permanent versus temporary, is questionable because both types of embolic materials were applied in the recent RCTs, with similar results.

In conclusion, we believe that this perspective paper has shown that a more balanced view towards GAE for the treatment of knee OA is warranted. In our opinion, the current trend towards implementation of GAE in routine clinical care is not yet supported by the current body of literature, and should be suspended until systematic reviews and meta analyses including the most recent, well designed studies have been performed.
