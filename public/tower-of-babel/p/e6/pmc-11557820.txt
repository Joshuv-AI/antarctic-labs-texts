Does Concomitant Use of Methotrexate with JAK Inhibition Confer Benefit for Cardiovascular Outcomes? A Commentary
Authors: Peter C Taylor, Alejandro Balsa, Anne-Barbara Mongey, Mária Filková, Myriam Chebbah, Solenn Le Clanche, Linda A W Verhagen, Torsten Witte, Daniela Opris-Belinski, Hubert Marotte, Jérôme Avouac
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis commentary explores the potential cardiovascular (CV) benefits of combining methotrexate (MTX) and Janus kinase inhibitors (JAKis) in the treatment of rheumatoid arthritis (RA). While European guidelines recommend MTX as first-line treatment, concerns about the CV risks associated with JAKis have emerged. This article reviews the existing literature to assess the role of concomitant MTX in reducing CV risk when used with JAKis. Clinical trials confirm the efficacy of JAKis in combination with MTX in terms of treatment outcomes in RA. However, the number of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) reported is too low to draw conclusions on adverse CV outcomes. Indirect evidence does, however, suggest potential protective effects of MTX on CV outcomes, as several mechanisms may contribute to MTX’s cardioprotective effects, including reduced inflammation, adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK) activation, increased cholesterol efflux, and adenosine accumulation. These mechanisms and the available data may support the case for CV benefits of concomitant MTX when JAKis are used in the treatment of patients with RA, although further research is needed. In particular, the lipid paradox associated with RA highlights the complex relationship between RA treatments (MTX, JAKis, tumor necrosis factor (TNF) inhibitors, and interleukin (IL)-6 receptor inhibitors), inflammation, different lipid profiles, and CV risk. In the absence of contraindications and when MTX is tolerated, this commentary suggests the concomitant use of MTX and JAKis as a preferred option for optimizing CV protection in patients with RA.Keywords: Rheumatoid arthritis, Combination therapy, Methotrexate, Janus kinase inhibitors, Cardiovascular

Key Summary Points

European guidelines recommend methotrexate (MTX) as the first-line treatment, but concerns about cardiovascular (CV) risks with Janus kinase inhibitors (JAKis) have emerged.Clinical trials confirm the efficacy of combining JAKis or biologics with MTX for rheumatoid arthritis (RA) treatment outcomes, but the low number of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) prevents definitive conclusions on CV risks.Indirect evidence suggests MTX may have cardioprotective effects through mechanisms like reduced inflammation and increased cholesterol efflux.Despite the lipid paradox in RA, the commentary advocates for the concomitant use of MTX and JAKis to optimize CV protection in patients with RA, pending further research.

Introduction

In recent years, an expansion of highly efficacious targeted therapies for rheumatoid arthritis (RA) has been seen, both small molecules and biologics [1]. The updated 2022 European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) guidelines for the management of RA still recommend methotrexate (MTX) as first-line therapy [2]. However, reservations have been expressed about Janus kinase inhibitors (JAKis), which are among treatment options for use as second, or subsequent, line therapy, since age and risk factors for cardiovascular (CV) events [3], venous thromboembolism (VTE), and malignancies must be taken into account before any treatment initiation (in line with the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Medical Agency (EMA) warnings) [4].

Current data show that inflammation in untreated active RA is a major contributor to increased CV risk. Data from previous studies provide indirect evidence that the use of MTX in RA populations may have cardioprotective benefits [5–8]. A cohort study [9] published in 2002 (and undertaken prior to the widespread use of advanced therapies) including 1240 patients with RA showed that most of the patients treated with MTX had a lower hazard ratio for CV mortality than patients treated with other conventional synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (csDMARDs).

Encouraged by such observations, the CV Inflammation Reduction Trial (CIRT) [10] set out to investigate possible cardioprotective effects of low-dose MTX in patients with a history of myocardial infarction or multivessel coronary disease and type 2 diabetes or metabolic syndrome. The trial did not demonstrate CV protection: this may be explained by the fact that the patients included did not have systemic inflammation with elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) [11]. On the other hand, the Canakinumab Anti-Inflammatory Thrombosis Outcomes Study (CANTOS) [12] evaluating canakinumab treatment in patients with myocardial infarction at least 1 month prior to study entry and elevated high-sensitivity CRP (hsCRP) showed that reduction of inflammation without affecting lipid levels reduced the number of second CV events.

Considering the inflammatory nature of RA, it is reasonable to hypothesize that other therapeutic interventions targeting systemic inflammatory responses could also positively impact CV disease (CVD) incidence in this population. However, recent concerns have emerged regarding potential CV risks associated with the use of JAKis. The ORAL Surveillance study compared tofacitinib with tumor necrosis factor (TNF) inhibitors in older patients with RA with CV risk factors and reported an increased relative risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) and VTE events with tofacitinib [4, 13, 14]. In contrast, in a similar disease population, the ENTRACTE trial did not show an increase in CV risk for tocilizumab compared to etanercept (a TNF inhibitor) [15] although the reported rates of MACE were higher in both treatment arms than those observed in the ORAL Surveillance study. Furthermore, although TNF inhibitors and JAKis increase low-density lipoprotein (LDL) levels and interfere with interleukin (IL)-6 signaling, their CV risk profile seems to be different. It is essential to emphasize that per se drugs do not reduce the risk of CVD; their effect is only present when these medications decrease disease activity [16].

The objective of this commentary article is to review the published literature to identify evidence that would argue for the potential role of MTX in reducing CV risk in patients with RA when used in combination with JAKi.

Relative Risk Differences Between DMARDs for CV Morbidity

CVD remains the leading cause of death in RA, contributing to approximately 40% of excess mortality [17], with a 50% higher mortality rate compared to the general population [18]. As such, reducing CV events is a critical consideration in RA treatment decisions for practitioners. A prospective cohort study (FORWARD study) [19] compared the effects of biological DMARDs (bDMARDs) and tofacitinib on incident CVD in patients with RA. The study revealed that TNF inhibitors and abatacept (ABA) were associated with a reduced risk of CVD compared to csDMARDs including MTX. In contrast, tofacitinib was not associated with any CVD risk alteration compared to csDMARDs. Additionally, high-dose glucocorticoid (GC) exposure was linked to an increased CVD risk. Disease activity, as measured by the patient activity scale (PAS), showed that high/moderate disease activity was associated with an 18% increase in CVD risk compared to remission/low disease activity.

CV Outcomes of MTX, JAKis, and bDMARDs When Used in Monotherapy Versus Combination Therapy in Patients with RA: Clinical Evidence

Several clinical trials have assessed the efficacy and safety of JAKis either alone or in combination with MTX. For instance, the phase 3b/4 ORAL Strategy trial [20] included 1146 patients across 194 centers in 25 countries, comparing tofacitinib monotherapy, tofacitinib with MTX, and adalimumab with MTX. In the phase 3 RA-BEGIN trial [21], baricitinib monotherapy, MTX monotherapy, and the combination of baricitinib and MTX were studied in 588 patients. The phase 3 FINCH 3 clinical trial [22] evaluated filgotinib monotherapy, MTX monotherapy, and filgotinib combined with MTX in 1252 patients with active RA. Additionally, within the SELECT phase 3 trial program, upadacitinib was examined in two monotherapy trials (SELECT-MONOTHERAPY [23] and SELECT-EARLY [24]) and one combination therapy trial (SELECT-COMPARE) [25], involving patients with an inadequate response to MTX.

All these trials confirm the efficacy of the JAKis combined with MTX in terms of American College of Rheumatology (ACR) responses as well as low disease activity and remission outcomes. However, whether in monotherapy or combination therapy, the number of MACE reported is too low to draw any conclusion regarding differences in adverse CV outcomes between JAKi used as monotherapy or in combination with MTX.

However, the indirect data cannot be ignored concerning the protective effect of MTX on CV outcomes, and the proposed MTX mechanisms of action which would suggest that there may be CV safety advantages in the use of JAKi in combination with MTX over monotherapy with JAKi when there is no contraindication to MTX.

When MTX is used in combination with bDMARDs, emerging evidence suggests that this regimen may reduce CV risk more effectively than bDMARDs alone. A large retrospective cohort study [26] using Medicare claims data found that patients with RA on biologics who also used MTX had a 24% lower risk of CV events compared to those not using MTX. The adjusted hazard ratio for CV events with MTX use was 0.76 (95% CI 0.68–0.85), with significant reductions observed for myocardial infarction, stroke, and a composite CV outcome. Further exploration of this relationship could be pivotal in optimizing treatment strategies to minimize CV risk in patients with RA.

Pathways Investigated for CV Outcomes of RA Therapies

Lipid Profile

The RA-associated lipid paradox highlights the intricate association between inflammation, lipid profiles, and CV risk in patients with RA. The inverse relationship between CV risk and lipid levels, which contradicts the conventional view, is known as the lipid paradox [5, 27, 28] and the mechanisms by which the inflammatory process can lead to these lipid changes are not fully understood [5].

In the presence of systemic inflammation, such as in untreated RA, an inverse association is observed between cholesterol levels and CV risks. This paradoxical effect might be partially due to the transformation of high-density lipoprotein (HDL) particles from their usual anti-inflammatory and protective state in the absence of acute or chronic inflammation into a pro-inflammatory and non-protective form, thereby increasing CV risks [28–30]. Inhibition of RA-related inflammatory events coincides with certain increases in lipid concentrations, along with improvements in CV events [5]. While investigations provide some evidence regarding the role of csDMARDs and bDMARDs in this context, further clinical research is needed [27]. Several antirheumatic drugs, including MTX, TNF inhibitors, and IL-6 receptor inhibitors, have demonstrated significant improvements in lipid profiles and the mitigation of CV risk factors [6, 31–34]. Adalimumab therapy for 16 weeks led to significant decreases in macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF) levels (p = 0.020), which were sustained up to week 52 (p = 0.012) associated with an improved apolipoprotein B/A-I ratio as well as reduced CRP levels, suggesting that sustained downregulation of MIF might contribute to reduce vascular inflammation in association with anti-TNF [35]. Regarding anti-IL-6 therapy, mixed effects on lipid profiles have been described. An increase in LDL-C levels by around 15–20% has been reported by some studies [27, 34]. But other data observed that tocilizumab significantly increased adiponectin levels from baseline to month 3, peaking at month 1 (p < 0.0001) and month 3 (p = 0.0105), but decreasing progressively from month 6 to 12 and then going back to baseline levels [31]. MTX has been associated with a 21% reduction in CV events [36], with additional atheroprotective effects such as facilitating reverse cholesterol transport and reducing foam cell formation in macrophages [37]. However, some studies have reported no changes in lipid profiles with MTX [32, 38], while others have indicated improvements, particularly in HDL function profiles, either with MTX monotherapy or combination therapy (reduction in the cholesterol incidence (odds ratio 0.517; 95% CI 0.33–0.80) and lower HDL by 11.78% (p = 0.02)) [6, 33]. TNF inhibitors have been shown to increase triglyceride (TG) levels and improve the apolipoprotein B/A ratio during long-term treatment [27, 39]. However, certain studies have suggested a neutral impact of infliximab on lipid patterns, with no significant alterations in LDL-Cholesterol (C), Total Cholesterol (TC)/HDL-C, or TG/HDL-C levels during treatment [39–41]. In all cases, it has been suggested that TNF inhibitors affect the levels of TC and HDL-C without exerting any effect on the atherogenic index in patients with RA and reduce inflammation [34]. Moreover, when used in combination with MTX or other DMARDs, they have been linked to regulation of the lipoprotein spectrum and potential improvement in CV risk among patients with RA [42]. Some data also indicate that patients with RA treated with TNF inhibitors have lower levels of cholesterol, phospholipids, and proteins like Apo B and very low density lipoprotein (VLDL) particles. These changes in VLDL-related parameters align with a reduction in atherogenic risk, as VLDL particles contribute to “remnant cardiovascular risk,” which is not typically assessed through standard lipid tests [43]. JAKis have also been associated with significant increases in LDL and HDL levels in patients with RA [44], as indicated by a systematic review and network meta-analysis (mean increase of 11.37 mg/dl (95% CI 7.84–14.91; I2 = 88%) in LDL levels from baseline) [45]. Moreover, it is important to note that CV risk in the ORAL Surveillance trial is determined by myocardial infarction, for which dyslipidemia is a major risk factor [20, 46]. Further long-term research is needed to fully understand the implications of these lipid level changes in patients with RA and their potential impact on CV risk. In particular, to understand how treatment classes targeting different cytokines and signaling pathways but with similar lipid profiles have different CV risk profiles [34].

Other Mechanisms

There are other mechanisms beyond alterations in lipid profiles by which JAK inhibition might potentially contribute to CV risk, some of which might be countered in part by concomitant use of MTX although this possibility remains speculative. Some preclinical studies indicate that JAK inhibition can negatively impact the phenotype and function of natural killer (NK) cells, potentially influencing the progression of atherosclerosis and plaque stability [47, 48]. Furthermore, inhibition of the JAK2/STAT3 pathway may potentially predispose myocardium to ischemia/reperfusion injury and increase apoptosis of coronary artery endothelial cells [49]. Another recent study reported that JAK inhibition induces significant alterations in microvascular capillaroscopic parameters within 3 months potentially implicating effects on the microvasculature that could adversely influence CV risk [50]. JAKis have also been associated with a dose-dependent risk of VTE contributing to an overall increase in adverse CV outcomes. While the exact mechanisms remain unclear, they may involve impacts on platelet function, coagulation factors, or endothelial cells, thereby raising the risk of thrombosis [51].

Mechanisms of Action Suggested to be Involved in MTX CV Protection

Several mechanisms of actions by which MTX potentially has a cardioprotective effect have been described in the literature (Fig. 1).

Inflammation reduction: MTX has been shown to reduce systemic inflammation by affecting some of the cellular mechanisms (CRP, IL-6, IL-1, TNF, and other cytokines) known to drive the development of atherosclerosis cytokines, either directly or indirectly through the activation of adenosine receptors and/or adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK) [6, 7].

Adenosine accumulation: MTX inhibits adenosine catabolism, leading to its accumulation. Aside from its anti-inflammatory properties, adenosine lowers blood pressure through direct vasodilation, increased nitric oxide (NO) production, and central nervous system-mediated mechanisms [7]. A prospective cohort of 26 patients with RA and 15 patients with psoriatic arthritis before or under MTX treatment showed that MTX restores CD73 expression on Th1.17 cells and may contribute to its anti-inflammatory effect through adenosine production [52]. Furthermore, a recent randomized controlled trial in RA has shown that MTX decreases blood pressure, although the mechanisms involved have not yet been investigated [53].

This range of putative cardioprotective mechanisms may be both interdependent and independent, and argue for the value of combining MTX with JAKi for the cardioprotection of patients with RA.

Recommendations for Daily Clinical Practices in RA

It is recommended practice to use concomitant MTX together with a targeted therapy following inadequate response to MTX alone [2]. However, where MTX is contraindicated or poorly tolerated, biologic IL-6 receptor inhibitors or JAKis are recommended treatment choices. In routine practice, even in the absence of any contraindication or intolerance to MTX, JAKis are often used as a monotherapy option to reduce polypharmacy and for reasons of patient preference. For example, in the JAK-POT real-world observational study which included 7686 patients with RA taking JAKis, one-third were taking JAKis as monotherapy [54]. Given the reported positive impact of MTX on CV morbidity and mortality, the question arises as to whether the preferred use of JAKis should be with concomitant use of MTX, where it is not contraindicated, rather than as a monotherapy. The reported CV benefits that have been documented for MTX, including anti-inflammatory and vasculoprotective properties [5–8], argue for the use of combination therapy by preference.

Conclusion

Our review of the available data indicates that the association of JAKi and MTX is safe and suggests that MTX may have a beneficial cardioprotective effect. Therefore, in the absence of contraindications, and where MTX is tolerated, the use of concomitant MTX with JAKi can be proposed as a preferred option as part of a strategy to optimize CV risk reduction.
