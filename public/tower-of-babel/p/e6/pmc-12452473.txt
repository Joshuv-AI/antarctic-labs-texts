ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAccording to Information Vortex Theory, the spatially distributed wave energy associated with the constituent molecules of an incepting cell interacts with the surrounding space to generate a rotating bioinformation field, forming a vortex. This vortex, characterized as a local maximum of energy density, constitutes both inbound and outbound energy fluxes, corresponding to signal reception and dispersal, respectively. The vortex represents a foundational step in the emergence of life, facilitating both the storage of information and, through successive wave superpositions, the basic processing of information. This mechanism is posited to underpin the self-organizing principles that are essential to life’s origin. This study delineates the sequence of events within the information vortex that are causative to the emergence of plant life, emphasizing the role of a central information processing means, which determines evolutionary steps. An environmental context that resists cytoplasmic motion leads to signals favoring pinocytosis, which progressively intensify within the emerging information vortex while concurrently diminishing the expression of phagocytic wave forms. Furthermore, asexual reproductive events, represented by self-division waveforms, propagate this encoded information across successive generations. To elucidate these mechanisms, system-level modeling incorporating feedback loops and adaptive interventions is developed, illustrating the iterative nature of learning and pattern reinforcement. Introduction

The evolution of plant life

The evolution of plant life on Earth reflects an intricate continuum of biochemical, cellular, and ecological transitions that have unfolded over billions of years, commencing with the advent of life and culminating in the vast diversification of terrestrial flora. The primordial biosphere, established approximately 3.5 to 4.0 billion years ago, was dominated by anaerobic prokaryotic microorganisms inhabiting aquatic environments devoid of molecular oxygen. A pivotal evolutionary step occurred with the emergence of oxygenic photosynthesis in cyanobacteria circa 3.5 billion years ago, wherein water served as the terminal electron donor. The resultant liberation of molecular oxygen into the atmosphere initiated the Great Oxygenation Event (~2.4 Ga), marking a profound redox transformation of Earth’s environment and enabling the subsequent evolution of aerobic respiration and complex cellular architectures [1,2].

Around 2.1 billion years ago, the first eukaryotic cells emerged through a supposedly symbiogenic event in which an ancestral archaeal host engulfed an alpha-proteobacterium, giving rise to mitochondria. A second primary endosymbiotic event, involving the integration of a cyanobacterial lineage, led to the evolution of plastids (chloroplasts) and established a clade of photosynthetic eukaryotes. Within this group, Viridiplantae (notably green algae, including both Chlorophyta and Charophyta) are phylogenetically closest to modern land plants and exhibit molecular and structural precursors critical for terrestrial adaptation [3–5].

Plant life and bio-information fields

The precise mechanisms underlying the emergence of life and the origins of biological information processing remain among the most enigmatic questions in science. Microfossil evidence embedded in banded iron formations, dated to as early as 4.28 billion years ago, is widely regarded as indicative of the Earth’s earliest life forms. Genomic analyses comparing bacterial and archaeal lineages have identified 355 conserved gene families from a dataset of over 286,000 protein clusters, pointing to a theoretical entity – the Last Universal Common Ancestor (LUCA), as the best-characterized model of early life [6]. Nevertheless, a growing consensus suggests that the origin of life predates and is qualitatively distinct from LUCA, and remains largely unresolved. If phenomena such as qualia, memory, and sentience are solely products of classical biochemical interactions, then they should, in principle, be fully explicable through molecular biology [7]. The persistent inability to reduce these phenomena to biomolecular mechanisms suggests that fundamental principles may be missing in mainstream evolutionary biology.

Previous work on information vortices brought the notion of energy-information flow to become central to explaining the emergence of life [8]. The emergence of memory and proto-sentience within the evolutionary trajectory toward LUCA underscores the need to investigate when and how information-processing systems originated in prebiotic conditions. Bioinformation fields incorporating energy, resonance, and information transfer mechanisms offer a compelling theoretical construct for bridging the explanatory gap between abiogenesis and LUCA [7,8]. The current study builds upon this foundation, extending the theoretical framework to the origin and evolution of plant life, thereby addressing the transition from single-cellular to multicellular architecture in the context of prebiotic signal processing and bioinformation fields.

While there has been vast research in electrical and magnetic field interactions with plants [12–14], the interconnections between energy and information management in bio-information fields and plant life evolution events have not yet been explained in systems biology.

Origination of bio-information field with vortices

This section briefly revisits earlier work on information vortex formation [8] and then advances into the discrete events leading to the emergence of plant life.

The Miller-Urey experiment demonstrated that organic molecules, including amino acids, can be synthesized abiotically under conditions that simulate those of early Earth. By subjecting a gaseous mixture of methane, ammonia, hydrogen, and water vapor to electrical discharges, the experiment provided compelling evidence that prebiotic chemistry could generate the fundamental building blocks of life.

Building on this, the information vortex theory proposes that the energy released during such biochemical reactions formed the first energy envelope of an incipient cell. This dissipated energy, primarily in the electromagnetic spectrum, is modulated by the intrinsic energy pulsations of the ubiquitous continuum. Analogous to the Casimir effect, this dissipation is spatially confined by the internal energy of the continuum, resulting in a contained energy envelope. Notably, this dynamic boundary condition induces a rotational motion, giving rise to a vortex structure within the bio-energy envelope of the forming cell [8].

State changes, such as chemical, mechanical, or thermal, within this envelope introduce perturbations in the bio-energy field. These perturbations manifest as waveforms that represent the fundamental informational units of a biological system. Propagating through the field, these signals converge at the vortex, effectively rendering it a primitive signal-storing mechanism in the earliest biological matter [8].

While several studies have described intrinsic bio-energy fields and their multicellular counterparts, they have not addressed how such fields may emanate during the spark of life. The information vortex theory proposed the emanation process and identified it as a critical step in the origin of organized biological information. Furthermore, it introduced the concept that vortices forming within these fields act as localized structures for information storage and processing, thereby providing a potential mechanism that links field dynamics with evolution.

To add, the information vortex theory does not posit that a bulk agglomeration of biomolecules physically rotates. Rather, the vortex refers to a rotational energy density pattern in the bio-information field, an emergent property of the quantum fields of the constituent molecules. This phenomenon is analogous to rotating wave-fields observed in excitable media, and electromagnetic vortices, where the underlying medium is not rotating as a rigid mass [15–17].

It is also important to mention the nature of reality dealt in the information vortex proposition. Analogous to Wheeler’s concept of spacetime foam, space can be understood as a continuum of energy that oscillates at Planck-scale vibrations. Variations in the energy-pressure gradients within this continuum give rise to localized peaks, which fragment into numerous discrete quanta. These quanta, subjected to persistent energy-pressure pulsations, coalesce into clusters that form the so-called fundamental particles. The pressure pulsations of the continuum acting on these particles induce rotational motion, which is characterized by specific degrees of freedom. Owing to the spin velocity, quanta from the outer regions of the formed cluster tend to escape radially. However, this escape is constrained by the internal energy of the continuum, resulting in the formation of a quantum field around the particle [8,18]. The bio-field is one such aggregated field from the fundamental particles, the molecular structures of the incepting cell are made of [8].

Emergence of physiological functions in early life – based on information vortex theory

Among the various theories of early life evolution, the environmental interactions of primitive unicellular organisms, particularly archaea, offer compelling insights into how fundamental biological functions emerged. This section proposes a perspective wherein core cellular processes namely, sensation, motion, nutrition, and reproduction emerged from direct, physical interactions between primitive cells and their environment, processed by the information vortex and the cytoskeletal components, playing a central role in information management and cellular learning.

Sensation likely began with the earliest physical interactions between the cell and its aquatic environment. In a hot spring context, the inceptive cell would have been exposed to varying underwater pressures and gravitational forces, either of planetary origin or from local topological features such as ridges and pits in the seabed. These forces likely provided the mechanical inputs that, once transduced via spiral pathways in the bio-information field, converged within the information vortex, where they persisted as structured energy patterns. This persistence represents an implicate form of storage, which, while not molecular memory, constitutes a substrate of informational potentials that can be coupled to biochemical mechanisms during evolution. Thus, the cell membrane in early cells could have served as the first ‘sensors’ interpreting pressure, vibration, and tension as primitive signals, while the information vortex acting as the information storage and processor.

Locomotion, another hallmark of life, could have originated not from deliberate movement but from passive displacements. Surface irregularities of the seabed, coupled with currents, buoyancy, and gravitational gradients, might have induced cytoplasmic shifts or slips. These accidental movements may have generated internal stress patterns, prompted subsequent motion. Over evolutionary time, such incidental shift signals transduced and stored at a cellular level in the information vortex led to learned motion responses. This kind of emergent motility or locomotion, shaped and refined through continual interaction with the environment, aligns with the principle that behavior in early life was more reactive than volitional.

Nutrition in early cells, in the absence of any specialized intake mechanisms, may have been governed by osmosis across porous, semi-permeable membranes. The thermal and chemical gradients in hydrothermal vents could have facilitated the spontaneous influx of minerals and nutrients. These solutes, once inside, would trigger exothermic or endothermic reactions, altering the internal energy state of the cytoplasm. Such periodic events manifesting as electro-magnetic energy release might have been transduced and stored at cellular level in the information vortex. Over time, this learning loop between nutrient percolation and cytoplasmic storage may have evolved into an early metabolic framework. Here again, the information vortex must have played a vital role in information management, facilitating learning.

Reproduction a defining feature of life could also be traced to mechanical interactions. Motion within the cytoplasm, especially in viscous or semi-solid environments, could have produced internal shear stresses. Under certain thresholds, these stresses may have resulted in local ruptures or pinching, unintentionally dividing the cytoplasm into two or more bodies. External shocks such as slips, micro-earthquakes, or even electrical discharges (e.g., lightning in water) might have further contributed to such divisions. These early cytoplasmic division events, via signal transduction, reaching information vortex, represent the seed of biological reproduction. With repeated occurrences, cells may have developed biochemical pathways to regulate and preemptively initiate division.

This perspective offers a coherent framework for understanding how primitive life might have initiated self-organization and environmental learning. It underscores the role of the information vortex not merely as a memory but also as an active information processing means for the emergence of sensation, locomotion, nutrition, and reproduction. By treating early life functions as emergent properties of physical and chemical interactions with the environment, this view bridges molecular biology with systems theory, proposing that the roots of cell signaling along with environment interaction resulted in the very architecture of physiological functions.

In this work, the term “information” is used in an operational, observer-independent sense, defined as the reduction of uncertainty within a physical system through the formation of stable energy configurations. Within the bioinformation field, these configurations emerge from constructive wave interference and persist as metastable patterns traversing to the vortex. These early informational patterns are treated as objective physical phenomena of the bioinformation field, forming the substrate upon which more complex cognitive phenomena could later emerge.

Origin of plant life – a derivative hypothesis

The Information Vortex Theory posits that during abiogenesis, the spatially distributed wave energy of molecules of an incepting cell interacts with the fabric of reality, generating a rotating biofield and subsequently forming a vortex. The inbound and outbound energy fluxes of this vortex represent signal reception and dispersal, initiating information storage, and with incremental wave superpositions, exhibit information processing, thus establishing memory and sentience in biological systems. In evolution, the first appearance of fundamental biological functions namely sensation, motion, nutrition, and reproduction are described in information vortex theory as signals (perturbations of specific modulation in amplitude and/or frequency generated in bio-information field), induced by environmental interactions, reaching vortex.

Phagocytosis

While in motion, a protist incidentally glides over another, resulting in the entrapment of a decomposable hydrocarbon chain. These events (1) incidental entrapment and (2) the decomposition generate signals that reach the information vortex, eventually forming the memory basis for feeding behaviors.

Pinocytosis

Nutrients diffuse through the porous cell wall owing to thermal-mechanical forces prevailing in the immediate environment, where biochemical reactions release energy and trigger distinct signals. This forms the basic or early waveform for pinocytosis.

In a scenario where in the cytoplasmic matrix motion is physically constrained by its environment, such as being suspended in a highly viscous substrate, movement becomes restricted, leading to a loss of viability for entrapment-based feeding. In this stationary state, owing to the absence of new phagocytosis waveforms being induced, signals and the response waves for phagocytosis diminish significantly in the information vortex. Whereas the transport of molecules via semi-permeable membrane can continue to occur, implying signals for the primitive pinocytosis are increasing in the information vortex.

Additionally, the signals that pertain to cytoplasm-based action of hydrocarbon chains in presence of sunlight getting broken into carbon-di-oxide and water also appear in the information vortex. In the absence of phagocytosis, these signals also increase in occurrence.

Consequently, the information vortex ceases to propagate response signals associated with movement or predatory intent, instead perpetually transmitting signals focused on nutrient absorption and photosynthetic activity.

Simultaneously, cell division proceeds independently, supported by the memory registry already established within the vortex. Over time, this multiplying cell complex, characterized by an information vortex with a high signaling rate for osmosis and photosynthesis, alongside a diminishing capacity for motion and entrapment, gives rise to plant life.

The primitive form of pinocytosis is the transport of molecules via semi-permeable membrane of the cytoplasm. In advanced cells, pinocytosis includes, ligand binding, cytoskeletal member curvature, vesicle formation, scission of vesicle, and ATP utilization, etc. Such sophistications are also dependent on the learning loops of information vortex.

Epigenetic information storage

Early genomes must have encoded information from cytoplasmic or membrane signals; however, they neither directly interact with external prompts nor play a role in sensory signal acquisition. This raises questions about how genomes function as system-level memory.

According to information vortex theory, the quantum field corresponding to the nucleic acid precursor molecules in the prebiotic soup interacts with a signal repository, the vortex of the bio-information field, and undergoes energy transfer. This interaction, involving mechanisms such as constructive interference and superposition between vortex signals and the quantum field of nucleic acid molecules, represents a primitive form of information encoding. This process resulted in the emergence of the first biosystem-level hard memory, the genome.

In terms of plant life, the encoding involved increased rates of pinocytic and decreasing rates of phagocytic waveforms. Thus, the genomic characteristics of plant life are eventually distinctly different from those of other forms.

Also, it is important to distinguish between two forms of storage in this framework. The information vortex, as described here, represents a field of implicate possibilities – a substrate of structured energy patterns that bias the behavior of the incepting cell. However, this field is not “memory” in the molecular sense. True retrievable storage, required for biological function, arises only when such implicate field patterns are coupled to molecular substrates such as nucleic acids, proteins, or membrane configurations. Thus, the vortex serves as a pre-molecular scaffold for informational potential. During evolution, these potentials become progressively mapped onto biochemical carriers, thereby converting field-level implications into explicit, retrievable memory traces within the cell.

In essence, this work argues that the vortex functions as an energy-information repository, where modulations in the wave-field can interact with the local biomolecular precursors via wave superposition.

Conclusion and next steps

This investigation advances a foundational framework wherein Information Vortex Theory is postulated as a unifying paradigm to elucidate the emergent phenomena underpinning the origin of plant life. The study recontextualizes abiogenesis through the lens of wave – matter interactions, wherein spatially distributed quantum energy fields, emerging from molecular constituents, self-organize into localized vortical structures characterized by rotational energy density gradients. These bio-information vortices are conceptualized not merely as passive energy sinks, but as active loci for information encoding, signal superposition, and primitive computation, embodying a dynamic memory substrate essential for early cellular functionality.

While the previous paper [8] focused on general abiogenesis and early information processing, this manuscript introduces a derivative hypothesis specific to plant physiology, integrating system-dynamics modeling, epigenetic encoding concepts, and pinocytosis and photosynthesis driven signal reinforcement. By framing the canonical life-defining processes viz., sensation, locomotion, nutrition, and reproduction, as perturbative interactions within the bio-field continuum, the work transcends the reductionist confines of molecular biology, offering a pre-genomic systems-level interpretation of cellular learning and functional emergence. The preferential emergence of pinocytic over phagocytic waveform responses under physical constraints is particularly significant, marking a transition toward osmotic nutrient uptake and photosynthetic competence, the hallmarks of plant-like physiology. This mechanistic asymmetry, captured within the information vortex, suggests a differential signal reinforcement regime that may have been selected for the immotile, autotrophic cellular states.

The formulation of wave-theoretical constructs governing signal propagation, superposition, and vortex reinforcement, along with a quantum field-based analogy for genome emergence, provides a robust dual-layered model of bioinformation processing. Energy-based equations describing field interactions, vortex dynamics, and signal coherence across both cytoplasmic and genomic domains substantiate a plausible mechanism for the evolution of hard memory systems, independent of nucleic acid templating alone.

Experimental observations have revealed vortex-like patterns in the microtubules, particularly under conditions involving motor protein activity and external fields. These dynamic vortices emerge from the collective organization and movement of tubulin polymers and are thought to contribute to intracellular transport and spatial organization. Recent studies also suggest that such structures may play a role in bioelectrical signaling and quantum-level coherence in neural systems [16].

Spiral waves are well documented in excitable biological media and play a central role in cardiac arrhythmias by sustaining reentrant electrical activity. In the nervous system, such dynamics have been observed in turtle visual cortex and rodent brain slices, indicating their relevance to cortical population activity. These spiral rhythms can entrain ongoing neural activity, and their occurrence is tightly linked to brain state [15,17].

In this work, we employ an objective definition of “information,” consistent with Shannon’s theory, in which information is a measurable property of system states and their transformations, rather than a semantic quantity requiring a subjective observer. Accordingly, the “inbound” and “outbound” energy fluxes described in the information vortex framework are structured, differentiable perturbations in the bio-information field, and by the virtue of their storage and iterative processing within the vortex, they constitute a primitive form of physical signaling. This approach aligns with physical information theories used in molecular biology and dynamical systems, where informational content is defined by state-change potential, not subjective interpretation. These energy-mediated perturbations do not in themselves instantiate consciousness. Rather, they provide a substrate for progressively more complex feedback loops that may, in later evolutionary stages, support proto-subjective states. Thus, the terminology of “signal” is used operationally to denote any structured energy variation that is both registrable and processable within the system, independent of any external observer. Consciousness and subjectivity, while related to information in more evolved systems, are not required for these early-stage vortex dynamics to function as informational substrates.

To add, in this framework, the information vortex does not compute or act as a conscious agent. Instead, it serves as a dynamic energy-density focal region where incoming perturbations (signals) converge, undergo superposition, and generate structured field patterns. These patterns can be reinforced, diminished, or re-emitted as outbound fluxes, thereby creating a primitive feedback mechanism.

This process is analogous to self-organizing physical systems, such as resonant cavities or dissipative structures, where energy interactions yield emergent order without invoking any form of cognition. Thus, the information vortex represents an early substrate for pattern formation and information integration. While this work acknowledges that this is not molecular memory in the strict biochemical sense, it proposes that the vortex can serve as a primitive memory substrate, capable of storing and retrieving energy patterns that modulate cellular or proto-cellular responses.
