ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction with refractory cardiogenic shock due to coronary spasm: a case report
Authors: Stephan Renziehausen, Stephan Stöbe, Christian Spies, Michael Metze, Andreas Giannopoulos, Vincent Ehreiser, Piotr Nikodem Rudzínski, Milenko Zoran Cankovic, Gal Tsaban
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute coronary syndrome (ACS) is primarily due to obstructive coronary artery disease (CAD). Nevertheless, in 1–14% of cases, ACS is present without evidence of obstructive CAD. Coronary artery spasm is an uncommon cause of ACS. Diagnostic work-up includes acute invasive coronary angiography and afterwards provocation testing. The optimal patient management is for patients presenting with cardiogenic shock due to ACS caused by coronary artery spasm is unclear.Case summaryA 67-year-old Caucasian, who underwent elective revision of hip arthroplasty, presented with ST elevations with circulatory collapse, leading to resuscitation due to anaesthesia induction. Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) implantation led to restoration of spontaneous circulation. Coronary angiography revealed coronary vasospasm, which was successfully treated with nitrates i.c. Later, despite of implanted ECMO, recurring haemodynamic deterioration required continuous administration of nitrates i.v., which finally resulted in the stabilization of circulatory system. Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation removal was possible 48 h after implantation and another 12 h later we extubated the patient. Furthermore, we administered calcium antagonists and an intra-cardiac defibrillator was implanted. Finally, the patient was discharged 12 days after admission with no physical or neurological restrictions after resuscitation.DiscussionThis unique case highlights that rare causes of severe ACS with cardiogenic shock need to be considered. Administration of vasodilators, which are not part of the standard care in cardiogenic shock, represents the adequate treatment of a patient with spasm of coronary arteries. Furthermore, the recurrence of acute coronary events must be prevented by drug and device therapy in these patients.Keywords: Vasospastic coronary syndrome, Myocardial infarction, Case report

Introduction

Acute coronary syndrome (ACS) is primarily caused by obstructive coronary artery disease (CAD). However, in 1–14% of cases, ACS occurs without evidence of obstructive CAD. Common causes of acute myocardial infarction without obstructive coronary artery disease (MINOCA) include takotsubo syndrome, myocarditis, coronary embolism, spontaneous coronary artery dissection, coronary spasm, etc.1,2

The prevalence of coronary spasm is unknown, mainly due to the high incidence of under-reporting and coincidence with other forms of ACS. Coronary spasm is characterized by transient reversible vasoconstriction, resulting in varying degrees of myocardial ischaemia.3 Clinical symptoms are indistinguishable from other forms of ACS and range from mild symptoms of stable angina to severe ACS with cardiogenic shock or sudden cardiac death.1,3

In some cases, it is difficult to initially distinguish between the primary cause of myocardial infarction or even cardiogenic shock and possible side effects. Diagnosis of coronary spasm is often made invasively by coronary angiography performed in patients with suspected CAD. After the acute phase, provocative tests, such as acetylcholine, ergonovine or ergotamine, may also be used.1

Case summary

A 67-year-old female Caucasian presented for revision of total hip arthroplasty due to increasing hip pain. The patient had no known cardiac history. Characteristic symptoms, such as exertional dyspnoea or angina pectoris, were absent. The only cardiovascular risk factors were cigarette smoking and arterial hypertension. Vital signs at admission were normal except for an elevated blood pressure of 156/81 mmHg. The electrocardiogram (ECG) was normal (Figure 1). The patient was taking beta-blockers (metoprolol 47.5 mg ½/day) as well as cholecalciferol, alendronic acid, folate, and cobalamin.

Electrocardiogram at time of presentation at premedication ambulance; normal ECG.

During induction of anaesthesia with propofol (cumulative 160 mg i.v.), sufentanil (60 µg i.v.), and rocuronium bromide (30 mg i.v.), sudden hypotension occurred. Theodrenaline hydrochloride/cafedrine hydrochloride (40 mg i.v.) was initially administered. However, its efficacy was limited, and norepinephrine (0.22 µg/kg/min) was started. Further circulatory deterioration was observed. Monitoring ECG showed sinus rhythm with ST-segment elevation in Leads II and III, but pulseless electrical activity (PEA) was present. Cardiopulmonary resuscitation and intubation were performed, and the patient was immediately transferred to the cardiac catheterization laboratory under resuscitation with a mechanical resuscitation device (LUCAS). Echocardiography showed a severely depressed left ventricular (LV) systolic function (about 30% LV ejection fraction) with global hypokinesis, pronounced in septal regions, with normal size and systolic function of the right ventricle, without signs of right ventricular overload or pericardial effusion, and with unremarkable valve status (see Supplementary material online, Video S1).

Based on the PEA, we decided to implant femoral–femoral veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) instead of an Impella pump, which required an intact right heart function. After ECMO implantation, spontaneous circulation returned suddenly, so directly afterwards, we were able to perform angiography with excess via the left femoral arterial. Angiography showed only very slightly irregular coronary walls with no evidence of stenoses or collaterals in the left system and revealed a mild stenosis in the midsegment of the right coronary artery (RCA) without obvious plaque rupture. After stabilization, optical coherence tomography was planned to further investigate possible intimal lesions (see Supplementary material online, Video S2). After placement of the right Judkins guide catheter, a circumscribed vasospasm was noted in the middle segment of the RCA, which was terminated after administration of 0.4 mg i.c. nitroglycerin (see Supplementary material online, Videos S3 and S4). ST-segment elevations were resolved immediately and completely. Optical coherence tomography showed no evidence of endothelial lesions in the proximal and midsegments of the RCA but confirmed the plaque of the midsegment of the RCA as non-relevant. Laboratory results showed elevated troponin-T (499 pg/mL), creatinine kinase (CK) (3.19 μkat/L), CK of muscle-brain type (CK-MB) (1.38 μkat/L), N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (381 pg/mL), and lactate (4.3 mmol/L).

Despite technically adequate ECMO, the patient experienced recurrent episodes of hypotension requiring volume replacement and low doses of catecholamines (Figures 2 and 3).

Finally, continuous i.v. administration of nitroglycerin (3 mg/min; 0714 µg/kg/min) led to circulatory stabilization, so subsequently weaning of the initially high doses of catecholamines became possible. After another 12 h, the patient could be contacted with eye reaction. After 48 h, the ECMO could be removed, and the patient could be extubated after another 12 h. For the first 12 h after extubation, the patient was disorientated and sometimes aggressive towards herself and others. Further neurological deficits were absent. The physical examination was unremarkable at all times.

During treatment with verapamil, which was started on the second day after resuscitation with 80 mg three times a day via gastric tube, high grade sinoatrial (SA) and atrioventricular (AV) block occurred with depression of circulation requiring support with dobutamine. Substitution of verapamil with amlodipine allowed us to initially reduce and discontinue the administration of dobutamine and nitroglycerin. Short-term transthoracic echocardiographic monitoring revealed a completely recovered LV systolic function with only mild hypokinesia mid-ventricular (see Supplementary material online, Videos S5–S8).

Troponin-T decreased rapidly (40 pg/mL), and CK and CK-MB were even normal after 8 days. Due to a history of ventricular fibrillation during another surgery in the past, we decided to implant a subcutaneous implantable cardioverter-defibrillator. The patient was discharged for follow-up treatment after 12 days, without any physical restriction after resuscitation despite of a displaced serial rib fracture treated conservatively. Medications at the time of discharge included amlodipine 5 mg/day, ramipril 2.5 mg/day, atorvastatin 20 mg/day, novaminsulfon 1.5 g/day, hydromorphone 4 mg/day, colecalciferol 1000 IE/day, mirtazapine 45 mg/day, and folate 5 mg/week.

The first follow-up occurred 3 months after discharge. The patient described a stable physical performance state with stable dyspnoea New York Heart Association II and occasional burning shoulder pain. Ranolazine was prescribed.

Discussion

Coronary spasm is an underestimated cause of myocardial infarction that seems to be more common in Asian populations and younger age groups.3–5 Risk factors include smoking and elevated high-sensitivity C-reactive protein. Various precipitating factors have also been described, such as physical or emotional stress, alcohol and other stimulants (cocaine and amphetamines), sympathomimetics, beta-blockers, etc.1,6,7 Certain gene mutations and generalized vascular dysfunction in all arteries may also play a role in patients with variant angina.8,9

Clinical symptoms of coronary spasm can vary widely.1,3,8 In most cases, initially the underlying cause of haemodynamic deterioration until the need of resuscitation is unclear. In our case, some ST-segment elevations appeared, so the indication for coronary angiography was clear. Possible differential diagnoses were a thrombotic occlusion of the coronary artery as well as vasospasm, drug-related vasoplegic response with secondary hypotension or severe stress cardiomyopathy. The manifestation with ST-segment elevation and protracted cardiogenic shock with the need of both the implantation of a mechanical circulatory system and the administration of IV nitrates, as happened in our case, is rare. In case of cardiogenic shock, it seems contradictory to administer IV nitrates because of its preload-reducing effect. In our case, we decided to primarily implant the VA-ECMO because of ongoing resuscitation. Both during coronary angiography and treatment at the intensive care unit, there were alternating hypotensive episodes with laminar arterial flow conditions associated with markedly reduced cardiac output and episodes with pulsatile flow conditions and nearly preserved LV systolic function. Episodes of low cardiac output reflected recurrent coronary spasm, as seen in the coronary angiography, that could be rapidly resolved by nitroglycerin i.a. during angiography.10 However, some cases of refractory coronary spasm despite administration of nitroglycerin have been described.11,12 Some cases of vasospastic angina associated with general anaesthesia have also been reported, but none with such severe and long-lasting cardiogenic shock despite the administration of IV nitroglycerin and under support of mechanical circulatory system (Figures 1, 4 and 5).

If vasospasm could not be seen in the primary coronary angiography, provocative testing with acetylcholine, ergonovine or ergotamine, should be performed after the acute phase.1

Current guidelines and reviews recommend the use of calcium channel blockers (CCBs) and/or long-acting nitrates, either alone or in combination, in addition to lifestyle changes.3,10,13 Of cause, smoking cessation is an urgent goal to be achieved, even if there seem to be some individuals who could be at higher risk for vasospastic angina through smoking cessation.14 In our case, in the acute setting, continuous administration of high doses of nitroglycerin was necessary to achieve stabilization and weaning of the patient from catecholamines. For long-term medical management, we decided to use CCB in combination with long-acting nitrates. Verapamil led to high grade conduction disturbances (SA and AV block) as described in less recent studies, so the change to amlodipine was necessary, because amlodipine does not affect the heart conduction system.15

Implantation of intra-cardiac defibrillator (ICD) in cases of coronary spasm is still controversial and could be recommended in patients with survived sudden cardiac death (SSCD), especially due to secondary prevention of ventricular arrhythmia. According to current guidelines, there is a Class IIa recommendation for coronary spasm and SSCD while on optimal medical treatment (OMT) and a Class IIb recommendation before assessing the response to OMT.3,16 In the present case, we decided to implant an ICD due to secondary prevention of ventricular fibrillation.

Conclusion

This case highlights the need to consider vasospasm as a rare cause of cardiogenic shock. Administration of vasodilators, which are not part of the standard care of cardiogenic shock, is the appropriate treatment for patients with coronary artery spasm. In addition, the recurrence of acute coronary events in these patients must be prevented by drug and device therapy.
