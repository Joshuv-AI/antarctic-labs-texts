Structural and Chemical Modification of Cellular Wood Composite for Enhanced Strength, Fire, and Biological Resistance
Authors: Meen Sung Koo, Ragab Abouzeid, Mohammad Shayan, Timo Kärki, Qinglin Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
High-performance cellular wood composites with enhanced strength, flame retardancy, and termite resistance were developed through delignification, densification, and impregnation with amino-functionalized polyglycerol phosphate (APGP) and disodium octaborate tetrahydrate (DOT). Compared with the performance of control samples, the flexural strength of treated wood samples increased by 56%. Flame resistance was enhanced with increased char yield (from <1 to 42.6%), improved limiting oxygen index value (from 23 to 56%), and a reduction in peak heat release rate (127 to 48 W/g). Termite resistance was also enhanced, with the sample weight loss reduced from 83.75 to 30.45% and termite mortality increased from less than 20 to 100% after 56 day testing. These improvements were attributed to a dual-phase flame-retardant mechanism involving gas-phase intumescent char formation by APGP and solid-phase B2O3 barrier formation from DOT, along with the borate-induced enzymatic disruption of termite digestion. These findings demonstrate that optimized chemical impregnation and densification strategies can yield multifunctional wood composites with superior mechanical durability, fire resistance, and biological protection, suitable for demanding structural and outdoor applications.

Introduction

Wood, a renewable and versatile cellular biocomposite, has been a fundamental material to construct traditional and advanced engineered wood products for building construction, furniture, transparent composites, and various other applications. Despite its many advantages, including aesthetic appeal, workability, and relatively low cost, wood possesses inherent limitations such as susceptibility to biological, flame and mechanical damages, which can significantly shorten its lifespan, especially in harsh environments. , Biological deterioration is primarily caused by fungi and insects, which break down cellular structures and weaken mechanical integrity, while fire hazards stem from the high cellulose content and low density of wood. Targeted treatments are therefore essential to extend the lifespan, ensure safety, and enable its use in more demanding environments.

Conventional wood treatments encompass chemical, thermal, and surface modification strategies designed to enhance the durability, stability, and performance. Pressure impregnation with preservatives such as chromated copper arsenate (CCA), alkaline copper quaternary (ACQ), and copper azole (CA) remains widely used for structural and outdoor applications, although environmental regulations have driven the development of less toxic alternatives. , Borate-based treatments offer a less toxic alternative for interior applications due to their high efficacy and low mammalian toxicity, although their leachability limits exterior use. Other biological protection methods include the use of copper-based compounds, quaternary ammonium compounds, azoles, silicates, and oil-borne preservatives such as creosote and pentachlorophenol. ,

In recent years, attention has shifted toward environmentally compatible and multifunctional approaches. Biobased and nanoenabled preservatives, including lignin-derived phenolics, chitosan–metal complexes, nanosilica, and nanoclay, have shown promise in providing synergistic biological protection alongside improved dimensional stability. − For flame retardancy, phosphorus- and nitrogen-containing systems have demonstrated multifunctional benefits by reducing flammability, promoting char formation, and in some cases enhancing mechanical performance. , Notably, Yan et al. applied a layer-by-layer coating of ammonium polyphosphate (APP), chitosan, and graphene oxide to wood, achieving significant fire resistance via an intumescent char layer that lowered heat release and combustion rates. Similarly, Tour et al. incorporated a phosphorus-containing polyglycerol (PPEG) into poly­(urea–urethane) elastomers, improving both tensile strength and flame-retardant properties. Although polyglycerol phosphate (PGP) has demonstrated promise in enhancing flame resistance in polymer systems, its application to improve the fire performance of wood remains largely unexplored.

Despite these advances, most existing treatments address a single hazard in isolation, rely on chemicals with environmental or regulatory concerns, or require high energy inputs, limiting their sustainability and broader applicability. This has driven research toward integrated strategies that combine structural reinforcement with multihazard protection. In particular, coupling physical modifications such as delignification and densification increases micro/nanofibril bonding within the cell wall and improves strength. The process, coupled with functional chemical treatments, offers a promising route to achieve simultaneous mechanical enhancement, flame retardancy, and biological resistance. − Moreover, while phosphorus-containing flame retardants and borate-based biocides are both well-established, their combined use in a single treatment system, particularly within a structurally reinforced wood matrix, has not been systematically explored.

To address this gap, we report a multifunctional wood modification strategy that integrates delignification and densification processes to enhance micro-/nanofibril bonding within the cell wall and wood mechanical strength with the impregnation of amino-functionalized polyglycerol phosphate (APGP) and disodium octaborate tetrahydrate (DOT). To the best of our knowledge, this work represents the first application of APGP to wood materials and the first demonstration of an APGP-DOT system designed to achieve synergistic flame-retardant and antitermite performance within a structurally reinforced wood matrix. In this approach, APGP introduces phosphorus–nitrogen functionalities that promote intumescent char formation and gas-phase flame inhibition, while DOT contributes to the development of a solid-phase B2O3 barrier and exerts biocidal effects through enzymatic disruption in termites. The combined chemical structural modification confers enhanced fire resistance, biological durability, and mechanical performance while maintaining alignment with environmental compatibility and scalability requirements for advanced engineered wood composites.

Experimental Method

Materials and Chemicals

Southern pine wood was purchased from a local supplier. Phosphoric acid (PA, 85.0% in water), N-butyl alcohol (n-BA), glycerol (GL), tin­(II) chloride, ammonium hydroxide (NH4OH), and branched polyethyleneimine (PEI) were purchased from Fisher Scientific Co. (Hampton, NH, USA). Disodium octaborate tetrahydrate (DOT, dry powder) was supplied by Nisus Corp (Rockford, TN, USA). Sodium chlorite (purity p.a., 80%) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Saint Louis, MO, USA). All reagents were used as received without any further purification.

Preparation of APGP Fire Retardant (FR)

Amino-functionalized polyglycerol phosphate FR was synthesized through a two-step process. First, glycerol (GL) and phosphoric acid (PA) were mixed in molar ratios of 0.5:1 and 1:1, with 0.1% tin­(II) chloride (SnCl2) added as a catalyst. The mixture was heated under reflux at 100 °C, during which the pressure was gradually reduced to remove water, resulting in the formation of PGP. In the second step, PGP was cooled in an ice bath, followed by the slow addition of TsCl under basic conditions (e.g., pyridine) to convert reactive hydroxyl groups into tosyl esters for enabling efficient coupling with PEI. Then, PGP was heated to 95 °C, and PEI was introduced dropwise at 5% by weight for activation, allowing nucleophilic substitution between the primary amine groups of PEI and the tosylated hydroxyl groups of PGP. The mixture was stirred continuously for 2 h to ensure proper functionalization. After it cooled, the APGP was isolated and purified. Finally, ammonium hydroxide (NH4OH) was added to neutralize the acidic phosphate groups, forming stable ammonium phosphate salts and precipitating APGP as a fine powder at pH 6.

Preparation of APGP/DOT Mixture Solution

APGP/DOT solution with a fixed concentration of 2% was prepared by incorporating varying amounts of DOT at 1, 3, and 5% in the formed AGPA for antitermite treatment. The mixture was ultrasonicated for 10 min to ensure the homogeneous dispersion of DOT within the APGP solution until a transparent solution was obtained.

Impregnation of APGP/DOT Solution into Wood Material

Wood samples were delignified under 4% NaClO with an acetic acid solution (pH 4.6) at 85 °C for 6 h. Treated wood samples were rinsed with ethanol and deionized water three times each. Then, processed woods were freeze-dried at a sublimating temperature of −50 °C using a model Freezone 4.5 freeze-dryer (Lobconco Corp, Kansas City, MO, U.S.A.) under 200 mTorr vacuum pressure for 3 days. Delignified and freeze-dried woods with dimensions of 150 × 20 × 3 mm (length × width × thickness) were placed into prepared treating solutions and vacuumed with a diaphragm pump (D-Lab 20–100, Edwards, Stockholm, Sweden) at 100mbar for an hour to remove air trapped within the wood’s macropores. Subsequently, vacuumed wood samples were placed inside an impregnation cylinder, which was pressurized to 414 kPa for 1 h to ensure deep penetration of the APGP/DOT solution into the wood structure. After the impregnation process, the cylinder pressure was gradually released, and the wood samples were then removed from the cylinder. The samples were then thoroughly washed to remove any residual solution on the surface. The treated wood was hot-pressed at 125 °C under 3 MPa for 30 min using a mold that constrained the specimen’s width and length, allowing dimensional changes to occur only in the thickness direction. The untreated and pressed/untreated wood samples were designated as uTW and p-uTW, respectively. The pressed and delignified wood samples were labeled p-dLW (w). Treated p-dLW wood samples were named according to their specific formulations, including wF2, wD3, wD1F2, wD3F2, and wD5F2, to reflect the applied FR APGP (2%) and DOT (1, 3, and 5%) concentrations to the pressed/delignified p-dLW (w) samples.

Characterization of the Wood Material

Lignin contents for untreated and delignified woods were measured to define the change in the amount of wood after treatment, following modified TAPPI methods (T222 om-21). The FTIR spectra analysis was conducted via a spectrophotometer with an attenuated total reflection (ATR) (VERTEX80, Bruker Corp, Billerica, MA, USA) to investigate the chemical structural changes of the treated wood. Each sample was measured in the range from 4000 to 400 cm–1 (4 cm–1 spectral resolution) with 64 scans. APGP/DOT impregnated wood samples were analyzed using a Quanta 3D DualBeam FEG-FIB-SEM-EDS instrument (FEI Co., Eindhoven, Netherlands). The XPS spectra analysis was conducted to investigate the chemical composition of APGP/DOT wood composite samples using a Kratos AXIS 165 (Kratos Ltd, Manchester, UK). The instrument was equipped with Al Kα excitation radiation (hv = 1486.6 eV), enabling precise surface chemical composition and elemental state analysis. The weight of wood before and after treatment was measured to calculate the chemical uptake efficiency rate. The compression set-recovery ratio was examined by determining the compression ratio (CR, %) and recovery ratio (RR, %) of each APGP/DOT specimen. The thickness was measured before hot pressing (t 0), immediately after pressing (t p), and after recovery (t r) for 7 days under ambient laboratory conditions with temperature at 25 ± 2 °C and relative humidity at 70 ± 5%. The CR (%) was calculated as CR=t0−tpt0×100 1

The RR (%) was calculated as RR=tr−tpt0−tp×100 2

CR quantifies the extent of thickness reduction during pressing, while RR indicates the proportion of the compressed thickness recovered after the specified conditioning period. All values are reported as mean ± standard deviation from triplicate specimens. The FEG-SEM-EDS analyzer was operated at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV, providing high-resolution imaging and detailed microstructural analysis. Before SEM observation, the samples were coated with a thin layer of platinum (Pt) by using a sputter coater to enhance conductivity and imaging quality.

Thermal Stability and Flame Retardance Properties

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed using a Q500 device (TA Instruments Inc., New Castle, DE, USA) over a temperature range of 30 to 600 °C under a nitrogen (N2) atmosphere at a heating rate of 10 °C/min, with the sample mass of approximately 5 mg. The heat release rate (HRR) of untreated and treated wood samples was measured as a function of temperature using a microscale combustion calorimeter (MCC) (Fire Testing Technology, London, UK) in an ASTM D7309–13. The tests were conducted under pyrolysis and thermooxidative decomposition conditions. Flammability parameters, including peak heat release rate (pHRR), temperature at pHRR (Tp), heat release capacity (HRC), and total heat release (THR), were obtained using MCC CurveFit software based on the guidelines outlined in the FAA report. The limiting oxygen index (LOI) tests were performed on an oxygen index apparatus (Fire Testing Technology, London, UK) with dimensions of the wood specimens 100 × 12 × 5 mm (length × width × thickness).

Mechanical Properties

A three-point bending test of the raw southern pine and modified wood was conducted with an Instron 5900R testing machine (Instron Corp, Norwood, MA, USA) at a 2.5 mm/min crosshead speed using a 90.7 kg load cell. The wood samples were 10, 25, and 100 mm in thickness, width, and length, respectively. Triplicate analysis was used to get the average bending properties of each sample.

Termite Resistance Properties

The termite resistance of treated woods was examined, following the American Wood Protection Association (AWPA) E1–23 Standard Method for Laboratory Evaluation to Assess Resistance to Subterranean Termites (AWPA 2023). The dimensions of the treated specimen were 25 × 25 × 10 mm, and the weight of all specimens was measured before the test. The specimens were placed on an aluminum barrier film located on the top of the sand to prevent direct water exposure. A total of 400 termites, collected from New Orleans, Louisiana, were introduced into each test jar. After a 28 day (1st set) and 56 day (2nd set) exposure period, the samples were removed, rinsed with distilled water, and dried in an oven to remove moisture in the samples. After the testing, the weight of the specimen was measured and compared with the weight before the testing to measure the weight loss, and the mortality rate of the termites after testing was examined.

Results and Discussion

Structural Characterization

Figure illustrates the synthesis process of APGP, which is achieved through a three-stage reaction mechanism. The synthesis began with the reaction of phosphoric acid and glycerol, using SnCl2 as a catalyst, resulting in PGP with multiple hydroxyl groups. , In the second stage, those hydroxyl groups in PGP were activated with TsCl, which enabled efficient coupling with the branched polyamine, PEI. PEI let the introduction of nitrogen-rich amino groups (−NH2) into the PGP backbone through covalent bonding, which led to the final product APGP. The incorporation of nitrogen-containing compounds, such as PEI, has been widely reported to improve the intumescent properties of flame retardants, leading to enhanced fire resistance. The final stage involves neutralizing the acidic phosphate groups in APGP using NH4OH, which adjusts the pH to 6. This neutralization step results in the formation of stable ammonium phosphate salts and precipitates APGP as a fine powder. The incorporation of both phosphorus and nitrogen elements in APGP is essential for its flame-retardant properties. Phosphorus promotes the formation of a stable char layer during combustion, which reduces heat release and protects the underlying material. Meanwhile, the nitrogen-containing fragments promote expansion of the char, forming a compact and intumescent structure. These dense, expanded chars effectively inhibit heat transfer and reduce smoke release during the combustion process. The synergistic effect of phosphorus and nitrogen in APGP contributes to the overall fire retardancy of treated wood.

The lignin content, measured following a modified TAPPI method for wood before and after the delignification process, was found to be 26.3 and 14.7%, respectively, indicating a reduction by 11.6%. The structure of the impregnated chemicals and treated wood investigated via FTIR is shown in Figure a,b, respectively. For formulated APGP, the key components contributing to the fire retardancy performance of the formulated chemicals were identified as phosphate (P) groups and ammonium salts. Phosphates were detected in the spectra at 1200 (P = O stretching), 1040 (P–O–C stretching), and 949 cm–1 (P–O–H stretching) peaks. The 3200 and 3050 cm–1 peaks were from O–H and N–H stretching, which were derived from the hydroxyl group from PGP and ammonium groups in NH4OH, respectively. The 2800 and 1440 cm–1 peaks indicate C–H stretching and bending, respectively, from the organic backbone of PGP and PEI. These findings confirm the successful formulation of APGP by detecting the expected peaks from PGP, PEI, and ammonium components. As for DOT, the main substance related to antitermite and insecticidal properties is boron. Borate ions have the capability to interfere with the metabolic processes of termites by inhibiting enzymes that digest cellulose, leading to starvation and death. Borate components were found in peaks at 1370 and 1320 cm–1 for B–O stretching, 912 cm–1 for B–O–H stretching, and 726 and 678 cm–1 for the B–O–B linkage. Other peaks, such as 3346, 3176 (O–H stretching), and 1660, were associated with water hydration of DOT. Figure b shows the IR spectra for impregnated wood samples treated with AGAP and DOT in different formulations. The p-dLW (w) samples exhibited corresponding chemical peaks in comparison to the wood without any treatment (uTW). However, cellulose-related peaks at 1370, 1300, and 890 cm–1 were sharper and more evident, whereas lignin-related peaks, including 1500 cm–1, became significantly less prominent. For FR impregnated wood, there were notable differences compared with that of p-dLW in the peaks at 2900, 2850, and 1200 cm–1. The 2900 and 2850 cm–1 peaks were more prominent for APGP wood due to the introduction of additional C–H bonds from PGP, PEI, and N–H stretching from NH4OH. Also, the small shoulder peak was observed at 1200 cm–1 for APGP-treated wood, which could be attributed to PO stretching from APGP. For DOT applied wood (wD3), a minor difference was detected compared to that after p-dLW. For example, the reduction in the 1720 cm–1 peak was found, which could be ascribed to the interaction between DOT and carbonyl parts of the wood. The 1320 cm–1 peak became more prominent due to the introduction of B–O stretching overlapped with C–H bending in the wood structure. Also, the peak at 890 cm–1 from p-dLW was altered to 908 cm–1 in wD3, which could be attributed to DOT-cellulose interaction (e.g., hydrogen bonding) or peak overlapping. Despite the impregnation process, the FTIR spectra exhibited only minor changes, as the treated samples still consisted of more than 90% wood, based on the weight gain data in Figure c. However, characteristic changes were still visible in the structures of APGP- and DOT-treated wood compared to the properties of p-dLW, confirming the successful incorporation of phosphate and borate groups. These findings are further discussed with XPS and EDS analysis.

Figure c shows the weight percentage gain (WPG) and deformation behavior of wood samples treated with different DOT and APGP combinations. The WPG was measured after the treated wood. The WPG was measured after oven-drying the treated wood, and the uptake efficiency was calculated as the ratio of actual weight gain to the theoretical maximum uptake based on the solution concentration and uptake volume. The 2% APGP-treated wood exhibited a weight gain of 1.2 ± 0.6%, corresponding to an impregnation efficiency of approximately 60%, while DOT3-treated wood (wD3) showed a weight gain of 2.6 ± 0.7%, reflecting an efficiency of 86.7%. The difference in the uptake efficiency was attributed to the chemical structure and characteristics between APGP and DOT. According to the IR spectra and preparation step, the FR contains a polyglycerol backbone with phosphate and ammonium groups attached, leading to complex and large molecular size, which limits the penetration into wood. DOT, which is an inorganic borate salt, is composed of disodium, boron, and water molecules that give rise to easier diffusion into the wood structure, resulting in higher uptake efficiency. The WPGs of the wD1F2, wD3F2, and wD5F2 samples were 2.0, 3.6, and 5.6%, with efficiencies of 66.7, 72.0, and 80.0%, respectively. These results indicate that FR tends to hinder DOT penetration by blocking the surface or competing for interactions within the wood structure. At low DOT concentrations, 2% FR dominated the interaction with wood, leading to lower uptake efficiency. As the DOT concentration increased, it overcame the effect of APGP, resulting in improvements in efficiency (e.g., wD5F2). These findings suggest that sequential impregnation, DOT followed by APGP, could enhance the chemical uptake efficiency and that optimizing the DOT-APGP ratio is crucial to maximizing the uptakes and wood treatment effectiveness.

The deformation behavior, expressed as the compression ratio (CR) and recovery ratio (RR), is shown in Figure d. Due to the pressing mold structure, dimensional changes in width and length were negligible (<0.5%), and thickness deformation dominated the response. The untreated densified wood (p-dLW) without impregnation showed the highest CR (27.5%) and density increase by 21.6%, coupled with a relatively high RR (∼13%), indicating substantial elastic rebound after hot pressing. The APGP treatment induced an insignificant change in CR and RR, with less than 0.6% difference, confirming its limited effectiveness in resisting both deformation and recovery. In contrast, DOT3-treated wood (wD3) displayed the lowest CR (20.1%), and density increased by 12.8%, along with a markedly reduced RR (6.82%), suggesting enhanced dimensional stability and greater resistance to elastic recovery. This stability is attributed to DOT molecules penetrating the wood cell wall and forming B–O–B and B–O–C linkages with cellulose and hemicellulose, as evidenced by the IR peak at 908 cm–1, thereby reinforcing the internal structure. As for the wood samples treated with both DOT and APGP, the effect of APGP was more dominant at low DOT concentrations (e.g., wD1F2). As DOT concentration increased (i.e., wD5F2), CR decreased to 22.7%, density increased by 18.2%, and RR to 7.75% reflecting improved internal stabilization performance of DOT despite partial interference from APGP. These results indicate that higher DOT loading reduced both the extent of compression deformation and the tendency for postpressing recovery, while APGP alone did not provide this benefit. Optimizing the DOT–APGP ratio or using sequential impregnation methods may further enhance internal stabilization and reduce irreversible thickness deformation during densification.

Figure shows the morphological structure after impregnation and heat-press treatments. uTW wood retained a well-defined, intact cellular structure with smooth, unaltered cell walls, reflecting the uTW’s original state. The heat-pressed p-dLW wood showed substantial structural collapse in the SEM, consistent with its significant thickness reduction due to delignification and weakened cell walls. The APGP-treated wood, despite its low weight gain, retained a well-defined structure with minor surface deposits, indicating that APGP treatment alone did not significantly contribute to structural integrity. wD3 showed moderate deformation with partially compressed cells but thicker cell walls, reflecting the consequences of both chemical uptake and mechanical stress from heat pressing. For DOT/APGP-treated woods, higher loading of DOT gave rise to bulk swelling and internal densification, even though visible structural collapse is minimal, indicating effective reinforcement. The SEM and deformation data together reveal that delignification led to structural failure (p-dLW), while DOT/APGP treatments caused internal densification. In Figure b, the SEM EDS energy spectrum analysis of wD5F2 confirmed the presence of C, N, P, and B, which are the main components of APGP and DOT impregnated wood. These elements were uniformly distributed around the wood cell walls, suggesting effective penetration and interaction within the wood matrix. In the residual char from wD5F2 (Figure c), phosphorus was clearly detected, confirming the presence of phosphate species from the APGP treatment. However, nitrogen was not observed due to its volatilization during combustion. Boron was detected with a weak signal, which can be attributed to the low-energy B–Kα peak (∼183 eV) and the limited sensitivity of conventional EDS detectors to light elements. This elemental retention in the char further supports the role of phosphate and borate species in postcombustion protection.

The surface chemistry of DOT/APGP-impregnated wood samples was investigated via XPS analysis (Figure ). The C 1s spectrum of p-dLW showed characteristic peaks at 285.2 eV (C–C/C–H), 286.8 eV (C–O), and ∼288.3 eV (O–CO), corresponding to aliphatic carbon, carbon singly bonded to oxygen, and carbonyl/ester groups, respectively, which are typical of cellulose, lignin, and hemicellulose. For wD5F2 samples, the additional peaks at 287.2 (O–C–O), 288.4 (OC–O), and 289.3 eV (O–CO–Na) were observed. 287.2 peak indicates the oxidation or carboxylation of phosphate in APGP, and 288.4 eV peak could be due to the formation of carboxylate species derived from interaction with oxidized phosphate (−P–O = O) and hydroxyl group (O–H) from wood. 289.3 eV (OC–O–Na, borate-, or phosphate-linked Carboxylates) peak was evidence of Na+-stabilized carboxylate species, indicating the interaction between DOT (Na2B8; O13·4H2O) and hydroxyl groups from wood. The O 1s spectrum of dLW exhibited peaks at 532.5 (CO/OC–O) and 533.3 eV (C–OH/C–O–C), confirming the presence of hydroxyl and ether groups in cellulose and lignin. APGP and DOT impregnation rendered multiple new oxygen peaks (e.g., 531.2, 532.2, and 532.4 eV) due to their strong bonding with hydroxyl and carboxyl functional groups. 531.2 eV peak was the evidence of OC–O–Na or B, 531.2 eV was for B–O/P–O, 532.4 eV was for P–O/O–CO. The N 1s spectrum showed peaks at 399.7 eV (CN), 400.4 eV (NH2 or NH4 +), and 401.6 eV (R–NH3 +), indicating the successful incorporation of amine APGP. The Na 1s spectrum exhibited peaks at 1072.4 (B–O–Na) and 1071.2 eV (OC–O–Na), suggesting interactions between sodium and both borate and carboxylate groups. The P 2p spectrum features peaks at 134.2 (P2O7, P–O–B), 133.9 (P–O–NH4 +), and 133.2 eV (NaH2PO4), confirming the presence of phosphate species in various binding environments. The B 1s spectrum highlights peaks at 192.7 (B–O), 192.1 (B–O–Na), and 191.6 eV (B–O–B). These results strongly indicate the successful integration of DOT and APGP into the wood structure.

Thermal Stability

Figure shows the thermal degradation of treated wood samples with different combinations of APGP and DOT. The TG curve for the p-dLW sample reflected a rapid decomposition between 250 and 400 °C, with the onset temperature (T onset) at 268 °C, the maximum decomposition temperature (T max) at 347 °C, and minimal char residue of less than 1%. Two early shoulder peaks for uTW wood at 276 and 315 °C were derived from early decomposition of lignin and hemicellulose remainders, respectively, after delignification. APGP2 (wF2)- and DOT3 (wD3)-treated wood samples revealed notable differences in decomposition behavior, showing T onset values at 246 and 277 °C and T max values at 282 and 303 °C, respectively. Borates in DOT facilitated the formation of B2O3 barrier on wood samples through interaction with cellulose. This barrier reduced the diffusion of combustible volatile byproducts and restricted the access of oxygen to the inner wood structure, thereby promoting anaerobic oxidation and enhancing fire resistance. This also contributes to a higher T max of wD3; however, it was not able to hinder further pyrolysis over T max, leading to less residuals as 13.4% compared to APGP2-treated wood (wF2). Meanwhile, although wF2 started pyrolysis earlier than wD3, it exhibited a markedly larger char residual of 34.1%. Before T max, APGP decomposed at relatively low temperature (170–280 °C) due to the release of phosphoric acid, which accelerated the dehydration of cellulose and breakdown of hemicellulose. Also, this facilitated the formation of a thermally insulating char layer on the wood surface. At high temperatures after T max (>300 °C), the APGP released ammonia, which caused char to expand and create a protective foam-like layer. This expanded intumescent char layer hindered further thermal degradation by blocking heat transfer and limiting oxygen access to the inner wood. The feedback mechanism created by this action helped maintain a higher char residue, limit the release of combustible gases, and provide enhanced thermal resistance under high heat conditions compared to DOT-treated wood. The combined use of DOT/APGP demonstrated synergetic effects on the thermal degradation stability of wood. The DOT/APGP-treated wood exhibited an insignificant difference in T onset, ranging from 270 to 280 °C, and T max between 310 and 315 °C. As the concentration of DOT in the impregnated solution increased (DOT 1 to 5%), the rate of weight loss as a function of temperature significantly decreased from 1.4 to 0.6 wt %/°C, indicating improved thermal stability. Additionally, the final char residue for wD1F2 was recorded at 34.4%, increasing to 42.6% for wD5F2, highlighting the enhanced char-forming ability with higher DOT content. Although DOT and APGP exhibit distinctive mechanisms, a combined treatment of DOT/APGP could optimize fire retarding performance by combining early stage dehydration and char formation with the barrier effect and delayed pyrolysis, providing both short-term and long-term thermal protection.

TG curves (a) and DTG curves (b) from the TGA testing of treated wood samples with different formulations. HRR curves for DOT/APGP-treated woods (c) and LOI test results with the tested specimen (d).

Figure c shows the heat release rate HRR curves of DOT/APGP-treated wood samples as a function of temperature to investigate the effectiveness of fire-retardant performance, obtained from MCC. While TGA focuses on mass loss during the temperature ranges of decomposition and char residual after thermal treatment, MCC focuses on heat release, which is energy output during combustion, directly showing flammability and the effectiveness of APGP treatments. The p-dLW exhibited the peak heat release rate (pHRR) of 127W/g at 335 °C, corresponding to the rapid pyrolysis of cellulose, which is consistent with the TGA result. TGA data showed a rapid weight loss and minimal residual char due to the lack of protective barriers or char-promoting mechanisms. DOT- and APGP-treated woods displayed significant reductions in pHRR to 97 and 61 W/g with T max shifting to lower temperatures to 323 and 290 °C, respectively. The combined use of DOT and FR demonstrated synergistic effects on fire resistance, enhancing both the early stage and long-term thermal stability. Although for single treated woods, T max initially shifted to lower temperatures due to early decomposition, the combined DOT/APGP treatments led to higher final T max due to enhanced char stability and reduced flammable volatile release. As the DOT concentration increased, pHRR progressively decreased from 74 W/g (wD1F2) to 48 W/g (wD5F2), correlating with the formation of a more thermally stable char layer. The solid-phase protection provided by DOT involved boron-catalyzed dehydration and cellulose cross-linking, enhancing stable char formation and limiting flammable gas release. Meanwhile, the gas-phase protection from FR2 was driven by the release of phosphoric acid and ammonia, which facilitated intumescent char formation and free radical scavenging in the flame zone. This effective suppression of heat release and oxygen diffusion underscores the optimized fire resistance achieved through the combined solid-phase DOT and gas-phase APGP mechanisms, ensuring comprehensive thermal protection. This could be demonstrated by further LOI analysis. Figure d presents the LOI test results at various treated formulations. APGP-treated wood samples show an increase in LOI to 34% with burn length (BL) 4 mm for 12 s, reflecting improved fire resistance due to phosphoric acid-induced dehydration and intumescent char formation. The wD3 sample exhibited a slight improvement in LOI to 36%. However, its BL was markedly larger at 21 mm, with 33 s of burn time as compared to the data of APGP 2-treated wood (wF2), indicating less effective oxygen diffusion control and char formation. As discussed previously, the intumescent action in wF2 formed an expanded, thermally insulating char that restricts oxygen diffusion and limits flame spread. In contrast, DOT3-treated wood (wD3) relies primarily on a solid-phase B2O3 barrier, which, at low concentrations, was less effective in fully suppressing oxygen diffusion and combustible volatile release, leading to a larger burnt area. For DOT/APGP-treated woods, the LOI value increased from 40 (wD1F2) to 56% (wD5F2) as the concentration of DOT increased with reduced BL of 9 to 4 mm, meaning higher DOT enhanced the oxygen barrier properties. Overall, the data in Figure demonstrated the biphasic protection mechanism, characterized by the early gas-phase suppression of flames by APGP (phosphoric acid and ammonia release) and the solid-phase oxygen barrier provided by the B2O3 layer formed by DOT. This combination ensures comprehensive protection throughout the thermal degradation process, effectively minimizing flammability and enhancing fire resistance.

Mechanical Properties

The three-point bending test results for treated and DOT/APGP-treated woods are shown in Figure . The results showed a significant improvement in flexural stress due to densification and delignification, with peak stress increasing by 85% from 76.8 (uTW) to 142.4 MPa (p-dLW). The increase in flexural strength aligns with a previous study, which indicates that densification enhances fiber compaction, reduces porosity, and microfibril alignment, leading to improved load-bearing capacity. The removal of lignin in the wood system induced an improvement in the crystalline structure of cellulose components in the wood matrix, which led to an increase in stiffness and resistance to elastic deformation. As for densified DOT/APGP-treated wood (Figure b), the addition of APGP 2 and DOT3 rendered a minor reduction in flexural strength, showing 101.5 and 114.3 MPa of flexural stress, respectively, compared to that of p-dLW (142.4 MPa). Also, DOT/APGP-impregnated wood exhibited a similar level of flexural strength as 101.5 (wD1F2), 119.8 (wD3F2), and 120.2 MPa (wD5F2) with relatively small differences compared to that of p-dLW. The minor reduction in peak stress (i.e., strength) for DOT- and APGP-treated wood in comparison to the p-dLW property could be attributed to microstructural alterations within the wood cell walls. The borate from DOT and amine groups in APGP could interfere with hydrogen bonding among cellulose microfibrils by interacting with (−OH) groups in cellulose and hemicellulose, which is attributed to the reduction in interfiber adhesion within the densified wood structure. , However, compared to natural wood (uTW), the results showed significant improvement in the mechanical strength of wood. The modulus and maximum energy absorption for each sample were depicted in Figure c. The treated wood specimens exhibited a higher modulus compared to uTW, confirming the stiffening effect of densification and delignification. Among the impregnated wood samples, DOT/APGP-treated wood exhibited modulus values similar to those of nonimpregnated densified wood (p-dLW), reinforcing the observation that impregnation did not significantly increase the sample stiffness. The maximum energy absorption values also suggest that densification increased the energy required for fracture, which is essential for structural applications. Compared to the flexural strength discussed previously in Figure a,b, the maximum strain remained relatively stable across impregnated samples, suggesting that elasticity was not significantly compromised by chemical treatment (Figure d), further supporting that fiber network interactions remained stable despite chemical modification. Although the flexural strength of the partially delignified DOT/APGP-treated samples (101.5–120.2 MPa) was lower than the values reported in previous studies for highly densified, fully delignified wood without chemical additives (e.g., 336.8 MPa in Song et al., 2018, and 322.5 MPa in Lu et al., 2022), , the present study prioritized multifunctionality over the maximum strength. It also needs to be pointed out that different woods, densities, pressing times, degrees of delignification (partial vs full), and compaction ratios among various studies significantly alter final wood sample properties (i.e., strength). Freeze-drying was employed prior to densification to improve impregnation uniformity, enabling the effective integration of flame-retardant and termite-resistant functionalities. This approach allowed for a balance between mechanical enhancement and added durability, which is more suitable for structural applications requiring fire and biological protection.

Termite Resistance

The termite resistance of the treated and uTW was evaluated over 28 and 56 days, as shown in Figure . The images in Figure a,b depict the physical conditions of the wood specimens after 28 and 56 days of termite exposure, respectively. Severe structural damage was observed in untreated wood (w), dLW, and APGP-treated wood samples with extensive surface erosion and fragmentation. In contrast, DOT- and DOT/APGP-treated specimens (wD1F2, wD3F2, and wD5F2) exhibited visibly less degradation, with wD3 and wD5F2 showing the most intact surfaces and minimal termite-induced damage. The weight loss (%) data (Figure c) further quantify the extent of termite-induced degradation. The p-dLW showed the highest weight loss, indicating severe termite damage, with 63.20% (28 days) and 83.75% (56 days). The APGP-treated wood (wF2) also exhibited substantial degradation, with weight losses of 61.54 (28 days) and 75.83% (56 days), indicating that APGP alone did not provide effective termite resistance. However, a slight reduction compared to the untreated wood (w) was observed. This may be attributed to the presence of ammonium and phosphate groups in the APGP formulation, which could exert mild toxic or deterrent effects on termite activity. Additionally, surface chemical modification or stiffening caused by the FR might have rendered the wood slightly less palatable or physically harder to penetrate. Nonetheless, this effect was minimal, reaffirming that DOT was the primary contributor to termite resistance. DOT-treated wood (wD1F2, wD3F2, and wD5F2) exhibits significantly lower weight loss as 39.95, 31.68, and 25.46% for 28 days, respectively, and these values further declined to 57.00, 37.45, and 30.45% at 56 days, with wD5F2 showing excellent performance, indicating that borate treatment effectively inhibits termite activity. The wD3 group exhibited the best performance regarding weight losses of 19.92 (28 days) and 21.73% (56 days), even compared to the wD5F2 wood. This could be due to the reduction in bioavailable boron efficacy due to the interaction within boron-phosphate, such as P–O–B and B–O–Na, according to XPS results, making it less effective as a termite deterrent. Termite mortality observations are summarized in Figure d, which categorizes outcomes into four qualitative levels: low, moderate, high, and full. Both untreated (w) and APGP-treated (wF2) specimens exhibited low mortality, indicating poor protection. DOT-treated samples showed progressively increased termite mortality with higher DOT concentrations. While wD1F2 and wD3F2 achieved moderate to high mortality after 56 days, wD5F2 and wD3 reached full category, suggesting that DOT is the primary active agent in eliminating termites. These findings align with the weight loss trends and support the role of borate in disrupting the termite digestion mechanisms through enzymatic inhibition. These findings suggest that the combined use of DOT and APGP does not compromise their individual efficacy, as the termite resistance remains superior to that of uTW while maintaining the fire-retardant properties. This indicates that DOT and FR can coexist without negatively affecting each other’s functionality, ensuring both enhanced durability and fire resistance in treated wood.

Conclusions

The present work demonstrates the potential of a combined chemical-mechanical strategy to transform native wood into a structurally robust, fire- and termite-resistant composite via delignification, densification, and the impregnation of APGP and DOT. Mechanical testing demonstrated that densification alone led to a substantial increase in flexural strength, from 76.8 MPa in untreated wood (uTW) to 142.4 MPa in predensified delignified wood (p-dLW), indicating the structural reinforcement effect of the compaction process. Although impregnation with DOT/FR slightly reduced the flexural strength to 101.5–120.2 MPa (wD1F2 to wD5F2), it helped retain structural integrity by maintaining stable modulus and maximum strain, indicating minimal compromise to mechanical properties. Flame resistance was markedly improved, with char residue increasing from <1% (uTW) to 42.6% (wD5F2) and pHRR decreasing from 127 W/g to 48 W/g. The limiting oxygen index (LOI) rose from 23% (uTW) to 56% (wD5F2), confirming enhanced fire resistance. Termite resistance was substantially enhanced, with wD5F2 achieving complete (100%) termite mortality and a weight loss of 30.45%, while wD3 exhibited the lowest weight loss of 21.73% after 56 days. This suggests that boron-phosphate interactions in the DOT/APGP cotreatment may reduce the availability of free boron, slightly limiting antitermite efficacy compared to DOT alone. The combined effects of APGP and DOT created a synergistic biphasic flame-retardant mechanism with gas-phase protection from intumescent char formation and solid-phase protection via B2O3 barrier development. The resulting wood composites exhibited durability, fire resistance, and biological protection, making them well-suited for use in structurally demanding and outdoor environments.

Despite these promising results, the current process involves high operational complexity and long processing times. Future research should focus on simplifying the treatment protocol, reducing reaction time and temperature, and exploring alternative scalable fire-retardant formulations or green synthesis routes for APGP. Additionally, optimizing the DOT/APGP impregnation sequence and concentration balance could further enhance termite resistance and chemical uptake efficiency while preserving mechanical performance. Addressing these challenges will be critical for translating this multifunctional wood treatment strategy into commercial applications.
