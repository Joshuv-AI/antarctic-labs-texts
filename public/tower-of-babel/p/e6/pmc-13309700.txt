Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Short QT syndrome (SQTS) is an inherited primary electrical disorder characterized by abnormally short QT intervals on the surface electrocardiogram and is associated with an increased risk of atrial and ventricular arrhythmias.1,2 Secondary causes of a short QT interval must be excluded before considering the diagnosis of SQTS. Short QT interval is a rare finding in the pediatric population, with a prevalence of 0.05%.3 The case reported by Wang et al4 in this issue of JACC: Case Reports provides useful clinical lessons for managing SQTS in pediatric patients. Specifically, the case provides alternative treatment options when standard therapy is ineffective and is valuable because the evidence base offers little additional guidance.

Meaning of a Negative Genetic Result

In this case report, whole-exome sequencing revealed no mutations in ion channel–related genes. In SQTS, definitively deleterious variants have been identified only in genes encoding potassium channels (KCNQ1, KCNH2, and KCNJ2). Variants in calcium and sodium channel genes produce a Brugada-like phenotype with modest QT shortening, but do not constitute definitive SQTS.5 A novel SQTS-associated variant in SLC4A3 has been described recently.6,7 Although the diagnostic yield of genetic testing for SQTS is low,8 sequencing should still be performed for prognostic and family-screening purposes.

Quinidine and SQTS

Quinidine is classified as a class IA antiarrhythmic drug according to the Vaughan-Williams classification. It is the cornerstone of pharmacological management in SQTS because it prolongs the QT interval and reduces the incidence of sudden cardiac death.9, 10, 11 However, quinidine requires careful monitoring for potential adverse effects, including gastrointestinal disturbances and proarrhythmia.12,13 Limited access to quinidine is a significant practical concern. A survey published in 2013 reported that quinidine was readily available in only 19 countries (14%), not accessible in 99 countries (76%), and obtainable only through special regulatory processes in 13 countries (10%) requiring 4 to 90 days for approval.14 In the described case, the quinidine dose was gradually increased to 600 mg/d, but ventricular fibrillation (VF) recurred despite QT prolongation, a finding explained by the arrhythmia being driven by a focal ectopic trigger rather than by repolarization abnormalities alone.

Using the Bedside to Guide Drug Selection

In the described case, VF recurred, and the authors administered intravenous lidocaine, which reduced the burden of premature ventricular contractions (PVCs) and ventricular arrhythmias, but did not eliminate short-coupled PVCs or normalize the shortened QT. Lidocaine is a class IB antiarrhythmic agent that decreases action potential amplitude and upstroke velocity, shortens action potential duration and effective refractory period in ventricular and Purkinje fibers, and has minimal effect on the QT interval.15 A positive response prompted addition of mexiletine and gradual discontinuation of lidocaine. In SQTS with electrical storm, isoproterenol may be considered.11 Careful analysis of PVC morphology suggested a papillary muscle or Purkinje origin, supporting initiation of verapamil; verapamil has been reported to prevent electrical storm associated with short-coupled PVCs.16 Similar benefit is reported in pediatric long QT syndrome with PVC-triggered torsade de pointes.17 The combination of verapamil and quinidine was effective in suppressing electrical storm in a case of short-coupled VF.18 The use of quinidine, mexiletine, and verapamil in this patient was driven by specific electrocardiographic characteristics rather than by classic SQTS management. This patient presented with 2 overlapping arrhythmogenic mechanisms: short QT syndrome, which globally shortens ventricular refractory periods, and short-coupled PVCs, which act as triggers for potentially fatal ventricular arrhythmias. Management therefore required 2 complementary strategies—prolongation of repolarization and suppression of triggers. Quinidine was used to prolong repolarization, whereas mexiletine and verapamil were added to suppress ectopy and reduce PVC-initiated ventricular arrhythmias. Although the combination substantially reduced arrhythmic burden in the presented case, ventricular arrhythmias still recurred during follow-up.

Implantable Cardioverter-Defibrillator in Pediatric Patients

Implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) implantation is recommended in patients with SQTS who survive an aborted cardiac arrest or have documented spontaneous sustained ventricular tachycardia.11 Inappropriate ICD shocks are common: in a small cohort, among 11 pediatric SQTS patients with ICDs, 64% experienced inappropriate shocks (atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular conduction in 1 patient, sinus tachycardia in 3 patients, supraventricular tachycardia in 1 patient, and 3 patients had ventricular lead fracture).19 T-wave oversensing is another cause of inappropriate shocks in this population.20 Furthermore, antitachycardia pacing has been reported to be ineffective or harmful in young ICD recipients.21 Appropriate ICD programming is therefore crucial to reduce adverse outcomes, particularly in pediatric patients. Accordingly, the authors disabled antitachycardia pacing and set VF detection zone at 230 beats/min over 30 intervals to minimize inappropriate therapies. Pediatric Recurrent VF: Management Dilemma

The child remained on triple antiarrhythmic therapy with intermittent VF at 6-month follow-up. Catheter ablation of the triggering PVCs would be the most obvious next consideration. Multifocal PVCs and the patient's young age make the procedure technically demanding, but if ICD shock burden remains high despite medical therapy, ablation should be seriously considered. Catheter ablation of the culprit PVCs has shown good results in recurrent short-coupled torsade de pointes or electrical storm.22, 23, 24 Successful radiofrequency ablation of PVCs triggering VF has also been described in a 50-year-old woman with SQTS.25

Conclusions

No specific guidelines exist for managing pediatric SQTS with multifocal short-coupled PVCs and refractory electrical storm. Despite technical difficulties and a high risk of complications—particularly inappropriate ICD shocks—ICD insertion remains the cornerstone of secondary prevention. Quinidine is considered the most effective antiarrhythmic agent; it prolongs the QT interval and reduces the incidence of sudden cardiac death. If quinidine fails to adequately reduce arrhythmic burden, combination antiarrhythmic therapy may be initiated.
