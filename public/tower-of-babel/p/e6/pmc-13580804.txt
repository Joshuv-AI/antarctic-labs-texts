Cancer‐Directed Therapy in the Last Month of Life and Its Association With ICU Utilisation in a Tertiary Care Centre in India
Authors: Kunal Das, Arun Ghoshal, Anuja Damani, Krithika S Rao, Seema Rajesh Rao, Shreya Nair, S Gayatri, Arnavi Udapure, Naveen Salins
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundUse of cancer‐directed therapies (CDT) near the end of life is an established indicator of quality in oncology care. However, evidence from low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs), including India, remains limited.ObjectivesTo describe patterns of cancer‐directed therapy use in the last month of life and to evaluate the association between palliative care referral and ICU utilisation.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included adult cancer patients who died in hospital between August 2020 and July 2023 and received at least one CDT within the LMOL. Data were extracted using a standardised proforma. The primary outcome was the pattern and timing of CDT use. Secondary outcomes included ICU utilisation, palliative care referral and documentation of end‐of‐life care processes. Associations between palliative care referral and ICU utilisation were evaluated using unadjusted odds ratios.ResultsAmong 4211 in‐hospital deaths, 669 patients had cancer, of whom 384 received CDT in the LMOL and were included. Chemotherapy was the most common modality (58.3%). CDT was administered within 14, 7 and 3 days of death in 72.3%, 45.0% and 23.4% of patients, respectively. ICU admission occurred in 62.7%. Palliative care referral was documented in 76.8% of cases, though often late. Patients with a palliative care referral had significantly lower odds of ICU admission than those without referral (OR 0.07, 95% CI 0.03–0.18, p < 0.001). End‐of‐life care documentation was present in 40.6% of cases.ConclusionsHigh‐intensity care near the end of life, including frequent CDT use and ICU admission, remains common in this LMIC setting. Palliative care referral was strongly associated with lower ICU utilisation, suggesting a potential association that warrants further investigation in prospective studies.Keywords: advance care planning, health care utilisation, India, oncology, terminal care

Introduction

The use of cancer‐directed therapies (CDT) near the end of life has become an important indicator of quality in oncology care [1]. While systemic therapies, radiotherapy, and surgical interventions may offer clinical benefit earlier in the disease trajectory, their role in the last month of life (LMOL) is often limited [2, 3, 4, 5]. In this phase, continued administration of CDT may expose patients to treatment‐related toxicity, increase healthcare utilisation, and potentially detract from opportunities to prioritise symptom control, communication and goal‐concordant care [6]. Consequently, patterns of treatment intensity near the end of life have been widely examined as markers of appropriateness of care [7].

A substantial body of evidence from high‐income countries (HICs) has demonstrated that a significant proportion of patients with advanced cancer continue to receive CDT in the final weeks of life [4, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14]. These patterns have been associated with increased hospitalisations, higher rates of intensive care unit (ICU) admission, and lower likelihood of receiving timely palliative care. Such observations have informed the development of quality indicators for end‐of‐life care, including administration of chemotherapy within the last 14 or 30 days of life, ICU utilisation, and timing of referral to palliative care (PC) [1, 15, 16]. These indicators aim to assess whether care delivered near the end of life is proportionate, appropriate and aligned with patient preferences.

However, most of the available literature originates from HIC settings, where healthcare systems are characterised by relatively greater access to oncology services, structured palliative care integration and more standardised clinical pathways [7]. In contrast, low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs), including India, operate within healthcare environments that differ substantially in terms of infrastructure, accessibility and financing [17]. In such settings, several factors may influence end‐of‐life care practices, including limited availability and late integration of palliative care services, high out‐of‐pocket healthcare expenditure, variability in access to specialised oncology care, and fragmented referral pathways [18]. In addition, sociocultural expectations and family dynamics may favour continuation of active treatment even in advanced stages of illness, further contributing to the persistence of intensive interventions near the end of life [19, 20].

India bears a substantial cancer burden, with a large proportion of patients presenting with advanced or metastatic disease at diagnosis [21, 22]. Decision‐making in this context is inherently complex and influenced by multiple factors, including disease characteristics, expected treatment benefit, patient and caregiver preferences, financial considerations and clinician judgement [13]. Prognostic uncertainty and the absence of routine, structured advance care planning may further complicate decisions regarding continuation or limitation of CDT. As a result, patients may continue to receive treatment close to death, often in conjunction with high‐intensity interventions such as ICU admission [13].

The timing of palliative care referral is a critical determinant of care quality in this setting. Early integration of palliative care has been associated with improved symptom control, better quality of life, enhanced communication and reduced use of potentially non‐beneficial interventions [23, 24, 25]. Despite this, referral to palliative care services in many LMIC settings occurs late in the disease trajectory, limiting the potential benefits of such care [26, 27]. Evaluating the relationship between palliative care involvement and healthcare utilisation near the end of life is therefore particularly relevant.

Although some studies from India have examined individual aspects of end‐of‐life cancer care, such as chemotherapy use or palliative care referral, there remains a relative paucity of data that simultaneously evaluates multiple dimensions of care intensity within a single cohort [28, 29]. There is limited evidence describing the interplay between CDT use, ICU utilisation, palliative care referral, and documentation of end‐of‐life care processes among patients receiving treatment close to death [28].

However, existing studies from India have largely examined these components in isolation, without integrating treatment patterns, palliative care involvement, and healthcare utilisation within a single analytical framework. Furthermore, there is limited evidence examining the relationship between palliative care referral and ICU utilisation among patients receiving cancer‐directed therapy near the end of life. Addressing this gap is essential to inform context‐specific strategies to improve the quality of care in resource‐constrained settings.

We hypothesised that CDT use near the end of life would be associated with high healthcare utilisation and that palliative care referral would be associated with lower ICU utilisation.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This retrospective cohort study followed all eligible patients who met the inclusion criteria from hospital admission until death. The main exposure was whether the patient received a palliative care referral (yes/no), and the primary outcome measured was ICU utilisation during the final hospital stay. No sampling based on outcomes was performed, so it is not a case–control study. The research was carried out at a tertiary care university hospital in India. Electronic medical records (EMR) were analysed to assess end‐of‐life care patterns among adult cancer patients who died during hospitalisation over 3 years, from 1 August 2020 to 31 July 2023.

Study Population

All in‐hospital deaths during the study period were screened because there was no dedicated cancer‐specific mortality registry within the EMR system. Patients were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria: (1) age ≥ 18 years, (2) histopathologically confirmed malignancy, (3) in‐hospital death and (4) receipt of at least one form of cancer‐directed therapy (CDT) within the last 30 days of life (LMOL). Patients with incomplete or missing key clinical data, including date of death or treatment details, and those without confirmed malignancy were excluded.

This study specifically focused on patients receiving CDT in the LMOL to characterise treatment intensity in this subgroup; therefore, findings are not intended to represent all cancer decedents.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were summarised using mean with standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), as appropriate. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages.

Comparisons between groups (palliative care referral vs. no referral) were performed using chi‐square or Fisher's exact tests for categorical variables and independent t‐tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables, as appropriate.

The association between palliative care referral and ICU admission was evaluated using unadjusted odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Given the study's primarily descriptive objective, inferential analysis was limited to evaluating the association between palliative care referral and ICU utilisation. Multivariable modelling was not pursued due to quasi‐complete separation and insufficient covariate balance; therefore, the results should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis‐generating rather than causal.

Timing of CDT administration was reported using clinically relevant categorical thresholds (≤ 14, ≤ 7 and ≤ 3 days before death), consistent with established end‐of‐life quality indicators in oncology [1, 3, 7, 16]. The objective was to characterise treatment intensity proximate to death rather than to estimate survival distributions; therefore, clinically relevant categorical thresholds were used, consistent with established end‐of‐life quality indicators.

All statistical analyses were conducted using Python Software Foundation, 2016, version 3.12 [30]. A two‐sided p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Bias and Ethical Considerations

This study is subject to selection bias due to the inclusion of only in‐hospital deaths and may not reflect patterns of care among patients who die in the community. Measurement bias may arise from reliance on retrospective documentation in electronic medical records, and residual confounding cannot be excluded.

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC2: 650/2023). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design and use of anonymised data.

Results

Participant Flow

During the study period, a total of 4211 in‐hospital deaths were screened. Of these, 669 patients had a confirmed malignancy diagnosis. Among them, 384 patients received at least one form of cancer‐directed therapy (CDT) within the last month of life (LMOL) and were included in the final analysis (Figure 1).

Study flow diagram illustrating selection of study participants: 4211 in‐hospital deaths screened, 669 cancer patients identified and 384 patients receiving cancer‐directed therapy in the last month of life included in the final analysis.

Patient Characteristics

The cohort's mean age was 57.4 ± 14.0 years, with a predominance of male patients (60.4%). Most patients had advanced disease, with 65.1% classified as stage IV or relapsed disease. Palliative intent was the treatment goal in 60.4% of cases.

Hematolymphoid malignancies constituted the largest subgroup (28%), followed by lung (11%) and breast cancers (8%), with other solid tumours comprising the remainder. Most patients had poor functional status, with over two‐thirds having an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of ≥ 2 (see Table 1).

Patterns of Cancer‐Directed Therapy Use

Chemotherapy was the most administered modality, received by 58.3% of patients. Surgery was performed in 23.4%, radiotherapy in 4.2%, and multimodality treatment in 14.1%.

A substantial proportion of patients received CDT close to death. Overall, 72.3% received treatment within 14 days of death, 45.0% within 7 days and 23.4% within 3 days, indicating frequent continuation of treatment into the terminal phase (please see Table 2).

Intensive Care Unit Utilisation

ICU admission during terminal hospitalisation occurred in 241 patients (62.7%). Among those admitted, the average survival was 7.7 days following ICU admission. ICU use varied significantly by palliative care referral status. Among patients with documented palliative care referral, 157 of 295 (53.2%) were admitted to the ICU, compared to 84 of 89 (94.4%) among those without referral (p < 0.001).

Palliative Care Referral and End‐of‐Life Care Processes

Palliative care referral was documented in 295 patients (76.8%). However, referral often occurred late in the disease trajectory.

Documentation of end‐of‐life care processes, including goals‐of‐care discussions or advance care planning, was present in 156 patients (40.6%). Multidisciplinary team (MDT) discussions were documented in 126 patients (32.8%).

Patients with a palliative care referral were significantly more likely to have documented end‐of‐life care discussions than those without referral (52.9% vs. 0%, p < 0.001). MDT involvement was also more frequent among patients with palliative care referral (35.6% vs. 23.6%, p = 0.047).

Association Between Palliative Care Referral and ICU Admission

A strong association was observed between palliative care referral and ICU utilisation. Among patients with palliative care referral, ICU utilisation was substantially lower (53.2%) compared to those without referral (94.4%) (p < 0.001) (please see Table 3).

Discussion

This study examines patterns of cancer‐directed therapy use during the last month of life in hospitalised cancer patients at a tertiary care facility in India. The results offer context‐specific insights from an LMIC setting, where healthcare utilisation and palliative care access vary significantly from high‐income countries. The data show that many patients continue receiving CDT near death, with high ICU usage and incomplete documentation of end‐of‐life care processes. While most patients had documented palliative care referrals, these often occurred late in the illness, and there was a strong link between lack of palliative care involvement and ICU use.

Importantly, the association between palliative care referral and ICU utilisation should not be interpreted as causal. The study was not designed to establish causal relationships, and residual confounding—such as disease severity, clinician decision‐making or patient preferences—may influence both referral patterns and ICU admission. Therefore, the findings should be considered hypothesis‐generating and warrant further prospective evaluation.

A key finding of this study is the frequent administration of CDT near the end of life. More than two‐thirds of patients received treatment within 14 days of death, and nearly one‐quarter within 3 days. These findings are broadly consistent with reports from high‐income settings, suggesting that continuation of anticancer therapy in the terminal phase is a common phenomenon across healthcare systems [2, 3, 4, 5, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14]. In the present context, such patterns may reflect a combination of prognostic uncertainty, ongoing attempts to achieve disease control, and sociocultural expectations that favour continuation of active treatment. In addition, the absence of clearly defined institutional thresholds for treatment discontinuation may contribute to variability in clinical decision‐making.

The high rate of ICU utilisation observed in this cohort further suggests the intensity of care delivered near the end of life. Nearly two‐thirds of patients required ICU admission during their terminal hospitalisation, with limited survival following admission. While ICU care may be appropriate in selected circumstances, particularly when reversible complications are anticipated, such high utilisation may reflect high‐intensity care, particularly in the context of limited expected benefit and poor alignment with patient goals in advanced disease. These findings are particularly relevant in resource‐constrained settings, where ICU capacity is limited, and resource allocation requires careful consideration [31].

A central observation of this study is the strong association between palliative care referral and ICU utilisation. Patients who received palliative care were significantly less likely to be admitted to the ICU compared to those without referral, with a marked reduction in the odds of ICU admission. This finding is consistent with the existing literature, which suggests that palliative care involvement is associated with more appropriate utilisation of healthcare resources and reduced‐intensity interventions near the end of life [32, 33, 34]. However, this association should be interpreted with caution. The observational design of the study precludes causal inference, and it is possible that unmeasured confounders, such as disease trajectory, clinician judgement or patient preferences, influenced both referral patterns and decisions regarding ICU admission.

Despite the relatively high proportion of patients referred to palliative care, the timing of referral appears suboptimal. Although precise timing metrics were limited in this dataset, the clinical context suggests that referral frequently occurred late in the disease trajectory. Late referral restricts the potential benefits of palliative care, including proactive symptom management, psychosocial support and advance care planning. Earlier integration of palliative care has been associated with improved quality of life and may facilitate more informed decision‐making regarding continuation or limitation of CDT [35, 36, 37, 38].

The relatively low rate of documented end‐of‐life care processes, including goals‐of‐care discussions and advance care planning, represents another important finding. Less than half of patients had documented discussions regarding end‐of‐life care. This may reflect gaps in communication practices, time constraints, or a lack of structured processes to support such discussions. The higher frequency of documentation among patients receiving palliative care suggests that palliative care teams play a critical role in facilitating communication and supporting shared decision‐making.

From a systems perspective, the findings of this study have implications for both quality of care and healthcare resource utilisation. In LMIC settings, where resources are constrained, the continuation of high‐intensity interventions with limited expected benefit may not only affect patient experience but also impact system efficiency. Strategies aimed at improving care at the end of life may include developing institutional guidelines for the appropriate use of CDT in advanced disease, implementing triggers for earlier palliative care referral, and strengthening multidisciplinary team processes to support shared decision‐making.

This study has several limitations. As a single‐centre retrospective analysis, the findings may not be generalisable to other settings. Inclusion of only in‐hospital deaths introduces potential selection bias and may not capture patterns of care among patients who die in the community. Data were derived from electronic medical records and are therefore dependent on the accuracy and completeness of documentation, introducing the possibility of measurement bias. The restriction of the cohort to patients receiving CDT in the last month of life limits comparability with patients not receiving such therapies and may overestimate the intensity of care observed. Residual confounding cannot be excluded, and the lack of patient‐level data for multivariable modelling limited the ability to adjust for potential confounders. Consequently, the observed association between palliative care referral and ICU utilisation should be interpreted as descriptive rather than causal.

Despite these limitations, this study provides integrated, real‐world data on multiple dimensions of end‐of‐life care within a single cohort in an LMIC setting. By examining CDT use, ICU utilisation, palliative care referral and end‐of‐life care documentation together, it contributes to a more comprehensive understanding of care delivery in the terminal phase of illness.

Conclusion

High‐intensity care near the end of life remains common in this setting. Palliative care referral was associated with lower ICU utilisation, although referral frequently occurred late. These findings highlight the need to integrate palliative care earlier to support goal‐concordant care in resource‐constrained settings.
