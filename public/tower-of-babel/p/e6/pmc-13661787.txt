Depression Beyond the 6‐Month Postpartum Period: A Mixed‐Method Study Among Women in Nepal
Authors: Sangita Pudasainee‐Kapri, Tumla Shrestha, Mary Wunnenberg, Kul Kapri, Apsara Pandey, Manisha Chapagai, Ram Hari Chapagain, Pradeep Gupta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimThis study aimed to comprehensively examine social and clinical factors contributing to postpartum depression among women in Nepal and identify their perspectives/experiences regarding postpartum mental health.Design and MethodsThe explanatory sequential mixed‐method study was conducted in two phases. In the quantitative phase, data were collected via survey of 200 postpartum women at immunisation clinics of two hospitals in Kathmandu Nepal. Measures included sociodemographic and perinatal factors and the Edinburgh Postpartum Depression Scale. Regression models were conducted to analyse data. In the qualitative phase, in‐depth interviews were conducted with 15 participants having depression scores ≥ 10. Data were analysed using the thematic content analysis method. Qualitative and quantitative findings were integrated using the joint display and the narrative weaving approach to generate results.ResultsFindings show that 28% of postpartum women screened positive for depressive symptoms, requiring referral for further evaluation. Integrated findings indicate that prenatal mental health problems, low birth weight or medical conditions among infants, intimate partner violence, partner substance abuse, and a lack of postpartum social support were major contributors to postpartum depression.ConclusionDepression is highly prevalent among women 6–12 months postpartum in Nepal and is associated with several clinical and social factors.ImplicationsThese findings have important implications, emphasising the need for routine screening using validated tools for early identification, timely treatment and referral of women at‐risk for postpartum depression. Evidence‐based clinical and community initiatives are needed to promote positive outcomes for women and their families.Patient or Public ContributionExternal experts were consulted for survey questions and qualitative interview guide, then refined those instruments based on their feedback to improve construct validity. The immunisation clinic staff provided input during study design, for participant recruitment and data collection. Key findings were presented to stakeholders to validate the interpretation of findings.

Introduction

Postpartum depression (PPD) is a significant public health concern worldwide due to its impact on not only mothers but also their infants and families. It has been identified as the most common mental health issue affecting postpartum women globally (Amer et al. 2024; Singh et al. 2021) and is a leading cause of maternal morbidity and mortality (Amiri and Behnezhad 2021; Subba et al. 2024). It is estimated to affect one in seven mothers globally (American Psychological Association [APA] 2022), with over 50% of those affected being untreated (Wang et al. 2021). The prevalence of PPD is reported to be even higher in lower and middle‐income countries (LMICs) (Carlson et al. 2025; Roddy Mitchell et al. 2023; Wasti et al. 2024). According to the World Health Organization (WHO) (2024), approximately 20% of childbearing women in developing countries such as Nepal experience PPD, which is significantly higher than those from other countries. Due to the lack of routine screening practices for mental health problems, national representative data on the prevalence of PPD in Nepal is lacking. However, recent community and hospital studies report the prevalence of PPD in Nepal between 9.4% and 41.5% (Dielemans et al. 2024; Pudasainee‐Kapri et al. 2024; Singh et al. 2021; Subba et al. 2024). Since PPD is a significant public health concern globally affecting the health and well‐being of women and children, investigation of this issue among women of childbearing age has significant international relevance.

PPD Diagnostic Criteria

PPD is described in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition‐text revision, as a subtype of Major Depression, with peripartum onset (APA 2022). Symptoms of PPD include a depressed or low mood negatively impacting a woman's appetite and sleep, anergia, avolition, somatic complaints, feelings of hopelessness and guilt, anhedonia, and suicidal ideations and behaviours (APA 2022). Some women also experience thoughts of harming their infant and other children (National Institute of Mental Health [NIMH] 2023). These symptoms can occur during pregnancy or anytime within the first year postpartum (Radoš et al. 2024; Subba et al. 2024). While it was originally thought that PPD primarily occurred within the first 3 months postpartum, current research has shown that its onset can occur throughout the first 12 months postpartum. In a recent study, Pudasainee‐Kapri et al. (2025) found that 9.4% of Nepalese mothers 1–6 months postpartum reported higher levels of depressive symptoms needing a referral for mental health evaluation. Wang et al. (2021) reported on pooled data from several studies indicating a 17.7% global prevalence of PPD within the first 3 months postpartum, 15.9% 3 to 6 months postpartum, and 18.19% 6 to 12 months postpartum. Such research supports the need for ongoing screening for mental health issues such as PPD and anxiety throughout the entire 12‐month postpartum period.

Contributing Factors of Postpartum Depression

Although the exact aetiology is unknown, several factors have been associated with the onset of PPD. This includes biologic, hormonal, obstetric and social factors. Women with a prior or family history of depression are at greater risk for developing PPD (Saharoy et al. 2023). Findings from a recent systematic review of 56 studies suggest that the presence of different inflammatory markers may be associated with symptoms of peripartum depression (Silva‐Fernandes et al. 2024). Hormonal changes during the prenatal period and following delivery have also been identified as potential contributing factors to the development of PPD (NIMH 2023). Obstetric and neonatal contributing factors include pregnancy complications, issues with the infant's health (Amer et al. 2024), such as low birth weight (Pudasainee‐Kapri et al. 2025), and in some geographic regions, female gender of the infant (Subba et al. 2024; Wasti et al. 2024).

Socioeconomic factors have also been associated with a higher incidence of PPD. A lack of social support has been identified as a contributing factor for PPD (Neupane et al. 2024; Pudasainee‐Kapri et al. 2025) as social support has been found to mitigate the negative effects of stress (Liu et al. 2021; Saharoy et al. 2023). An inverse relationship between lower socioeconomic status and increased symptoms of PPD has also been identified (Singh et al. 2021), as well as fathers' lower level of education (Dawadi et al. 2020). Other socioeconomic factors related to PPD include a history of intimate partner violence (IPV), partner substance use (Dawadi et al. 2020; Wasti et al. 2024), lower education, lack of social support and financial problems (Wang et al. 2021) and the stressors of caring for an infant (NIMH 2023). Although non‐significant effects, the prevalence of PPD among women were higher based on the sex of infant (male vs. female, 17.97% vs. 20.77%) and maternal age (adolescent vs. adult, 23.34% vs. 20.35) (Wang et al. 2021). Lastly, stigma and cultural factors related to mental health issues may deter women from discussing depressive symptoms and seeking appropriate treatment (Amer et al. 2024; Wasti et al. 2024).

Impact of Postpartum Depression

The short‐ and long‐term effects of PPD are widespread, impacting women, their infants and families. PPD can negatively impact an infant's growth and development as it can impair mother‐infant bonding, which is crucial for positive infant outcomes (WHO 2024). Several studies have demonstrated the association between perinatal depression and mother‐infant bonding (Liu et al. 2021; Pudasainee‐Kapri et al. 2024). KC et al. (2024) reported more neurodevelopmental delays in infants whose mothers had persistent depression within the first 3 months postpartum, compared to those who did not (48.6% vs. 5.1%; p < 0.001). Women with depressive symptoms have also been found to have issues with sleep and difficulty feeding their infants (Dadi et al. 2020; Saharoy et al. 2023). The effects of PPD can have a long‐lasting impact, with approximately 30% of infants of mothers with PPD having behavioural issues that persist in childhood and adolescence (Dielemans et al. 2024). Despite the prevalence and significant impact of PPD, there are limited studies exploring factors contributing to PPD among women in Nepal. The empirical study was needed to examine the factors contributing to postpartum mental health outcomes among Nepali women during 6–12 months postpartum. This timeframe is particularly critical, as many women may receive substantial social support during the initial phase of childbirth but may experience a lack of similar support during the later postpartum period along with cultural and social expectations with women. Additionally, screening and identification of contributing factors of PPD beyond the initial period is crucial as women may have higher risks of developing PPD symptoms throughout the first year, with some women carrying chronic depressive symptoms with its long‐term impact on infants, women and their family.

Present Study

The main purpose of this study was to examine factors influencing postpartum mental health outcomes, specifically PPD among Nepalese women 6–12 months postpartum. This study also explored women's perceived experiences related to factors contributing to their postpartum mental health outcomes. To address gaps in the literature, this project investigates: (1) What is the prevalence of PPD among women 6–12 months postpartum in Nepal (2) What are the contributing factors of PPD among women 6–12 months postpartum? (3) What are the lived experiences of women regarding their postpartum mental health/PPD outcomes?

Materials and Methods

Study Design

The study adopted an explanatory, sequential, mixed‐method research design to understand the complex phenomena of PPD among women in Nepal. The first phase of the study included quantitative data collection and analysis. Based on the quantitative findings, qualitative sampling, data collection and analyses were done. Finally, qualitative findings were merged to expand quantitative results (Figure 1). Hence, the design included two points of integration: connecting quantitative findings for qualitative sampling and linking major quantitative and qualitative findings (Draucker et al. 2020; Fetters et al. 2013).

Explanatory sequential mixed‐method design. EPDS, Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale.

Quantitative Methods

Research Sites and Participants

The study was conducted in immunisation clinics of two hospitals in Kathmandu, Nepal: Tribhuvan University Teaching Hospital (TUTH) and Kanti Children's Hospital (KCH). Two hundred postpartum women having low birth weight (LBW: infants weighing < 2500 g during birth) (n = 74) and normal birth weight (NBW: birth weight 2500 g or more, n = 126) infants were enrolled in the study. Women attending well‐baby clinics for routine vaccination of their infants were enrolled in the study using convenience sampling techniques. The inclusion criteria for participants include postpartum women who (1) are age 19 to 49, (2) have an infant six to 12 months old, (3) utilise one of the immunisation clinics of the research sites, (4) had a singleton birth with this pregnancy, (5) can report the infant's birth weight verbally or via the immunisation card, (6) is willing and able to provide written informed consent and (7) is able to read and write Nepali.

Measures

Sociodemographic and Perinatal Factors

Data on sociodemographic variables included participants' age, ethnicity, educational level and income sufficiency for their household. Infant characteristics data included age, birth weight and infants' gestational age/preterm status. Data on perinatal risk factors included maternal history of anxious and depressive thoughts during pregnancy. In addition, the history of prior LBW and/or preterm births and delivery types were recorded.

Postpartum Social Support

The Maternity Social Support Scale (MSSS) was used to measure mothers' perceived social support from family, husbands/partners and friends during the postpartum period (Webster et al. 2000). It is a publicly available six‐item questionnaire using a five‐point Likert scale (1 = never, 5 = always), with higher scores indicating greater social support (Webster et al. 2000, 2011). Sample items include ‘I feel controlled by my partner’ and ‘my family is always there for me’. The MSSS has shown high reliability across prior studies, with Cronbach's alpha ranging from 0.82 to 0.90 (Mohammad et al. 2021; Webster et al. 2000).

Postpartum Depression

The Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) was used to screen for depressive symptoms during the postpartum period (Cox et al. 1987). This 10‐item questionnaire (Cronbach Alpha [α] = 0.85) assesses PPD symptoms over the past 7 days, with scores ranging from 0 to 30. Sample items include ‘I have been able to laugh and see the funny side of things’ and ‘I have blamed myself unnecessarily when things went wrong’. Participants rated their feelings on a 4‐point scale, with scores of 10 or higher indicating the need for referral and further evaluation by the mental health provider (Cox et al. 1987). Items were averaged to create a composite scale in which higher scores represent greater PPD symptoms. This study used the validated Nepali version of the EDPS for screening PPD among mothers in Nepal (Bhusal et al. 2016). The EPDS has demonstrated strong validity and reliability, with Cronbach's alpha ranging from 0.74 to 0.87 in prior studies (Bhusal et al. 2016; Cox et al. 1987; Pudasainee‐Kapri et al. 2025).

Control Variables

The models controlled for key perinatal and sociodemographic variables, including infant sex (male/female), participants' age (in years) and prenatal depressive thoughts (yes/no). In addition, sufficient income for households (i.e., less than a year vs. sufficient for 1 year or more) was controlled to avoid erroneous associations among key study variables in the regression model.

Data Collection Procedures

After obtaining necessary approvals, data collection began in August 2023 and ended in January 2024. Research assistants identified eligible participants and obtained written informed consent. Then, data were collected from participants in a private area at the immunisation clinic using a paper‐based survey. The survey was first developed in English, then translated into Nepali by language and content experts. Recommendations for cross‐cultural studies were followed (Brislin 1970) and standardised and validated tools were used to measure sociodemographic characteristics, perinatal factors and depressive symptomatology.

Data Analysis

The quantitative data collected were analysed using STATA version 17.0. Descriptive statistics were calculated for all demographic and control variables to determine participants' characteristics. Ordinary least squares regression models were conducted in STATA to analyse research questions for the effect of independent variables on the dependent variable (PPD), adjusting for all the covariates. To assess the validity of the OLS regression results, several tests were conducted to examine the key assumptions of the OLS regression model, including linearity, homoscedasticity, normality and multicollinearity. Among those, the homoscedasticity assumption was violated. To address this issue, robust standard errors were employed in the study, as they provide accurate and valid statistical inference even when the assumption of homoscedasticity (constant error variance) is not satisfied.

Qualitative Methods

Participants Selection and Recruitment

Postpartum women with an EPDS score ≥ 10 identified in the quantitative phase were invited to participate and simultaneously recruited for the qualitative interviews (n = 15). Postpartum women who met the eligibility criteria, were willing to share their experience, and provided written consent for both participation and audio recording were purposively recruited for the study.

Instrument and Data Collection Procedure

A semi‐structured interview guide including open‐ended questions was developed for the qualitative interview. Participants' sociodemographic information obtained in the quantitative phase were linked with qualitative data using a de‐identified identification number. To enhance the trustworthiness of the instrument, the instrument was reviewed by subject matter experts and qualitative research experts. The English version of the instrument was translated into the Nepali language, and pilot interviews were conducted with two participants, which led to minor revisions to the interview guide. Individual in‐depth interviews (IDIs) were conducted in a private, quiet area within the clinic. All interviews were digitally audio‐recorded, and field notes were taken to capture verbal and non‐verbal cues and contextual observations. Each interview lasted about 60 min. Recruitment ended after 15 interviews in which the sample size was determined based on data saturation as no new substantial information emerged.

Data Analysis

Qualitative data collection and analyses were conducted simultaneously. Recorded interviews were transcribed and translated into English. The research team and research assistants reviewed interview recordings for consistency by repeatedly listening to the audio recordings, reading the transcripts and conducting cross‐validation with notes taken during the interviews. Data were analysed manually using the thematic content analysis method. The themes and subthemes were comprehensively described and contextualised with relevant quotes.

Trustworthiness

Lincoln and Guba (1986) describe four criteria for conducting qualitative research: credibility, dependability, transferability and confirmability to ensure the rigour of the study. Credibility was upheld by expert consultations, by building trust and mutual rapport with participants, spending time in in‐depth interviews and voluntary participation in the interview to share their descriptive experiences of PPD. Field notes were maintained during the interviews. Dependability was ensured by using a consistent interview guide during in‐depth interviews and prompt translation/transcription of audio recordings. Transferability was maintained by a comprehensive research process along with direct quotes to support themes/subthemes. For confirmability, the authors comprehensively reviewed the process of data analysis and study findings. All the authors read and agreed on the final manuscript before submission.

Integration of Findings

Integration in mixed‐methods research involves combining qualitative and quantitative strands to generate a comprehensive understanding of the phenomenon under study. In this study, integration occurred at two distinct points (Figure 1). First, quantitative findings guided the recruitment of participants for the qualitative phase (Draucker et al. 2020; Fetters et al. 2013). The second level of integration was performed after qualitative data analysis for the interpretation and reporting of overall findings. Two approaches were used for integration of both findings: the joint display and the narrative weaving approach based on prior research (Draucker et al. 2020; Fetters et al. 2013). Using the joint display approach, qualitative and quantitative findings were presented together in a table to facilitate comparison and interpretation. In the narrative weaving approach, findings from both strands were integrated and described together on a concept‐by‐concept basis for a comprehensive understanding of the study phenomena. The integration results were interpreted as confirmation (when findings from both data strands supported each other), expansion (when findings complemented each other by providing additional insights into different aspects of the phenomenon), or discordance (when qualitative and quantitative findings were inconsistent or contradictory) (Fetters et al. 2013).

Results

Sociodemographic Characteristics

Descriptive statistics of participants are displayed in Table 1. The results indicate that the mean infant age was 9.96 months (SD = 1.424) and the mean age of participants was 29.02 years (SD = 4.631). Participants were ethnically diverse, with 56.5% Brahmin/Chhetri, 36.5% Janatati, 6% Dalit and 1% Madhesi. About 37% of the participants had a high school education. Approximately 53% of participants reported having caesarean delivery, and 90% gave birth in a public hospital. A significantly large proportion (90.0%) of participants were currently breastfeeding or feeding pumped breastmilk to their infants. Thirty‐nine percent of participants reported having anxious thoughts, and 29% reported having depressive thoughts during pregnancy. Fifty nine percent of participants had four or more prenatal check‐ups during their pregnancy.

Qualitative Findings

Theme 1. Mental Health Symptomatology

Qualitative exploration of mental health problems among women 6–12 months postpartum has emerged as a theme, namely ‘Mental Health Symptomatology’ based on subthemes. Findings show that depressive symptoms exist among women during the 6–12 months postpartum period. Some of the participants reported being depressed and experiencing suicidal thoughts during both pregnancy and the postpartum period. They shared their experience of multiple somatic symptoms, self‐harming behaviours, and a lack of strength and confidence. The theme is based on two different subthemes.

Negative Emotions and Self‐Harming Behaviour

Participants shared their experiences of negative emotions such as stress and worry, fear and anxiety, feelings of regret, hopelessness, and disappointment regarding their pregnancy, delivery and care of their infant. Some of the participants reported harming self and experiencing suicidal thoughts related to negative emotions during the postpartum period.When there is a lot of tension, I injure myself, sit and hit the wall. My mind does not work, so I do so. I cry, I cry now and then there is no one to support me and I want to do so. (P8) It was very difficult at that time, it was really difficult at that time, I felt like I wanted to die, that was it. (P12, 207‐8, pp2)

Physical Symptoms and Reality Unacceptance

Along with mental health symptoms, some participants reported experiencing somatic symptoms, such as headaches, eye pain and knee pain. They frequently sought medical help for diverse health problems. Although some reported being referred to a mental health provider, some participants reported not consulting the psychiatrist, despite the recommendations. A participant verbalised,Sometimes I have trouble swallowing my throat, it feels like my throat is obstructed. Ears become dull, headache, eye pain etc. The knee was also painful, and consulted with rheumatic specialist, again nothing was found. The doctor finally advised for psychiatrist consultation. Contributing Factors of Postpartum Depression

Quantitative Findings

The ordinary least squares regression model indicated that LBW was modestly associated with PPD among women 6–12 months postpartum (β = −1.153, SE = 0.683, p < 0.10). Additionally, anxious thoughts during pregnancy significantly predicted a higher risk for PPD (β = 2.758, SE = 0.667, p < 0.01).

In terms of social determinants, results indicated that variables including social support, educational status, exposure to violence and victimisation, and food insecurity significantly predicted PPD in Nepali women 6–12 months postpartum, controlling for several factors in the model. More specifically, exposure to violence from husbands/partners or other individuals was significantly associated with PPD among women (β = 0.330, SE = 0.196, p < 0.10). Women who experienced higher levels of social support from partners/husbands, family, and/or friends during the postpartum period had significantly lower rates of PPD (β = −0.462, SE = 0.155, p < 0.05). Similarly, food insecurity was significantly associated with PPD among women (β = −1.118, SE = 0.316, p < 0.01). Interestingly, results also indicated that higher levels of education among mothers significantly predicted more depressive symptoms among Nepali women (β = 0.520, SE = 0.250, p < 0.05).

Participants who had support from their husbands, in‐laws, parents and other maternal‐side relatives seemed to experience less psychological distress such as stress and a low mood. Theme 3: Socioeconomic Factors

Socioeconomic factors including the infant's sex preference, unplanned pregnancies, IPV, exposure to substance use, and financial hardship play a crucial role in contributing to PPD among women in Nepal. Some participants reported experiencing stress and emotional problems due to unplanned pregnancies, with others facing increased pressure from their husbands regarding infant sex, leading to emotional threats. Violence was often exacerbated by spousal substance abuse, contributing to their fear, helplessness and isolation as they endured physical and emotional abuse. Additionally, financial hardship was a common theme experienced by women who were financially dependent on their husbands, contributing to increased anxiety and depressive symptoms. The interaction of these socioeconomic stressors contributed to feelings of hopelessness, low self‐esteem and emotional distress.

Discussion

The aim of this mixed‐method study was to examine the social and clinical factors contributing to PPD among women 6–12 months postpartum in Nepal and identify their perspectives and/or lived experiences regarding their postpartum mental health. While evidence on the prevalence of PPD during the early postpartum period is available, there is limited data of PPD prevalence and its contributing factors specifically for the 6–12‐month postpartum period, particularly in Nepal. In this study, 28% of women were identified as experiencing symptoms of PPD during 6–12 months postpartum that warranted referral for further evaluation. This finding falls within the previously reported range for Nepal (9.4%–41.5%) (Dielemans et al. 2024; Pudasainee‐Kapri et al. 2024; Subba et al. 2024; Wasti et al. 2024). However, the rate of PPD is higher for women 6–12 months postpartum in Nepal than the 20% prevalence (one in five women) reported for developing countries (WHO 2024) and the pooled prevalence of 23.1% found in a meta‐analysis of PPD in low‐ and middle‐income countries (Roddy Mitchell et al. 2023). These findings are also consistent with longitudinal studies conducted in Italy and Spain, which have reported persistent prevalence of PPD throughout the first year, with the highest rates observed at 12 months postpartum (Míguez and Vázquez 2023; Molgora et al. 2022). Similarly, qualitative findings of this study revealed manifestation of depressive symptoms among women in various forms such as feeling depressed, experiencing suicidal thoughts, and engaging in self‐harming behaviours coupled with multiple somatic symptoms. These findings underscore the burden of perinatal depression throughout the first year postpartum and highlight the need for ongoing screening and preventive interventions (e.g., educational programs) for postpartum women (Míguez and Vázquez 2023; Molgora et al. 2022).

Mixed‐method findings of our study indicated several key factors contributing to depressive symptoms among women 6–12 months postpartum in Nepal. Specifically, the quantitative results indicated that several key clinical and social factors including LBW, anxious thoughts during pregnancy, lack of social support, education status, exposure to violence and victimisation with husband or others, and food insecurity were significant predictors of PPD among women 6–12 months postpartum in Nepal. Consistent with recommendations of prior studies (Draucker et al. 2020; Fetters et al. 2013), the joint display and narrative weaving approaches were used for presenting both quantitative and qualitative findings (Table 4). Notably, the mixed‐method findings revealed a lack of spousal and social support and IPV as important contributing factors to depression in women during the 6–12 months postpartum period. This aligns with a systematic review of 589 studies across 51 countries, which found the highest PPD prevalence (38.9%) among women who experienced IPV during pregnancy and up to 12 months postpartum (Roddy Mitchell et al. 2023). A meta‐analysis of 12 studies further reported that verbal and physical abuse significantly increased the odds of PPD (aOR = 2.89 and 2.29, p = 0.001 and 0.002, respectively) (Putri et al. 2023). Another meta‐analysis found stronger associations between IPV and PPD in low‐ and middle‐income countries compared to high‐income settings (Wang et al. 2021). Our findings are also consistent with other prior studies, indicating that IPV is a significant risk factor for PPD (Abdoli Najmi and Mirghafourvand 2024; Dadi et al. 2020; Mulyani et al. 2023). Other prior studies also indicated IPV as a significant risk factor for PPD (Abdoli Najmi and Mirghafourvand 2024; Dadi et al. 2020; Mulyani et al. 2023). Some participants in this study also reported self‐harming behaviours and violence tendencies, which is similar to recent study results among a Nepali sample (Neupane et al. 2024).

Similar to this study's findings, prior evidence from Nepal and Ghana reported women experiencing a range of negative emotions such as fear, anxiety, frustration, sadness, worthlessness, hopelessness and low self‐esteem during postpartum (Dartey et al. 2024; Holopainen and Hakulinen 2019; Neupane et al. 2024; Subba et al. 2024). In a systematic review by Holopainen and Hakulinen (2019), women expressed feelings of anger, despair, hopelessness and helplessness due to inadequate support from their spouses, families, and significant others and financial worries. Consistent with findings of a prior study in Nepali sample (Subba et al. 2024), postpartum women in this study also reported having various physical symptoms, including headaches, bodyaches, tachycardia, chest heaviness and difficulty breathing requiring frequent medical attention for these complaints. Although a referral for consultation with a psychiatrist were recommended for women with higher depressive symptoms and increased mental, emotional and somatic complaints, many women declined these referrals—likely due to a lack of awareness of mental health services and the stigma associated with mental health care in Nepal. Crying was identified as one of the major coping mechanisms used by participants in our study which aligns with findings from an earlier study among Nepali sample (Subba et al. 2024). Additionally, a study in Ghana reported that some women adopted self‐medication and seeking friends or family support as coping strategies for PPD (Dartey et al. 2024).

A meta‐analysis of 18 cross‐sectional studies (2013–2023) found that high family income significantly reduced the risk of PPD (aOR = 0.50; 95% CI: 0.28–0.90; p = 0.020), identifying low income as a major contributing factor (Mulyani et al. 2023). Consistent with a prior systematic review of 86 studies across various countries (Pan et al. 2024), our mixed‐method findings highlight strong social support, particularly daily family involvement in childcare, as a protective factor against PPD. For instance, spouse support and strong family engagement further contribute to reducing PPD symptoms (Wasti et al. 2024). In many cultural contexts, including Nepal, family support during the early postpartum period is both expected and essential for women's physical and emotional well‐being.

Infant care during the postpartum period is particularly challenging for women, especially first‐time mothers. Consistent with previous findings (Coates et al. 2014; Neupane et al. 2024), in this study, qualitative participants reported experiencing negative emotions and difficulty adjusting to their new role—such as difficulty with breastfeeding—that sometimes led to inadequate bonding with their child. These challenges were more common among mothers of LBW/preterm or sick infants (Neupane et al. 2024), highlighting the need for additional emotional and practical support during the postpartum period for at‐risk women (Coates et al. 2014). Other studies have also identified that infant health‐related complications and LBW/preterm birth as additional risk factors for PPD (Amer et al. 2024; Pudasainee‐Kapri et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2021). Interestingly, women with preterm and low birth weight infants having adequate spouse and family support expressed less stress and good adjustment indicating social support as an important factor. While families focus primarily on the newborn, the mothers' physical and emotional needs are frequently overlooked. Additionally, many women reported feeling unprepared to take care of their infant and handle postpartum challenges, contributing to psychological distress (Neupane et al. 2024). Another study highlighted higher levels of education as protective factors for PPD (Pan et al. 2024). Consistent with findings from prior studies (Dadi et al. 2020; Pan et al. 2024), this study also identified that anxious and depressive thoughts during pregnancy, low family income, partner substance use, male‐child preference, and food insecurity as major risk factors for PPD.

Similarly, a cross‐sectional study among 300 women at a hospital in Kathmandu, Nepal, identified several risk factors for increased prevalence of perinatal depression including unsupportive family members, delivery complications, history of IPV and first pregnancy at age ≤ 25 years (Wasti et al. 2024). Women who participated in the qualitative study also reported emotional distress stemming from difficulty adapting to a different culture or social context of their spouse's home due to inter‐caste marriage, restricted personal freedom, limited decision‐making power and a lack of partner and/or family support. Unsatisfying marital relationships further contributed to feelings of loneliness, sadness and hopelessness (Subba et al. 2024). Likewise, financial constraints or being dependent on their spouse, the burden of household work, unplanned pregnancies, cultural preference for a son, and past traumatic experiences were some of the leading factors for depressive symptoms among postpartum women.

In particular, the findings of this study are also supported by a recent study among 173 postpartum women attending an immunisation clinic in Nepal in which having a female child, unplanned pregnancy, and childbirth‐related health problems were significantly associated with PPD (Bhusal et al. 2023). A prior study of postpartum women 1–6 months postpartum also identified low birth weight and lack of social support as significant factors associated with PPD (Pudasainee‐Kapri et al. 2025). Consistent with these findings, Subba et al. (2024) emphasised that a lack of family support, cultural pressure and partner neglect frequently exacerbated emotional struggles among postpartum women. Although husbands were often seen as a major source of strength, the absence of their support, coupled with poor family dynamics, left many women feeling isolated and hopeless. Prior research described peer support as an accessible, effective and non‐stigmatising form of emotional assistance for postpartum mothers and played a key role in enhancing women's ability to seek and utilise mental health services (Rice et al. 2022). Participants in this study also perceived that peer support and family support were protective factors against their poor postpartum mental health.

Limitations

Although this study offers valuable insights on depression during a later postpartum year, there are some notable limitations. First, the use of self‐reported data on depressive symptoms introduces the potential for recall bias. Similarly, stigma related to mental health issues may impact participants' responses. Likewise, self‐reported data on IPV has the potential for social desirability bias and stigma rooted in patriarchal norms. Second, this study cannot establish causal relationships due to its cross‐sectional design; longitudinal studies with data collected at multiple time points are needed. Third, the study was conducted at the immunisation clinics of two hospitals in Kathmandu using convenience sampling, thereby limiting the generalizability of the findings to all postpartum women in other geographic regions or at the national level.

Conclusion and Implications

To our knowledge, this is the first mixed‐method study that focused on depression among women 6–12 months postpartum in Nepal. Findings from this study highlight the importance of addressing depression among women not only during the immediate postpartum period but throughout the first year following childbirth. Results of this study have important implications to improve maternal mental health, promote healthy families and communities, and influence policymaking regarding practices around perinatal mental health. Most notably, nurses, midwives, obstetric and paediatric providers should conduct routine screening using well‐validated instruments to identify women at‐risk for depression and ensure timely treatment and referral during pregnancy and throughout the first year postpartum. Findings can be used to implement actionable practice change for routine screening at immunisation clinics, strengthening partner and family support throughout the first postpartum year, and enhancing referral pathways for women experiencing depression and family/partner violence. Additionally, societal awareness campaigns are needed to reduce the stigma surrounding mental health issues in Nepal. Culturally tailored, community‐based interventions are also essential in improving maternal mental health, promoting health equity, and enhancing maternal and infant outcomes. These efforts align with Nepal's Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) to improve maternal and child health outcomes. Emerged from this study is the need for additional research examining mechanisms and longitudinal pathways surrounding perinatal depression among women.
