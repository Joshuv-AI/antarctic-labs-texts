SIRT3 activation protects from nabumetone-induced mitochondrial toxicity in adult human cardiomyocytes
Authors: Yafei Huang, Hong Liu, Chao Tong, Zhimin Wang, Miaomiao Xu, Mengqi Dong, Rongjia Rao, Xianqiang Wang, Wei Feng, Zhan Hu, Fei Xu, Wei Zhao, Li Wang, Shengshou Hu, Bingying Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDrug-induced mitochondrial toxicity is a major contributing factor to cardiotoxicity, which can cause drug attrition and adverse cardiac events. To assess the toxicity of anti-inflammatory agents, we used adult human primary cardiomyocytes (hPCMs) to screen 18 clinically available anti-inflammatory drugs in a high-content manner, and revealed widespread mitochondrial dysfunction without affecting cell viability. Nabumetone, a representative nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug with profound mitochondrial toxicity, induced mitochondrial fission, inhibited mitophagy, and impaired both electrophysiological and metabolic functions in adult hPCMs. Mechanistically, we uncovered that nabumetone (Nab) exerted its toxic effects through the prostaglandin E2- E-type prostanoid receptor 4 (PGE2-EP4) pathway, which was essential for its anti-inflammatory functions. To find an alternative route to ameliorate mitochondrial damage, we identified SIRT3 as a downstream target of nabumetone. Its mRNA, protein, and activity levels were significantly reduced upon nabumetone treatment. SIRT3 activator honokiol exhibited protective potential against NSAID-induced mitochondrial toxicity both in hPCMs and in nabumetone-treated mice. Finally, through screening mitochondrial liability in various common cardiomyocyte models, we identified mitochondrial abundance as an important determinant of the sensitivity of cells towards mitochondrial toxicants. Our study demonstrates the vast presence of mitochondrial dysfunction in human adult cardiomyocytes imposed by clinically used anti-inflammatory drugs, and identified both toxicity and protective pathways that may serve future therapeutic purposes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-026-06142-z.Keywords: Adult human primary cardiomyocyte, Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug, Nabumetone, Mitochondrial toxicity, Cell model

Introduction

Cardiovascular toxicity is an important safety concern both during drug development and post-marketing. The failure to detect which can pose an enormous financial burden for both pharmaceutical industries and health care. It has been estimated that cardiovascular adverse drug reactions account for 18.8%−45% of market withdrawals [1, 2]. Drug-induced cardiotoxicity can be caused by various mechanisms, including disruption of electrophysiology, contractility, mitochondrial functioning, growth factor signaling, and cytokine regulation [3]. Mitochondrial toxicity is increasingly recognized as a critical component in drug-induced cardiotoxicity [4].Drug-induced mitochondrial toxicity is often defined as the disruption of mitochondrial function by drugs via multiple mechanisms, including impairment of the electron transport chain, alteration of mitochondrial membrane integrity, overproduction of reactive oxygen species, and inhibition of mitochondrial DNA transcription [5]. Mitochondria occupy 30%−40% of the cellular volume in adult cardiomyocytes, and generate approximately 90% of the total ATP needed for proper cardiac functioning [6, 7]. Therefore, cardiomyocytes are one of the major cellular targets of mitotoxic agents. These agents can trigger pathological changes in the heart, which can, in severe cases, lead to hospitalization or even death. Therefore, identifying and controlling drug-induced mitochondrial toxicity is a critical step toward improving cardiovascular health.

Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are one of the most commonly used medications in the world, with approximately 30 million people consuming them daily [8]. However, NSAIDs have long been known to increase the risk of cardiovascular events, such as heart attack, stroke, and heart failure [9, 10]. The primary mechanisms responsible for the cardiovascular risks associated with NSAIDs include reduced production of vasodilator prostacyclin, as well as increased blood pressure, platelet aggregation, and fluid retention [11]. Besides their classical side effects, mitochondrial toxicity is now increasingly recognized as an adverse effect of NSAIDs [12]. For example, diclofenac was shown to enhance mitochondrial oxidative stress, depolarize cardiac mitochondria, and inhibit mitochondrial complex III in mice [13, 14]. Meclofenamate and naproxen differentially inhibited mitochondrial complexes in rat H9c2 cardiomyoblasts and murine neonatal cardiomyocytes, leading to accumulation of ROS [15]. Isolated rat heart mitochondria treated with naproxen, diclofenac and celecoxib all exhibited increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) formation, mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) collapse, mitochondrial swelling, lipid peroxidation, and glutathione and ATP depletion [16]. However, a systematic understanding of the effect of NSAIDs on mitochondria in cardiomyocytes, particularly adult human cardiomyocytes, is still lacking.

We previously optimized isolation and culture techniques of adult human primary cardiomyocytes (hPCMs), and established high-throughput methods to assess drug-induced mitochondrial toxicity using hPCMs [17, 18]. In this study, we aim to employ this platform to investigate hidden cardiotoxicity of clinically used therapeutics, and decipher their underlying mechanisms. We observed widespread mitochondrial toxicity of anti-inflammatory agents, including both NSAIDs and glucocorticoids. Using nabumetone (Nab) as an example, we found that NSAIDs can perturb cardiomyocyte metabolism and electrophysiology. We also revealed that the PGE2-EP4 pathway was sufficient but not necessary for mitochondrial health in human cardiomyocytes. The SIRT3 activator honokiol demonstrated potential protective effects on NSAID nabumetone-induced mitochondrial toxicity both in vitro and in vivo. Finally, we screened common cardiomyocyte models for their toxic response to anti-inflammatory drugs, and uncovered that mitochondrial abundance may underlie cell sensitivity to mitotoxicants. Together, our study demonstrates the widespread mitochondrial toxicities of anti-inflammatory agents, and provides insight into the mechanisms and potential protective measures of mitochondrial toxicity in human adult cardiomyocytes.

Materials and methods

Human samples

hPCMs used in this study were isolated from the auricular tissues of patients who underwent cardiac surgery at Fuwai Hospital. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fuwai Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (No. 2021 − 1533). All patients in this study signed an informed consent form. This study included samples from a total of 147 patients (127 males and 20 females) with an age range of 22–79 years.

Isolation and culture of adult human primary cardiomyocytes

The isolation of adult hPCMs was performed as previously described [17]. In brief, myocardial tissue was rapidly sectioned and cut into tiny fragments, which were subsequently subjected to enzymatic digestion. Digestion was first performed using protease XXIV (Sigma-Aldrich, P8038, USA) and 200 U/mL type I collagenase (Worthington, LS004196, USA), and then again using type I collagenase for multiple digests. After digestion, hPCMs were centrifuged at 100 × g for 3 min at 22 °C, and then calcium chloride was added gradually to a final concentration of 1.8 mM. After cell counting, hPCMs were inoculated onto culture plates coated with laminin (Thermo, 23017015) and cultured in MEM GlutaMAX™ medium (Gibco, 42360099) containing 1% bovine serum albumin (MP Biomedicals, 199896), 100 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, 15140122) and 10 µM blebbistatin (Selleck, S7099) at 37 °C in a humidified environment with 5% CO2/95% air.

Animals

The experimental animals used in this study were 8-week-old male C57BL/6 mice (weighing 20–30 g), purchased from SPF Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Four to six mice were housed in each cage, with free access to food and water, at a room temperature of 19–23 °C and a 12-h light-dark cycle. All animal protocols complied with all relevant ethical regulations and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Laboratory Animal Center of Fuwai Hospital, National Center for Cardiovascular Diseases (Approval No. 0108−7−1000−ZX).

Eight-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were acclimatized for 1 week and randomly divided into three groups (n = 15 per group): vehicle control, Nab, and Nab+Honokiol. Mice received daily intraperitoneal (i.p.) injections for 14 consecutive days: mice in the Nab group were administered nabumetone at a dose of 100 mg/kg/d; the Nab+Honokiol group received 100 mg/kg/d Nab combined with 40 mg/kg/d honokiol (a SIRT3 agonist); and the control group received the corresponding vehicle. Transthoracic echocardiography was performed noninvasively before and after treatment using a Vevo 2100 Imaging System (VisualSonics, Toronto, Canada) under isoflurane anesthesia to assess left ventricular function. At experimental endpoint, hearts were harvested for transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Cardiomyocytes were isolated via Langendorff perfusion for mitochondrial function analysis using a Seahorse XF Analyzer.

Isolation of adult mouse cardiomyocytes

Adult mouse cardiomyocytes were isolated using a conventional Langendorff perfusion method [18]. In brief, mice were anticoagulated with 5 IU/g heparin by intraperitoneal injection, and were anesthetized with 17.5 mg/mL tribromoethanol (0.2 mL/20 g body weight) 15 min later. After anesthesia, the chest was rapidly opened. The heart was excised and quickly placed in perfusion buffer to remove blood. The aorta was cannulated after trimming the periaortic tissues, and perfused sequentially using a perfusion buffer (120 mM NaCl, 5.4 mM KCl, 1.2 mM MgCl₂, 0.3 mM NaH₂PO₄, 10 mM dextrose, 10 mM HEPES, 10 mM blebbistatin (Bleb) and 10 mM taurine, pH 7.4) and a digestion solution (160 U/mL collagenase II and an equal concentration of BSA in perfusion buffer) for about 17 min until the heart tissue became soft. The heart was then removed from the perfusion apparatus, and placed in a dish filled with digestion buffer for further cutting and dissociation. Following centrifugation at 100 × g for 3 min and discarding the supernatant, cardiomyocytes were resuspended with termination buffer (0.5% BSA in perfusion buffer) to terminate the digestion, centrifuged again at 100 × g for 3 min, and the final pellet was resuspended for subsequent experiments.

Cell culture

Human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes (hiPSC-CMs) were derived from the 5−1 hiPSC cell line constructed previously in our laboratory [17] and were used to assay for Nab-induced mitochondrial toxicity between differentiation days 40–60. For high-content imaging, cells were seeded at a density of 3 × 104 cells/well in Matrigel (Corning, 354234)-coated 384-well plates (PerkinElmer, 6057300) and cultured with RPMI1640 (Gibco, 11875093) and serum-free B27™ (1×) (Thermo Fisher, 1750404).

Mouse cardiomyocyte cell line HL-1 was purchased from Shanghai Enzyme Research Biotechnology Co., Ltd., and cultured in MEM (Gibco, 42360099) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, A5256701) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin.

Rat cardiomyocyte cell line H9c2 and human cardiomyocyte cell line AC16 were purchased from Wuhan Procell Life Sciences Co, and were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, 11966-025) containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and DMEM/F12 (with HEPES) (Gibco, 11330-057) containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, respectively. All cells were cultured in a 5% CO2/95% air incubator at 37 °C.

Mitochondrial membrane potential

Mitochondrial membrane potential was evaluated using the Image-iT™ TMRM reagent (Invitrogen, I34361) following the manufacturer’s protocol. In brief, cells were incubated with a dye mixture containing 100 nM TMRM, 5 µg/mL Hoechst 33342 (Thermo, H21492), and 2 µM nonyl acridine orange (Thermo, A1372), at 37 °C for 15 min, and then briefly rinsed using Hanks’ balanced salt solution (HBSS, Gibco, 14025–092) to remove excess dye. Imaging was performed using the Operetta CLS high-content analysis system (PerkinElmer) or a laser-scanning confocal microscope (Leica, SP8), depending on the specific experiment. Laser wavelengths were set to 530 ± 21 nm for TMRM, 488–495 nm for NAO, and the DAPI channel for Hoechst 33342. Image analysis was performed using the Harmony software (v4.1).

Mitochondrial permeability transition pore opening

Assessment of mitochondrial permeability transition pore opening was performed as previously described [18]. In brief, a mixture of 1 µM Calcein AM (Thermo, 65–0853−78), 3 µM CoCl₂ (Hushi, 10007216), 5 µg/mL Hoechst 33342, and 1 µM MitoTracker Red CMXRos (Thermo, M7512) in HBSS was added dropwise to the drug-treated cells and incubated for 10 min at 37 °C. After incubation, the dye mixture was replaced with fresh HBSS, and incubated at 37 °C protected from light for 30 min to 1 h to allow intracellular esterases to fully hydrolyze Calcein AM. Finally, cells were imaged and analyzed as described above. Laser wavelengths for Calcein AM and MitoTracker Red CMXRos were 495 and 579–599 nm, respectively.

Mitochondrial ROS

Mitochondrial ROS werewas measured using the MitoSOX™ reagent (Thermo, M36008) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were seeded at 3 × 104 cells/cm 2 density in black, clear-bottom 96-well plates (Thermo, 165305). A 5 µM working solution of MitoSOX™ was prepared by diluting the master mix 1000-fold in HBSS. Cells were incubated with 80 µL of the MitoSOX™ working solution at 37 °C for 10 min protected from light. At the end of incubation, the working solution was carefully discarded and HBSS was added to wash the cells. Then, red fluorescence intensity was detected by using a plate reader (BioTek, Synergy LX). A group of untreated cells was set up as a control in order to assess the effect of drugs or treatments on cellular ROS level.

High-content imaging

High-content imaging and analysis were performed as previously described [18]. In brief, imaging settings were optimized for each individual readout: mitochondrial membrane potential analysis was performed by using a 20 × air objective (with a numerical aperture (NA) of 0.6), and images were scanned at 16 fields of view per aperture; and the mPTP detection was performed by using a 40 × air objective, scanning images in 25 fields of view per well. Mitochondrial mass was measured by calculating the total area and number of all NAO-stained mitochondria in the cardiomyocyte, which were automatically calculated by the Harmony software. It should be noted that the imaging parameters should be adjusted according to the cell type and experimental requirements to capture crisp images, and the appropriate analysis module and parameters should be selected according to the experimental purpose during data analysis. The number of mitochondria in Fig.7 was calculated by randomly counting the number of mitochondria in 500 cells.

qPCR

Cells were harvested 24–48 h after drug treatment. RNA was extracted with Trizol (Life, 15596026) using a previously described method [18] and stored at −80 °C. RNA concentration was determined by NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo, 0213096032), and reverse transcribed using the Script cDNA Synthesis Kit (BioRad, 1708891). After determining the cDNA concentration, qPCR was performed in a 384-well (Thermo, 4309849) format using the iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix kit (BioRad, 1725124) on a QuantStudio™ 7 Pro Flex system (Thermo, 0222120312) with GAPDH as the internal reference. Detailed information regarding primers used in this study is included in the Supplementary materials (Table S2).

Western blotting

Western blotting of hPCMs was performed as previously described [18]. In brief, cells were lysed using RIPA buffer (150 mM NaCl, 1% Nonidet P-40, 0.5% Sodium deoxycholate (DOC), 0.1% SDS, 25mM Tris (pH 7.4)) containing 1 × Protease and Phosphatase inhibitor (Thermo, 1861281). Protein concentration was determined using a BCA kit (Beyotime, P0012). An appropriate volume of 5 × sample buffer (300 mM Tris (pH 6.8), 10% SDS, 50% Glycerol, 0.0125% bromophenol blue, 15% β-mercaptoethanol) was added to the cell lysate, which was then boiled for 5 min to denature. Following electrophoresis, proteins were transferred onto a PVDF membrane (Merck, IPVH00010), and then blocked in 5% skimmed milk (OXOID, LP0033B) in PBST (0.15 M NaCl, 20 mM Tris base, 0.1% Tween-20, pH 7.5) for 60 min at room temperature. After blocking, membranes were incubated with diluted primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, and then with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature the next day. Finally, blots were developed using an ECL substrate (BioRad, 1705060), and imaged on ChemiDoc XRS+ System (BioRad, 0219110036). Western blots were quantified using the Image Lab software (BioRad, v6.1). Detailed information regarding primary and secondary antibodies used in this study is included in the Supplementary materials (Table S3).

Immunofluorescence staining

Immunofluorescence staining for hPCMs was performed as described previously [18]. In brief, mitochondria were labeled with 2 µM nonyl acridine orange (NAO, Thermo, A1372), and incubated for 15 min at 37 °C in the dark. Subsequently, cells were fixed with 4% PFA for 10 min at room temperature, gently washed with pre-cooled PBS, and permeated with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Sigma, T8787) in PBS (Gibco, 20012-027) for 7 min. Then, cells were blocked with a blocking solution (1% BSA, 22.52 mg/mL glycine in PBST) for 30 min at room temperature. Samples were then sequentially incubated with primary antibodies (overnight at 4 °C) and secondary antibodies (1 h at 37 °C) before mounting with Prolong™ Glass Antifade Mountant with NucBlue™ (Thermo, P36981). Detailed information regarding primary and secondary antibodies used in this study is included in the Supplementary materials (Table S3). Images were taken on a laser-scanning confocal microscope (Leica, SP8) or a STED (Stimulated emission depletion) system (Leica TCS SP8 STED). Co-localization analysis was performed using Image J (v1.8.0.112).

Seahorse assay

Seahorse assays were performed as previously described. In brief, hPCMs were seeded in Seahorse XF96 cell culture plates at 5 × 10³ cells/well in 100 µL medium per well, with 4 replicate wells per group, cultured and treated in an incubator at 37 °C with CO₂. The cells were quantified with PrestoBlue (Thermo, P50201) prior to the experiment. An assay medium containing 1 mM pyruvate, 2 mM glutamine, 10 mM glucose and DMEM (Agilent, 103575−100) was prepared and placed in a CO2-free incubator. At the time of assay, cell medium was changed to the assay medium, and oxygen consumption was recorded at baseline, as well as following sequential injections of oligomycin (20 µM), FCCP (10 µM), rotenone and antimycin A (5 µM) on a Seahorse XF Pro analyzer (Agilent). Data analysis was performed using the Wave pro software (Agilent).

Whole-cell patch clamping

Electrophysiologic recordings were performed under visual control under the microscope. The borosilicate glass microelectrodes used had a tip resistance of 2 −5 MΩ when injected into the pipette solution, which consisted of the following (mM): potassium aspartate 110, KCl 20, MgCl2 1, Na2ATP 5, HEPES 5, and EGTA 10 (pH 7.2, adjusted with 1 M KOH). Action potentials were recorded using the whole-cell patch method at 35 −37 ℃, reaching 1 GΩ or more to break the membrane, with the following bath solution (mM): NaCl 136, KCl 5, EGTA 10 (pH 7.2, adjusted with 1 M KOH): evoked action potentials were recorded in current mode. Cells were kept at 0 pA and action potentials were evoked by injecting a current into the cells for 1000 ms to find the rheobase current, which was recorded for more than 30 s. Electrophysiologic signals were recorded using the amplifier EPC10. Offset potentials were zeroed directly before forming a seal. No leakage subtraction was performed. Data were first stored with Patchmaster, then formatted with the ABF file utility and analyzed with Clampfit.

Transmission electron microscopy

Transmission electron microscopy was performed as previously described [18]. hPCMs or tissues were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde (v/v) (Sigma-Aldrich 111−30−8) in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (PB, pH 7.4), followed by two rinses in PBS and double-distilled H2O, respectively. Samples were then immersed in 1% (w/v) osmium tetroxide (OsO₄) and 1.5% (w/v) potassium ferrocyanide aqueous solution (Sigma, BCBV7953) at 4 ℃ for 2 h. After washing, samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, 100%×3 for 10 min each at 4 ℃), followed by two 10-min acetone rinses. Specimens were subsequently infiltrated with graded mixtures of acetone and Embed 812 resin (composition: 20 ml 812 resin, 9 ml DDSA, 8 ml NMA) using sequential ratios of 3:1, 1:1, and 1:3 (v/v), followed by pure resin immersion. The tissues were then embedded in pure resin with 1.5% N, N-dimethylbenzylamine (BDMA, SPI-CHEM, 02821-CA) and polymerized for 12 h at 45 °C, followed by 48 h at 60 °C. Ultrathin sections (70−nm thickness) were obtained using an ultramicrotome (Leica EM UC6), double-stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Imaging was performed using a transmission electron microscope (Tecnai Spirit120kV, Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with an EMSIS CCD camera (VELETA). TEM images were taken at the Center for Biological Imaging (CBI), Institute of Biophysics, Chinese Academy of Sciences.

Fluorescent non-canonical amino acid tagging

Fluorescent non-canonical amino acid tagging was performed as previously described [19]. Cells were incubated in pre-warmed Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS, Thermo Fisher,14025-002) for the labeling procedure. To inhibit cytoplasmic translation, cells were treated with 50 µg/mL cycloheximide (CHX; Aladdin, C729197) for 20 min. For specific inhibition of mitochondrial translation, 150 µg/mL chloramphenicol (CHL; OKA, D11163) was added 50 min prior to the start of labeling. Subsequently, 500 µM L-homopropargylglycine (HPG, Thermo Fisher, C10186) was added, and cells were incubated for 30 min.

Following HPG incorporation, cells were permeabilized in ice-cold Buffer A (containing PBS, 0.015% digitonin, 1% BSA, 0.1% Tween 20, and 0.1 M glycine) for 2 min, then quickly rinsed in Buffer A without digitonin for 15 s. Cells on coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (in PBS, pH 7.5) for 10 min at room temperature. After fixation, coverslips were washed with PBS for 5 min, followed by quenching with 100 mM NH₄Cl in PBS for 15 min.

Blocking and permeabilization were performed in staining solution (PBS containing 5% BSA, 5% tryptone peptone, and 0.1% Triton X-100) with three 5-min washes with the solution. After a brief wash with PBS containing 3% BSA, click chemistry was performed using a commercial kit (Click-iT Cell Reaction Buffer Kit, Thermo Fisher, C10269) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The reaction mixture contained 3 µM AlexaFluor 647-azide (Thermo Fisher, A10277) and was carried out for 30 min. Following another quick wash with PBS containing 3% BSA, cells were incubated with Anti-TOM20 antibody (Abcam, ab56783) at 4℃ overnight. After primary antibody incubation, coverslips were washed three times for 5 min each with staining solution. Secondary antibody incubation was carried out for 1 h at room temperature. A comprehensive washing series was then performed: three 5-min washes with blocking solution (PBS with 5% BSA and 5% tryptone peptone), three 5-min washes with high-salt PBS (PBS containing 500 mM NaCl), and three final 5-min washes with PBS. Coverslips were mounted using DAPI-Fluoromount-G mounting medium for subsequent imaging analysis. Images were taken on a laser-scanning confocal microscope (Leica Stellaris). HPG intensity mean analysis was performed using Image J (v1.8.0.112).

Correlation analysis

In this study, we used GraphPad Prism 8.0 software for Pearson correlation analysis and linear regression analysis. For Pearson correlation analysis, we evaluated the predictive efficacy of mitochondrial toxicity in commonly used cardiomyocyte models (including AC16, HL-1, H9c2, mPCM, and hiPSC-CMs) and human primary cardiomyocytes, and calculated the correlation coefficient (r). Then, we calculated the correlation between Pearson similarity and log10 of mitochondial number using linear regression analysis. The goodness of fit of the model was assessed by the r value.

Statistical analysis

All data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean. Statistical tests were performed using GraphPad Prism version 8.0. For statistical analyses, data were analyzed using two-tailed Student’s t-test for two groups (unpaired or paired, depending on data type), or one-way ANOVA for multiple groups(ordinary or repeated measures, depending on data type). Significance levels are indicated by asterisks (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001). Schematics were drawn using Adobe Illustrator 2023 and BioRender.

Results

Adult human primary cardiomyocyte-based mitochondrial toxicity screening of anti-inflammatory drugs

To screen for potential cardiac toxicities of anti-inflammatory agents, we selected 18 marketed anti-inflammatory drugs from different categories, including 12 NSAIDs and 6 glucocorticoids (Fig. 1A and Table S1). First, we determined whether these drugs induced cytotoxicity by measuring cell viability at 10 µM treated for 48 h. None of these agents significantly reduced cell viability, and their mean viability value was all greater than 85% of that of control (Fig. 1B). These results show that the anti-inflammatory drugs did not compromise the viability of adult human cardiomyocytes, which was expected, given that these were all marketed drugs that had passed major safety tests.

However, given the accumulating evidence of mitochondrial toxicities of NSAIDs, we set up screens to test for their effects on mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) opening, mitochondrial abundance using high-content imaging, as well as mitochondrial ROS (mtROS) in a high-throughput manner [18] (Fig. 1A).

Except for aspirin, phenylbutazone, and mefenamic acid, all other drugs led to decreases > 20% in MMP, with nabumetone reaching a 63.49% decrease (Fig. 1C). Of these, 10 reached statistical significance. Eight NSAIDs and 4 glucocorticoids led to > 20% decreases in calcein intensity, indicating mPTP opening, although none reached statistical significance (Fig. 1D). Five NSAIDs (diclofenac acid, ibuprofen, naproxen, piroxicam and nabumetone) and 4 glucocorticoids (methylprednisolone, deflazacort, betamethasone, and dexamethasone) led to > 15% decreases in mitochondrial number/cell (Fig. 1E). Mitochondrial areas exhibited similar trends (Fig. 1F). Of all drugs, nabumetone induced the greatest reductions in both mitochondrial number and area, suggesting a strong mitotoxic effect. In contrast to the above parameters, all drugs led to marked increases in mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS), suggesting that anti-inflammatory agents generally induce mitochondrial oxidative stress in adult hPCMs (Fig. 1G).

These findings indicate that many anti-inflammatory drugs are mitochondrial toxicants, but not general cytotoxicants, as they disrupt mitochondrial health at concentrations lower than those that induce cell death [20, 21].

Since we only included a single COX-2-specific inhibitor celecoxib in the primary screen, we selected two additional marketed COX-2 inhibitors, parecoxib and etoricoxib, to test whether there were differences in mitochondrial toxicities between non-selective and COX-2-selective inhibitors. While celecoxib led to non-significant reduction in MMP, both parecoxib and etoricoxib significantly reduced MMP, suggesting that both non-selective and COX-2-selective inhibitors can impair mitochondrial health (Fig. S1). Together, these data demonstrate that a significant proportion of anti-inflammatory agents have a detrimental effect on mitochondrial health in adult human cardiomyocytes.

Nabumetone perturbs mitochondrial dynamics and quality control in adult human cardiomyocytes

Of the tested anti-inflammatory drugs, nabumetone was one of the drugs that caused the greatest impairments in mitochondrial well-being. Therefore, we used nabumetone as a representative mitotoxicant to more comprehensively study drug-induced mitochondrial toxicity.

In response to stress, mitochondria often undergo hyperfission [22]. Therefore, we examined expression and phosphorylation levels of fission-related proteins, including DRP1 and MFF. Protein expression and total phosphorylation levels of both proteins significantly increased in response to nabumetone treatment (Fig. 2A and B), suggesting increased mitochondrial fission. Immunofluorescence also showed increased levels of DRP1, as well as enhanced co-localization of DRP1 with mitochondrial marker TOM20 (translocase of outer mitochondrial membrane 20) (Fig. 2C and D). Consistently, ultrastructural analysis using transmission electron microscopy also showed that area of single mitochondria decreased upon nabumetone treatment (Fig. 2E and F). In accordance with screening results, mitochondrial number per cell was reduced in nabumetone-treated cells (Fig. 2F). By contrast, mitochondrial fusion-related proteins, including OPA1, MFN1, and MFN2, all displayed significant decreases following nabumetone treatment, indicating reduced mitochondrial fusion (Fig. 2G-I).

The reduced number of mitochondria observed with nabumetone treatment led us to hypothesize that mitochondria had undergone mitophagy by 48 h, possibly as a result of mitochondrial stress. Autophagy marker LC3BII exhibited significant increase at 24 h (Fig. 2J). Mitochondria and lysosomes also showed dose-dependent increases in co-localization at 24 h, which was absent at 48 h (Fig. 2K and L). However, p62, which is degraded during autophagy, displayed significant increase, suggesting inhibition of autophagy (Fig. 2M). We then used bafilomycin A1, an inhibitor of autophagosome-lysosome fusion, and observed that LC3BII levels were not further enhanced with nabumetone treatment, suggesting that autophagic flux was inhibited upon nabumetone treatment (Fig. 2N).

Some mitochondrial biogenesis-related genes showed decreased expression after nabumetone treatment, but few reached statistical significance (e.g., MRPL24, PGC1α) (Fig. S2A and 2B). Furthermore, protein expression levels of MRPL24 significantly decreased in response to nabumetone treatment (Fig. S2J and S2K). Given the established role of MRPL24 in mitochondrial translation, its downregulation at the protein level may affect mitochondrial protein translation. To directly test this hypothesis, we performed fluorescent non-canonical amino acid tagging (FUNCAT) assay in hPCMs. Treatment with nabumetone led to a marked decrease in the incorporation of the methionine analog L-homopropargylglycine (HPG), a specific indicator of newly synthesized proteins, within mitochondria (Fig. S2L and S2M).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that nabumetone treatment perturbed mitochondrial dynamics and quality control mechanisms in adult human cardiomyocytes, contributing to mitochondrial dysfunction.

Nabumetone impairs metabolic and electrophysiological function of adult human cardiomyocytes

Next, we sought to examine whether cardiomyocyte function was impaired following mitochondrial dysfunction. We first measured cellular oxygen consumption using the Seahorse assay. Both maximal respiration and spare respiratory capacity were significantly impaired upon nabumetone treatment (Fig. 3A and B).

The perturbed cellular metabolism led us to further assess the activities of mitochondrial complexes using a modified Seahorse assay [23].It was previously shown that NSAIDs affected complex I and III [15, 24]. However, in adult human cardiomyocytes, we observed that the activities of complexes II and complex III were inhibited, while those of complexes I, IV and V, remained intact (Fig. 3C and D). To further confirm this result, we isolated mitochondria from hPCMs, and measured the activity of complexes II and III using a colorimetric assay. Consistently, we observed significant reduction in complex II activity, as well as a decreasing trend of complex III activity (Fig. 3E).

Next, we measured the electrophysiological properties of nabumetone-treated cardiomyocytes. Upon nabumetone treatment, we observed significant decreases in action potential amplitude, depolarization velocity, as well as action potential durations, indicating impaired electrophysiology of adult human cardiomyocytes (Fig. 3F and G).

Together, these findings demonstrate metabolic and electrophysiological aberrancies upon nabumetone treatment, suggesting functional impairment of hPCMs.

The PGE2-EP4 pathway is critical to mitochondrial dysfunction in human cardiomyocytes

The major mechanism of action of NSAIDs is to inhibit prostanoid production via suppression of COX enzyme activity. We first examined whether the synthases of the various prostanoids are expressed in hPCMs. All synthases were expressed in hPCMs (Fig. S3A). Next, we measured the levels of their respective products, i.e., PGE2, PGD2, TBXA2, PGF2α, and PGI2, in the cardiomyocyte supernatant after nabumetone treatment. Among all prostanoids measured, only PGE2 exhibited a significant reduction after treatment with nabumetone, suggesting that this prostaglandin may mediate the mitochondrial effect of nabumetone (Fig. 4A). To test this, we treated cells with PGE2 at varying concentrations. PGE2 fully rescued the collapse in mitochondrial membrane potential, and partially rescued mPTP opening (Fig. 4B and C). These observations suggested that impaired PGE2 signaling pathway may be responsible for nabumetone-induced mitochondrial toxicity.

Further downstream, the biological effects of PGEs are mediated by signaling through four distinct E-type prostanoid (EP) receptors 1–4 (EP1−4). It was previously shown that EP3 and EP4 were the most abundant EP receptors in the heart, and their expression was frequently altered in cardiac diseases [25]. Similar to PGE2 itself, and EP4 receptor agonist (compound 31) fully rescued MMP collapse. It also demonstrated a rescuing trend for mPTP opening and mitochondrial ROS production (Fig. 4D and E). EP3 agonist sulprostone exhibited similar, but milder, effects on MMP and mPTP. mtROS was also partially dampened upon EP4 agonist, but not EP3 agonist, treatment (Fig. 4F). We also tested whether EP4 agonism also rescued mitochondrial impairment caused by other NSAIDs. All NSAIDs that previously exhibited marked reductions in MMP, did not reduce MMP in the presence of EP4 agonist, indicating that the PGE2-EP4 pathway may be a common pathway to restore mitochondrial health (Fig. S3B).

On the contrary, we also used EP3 and EP4 receptor antagonists (DG-041 and ONO-AE3-208) to investigate the necessity of these pathways. EP4 antagonism with ONO-AE3-208 significantly induced mPTP opening, and showed trends towards decreased MMP and increased mtROS (Fig. 4G). EP3 antagonism using DG-041 displayed similar, but milder, trends. Together, these results showed that the PGE2-EP4 pathway was critical in mediating the mitotoxic response of nabumetone in hPCMs.

Next, we sought to test whether EP4 agonism also rescued cardiomyocyte dysfunction. EP4 agonist showed a trend towards rescuing maximal respiration and spare respiratory capacity (Fig. 4H and I). It also rescued complex II and III activities to control level, while not impacting the other complexes (Fig. 4J-L). However, EP4 agonist failed to show any effect on modifying the action potentials (Fig. 4M-O). Together, these findings showed that the PGE2-EP4 pathway exerted a regulatory effect on hPCM mitochondrial metabolism, but not cellular electrophysiology.

The observation of suppressed activities of complexes II and III upon nabumetone treatment led us to examine the protein expression of their critical subunits. Complex II subunits SDHA and SDHB, as well as complex III subunit CYB, did not show altered protein expression (Fig. 5A). SIRT3 (sirtuin 3) is an established regulator of mitochondrial health, and was shown to regulate the activities of mitochondrial complexes [26, 27]. Interestingly, SIRT3 expression was reduced at both the protein and mRNA level following nabumetone treatment (Fig. 5C and D). Accordingly, SIRT3 activity was also compromised (Fig. 5E).

To this end, we used honokiol, a well-established SIRT3 activator, to salvage mitochondrial aberrancies. As anticipated, honokiol fully rescued the drop in MMP caused by nabumetone (Fig. 5F). It also significantly improved maximal respiration, and demonstrated a trend of rescuing spare respiratory capacity, to a level that was insignificantly different from control (Fig. 5G). Furthermore, honokiol restored the drop in complex II activity, and showed a trend toward rescuing that of complex III. In addition, unlike EP4 agonist, honokiol showed a trend toward rescuing the alterations in cardiomyocyte electrophysiology imposed by nabumetone, including action potential amplitude, action potential duration, and depolarization velocity (Fig. 5I).

These findings suggest that SIRT3 potentially mediated some of the deleterious effects of nabumetone treatment, and that SIRT3 activation may provide a means to compensate for nabumetone-induced functional impairment in cardiomyocytes without negating its on-target actions.

Honokiol rescues nabumetone-induced mitochondrial toxicity in vivo

To verify these findings in vivo, we administered nabumetone at a dosage of 100 mg/kg for two weeks (control group and nabumetone group), and used honokiol as a mitoprotectant in a third group (nabumetone + honokiol) (Fig. 6A). The maximum recommended human dose is 2000 mg per day, which corresponds to approximately 351.4 mg/kg in mice.

Similar to observations in hPCMs, SIRT3 expression in mouse cardiac tissues showed a decreasing trend upon Nab treatment, while honokiol did not directly impact its expression level (Fig. 6B and C). Then, we examined mitochondria of cardiomyocytes by transmission electron microscopy. Mitochondria in the nabumetone group demonstrated marked mitochondrial disarray and fragmentation, while honokiol partially rescued these phenotypes (Fig. 6D). Areas of single mitochondria and average mitochondrial densities decreased in nabumetone-treated mice, but were respectively fully and partially restored when honokiol was co-administered (Fig. 6E).

To further examine mitochondrial fitness, we also isolated ventricular cardiomyocytes. Similar to hPCMs, nabumetone showed a trend toward reduced MMP, while the addition of honokiol exhibited an increasing trend (Fig. 6F). However, the changes in mPTP opening and ROS production, as observed in hPCMs, were not present in isolated mouse cardiomyocytes (Fig. 6G and H). Strikingly, nabumetone significantly suppressed maximal respiration, which was prevented by honokiol (Fig. 6I and J). Similar, but milder, effects were observed with spare respiratory capacity.

We assessed cardiac function at baseline and at experimental endpoint. Neither nabumetone nor nabumetone+honokiol induced any changes in echocardiographic parameters during this time frame, suggesting that nabumetone did not induce organ-level dysfunction despite marked mitochondrial dysfunction (Fig. S4). Together, these findings demonstrate that while nabumetone did not induce cardiac dysfunction, it led to metabolic changes in cardiomyocytes.

Mitochondrial abundance potentially underlies predictability of drug-induced mitochondrial toxicity in common cardiomyocyte models

Finally, to investigate whether other cardiomyocyte models recapitulate the drug-induced mitochondrial toxicity detected in adult hPCMs, we selected the human cardiomyocyte cell line AC16, the immortalized mouse cardiomyocyte cell line HL-1, the rat cardiomyocyte cell line H9c2, adult mouse primary cardiomyocytes (mPCMs), as well as human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes (hiPSC-CMs) to perform the same drug screen (Fig. 7A-E). Interestingly, HL-1 demonstrated a pattern that closely resembled hPCMs. Fifteen out of 18 drugs caused decreased MMP by over 20%, of which nabumetone reached 48.21%. Similar, but milder, trends were observed in mPCMs and AC16 cells. Pearson correlation of quantified mitochondrial responses to drugs showed that HL-1 and mPCMs were most closely correlated with hPCMs (Fig. 7F).

To understand the cellular basis of this correlation, we hypothesized that mitochondrial density may underlie cellular sensitivity. To this end, we quantified mitochondrial number per cell, and ranked them in a descending order (Fig. 7G). We observed that this ranking resembled the drug response similarities. Therefore, we performed linear regression of similarity and mitochondrial abundance, and revealed a correlation coefficient of 0.7714 (Fig. 7H). These results imply that mitochondrial density of cardiomyocyte models may determine cellular sensitivity to mitochondrial toxicants.

Discussion

Drug-induced mitochondrial toxicity in cardiomyocytes has gained less attention than proarrhythmic risk during drug development, and yet is undeniably an important factor in drug-induced cardiotoxicity. Development of new drugs and clinical management of existing medications may benefit from recognizing, detecting and screening for such hidden toxicity.

In this study, we utilized adult human primary cardiomyocytes (hPCMs) as the cellular model, for which we have previously established relevant methodologies [17, 18]. Adult cardiomyocytes are different from many other cardiomyocyte models in terms of mitochondrial abundance and dynamics, and thus may differ in their susceptibility to mitotoxicants. Using this model, we uncovered noticeable mitochondrial toxicity of some clinically used anti-inflammatory drugs. They not only directly damage mitochondria, but also exert deleterious effects at the cellular level, including compromising cellular metabolism and electrophysiology.

Mechanistically, we revealed that the PGE2-EP4 pathway mediates some of the mitotoxic effects of nabumetone, suggesting that this pathway is essential to mitochondrial health. In past studies, PGE2 was reported to impair mitochondrial fitness and function in T cells, macrophages, and microglia [28–31]. However, in skeletal muscle, PGE2 augmented mitochondrial function, and ameliorated muscle atrophy [32]. Cardiomyocyte-specific deletion of EP4 receptor in mice exhibited mitochondrial disarray and disordered cristae, suggesting an important role of the PGE2-EP4 pathway in mitochondrial health [33].Therefore, our study sheds light on the role of this pathway in human cardiomyocytes, and how NSAIDs potentially injure mitochondria in human cardiomyocytes.

While EP4 agonism partially restores mitochondrial health in the presence of nabumetone, this method cannot be implemented therapeutically, because it resides downstream of the on-target effect of COX inhibitors. Alternatively, we explored the possibility to activate SIRT3, which is a known regulator of mitochondrial health, and was inhibited upon nabumetone treatment. Activation of SIRT3 not only ameliorated nabumetone-induced mitochondrial dysfunction, but also prevented electrophysiological alterations that EP4 agonism failed to rescue. These findings highlight SIRT3 activation as a promising means to attenuate cardiac toxicity imposed by NSAIDs.

Although our study did not delve into the mechanisms by which nabumetone inhibited SIRT3 expression, some studies using other NSAIDs made similar observations. Indomethacin was shown to competitively inhibit SIRT3 by binding to its nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD)-binding site, and to downregulate transcriptional regulators of SIRT3, namely PGC1α and ERRα [24, 34]. A decrease in PGC1α mRNA was also observed in our study. Furthermore, it remains to be determined whether the reduction in SIRT3 expression is a common path for all NSAIDs. It would also be interesting to explore whether crosstalk exists between the PGE2-EP4 pathway and SIRT3 in the future.

Interestingly, while most genes related to mitochondrial biogenesis remain unchanged, mitochondrial ribosomal protein MRPL24 was significantly downregulated upon nabumetone treatment, both at the transcriptional and translational levels. Global mitochondrial protein translation was also impaired, suggesting that it is a part of the mitochondrial toxicity response. However, the specific pathway of MRPL24 inhibition, as well as the impact of the suppression of mitochondrial translation, remains to be determined.

Finally, although many other cardiomyocyte models are routinely used in cardiac studies, little is known about their differential responses to drugs. By focusing on mitochondrial toxicity specifically, we identified unexpectedly identified HL-1, a mouse cardiac muscle cell line, as the cell model that most closely mimicked hPCMs when challenged with anti-inflammatory agents [35]. The linear correlation between mitochondrial response and abundance underscores the importance of considering cell models with high mitochondrial density when testing the cardiac safety of drugs.

In summary, we show that anti-inflammatory drugs used in the clinic, particularly nabumetone, can trigger mitochondrial and cardiomyocyte dysfunction in the absence of cell death, which can be partially restored upon SIRT3 activation. These results unveil cardiac toxicities of drugs that may elude clinical observations and monitoring, and suggest potential therapeutic options for cardioprotection.
