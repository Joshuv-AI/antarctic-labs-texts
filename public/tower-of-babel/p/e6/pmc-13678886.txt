Technical feasibility of an angle-free ultrasound-assisted approach for challenging lumbar puncture in complex spinal anatomy: a two-patient case series
Authors: Xuan Wang, Yijun Luo, Xiaolong Zhang, Xiaolin Lu, Daniel A. Diedrich, Chanyan Huang, Ying Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLumbar puncture is particularly challenging in patients with abnormal spinal anatomy, such as severe scoliosis or obesity. Conventional ultrasound‑assisted neuraxial techniques have two fundamental limitations: inaccurate projection of the probe’s geometric center onto the skin when the probe is tilted and difficulty replicating the exact probe angle during needle insertion. These limitations often result in multiple needle passes, prolonged procedure time, and increased patient discomfort and complication risk. We developed an ultrasound‑assisted technique designed to standardize needle trajectory by eliminating the need for lateromedial and craniocaudal angulation through positional adjustment and report its technical feasibility in a case series.Case presentationThis angle‑free technique was applied to two patients with spinal muscular atrophy requiring repeated intrathecal nusinersen injections. Both patients had extremely complex spinal anatomy, including high body mass index (30 and 35.1 kg/m²) and severe scoliosis (Cobb angles 90° and 83°). The technique followed a four‑step protocol: (1) planning using available imaging; (2) ultrasound‑guided positioning; (3) marking the needle insertion point; and (4) inserting the needle parallel to the floor and perpendicular to the bed edge without lateromedial or craniocaudal angulation. All 22 procedures (11 per patient) were completed successfully without persistent neurological complications. First‑attempt success rates were 81.8% in Case 1 and 100% in Case 2, with first‑pass success rates of 45.5% and 54.5%, respectively. Longitudinal data across sequential procedures showed a rapid decreasing trend in both the number of needle passes and needling time.ConclusionsThis angle‑free ultrasound‑assisted lumbar puncture appears technically feasible for patients with complex spinal anatomy. The structured “4P” protocol (planning, positioning, perpendicular, and parallel) may offer a practical guide for clinical adoption. The observed reduction in needle passes and needling time may suggest potential learnability, but this finding is preliminary and requires confirmation. Further investigation is warranted to definitively establish its learning curve.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12871-026-04079-1.

Background

Lumbar puncture (LP) carries inherent procedural risks, which are significantly amplified in patients with challenging spinal anatomy. Technical challenges, often characterized by multiple needle insertion attempts, can lead to considerable patient anxiety, discomfort, and an increased risk of complications such as post-dural puncture headache, neurological injury, and epidural hematomas [1, 2].

In obese patients, these challenges are compounded by two key anatomical factors: increased skin-to-thecal sac distance and excessive subcutaneous tissue mobility. These factors obscure surface landmarks, magnify the impact of angular deviation, and compromise the accuracy of ultrasound-guided skin markings [3, 4].

Although ultrasound guidance has been shown to improve success rates for LP in difficult anatomical conditions [5–9], conventional ultrasound-assisted techniques remain constrained by their angle-dependent nature [10, 11]. In these approaches, the probe’s center marks the needle entry point, and its angulation determines the needle trajectory. This reliance on probe angulation introduces two critical limitations. First, when the probe is tilted, its geometric center is projected inaccurately onto the skin due to parallax. Second, replicating the exact probe angle during needle advancement is technically challenging. These limitations are especially problematic in obese patients, where the margin for angular error is narrow and any misalignment is amplified at depth.

To address these issues, we developed an angle-free, ultrasound-assisted approach designed to eliminate the need for probe angulation and to enable a standardized needle trajectory (parallel to the floor and perpendicular to the bed edge). This report describes the technical details and clinical strategies for performing LP in patients with obesity and scoliosis. In addition to describing the technique, we present a longitudinal evaluation of its performance across sequential procedures to assess the acquisition of proficiency.

Case presentation

This report describes two patients diagnosed with spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) who required repeated intrathecal nusinersen administration. All 22 procedures (11 per patient) were performed every four months. Written informed consent was obtained for the inclusion of videos, images, and clinical descriptions. The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University. A video camera recorded all LP procedures. Two investigators independently reviewed the video recordings at a later time; any discrepancies were resolved by consensus with a third senior investigator. Case 1

A 27-year-old male with SMA type 2 was referred for repeated intrathecal nusinersen injections on a four-month schedule. He had severe muscle weakness (unable to maintain a sitting position) and severe thoracolumbar scoliosis (Cobb angle 90°). The angle-free, ultrasound-assisted lumbar puncture was performed using the following four-step protocol.

Planning using available imaging

CT imaging showed that the L5/S1 interlaminar space was the widest; however, near‑complete obliteration of the dural sac at this level made it unsuitable for puncture. Among accessible levels, the right L3/4 interlaminar space was chosen as the optimal puncture site because of its relatively larger width (4.6 mm) and preserved dural sac cross‑sectional area (Fig. 1a-d).

To simulate the right lateral decubitus position for virtual planning, the axial CT image at L3-4 was rotated 90° counterclockwise. This simulation indicated that a standard lateral‑to‑medial angulation of 10° would typically be required to reach the center of the spinal canal from a lateral entry point. To avoid having to reproduce this angulation during needle insertion, a 10° anterior oblique patient position was planned. This positioning induced vertebral rotation, enabling a horizontal needle trajectory from a lateral entry point (Fig. 1e-e’).

A sagittal reconstruction along this planned horizontal trajectory confirmed that moving the patient’s hips forward relative to the bed edge would allow the needle path to be perpendicular to the bed edge (Fig. 1f).

Ultrasound-guided positioning

The patient was initially placed in the standard right lateral decubitus position, with shoulders and hips aligned parallel to the bed edge (Fig. 2a). Preprocedural scanning was performed using a low-frequency (5 − 2 MHz) curvilinear transducer, following established protocols for scoliosis [12].

During the paramedian sagittal lamina scan, we adjusted the probe orientation to optimize visualization of the anterior complex (AC). According to ultrasound physics, the clearest image and strongest echo signal are obtained when the ultrasound beam is perpendicular to the target structure [13, 14]. Because the posterior aspect of the L4 vertebral body was oblique relative to the bed edge (Fig. 2b), we rocked the probe caudally to achieve a perpendicular beam angle to the posterior surface of L4 (Figs. 2a, b). This allowed clear visualization of the AC in the paramedian sagittal interlaminar view (inset, Fig. 2a).

To eliminate the need for cephalad-to-caudad probe angulation, we gradually shifted the patient’s hips forward from the bed edge (Figs. 2c, d) until the probe could be held perpendicular to the bed edge while still obtaining an adequate paramedian sagittal interlaminar image (inset, Fig. 2c).

The needle insertion site was then identified using the transverse interlaminar view. Based on the preprocedural CT planning, we placed the patient in a slight anterior oblique position (leaning away from the operator). This positioning induced vertebral rotation, which removed the need for medial needle angulation typically required in conventional paramedian approaches by converting it into a standardized horizontal trajectory (Fig. 1e-e′). Adhesive tape was applied to prevent excessive anterior oblique positioning (Fig. 2e).

Throughout the transverse median scan, we kept the ultrasound probe parallel to the floor and perpendicular to the bed edge. By sliding the probe slightly cephalad or caudad, the L3-4 intrathecal space could be visualized lateral to the acoustic shadow of the spinous process which was an effect of the vertebral rotation achieved through anterior oblique positioning (Fig. 2e, inset).

Mark the needle insertion point

When the intrathecal space was centered on the ultrasound screen, the needle entry point was marked at the intersection of the midpoints of the probe’s long and short axes (Fig. 2f).

Needle insertion: parallel to the floor and perpendicular to the bed edge

The needle was inserted at the marked site, aligned parallel to the floor and perpendicular to the bed edge. All 11 lumbar punctures were performed using a 22‑gauge Quincke‑type spinal needle. Case 2

On March 20, 2023, a 35-year-old woman with SMA type 2 (Cobb angle 83°) with a body mass index (BMI) of 35.1 kg/m² was scheduled for intrathecal nusinersen administration. Three-dimensional CT imaging confirmed significant thoracolumbar scoliosis with leftward curvature and vertebral rotation.

Planning using available imaging

Although the L5-S1 interlaminar space was the widest, it was not selected due to significant tapering of the dural sac at that level (Fig. 3b). Among accessible levels, L4-5 was identified as the second largest interlaminar space (Fig. 3c-e). The skin‑to‑thecal sac distance was shorter on the left, but the right L4-5 interlaminar space offered a greater lateromedial width (12.4 mm; Fig. 3c). Therefore, the right L4-5 space was chosen as the target.

Axial CT image, at L4-5 was rotated 100° counterclockwise to simulate the right anterior-oblique lateral decubitus position, demonstrating that a horizontal needle trajectory could reach the center of the spinal canal from a lateral needle entry point (Fig. 3f).

Sagittal reconstruction along this horizontal path showed a craniocaudal interlaminar length of 8.2 mm (Fig. 3g). Given a canal depth of 10.76 cm, a needle-angle deviation of approximately 5.6° would lead to contact with the bony structure (Fig. 3f). The acceptable craniocaudal angular range was only 3.5° (Fig. 3g), necessitating precise angular control during needle advancement.

Ultrasound-guided positioning

The patient was placed in the right lateral decubitus position, with her shoulders positioned close to the bed edge and her hips shifted away from it (Fig. 4a). This positioning allowed the ultrasound probe to be held perpendicular to the bed edge while obtaining a paramedian sagittal view (Fig. 4b). Based on the preoperative CT analysis, we then secured the patient in a slight anterior oblique position using adhesive tape, pillows, and fluid‑filled positioning cushions.

We then rotated the probe 90° to obtain a transverse view of the L4-5 interlaminar space, holding it perpendicular to the bed edge and parallel to the floor (Fig. 4c). Owing to the vertebral rotation induced by the anterior oblique positioning, the L4-5 intrathecal space was clearly visualized lateral to the acoustic window of the spinous process or interspinous ligament (Fig. 4d).

Because this patient had a high body mass index (35.1 kg/m²) and loose subcutaneous tissue, we applied adhesive tape to gently tighten the skin, thereby reducing soft tissue mobility. Beyond taping, proper probe contact force is critical for accurate skin marking. Insufficient force degrades image quality, whereas excessive force can indent the skin and introduce marking errors. Therefore, when locating the spinal canal, we apply adequate compression to obtain a clear image. However, when marking the needle entry site, we release probe pressure slightly, allowing the skin to return to its natural contour, while ensuring the intrathecal space remains centered on the screen.

Mark the needle insertion point

We perform skin marking only when the skin is not visibly deformed, ensuring that the needle insertion site is accurately localized. Once the target interlaminar space was centered on the ultrasound screen, the needle entry point was marked at the intersection of the midpoints of the probe’s long and short axes.

Needle insertion: parallel to the floor and perpendicular to the bed edge

The needle was advanced following the standardized orientation described above (perpendicular to the bed edge and parallel to the floor) at the intersection of the two markings. When CSF outflow was confirmed, nusinersen 12 mg (5 mL) was administered intrathecally.

Results

A total of 22 procedures for these two patients were technically successful and well tolerated and all 22 procedures were performed consecutively without exclusion. The outcomes of this ultrasound-assisted LP are presented in Table 1. The first-attempt success rate was 81.8% in Case 1 and 100% in Case 2. Successful dural puncture on the first pass was achieved similarly (45.5% and 54.5%, respectively) for each patient. Although redirections were frequently required initially, their frequency decreased over time as experience was gained, as shown in Table 1. The median needling time for LP was 1.3 [0.8–3.5] min for case 1 and 1.0 [0.6–4.4] min for case 2. The median time for locating the needle insertion site was 11.0 [9.0–14.0] min and 9.0 [7.0–11.0] min, respectively. Transient paresthesia occurred in 2 LPs during the procedures but did not result in persistent symptoms. As shown in Fig. 5 (presented for descriptive and hypothesis-generating purposes only), both patients demonstrated a trend toward fewer needle passes and shorter needling time over sequential procedures. Descriptively, in Case 1, the number of needle passes decreased from 11 in the first procedure to 1 in the final procedure (90.9% reduction), and needling time decreased from 250 s to 45 s (82.0% reduction). In Case 2, passes decreased from 10 to 1 (90.0% reduction), and needling time decreased from 380 s to 36 s (90.5% reduction).

Discussion

This study introduces an ultrasound-assisted, angle-free lumbar puncture technique in patients with severe scoliosis and obesity and demonstrates its technical feasibility in this challenging population. All procedures were performed by a single anesthesiologist with extensive expertise in ultrasound‑assisted lumbar punctures (over 200 procedures). This level of experience should be considered when interpreting the reported success rates, as outcomes may differ for less experienced operators.

While our prior work has validated a horizontal and perpendicular interlaminar approach in scoliotic patients [7, 9], the current report addresses the distinct challenges posed by obesity and describes specific refinements to overcome them.

The first and perhaps most critical challenge in obese patients is depth‑related angular precision. The increased skin‑to‑thecal sac distance in obese patients means that any minor angular deviation during needle insertion is magnified at depth. A 3–5° deviation can result in a displacement of several millimeters at the needle tip, sufficient to miss a narrow interlaminar window. Therefore, precise angular control during needle insertion is of critical importance in obese patients. We attempt to improve the precision of needle insertion using an angle‑free technique that eliminates the need for lateromedial and craniocaudal angulation. While we acknowledge that maintaining a trajectory parallel to the floor and perpendicular to the bed edge also depends on operator experience, we contend that this is substantially easier to achieve and reproduce than replicating a specific probe angulation (e.g., 15° medial tilt or 10° cephalad angulation).

This distinction rests on two key considerations. First, a trajectory parallel to the floor and perpendicular to the bed edge relies on visually verifiable external references (floor and bed edge) that are fixed, flat, and easily confirmed. In contrast, replicating a specific oblique angle requires the operator to estimate and reproduce a precise degree from memory—a task that is inherently operator‑dependent and prone to error. Second, conventional approaches use the patient’s back as the reference surface. However, in patients with scoliosis, kyphosis, or hyperlordosis, the back is a curved surface. Advancing a needle perpendicular to a curved surface introduces inaccuracies, as the “perpendicular” direction varies from point to point. By using the bed edge and the floor as external references, both of which are fixed, flat, and independent of the patient’s spinal curvature, our technique simplifies angular alignment and reduces the need for patient‑specific angular estimation and minimizes operator‑dependent visuospatial skills.

Thus, while our technique does not eliminate the need for angular control entirely, it converts a difficult, operator‑dependent task (replicating the probe angle in two planes) into a simpler, more standardized task (maintaining a horizontal and perpendicular trajectory relative to fixed external references).

Beyond depth‑related angular precision, obesity also presents a second challenge that can undermine the reliability of the procedure: skin marking accuracy. In ultrasound‑assisted techniques, the needle insertion site is identified by marking the midpoints of the probe’s long and short edges on the skin, with their intersection corresponding to the center of the probe. Obese patients typically have loose and highly mobile subcutaneous tissue. To obtain a clear image in such patients, the ultrasound probe must often be applied with sufficient pressure; however, this pressure can displace the skin and underlying tissue, introducing error in locating the needle insertion site. Furthermore, substantial probe compression can indent the skin. If skin marking is performed while the skin remains compressed, the accuracy of the mark is compromised.

To address these challenges, we have incorporated two specific refinements: (1) applying self‑adhesive tape to gently tighten the skin, reducing tissue mobility; and (2) performing skin marking only after releasing probe pressure, allowing the skin to rebound to its natural contour while ensuring the intrathecal space remains centered on the screen. Releasing the probe pressure before marking, even if the intrathecal image becomes somewhat blurred, can improve the accuracy of skin marking.

The refinements described above are built upon a reproducible workflow. The key principles of this workflow can be summarized as the “4P” framework: Planning, Positioning, Perpendicular, and Parallel (Table 2).

Regarding planning, review of preprocedural CT images and oblique reconstructions along the intended needle trajectory not only guided the selection of the optimal puncture level but also indicated the appropriate body tilt for a horizontal needle trajectory. However, CT scans are typically performed with the patient in the supine position. As a result, the anatomical relationships observed on CT (particularly vertebral rotation) may not be fully representative of the patient’s anatomy in the lateral decubitus position. In contrast, ultrasound can be performed with the patient in the actual procedural position (lateral decubitus) and allows in‑situ adjustment of patient posture based on the size and position of the anterior complex to achieve a horizontal and perpendicular needle trajectory.

Regarding positioning, previous research has shown that proper positioning is pivotal to increasing success rates [15, 16]. Our approach uses live ultrasound feedback to adjust patient posture until the probe can be aligned parallel to the floor and perpendicular to the bed edge, with the thecal sac centered on the ultrasound screen.

Regarding the needle trajectory, advancing the needle perpendicular to the bed edge and parallel to the floor is simpler than using specific craniocaudal and lateromedial angles. Any minor deviation from the intended trajectory can lead to exaggerated changes in the final position of the needle tip, especially at greater depths of neuraxial structures.

The procedural refinements described above translated into favorable clinical outcomes. Our first‑attempt success rate (81.8‑100%) compares favorably with the 87.5% reported by Li et al. [17] in obese parturients. Similarly, our first‑pass success rate (45.5‑54.5%) compares favorably with the 24% reported by Chin et al. [18] in adults with difficult surface landmarks. However, because our study lacks a control group and includes only two patients, these comparisons should be interpreted cautiously and considered hypothesis‑generating only.

An additional observation from our longitudinal data deserves mention: we observed progressively better performance over time, as reflected by markedly fewer needle passes and shorter needling time across the 22 procedures. The observed improvement likely reflects two factors: on one hand, the operator’s increasing familiarity with each patient’s unique anatomy; on the other hand, the inherent learnability of the angle‑free technique itself. Because all procedures were performed by a single experienced operator on two patients, the relative contribution of these two factors cannot be separated. Repeated procedures in the same individual create a strong patient-specific learning effect and the current data cannot distinguish operator learning from anatomical familiarity. Therefore, no formal learning-curve analysis is possible from these two patients. These findings should be considered hypothesis‑generating; a formal learning curve analysis involving multiple operators and a larger, more heterogeneous patient population is warranted to further evaluate its learnability.

Beyond the learning‑related limitations in interpreting our longitudinal data, the broader applicability of our findings is also constrained by the characteristics of our study cohort. The small sample size, the specific patient population (obese patients with severe scoliosis), and the reliance on a single experienced operator limit the broader applicability of our findings. However, our technique’s core strategy (using positional adjustments to convert patient‑specific oblique angles into a standardized horizontal and perpendicular trajectory) is conceptually adaptable to various challenging lumbar puncture scenarios. We are currently undertaking further investigations to evaluate the efficacy of the angle‑free approach in broader clinical scenarios, including obese parturients, elderly patients with degenerative spinal disease, and patients with previous spinal surgery.

Another limitation of this study warrants acknowledgment. We did not capture objective angular measurements of needle trajectory (e.g., using an inclinometer, digital goniometer, or motion‑tracking system), which could have quantified trajectory consistency and reproducibility across procedures. Such measurements are clinically challenging to perform in a sterile operating environment. The core premise of our technique (a standardized horizontal and perpendicular trajectory) offers improved geometric reproducibility compared with patient‑specific oblique angles; therefore, it remains mechanistically plausible but is not supported by quantitative data in this report. Nonetheless, the first‑pass and first‑attempt success rates may serve as indirect indicators of the operator’s ability to control the standardized trajectory.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this descriptive case series demonstrates the technical feasibility of an angle‑free ultrasound‑assisted lumbar puncture technique in two patients with complex spinal anatomy, including severe scoliosis and obesity. The “4P” principle (Planning, Positioning, Perpendicular, and Parallel) provides a structured framework that may help reduce technical barriers in challenging scenarios. The observed reduction in needle passes and needling time over sequential procedures may suggest potential learnability, but this finding is preliminary and requires confirmation. Further studies involving multiple operators and a larger, more heterogeneous patient population are needed to establish the feasibility and the learning curve associated with this technique.
