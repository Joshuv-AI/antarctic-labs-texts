Introduction

Lyme disease is the most common vector-borne illness in North America. The causative organism is mainly Borrelia burgdorferi and is transmitted by ticks. Lyme myocarditis is the cardiac manifestation of Lyme disease that usually occurs after 1–2 months of acquiring the infection.1 The most common manifestation of Lyme myocarditis is atrioventricular block (AVB) occurring in 90% of cases. Early treatment of Lyme myocarditis with antibiotics has a favorable prognosis, with complete resolution of conduction disease in most patients. AVB usually resolves within 10 days of antibiotic initiation.1,2 Diagnosis of Lyme myocarditis is based on exposure history, presence of conduction disease, and positive serology. Although imaging confirmation is not necessary for definitive diagnosis of Lyme myocarditis, ventricular hypokinesis on echocardiogram and myocardial edema on cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can be supportive of Lyme myocarditis diagnosis. To the best of our knowledge, there are no previous reports of Lyme myocarditis being detected with fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (18F-FDG PET).

Case report

A 39-year-old woman presented to a peripheral hospital with a 1-month history of fatigue, presyncope, exertional dyspnea, joint pain, and fever. Her medical history was significant for Child-Pugh A liver cirrhosis secondary to alcohol, gestational diabetes, hypertension, and mononucleosis one month ago. Her examination demonstrated a heart rate of 30 beats per minute but was otherwise unremarkable. Preliminary investigations in a peripheral hospital showed lymphopenia and 2:1 AVB. Further monitoring on telemetry showed alternating right bundle branch block and left bundle branch block, as well as intermittent complete heart block. The patient was transferred to a tertiary center, and a temporary transvenous pacing was placed owing to symptomatic heart block.

Differential diagnosis

AVB in a young patient has a wide differential, including infectious processes such as Lyme myocarditis, infiltrative processes such as sarcoidosis and hemochromatosis, myocardial ischemia, and drug toxicity, to name a few. Given the acute onset of constitutional symptoms, an infectious process was the favored diagnosis.

Investigations

On further history, the patient revealed exposure to ticks more than 3 months ago, but none recently. Hence, serology for Borrelia burgdorferi was sent. An echocardiogram showed normal biventricular function without any evidence of valvulopathy or pulmonary hypertension. A chest CT showed prominent mediastinal lymph nodes, which were likely secondary to a reactive process. However, the extent of the mediastinal lymphadenopathy increased the suspicion for sarcoidosis. Therefore, the patient underwent an 18F-FDG PET to rule out cardiac sarcoidosis (CS). This showed no evidence of extracardiac FDG uptake; however, there was mild to moderate focal uptake at the basal septum (maximum standardized uptake value 3.14), which is illustrated in Figures 1 and 2. These PET findings were interpreted as meaning that CS was unlikely.

Treatment and outcomes

In view of the PET findings and the high clinical suspicion for Lyme myocarditis based on exposure history, the patient was empirically started on ceftriaxone IV on day 9 after medical contact. She had positive serology on day 14 using the 2-tiered testing protocol (enzyme immunoassay is followed by immunoglobulin M and immunoglobulin G immunoblot), confirming Lyme myocarditis. The patient’s bradyarrhythmias improved with the initiation of antibiotics, and the temporary transvenous pacing was removed. She completed 21 days of IV antibiotic therapy, and permanent pacing was not required. At 20 months, there was no recurrence of AVB. The timeline of the case is presented in Table 1.

Discussion

Lyme disease is the most common vector-borne illness in North America. The causative organism is mainly Borrelia burgdorferi and is transmitted by ticks. Lyme myocarditis is the cardiac manifestation of Lyme disease, which usually occurs after 1–2 months of acquiring the infection.1 It occurs owing to direct myocardial invasion by the spirochete bacteria and subsequent immune processes leading to lymphocyte- and macrophage-driven inflammation.2 Although historically Lyme myocarditis occurred in 10% of Lyme disease cases, most recently this has decreased to 0.3%–4%. The most common manifestation of Lyme myocarditis is AVB occurring in 90% of cases. Early treatment of Lyme myocarditis with antibiotics has a favorable prognosis, with complete resolution of conduction disease in most patients. AVB usually resolves within 10 days of antibiotic initiation.1,2

Lyme myocarditis is a diagnosis based on clinical symptoms, exposure history, evidence of conduction disease, and positive Lyme serology. The turnaround time for Lyme serology takes 7–14 days and includes a 2-tiered test method with enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay followed by western blot assay for reactive or indeterminate samples.3 Although imaging confirmation is not mandatory for the final diagnosis, it is often helpful with a timely diagnosis, especially when serology is delayed. In this case, we were convinced of the diagnosis of Lyme disease based on the following:

1.She had positive serology using the recommended 2-tiered testing protocol in which an enzyme immunoassay is followed by immunoglobulin M and immunoglobulin G immunoblot.

She had positive serology using the recommended 2-tiered testing protocol in which an enzyme immunoassay is followed by immunoglobulin M and immunoglobulin G immunoblot.

2.She had a classic response of atrioventricular conduction function recovery after the initiation of antibiotics. A high-degree AVB usually resolves in a stepwise progression from a third-degree block to a Wenckebach second-degree block, to a first-degree block, to a decreasing PR interval, and to normal, usually within 1–2 weeks of antibiotics.1 Furthermore, before the initiation of antibiotics, her condition had been progressively declining over a few days. In addition, she had daily fever before antibiotics, which quickly resolved.

In this case, the findings of mediastinal lymphadenopathy on chest CT led to clinical suspicion for CS and hence the decision to order an 18F-FDG PET. The patient’s 18F-FDG PET findings of focal uptake at the basal septum without systemic uptake had the potential to falsely diagnose isolated CS. However, recent studies have shown that although clinically isolated CS is common, both clinically and imaging isolated CS is rare,4,5 In light of this, other diagnoses including Lyme myocarditis were pursued, which ultimately led to the timely diagnosis and treatment of this patient.

It should be noted that, in almost all clinical scenarios, MRI is the first choice investigation for CS. However, in our centers and others where PET is readily accessible, in cases like this, PET is the preferred first-line test because of the following:

•The temporary pacing wire makes obtaining a diagnostic MRI more difficult.

The temporary pacing wire makes obtaining a diagnostic MRI more difficult.

•We have described patients with CS presenting with AVB where the PET scan is positive and the MRI is negative.6 That is, the disease is so early in its course that there is inflammation but no scarring.

We have described patients with CS presenting with AVB where the PET scan is positive and the MRI is negative.6 That is, the disease is so early in its course that there is inflammation but no scarring.

To the best of our knowledge, there are no previous reports of Lyme myocarditis being detected with 18F-FDG PET. As a diagnostic tool, the use of 18F-FDG-PET in this setting could help to confirm the clinical suspicion and guide therapy. More studies are needed to further evaluate the utility of 18F-FDG PET in Lyme myocarditis management. With the increasing prevalence of tick-borne diseases, tools to assist Lyme disease detection and management such as 18F-FDG PET may well grow in demand.
