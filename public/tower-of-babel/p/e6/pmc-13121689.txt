Modeling and optimization of performance and emissions in a gasoline-isopropanol SI engine: multi-model prediction and a PID-based search algorithm
Authors: Esref Bogar, Emre Arabaci, Serdar Halis, Ahmet Uyumaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, the effects of fuel blend ratio and engine speed on the performance and emissions of a spark-ignition (SI) engine fueled with gasoline–isopropanol blends were experimentally investigated, modeled, and optimized. Despite the potential benefits of gasoline–alcohol blends for SI engines, many response-surface-based studies adopt simplified surrogate models and fixed second-order formulations, which may not adequately capture coupled and non-linear effects, particularly when experimental data are limited. To address this gap, a data-driven multi-model strategy is adopted to systematically evaluate seven multivariate polynomial regression structures for each response, instead of imposing a single fixed-form model. Model performance is assessed using a hold-out validation scheme, and the model with the highest predictive accuracy is selected for each response as the final predictive model, enabling accurate prediction of torque, fuel consumption (FC), and carbon monoxide (CO), hydrocarbons (HC), and carbon dioxide () emissions. As an additional contribution, a multi-objective scalarized function constructed from normalized response values is minimized using a PID-based search algorithm (PSA) to simultaneously maximize torque, power, and brake thermal efficiency (BTE) while minimizing FC, CO, HC, , and brake-specific fuel consumption (BSFC). The results indicate that increasing the isopropanol ratio has pronounced and non-linear effects on engine behavior: torque and power increase up to an intermediate isopropanol fraction and then decrease at higher ratios, BSFC rises with increasing isopropanol content, and HC, CO, and emissions decrease as the isopropanol share increases. The optimization identifies an optimal operating condition at a 50% isopropanol–50% gasoline blend and an engine speed of 2783 rpm. Overall, the study delivers a compact modeling–optimization framework for gasoline–isopropanol operation in SI engines and supports the design of more efficient and environmentally friendly fuel strategies based on alternative fuel usage.Keywords: Gasoline–isopropanol blends, Spark-ignition engine, Performance and emissions, Multi-model prediction, Response surface modeling, PID-based search algorithmSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Internal combustion engines (ICEs), developed in the 19th century, have become some of the most complex and widely used machines in modern society. Among the many great inventions made by humanity, reciprocating internal combustion engines stand out as one of the most impactful in terms of their effect on society, the economy, and the environment1,2. This can be attributed to the heavy reliance on ICEs in industries such as transportation, trade, and energy production, where research on these engines has been ongoing for over a century and continues to remain highly active today. These engines are mainly used in applications such as motor vehicles, agricultural machinery, electric generators, and heavy construction equipment. In this framework, ICEs significantly contribute to improving efficiency in transportation, agriculture, and energy production, thereby making life more productive and manageable3.

The operation of numerous vehicles and engines worldwide often leads to excessive petroleum consumption. It is estimated that nearly 70% of the crude oil used today is consumed by internal combustion engines, contributing to the millions of barrels utilized daily. In countries lacking sufficient oil reserves, a significant portion of this fuel must be imported to meet demand. This situation is unsustainable from an economic perspective for these nations3,4. To mitigate this dependency and promote sustainability, the exploration of non-conventional fuel sources has gained momentum. For instance, recent experimental and numerical investigations into mahua oil-based biodiesel have demonstrated the potential of bio-derived resources to serve as viable alternatives to fossil fuels5. Therefore, the development of advanced ICE technologies that offer the highest possible efficiency while minimizing emissions of all exhaust gases is of paramount importance. From this perspective, hybrid electric vehicles (HEVs), which represent a remarkable integration of internal combustion engine and electric motor, are expected to become a crucial component of future transportation systems due to their environmentally friendly characteristics and versatility. Additionally, challenges such as energy storage options for HEVs must also be addressed. Consequently, HEVs are expected to emerge as a key class of green vehicles with broad global appeal. To achieve this, it is essential that the technology in the internal combustion engines of HEVs, as well as the fuel used, aligns with sustainable environmental policies6,7.

Emissions from motor vehicles are major contributors to atmospheric pollution. These emissions create a foundation on which various carcinogenic and non-carcinogenic health issues can be created and spread8. Previous research into combustion fuels used in motor vehicles has primarily been focused on the particulate matter (PM) fraction of the emissions in diesel fuel. However, these emissions also contain semi-volatile organic compounds in addition to solid and gas-phase components. Whereas diesel combustion emits higher amounts of organic carbon (OC) and elemental carbon (EC) than gasoline engines, the latter usually emits higher quantities of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) and other organic compounds8–10. Lower PM emissions are usually expected from gasoline engines; however, their exhaust gases remain risky to human health due to the accumulation of PAHs and oil by-products rather than the high content of PM. Therefore, whereas gasoline engines may be more harmful to certain organic compounds, especially PAHs, diesel engines pose a greater problem because of their higher emissions of PM. Thus, neither engine type is universally superior; the more environmentally appropriate choice depends on the pollutant profile under consideration. However, when factors such as ease of production, maintenance costs, fuel variety, and acoustic emissions are considered, gasoline engines may be regarded as more advantageous9–11.

Due to both the depletion of fossil fuel reserves and the need to reduce harmful environmental pollutants, the application of alcohol fuels in ICEs has been widely considered. Alcohol fuels are unique because they are renewable and can improve engine performance, especially ethanol and methanol. Recent thermodynamic simulation and optimization studies have shown that optimizing intake pressure together with late intake valve closing (LIVC) can yield significant performance improvements in methanol-fueled SI engines12. Alcohol blends are also an environmentally friendly alternative fuel because they can reduce pollutants and enhance the combustion efficiency of the engine. For example, blends of methanol and ethanol have a high heat of vaporization and high combustion efficiency, lowering exhaust emissions by a large margin13,14. Moreover, due to the high octane rating of alcohol fuel, engines can run at higher compression ratios, improving knock resistance15,16. Adding ethanol to ICEs, based on experimental research, reduces emissions of hydrocarbons (HC) and carbon monoxide (CO), but can increase the amount of nitrogen oxide () emissions. Indeed, comprehensive studies into the influence of fuel composition-such as toluene content and octane sensitivity-have demonstrated the success of alcohol fuels in reducing harmful emissions. However, the fuel blend ratio determines the effect of alcohol on engine performance; while a higher ethanol content can result in higher engine efficiency, it may also lead to a reduction in volumetric efficiency17,18. Furthermore, different types of alcohols-methanol, ethanol, and n-butanol-added to fuel mixtures can reduce soot and PM emissions from ICEs. Dual-fuel injection technology, which can be used with gasoline and alcohol, has the potential to reduce CO and PM emissions. Alcohol fuels, with their inherently high octane rating and overall lower carbon content, can result in significant reductions in emissions. Mixtures of methanol and ethanol have demonstrated a reduction in emissions by as much as 10%19,20. In conclusion, the use of alcohol fuels in ICEs presents many advantages from both ecological and performance perspectives. Such fuels not only improve engine performance but also serve as a viable alternative to harmful exhaust gas emissions. However, the high vaporization heat of alcohols and fuel blend stability issues must be considered, as some modifications may be required in the engine structure21,22.

Although gasoline-isopropanol blends are considered promising candidates, their use in ICEs could further optimize performance and reduce harmful emissions. Isopropanol has a high octane rating and specific combustion characteristics that create the potential for better efficiency in the spark-ignition (SI) engine, especially under dual-fuel conditions23,24. It has been shown that adding isopropanol to mixtures can improve the maximum pressure and the rate of heat release while suppressing ignition delays. The overall combustion characteristics are considerably improved for various isopropanol proportions, with significant reductions in unburned HC and CO emissions25. Other properties of isopropanol also contribute to good performance in homogeneous charge compression ignition (HCCI) engines, where it has shown a wide range of operational loads compared to conventional fuels26. When a combustion promoter like 2-ethylhexyl nitrate is blended, combustion efficiency is enhanced and emissions are reduced in such systems26. There are several disadvantages associated with this fuel, however. For instance, the fuel has corrosion effects on some engine parts, so proper handling is needed27. Comparing studies, it can be seen that gasoline-isopropanol blends yield higher values in different performance aspects, such as brake thermal efficiency (BTE) and brake specific fuel consumption (BSFC), with relatively lower emissions of CO and HC compared to conventional gasoline25–27. The environmental benefits associated with isopropanol produced from renewable resources make it a viable alternative to conventional fossil fuels in the quest to develop sustainable energy solutions28. Generally, isopropanol as a fuel additive or when combined with gasoline may lead to cleaner combustion and better engine performance23.

In recent years, computational modeling and optimization have become indispensable tools for enhancing engine performance across a wide spectrum of alternative fuels. For instance, thermodynamic modeling approaches have been successfully applied to optimize the operating conditions of engines fueled with complex gaseous blends, establishing a robust framework for performance improvement29,30. Similarly, various studies have focused on modeling the effects of gasoline-alcohol blends. Within this scope, Artificial Neural Networks (ANN) and Response Surface Methodology (RSM) stand out as two fundamental techniques widely used in this field31–33. ANN, a machine learning method, is widely employed in numerous studies due to its capability to model complex and nonlinear relationships between inputs and outputs34–41. However, ANN has limitations, including its dependence on large datasets, high computational costs, and low interpretability of results42,43. On the other hand, RSM is frequently used in experimental studies due to its advantages, such as lower data requirements, ease of implementation, better interpretability of the results, and its ability to simultaneously perform modeling and optimization processes44–46. Due to these advantages, RSM plays a pivotal role in studies aimed at modeling and optimizing engine performance and emissions using gasoline-alcohol blends47–58.

Despite the widespread use of RSM and ANN in engine performance and emission modeling, practical limitations may arise in SI-engine studies involving gasoline–alcohol blends. In RSM-based studies, the surrogate is often fixed a priori to a second-order polynomial, which can be restrictive when responses exhibit noticeable coupling or nonlinearity across the operating domain. ANN-based models are flexible, but they typically require larger datasets for robust prediction and do not naturally yield compact, closed-form analytical expressions, which can be less convenient for calibration-oriented decision making. These considerations motivate a transparent surrogate-modeling strategy that enables response-wise model-structure selection based on predictive performance and can be embedded into a tractable optimization formulation.

Accordingly, this study experimentally quantifies the coupled effects of gasoline–isopropanol blend ratio and engine speed under full-load conditions and develops an integrated modeling–optimization workflow to predict and optimize performance, emissions, and fuel-economy responses. Compared with many conventional RSM and ANN approaches, the proposed framework couples out-of-sample, response-wise surrogate selection with PSA-based multi-response optimization using closed-form analytical models. To obtain an accurate and generalizable representation of the system responses, seven alternative multivariate polynomial regression structures—linear, interaction, pure quadratic, full quadratic, quadratic interaction, pure cubic, and full cubic—are fitted and compared for each response variable, rather than adopting a single, fixed second-order model as is often done in RSM-based studies. Model performance is evaluated using a hold-out validation scheme, and for each response the final surrogate model is selected in a data-driven manner based on predictive accuracy, thereby improving generalization and mitigating the risk of model misspecification with a limited experimental dataset. The selected regression models are then combined into an explicit multi-response scalarized objective that simultaneously accounts for performance (torque, power, BTE), emissions (CO, HC, ), and fuel consumption/economy metrics (FC, BSFC), providing a compact analytical framework that can be directly embedded in engine calibration workflows. Finally, a PID-based search algorithm (PSA)59 is employed to solve the resulting coupled optimization problem and identify operating conditions that provide a balanced compromise among the considered responses within the tested range. Taken together, the experimental dataset, the multi-model regression framework, and the PSA-based optimizer yield a practical decision-support tool for the design and calibration of small SI engines operating on gasoline–isopropanol blends, with implications for cleaner and lower-emission fuel utilization.

Experimental setup and procedure

An air-cooled, single-cylinder, four-stroke, 8.5 compression ratio gasoline engine (Honda GX-160) was used in the experimental setup to investigate the effects of isopropanol fuel addition to pure gasoline on engine performance and exhaust emissions. The experiments were carried out at 9 different engine speeds (ranging from 2400 to 4000 rpm) and 6 different blend ratios (0%, 10%, 20%, 30%, 40%, and 50%) by adding isopropanol to gasoline at a constant intake air temperature.

As shown in Fig. 2, the experimental setup consists of a spark-ignition engine, AC dynamometer, rheostat, fuel tanks, precision scale, and emission measurement device. In addition, a torque sensor (Burster 8661) was mounted between the test engine and the dynamometer to measure the engine torque. Instantaneous measurements were taken for engine torque versus engine speed with this sensor. Technical details of the test engine are presented in Table 1.

To support reliable model selection among seven alternative regression forms and mitigate overfitting risk given the relatively limited dataset size, a hold-out validation scheme was adopted. Specifically, the experimental operating points were randomly split into training and testing subsets, and the test subset was kept independent and not used during model fitting. This choice is motivated by the tendency of higher-order polynomial response surfaces to fit noise and spurious interactions when model complexity is not controlled, particularly in small datasets. Therefore, candidate surrogate models were compared using out-of-sample predictive accuracy quantified by the test-set Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), which prioritizes generalization rather than in-sample goodness-of-fit. This procedure provides an independent estimate of predictive performance within the investigated operating range and reduces the likelihood of selecting an overly complex or mis-specified surrogate model for subsequent optimization.

PID-based search algorithm (PSA)

Metaheuristic optimizers are powerful and derivative-free optimization tools developed to overcome the issue of local optima encountered by gradient-based algorithms in complex optimization problems. In this study, PID-based Search Algorithm (PSA), a new and promising metaheuristic optimizer, was applied to determine the optimal factor values to attain higher torque, power, and BTE, as well as lower CO, HC, , BSFC, and fuel consumption.

PSA is a population-based metaheuristic optimizer introduced by Gao59 inspired by the incremental Proportional-Integral-Derivative (PID) control strategy. Broadly, PSA considers each individual/solution in the population as the actual value and the historical best solution as the desired value. Then, similar to the regulation process of PID, it iteratively adjusts the deviation between each individual and the best solution to drive the population towards the global optimum. To position the present modelling results within the recent SI-engine literature, it is noted that many studies on gasoline–alcohol blends frequently adopt a fixed second-order RSM formulation due to its simplicity and interpretability, and often integrate it with optimization frameworks. In contrast, in this study the predictive model selection was not restricted to a fixed second-order assumption; instead, multiple polynomial RSM candidates were compared based on their RMSE values on an independent test set, and for each response the model structure yielding the lowest test error was selected (Table 5). The results in Table 5 show that, for torque, CO, HC, and FC, the minimum test RMSE is achieved not by second-order models but by higher-order polynomial structures (particularly the pure cubic form). For , although the test RMSE values of the second-order models (pure/full quadratic) are very close to each other, the lowest value is obtained with the pure quadratic model. This strategy enables a response-dependent, data-driven determination of the most appropriate model form. In addition, the selected polynomial surrogate models yield high coefficients of determination, with values ranging from 0.881 to 0.969 (Table 6). While direct numerical comparisons across different studies are inherently limited by differences in engine platforms, fuel blends, measurement systems, and operating ranges, these accuracy levels are comparable to those commonly reported in the SI-engine modelling literature. For example, Awad et al.54 reported for CO and for selected performance-related responses using RSM-based modelling, while Abdalla et al.47 reported values ranging from 0.7261 for CO to 0.9298 for brake power within an RSM framework. Similarly, Uslu and Celik40 reported ANN-based values in the range of 0.9447–0.9891 for emission prediction and RSM-based values of approximately 0.906–0.945 for selected performance metrics. More recently, Sathish Kumar et al.53 presented quadratic RSM models (with appropriate response transformations) reporting response-dependent levels (e.g., from for up to for BTE), further indicating that the achieved goodness-of-fit in SI-engine surrogate modelling can vary across responses and operating conditions. Overall, the obtained range indicates that the proposed surrogates provide a competitive level of predictive performance within the investigated operating range.

Regarding the correlation between torque, engine speed, and the isopropanol blend ratio in the gasoline mixture, the torque increases to an optimum value in the range of about 2400-2800 rpm at an increase in engine speed and then decreases slightly at higher engine speeds. The effect of the blend ratio on torque can also be seen that higher isopropanol content moderately increases torque at low engine speeds. This would likely be due to the improved combustion characteristics of isopropanol, enhancing overall combustion efficiency at certain engine speeds leading to a higher torque output74. It is observed that the power output increases as the engine speed increases, reaching a maximum at approximately 3200 rpm and then decreasing slightly. The effect of the blend ratio on power is quite limited at low engine speeds, while a significant increase in power is observed with increasing isopropanol content at higher speeds. This can be attributed to the higher knock resistance of isopropanol, which allows the fuel to perform better at high engine loads and speeds, increasing power output23. Fuel consumption (FC and BSFC) values exhibit an increasing trend with rising engine speed and blend ratio. This increase is particularly pronounced at higher isopropanol content due to the significantly lower energy density of isopropanol compared to gasoline, necessitating greater fuel usage to sustain equivalent power output75. Additionally, as engine speed rises, fuel consumption correspondingly increases, which is a characteristic behavior of internal combustion engines. This is attributed to the heightened demand for the air-fuel mixture and the subsequent increase in combustion cycles at higher speeds. The graph depicting the variation of thermal efficiency to engine speed and blend ratio reveals that the highest thermal efficiency is achieved at an engine speed of approximately 2600 rpm with a 50% isopropanol blend ratio. These conditions indicate optimal engine operation, resulting in enhanced efficiency. The oxygenated nature of isopropanol contributes to improved combustion, which in turn enhances thermal efficiency under these operating parameters75.

When the change in CO, HC, and emissions depending on engine speed and blend ratio is considered, CO emission decreases with increasing isopropanol blend ratio as expected. This can be seen more clearly, in particular, at engine speeds between 2400 and 2800 rpm. Isopropanol is an oxygenated fuel and therefore provides more complete combustion, resulting in a reduction in CO emissions. Also, the level of CO appears to be slightly reduced at higher engine speeds; the effect of speed is far less important, however76. In the meantime, HC emissions exhibit a significant reduction with increasing isopropanol content in the fuel blend, particularly at higher engine speeds. This reduction can be attributed to the oxygenated nature of isopropanol, which facilitates more complete combustion and thereby minimizes the presence of unburned hydrocarbons in the exhaust. Additionally, HC emissions decrease progressively with rising engine speed, further highlighting the enhanced combustion efficiency achieved at higher engine speeds77. emissions appear to decrease with increasing isopropanol blend ratio, especially at low engine speeds. This can be explained by the oxygen content of isopropanol, which provides cleaner combustion and reduces carbon dioxide emissions. Also, the lower energy density of isopropanol compared to gasoline may lead to fewer carbon-based combustion products78,79.

The selection of a high isopropanol ratio (50%) can be attributed to the oxygenated nature of the alcohol, which promotes complete combustion and reduces harmful exhaust emissions. Although different alternative fuels are used, the necessity of determining the optimal operating conditions to balance the trade-off between performance and emissions is a common challenge in engine research. Recent studies by Prajapati et al.81 and Prajapati and Tirkey82 have similarly employed simulation and optimization methodologies to enhance the performance of engines fueled with alternative gas blends (biogas/producer gas). Their findings demonstrate that numerical optimization is a robust tool for predicting engine behavior and improving efficiency, which supports the methodological approach adopted in this study.

To determine the accuracy of these optimized operating parameters and the obtained responses, a validation test involving the existing conditions should be performed. Therefore, the results of the experiments carried out under the conditions closest to the parameter values obtained with the optimization studies (50% isopropanol blend ratio and 2800 rpm engine speed) were taken as a reference for the validation test. The data obtained from the optimization and the experimental data used for the validation test are given in Table 7. Overall, the agreement is reasonable; however, the relative deviation marginally exceeds 5% for HC and FC. This is mainly because the PSA optimum corresponds to a continuous operating point (, rpm), whereas the experimental campaign was conducted at discrete engine-speed levels; therefore, the validation necessarily relied on the nearest tested condition (50% blend, 2800 rpm). In the vicinity of the optimum, HC and fuel-consumption-related responses are often locally sensitive to small speed variations under full-load operation, and the repeatability/uncertainty of exhaust-gas and fuel-flow measurements may further contribute to the observed differences. Importantly, these deviations do not materially affect the overall reliability of the surrogate-based optimization within the investigated operating range; rather, they reflect the expected tolerance when transferring a model-derived optimum to discrete experimental settings.

Conclusions

The experiments were conducted on a spark-ignition engine at different fuel blend ratios (0–50%) and different engine speeds (2400-4000 rpm). Analyses were applied by modeling and optimization of the data obtained. The measurements were modeled using seven regression models. Hold-out validation technique was used to determine the most appropriate model. Finally, with the PSA algorithm, torque, power, and thermal efficiency (BTE) were maximized while fuel consumption (FC), specific fuel consumption (BSFC), HC, CO, and emissions were minimized. The parameters that maximize and minimize these values were found to be 50% fuel mixture ratio and 2783 rpm engine speed. The best response values obtained according to the optimized operating parameters were determined as follows: The torque value was obtained as 8.31 Nm, which stands out as a factor that increases the mechanical efficiency of the engine. The power obtained was found at 2.42 kW, indicating the energy conversion capacity of the system. FC was calculated as 1.23 kg/h and BSFC was 0.51 kg/kWh, optimizing the amount of fuel consumed by the engine per unit power. The BTE was determined as 19.15%, thus demonstrating the efficiency of the system in converting the chemical energy of the fuel into mechanical energy. When the emission values were examined, HC emission was obtained as 214.18 ppm, CO emission as 7.47% and emission as 6.56%, thus environmental impacts were also evaluated. These data show that under optimized conditions, the system provides a balanced operation in terms of both performance and environmental sustainability.
