Dementia diagnostic deserts: workforce and geographic inequities in the diagnostic pathway for cognitive impairment
Authors: Zhijing Xu, Ying Xue, Yaguang Zheng, Bei Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs disease-modifying therapies for Alzheimer's disease become available, ensuring equitable access to timely and accurate diagnosis of cognitive impairment is critical. This article conceptualizes “dementia diagnostic deserts,” defined as geographic areas where rurality, primary care professional shortages, and high social deprivation converge to exacerbate access to the diagnostic continuum, from initial screening and comprehensive evaluation to the appropriate referral for specialist-based diagnostic confirmation. Using a multidimensional framework, we identify “Triple Disadvantage Zones” where these barriers intersect most severely, leading to diagnostic errors, characterized by failure to differentiate subjective complaints from objective impairment. This diagnostic failure produces 2 distinct forms of diagnostic inequity: delayed diagnosis for those with true pathology, and inappropriate over-testing for those who do not meet clinical criteria. Nurse practitioners and physician assistants are frontline providers in these underserved regions but face significant training gaps and practice-level infrastructure barriers. Addressing these disparities requires a multidimensional policy approach that targets the convergence of geographic, workforce, and socioeconomic barriers rather than rurality alone. We propose a coordinated policy agenda focused on comprehensive clinical triage training, appropriate reimbursement for clinician time, financial assistance for diagnostic testing, and investments in practice-level capacity for the entire diagnostic pathway and subsequent care.

Introduction

The growing availability of disease-modifying therapies (DMTs) for Alzheimer's disease has underscored the importance of timely and equitable access to a complete and accurate diagnostic approach for cognitive impairment.1,2 This process encompasses initial screening, a comprehensive clinical evaluation to rule out reversible causes, and, when clinically indicated following this evaluation, referral for specialist-based diagnostic confirmation.1 This pathway must begin with a clinical evaluation to establish the cognitive syndrome before advanced diagnostic confirmation is pursued in specialist settings.3,4 Although clinical appropriateness criteria support the use of amyloid positron emission tomography (PET) and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers in specialist settings for patients with objective cognitive impairment,5 their adoption remains limited and uneven across regions and populations.6 While blood-based biomarkers (BBMs) offer a less invasive alternative, their routine uptake remains low due to evolving guidelines and infrastructure needs.7,8 The primary disparities in dementia care begin long before biomarker consideration.7,9,10 They exist in the rates of timely syndrome diagnosis, access to providers trained in cognitive assessment, and the ability to perform a comprehensive evaluation to rule out reversible causes.11 These fundamental gaps in the diagnostic pathway disproportionately affect rural and underserved communities. Disparities in uptake and implementation suggest that geographic, workforce, and infrastructure factors continue to shape access.12-14

This article draws on evidence from prior studies and policy reports to conceptualize dementia “diagnostic deserts,” where barriers to initial screening, primary care access, and comprehensive evaluation converge relative to population need.15 The concept of healthcare “deserts” has previously been employed to examine how geographic access to essential health services, such as infectious disease testing, pharmacy services, and preventive care, shapes population health outcomes.16,17 Applying this framework to dementia diagnostics, we anticipate that dementia diagnostic deserts will be disproportionately concentrated in rural communities, primary care health professional shortage areas (HPSAs), and socially deprived regions.18,19 These overlapping structural disadvantages define ‘Triple Disadvantage Zones’ where diagnostic errors are most prevalent.20-22

Extending this framework, this article aims to: (1) synthesize evidence from workforce studies, health services research, and policy reports to develop a multidimensional framework for identifying dementia diagnostic deserts; (2) inform a policy-oriented analysis of the relationships among community characteristics, diagnostic availability, and social vulnerability; and (3) introduce the concepts of accurate triage and the dual failures of diagnostic inequity, namely delayed diagnosis and inappropriate over-testing, as targets for policy intervention.

Approximately 80% of dementia cases in the United States are managed within primary care settings, which serve as the entry point for cognitive assessment and ongoing care coordination.1 Yet these same settings face severe workforce shortages, time constraints, and limited specialist backup—particularly in rural and underserved communities, making them both the linchpin and the bottleneck in the diagnostic pathway.

Dementia diagnosis as a policy problem

Alzheimer's disease and related dementia (ADRD) represent a growing public health challenge in the United States, with prevalence projected to increase significantly in coming decades, potentially exceeding 13 million by 2050, though the exact magnitude of this increase may be moderated by birth cohort-related changes in risk.1 Notably, Black Americans are twice as likely to develop ADRD as White Americans, yet face a 35% lower likelihood of receiving a timely diagnosis.23

Accurate identification of the underlying pathology supported by validated biomarkers for Alzheimer's disease, including amyloid PET, CSF analysis, and BBMs (eg, p-tau181, p-tau217),24-26 is increasingly emphasized as essential for optimizing care and enabling timely treatment, particularly following the recent approval of DMTs.13,27 Biomarkers identify pathology, not the dementia syndrome; thus, they are indicated only once objective impairment is established and reversible causes have been ruled out.28,29 As Widera and Covinsky30 articulate in a recent viewpoint, primary care clinicians must approach BBMs with caution. They pose 3 fundamental questions: what is Alzheimer disease, how should it be diagnosed, and what is the role of biomarkers in that pathway? The answers reinforce that Alzheimer disease is a clinical-pathological construct; its diagnosis requires a comprehensive clinical evaluation documenting objective cognitive impairment and excluding reversible causes; and biomarkers serve only to confirm the presence of amyloid pathology in patients already diagnosed with MCI or dementia. For asymptomatic patients, biomarkers lack actionable benefit and risk over diagnosis and anxiety.30 While biomarker testing is appropriately reserved for specialist evaluation following a comprehensive clinical diagnosis, access to such testing remains suboptimal and inconsistent in clinical practice.6 Recent data from the Adelphi Real World Dementia Disease Specific Programme (2022-2024), a cross-sectional survey of 779 physicians reporting on 5551 patients across the US, Europe, and Japan with diagnosed cognitive impairment (including mild cognitive impairment and dementia), indicate that few patients received AD biomarker tests: CSF testing 9.5%, amyloid PET 3.7%, and blood-based AD tests 5.3% in the overall sample. In US-specific patients (n = 1275), rates were similar: CSF 7.1%, amyloid PET 3.8%, and blood tests 7.3%.6,31 These low utilization rates, which reflect testing ordered primarily by specialists, are further exacerbated by structural barriers in rural, HPSA, and high-deprivation areas.19

Racial/ethnic minorities face additional barriers. For instance, CSF testing acceptance is 20% to 30% lower in Black populations due to historical medical mistrust and the perceived invasiveness of lumbar punctures.32 Policy factors, such as variable Medicare/Medicaid reimbursement and insurance coverage for PET imaging, interact with structural barriers to influence uptake, highlighting the role of social determinants in shaping access to advanced diagnostics.33

Primary care at the center of the diagnostic bottleneck

Primary care serves as the primary entry point for cognitive assessment and ongoing care coordination for the vast majority of dementia cases.1,19 At the same time, more than 83 million people reside in primary care HPSAs,34,35 and over 63.9% of U.S. counties lack geriatric specialists, neurologists, and other healthcare providers who are capable of performing complex diagnostic interpretation and management.36,37 These structural shortages place substantial diagnostic responsibility on primary care clinicians, particularly in rural areas classified by Rural-Urban Commuting Area (RUCA) codes 7-10, where nurse practitioners (NPs) and physician assistants (PAs) often serve as the main providers of clinical care.38

Yet, significant diagnostic delays for cognitive impairment remain common. For the subset of patients for whom Alzheimer's pathology testing is clinically indicated following a comprehensive evaluation, under-utilization of these biomarkers also persists, however, delays are more directly driven by limited access to specialist services, fragmented referral pathways, and systemic barriers in care coordination in underserved settings.3 Prior surveys and studies of NPs and primary care providers (PCPs) indicate significant barriers to diagnosing mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and dementia, primarily centered on a lack of familiarity with diagnostic criteria and time constraints during clinical encounters.39-41 A core competency required to navigate diagnostic deserts is “accurate triage” accurately differentiating subjective complaints from objective impairment to avoid the dual failures of delayed diagnosis and inappropriate over-testing. Qualitative studies of U.S. PCPs further identify fragmented reimbursement policies, lack of clear referral pathways, and delays in specialist access—barriers that are most severe in rural and high SDI counties.42

Emerging evidence also highlights opportunities and challenges for mitigating geographic barriers through telehealth-enabled cognitive assessments and remote specialist collaboration, though uptake varies widely depending on local broadband access, clinician comfort with technology, and reimbursement structures.43 While BBMs may streamline pathways, a negative or ambiguous result may actually increase uncertainty, necessitating timely specialist access.10,44 Their appropriate interpretation and use typically require specialist involvement, and without such sufficient support, primary care practices might risk a bottleneck in the dementia diagnostic pathway as diagnostic technologies become clinically and therapeutically consequential.

Nurse practitioners and physician assistants: expanding capacity amid gaps in training and resources

NPs and PAs constitute a rapidly expanding segment of the U.S. primary care workforce and are often the frontline providers in underserved and rural settings—areas at the heart of the identified “diagnostic deserts”. More than 355 000 NPs and approximately 118 000 PAs practice nationwide, with particularly high representation in these resource-limited regions.45-47 Prior research demonstrates that, in supportive settings, NPs can manage a full continuum of care with outcomes comparable to those of physicians, including diagnosis, care planning, and follow-up.48

However, the potential for NPs and PAs to expand access to dementia diagnostics is constrained by persistent gaps in training and an incomplete understanding of the limitations of new technologies.49 While BBMs hold promise, their performance in heterogeneous primary care populations, where patients often have multiple chronic conditions that can lead to false positives, may differ from the controlled cohorts in which they were initially validated.50 Training must therefore move beyond simple test interpretation to include accurate triage, the ethics of disclosure, and the management of results in patients with comorbidities. Many advanced practice clinicians report limited confidence in diagnosing cognitive impairment, particularly in complex cases or when using advanced biomarkers, with surveys showing only about 30% reporting confidence in these areas compared with approximately 40% of physicians.51 Training limitations remain common, and a sizable proportion of NPs report insufficient familiarity with MCI criteria and evolving biomarker interpretation.39

These dual constraints of resource scarcity and training deficits mean that without adequate support, such as protected training time, integrated referral networks, and reimbursement for testing and comprehensive evaluation, the very clinicians positioned to expand access may struggle to navigate complex diagnostic pathways.

Therefore, absent targeted policy attention to build both diagnostic infrastructure and clinical competency, the expanded reliance on NPs and PAs in underserved settings risks perpetuating, rather than ameliorating, diagnostic inequities.52,53

Geographic “dementia diagnostic deserts”: a multidimensional policy typology

Geographic disparities in access to dementia diagnostics are well documented. Rural-urban differences in PET utilization, for example, have been attributed to workforce shortages, limited availability of imaging facilities, and long travel distances for patients.32 Nearly one-fifth of Americans live in rural areas that lack specialist centers offering advanced diagnostics, contributing to under diagnosis and delayed treatment.54 However, single-axis geographic classifications risk obscuring the compounded nature of disadvantage.

We propose a multidimensional geographic typology to identify dementia diagnostic deserts, defined by the intersection of rurality, primary care professional shortages, and social deprivation as they relate to accessing the full continuum of services for diagnosing cognitive impairment. This approach leverages RUCA classifications, HPSA designations, and SDI gradients to characterize counties where multiple structural barriers converge. We term counties meeting all 3 criteria—remote rural (RUCA 7-10), HPSA, and high SDI—as “Triple Disadvantage Zones.” These represent priority diagnostic deserts, characterized by diagnostic errors: a systemic failure to accurately channel patients into the appropriate diagnostic pathway due to compounded resource constraints. By moving beyond binary rural-urban distinctions, this policy-relevant typology offers a more precise framework for identifying high-priority regions for diagnostic support. Zones meeting multiple disadvantage criteria are likely to face fundamentally different diagnostic constraints than areas disadvantaged along only 1 dimension, warranting tailored policy responses that address geography, workforce, and infrastructure simultaneously. Practice-level infrastructure and accurate triage as the missing middle layer

This typology reveals macro-level geographic disadvantage. However, the critical conduit that translates these structural barriers into clinical reality is the often-overlooked determinant of practice-level infrastructure and clinician capacity for accurate triage. As illustrated in Figure 1 (Panel B), structural barriers (provider shortages, geographic isolation, social deprivation) manifest at the practice-level through 2 interconnected pathways: clinician gaps and patient barriers.

Clinician Gaps: In underserved areas, NPs and PAs often face clinical assessment gaps and a lack of triage competency. Time constraints, insufficient training in comprehensive evaluation, and limited access to specialist consultation impede their ability to reliably differentiate subjective cognitive complaints from objective impairment. This results in diagnostic errors, where patients with true pathology may be dismissed (leading to delayed diagnosis), while those with benign subjective concerns may be subjected to unnecessary biomarker testing (inappropriate over-testing).

Patient Barriers: High-risk populations face a convergence of travel burdens, out-of-pocket costs, and systemic mistrust, which often deters timely clinical presentation. Even when services are available, the cognitive and administrative load on families to navigate fragmented referral pathways can be overwhelming, underscoring the need for patient navigation support.

The Outcome: Diagnostic Inequity as Dual Failures. The combination of clinician gaps and patient barriers produces 2 distinct forms of diagnostic failure:

Delayed Diagnosis: Individuals with true pathology are missed due to under-recognition, lack of screening, or inability to access timely follow-up.

Addressing diagnostic deserts therefore requires policy attention not only to geography and workforce supply but also to the organizational capacity of primary care practices to adopt and operationalize guideline-recommended comprehensive diagnostic processes, with a focus on enhancing accurate triage skills and supporting patient navigation.

Policy implications: what can be done now

Existing studies point to several policy levers that could mitigate emerging inequities in dementia diagnostics. Effective interventions must address diagnostic errors to prevent both delayed diagnosis and inappropriate over-testing.

Targeted Reimbursement Strategies for a Comprehensive Diagnostic Pathway: Medicaid and Medicare reimbursement strategies could be tailored to support each step of the diagnostic pathway. This includes adequate reimbursement for the time required for comprehensive cognitive assessments in primary care, as well as for the cognitive work of triage and differential diagnosis. To address access in high-priority counties, financial assistance for specialist-based diagnostic services, including advanced testing when clinically appropriate, could be considered for eligible patients, reducing out-of-pocket barriers. Any incentives for advanced diagnostic technologies must be strictly conditional upon comprehensive clinical workups and appropriate specialist involvement, and paired with quality measures to ensure appropriate, evidence-based use. State and federal programs might adopt sliding-scale reimbursement models or direct financial assistance for diagnostic testing for practices in underserved areas.

Workforce Development in Comprehensive Dementia Diagnosis: Dementia-specific training initiatives, especially for NPs and PAs practicing in HPSAs and rural areas, could strengthen diagnostic capacity. Training must prioritize accurate triage—efficiently distinguishing subjective from objective impairment—alongside comprehensive assessment and biomarker interpretation. Simulation-based training and telementoring programs (eg, ECHO models) can help build these competencies. Building on this foundation, training should extend to include the appropriate use and interpretation of Alzheimer's biomarkers, patient communication, and culturally responsive care.

Addressing the Post-Diagnostic Treatment Desert: Even when a timely and accurate diagnosis is achieved in Triple Disadvantage Zones, patients face a parallel “treatment desert.” For the minority of patients who meet clinical and biomarker criteria for DMTs, subsequent steps, including specialist confirmation, access to infusion centers, and frequent MRI monitoring for amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (ARIA), remain severely constrained in rural and underserved regions. Without addressing these infrastructure gaps, an accurate diagnosis may become therapeutically moot. Policies aimed at diagnostic equity must therefore be paired with investments in regional infusion capacity, mobile health units, tele-neurology for monitoring, and care coordination to ensure that diagnosis translates into accessible treatment and ongoing clinical management.

A forward-looking research and policy agenda

While further empirical work is needed to quantify the magnitude of geographic and practice-level variation in dementia diagnosis, existing workforce and health system evidence already highlights clear structural vulnerabilities. Future research should leverage linked workforce, geographic, claims, and policy data to examine how state-level factors—such as scope-of-practice laws, telehealth reimbursement, and Medicaid policies—affect diagnostic behaviors among primary care clinicians and the resulting patient outcomes across the entire care continuum. Specifically, studies should measure the prevalence of diagnostic errors in Triple Disadvantage Zones, evaluate interventions designed to enhance accurate triage skills, and assess the impact of patient navigation programs on reducing both delayed diagnosis and inappropriate over-testing.

More broadly, ensuring equitable access to a timely and accurate dementia diagnosis will require sustained attention to the design of primary care systems. As DMTs and other evidence-based interventions become available, policies addressing workforce capacity, practice infrastructure, and geographic disadvantage will be essential to translating scientific advances into equitable clinical practice.
