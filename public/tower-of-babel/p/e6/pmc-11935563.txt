Loss of NAT10 Reduces the Translation of Kmt5a mRNA Through ac4C Modification in Cardiomyocytes and Induces Heart Failure
Authors: Ting Xu, Tailai Du, Xiaodong Zhuang, Xin He, Youchen Yan, Jialing Wu, Huimin Zhou, Yan Li, Xinxue Liao, Jiangui He, Chen Liu, Yugang Dong, Jingsong Ou, Shuibin Lin, Demeng Chen, Zhan‐Peng Huang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn the past decade, the biological functions of various RNA modifications in mammals have been uncovered. N4‐acetylcytidine (ac4C), a highly conserved RNA modification, has been implicated in human diseases. Despite this, the involvement of RNA ac4C modification in cardiac physiology and pathology remains incompletely understood. NAT10 (N‐acetyltransferase 10) stands as the sole acetyltransferase known to catalyze RNA ac4C modification. This study aims to explore the role of NAT10 and ac4C modification in cardiac physiology and pathology.Methods and ResultsCardiac‐specific knockout of NAT10, leading to reduced RNA ac4C modification, during both neonatal and adult stages resulted in severe heart failure. NAT10 deficiency induced cardiomyocyte apoptosis, a crucial step in heart failure pathogenesis, supported by in vitro data. Activation of the p53 signaling pathway was closely associated with enhanced apoptosis in NAT10‐deficient cardiomyocytes. As ac4C modification on mRNA influences translational efficiency, we employed ribosome footprints coupled with RNA sequencing to explore genome‐wide translational efficiency changes caused by NAT10 deficiency. We identified and validated that the translational efficiency of Kmt5a was suppressed in NAT10 knockout hearts due to reduced ac4C modification on its mRNA. This finding was consistent with the observation that Kmt5a protein levels were reduced in heart failure despite unchanged mRNA expression. Knockdown of Kmt5a in cardiomyocytes recapitulated the phenotype of NAT10 deficiency, including increased cardiomyocyte apoptosis and activated p53 signaling. Finally, overexpression of Kmt5a rescued cardiomyocyte apoptosis and p53 activation induced by NAT10 inhibition.ConclusionsOur study highlights the significance of NAT10 in cardiomyocyte physiology, demonstrating that NAT10 loss is sufficient to induce cardiomyocyte apoptosis and heart failure. NAT10 regulates the translational efficiency of Kmt5a, a key mediator, through mRNA ac4C modification during heart failure.Keywords: ac4C modification, heart failure, Kmt5a, NAT10, p53 signalingSubject Categories: Heart Failure, Myocardial Biology, Basic Science Research

In tandem with the extensive exploration of various protein modifications, the past decade has ushered in a wave of discoveries in novel RNA modifications, such as N6‐methyladenosine, 5‐methylcytosine, N1‐methyladenosine, N7‐methylguanosine, N6,2′‐O‐dimethyladenosine, N4‐acetylcytidine (ac4C), and 8‐oxoguanine modifications. These modifications intricately participate in diverse biological processes, including RNA folding, stabilization, translational enhancement, and alteration of nucleobase pairing. 1 , 2 In mammalian cells, a cadre of “writer” and “eraser” proteins meticulously regulates the levels of these RNA modifications, and “reader” proteins translate these modifications into biological effects. 3 Recent studies have underscored the close association of these modifications with the pathogenesis of numerous diseases, including cardiac diseases. 4 For instance, studies have revealed that the equilibrium of N6‐methyladenosine modifications plays a pivotal role in regulating cardiac remodeling and heart failure. 5 , 6 Another investigation demonstrated that 8‐oxoguanine on miR‐1 alters the base‐pairing of miR‐1 and its target mRNA, thereby inducing cardiac hypertrophy. 7 Our prior research also identified the impact of METTL5 loss on N7‐methylguanosine modification in 18s rRNA, contributing to cardiac remodeling. 8 Nevertheless, the comprehensive understanding of the roles of RNA modifications in cardiac diseases remains elusive.

Ac4C emerges as a highly conserved RNA modification throughout evolution, initially identified on tRNA. 9 Specifically, ac4C on the Wobble position of Met‐tRNA aids in correcting codon recognition. 10 Subsequently, ac4C was identified on 18s RNA, facilitating the biogenesis of pre‐18s RNA. 11 Recent investigations have unveiled the presence of ac4C in mRNA, primarily in the coding sequences (CDS) region. 10 This CDS‐located ac4C modification on mRNA has been implicated in influencing translation efficiency by modulating mRNA stability and nucleobase preferences during translation. In contrast, a recent study showed that ac4C modification presented in the 5′ UTR promotes the translational initiation at upstream sequences and, therefore, competitively inhibiting the translational initiation of main open reading frame and the subsequent mRNA translation. 12 Accumulating evidence suggests the intricate relationship between ac4C and pathological processes, such as tumorigenesis 13 and the generation of neuropathic pain. 14 However, its role in cardiac physiology and pathology remains largely unknown.

NAT10 (N‐acetyltransferase 10) stands as the sole known acetyltransferase responsible for catalyzing ac4C modification. 15 Loss of NAT10 significantly diminishes ac4C modification levels across various RNA molecules, positioning NAT10 as a pivotal gene for unraveling the biological functions of RNA ac4C modification. Previous reports have implicated NAT10 and RNA ac4C modification in the pathogenesis of heart disease and cardiac regeneration. 16 , 17 , 18 However, the genetic evidence substantiating the pathological role of NAT10 in cardiomyocytes is currently lacking. Given the embryonic lethality associated with NAT10 germline deletion, 19 a viable genetic model for studying NAT10 and RNA ac4C modification in cardiac disease has been elusive. In this study, we present mouse models featuring cardiac‐specific knockout of NAT10 during both neonatal and adult stages, resulting in reduced RNA ac4C modification levels. Loss of NAT10 in cardiomyocytes precipitated heart failure in both models. Mechanistically, diminished RNA ac4C modification compromised the translation of Kmt5a, activated the p53 signaling pathway, and induced cardiomyocyte apoptosis. Our findings elucidate the intricate role of NAT10 and RNA ac4C balance in the pathogenesis of heart failure.

METHODS

All supporting data are available within the article and its online supplementary files. All raw sequencing data generated in this study are available at the National Center for Biotechnology Information BioProject (PRJNA1044432).

Human Samples

Human heart samples were collected following the ethical guidelines and protocols approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital, Sun Yat‐sen University. Informed written consent was obtained from all subjects, and the investigation adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Animals

All animal experiments were approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital, Sun Yat‐sen University, and conducted following the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the National Academy of Sciences and the National Institutes of Health. Animal protocols and performances complied with the principles of animal care by the National Society for Medical Research and the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

The NAT10Flox/Flox mice, in which the fourth exon of NAT10 was floxed, on a C57BL/6N background were generated by Beijing Biocytogen Co., Ltd. Deletion of exon 4 by Cre recombinase will result in a truncated protein and lead to nonsense‐mediated decay. 13 Subsequently, NAT10Flox/Flox mice were crossbred with mice expressing Cre recombinase under the control of the cardiac‐specific Myh6 promoter (α‐MHC [α‐myosin heavy chain]) to achieve cardiac‐specific deletion of NAT10 (NAT10‐cKO). Mixed sex of NAT10‐cKO mice and control littermates were used in the study. Inducible cardiac‐specific NAT10 knockout mice (NAT10‐iKO) was obtained by crossing NAT10Flox/Flox mice with mice expressing Mercremer recombinase under the control of the cardiac‐specific Myh6 promoter. Eight‐week‐old NAT10‐iKO male mice were subjected to intraperitoneal injection of tamoxifen for 5 consecutive days to establish a cardiac‐specific NAT10 knockout mouse model in adult cardiomyocytes. Only male NAT10‐iKO mice and control littermates were used in the study due to differences in cardiac parameters between sexes. NAT10Flox/Flox or NAT10Flox/+ littermates, in which both copies of NAT10 are expressed, were used as control in both models.

Anesthesia of the mouse was performed with a nose cone delivering 2% to 4% isoflurane in oxygen via small animal ventilator. The setting of ventilator was 110 to 120 breaths per minute with a tidal volume of 0.1 mL under constant monitoring of the inspiratory pressure. At the end of the experiments, mice were euthanized with the intraperitoneal injection of an overdose of sodium pentobarbital (200 mg/kg).

Echocardiogram

Echocardiographic measurements were conducted using a Visual Sonics Vevo 2100 Imaging System (Visual Sonics, Toronto, Canada) with an 18 to 38 MHz MicroScan transducer (model MS400). Parameters including heart rate, left ventricular (LV) dimensions (LV posterior wall at end‐systole, LV posterior wall thickness at end‐diastole, LV internal dimension at end‐diastole, and functional parameters (percentage of fractional shortening and ejection fraction) were calculated using the primary measurements and accompanying software.

Histology and Immunostaining

Mouse heart was dissected, rinsed with PBS, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (pH 8.0) overnight. After dehydration and paraffin embedding, 5‐micrometer sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin for routine histological examination. Sirius red staining was performed to assess fibrosis. Wheat Germ Agglutinin (Alexa Fluor 594 Conjugate WGA) staining was used for labeling cardiomyocyte membranes. Histological images were examined using a light microscope or a fluorescence microscope (Zeiss; Imager.Z2), and quantification was performed using Image‐Pro Plus or ImageJ software for histological and immunostaining analyses, respectively.

Cell Culture and Transfection

Neonatal rat cardiomyocytes (NRVCs) were isolated from 1‐ to 2‐day‐old neonatal Sprague–Dawley rat hearts through repeated enzymatic dissociation. Following a 1‐hour preplating step to eliminate fibroblasts, nonadherent cells were plated on 0.5% gelatin‐coated plates and cultured in DMEM high‐glucose medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, sodium pyruvate (1×), GlutaMAX (1×), and antibiotics. Cardiomyocytes received a medium change 18 hours after plating, followed by adenovirus transduction for 24 hours in the presence of DMSO or remodelin (20–75 μmol/L) treatment for an additional 24 hours. H9c2 cardiomyocytes (ATCC, CRL‐1446) were cultured in DMEM with 10% fetal bovine serum at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Transfection of H9c2 cells with specified plasmids used Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen; L3000‐015) or polyethylenimine (1 mg/mL), as appropriate. Then, 25 to 50 μmol/L remodelin was used to treat H9c2 cardiomyocytes for NAT10 inhibition.

Gene Editing for NAT10 Deficiency in H9c2 Cardiomyocytes

CRISPR‐Cas9 gene editing technology was employed to knockout NAT10 in H9c2 cardiomyocytes. Transfection with the PX459 vector containing targeting gRNA1 or gRNA2 induced a frameshift mutation in NAT10. After 24 hours, cells underwent puromycin selection (2.5 mg/mL) for an additional 24 hours. Mixed cells were passaged in full medium after selection. Western blot analysis confirmed the loss of NAT10 protein, and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) products were used to validate mutations in regions flanking the targeting sites by Sanger sequencing. After confirmation, these 2 lines of mixed cells were used in the study.

Knockdown of Kmt5a Expression in Cells With siRNA

To knockdown Kmt5a expression in NRVCs, 50 nmol/L targeting siRNA (si‐Kmt5a‐1 and si‐Kmt5a‐2) or control siRNA was transfected using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX Transfection Reagent. After 6 hours, the transfection reagent was removed, and cells were cultured with full medium for 48 hours before protein extraction.

Kmt5a and NAT10 Expression Vector and Adenoviral Construction

Rat Kmt5a, human wild‐type NAT10 and mutant NAT10 (G641E) coding sequence were cloned into the adenoviral empty vector with the murine cytomegalovirus promotor. Following transfection of the shuttle vector and an adenoviral backbone plasmid (pAd‐ΔE1E3) into 293AD cells with polyethylenimine (1 mg/mL), adenovirus was produced. Subsequent transduction of 293AD cells with adenovirus allowed for viral amplification. Adenovirus was then collected, purified, and concentrated using the ViraTrap Adenovirus Purification Miniprep Kit.

mRNA Stability Assay

NAT10‐Control (Ctrl), NAT10‐gRNA1, and NAT10‐gRNA2 cell lines were cultured, and actinomycin D was added to the medium at a concentration of 200 nmol/L for experimental groups. After 0, 3, and 6 hours of treatment with actinomycin D, total RNA was extracted from cells for real‐time quantitative PCR (RT‐qPCR) to detect Kmt5a levels at each time point. The mRNA half‐life (t 1/2) was determined from the initial and final RNA amounts.

The statistical significance of difference between groups in data with repeated measurements was tested by mixed model repeated measures.

Western Blot

Protein lysates were prepared using RIPA buffer (Beyotime, P0013B) with protease inhibitor (Beyotime, ST506). After denaturation at 95 °C, protein samples were loaded onto SDS‐PAGE and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoridePVDF membranes (Merck‐Millipore, Germany). Polyvinylidene difluoride membranes were blocked with 5% BSA for 1 hour and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: anti‐NAT10 (Proteintech, 13 365‐1‐AP), anti‐Kmt5a (Proteintech, 14 063‐1‐AP), anti‐Bax (Proteintech, 50599‐2), anti‐Bcl‐2 (Proteintech, 12789‐1‐AP), anti‐p53 (CST, #2524), anti‐α‐tubulin (Proteintech, 11224‐1‐AP), and anti‐GAPDH (Proteintech, 60004‐1‐IG). After washing with TBST, secondary antibodies of the corresponding species were applied at room temperature for 1 hour. Immunoblot analysis was performed using Tanon image software (BioTanon, China), and ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD) was used for blot intensity quantification.

RNA Extraction and RT‐qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using RNAiso Plus (Takara Biomedical Technology, 9019) and reverse‐transcribed using the PrimeScript RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara Biomedical Technology, RR047B). RT‐qPCR used TB Green Advantage qPCR Premix (Takara Biomedical Technology, 639676). The mRNA expression levels were normalized to GAPDH. Primer sequences are listed in Table S1.

Immunofluorescence

Tissues and NRVCs were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 minutes at room temperature, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X‐100 in PBS for 10 minutes at room temperature, then blocked with 5% BSA in PBS with Tween 20 (PBST) for 1 hour, followed by incubation with TMR‐5‐dUTP (Servicebio, G1502, for TUNEL staining) for 1 hour at 37 °C. After washing with PBST 3 times, Tissues were incubated with DAPI (0.1 mg/mL, Sigma, D9542) in PBST for 10 minutes, then washed with PBST 3 times. NRVCs then incubation with anticardiac troponin I (1:200, Sigma‐Aldrich, ZRB1355) or anti‐α‐actinin (1:200, Sigma‐Aldrich, A7811) in 3% BSA overnight at 4 °C. After washing with PBST 3 times, cells were incubated with antimouse or rabbit secondary antibodies conjugated with Alexa 488 (1:1000; Thermo Fisher Scientific, A‐11029) or Alexa 647 (1:1000, Beyotime, A0473), together with DAPI (0.1 mg/mL, Sigma, D9542) in PBST for 1 hour.

Crystal Violet Test

NAT10‐Ctrl, NAT10‐gRNA1, and NAT10‐gRNA2 cells were cultured in 6‐well plates. Cells were fixed with 100% methanol for 10 minutes then washed with precooled PBS. Crystal violet dye was mixed with PBS and evenly sprayed on the surface of the cells using a pipette or sprayer. The staining time is usually between 10 and 30 minutes. The cell surface was washed with PBS to remove unbound dye. The stained cells were observed under a microscope.

Flow Cytometry Analysis

Cells were collected, washed with PBS, and centrifuged to remove culture medium and noncellular components. The obtained cells were washed and then suspended in PBS buffer supplemented with sterile 3% FBS and 1% BSA. Cells were mixed with fluorescent dyes, annexin V/PI or annexin V‐APC/7‐AAD dual dyes (Elabscience, E‐CK‐A211/ E‐CK‐A218, Guangzhou) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Flow cytometric analysis was performed on Beckman Coulter's CytoFlex LX. Data acquired were analyzed using FlowJo software (Tree Star).

Dot Blot

Dot blot was performed as previously described. Briefly, 5 μg of total RNA were denatured at 75 °C for 5 minutes and immediately cooled on ice for 3 minutes, then loaded onto Hybond‐N+ membranes. Denatured RNAs and crosslinked 3 times with 150 mJ/cm2 (UV 254 nm) in the Stratalinker 2400 (Stratagene), then blocked with 5% BSA in PBST for 1 hour at room temperature. Samples were probed with anti‐ ac4C antibody (1:2000, ab252215, Abcam) in 3% BSA at 4 °C overnight. RNA‐loaded membranes was washed with TBST 3 times, then incubated with antirabbit secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 hour. Image acquisition and analysis was performed using Tanon image software (BioTanon, China), and ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD). After washing with TBST, methylene were applied at room temperature for 1 hour, followed by TBST wash 3 times and scanning with Canon Scanner.

Prediction of mRNA ac4C Modification

The mRNA ac4C modification sites were predicted with the algorithm of prediction of N4‐acetylcytidine (ac4C) modification sites in mRNA (PACES) (http://www.rnanut.net/paces/). PACES combines 2 random forest classifiers: position specific dinucleotide sequence map and K nucleotide frequency. Using genomic sequences as input, the algorithm will list all the possible acetylated sequences in the query sequences and their ID. If there are overlapped motifs (>5 continuous CXX repeats), the prediction score is calculated and compared for every 5 consecutive CXX motifs among the overlapped motifs and only the CXX motif with the highest score will be shown. The possible acetylation motifs are marked red in the sequences and their starting and ending sites are also given. In addition, the score given by the predictor for each shown sequence is displayed for comparison.

acRIP‐qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from control and NAT10‐cKO mouse hearts. Then the total RNA was fragmented, the RNA sample (10% of total volume) was used as the input control and the rest of the RNA sample was incubated with anti‐ ac4C antibody (Abcam, ab252215) or control IgG (Proteintech, 30 000‐0‐AP) in immunoprecipitation buffer containing with RNase inhibitor at 4 °C for 2 hour, followed by adding Protein A/G Magnetic Beads (Thermo Fisher ScientificTM). The ac4C‐containing RNA sample was eluted from the beads. qRT‐PCR was performed with Kmt5a primers for both the input control and ac4C‐immunoprecipitated samples.

RNA‐Seq Analysis

The heart tissues from control and NAT10‐cKO mice were collected and placed into corresponding grinding tubes. 1.5 mL of TRIzol lysis buffer was added for sample extraction and purification. Poly(A) RNA was enriched using oligo(dT)‐coated magnetic beads, followed by fragmentation and synthesis of cDNA, end repair, and adaptor ligation. The PCR reaction was employed for amplification of the product. The library quality control protocol was selected based on specific requirements. Raw reads obtained from the deep sequencing were aligned to the mouse genome (Mus_musculus.GRCm38) using HISAT2. StringTie (v2.1.4) with default settings, then used to generate read counts. Read counts were filtered and standardized across samples. Differential gene expression analysis between groups was conducted using DESeq2 with criteria of fold change ≥2 and adjusted P value ≤0.05. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed genes was performed using the phyper function in R software.

Ribo‐Seq Analysis

Heart tissues from control and NAT10‐cKO mice, with 300 mg of samples from each group, were processed for Ribo‐seq analyses. The resuspended tissue extracts in lysis buffer were transferred to new microtubes, gently pipetted, and incubated on ice for 10 minutes. Following centrifugation at 20 000g for 10 minutes at 4 °C, the supernatant was collected. Ribosome footprints (RFs) were prepared by adding 7.5 μL of RNase I and 5 μL of DNase I to 300 μL of lysate, followed by a 45‐minute incubation at room temperature with gentle mixing. Nuclease digestion was halted by adding 10 μL of SUPERase·In RNase inhibitor. Size exclusion columns (illustra MicroSpin S‐400 HR Columns; GE Healthcare; catalog no. 27‐5140‐01) were equilibrated with 3 mL of polysome buffer and centrifuged at 600g for 4 minutes at room temperature. Subsequently, 100 μL of digested RFs were added to the column and centrifuged at 600g for 2 min. Elution was performed by adding 10 μL of 10% (wt/vol) SDS, and RFs larger than 17 nt were isolated using the RNA Clean and Concentrator‐25 kit (Zymo Research; R1017). Removal of rRNA was achieved using DNA probes complementary to rRNA sequences, followed by digestion of the probes using RNase H and DNase I. RFs were purified using magnetic beads (Vazyme). Ribo‐seq libraries were constructed using the NEBNext Multiple Small RNA Library Prep Set for Illumina (catalog no. E7300S, E7300L). This involved adding adapters to both ends of RFs, followed by reverse transcription and PCR amplification. The resulting PCR products of size 140 to 160 bp were enriched to generate a cDNA library, which was sequenced using the Illumina HiSeqTM 2500 platform by Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co. (Guangzhou, China). For Ribo‐seq analysis, clean reads were obtained after quality trimming and contaminant filtering. Adaptor sequences, low‐quality, and unpaired reads from raw sequencing data were removed using Cutadapt (v.0.dev0), Short reads alignment tool Bowtie was used for mapping reads to ribosome RNA (rRNA) database. The rRNA mapped reads will be removed. Clean reads were then aligned to the mouse genome (Mus_musculus.GRCm38) using STAR (v.2.4.2a) with ENCODE standard options for the long RNAseq pipeline. The mouse Gencode (M25/gencode.vM25) served as a reference. Read counts were generated using featureCounts (v.1.6.4) with default options. Differentially expressed genes were analyzed using DESeq2 and edgeR. Translation efficiency (TE) represents the rate of mRNA translation into proteins. We calculated TE using the xtail package (version 1.1.5), using the read counts of ribosome‐protected fragments from Ribo‐seq and the read counts of mRNA from RNA‐seq. The ratio of fold changes between ribosome‐protected fragments and mRNA was used to quantify the extent of differential translation. Differentially translated mRNAs were identified with the criteria: |fold change|≥2 and adjusted P value ≤0.05. Selected genes with significant alteration in TE were subjected to gene pathway analysis ionn the website of ToppGene (https://toppgene.cchmc.org/) with default parameters.

Statistical Analysis

The data in this study are presented as mean±SD unless noted otherwise. Error bars in scatterplots or bar plots represent the SD. Normality of data was evaluated by Shapiro–Wilk test when appropriate. For comparison between 2 groups, Student's t test was performed if the variable followed normal distribution, and Mann–Whitney U test was performed if it did not follow normal distribution. For comparison among multiple groups, either 1‐way or 2‐way (if there were 2 factor levels) ANOVA was performed for variables with normal distribution, otherwise Kruskal–Wallis H test was performed. For pairwise comparisons, post hoc tests were performed with Tukey's correction. Values of P<0.05 were considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Cardiac‐Specific NAT10 Deletion Induces Heart Failure

Our investigation initiated with the observation of a gradual decline in NAT10 protein expression in the heart postbirth, persisting beyond the weaning stage (Figure 1A). Correspondingly, the ac4C modification, catalyzed by NAT10, mirrored this trend in the cardiac total RNAs (Figure 1B). Recognizing the indispensability of NAT10 in development, germline deletion resulted in embryonic lethality. 19 To dissect the role of NAT10 in cardiac physiology and pathology, we strategically employed NAT10‐flox mice to circumvent embryonic lethality, enabling tissue‐specific NAT10 knockout. 13 The NAT10‐cKO was accomplished by crossing NAT10‐flox mice with αMHC‐Cre transgenic mice (Figure 1C), initiating Cre recombinase activation shortly after birth. As anticipated, NAT10‐cKO hearts exhibited a discernible reduction in RNA ac4C modification (Figure 1D). The postnatal ablation of NAT10 culminated in premature mortality, with no NAT10‐cKO mice surviving beyond 27 days (Figure 1E). Initial echocardiographic evaluations at 1 week showed no significant alterations in NAT10‐cKO mice. However, by 2 weeks, NAT10‐cKO hearts manifested significant ventricular dilation, thinner ventricular walls, and a larger internal diameter (Figure 1F through 1H and Figure S1). Whereas systolic function mildly decreased at 1 week, a pronounced decline and evident heart failure were observed at 2 weeks (Figure 1I and Figure S1), indicative of advanced heart failure. Although 1‐week‐old NAT10‐cKO hearts exhibited no obvious change, by 2 weeks, they displayed an enlarged ventricular chamber (Figure 1J), consistent with echocardiographic findings. Intriguingly, the dilated cardiomyopathy in NAT10‐cKO mice occurred in the absence of cardiac fibrosis (Figure S2A). Cardiac dilation often correlates with increased cardiomyocyte apoptosis. Notably, loss of NAT10 amplified cardiomyocyte apoptosis, evidenced by TUNEL assays at both 1 and 2 weeks (Figure 1K), along with an increase in the apoptosis marker Bax/Bcl‐2 protein ratio (Figure 1L). Despite the absence of apparent cardiomyocyte hypertrophy in NAT10‐cKO hearts (Figure S2B), markers associated with cardiac remodeling (Nppa and Nppb) and heart failure were markedly elevated at 2 weeks (Figure 1M).

Remarkably, NAT10 protein levels decreased significantly in heart samples from adult patients with end‐stage heart failure (Figure 2A). Consistently, lower levels of RNA ac4C modification were detected in the diseased group (Figure 2B). Considering the postnatal deletion of NAT10 precipitated heart failure and premature death, we interrogated whether NAT10 loss in the adult stage would also induce heart failure. To address this, we crossed NAT10‐flox mice with αMHC‐MerCreMer transgenic mice, incorporating tamoxifen‐induced Cre recombinase activation for the generation of NAT10‐iKO (Figure 2C). Following tamoxifen administration to adult (8‐week‐old) NAT10‐iKO mice, a reduction in RNA ac4C modification was evident (Figure 2D). By 4 weeks posttamoxifen administration, NAT10‐iKO mice exhibited a thinner ventricular wall, enlarged LV internal diameter and compromised systolic function compared with control littermates (Figure 2E through 2H, Figure S3), indicative of heart failure. Histological examination further confirmed ventricular dilation (Figure 2I). In contrast to NAT10‐cKO hearts, profound cardiac fibrosis (Figure 2J) and hypertrophic growth of cardiomyocytes (Figure 2K) were observed in NAT10‐iKO hearts, with these changes potentially being secondary effects of increased cardiomyocyte apoptosis. In line with results from NAT10‐cKO mice, cardiomyocyte apoptosis significantly increased in NAT10‐iKO hearts (Figure 2L), as supported by the increased Bax/Bcl‐2 protein ratio (Figure 2M). Concordantly, cardiac remodeling markers (Nppa and Nppb) and cardiac fibrosis markers (COL1a1 and COL3a1) were upregulated in NAT10‐iKO hearts (Figure 2N). Collectively, these findings underscore that cardiac‐specific NAT10 deletion induces heart failure in both postnatal and adult stages.

Induction of Cardiomyocyte Apoptosis by NAT10 Loss of Function

The in vivo findings underscore the pivotal role of abnormal cardiomyocyte apoptosis in the pathogenesis of heart failure following NAT10 loss. To further corroborate these observations in vitro, attempts were made to generate NAT10‐null H9c2 cardiomyocytes through CRISPR/Cas9‐mediated genome editing. However, the endeavors were unsuccessful, suggesting the indispensable role of NAT10 in cardiomyocyte survival. Nevertheless, two independent lines of mixed H9c2 cardiomyocytes with downregulated NAT10 expression, named NAT10‐gRNA1 and NAT10‐gRNA2, were successfully established using 2 distinct gRNAs (Figure 3A, Figure S4A). Consequently, a reduction in RNA ac4C modification was observed in these genome‐edited cardiomyocytes (Figure 3B). These 2 cell lines exhibited slower growth rates (Figure S4B), diminished colony‐formation capacities (Figure 3C), and increased apoptosis (Figure 3D). Employing an alternative strategy, we conducted a loss‐of‐function study using a specific NAT10 inhibitor, remodelin. 20 As anticipated, remodelin treatment reduced RNA ac4C levels and augmented apoptosis in neonatal rat cardiomyocytes (Figure S5, Figure 3E and 3F). Concurrently, temodelin treatment elevated the Bax/Bcl‐2 protein ratio in both neonatal rat cardiomyocytes (Figure 3G) and H9c2 cardiomyocytes (Figure 3H). Collectively, our in vivo and in vitro data unequivocally demonstrate that NAT10 deficiency induces cardiomyocyte apoptosis.

NAT10 Deficiency‐Induced Cardiomyocyte Apoptosis Linked to p53 Signaling Activation

To unravel the molecular mechanism underlying NAT10's function, we conducted RNA‐seq on NAT10‐cKO and control hearts at 1 week and 2 weeks after birth. In 1‐week‐old cKO hearts, 379 genes exhibited dysregulation, with 230 upregulated and 149 downregulated genes (Figure 4A, Table S2). In 2‐week‐old cKO hearts, 1444 genes displayed dysregulation, consisting of 718 upregulated and 726 downregulated genes (Figure 4B, Table S3). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway analysis revealed significant enrichment of the p53 signaling pathway, a key pathway regulating heart failure, 21 among upregulated genes at both time points (Figure 4C and 4D). Downregulated genes in 2‐week‐old cKO hearts were predominantly associated with metabolism and mitochondria‐related pathways (Figure 4D). Despite the absence of evident morphological or functional cardiac defects at 1 week, upregulation of the p53 signaling pathway with the most significant enrichment was observed, suggesting it as the primary response to NAT10 deficiency in the heart. Gene set enrichment analysis (analysis further confirmed p53 pathway upregulation (Figure 4E), validated by RT‐qPCR demonstrating increased expression of downstream p53 targets, including Bax, Bcl‐2, and p21 (Figure 4F). Interestingly, p53 mRNA and protein levels remained unchanged in NAT10‐cKO hearts (Figure 4G). Similar results were obtained in NAT10‐iKO hearts (Figure 4H and 4I). In vitro investigations also revealed that NAT10 loss of function increased the expression of p53 downstream targets in cardiomyocytes without altering p53 expression (Figure 4J and 4K). Collectively, these findings suggest that NAT10 deficiency induces cardiomyocyte apoptosis by activating the p53 signaling pathway, potentially involving mechanisms beyond p53 expression level modulation.

NAT10 Regulates the Translation of Kmt5a Via mRNA ac4C Modification

Previous studies have indicated that NAT10 catalyzes mRNA ac4C modification, influencing translational efficiency of mRNA molecules. 12 Indeed, NAT10 deficiency in cardiomyocytes led to reduced RNA ac4C levels both in vivo and in vitro (Figure 1D, 2C, 3B, Figure S5). In order to explore the translatomic change caused by NAT10 deficiency, we performed Ribo‐seq using NAT10‐cKO and control hearts (Figure S6). Ribo‐seq analysis, combined with RNA‐seq data, revealed significant changes in TE in nearly 100 genes, with 55 genes exhibiting higher TE and 40 genes showing lower TE in NAT10‐cKO hearts (Figure 5A, Table S4). Notably, many ribosomal proteins were among the genes with higher TE. Given that ac4C in CDS promoted translation, 12 upregulated TE in these ribosomal protein genes in NAT10‐cKO hearts were likely a secondary effect. Therefore, we focused on genes with lower TE. As a result, the Reactome term “Regulation of TP53 activity” was shown significantly enriched in genes with lower TE (Figure 5B), connecting to p53 signaling activation in NAT10‐cKO hearts. In detail, 4 genes within this term, Kmt5a, Chek1, Aurkb, and Ccna2, had lower TE (Figure 5B). Next, we predicted whether there was ac4C modification in CDSs of these 4 genes using PACES. 22 It turned out that only CDS of Kmt5a was predicted to possess ac4C modification sites. Similar modification site was also predicted present in Kmt5a CDSs of human and rat (Figure 5C). This highlighted Kmt5a as a potential mediator of NAT10's antiapoptotic effect.

Previous research has identified Kmt5a as a methyltransferase that inhibits p53 transcriptional activity through methylation. 23 Indeed, cardiac protein levels of Kmt5a were diminished in NAT10 deficiency both in vivo and in vitro, with no significant change in mRNA expression (Figure 5D through 5F). These data confirmed the downregulation of Kmt5a TE induced by NAT10 deficiency. Adenovirus‐mediated Kmt5a overexpression (contain only CDS) in primary neonatal rat cardiomyocytes was further employed to validate the impact of NAT10 on Kmt5a TE. Remodelin‐induced NAT10 inhibition suppressed ectopic Kmt5a protein expression without affecting mRNA (CDS) levels (Figure 5G). Unlike Kmt5a, METTL1, a gene lacking predicted ac4C modification in its CDS, remained unaffected by remodelin treatment in the adenovirus‐mediated overexpression (Figure S7). Ac4C‐RIP‐qPCR confirmed the presence of ac4C modification in Kmt5a's CDS, decreased in NAT10‐cKO hearts (Figure 5H). Ac4C modification is known to enhance mRNA stability. 10 Consistently, Kmt5a mRNA stability decreased in NAT10‐edited H9c2 cardiomyocytes (Figure 5I). Furthermore, overexpression of wild‐type NAT10, which increased ac4C RNA modification level (Figure 5J), induced the protein expression of Kmt5a in NRVCs (Figure 5K). In contrast, overexpression of a catalytic dead mutant NAT10 (NAT10‐G641E) failed to induce Kmt5a protein expression (Figure 5K), suggesting that ac4C RNA modification is required for NAT10‐mediated Kmt5a expression regulation. Together, these results indicate that NAT10 catalyzes ac4C modification on Kmt5a mRNA, promoting its translation and Kmt5a protein levels. The subsequent downregulation of Kmt5a protein expression in NAT10‐deficient cardiomyocytes may contribute to heightened p53 signaling activity.

Loss of Kmt5a Induces Cardiomyocyte Apoptosis

We sought to determine the involvement of Kmt5a in cardiomyocyte apoptosis and heart failure. In heart samples from patients with end‐stage heart failure, the protein level of Kmt5a was markedly downregulated, despite unchanged mRNA expression (Figure 6A and 6B). This observation, consistent with decreased RNA ac4C modification in human failing hearts (Figure 2A), indicated that Kmt5a TE is reduced in heart failure (Figure 6C). Single‐cell RNA‐seq data from public databases revealed predominant expression of Kmt5a in cardiomyocytes (Figure S8), underscoring its potential significance in cardiac physiology and pathology. Knockdown of Kmt5a using 2 independent siRNAs in neonatal rat cardiomyocytes resulted in cardiomyocyte apoptosis (Figure 6E and 6F), as supported by the reduced cell density (Figure S9) and the increased Bax/Bcl‐2 protein ratio (Figure 6G). Loss of Kmt5a also activated the p53 signaling pathway, as indicated by elevated expression of downstream targets Bax, Bcl‐2, and p21 (Figure 6H). Collectively, these findings established that loss of Kmt5a induces cardiomyocyte apoptosis by activating the p53 signaling pathway, a process closely associated with heart failure pathogenesis.

Kmt5a Overexpression Rescues NAT10 Deficiency‐Induced Cardiomyocyte Apoptosis

To further validate Kmt5a as a key downstream effector of NAT10 in cardiomyocytes, we investigated whether overexpressing Kmt5a could rescue NAT10 deficiency‐induced cardiomyocyte apoptosis. Consistently, Remodelin treatment increased cardiomyocyte apoptosis and reduced cell density in neonatal rat cardiomyocytes (Figure 7A and 7B, Figure S10). Notably, adenovirus‐mediated overexpression of Kmt5a counteracted this effect (Figure 7A and 7B, Figure S10), as supported by the decreasing of Bax/Bcl‐2 protein ratio induced by remodelin (Figure 7C). Furthermore, remodelin‐induced upregulation of p53 downstream targets, including Bax, Bcl‐2, and p21 mRNA expression, was mitigated by Kmt5a overexpression (Figure 7D). These findings support a model in which Kmt5a serves as a key downstream effector of NAT10 through RNA ac4C modification, regulating p53 signaling and cell apoptosis in cardiomyocytes (Figure 7E).

DISCUSSION

Previous investigations have characterized NAT10 as a multifaceted acetyltransferase, contributing to various biological processes both in physiological and pathological contexts. 24 Physiologically, maternal NAT10 orchestrates the meiosis and maturation of oocytes, 25 while also playing a crucial role in regulating mitosis through the acetylation of the motor protein Eg5. 26 Pathologically, NAT10 involvement has been observed in diverse scenarios, such as promoting histone acetylation in the Kank1 promotor, influencing T‐cell apoptosis, 27 and enhancing cellular proliferation in multiple myeloma via acetylation of CEP170 mRNA. 28 Notably, NAT10's role in translation was highlighted in a recent study, where it facilitated VEGFA translation through mRNA ac4C modification, contributing to neuropathic pain after neural injury. 14

During the course of our investigation, 2 relevant studies on NAT10 in cardiac diseases were published, 16 , 17 demonstrating its mediation of heart‐apoptosis‐ssociated piRNA in cardiomyocyte apoptosis during ischemia and its role in cardiac hypertrophy via mRNA ac4C modification. In our study, using genetic animal models, we elucidated the important role of NAT10 in cardiomyocytes during heart failure. Our findings indicated that NAT10 deficiency alone was sufficient to induce cardiomyocyte apoptosis. It appears that NAT10 may have different roles, which are likely to be dosage‐dependent, in cardiomyocyte. Partial loss of NAT10 does not affect cardiomyocyte survival but decreases the capacity for hypertrophic growth. In contrast, complete or near‐complete loss of NAT10 significantly affects cardiomyocyte survival. Mechanistically, we uncovered that NAT10 deficiency activated the p53 signaling pathway by decreasing mRNA ac4C modification on Kmt5a, a repressor of p53, ultimately affecting its translation. Rescue experiments supported that Kmt5a mostly, if not all, mediates the phenotype of cardiomyocyte apoptosis induced by the loss of NAT10. Other potential downstream effectors in this model need to be determined in the future study. Interestingly, previous studies have reported inconsistent effects of NAT10 on p53 signaling. For example, a previous study showed NAT10 downregulated p53 protein level by acetylating MDM2 (mouse double minute 2 homolog) mRNA and increasing MDM2 protein level, 29 and another report showed NAT10 acetylated p53 to prevent ubiquitination by MDM2 and stabilized p53 protein. 30 These observations suggest that the biological function of NAT10 is context dependent.

Despite extensive research on Kmt5a in tumors, 31 its role in the heart has remained largely unexplored. Our study revealed a tight association between downregulation of Kmt5a and heart failure, with knockdown of Kmt5a inducing cardiomyocyte apoptosis through activation of the p53 signaling pathway. Importantly, overexpression of Kmt5a rescued remodelin‐induced p53 signaling activation and cardiomyocyte apoptosis, emphasizing the crucial role of Kmt5a in cardiomyocyte function and heart failure. Similar findings in cancer cells, where Kmt5a regulates cell proliferation/apoptosis in a p53‐dependent manner, 32 , 33 further underscore its significance. Kmt5a is a lysine methyltransferase that can methylate histone as well as other protein substrates. 31 Kmt5a has been reported to methylate p53 or its interacting protein Numb, which further regulated the p53 signaling. The detailed molecular mechanisms by which Kmt5a regulates the p53 signaling pathway in cardiomyocytes, unaddressed in this study, warrant future investigation, potentially revealing Kmt5a as a promising therapeutic target for heart failure.

CONCLUSIONS

Traditionally, the focus in human diseases, including heart diseases, has been on transcriptional regulation. However, emerging evidence, including findings of ours and others, suggests that translational regulation is a crucial aspect of pathogenesis. 34 , 35 , 36 Previous studies have demonstrated an upregulation of TE in cardiac remodeling, 35 leading to increased ribosome amount and translation capacity in cardiomyocytes. A similar phenomenon extends to other cell types, such as vascular smooth muscle cells. 37 Interestingly, our study revealed increased translational efficiency of multiple ribosomal proteins in NAT10‐cKO hearts, potentially a secondary effect of cardiac remodeling due to the loss of cardiomyocytes. The detailed mechanisms governing the upregulation of translational efficiency of ribosomal proteins during cardiac remodeling remain unknown. Unlike global translational regulation, our study, along with others, highlights the significance of RNA modification in regulating the translational efficiency of specific genes, influencing disease pathogenesis. 8 , 13 , 38 Context‐dependent translational regulation through RNA modifications poses an intriguing avenue for future exploration. Understanding translational regulation mechanisms will deepen our comprehension of pathogenesis in human diseases, offering potential therapeutic interventions through targeted translation modulation.
