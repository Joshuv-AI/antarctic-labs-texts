Geospatial analysis of vegetation and land surface temperature for urban heat island mitigation in Hawassa City, Ethiopia
Authors: Zenebe Reta Roba, Kenate Worku Tabor
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Land Surface Temperature (LST) has emerged as a critical environmental parameter globally due to its profound impact on urban microclimates. To mitigate urban heat islands, it is crucial to use advanced geospatial techniques to map and analyze vegetation and land surface temperature for informed urban planning decisions. The main objective of this study was to examine the relationship between land surface temperature (LST) and vegetation cover in Hawassa City, employing GIS and remote sensing techniques to inform strategies for mitigating the urban heat island effect. Three different satellite datasets of Landsat 5 TM (1991), Landsat 7 ETM + (2005), and Landsat 8 OLI (2021) were employed. LST were computed from these thermal bands of datasets using mono-window algorithms. Regression and correlation analyses were conducted among LST, Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI), and Normalized Difference Built-up Index (NDBI). The results of the land cover change analysis indicate that between 1991 and 2021, dense and sparse vegetation cover declined by 1.6% and 19.3%, respectively, while built-up areas increased substantially by 23.2%. This reflects rapid urban expansion at the expense of vegetated areas. A consistent decline in urban vegetation relative to built-up areas over the three decades, contributing significantly to the increase in LST. Furthermore, the analysis revealed a negative correlation between Land Surface Temperature (LST) and the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI), and a positive correlation with the Normalized Difference Built-up Index (NDBI), indicating that NDBI is a more effective predictor of LST than NDVI. Increasing urban green spaces and water bodies was shown to effectively reduce temperatures, with water bodies contributing to a decrease in maximum temperatures by 2.1 °C in 1991, 1.2 °C in 2005, and 0.5 °C in 2021. Therefore, expanding urban green infrastructure and rehabilitating vacant lands are vital strategies for mitigating urban heat island effects and promoting sustainable, climate-resilient development in Hawassa City

Introduction

Urban environmental challenges are increasingly pressing due to the rapid pace of global urbanization, resource scarcity, and environmental degradation1. Among these, the Urban Heat Island (UHI) effect characterized by elevated temperatures in urban areas compared to surrounding rural regions has emerged as a critical concern. A key driver of UHI is the alteration of surface energy balance due to reduced vegetation cover and increased impervious surfaces, which trap and re-emit solar radiation, thereby intensifying Land Surface Temperature (LST)2,3.

LST is a key parameter influencing urban climate dynamics and has become a widely used indicator in studying the UHI effect4. Studies have established that higher LST levels are positively associated with urban built-up areas and negatively associated with vegetation cover, highlighting the importance of green infrastructure in mitigating urban thermal stress5,6. Vegetation enhances evapotranspiration and provides shading, significantly lowering urban temperatures and improving microclimatic conditions7.

Across African cities, the UHI phenomenon has become increasingly evident due to uncontrolled urban expansion and the conversion of vegetated land into built-up surfaces8. In particular, recent studies emphasize that Sub-Saharan cities are highly vulnerable to the adverse effects of UHI due to limited adaptive infrastructure and rapid land use changes9. The spatial configuration of land cover types significantly influences LST distribution, as urban thermal patterns are closely linked to the proportion and spatial arrangement of vegetation, water bodies, and built-up areas10.

In the Ethiopian context, rapid urbanization has led to significant land cover changes and alterations in the local climate. The expansion of built-up areas has resulted in the substantial loss of vegetation and agricultural lands, contributing to increased land surface temperatures (LST) and intensifying the Urban Heat Island (UHI) effect. These changes have also negatively impacted local biodiversity, water resources, and overall environmental sustainability11–13. Specifically, between 1999 and 2020, Hawassa’s built-up area expanded dramatically, largely at the expense of green spaces and agricultural land14,15. This transformation has coincided with rising urban temperatures and a higher frequency of heat stress events, posing significant challenges to urban sustainability, human health, and ecological function.

Although the Ethiopian government has initiated urban greening programs to address these challenges, the effectiveness of such initiatives remains under-studied, particularly concerning spatial variations of LST and their integration into urban planning. Globally, research on LST and UHI effects has accelerated in recent years; however, most studies focus on major metropolitan areas in developed countries. There remains a critical gap in understanding how these dynamics manifest in sub-Saharan African cities, where rapid urbanization often occurs amid limited planning and inadequate green infrastructure.

While some studies related to urban heat mitigation through vegetation have been conducted in Ethiopian cities, their methodologies are often inadequate for capturing the complex interactions between land use/land cover (LULC) changes and LST16. The specific degree to which LULC influences urban LST remains poorly quantified, limiting the ability to provide precise recommendations for land-use optimization and landscape planning. Moreover, the roles of heat sources and sinks within urban environments particularly the cooling effects of vegetation have not been sufficiently explored using methods such as the heat contribution index. Previous research has generally overlooked the abundance and spatial distribution of vegetation and its potential to mitigate UHI effects17,18.

In particular, few studies have quantitatively assessed the long-term interactions among vegetation cover, built-up expansion, and LST using advanced geospatial techniques in the Ethiopian context. Hawassa City, as a rapidly growing urban center, exemplifies these challenges but has received limited scientific attention regarding its thermal environment and land cover dynamics. This study aims to fill this gap by integrating multi-temporal satellite datasets and geospatial indices specifically the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) and Normalized Difference Built-up Index (NDBI) to analyze the spatiotemporal evolution of LST over the past three decades. By examining the correlations between vegetation loss, urban expansion, and temperature rise, this study provides evidence-based insights essential for informing urban climate adaptation, sustainable planning, and environmental management toward resilient and sustainable urban development.

Material and methods

Description of the study area

Hawassa City is the regional capital of the Sidama Regional State. The city spreads over 157.2 km2 and is located in the central part of the main Ethiopian Rift in Southern Ethiopia, about 270 km south of the capital Addis Ababa. It is located at the latitudes of 6.55N in the south and 7.6N in the north and longitudes of 38.25E in the west and 38.34E in the east. The city is characterized by rapid urbanization and is one of the most rapidly expanding cities in the country (Fig. 1). Hawassa city is categorized under the sub-tropical climatic zones; the area has hot and dry weather for the greater part of winter and warm and sunny in summer. There are four climatic seasons: Kremt (rainy season), Bega (dry season), Belg (small rains), and Meher (a period in between the long and small rain seasons). Hawassa city and its immediate surroundings lie on the border between various agro-climatic zones. Topographically, Hawassa City is predominantly flat, with a few scattered hills, including Mount Tabor (1,800 m) and Mount Alamura (2,019 m). The elevation across the rift escarpments and ridges to the east and west varies from low to high. The eastern escarpment marks the edge of the Rift Valley, while the southern and western scarps part of the caldera structure form an arc-like shape and exhibit relatively moderate elevation differences compared to the valley floor. Overall, the elevation in the area ranges from 1,652 to 2,134 m above mean sea level.

Methods

Data types and sources

Reliable data are essential to achieving the objectives of this study. Therefore, both primary and secondary data were collected from various sources. Primary data were gathered through field observations conducted across the study area to gain insight into land use and land cover (LULC) practices and to accurately identify each LULC type. Secondary data included reviews of relevant published and unpublished articles, Digital Elevation Model (DEM) data, climate data, satellite images, and shapefiles. The major secondary data source was Landsat satellite imagery, which was used to retrieve LULC thematic layers and investigate land surface temperature (LST). Specifically, Landsat 5 TM (Thematic Mapper) images for 1991, Landsat 7 ETM + (Enhanced Thematic Mapper Plus) images for 2005, and Landsat 8 OLI/TIRS (Operational Land Imager/Thermal Infrared Sensor) images for 2021 were acquired. All images were collected during the dry season (January and February) to reduce seasonal variability and cloud cover. The data correspond to Path 168 / Row 55, covering Hawassa city and its surroundings, with a spatial resolution of 30 m for multispectral bands and 120 m, 60 m, and 100 m for the thermal bands of Landsat 5, 7, and 8 respectively (Table 1). Additionally, GPS points collected during fieldwork were used to support classification and accuracy assessments. The use of 30-m resolution imagery (Landsat) in this study is justified by its proven effectiveness in land cover classification, particularly for large-scale environmental assessments. Although moderate in resolution, Landsat data have been widely employed in previous study to distinguish vegetation density levels using spectral indices such as the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI). In this study, NDVI thresholds were applied to classify vegetation into grass, sparse, and dense categories. These thresholds were based on literature and validated through ground reference data and visual interpretation. Software used for the study

Software packages play a critical role in generating accurate maps and performing spatial analyses in geospatial studies21. In this study, ArcGIS version 10.8 was used for mapping, spatial analysis, and visualization. Additionally, ERDAS IMAGINE 2015 was employed for satellite image preprocessing and classification. The integration of these software tools ensured effective handling, interpretation, and presentation of geospatial data.

Data processing and analysis

Landsat image preprocessing refers to the series of steps applied to raw satellite data to prepare it for further analysis. Preprocessing ensures the images are corrected, standardized, and enhanced to improve the accuracy and reliability of the results22. In this study, preprocessing steps such as band combination, layer stalking, and subseting to the study area were applied prior to analysis.

LULC classifications

Following the preprocessing steps, image classification was performed to categorize the land cover types in the study area. This involved using supervised classification techniques such as Maximum Likelihood Classification (MLC) to assign pixels to different land cover classes based on spectral signatures. The classified images were then validated using ground truth data to assess the accuracy of the classification results. Moreover, post-classification analysis was conducted to quantify the extent of each land cover class and assess changes over time. This analysis provided valuable insights into the trends of urbanization, vegetation cover, and built-up areas in the study area, which are crucial for understanding the dynamics of LST and its relationship with land cover types23. Supervised classification approaches were applied to classify the images of the 1991, 2005, and 2021 years. A MLC algorithm was employed for the image classification. It is profoundly based on the quality and quantity of training samples to produce good quality classification results24. The classification process was begun by selecting training areas with careful observation of the images and feature cover types within the images. The classes of land cover were: (i) water; (ii) built-up; (iii) barren land; (iv) grassland; (v) sparse vegetation; and (vi) dense vegetation25. The accuracy assessment also was assessed to quantify the output quality of the categorization process. To examine the pixel-based classification output for LULC classification, an error matrix based on the evaluation of the overall accuracy, the producer’s accuracy, the user’s accuracy, and the kappa coefficient was used. The accuracy assessment’s goal is to determine quantitatively how effectively the pixels were classified into the appropriate land cover groups26.

Accuracy assessment

To reduce errors during land cover classification, an accuracy assessment was conducted using observed ground truth data27. A stratified random sampling technique was used to select 60 samples for each LULC class from Google Earth Pro for each year of imagery. Ground control points were collected from Google Earth Pro for inaccessible areas and with GPS for accessible areas. ArcGIS software was then used to compile and statistically evaluate the classified results28.

Classification accuracy was assessed using a confusion matrix29. The confusion matrix consists of rows and columns, where the columns represent reference (ground truth) data and the rows represent the classified data. The diagonal values of the matrix indicate the number of correctly classified pixels. Overall accuracy is calculated by dividing the total number of correctly classified pixels by the total number of reference pixels (Eq. 1). Additionally, the Kappa coefficient (or Khat) was computed to measure the level of agreement between the classified map and the reference data, accounting for all elements of the error matrix30. Extraction of built-up index

The Normalized Difference Built-up Index (NDBI) is a widely used remote sensing index that helps identify urban built-up areas by analyzing the spectral characteristics of Landsat TM, ETM, and Landsat 8 imagery. By comparing the reflectance values from the mid-infrared and red bands, the NDBI formula calculates a ratio that highlights built-up urban areas. However, vegetation signals can sometimes interfere with the accuracy of NDBI results. Despite this limitation, the NDBI remains a valuable tool for detecting urban areas and studying land use changes using satellite imagery. Extraction normalized difference vegetation index

The spatial distribution of vegetation in Hawassa city was identified and assessed by analyzing the NDVI values. NDVI values typically fall within the range of − 1 to + 1, with -1 indicating a lack of vegetation and + 1 indicating the presence of dense vegetation. These values are useful for conducting change detection analysis to track changes in vegetation cover over time. The very low value of NDVI (0.1 and below) corresponds to barren areas of rock, sand, or snow42. The values of NDVI can further be categorized as non-vegetated for the value < 0, unhealthy vegetation for the value between 0.02 and 0.03, and moderate values represent bush and grasses (0.2 to 0.3), whereas, high NDVI value correspond to dense vegetation (0.6 to 0.8)43. NDVI was acquired from spectral reflectance measurements in the visible (Red) and near-infrared regions (NIR) in the ArcGIS environment. LULC changes between 1991, 2005 and 2021

The LU/LCC analysis conducted in this study aimed to assess the changes in land use and land cover over a 30-year period and examine how these changes relate to variations in land surface temperature. The results of the analysis for the years 1991, 2005, and 2021 revealed significant shifts in land use and land cover within the city.

Specifically, the built-up area of the city expanded considerably over the three decades, with total areas of 16.7 km2 (6.8%), 49.7 km2 (20.2%), and 73.3 km2 (30%) in 1991, 2005, and 2021, respectively. In contrast, vegetation cover, comprising both dense and sparse vegetation, experienced a decline in coverage. The total area covered by vegetation was 13.9 km2 (5.7%), 11 km2 (4.5%), and 10.2 km2 (4.1%) for the same years. Sparse vegetation, in particular, decreased significantly, with total areas of 92.4 km2 (37.6%), 64.6 km2 (26.3%), and 44.8 km2 (18.3%) in 1991, 2005, and 2021, respectively.

The study revealed a substantial increase in built-up areas and a decrease in urban green vegetation, particularly dense vegetation like indigenous forest and thicket, from 1991 to 2021. The proportion of built-up areas expanded significantly from 6.8% to 30%, making it the most notable change in land cover type within the city. This shift towards increased urbanization has important implications for land surface temperature dynamics and overall environmental quality in the area (Table 4 and Fig. 2). It is rapidly increasing with parallels and resembles to the context of rapid urbanization process in Hawassa high population growth trends. From this result it’s possible to deduce that, the LU/LCC experienced across Hawassa city shows as a sign of population growth and the increase of needs of land for settlement, urban infrastructure, and industries. These changes have brought about a series of adverse impacts on the environment. The conversion of land use and land cover into built-up areas has accelerated significantly, as indicated by49. The expansion of impervious areas, such as built-up areas, has indeed led to the significant increase in land surface temperature within urban areas. As impervious surfaces like roads, buildings, and parking lots replace natural vegetation, they absorb and retain heat, contributing to the urban heat island effect. This phenomenon results in higher temperatures in urban areas compared to surrounding rural areas, impacting local climates, energy consumption, and public health. The dominance of built-up areas in the land use/land cover composition further emphasizes the need for sustainable urban planning strategies to mitigate the adverse effects of urbanization on the environment and human well-being.

Policy implications

The findings of this study underscore the critical need for coordinated, multi-level policy interventions to address the rise in Land Surface Temperature (LST) and the intensifying Urban Heat Island (UHI) effect driven by rapid urban expansion and vegetation loss in Hawassa City. At the local level, municipal authorities must integrate urban green spaces into all development plans, enforce regulations mandating green buffers, parks, green roofs, and the rehabilitation of degraded urban lands. Public engagement through awareness campaigns and community-based stewardship programs is essential to ensure the sustainability of these green initiatives. At the regional level, establishing interconnected ecological networks and harmonizing planning frameworks across municipalities will enhance ecosystem connectivity and resilience. Regional coordination should also support technical capacity-building and knowledge exchange. At the national level, governments should enact protective legislation for UGS, provide financial incentives for green infrastructure, and allocate dedicated resources for urban greening programs. Furthermore, integrating urban green spaces into national climate adaptation and public health strategies, coupled with public education campaigns, can amplify environmental and social co-benefits. A cohesive policy approach across governance levels is vital for fostering climate-resilient, livable urban environments.

Conclusions

Cutting-edge geospatial technologies are indispensable for understanding human -environment interactions and their environmental consequences. In this study, Landsat TM 5, ETM + 7, and Landsat 8 OLI/TIRS satellite data were utilized to assess land use/land cover (LU/LC) dynamics and their relationship with Land Surface Temperature (LST) in Hawassa City. The analysis focused on the correlation between LST and key indices such as the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) and the Normalized Difference Built-up Index (NDBI). The findings indicate that the rapid expansion of impervious built-up areas and the reduction of vegetation cover are strongly associated with increased surface temperatures. Between 1991 and 2021, NDVI values declined from 0.67 to 0.36, while built-up areas expanded significantly from 16.7 km2 to 73.3 km2. These changes illustrate considerable transformations in land cover that have contributed to LST variation. Although strong correlations were found between LST and both NDVI (R2 = 0.935) and NDBI (R2 = 0.991), these relationships suggest that vegetation cover significantly mitigates surface temperature, while built-up areas exacerbate it, underscoring the crucial influence of land cover on urban thermal dynamics. Therefore, preserving and expanding green spaces is vital for regulating urban heat, enhancing thermal comfort, and improving overall livability in rapidly growing cities.

Recommendation and future Directions

To mitigate the Urban Heat Island (UHI) effect and promote climate-resilient urban development, this study recommends a set of actionable strategies. These include expanding urban greening initiatives such as the establishment of parks, green corridors, and rooftop gardens enforcing zoning regulations to protect existing vegetation and curb unplanned urban sprawl, and promoting the use of high-albedo and permeable construction materials to reduce surface heat absorption. These integrated approaches can significantly enhance urban thermal comfort, support ecological integrity, and foster sustainable growth in rapidly urbanizing cities.

For future research, it is essential to incorporate a broader range of environmental and socio-economic variables to better understand the multifaceted drivers of Land Surface Temperature (LST) dynamics. Additionally, future studies should utilize higher-resolution satellite imagery to capture finer-scale spatial variations, enabling more precise assessments of urban heat patterns and green space effectiveness. Such advancements will contribute to more targeted and informed urban climate adaptation strategies.
