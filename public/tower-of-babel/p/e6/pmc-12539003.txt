The planetary diet: a nutritional utopia in conflict with human evolution
Authors: Jean Pierre Spinosa, Derek C. Lee, Tomas Duraj, Thomas N. Seyfried
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Planetary Health Diet proposed by the EAT–Lancet Commission aims to promote global health and environmental sustainability through a predominantly plant-based, carbohydrate-rich nutritional model. Despite its appeal, its universal application raises concerns regarding human metabolic compatibility, nutrient sufficiency, and evolutionary coherence.ObjectivesThis article critically evaluates the Planetary diet through the lenses of evolutionary biology, clinical nutrition, cancer metabolism, and anthropological diversity. An alternative framework is proposed: The autogenous diet, which aligns more with human physiological and ecological heritage.MethodsA narrative synthesis was conducted of peer-reviewed literature from evolutionary nutrition, clinical trials, oncology, and chronobiology. Comparative analysis highlights the metabolic divergence between high-carbohydrate, standardized diets and fat-adapted, ancestral nutritional models.ResultsThe planetary diet fails to meet several human biological requirements. It neglects nutrient-dense animal foods rich in DHA, vitamin B12, and bioavailable iron; promotes chronic glycemic load and insulin signaling; and ignores interindividual genetic and epigenetic variability. By contrast, the autogenous diet contains a species-appropriate macronutrient distribution (moderate protein, high fat, low carbohydrate), prioritizes local and minimally processed foods, aligns with circadian biology and fasting cycles, and elicits tumor-suppressive mechanisms.ConclusionThe universalization of the planetary diet represents a biological and cultural oversimplification. Nutritional strategies must account for evolutionary metabolism, regional diversity, and clinical outcomes. Ancestral, autogenous diets may offer a more effective and ecological solution that can meet nutritional requirements based on fundamental physiology.

Introduction

In 2019, the EAT-Lancet Commission introduced the planetary health diet —a nutrition initiative designed to ensure global health while respecting planetary boundaries, notably climate, land use, and biodiversity [1]. Presented as the solution to feeding 10 billion people sustainably by 2050, this diet promotes a high-carbohydrate, plant-based eating pattern with minimal intake of animal products. While the initiative appears commendable, a critical analysis reveals that this model is not only nutritionally precarious but is also clinically inappropriate in many cases. This article provides a scientific, metabolic, and anthropological critique of the Planetary Diet, highlighting the disconnect from human physiological requirements, potential health risks, and dependence on artificial supplementation and industrial food chains.

The planetary diet: a metabolic mismatch with homo sapiens

Homo sapiens and their ancestors survived as omnivores, relying heavily on hunting, scavenging, and foraging [2]. The Homo sapiens diet was rich in animal fats, proteins, and organ meats, and low in digestible carbohydrates [2]. The genus Homo survived as hunter-gatherers under conditions of seasonal food scarcity, intermittent fasting, and minimal carbohydrate availability for nearly 2.5 million years [3]. In this context, fat oxidation and ketone body production were not pathological states, but instead central metabolic strategies for survival and cerebral development [4, 5]. Humans possess a remarkable capacity for fat storage and metabolism; the liver has a high capacity for ketogenesis, preferential preservation of muscle mass during starvation, and adaptive thermogenesis in cold climates [6]. These traits suggest an evolutionary preference towards lipid-based metabolism, especially under carbohydrate-restricted ecological environments. The metabolic state of nutritional/fasting ketosis is replicated by well-formulated ketogenic diets [7, 8]. The human species evolved under ketogenesis-inducing metabolic pressures, relying on animal fats, fasting cycles, and carbohydrate scarcity [3].

The development of the human brain, especially its size and complexity, depended on specific nutrients available largely from animal sources, e.g., DHA, vitamin B12, cholesterol, and essential amino acids [9]. These dietary elements were critical for encephalization and cognitive evolution. The human brain, unlike that of most other primates, is extraordinarily energy-demanding, consuming over 20% of daily calories, despite its relatively small size compared to the rest of the body [10]. This demand was historically not met by continuous carbohydrate intake, but by endogenous ketone production from adipose-derived fatty acids —especially during periods of fasting or low dietary carbohydrate availability [11, 12]. By contrast, the Planetary Diet, rich in grains, sugars, and low in animal-based protein and fat, is fundamentally misaligned with human evolutionary biology [13]. The Planetary Diet suggests a chronically high carbohydrate intake (~ 55–60% of total calories), often from grains and fruits, with an extremely low intake of animal fats [14]. This dietary regimen is inconsistent with the evolutionary feeding habits of Homo sapiens. Indeed, high-carbohydrate diets are associated with chronic diseases including insulin resistance, type II diabetes, obesity and cancer [15–18].

Nutritional deficiencies: evolutionary perspectives

The Planetary Diet promotes a one-size-fits-all model that disregards the immense variability in genetic, microbiotic, environmental, and cultural contexts across human populations [14]. Moreover, it creates nutritional dependence on synthetic supplements, highly processed plant-based substitutes, and industrial supply chains. The Planetary Diet suppresses the evolutionary signals to which our epigenome is adapted —seasonal variation, natural fasting cycles, and animal-derived nutrients—, potentially leading to metabolic, inflammatory, and neuroendocrine dysregulation [19, 20].

In contrast to the suggested planetary model, the concept of an ‘autogenous diet’ proposes a return to individualized, evolutionarily-consistent nutritional patterns. This approach emphasizes species-appropriate macronutrient distribution (moderate protein, high fat, low carbohydrate), inclusion of nutrient-dense animal products (organ meats, fish, eggs, etc.), seasonal and minimally processed foods, natural fasting periods and circadian synchronization [21, 22].

The rapid expansion of the human neocortex —a hallmark of hominid evolution— required high-quality lipids, particularly DHA, cholesterol, and other long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acids [9]. These nutrients are almost exclusively available in animal tissues [4, 23]. Conversely, the ketogenic diet naturally promotes a high intake of these lipids, while the Planetary Diet practically excludes them. The near elimination of animal products from the Planetary Diet results in a high risk of deficiency in several key micronutrients, including but not limited to; vitamin B12, heme iron, zinc, iodine, selenium, taurine, and DHA [13]. These deficiencies are well-documented, even among informed vegetarians and vegans, and represent significant health risks for vulnerable populations such as children, pregnant women, the elderly, and the chronically ill [24].

In contrast, the autogenous diet al.igns more closely with human physiology and evolutionary biology that is particularly relevant for preventing and managing chronic diseases, metabolic syndrome, and cancer [25]. Additionally, the autogenous diet promotes beneficial gut microbiota species, aligning with ancestral diversity patterns and metabolic health, contrary to the dysbiosis associated with ultra-processed plant-based diets [26]. Moreover, accumulating evidence supports the need to tailor diets to individual genetic and epigenetic profiles, as nutrient processing and hormonal responses vary significantly among populations [27].

Ketogenic metabolic therapy and cancer

Emerging evidence, based on Otto Warburg’s original hypothesis, indicates that carcinogenesis involves a two-step transition of cellular energy metabolism [28, 29]. This process involves chronic, sublethal oxidative phosphorylation insufficiency followed by protracted compensatory ATP synthesis via substrate-level phosphorylation (fermentation) [27]. This metabolic shift has been widely documented by extracellular lactate accumulation even in the presence of oxygen, or the Warburg Effect. The dependency of cancer cells on substrate-level phosphorylation, i.e. metabolic inflexibility, can be exploited by dual inhibition of glucose and glutamine fermentation via the glycolysis and glutaminolysis pathways, respectively [30, 31]. Dietary approaches, such as the ketogenic diet and fasting, decrease circulating glucose and insulin levels while simultaneously increasing ketone bodies, which are efficiently utilized by most healthy cells [32]. This shift in the whole-body metabolic environment triggers a multi-pronged anti-tumor effect: Inhibition of tumor growth, reduction in angiogenesis, and enhancement of mitochondrial function in normal tissues [33]. Finally, tumor cells are unable to sustain proliferation on ketone bodies alone due to abnormalities in the number, structure, or function of mitochondria, which have been documented across all major cancers [28].

High carbohydrate diets, particularly when in caloric excess or chronically insulinogenic, can increase the risk for metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes, and certain cancers [34, 35]. Accordingly, the high carbohydrate Planetary Diet is incompatible with nutritional strategies to reduce glycolysis. The Planetary Diet proposes a daily caloric intake composed of over 55% carbohydrates, primarily from whole grains, legumes, fruits, and vegetables [13].

Although added sugars are limited to 31 g per day, the overall glycemic load remains high under the Planetary Diet. In contrast, the diet of paleolithic humans contained only minimal amounts of fruits, despite common assumptions. Moreover, the fruits available at that time were seasonal, fibrous, bitter, or sour, with a far lower sugar content than modern varieties, which have been selectively bred for high fructose content [36, 37]. Modern fruit, while marketed as ‘natural,’ can contain sugar contents comparable to processed desserts. Excessive fructose intake is now recognized as a key driver of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, insulin resistance, mitochondrial oxidative stress, and even tumor progression [38]. Excessive glucose availability enhances tumor glycolysis (the Warburg effect), increases insulin and IGF-1 signaling, while inhibiting autophagy and mitochondrial flexibility [39]. This information challenges the view that the Planetary Diet would be an effective strategy to prevent or combat chronic diseases such as cancer.

A growing body of evidence supporting ketogenic metabolic therapy (KMT) in oncology challenges the narrative of plant-based, carbohydrate-dominant diets as the universal ideal. KMT emphasizes the need to realign public dietary recommendations with evolutionary and mitochondrial metabolism —principles central to both the mitochondrial metabolic theory of cancer and to a broader application of ketogenic interventions in Medicine [40, 41]. Therapeutic strategies that rely on carbohydrate restriction and endogenous ketogenesis have shown promise in preclinical and clinical oncology studies [31, 35, 42, 43]. Similarly, dietary approaches that induce nutritional ketosis have been clinically successful in the management of chronic diseases such as insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, obesity, and neuroprogressive disorders [44, 45].

Scientific critiques and conclusion

A worthwhile initiative is to design dietary recommendations that promote human health while, at the same time, respecting global environmental effects. Dietary recommendations must, however, be biologically coherent, ancestrally aligned, and clinically validated —not ideologically imposed [46]. Indeed, strategies based on external initiatives, e.g., cropland and water use, put health outcomes as a secondary endpoint [14]. The Planetary Diet is often portrayed as a universal solution to the intertwined challenges of health and sustainability. However, the Planetary Diet ignores regional biodiversity, cultural diversity, and genetic variation by advocating for a globally uniform dietary approach, violating both principles of nutritional anthropology and personalized medicine [47, 48]. This critical analysis reveals that such a diet, far from being a neutral or an optimal model, is actually misaligned with fundamental aspects of human biology, evolution, and clinical outcomes thus posing a real risk to human populations.

Human evolution has been shaped by a metabolic environment with intermittent access to food (fasting ketosis) and carbohydrates (nutritional ketosis), with consistent reliance on lipid oxidation and ketogenesis [3]. The planetary model, by contrast, relies heavily on carbohydrates and systematically suppresses metabolic flexibility —contradicting the metabolic heritage of Homo sapiens [49]. Modern accessibility to ultra-processed carbohydrates creates a biological mismatch between fat storage and food scarcity [50]. Clinically, the evidence is mounting against high-carbohydrate, low-fat dietary patterns for the prevention and management of metabolic disorders, neurodegenerative diseases, and cancer [51]. The efficacy of ketogenic and low-carbohydrate interventions in these fields continues to grow in the literature, while the planetary model remains theoretical and untested at scale [49, 52].

Ultimately, the Planetary Diet is presented as a forward-looking and environmentally sound solution to global health, yet may in fact represent a profound biological regression. The Planetary Diet risks overriding key evolutionary, immunological, and cultural variables that are central to human dietary adaptation and resilience. A sustainable future requires not ideological orthodoxy but evidence-based plurality. Diets rooted in evolutionary coherence, clinical outcomes, and ecological resilience —such as autogenous, ancestrally aligned diets— represent a more rational path forward. It is time to rethink not only what we eat, but also why we eat it, how we evolved to do so, and what forms of life our nutrition ought to sustain: human, cultural, and planetary.
