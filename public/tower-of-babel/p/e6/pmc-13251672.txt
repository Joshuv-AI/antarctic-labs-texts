TFPIα inhibition by andexanet alfa is partially restored by protein S and factor V-short
Authors: Christina Crossette-Thambiah, Krister Bamberg, Michael Laffan, Josefin Ahnström
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•Although AndA sequesters TFPIα, rendering it unable to bind and inhibit FXa directly, some TFPIα function remains in plasma.•The TFPIα cofactors, protein S and FV-short, together localize AndA-bound TFPIα to membrane surfaces for successful inhibition of TF-FVIIa.

Introduction

Thrombosis is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide. Consequently, factor Xa (FXa) inhibitors (FXaI), such as apixaban and rivaroxaban, were the 2nd and 27th highest selling drugs worldwide in 2024, respectively.1 They are the predominant class of drugs used for stroke prevention in atrial fibrillation and venous thromboembolism, owing to their predictable kinetics, no requirement of monitoring, and enhanced safety profiles. However, the risk of major bleeding during FXaI treatment is 2% to 4% per year,2 with a case fatality rate for anticoagulant-associated major bleeding at ∼8% to 15%.3 Considering the number of FXaI prescriptions annually, major life-threatening bleeding in patients treated with FXaI is a frequent and serious problem, with UK data suggesting approximately a third of patients with major bleeding receive a reversal agent.2

Andexanet alfa (AndA) is a modified, recombinant, inactive form of human FXa developed to serve as a decoy to specifically bind FXaI.4 It is currently the only selective reversal agent indicated for patients with life threatening or uncontrolled bleeding secondary to rivaroxaban and apixaban. AndA is identical to FXa apart from lacking the FXa Gla domain (responsible for binding negatively charged phospholipid membranes) and an active site serine substitution in the serine protease domain, removing FXa protease function while retaining exosite interaction sites.4 Given at the approved low (400-mg bolus, followed by a 4-mg/mL 2-hour infusion) and high (800-mg bolus, followed by an 8-mg/mL 2-hour infusion) doses,5 AndA efficiently binds and reverses FXaI. Clinical trials, such as ANNEXA-4 and ANNEXA-I, have shown its efficiency in reversing FXaI-associated gastrointestinal and intracranial bleeds.6,7 However, 10% of AndA recipients in ANNEXA-4 and ANNEXA-I had thrombotic events within 30 days of treatment.6 Although not entirely known, the thrombotic events could be due to AndA exposing patients to their underlying prothrombotic condition.

In addition to binding prothrombin, the FXa active site also binds natural FXaI, such as tissue factor pathway inhibitor (TFPI), which has the potential to cause an imbalance of the hemostatic system.8 TFPI is the primary endogenous inhibitor of the initiation of coagulation by directly inhibiting FXa and inhibiting FIXa and FXa generation by tissue factor (TF)–FVIIa in a FXa-dependent manner.9 The importance of TFPI is evident from studies in both mice and humans. The complete loss of TFPI is embryonically lethal in mice, whereas a plasma TFPI level of <10th percentile in humans is associated with thrombosis.10, 11, 12 Humans have 2 isoforms of TFPI. TFPIβ is a membrane-bound form, specifically found on the endothelium,13 whereas TFPIα is soluble and circulates in plasma as well as present in platelets from where it is released following their activation.9 In contrast to TFPIβ, TFPIα requires 2 cofactors, protein S and a splice isoform of FV (FV-short), for full functionality. TFPIα consists of 3 Kunitz domains (K1, K2, and K3) and a negatively charged C-terminal tail. K1 and K2 bind and inhibit TF-FVIIa and FXa9 respectively, whereas K3 is essential for the interaction with protein S14,15 and FV-short binds the C-terminus with high affinity.16,17 Both cofactors interact with TFPIα directly, forming a trimolecular complex in plasma,18 with most plasma TFPIα being bound to FV-short in healthy individuals.19 Importantly, the presence and binding of TFPIα to its cofactors enhance TFPIα anticoagulant functions by ∼10 to 30 fold.17,20

Understanding the impact of AndA on the TFPI pathway is essential for safe utilization of AndA and potential development of future FXaI reversal agents. Although it has been shown that AndA increases thrombin generation (TG) potential in plasma in a TFPI-dependent fashion, the molecular mechanisms delineating how AndA may impair the various functions of TFPI and the magnitude of the effect are not yet known.8 In this study, we determine how AndA influences the function of the TFPI pathway and how this is influenced by the TFPIα cofactors, protein S and FV-short.

Methods

TFPIα and FV-short

TFPIα was transiently expressed in mammalian HEK293T cells and then purified, and quantified as previously described.17 FV-short was stably expressed in baby hamster kidney cells, purified, and quantified according to previously published protocols.17

Phospholipid vesicle preparation

Synthetic phospholipids (Avanti Polar Lipids) 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine, 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoserine, and 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine were mixed in a molar ratio of 60:20:20. Unilamellar phospholipid vesicles were obtained via extrusion as described previously.21,22

TG by CAT

TG was assessed using a Fluoroscan Ascent FL plate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Thrombinoscope software (Synapse BV) as described previously.14,19 Calibrated automated thrombography (CAT) assays were performed in platelet-poor plasma (CCNRP-05; Alpha Laboratories) supplemented with corn trypsin inhibitor (65 μg/mL of plasma) during assay setup and in a plasma pool generated from 15 healthy individuals in-house, collected into corn trypsin inhibitor (18 μg/mL of blood).14 Plasma was supplemented with 4μM phospholipids, and TG was tested at increasing concentrations of rivaroxaban (Sigma-Aldrich) and AndA (0μM-4μM; AstraZeneca) in the presence and absence of saturating inhibitory antibodies against TFPI (78 μg/mL; PAHTFPI-A; Prolytix)19 or protein S (420 μg/mL; Dako).14 TG was initiated by 1pM TF (Dade Innovin) and monitored by the fluorogenic thrombin substrate, Z-Gly-Gly-Arg-AMC-HCl (0.42mM; Bachem).

FXa inhibition assay

FXa (0.5nM; Enzyme Research Laboratories [ERL]) activity was monitored in real time using S-2765 (Chromogenix; 200μM) at 405 nm in the presence of 20μM phospholipids and 5mM CaCl2 as described previously.14,17 Assays were performed at increasing concentrations of AndA (0μM-4μM) in the presence and absence of rivaroxaban (10nM), TFPIα (0.5nM or 3nM), protein S (5nM; ERL), and FV-short (2nM). All reactions were initiated by the addition of FXa. Relative Absorbance at 405 nm at 30 minutes was calculated as the percentage of the absorbance achieved by FXa alone (in the absence of inhibitor or AndA).

Inhibition of TF-FVIIa peptidolytic activity

FXa-TFPIα and AndA-TFPIα complexes were preformed by incubating the proteins at a 1:1 and 4:1 molar ratio, respectively, for 1 hour at room temperature. At these ratios, minimal/no free TFPIα or FXa remained according to functional characterization (data not shown). Subsequently, FXa-TFPIα or AndA-TFPIα (0nM-1nM; based on TFPIα concentration) were mixed with 5nM TF (Dade Innovin), 300pM FVIIa (ERL), and 5mM CaCl2 in the presence and absence of protein S (10nM) and/or FV-short (3nM) in 20mM Tris pH 7.4, 0.15M NaCl (Tris-buffered saline [TBS]) supplemented with 0.5% bovine serum albumin (BSA). Given that TF was lipidated, no further phospholipids were required. Following 10 minutes of incubation, the reaction was initiated by the addition of 0.5mM Pefachrome FVIIa. The inhibition of TF-FVIIa peptidolytic activity was followed in real time at 405 nm. TF-FVIIa activity was determined by the linear regression of the final slope in the assay (generally 60-90 minutes) and normalized to the activity observed in the absence of FXa/AndA-TFPIα (100%). The 50% inhibitory concentration (IC50) was determined by a 1-phase exponential decay nonlinear curve fit.

FIXa generation assays

FXa-TFPIα and AndA-TFPIα complexes were preformed as described earlier. TF (3nM), 100pM FVIIa, 1mM Pefachrome FIXa, 20μM phospholipids, and 5mM CaCl2 were incubated with increasing concentrations of FXa-TFPIα (0nM-0.25nM) or AndA-TFPIα (0nM-1nM) in the presence and absence of protein S (10nM) and FV-short (3nM). All dilutions were made in 0.5% BSA in TBS. Following 10 minutes of incubation, 200nM FIX (ERL) was added to start the reaction. FIX activation was followed in real time at 405 nm. FIXa generation at each concentration of FXa/AndA-TFPIα was determined by the linear regression of the progress curves (∼40-70 minutes) and normalized to FIXa activity achieved in the absence of FXa/AndA-TFPIα (100%). The IC50 was determined by a 1-phase exponential decay nonlinear curve fit.

FXa generation assays

Increasing concentrations of AndA (0μM-4μM) were incubated with 5nM TF, 1pM FVIIa, 0.5nM or 2.5nM TFPIα, S-2765 (200μM), 20μM phospholipids, and 5mM CaCl2 in the presence and absence of protein S (10nM), FV-short (3nM), and rivaroxaban (10nM) at 37°C for 10 minutes. All dilutions were made in 0.5% BSA in TBS. FXa generation was initiated by the addition of FX (100nM; ERL) and followed in real time at 405 nm. Readings were corrected for chromogenic substrate consumption and the first derivative of the corrected data gave FXa activity.23 FXa activity was normalized to that achieved in the absence of TFPIα, rivaroxaban, and AndA (100%).

The study was approved by the Imperial College Research Ethics Committee and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Impact of AndA on TFPI function in plasma

TG using CAT was performed to assess the impact of AndA (0μM-2μM) on the TFPI pathway in platelet-poor plasma triggered by low (1pM) TF concentrations.14,19,24 Importantly, 2μM and 4μM AndA correspond to the maximal concentrations observed in humans after the administration of the low- or high-dose regimens, respectively.5 The addition of 0.25μM or 2μM AndA to plasma resulted in an ∼22-fold increase in peak thrombin and an ∼2.5-fold reduction in lag time (Figures 1A and 2A,C). Rivaroxaban (1μM) completely abolished TG under these conditions, whereas therapeutic levels of AndA (2μM) restored peak thrombin and lag times similar to those achieved in the presence of AndA alone (Figures 1B and 2). This indicates that although AndA reverses rivaroxaban anticoagulation, the resulting TG is greater than in plasma alone. The addition of inhibitory antibodies against TFPI resulted in an ∼30-fold increase in peak thrombin and an ∼3-fold shortening of the lag time (Figures 1C and 2A,C) in the absence of AndA.19 Importantly, when TFPI was inhibited, the addition of AndA did not have any impact on TG, whereas reversing rivaroxaban anticoagulation led to the same TG as achieved in the absence of FXaI and AndA (Figures 1C-D and 2).

Although the addition of AndA alone increased TG, peak thrombin was elevated further by adding anti-TFPI antibodies (∼135nM vs ∼195nM; Figure 2A), suggesting AndA only partially inhibits TFPI function. The shorter lag times and higher peak thrombin at 0.25μM and 2μM AndA in the presence of anti-TFPI antibodies, suggest ∼25% (lag time) to ∼30% (peak thrombin) TFPI function remains in the presence of AndA (Figure 2A,C). Similar results were obtained when the experiment was repeated with the same plasma batch (reference plasma batch number 1), a second commercial batch (reference plasma batch number 2), and an in-house plasma pool (in-house plasma), indicating the findings were not batch-specific (supplemental Figure 1). The same experiment was then conducted at lower TF concentrations (0.25pM and 0.5pM). Although little/no TG was observed in the absence of AndA/anti-TFPI, the retained TFPI function was ∼45% to 50% and ∼30% on peak thrombin in reference plasma number 1 and number 2, respectively (data not shown).

Repeating these assays at 0μM, 0.5μM, and 1μM rivaroxaban and AndA concentrations of 0μM to 4μM showed efficient rivaroxaban reversal and partially retained TFPI function under all conditions (supplemental Figure 2A-H). This finding was most apparent on peak thrombin when comparing the TG parameters across all AndA concentrations in the absence of rivaroxaban (Figure 3). To investigate whether the retained TFPI function was related to protein S and FV-short, the assays were repeated in the presence of inhibitory anti–protein S antibodies, blocking the synergistic enhancement by both cofactors.20 As previously shown, the addition of anti–protein S antibodies results in an increase in TG, observed by an ∼16-fold increase in peak thrombin and an ∼2.5-fold reduction in lag time (Figures 1E-F and 2A,C) by partial reduction in TFPI function.14,25 Moreover, under these conditions, AndA efficiently reversed rivaroxaban (Figure 2B,D). Importantly, blocking TFPIα enhancement by protein S and FV-short resulted in AndA bringing the TG to the same level as that observed in the presence of anti-TFPI antibodies (Figure 2), suggesting that protein S and FV-short protect a proportion of TFPI anticoagulant function in the presence of AndA. This is most apparent in the absence of rivaroxaban (Figure 3), during which all CAT variables achieved in the presence of anti–protein S overlapped with those obtained in the presence of anti-TFPI antibodies.

Impact of AndA on direct inhibition of FXa activity by TFPIα

FXa inhibition assays were used to assess the impact of AndA on TFPIα-mediated inhibition of FXa activity in the presence and absence of rivaroxaban. As expected, AndA efficiently reversed rivaroxaban (Figure 4A-B,G). At 3nM TFPIα, AndA reduced its anti-FXa function in a concentration-dependent manner, with 84% FXa activity observed at 0.13μM AndA and 100% at 0.5μM to 1μM AndA (Figure 4C,G). When TFPIα and rivaroxaban were added together, AndA abolished both of their anticoagulant properties (Figure 4D,G). The same experiments were repeated with TFPIα (0.5nM) together with protein S (5nM) and FV-short (2nM), resulting in almost the complete inhibition of FXa activity (Figure 4E-F). Furthermore, in the presence of its cofactors, TFPIα-mediated inhibition of FXa activity was blocked by AndA in a concentration-dependent manner, with 92% FXa activity observed at 2μM AndA (Figure 4E,H). As observed in the absence of protein S and FV-short, AndA also inhibited TFPIα anti-FXa function when added together with rivaroxaban (Figure 4F,H). These results demonstrate that at therapeutic concentrations of AndA (2μM-4μM), AndA removes the anti-FXa functions of both rivaroxaban and TFPIα (Figure 4F).

In the assays presented earlier, TFPIα, rivaroxaban, AndA, and FXa were all added simultaneously. The same assays were repeated after preincubating TFPIα, rivaroxaban, and AndA for 15 minutes before the addition of FXa (supplemental Figure 3). The results were similar to those obtained without preincubation, but with lower levels (0.06μM) of AndA required for inhibiting anti-FXa functions by TFPIα in the presence of protein S and FV-short. These results demonstrate the full inhibition of both rivaroxaban and TFPIα-mediated anti-FXa functions at therapeutic concentrations of AndA, both in the presence and absence of protein S and FV-short.

Inhibition of TF-FVIIa peptidolytic activity by AndA-bound TFPIα

We next tested whether AndA-bound TFPIα could inhibit TF-FVIIa peptidolytic activity. For these experiments, we first created tight FXa-TFPIα and AndA-TFPIα complexes. This was essential because FXa cleaves the FVIIa substrate. FXa-TFPIα were incubated at a 1:1 molar ratio, whereas an excess of AndA:TFPIα (4:1) was used to generate AndA-TFPIα complexes to ensure the full saturation of TFPIα. As previously demonstrated,26 FXa-TFPIα inhibited TF-FVIIa in a concentration-dependent manner, with an IC50 of ∼0.20nM and over 90% inhibition achieved at 0.5nM FXa-TFPIα (Figure 5A,E). In contrast, AndA-TFPIα was unable to inhibit TF-FVIIa peptidolytic activity, with little/no inhibition achieved even at 1nM AndA-TFPIα (Figure 5B,F). The experiments were next repeated in the presence of protein S (10nM) and FV-short (3nM). Here, the cofactors did not significantly affect TF-FVIIa inhibition by FXa-TFPIα (Figure 5C,E). In contrast, the presence of protein S and FV-short enabled AndA-TFPIα to successfully inhibit TF-FVIIa peptidolytic activity, with an IC50 of ∼0.22nM, similar to that of FXa-TFPIα (Figure 5D,F). The same experiments repeated at 1nM AndA-TFPIα in the presence and absence of protein S and FV-short, alone and together, showed that both protein S and FV-short must be present for AndA-bound TFPIα to inhibit TF-FVIIa efficiently (Figure 5G-H).

Inhibition of TF-FVIIa–mediated FIXa generation by AndA-bound TFPIα

To establish the inhibition of TF-FVIIa proteolytic activity, FIX activation was followed in real time using a chromogenic FIXa substrate. Protein S has been shown to multimerize upon purification, resulting in high-affinity binding to negatively charged phospholipid surfaces.27 As such, protein S may reduce FIXa activation independent on TFPIα, due to competition for membrane surfaces. To avoid this problem, these experiments were performed in the presence of additional phospholipids (20μM). Given that this substrate is also cleaved by FXa, the assays were again performed in the presence of preformed FXa-TFPIα and AndA-TFPIα complexes. The increasing concentrations of FXa-TFPIα (0nM-0.25nM) demonstrated a concentration-dependent inhibition of FIXa generation (Figure 6A), which was not significantly altered when protein S and FV-short were present (IC50 of ∼0.03-0.05nM; Figure 6B,E). In keeping with the results from the peptidolytic activity assays, AndA-TFPIα achieved negligible inhibition at all concentrations tested (0nM-1nM) in the absence of protein S and FV-short, whereas it inhibited FIXa generation in a concentration-dependent manner in their presence, with an IC50 of ∼0.14nM (Figure 6C-D,F). In the presence of protein S and FV-short, 1nM AndA-TFPIα achieved 86% inhibition of FIXa generation, similar to the inhibition achieved by 0.25nM FXa-TFPIα (Figure 6E-F).

Inhibition of TF-FVIIa–mediated FXa generation by AndA-bound TFPIα

Subsequently, we performed FXa generation assays, in which FX activation by TF-FVIIa was assessed in real time using a FXa substrate. Unlike FIXa generation assays, FXa is first generated by TF-FVIIa, followed by its interaction with TFPIα, which then forms a quaternary complex together with TF-FVIIa. In the absence of AndA, these assays reflect TFPIα-mediated inhibition of FXa generation as well as FXa activity (Figure 7A). The first derivative of the data provided the FXa activity (Figure 7B). Here, FXa generation was gradually inhibited due to the slow formation of the TF-FVIIa–FXa-TFPIα quaternary complex.23 The complete inhibition of FXa generation was observed by 2.5nM TFPIα after ∼10 minutes, followed by a reduction in FXa activity due to it complexing with TFPIα (Figure 7B). The addition of AndA resulted in a concentration-dependent reduction in TFPIα anticoagulant functions, with no TFPIα-mediated inhibition observed at ∼0.5μM AndA due to AndA sequestration of TFPIα rendering it unable to inhibit both TF-FVIIa and FXa activity.

In the presence of protein S (10nM) and FV-short (3nM), a more efficient inhibition of FXa generation/activity was observed, with TFPIα (0.5nM) completely inhibiting any FXa generation/activity (Figure 7C-D). As in the absence of protein S and FV-short, AndA reduced TFPIα anticoagulant properties in a concentration-dependent manner due to AndA sequestration of TFPIα. However, the effects of the sequestration of TFPIα were saturated at ∼0.5μM AndA, with partial TFPIα functionality remaining at therapeutic concentrations of AndA. The first derivative showed a complete lack of FXa activity in the absence of AndA, with some FXa generation upon its addition (Figure 7D). At 4μM AndA, the amount of FXa generated was only ∼50% of that observed in the absence of TFPIα and AndA (Figure 7D). The complete inhibition of TF-FVIIa through the formation of the quaternary complex was observed ∼40 to 45 minutes even at 0.5μM to 4μM AndA (data not shown). However, in contrast to the reactions performed in the absence or at the lowest concentrations of AndA, no reduction in FXa activity occurred over time, suggesting AndA-TFPIα being unable to bind and inhibit FXa. Similar results were observed also in the presence of rivaroxaban (supplemental Figure 4), suggesting that although AndA binds both TFPIα and rivaroxaban, any competition for AndA is likely minimal at therapeutic concentrations of AndA.

Discussion

Fatality rates for major bleeding in individuals taking FXaI are as high as 8% to 15%.3 Consequently, anticoagulant reversal strategies for FXaI remain a subject of great importance. AndA effectively reduced gastrointestinal and intracranial bleeds in both the ANNEXA-4 and ANNEXA-I studies.6,7 The effective reversal of rivaroxaban by AndA was also evident from the results from the plasma-based TG (Figures 1 and 2), FXa inhibition (Figure 4), and FXa generation assays (supplemental Figure 4) performed in this study. However, the reversal of anticoagulation exposes patients to the thrombotic risk of their underlying disease. Trial data demonstrated 10% of patients in the AndA arms experienced thrombotic events within 30 days of treatment.6 Although the cause of the thrombotic events after AndA administration remain unknown, several reports have attributed these to AndA binding and sequestering TFPI.8,28,29 In this study, we aimed to determine the mechanism and impact of AndA on TFPIα function, with a specific focus on the contribution of the TFPIα cofactors, protein S and FV-short.

TG was initially used to demonstrate the net effect of AndA on TFPIα functions. Here, AndA was titrated up to 2μM and 4μM, representing the maximum plasma concentration achieved by the “low-” and “high-”dose AndA therapeutic regiments, respectively.5 Similar to a recent report by Hoornstra et al, these assays identified an increase in TG after the addition of AndA to plasma8,30 that was TFPI-dependent (Figure 1., Figure 2., Figure 3.). However, in contrast to this previous report,8 our results showed that the addition of AndA did not result in the complete inhibition of the TFPI pathway even at saturating concentrations of AndA (Figure 3). Considering that this plasma resulted in unusually low TG,31 the experiment was repeated in another 2 plasma batches that confirmed our result and showed that it was not batch or isolation protocol–specific (supplemental Figure 1). The discrepancy between our result and that previously published may be due to our use of inhibitory antibodies directed against both the TFPI K1 and K2 domains, whereas Hoornstra et al used monoclonal antibodies against the K2 domain (ie, blocking the TFPI-FXa interaction only).8 According to recent data from our laboratory, TFPIα–protein S–FV-short trimolecular complexes represent the majority (∼75%) of plasma TFPIα,19 with only a small proportion of plasma TFPIα not bound to FV-short.32 In agreement with this observation, protein S–depleted plasma is essentially devoid of TFPIα, suggesting that most plasma TFPIα is also bound to protein S.33 Interestingly, blocking TFPIα enhancement by protein S and FV-short in the presence of AndA resulted in the complete loss of TFPI anticoagulant function, showing how the cofactors allow some TFPIα functionality in the presence of AndA (Figure 3).

Considering that all anticoagulant functions of TFPIα are dependent on its interaction with FXa, we probed for an AndA-TFPIα interaction using FXa inhibition assays and showed that AndA does indeed interact with TFPIα, resulting in the abolition of TFPIα-mediated inhibition of FXa at therapeutic concentrations of AndA (Figure 4). This result was expected as a similar truncated FXa molecule has been shown to interact with TFPIα previously.34 Protein S and FV-short did not majorly affect the result because AndA binds to TFPIα K2, which is separate from the protein S (K3)14,15,17 and FV-short (C-terminus)16,17 interaction sites. These experiments were also performed with an incubation step in which AndA was allowed to form a complex with TFPIα before the addition of FXa. After AndA-TFPIα complex formation, a lower AndA concentration was required for TFPIα functional sequestration (supplemental Figure 3), suggesting that, similar to the TFPIα-FXa interaction,35 the TFPIα-AndA interaction may involve isomerization to a stable complex, preventing TFPIα dissociation from AndA. However, this still needs to be formally tested in dedicated binding assays, sensitive to the 2 stages of interaction.

Another important function of TFPIα is to inhibit the TF-FVIIa activation of FIX and FX.36 Although TFPIα can inhibit TF-FVIIa in the absence of FXa, such inhibition is inefficient and considered unphysiological.23,26 This implies that AndA-sequestering of TFPIα would result in abolishing its inhibition of TF-FVIIa. Indeed, although the active site serine was not required for AndA interaction with TFPIα (Figure 4), the deletion of the FXa Gla domain in AndA was detrimental for allowing TFPIα localization to the membrane surface and TF-FVIIa (Figures 5B and 6C). However, given that most plasma TFPIα is bound to protein S and FV-short, which in contrast to TFPIα,37,38 bind negatively charged membranes with high affinity,22 protein S and FV-short enabled AndA-TFPIα to inhibit TF-FVIIa (Figures 5D and 6D). Importantly, although both protein S and FV-short bind membrane surfaces with high affinity, both were required for allowing AndA-TFPIα to inhibit TF-FVIIa, suggesting there may also be other protein-protein interactions, or allosteric effects, involved in TF-FVIIa inhibition (Figure 5H).

In contrast to TF-FVIIa peptidolytic activity and activation of FIX, interpreting TFPIα-mediated inhibition of FXa generation is more complex. In these assays, FXa generation and its inhibition can be followed in real time through the slow formation of the TF-FVIIa–FXa–TFPIα quaternary complex. The results mirrored those of the FXa inhibition and FIXa generation assays. In the presence of protein S and FV-short, some inhibition of TF-FVIIa–mediated FX activation remained. At saturating concentrations of AndA (0.5μM-4μM), the FXa activity was ∼50% of that achieved in the absence of both AndA and TFPIα. Over time, the full inhibition of TF-FVIIa occurred, suggesting that AndA-bound TFPIα can form a quaternary complex with TF-FVIIa in the presence of protein S and FV-short. In agreement with the FXa inhibition assay, although fully inhibiting TF-FVIIa, no reduction in FXa activity was observed in these reactions, suggesting complete sequestration of TFPIα by AndA and therefore, the lack of TFPIα-mediated inhibition of the already generated FXa (Figure 7). Importantly, these results explain the remaining TFPI function observed in the TG assays at saturating levels of AndA (Figure 3) and successfully delineate the molecular mechanisms behind the results obtained in plasma. They also highlight the importance of the TF-FVIIa interaction with TFPI when assessing the full impact of AndA on the TFPI pathway because this may explain the difference in results from this study and those recently published.8

This work demonstrates that TFPIα circulating in a complex with its 2 cofactors, protein S and FV-short, the main functional form of TFPIα in plasma, is sequestered by AndA. Although rendering TFPIα unable to inhibit FXa, this interaction does not abolish TFPIα function because the ability to inhibit TF-FVIIa still remains. Platelet TFPIα, similar to plasma TFPIα, is functionally enhanced by protein S.13 Whether and how it may be enhanced by FV-short or partially activated species of FV is currently not known. It will be important to establish how platelet TFPIα function is affected by AndA sequestration in future studies. The second isoform of TFPI in humans, the endothelial TFPIβ, is likely to be sequestered by AndA because this isoform retains K2 and the same FXa interaction site. Indeed, the previous study by Hoornstra et al, suggested that AndA may block TFPI anticoagulant functions when assessed over an endothelial cell surface.8 However, further biochemical studies are required to study this in further detail. Whether AndA-TFPIβ can inhibit TF-FVIIa still remains to be determined.

Furthermore, the half-life of AndA is established as 5 to 6 hours.39 It is currently not known what the half-life of TFPIα–protein S–FV-short is,9 but it is possible that this could be extended upon interaction with AndA, which could result in an increase in TFPIα anticoagulant properties. This has not yet been established but is important to address in the future.

The differential effect of AndA on these 2 aspects of TFPIα function makes it difficult to predict the magnitude of the procoagulant effect in patients treated with AndA for FXaI-associated bleeding. Further tests of clinical samples, such as samples collected from patients treated with AndA, would potentially allow assessment of the functional impact on the TFPI pathway and its correlation with thrombotic and bleeding outcomes. However, these in vitro data show that although effectively reversing therapeutic FXa inhibition, AndA can simultaneously inhibit TFPIα resulting in increased TG for any given stimulus. Considering the patients’ combined risks of bleeding and thrombosis, any potential beneficial prohemostatic effect of AndA should be weighed against the individual’s prothrombotic risk before AndA administration.
