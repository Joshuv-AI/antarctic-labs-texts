Expression and regulation of lncRNAs in the testes of Yili geese with high and low sperm motility
Authors: Wu Yingping, Zhao Xiaoyu, Li Haiying, Ding Yawen, Yao Yingying, Zhao Quanzhuang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLong non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) play an important role in the regulation of sperm motility and are involved in maintaining sperm functions by influencing processes such as spermatogenesis, metabolism, motility, and the response to oxidative stress. In this study, transcriptome sequencing technology was used to perform lncRNA sequencing on the testes of Yili geese with high and low sperm motility. Combined with bioinformatics analysis, RT-qPCR and other detection techniques, this study deeply explored and identified the genes and lncRNA functions that affect the sperm motility of Yili geese.ResultsIn this study, 5 male Yili geese with high sperm motility and 5 with low sperm motility were selected. High-throughput sequencing was then performed on the cDNA libraries constructed from their testicular tissues. A total of 41,308 novel lncRNAs were identified in Yili goose testicular tissues. Comparative analysis between the high sperm motility group and low sperm motility group revealed 2,760 differentially expressed lncRNAs, including 1,565 upregulated and 1,195 downregulated lncRNAs. The general characteristics of lncRNAs are as follows: The average length of lncRNAs was shorter than that of mRNAs, the lncRNAs had fewer exons on average compared to mRNAs., and the average open reading frame (ORF) length of lncRNAs was shorter than that of mRNAs. Prediction of target genes for lncRNAs with high and low sperm motility differences between testes, including 260 genes that are position related and 445 genes that are gene expression related. Enrichment analysis of predicted target genes revealed that they were mainly concentrated in biological processes such as ion channel activity, metabolic processes, and immune processes, with a total of 310 genes enriched in pathways related to spermatogenesis function and energy metabolism. Through constructing a differential lncRNA-mRNA regulatory network, it is suggested that seven core regulatory factors, including LINC23234-CCDC122, LINC19247-Grxcr1, and LINC1214-Cep83, may play important roles in testicular development and sperm motility traits.ConclusionsThis study provides a preliminary dataset and identifies candidate lncRNAs and their potential target genes associated with sperm motility in Yili geese. These findings offer a basis for future functional studies aimed at elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying male reproductive performance in geese.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12731-w.Keywords: Yili Goose, Sperm motility, Testicular development, Long non-coding RNAs

Background

Yili goose is the only goose species in China that originated from grey geese(Anser anser), and it is also one of the goose species that can fly short distances in China. The low reproductive capacity and low fertilization rate of breeding eggs of Yili goose male geese have limited the development of Yili goose industry.Research has shown that male poultry with high sperm motility often exhibit higher fertilization rates and reproductive capacity [1].The formation and maturation of spermatozoa in the testis is called spermatogenesis, which is a series of complex molecular processes and morphological changes, including mitosis and meiosis, as well as the differentiation from germ cells to mature spermatozoa [2]. Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) are considered to be important factors regulating countless biological processes. They mainly regulate the biological processes in the body by modulating gene expression and interacting with proteins. For example, research reports that 55 genes with differential expressions between the testis, epididymis, and vas deferens are involved in 12 processes related to sperm motility. Almost all genes in each of these processes exhibit a high degree of correlation with the expressions of differentially expressed lncRNAs [3].Soumillon et al. [4]reported that the transcription of lncRNAs is widespread in the testes of mice. The maturation of germ cells during spermatogenesis leads to promiscuous transcription of the genome, thus generating important functions. Current research on lncRNAs in relation to the reproductive performance of geese primarily focuses on female individuals. For instance, Li et al. [5] conducted sequencing of long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) in the hypothalamus, pituitary, and ovary tissues of Wanxi White geese during the egg-laying and brooding periods. They identified 184, 180, and 880 lncRNAs in the hypothalamus, pituitary, and ovary, respectively, and further validated the targeting relationship within the MSTRG.1166.1/miR-450-x/SOX8 axis using dual-luciferase reporter assays. Zhao et al. [6]performed whole-transcriptome sequencing on ovarian tissues from Yili geese during four stages: pre-laying period (PP), laying period (LP), and cessation period (CP). They identified reproduction- and ovarian development-related lncRNAs, including MSTRG.129094.34, MSTRG.5970.28, and MSTRG.3524.1, and demonstrated that the long non-coding RNA MSTRG.5970.28 regulates the proliferation and apoptosis of goose follicular granulosa cells via the miR-133a-3p/ANOS1 pathway. In recent years, studies have gradually extended to the regulatory mechanisms of lncRNAs on reproductive performance in male geese. Hao et al. [7]constructed transcriptomic expression profiles of testicular tissues from Zi geese with high and low sperm motility, revealing that the LOC106049708-oha-miR-182-5p-MSTRG.2479.6 and PRKAG3-ocu-miR-10b-5p-MSTRG.9047.14 regulatory pathways may serve as key modulators of sperm motility in geese. However, the key characteristics of lncRNAs related to the testicular development and sperm motility traits in geese are still unclear at present. Therefore, based on the analysis of the differences in reproductive performance and testicular development between Yili geese with high and low sperm motility, this study further utilized RNA sequencing technology to explore the expression of lncRNAs associated with sperm motility traits in Yili geese. The aim is to understand the relationship between sperm motility and gene transcriptional regulation, and to lay a foundation for studying the genetic mechanism affecting the sperm motility of male Yili geese at the epigenetic level.

Materials and methods

Experimental animals and sample collection

The experimental animals used in this study were provided by Xinjiang E’min County Hengxin Industrial Co., Ltd. Two weeks before the official experiment commenced, the ganders were given semen collection training. The method of semen collection was the dorsoventral massage method, and the interval between each semen collection was 3 days. A total of 100 healthy ganders at the age of 3 years, whose body weights were close to the average body weight, were selected for the determination of semen quality six times. Sperm motility was assessed as follows: 2 µL of semen was mixed with 40–60 µL of pre‑warmed physiological saline (37 °C) using a micropipette. A drop of the mixture was placed on a pre‑heated glass slide, covered with a 20 mm × 20 mm coverslip, and observed under an optical microscope at 200× magnification. The movement of spermatozoa was examined and counted in three different fields of view. The percentage of progressively motile spermatozoa was calculated for each field, and the average value was recorded as the final motility rate.

The method for determining semen quality referred to the previous research [8]. The selected ganders were subjected to a small-group feeding experiment at a male-to-female ratio of 1:4, and their fertilization rates were counted. Fertility rate is defined as the percentage of fertilized eggs relative to the total number of eggs set for incubation.

To effectively control individual biological variation and experimental error in the transcriptomic analysis of phenotypic differences, and to markedly improve the reliability of the results (3–5 biological replicates are generally sufficient for this purpose), the present study initially classified Yili geese into a high sperm motility group and a low sperm motility group based on six rounds of semen quality assessments. Subsequent sample screening was performed in combination with fertilization rate indices, and five individuals were ultimately selected for each experimental group. The results revealed that the mean sperm motility of the high and low sperm motility groups was 65.63% and 35.84%, respectively (Table 2), and sperm motility was positively correlated with fertilization rate, which is consistent with the findings of our previous research. After slaughtering, the left testicular tissue was taken and placed in liquid nitrogen for preservation pending testing, while the testicular tissue from the other side was placed in a 4% paraformaldehyde solution for fixation. And after removing connective tissue, it was incised along the equatorial plane and divided into three equal tissue blocks (1 cm × 0.5 cm × 0.3 cm). Following fixation, dehydration, and embedding, 5 μm‑thick paraffin sections were prepared. From each sample, 6–8 sections were selected at intervals of 10–15 μm, resulting in a total of 12–16 sections per animal. After deparaffinization, hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining, and mounting, sections were observed under a Nikon Eclipse Ci‑L microscope. Images were captured randomly from five fields of view at 40× magnification using Nikon E.Z‑MET software. Evaluation was performed in a double‑blind manner: sections were randomly coded, and personnel involved in sampling, sectioning, and processing were unaware of the group assignment; decoding was carried out independently after the experiment.

RNA extraction and library construction

The total RNA of the testicular tissue was extracted by the Trizol method (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The concentration and purity(OD260/280) of the total RNA were detected using a Nanodrop (IMPLEN, CA, USA), and the integrity of the RNA was examined by an Agilent 2100 (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). RNA samples were deemed acceptable if the concentration ranged from 30 to 1000 ng/µL and the OD260/280 ratio was between 1.8 and 2.2. Samples with an RNA integrity number (RIN) value ≥ 8.5 were used for subsequent analysis. After the samples passed the quality control, a cDNA library was constructed according to the instructions of the Illumina kit (Illuminia, San Diego, CA, USA). The library preparations were sequenced on an Illumina Hiseq platform and 125 bp/150 bp paired-end reads were generated. The sequencing work was provided by Beijing Novogene Technology Co., Ltd.

Transcript assembly and transcript filtering

The raw image files generated from high-throughput sequencing were base-called by CASAVA and converted into sequenced reads, which were then stored in FASTQ format. Sequencing reads were quality-checked with FastQC, and those containing adapters, numerous low-quality bases, or an N ratio > 0.002 were removed [9]. The HISAT2.0.4 software [10]was used to map the clean reads to the goose reference genome (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bioproject/589208) [11]. The reference genome assembly version used was GCA_013030995.1, which is deposited in the Whole Genome Shotgun (WGS) database under accession number WTSS00000000. The Stringtie software was employed to assemble the reads into transcripts and perform quantification [12]. Subsequently, the Cuffmerge software [13] was used to merge the assembled transcripts. Transcripts with determined strand directions were screened according to the length characteristics of lncRNA transcripts (> 200 nt). Then, the Cuffcompare software was used to compare with the known databases. Finally, the coding potential of the screened transcripts and the newly obtained ones was predicted, and ultimately lncRNAs and Novel lncRNAs were obtained [14].

Differential expression analysis of LncRNAs

The HTSeq software [15] (Anders et al., 2015) was used to analyze the gene expression levels of each sample, and the edgeR software [16] (Robinson et al., 2010) was employed to conduct the analysis of significant differences in expression. The p-values were adjusted for multiple testing, and the corrected p-value (padj) was used to determine statistical significance. Genes with P < 0.05 and |log2foldchange| ≥ 2 were regarded as differentially expressed lncRNAs (DELs).

Target gene prediction and functional annotation of LncRNA

The target genes of lncRNAs were predicted through the positional relationship (co-location) and expression correlation (co-expression) between lncRNAs and protein-coding genes. When the number of samples exceeded 5, both co-location and co-expression analyses were carried out simultaneously; if the number of samples was less than 5, only the co-location analysis was performed. Regarding the co-location regulatory mechanism, the threshold of co-location was set to 100 kb upstream and downstream of the lncRNA, and a correlation coefficient of |cor| ≥ 0.4 with a p value < 0.001 was applied for co-expression analysis. In this study, the GOseq (Release2.12) software was used to conduct GO enrichment analysis on the target genes of differentially expressed lncRNAs, and KOBAS (v2.0) was used for Pathway enrichment analysis [17, 18]. In this study, the background gene set was rigorously defined as all genes detected in the current lncRNA sequencing data. The p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) method, and the corrected p-value (padj) was used to determine enrichment significance. Terms or pathways with a padj < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched in both GO and KEGG analyses.

Construction of LncRNAs mRNAs interaction network

For the analysis of the lncRNAs-mRNAs interaction network, based on the regulatory relationships between the differentially expressed lncRNAs and their targets, the Cytoscape (v3.7.1) [19] software was used to construct and visualize the lncRNAs-mRNAs interaction network.

Validation by quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR)

In order to verify the accuracy and reliability of the lncRNA sequencing results, 9 differentially expressed lncRNAs were randomly selected from the sequencing results. Primer design was carried out using the Oligo 7.0 software (Table 1). The relative expression levels of the target genes and the reference gene (GAPDH) were detected using SYBR GEEN reagents (TaKaRa) in a ROCHE 480 quantitative PCR instrument. The PCR reaction system (20 µL) included the 2 µL total RNA as the template, 1 µL upstream primer (10 µmol/L), 1 µL downstream primer (10 µmol /L), 10 µL 2× master mix, and 6 µL ddH2O. The reaction condition was as follows: 95 °C for 15 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s, 58–60 °C for 20 s, 72 °C for 20 s. The reverse transcription reaction system (20 µL) consisted of the following components: X µL RNA template (approximately 1 µg), 1 µL Random Hexamer Primer (20 µM), RNase-free H₂O added to a total volume of 12.5 µL. The mixture was gently mixed, denatured at 65 °C for 5 min, and then immediately placed on ice for 2 min. Subsequently, 4 µL of 5× RT Reaction Buffer, 2 µL of dNTP Mix (10 mM), 0.5 µL of RNasin Ribonuclease Inhibitor, and 1 µL of RevertAid Reverse Transcriptase were added. The 2−△△Ct method [20] was used to calculate the relative expression levels of the genes and lncRNAs, and their log2 (fold change) values were calculated. Then, these values were compared with the expression trends of the transcriptome results.

Results

Sperm motility and testicular development of Yili geese

According to 6 semen quality tests, the sperm concentration, sperm motility, and sperm viabilityin the HFR group were significantly higher than those in the LFR group (P < 0.01), while the sperm abnormality rate was significantly lower than that in the LFR group (P < 0.05). There was no significant difference between the two groups in terms of semen volume and semen pH(Table 2).

Histological analysis was conducted on the testicular tissues of the high and low sperm motility Yili geese. In the male geese of the high sperm motility group, the seminiferous tubules were well-developed, and the spermatogenic cells at all levels were arranged neatly and closely, with a large number of spermatozoa visible. In the male geese of the low sperm motility group, the lumen of the seminiferous tubules was relatively smaller compared with that of the male geese with high fertility, and spermatozoa were rarely seen (Fig. 1).

Sequencing quality assessment

After high-throughput sequencing analysis, an average of 99,080, 258.8, and 95,080, 524 clean reads were obtained from Yili goose samples with high and low sperm motility, respectively(Table S1). The data quality assessment shows that Q30 ranges from 93.06% to 93.48%, and the GC content distribution is 45.40% to 47.40%, indicating that the sequencing data quality is reliable and meets the standard requirements for subsequent bioinformatics analysis. The reference genome alignment results showed that the average overall alignment rate of the 10 samples was 91.75%, of which 87.92% of the reads were accurately located in the genome annotation region through unique mapping.

Identification and characteristics of LncRNA

Three software programs, CPC, CNCI, and Pfam, were used for the analysis of coding potential. The intersection of the transcripts without coding potential from the results of these software programs was used as the candidate lncRNA dataset for this analysis. A total of 41,308 lncRNA transcripts were obtained from the high and low sperm motility groups (Fig. 2A). According to the classification of lncRNAs based on their genomic locations, lncRNAs were mainly derived from long intergenic non - coding RNAs (lincRNAs) (76.1%), followed by antisense lncRNAs (16.7%), sense overlapping lncRNAs (7.2%), and sense intronic lncRNAs (0.0%) (Fig. 2B).

In this study, we further compared the transcript length, the number of exons, and the open reading frame (ORF) length of lncRNAs. The results showed that the average length of lncRNAs was 4,530.13 bp, which was shorter than that of mRNAs (11,444.42 bp) (Fig. 3A). The average number of exons in lncRNAs was 3.39, lower than the average number of 13.49 exons in mRNAs (Fig. 3B). The average ORF length of lncRNAs was 149.18, which was shorter than the average ORF length of mRNAs (590.10) (Fig. 3C).

In this study, with the screening criteria of P < 0.05 and |log₂(fold change)| ≥ 2, a total of 2,760 differentially expressed lncRNAs were screened out. Among them, 1,565 lncRNAs were up - regulated and 1,195 were down - regulated in the HFR group compared with the LFR group (Fig. 4).

Enrichment analysis of target genes for differential LncRNAs

The experiment aims to elucidate the functions of lncRNAs by predicting their target genes. Among the 2,760 DELs, 1,190 differentially expressed lncRNAs predicted that 260 genes were target genes with positional correlation, and 445 genes were target genes with expression correlation. Due to the different modes of action of lncRNAs, they were analyzed based on positional and expression relationships. The co-expression relationship GO enrichment analysis showed that a total of 133 GO terms were significantly enriched (P < 0.05). The top 5 GO Terms were RNA-DNA hybrid ribonuclease activity, deoxyribonucleoside triphosphate metabolic process, pyrimidine deoxyribonucleoside triphosphate metabolic process, dUTP metabolic process, and phospholipase activity (Fig. 5A). KEGG enrichment analysis identified a total of 108 pathways, and 3 pathways were significantly enriched (P < 0.05), namely Herpes simplex virus 1 infection, Tight junction, and Influenza A (Fig. 5B).

Discussion

As the only place for animal sperm production, the testicles are strictly regulated in their normal development and participation in the process of spermatogenesis [21]. The entire process of spermatogenesis occurs in the seminiferous tubules of the testes, therefore, the association between testicular tissue and spermatogenesis is the most direct [22]. LncRNA is considered an important regulatory factor in mammalian spermatogenesis [23], but its expression pattern and functional role in goose spermatogenesis are still unclear. In this study, transcriptome sequencing was performed on testicular tissues from Yili geese with high and low sperm motility. Differentially expressed lncRNAs were screened and their biological functions were predicted. 9 lncRNAs and their target genes potentially associated with spermatogenesis were identified, providing supporting material for further exploration of the molecular mechanisms by which lncRNAs regulate spermatogenesis in geese.

Liu et al. [24] analyzed lncRNAs related to chicken sperm motility and found that 84.58% -86.23% of effective reads could match the chicken reference genome after sequencing the testicular tissue; Zhang et al. [25] sequenced sheep testicular tissue and found that 77% of valid readings could match the sheep’s reference genome; Weng et al. [26]sequenced pig testicular tissue and found that 73.66% -75.78% of valid readings could match the reference genome of pigs. The alignment efficiency between the effective readings obtained by this study and the reference genome ranges from 91.29% to 92.37%, which is higher than previous research results in other livestock and poultry. Due to the low conservation of lncRNAs among different species, the identification of lncRNAs can only be based on their own length and ability to encode proteins. This study selected three different software (CPC2, Pfam, CNCI) for analysis and obtained 41,308 new lncRNAs. The new lncRNAs were obtained by taking the intersection of these three software, which greatly improves the authenticity of the results. According to the classification of lncRNAs at genomic locations, it was found that lncRNAs mainly originate from the intergenic region of lincRNAs (76.1%) (Fig. 2B). Comparing the structures of known and unknown lncRNAs with known mRNAs, it was found that the length, number of exons, and ORF length of mRNAs in testicular tissue were higher than those of lncRNAs. This is consistent with the characteristics of lncRNAs identified in other species, indicating that the lncRNAs identified in this study are reliable [27, 28].

This study identified 2760 differentially expressed lncRNAs in the testicular tissues of Yili geese with high and low sperm motility, indicating that these differentially expressed genes play an important role in testicular development and sperm motility traits. Due to the relatively incomplete and incomplete lncRNA database in poultry, this study was unable to obtain its function solely from the sequence of lncRNA. Therefore, indirectly analyzing the function of lncRNAs by predicting and exploring their target genes is an effective method. Enrichment analysis of target genes predicted by DELs between high and low sperm motility testicular tissues revealed that they are associated with biological processes such as ion channel activity, amino acid metabolism, and viral infection.

As one of the basic functions of the body, immune function plays an important role in the testes, which can prevent virus invasion and maintain normal physiological processes such as spermatogenesis [29]. In this study, target genes were significantly enriched in the Herpes simplex virus 1 infection and Influenza A pathways. This suggests that testicular tissue may reduce virus‑induced testicular damage and spermatogenic dysfunction by enhancing antiviral immune responses, thereby potentially contributing indirectly to the maintenance and regulation of sperm motility. Actin is a rich and highly conserved protein in eukaryotes, crucial for various cellular functions such as shape maintenance, movement, membrane organization [30], and cytokinesis [31]. Gervasi et al. [32]found through the use of a three-dimensional stochastic optical reconstruction microscope (STORM) that the middle and main segments of sperm have special actin structures, and different types of actin structures also exist in the tail of sperm. The movement and fertilization potential of mammalian sperm are regulated by ion homeostasis, which in turn is strictly controlled by ion channels and transport proteins [33]. The main ions involved in the fertilization ability of sperm are Na+, K+, Ca2+, Cl−, and H+ [34], similar to ions in neurons. In addition, Na+/K+ATPase plays an important role in establishing resting membrane potential and maintaining the electrochemical gradient of Na+and K+on the sperm plasma membrane [35]. Among all the ions involved in sperm motility, Ca2+is the most important. Ca2+is a common second messenger involved in various cellular signaling pathways and is also an essential “ion mediator” for activating the sAC/cAMP/PKA pathway [36], maintaining sperm mitochondrial function, and ATP production [37]. Nguyen et al. [38]demonstrated that multiple calcium channels are present and expressed in chicken sperm, including high-voltage-activated (HVA) Ca²⁺ channels (L-type and R-type). L-type and R-type Ca²⁺ channels are predominantly localized to the acrosome and midpiece of chicken sperm.Pereira et al. [36]found that low intracellular concentrations of Ca2+(10 nM-40 nM) trigger uniform flagellar oscillation, while higher concentrations of Ca2+(100 nM-300 nM) lead to excessive sperm activity, and higher concentrations of Ca2+(approximately 9 µM) inhibit sperm movement.

The LncRNAs mRNAs interaction network further reveals the co regulatory relationship between LncRNAs and mRNAs. In the interaction network, coiled-coil domain containing 122(CCDC122), It is a member of the Coil Coil Domain Containing (CCDC) protein family. Research has found that some CCDC proteins are closely related to multiple important physiological processes in male gametes, including maintenance and regulation of sperm motility, formation of centromeres, and normal development of sperm flagella [39]. In this study, CCDC122 was involved in pathways such as ciliaum or flagellum dependent cell motility, tubulin binding, and dynein binding, suggesting a potential role for CCDC122 in processes related to flagellar function, which warrants further investigation. The glutaredoxin and cysteine rich domain containing 1 (Grxcr1) gene plays a key regulatory role in the differentiation and maturation of hair cells. Previous studies have shown that Grxcr1 is involved in static ciliary morphogenesis and auditory perception in mice and zebrafish [40, 41, 42]. In this study, Grxcr1 was involved in cellular processes oxidoreductase activity, acting on a sulfur group of donors、protein disulfide oxidoreductase activity. It is thus suggested that Grxcr1 may participate in the formation and maintenance of ciliary structures by modulating ATPase activity, thereby influencing ciliary motility. Centrosomal protein 83 (CEP83) is a member of the centrosomal protein family and a component of the distal attachment of the centromere (DAP), which is an essential structure for the formation of cilia [43]. The phosphorylation of CEP83 by TTBK2 (Tau microtubule kinase 2) is crucial for ciliary initiation, which is a key process in cell signaling and development [44]. In this study, CEP83 was involved in pathways such as dynein binding, cell motility, and motif cilium. It is speculated that CEP83 may play an important role in maintaining the structure and function of sperm flagella by regulating the interaction between motor proteins and microtubules.

In summary, this study identified numerous differentially expressed lncRNAs in the testicular tissue of Yili geese via high-throughput sequencing. Through target gene prediction, seven core regulatory factors were screened out, providing crucial clues for elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying sperm motility. However, the findings are confined to bioinformatics predictions, necessitating larger sample sizes and experimental validation to further confirm the regulatory mechanisms.

Conclusion

In summary, this study analyzed RNA-seq data from testicular tissues of Yili geese with high and low sperm motility, established a novel lncRNA dataset for this species, and identified lncRNAs potentially involved in spermatogenesis. Enrichment analysis of their target genes based on positional and expression correlations suggested potential regulatory roles in biological processes such as ion channel activity, metabolism, and immune response. Furthermore, an lncRNA-mRNA interaction network was constructed, from which key molecular pairs (e.g., LINC23234‑CCDC122, LINC19247‑Grxcr1, LINC1214‑Cep83) were screened as important candidates for further investigation into male avian fertility. This work provides essential data support for elucidating the role of lncRNAs in testicular development and sperm motility regulation, thereby contributing to a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms governing avian reproduction.
