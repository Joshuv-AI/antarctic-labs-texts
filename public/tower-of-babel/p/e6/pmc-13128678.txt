Sexualized Drug Use Linked to Higher HIV Testing Frequency Among Men Who Have Sex with Men in Three Chinese Cities with High HIV Prevalence
Authors: Danyang Luo, Sainan Wang, Xiuping Zhao, Zhizheng Huang, Xuejiao Hu, Cong Liu, Zhen Dai, Haipeng Zha, Pan Gao, Jun Wang, Yehuan Sun, Shufang Sun, Cui Yang, M Kumi Smith, Melvin D Livingston, Don Operario, Hongbo Zhang, Zhihua Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Men who have sex with men (MSM) in China experience high HIV prevalence, yet little is known about the HIV testing behaviors of MSM among those who engage in sexualized drug use (SDU). Clarifying these patterns is essential for designing targeted HIV prevention interventions.Methods:We analyzed baseline survey data from 1,800 MSM recruited in Chengdu, Suzhou, and Wuhan (2021–2022). Participants reported HIV testing history and SDU, including alcohol, rush/ poppers, amphetamines, and other drugs (ketamine, marijuana, heroin, Ma Gu). Logistic regression estimated associations between SDU and HIV testing within 6 months, adjusting for demographics.Results:The mean age was 31.1 years; nearly half held a bachelor’s degree or higher. Alcohol (68%) and rush/poppers (43%) were the most common substances used before or during sex, while amphetamines (1.8%) and other drugs (1.7%) were rarely used. Any drug use excluding alcohol was positively associated with recent HIV testing (adjusted OR = 1.35; 95% CI: 1.08–1.68). Rush/poppers use specifically was linked to higher odds of testing (adjusted OR = 1.32; 95% CI: 1.05–1.63).Conclusions:SDU—particularly rush/poppers use—is prevalent among Chinese MSM and associated with greater HIV testing uptake. Tailored interventions that integrate substance use counseling with HIV testing promotion may help sustain high testing uptake and reduce transmission risk.Keywords: MSM, sexualized drug use, China, HIV/STI

Introduction

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) remains a critical public health issue for men who have sex with men (MSM), who continue to face disproportionately high rates of new infections globally (UNAIDS, 2020). MSM experience elevated risk due to overlapping biological, behavioral, and structural vulnerabilities, including sexual network dynamics, stigma, and barriers to accessing tailored prevention services. In many global settings, there is evidence that subgroups of MSM with co-occurring behavioral and mental health vulnerabilities are at especially high risk for HIV (Operario et al., 2022). Accordingly, tailored interventions that address specific co-occurring health issues are needed for MSM at higher levels of transmission risk (Pantalone et al., 2020).

In China, MSM experience one of the highest HIV prevalence levels among all key populations. National AIDS sentinel surveillance data from 2022 indicate that HIV prevalence among MSM reached 7.0%, with some regions reporting levels exceeding 20.0% (Han, 2023). This prevalence substantially exceeds the global median among MSM, highlighting a persistent and concerning HIV epidemic and reinforcing the need to prioritize MSM within the national HIV response (Su et al., 2025).

HIV testing is a proven prevention strategy, as early diagnosis and treatment not only improve individual health outcomes but also significantly reduce viral load, lowering the risk of transmission (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention [CDC], 2020). HIV testing also plays a pivotal role in normalizing discussions around sexual health, empowering MSM to take proactive steps in managing their health. Despite national campaigns, barriers such as stigma, confidentiality concerns, and uneven access to MSM-friendly services contribute to suboptimal testing uptake (Li et al., 2012; Wei et al., 2014). Data from MSM surveys have shown that the testing rate ranged from 48.6%–83.8% in some Chinese provinces (Liu et al., 2021). Integrating HIV testing into routine healthcare and expanding access through community-based and self-testing initiatives are essential for reducing the burden of HIV in MSM communities (Rocha et al., 2023). Addressing other ongoing health needs in the context of HIV testing for MSM is critical, as is ensuring that services are inclusive, confidential, and non-stigmatizing in order to reinforce engagement and reduce anxiety about seeking services.

Sexualized drug use (SDU)—the use of substances and drugs before or during sex—has emerged as a growing factor shaping HIV risk among MSM. Globally, SDU has been associated with higher rates of condomless sex as well as variable HIV testing behaviors, both of which influence HIV diagnosis and seroconversion (Luo et al., 2018; Nevendorff et al., 2023; H. Wang et al., 2023). Gaining a better understanding of the relationship between SDU and HIV testing behaviors is therefore crucial. Research on SDU often examines substances individually, but real-world use frequently involves patterns—such as the use of specific single substances (e.g., poppers), concurrent use of multiple substances, or the combination of substances with alcohol. These distinct patterns may be differentially associated with sexual risk contexts, healthcare engagement, and, consequently, HIV testing behaviors (J. Sun et al., 2024). The use of rush/ poppers among MSM in China has been widely reported (Chen et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2022; She et al., 2025), yet detailed behavioral data on the prevalence of other substances (such as amphetamines, ketamine, marijuana, heroin, and Ma Gu etc.) remains sparce (Schreck et al., 2021). This lack of granularity poses a significant barrier to developing effective public health messaging and designing targeted interventions that address the unique needs of individuals who use different substances.

Most existing research on SDU among Chinese MSM has focused on megacities like Beijing, Shanghai and Guangzhou. To address this gap and generate evidence from other key urban contexts, our study was conducted in three other major Chinese cities—Chengdu, Suzhou, and Wuhan—which represent large, first-tier metropolitan areas with documented concentrated HIV epidemics among MSM (S. Sun et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2023). Accordingly, our study aims to: (1) provide a detailed description of substance use patterns among MSM in these three cities, and (2) examine the association between different SDU patterns and HIV testing behaviors. Findings will inform the design of prevention strategies that address both substance use and HIV testing among Chinese MSM.

Method

Participants and setting

The data presented in this article originate from an NIH-funded research study addressing an mHealth approach to promoting HIV testing (see Lu et al., 2021; Zhu et al., 2024 for further details). The study was conducted in three major cities in China: Chengdu (Sichuan Province), Suzhou (Jiangsu Province), and Wuhan (Hubei Province). These sites were selected due to documented concentrated HIV epidemics among MSM (L. Zhang et al., 2013), established research infrastructure, and long-standing collaborations between MSM-serving community-based organizations, local CDCs, and researchers.

Participants were recruited through targeted in-person and online outreach, as well as referrals from nongovernmental organizations that provided HIV prevention and education services to MSM in each city. Eligibility criteria required participants to be Chinese men aged 18 or older, who had engaged in condomless anal sex with another man in the past 6 months, owned a smartphone, were experienced WeChat users (a widely used mobile apps in China), and had resided in one of the study sites for at least 6 months. Participants completed a baseline questionnaire using StarSurvey (an online and highly secured data collection platform) and received 150 RMB (approximately USD $23.50) for participation. The present analysis draws on the baseline data, which were collected between December 2021 and May 2022. All procedures above were reviewed and approved by Anhui Medical University Internal Review Board (Approval # IRB00011146).

Procedure

A team of research assistants from local MSM communities was hired, all of whom had prior experience conducting outreach in their respective areas. The recruitment strategy was multifaceted, involving online advertisements on social media, distribution of flyers in targeted venues, and outreach to commercial spaces, public areas, clinics, and community organizations frequented by MSM. Strong partnerships with local MSM-serving agencies and NGOs supported these efforts.

Individuals who responded to recruitment efforts underwent an initial phone screening to assess eligibility. Those who met the inclusion criteria were invited for an in-person intake at the project office housed within each partnering NGO. During intake, eligibility was re-confirmed, informed consent was obtained, before participants began their involvement in the study. Surveys were self-administered on tablets and took approximately 30 min to complete.

Measures

All data were drawn from the baseline survey, which included topics such as demographics, HIV testing history and preferences, sexual behavior and condom use, and substance use.

HIV testing

All participants were asked to report the date of their most recent HIV test, regardless of the testing modality (e.g., facility-based or self-test). Response options were: within 3 months, within 3–6 months, more than 6 months ago, or never. For the primary analysis, we constructed a binary variable indicating whether a participant had reported receiving an HIV test result (via any method) in the past 6 months. Sexualized Drug Use: Participants were asked how often they used the following substances before or during anal or vaginal sex during the past 6 months? Response options were: never, rarely, about half the time, more than half the time, every time. Substance assessed were 5 type/ group of substances: 1: alcohol; 2: rush/poppers; 3: Capsule for receptive role in anal sex (or “zero capsule”, 5-MEO- DIPT)/Rhino Liquid/G-Spot(Dimethyl tryptamine); 4: amphetamines; 5: ketamine; marijuana; heroin; and Magu. For descriptive analyses, we report prevalence according to the original five-scale prevalence in Table 2. For regression models, we constructed five binary indicators to represent whether the participant used each substance type (yes use vs no use, in Table 1).

Data analysis

We used logistic regression to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between each SDU type and HIV testing within the past 6 months. Both crude and adjusted models were estimated for each substance use measure. Multivariable models were run controlling age, income, education, registered residence (Hukou). Analyses were conducted using PROC LOGISTIC in SAS (version 9.4M9). Statistical significance level was set at p < .05.

Result

Demographic characteristics of participants

Across the three study sites, we recruited 1,800 participants total (600 per site). The average age of participants was 31.12 years (SD = 9.27). Nearly half (47.7%) reporting having a bachelor’s degree or higher. More than half (58.1%) had a monthly income ranging from 1,800 to 7,000 Yuan. Notably, a substantial portion (41.39%) did not possess a Hukou (household registration) for their city of residence. In fact, the rates of local Hukou were low across the sites, ranging from 10.83% in Suzhou to 24% in Wuhan, indicating that a significant proportion of the MSM in our sample were migrants (Table 1).

SDU prevalence

Table 2 presents prevalence estimates for five types of SDU. In the past 6 months, alcohol was the most commonly used substance during sex, with 68.4% of the sample (n = 1,232) reporting use ranging from rare occasions to every time before or during anal or vaginal sex. Rush/pop- pers stood out as the substance most commonly used “every time” during sex in the past 6 months, with 6.6% of the sample (n = 119) stating they use it every time they engage in sexual activity. Notably, rush/poppers were used by 43.3% of the sample (n = 780) at least once during sex in the past 6 months. Substances like ketamine, marijuana, heroin, and Ma Gu were the least commonly used during the past 6 months, with only 31 participants (1.72%) reporting use. Similarly, amphetamines were rarely used, with just 32 participants (1.78%) indicating any use in the past 6 months.

Associations between SDU behavior and HIV testing

We conducted crude and adjusted logistic regression analyses to examine associations between SDU and HIV testing (see Table 3). Adjusted models controlled for age, income, education, and registered residence (Hukou). Results from both models were largely consistent. A statistically significant association was observed between any type of any SDU (excluding alcohol). Participants who reported any SDU (excluding alcohol) had higher odds of HIV testing in the past 6 months (OR = 1.39, 95% CI: 1.12–1.73, p = .003). After adjustment for demographic characteristics, the odds ratio remained significant (AOR = 1.35, 95% CI: 1.08–1.68, p = .008). The association continued to remain significant in a sensitivity analysis which further adjusted for drinking (AOR = 1.53, 95% CI: 1.22–1.92, p < .001). Similarly, participants who reported any rush/poppers use during sex had higher odds of HIV testing in the past 6 months (OR = 1.36, 95% CI: 1.09–1.69, p = .006). The adjusted association between rush/poppers use during sex and HIV testing remained significant after adjustment for demographic characteristics (AOR = 1.32, 95% CI: 1.05–1.63, p = .01) as well as drinking (AOR = 1.50, 95% CI: 1.19–1.88, p < .001).

Discussion

Our study provides nuanced data on the SDU patterns of 1,800 MSM recruited from three major first-tier Chinese cities. Beyond detailing substance-specific patterns, a key contribution is its focus on urban contexts outside the most frequently studied megacities (e.g., Beijing, Shanghai) in China. Furthermore, our sample included a substantial proportion of migrants without local household registration (Hukou), underscoring the importance of examining HIV prevention within the dynamic and mobile populations that characterize China’s diverse urban landscapes. The findings reveal a distinct epidemiological profile that necessitates tailored public health strategies.

Consistent with a decade of prior research (Dai et al., 2019; He et al., 2018; Luo et al., 2018; Mao et al., 2021; J. Wang et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2014; H. Zhang et al., 2016; Zhao et al., 2017), rush/poppers emerged as the most prevalent drug used among Chinese MSM. Our use of a 5-point Likert scale, however, reveals crucial heterogeneity within this trend: while 119 (6.6%) reported using rush/poppers every time they had sex, and over one-fifth (21.6%) reported using rush/ poppers during at least half of their sexual episodes in the past 6 months. This gradient suggests that popper use is not a monolithic behavior but varies significantly in its integration into sexual routines. Alcohol was also commonly used during sex—over half (52.89%) combined alcohol and sex at least once in the past 6 months though only 2.2% reported alcohol use every time that had sex. Few participants reported any use of other drugs (ketamine, marijuana, sexual enhancement pills, heroin, Ma Gu) during sex in the past 6 months. This pattern may reflect specific MSM subcultural norms and the focus of national anti-drug campaigns, which have historically emphasized substances like methamphetamine over others. (X. Wang et al., 2023). For example, methamphetamine use during sex was rare in our sample (1.8%), much lower than the 15% prevalence reported in Western MSM populations (Lodge et al., 2024) and 16% among MSM in other parts of Asia (H. Wang et al., 2023). These findings suggest that distinct intervention strategies are necessary to address the differential needs of individuals, with emphasis on MSM who use rush/poppers at varying frequencies.

While the majority of previous research has focused on the association between drug use and HIV infection status (Luo et al., 2018; Mao et al., 2021; J. Wang et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2014), our analysis focused on HIV testing behavior. Consistent with Zhao et al. (2017), we found a positive association between SDU, specifically rush/popper use, and recent HIV testing behavior. The causal relationship between these behaviors, if any, remains undetermined (Bryant et al., 2018). One possible interpretation is that long-standing HIV prevention messaging has effectively reached diverse MSM subpopulations, leading individuals who perceive themselves as high-risk—such as those who use rush/poppers before or during sex—to test more frequently. This finding underscores the value of sustained investment in community-based HIV testing initiatives to maintain engagement among MSM with greater risk and service needs. Moreover, it highlights HIV testing encounters as a timely opportunity to provide counseling on substance use risk reduction. These findings suggest the potential benefits of integrating brief, non-stigmatizing screening for SDU—with a focus on popper use frequency—into standard HIV testing protocols. Future longitudinal research is needed to disentangle the causal pathways between SDU, risk perception, and testing behaviors, and to evaluate the efficacy of integrated counseling models in this distinct epidemiological context.

Limitation

Our study has several limitations. Data were collected only from Chengdu, Suzhou, and Wuhan, and findings may not be generalizable to other settings. Additionally, data collection occurred between December 2021 and May 2022, during the dynamic COVID-19 pandemic in China. Public health restrictions, including lockdowns, mobility limitations, and reduced access to non-essential healthcare services, were in effect at varying intensities across the study sites during this period. These conditions may have significantly influenced the key variables under study. For instance, lockdowns could have altered patterns of sexualized drug use by limiting access to social venues and substances, while simultaneously increasing solitary or private use. Access to facility-based HIV testing may have been directly restricted, potentially inflating the reported intervals since last test or increasing reliance on HIV self-testing. Furthermore, pandemic-related stress and isolation may have independently affected both substance use and sexual risk behaviors. Consequently, the prevalence estimates and observed associations reported here may reflect a unique period of public health crisis and should be interpreted within this specific temporal context. Future research should examine whether these patterns persist in a post-pandemic era. Third, state policies toward substance use in China may have affected participants’ comfort in reporting their behaviors. Lastly, the categories for substance use were predefined in the original survey and do not reflect optimal pharmacological groupings (e.g., heroin was grouped with other substances like ketamine). This limits the granularity of our analysis regarding specific drug classes.

Conclusion

HIV testing is a proven prevention strategy, yet testing behaviors remain unevenly distributed within MSM communities. Our data indicate that rush/poppers remain the most commonly used drug among Chinese MSM and that drug use is positively associated with HIV testing. Future interventions should build on these insights by strengthening institutional and community-level supports to sustain HIV testing and integrate substance use risk reduction screening and counseling for MSM.
