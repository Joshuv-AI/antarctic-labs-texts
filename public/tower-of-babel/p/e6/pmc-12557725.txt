Variation exists in venous thromboembolism prophylaxis in traumatic brain injury despite national guidelines: insights from a recent AAST survey study
Authors: Keith Walter Burczak, Jan-Michael Van Gent, Devi Bavishi, Gabrielle E Hatton, Patrick B Murphy, Asanthi Ratnasekera, Thomas W Clements, Rachel S Morris, Christopher Tignanelli, Bryan A Cotton, Thaddeus J Puzio
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBest practice guidelines from trauma-focused organizations support early (<72 hours) venous thromboembolism (VTE) chemoprophylaxis initiation in traumatic brain injury (TBI) patients. Recent literature suggests that an even more aggressive initiation (24 hours) is safe and efficacious. It is unknown how current practice aligns with the existing literature and best practice guidelines. We performed a national survey to characterize variation in trauma surgeons’ reported practices and attitudes regarding VTE chemoprophylaxis initiation in TBI patients with the hypothesis that they will vary widely.MethodsAll 1700 American Association for the Surgery of Trauma members were surveyed online. Members who routinely cared for patients with TBI were asked to proceed with the survey. Descriptive data were reported. The questions characterized institutional guidelines, and if none existed, individual practice patterns regarding goal timeline of VTE chemoprophylaxis in TBI patients.Results374 members (22%) completed the survey. Most respondents worked at academic institutions (40%) and represented a level one trauma center (44%). Enoxaparin was the most common VTE chemoprophylactic (73%) with most using weight-based dosing (76%) and anti-Xa testing (75%). Institutional policies that included a protocol for VTE chemoprophylaxis initiation in TBI patients were used by 86% of respondents. In those with established protocols, 59% reported being mostly compliant. Reasons for delay of VTE chemoprophylaxis included worry about progression of TBI and disagreement with consulting services. 293 (80%) of respondents reported protocolized repeat head CT within 4–12 hours after initial CT (60%). In response to a clinical scenario, 43% of respondents reported that their goal timeline for initiating VTE prophylaxis was within 24 hours of a stable head CT.ConclusionsDespite national guidelines, substantial variation remains in the timing of VTE chemoprophylaxis in TBI patients. Passive strategies, such as institutional guidelines alone, are insufficient. Institutions must adopt more effective implementation tactics—including protocol-enforced order sets, automated clinical decision support, and shared governance models with ancillary services—to ensure timely, evidence-based care.Level of evidenceLevel V.Keywords: Thromboembolism; traumatic brain injury; Practice Patterns, Physicians'; Time-To-Treatment

Background

Venous thromboembolism (VTE) adversely impacts patient outcomes by elevating morbidity and mortality rates, contributes to increased healthcare costs, and strains healthcare systems by requiring extensive medical resources.1 2 VTE is a well-known complication after trauma, with over half of polytrauma patients also sustaining traumatic brain injuries (TBI). TBI independently elevates the risk of VTE.3 This presents a significant challenge in clinical management in this patient population as the foundation of VTE prevention is early chemoprophylaxis (CP) in the form of subcutaneous unfractionated heparin or enoxaparin. In patients with TBI, the risk of VTE must be balanced with the risk of progression of intracranial hemorrhage.

Historically, no guidelines existed regarding initiation of VTE CP in patients with TBI. However, numerous studies now establish the safety of initiating VTE CP early in this patient population, within 24–72 hours post admission, including multiply injured patients and those with severe isolated head injuries.4,9 This approach is now endorsed by national guidelines.10 11 Despite these recommendations, there remains a gap in the implementation of optimal VTE prophylaxis initiation in patients with TBI.12 This study was designed to evaluate variation in the timing of VTE CP initiation following radiographic stability, rather than from the time of injury. When the survey was developed in early 2023, many institutional protocols remained anchored to imaging-based decision points—a practice pattern that was reflected in the responses we received. Our aim was to characterize trauma surgeons’ reported practices and attitudes regarding VTE CP in patients with TBI, specifically focusing on variation in timing after stable head CT. While national guidelines are evolving toward injury-based initiation, this imaging-based approach remains a relevant clinical gap. We hypothesized that, despite existing guidelines and supporting evidence, significant variation in reported practice would persist.

Methods

Qualtrics XM, an online survey and distribution software, was used to develop, disseminate, and analyze an online survey. As seen in online supplemental appendix A, the survey consisted of a total of 18 questions. The survey used display and branch logic, displaying questions conditionally based on previous answers (figure 1). Branch logic was used such that only respondents who answered ‘Yes’ to the question, ‘Do you care for patients with TBI?’ were allowed to proceed to the remainder of the survey. This question served as a practical screen for routine involvement in TBI care, though the term ‘routine’ was not further defined. Compliance categories were predefined in the survey as: very compliant (>75% of TBI patients), mostly compliant (25–75%), rarely compliant (<25%), or unsure. Furthermore, we embedded two clinical case scenarios within the final questions of the survey (online supplemental appendix A). This was done to delve deeper into the respondents’ attitude regarding goal timeline to VTE CP initiation in TBI patients. Prior to distribution, the survey was pilot tested for face and content validity by four trauma surgeons, but no formal psychometric validation was conducted. As such, findings reflect descriptive perceptions and self-reported practices.

The survey was accessible for a total of 12 weeks on the Qualtrics platform. The American Association for the Surgery of Trauma (AAST) membership received an anonymous introductory email notifying them of the purpose of the study as well as a link to the survey in June 2023. Two additional reminder emails were sent at 4 weeks and 8 weeks post survey publication. Additionally, the survey link was available on the AAST webpage for the duration of publication. Participation was voluntary, and respondents provided consent by actively choosing to complete and submit the survey. Incomplete surveys were excluded from analysis. The survey was anonymous, and no personally identifiable information was collected unless voluntarily provided for the raffle. Access was managed via the Qualtrics platform, which used browser cookies to prevent duplicate entries. Despite this, the survey link was available both via email and publicly on the AAST website over 12 weeks, which could allow for potential duplicate participation.

Respondents were compared based on practice setting (academic vs non-academic), surgeon experience (<10 years vs >10 years), and level trauma center of primary practice. Median values with IQRs were used to describe continuous data, and discrete data were reported as frequency and percentage. χ2 and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were used to compare categorical and continuous demographic data and outcomes, respectively. All data analyses were completed using R V.3.53 (R Core Team. 2013. R: a language and environment for statistical computing. R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). This international, cross-sectional survey was exempted by our institutional review board and approved and endorsed by the AAST. The Checklist for Reporting of Survey Studies (CROSS) guideline was used to ensure proper reporting of methods, results, and discussion .

Given the voluntary nature of participation and the AAST membership base, the respondent pool included a higher proportion of surgeons at level 1 trauma centers than is present nationally. The survey did not employ stratified sampling or apply post-hoc weighting, and thus findings are interpreted in light of this potential imbalance. No formal sample size calculation was performed. This was a convenience sample of AAST members recruited through email invitations and website posting.

Results

Demographics

A total of 411 responses were obtained from the 1700 AAST members surveyed. Only 374 of the respondents completed the survey in full, resulting in a survey response rate of 22%. Of the 374 respondents who completed the survey, 365 (98%) indicated that they cared for patients with TBI and continued to the main content. The remaining nine respondents were excluded via branch logic after indicating they did not provide TBI care. The calculated margin of error for the target population is 4%.

Most respondents (67%) had been in practice for 10 years or less at the time of the survey and worked at level 1 designated trauma centers (44%) and/or academic medical centers (41%). Most of the respondents also indicated that they took care of TBI patients daily (52%).

Institutional VTE chemoprophylactic protocols and personal practice habits

A vast majority of respondents indicated that they use institutional clinical practice guidelines and clinical care algorithms to guide management of trauma patients (92%). When the response was broken down into academic versus non-academic surgeons, 96.1% of academic surgeons used institutional guidelines compared with 89.1% of non-academic surgeons (p=0.04) (table 1). In those with no institutional protocols, the decision when to start VTE CP was most often analyzed by the neurosurgical team (45%). Among respondents without institutional protocols, 21% reported using national guidelines to guide their individual practice. Other factors that respondents used for determining timing of VTE initiation included the respondents’ own level of concern for bleeding progression (19%), and what the respondent ‘thought was best’ (13%) (figure 2).

Most institutional VTE chemoprophylactic protocols included interval CT head axial imaging prior to initiation of CP (97%). This was most commonly obtained at 4 hours to 6 hours after admission imaging (34%), though substantial heterogeneity was observed. Surgeons at level 1 and 2 centers tended to favor earlier interval head CT scans (within 4 hours to 6 hours) compared with those at lower-level designations, who are more likely to delay or vary their approach (p<0.01) (table 1). In addition, surgeons in practice less than 10 years were more likely to opt for earlier interval head CT scans (within 4 hours to 6 hours) compared with their more experienced counterparts (p<0.01) (table 1). Finally, academic surgeons tend to favor earlier timing (within 4 hours to 6 hours) compared with non-academic surgeons, who show a wider distribution across timing options (p<0.01) (table 1).

Nearly half of the respondents (43%) reported the goal timeline to initiation as dictated by their institutional protocol was at or within 24 hours from time of a stable interval head CT. Academic surgeons in practice <10 years and surgeons at levels 1 and 2 were more likely to have an earlier goal timeline of VTE CP initiation as compared with their respective counterparts (table 1). Surgeons in practice <10 years were more likely to have an earlier goal timeline to initiate VTE prophylaxis, particularly within 24 hours of a stable head CT (p<0.01) (table 1). Timing goals for VTE prophylaxis were similar between the academic versus non-academic physicians (p=0.08) (table 1). Surgeons at level 1 and 2 centers are more likely to aim for VTE prophylaxis within 24 hours of injury, while those at lower-level designations had a greater variation in their goal timeline to VTE CP initiation (p<0.02) (table 1).

Enoxaparin was the most preferred VTE CP agent (73%). Surgeons at level 1 and 2 centers were more likely to choose enoxaparin as the preferred prophylactic agent, while those at lower level designations preferred subcutaneous unfractionated heparin (table 1). There was also a significant difference between academic surgeons who are more likely to choose enoxaparin (78.5%) compared with non-academic surgeons (67.0%) (table 1). Enoxaparin was the preferred prophylactic agent across both compared experience levels (table 1).

In those that chose enoxaparin, most indicated they employed weight-based dosing (73%) as well as anti-Factor Xa monitoring to adjust dosing (75%). Nonacademic surgeons were more likely to choose enoxaparin with weight-based dosing (p<0.05).

Perceived compliance and reasons for delay to timely CP initiation

Respondents using institutional VTE chemoprophylactic protocols were asked to estimate their compliance as well as identify reasons for delay from a single, closed-ended survey item with pre-specified choices that prevent adherence to their institutional guidelines. A majority of the respondents (59%) stated that they were mostly compliant with their respective institutional protocol, with 25% to 75% of their TBI patients receiving timely VTE CP according to their protocol. Perceived barriers to compliance with institutional-specific protocols were reported via a single multiple-choice question with prespecified options. The three most selected reasons were: concern for progression of intracranial hemorrhage (30%), overall injury burden (28%), and disagreement with neurosurgery (27%). These selections were relatively evenly distributed, suggesting no single predominant reason for delay (figure 3).

Clinical scenarios

When presented with a previously healthy 50 year-old male with an isolated subarachnoid hemorrhage and intraparenchymal contusion found at admission head CT, the majority of respondents chose to initiate VTE CP at or within 24 hours from time of interval stable head CT (n=157, 43%). A minority indicated they would forego interval CT head imaging and initiate CP at or within 24 hours of injury (n=36, 10%). On the other hand, a very small portion of respondents (n=3, <1%) chose to forego VTE CP altogether and instead place an inferior vena cava (IVC filter) (figure 4).

The second scenario presented a 60 year-old polytrauma patient with multiple extremity fractures and a subdural hematoma that progressed on interval head CT. Again, most respondents chose to initiate VTE CP at or within 24 hours from the time of a stable head CT (n=143, 39%), and 92% (n=325) of the respondents would initiate VTE CP within 72 hours. There was a similar trend to scenario one, in which a small portion (n=3, <1%) would elect to hold VTE CP in lieu of placing an IVC filter (figure 5).

While several subgroup comparisons reached statistical significance, the calculated survey margin of error is ±4%. Accordingly, subgroup differences greater than 4% are considered likely to reflect meaningful variation. Differences within or near this margin may reflect sampling variability and should be interpreted with caution. For example, differences in interval CT timing at 6hours to 12 hours (24% vs 32%), 12hours to 24 hours (11% vs 22%), and prophylaxis within 24 hours of injury (17% vs 11%) exceed the 4% margin of error (MOE) and are likely clinically significant. In contrast, the difference in VTE prophylaxis within 24 hours of a stable head CT (43% vs 39%) falls at the threshold of the MOE and may not be meaningful.

Discussion

The present study provides insights into the current practices and attitudes of trauma surgeons regarding the goal timeline to initiation of VTE CP in patients with TBI. Despite the well-documented risk of VTE in TBI patients, our findings reveal variation in practice patterns, with differences observed between academic and non-academic surgeons, varying levels of experience, and those working at different levels of trauma centers. This study highlights significant variation in trauma surgeons’ reported goals for VTE CP timing in TBI patients, with only 43% indicating initiation within 24 hours of stable head CT—underscoring a gap between guidelines and perceived practice. Academic surgeons, those at higher-level trauma centers, and less-experienced surgeons were more likely to report earlier initiation and interval imaging, potentially reflecting generational differences in risk perception or guideline interpretation. Respondents most commonly cited concern for hemorrhage progression, injury burden, and disagreement with neurosurgery as reasons for delay, suggesting systemic cultural and communication challenges. While many institutions report guideline adoption, persistent variability reinforces that protocols alone are insufficient without effective implementation strategies. Interpretation of subgroup differences must consider the survey’s ±4% margin of error, with larger differences likely reflecting true variation and smaller differences requiring cautious interpretation.

Prior research has been performed looking at optimal VTE CP in patients with all severities of TBI. More recent publications have shown safety and efficacy in severe TBI patients when VTE CP is given as early as 24 hours post admission.9 13 In addition, it has been demonstrated that each hour of delay of CP increases the likelihood of VTE by 1.5%, making early administration critical.14 The evidence available at the time of this survey stands in stark contrast to the limited data during a similar 2018 survey by Strollo et al. In their survey of Eastern Association for the Surgery of Trauma members, no national guidelines existed to direct the optimal timing of VTE CP initiation in TBI patients.15 In contrast to Strollo et al’s findings, where 52.6% of respondents reported a lack of standardized protocols, our study shows only 8% of respondents reported lacking or were unsure of institutional clinical practice guidelines.15 Interpretation of subgroup variation must also consider the survey’s ±4% margin of error. Several comparisons exceeded this threshold and likely represent true differences in practice, including CT timing at 6 hours to 12 hours and 12 hours to 24 hours, and earlier VTE prophylaxis initiation based on injury time. However, some statistically significant differences—such as the 4% variation in VTE initiation based on stable CT—fall within this margin and may reflect random sampling variability. This underscores the importance of interpreting smaller numerical differences with caution, even when p values are <0.05.

Although this demonstrates an improvement in the advent of literature supporting national guidelines, implementation of such guidelines remains a challenge. This is reflected by only 34% of respondents who stated they are ‘very compliant’ with their protocols. A systematic review by Tooher et al found that ‘passive dissemination of guidelines is unlikely to improve VTE prophylaxis practice’ and that multiple active strategies used together are more likely to result in optimal outcomes.16 Implementation science is the study of methods to promote the adoption and integration of evidence-based practices, interventions, and policies into routine healthcare and public health settings.12 A cornerstone of implementation research is identifying barriers; however, our survey only captured perceived barriers through a single, prespecified response item, which limits the depth and generalizability of these findings.

The most cited reason for delay to timely initiation of VTE CP was concern regarding the progression of head injury, followed closely by the overall injury burden and disagreements with neurosurgery teams. These concerns highlight the complex decision-making process involved in managing TBI patients, where the risk of hemorrhage progression must be balanced against the risk of VTE. Practice pattern variations exist between institutions and specialties caring for TBI patients. These variations may be mitigated by regular joint educational grand rounds, multidisciplinary process improvement meetings to review current literature and creation of clinical practice guidelines with the support of various service lines. Further, active implementation strategies such as computerized decision tools and alerts aimed at improving compliance with such guidelines may have the most impact.16,18 These multifaceted approaches, including passive and active strategies, have been found to have the largest effects on VTE CP prescribing practices.18 Al Tannir et al demonstrated that expansion of their institutional multidisciplinary trauma program improvement team significantly improved CP initiation in non-operative TBI patients.19

Human alerts such as the addition of clinical pharmacists to the trauma team are an approach that was found to positively impact VTE CP prescribing practices. Alexander et al in their survey of AAST members examining VTE CP prescribing practices in trauma patients demonstrated the addition of clinical pharmacists led to more guideline-directed dosing as well as dose adjustments for patients with obesity.20 Incorporating a multidisciplinary approach that includes other service lines may help alleviate decision-making burdens on trauma practitioners and inform them of up-to-date evidence. Further studies should be aimed at delineating what implementation strategies are utilized by institutions that provide the best compliance of CP initiation.

The responses to the two clinical scenarios in this survey demonstrate the variation in individual VTE CP prescribing patterns. However, this may reflect aspirational or idealized responses rather than actual behavior. The gap between reported goals in scenarios and described institutional timelines may reflect variability between policy and perception, or between intention and real-world practice. Regardless, it must be stated most respondents (86% and 82%, respectively) chose to administer VTE CP within 72 hours which is consistent with guidelines available at the time of the survey.10 11 Compared with the responses in the two clinical scenarios presented by Strollo et al, our study demonstrates a trend toward earlier administration of initiation of CP in similar clinical situations.15 This highlights the quality research conducted on VTE mitigation strategies and the adoption of national best practice guidelines. However, practice variation and knowledge gaps still remain, as evidenced by a very few respondents opting to place prophylactic IVC filters despite evidence against their routine use.10 11

This study has several limitations. Recall and response bias may impact accuracy, and the use of a convenience sample of AAST members limits generalizability. Over-representation of academic surgeons and level I trauma centers may skew reported practices, reducing applicability to community, rural, or lower-volume centers. The absence of outcome data and longitudinal follow-up provides only a snapshot of current practices without insight into changes over time. Additionally, self-reporting introduces the risk of social desirability bias and variability in guideline interpretation, whereas the survey’s closed-ended design restricts exploration of the full range of reasons for delayed prophylaxis. Specifically, the study’s identification of perceived barriers was limited to a single, closed-ended survey item with prespecified choices. Future studies using open-ended or qualitative methods may better capture these factors. The survey’s anonymous design also prevented accounting for multiple respondents from the same institution, and duplicate entries remain possible given the public link distribution. Timing preferences reflect practices as of early 2023 and may not capture shifts toward injury-based initiation recommended in newer guidelines. Finally, subgroup differences should be interpreted in light of the ±4% margin of error; while some exceeded this threshold and likely represent true variation, smaller differences may reflect sampling variability despite statistical significance.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates significant variation in trauma surgeons’ reported goals for the timing of VTE CP in TBI patients, even after stable head CT. These findings highlight an added delay relative to evolving national benchmarks, which increasingly recommend initiation based on time from injury rather than radiographic stability. Passive strategies, such as institutional guidelines alone, appear insufficient to bridge this gap. Closing the divide between best evidence and perceived practice will require more active implementation approaches, including protocol-enforced order sets, automated clinical decision support, and shared governance with neurosurgery to promote timely, consistent care.
