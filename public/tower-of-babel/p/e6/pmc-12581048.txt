Culture, context and crisis: understanding community engagement in Ghana’s Ashanti and Northern regions amid COVID-19 – a qualitative study
Authors: Gyesi Razak Issahaku, Hanna-Tina Fischer, Emmanuel Appiah-Brempong, Daniel Opoku, Johanna Hanefeld
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe COVID-19 pandemic highlighted the critical role of community engagement in public health responses. While national-level interventions in Ghana have been documented, limited research exists on how regional differences shape engagement strategies. This study compares the Ashanti and Northern regions, two areas with contrasting socioeconomic, cultural and epidemiological contexts, to examine how community engagement was implemented during the COVID-19 response.MethodsA qualitative study was conducted in the two regions from November 2023 to February 2024. We conducted interviews with 24 key informants and facilitated two focus group discussions with 20 participants. Data collection involved community mapping, key informant interviews and focus group discussions with community leaders, health officials and vulnerable groups in the selected communities. Data were coded and analysed using MAXQDA analysis software employing thematic analysis, guided by frameworks from Gilmore and Popay.ResultsIn the Ashanti region, engagement strategies were more centralised and formal, using mass media and structured messaging from health professionals. Conversely, the Northern region implemented decentralised, community-led approaches through traditional leaders, interpersonal communication and community-based surveillance volunteers. Logistics provision also differed; Ashanti benefited from structured supply chains, while the Northern region relied on community-led innovations due to resource limitations. Centralised approaches in Ashanti enabled coordinated messaging, whereas Northern strategies were perceived to foster deeper trust and participation.ConclusionCommunity engagement strategies varied across regions, reflecting underlying governance structures and socio-cultural dynamics. Decentralised approaches were associated with higher levels of community participation and trust, while centralised models supported efficient coordination. While this study does not assess the impact of engagement strategies on health outcomes, it highlights the need for contextually adapted strategies that balance structured public health directives with meaningful community involvement.Keywords: COVID-19, Public Health, Epidemiology, Epidemics

Background

The COVID-19 pandemic has had a significant global impact, affecting health systems, economies and daily lives. In many countries worldwide, including in sub-Saharan Africa, the pandemic has highlighted the need for improved pandemic preparedness.1 Community engagement is widely regarded as a vital pillar for effective pandemic readiness and response, serving as a foundation for building resilient, cohesive and responsive public health systems.2 It involves a collaborative effort between communities and public health organisations in decision-making, planning and executing strategies to enhance health and address public health challenges.3,5

In the context of pandemics and public health emergencies, the adoption of context-specific strategies for community engagement is critically important. These strategies need to be tailored to the unique cultural, social, economic and geographic contexts of communities, understanding that a one-size-fits-all approach is neither appropriate nor effective.6 By considering cultural norms and community structures, interventions can be tailored to align with local values and practices. This specificity in public health measures enhances acceptance and adherence within each community.7 The diversity between northern and southern Ghana is crucial for effective pandemic response strategies. The contrasting cultural, social and economic landscapes of the Ashanti and Northern regions have a significant influence on community engagement and responses to COVID-19.

While previous studies on COVID-19 responses in Ghana have explored public health measures and government interventions, there has been limited research on how regional differences influence community engagement strategies and the extent of community participation. This study addresses this gap by comparing the Ashanti and Northern regions, which represent contrasting socioeconomic, demographic and cultural contexts, as well as regions with marked differences in the prevalence of COVID-19 during the pandemic. The Ashanti region of Ghana is densely populated and urbanised, suffering significantly during the COVID-19 pandemic. In contrast, the Northern region is rural, with lower population density.8 These differences make both regions useful for studying how community engagement strategies adapt during public health crises. Understanding these variations can help tailor interventions to be contextually relevant and culturally sensitive, promoting community ownership and participation in pandemic response efforts.

By applying Gilmore’s framework for engagement strategies and Popay’s model for participation levels, we offer a systematic classification of engagement strategies, demonstrating how governance structures and socio-cultural factors influence public health mobilisation during pandemics.

Methods

Study setting

The study was conducted in two of Ghana’s 16 regions: the Ashanti and Northern regions (figure 1). The Ashanti region was chosen because it was one of the worst-hit regions during the COVID-19 pandemic and had a relatively higher rate of COVID-19 deaths compared with the rest of the country.9 The Northern region of Ghana had fewer COVID-19 cases compared with the Ashanti region. The Ashanti Region, with Kumasi as its capital, is the second-most populous, featuring a well-developed health system and better access to media. In contrast, the Northern region is comparatively more rural, has limited infrastructure and fewer health facilities. For this study, two metropolitan areas (Kumasi and Tamale) and two municipal areas (Ejisu and Savelugu) (figure 1) were selected from each region, as each contributed to understanding cultural diversity in pandemic response.

Map of Ghana showing the study districts.

The Tamale metropolis has a population of 374 744, while Savelugu has 122 889 residents. The Dagombas, who predominantly inhabit the Northern region, mainly practise Islam. Meanwhile, Kumasi has a population of 443 981, and Ejisu has 180 723 residents, with Christianity as the dominant religion among the Akan people.10 The communities in both regions exhibit strong social cohesion and reciprocity, with high levels of participation in communal activities. Faith-based organisations, market associations and youth groups are often mobilised during public health interventions. These dynamics are key to understanding how health messages are received, adapted and acted on at the local level.

Study design and study population

We conducted a qualitative study in four community sites in two metropolitan and two municipal areas across Ashanti and Northern regions from November 2023 to February 2024. We first conducted community mapping exercises in each study site to ensure an in-depth understanding of community structures and identify key populations for engagement. This process involved a desk review of academic and grey literature on the socio-cultural aspects of the selected study sites and a scan of local community websites and social media platforms to identify relevant community leaders and organisations. In addition, informal conversations were held, and observations were conducted to purposively select informants based on their background and relevant expertise in the selected communities and healthcare settings. This mapping exercise led to the identification of relevant stakeholders to be included in the subsequent key informant interviews (KIIs) and the focus group discussions (FGDs).

In addition to the mapping, we conducted KIIs and FGDs. Key informants were purposively selected based on their involvement in community engagement efforts during the COVID-19 response (table 1). Selection criteria included leadership role, level of direct engagement in health interventions and knowledge of community-level public health strategies. The FGDs served to better understand the experiences of vulnerable groups in the sites and the strategies used to engage them during the response to the COVID-19 pandemic. The concept of vulnerability in this study was defined based on social and economic factors rather than biological vulnerability.6

Participants for the KIIs and FGDs were purposively selected to ensure representation of the range of stakeholders involved in the COVID-19 response in both the Ashanti and Northern regions. FGD participants were identified through insights gained from initial community mapping exercises and early KIIs. The community mapping process highlighted key stakeholder groups, social networks and population segments relevant to the response, while KIIs provided guidance on which groups were most engaged or affected. This process ensured that FGD participants reflected community diversity and were able to provide rich, context-specific perspectives.

The number of FGDs and KIIs was determined by the need to capture perspectives across stakeholder categories, achieve regional representation and reach data saturation. In total, 2 FGDs and 24 KIIs were conducted, with data collection continuing until no new themes emerged.

Sampling strategy

Participants were selected through purposive sampling, ensuring representation from key stakeholder groups involved in community engagement during the COVID-19 response. Selection criteria included individuals with direct experience implementing or receiving engagement interventions. To ensure diversity, we considered participants’ roles (eg, health workers, community leaders, policymakers), geographic locations (urban and rural settings) and engagement with different COVID-19 response strategies.

Data saturation was determined through an iterative approach. Interviews continued until no new themes emerged. Transcripts were reviewed after each round of data collection, and coding was conducted in parallel. Once additional interviews yielded redundant insights without introducing novel perspectives, saturation was achieved.

Data collection and management

Four graduate researchers conducted the qualitative interviews. The interview guides were piloted with a pretest in two communities from two districts (Sagnarigu and Sekyere East), one in each region, to enhance the diversity of environments in which they were piloted. Following this pilot, we revised the interview guides to enhance the clarity and completeness of the questions in scope.

KIIs and FGDs sought to understand the community engagement strategies that were used to reach out to the communities during the response to the pandemic and the challenges in engaging communities. Community KIIs were conducted in the predominant local language of the area, Twi for those in the Ashanti Region and Dagbani for those in the Northern region. KIIs involving local health officials were, however, in English. The two FGDs were conducted in Dagbani. Each KII lasted about 30–45 min, while the FGDs were an average of 90 min. Interviews were audio-recorded, and those in Dagbani and Twi were translated and, together with those in English, transcribed verbatim with time stamps using MAXQDA Analytic Pro developed by Verbi GmbH. The researchers carefully listened to all the audio recordings of the interviews, ensuring that the transcriptions were accurate and spelling errors were corrected while maintaining the original wording. The transcripts were then proofread, and pseudonyms were used to maintain confidentiality. Interview and FGD transcripts were returned to participants for comment and correction, allowing them to verify accuracy and clarify their perspectives where needed. Finally, the validated and pseudonymised transcripts were imported back into MAXQDA Analytic Pro (V.24.2.0) for coding and analysis.

Research reflexivity

The interviews and FGDs were conducted by three members of the research team (GRI, EA-B and DO). EA-B and DO are both trained qualitative researchers with postgraduate qualifications in public health and epidemiology. At the time of the study, the researchers (EA-B, DO, H-TF and JH) were employed as academic researchers with extensive experience in community health and qualitative fieldwork. Three of the researchers were male, and the other two were female, which helped participants feel comfortable engaging across different social groups. Before data collection, the facilitators had no established personal or professional relationships with participants. Community entry and rapport building were achieved through collaboration with health directorates and local leaders, fostering trust and open dialogue. Researcher reflexivity was essential for transparency and minimising bias. The research team maintained a reflexivity journal to document assumptions and reflections, helping mitigate the risk of imposing beliefs on the data. Regular debriefing sessions allowed researchers to assess themes and challenge interpretations, enhancing credibility.

Researcher–participant interactions were managed to minimise social desirability bias through open-ended questioning and active listening, fostering a comfortable environment for genuine sharing. Triangulation of data from interviews, focus groups and document reviews validated findings across different perspectives. Multiple researchers independently coded and developed themes, reaching a final consensus to ensure that findings reflected participants’ experiences rather than researchers’ expectations.

Data analysis

The data were coded deductively using a priori parent codes drawn from Gilmore et al’s framework for community engagement strategies. To assess the depth of community participation within each strategy, we applied Popay’s model, which categorises engagement into different levels of participation (informing, consulting, co-producing and empowering). However, Popay’s model was initially designed to examine how engagement levels impact health outcomes, a relationship we do not directly assess in this study. Instead, we use Popay’s framework as a descriptive tool to systematically classify the extent of community participation in each engagement strategy. Any references to engagement levels (eg, co-producing, empowering) should be understood as involvement classifications rather than indicators of improved health outcomes.

Thematic analysis was conducted iteratively, allowing for the identification of patterns linking specific engagement strategies (eg, risk communication, community surveillance) to different levels of participation (informing, consulting, co-production, empowerment). While the primary coding structure was deductive, we adopted a flexible approach that accommodated inductively emerging sub-codes related to, but not explicitly outlined in, the Gilmore model.

To ensure coding consistency, we developed a codebook and assigned descriptive memos to all subcodes coherently aligned with the model’s constructs. Two researchers conducted coding independently using MAXQDA analysis software, and disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third researcher.

The coded segments were paraphrased to obtain descriptive summaries in MAXQDA. An interpretative analysis of these summaries was performed to extract key messages related to strategies for community engagement during the COVID-19 response. The findings were refined through team discussions to enhance clarity and conceptual alignment. Participants were given the opportunity to provide feedback on preliminary findings during dissemination meetings in each region, which helped validate interpretations and ensured that the themes reflected community perspectives.

Results

General results

Our study participants included six categories of resource persons involved in implementing community engagement strategies during the COVID-19 response in Ghana. The majority of the Key Informants were male (20/24). Interviews typically lasted about 45 min, ranging from 38 to 65 min. Of the 20 focus group participants, just over half (11/20) were female.

Strategies for community engagement

Community engagement strategies during the COVID-19 response varied significantly between the Ashanti and Northern regions, particularly in terms of who led the efforts, how communities were involved and engaged. Our analysis is structured according to Gilmore et al’s framework for community engagement for epidemics, which categorises strategies for community engagement into six broad areas: social and behavioural change communication (SBCC), risk communication, surveillance and contact tracing, community entry and trust building, provision and administration of logistics, and community involvement in planning interventions.11 To systematically assess the depth of community involvement in each strategy, we also applied Popay’s model of community participation, which categorises engagement into four levels: informing, consulting, co-producing and empowering.5 These levels represent a continuum from one-way information delivery to active, community-led decision-making. While Popay’s framework was originally developed to evaluate the impact of participation on health outcomes, we use it here as a descriptive tool to classify the nature and depth of engagement across the different strategies in each region.

Each strategy was implemented differently across the two regions, with varying levels of participation (table 3). We analyse how engagement strategies were carried out and the extent to which they involved communities based on Popay’s framework (Table 3).

Social and behavioural change communication

This strategy focuses on shaping public attitudes and behaviours towards COVID-19 prevention, including mask-wearing, handwashing and social distancing. In the Ashanti region, a majority of the study respondents reported that SBCC relied heavily on formal media channels such as radio, television and organised public announcements. The focus was on widespread dissemination through mass media to reach a broad audience. The health directorate often collaborated with local radio stations to air programmes to educate the public on COVID-19 prevention measures.

A health official in Kumasi stated:

We used radio stations and community information centers extensively to reach out to the general public and educate them on COVID-19 protocols. (ASH_KSI_HP_KI_02)

Another official in the Kumasi Metro health directorate noted:

… Most of our traditional channels, like our community deburs, were not appropriate for that period. What we’re using most in Kumasi, which is a cosmopolitan area, was radio stations. The radio stations offered airtime free of charge for us to communicate with both the radio and television stations to communicate to people …. (ASH_KSI_DC_KI_03)

A health promotion officer in Ejisu also recounted:

We tried to target the communities largely through radio discussions. We supported this by using community information centers as channels of our communication … (ASH_EJS_HP_KII_02)

While these communication efforts provided broad coverage, they were largely one-directional, with limited community involvement in shaping the messages. This approach reflects an ‘informing’ level of engagement, leaving communities as passive recipients rather than active participants. In contrast, participants in the Northern region, especially those in rural areas, noted that SBCC leaned towards traditional community-based channels, primarily engaging through personal interactions in settings such as mosques, churches and local gatherings.

A community chief emphasised:

We relied on announcements in mosques and churches, as not everyone had access to radio or television. (NR_SAV_HP_KI_06)

Additionally, some local leaders adapted messaging to better align with community beliefs, ensuring that COVID-19 prevention messages were culturally appropriate. The Bilpella Sub-metro coordinator in the Tamale metro stated:

… We were using the communities to make announcements through “gongon beatings”, there were times we had to give logistics to the community, and the chief would announce that through “gongon beatings” …. (NR_TAM_CL_KI_03)

The Northern region’s use of local structures allowed for a higher level of participation, representing a ‘co-producing’ level, as the community played an active role in shaping how messages were framed and disseminated. This approach was described as making messages more relatable and actionable for community members.

Risk communication

The approach to risk communication varied across the two regions, shaping levels of public compliance and trust in government-led directives. In the Ashanti region, the majority of the respondents reported that risk communication was predominantly state-led, with health professionals and government officials disseminating information through mass media channels.

A Hhealth Official in Ejisu explained:

We followed national guidelines strictly and communicated the risks through official channels like health directorate announcements and media broadcasts. (ASH_EJS_HP_KI_02)

This was further supported by the Metro Disease Control Officer in Kumasi who noted that:

… a lot of risk communications initiatives were national initiatives, they were decisions that were taken by the Ministry and Ghana health service and we had no option other than to comply …. (ASH_KSI_DC_KI_01)

Although these efforts ensured a broad reach, they remained largely unidirectional, with limited community feedback or engagement mechanisms. This ‘informing’ level of engagement ensured message consistency but limited community input. Traditional leaders were informed about risk communication efforts but were not actively involved in shaping messaging.

In contrast, risk communication in the Northern region was reported to be more personalised and community-driven, with local leaders, including chiefs and assembly members, actively facilitating community discussions about risks.

The assembly member of a community in the Northern region shared:

I personally met with community members to explain the risks and importance of adhering to COVID-19 protocols, which helped in gaining their trust and compliance. (NR_SAV_CL_KI_04)

A community chief in Nalun, in the Bilpiella sub-metro of Tamale, recounted:

… We, as community leaders, also insisted on people taking the education seriously for their own good. Even with that, I made announcements to the whole community to gather for the health workers to brief them about the effects of COVID and possible ways to avoid it …. (NR_TAM_CL_KI_04)

This represents a ‘co-producing’ level of engagement, as community leaders not only relayed information but also helped adapt messages to local concerns, increasing trust in the information provided.

Surveillance and contact tracing

Surveillance and contact tracing in the Ashanti region were primarily handled by government health authorities, with support from local health workers. Health workers were trained and equipped to carry out contact tracing efficiently, a process that involved the use of technology and formal reporting mechanisms.

A health official in Kumasi mentioned:

… We used a systematic approach to contact tracing, relying on trained personnel and established protocols to track and manage cases …. (ASH_KSI_DC_KI_01)

The Subin sub-metro coordinator in Kumasi also explained:

… During the COVID 19COVID-19 pandemic we were doing a contact tracing. then coordinating testing effort and ensuring compliance with quarantine and isolation protocols. We also collaborated with health professionals to control the spread of the disease …… (ASH_KSI_MC_KI_03)

This approach aligns with a ‘consulting’ level of engagement, where communities played a passive role in implementing rather than shaping the process.

In the Northern region, surveillance and contact tracing were largely community-based, relying on CBSVs and local knowledge. A volunteer from the Moaglaa community in the Northern region noted:

We moved from house to house, using our knowledge of the community to trace contacts and report any suspected cases. (NR_SAV_CL_KI_05)

The Bilpella Sub-metro coordinator in the Tamale metro stated th:

… every community with its dynamics, so you look at that community and use the structures there, for instance, communities with active community volunteers were able to mobilize the people, report suspected cases and encourage uptake of directives …. (NR_TAM_CL_KI_03)

This reflects a ‘co-producing’ level of engagement, where communities actively contributed to implementing surveillance strategies, leading to stronger adherence to public health measures.

Community entry and trust-building

Community entry and trust-building were crucial for public health acceptance. Approaches varied between Ashanti and Northern Ghana, influenced by different governance and social structures. In Ashanti, community engagement relied heavily on official government channels, with health professionals adding credibility. However, this made the process more formal and bureaucratic, often prioritising health directives over genuine trust-building, reflecting a ‘consulting’ level of engagement.

A health official in the Ashanti region remarked:

We focused on delivering the message and ensuring that the health directives were followed, with community entry being handled by health professionals. (ASH_KSI_HP_KI_02)

In contrast, trust-building in the Northern region was reported by the majority of the participants to be deeply rooted and embedded in the involvement of local leaders and traditional structures. The entry into communities was often facilitated by chiefs and assembly members, who used their influence and relationships to foster trust and cooperation. Chiefs served as the main intermediaries between health officials and the public, ensuring that interventions were presented in ways that aligned with cultural norms and local priorities. This approach was more relational, focusing on gaining community buy-in before implementing health measures. A community chief stated:

We worked closely with the health workers to ensure that they had our support, which made the community more receptive to their messages. (NR_SAV_CL_KI_06)

A health promotion officer in the Northern region stated:

… We used persons that were deemed influential. Because they have the “magaziyas” (Women leaders) within their settlement, then you have to use them because the “magaziyas” in most of the communities are influential persons, then you will have to involve such persons to gain community access …. (NR_SAV_HP_KI_02)

This represents a ‘co-producing’ level of engagement, as community leaders played a central role in shaping public health engagement strategies rather than simply communicating directives.

Provision and administration of logistics

In the Ashanti region, logistics such as personal protective equipment (PPE), sanitisers and vaccines were reported by the majority of the participants to be centrally managed and distributed through government channels. The process was regarded as being more efficient, with transparent supply chains ensuring that resources reached the targeted populations. While community leaders were occasionally consulted on priority areas for distribution, they had no decision-making power, making this an ‘informing’ level of engagement. A participant from the Ashanti region noted:

Logistics like PPE and sanitizers were distributed through formal channels, and we had adequate supplies to meet the demand. (ASH-KSI_KY_FG-05)

In contrast, the Northern region, according to the participants, faced more challenges in logistics provision, leading to a greater reliance on community-generated resources, especially in rural communities. The scarcity of central-level supplies necessitated local innovations, such as creating homemade handwashing stations and producing local masks. Some communities established their own local supply networks, particularly for mask production using locally available fabrics and food redistribution among vulnerable groups. A community chief of Moaglaa highlighted:

We created our own locally made handwashing equipment and distributed them among the community because we didn’t have enough formal supplies. (NR_SAV_CL_KI_06)

A focus group participant from the Northern region noted:

… when it came (COVID-19), we, the members of Ghana Blind Union, mobilised some money and sewed nose masks, and we distributed them to our members and members of other disability groups, even before other support from the government came to us. (NR_TML_DG_FG_03)

Given that communities had initiated and managed resource distribution independently, this represents a ‘co-production’ and to some extent, an ‘empowering’, level of engagement, as communities not only received logistical support but also played a role in adapting and managing their own distribution efforts.

Community involvement in planning interventions

Community involvement in planning interventions in the Ashanti region was somewhat limited, with most initiatives being driven by the health directorate and government agencies. While community leaders were engaged, the planning process and decision-making were largely directed by formal health structures. A health official in Kumasi mentioned:

We engaged the community leaders in the rollout of the interventions, but the planning and execution were mainly handled by the health directorate. (ASH_KSI_SM_KI_03)

Here, a ‘consulting’ level of engagement is most aligned given that community members had little direct influence over intervention design.

In contrast, the Northern region exhibited a more inclusive approach to planning interventions, with significant input from community leaders and members. The planning was often collaborative, with local leaders playing a crucial role in shaping the strategies to ensure they were culturally appropriate and effective. The assembly Member of the community in Savelugu noted:

The community leaders worked as a team with health service officials to achieve the targeted goal, and our input was crucial in the planning process. During vaccination community leaders help to identify the various community groups such as women and youth groups and the most appropriate time to be able to meet them to ensure good vaccine coverages. (NR_SAV_CL_KI_O4)

Another participant notedt:

Yes, you know we also have the youth groups such as the “Ataya base groups” in some of the communities and also the community health management committee that supports the health facilities, especially in the vaccination drive …. (NR_TAM_CL_KI_O5)

This represents a ‘co-producing’ to ‘empowering’ transition, as community members took a level of leadership in response planning.

Discussion

This study examined community engagement strategies during the COVID-19 response in the Ashanti and Northern regions of Ghana, with a focus on SBCC, risk communication, community trust building, surveillance and logistics. Although both regions used similar strategies, their approaches differed significantly due to socio-cultural, governance and infrastructural contexts.

In both regions, SBCC was a cornerstone of engagement, but delivery channels varied. In Ashanti, mass media channels like television, radio and print were predominantly used, benefiting from the region’s higher urbanisation and media access. This approach allowed for quick dissemination but limited two-way communication.12 The approach enabled quick dissemination but limited two-way communication. In contrast, the Northern region used interpersonal channels, with community health volunteers and local leaders promoting face-to-face interactions, which better addressed cultural nuances. Similar trends were observed in rural Uganda, where interpersonal SBCC fostered trust and improved health behaviour uptake, unlike in urban areas dominated by mass media.13 The reliance on community-based channels in Northern Ghana is reinforced by other health initiatives, such as SPRING’s programme aimed at improving nutrition which effectively employed community engagement strategies.14

Risk communication in Ashanti relied on a health-system-led model, focusing on official press briefings. In the Northern region, local leaders worked with health officials to tailor messages to community concerns and misinformation. This approach aligns with findings from the Ebola outbreak in Liberia and Sierra Leone, where using respected local figures enhanced communication effectiveness.11 15 The structured approach in Ashanti aligns with traditional top-down communication models, while the community-driven approach in the Northern region resonates with community-based participatory models.16,19 The differences in these strategies emphasise the need for adaptable and context-specific risk communication methods in public health initiatives.

Different strategies emphasise the need for context-specific communication in public health. Urban areas may benefit from a top-down approach, while rural regions often require community-driven methods. In the Northern region, community entry was more authority-driven, involving consultations with leaders, which fostered legitimacy and compliance. In contrast, Ashanti relied on health and local government officials for entry. Both methods were effective, but the Northern region’s approach aligns with anthropological insights that culturally embedded processes promote long-term cooperation in rural West Africa.20

Ashanti leveraged a more formalised surveillance network, supported by electronic data reporting and trained health workers. In the view of Palmer and Duclos, the economic realities of urban life make it challenging for individuals to engage in unpaid volunteer work, as the opportunity cost of time spent volunteering is higher.21 The Northern region relied more on CBSVs who had pre-existing relationships within their communities. Comparable findings from rural Ethiopia during COVID-19 showed that volunteer-led surveillance can be highly effective where formal systems are limited.22 23 This is also consistent with evidence from the Ebola outbreak in Sierra Leone, Liberia and Rwanda, where empowering traditional leaders improved trust and intervention uptake.24 25 However, in urban South Africa, highly centralised responses were effective in rapid message dissemination but encountered challenges in sustaining long-term community ownership.26

Logistical support, including PPE, handwashing stations and transport, was more consistently available in Ashanti due to better infrastructure and supply chain access. In the Northern region, logistical provision was less predictable, leading to interruptions in engagement activities. Studies from other low-resource settings, such as rural Malawi, have similarly noted that the irregular supply of logistics undermines community trust and continuity of interventions.27 28

Limitations

This study has some limitations. Recall bias may have affected participants’ memories of community engagement strategies during the COVID-19 response, and social desirability bias could have influenced their interview answers. To address these issues, triangulation was used by comparing interviews with policy documents and pandemic reports. Additionally, findings are specific to the urban Ashanti and rural Northern regions of Ghana, which may limit generalisability, but this diversity allowed for a broader exploration of community engagement approaches.

Despite these limitations, the study’s rigorous qualitative approach, use of multiple data sources and regional diversity contribute to the validity and relevance of the findings.

Conclusion

Our study highlights how community engagement strategies vary based on governance structures and socio-cultural contexts, reinforcing the need for regionally adapted models in public health emergencies. While centralised approaches in the Ashanti region facilitated coordination, the Northern region’s community-led strategies fostered greater trust and participation. This study offers a comparative perspective on engagement strategies for public health interventions beyond Ghana. We recommend that responses to public health emergencies be tailored to the socio-cultural and governance contexts of communities. In areas with strong traditional authority, local leaders should be empowered to co-produce interventions. In urban or well-resourced regions, centralised messaging should include structured opportunities for community feedback.
