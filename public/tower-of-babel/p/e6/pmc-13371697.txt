Mapping barley leaf stripe resistence in Tibetan hulless barley based on BSR-seq and Iso-seq
Authors: Hongyan Li, Xue Yang, Youhua Yao, Xiaohua Yao, Kunlun Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBarley leaf stripe (BLS) is a common fungal disease caused by P yrenophora graminea in hulless barley (Qingke). Once leaf stripe occurs, it will seriously affect the yield and quality of Qingke.ResultsThis study utilized the disease-resistant variety Kunlun14 (KL14) and the susceptible variety Z1141 as materials to analyze the changes in the disease index, soluble protein content, relative conductivity, proline (Pro) content, malondialdehyde (MDA) content, and chlorophyll content (CC) in leaves before and after infection with barley leaf stripe (BLS), which is caused by P. graminea. After infection, both the disease incidence and severity in the Z1141 group were significantly higher than those in the KL14 group. BSR-seq analysis was performed on the resistant and susceptible bulks derived from the parents and the F2 population (Z1141 × KL14), and three candidate regions were identified: one locus (648,449,784 base pairs (bp)) and one interval (659.7–668.0 Mb) on chromosome 5H, and one interval (12.1–16.3 Mb) on chromosome 6H. A total of 3,126 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified by Isoform sequencing (Iso-seq) using leaves of KL14 and Z1141 before and after P. graminea infection. An integrated analysis of BSR-seq and Iso-seq data, supplemented by qRT-PCR validation and functional annotation, pinpointed HvWAK and HvRGA as candidate genes for BLS resistance. Expression analysis demonstrated that the expression levels of both genes peaked at the week seven following P. graminea infection, and the timing of transcriptional activation in Z1141 preceded that in KL14.ConclusionThis study analyzed the resistance mechanisms of Qingke against P. graminea through both physiological response analysis and identification of disease resistance genes. The results of this study not only contribute to a deeper understanding of plant-pathogen interaction mechanisms but also establish a valuable genetic resource base for molecular marker-assisted breeding of BLS-resistant Qingke and the functional characterization of the key candidate genes HvWAK and HvRGA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12947-w.Keywords: P. graminea, Multi-omics analysis, HvWAK, HvRGA

Introduction

Qingke (Hordeum vulgare L. var. nudum Hook. f.), a variety of cultivated barley (H. vulgare), is characterized by the separation of the glume from the grain at maturity [1]. As the fourth largest cereal crop, H. vulgare production is frequently challenged by various pathogens, including airborne, seed-borne, and soil-borne diseases, resulting in substantial agricultural losses [2]. Among them, infection by fungal pathogens is one of the most destructive threats to its annual yield, often severely damaging the leaves [3, 4]. Barley leaf stripe (BLS) is a seed-borne systemic fungal disease caused by P yrenophora graminea, which can infect H. vulgare during the seed germination stage [5–7]. In the Qinghai region, the low-temperature and humid climatic conditions are conducive to the occurrence of this disease, resulting in an average annual yield loss of approximately 10%, with losses reaching up to 25% in severely affected fields [7]. Plant resistance to pathogens involves processes such as signal transduction, redox reactions, and metabolic regulation. Changes in the expression of related genes can lead to alterations in protein products, thereby enhancing plant disease resistance [8]. However, P. graminea possesses a remarkable capacity for genetic variation, leading to the gradual loss of resistance in many H. vulgare germplasm resources [9].

Although progress has been made in research on plant disease resistance mechanisms, consensus has not yet been reached on issues such as whether resistance genes are specific among different species [6]. Meanwhile, the continuous evolution of pathogenic bacteria often renders disease resistance ineffective [10]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to identify new disease-resistance genes for use in plant breeding [10].

Previous studies have identified relevant resistance genes through the analysis of segregating populations derived from crosses between resistant and susceptible barley varieties, such as the gene Rdg1a identified in H. spontaneum [11], and the gene Rdg2a identified in the telomeric region of chromosome 7HS in the H. vulgare variety “Thibaut” [12, 13]. In addition, wall-associated kinase (WAK) [14] and resistance gene analogues (RGAs) are related to plant resistance to diseases [15]. For example, TaWAK2A-800 in Triticum aestivum is involved in resistance to Fusarium head blight (FHB) and sharp eyespot [16], and WAK-like proteins in Arabidopsis are capable of sensing cell wall status and regulating defense responses [17]. However, detailed studies on them are still lacking in H. vulgare [18]. RGAs are involved in both quantitative and qualitative disease resistance [19]. Therefore, identifying candidate RGAs associated with disease resistance is of great significance for understanding disease mechanisms and advancing resistance breeding [19]. In the breeding process, the screening of RGAs has also emerged as a key approach to reducing crop disease susceptibility [20]. With the development of high-throughput sequencing and analytical technologies, RGAs have been identified in various crops. For example, Amas et al. [21] identified 138 candidate RGAs within quantitative trait loci (QTL) regions associated with disease resistance in Brassica rapa, and related studies have also been conducted in species such as B. nigra, Sinapis arvensis, and S. alba [20, 22]. However, systematic studies on RGAs and WAK-related genes associated with leaf stripe disease in Qingke are still relatively scarce.

In recent years, BLS has occurred frequently in Qingke growing regions, posing a serious threat to its yield and quality [23]. However, the discovery of related resistance genes remains insufficient, and traditional gene mapping methods face certain limitations. BSR-seq is a method that combines bulked segregant analysis (BSA) with transcriptome sequencing, enabling the simultaneous identification of single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) loci and DEGs [24]. It has been successfully applied in the discovery of disease resistance genes in crops such as Pisum sativum [25] and H. vulgare [26]. Therefore, in this study, BSR-seq technology was used to perform genetic mapping in an F2 segregating population derived from disease-resistant and susceptible varieties. By combining Iso-seq for full-length transcript validation and expression pattern analysis of candidate genes, this approach enables systematic screening of candidate genes associated with BLS resistance, providing a theoretical basis for further elucidating the resistance mechanism and for resistance breeding.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and treatment

The experimental materials include disease-resistant varieties: Kunlun14 (KL14), Kunlun13 (KL13), Kunlun12 (KL12), and THIBAUT, moderately disease-resistant variety: Z1140, susceptible variety: Z1141. These materials were provided by the Institute of Crop Breeding and Cultivation, Qinghai Academy of Agriculture and Forestry Sciences.

Using Z1141 as the female parent and KL14 as the male parent, an F2 population consisting of 300 individual plants was constructed. The two parents exhibited marked differences in disease symptom phenotypes: the infected leaves of KL14 exhibit thin light yellow stripes or discontinuous stripes, while the infected leaves of Z1141 have yellow–brown stripes in the middle and edges, and some leaves exhibit brown, dry lesions (detailed data are available in the reference [27]). Based on indicators such as the severity of stripe disease, color intensity, and infection area, the F2 population was classified into four phenotypic variety types: highly resistant, moderately resistant, susceptible, and highly susceptible. Leaves were collected from plants with extreme phenotypes, including 30 highly disease-resistant and 30 highly susceptible individuals, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C for subsequent BSR-seq analysis. Seeds of the tested varieties were infected with P. graminea.

The establishment of the BLS pathogen infection system required sequential steps including isolation, purification, infection, transplantation, and disease development, with the entire process taking approximately 40–45 days. Subsequently, the control group (non-infected) and the treatment group (infected) were placed in an artificial climate incubator for cultivation (14 h of daylight at 20℃ and 10 h of darkness at 10℃). Sampling commenced at six weeks post-infection, corresponding to the onset of the disease, and continued until 10 weeks post-infection. Normal and diseased parts of the second leaf from the bottom were collected from both the control group and the treatment group, respectively. Each sample had at least three biological replicates. Detailed methodological references are provided in the cited literature [27]. Leaves of different varieties were collected at six-10 weeks post-infection (with non-infected leaves serving as controls) and preserved for subsequent qRT-PCR analysis. In addition, leaves of the two varieties Z1141 and KL14 were collected at six-eight weeks post-infection for Iso-seq analysis, with non-infected leaves serving as controls. The sequencing was performed by Biomarker Technologies Co., Ltd.

Physiological index detection and data analysis

The incidence area of Qingke leaves was scanned and calculated by the kernel and needle image analysis system (Regent Instruments Inc), and the incidence severity was expressed by the proportion of the infected area in the total area of leaves of different varieties. Total protein quantification kit A045-3-2 (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute) was used to determine the content of soluble protein. Total protein concentration (μg/mL) = determination (OD value—blank OD value)/(Standard OD value-blank OD value) × standard concentration (563 g/mL) × dilution before determination. The relative electrical conductivity was measured using a conductivity meter. The relative electrical conductivity = (conductivity value after soaking/conductivity value after boiling) × 100%. Determined by Proline (Pro) kit A107-1-1 (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute) was used to determine the content of Pro. Pro content (μg/g tissue wet weight) = (measured OD value-blank OD value)/(Standard OD value-blank OD value) × standard concentration (5 μg/mL)/homogenate concentration (g tissue wet weight/mL) × sample dilution multiple before this test. Plant malondialdehyde (MDA) kit A003-3-1 (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute) was used to determine the content of MDA. MDA content (nmol/g) = (measured OD value-blank OD value)/(Standard OD value-blank OD value) × standard product concentration (10 nmol/mL)/sample concentration (g/mL). Chlorophyll content (CC) was measured using a SPAD-502 chlorophyll meter between 11:00 am and 12:00 pm after disease onset (i.e., six weeks post-infection). During measurement, the clamping probe of the instrument was vertically clamped onto the leaf blade, avoiding the main vein. Measurements were performed every five days until the 8 th week. For each leaf, three measurements were taken at the upper, middle, and lower parts, with three repeated readings at each position, and the average value was finally calculated.

Preliminary mapping of barley leaf stripe gene based on BSR-seq

RNA was extracted from the leaves of the parents and the extreme mixed pool samples (leaves from 30 highly disease-resistant individuals and 30 highly susceptible individuals), and after quality assessment, sequencing libraries were constructed. Eukaryotic mRNA was firstly enriched, and the mRNA was randomly interrupted by adding fragmentation buffer, and then reverse transcribed into cDNA using the mRNA of KL14, Z1141, and the extreme mixing pool as templates, and then modified and processed to obtain the cDNA library. Sequencing was performed using the Illumina HiSeq sequencing platform, strict filtering of sequencing data (original reads): reads with adapters were excluded; For single-end reads, those with an N content exceeding 10% of the fragment length, or with a number of low-quality bases (Q ≤ 5) exceeding 50% of the read length, were removed. Ultimately, high-quality reads were generated. Use the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA) software will either parent and offspring of the clean data matching end reading matching with the reference genome (ftp://ftp.ensemblgenomes.org/pub/release-36/plants/fasta/hordeum_vulgare/dna/), the result is converted to a Sequence/Binary Alignment Map (SAM/BAM) file. Perl scripts are used to calculate match rates and coverage. SNP detection was performed using GATK software package. To eliminate false positive loci, the SNPNUM method was used to fit the Euclidean Distance (ED) values and the ΔSNP-index based on the genomic positions of the markers.

The 99 th percentile of the fitted data was used as the association threshold, and the intersection of the results from the two association analysis methods (ED and SNP-index) was taken to obtain the candidate regions. Functional enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) within the candidate regions was performed using Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG).

Screening of barley leaf stripe genes based on Iso-seq

Total RNA was extracted from the leaves of KL14 and Z1141. The purity, concentration, and integrity were quantified using an ultra-micro nucleic acid analyzer (IMPLEN, NanoPhotometer, Germany). cDNA libraries were prepared from 1 µg of total RNA using the protocol supplied by Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT). SuperScript IV First-Strand Synthesis System (Invitrogen) was used for full length mRNA reverse transcription and following cDNA PCR for 14 circles with LongAmp Tag (NEB). The PCR products were then subjected to FFPE DNA repair and end-repair (NEB) steps and following adaptor ligation using T4 DNA ligase (NEB). Agencourt XP beads was used for DNA purification according to ONT protocol. The final cDNA libraries were added to FLO-MIN109 flowcells and were run on PromethION platform at Biomarker Technology Company (Beijing, China). Data format conversion was performed using the Guppy software within the MinKNOW 2.2 software package for quality control analysis. The alternative splicing types present in each sample were identified using Astalavista software [28]. The RNASeqPower value (Table S1) was provided by Biomarker Technology Company. Full length reads were mapped to the reference transcriptome sequence. Reads with match quality above five were further used to quantify. Expression levels were estimated by reads per gene/transcript per 10,000 reads mapped. Differential expression analysis of diverse groups was performed using the DESeq R package (1.18.0). The resulting p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini and Hochberg’s approach for controlling the false discovery rate (FDR). Genes with a FDR false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.01 and foldchange ≥ 2 found by DESeq were assigned as differentially expressed. Perform GO and KEGG enrichment analyses on candidate genes, the number distribution of DEGs was visualized using Venn diagrams, and gene expression levels were visualized using violin plots.

Barley leaf stripe related gene screening

This study employed an integrative analysis strategy, combining BSR-seq and Iso-seq data, to preliminarily identify candidate genes for barley leaf stripe disease. Subsequently, the full-length transcripts of these genes were obtained using Iso-seq technology. Through systematic functional annotation and gene expression level analysis, reliable resistance-related genes were ultimately screened out.

Expression pattern analysis of the candidate gene

Leaves from two parents and different varieties under P. graminea infection or control treatment were collected for RNA extraction (RNA Extraction Kit (TaKaRa, Beijing, China)). RNA concentration and purity were determined using an ultra-micro nucleic acid and protein measurement instrument (NanoPhotometer, Germany), and RNA quality was assessed by 1.0% agarose gel electrophoresis. cDNA Synthesis Kit (TaKaRa, Beijing, China) was used to reverse transcribe RNA into cDNA. qRT-PCR was performed for three biological replicates and three technical replicates.

The gene sequences were retrieved from the barley reference genome, and the qRT-PCR primers were designed using Primer5 software (Table S2). cDNA templates derived from leaves of various Qingke varieties were amplified, with 18S rRNA serving as the internal reference gene, and TB green premix Exaq II was used as a fluorescent dye. The Light Cycler 480 II System (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Germany) (40 cycles) was utilized to perform qRT-PCR. The reaction system was based on reference [29], and the expression level of the target gene was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Three biological replicates and three technical replicates were set up for each sample. Data processing and visualization analysis were performed using Excel 2019 and TBtools.

Results

The severity of leaf infection by P. graminea in different Qingke varieties

The leaves of different Qingke varieties exhibited significant differences in resistance to P. graminea. After infection with P. graminea, the disease-resistant variety KL14 only developed light yellow or intermittent streaks on its leaves, whereas the susceptible variety Z1141 showed distinct yellowish-brown streaks along the midrib and leaf margins, with severe infections leading to extensive necrosis. Consistent with the phenotypic observations, statistical analysis revealed that the disease index of Z1141 was significantly higher than that of KL14, approximately four times greater than the latter (published [27]).

Effects of P. graminea infection on physiological indices of parental leaves

Protein solubility plays a pivotal role in determining the physicochemical properties, sensory attributes, and nutritional characteristics of foods formulated with protein-rich ingredients [30]. The soluble protein content of the disease-resistant variety KL14 significantly increased by 19.35% compared to the control group (non-infected) (p < 0.05). In contrast, the soluble protein content of the susceptible variety Z1141 significantly decreased by 37.14% (p < 0.05) (Table S3). As a physiological parameter reflecting the degree of damage to plant cell membranes under stress conditions, relative conductivity is also widely used to evaluate plant disease resistance [31]. The results showed that after infection with P. graminea, the relative conductivity of KL14 increased significantly by 28.08% compared with the control group (non-infected). Moreover, the relative electrical conductivity of Z1141 was significantly higher than that of KL14 (p < 0.05). Furthermore, the Pro contents in KL14 and Z1141 were 6.16-fold and 11.03-fold higher than those in the control group (non-infected), respectively, with the proline content in Z1141 being significantly higher than that in KL14 (p < 0.05). After infection with P. graminea, the MDA contents in KL14 and Z1141 significantly increased by 35.40% and 45.13%, respectively, and the MDA content in Z1141 was significantly higher than that in KL14 (p < 0.05). These results further reveal the differences in physiological responses among different varieties under stress conditions. In conclusion, during the infection of Qingke by P. graminea, the changes in key physiological indicators in leaves, including soluble protein, relative electrical conductivity, Pro, and MDA, play a crucial regulatory role.

Furthermore, the CC in the leaves of different Qingke varieties differed significantly after P. graminea infection. Specifically, as the growth period progressed, the CC of both varieties (KL14 and Z1141) showed a decreasing trend after P. graminea infection. Compared with the elongation stage, the CC values of KL14 and Z1141 at the heading stage exhibited significant decreases of 60.74% and 74.28%, respectively (p < 0.05) (Table S3). In the control group (non-infected), the CC of KL14 decreased by 10.17% from the elongation stage to the heading period, while the CC of Z1141 increased by 6.11%. Overall, the reduction in CC was significantly greater in Z1141 than in KL14, indicating that the photosynthetic capacity of the susceptible variety was more severely impaired during disease development.

Mapping of barley leaf stripe gene based on BSR-seq

BSR-seq data quality analysis

The parental pool and extreme phenotype mixed pool were constructed with F2 generation. The male parent pool, female parent pool, susceptible mixed pool, and disease-resistant mixed pool were KL14 (T01), Z1141 (T02), T03, and T04, respectively (Table S4). To identify BLS-related mRNA in Qingke, BSR-seq was performed on the cDNA of T01, T02, T03, and T04 leaves. After strict quality control, a total of 37,685,305, 42,797,703, 60,056,909, and 51,405,772 raw reads were generated from T01, T02, T03, and T04, respectively. There were 35,557,718, 36,190,877, 59,486,222, and 50,823,550 clean reads in the T01, T02, T03, and T04, respectively. The Q30 quality scores of all four libraries exceeded 93.62%, and the average mapping efficiency to the reference genome was above 71.72%, meeting the requirements for subsequent BSR-seq analysis (Table S4).

High quality SNP screening and analysis

Through high-throughput sequencing analysis, a total of 60,735 SNP sites were identified. Among these, 42,111 SNPs had read support < 4, 13,926 SNPs showed identical genotypes between the mixed pools, and 1,960 SNPs were parental-specific loci. Following rigorous quality control filtering, a total of 2,738 high-quality SNPs were ultimately obtained for comparative analysis between sample groups exhibiting significant differences (Table S5).

Further analysis revealed 23 non-synonymous mutation SNPs between the disease-resistant parent KL14 and the susceptible parent Z1141, involving 13 functionally annotated genes, as well as 11 non-synonymous mutation SNPs between the disease-resistant pool and the susceptible pool, involving seven annotated genes. Notably, the two comparative analyses jointly identified six key genes carrying non-synonymous mutations, including the WD domain protein gene (ID: HORVU5Hr1G122810) associated with stress resistance and the NB-ARC domain protein genes (ID: HORVU5Hr1G122180, HORVU5Hr1G122660) involved in disease response (Table S6). The proteins encoded by these genes may be directly or indirectly involved in the resistance response of Qingke to BLS via the modulation of signal transduction pathways or stress resistance mechanisms.

Identification of DEGs related to barley leaf stripe in Qingke

To identify mRNAs associated with BLS in Qingke, this study conducted a systematic comparative transcriptomic analysis of parental pools (T01 vs T02) and extreme phenotype bulked pools (T04 vs T03) (Fig. 1). The results demonstrated that a total of 4,522 DEGs were identified between parental pools, including 1,956 upregulated and 2,566 downregulated genes (Fig. 1A). Comparative analysis of extreme bulked pools revealed 2,560 DEGs, including 1,897 upregulated and 663 downregulated genes (Fig. 1B). Further Venn analysis of the differential genes from the two comparison groups identified a total of 6,083 DEGs (including 759 novel genes). After removing redundant transcripts, 999 DEGs were ultimately screened. Notably, the parental varieties exhibited a richer array of defense response-related genes after infection with P. graminea (Fig. 1C). This likely reflects their homozygous genetic backgrounds conferring extreme phenotypic traits. In contrast, although the extreme mixing pools also displayed extreme phenotypes, the genetic heterogeneity introduced by sample pooling likely diluted inter-individual variations in gene expression, thereby reducing the detectable number of DEGs.

Screening of candidate regions

The ED algorithm utilizes sequencing data to identify significantly differentiated markers between bulked pools, thereby evaluating genetic regions associated with traits [32].

The SNP-index method performs association analysis by comparing genotypic frequency differences between mixed pools, using Δ(SNP-index) as the statistical metric [32]. By taking the intersection of the results obtained from these two association analysis methods, this study identified a total of three significantly associated regions: the two intervals are located on chromosome 5H (648,449,784 base pairs (bp), 659,697,400 bp-668,009,772 bp), spanning 8.31 Mb and contains 1,203 candidate genes; the other interval is located on chromosome 6H, spanning from 12,058,310 bp to 16,256,062 bp, covering 4.19 Mb and contains 476 candidate genes. The total length of these intervals is 12.5 Mb, containing a total of 1,679 genes. After removing different transcripts of the same gene, 310 unique genes were retained (Fig. 2) (Table S7).

GO and KEGG enrichment analysis of candidate region gene

To elucidate the biological functions of the 310 candidate genes, GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were performed. The GO analysis revealed that these genes were significantly enriched in three major categories: biological processes, cell components, and molecular functions. At the molecular function level, these genes were primarily associated with protein heterodimerization activity (17 genes) and nitrate transmembrane transporter activity (five genes). In terms of biological processes, they showed significant enrichment in nitrate transport (five genes) (Fig. 3A; Table S8). KEGG analysis identified nitrogen metabolism as the most significantly enriched pathway (nine genes) (Fig. 3B; Table S9). Notably, KEGG analysis identified a key gene (ID: HORVU5Hr1G121530) (Table S9) annotated to the plant-pathogen interaction pathway (ko04626), which encodes RPM1-interacting protein 4 (RIN4) (Table S10). This finding provides a crucial molecular target for both the screening of BLS-resistant genes and functional genomic studies in Qingke.

Screening of genes associated with barley leaf stripe based on Iso-seq

Correlation analysis of expression levels among samples

The gene expression correlations across samples from different treatment conditions were systematically examined employing Iso-seq technology. Significant positive correlations were observed between P. graminea-infected samples (KL14S and ZS), as well as between non-infected control samples (KL14N and ZN). In contrast, the correlations between infected and non-infected groups were significantly weaker (Fig. S1). Genome-wide distribution analysis revealed that 2,534 structurally optimized genes exhibited a significant non-random distribution across seven chromosomes (Fig. 4). Specifically, chromosome 2 has the highest gene distribution density, with 415 genes accounting for 16.38% of the total, while chromosome 4 has the lowest density, with 282 genes making up 11.13%. The numbers of genes on chromosomes 1, 3, 5, 6, and 7 are 344, 362, 395, 338, and 398, respectively.

Evaluation of Iso-seq data

After stringent data filtering, 8,266,149, 7,769,734, 7,830,032, and 8,348,577 reads were obtained for the four samples KL14N, KL14S, ZN, and ZS, respectively, corresponding to total bases of 6,817,015,389, 6,936,647,563, 7,836,582,028, and 7,816,911,781, respectively. After further quality control, the clean reads for each sample were 8,235,369, 7,727,732, 7,799,005, and 8,314,410, respectively. By aligning to the barley reference genome, the numbers of successfully aligned reads for KL14N, KL14S, ZN, and ZS were 8,183,194, 6,963,990, 7,734,771, and 7,594,932, respectively, with average alignment rates of 99.37%, 90.01%, 99.16%, and 91.33% (Table S11). The results indicate that, except for the slightly lower alignment rates in the KL14S and ZS samples, the genome alignment efficiency for the remaining samples exceeded 99%, confirming the effectiveness of data preprocessing and the suitability of the reference genome.

Analysis of DEGs reveals mRNAs associated with barley leaf stripe resistance

To identify BLS-related mRNAs, the DEGs between infected and non-infected plants within each variety were analyzed (Fig. 5). KL14 exhibited 2,740 DEGs (KL14S vs KL14N), with 1,411 upregulated and 1,329 downregulated genes (Fig. 5A). Z1141 showed 871 DEGs (ZS vs ZN), comprising 585 upregulated and 286 downregulated genes (Fig. 5B). Further comparison of transcriptomic changes before and after P. graminea infection between the two varieties revealed a total of 3,126 genes, with the number of genes involved in the disease resistance response was significantly higher in KL14 than in Z1141 (Fig. 5C). Based on the violin plot and expression clustering analysis (Fig. 5D), the gene expression patterns of the four samples can be classified into two categories: the non-infected group (KL14N and ZN) exhibited similar expression profiles, with a relatively dispersed data distribution clustered together; whereas the infected group (KL14S and ZS) displayed similar expression profiles, with a more concentrated data distribution clustered in another category. This result indicates that, when not infected by P. graminea, the leaf gene expression patterns of the disease-resistant variety KL14 and the susceptible variety Z1141 are relatively similar. However, after infection with P. graminea, they exhibit a distinct expression pattern. It is hypothesized that P. graminea infection may affect the physiological state of leaves, thereby regulating the expression levels of specific genes.

Enrichment analysis of genes related barley leaf stripe

GO is utilized to delineate the functional categories for annotating unigenes and to classify 3,126 transcripts of proteins with known annotations. GO analysis (Fig. 6A) revealed that among the three major categories of biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions, the top 20 significantly enriched GO terms were primarily associated with chloroplast functions. Specifically, these genes were significantly enriched in the chloroplast stroma (GO:0045037, GO:0009570, and GO:0009533), chloroplast envelope (GO:0009941), and photosynthesis, light harvesting in photosystem I (GO:0009768) (Table S12). This finding suggests that infection by P. graminea may primarily affect photosynthesis and the functions of related organelles in plants. KEGG pathway analysis further revealed the metabolic networks and signal transduction pathways in which these genes are involved. The top three metabolic pathways with the highest enrichment degrees were carbon metabolism (ko01200), glutathione metabolism (ko00480), and biosynthesis of amino acids (ko01230) pathways (Fig. 6B; Table S13). These results suggest that infection by P. graminea may affect the plant’s disease resistance response by interfering with its primary metabolic processes, particularly key pathways involved in energy metabolism and redox balance.

Alternative splicing event statistics

Alternative splicing is a key factor driving the high complexity of the transcriptome in multicellular eukaryotes [33]. This process refers to the selective retention or removal of specific exons and introns from the precursor messenger RNA (pre-mRNA) of a single gene through different splicing patterns, thereby generating multiple structurally distinct mature messenger RNA (mRNA) isoforms [34]. Through the selective inclusion or exclusion of exons, alternative splicing significantly increases the complexity of the proteome, finely regulates gene expression, and participates in supporting various biological processes [35].

Five alternative splicing types were identified in the four samples via Iso-seq technology, with intron retention showing the highest proportion and mutually exclusive exon the lowest (Fig. 7). These results indicate that intron retention is the major form of alternative splicing regulation in Qingke leaves.

Screening and expression analysis of barley leaf stripe candidate genes

To further enhance the confidence of candidate gene identification, a total of 26 candidate genes (Table S10) associated with BLS were identified through an intersection analysis of 3,126 DEGs and 310 genes based on BSR-seq and Iso-seq technologies.

Functional annotation of the genes revealed the presence of resistance-related genes such as wall-associated receptor kinase (ID: HORVU6Hr1G005360), LRR receptor-like serine/threonine protein kinase (ID: HORVU5Hr1G122510), and resistance protein RPP13 (ID: HORVU6Hr1G005690), which were hypothesized to be potentially involved in the resistance response of the Qingke BLS.

To further screen 26 genes in the candidate interval, their expression levels were quantitatively examined. The results showed that 20 of the genes were barely expressed in the samples (expression levels approaching zero) and had no value for further analysis, so they were excluded. The expression levels of the remaining six genes (ID: HORVU6Hr1G008550, HORVU5Hr1G123880, HORVU5Hr1G124210, HORVU6Hr1G006860, HORVU5Hr1G121570, and HORVU6Hr1G008640) were shown in Fig. 8. After infection with P. graminea, the expression levels of these genes varied. For genes with low expression levels, it is speculated that they may not function in the leaves, or may be expressed in other tissues following infection by P. graminea. In contrast, the expression level of wall associated receptor kinase 5 (ID: HORVU6Hr1G008550, the most recent version ID: HORVU.MOREX.r3.6HG0545390) was significantly upregulated following infection by P. graminea, the expression levels of KL14 and Z1141 in the treatment group were 3.66 times and 2.00 times that of the control group, respectively (Fig. 8; Table S14). Based on its potential important role in resistance to BLS and its functional annotation (Table S10), the gene was identified as a candidate gene and designated as HvWAK.

Within the candidate interval, four disease resistance genes (ID: HORVU5Hr1G123480, HORVU5Hr1G122470, HORVU5Hr1G121640, and HORVU5Hr1G121530) were identified.

The gene annotation results were RPM1, F-box/FBD/LRR-repeat, putative disease resistance protein At3g14460, RIN4, and other proteins (Table S10) involved in plant stress or disease resistance related proteins, respectively. To further identify related resistance genes that can resist BLS stress, this study analyzed the expression levels of four candidate genes in the leaves of the disease-resistant variety KL14 and susceptible variety Z1141 following infection with P. graminea. Among them, HORVU5Hr1G122470 and HORVU5Hr1G121530 showed no significant change in the expression levels in the leaves of the two varieties after infection with P. graminea, which indicated that these two genes were not sensitive to BLS-responsive Qingke leaves. The expression of disease resistance protein RPM1 (ID: HORVU5Hr1G123480) was significantly upregulated in Z1141, but not in KL14. In contrast, following infection with P. graminea, the expression level of the putative disease resistance protein (ID: HORVU5Hr1G121640, the most recent version ID: HORVU.MOREX.r3.5HG0533920) increased significantly, with the expression of putative disease resistance protein in the leaves of KL14 and Z1141 in the treatment group was 12.40 times and 7.82 times of that in the control group, respectively (Fig. 8). Based on its potential crucial role in resistance to BLS and its functional annotation (Table S10), this gene was designated as HvRGA and identified as a candidate gene.

Expression patterns of HvWAK and HvRGA following P. graminea infection

The expression levels of HvWAK (Fig. 9A-C) and HvRGA (Fig. 9D-F) (Table S15) were analyzed in the leaves of six Qingke varieties (KL12, KL13, KL14, Z1141, 1140, and THIBAUT). The results showed that after infection with P. graminea, the expression levels of both HvWAK and HvRGA genes in the susceptible variety Z1141 were extremely significantly higher than those in the disease-resistant variety KL14 (p < 0.01). The relative expression levels in Z1141 were 1.27 times (Fig. 9A-C) and 5.97 times (Fig. 9D-F) those of KL14, respectively. To further explore the resistance mechanisms of the HvWAK and HvRGA genes in the KL14 and Z1141 varieties, this study analyzed the dynamic expression patterns of these two genes in Qingke leaves during six-10 weeks post-infection with P. graminea. Analysis revealed that the HvWAK (Fig. 9G; Table S16-S17) and HvRGA (Fig. 9H; Table S16) genes exhibited rapidly induced expression in the susceptible variety Z1141, with transcript levels peaking at seven-eight weeks post-infection (early to mid-disease stage). In contrast, the disease-resistant variety KL14 displayed a delayed expression pattern, where maximal gene expression occurred at nine-10 weeks post-infection (mid to late disease stage). Notably, the peak expression of both genes in KL14 was significantly delayed compared to Z1141. In summary, both HvWAK (Fig. 9G) and HvRGA (Fig. 9H) genes exhibited similar temporal expression patterns in the two varieties. In the control group (non-infected), there was no difference in gene expression levels between the two varieties. However, after infection with P. graminea, KL14 reached its peak expression at nine weeks, whereas Z1141 reached its peak at seven weeks (p < 0.05). These distinct temporal expression patterns showed significant correlation with cultivar resistance phenotypes, demonstrating that HvWAK and HvRGA genes play crucial roles in Qingke’s defense response against P. graminea infection.

Discussion

When plants are infected by pathogens, the contents of protein [36], Pro [37], MDA [38], relative conductivity [39], and chlorophyll [40] in the body will change, and these changes are closely related to plant disease resistance. Disease stress can cause peroxidation of plant membrane lipids, seriously damage membrane structure, and lead to changes in relative conductivity and MDA content [41]. The present study revealed that following P. graminea infection, the disease-resistant variety KL14 and susceptible variety Z1141 exhibited significant differences in physiological indicators. The contents of Pro and MDA, as well as the relative conductivity, significantly increased in both varieties, but the increase in KL14 was notably lower than that in Z1141, indicating that the disease-resistant variety could more effectively maintain cell membrane integrity. This result is consistent with the theory of membrane lipid peroxidation [41], which suggests that pathogen infection leads to damage to the membrane system, while disease-resistant variety possess stronger tolerance to oxidative stress. The changes in CC further validate the resistance differences between the varieties.

After infection P. graminea, both varieties exhibited typical leaf yellowing symptoms, with Z1141 showing a higher disease incidence index compared to KL14. Dynamic monitoring revealed that from the elongation stage to the heading period, the CC of KL14T and Z1141T decreased by 60.74% and 74.28%, respectively. This difference may stem from the fact that, during the early stage of P. graminea infection, chlorophyll adapts to environmental stress through biosynthesis. However, as the disease progresses, the pathogen damages the chloroplast structure, leading to accelerated degradation. At this point, the rate of chlorophyll synthesis is significantly lower than the degradation rate. These findings provide important physiological evidence for elucidating the resistance mechanism of Qingke against leaf stripe.

BSR-seq, as a widely applied method for mapping extreme traits, can rapidly identify loci associated with the trait of interest by comparing the differences in SNP and allele frequencies at parental loci between two extreme phenotype pools [24]. In recent years, the BSR-seq technology has gained significant popularity in plant disease resistance research, assisting researchers in uncovering numerous key genes that control plant diseases. For instance, Li et al. [42] mapped the stripe rust resistance gene Yr041133 in T. aestivum to a genetic interval of 1.7 centimorgans (cM) on chromosome arm 7BL using BSR-seq. Zhao et al. [43] identified the stripe rust resistance locus YrCf75 in T. aestivum. Saxesena et al. [44] identified transcripts and SNPs on chromosomes 3B and 5B associated with spot blotch (SB) in T. aestivum. Ma et al. [45] mapped the powdery mildew resistance gene PmYD588 to a 4.2 cM interval on chromosome arm 2B. Zhan et al. [46] used markers of BSR-seq localization region to type T. aestivum powdery mildew genes, and the region was narrowed to 9.68 Mb.

In this study, BSR-seq technology was used to map the F2 population. Combined with ED and ΔSNP-index analyses, three candidate regions associated with BLS were successfully identified. These regions contain multiple SNPs with non-synonymous mutations, primarily involving genes closely associated with disease resistance or pathogen defense, such as RPM1-interacting protein 4 (RIN4), disease resistance protein RPM1, and Wall-associated receptor kinase (Table S10). However, due to the limited sample size of the bulked pools in this study (30 individuals per pool), it was not possible to determine whether the two regions on chromosome 5H are truly independent or belong to a single larger region. Therefore, validation of the candidate regions will require expanding the population size or employing fine-mapping approaches in future studies. Research indicates that NBS-LRR genes responding to powdery mildew may potentially be utilized to enhance the resistance of Vitis vinifera [47]. The Zea mays NBS-LRR gene ZmNBS25 has been reported to enhance disease resistance in both Oryza sativa L. and Arabidopsis [48]. In Arabidopsis, RPM1, a CC-type NLR protein, confers resistance to avrRpm1 or avrB, which are effector proteins expressed in Pseudomonas syringae [49]. As a negative regulator of PAMPs (pathogen-associated molecular patterns)-triggered immunity (PTI), RIN4 can be modified by many different bacterial effector proteins, thereby compromising plant immunity and destabilizing several NB-LRR proteins, including RPM1 (resistance to P. syringae pv. maculicola 1), RPS2 (resistance to P. syringae 2) guard RIN4 [50]. Based on this, it is speculated that the gene loci with SNP mutations may be involved as components in the resistance response mechanism of Qingke to P. graminea.

In recent years, the WAK gene has demonstrated a significant role in the study of plant disease resistance mechanisms [16]. Research has shown that WAK proteins participate in the regulation of plant immune responses and growth and development processes by recognizing oligogalacturonides (OGs), which are damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) derived from cell wall degradation products [51–53]. In O. sativa L., overexpression of OsWAK25 can significantly upregulate the expression of disease resistance-related genes such as PR10, thereby enhancing resistance to Xanthomonas oryzae pv. oryzae (Xoo) and Magnaporthe oryzae [54]. Further research in Arabidopsis has unveiled the expression regulation characteristics of WAK genes: the expression of Wak1 is induced by pathogen infection as well as by salicylic acid (SA) and its analog INA, while Wak2, Wak3, and Wak5 can also be significantly induced by SA or INA [55]. These genes exhibit distinct tissue-specific expression patterns. Specifically, Wak1, Wak3, and Wak5 are predominantly expressed in leaves and stems, Wak4 is specifically expressed in siliques. In contrast, Wak2 is highly expressed in leaves and stems, shows lower expression levels in flowers and siliques, and is only minimally expressed in roots [55]. In this study, the HvWAK gene was primarily expressed in leaves and was significantly induced in both KL14 and Z1141 varieties during the early stage (seven-eight weeks) of P. graminea infection. Notably, overexpression of Arabidopsis WAK1 can enhance resistance to Botrytis cinerea, and this function is regulated by the binding of an extracellular glycine-rich protein in Arabidopsis (AtGRP-3) to the cell wall domain of Wak1 [56, 57]. However, no similar protein interaction phenomenon was detected in Qingke in this study. This discrepancy suggests that species-specific mechanisms of WAK action may exist across different organisms. This study revealed that HvRGA possesses both NB-ARC and PTI-related domains, implying that it may activate immune responses by recognizing pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs/DAMPs) [58, 59].

According to the research report, the RGA5 mutant RGA5HMA5 confers complete resistance to transgenic rice plants against Magnaporthe oryzae strains expressing the non-corresponding effector protein AVR-PikD [60]. Transcriptional analysis revealed that the expression levels of the HvWAK and HvRGA genes were significantly upregulated in Qingke leaves seven-eight weeks after P. graminea infection. Notably, gene induction was observed as early as seven weeks post-infection, suggesting that the pathogen might have breached the basal immune defenses at this stage, thereby triggering a specific immune response. These findings provide important clues for elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying Qingke’s resistance to P. graminea, while also revealing differences in the immune response characteristics among varieties with varying levels of resistance.

Conclusion

In this study, the disease-resistant variety KL14 and susceptible variety Z1141 were used as research materials, and the changes in disease severity, soluble protein content, relative electrical conductivity, Pro content, MDA content, and CC in the leaves of both varieties were systematically examined before and after infection with P. graminea. The results indicated that the disease severity in Z1141 was significantly higher than that in KL14, confirming that the disease-resistant variety possesses stronger physiological regulatory capabilities. Using BSR-seq technology combined with ΔSNP-index analysis, three genomic regions associated with resistance were identified, encompassing a total of 310 genes. This has enriched the understanding of plant-pathogen interaction pathways and provided valuable information for screening BLS resistance genes. The expression level validation indicated that HvWAK and HvRGA are likely important candidate resistance genes, providing valuable genetic resources for future functional validation using techniques such as gene editing or overexpression.
