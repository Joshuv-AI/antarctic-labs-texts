Deciphering subsidence risk after ACDF: a biomechanical study on bone density and endplate thickness
Authors: Guiyu Liu, Jinyang Zhang, Xiangqian Tian, Fasheng He, Xiandong Sun, Liangxiu Xiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) is a well-established surgical procedure for restoring neurological function and reconstructing cervical spine alignment. However, postoperative subsidence remains a common complication, and its underlying mechanisms are not yet fully understood. This study aims to investigate the biomechanical effects of varying bone density and endplate thickness on subsidence risk following ACDF.Materials and methodsA validated finite element model of the cervical spine (C2–C7) was used to construct a postoperative single-level ACDF model. Thirteen endplate thicknesses (0.00–0.40 mm), three bone density conditions (normal, osteopenia, osteoporosis), different vertebral bodies and loads (50–150 N) were simulated to assess their effects on von Mises stress distribution and subsidence risk by multivariate linear regression analysis.ResultsBoth decreased bone density and reduced endplate thickness led to more dispersed stress distribution and increased subsidence risk. Multivariate regression identified endplate thickness as the primary factor affecting stress distribution, while bone density was the primary factor affecting vertebral subsidence. Subgroup analysis found that von Mises stress and subsidence risk exhibited exponential changes when endplate thickness fell below 0.1 mm, particularly in osteoporosis. The subsidence risk in osteoporosis was approximately twice that of normal bone density. Complete endplate loss increased subsidence risk by approximately 1.3 to 1.4 times compared to the intact endplate condition.ConclusionsEndplate thickness and bone density significantly influence biomechanical stability after ACDF, highlighting the importance of preserving endplate integrity and evaluating bone quality to mitigate the risk of postoperative subsidence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-025-09255-x.

Introduction

Anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) is a standard operative treatment for cervical degeneration disc diseases [1, 2]. Recently, spine surgeons have widely used synthetic cages to fill the interbody space after discectomy. In comparison to traditional analogous bone implanted in interbody space, synthetic cages avoid iliac crest harvesting, which increase operative time, patient discomfort [3]. Synthetic cages also provide stronger support to help maintain disc height and promote fusion [4]. Despite the increased risks in alterations in spine geometry, like subsidence, the use of interbody devices is still common [5].

The definition of subsidence is a reduction in the anterior or posterior disc height of greater than 2–3 mm during the follow-up period after ACDF. Cage subsidence after ACDF may lead to loss of foraminal height, segmental kyphosis, recurrent neural compression, and even pseudarthrosis, potentially compromising clinical outcomes [5, 6]. According to a previous systemic review, the incidence of subsidence ranged as high as 19.3% to 42.5% [7]. Therefore, disc height loss is a common occurrence after ACDF, with several factors—such as low bone mineral density (BMD) and endplate thickness—being associated with cage subsidence [8, 9].

Finite element analysis (FEA) is a commonly employed biomechanical research method that can enhance our understanding of cervical biomechanical characteristics. There is a lack of biomechanical studies on different bone density and endplate integrity when the initial fusion cage remains unchanged, particularly for cases where subsidence develops both above and below the ACDF segment and requires revision surgery. Therefore, this study aims to clarify the biomechanical characteristics of adjacent segments based on different bone density and endplate thickness by analyzing primary C4/5 ACDF models. The goal is to identify the most biomechanically favorable bone density and endplate thickness to reduce the risk of subsidence following ACDF.

Materials and methods

All procedures performed in this study involving human participants were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee and with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xiamen Humanity Hospital (Ethical No. 2023089). Written informed consent was obtained from participants, and all methods were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

CT

To create precise finite element models (FEMs), this study utilized computed tomography (CT) data from a healthy 25-year-old male volunteer (height: 176 cm, weight: 72 kg) with no history of cervical spine deformity, fracture, tumor, infection, systemic disease, or prior cervical trauma or surgery.

CT scanning was conducted from the skull base to the C7 vertebra, generating three-dimensional images of the cervical spine with both a slice thickness and an interval of 1 mm. The images were saved in DICOM format and imported into Mimics Medical (version 21.0; Materialise Mimics, Leuven, Belgium) for three-dimensional modeling using the default CT orientation. After adjusting the image threshold, vertebrae C3 to C7 were retained for analysis and was exported in STL format. The data were then imported into Geomagic Wrap (version 2021; Geomagic, Research Triangle Park, North Carolina, USA) for mesh reconstruction and surface refinement. The final surface model was saved in STP format.

Based on anatomical data from previous publications, cortical bone was modeled as a 1 mm-thick outer shell encapsulating cancellous bone. The bony endplate was represented as a 0.4 mm-thick layer covering the surface of the cortical bone. In the non-surgical regions, a 0.25 mm-thick cartilage endplate was added over the bony endplate. The intervertebral disc consisted of a nucleus pulposus, accounting for 50% of the disc volume and located slightly posterior to the center, and a surrounding annulus fibrosus. The cartilage endplate was directly connected to the disc. Ligaments between C3 and C7 were simulated using one-dimensional linear elements (axial connectors), defined based on mesh node positions and modeled as tension-only spring elements to reflect their biomechanical properties [10, 11].

The STP file was subsequently imported into SOLIDWORKS (v2020; Dassault Systèmes, Waltham, MA) for the construction of individual anatomical and surgical components, including the endplates, articular cartilage, intervertebral discs, PEEK cage, titanium plate, and screws. Each structure was assigned appropriate geometric parameters and assembled into a comprehensive 3D model. The interbody cage was designed as a hexahedral construct filled with bone graft material, modeled with the mechanical properties of cancellous bone to ensure intimate contact with the adjacent vertebral endplates.

According to diagnostic criteria for osteoporosis established by the World Health Organization (WHO), the T-score is a key indicator of bone mass [12]. Following this standard—and based on previously reported material property definitions for varying bone qualities—the elastic modulus of cortical bone, endplates, and cancellous bone was reduced by 33% in the low bone mass group compared to the normal bone group. In the osteoporosis group, the elastic modulus of cortical bone and endplates was likewise reduced by 33%, while that of cancellous bone was reduced by 66% (Table 1).

Mesh convergence analysis

To evaluate the impact of mesh size on the predictive accuracy of the finite element model and to determine the optimal mesh parameters, a mesh convergence analysis was conducted. The model was discretized using tetrahedral elements, testing four element sizes: 2 mm, 1.5 mm, 1 mm, and 0.5 mm. Computational time and peak von Mises stress on the endplates of the C4 and C5 vertebral bodies were compared across these mesh sizes. Results indicated that a 1 mm mesh size achieved a favorable balance between computational efficiency and result accuracy, with peak von Mises stress differences maintained within 5%. Consequently, a 1 mm element size was adopted for the final model, comprising 582,629 nodes and 320,887 elements (Table 2).

Mesh convergence test results

To ensure solver stability in nonlinear contact analyses, loads were applied incrementally under quasi-static conditions. Convergence criteria and contact controls followed ANSYS default settings, and residual curves were monitored throughout. Repeated runs confirmed that primary outputs (subsidence and contact pressure) varied by less than 5%, meeting widely accepted convergence standards.

Model validation

The physiological model was validated by comparison with experimental data from the studies of Panjabi et al. and Finn et al. (Fig. 1) [13, 14]. The loading and boundary conditions were as follows: all degrees of freedom at the inferior surface of the C7 vertebra were constrained, and a 1 Nm moment was applied to the C3 vertebra to simulate flexion, extension, lateral bending, and axial rotation. The range of motion (ROM) for each intervertebral functional unit was calculated and compared under identical conditions to validate the physiological model. The results found consistency with previous studies and fell within acceptable ranges, indicating the model’s reliability for subsequent comparative analyses.

To ensure the accuracy of simulation results, comprehensive validation was performed, particularly focusing on soft tissue stress under a compressive preload of 73.6 N. ACDF technique

The simulation of the ACDF procedure involved the removal of the C4/5 intervertebral disc and the cartilage layers of the inferior endplate of C4 and the superior endplate of C5. A LIBEIER cervical cage (dimensions: 5 × 14 × 16 mm) was implanted in the decompressed area, positioned 1 mm posterior to the anterior vertebral edge and closely fitted to the lateral aspects of the vertebral bodies. Additionally, a titanium plate (dimensions: 1 × 20 × 16 mm) was transversely placed anterior to the C4–C5 segment and secured with four screws at both ends. To investigate the effects of bony endplate thickness, 13 levels were modeled (0.00, 0.03, 0.07, 0.10, 0.13, 0.17, 0.20, 0.23, 0.27, 0.30, 0.33, 0.37, and 0.40 mm). The upper bound of 0.40 mm was selected according to anatomical studies reporting cervical endplate thicknesses of approximately 0.4–0.6 mm, while the lower bound of 0.00 mm represented the extreme condition of complete intraoperative removal of the bony endplate [15]. For each thickness level, the cage height was adjusted accordingly to ensure intimate contact with the adjacent vertebral surfaces (Fig. 2).

Boundary conditions and loading

The study assumed continuous and homogeneous material properties across the intervertebral structures. The cage–endplate interface was modeled as perfectly bonded, with no initial gaps, press-fit conditions, or allowance for interfacial slip. A Coulomb friction coefficient (µ) of 0.1 was adopted, consistent with previous finite element studies [11, 16]. This value lies within the clinically relevant range (0.08–0.2) for PEEK and Ti alloy cages in contact with prepared endplates. Screws and cages were modeled as being in direct contact and firmly attached to the vertebral surfaces. Screws and cages were assumed to be in direct contact with and rigidly fixed to the vertebral surfaces. Tangential friction behavior was implemented using a penalty formulation, while normal contact was treated with an augmented Lagrange method to prevent unrealistic penetration. As the present study was conducted under quasi-static loading conditions, no damping factor was incorporated. This approach ensured that the resulting stress distribution and subsidence trends remained directly comparable.

Boundary conditions were defined based on specific measurement goals. To assess vertebral surface stress, all degrees of freedom were fixed at the inferior surface of C7, with no constraints on other vertebrae. The subsidence in the model was calculated and quantified in terms of endplate displacement. For C4, subsidence was calculated by fixing the superior surface of the cage and extracting displacement from the non-contact region of the C4 superior endplate. For C5, subsidence was calculated by fixing the inferior surface of C5 and measuring displacement on its superior endplate. All displacement values were automatically calculated in ANSYS 14.5 software. The calculation area and boundary constraints were limited to a single vertebra to avoid interference from surrounding soft tissues, such as intervertebral discs. As 73.6 N is widely adopted in cervical finite element studies as a physiological preload, we applied an axial load ranging from 50 to 150 N to the superior surface of C3 in finite element study [17]. Final stress and displacement results were presented using contour plots, and the area proportions of different stress intervals were calculated for each model.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 23.0 (IBM, New York, USA). After standardizing continuous data, linear regression was employed to analyze the effects of various factors on the dependent variables. Effect sizes were expressed as β coefficients with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and comparisons were made across different factors (vertebral type and bone mass were treated as categorical variables, with C4 vertebra and normal bone mass set as reference groups). Curve fitting and visualization were conducted using Origin 2022 (OriginLab, Massachusetts, USA) to predict trends of dependent variables under different conditions. The coefficient of determination (R²) was used to assess the goodness-of-fit of regression models, with R² ≥ 0.8 indicating a strong correlation, 0.5 ≤ R² < 0.8 indicating a moderate correlation, 0.3 ≤ R² < 0.5 indicating a weak correlation, and R² < 0.3 indicating a very weak correlation. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Validation of ACDF model

A well-performing non-pathological ACDF model was successfully developed (Fig. 3). Using consistent loading protocols, the ACDF model’s range of motion and von Mises stress were similar with previous publications, showing discrepancies within 5% of reported values. These results confirm the validity and reliability of the finite element model established in this study.

Multivariate linear regression analysis

A comprehensive analysis of various risk factors for on von Mises stress and vertebral subsidence was conducted, with vertebral level and bone density included as categorical variables. The results found that vertebral level, bone density, load, and endplate thickness had statistically significant effects on von Mises stress (p < 0.001). Among these, endplate thickness exhibited the strongest influence on stress, followed by bone density (Table 3). Similarly, vertebral level, bone density, load, and endplate thickness had significant effects on vertebral subsidence (p < 0.001). Among these factors, bone density was the primary risk factor for vertebral subsidence, while endplate thickness served as a significant protective factor (Table 4).

To ensure the robustness of these findings, we conducted a systematic diagnostic check of the linear regression assumptions (Supplementary Fig. 1 ~ 6). When stress was used as the dependent variable, the variance inflation factor was around 1.0, the Durbin–Watson statistic was 0.75 (p < 0.001), and the Breusch–Pagan test value was 45.89 (df = 5, p < 0.001). When subsidence was the dependent variable, the variance inflation factor was also around 1.0, the Durbin–Watson statistic was 0.19 (p < 0.001), and the Breusch–Pagan test value was 102.46 (df = 5, p < 0.001). These results indicate mild residual autocorrelation and heteroscedasticity, which we attribute mainly to the deterministic structure of the finite element simulations and repeated parameter combinations, rather than to model misspecification. To address these issues, HC3 robust standard errors were employed for inference. The adjusted results confirmed that both the direction and statistical significance of the key predictors remained unchanged, supporting the stability and reliability of our conclusions (Supplementary table).

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analysis was conducted to further elucidate the interaction effects between vertebral level, bone quality, and endplate thickness on local stress distribution and subsidence risk. Von Mises Stress and subsidence outcomes were visualized across stratified groups by vertebral level and endplate thickness. In cases with intact endplates, osteoporotic C4 vertebrae exhibited higher von Mises stress than other bone quality groups; however, this difference diminished as endplate thickness decreased. When the endplate was completely absent, stress became more concentrated in groups with higher bone density. With decreasing endplate thickness, we can see an increase in the subsidence in C4 vertebra. Besides, cross-group comparison revealed that lower bone density was associated with greater sensitivity to endplate damage. Similar trends were observed for the C5 vertebra (Fig. 6).

Discussion

Subsidence is a common postoperative complication following ACDF, potentially compromising clinical outcomes by causing loss of disc height, segmental kyphosis, and neural compression [18, 19]. These complications may lead to recurrent symptoms, diminished clinical outcomes, and, in some cases, necessitate revision surgery. Understanding the biomechanical contributors to subsidence is critical for improving surgical planning and implant design. Among the potential risk factors, bone density and endplate thickness have been suggested to play crucial roles, yet their individual and combined effects remain inadequately explored [9]. Our study employed a validated finite element model to investigate how variations in these two parameters influence stress distribution and subsidence risk after ACDF and found that endplate thickness and bone density significantly influence the stability of implant after ACDF.

Our results suggest a strong association between bone density and vertebral subsidence, which is in line with previous findings that BMD is one of the most important factors to link with cage subsidence after ACDF [5]. Our results found that decreasing bone density led to a more diffuse stress distribution across the vertebral endplate and was accompanied by a significant increase in subsidence risk, partly due to the diminished structural support resulting from decreased bone mass. This trend was especially pronounced in the osteoporotic group, where the risk of subsidence was nearly twice that of individuals with normal bone density. Furthermore, a positive correlation was observed between bone density and subsidence risk, suggesting that even osteopenia may contribute to an increased likelihood of subsidence. However, the previous research regarding whether osteopenia linked with postoperative subsidence remained controversial. Opsenak et al. found a 56% incidence of cage subsidence after ACDF, and lower bone density was significantly linked with a higher incidence of subsidence [20]; while Ahmed et al. found that prior diagnosis of osteopenia didn’t link with postoperative subsidence [21]. These conflicting findings may be partially explained by our results. On the one hand, the definition of subsidence varies across studies, which can lead to inconsistent outcomes. On the other hand, our analysis reveals a significant positive correlation between bone density and subsidence risk, indicating that lower bone density increases the likelihood of subsidence. However, the variability in how subsidence is defined may obscure statistically significant associations. Additionally, other contributing factors—such as cervical level, age, and anatomical differences—may also influence the incidence of subsidence and contribute to nonsignificant or inconsistent findings across studies [2, 22].

In response to this clinical concern, several studies have attempted to develop predictive tools for subsidence risk based on BMD, CT, or MRI data [23–25]. Unlike previous approaches, our study employed a validated finite element model that simulates a range of bone density conditions according to the clinical definitions of osteopenia and osteoporosis. The findings indicate that both osteoporosis and osteopenia are associated with an increased risk of subsidence, underscoring the importance of preoperative BMD evaluation in patients undergoing ACDF.

In addition to bone density, endplate thickness appears to play a significant role in stress distribution and subsidence risk, though the optimal extent of endplate removal has yet to be clearly defined [26, 27]. There is ongoing disagreement among surgeons regarding the optimal extent of endplate removal. Moreover, it has yet to be experimentally demonstrated that the degree of endplate decortication can be intentionally varied in a realistic and reproducible manner. Based on this consideration, we utilize a reliable finite element analysis to explore the potential link between the integrity of endplate and subsidence. Our results revealed that as endplate thickness decreased, especially below 0.1 mm, both von Mises stress and subsidence increased exponentially. These findings imply that even subtle damage to the endplate, often difficult to detect intraoperatively—may significantly elevate the risk of postoperative subsidence.

Since bone density and endplate thickness were rarely present as separate issues in clinical practice and often interact, subgroup analysis was performed to examine their combined effect on subsidence risk. Reduced endplate thickness was significantly correlated with greater subsidence risk, a pattern observed across bone density groups and especially evident in osteoporotic bone, where the diminished structural integrity impaired load distribution. When the bony endplate was completely absent (0 mm), stress became highly concentrated in the underlying cancellous bone, which is less capable of resisting compressive forces. Therefore, avoiding excessive endplate removal may be essential to maintaining biomechanical stability following ACDF, particularly in patients with or at risk of osteoporosis, which is highly corresponding with the research published by Lim et al. [16].

Our study employed finite element analysis to explore the biomechanical impact of bone density and endplate thickness on subsidence following ACDF. As an increasingly refined and widely adopted simulation technique, finite element modeling continues to advance in terms of anatomical realism and material property representation. Nonetheless, several limitations must be acknowledged. Firstly, the model was derived from a single healthy male volunteer, while actual patients undergoing ACDF often present with a range of physiological variations—including age-related changes, sex differences, or pathological bone remodeling—that may affect bone quality, endplate structure, and biomechanical response. Secondly, bone and soft tissues were simplified as homogeneous, isotropic, and linearly elastic. This does not fully capture the anisotropic and time-dependent behavior of living tissues, particularly in osteoporotic bone. Muscles and ligaments were also modeled in a simplified manner, and dynamic physiological loads (e.g., walking, coughing, or postural changes) were not incorporated, which limits the ability of the model to represent patient-specific loading environments. Thirdly, the model assumed ideal implant positioning and perfect cage–endplate contact, with the inferior surface of C7 fully constrained to enhance convergence and ensure comparability across scenarios. However, in clinical practice, malposition, subsurface gaps, and micromotion are common; these factors can compromise uniform implant–endplate contact, leading to uneven load distribution and localized stress concentrations, which in turn accelerate cage subsidence. While our simulations employed a boundary distance of approximately two vertebral levels to minimize boundary effects, further analyses with extended spans and alternative constraints could strengthen the assessment of result robustness. Likewise, additional friction sensitivity analyses and micromotion outputs might yield deeper insights into interfacial behavior, but these were beyond the scope of the present study. Finally, our simulations did not incorporate cyclic or fatigue loading conditions. Cage subsidence is a progressive process that often develops under repetitive stress. Incorporating cyclic or fatigue loading could therefore provide a more pathologically relevant representation of long-term subsidence risk. However, our current finite element framework cannot precisely capture fatigue-driven bone adaptation or damage accumulation, as these would require advanced constitutive models like cadaveric cyclic loading experiments. Future studies incorporating patient-specific models, boundary sensitivity analyses, friction parameter variations, dynamic loading, and long-term biological responses will be essential to further validate and expand upon these findings.

Conclusions

This finite element analysis suggests that both bone density and endplate thickness play critical roles in influencing biomechanical stability following ACDF. Decreased bone density, particularly in osteoporotic conditions, and reduced endplate thickness were associated with increased stress dispersion and a higher risk of vertebral subsidence. Notably, even osteopenia and submillimeter endplate damage significantly elevated subsidence risk. These findings underscore the importance of preoperative bone quality assessment and intraoperative preservation of endplate integrity. While the study provides valuable biomechanical insights, further validation through in vitro, in vivo, and patient-specific dynamic modeling is necessary to guide clinical decision-making and optimize surgical outcomes.
