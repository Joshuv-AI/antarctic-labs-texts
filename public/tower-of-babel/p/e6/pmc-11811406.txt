The effect of inner limiting membrane peeling on visual fields: Unravelling a source of bias in glaucoma management
Authors: Meryem Nurkan, Jan V. Eijgen, Ivo De Clerck, Ingeborg Stalmans, João Barbosa-Breda
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Pars plana vitrectomy (PPV) with inner limiting membrane (ILM) peeling aims to improve visual function. Previous studies have focused on visual acuity, often neglecting visual field (VF) changes. This systematic review examines VF changes after PPV with ILM peeling in patients with epiretinal membrane (ERM) or macular hole (MH), including those with glaucoma, who have preexisting VF damage, to identify potential biases in glaucoma management. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (A) Patients undergoing ILM peeling for ERM or MH and (B) VF outcomes assessed through standard automated perimetry (SAP) or Goldmann kinetic perimetry. Exclusions were non-English studies and those with fewer than 30 subjects. Data included retinal disease, demographics, VF outcomes (mean deviation [MD], pattern standard deviation, and central VF sensitivity [CVFS]), study design, and use of stains. Out of 612 studies, 23 met our criteria. Of these, four included glaucoma patients and seven involved concomitant phacoemulsification. In glaucoma patients, two studies showed MD improvement in the central 24°, while one showed deterioration in the central 10°, linked to worse preoperative MD and older age. Among studies without glaucoma patients, three of eight using SAP reported increased VF defects (VFDs); two showed lower outer nasal CVFS postoperatively. Indocyanine green staining was linked to higher VFD incidence while triamcinolone acetonide or tryptane blue staining led to no significant VFDs. The nasal half of the central VF was more affected, possibly due to retinal ganglion cell distribution. Further prospective studies, including randomized controlled trials, are needed to better understand ILM-peeling effects.

INTRODUCTION

Within the intricate tapestry of visual perception lies the gateway to sight-the retina, an astonishing mosaic of cells that transforms light into action potentials constructing a visual world.[1] A component of the retina called the inner limiting membrane (ILM) is essential for preserving its health and functionality. This structure represents the structural boundary between the vitreous humor and the retina, formed by ends of the retinal Müller cells [Figure 1]. The ILM can become dysfunctional and be responsible in the process leading to vision loss in several retinal diseases such as macular holes (MHs) and epiretinal membranes (ERMs). Pars plana vitrectomy (PPV) with ILM-peeling [Figure 2], a surgical procedure that aims to improve general visual function and improve visual acuity (VA), has become popular as one of the many surgical procedures required to treat these disorders.[2] The surgical maneuver required to peel the ILM is not simple since this structure has 0.4–1.4 µm of thickness and immediately beneath it lies the retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL).[3] Damage to the axons in this layer can potentially lead to vision loss in the area from which those axon fibers come from. There are many ways of measuring vision, the most common being VA and visual field (VF). The evaluation of VF changes following ILM-peeling has received less attention in most of the prior studies, since their focus was mostly on the anatomical outcomes and, when assessing functional outcomes, the focus has usually been centered on VA.[4] Glaucoma patients have VF damage that results from damage to the retinal nerve fiber bundles. Since these bundles have a unique anatomy, glaucomatous axonal damage causes the characteristic patterns of VF defects (VFDs) that can exist in the central region already at early disease stages. Being that the ILM is anatomically adjacent to the RNFL, damage can occur when surgically peeling the ILM. It is important to know if this can lead to VF changes, and also how this can impact an already damaged VF, which is the case in glaucoma patients. This would introduce a bias in glaucoma management because additional VF damage can be a result of ILM peeling and not necessarily glaucoma progression. We are aiming to investigate the potential ILM peeling has to lead to VFDs and also if the presence of glaucoma preoperatively leads to a different outcome. Furthermore, understanding how ILM-peeling can affect the VF can help us improve patient rehabilitation strategies and provide important insights into the effectiveness of this surgical technique.

METHODS

Research objectives

We aim to evaluate the studies conducted with patients who underwent PPV with ILM-peeling for ERM or MH, to measure and characterize the changes in VFs and find potential risk factors for VF damage. Moreover, we aim to investigate the relationship between VF outcomes and the presence of glaucoma, looking forward to unravelling a possible source of bias in glaucoma management.

Search strategy

Databases Searched: On November 1, 2022, we searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science (Core collection), and Central (Cochrane library) with a search strategy (adapted to each database). Search Terms: We performed the search according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.[5] The concepts and keywords have been determined before starting the search. The concepts have been labeled as “inner limiting membrane peeling = ILM peeling,” “visual fields = perimetry = visual field tests,” and “surgery.” We used MeSH terms on PubMed and Cochrane, as well as Emtree and topic terms on Embase and Web of science, respectively. These terms were interchangeable with the concepts we had predetermined. The detailed search strategy can be found as Supplementary Table 1 (cf. Supplements).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria and study selection process

With the search strategy we used, 612 studies were initially found. After duplicate removal, 442 studies remained. We performed title and abstract screening, followed by full-text screening, based on predetermined inclusion and exclusion criteria. The inclusion criteria were: Patients having undergone ILM peeling for ERM or MH and studies assessing VF changes, with pre as well as postoperative VF measurement, assessed through standard automated perimetry (SAP) or Goldmann kinetic perimetry. Studies in other languages than English and studies with <30 subjects were excluded. The study selection process can be visualized in Flowchart 1.

Data extraction

We extracted the data relating to the underlying retinal disease, demography (patients to eyes ratio, ethnicity, mean age, and male percentage), VF outcomes (mean deviation [MD], pattern standard deviation [PSD], central VF sensitivity [CVFS], and peripheral mean VF sensitivity [MVFS] values when available; otherwise we collected the qualitative analysis reported), study design (prospective and retrospective), and type of stains used during surgery). We performed the data extraction manually. The data extraction was done by one junior researcher (MN) under supervision by a senior expert (JBB).

Studies that included glaucoma patients (n = 4)

In this group, we identified three studies which used SAP and one study which used Goldmann kinetic perimetry for VF testing. None of the studies included patients with concomitant cataract surgery. Three studies compared pre-with postoperative MD values,[678] two of which compared these changes with those present in a control group of nonglaucomatous eyes.[67] All four studies performed SAP (Humphrey Field Analyzer) with the 24–2 program except one study that only performed 10–2 VF testing.[7] The studies that compared glaucoma patients with a matched control group, compared the postoperative MD to preoperative within each group and no significant improvement was found in glaucoma patients after ILM-peeling. While one study found a significant MD deterioration (in the central 10°),[7] there was a trend for MD improvement (in central 24°) in another.[6] In the latter study, the VF sensitivity between the 10° and 24° improved in both groups; however, the CVFS (in the central 10°) worsened in the glaucoma group while the control group showed no changes.

Conversely, the control group across these studies showed a stable or increased MD postoperatively.[67] Furthermore, in another study with a cohort with glaucoma patients, a significant improvement in MD was seen after ILM peeling.[8] Two studies compared glaucoma patients with a control group matched in age, disease type (ERM/MH), MH stage, and the time points of the first and second postoperative follow-up sessions. Both studies were done by Tsuchiya et al. in 2017 and 2021 with a sample size of 54 and 66 eyes (of 54 and 66 patients), respectively.[67] The former compared the postoperative MD and PSD at 6- and 12 months with the preoperative values. There were no significant (though perhaps clinically relevant) VF changes postoperatively in the glaucoma group (preoperative mean MD −7.8 ± 5.6 dB, postoperative mean MD at 12 m: −5.36 ± 0.84 dB, P = 0.71; preoperative mean PSD: 6.8 ± 4.1 dB, postoperative mean PSD at 12 m: 4.94 ± 0.62 dB, P = 0.17). On the other hand, in the control group, the mean MD significantly increased postoperatively (preoperative mean MD −2.7 ± 1.6 dB, postoperative mean MD at 12 months: −1.8 ± 1.5 dB, P = 0.02) while the mean PSD showed no significant change (preoperative mean PSD: 2.1 ± 1.1 dB, postoperative mean PSD at 12 months: 2.2 ± 2.6 dB, P = 0,07). In the glaucoma group, the postoperative mean CVFS, within the central 10°, decreased significantly after surgery (preoperative: 27.9 ± 1.9 dB; postoperative, at 12 m: 26.4 ± 1.8, P = 0.03), while the postoperative peripheral MVFS, described as the field between the central 10° and 24°, improved significantly (preoperative: 24.5 ± 2.1 dB; postoperative, at 12 m: 25.7 ± 2.3 dB, P = 0.01).[6] In the control group, there were no significant changes in central MVFS (preoperative central MVFS: 30 ± 0.8 dB, postoperative: 30.2 ± 1.2 dB, P = 0.09), while the peripheral MVFS significantly improved after surgery (preoperative: 24.8 ± 1.4 dB, postoperative: 27.8 ± 2.1, P = 0.02). In 2021, Tsuchiya et al. reported that there were no significant 10–2 mean MD changes in the control group (preoperative MD: −2.3 ± 1.9 dB, postoperative MD at 3 months: −2.2 ± 2.1 dB, P = 0.024), while there was a significant mean MD deterioration postoperatively in the glaucoma group (preoperative MD: −7.4 ± 2.6 dB, postoperative MD at 3 months: −11.1 ± 3.1 dB, P = 0.001). In this study, 76% of the patients had normal-tension glaucoma, 18% primary open-angle glaucoma, and 6% exfoliation glaucoma. Moreover, the authors reported that a worse preoperative 10–2 MD was a significant risk factor for MD deterioration in the glaucoma group (P = 0.024), with other risk factors being older age (P = 0.038), longer axial length (P = 0.002), and thinner ganglion cell complex (P = 0.012) preoperatively.[7] In the study of Reddy et al., with a sample of 80 eyes (of 40 patients), VF MD 3 months post ILM-peeling was compared with the preoperative values. A significant mean MD improvement was identified in this study (preoperative MD: −2.75 ± 2.4; MD at 3 months: −1.61 ± 2.2, P = 0.02).[8] We identified a fourth study that included glaucoma patients (n = 2 out of 53 eyes, 5%), where the use of indocyanine-green (ICG) dye was assessed by comparing eyes with and without the use of stain.[9] The glaucoma patients were the part of the stained group and their VF (Goldmann kinetic perimetry) did not show significant changes after surgery. In this study, when analyzing all patients, there was a trend for VFDs appearing only in the stained group (P = 0.081, Fisher’s exact probability test). The two glaucoma patients included in the study showed no VFD worsening postoperatively, even though they were in the stained group.

Studies that excluded glaucoma patients (n = 19)

Out of the 19 studies that excluded glaucoma patients, eight provided quantitative metrics and used SAP while ten used Goldmann kinetic perimetry and only had a descriptive VF analysis, which we will describe under “Studies with qualitative VF analyses.” In 7/19 studies, ILM-peeling was combined with concomitant cataract surgery.

Studies with standard automated perimetry and quantitative visual field metrics (n = 8)

Out of eight studies, three found a significant increase in VFDs post-ILM-peeling. According to Akino et al., the number of VFDs increased in 22 eyes (73.3%) out of 30, at 1 month and 3 months postoperatively. Regarding the central 30°, the nasal half was involved more frequently than the temporal one (nasal 19 eyes (86.4%) and temporal 9 eyes (40.9%), P = 0.002).[10] Concomitant cataract surgery was performed in 19 eyes (63.3%); however, the difference in new postoperative VFDs was not significant (P = 0.64) between eyes undergoing phacoemulsification and the eyes which did not. In this study, a significant odds ratio (OR) for developing new VFDs after ILM-peeling was found compared with PPV without ILM peeling (OR 10.44, P = 0.035, confidence interval [CI] 1.2–92.8).[10] The study of Terashima et al. corroborated the previous study with the findings of significantly lower outer nasal CVFS (of the central 10°) at 3 months postvitrectomy. Of note that 38/45 eyes underwent concomitant cataract surgery. In this study, the authors deemed the effect of cataract surgery to be nonsignificant since the “overall” CVFS postoperatively did not show a significant improvement.[11] Furthermore, in another study by Terashima et al., when comparing patients according to ERM severity (stage 1–3 vs. Stage 4), the nasal CVFS in the central 10° was significantly worse in Stage 4 ERM than stage 1–3 ERM (= intact retinal layers) at 12-month follow-up (stage 4 at 12 m: 27.8 ± 4.1 dB; stage 1–3 at 12 m: 29.7 ± 2.1 dB, P = 0.02) while there were no significant differences preoperatively (stage 4, preoperative: 28.3 ± 3.4 dB; stage 1–3, preoperative 29.6 ± 2.3 dB; P > 0.05).[12]

On the other hand, Lalezary et al., with a sample of 80 eyes (of 40 patients), found that there was a significant MD improvement (in the central 24°) at 12 months compared with the preoperative value (preoperative: −4.0 dB, at 12 m: −2.2 dB, P = 0.02).[13] Nevertheless, Lee and Yu found, in their study with a sample of 62 patients, that a greater decrease in ganglion cell-inner plexiform layer (GCIPL) thickness was correlated with a greater decrease in macular MVFS, among patients with intact photoreceptor layers (r = −0.2845; P = 0.0390).[14] These correlations were not significant in the subgroup of nine patients who had disrupted photoreceptor layers (macular MVFS: r = −0.1242, P = 0.75). In this study, 55 eyes (88.7%) had undergone concomitant phacoemulsification.

Two additional studies in this category found no significant changes in VF after ILM-peeling.[1516] The study by Balducci et al. (2014),[15] with a sample of 30 eyes (of 30 patients), found no significant changes in VF when the postoperative MD or PSD was compared to the preoperative value.[16] A study with a larger sample of 91 eyes (of 91 patients) by Ito et al. (2005)[16] that compared PPV with ILM-peeling to PPV without ILM peeling found that preoperative and postoperative macular sensitivity was not different between the groups (one-way analysis of variance, with P > 0.05 for all comparisons).[17]

One study with SAP focused on the influence of stain agents in VFDs, comparing infracyanine-green (IFCG) with tryptane blue (TB). In this study by Garweg et al., the mean CVFS significantly increased in the IFCG group (preoperative: −5.3 ± 3.7 dB; at 6 months: −7.9 ± 4.2 dB; at 12 months: −8.0 ± 5.2 dB; P = 0.027), while it remained unchanged in the TB stain group (preoperative: −4.3 ± 2.1 dB; at 6 months: −4.0 ± 1.8 dB; at 12 months: −4.0 ± 2.1 dB, P = 0.15). The changes in the mean sensitivity of the central VF mirrored those found in the mean size of the defect.[17]

Studies with goldmann kinetic perimetry and qualitative visual field analysis (n = 10)

In this group, we have found five studies comparing ICG staining with no staining for ILM-peeling. The patients in three of these studies underwent concomitant phacoemulsification as well as in one study comparing triamcinolone acetonide (TA) staining to no staining. None of these studies found VFDs after conventional ILM-peeling (without staining). Ando et al. and Haritoglou et al. found a higher number of patients with VFDs after ICG staining (66% and 35% of the patients in the stained group, respectively) than the other studies.[1819] The studies of Haritoglou et al. and Hillenkamp et al. with samples of 99 and 60 eyes, respectively, had only one and two patients with this phenomenon.[2021] In the former study, 13% of the patients and in the latter study all patients underwent simultaneous cataract surgery. Since patients undergoing phacoemulsification can potentially improve more than patients not undergoing this operation, this is an important bias to be mentioned. Studies with larger samples by Ando et al. and Tsuiki et al. (n = 96 and n = 140 respectively), described that the VFDs in the group operated with ICG, were predominantly in the nasal field, compared to the groups undergoing standard PPV without ILM peeling and ILM-peeling without a dye, where no VFDs were observed.[1822] Hillenkamp et al., with a sample of 39 eyes (of 39 patients), described one patient with VFD in the ICG group and three patients in the group without ICG staining.[23] This study is an exception among the other studies which state the contrary. In this study, a company was mentioned in the acknowledgements for gifting them ICG dye. Some patients (exact number not specified) underwent concomitant cataract surgery in this study, however, it was stated that the number of patients with cataract surgery was not significantly different between the two groups (P = 0.7).[23] There were no postoperative VFDs found in the studies comparing TA or TB staining with no staining.[2425] Schmid-Kubista et al. compared the peripheral VF between eyes stained with IFCG or membrane blue (MB). This study included 30 eyes (of 30 patients) with ERM or MH and found a significant difference in increase in (the superior, inferior, and nasal region of the) peripheral VF between groups, with a greater number of patients with the increase of VF size in the group with MB stain compared to the IFCG stain group (P = 0.021).[26] Increase or decrease in VF meant a change in 5° of at least two regions after 6 months. In this study, the intraocular pressure (IOP) remained stable in both groups, without any significant change or difference. The surgery was combined with phacoemulsification in 93% of the patients in IFCG group and only 73% of the patients in MB group. Even though this could have been an advantage for the patients in IFCG group, the MB group still had a significantly better VF outcome.

Yan et al. found postoperative VFDs in eyes with MH but not in the eyes with ERM (P < 0.05, Chi-square). This was a retrospective study on 50 eyes (of 48 patients) where the mean follow-up time was 11 months. In this study, no significant correlation was found between VFD and preoperative IOP, postoperative IOP, or patient’s age.[27]

DISCUSSION

The outcomes of this systematic review indicate contrasting patterns in VF changes after PPV with ILM-peeling for ERM or MH, with numerous studies showing deterioration in MD, whereas other studies showed no significant change or a significant improvement in this variable, including some studies with glaucoma patients. Much of the variation between studies may be due to the surgical technique. Some surgeons tend to peel deeper than others. Diabetic ILMs are also more difficult to peel, often in much smaller pieces, with the risk of causing damage each time they are re-gripped.[69]

Regarding the distribution of visual sensitivity, there seems to be a difference related to the area of the central VF being tested. A study by Tsuchiya et al., presented an increase in the peripheral MVFS (between 10° and 24°), while the mean CVFS (within 10°) decreased.[6] Other studies that identified an improved postoperative MD mostly performed a 24–2 or a 30–2 perimetry, while deteriorated MDs were more seen in studies using a 10–2 program. As such, these findings point to a possible bias regarding the extent of VF studied related to the size and region of ILM peeling being performed.[67]

Moreover, the nasal half of the central VF seems to be more affected than the temporal one. The uneven distribution of retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) can be one of the explanations for this. The nasal area of the central retina contains a higher density of RGCs compared to the temporal area.[29] The uneven distribution of RGCs means that the temporal retina may be less resilient to damage compared to the nasal retina. Therefore, even relatively minor disturbances caused by surgical manipulation during ILM peeling could disproportionately affect the temporal retina and contribute to more prominent alterations in the corresponding nasal VF. Furthermore, in a study by Lee and Yu, a greater decrease in GCIPL thickness after ILM peeling was correlated with a greater decrease in macular MVFS among patients with intact photoreceptor layers (r = −0.2845; P = 0.0390), which is an additional argument for this possible explanation.[14] Thus, it is important to keep the size and location of the peeled area in mind when choosing the optimal surgical technique, to minimize potential damage and subsequent VF deterioration. In addition to the different distribution of RGC, the surgical technique may also play a role. Vitreoretinal surgeons almost never attempt to grasp the ILM in the nasal macula due to fear of damaging the papilomacular bundle.

Regarding the use of dyes to assist in the visualization of the ILM, VFDs were seen mostly with the usage of ICG or IFCG stain. A meta-analysis by Li et al. in 2019 found ICG to be the worst type of dye used in ILM-peeling for idiopathic MH treatment concerning the safety of the dye and the postoperative best-corrected VA compared with other dyes, even though it was better than using no dye at all when the rate of MH closure after surgery was regarded.[30] Our findings in this review corroborate this, with worse VF outcomes related to the usage of ICG/IFCG, when compared to other dyes such as TA and TB. The only exception herein was the study by Hillenkamp et al. (n = 39), that found one patient with VFD post-ILM-peeling in the group with ICG stain and three patients in the group without.[23] The occurrence of VFDs in ICG/IFCG groups of the studies comparing stains used during the surgery, is a potential argument for considering other dyes than ICG/IFCG when planning a PPV with ILM-peeling.

In addition to the possible damage caused by ILM peeling, we should weigh the risks of not performing ILM peeling. When ILM peeling is not performed, the closure rate of MH is dramatically lower and recurrence rate with ERM significantly higher.[3132]

On the other hand, when simultaneous cataract surgery is performed, the improvement in VFs could form a bias when evaluating the effect of ILM peeling on VFs, as the positive changes might be erroneously attributed to the ILM peeling rather than the cataract surgery. Further, the use of tamponades after PPV can affect postoperative VF, potentially causing scotomas or VFD due to the pressure exerted on the retina and the varying absorption rates of different tamponade agents.[33] None of the included studies analyzed the effect of different tamponades.

With the comprehensive search strategy that we used to identify all relevant studies, the risk of selection bias has been reduced and a wide range of evidence has been considered. Since the number of studies including glaucoma patients is limited and the studies mostly focused on other primary outcomes such as VA, this has led us to include studies that only mentioned VFD qualitatively, with descriptions rather than more objective quantifications. A meta-analysis was not feasible within the scope of this systematic review. Including only studies in English might have led to a language bias in this study. To enhance the design and outcomes of future studies, we would like to propose the following recommendations. First, implementing a 6–12 month’ follow-up period would help to balance the ability to identify visual function changes with patient retention feasibility. Second, using the change in MD (postoperative MD-preoperative MD) as a primary outcome measure to provide a more accurate and quantifiable assessment. Moreover, we recommend multi-center studies as a way of reaching higher sample sizes in a topic where low patient inclusion numbers are expected. Furthermore, we recommend focusing in pseudophakic patients to avoid the bias caused by simultaneous cataract surgery or the development of cataract postoperatively. Finally, to facilitate more detailed and applicable findings, we recommend stratifying patients by the severity of their glaucoma.

CONCLUSION

The variability in study designs, patient populations, and outcome measures makes it challenging to draw a definitive conclusion about the effect of ILM peeling on VFs. There is a need for well-designed prospective studies, including randomized controlled trials with the long-term follow-up. Comparative studies examining different surgical techniques and variations in ILM peeling extent with VF changes as primary outcome may help optimize surgical strategies and improve the visual outcomes for patients. Microperimetry may be of added value for future studies, by matching VF changes with the zone of ILM peeling.
