Low estimated glomerular filtration rate and proteinuria among adult diabetic patients in a tertiary hospital in Eastern Uganda - a cross-sectional study
Authors: Moses Kirya, Denis Bwayo, Michael E Otim, Paul Bukhota Mutoo, John Peter Masete Masaba, Okibure Ambrose, Richard Katuramu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic kidney disease (CKD) is one of the most common complications of diabetes mellitus (DM). Diabetes mellitus contributes to about 66% of CKD cases globally. CKD results in increased morbidity and mortality and advanced stages often require kidney replacement therapy that is unaffordable for the majority of the patients. Developing countries have scanty data regarding CKD burden in diabetic patients.ObjectivesThis study aimed at determining the prevalence of low estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and proteinuria and associated clinical and socio-demographic factors among adult diabetic patients attending the diabetic clinic of Mbale Regional Referral Hospital (MRRH).MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted at the adult diabetic clinic of MRRH in Eastern Uganda. A total of 374 adult diabetic patients were enrolled. A urine sample for urine albumin creatinine ratio (UACR) determination and a venous blood sample for measurement of serum creatinine were obtained from each participant. The eGFR was determined using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation and CKD was staged according to the Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) classification.ResultsA total of 318 (85%) participants had an eGFR of ≤ 60 mL/min/1.73m2, UACR of ≥ 30g/g, or both. Only 6.1% were aware. Age, duration of DM, hypertension, and dyslipidemia were associated with low eGFR and proteinuria.ConclusionThere is a high prevalence of low eGFR and proteinuria among DM patients, 85% of the participants had these markers of CKD and the majority of them were undiagnosed. Over half of the DM patients had an eGFR consistent with advanced CKD. Strengthening routine screening for CKD biomarkers and equipping DM clinics with more diagnostic resources is recommended.Keywords: Diabetes mellitus, Chronic kidney disease, Proteinuria, Glomerular filtration rate

Introduction

The International Diabetes Federation (IDF) estimated that the number of adults with diabetes will rise by 55% - an increase from 381.8 million (in 2013) to 591.9 million in 2035. The largest increase is estimated to occur in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) [1]. In 2021, the IDF estimated that 537 million people were living with diabetes worldwide [2]. Globally, the overall prevalence of diabetes is now estimated to be over 10%, the highest prevalence being observed in middle-income countries. A 12.9% increase in diabetes prevalence from 2019 was reported [2]. Future projections suggest that by 2045, the absolute number of people with diabetes will have increased by 46%, with the greatest absolute growth occurring in middle-income countries [2].

Currently, about 24 million adults are living with diabetes in Africa. This figure is projected to rise by 129% to 55 million by 2045 [3]. In 2021, an estimated 716,000 adults in Uganda had diabetes, 89% of Ugandans with diabetes were neither on medication nor aware of their status and therefore presented to the health system with difficult-to-treat complications [3]. These complications range from macro and micro-vascular complications among which diabetic nephropathy has been noted to be on the rise, with diabetes being the top known cause of chronic kidney disease (CKD) [2].

Chronic kidney disease is currently a major public health challenge worldwide, with an estimated global prevalence of over 10% (over 800 million cases) [4, 5]. The global burden of CKD has increased by about 29.3% since 1990. According to the global burden of disease (GBD) study in 2017, CKD was the 12th leading cause of death globally - an increase from the 17th in 1990 [4].

About 30% to 50% of patients with DM develop manifestations of diabetic nephropathy in their lifetime [1]. In SSA, the prevalence of DM nephropathy is estimated to be 11% to 83.7% [1, 6, 7].

The risk of developing CKD among diabetic patients is influenced by the duration of diabetes, hypertension, advancing age, obesity, and poor glycemic control [8–11]. It is also more common in least-developed countries compared to developed countries and this is mainly attributed to; delayed diagnosis, limited screening and diagnostic resources, and poor glycemic control, among other risk factors [1]. This late detection in turn leads to inadequate treatment of CKD at an early stage in such populations and a higher burden of advanced disease with poorer outcomes. Early detection of CKD in diabetic patients is associated with improved care to the patient and delay of progression of kidney disease. This relies largely on the detection of certain biomarkers that are associated with kidney disease.

The American Diabetes Association (ADA), Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO), and the National Kidney Foundation (NKF)'s Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative (KDOQI) recommend the use of the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and the urine albumin creatinine ratio (UACR) as the tests for early detection of CKD especially among the major risk groups which include diabetic and hypertensive patients [12]. The combination of these two, eGFR and UACR guides therapeutic interventions as well as helping to predict the risk for CKD progression plus cardiovascular events, and mortality.

The evidence to guide screening programs and thereby address the burden of kidney disease among diabetic patients in Africa remains scanty [1]. Studies carried out in Uganda about CKD show that the prevalence is about 15.2% [13]. However, this was carried out in the general population in an urban setting. Among the diabetic patients, two studies were done in central Uganda that found the prevalence of proteinuria to be at 17.1% - Nambuya et al study [14] and 47.4% - Muddu et al study [15]. However, all these studies were conducted in central Uganda more than a decade ago.

The paucity of local evidence regarding the prevalence of CKD among diabetic patients in developing countries where Uganda is inclusive creates challenges in designing locally tailored prevention and management programs. This knowledge gap also contributes to the reluctance to incorporate screening for low eGFR and/or proteinuria in the routine care of diabetic patients in our setting which partly contributes to the delayed detection.

Therefore, our study aimed to determine the prevalence of low eGFR and proteinuria and the associated clinical and socio-demographic factors among adult diabetic patients seeking care in the diabetic clinic of Mbale Regional Referral Hospital in Eastern Uganda.

Methods

A descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted between December 2022 to May 2023.

Study site

The study was conducted in the adult diabetic outpatient clinic of Mbale Regional Referral Hospital (MRRH). It is located in the Eastern part of Uganda, within Mbale city. The diabetic clinic runs within the MRRH outpatient clinic and is one of the 16 regional referral public health facility treatment centers nationwide. The clinic runs every Thursday, attending to about 50-70 patients per clinic day.

Study population

The study targeted all adult type 2 diabetic patients. Known DM patients enrolled for routine care in the diabetic outpatient clinic of MRRH (a government-funded facility that offers free services) and had attended the clinic for at least a month, were recruited. Our study included adult type 2 diabetic patients (≥ 18 years), who consented to participate in this study and excluded those patients who were diagnosed with DM on the day of enrollment and those found to have diabetic emergencies (hypoglycemia or any hyperglycemic state).

Sample size and sampling procedure

Using the Kish -Leslie formula for revalence studies [18], n=p1-pZ2d2 , where p (the assumed true population prevalence) was 83.7% from a study by Muhammed et al [6], d (the margin of error) of 0.05 at 95% confidence interval, and Z (the Z-score for the desired confidence level) was 1.96, a sample size (n) of 374 was arrived at. Continuous random sampling was used to recruit participants by the trained research assistants who informed them about the study and procedures involved, using both English and local languages. Those who consented and met the inclusion criteria participated in the study.

Data collection methods

Participants were reviewed by the research assistants and the principal investigator, who collected data using the World Health Organization (WHO) modified stepwise questionnaire [16], adopted and contextualized to the Ugandan context to collect data. Those who fulfilled the study criteria were enrolled and assigned specific identification codes. Chart reviews, medical history was collected and a physical examination was performed to ascertain patients’ demographics, biophysical parameters, lifestyle, and social characteristics, disease-specific characteristics including; duration of diabetes, type of anti-glycemic agent used, subjective adherence to the anti-glycemic drugs, presence of any co-morbidity such as hypertension, and obesity. On every data collection day, 15 to 25 participants were interviewed without interrupting routine patient care.

The blood pressure of each participant was measured in a standard way (after the patient had rested for 5 min, in a sitting position, and two measurements were taken). Patients with a value greater than 140/90 mmHg were considered hypertensive. Blood pressure > 140mmHg was considered systolic hypertension and a value of > 90 mmHg was considered diastolic hypertension. A documented diagnosis of hypertension and/or a history of treatment for hypertension was said to be hypertensive.

Each participant was given a sterile urine container and requested to provide 20 mls of mid-stream urine following instructions that were given by the research assistant. This sample was safely transferred to Pallisa General Hospital Laboratory, an accredited laboratory per the recognized international standards, ISO 15189-2012, where it was used for determining the urine albumin creatinine ratio (UACR) using the CLINITEK® Microalbumin 2 reagents. A UACR of ≥ 30mg/g and above was defined as significant proteinuria (a biomarker for CKD) in line with the KDIGO guidelines. A UACR of 30-299mg/g was categorized as moderate albuminuria while a UACR of 300mg/g and above was classified as severe albuminuria.

Following an aseptic technique, a qualified laboratory technician drew 7 mls of venous blood from each participant into a syringe. The blood was placed in a red top vacutainer for renal function tests & lipid profile. All these were adequately packaged in Ziploc bags and carrier for processing in the Pallisa General Hospital laboratory. Serum creatinine was measured for all the study participants using a HumaStar 200 clinical chemistry machine, calibrated by the creatinine Jaffe 2 method from which the eGFR was calculated using the chronic kidney disease epidemiology collaboration (CKD-EPI) formula. Before testing the samples for serum creatinine, a qualified laboratory technician performed a quality control procedure using normal pooled serum control, and a Roche calibrator for automated systems was used for calibration and calibration verification. An eGFR of ≤ 60 mL/min/1.73m2 was considered a marker for CKD. The eGFR was also used for CKD staging as per KDIGO-CKD classification. The primary outcome was proteinuria, low eGFR, or both.

As is the routine practice in the DM clinic of MRRH patients are instructed not to eat any caloric foods for 8 hours before coming to the clinic, fasting blood glucose (FBG) was measured for every participant using a Glucometer - SD CodeFree, Thailand from a capillary blood sample obtained by a pricker by a trained research assistant. For each tin of glucose test strips, a quality assurance test was performed by ensuring that the code numbers match and cross-checking the expiry date before testing the participants. A fasting blood glucose of ≥ 7mmol/L was said to be poor glycemic control.

The other test conducted on the blood sample collected was a fasting lipid panel using the enzymatic method. Quality control testing was done using the Randox lipid control before performing the lipid profile test. Dyslipidemia was defined as total cholesterol levels of ≥ 200mg/dl and/or high-density lipoprotein (HDL) of ≤ 40mg/dl.

All laboratory test results were issued after a professional judgment and cross-checking by the designated laboratory quality assurance officer on duty.

To determine the participants' body mass index (BMI), their heights and weights were measured using a medical giraffe height measuring stadiometer (Model HMS PL) and an analog flat weighing scale respectively. The above equipment was well-calibrated and standardized for quality assurance before measuring the participants’ weights and heights. Measurements were taken to the nearest 0.1 m and 0.1 kg for height and weight, respectively. BMI was computed and participants were categorized following the WHO BMI classification into underweight (<18.5kg/m2), ideal weight (18.5- 24.9kg/m2), overweight (25.0-29.9 kg/m2), and obese (≥30 kg/m2) [16].

Data analysis

The primary outcome was the prevalence of low eGFR ≤ 60 mL/min/1.73m2 and/or proteinuria – UACR of ≥ 30mg/g within the study population The predictor variables included; Lifestyle factors (smoking, inactivity, alcohol consumption), biophysical measurements (BMI), DM related clinical factors (duration of DM, hypoglycemic agents used, subjective adherence to the anti-diabetic drugs, glycemic control, dyslipidemia), non-modifiable factors (age, sex, family history of renal disease) and co-morbidities (HIV status, hypertension among others).

Raw data was entered into Microsoft Excel and analyzed using STATA version 17 (Stata Corp, College Station, TX). Categorical variables were described as proportions (%), and continuous variables were described as medians [interquartile range (IQR)]. In the univariate analysis, Fisher's exact and chi-squared tests were used for categorical and binary variables respectively and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for continuous variables. Predictors with a p-value < 0.05 were included in the multivariate model using logistic regression and the effect was measured using adjusted Odds Ratios (ORs).

Results

Throughout the study period, four hundred ten (410) patients were conveniently screened in Mbale Regional Referral Hospital diabetic clinic for study enrolment. Three hundred seventy-four (374) patients met the eligibility criteria and were enrolled. Thirty (30) patients were excluded for having a first-time diagnosis of DM on the day of enrolment and six (6) patients did not consent to the study.

Discussion

Overall, in every 10 diabetic patients enrolled for care in the adult diabetic clinic of MRRH, nearly 8 had at least a low eGFR and/or proteinuria. Proteinuria prevalence is much higher than the other estimated prevalence within Uganda, almost doubling the 47.4% estimated prevalence from the Muddu et al study [15], and almost triple the 17.1% estimated prevalence among diabetic patients from the Nambuya et al study [14]. This could be accounted for by the rising prevalence of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) over time, which has been projected to be more in developing countries like Uganda. The two studies quoted above were conducted close to a decade ago, hence the change in the prevalence estimates. The other explanation for the huge difference in the proteinuria prevalence estimate could be because of regional variations in the prevalence of CKD, the above-quoted studies having been conducted in central Uganda, plus a few more studies conducted in western Uganda, which could yield different prevalence and this may be attributed to the geographical, cultural, dietary as well as lifestyle differences. However the prevalence of 85% is consistent with the continental prevalence rate of CKD among diabetic patients on the African continent, estimated to vary from 11% to 83.7%, results from a systematic review of 32 similar studies [1].

Among those with low eGFR, a significant proportion, 54% fell under the category of advanced CKD, KDIGO G-stage ≥ G3b with 2% falling under stage 5 (End Stage Kidney Disease, ESKD). Similarly, for those with proteinuria, a significant proportion, 25%, had severe albuminuria (stage A-3) of CKD. Despite these alarming numbers of DM patients with low eGFR and/or proteinuria, a few (6.1%) were aware of their kidney impairment. Thus, among diabetic patients, a significant number progress to advanced kidney disease without clinical or laboratory detection, which is a threat to the lives of such people, since the management of advanced kidney disease is still not readily affordable to a majority of Ugandans and is also associated with increased cardiovascular mortality and morbidity. This calls for the implementation of routine screening for CKD among diabetics in sub-Saharan Africa(17), a region where the screening for nephropathy among adult diabetics is not a routine practice due to limited resources.

Advanced age, duration of DM illness, co-morbid hypertension, and dyslipidemia were significantly associated with low eGFR and/or proteinuria in our study population. This has been a consistent finding in other studies [1, 6, 8–12, 18]. Age is an independent risk factor for most cardiovascular diseases and with advancing age, there is an associated loss of nephron mass [19]. The longer the time of exposure to hyperglycemia by the glomerular structures, the greater the risk of structural damage of the glomerulus as a result of the formation of advanced glycated end products that result from non-enzymatic glycation of the endothelial structures [20]. Hypertension independently contributes to hypertensive nephropathy via glomerulosclerosis due to persistent high blood pressure [21]. However, hypertension could also result from chronic kidney disease following the activation of the rennin angiotensin aldosterone system (RAAS), among other mechanisms [22]. Dyslipidemia is one of the known risk factors for cardiovascular disease, but similarly, CKD in advanced stages can be complicated by dyslipidemia, a mechanism attributed to the persistent and excessive proteinuria that occurs in diabetic kidney disease leading to a compensatory effect by the liver resulting into excessive synthesis of lipoproteins which are involved in the synthesis of lipids thus causing hyperlipidemias [23].

Unlike other studies, our study did not find a significant association between obesity and overweight with low eGFR and/or proteinuria. Most studies established that DM patients with obesity had higher prevalence rates of CKD as determined by the low eGFR and proteinuria; CREDO study [12], and many other similar studies; Ekoru et al, Low & Chi Lim et al, Middleton et al, Sabanayagam et al [8–11]. Among our study participants, the majority had normal weight (50.8%), the overweight category being 33.4%, only 12.6% were obese and another 3.2% were underweight. These findings are consistent with the findings of Kibirige's study [24] which identified that, compared to the Western world; the majority of patients with DM in Africa are younger and relatively lean in body size.

Study strengths

This study looked at low eGFR, proteinuria, or both biomarkers to arrive at the overall prevalence, compared to several similar studies that used single entities to estimate CKD prevalence.

The research provides potential information in the development of a clinical protocol for routine care for DM patients, to cater for routine screening for low eGFR and/or proteinuria as well as timely assessment and treatment of kidney impairment in patients with DM.

Limitations of the study

Being a cross-sectional study design, we did not determine the temporal effect of DM and/or hypertension on low eGFR and/or proteinuria.

HBA1C could have been the preferred predictor for glycemic control, but our study was limited by finances thus we relied on fasting blood sugar, a weaker predictor for longer-term glycemic control.

The nature of the clinic visits could not allow the UACR to be conducted on the early morning urine sample for all participants as recommended by KDIGO. The clinic opens at 8 am and participants walk in starting at 8 am to 2 pm, this could not allow the research assistants to obtain an early morning urine sample from all the participants.

During enrollment of study participants, those on Angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs) were not excluded, which could underscore the outcome of proteinuria.

There was no follow-up of the participants to identify the persistence of low eGFR and/or proteinuria for the required duration of 3 months to match the required criteria for CKD and as such acute kidney disease (AKD) and acute kidney injury (AKI) couldn’t be ruled out.

Conclusion

The prevalence of low eGFR and proteinuria among adult diabetic patients is quite high compared to the global and national prevalence of CKD in the general population. This remains a threat to the life span of the diabetic population as well as the quality of life of these patients, yet only a handful of these patients are aware of their impaired kidney functioning, implying that the majority of patients with low eGFR and/or proteinuria among diabetic patients are not aware of their condition and thus not on any measures to help slow down the progression of kidney disease. The other key observation drawn from our study is that a significant proportion of diabetic patients had markers of severe kidney disease, with 2% falling within ESKD, eGFR ≤ 15 mL/min/1.73m2. We therefore recommend that; screening for CKD, among diabetic patients should be readily incorporated into the routine care package. Routine care for diabetic patients should emphasize screening and managing co-morbidities including hypertension, and dyslipidemia.
