Assessing the microstructure of simple rhamphothecae in chickens (Aves: Galliformes: Gallus gallus domesticus)
Authors: Khanh H. T. To, Michelle R. Stocker, Tobin L. Hieronymus
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCorneous sheaths on the jaws of beaked tetrapods, or rhamphothecae, are classified as simple or compound based on their external morphology. In birds, simple rhamphothecae possess single corneous sheaths covering the upper jaws and the mandibles, respectively, rather than the several corneous plates of compound rhamphothecae. More than 60% of modern birds have simple rhamphothecae, but not all birds with simple rhamphothecae use their beaks for the same feeding style or dietary preferences. Grossly similar simple rhamphothecae may possess unreported microanatomical differences in response to different functional demands, but this requires first exploring whether the microanatomy of simple rhamphothecae is homogenous. We hypothesized based on foraging biomechanics that the rostral portion of the rhamphotheca is more mechanically resistant than the caudal portion, and the microanatomy will reflect this. We examine the microanatomy and biomechanical properties of rhamphothecae using chickens (Gallus gallus domesticus). We map the microstructure and relative hardness of corneous materials throughout the rhamphotheca using plastic‐embedded histology and microindentation. Histological sections reveal eight corneous regions, more numerous on the rostral portion of the beak, making up the seemingly continuous corneous sheath of the upper jaws and mandibles. Microindentation of individual regions reveals hardness differences, with the rostral rhamphothecal tomia (cutting edge) having the highest hardness, and layers comprising the caudal portion of the rhamphotheca having the lowest. This configuration shows that more resistant corneous layers are found rostrally to accommodate the mechanical interactions happening at the tip. Our study shows that unreported complexity within simple beaks will allow us to determine simple rhamphotheca diversity and address the homoplasy of simple rhamphothecae in modern birds.

INTRODUCTION

Beaks composed of edentulous jaw bones covered by a corneous sheath, the rhamphotheca, are seen today in birds and turtles (Alibardi et al., 2009; Bragulla & Homberger, 2009; Davit‐Béal et al., 2009; Fraser et al., 1969; Genbrugge et al., 2012; Holthaus et al., 2018; Mckittrick et al., 2012; van Hemert et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2017), with additional extinct taxa hypothesized to have had rhamphotheca as well (Wang et al., 2017; Aguilar‐Pedrayes et al., 2024). The rhamphotheca provides shearing and protective surfaces in the jaws (Alibardi, 2021; Lee et al., 2014; Moldowan et al., 2016), allowing the mouth to effectively be used as a multipurpose feeding and manipulation apparatus. Avian rhamphothecae have been modified and occupy significantly larger morphological and functional spaces beyond being a protective sheath or crushing/shearing surfaces for the jaw bones. For example, avian rhamphothecae can contribute to thermoregulation (Friedman et al., 2017; Tattersall et al., 2009, 2017), ectoparasite control (Clayton et al., 2005), and sexual display (Cuervo et al., 2009). The functional flexibility and diverse forms of the avian beak, in combination with flight, have been hypothesized to have allowed the clade to rapidly globalize and reach their current diversity of ~10,000 species (Bhullar et al., 2016; Felice et al., 2019; Louchart & Viriot, 2011; Navalón et al., 2019; Prum et al., 2015).

Avian rhamphotheca can be divided into two categories based on external morphology: compound and simple. Compound rhamphothecae are composed of unfused corneous plates sitting on the jaw bones, with each plate clearly defined by external grooves (Hieronymus & Witmer, 2010). Simple rhamphothecae, on the contrary, are composed of one continuous corneous sheath covering each of the upper and lower jaws, respectively, and comprise the majority of rhamphothecae across Neornithes (Hieronymus & Witmer, 2010; Urano et al., 2018, 2019). The presence of simple rhamphotheca across Neornithes is thought to have arisen homoplastically from various compound rhamphotheca origins (Hieronymus & Witmer, 2010). We follow current veterinary literature in referring to the corneous sheaths that cover the upper jaw bones and mandibles in simple rhamphothecae as the rhinotheca and gnathotheca, respectively (Ritchie et al., 1994; Urano et al., 2019).

Despite the external simplicity of a simple rhamphotheca, the internal structure is poorly understood among various simple rhamphotheca‐bearing avian families. Internally, simple rhinothecae in chickens have been reported to contain three distinct layers that are differentiated by their extinction patterns under cross‐polarized light (XPL) microscopy: outer layer, intermediate layer, and inner layer (Urano et al., 2019). Within toucans and hornbills, their large simple rhamphothecae are also composed of sandwiched corneous layers like chickens (Seki et al., 2010). The homology of corneous plates is difficult to assess across different avian clades because of the lack of internal morphological comparisons and osteological correlates. Therefore, we currently are unable to address whether all simple rhamphothecae are built the same, externally and internally, without first knowing how a simple rhamphotheca is built.

While the mechanical properties of rhamphothecae in general have been explored (Bonser, 1996; Bonser & Mark, 1993; Fecchio et al., 2010; Lee et al., 2014; Seki et al., 2005, 2006, 2010; Soons et al., 2010, 2012), variations within the cornified sheath are still unknown, leading to unexplored variations within a supposedly simple structure. Due to the many appearances of simple rhamphotheca from compound origins, the internal structure may provide new information related to the evolution and fusion of multiple corneous sheaths into one. We hypothesize that the corneous sheath will have variable hardness across the jaw and predict that the rostral portion of the rhamphotheca is more mechanically resistant to wear and tear (=harder) than the caudal portion of the rhamphotheca. Another area of interest is the tomium (plural: tomia), which is the cutting edge of the corneous sheath. We hypothesize that as a shearing surface, the tomium will also be harder than non‐tomial areas of the corneous sheaths.

In this study, we examine the simple rhamphotheca of the chicken (Gallus gallus domesticus) with the goal of determining how microanatomy changes throughout the rhamphotheca using histology and microindentation to clarify how a simple beak is constructed from both an anatomical and biomechanical viewpoint and elucidate the mechanical properties of corneous materials across the jaw. Based on the two areas of expected mechanical stress, we targeted the rostralmost portion of the rhamphotheca and the tomia as areas of interest (Bonser, 1996; Hieronymus & Witmer, 2010; Homberger & Brush, 1986; Krishnan, 2023; Lautenschlager et al., 2013; Lee et al., 2014; Soons et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2017).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Three fresh frozen chicken heads (VTPE.AV.0054, VTPE.AV.0055, and VTPE.AV.0057) were selected from a large sample (40+) of similarly sized chicken heads donated to the Virginia Tech Paleobiology Extant Collection by Weathertop Farm LLC. The chickens were processed by a meat processing plant once they reached adult size range as defined by husbandry standards. Due to processing procedures, many chicken heads in the larger sample did not have a complete rhamphotheca still attached and could not have been used for the study. Several osteological indicators in the cranium can be utilized to determine the approximate ontogenetic category for the heads used in the study. We chose to classify these three specimens as either skeletally immature or skeletally mature because we do not have data on whether they were sexually mature (Griffin et al., 2021). VTPE.AV.0054 and VTPE.AV.0055 were determined to be skeletally immature based on these indicators: the postorbital process has not fully fused with the zygomatic process; the bones making up the skull roof are fully articulated with each other, but the sutures are still visible and not fully obliterated; and the frontal–parietal fontanelle is closed, but the sutures between the bones are still visible. VTPE.AV.0057 was determined to be skeletally mature by these indicators: The postorbital process and the zygomatic process have fused together to form a postorbital foramen (M. pseudotemporalis profundus goes through this foramen to attach to the lateral surface of the squamosal), and the majority of the sutures, particularly of the skull roof, are obliterated. Despite differences in skeletal maturity, we deemed overall size and rhamphothecal state to be the most important factors for inclusion in the study from the opportunistic larger sample.

Specimens were thawed, fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 2 weeks, and immersed in 70% ethanol solution for long‐term storage. All specimens were μCT scanned at Ohio University MicroCT Scanning Facility for skeletal data before histological processing. Scanning parameters utilized are outlined in Supplementary Materials. Scan data are available on MorphoSource under project P523928.

Histological embedding followed a modified version of polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) preparation from Sterchi and Eurell (1989). Specimens were dehydrated by increasing ethanol concentration in +10% increments from 70% to 100% with changes at 12‐ to 48‐h intervals depending on size, then cleared in methyl salicylate for 2 to 3 days. Cleared specimens were infiltrated with three rounds of PMMA monomer of increasing long‐chain and catalyst concentration, with change intervals at 2, 4, and 8 days, respectively. At each monomer change, specimens were placed under vacuum to assist infiltration. After a final change to embedding PMMA, specimen blocks were allowed to fully polymerize at room temperature.

All three specimens were cut for histology in the same manner (Figure 1). We used a tile saw to make a midsagittal cut, creating left‐ and right‐side blocks (Figure 1a). We then used a Buehler Isomet 1000 Precision Saw to cut thin sections at 1 mm intervals. One side of each specimen was cut into transverse sections and the other side into parasagittal sections (Figure 1b,c). Thin sections were mounted onto glass petrographic slides using LocTite AA349 cured with 30 s of short‐wave UV (254 nm) and 2 min of long‐wave UV (350 nm). Slides were then ground and polished on a Buehler EcoMet 4000 Variable Speed Grinder‐Polisher to a P1200 grit finish. Slides for imaging were ground to a final thickness of ~100 μm, while those used for microindentation testing were ground to a final thickness of ~175 μm to minimize interaction of the indenter with the underlying adhesive and slide. Imaging slides were photographed on an Olympus BX51 fluorescence microscope using Infinity Capture software. Composite images used in Figures were generated with the “merge photo” function of Adobe Photoshop. We identified corneous region boundaries using differences in extinction pattern under crossed polarizers and a quartz plate (XPL), and used these images for reference during microindentation.

We used a Leco LM810AT to perform Vickers hardness testing. In this approach, an equiangular diamond‐profile indenter head is used to induce plastic deformation on a polished surface at a specified force and dwell time. Vickers hardness is a widely used approach to biomaterials testing (Basu, 2017), and has previously been used to assess the hardness of cornified beaks in other avian taxa (Lee et al., 2014; Seki et al., 2005, 2006, 2010). Cornified tissue is relatively similar to some cancellous and compact bone based on toughness versus Young's modulus comparison (Ashby et al., 1995; Wegst & Ashby, 2004). We used the recommended test loads for bone of 0.05 kilogram‐force (kgf) test load and dwell duration of 10 s (Johnson & Rapoff, 2007). Hardness value (hv) was determined as: hv=1.854*Fd2, where F is the load in kgf, and d is the arithmetic mean of d1 and d2 (diagonal measurements of the indent square) in mm. Vickers hardness for the same material may show non‐linear relationships with load and dwell time, so it is normally reported as [hardness value]HV[load used in kgf]/[dwell duration in seconds]. Because all our test slides were sampled with the same load and dwell time, we report hv as just the Vickers hardness value for brevity (e.g., hv = 23 HV.05/10 will be reported as hv = 23). We collected hardness values from up to five sites per region per slide (Figure 1e) on every other transverse slide of the series along with five control sites in the acrylic. We made microindentation tests in specified directions depending on which corneous layer was being tested (Figure 1f). This spacing between microindentation tests differed depending on which corneous layers are being tested because the area available for microindentation varies—for example, corneous beta proteins (CBP)‐B has more area than CBP‐C, so spacing between microindentation varies accordingly. Regardless, we left space between each microindentation mark to prevent overlapping indentations, which could skew the results through material deformation.

For all three specimens, the intermediate layer of the rhinotheca (CBP‐C) and the middle layer of the gnathotheca (CBP‐F) taper and end around 50% (rostrocaudally) through their respective corneous sheaths (Figure 3). We used the extent of these two layers to determine the midpoint of their respective corneous sheaths for consistency. The more rostral portion of the rhinotheca is the portion where the intermediate layer is present, and the more caudal portion of the rhinotheca is the portion where the intermediate layer is absent. The same reasoning was applied to the gnathotheca with respect to presence/absence of CBP‐F.

We used mixed‐effects modeling to assess corneous material hardness (hv, Vickers hardness) as a function of region within the rhinotheca and gnathotheca (region, factor with levels for CBP‐A to CBP‐G), rostral (overlapping with tomium‐forming CBP‐C & F, Figure 2), or caudal position overall (rcAxis, a binary factor), specific rostrocaudal position within the slide series (slide, serial section # centered within rcAxis as a continuous covariate, Figure 1c), and position relative to the tomia (site, microindent site # as an ordered covariate, Figure 1e) as fixed effects. All models included slide thickness and specimen as random effects. The inclusion of individual as a random effect provides a means to detect significant departures from the assumption that adult size (not skeletal maturity) justifies our choice of sample replicates. Modeling was conducted in the R Statistical Computing Environment (R Core Team 2024) using the nlme package (Pinheiro et al., 2023; Pinheiro & Bates, 2000).

We used estimated marginal means (package emmeans, Lenth, 2024) from the model to test for pairwise differences in hardness among different corneous regions. We used estimated marginal trends to assess rostrocaudal and tomial gradients in corneous materials hardness, both to test for significant difference from the control measure of acrylic hardness and to assess the overall magnitude and direction of trends. RESULTS

Eight corneous layers were observed across the rhinotheca (Figure 3) and gnathotheca (Figure 3). (2019). In the transverse sections, we identified the outer layer of the rhinotheca from Urano et al. (2019) to have two parts separated by a distinctive birefringence with CBP‐A as the covering layer of the outer layer, denoted by CBP‐B (Figure 3c,d). (2019) was seen and identified on the histological slides, but not assigned a label and tested here because the area in which it was observed was too small for an accurate indentation to be made. Rhinotheca description

For all three specimens, the inner layer of the rhinotheca, which is the layer present between the intermediate layer and the palatal layer, is only visible within the first ~4 mm or the first 2 to 4 transverse slides (Figure 3c). When the inner layer tapers to an end, the intermediate layer then borders the palatal layer until the intermediate layer tapers and ends around ~50% through the transverse series (Figure 3c–f). In VTPE.AV.0054 and VTPE.AV.0055, the intermediate layer disappears after transverse slide 9 (Figure 3f,g). In VTPE.AV.0057, the intermediate layer disappears after transverse slide 10. Once the intermediate layer is gone, the outer layer then borders the palatal layer for the remaining caudal portion of the rhamphothecal nail. The outer layer comprises two distinctive layers (CBP‐A and B) that differ in their birefringence brightness and HV but have the same extinction pattern under XPL. Based on the brightness of the birefringence, the thinner, more lateral unit (CBP‐A) of the outer layer has a different organization compared with the layer that makes up the majority of the outer layer (CBP‐B) (Figure 3c,d). This CBP‐A and B pattern is mostly consistent from the rostral portion to the caudal portion. CBP‐B tapers out at the more caudal portion of the rhinotheca as the rhinotheca becomes thinner overall (Figure 3g,h). In the more caudal portion of the rhinotheca (e.g., transverse slide 11 in VTPE.AV.0054), CBP‐B is mostly at the apex of the tomia with the majority of the lateral wall of the rhinotheca composed of CBP‐A until slide 15 where CBP‐B is no longer visible (Figure 3h).

Gnathotheca description

The gnathotheca has two identifiable corneous layers on the lateral surface: the outer layer (CBP‐E) and the middle layer (CBP‐F), and an attaching dentary corneous material (CBP‐G). CBP‐E is found on the lateral surface of the gnathotheca (Figure 3e,f). The middle layer, CBP‐F, sits between the outer layer and the dentary corneous layer and is usually at the apex of the gnathotheca tomia. The dentary corneous plate covers the dorsal surface of the bony dentary shelf (Figure 3d). No layer similar to the inner layer of the rhinotheca is observed in the gnathotheca. In VTPE.AV.0054, the middle layer disappears after transverse slide 8, ~6 mm from the rostralmost point of the corneous sheath (Figure 3e,f). In VTPE.AV.0055, the middle layer disappears after transverse slide 9, ~9 mm. In VTPE.AV.0057, the middle layer disappears after transverse slide 11, ~9 mm.

Tomium description

The tomium of the rhinotheca overall becomes mediolaterally broader caudally. The composition of the tomium changes from the rostral portion to the caudal portion as well. At the rostral portion (e.g., VTPE.AV.0054 slide 2, Figures 2a and 3c,d), the tomium has a sharp apex, and it is composed of CBP‐A and CBP‐B (outer layer), CBP‐C (intermediate layer), and CBP‐D (palatal layer), along with the inner layer (not given a corneous material identification label as it was not tested for hardness due to limited visibility, small surface area, and appearing in only one slide per specimen). The majority of the tomium of the rostral portion is composed of CBP‐C. Caudally, in VTPE.AV.0054 slide 8, CBP‐C is significantly smaller, and the tomium is now composed of mostly CBP‐A, CBP‐B, and CBP‐D (Figures 2b and 3e,f). By slide 11 for VTPE.AV.0054, the apex of the tomium is composed of CBP‐A, CBP‐B, and CBP‐D (Figure 3g). CBP‐B is thinner mediolaterally and located more towards the apex of the tomium (Figure 3g). At slide 15 for VTPE.AV.0054, the apex of the tomium is composed of the outer layer (CBP‐A and CBP‐B) and the palatal layer (CBP‐D, Figure 3h). The boundary between the outer layer and the palatal layer is barely visible, but the extinction pattern for the two layers is still somewhat visible and is how we differentiated the two layers in these slides.

The tomium apex of the gnathotheca becomes broader mediolaterally towards the caudal portion. The tomium is composed of CBP‐E, CBP‐F, and CBP‐G, and the composition of the tomia changes throughout the gnathotheca (Figure 2). At the rostral portion of the gnathotheca, the apex of the tomium is composed of all three corneous material layers with the majority of it occupied by CBP‐E and CBP‐F (Figure 3d). At slide 8 in VTPE.AV.0054, CBP‐F is barely observed and makes up the sharpest part of the apex with CBP‐E and CBP‐G making up the lateral and medial portion of the tomium, respectively (Figure 3e). The middle layer (CBP‐F) tapers and disappears ~40–50% of the way through rostrocaudally just like the intermediate layer did in the rhinotheca. As CBP‐F disappears, the tomia is then only composed of CBP‐E and CBP‐G, the outer layer and dentary layer, respectively (Figure 3f,g). As in the rhinotheca, the caudalmost portion of the gnathotheca tomia (e.g., slide 15 in VTPE.AV.0054) is covered by a thin corneous sheath, and the boundary between CBP‐E and CBP‐G is barely visible (Figure 3h).

The best‐fit model for CBP‐C and CBP‐F is similar but necessarily excludes the rostrocaudal axis term. The internal layers of corneous material across the dorsal surface of the upper beak or culmen (both rostral and caudal portions of CBP‐B) also have a relatively high hardness value. The caudal half of corneous materials around the tomia (caudal portions of CBP‐D, CBP‐E, and CBP‐G) show broad ranges of hardness and consistent trends of decreasing hardness as they extend caudally (Figure 4). The external layer around the culmen (CBP‐A) and corneous material of the palatal surface (CBP‐D2) are both comparatively soft throughout their respective lengths.

DISCUSSION

Our observations confirmed the corneous rhinotheca layers seen by Urano et al. (2019). The rhinotheca has three identifiable lateral corneous layers—the outer, intermediate, and inner layers—and an attaching palatal corneous layer (Figure 3). The attaching palatal corneous plate is made up of two distinct regions in which the more lateral region (CBP‐D) is more heavily cornified and tested with a higher HV than the more medial region (CBP‐D2, Figure 3d–h). The tomium of the gnathotheca has a pattern similar to that of the rhinotheca.

Mechanical resistance of the corneous sheaths

Our data corroborate the findings of Urano et al. (2019) that the rhinotheca is not one continuous structure but is composed of sandwiched corneous layers. We show new data supporting that the gnathotheca is made up of this sandwiched corneous layers structure as well, though the gnathotheca is simpler and has fewer corneous layers than the rhinotheca. Under XPL, each of the corneous layers has their own extinction pattern, indicating that the layers potentially have both different corneous fiber orientations and a mixture of corneous beta proteins compositions (Urano et al., 2019). We show that the gnathotheca mechanical resistance as a multi‐layered structure is comparable to the rhinotheca mechanical resistance based on the reported hv and its corneous layers having different orientations based on the observation that the various gnathothecal corneous layers have their own extinction patterns, similar to the rhinotheca observed structure (Sun et al., 2010; Urano et al., 2019).

Because our specimens are fully enveloped in methylmethacrylate, the hardness values found in this study likely do not reflect the true hardness of the materials and may instead be influenced by both the corneous materials and methylmethacrylate. However, because all the specimens utilized here were treated and fixed in the same manner, we can reasonably compare relative hardness among the specimens and within a single specimen. Though we did see the internal bone structure of the beak on the histological slides (Figure 3), which matched previous descriptions of the beak internal structure (van Hemert et al., 2012), we decided not to microindent the bone because the trabecular structure made it difficult to determine whether the microindentor sampled the bone itself, the methylmethacrylate, or both. Additionally, we aimed the microindentor to be in the mediolateral middle of the corneous material layer that we tested and avoided the edges of the corneous layers because: (1) we did not want to accidentally test for the hardness of two corneous layers simultaneously or their boundaries; (2) when possible, we wanted to leave space around the microindentation mark to account for material deformation.

Differences in hv among corneous layers show that there are regions across both the rhinotheca and gnathotheca that are not statistically different from each other, meaning that both the rhinotheca and gnathotheca share similar mechanical resistance to abrasive wear. Significantly higher hv are recorded in areas corresponding to where we would expect the rhamphotheca to incur the most mechanical stress: the rostral portion and the tomium (Table 1). The rostral portion of the rhamphotheca is where object manipulation mainly occurs in birds (Rico‐Guevara et al., 2019); it was hypothesized and shown in this study that corneous layers in the rostral portion are harder and more mechanically resistant than those in the caudal portion of the rhamphotheca.

The tomia of the rhinotheca and gnathotheca also have similar hardness and mechanical resistance. The tomia were expected to have high hv because of their functionality as the sharp edge of the beak for food processing and object manipulation. The tomium as a structure is not consistent in what corneous region comprises it (Figure 3), yet this structure consistently maintains a higher hv than the surrounding areas.

Inner layer and its function

The inner layer is a corneous unit that is specific to the rhinotheca, and a similar layer is not found in the gnathotheca (first reported by Urano et al., 2019). In our transverse sections, the inner layer tapers out within the first four slides (4000 μm), and Urano et al. (2019) (Figure 3c) showed the inner layer can be seen to span mediolaterally across the rostral tip of the premaxilla. This supports that the inner layer of the rhamphotheca does not extend rostrocaudally through the entire rhinotheca but instead seems to be present at only the most rostral portion of the rhinotheca. In chickens, the upper jaw protrudes farther rostrally than the mandible, so when they forage for food, the tip of the premaxilla would be the first area to contact the ground (Figure 3b). The location of the inner layer might indicate that it is providing reinforcement to the tip of the premaxilla and/or protection to the sensitive sensory region at the tip of the premaxilla, the bill tip organ during foraging. Even though chickens do have a bill tip organ (Gentle & Breward, 1986), we are unable to test whether the inner layer is contributing to the protection or enhancement of the bill tip organ with our sample alone. Adding birds with prominent bill tip organs such as kiwis and ducks would provide a more extensive test of our hypothesis (Cunningham et al., 2013, du Toit et al., 2024).

Osteological relationships of rhamphothecal structure

The caudal extent of specific rhinothecal tomial corneous materials (CBP‐C) corresponds to the caudalmost extent of the premaxillary neurovascular foramina at the junction of the nasal and maxillary processes of the premaxilla (Figure 3). This spatial relationship could conceivably represent afferent innervation under this corneous region by maxillary rostral branches of the medial branch of the ophthalmic division of the trigeminal nerve (CN V1) and/or a dedicated vascular supply for the tomial epidermis that is shielded from compression between the hardened rhamphotheca and the underlying bony rostrum (Baumel, 1993). Either case might provide an osteological correlate for the presence of a hardened corneous region at the rostral tip of the rhamphotheca. This osteological correlate provides an additional bit of topological information to address the homology of simple rhamphotheca. While additional studies that relate tomial corneous material hardness and bone morphology in other extant taxa are needed to determine whether this is a consistent osteological correlate, this relationship can also provisionally be used to model the extent of hardened tomial corneous regions for extinct taxa.

Rhamphothecal growth and its effect on mechanical resistance

Harder corneous material can be attributed to displacement of the corneous material from its growth site, where the older corneous material is harder than more recently formed corneous material. This is comparable to feathers, where the distal portion of the feather sheath and feather tissues are more dehydrated as the feather tissues grow, mature, and die (Wang et al., 2016). Both intermediate filamentous keratin and corneous beta proteins (formerly known as alpha‐ and beta‐keratin, respectively) show a decrease in Young's modulus (=stiffness) and strength when there is an increase in water in the protein matrix, and avian feathers and claws show the same pattern (Bertram & Gosline, 1987; Fraser & Macrae, 1980; Taylor et al., 2004). Dehydrated, older, rostral corneous material is likely to have a higher Young's modulus and therefore greater hv. We assume that a similar growth and dehydration process happens to the rhamphotheca as happens in the feather because they are both corneous structures. Therefore, the most rostral portion of the rhamphotheca (=the most distal portion from the area of growth) is likely to be more dehydrated and have a higher Young's modulus and thus strength than the more caudal portion of the rhamphotheca. The regional differences within the Young's modulus of a feather can also be attributed to the different regional compositions of feather corneous beta proteins, intermediate filamentous keratins, and other proteins produced by the Epidermal Differentiation Complex gene families (Alibardi, 2017, 2025; Spearman & Hardy, 1985). This same regional difference owed to different mixtures of proteins among regions can also be reasonably applied to the rhamphotheca. Testing for the exact proteinaceous mixtures of different rhamphothecal regions might answer this question, but potentially convoluted due to intrinsic (what genes are active, health of the animal, etc.) and extrinsic (food/nutrient availability, environment, etc.) factors that are difficult to control in a study.

Given that the simple rhamphotheca forms one continuous sheath from hatching, determining homology between different simple rhamphothecae across different avian families is difficult and would need to rely on the microstructure and potential mechanical properties. Hieronymus and Witmer (2010) mentioned that the development of a simple rhamphotheca likely follows the simplification of the bony rostrum, but how the bony rostrum develops and how it affects the rhamphotheca development is unclear. A nanoindentation study might be able to parse out the distinct hardness difference within each corneous layer and how it relates to the corneous growth patterns. We recognize that there are at least 20 corneous beta protein genes that can be active within the Gallus genome and the interplay between what genes are coding for what protein and depositing that protein where might be important for how these different corneous layers that we are observing (Alibardi, 2017). However, that is currently out of the purview of this study but would serve to greatly enhance our understanding of how the continuous corneous sheath develops.

In this study, we demonstrated and documented the varying mechanical properties within the simple rhamphotheca, providing the first step in future correlation among other simple rhamphothecae. As simple rhamphothecae likely arose from compound rhamphothecae, how the corneous layers found in simple rhamphothecae correlate with the morphology of corneous plates in compound rhamphothecae will be crucial to further understanding of the convergent evolution of the corneous sheaths and the drivers for a continuous corneous sheath over multiple corneous plates. Whether compound and simple rhamphothecae have varying mechanical properties due to their external morphology has yet to be tested and might reveal underlying selective pressures for simple rhamphotheca development and repeated evolution.
