Autism-linked protein POSH balances NMDAR signaling via a self-limiting kinase-scaffold loop
Authors: Minghui Yao, Shuo Wang, Feng Zhang, Lei Shi, Zhiheng Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryPOSH (plenty of SH3 domains), an autism-linked scaffold protein, is essential for N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (NMDAR) complex assembly, and its loss causes ASD-like social deficits in mice. However, the synaptic mechanism remains unclear. Here, we reveal that POSH loss reduces SRC postsynaptic enrichment and weakens the interactions within the SRC-NMDAR-PSD-95 complex. Mechanistically, NMDAR activation triggers RAC1-GTP-dependent recruitment of POSH to membrane, leading NMDAR-induced activation of SRC, a key positive regulator of NMDARs. Notably, prolonged NMDAR activation induces the depletion of both POSH and SRC, establishing a negative feedback loop. This dual spatiotemporal mechanism—transient kinase activation coupled with scaffold degradation—constitutes a self-limiting circuit that prevents NMDAR hyperexcitation. Our findings establish POSH as a molecular rheostat that integrates RAC1-driven membrane targeting with SRC activation to precisely regulate NMDAR signaling. These insights would advance our understanding of synaptic homeostasis and may inform potential therapeutic strategies for ASD and glutamatergic disorders.

Introduction

Autism spectrum disorders (ASDs) are strongly associated with synaptic dysfunction. Mutation or deletion of ASD linked genes, including genes encoding N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (NMDAR) GRIN1 or scaffolding protein POSH (plenty of SH3 domains) have been shown to disturb synaptic signaling and ASD-like phenotypes.1,2,3,4,5,6 Our previous studies have shown that POSH, a multidomain scaffold protein enriched at postsynaptic densities (PSDs), orchestrates the assembly of the NMDAR/PSD-95/SHANK supramolecular complex.7 In addition, POSH undergoes liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) and co-condenses with SHANK2/3 to shape dendritic spine morphogenesis.8 Posh knockout mice exhibit destabilized synaptic NMDAR clusters, impaired dendritic spine maturation, and pronounced autism-relevant behaviors, as well as learning and memory deficits.7 POSH deficiency produces aberrant NMDAR-mediated excitatory postsynaptic currents (EPSCs) and impaired long-term potentiation (LTP) at hippocampal CA1 synapses,7 mirroring patient data, which indicates that NMDAR hypofunction contributes to cognitive impairment in ASD.5,9 Although the role of POSH in JNK signaling has been documented,10,11 its regulatory mechanism in synaptic signaling remains to be elucidated.

SRC family kinases (SFKs), with SRC being the prototypical member, modulate NMDAR activity by phosphorylating tyrosine residues within the C-terminal tails of GluN2A and GluN2B.12,13,14,15 SRC binds the NMDAR complex via interactions with ND2 (NADH dehydrogenase subunit 2)16,17 and PSD-95.18,19 SFKs share a conserved architecture in which an N-terminal myristoylation motif20,21,22 and polybasic cluster anchor the kinase to the membrane,23 while the SH3, SH2, and catalytic domains orchestrate conformational switching.24,25 At rest, SRC is autoinhibited by the engagement of its SH2 domain with phosphorylated Tyr527.26 NMDAR activation triggers the displacement of this inhibitory interaction by calcium/calmodulin, enabling autophosphorylation at Tyr416 (activation loop) and subsequent kinase activation.12,27 Once activated, SRC phosphorylates GluN2A and GluN2B, thereby increasing channel open probability and calcium permeability.14 Conversely, C-terminal Src kinase (Csk) was recruited to SRC-phosphorylated subunits within the NMDAR complex, where it inactivates SRC, thereby stabilizing synaptic strength under baseline conditions.28,29 Therefore, SFKs act as bidirectional regulators, potentiating NMDARs during LTP induction30,31 while also constraining overactivation via negative feedback.32

Here, we show that POSH controls the affinity between SRC and the NMDAR complex, and that loss of POSH markedly decreases SRC abundance at the PSD. NMDA stimulation drives the RAC1-GTP-dependent recruitment of POSH to the plasma membrane, which occurs alongside SRC conformational activation. Prolonged NMDAR activation triggers the depletion of both POSH and SRC, thereby establishing a self-limiting feedback loop. This dual spatiotemporal mechanism ensures the precise regulation of NMDAR activity.

Results

POSH couples SRC to the NMDAR complex and SRC activation

As POSH deficiency destabilizes the NMDAR complex,7 we investigated whether loss of POSH affects the recruitment of SRC to this complex. We found that the association of SRC with the GluN2A subunit of NMDAR and PSD-95 was reduced by 75% ± 2% and 85% ± 3%, respectively, in Posh-cKO mice compared to their littermate controls (Figure 1A). Reciprocal co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) experiments revealed a 67% ± 5% and 84% ± 4% reduction in the GluN2A-PSD-95 association in Posh-cKO mice (Figure 1A). Endogenous reciprocal Co-IP confirmed the association between POSH and SRC in hippocampal lysates (Figure 1B). Subcellular fractionation revealed a 38% ± 5% reduction in SRC levels in PSD-enriched fractions from Posh-cKO mice, whereas minimal effects were observed in total homogenate lysates (Figure 1C). To investigate the role of POSH in modulating NMDAR function through SRC, we utilized a well-established model of recombinant NMDAR expression in HEK293 cells.12 Functional NMDARs (GluN1/GluN2A) were co-expressed alongside an empty vector, Flag-POSH, Myc-SRC, or both. NMDA stimulation (50 μM for 5 min) increased SRC Tyr416 phosphorylation only when POSH and SRC were co-expressed (Figure 1D, 2.9 ± 0.3 vs. 1.0 ± 0.1). Furthermore, we treated primary hippocampal neuronal cultures with NMDA to activate synaptic NMDARs preferentially. While NMDA stimulation induced a robust increase in phosphorylation of SRC at Tyr416 (pY416) in Poshf/f neurons, this response was severely blunted in Posh-cKO neurons (Figure 1E, 2.37 ± 0.16-fold increase in F/F vs. 1.11 ± 0.08 in cKO, p < 0.001). Moreover, NMDA stimulation failed to induce phosphorylation of the SRC downstream target GluA1 at Ser831 (pS831), a critical marker for synaptic strengthening, in Posh-cKO neurons (Figure 1E, 2.04 ± 0.19-fold increase in F/F vs. 1.12 ± 0.15 in cKO, p = 0.0036). To validate the role of POSH in SRC activation under a more physiological paradigm, we employed a glycine-mediated chemical LTP (cLTP) protocol. While cLTP induction robustly increased SRC pY416 levels in Poshf/f neurons, this effect was completely abolished in Posh-cKO neurons (Figure 1F). This result firmly establishes that POSH is essential for coupling physiological patterns of synaptic activity to SRC kinase activation. Taken together, these data establish POSH as a potent facilitator of NMDAR-induced SRC activation.

GTP-RAC1 mediates NMDA-dependent POSH membrane targeting and subsequent SRC activation

Using surface biotinylation in HEK293 cells expressing NMDAR, we sought to biochemically assess the NMDAR-dependent recruitment of POSH and SRC to the plasma membrane. A brief NMDA stimulus (50 μM for 5 min) significantly increased the level of biotinylated POSH at the cell surface (Figure 2A, 1.30 ± 0.03-fold relative to untreated controls). To identify the region critical for POSH membrane targeting, we generated progressive POSH truncations and assessed their membrane association upon NMDA stimulation (Figure 2B, [amino acid] aa 203–892, 1.36 ± 0.08; aa 257–892, 1.41 ± 0.05; aa 363–892, 0.78 ± 0.04; and aa 459–892, 0.40 ± 0.07). The membrane accumulation of wild-type (WT) POSH was significantly enhanced by NMDA stimulation, whereas deletion of the RAC1-binding domain (aa 257–362, mutant Δ257–362) abolished this increase (Figure 2C, 1.82 ± 0.17 for WT, 0.60 ± 0.07 for Δ257–362). Accordingly, the Δ257–362 mutant failed to enhance SRC pTyr416 levels upon NMDA stimulation (0.81 ± 0.05 vs. 1.48 ± 0.09 for WT, Figure 2C), suggesting that POSH binding to RAC1 and POSH membrane anchoring are prerequisites for SRC activation. Similarly, the Δ363–458 mutant showed no increase in membrane accumulate and SRC pTyr416 upon NMDA stimulation (Figure 2C), indicating that residues 363–458 are indispensable for stimulus-dependent membrane targeting and SRC activation.

We next visualized this recruitment event using confocal microscopy. In NMDAR-expressing cells, NMDA stimulation triggered the synchronous translocation of both POSH and RAC1 to the plasma membrane within 5 min (Figure 2D). Co-expression with constitutively active RAC1-V12 resulted in robust colocalization of POSH and RAC1 at the plasma membrane (Figure 2E, Pearson’s r = 0.94 ± 0.02), whereas dominant-negative RAC1-N17 or POSH Δ257-362 mutant abolished this enrichment (Figure 2E). These data demonstrate that GTP-RAC1 is required for NMDA-dependent POSH membrane recruitment.

Conformational state of SRC determines its interaction with POSH

Co-IP experiments showed that truncations up to aa 363 bound to SRC at a level comparable to WT POSH (Figure 3A, 1.43 ± 0.63). In contrast, truncation to aa 459 significantly reduced binding to 0.45 ± 0.08, and truncation to aa 510 completely abolished the interaction. Therefore, aas (363–510) of POSH are likely essential for POSH–SRC association. We then investigated whether the membrane affinity or conformational state of SRC dictates its association with POSH. We constructed SRC mutants G2A (myristoylation-deficient, membrane association-impaired, and closed conformation) and Y527F (constitutively open and increased membrane-affinity). Compared with WT SRC, SRC Y527F exhibited a significant reduction in POSH binding (Figure 3C, 0.5 ± 0.1), while the binding level of SRC G2A was not significantly different from that of WT SRC (Figure 3C, 1.2 ± 0.2). Therefore, POSH appears to preferentially recognize the closed, auto-inhibited conformation of SRC, independent of SRC’s membrane affinity.

POSH and SRC undergo depletion following NMDAR activation

We went on to inspect the expression of POSH and SRC following prolonged NMDAR activation in primary hippocampal neurons. Prolonged NMDA stimulation (20 μM, 120 min) reduced the levels of POSH and SRC proteins to 0.51 ± 0.06 and 0.50 ± 0.12, respectively (Figure 4A). In HEK293 cells expressing NMDAR, NMDA stimulation decreased the half-life of SRC to 50.2 ± 9.1 min when co-expressed with POSH, compared to 72.4 ± 11.7 min for SRC alone (Figure 4B). POSH abundance also decreased concurrently (t1/2 = 85.38 ± 15.34 min), compared to POSH expressed alone (t1/2 > 120 min). POSH depletion depended on its ubiquitination-active RING finger motif and was blocked by a proteasome inhibitor (MG132) (Figure 4B). In contrast, SRC depletion was blocked by chloroquine (CQ), an inhibitor of lysosomal degradation (Figure 4B, right). Consistently, CQ treatment caused a marked accumulation of SRC within LAMP1-positive lysosomes, as demonstrated by a significant increase in the Manders’ coefficient M2 (fraction of SRC overlapping with LAMP1) from 0.34 ± 0.02 to 0.66 ± 0.02 (p < 0.001, Figure 4C). Taken together, these data establish a model in which NMDAR activation triggers the proteasomal degradation of POSH and the lysosomal degradation of SRC.

POSH with deletion of RAC1 binding domain (aa 257–362) completely abolished NMDA-induced depletion of both POSH and WT SRC (Figure 4C, left, t1/2 > 120 min for each protein). These data suggest that NMDA-induced POSH membrane targeting is required for subsequent POSH and SRC co-degradation. Disrupting POSH-SRC binding using mutants that are unable to interact, such as POSH Δ363-458 or SRC Δ8-39, also abolished the NMDA-induced depletion of both proteins (Figure 4C, t1/2 > 120 min for each mutant). The membrane-anchoring mutant SRC G2A exhibited delayed turnover (Figure 4C, right, t1/2 > 120 min), whereas the open-conformation mutant SRC Y527F was degraded more rapidly (Figure 4C, right, t1/2 = 42.3 ± 6.8 min). These data demonstrate that SRC degradation is primarily driven by its open conformation. Co-expressed POSH levels remained unchanged in both the SRC G2A and SRC Y527F mutant groups, indicating that membrane-anchored POSH-SRC interactions are essential for NMDAR-triggered POSH degradation (Figure 4C).

Taken together, these data establish that the membrane recruitment of POSH induced by RAC1 is essential for the conformation-dependent co-degradation of the POSH-SRC complex after NMDAR activation, forming a self-limiting feedback loop.

Discussion

Our findings indicate that POSH promotes the affinity of SRC to the NMDAR complex and subsequent SRC activation and thereby potentiating NMDAR function (Figure 5). Mechanistically, NMDA activation recruits POSH to the membrane via RAC1-GTP. At the membrane, POSH promotes the conversion of SRC to an active conformation to enhance NMDAR function. Subsequently, both POSH and SRC are degraded to prevent excessive activation. Dynamic, conformation-dependent regulation of SRC by POSH

The non-catalytic SRC segment (aas 40–58) is associated with NMDA receptors and modulates their activity.16,27 Here, we demonstrate that POSH binds to an adjacent motif (aa 8–39) regardless of SRC membrane affinity, as binding to the myristoylation-deficient G2A mutant exhibits WT levels (Figures 3B and 3C). Quantitative analysis revealed that the POSH (257–892) truncation exhibited enhanced SRC binding (Figure 3A). This is likely due to the truncation at residue 257 disrupting the multidomain module (intrinsically disordered regions, IDR, aa 57–139; tandem SH3 domains, aa 140–257) required for LLPS,8 thereby increasing the pool of POSH available for SRC interaction by shifting the equilibrium away from condensation. This potential mechanism, however, does not detract from our core conclusion that the essential SRC-binding domain lies within aas 363–510.

Our data show that POSH exhibits markedly reduced binding to the constitutively active SRC (Y527F mutant; Figure 3C), indicating its preference for the closed, autoinhibited conformation of SRC. Based on this preference and established Ca2+/calmodulin-dependent SRC activation, we propose a sequential model: POSH first scaffolds inactive SRC, recruiting it to the NMDAR/PSD-95 microenvironment—consistent with complex destabilization upon POSH loss (Figure 1A). Within this localized context, NMDAR-induced Ca2+ influx promotes SRC activation and conformational opening. This model is strongly supported by reconstitution in HEK293 cells, where NMDA-induced membrane recruitment of POSH-SRC (Figures 2A–2C) and the essential role of their interaction for SRC activation (Figure 2C) define a core NMDAR-RAC1-POSH-SRC cascade (Figures 2D and 2E). Subsequent SRC conformational opening reduces its affinity for POSH, releasing active SRC to phosphorylate targets like NMDARs. This dynamic cycle culminates in the degradation of both proteins after sustained stimulation (Figure 4), forming a coherent spatiotemporal regulatory module.

RAC1 couples NMDAR activation to the POSH-SRC module

How is this POSH-SRC module recruited and activated specifically upon NMDAR stimulation? Our findings reveal a novel function of RAC1 in regulating SFK activity by targeting POSH to the membrane (Figure 2). NMDAR-dependent Ca2+ influx activates RAC1 via established effectors, including Tiam1,33 TGase,34 and PKCα,14,35 promoting its translocation to the plasma membrane in a GTP-bound state. This process aligns with the well-documented role of RAC1 in cytoskeletal remodeling during neuronal migration36 and integrin signaling.37,38 We demonstrated that membrane-localized RAC1-GTP is necessary and sufficient to recruit POSH and SRC to the plasma membrane (Figures 2C–2E). This recruitment is supported by biochemical data from surface biotinylation, which captures transient NMDAR-dependent events. However, the assay primarily reflects dynamic recruitment rather than stable complexes, as validated by controls excluding intracellular biotinylation and NMDAR-independent signals. Our human embryonic kidney (HEK) data confirm that NMDAR activation is sufficient to trigger this RAC1-dependent pathway. Although we cannot rule out associations with other membrane proteins, this limitation is mitigated by the fact that our results are consistent with direct imaging data showing co-localization of POSH and RAC1 at the membrane. Thus, RAC1 establishes a feedforward loop that enhances POSH membrane recruitment and SRC activation (Figure 2), expanding its role beyond spine morphogenesis.39 Given the implication of POSH,6,7 and RAC1 regulators40,41 in ASD susceptibility, and the hypothesized dysfunction of NMDAR signaling in certain ASD forms, our discovery of the POSH-RAC1-SRC pathway offers a potential molecular mechanism for synaptic dysregulation.

Although the co-IP approach may preferentially capture dynamic or extrasynaptic interactions more effectively (see limitations of the study), the synaptic relevance of the POSH-SRC regulatory axis is robustly supported by converging evidence from complementary assays. This evidence includes the finding that POSH loss reduces SRC in isolated PSDs (Figure 1C), impairs synaptic signaling (GluA1 pS831, Figure 1E), and abolishes both SRC activation in cLTP (Figure 1F) and hippocampal LTP.7 Therefore, our data collectively demonstrate that this mechanism is critical in regulating SRC at the synapse to control NMDAR function.

Dual spatiotemporal regulation of NMDAR activity

Maintaining NMDAR activity within a certain dynamic window is essential for excitatory-inhibitory balance, calcium homeostasis, and the prevention of excitotoxicity.42 Positive feedback (e.g., SRC-mediated phosphorylation of NMDAR subunits) must therefore be counterbalanced by precise negative feedback. We found that NMDA stimulation triggers POSH-mediated SRC activation, followed by a significant reduction in the protein levels of both POSH and SRC (Figures 2A and 4). Co-expression with POSH reduced SRC half-life following NMDAR activation, whereas disrupting their interaction prolonged it (Figure 4D). This suggests that their association is a prerequisite for both activation and the subsequent depletion. Notably, membrane-nonlocalized SRC mutants exhibited prolonged half-lives when co-expressed with POSH, whereas the constitutively active SRC mutant Y527F was degraded more rapidly than WT SRC (Figure 4D), indicating that SRC degradation follows a conformational rule. Mechanistically, MG132-mediated proteasome inhibition selectively stabilized POSH, whereas lysosomal inhibition with CQ—but not MG132—prevented SRC loss (Figures 4B and 4C). These data reveal that the coupled turnover of the POSH-SRC complex occurs via two distinct pathways. POSH is routed to proteasome, while SRC is targeted to the lysosome upon NMDAR activation. This degradation-based brake mechanism complements the well-established inhibitory role of PSD-95, which directly suppresses SRC kinase activity through SH2 domain binding to prevent NMDAR overactivation in stable synaptic states.18 Together, these findings reveal a multi-layered regulatory network, while PSD-95 provides immediate, tonic inhibition of SRC to constrain basal NMDAR signaling, the POSH-SRC degradation loop adds a dynamic, activity-dependent negative feedback layer that ensures long-term synaptic homeostasis after sustained stimulation.

Our findings reveal that beyond the established modulation of SRC through phosphorylation,15 NMDAR activation elicits an additional layer of control at the level of protein-abundance. Given our previous findings that loss of POSH alters NMDAR currents, we propose that POSH-mediated SRC activation, coupled with turnover of both proteins, serves to reset NMDAR function after synaptic activation (Figure 5). This regulatory mechanism may be critical for synaptic plasticity, preparing the synapse for subsequent stimulation, and maintaining neuronal homeostasis and stability.

Limitations of the study

One technical limitation of our study concerns the detergent used for Co-IP. NP-40 efficiently extracts extrasynaptic complexes but solubilizes the PSD poorly. Consequently, our co-IP data (Figure 1A) probably represent protein interactions that are enriched in the extrasynaptic pool rather than in the stable PSD core, limiting direct biochemical characterization of stable synaptic complexes with this method. Additionally, while our data strongly support that membrane-localized RAC1-GTP recruits the POSH-SRC module upon NMDAR activation, we cannot fully rule out its potential associations with other membrane proteins beyond the NMDAR complex. This limitation is mitigated by imaging data showing co-localization and the convergence of evidence from functional assays.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animals

Posh cKO mice were described previously (Yao et al.). Posh flox/flox mice were generated by Beijing Biocytogen Co., Ltd.. Posh cKO mice were generated by crossing Posh flox/flox homozygous mice with Nestin-Cre mice (Jackson Laboratory, B6.Cg (SJL)-TgN(Nes-Cre)1Kln). All experimental procedures involved were performed according to protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Institute of Genetics and Developmental Biology (IGDB), Chinese Academy of Science. The approval number for this study is IACUC-AP2022035.

For biochemical assays, including co-immunoprecipitation and subcellular fractionation, brain tissues from 12- to 14-week-old adult mice were used. Both male and female mice were included in the study; data were not stratified or analyzed by sex, and thus potential sex-related influences on the findings cannot be determined.

Primary neuron cultures

Hippocampal neuron cultures were prepared following our previously described procedures. Briefly, hippocampi from E16.5-E18.5 mice embryos were dissected and dissociated with 0.125% trypsin in Hank’s balanced salt solution without Ca2+ and Mg2+ for 15 min at 37°C and then seeded onto the poly-L-lysine coated coverslips in DMEM supplemented with 10% F12 and 10% FBS. The medium was changed to the serum-free Neurobasal (Gibco) medium supplemented with 2% B27 (Gibco) supplement and GlutaMAX (Gibco) 1 h after plating and was half replaced with fresh medium every 4 days.

Cell line

Human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293T cells (ATCC, CRL-3216) were used for heterologous expression and biochemical experiments. Cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2.

Method details

NMDA treatment in primary hippocampal neurons

Primary hippocampal neurons (DIV14-16) from Poshf/f or Posh-cKO mice were pre-incubated with 1 μM tetrodotoxin (TTX) and 50 μM DL-2-amino-5-phosphonopentanoic acid (DL-AP5) in Mg2+-free extracellular solution for 30 min at 37°C. Following pretreatment, neurons were exposed to NMDA/glycine (20/1 μM) in Mg2+-free extracellular solution for 5 min at 37°C to specifically activate synaptic NMDARs. After treatment, neurons were immediately placed on ice, washed with ice-cold PBS, and harvested for subsequent biochemical analyses.

cLTP induction

Primary hippocampal neurons (DIV 14–16) from Poshf/f or Posh-cKO mice were first pre-incubated for 30 min at 37°C in Mg2+-free extracellular solution containing 20 μM bicuculline (Bic) and 50 μM DL-AP5 to block GABAA receptors and NMDARs, respectively. Subsequently, neurons were subjected to chemical LTP induction by replacing the pretreatment solution with Mg2+-free extracellular solution supplemented with 200 μM glycine and 20 μM Bic for 5 min at 37°C. After induction, the glycine solution was replaced with standard Mg2+-containing extracellular solution including 20 μM Bic, and neurons were returned to the incubator for designated time points (e.g., 0, 5, 10, 15, 20, or 30 min) before harvesting for protein analysis.

Cell culture and transfections

HEK 293T cells were transfected using VigoFect (Vigorous) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After cotransfection with GluN1 and GluN2A plasmids, the culture medium was changed after 2–3 h and the selective NMDA receptor antagonist DL-AP5 (200 μM) was added to protect HEK293T cells from NMDA receptor-mediated excitotoxicity. Cells were collected and lysed 18–20 h after transfection, at which time there was no apparent degradation of proteins, or cell death.

Surface biotinylation

HEK293T cells were cotransfected with a mixture containing GluN1, GluN2A, and empty vector or POSH and SRC wild type or mutants’ plasmids. After 18–20 h of transfection, cells were surface biotinylated for 20 min on ice using Ez link sulfo-NHS-SS-Biotin (Pierce). After incubation, cells were washed once with PBS, and the unbound biotin was quenched via two 8 min incubations with quenching buffer (20 mM glycine in PBS). Lysis was performed by mechanical scraping in ice-cold lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 50 mM NaF, 1% NP-40, 20 mM EDTA, 0.1% SDS). After rotating for 30 min at 4°C, lysates were centrifuged for 10 min at 16,000× g at 4 C. Balanced NeutrAvidin Agarose beads (Pierce/Thermo) were added to the supernatant. NeutrAvidin Agarose consists of a specially purified and deglycosylated form of avidin that has been covalently immobilized onto cross-linked beaded agarose and can capture biotinylated proteins specially. Surface proteins and the total cell lysates were then subjected to immunoblot analysis.

Subcellular fractionation and PSD preparation

Membrane and cytosolic fractionations were prepared as described previously (Yao et al.). Hippocampi were isolated from adult mice and homogenized in ice-cold sucrose buffer (0.32 M sucrose, 10 mM Tris, pH 7.4, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM EGTA) supplemented with freshly added protease inhibitors (Complete mini, Roche) and phosphatase inhibitors (phospho - Stop cocktail tablets, Roche). The homogenate was centrifuged at 1,000×g for 10 min at 4°C, yielding pellet (P1) and supernatant (S1) fractions. The S1 fraction was centrifuged at 10,000×g for 15 min at 4°C generating fraction P2 (crude synaptosomal membranes) and S2 (cytosol and light membranes). The P2 was resuspended in 120 μL sucrose buffer and mixed with 8 volumes of 0.5% Triton X-100 buffer (10 mM Tris, pH 7.4, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM EGTA) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. The mixture was homogenized again and incubated on ice for 30 min with gentle agitation, then centrifuged at 32,000×g for 30 min at 4°C using a TL - 100 tabletop ultracentrifuge (Beckman). The Triton X-100 - insoluble PSD protein - containing pellet (PSD) was dissolved in TE buffer with 1% SDS. All fractions were stored at - 80°C until further analysis.

Immunoprecipitation

Frozen hippocampi were homogenized in lysis buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 10 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, 50 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM EDTA, 0.5 mM EGTA, 0.5% (vol/vol) NP-40) supplemented with protease inhibitors and incubated at 4°C for 1 h (Figure 1A). P2 pellets were solubilized in extraction buffer composed of 1% sodium deoxycholate, 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 9.0), and 10 mM EDTA, followed by incubation at 37°C for 30 min. An equal volume of RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% SDS) was subsequently added to the extracts (Figure 1B). S2 fractions were directly combined with an equal volume of RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% SDS) and processed for downstream applications. Solubilized proteins were centrifuged at 16,000× g for 15 min and the supernatant was collected. Antibodies or nonspecific IgG (1 μg) were incubated with Protein A/G PLUS-agarose (Santa Cruz) at 4°C for 2 h, followed by incubation with the high-speed supernatants overnight at 4°C. Precipitates were washed five times with lysis buffer.

HEK293T cells were harvested 24 h post transfection and lysed with buffers (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 100 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA, 1% [vol/vol] NP-40) supplemented with protease inhibitors. Lysates were then centrifuged at 12,000× g for 15 min at 4°C. The supernatants were incubated with anti-Flag beads (Sigma-Aldrich) at 4°C for 2–4 h.

Immunoprecipitates were eluted from the agarose by boiling in 2× SDS Gel loading buffer (100 mMTris-HCl, pH 6.8, 4% SDS, 0.2% romophenol blue, 20% [vol/vol] glycerol, 10% [vol/vol] 2-mercaptoethanol) and resolved by SDS-PAGE and immunoblotted with indicated antibodies. For quantification, the densitometry measurement of each band (ImageJ, NIH, Bethesda, MD) was normalized to that of Tubullin or GAPDH and then averaged from at least three independent experiments. Images have been cropped for presentation.

Fluorescence confocal microscopy

Transfected cells were fixed with 4% PFA in PBS. For antibody labeling, they were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 and immunostained as detailed in the specific figure legend. Confocal images were acquired using a Zeiss LSM 800 confocal microscope with a 60 × 1.40 N A. oil objective lens and exported to ImageJ (NIH, Bethesda, MD).

Immunofluorescence images were analyzed for co-localization using the JACoP plugin in ImageJ. Costes' automatic thresholding was applied to define positive signals for each channel. Manders' overlap coefficients M1 and M2 were calculated, where M1 represents the fraction of LAMP1 signal that co-localizes with SRC, and M2 represents the fraction of SRC signal that co-localizes with LAMP1.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All data are presented as the mean ± SEM except for boxplots, which are depicted as centerline (median), box limits(Q1, Q3), whiskers [max(minimum value, Q1-1.5×IQR), min(maximum value, Q3+1.5×IQR)], where Q1, Q3, and IQR are the first quartile, third quartile, and interquartile range, respectively). For two independent data comparisons, paired or unpaired Student’s t tests were used. For multiple comparisons, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test was used. All statistical analyses were performed using Origin 8.0 software (OriginLab). Statistical values are considered significant when p-values are less than 0.05. For all tests, ∗∗∗, p < 0.001, ∗∗, p < 0.01 or as indicated.
