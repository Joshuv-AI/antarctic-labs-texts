Echocardiography used for treatment in heart failure accompanied by thyroid storm: Case report and literature review
Authors: Su Wang, Yijing Zhao, Yu Yang, Yuting Pan, Kun Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Key Clinical MessageThis case highlights the importance of carefully weighing the benefits and risks of beta‐adrenergic blockade therapy based on symptoms, echocardiography, and BNP values in thyroid storm patients.AbstractThyroid storm is a rare but life‐threatening condition in thyrotoxic patients. The keys to successful management of thyroid storm are early diagnosis, immediate anti‐thyroid medications, and preventing multiorgan failure. We present a case of thyroid storm, acute decompensated heart failure, and atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response. We initiated propranolol to control thyroid storm. Soon after, the patient developed more severe heart failure with decreased ejection fraction (EF). We switched to diltiazem to control tachycardia, but the therapeutic effect was unsatisfactory. Finally, we used an ultra‐short‐acting beta‐adrenergic blockade with strict monitoring of heart rate and echocardiography, and the patient survived. Beta‐adrenergic blockades should be used cautiously in thyroid storm, especially patients with severe heart failure. Echocardiography can be used to aid in selection and monitoring of therapeutic drugs and prognostic outcomes in patients with thyroid storm and heart failure.

INTRODUCTION

Thyroid storm is the most serious and life‐threatening emergency in patients with hyperthyroidism, with a mortality rate of 10%–30%. 1 , 2 It sometimes occurs in patients with untreated or inadequately treated hyperthyroidism and is usually triggered by severe physical or mental stress. It represents the extreme manifestation of thyrotoxicosis with severe circulatory, gastrointestinal, hepatic, and neurological dysfunction. Among patients with thyroid storm, approximately 40% had features of congestive heart failure (CHF), 3 and CHF occurred in 21% of patients who died. 1 Therefore, how to control CHF quickly and effectively is helpful in reducing the risk of death from thyroid storm. To date, physicians continue to have difficulty treating this condition. Evaluating the severity of CHF with thyroid storm in order to timely adjust treatment is still conventional. In our case, we used echocardiography to guide drug selection for treating CHF at different stages of thyroid storm and ultimately saved the patient's life.

CASE HISTORY AND EXAMINATION

A 32‐year‐old woman was emergently admitted to our hospital with chief complaints of palpitations and dyspnea for 3 days and a coma for half an hour. She had a 3‐year history of Grave's disease, previously treated with methimazole, but discontinued medication over a year prior for pregnancy preparation. There was no regular follow‐up after drug withdrawal. 1 month ago, the patient was diagnosed with atrial fibrillation by physical examination, with a heart rate of approximately 160 beats per minute. However, she refused treatment. 3 days ago, the patient experienced chest tightness and did not seek medical attention. 1 day ago, the patient self‐administered 20 mg of propranolol hydrochloride without any improvement in symptoms.

A physical examination upon admission revealed the following: blood pressure 120/99 mmHg, heart rate 163 beats per minute, rectal temperature 39.7°C, respiratory rate 25/min, and oxygen saturation 91% on room air. She had an exophthalmos. Her thyroid gland was diffusely enlarged. Cardiac auscultation revealed irregular heart sounds. Moist rales were auscultated over the lower lung fields. Edema in the lower extremities was also present.

INVESTIGATION AND TREATMENT

Given the patient's critical symptoms in the emergency room, before blood test results were released, the emergency department doctor considered the patient to have thyroid storm and administered tracheal intubation, nasal feeding of 600 mg PTU, nasal feeding of 40 mg propranolol, and intravenous infusion of 40 mg methylprednisolone. Simultaneously, the hemodialysis room was notified and plasma exchange treatment was prepared. The patient's blood pressure dropped to 80/69 mmHg after above treatment, she was sent to the intensive care unit (ICU) for further treatment.

After the patient was admitted to the ICU, blood testing revealed markedly suppressed thyroid‐stimulating hormone (TSH) and elevated free triiodothyronine (FT3) and free thyroxine (FT4) levels, markedly elevated N‐terminal pro‐brain natriuretic peptide (NT‐pro BNP) and myoglobin, slightly elevated troponin I levels, and elevated white blood cell count. Electrocardiogram (EKG) on admission showed atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response. Chest computed tomography (CT) showed enlarged heart, multiple exudates in both lungs, a small amount of effusion in the pericardial cavity, and bilateral pleural effusion. Echocardiogram showed left ventricular (LV) ejection fraction (EF) of 46%, left atrial enlargement, normal LV size and thickness, mild mitral regurgitation, and mild tricuspid regurgitation. Subsequent thyroid sonography showed a diffuse lesion in the thyroid gland. In addition, laboratory workup on admission showed total bilirubin of 52.9 μmol/L (direct bilirubin: 32.2 μmol/L), aspartate aminotransferase of 4320 IU/L, alanine aminotransferase of 4955 IU/L, and international normalized ratio (INR) of 2.53. Ultrasound and computed tomography scan of the abdomen revealed fatty liver, with no intra‐ or extrahepatic biliary dilation. Negative hepatitis virus and autoantibody test results indicated that severe acute liver failure was accompanied by thyroid storm.

At this point, she was diagnosed with thyroid storm by Burch Score (95 points), 4 acute decompensated heart failure, atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response, and severe liver dysfunction. We used propranolol with 20 mg three times daily to slow her heart rate. Additionally, propylthiouracil and hydrocortisone were administered to treat hyperthyroidism, and cedilanid was used to manage heart failure.

Despite the initiation of medical treatment, her condition deteriorated. We must use norepinephrine to maintain her blood pressure. On the second day of admission, echocardiography showed that EF decreased to 37%. To compensate for metabolic acidosis and remove congestion, continuous hemodiafiltration (CHDF) was simultaneously performed. On the third day of admission, the patient's symptoms did not improve significantly. We stopped propranolol and used diltiazem hydrochloride to control the heart rate. On the fifth day of admission, her blood pressure was stable and vasoactive drugs were withdrawn, but her heart rate was still very fast. We used diltiazem hydrochloride and dronedarone to control her heart rate. The patient's blood pressure and corresponding medication are shown in Figure 1. On the thirteenth day, the serum free thyroxine levels decreased from 41.75 nmol/L to 22.91 nmol/L, with elevated NT‐pro BNP levels (from 8725 to 532 mIU/mL). Aspartate aminotransferase decreased to 48 IU/L. Alanine aminotransferase decreased to 167 U/L. Then, she was transferred to Endocrinology ward. Although the thyroid function of the patients has been improved obviously, the heart rate of the patients was not well controlled. On the thirteenth day, we used esmolol intravenous infusion to control heart rate. The heart rate was stable and no decreased blood pressure occurred. Since then, we have gradually switched to metoprolol succinate to control heart rate. On the 27th day, the heart rate of the patient was controlled between 66 and 73 beats/min. Echocardiogram showed left ventricular ejection fraction of 60%. The size of atrium and ventricle was normal. The symptoms were well controlled and patient was subsequently discharged home on medical treatment. She was followed up regularly after discharge and showed signs of improvement and was advised to continue with her medication.

OUTCOME AND FOLLOW‐UP

One month later, we followed up for her clinic appointment. Examinations showed normal FT3, FT4, and low TSH. Echocardiography showed no obvious abnormality with 68% LVEF. Patient remained asymptomatic during half‐year follow‐up post‐discharge.

DISCUSSION

Due to more frequent screening and greater attention to thyroid diseases, leading to earlier diagnosis of hyperthyroidism and decreasing prevalence of thyroid storm. However, the high mortality of thyroid storm still confuses many clinical endocrinologists. Among the most common causes of death in patients with thyroid storm, heart failure ranks second only to multiple organ failure. 1 , 3

Generally, heart failure manifests as decreased cardiac contractility, abnormal diastolic compliance and reduced cardiac output. Heart failure caused by hyperthyroidism differs from that caused by other reasons. 5 , 6 Hyperthyroidism is characterized by low TSH level and elevated T4, T3, or both. As a major thyroid hormone acting on the heart, T3 can increase the force and speed of systolic contraction and the speed of diastolic relaxation, leading to heart failure with features of enhanced cardiac output and contractility. 7 , 8 This state has been called “high‐output heart failure.” An untreated high‐output state and hyperthyroidism can lead to ventricular dilation, persistent tachycardia, and eventual CHF with low output. 7 , 8 Hyperthyroidism can also aggravate underlying coronary disease, hypertensive or valvular disease or other heart diseases, and further lead to fatal heart failure. In the case of thyrotoxic heart failure or atrial fibrillation, most patients are asymptomatic for heart failure within 6 months, 9 and over half of the patients return to sinus rhythm within 6 months. 10 , 11 However, 6% of patients with hyperthyroidism presented with CHF as an initial clinical symptom. 12 And, for these patients, although thyroid function and LVEF improved after treatment for hyperthyroidism, one third of patients developed persistent hyperthyroid cardiomyopathy. 12

BNP and NT‐pro BNP are commonly used to evaluate the severity of heart failure. 13 , 14 In vitro experiments, BNP secretion was increased 6‐fold, and BNP mRNA levels were increased 3‐fold with T3 treatment from cultured myocytes. 15 A study including 101 patients without heart failure showed that plasma levels of NT‐pro‐BNP were significantly increased in subjects with hyperthyroidism compared to healthy controls. 16 It is necessary to consider cardiac function and thyroid function together when analyzing BNP values in thyroid storm patients.

In terms of observing therapeutic effects, echocardiography is superior to BNP. In our case, after the use of propranolol, the patient's EF significantly decreased. We adjusted drugs timely according to echocardiography and finally improved cardiac function in patients. However, on the fourteenth day of admission, the BNP was still high despite improved heart failure. The decrease in BNP might occur later than the improvement of clinical symptoms. 17 This delay could potentially cause confusion for doctors trying to comprehensively evaluate a patient's heart function.

Echocardiography is also of great significance for drug selection in patients with hyperthyroidism and heart failure. Different from BNP, echocardiography can directly provide left ventricular ejection capacity and ventricular cavity size. A study showed that half of the hyperthyroidism patients who presented with heart failure had normal EF. It is hypothesized that their symptoms may be caused by increased sodium and fluid retention. Tachyarrhythmia may also be a reason of diastolic heart failure. 12 These patients responded well to treatment with diuretics and beta‐adrenergic blockades. 18 In addition, for the other half of hyperthyroidism patients whose EF has decreased, 12 inappropriate beta‐adrenergic blockade treatment may aggravate their clinical symptoms. 19

Among patients with current or previous symptomatic heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), the use of beta‐adrenergic blockades may be considered to reduce the risk of HF hospitalization and cardiovascular mortality. 20 However, beta‐adrenergic blockades are contraindicated in all cases of tachycardia‐induced acute heart failure and cardiogenic shock. Some endocrinologists often use beta‐adrenergic blockades to control tachycardia and decrease T4‐to‐T3 conversion in patients with thyrotoxicosis, ignoring the possibility of tachycardia‐induced acute heart failure and cardiogenic shock. 21 The negative inotropic effect of beta‐adrenergic blockades can further exacerbate heart failure. Abubaker et al. reviewed a total of 11 thyroid storm cases developing circulatory collapse with the use of beta‐adrenergic blockades. 22 In addition, Ikram observed that, after 2 mg of intravenous propranolol, cardiac output decreased 30% from normal to subnormal levels, and venous pressures were increased in patients with thyrotoxic cardiac failure. 23 It is suggested that in patients with thyrotoxic cardiac failure, the excited state of the sympathetic nervous system and hyperadrenergic state induced by thyroid hormone play a compensatory role in maintaining cardiac output state. 23 The use ofβ‐blocker in this circumstance may break the compensatory mechanism and cause a significant fall in cardiac output and further lead to hemodynamic instability. 23 In addition, patients with thyrotoxic cardiac failure might have subclinical cardiomyopathy, which may also increase the risk of the exaggerated response to β‐blockade. 23 In our case, after the use of propranolol, the symptoms of heart failure worsened. We used echocardiography to rapidly capture the decrease in EF value, and observed the enlargement of the atrium and ventricle.

Considering the decreased EF value after using propranolol, we changed diltiazem hydrochloride to control tachycardia. After adjusting the treatment, the patient's blood pressure stabilized and the EF value gradually returned to normal. However, the patient's heart rate remained very fast. In fact, cessation of propranolol improved resultant hemodynamic instability, but was accompanied by uncontrolled tachycardia. Esmolol is an intravenous cardioselective β‐1 adrenergic antagonist with a short half‐life (9 min) used for the treatment of supraventricular tachycardia. 24 These advantages of esmolol may allow for much safer dose adjustment in control of tachyarrhythmia in thyroid storm patients in comparison with conventional drugs such as digoxin, diltiazem, and other b‐blockers. Esmolol was carefully titrated to control tachycardia without hypotension in this case. Our case showed that complete withdrawal of β‐blocker therapy may not be possible in patients with hyperthyroidism due to uncontrolled tachycardia. Ultra‐short‐acting β‐blockers may be a reasonable alternative for these patients due to their short half‐lives. It is easier to titrate dose and rapidly withdraw the β‐blocking effect after discontinuation of therapy. Although there are more advantages of Ultra‐short‐acting β‐blockers than propranolol, we could not ignore its underlying risk. To date, there is one report of circulatory collapse with intravenous esmolol use, 25 and one case report with landiolol. 26 Patients with thyroid storm require close monitoring, especially of ejection fraction, blood pressure, and heart rate when using propranolol. In our case, during the use of esmolol, the blood pressure of the patient was stable and the heart rate was gradually controlled within the normal range. Additionally, we utilized echocardiography to monitor the EF value of patients, and observed no significant decrease in EF value.

Echocardiography can not only be used to evaluate cardiac function before treatment and assess the therapeutic effect, but also be recommended to observe the prognosis of patients. In our case, the patient's condition improved with EF value increasing from 35% to 60%. Similarly, Umpierrez et al. followed seven thyrotoxic patients with congestive heart failure, finding a mean LVEF increase from 28% to 55% after ATDs therapy. 27 This suggests that some patients with hyperthyroidism might have congestive heart failure due to reversible cardiomyopathy. Early use of echocardiography to diagnose these rare and potentially reversible cases is important to improve low‐output heart failure and patient outcomes.

Based on our case, we recommend that patients with thyroid storm undergo echocardiography as soon as possible. There are several reasons for this recommendation. First, heart failure often presents with non‐specific signs and symptoms, overlapping with other clinical issues. Patients with acute decompensation are often unable to provide a complete medical history or chief complaint immediately. In addition to clinical symptoms and signs, we also recommend using echocardiography to assist in the diagnosis of heart failure. Second, due to many factors affecting blood pressure and heart rate, and delayed BNP improvement relative to symptomatic improvement, we recommend using echocardiography to track heart failure improvement in patients. Third, patients with hyperthyroidism and heart failure have different responses to beta‐adrenergic blockades based on their EF values. Those with normal EF values have better therapeutic effects with beta‐adrenergic blockades, while those with decreased EF values may face life‐threatening consequences with beta‐adrenergic blockades. Therefore, echocardiography is recommended before selecting medication. Lastly, this patient represents heart failure with improved EF, and echocardiography beneficially tracks prognosis, informing whether long‐term heart failure drug therapy is needed.

In conclusion, echocardiography should be checked as soon as possible when considering thyroid storm, before using β‐blockers and throughout treatment for all thyroid storm patients. Propranolol may not be a wise choice for patients with thyroid storm complicated with heart failure. But, if the tachycardia cannot be well controlled by other drugs, ultra‐short‐acting β‐blockers maybe an alternative under careful titration and the strict monitor of echocardiography.
