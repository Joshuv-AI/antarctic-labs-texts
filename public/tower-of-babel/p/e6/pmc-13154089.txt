Coronary artery anomalies are defined as an abnormal origin and/or course of 1 or more epicardial coronary arteries.1 They represent a rare congenital condition, with an incidence ranging from 0.17% to 1.2% in patients undergoing coronary angiography.2 Most coronary artery anomalies are detected incidentally during coronary angiography. Nevertheless, coronary anomalies are considered the second most common cause of sudden cardiac death in young athletes.3 The prognostic implications of coronary artery anomalies remain poorly understood, and guideline-recommended therapeutic decisions are supported by a low level of scientific evidence. Management of asymptomatic patients with coronary anomalies may be challenging, particularly in younger individuals.4,5Take-Home Messages•In case of suspected coronary anomalies or unclear coronary course and/or origin, a computed tomography angiography is strongly recommended.•Do not rush into a complex procedure without careful analysis of the angiogram and planning for the procedure.

Case Presentation

A 68-year-old female patient presented to our heart center for elective coronary angiography. She had been experiencing angina pectoris for the past 4 months before admission. The pain was described as compressing and sometimes stitching chest pain without radiation, occurring intermittently at rest and on exertion. The patient sought medical advice and presented herself to a cardiologist, who performed a resting electrocardiogram and echocardiography that showed no abnormalities. The patient was a heavy smoker for more than 25 years and has dyslipidemia. Notably, she had a history of a double left kidney.

Baseline laboratory investigations showed a serum creatinine level of 0.66 mg/dL, a glomerular filtration rate of 95 mL/min, a low-density lipoprotein level of 73 mg/dL, and a hemoglobin A1C level of 6%. An electrocardiogram at rest showed sinus rhythm at 95 beats/min, a normal axis, and no significant ST-T changes (Figure 1). The first differential diagnosis was an ostial RCA-chronic total occlusion (CTO) with retrograde filling from a large septal branch from the left anterior descending coronary artery (LAD), and we decided to plan for elective percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) and recanalization of the RCA-CTO. A meticulous evaluation of the angiographic views by our CTO/complex high risk indicated PCI team during planning for PCI revealed that the RCA flow seems to be an antegrade rather than retrograde flow, contrary to the initial assumption. Tracing the course of the large second septal branch demonstrated that it follows the anatomical course of the RCA.

The decision was made to perform a computed tomography coronary angiography (CTA) to confirm the diagnosis and exclude a malignant course of the suspected anomalous RCA. In addition, an abdominal CT was performed to evaluate the abdominal organs for any associated congenital anomalies.

CTA confirmed the diagnosis, demonstrating that RCA was originating anomalously from the mid-segment of the LAD. The anomalous RCA runs first between the 2 great arteries (aorta and pulmonary arteries), then anterolateral in the groove between the right atrium and right ventricle, and curved posterior to reach the posteroinferior wall between the left and right ventricles (Figure 4). Our plan was to perform a stress imaging to evaluate for stress-induced ischemia and to assess the patient risk. Regadenoson stress cardiac magnetic resonance was performed and revealed no evidence of stress-induced ischemia with normal left ventricular morphology and function, without any wall motion abnormalities. Late gadolinium enhancement did not show any evidence of myocardial ischemia or previous myocarditis (Video 4). A few months later, we performed dobutamine stress echocardiography without any evidence of stress-induced ischemia. So we decided for conservative treatment and control of the risk factors.

Discussion

Coronary artery anomaly is a rare congenital anomaly. However, the widespread use of invasive and noninvasive coronary imaging for the assessment of ischemic heart disease has increased the reported prevalence of coronary artery anomalies in adults.1 The course of epicardial arteries and the distribution of their branches may show significant interindividual variability in the general population. Various phenotypes have been identified as normal variants, such as anterior origin of RCA, separate LAD, and left circumflex artery ostia, which are distinguished from coronary anomalies by their higher prevalence (>1% among unselected individuals) and not having been associated with any adverse events.6

We present a case of a single coronary artery, particularly a subtype of abnormal RCA origin from the midportion of the LAD. A reported incidence of this subtype is 0.009%, and mostly from the proximal part of the LAD.7 Two courses of the RCA were previously described in case reports, mostly anterior to the pulmonary artery, and more rarely, a retroaortic course.8, 9, 10, 11

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case of a malignant interarterial course anomalous RCA originating from the mid-LAD. A malignant course of the RCA between the aorta and the pulmonary artery is commonly reported in the abnormal origin of the RCA from the left main coronary artery.

Given the rarity of this RCA anomaly, our initial suspicion was directed toward a more common scenario—an RCA CTO with retrograde collateral flow from the left coronary system. Nevertheless, this diagnosis seemed unlikely, as the coronary arteries appeared angiographically normal, without evidence of stenosis or atheromatous disease, which is uncommon in the presence of a CTO. On meticulous review of the coronary angiogram, it became evident that the RCA flow was antegrade rather than retrograde.

Guidelines recommend always a CTA if coronary anomalies are suspected.4,5 In our case, CTA not only easily clarified the diagnosis, but also delineated the course of the RCA. It is also an important clinical point in cases of CTOs with an ambiguous origin or course to perform a CTA for accurate planning for PCI, as well as to exclude any anomalies.

Although coronary anomalies are the second most common cause of sudden cardiac death in young athletes,3 till now there is no strong evidence for the management of symptomatic patients. In our case, with a malignant interarterial course of the RCA, a stress imaging test was mandatory for further evaluation and risk stratification. The guidelines recommend nonpharmacological functional imaging (eg, nuclear study, echocardiography, and cardiac magnetic resonance with physical stress) in patients with coronary anomalies to confirm or exclude myocardial ischemia.

Conclusions

An RCA arising from the mid-LAD with an interarterial course is an exceptionally rare finding that can easily mimic complex coronary pathology. In any case of suspected coronary anomaly, coronary CTA serves as an invaluable tool for confirmation and anatomical clarification. Ultimately, careful angiographic interpretation remains the key to avoiding unnecessary procedures and guiding appropriate therapy.
