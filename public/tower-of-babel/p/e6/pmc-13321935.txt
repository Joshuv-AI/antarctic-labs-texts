Prevalence and Prognostic Role of Nutrition Impact Symptoms in Patients With Gastrointestinal Cancer: A Multicenter Cohort Study
Authors: HE-YANG ZHANG, MING YANG, YI-ZHONG GE, QI ZHANG, XI ZHANG, MENG TANG, ZI-WEN WANG, MENG-MENG SONG, GUO-TIAN RUAN, XIAO-WEI ZHANG, TONG LIU, HAI-LUN XIE, CHEN-AN LIU, JIN-YU SHI, KANG-PING ZHANG, XIAO-YUE LIU, SHI-QI LIN, YUE CHEN, XIN ZHENG, HAN-PING SHI, XIAN-JUN QU
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/AimPatients with gastrointestinal cancer (GIC) often experience prevalent and severe malnutrition. Nutrition impact symptoms (NIS) can cause malnutrition but have not been fully addressed in GIC. This study comprehensively evaluated the prevalence and prognostic value of NIS in GIC.Patients and MethodsBaseline and laboratory characteristics of 6,732 patients with GIC were obtained from the INSCOC study. NIS includes inappetence, dysphagia, pain, nausea, early satiety, astriction, vomiting, xerostomia, diarrhea, dysgeusia, dysosmia, and mouth ulcer. The association between NIS and overall survival (OS) was determined using Cox regression analysis. NIS that showed independent prognostic values were included in subsequent analyses and defined as GIC-prognosis-related NIS (GICPNIS). GICPNIS score and weighted GICPNIS score were formulated based on the number and weight of GICPNIS, respectively.ResultsOf 6,732 patients, 2,877 patients had NIS. Multivariate analysis showed that inappetence [hazard ratio (HR)=1.20, 95% confidence interval (CI)=1.09-1.32], nausea (HR=1.31, 95%CI=1.16-1.47), vomiting (HR=1.40, 95%CI=1.22-1.60), dysphagia (HR=1.47, 95%CI=1.31-1.64), early satiety (HR=1.35, 95%CI=1.18-1.54), and pain (HR=1.53, 95%CI=1.29-1.81) were independent prognostic factors of patients with GIC (all p<0.001). Weighted GICPNIS score could better discriminate OS than GICPNIS score.ConclusionAmong patients with GIC, 42.7% experienced various NIS. Inappetence, nausea, vomiting, dysphagia, early satiety, and pain were independent prognostic factors of patients with GIC. Weighted GICPNIS score could better discriminate OS than GICPNIS score.

Introduction

Gastrointestinal cancer (GIC), which includes esophageal cancer (EC), gastric cancer (GC), liver cancer (LC) and colorectal cancer (CRC), is the most common type of cancer worldwide (1). GIC is responsible for approximately 26% of cancer incidence and more than 33% of cancer-related deaths globally, which places a significant socioeconomic, psychological, and physical burden on patients (1).

Patients with GIC often experience prevalent and severe malnutrition, which is partly caused by nutrition impact symptoms (NIS) and associated with poor prognosis (2). NIS refers to symptoms that prevent adequate nutritional intake, such as inappetence, dysphagia, pain and nausea (3, 4). NIS was first reported in patients with head and neck cancer (HNC) and has a substantial negative effect on nutrition status and survival (5-7). Notably, patients with GIC show a much higher prevalence of NIS than those with other cancers (2). Gagnon et al. and Amano et al. have reported that the prognosis of patients with intervention for NIS is better than those without intervention (8, 9). Therefore, the clinical evaluation and management of NIS are crucial to improving the prognosis of patients with GIC.

Various countries have included recommendations for managing NIS in their treatment guidelines for patients with cancer. The guidelines of the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) suggest that patients with cachexia frequently experience uncontrolled NIS and are at risk for poor outcomes (10). The European Society of Medical Oncology (ESMO) guidelines recommend screening for NIS in at-risk patients (11). The European Society for Parenteral and Enteral Nutrition (ESPEN) suggests a formal and objective evaluation of NIS (12). The Clinical Oncology Society of Australia (COSA) guidelines demonstrate a multidisciplinary approach to managing NIS (13). However, these guidelines vary in their recommendations to prioritize and manage NIS, and managing NIS is not considered a top priority among the recommendations for patients with GIC.

Therefore, we believe it is necessary to comprehensively evaluate the prevalence of NIS in patients with GIC, explore the association between NIS and survival, and establish a reasonable NIS scoring system. This would provide clinicians with more specific guidelines to evaluate and manage NIS, ultimately improving the prognosis of patients with GIC.

Patients and Methods

Study population. The main methods, results, and a specific description of the Investigation on Nutrition Status and Clinical Outcome of Common Cancers (INSCOC) study have been previously published (14). In short, the INSCOC study is a multicenter cohort study that included over 40 hospitals, and the assessments were performed between January 1, 2012, and October 31, 2020, for the enrolled patients (15). The inclusion criteria of patients were as follows: (i) patients aged 18 years or older; (ii) a pathological diagnosis of cancer; (ii) willingness and ability to provide written informed consent; (iv) consciousness throughout treatment with no communication disorders. The exclusion criteria of patients were as follows: (i) pregnant women; (ii) acquired immune deficiency syndrome; (iii) mental or cognitive impairment; (iv) organ transplant recipients. Cases in which patients required more than two hospitalizations during the study were considered single cases.

The INSCOC study was approved by the Medical Ethical Review Committees and Institutional Review Boards of the participating registered hospitals and conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was registered in Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (http://www.chictr.org.cn) on 24/12/2018 (registration number: ChiCTR1800020329). All patients signed the informed consent.

In this study, 7765 patients with EC, GC, LC or CRC were enrolled from the INSCOC study. We excluded 1,033 patients with missing or inaccurate data for one or more critical variables: A total of 27 patients with inaccurate age data and 1,006 patients with missing TNM stage data (Figure 1).

Procedures for selection of patients with gastrointestinal cancer (GIC) from the Investigation on Nutrition Status and Clinical Outcome of Common Cancers (INSCOC) study.

Patient characteristics. Oncologists and nutritionists with relevant project training interviewed and evaluated all patients. Within 48 h of admission, the following demographic and clinicopathological data were collected: sex, age, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), cancer site, smoking status, alcohol consumption, TNM stage, history of surgery, radiotherapy and chemotherapy, serum albumin levels (g/l), neutrophil counts (×109), lymphocyte counts (×109), neutrophil to lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and prognostic nutritional index (PNI). We recorded and assessed the 12 symptoms, including inappetence, dysphagia, pain, nausea, early satiety, astriction, vomiting, xerostomia, diarrhea, dysgeusia, dysosmia, and mouth ulcer, based on the Patient-Generated Subjective Global Assessment (PG-SGA) and defined as NIS (2).

A patient was considered a smoker if they smoked more than 20 cigarettes in their lifetime. A regular drinking habit over the past year defined alcohol consumption. TNM staging followed the guidelines of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (16). Indicators mentioned above were calculated as follows: BMI=weight ÷ height2; NLR=neutrophil count ÷ lymphocyte count; PNI=serum albumin level + 5 × lymphocyte count (17, 18).

Outcome. All cause death was defined as the primary outcome of patients with GIC. The overall survival (OS) was defined as the interval between the first clinical evaluation and the last clinical evaluation or the patient’s death, which was investigated by phone call or outpatient.

Statistical analysis. Patient characteristics are expressed as simple numbers and percentages or median and interquartile range (IQR). The chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used to assess categorical variables. Student’s t-test was used to assess continuous variables with normal distribution, and the Mann-Whitney test was used to assess continuous variables with non-normal distribution.

A radar chart was used to visualize the prevalence of NIS in all patients and different subgroups, with NIS ranked according to their prevalence. Hazard ratios (HR) and the related 95% confidence interval (CI) from three analysis models (Models 1, 2, and 3) used in this study were determined applying univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses. Model 1 was not adjusted for any covariates, while Model 2 was adjusted for sex, age, BMI, and TNM stage, and Model 3 was adjusted for sex, age, BMI, TNM stage, smoking status, alcohol consumption, history of surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy.

Stratification analysis was conducted for the association between NIS and the prognosis of patients with GIC under different stratifications. Sensitivity analysis was conducted after excluding patients with a survival time of less than one year to eliminate causal inversion. The indicators NLR and PNI were used to reflect the inflammation level and nutrition status of patients with GIC, respectively (17, 18). The role of inflammation level and nutrition status in the association between NIS and prognosis was also studied using mediation effect analysis. The principle of mediation effect analysis is shown in Supplementary Figure 1.

A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.1.3, developed by R Core Team, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Patient characteristics. Of all the enrolled patients, 4590 (68.2%) were men. The median age of the patients was 61 years (IQR=53-68 years), and 2,354/6,732 patients (35.0%) were in TNM stages IV. The average follow-up period was 26.88 months, during which 2,567 deaths occurred. Patients with upper GIC had a higher proportion of men, smokers, and drinkers, a higher prevalence of NIS, a lower proportion of those undergoing surgery and chemotherapy, a higher age, and lower BMI and PNI compared to patients with lower GIC. Prognostic evaluation of NIS in all patients and subgroups. Multivariate analysis showed that inappetence (HR=1.20; 95%CI=1.09-1.32; p<0.001), nausea (HR=1.31, 95%CI=1.16-1.47, p<0.001), vomiting (HR=1.40, 95%CI=1.22-1.60, p<0.001), dysphagia (HR=1.47, 95%CI=1.31-1.64, p<0.001), early satiety (HR=1.35, 95%CI=1.18-1.54, p<0.001), and pain (HR=1.53; 95%CI=1.29-1.81, p<0.001) were independent prognostic factors for patients with GIC, while mouth ulcer, astriction, diarrhea, xerostomia, dysgeusia, and dysosmia were not (all p>0.050) (Supplementary Table I). Based on these results, inappetence, nausea, vomiting, dysphagia, early satiety, and pain were defined as GIC prognosis-related NIS (GICPNIS) and were enrolled in further analyses.

Prognostic role of GICPNIS, GICPNIS score, and weighted GICPNIS score in all patients and stratifications.The prognostic role of GICPNIS, GICPNIS score, and weighted GICPNIS score was investigated in all patients with GIC and various stratifications. Multivariate analysis showed that GICPNIS (HR=1.53, 95%CI=1.41-1.66, p<0.001) was independently associated with patient prognosis. The results of stratification analysis indicated that the association between GICPNIS and OS was consistent across all stratifications, including (men vs. women), age (<65 years vs. ≥65 years), BMI (<24 kg/m2 vs. ≥24 kg/m2), TNM stage (I-III vs. IV), smoking status (Yes vs. No), alcohol consumption (Yes vs. No), cancer site (upper GIC vs. lower GIC), NLR (<3 vs. ≥3), PNI (<45 vs. ≥45), surgery (Yes vs. No), chemotherapy (Yes vs. No), and radiotherapy (Yes vs. No) (Supplementary Figure 10).

To explore the association between the number of GICPNIS and OS of patients with GIC, a NIS scoring system called “GICPNIS score” was constructed. GICPNIS score was calculated as follows: score 0=no GICPNIS, score 1=one GICPNIS, score 2=two GICPNIS, and score 3=three or more GICPNIS. In patients with GIC, a higher GICPNIS score (as a continuous variable: HR=1.23, 95%CI=1.18-1.29, p<0.001) was associated with a poor OS (Table II). When GICPNIS score was assessed as a category variable, compared to GICPNIS score 0, score 1 (HR=1.50, 95%CI=1.37-1.64, p<0.001), score 2 (HR=1.59, 95%CI=1.40-1.80, p<0.001), and score 3 (HR=1.63, 95%CI=1.38-1.94, p<0.001) showed higher death risks (Supplementary Figure 11, Table II). The results of stratification analysis suggested that the association between GICPNIS score and OS was stable across all stratifications (Supplementary Figure 12).

Considering that each GICPNIS has a different impact on OS, we further weighted each GICPNIS and constructed the “weighted GICPNIS score”. The weights of GICPNIS, including dysphagia, inappetence, vomiting, early satiety, nausea, and pain, were calculated using a nomogram and determined as follows: dysphagia (100), inappetence (68), vomiting (50), early satiety (43), nausea (31), and pain (29) (Supplementary Figure 13). By adding the weights of GICPNIS, the weighted GICPNIS score was divided into six intervals: score 0=0-<29, score 1=29-<60, score 2=60-<103, score 3=103-<153, score 4=153-<221, and score 5=221-321. Based on the OS differences of each interval evaluated using the Kaplan-Meier survival curve (Figure 3A), we modified the weighted GICPNIS score as follows: score 0=0-<29, score 1=29-<60, score 2=60-<153, score 3=153-<321. As illustrated in Figure 3B, the modified weighted GICPNIS score demonstrated much better discrimination of OS compared to the original score. It was also able to discriminate the OS of patients with GIC better than the GICPNIS score (Figure 3, Supplementary Figure 11).

We observed a positive association between weighted GICPNIS score (as a continuous variable: HR=1.29, 95%CI=1.19-1.29, p<0.001) and OS of patients with GIC. Stratification analysis revealed that the association between weighted GICPNIS score and OS was stable across all stratifications (Supplementary Figure 14).

Mediation effect of inflammation level and nutrition status. In the association between inappetence, nausea, vomiting, pain and OS, inflammation level accounted for 9.87%, 7.17%, 9.36%, and 9.88%, respectively, while nutrition status accounted for 18.70%, 16.80%, 19.00% and 17.36%, respectively (all p<0.001) (Table III). Interestingly, neither NLR nor PNI showed a mediation effect in the association between dysphagia (NLR: prevalence of mediation=0.25%, p=0.840; PNI: prevalence of mediation=1.51%, p=0.420) and OS of patients with GIC.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study with the largest number of participants to comprehensively evaluate NIS prevalence, the association between NIS and OS, and to establish a reasonable NIS scoring system in patients in GIC. First, we found that 42.7% of patients with GIC suffered various NIS during follow-up. Inappetence, dysphagia, pain, and nausea were the four most common NIS suffered by patients with GIC, which differs from the most prevalent NIS in patients with HNC, such as chewing difficulty or dry mouth (7). This difference may be attributed to the underlying cause of NIS. In patients with HNC, NIS frequently occur due to surgery, tumor compression, and local radiotherapy (19). In contrast, NIS in patients with GIC are commonly caused by tumor-induced gastrointestinal dysfunction, tumor obstruction, systemic inflammation, and severe gastrointestinal mucosal toxicity induced by traditional systemic chemotherapeutic agents (such as 5-fluorouracil) (5, 20, 21).

We further investigated the association between each NIS and OS of patients with GIC. Our results indicated that inappetence, nausea, vomiting, dysphagia, early satiety, and pain were independent prognostic factors of patients with GIC. Although inappetence and early satiety are common NIS in patients with GIC, they are often ignored. A variety of factors may contribute to their occurrence, such as tumor burden, treatment response, and toxic reactions from systemic chemotherapy (22, 23). Cytotoxic drugs, while exerting antitumor effects, often trigger systemic inflammatory storms and gastrointestinal smooth muscle dysfunction (21). Inflammation also plays an important role in inappetence and early satiety. Inflammatory factors, such as Interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and tumor necrosis factor (TNF), enhance and modify the medio-basal hypothalamus, creating a paracrine inflammatory milieu that initiates and sustains alterations in the activity of neuronal populations that regulate appetite and food intake, eventually exacerbating inappetence, early satiety, nausea, and vomiting (24, 25). All of these factors that lead to or are caused by inappetence and early satiety can shorten the OS of patients with GIC.

Nausea is an intragastric discomfort and a precursor sensation of vomiting. Vomiting means gastric contents enter the esophagus and are discharged through the mouth, which is a primitive low-threshold brainstem response (26). They are often caused by gastrointestinal tract dysfunction and tumor obstruction in GIC (24, 25, 27). According to the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guideline, most patients undergoing chemotherapy or radiotherapy will suffer nausea and vomiting (28). Particularly for patients with GIC, the toxic catabolites of traditional chemotherapeutic drugs in vivo (such as the degradation products of 5-FU) cause severe damage to the gastrointestinal mucosal barrier, acting as a direct iatrogenic driver that induces key NIS like intractable nausea and frequent vomiting (29). As mentioned earlier, high inflammation levels can also lead to nausea and vomiting (24, 25). These factors that can lead to nausea and vomiting are associated with poor prognosis of patients with GIC (4, 17, 30).

Dysphagia is a common GICPNIS in patients with EC. Boyce et.al demonstrated that as EC progresses, the tumor gradually increases in size, eventually blocking the esophagus (31). Dysphagia in GC and CRC occurs most commonly from tumor invasion or metastases in the brain, oropharynx, and mediastinum (32). Sarcopenia dysphagia reported by Maeda et al. are also common in patients with cancer (33). Furthermore, severe oral and esophageal mucositis induced by chemotherapeutic agents also significantly exacerbates dysphagia in patients (34). Moreover, dysphagia could significantly reduce food intake and lead to severe malnutrition (31). Each condition that can lead to or is caused by dysphagia usually leads to poor OS (2, 35, 36).

According to the ESMO clinical practice guideline, pain of patients with GIC mainly comes from tumor tension, nerve invasion, metastatic and GI obstruction, which usually indicates the progression of cancer, even the end of the patient’s life (37). Meanwhile, peripheral neurotoxicity and gastrointestinal toxic spasms, induced by chemotherapy, often accompanied with cancer pain, are further weakening patients’ willingness to eat and capacity for nutritional intake. Moreover, pain significantly impacts the mental health and life quality of patients with GIC, leading to poor prognosis, and in some cases, even suicide, which is an urgent intervention (37).

We also investigated the associations between GICPNIS, GICPNIS score, and OS in patients with GIC. The negative association between GICPNIS and OS indicated that patients with any one GICPNIS had a higher death risk than those without any GICPNIS (38). Results of GICPNIS score and OS also showed negative associations. Importantly, a higher death risk was observed following a higher GICPNIS score, which implied that patients who exhibit multiple symptoms at the same time have a worse prognosis. In fact, Wang et al. have showed that multiple symptoms interacted with each other and had a greater effect on the survival of patients making clinical treatment more difficult (39). Hence, for patients with a high symptom burden, individualized treatment should be conducted, and the additional aggravating effects of drug toxicity on GICPNIS must be evaluated with extreme caution when formulating chemotherapy regimens. Attention should be given to the relationship and interaction between the multiple GICPNIS to comprehensively reduce the burden of GICPNIS and improve their prognosis (40).

Based on the study by Fu et al. and clinical experience, we believe that each GICPNIS has a different impact on OS (40). Hence, we weighted each GICPNIS and constructed a weighted GICPNIS score. The weighted GICPNIS score showed a better discriminatory ability for the prognosis of patients with GIC, which could provide clear evidence for GICPNIS management. Moreover, clinicians should intervene in an orderly manner according to GICPNIS weight. For high-weight GICPNIS, clinicians should pay more attention to early intervention. The results of stratification analysis and sensitivity analysis showed that our findings were stable and reliable.

We also conducted a mediation effect analysis between GICPNIS and OS of patients with GIC by using NLR to reflect inflammation level and PNI to reflect the nutrition status (41, 42). As demonstrated by Aoyama et al., nutrition and inflammation assessment tools have clinical impact on predicting GIC treatment outcomes (42). Furthermore, Watanabe et al. highlighted that baseline PNI is significantly related to the efficacy of nivolumab monotherapy in patients with GIC (41). Our results indicated that nutrition status has a dominant mediation effect as compared to inflammation level in the association between GICPNIS and OS of patients with GIC. Previous studies have revealed that GICPNIS show a negative effect on the prognosis of patients with cancer mostly by reducing food intake to malnutrition (3, 5, 6). The mediation effect of inflammation level was relatively obvious in pain, inappetence, and vomiting, which are associated with systemic inflammation (24, 25, 37). The highly toxic profile of traditional chemotherapy is a critical trigger driving the vicious cycle of systemic inflammation and destruction of intestinal nutritional absorption (43, 44). Furthermore, Arigami et al. reported that body weight loss during chemotherapy is associated with lower surgical conversion rates and decreased overall survival in patients with advanced GIC (45). A study by Feliciano et al. indicated that systemic inflammation could cause cancer progression and metabolic abnormalities, leading to a shorter OS in patients with GIC (17). A notable finding was that the mediation effects of inflammation level and nutritional status were small in the association between dysphagia and OS, which suggests that dysphagia may directly affect OS of patients with GIC. Yang et al. have reported that large tumor size can cause dysphagia, indicating a heavy tumor burden, late stage of cancer and poor prognosis (30). In terms of clinical management, Watanabe and Oshima reviewed recent treatments for cancer cachexia, noting that current approaches often involve a combination of nutritional support, anti-inflammatory agents, and exercise (46). Hence, our results from the mediation effect analysis indicate that reducing inflammation levels and providing nutritional supplements can partly reduce the impact of GICPNIS on OS and improve the prognosis of patients with GIC.

However, the present study has several potential limitations that are worth mentioning. First, NIS were evaluated only at baseline. More frequent evaluations could enable a more accurate assessment of the association between NIS and OS of patients with GIC. Moreover, because the study findings were obtained based on the sample size determined by the INSCOC study, the findings need to be validated in another study with a larger sample size and considering more confounders. In the future, we plan to conduct more in-depth clinical and laboratory studies to further investigate the underlying mechanisms.

Given that the systemic toxicity of traditional chemotherapeutic drugs (especially gastrointestinal mucosal damage and accumulation of toxic metabolites) is the core iatrogenic factor inducing and exacerbating NIS in patients with GIC, which subsequently leads to malnutrition and shortens overall survival, current clinical treatments face tremendous challenges (29). Therefore, to fundamentally improve the prognosis of patients with advanced GIC, there is an urgent clinical need to develop novel antitumor drugs or targeted delivery systems with lower toxicity that can significantly reduce the incidence of gastrointestinal NIS (such as nucleoside prodrugs with targeted activation characteristics developed using strategies like ProTide technology) (47, 48). By achieving effective decoupling of antitumor efficacy from systemic gastrointestinal toxicity, such novel agents are expected to maximize the preservation of patients’ metabolic and nutritional homeostasis while inhibiting tumor progression, thereby achieving a true survival benefit.

Conclusion

42.7% of patients with GIC suffered various NIS. Inappetence, nausea, vomiting, dysphagia, early satiety, and pain were independent prognostic factors of patients with GIC. Weighted GICPNIS score showed better prognostic discrimination ability than GICPNIS score. Inflammation level and nutritional status had a mediating effect on the association between GICPNIS and OS of patients with GIC. This study further emphasizes that reducing the gastrointestinal toxicity of antitumor treatments is crucial for controlling NIS. Our findings can provide clinicians with more specific guideline to evaluate and manage NIS and clear clinical rationale for the future development of novel targeted antitumor drugs with low toxicity and reduced risk of NIS.
