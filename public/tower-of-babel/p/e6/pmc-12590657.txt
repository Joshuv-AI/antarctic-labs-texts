Sun protection behaviors and knowledge of skin cancer and sun exposure among Emirati undergraduate students
Authors: Huda Anshasi, Hajer Almazrouei, Nojoud Rashed, Reem Salem, Mareyah Suhail, Amal Abdulla
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe aim of this study is to assess the level of knowledge about skin cancer and sun exposure among Emirati undergraduate students and evaluate the sun protection behaviors practiced by these students.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 455 undergraduate students from 17 campuses of one of the largest higher education institutions in the UAE.Data were collected using a structured survey comprising sociodemographic characteristics and two validated scales: the Skin Cancer and Sun Knowledge Scale (SCSKS) and the Sun Protection Behavior Scale (SPBS).ResultsParticipants demonstrated a moderate level of knowledge, with a mean SCSKS score of 13.01. The mean SPBS score was 25.60 out of 40, with notable variation across specific behaviors. The most frequently reported practice was sunscreen use, with 41.3% of participants indicating they “always” used it. In contrast, hat use was among the least practiced behaviors, with 100% of participants reporting they "never" wore a wide-brimmed hat and 41.3% stating they "never" wore a hat at all. Female students reported higher levels of sunscreen use and sun avoidance, while male students were more likely to wear hats. No significant correlation was found between knowledge and sun avoidance behaviors.ConclusionThis study highlights a gap between knowledge and behavior in relation to sun protection. Despite a moderate level of awareness about skin cancer and the risks of sun exposure, students do not consistently engage in protective behaviors. These findings highlight the need for targeted educational interventions that not only raise awareness but also encourage sustained behavioral change. Public health initiatives should adopt culturally relevant, gender-sensitive strategies to encourage the consistent use of multiple sun protection methods.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24988-z.Keywords: Skin cancer, Sun exposure, Sun protection behaviors, Undergraduate students

Introduction and background

Skin cancer ranks among the most prevalent cancers globally and presents a growing public health issue [1]. It is primarily divided into two main categories: malignant melanoma and non-melanoma skin cancers (NMSCs). Malignant melanoma is the most aggressive form due to its high metastatic potential, while NMSC, comprising basal cell carcinoma (BCC) and squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), is more prevalent but less likely to spread extensively [2].

The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that each year, there are between 2 and 3 million new cases of NMSC and at least 132,000 cases of malignant melanoma worldwide [3]. A key contributor to skin cancer development is ultraviolet (UV) radiation, a well-established and modifiable risk factor [4]. Solar UV radiation, recognized as a Group 1 carcinogen by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), remains the primary source of UV exposure in human [5]. The relationship between UV exposure and skin cancer varies by cancer type: BCC and melanoma are associated with intense, intermittent sun exposure and sunburn, whereas SCC is linked to cumulative, long-term sun exposure [6–8]. Given these associations, reducing UV exposure is a key public health priority in skin cancer prevention.

Countries with high UV radiation levels face an increased skin cancer risk [9]. The UAE is particularly vulnerable due to its geographical location and extreme climatic conditions. During peak summer months, temperatures in the UAE often exceed 48 °C, substantially increasing UV exposure, particularly for individuals frequently outdoors without adequate protection [10]. Reflecting this risk, skin cancer is the fourth most common cancer in the UAE, following breast, thyroid, and colorectal cancers [11]. In 2019, skin cancer represented 6.3% of all malignancies recorded in the UAE population [11].

Effective prevention strategies are crucial to reduce skin cancer incidence. The WHO recommends key preventive measures, including the consistent use of broad-spectrum sunscreen with a sun protection factor (SPF) of at least 15, wearing protective clothing, seeking shade, avoiding direct sun exposure when UV radiation is strongest, and refraining from using indoor tanning devices [12]. Implementing these strategies is particularly important in regions with high UV exposure, where consistent adherence to sun protection measures can significantly reduce the risk of skin cancer.

Young adults are particularly vulnerable to UV-related health risks due to increased sun exposure and inadequate protective behaviors [13, 14]. While sun protection behaviors may vary across individuals and populations, young people have a longer remaining lifespan, which provides a greater opportunity to reduce cumulative UV damage through early intervention. Epidemiological studies have shown that excessive UV exposure during early adulthood significantly contributes to the lifetime risk of developing skin cancer [15, 16]. For instance, research conducted in Saudi Arabia has demonstrated that UV exposure in earlier decades is frequently associated with the development of SCC, highlighting the importance of early-life sun exposure in skin cancer risk [15]. This underscores the need to promote sun-safe practices within this age group. Implementing targeted educational and behavioral interventions during early adulthood can play a critical role in mitigating future skin cancer risk [17].

Knowledge gaps and behavioral attitudes toward sun protection also play a critical role in skin cancer prevention. Although studies indicate that some university students possess a basic understanding of UV risks, this knowledge does not consistently translate into protective behaviors. For example, a study conducted among female university students in Saudi Arabia revealed that despite awareness of UV-related health risks, only 25% reported regular sunscreen use [18]. Similar findings were reported in Jordan, where a cross-sectional study found that while medical students demonstrated sound knowledge about skin cancer prevention, a substantial proportion did not consistently practice sun protection measures such as sunscreen use or sun avoidance during peak UV hours [19]. In Lebanon, although nearly all students recognized the link between UV exposure and skin cancer, less than one-third reported always applying sunscreen when exposed to sunlight [20]. These findings underscore a persistent gap between knowledge and practice, reinforcing the need for targeted and culturally tailored health education interventions among young adults.

Given the high levels of UV exposure and the rising incidence of skin cancer in the UAE, it is crucial to explore knowledge and sun protection behaviors among at-risk populations, particularly university students. Although a few UAE-based studies have explored aspects of sun protection, their focus has been limited to narrow domains such as sunscreen use or sun avoidance and involved heterogeneous or non-specific participant samples [21, 22]. For instance, Ibrahim et al. (2021) assessed awareness exclusively regarding the use of sunscreen among a mixed sample of female undergraduates, pharmacy patients, and pharmacists [21]. This heterogeneous sample does not adequately represent university students as a distinct population, limiting the applicability of the findings to this group. Furthermore, the study did not explore broader dimensions of sun protection or skin cancer-related knowledge.

Similarly, Thomas et al. (2010) developed and validated the Sun Avoidance Inventory (SAI) to assess attitudes and behaviors related to sun avoidance [22]. However, their sample included both university students and their non-student female relatives, and the study primarily focused on avoidance behaviors rather than offering a comprehensive assessment of protective practices or skin cancer knowledge. Additionally, as the study was conducted over a decade ago, its findings may not reflect current trends or awareness levels among today’s university student population.

To address these gaps, this study focuses exclusively on Emirati undergraduate students and aims to provide an up-to-date, comprehensive evaluation of both their knowledge about skin cancer and sun exposure, as well as their sun protection behaviors (See Table S1 in the Supplementary Material). This targeted approach offers a more precise understanding of risk-related attitudes and practices within this specific group. The findings will provide insight into the current situation and help guide the development of targeted awareness campaigns and prevention strategies aimed at reducing skin cancer risk in this vulnerable group.

Methods

Study design

A cross-sectional survey design was utilized to explore Emirati undergraduate students’ knowledge about skin cancer and sun exposure, as well as their sun protection practices. The study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines, ensuring clarity, methodological rigor, and reproducibility [23]. The STROBE checklist was used throughout the study design, data collection, analysis, and manuscript preparation to ensure comprehensive reporting of all relevant components, including participant selection, variable measurement, statistical methods, and study limitations.

Participants

The study targeted Emirati undergraduate students enrolled at one of the largest higher education institutions in the UAE that operates across 17 campuses in various regions, including Abu Dhabi, Al Ain, Al Dhafra, Dubai, Sharjah, Ras Al Khaimah, and Fujairah. A non-probability convenience sampling technique was employed, allowing for the efficient collection of data from a readily available population.

The inclusion criteria for participation were as follows: (1) aged between 18 and 29 years, and (2) proficient in reading and comprehending English. Students who met these criteria were invited to complete the survey. The survey reached students across all 17 campuses, representing eight academic divisions: health sciences, education, applied media, business, engineering, technology and science, and computer information science.

The minimum sample size required for the study was calculated using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7), considering a medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.5), a two-tailed significance level (alpha) of 0.05 (5% risk of Type I error), and a statistical power of 0.95 (95% probability of detecting a true effect). The power analysis revealed that a sample size of 289 participants was required to achieve adequate power. To account for potential non-responses or incomplete surveys, an additional 20% was added to the sample size, resulting in a final target sample size of 347 participants.

Data collection

Data collection occurred between October and December 2024. The survey was disseminated via the university’s diffusion center to students’ institutional emails. Eligible students received a secure online questionnaire link and QR code through their institutional email accounts. This method maintained participant anonymity and prevented undue influence, aligning with ethical research standards. To enhance response rates, weekly reminders were sent.

Data was collected using a structured questionnaire comprising three sections with a total of 34 items. The first section, developed specifically for this study by the research team, captured sociodemographic characteristics, including age, marital status, year of study, and academic division (See Supplemental Material).

The second section measured knowledge using the Skin Cancer and Sun Knowledge Scale (SCSKS), a validated instrument developed by Day et al. (2014) that assesses knowledge across five domains: sun protection, tanning, skin cancer risk factors, prevalence of skin cancer, and signs of skin cancer [24]. The scale comprises 25 items: 15 true/false questions and 10 multiple-choice questions. Each correct response is awarded 1 point, and incorrect responses receive 0 points. The total score ranges from 0 to 25, with higher scores indicating greater knowledge of skin cancer and sun exposure. The SCSKS has demonstrated acceptable psychometric properties. Its internal consistency reliability (KR-20) was 0.69, indicating that the items consistently measure knowledge about skin cancer and sun exposure [24]. Test-retest reliability over a two-week period was high (r = 0.83), showing that the scale produces stable results over time [24]. The scale also demonstrates acceptable levels of face, content, and incremental validity [24].

The third section evaluated sun protection behaviors using the Sun Protection Behavior Scale (SPBS), originally developed by Maddock et al. (1998) [25]. This 9-item Likert-type scale measures the frequency of sun protection behaviors when exposed to sunlight for more than 15 min. Responses range from 1 (never) to 5 (always), with higher scores indicating better sun protection practices. The SPBS consists of three subscales: regular sun avoidance (score range: 4–20), sunscreen use (score range: 3–15), and hat use (score range: 2–10). The overall SPBS score ranges from 9 to 45. The SPBS was previously applied to an adolescent population by Maddock et al. [25], and its three-factor structure-comprising sun avoidance, sunscreen use, and hat use-was validated [25].

A pilot study was conducted with 30 students, who were excluded from the final analysis to prevent potential bias, to assess the clarity, validity, reliability, and cultural appropriateness of the survey instrument. Feedback indicated that the items were generally clear and culturally appropriate, with no major concerns reported. Given the limited sample size, formal tests of reliability or validity (e.g., Cronbach’s alpha) were not conducted, as pilot studies are underpowered for inferential statistics and hypothesis testing [26]. A sample of 30 participants may allow only preliminary reliability insights but is insufficient for definitive psychometric validation [27].

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 30. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize categorical variables as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were expressed as means and standard deviations (SD). To assess differences in the SCSKS and the SPBS scores based on demographic variables, independent samples t-tests and one-way ANOVA were performed. Additionally, Pearson’s correlation coefficients were used to assess the associations between the SCSKS scores and the SPBS subscales, including regular sun avoidance, sunscreen application, and hat usage. A p-value < 0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the college's Research and Ethical Integrity Committee (Approval No. SRC-1–15/10/2024). Permission to use the SCSK and SPBS scales was obtained from the authors. Prior to participation, students were provided with an information sheet detailing the study’s objectives, risks, and benefits. Informed consent was obtained electronically; participants were required to click a button indicating their voluntary agreement before proceeding with the survey. They were informed that participation was entirely voluntary and that their responses would remain confidential.

Results

Characteristics of participants

A total of 455 undergraduate students participated, with a response rate of 92%. The mean age was 21.2 years (SD = 2.7), ranging from 18 to 29 years. The majority of participants were female (n = 336, 73.9%) and most were single (n = 443, 97.4%). Regarding the field of study, over one-third (36.3%) were health science students, followed by nearly one-quarter (21.8%) in computer information science, one-fifth (19.6%) in business, 13.0% in engineering technology and science, 5.5% in applied media, and 4.0% in education. In terms of year of study, around 29% were second-year students and a similar percentage were in their fourth year, while about one-fifth were in the first or third year. Discussion

This is the first study to assess the level of knowledge about skin cancer and sun protection behaviors among Emirati undergraduate students. The findings indicate a moderate level of knowledge, with a mean SCSKS score of 13.0. This is slightly lower than scores reported in Germany (M = 15.6) [28] and Turkey (M = 14.9) [29]. The higher scores in these studies may be attributed to the academic backgrounds of the participants, which included a greater proportion of health sciences students who are more likely to engage with skin cancer-related content as part of their curriculum.

In contrast, the present study involved a more diverse sample, with only about 36% of participants enrolled in health sciences programs, while the remainder came from education, applied media, and other disciplines. Students from non-health backgrounds may have had fewer opportunities to engage with skin cancer education, potentially contributing to their lower knowledge levels. These findings highlight the importance of targeted health campaigns that can reach students across all academic disciplines and promote practical sun protection habits.

Regarding sun protection behaviors, participants scored 25.60 out of 40 on the SPBS, with significant variations across behaviors. Notably, sunscreen use emerged as the most frequently practiced behavior, with 41.3% of participants reporting they “always” used sunscreen. In contrast, protective clothing behaviors were less common. Specifically, 41.3% of participants indicated they “never” wore a hat, and 100% reported “never” wearing a wide-brimmed hat, highlighting a significant gap in this form of sun protection.

Interestingly, the data revealed a negative correlation between knowledge and hat use, suggesting that students with higher knowledge scores were less likely to wear hats. This counterintuitive finding may be shaped by cultural clothing norms. In the UAE, traditional clothes such as the gutra for males and the hijab for females offer incidental sun protection for the head and neck. These garments may be perceived as sufficient substitutes for hats, particularly wide-brimmed hats, which are not commonly worn and may be viewed as culturally inappropriate or aesthetically undesirable. This may help explain why increased knowledge does not necessarily result in increased use of hats as a protective strategy.

Moreover, the lack of a significant correlation between knowledge and sun avoidance behaviors suggests that, while students are aware of sun exposure risks, they do not actively avoid the sun during peak hours. This finding aligns with studies conducted among university students in Saudi Arabia [18] and Jordan [19], both of which reported no significant association between skin cancer knowledge and sun avoidance practices. In Jordan, for instance, only 20% of medical students adhered to sun avoidance recommendations despite being aware of the risks associated with UV exposure [19].

However, it is important to note that other studies have demonstrated a positive relationship between knowledge and sun protection behaviors. Research conducted in Turkey revealed that higher levels of skin cancer and sun knowledge were positively correlated with sun protection behaviors, including regular sunscreen use and seeking shade [30]. Similarly, a study conducted among a sample of the Jordanian population found that individuals with higher knowledge about skin cancer and actinic keratosis were more likely to engage in sun protection practices, particularly the use of sunscreen [31].

These contrasting findings suggest that the relationship between knowledge and behavior is complex and may be influenced by various contextual factors, such as cultural norms, perceived susceptibility, and access to protective resources. In the UAE context, traditional clothing like abayas, kanduras, hijabs, and gutras provides incidental UV protection, potentially reducing the perceived necessity for additional sun safety measures. However, reliance on clothing alone may leave exposed areas- such as the face and hands-vulnerable to UV damage. Aesthetic preferences and beauty standards may also discourage the use of wide-brimmed hats or shade-seeking behaviors. Furthermore, social influences, including peer perceptions and family attitudes, significantly impact sun protection practices. Therefore, public health initiatives should go beyond education alone and incorporate culturally tailored strategies that leverage social norms, promote habit formation, and provide visible role models through policy and media-driven interventions.

This study also examined the relationship between demographic characteristics and sun protection behaviors, revealing notable gender differences with meaningful effect sizes. Females reported more frequent sun avoidance (Cohen’s d = 0.34) and significantly greater sunscreen use (Cohen’s d = 0.88) compared to males, indicating moderate to large effects. Previous research has suggested that males often perceive sunscreen use as a feminine behavior, while females are more motivated by aesthetic concerns such as maintaining appearance and preventing skin aging [32, 33].

In contrast, males reported significantly greater use of hats (Cohen’s d = 0.97), reflecting a large effect size. However, despite this gender difference, hat use overall was infrequent. Notably, 100% of participants reported “never” wearing a wide-brim hat, and 41.3% indicated they “never” wore any type of hat. Although statistically higher among males, hat use remains a largely underutilized form of sun protection. Again, cultural clothing practices like the gutra and hijab may contribute to this pattern, as students may rely on traditional clothes rather than adopt additional protective behaviors. These findings suggest that sun safety campaigns should incorporate gender-specific messaging: reinforcing sunscreen use and sun avoidance behaviors among females, while encouraging increased sunscreen use, sun avoidance, and culturally appropriate hat use among males. Future qualitative research is warranted to explore the cultural, social, and practical barriers influencing hat use, which may provide deeper insights to inform culturally tailored sun safety interventions for Emirati students.

To address the identified gaps in knowledge and sun protection behaviors, future interventions should employ culturally relevant and gender-sensitive strategies tailored to Emirati university students. For example, campaigns could leverage social media and peer influence to normalize regular sunscreen use among males, addressing perceptions of it being a “feminine” behavior [34]. For females, strategies could emphasize the aesthetic and health benefits of consistent sun protection. Additionally, educational initiatives could highlight that traditional clothing offers adequate protection while traditional clothing provides partial UV protection, it does not fully shield exposed areas like the face and hands. Interactive approaches, such as visual demonstrations of UV exposure through culturally acceptable tools (e.g., digital skin damage visualizers during campus health events) [35], could make the risks more tangible without conflicting with local norms. These context-specific interventions, designed in collaboration with students and cultural leaders, may enhance acceptability and effectiveness, while future research should evaluate their feasibility and impact.

Specifically, future studies should empirically test these culturally tailored, gender-specific interventions using controlled or quasi-experimental designs to determine their effectiveness in improving sun protection behaviors among Emirati university students.

Limitations and strengths

This study has several limitations that should be considered. First, the use of convenience sampling introduces potential selection bias. Participants who chose to respond may differ systematically from those who did not, potentially inflating observed knowledge levels or sun protection behaviors. This limits the representativeness of the sample and reduces the generalizability of the findings, particularly to non-institutionalized youth or non-student populations in the UAE. Second, the cross-sectional design prevents causal inferences between knowledge and protective behaviors. Third, reliance on self-reported measures introduces the risk of social desirability bias, where participants may overreport desirable behaviors, such as sunscreen use or sun avoidance. Fourth, interpretation of sunscreen use should be cautious, as 41.3% of participants reported “always” using sunscreen, but the survey did not clarify whether this included daily application or reapplication during sun exposure. Fifth, while hat use was extremely low among participants, the study did not capture the underlying reasons for this behavior. Finally, although the study provides important insights, these limitations indicate that the findings should be interpreted conservatively. Future research should use probabilistic sampling, objective measures of sun protection behaviors, and qualitative approaches to explore cultural, social, and practical barriers, which could inform culturally tailored interventions to improve sun protection practices among Emirati students.

Despite these limitations, this study offers several strengths. It targets a high-risk population, university students in the UAE, where sun exposure is high and skin cancer ranks as the fourth most common cancer [11]. The study included a relatively large and diverse sample of 455 Emirati undergraduate students from 17 campuses, providing valuable baseline data that can inform future research and public health initiatives on sun exposure and skin cancer prevention in this population. Moreover, this is the first study of its kind in the UAE, offering unique insights into knowledge levels and sun protection behaviors among Emirati undergraduates.

Conclusion

This study explored skin cancer knowledge and sun protection behaviors among Emirati undergraduate students, revealing a moderate level of knowledge and with notable variation across specific behaviors. Sunscreen use was the most common, while wide-brimmed hat use was notably low. Females reported higher sunscreen use and sun avoidance, whereas males were more likely to wear hats - though overall hat use remained limited. Interestingly, greater knowledge was negatively correlated with hat use, potentially due to reliance on traditional clothing such as gutras and hijabs. The lack of correlation between knowledge and consistent protective behaviors underscores the complexity of health behavior change and the influence of cultural, aesthetic, and social norms. These findings highlight the need for targeted, culturally sensitive interventions that go beyond awareness-raising to promote sustained adoption of multiple sun protection behaviors among university students in the UAE.

Relevance to clinical practice, education, and research

The findings of this study have significant implications for clinical practice, education, and research. From a clinical perspective, healthcare providers, particularly dermatologists, primary care physicians and nurses, should play a proactive role in educating young adults about effective sun protection strategies. Routine health screenings and consultations should include discussions on UV exposure risks and individualized recommendations for sun safety practices.

In education, peer-led initiatives can be particularly effective in promoting sun safety, as students may be more likely to listen to and engage with their peers on health-related topics. Training student leaders to become sun protection advocates can amplify the reach of awareness campaigns. Additionally, public health initiatives should emphasize comprehensive sun protection strategies that encourage both genders to adopt a combination of behaviors rather than relying on a single method.

For research, this study underscores the necessity of developing culturally tailored sun protection campaigns targeting university students in the UAE. Future research should focus on designing and evaluating intervention programs that address behavioral barriers to sun protection. More in-depth studies are needed to understand the underlying factors influencing students’ sun protection behaviors, including cultural norms, lifestyle choices, and gender-specific influences. In particular, future qualitative studies could explore why certain protective behaviors, such as wearing hats, remain underutilized, which may provide deeper insights to inform culturally sensitive interventions. Additionally, longitudinal studies should be conducted to assess whether increased knowledge translates into sustained sun protection behaviors.

By integrating these findings into national skin cancer prevention programs, policymakers and healthcare educators can enhance community awareness and promote long-term behavioral changes to mitigate the growing burden of skin cancer in the UAE.
