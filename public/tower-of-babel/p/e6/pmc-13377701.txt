Anesthetic management of hip fracture surgery in a patient with concurrent cardio-cerebral infarction: a case report

Introduction

Cardio-cerebral infarction (CCI) is an exceptionally rare medical phenomenon characterized by the simultaneous occurrence of acute myocardial infarction (AMI) and acute ischemic stroke (AIS) [1]. However, there is currently no established consensus regarding the optimal management of CCI and the optimal timing for different surgical interventions in patients with CCI. Therefore, the selection of proper surgical timing and appropriate anesthesia strategies becomes particularly crucial for this specific patient population.

Here, we report a 79-year-old female with simultaneous acute right parietal lobe infarction and acute myocardial infarction. She was treated with a multifaceted therapeutic approach including anticoagulation therapy, antiplatelet therapy, lipid-lowering therapy, myocardial remodeling modulation, dehydration therapy for cerebral edema control, and neuroprotective therapy. Following Multi-Disciplinary Treatment (MDT), she underwent femoral head replacement surgery under general anesthesia combined with fascia iliaca nerve block on the 29th day of hospitalization. On the 10th postoperative day, she achieved significant clinical improvement and was discharged from the hospital to continue rehabilitation therapy.

Case report

A 79-year-old female patient with a history of hypertension complained of severe right hip pain, due to a fall incident on the night of August 20th, 2024. She presented to the Emergency Department (ED) approximately 2 h post-injury for evaluation and treatment. The patient’s initial vital signs were recorded as follows: blood pressure of 207/115 mmHg, heart rate of 93 beats per minute, respiratory rate of 14 breaths per minute, body temperature of 36.6 °C, and oxygen saturation of 95% while breathing room air. Computed tomography scan revealed a left femoral neck fracture. Electrocardiogram (ECG) demonstrated pathological Q waves in leads III and aVF (Fig. 1). All other diagnostic investigations showed unremarkable findings. The patient was then admitted to the Cardiology Medicine for optimization of blood pressure control. After 24 h, the patient suddenly developed lethargy while maintaining response to noxious stimuli.

The MDT was convened immediately upon the patient’s presentation of the aforementioned symptoms. Participants included specialists from orthopedics, cardiology, neurology, anesthesiology, and the Intensive Care Unit (ICU), who jointly developed an emergency personalized treatment plan. The neurologist performed a neurological examination, revealing preserved motor function, though she exhibited limited cooperation during formal muscle strength testing. Additionally, her National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score was recorded as 4, indicating mild neurological impairment. Cerebral magnetic resonance plain scan revealed an acute ischemic lesion in the right parietal lobe. Cerebral Computed Tomography Angiography (CTA) demonstrated moderate stenosis in the M1 segment of the right middle cerebral artery, focal stenosis in the A2 segments of bilateral anterior cerebral arteries, and localized stenosis in the P2 segment of the right posterior cerebral artery. Following the diagnosis of acute ischemic stroke, the neurologist promptly controlled cerebral edema and implemented neuroprotective measures; the cardiologist monitored the patient’s baseline cardiac function and ruled out the possibility of acute myocardial injury at this stage; the orthopedist assessed the hip fracture and recommended temporary conservative management to avoid acute surgical stress; the anesthesiologist evaluated the high-risk profile for general anesthesia; and the ICU specialists advised admission to the ICU for respiratory and circulatory support.

Following the ICU admission, the patient developed progressive hypoxemia necessitating endotracheal intubation with mechanical ventilation. Five days later, the patient was found to have an elevated high-sensitivity troponin-I level of 3009 pg/ml. The ECG showed QS complexes in leads V1 and V2, accompanied by ST-T segment abnormalities (Fig. 2). An emergency MDT meeting was convened. Led by cardiologists, echocardiography demonstrated septal and anterior wall motion abnormalities, with the left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) at 41% compared to 60% six days prior, confirming AMI. Given the patient’s stable hemodynamics, absence of cardiogenic shock or life-threatening arrhythmias, priority was given to anticoagulation and dual antiplatelet therapy. Neurologists reassessed the stability of cerebrovascular lesions and the risk of stroke recurrence, guiding continued dehydration therapy to control cerebral edema and neuroprotective measures. Orthopedists, considering the patient’s age and unstable fracture, reaffirmed the indications for femoral head replacement. Anesthesiologists optimized the anesthesia protocol to minimize hemodynamic fluctuations, while intensive care physicians ensured comprehensive perioperative critical care support.

After 10 days of intensive care, the patient was successfully weaned from mechanical ventilation and transferred from the ICU to the cardiology ward for continued management. On hospital day 29, while the patient remained clinically stable, an MDT consultation was convened in response to the strong request from both the patient and family members, resulting in the decision to proceed with further surgical intervention.

Considering the risks of anesthesia and the principles of enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS), general anesthesia with nerve block remains the preferred choice. Ultrasound-guided right fascia iliaca compartment block was performed for surgical analgesia. We used an in-plane technique, the needle was advanced beneath the fascia iliaca, identified by the hourglass sign between the obliquus internus abdominis, sartorius, and iliopsoas muscles. After injecting 40 mL of 0.25% ropivacaine, block efficacy was confirmed by acupuncture at 5 and 10 min.

After blocking, sufentanil 15 µg, etomidate 10 mg, remimazolam 12 mg, and vecuronium 6 mg were intravenously injected to induce anesthesia. Subsequently, we performed endotracheal intubation and initiated mechanical ventilation. Anesthesia was maintained with a continuous infusion of remifentanil (0.1 µg/kg/min) and inhalation of 4% desflurane. We used forced-air warming system in prevention of intraoperative hypothermia and maintained the nasopharyngeal temperature above 36 °C. The procedure lasted 70 min, during which the bispectral index was consistently maintained within the range of 45 to 60. Vasoactive medications were used to stabilize blood pressure and heart rate, ensuring that vital signs remained stable throughout the perioperative period. There were no obvious hemodynamic changes during surgery, and her endotracheal tube was successfully removed after waking up at the end of surgery.

After a 41-day hospitalization, the patient was discharged with no obvious sequelae. The patient’s medications upon discharge included dual antiplatelet therapy with rivaroxaban 10 mg daily and metoprolol 50 mg twice daily, in addition to valsartan 100 mg twice daily and high-intensity atorvastatin. We performed a 10-month postoperative follow-up, which showed favorable patient recovery without notable sequelae.

Discussion

The risk factors for CCI include: atherosclerosis [2], hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, smoking and alcohol consumption [3], excessive activation of the cardiac sympathetic nerve or inhibition of the parasympathetic nerve caused by brain injury, cardiogenic embolism and circulatory dysfunction after AMI, neuroendocrine disorders and persistent severe inflammatory responses after stroke, all of which may increase the risk of acute brain injury.

The mechanism underlying CCI remains unclear. One longstanding hypothesis suggests that in patients experiencing AMI with myocardial dysfunction, the formation of a mural thrombus could lead to emboli, subsequently causing AIS [4, 5]. A similar theory is that the adrenergic surge associated with AIS may result in catecholamine-induced myocardial stunning and intracardiac thrombus formation [6]. Additionally, thrombus from AF is considered a common source of CCI [7, 8]. Hypercoagulable states associated with advanced malignancy or essential thrombocytosis have been linked to CCI [9, 10]. In our case, hypercoagulability from hip fracture is likely the possible cause of her CCI.

Given the narrow treatment window, timely and well-balanced decisions are crucial to mitigate both ischemic and hemorrhagic risks. However, due to the rarity of CCI and the absence of comprehensive clinical trials, there is currently no established consensus on its management.

Patients with a history of stroke or transient ischemic attack (TIA) are at increased risk of recurrent stroke or neurological deterioration. This risk declines over time due to attenuated inflammation, reduced hemorrhage risk, and gradual restoration of cerebral autoregulation. Current guidelines [11] recommend deferring elective non-cardiac surgery (NCS) for ≥ 3 months after the most recent cerebrovascular event to reduce the incidence of recurrent stroke and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE).

However, a retrospective study [12] suggests that acute cerebral infarction with hip fracture leads to worse neurological recovery, prolonged hospitalization period, increased complications, decreased patient prognosis, and increased one-year mortality. Surgical treatment improves the prognosis of patients with acute cerebral infarction.

The guidelines also indicate that patients with a history of myocardial infarction (MI) have a 3.5-fold increased risk of perioperative MACE, while preoperative coronary revascularization can effectively reduce MACE incidence. Evidence from retrospective studies demonstrates that hip fracture surgery performed in the immediate post-myocardial infarction period is associated with significantly elevated mortality at 1 and 6 months [13, 14].Among the reported cases, the combined approach is mostly recommended inmanaging CCI. A practical approach is to first administer thrombolytics when there are no contraindications, followed by appropriate endovascular interventions such as Percutaneous Coronary Intervention (PCI), Mechanical Thrombectomy (MT), or both [4, 15, 16]. After being diagnosed with acute ischemic stroke, the neurologist immediately controlled cerebral edema and implemented neuroprotective measures; the cardiologist monitored the patient’s baseline cardiac function; the orthopedic doctor evaluated the hip fracture and recommended temporary conservative treatment to avoid acute surgical stress.

After confirming AMI, the cardiologist assessed that the patient was hemodynamically stable, without cardiogenic shock or life-threatening arrhythmias, and focused treatment on anticoagulation therapy and dual antiplatelet therapy. The neurologist reassessed the stability of cerebrovascular lesions and the risk of stroke recurrence, guiding the continuation of dehydration therapy to control cerebral edema and implementing neuroprotective measures. Considering the patient’s age and the instability of the fracture, the orthopedic doctor reconfirmed the indications for performing a hip replacement. Nevertheless, the final treatment decision should be individualized and based on the cardiac and cerebral conditions.

Limitations

This case report has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, it is a single case report, so the conclusions drawn cannot be generalized to all patients with CCI undergoing hip fracture surgery. Second, the follow-up period was 10 months, which is relatively short, and long-term follow-up is needed to evaluate the long-term prognosis of the patient, including the risk of recurrent CCI and functional recovery. Third, coronary angiography was not performed to further confirm the cause of AMI, which may affect the in-depth analysis of the mechanism of CCI. Fourth, we did not collect detailed data on the patient’s long-term medication compliance, which may have an impact on the interpretation of follow-up results.
