Optimizing remote access to Urinary Incontinence Treatments for Women Veterans: Identifying salient features of participants’ experiences with two delivery modalities
Authors: Beverly R Williams, Kathryn L Burgio, Katharina V Echt, Karen M Goldstein, Camille P Vaughan, Ursula Kelly, Alayne D Markland
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImportance and objective:Behavioral treatments are recognized as evidence-based approaches for treatment of urinary incontinence (UI) in women. However, many women do not have in-person access to qualified providers, which has led to the emergence of remote methods of delivery for behavioral treatment. The purpose of this study was to identify salient features of participants’ experiences with two modalities for remote delivery of behavioral treatment for UI within a pragmatic randomized trial.Design setting and participants:Individual qualitative interviews were conducted to explore the views and experiences of women Veterans who completed behavioral treatment delivered by a mobile health application (MyHealtheBladder, MHB) or video visit via VA Video Connect (VVC). The study was conducted at 3 Southeast VA healthcare systems (April 2020 - September 2023).Interventions:MHB consisted of 56 self-administered daily sessions delivered remotely over 8 weeks. VVC consisted of a single session with a specialized UI provider. After the first 8 weeks, women identified as non-responders (based on self-reported symptom improvement) were randomized to an additional VVC session or no session.Analysis:A hybrid qualitative content analysis integrating deductive and inductive methods was conducted to identify the most frequently occurring and highly emphasized topics.Results:Of the 188 women Veterans who completed the first 8-week treatment, 41 completed the interview. Participants focused on three topics: (1) encountering technical and logistical issues in access and use; (2) interacting with the program content; and (3) finding motivation, encouragement, and inspiration. Participants in both arms of the study spoke of the importance of the structure and process of content presentation, emphasizing mechanisms for reminders, review and feedback. They also valued the woman Veterans’ UI stories and provider verbal and nonverbal expressions of care and support as sources of motivation and encouragement. MHB participants reported far fewer challenges with accessing information compared to their VVC counterparts.Conclusion:Future mobile health programs should incorporate mechanisms for smooth navigation of MHB and timely provider feedback, as well as strategies to ensure patients can access VVC and are prepared for participation in ways that optimize their engagement with the behavioral treatment program.Keywords: Urinary incontinence, Women veterans, Telemedicine, Mobile health, Behavioral treatment, Self-management

Introduction

Urinary incontinence (UI) is a prevalent condition with significant impacts on quality of life. Women Veterans are at increased risk of UI due in part to adverse conditions involved in active-duty military service, including limited access to toilets, postponed urination, fluid restriction, and strenuous activity and training [1,2]. Behavioral treatments are recognized widely as first-line approaches to UI in women [3–6]. However, they are not always available, due in part to limited numbers of professionals trained in these treatments. To increase access, mechanisms have been developed and tested to deliver behavioral treatments remotely.

Within the VA Healthcare System, continence providers have utilized the established VA Video Connect (VVC) videoconferencing system to conduct telehealth treatment sessions for UI. Building on an evidence-based, in-person behavioral program for women Veterans with UI, a multidisciplinary team of health educators, behavioral medicine experts, and clinicians designed MyHealtheBladder (MHB), an 8-week self-administered mobile telehealth program [7]. In a pilot study of MHB, women Veterans showed reductions in UI symptoms as measured by a validated questionnaire [7,8]. Subsequently, investigators conducted a randomized trial to compare the effectiveness of MHB to a single VVC session administered by a specialized UI provider [9]. The purpose of the current research with a subset of trial participants was to identify salient features of their experience of the two delivery modalities, with the goal of refining the programs and promoting future adoption on a larger scale.

Methods

Overview of parent trial

The Optimizing Remote Access to Urinary Incontinence Treatments for Women Veterans (PRACTICal) trial used a sequential, multiple assignment, randomized trial (SMART) design to compare the effectiveness of two remote modalities for delivery of behavioral UI treatments [9,10]. Participants were randomized to 8 weeks of treatment delivered by MHB vs. VVC visit with a specialized continence provider [9]. It also evaluated the addition of a VVC booster visit for non-responders in both groups, i.e. women who did not meet the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) threshold for symptom improvement on a validated questionnaire [8,11]. The primary aim of the trial was to determine the optimal modality for delivering remote behavioral treatment for UI to women Veterans. The trial was conducted at 3 Southeast VA healthcare systems (April 2020-September 2023). The current qualitative study included a subset of parent trial participants, who were invited to participate in semi-structured telephone interview about their views and experiences with the interventions.

Participants

Trial participants were women Veterans 18 years of age or older who reported UI for at least 3 months and had access to email via computer, tablet, or mobile phone. Exclusion criteria were: any new UI treatments in the prior 3 months or any planned UI treatments for study duration; unstable medical conditions potentially contributing to UI (e.g., uncontrolled diabetes); unstable housing; neurologic conditions contributing to UI; genitourinary cancer; current pregnancy; or <12 weeks post-partum.

The study was approved by the VA Medical Center’s Institutional Review Board at each site and all participants signed informed consent.

Interventions

Participants in both groups received evidence-based behavioral treatment either through 56 self-administered daily MHB sessions or the same standardized content delivered through a single VVC visit with a specialized continence provider. Major components of the behavioral intervention included education on bladder anatomy and function, pelvic floor muscle exercises, bladder control strategies, fluid management guidance, self-monitoring, and risk factor reduction. MHB included inspirational quotes and women Veterans’ continence stories to reinforce behavior change, as well as reminder features to promote adherence.

Both groups had access to an electronic informational booklet with written and visual content about incontinence, pelvic floor muscle (PFM) exercises, and bladder control strategies. Hard copies were mailed to VVC participants prior to their visit. Participants were instructed to preview the material, follow along with the booklet during the VVC session, and retain the material for reference. Periodically participants received brief questionnaires on UI symptom severity, satisfaction with treatment, and adherence to pelvic floor muscle exercises and strategies.

After the first 8 weeks of intervention, nonresponders in both groups were offered a VVC booster session.

Procedures

After completing the first 8-week program or a booster visit, participants were invited to participate in a semi-structured telephone interview about their views and experiences with their respective intervention. This subset of interviewed women comprised the sample for the current analysis (n = 41; MHB = 18, VVC = 23).

Telephone interviews were conducted by an experienced VA health communications expert (KE), using an open-ended semi-structured guide designed by the interdisciplinary investigative team (see Appendix). Interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim.

Analysis

Using hybrid qualitative content analysis, a medical sociologist (BW) analyzed the data. Hybrid content analysis involves the synthesis of deductive and inductive approaches, integrating a predetermined set of categories with themes emerging from in-depth immersion in the data [12]. Interviews were classified based on the arm of the intervention (MHB or VVC). Through a line-by-line reading of each interview text, the analyst identified recurring and highly emphasized features of participants’ experience with the programs. Next, a topical compilation of participants’ observations was developed to provide a comprehensive representation of the scope of participants’ views on each topic. A behavioral psychologist with expertise in treatments for UI refined topics and assisted in selecting exemplary quotes. Qualitative analysts had no prior contact with participants or involvement with the intervention delivery. All co-authors reviewed the material and contributed to the final version. De-identified interview data will be available upon request from the corresponding author (AM).

Results

Characteristics of participants

Interview participants (N = 41) ranged in age from 23 to 76 years (mean = 56.5; SD = 11.3). As planned, they were distributed across three sites (Atlanta-11, Birmingham-16, Durham-14). Twenty identified as Black, 15 as white, 3 as “other,” and 3 as more than one race. At the 8-week time point, 24 were deemed treatment responders and 17 as non-responders.

Findings

The final topical structure comprised: (1) encountering technical and logistical issues in access and use; (2) interacting with program content; and (3) finding motivation, encouragement, and inspiration.

Encountering technical and logistical issues in access and use

Both interventions necessitated use of electronic devices such as cell phones, tablets, and laptop or desktop computers. While technical problems occurred with MHB access and use, typically issues were resolved promptly by participants seeking technical support or troubleshooting on their own.

When I originally started the program, I couldn’t really get in, and I had to call [name] who had to contact his department because, apparently, they were having some kind of problem getting everything lined up. Once all that was taken care of, everything went very smoothly.

MHB-052

I think one of the sessions ended up starting all over again ... I just called the—when you get like that they give you a number to call and you call that number and it’s like a helpdesk. And they just go ahead and reset it for you.

MHB-054

It (access) got easier as the time went by. At first, I had a hard time trying to get on the website, trying to find the email to get back on the website. I wish I would’ve had an app on my phone that I could’ve just hit and obviously it would’ve made it a little bit better for me. But I figured it out.

MHB-024

VVC participants’ described the process of accessing and using the program as either user friendly or fraught with difficulties. Typically, those who found the program easy to use were familiar with VVC or had prepared by reading the directions and/or doing a solo practice run.

It was easy. I did it before. When they were starting the process, I tested it. So then I already knew how to use it.

VVC-001

It was pretty easy. Very. I’m familiar with VA Video Connect so it was a lot easier than

what I would’ve expected like doing a Zoom or anything like that I’m not comfortable with that ... I was just sent a link and I was able to log in from there.

VVC-042

As long as you followed the directions, it was fine. I keep thinking everything should be completely intuitive to a person that’s not real intuitive with personal computers. But seriously, if you followed the directions, it was fine.

VVC-044

In contrast, access issues or problems with VVC audio and video capability and network connectivity created difficulties for some participants who described switching to a cell phone, which was not optimal.

We had a few problems with our connection. We ended up having to go to a phone call.

I actually used my android telephone ... It wasn’t the best resolution, the best audio ... I didn’t feel like she could really see, I didn’t really feel like she had the exposure to me. I always feel like, if there’s some kind of video with a provider, that they should be able to see more of me, more of my body language, more of my countenance.

VVC-104

VVC participants who received the informational booklet in the mail or who accessed it on-line prior to the VVC session described “flipping through it” or reading it once and “putting it aside,” versus benefitting from its “wealth of information,” considering it “something you keep” and “go back to” as needed. (VVC-011, VVC-017 VVC-001, VVC-064, VVC-038)

Several VVC participants (n = 9) reported they never received the booklet or did not receive it before the VVC session. Additionally, for some patients who did not receive the booklet, accessing and using the digital version was not an option.

I don’t have a booklet ... If you do have some kind of a booklet send that out to your participants, send it out hard copy, because everybody does not have the same access to e-mail and to computers.

VVC-034

I did (receive the booklet) after the meeting ... I wish I could have had the book beforehand because it’s kind of hard to pay attention on the video while looking at the computer, digital version ... she was wanting to show me where in the book I could find them, so it’s nice to have the paper version rather than the digital when talking about it.

VVC-079

There were substantive differences between MHB and VVC participants’ accounts of the nature, frequency, and impact of technical issues on access and use. Because MHB sessions were available on a continuous basis, problems could be resolved over time. Conversely, for VVC users connectivity problems often could not be solved during the single synchronous telehealth visit.

Interacting with program content

Three common features of both telehealth modalities facilitated patient interaction with the educational content: (1) structured cumulative process, (2) provision for reviewing content, and (3) mechanism for receiving feedback.

Structured Cumulative Process.

MHB participants described engaging with the program’s instructional material as a structured process, where knowledge and skills were taught over eight weeks of daily sessions with each session adding to prior learning.

I think the flow of information was great, because everything started at the beginning, then you gradually build up. And it was presented in a way that it was easy to progress from one point to the next.

MHB-052

I thought it had a good buildup. Just like with the exercises, you know, you do three, three times a day, and then you went to four, three times a day, so you didn’t feel overwhelmed or you didn’t feel like you were missing the mark, because you was completely building ... For me it was logical, and I think with it being logical, it encouraged me to make sure I kept up with the sessions.

MHB-001

Some MHB participants reported problems keeping track of the sessions, picking up where they left off, and skipping and/or not completing them in the right order. The following quotes illustrate the difficulties arising when participants get out of sync with the flow of the program:

When you pull it up, first thing you see is the info video. You have to click on the session tab to get the sessions to come up. A suggestion is to have it when ... you log in, it’s where you need to be.

MHB-037

When I clicked on to available sessions, a few times it said to me, have you completed week something, , number whatever? I would be like, yes. Then I realized, maybe I didn’t. Maybe I forgot where I was. It just started in the wrong spot and I would have to go back.

MHB-011

Sometimes when the week would pop up, (session) seven would come up first instead of one. I think one week I completely skipped a week. I didn’t realize seven had come up and it finished off the week for me.

MHB-028

VVC participants spoke favorably of the “way the program content is structured and delivered,” its “very simple methods,” which involve “having a smaller goal to work up to a bigger goal,” and “formulizing something that wasn’t part of my life and so now it’s more of a part of my life.” [VVC-044, VVC-025] Many VVC participants found structure and continuity in recurring program elements such as periodic reminders to complete the self-assessment survey. Survey prompts held participants accountable by reminding them it was time to take stock of their improvement. The surveys also helped lapsed participants stay focused and involved, remain adherent or get back on track.

I liked the little survey questions that we did. Because it helped me remember, oh yes.

is what I’m supposed to be doing. And this is the focus of what we’re doing. So that was the most helpful for me, ’cause it brought back the scope of what we were doing.

VVC-067

You send the e-mail saying there’s ... a survey there to do, waiting to be done, and I appreciate that. And having to answer the survey questions just brings it to the forethought again of “That’s right. You’ve got a commitment to the program,” and we need to do this ... But just that little bit is enough to keep you connected to you folks, and you folks are my accountability.

VVC-044

Provision for reviewing content.

MHB participants reported that the capability of reviewing the content of the behavioral program for clarity and understanding was useful. They provided unprompted accounts of how the program provided a mechanism for revisiting the material during or after the sessions.

I mean it’s very effective, you do it at your own pace and you have the lessons ... I went back during the same session and took a look at some of the things to make sure I understood it, clearer.

MHB-006

(You proceed) with full understanding of why you’re doing what you’re doing, how you’re doing it, to prepare you. If you’re ready for the next step, fine. If not, you can always go back and start over.

MHB-052

MHB participants assigned to a subsequent VVC booster session characterized it as an opportunity for systematic content review. In the words of the participants, the booster session “brought everything home” by providing an overview of the material that “refreshed,” “reaffirmed,” “re-emphasized, “reinforced,” and “enhanced” their grasp of the information presented in the initial session. (MHB-060; MHB-028; MHB-035)

Mechanism for receiving feedback.

While the VVC booster session was designed to enhance nonresponders’ uptake of the intervention, many booster participants characterized it as a way to receive desired feedback on the PFM exercises.

They wanted to see if ... when I was doin’ the exercises ... that I was, I guess ... using it correctly. That was very important, because if we don’t, we’re not doin’ it right then it’s a waste of time, so it’s kinda good to know that you’re doin’ it right.

MHB-036 Booster session

She asked me to stand up. I hadn’t thought about that. I knew that I was doing it right when I was sitting down. But I hadn’t thought about it standing up

And actually, had me physically do the exercises in front of her ... Well it was information that I was doing it correctly.

MHB-028 Booster session

Participants in both interventions expressed preferences for ongoing monitoring and timely feedback during the eight-week intervention period.

To have like the opportunity to have a follow-up session with the nurse just to see how things are going ... to have someone reach out to them like halfway through the program ‘cause I’m not sure how much monitoring the program receives once people start.

MHB-011

I don’t know why I thought we were gonna have more Video Connect sessions, not just the one ... I think maybe one more as ... a check-in like maybe two weeks after, just to see how things are going ... They can ... assess what you’re doing, and then you can also bring up the special circumstances ... just discussing with someone else how you can improve and make things better while you’re in the study.

VVC-037

It may be a new process, a new exercise, a new way of looking at it, some new habits, and it’s good, once you lay it out the first time, to make sure that it’s going good. So that’s why it’s helpful to have that video follow-up a little bit closer to monitor what somebody is doing, or what they’ve been not doing.

VVC-034

Finding motivation, encouragement and inspiration

Women Veterans’ descriptions of being motivated, encouraged, and inspired by participation in the interventions were striking features. The MHB patient stories reinforced knowledge, fostered application, engendered positive sentiment, and minimized feelings of isolation. The vast majority of MHB participants connected with the stories that resonated with their incontinence experience. By identifying with real-life accounts from women Veterans, participants felt solidarity in their struggle with bladder issues.

I liked it (stories) because it was about women Veterans and how they would, it made me feel like okay well you’re not in this alone, you know. I know for me and I’m sure for a lotta other women ... it’s like your dirty secret. You don’t want anybody to know. And to hear these stories of other ... women, who have these same issues that you have and that they feel the same way you were feeling, like okay, “I’m the only one.”

MHB-042

I love the stories ‘cause you could read and sometimes you could relate, or you could see ‘cause you got other people that have the same issue as you ... I just like the story, love the story part ‘cause, it just lets you know that you’re not alone and other people have the issue that you have ... I think it was helpful in a positive way.

MHB-102

For VVC participants, the providers’ attentive, caring, supportive words and mannerisms generated enthusiasm. VVC participants and booster attendees lauded the providers’ interpersonal qualities which fostered a sense of connection, warmth and acceptance.

My visit was so enjoyable I literally told my husband afterwards that that was one of the best ... doctor’s visits I’ve had, whether in-person or ... via video. And really that’s just compliments to the lady who conducted it ... She was very personable, and I guess I felt something spiritual with her ... But because it seemed to me that she cared about me as a person, that made it good for me.

Discussion

Our study revealed that patients encountered technical and logistical issues in access and use of the telehealth devices. These findings align with the telehealth literature [13–16]. In research on patients’ and providers’ perceptions of VA video telehealth technology, issues of access to and knowledge about VVC utilization were among key factors shaping how users viewed the experience [13]. Research with Veterans found those receiving VA care reported more digital health skills and appeared more prepared to use e-health technology compared to Veterans who received care elsewhere [17]. Together, findings confirm an ongoing need for patient familiarity with telemedicine devices and facility using the devices to access care [15], as well as the need for system level technical support. While technical support was available in our study, not all who needed it reached out for assistance. Future adaptations should retain this capability and heighten awareness of its availability.

VVC provided both electronic and paper versions of the instructional booklet as a component of the intervention. Durable printed material often is requested by participants in short-term telehealth behavioral interventions [18]. A review of the research supports the development and distribution of durable materials for patient education in treating incontinence [19–21]. In one study, an instruction booklet was as effective as a mobile App for PFM training in reducing UI [22]. In another, conducted by VA investigators, a booklet plus consultation session resulted in significant reduction in UI compared to a control group [23]. With increasing emphasis on digital materials in telehealth, these findings highlight the need for retaining durable materials with potential benefit for many patients.

Our findings point to the importance of an interactive structure of telehealth programs, particularly the provision for periodic feedback. Although the interventions were designed as self-management programs, the woman Veterans wanted feedback on how well they were performing PFM exercises. This observation is consistent with literature emphasizing the importance of supervision and timely follow-up to ensure women are performing the exercises correctly [24,25]. The literature on the effectiveness of remote PFM training concludes that these programs did not provide an opportunity for real-time interactive assessment and communication of patients’ progress and that both supervised and unsupervised training can be effective when regular reassessments are provided [26,27]. Investigators implementing a women Veterans’ health educational and virtual consultation program concluded health information and advice provided at the time needed is highly beneficial to patients [28].

Our results offered consistent evidence of the value participants placed on motivating, encouraging, and inspirational messaging whether it came from reading UI stories of other women Veterans in the MHB program or from receiving support and affirmation from the provider in the VVC program. In a previous study of women Veterans’ experiences with MHB, we also found participants appreciated the stories, articulating how they provided alternate ways to think and feel about their experiences of incontinence [29]. These findings are consistent with the literature on narrative health communication which found that stories integrating inspirational and motivating messaging into digital content can increase patient engagement and activation and connect patients to the message, supporting assimilation of health promoting behaviors and boosting self-efficacy [30–32].

For VVC participants, the provider’s encouraging and empathetic manner was as important as the content itself. This finding aligns with other qualitative research on women’s adherence with prescribed PFM exercises indicating that a collaborative communication style can engender trust and facilitate adherence [32]. Research on provider-patient interaction has considered the emergence of technology in medicine and its effect on how clinicians provide in-person treatment as “high tech” versus “high touch.” [33] Yet, little has been written about the nuances of provider-patient communication in telemedicine or the clinician attributes patients value in virtual healthcare. Nagel and colleagues called for empirical evidence to inform the evolution of holistic nursing practices during telehealth encounters, such as communicating a caring stance and making a genuine connection with patients which can be more challenging in virtual care [34,35]. Research on provider interpersonal behaviors and therapeutic patient-provider connections indicated the use of virtual modalities requires different approaches than those characteristic of in-person encounters and noted the importance of visual cues in building rapport and communicating empathy [36,37].

Conclusion

Findings of this study revealed participants’ salient experiences with the interventions, highlighting shared features across delivery modalities. Participants in both groups spoke of the importance of the structure and process of content presentation, with an emphasis on mechanisms for reminders, review, and feedback. Similarly, MHB and VVC participants alike valued formational aspects of the program, describing how women Veterans’ UI stories and provider expressions of care and support were both affirmational and inspirational. Differences were found in logistical issues, where MHB participants reported far fewer challenges compared to VVC participants.

The findings suggest several opportunities for future program improvements.

To ensure timely access to the programs, technical support should be available and there should be a mechanism to ensure participants are aware of its availability.
