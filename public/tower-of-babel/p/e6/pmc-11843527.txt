Association between physical activity levels and mortality across adiposity: A longitudinal study of age‐specific Asian populations
Authors: Yunmin Han, Younghwan Choi, Yeon Soo Kim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThis study investigated the association of physical activity (PA) levels with all‐cause and cardiovascular disease (CVD) mortality risks stratified by adiposity and age.MethodsParticipants (36 703; mean age: 49.1; 57.1% women) were selected from the Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey 2007–2013. A Cox proportional hazards model was used to examine the association between PA and mortality across different adiposity categories and ages.ResultsMedian follow‐up was 9.22 years; 2393 individuals died, including 538 with CVD. Compared with normal‐weight controls, underweight individuals had increased all‐cause mortality risk (hazard ratio [HR]: 1.60, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.33–1.79). The overweight and obese groups demonstrated reduced mortality risks (HRs: 0.75 [95% CI: 0.67–0.84] and 0.59 [95% CI: 0.51–0.67], respectively). Mortality risk based on abdominal obesity had an HR of 1.22 (95% CI: 1.08–1.37). PA protected against mortality risk most in the obese group performing more than 1000 MET‐min/week and was particularly significant among those who were underweight and obese aged 65 and older.ConclusionsOverweight and obese Asian individuals (based on body mass index) had a lower mortality risk than those with normal body mass index, whereas obesity based on waist circumference was associated with increased mortality. PA protected against mortality across various weight categories, especially in individuals aged ≥65. Geriatr Gerontol Int 2024; 24: 1156–1164.Keywords: adiposity, mortality, obesity, physical activity, underweight

Introduction

Low levels of physical activity (PA) and obesity are globally recognized as significant public health challenges. 1 , 2 In Korea, the prevalence of obesity has markedly increased from 30.2% in 2012 to 38.4% in 2021. 3 The rate of abdominal obesity, a critical risk factor for chronic diseases, has reached 24.5% and continues to increase annually. Despite having the highest life expectancy and fastest aging rate, the proportion of physically inactive Korean older adults is significantly higher than that of the younger population. 4 , 5 Aging is associated with increased visceral fat and gradual loss of muscle mass. 6 Enhancing PA and maintaining optimal weight and body composition are pivotal in public health policy, particularly for older adults. 7

PA not only mitigates obesity and chronic diseases by improving cardiovascular health and regulating glucose levels but also substantially reduces mortality risk, as supported by robust evidence. 8 , 9 However, although obesity significantly increases the risk of cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) and various chronic conditions, mortality risk varies among different racial and ethnic groups. 10 Additionally, being underweight is significantly associated with a higher mortality risk. 11 The proportion of the population that is underweight in Korea is higher than that in the Unites States and European countries, highlighting the need to address this significant social issue. 12 , 13

Previous research has largely focused on comparing PA levels between obese and normal‐weight individuals, with limited studies exploring the risk of all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality in underweight and obese groups. Additionally, research investigating these risks among older adults stratified by obesity level is lacking. This study aimed to analyze the impact of PA levels on the risk of all‐cause and CVD mortality according to adiposity levels and to investigate how these effects differ across age groups.

Methods

Participants

The Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES) is an annual, cross‐sectional survey by the Korea Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (KCDC) that assesses the health and nutritional status of non‐institutionalized Koreans using a complex multistage probability sampling method for national representativeness. All participants provided written informed consent, and the study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the KCDC and Seoul National University (IRB NO. E2404/002–009), with informed consent waived for de‐identified data.

From 2007 to 2013, the KNHANES database included a total of 40 511 adults aged 19 years and older. Participants with missing body mass index (BMI) or waist circumference (WC) data (n = 301) were excluded, as were those with invalid or missing PA data (n = 1424) and those missing data on key covariates such as smoking status, alcohol consumption, educational level, marital status, diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia (n = 2083). The final analyzed sample size was 36 703 individuals.

Adiposity level

Adiposity levels were classified based on BMI and WC measurements. BMI was calculated by dividing weight in kilograms by the square of height in meters (kg/m2); according to Asian standards, underweight is defined as a BMI <18.5 kg/m2, normal weight as a BMI of 18.5–22.9 kg/m2, overweight as a BMI of 23–24.9 kg/m2, and obesity as a BMI ≥25 kg/m2. 14 WC was measured at the midpoint between the lower margin of the rib cage and the top of the iliac crest. Abdominal obesity was defined as a WC of ≥90 cm for men, and as ≥85 cm for women. 14

Physical activity assessment

Study participants self‐reported their PA levels using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire for the past week. They detailed the number of days they engaged in 1 vigorous PA for at least 10 min, 2 moderate PA for at least 10 min, and 3 walking for more than 10 min. For each activity, they provided the total time spent in hours and minutes. Metabolic equivalent (MET) values of 8 for vigorous PA, 4 for moderate PA, and 3.3 for walking were used to calculate intensity. The total PA level (MET‐min/week) was calculated by combining the intensity, frequency, and duration of each activity. Participants were then categorized into three groups based on their total PA levels: <500, 500–999, and ≥1000 MET‐min/week. 15 , 16

Covariates

Sociodemographic variables collected included age, sex, household income (low to high), and education level (≤ elementary to ≥ college). Lifestyle behaviors were assessed via self‐reported questionnaires. Smoking status was categorized as never, former, or current smoker. Alcohol consumption was measured by weekly intake, with heavy drinking defined as >7 drinks twice a week for males and >5 drinks twice a week for females. Comorbidities were evaluated, with hypertension defined by a systolic BP ≥140 mmHg, diastolic BP ≥90 mmHg, or antihypertensive medication use. Type 2 diabetes was diagnosed by a fasting plasma glucose level ≥126 mg/dL or diabetes medication use. Dyslipidemia was identified by total cholesterol ≥240 mg/dL, LDL ≥160 mg/dL, HDL <40 mg/dL for men and <50 mg/dL for women, triglycerides ≥200 mg/dL, or lipid‐lowering medication use.

Mortality assessment

Among 36 703 individuals, a total of 2393 deaths matched their resident registration numbers. The Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency created a database integrating KNHANES data with cause‐of‐death statistics from Statistics Korea. This included matching KNHANES data with cause‐of‐death data for participants aged ≥19 who consented to data linkage. The data were merged with anonymized survival status information until December 31, 2019. 17 Causes of death were categorized according to the 10th revision of the International Classification of Diseases. 18 We calculated the total number of deaths and the mortality rates related to CVD (I00–I99).

Statistical analysis

Participant characteristics were represented as means and standard deviations for continuous variables and as percentages for categorical variables. Continuous and categorical variables were compared using general linear models and chi‐squared tests, respectively. The relationship between PA level and all‐cause mortality was estimated using multivariate Cox proportional hazards models to estimate the risk of mortality associated with obesity and PA level, adjusting for age, sex, smoking status, alcohol consumption, educational level, marital status, diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia. BMI, abdominal obesity, and PA were mutually adjusted to examine their independent associations with mortality risk. The proportional hazards assumption was verified by inspecting the log plots. Stratified analyses were also conducted to examine the impact of PA levels on the risk of mortality across different adiposity levels, with additional subgroup analyses based on age. The age groups were divided into those aged 65 and older and those under 65. In each subgroup analysis, the reference group was defined as those with PA levels <500 MET‐min/week.

Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the findings, excluding patients with major CVDs (such as stroke, angina, and myocardial infarction) and cancer, as well as individuals who died within the first 2 years of follow‐up. All statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.2), and a two‐tailed P‐value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Hazard ratios (HRs) for all‐cause and CVD mortality according to BMI, PA, and WC are presented in Table 2. Compared with the normal‐weight group, the underweight group showed an increased HR of 1.60 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.35–1.88), whereas the overweight and obese groups demonstrated lower HRs of 0.75 (95% CI: 0.67–0.84) and 0.59 (95% CI: 0.51–0.67), respectively. Conversely, those with abdominal obesity showed a higher mortality risk, with an HR of 1.22 (95% CI: 1.08–1.37). Participants with PA levels below 500 MET‐min/week had a reduced mortality risk (HR: 0.88, 95% CI: 0.78–0.99), improving further at 1000 MET‐min/week (HR: 0.81, 95% CI: 0.74–0.89). Similar trends were observed in CVD mortality. Additionally, BMI, abdominal obesity, and PA were independently associated with mortality risk. For participants under 65 years, the underweight group showed an HR of 2.33 for all‐cause mortality (95% CI: 1.65–3.29) compared with the normal‐weight group (Table 3). The results for those aged 65 and over were consistent with those for the overall population.

In the subgroup analysis divided by adiposity category (Fig. 1), the underweight group showed an HR for all‐cause mortality of 0.59 (95% CI: 0.41–0.85) when engaging in PA of 1000 MET‐min/week compared with those doing less than 500 MET‐min/week. The results of the analysis divided by adiposity category for age groups under and over 65 years are presented in Figure 2. For those <65 years, no reduction in mortality risk associated with PA levels was observed. However, in the >65 years age group, a decrease in mortality risk with increasing PA levels was noted in the underweight, overweight, and obese categories. For the older obese group, compared with those with 500 MET‐min/week, the 500–999 MET‐min/week group had an HR of 0.77 (95% CI: 0.56–1.05), and the 1000 MET‐min/week group had an HR of 0.75 (95% CI: 0.60–0.95). Similarly, in the older abdominally obese group, engaging in 1000 met/min/week or more was associated with reduced all‐cause mortality risk, with an HR of 0.75 (95% CI: 0.61–0.92).

Discussion

In this study, we examined the association between all‐cause mortality, CVD mortality, adiposity, and PA levels. Underweight individuals showed a higher mortality risk compared with normal‐weight counterparts, while the overweight and obese groups had a decreased risk. Conversely, those with abdominal obesity faced a significant increase in mortality risk compared with those with normal WC. Additionally, higher PA levels were associated with reduced mortality risks. Subgroup analyses highlighted a marked protective effect of PA in both the underweight and overweight/obese groups, especially among individuals aged 65 and older.

The underweight group exhibited a higher risk of mortality than the normal‐weight group, a trend that was consistent across all age groups. Being underweight is closely associated with malnutrition, which likely contributes to increased mortality risk through various factors such as reduced muscle mass and an elevated risk of CVD. 11 , 19 As observed in several studies in Asian populations, mortality rates across different obesity levels display patterns indicative of the obesity paradox. 20 , 21 , 22 In Korea, the BMI cut‐point for obesity is set at 25 or higher, differing from that in the United States and other Western countries owing to factors such as increased sensitivity to health risks. 14 , 23 The prevalence of severe obesity, defined as a BMI of 35 or higher, is extremely low in those aged 65 and older, at about 0.3%. 24 This suggests that the patterns of mortality rates according to obesity levels differ between Korean, Caucasian, and European populations. 25 However, this manifestation of the obesity paradox may be partly due to the limitations of BMI, which does not distinguish between fat and lean mass. 26 Although higher fat mass increases the risk of mortality, lean mass exerts a protective effect. 27 A potential explanation for the inverse relationship between BMI and mortality is that being overweight or obese may serve as an indicator of better nutritional status, and the benefits of improved nutrition may outweigh the negative effects of a higher BMI. 28 Consequently, a low BMI due to low fat‐free mass might misclassify individuals and lead to biased outcomes. In contrast, mortality risk assessments based on abdominal obesity using WC showed significantly higher risks in those with abdominal obesity compared with those with normal WC, suggesting that WC is a more effective indicator of adiposity than BMI alone. 29 , 30

In populations characterized as obese according to BMI and WC criteria, PA exceeding recommended levels (≥500 MET‐min/week) significantly reduced the risk of CVD mortality. The protective effects of PA are more pronounced in groups with overweight or obesity. 31 To explain the pronounced effects observed in obese individuals, it is essential to consider that the protective effects of PA were particularly significant in this group. People with obesity generally face a higher risk of underlying health conditions such as hypertension and diabetes than individuals of normal weight. 32 Compared with the non‐obese population, individuals who are obese tend to have poorer blood glucose levels, lipid profiles, blood pressure, and inflammatory responses. In this population, the role of PA in improving or managing these health parameters is likely a major contributor to the observed protective effects. 30 , 33

In a previous study involving Korean individuals, those in the obese class 1 with sufficient PA showed an HR of 0.80 (95% CI: 0.75–0.92) compared with those with insufficient PA. 31 In the obese class 2, the group with sufficient PA had a lower mortality risk, with an HR of 0.72 (95% CI: 0.57–0.86), compared with those with insufficient PA, indicating a more substantial reduction than seen in the normal‐weight group. A meta‐analysis of eight cohorts demonstrated significant reductions in mortality risk in obese individuals with medium to high levels of PA compared with those in the same BMI or WC category but with low levels of PA. 34 Given that obese individuals typically have lower levels of PA and tend to have a sedentary lifestyle, promoting PA within this population is necessary to maximize health benefits. 4

Significant mortality risk reductions were noted in underweight older adults with over 1000 MET‐min/week of PA compared with those who were below recommended levels. Underweight older adults often have less muscle mass and lower metabolic rates, which increase the likelihood of decreased physical fitness. 35 Previous studies have confirmed that underweight older adults are more likely to have sarcopenia than their normal‐weight counterparts, primarily because of nutritional deficits and reduced PA. 36 , 37 However, PA can improve cardiorespiratory fitness and strengthen skeletal muscles, offsetting mortality risk. 38 , 39 In obese older adult populations, the observed reduction in all‐cause and CVD mortality risks compared with their counterparts with normal BMI may be explained by the potential adverse effects of dieting and fat loss, including malnutrition and detrimental effects on muscle and bone health. 40 Additionally, individuals with sarcopenic obesity are at high risk of negative health outcomes. 41 One study found that obesity alone did not provide sufficient evidence to link it directly to sarcopenia 42 however, muscle strength, rather than muscle mass, showed a significant relationship. To prevent CVD in older adults and reduce the associated mortality risk, encouraging them to engage in PA to improve physical functions such as muscle strength and gait speed should be emphasized to increase muscle mass. 43 While moderate to vigorous PA offers significant health benefits, even light PA has been shown to reduce mortality by nearly 30%. 44 Therefore, promoting light PA as a viable option for older adults can be beneficial in enhancing overall health outcomes.

This study has several limitations. First, reliance on self‐reported data for PA levels introduces potential bias, as participants may overestimate or underestimate their actual activity levels. This subjective measure could affect the accuracy of the relationship between PA and mortality rate across different levels of adiposity. Second, the absence of cardiorespiratory fitness data, which is a stronger predictor of prognosis than PA, limits the ability to fully evaluate the relationship between PA and mortality. Including such data would have provided a more comprehensive assessment of participants' overall health status. 45 Third, the issue of reverse causality should not be dismissed: lower PA levels may be a result of poor health status rather than a cause, which could misrepresent the direction of influence between PA and mortality. Fourth, only BMI and WC were used to measure adiposity; however, these measures do not provide a comprehensive assessment of body composition such as muscle mass and fat distribution, which are crucial for evaluating health outcomes related to PA and obesity. Finally, this study may not have adequately accounted for residual confounding factors that could influence the association between PA, adiposity, and mortality. The effects of previous cardioprotective medications, duration of obesity, and dietary patterns are critically important. This underscores the importance of designing future studies that effectively address these confounders to ensure more accurate elucidation of these complex relationships.

Conclusion

In this study of the Korean population, individuals categorized as overweight or obese by BMI had a lower mortality risk compared with those with normal BMI. Conversely, when obesity was measured using WC, those classified as obese had a higher mortality risk than their normal‐BMI counterparts. Additionally, PA was found to reduce mortality risk across all weight categories, with a particularly strong protective effect observed in individuals aged over 65.
