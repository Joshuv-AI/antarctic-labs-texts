Recreational Activities and Youth Outcomes: An Explainable Machine Learning Study
Authors: Yihong Zhao, Xuewei Han, Sarah W. Feldstein Ewing, Susan F. Tapert, Martin P. Paulus
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveRecreational activities are considered vital for emotional and cognitive health of youth, yet empirical research examining the specific associations between recreational activities and youth behavioral and cognitive outcomes is limited.MethodIn this cohort study, interpretable machine learning methods, specifically random forest paired with SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis, were applied to quantify the contribution of each activity to behavioral and cognitive outcomes in the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development℠ (ABCD) Study baseline data (ages 9-10). Data-driven activity groups were defined by SHAP contribution patterns for behavioral and cognitive outcomes. Engagement in these groups was then tested for association with the corresponding outcomes using linear mixed-effects models in the hold-out sample, both cross-sectionally and longitudinally.ResultsOf activities, 14 were identified with more favorable and 8 with less favorable behavioral patterns, and 13 were identified with more favorable and 2 with less favorable cognitive patterns. Activities with more favorable behavioral patterns (eg, skiing/snowboarding) were linked to fewer problems (β = −1.12, p < .001), whereas activities with less favorable patterns (eg, martial arts) were linked to more problems (β = 1.75, p < .001). For cognition, activities with less favorable patterns (eg, football) were associated with lower cognitive scores (β = −1.34, p < .001), whereas activities with more favorable patterns (eg, musical instruments) were associated with higher scores (β = .98, p < .001), and family income moderated this association (p < .001).ConclusionRecreational activities exhibit distinct patterns of association with cognitive and mental health outcomes. These data-driven findings highlight the importance of considering individual differences and contextual factors when investigating youth engagement in recreational activities.

Participation in recreational activities during childhood and adolescence is important in shaping multiple domains of development, including physical, emotional, and cognitive growth.1, 2, 3, 4 These activities range from team sports to artistic performances such as music and art, each offering unique opportunities for social engagement, confidence building, and community membership in addition to academic settings.5, 6, 7 However, many factors, such as time constraints, family income, and parental educational level, significantly influence recreational patterns and sports participation of children. For example, lower-income families often encourage their children to engage in unstructured play such as active play and walking within their immediate surroundings, whereas more affluent families tend to prioritize organized activities and structured programs for their children’s recreation.8,9 Although parents across all income levels value fun and fitness in sports and share concerns about injury risks and academic impact, lower-income parents may be more likely to see sports as a way to keep children engaged and as a potential opportunity for college scholarships.10 Existing literature offers limited guidance on how parents navigate decisions about sports and recreational activities for their children, particularly when faced with time or financial constraints. This highlights the need for better support in helping parents select activities that align with the developmental needs, interests, and long-term well-being of their children.

Studies have explored the association between recreational activities and mental and cognitive health in adolescence. Engagement in leisure activities has been associated with improved mental health outcomes11, 12, 13 and better cognitive performance.14, 15, 16 Despite this growing body of evidence, prior studies have largely relied on traditional statistical models that may overlook complex, nonlinear interactions between different types of activities and individual characteristics. Moreover, existing research has primarily focused on the aggregate- or population-level associations with limited individual-level insights into how specific activities relate to outcomes. To address these limitations, a novel analytical method such as an explainable machine learning approach, particularly the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) approach,17 can be used to uncover which specific recreational activities contribute most to mental health and cognitive outcomes at the individual level. The explainable approach enhances the interpretability of model predictions and provides insights that can help parents and policymakers make data-driven, personalized decisions about youth engagement in recreational activities.

In this investigation, we analyzed recreational activity data from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development℠ (ABCD) Study18 to understand how various activities relate to mental health and cognitive outcomes over time. Specifically, we grouped recreational activities using a data-driven procedure based on similarities in their SHAP contribution profiles across cognitive and mental health outcomes. We hypothesized that these data-driven groups would differ in their overall association patterns with the outcomes of interest. We also explored whether family income moderated these associations. This study aimed to provide new insights into the differential impacts of recreational activities (mental and cognitive) on health outcomes.

Method

Participants

Data were drawn from the ABCD Study® Release 5.1.18 For the present study, we included pre–COVID-19 assessment mainly from baseline, year 1, and year 2 follow-ups, as recreational activity patterns changed substantially following the onset of COVID-19–related shutdowns. Restricting analyses to pre–COVID-19 waves minimizes bias stemming from these historical shifts. Across these waves, participants ranged from 9 to 14 years of age. Participants were divided into discovery (n = 5,650), large-scale (n = 5,670), and small-scale (n = 299) hold-out datasets using the ABCD Study reproducible matched samples algorithm.19 The discovery sample was used for hypothesis generation, and the hold-out samples were used to test these data-driven hypotheses. Specifically, we used the baseline discovery dataset to identify patterns of association between recreational activities and behavioral and cognitive outcomes. The hold-out datasets were then used to assess the associations between these data-driven activity groups and behavioral and cognitive outcomes, both cross-sectionally at baseline and longitudinally across waves. The analysis pipeline is summarized in Figure S1, available online.

Recreational Activities Assessment

Main exposure variable was participation in 29 recreational activities, assessed by the Sports Activities Involvement Questionnaire (SAIQ), which was modeled after existing health behavior questionnaires.20 The baseline parent-reported SAIQ endorsed their child’s lifetime engagement in these activities. For each of the activities (Table S1, available online), the SAIQ captured binary participation status as well as time spent per session, number of days per week, number of months per year, and number of years participated. Based on the SHAP analysis,17 activities were classified into data-driven activity groups. In the association analyses, the primary predictors were the categorical activity counts within each data-driven activity group (coded as 0, 1, 2, and ≥3 endorsed activities). As a secondary exploratory measure, we also examined log-transformed time spent in each activity category to assess whether the observed associations were robust when using a continuous metric.

Behavior Problems and Cognitive Function

The behavioral outcome was evaluated through the parent-reported Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL) total problems raw scale. This scale is a valid measure for assessing a range of emotional (eg, withdrawn, depressed, anxious, and somatic symptoms) and behavioral (eg, aggressive and rule-breaking behaviors) problems,21 with higher scores indicating more behavioral problems. Cognitive function was measured using the National Institutes of Health Toolbox Cognition Battery, which provides an overall index of cognitive performance.22 Total cognitive function scores comprised the sum of all 7 domain-specific cognitive tests, including Picture Vocabulary, Oral Reading Recognition, Picture Sequence Memory, Flanker Inhibitory Control and Attention, List Sorting Working Memory, Pattern Comparison Processing Speed, and Dimensional Change Card Sort, with higher scores reflecting better cognitive function.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis took place from November 1, 2023, to March 26, 2024. An explainable machine learning model was employed to understand differential contributions of recreational activities on predicting behavioral problems and cognitive function.17 Specifically, 2 random forest models23 were first trained to predict these outcomes using binary participation status in 29 recreational activities. Optimal hyperparameters for the random forest models were selected through grid search. SHAP technique was then employed to analyze the contribution of each activity to the model predictions.17 SHAP is a game-theoretic approach that computes Shapley values, which represent the marginal contribution of each feature to the model’s prediction. Thus, SHAP values help explain how each activity affects the model’s prediction for an individual, considering the intricate associations among all activities in the model.

For each activity, SHAP values were calculated using the TreeSHAP algorithm17 to explain the predictions of the outcome. To obtain stable and reproducible SHAP values, we repeated the entire random forest training and TreeSHAP computation pipeline 10 times, each with a different random seed. For each run, TreeSHAP values were computed for every participant–feature combination. We then averaged the SHAP values across the 10 independent forests to produce a single, aggregated estimate for each participant–feature pair. All reported SHAP values, summary statistics, and beeswarm plots are based on these averaged SHAP estimates. The stability of SHAP estimates for each participant–feature pair was quantified using coefficient of variation (CV), defined as standard deviation of SHAP values divided by the mean SHAP values across 10 runs. Positive SHAP values indicate that the presence or absence of that activity contributes to an increase in the predicted outcome (eg, worse behavioral problems or better cognitive function), whereas negative SHAP values suggest that the activity contributes to a decrease in the outcome. For a specific individual and a specific outcome, the SHAP value of an activity represents the degree to which that person’s involvement (or lack thereof) in that activity contributes to prediction of his or her own outcome.

To analyze the effects of activities, participants were divided into engaged and not engaged groups. Linear mixed-effects models with family as random intercept compared the mean difference in SHAP values for each activity between 2 groups. Activities were then classified into 3 categories based on the following rules. Group1 and Group2 included activities that had consistently higher and lower mean SHAP values, respectively, in the engaged compared with the not engaged group, and no participants within the same group had SHAP values with opposite signs. In contrast, Group3 included activities with no significant group differences in mean SHAP value, opposite SHAP value signs within groups, and/or negligible contributions to outcome.

We conducted 2 sets of hypothesis tests in the hold-out samples: cross-sectional analyses at baseline and longitudinal analyses across all waves for behavioral problems. In both settings, we hypothesized that the data-driven activity groups would differ in their overall association patterns with the behavioral and cognitive outcomes of interest.

Associations Between Activity Types and Developmental Outcomes

Associations between activity types and developmental outcomes were evaluated using mixed-effects linear regression models, with family and study sites as random effects, controlling for age, sex assigned at birth, race/ethnicity, and family income. For the longitudinal validation analyses, we modeled outcomes across all available waves as repeated measures and included participant-level random intercepts to account for within-individual correlation over time. We also examined interaction effects between activities and family income and activities and sex assigned at birth, given their established relevance to activity access and participation patterns. Family income was treated as ordinal variable in the analysis. Model selection was guided by the Bayesian information criterion. Behavioral models used data from baseline and pre–COVID-19 follow-ups, whereas cognitive models used data from baseline only due to data availability.

Random forest regression models were implemented using RandomForestRegressor (https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.ensemble.RandomForestRegressor.html) from Python scikit-learn package, which follows Breiman’s original algorithm.24 The models were trained through mean squared error minimization and were evaluated using root mean squared error and R2. SHAP values were computed using the fitted random forest regression model to quantify the contribution of each feature to the predicted outcomes. Random forest models and SHAP value were calculated using scikit-learn and SHAP version 0.49.1 (https://github.com/shap/shap/releases/tag/v0.49.1) in Python version v3.10, with linear mixed-effects models fitted using lme4 in R version 4.4.2 (https://cran.r-project.org/bin/windows/base/old/4.4.2/). Data visualizations were generated using the Python package matplotlib and various R packages. The full analytical pipeline is available on GitHub (https://github.com/ZhaoCUMC/ABCD_PhyActivity).

Several sensitivity analyses were performed to evaluate the robustness and reproducibility of the activity classifications and associated findings in the large-scale hold-out sample. All sensitivity analyses were performed in independent, nonoverlapping samples to avoid circularity. First, we reestimated SHAP values in the independent large-scale hold-out dataset to assess whether the 3 data-driven activity categories remained stable across samples. Second, we ran the regression models again in a separate small independent hold-out sample (n = 299) to examine whether the general direction and relative magnitude of associations found in the large-scale hold-out sample (n = 5,670) were consistent. Third, we repeated analyses using frequency-based measures of activity time to explore whether similar patterns emerged when considering participation duration.

Types of Recreational Activity

For cognitive outcome (Figure 1B), 13 activities were associated with increased predictions on the total cognitive score, whereas 2 activities showed the opposite association. Specifically, cognitive (Cog)-Group1 activities, with mean SHAP values ranging from 0.15 to 1.84, include playing musical instruments, skiing/snowboarding, soccer, drama/theater, tennis, competitive games, martial arts, gymnastics, baseball/softball, crafts, lacrosse, ballet/dance, and field hockey. Cog-Group2 activities, with mean SHAP values ranging from −0.44 to −0.52, include football and skateboarding, indicating that participation might be related to lowered cognitive function. Cog-Group3 activities, such as rugby and horseback riding, had SHAP value magnitudes close to zero, indicating no or unclear association with cognitive function.

Figure 1 also shows the SHAP values of nonparticipation. For most activities, nonparticipation does not appear to be significantly associated with behavioral problems and cognition, as evidenced by the blue dots that are close to the zero line. Notable exceptions include playing musical instruments (mean [SD] SHAP: −1.25 [0.10]) and soccer (mean [SD] SHAP: −0.67 [0.14]), where nonparticipation seems to have a pronounced negative association with cognitive outcome.

Similar activity classification results were observed in the hold-out data (Figure S2, available online), thus confirming the robustness of these findings. Figure S3, available online, highlights a set of 9 activities, including musical instruments, skiing/snowboarding, soccer, tennis, competitive games, ballet/dance, field hockey, gymnastics, and lacrosse, that were associated with both fewer behavioral problems and enhanced cognitive function. Across repeated model runs, most activities demonstrated low to moderate CV (ie, CV ≤0.5) in their SHAP values, with only a few exceptions showing magnitude of CV >1 (Figure S4, available online). This indicates that SHAP contributions were highly stable and reproducible across runs. Figure S5, available online, illustrates the rank of feature importance based on random forest Gini importance from discovery data and SHAP-based feature importance from both discovery and large-sample hold-out datasets. They showed broadly consistent patterns, with several activities repeatedly emerging as relatively influential across methods. Finally, Table S3, available online, shows variance (R2) explained by activities; the modest R2 values (0.15 for cognitive function, and <2% for behavioral outcome) indicate that individual recreational activities alone have limited predictive value.

Discussion

This investigation used data-driven approaches to examine associations between recreational activity and behavioral and cognitive outcomes in youth. Using interpretable machine learning methods, we found that data-derived activity groups differed in their patterns of association with behavioral and cognitive measures. We identified 14 activities associated with more favorable behavioral outcome and 13 activities associated with favorable cognitive outcome. For cognitive function, although participants from high-income families had higher overall cognitive scores, this gap narrowed among participants who had more involvement in Cog-Group1 activies. These findings highlight emerging patterns and serve as hypothesis-generating insights into how recreational engagement relates to youth development.

Several mechanisms may help explain why engagement in certain activities is more strongly associated with beneficial behavioral outcomes. A primary mechanism involves the enhancement of executive functions, including inhibitory control, working memory, and cognitive flexibility, which are essential for regulating attention, emotions, and behaviors.25 Deficits in executive functions represent transdiagnostic risks for both internalizing (eg, anxiety, depression) and externalizing (eg, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, aggression) problems. Systematic reviews and randomized controlled trials consistently demonstrate that acute and chronic physical exercise significantly improves executive functions in youth, particularly in attention, inhibition, and cognitive flexibility.26 Neuroimaging studies support these findings. Higher fitness levels correlate with structural and functional brain changes, including increased gray matter volumes in the prefrontal cortex and greater frontal activation during inhibitory control tasks.25 Physically active youth also show greater gray matter volumes in brain regions implicated in emotional regulation and cognitive control, such as the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex.27 These structural changes are linked to reduced internalizing symptoms, likely via enhanced stress resilience and emotional regulation. Diffusion tensor imaging studies further reveal better white matter integrity in active youth, facilitating efficient cognitive and emotional processing.28 In addition, exercise increases neurotrophins, such as brain-derived neurotrophic factor, which promote neurogenesis, synaptic plasticity, and neuronal survival, especially in the hippocampus and frontal cortex.25 Elevated brain-derived neurotrophic factor levels in physically active youth may directly contribute to greater stress resilience and improved mood regulation.

Beyond neurocognitive factors, these recreational physical activities often provide structured, socially rich environments that facilitate social-emotional learning. Team-based activities, for instance, offer structured contexts in which youth learn essential skills, such as empathy, teamwork, conflict resolution, and emotional self-regulation.29,30 The structured nature of organized physical activities also provides supportive social contexts in which youth can develop positive peer relationships, receive mentorship, and improve their self-esteem.31,32 These psychosocial assets directly translate into better behavioral outcomes, reducing aggressive impulses, enhancing emotional regulation skills, and buffering against feelings of anxiety and depression.31,33, 34, 35, 36, 37 Physical activities also serve as prosocial outlets for emotional expression and stress release.38 Through supervised sports and structured play, youth can channel emotional energy constructively, reducing the likelihood of disruptive or maladaptive behaviors. Intervention studies, especially those focusing on team-based or structured individual activities, highlight the critical role of adult guidance and supportive social environments in magnifying the positive effects of physical activity on behavioral health of youth.

Several recreational activities showed favorable associations with both cognitive and behavioral outcomes. Notably, some activities were associated with both domains simultaneously, prompting the question of how to interpret this overlapping pattern. In this article, overlap refers to the observation that some organized physical activities appear in the model as being similarly associated with both domains of symptoms (ie, cognitive problems and behavior problems) at the population level, such that the same activity can be identified among the higher-ranked features for each outcome. It is important to note that this overlap should be interpreted as shared statistical association patterns in the ABCD Study data and not as evidence that any specific sport causes a particular symptom profile or as evidence concerning the mechanisms by which youth enter or remain in particular activities. Because activity participation is not randomized and is plausibly correlated with child-, family-, and community-level factors (eg, socioeconomic resources, school context, parental support, baseline temperament), overlapping associations may reflect selection and contextual confounding as much as effects of participation itself. Thus, we treat overlap as a hypothesis-generating signal that these activities may be embedded in shared social or developmental contexts relevant to multiple symptom domains, motivating future work with richer covariate measurement, temporally resolved designs, and (where feasible) quasi-experimental or randomized approaches to adjudicate alternative explanations.

Although our analyses do not test mechanisms, the observed overlap is consistent with broader literature documenting interconnections between cognitive functioning and behavioral health. This pattern is suggestive of potentially synergistic associations across domains, rather than evidence of causal effects. Although extensive research has documented benefits of physical activities on cognitive development,15,39 our findings highlight the interrelated nature of cognitive functions and behavioral problems. Improvements in one domain may facilitate parallel improvements in the other,40 consistent with intricate bidirectional associations.15 Cognitive abilities can shape psychological outcomes in youth.41,42 Conversely, mental health disorders may, in turn, impair cognitive function, particularly among young individuals.43 Yet, few studies have concurrently examined associations between recreational activities and both cognitive and behavioral domains. Therefore, our investigation provides timely and integrative evidence addressing this important gap.

Finally, we found recreational activity participation showed significant income-based disparities, with affluent families generally having higher participation rates across most activities (Table S2, available online). Although mean cognitive scores were higher among higher-income families, greater engagement in multiple activities in Cog-Group1 activities appeared to attenuate the cognitive differences observed between income groups. Football stands out as one of the few popular activities without significant income-related participation disparities. However, in this investigation, football participation, along with rugby, skateboarding, and others, was associated with both increased behavioral problems and lower cognitive function. This could potentially be explained by the higher risk of head injuries and repetitive head impacts associated with these activities,44, 45, 46 which have been linked to adverse effects on cognitive functioning, emotional regulation, and overall well-being.47

Our investigations highlighted the importance of considering heterogeneous impacts of various recreational activities. Although physical activity is broadly beneficial for youth development, the impacts may vary across activity types, and some activities may carry risks that warrant further evaluations. Random forest models showed that recreational activity features collectively explained approximately 15% of the variation in cognitive function and <2% of the variation in behavioral problems. We note that the small proportion of variance explained by recreational activity features is neither surprising nor indicative of unimportance. Adolescent mental health outcomes, such as those captured by the CBCL total problems scale, are inherently multifactorial, influenced by a confluence of genetic, familial, socioeconomic, and environmental factors.48,49 These modest effect sizes highlight the need to situate recreational activities within an ecological framework that considers the combined influence of multilevel factors on behavioral problems.50

Taken together, this work contributes preliminary evidence that the benefits of physical activities may differ by activity type and may plateau at higher levels of engagement. Building on these initial findings, future research should take a hypothesis-driven approach to examine the mechanisms underlying these patterns via advanced neuroimaging and well-designed intervention studies. Such efforts will be important for identifying physical activity engagement patterns that are optimally tailored to different youth populations and that can maximize cognitive and mental health benefits.

The strengths of this study lie in the application of an explainable machine learning technique, which enabled us to characterize the complex associations between recreational activities and youth developmental outcomes. The use of SHAP values provided interpretable insights into how individual activities contributed to model predictions, allowing us to identify nuanced patterns of association at both the population and the individual levels.17 This approach is particularly valuable for understanding the impact of lifestyle factors on mental health, as such factors often exhibit heterogeneous associations with health outcomes.51 By capturing this heterogeneity, an explainable machine learning approach offers a powerful framework for elucidating individualized patterns of risk and protection. This exploratory analyses approach also provides a hypothesis-generating foundation that can help refine and prioritize future research questions using more targeted, evidence-informed study designs.

This study has limitations. First, the categorization of recreational activities relied on SHAP values calculated by the TreeSHAP algorithm, which may introduce biases in estimating exact SHAP values.17 However, the robustness of our findings was strengthened by rigorous sensitivity analyses. Future research could benefit from employing other state-of-the-art machine learning and SHAP algorithms to replicate our study findings. Second, this study is observational, precluding the establishment of causal associations between engagement in recreational activities and child developmental outcomes. Although our findings provide valuable insights into the heterogeneous associations between recreational activities and child development outcomes, caution should be exercised in interpreting causality. Future research should continue to explore the intricate associations between different types of recreational activities and developmental outcomes. Longitudinal studies and experimental designs could help establish causal associations and identify the mechanisms underlying these associations. Additionally, further investigation into the risks associated with certain activities, such as football and skateboarding, is warranted. Despite these limitations, our study contributes meaningful insights into the diverse effects of recreational activities on child developmental outcomes.

This study used explainable machine learning approaches to characterize data-driven patterns linking recreational activities with behavioral and cognitive outcomes in the ABCD Study cohort. The findings suggest that different activities may relate to youth development in distinct ways and that these associations can vary across socioeconomic contexts. The observed income-related differences emphasize the importance of considering broader environmental and contextual factors when interpreting activity-outcome associations.

Although these results are exploratory and not intended to guide clinical or policy decisions, they provide a starting point for future, hypothesis-driven work aimed at clarifying how recreational engagement fits within a broader ecological framework that includes genetic, familial, school, and neighborhood influences. Replication in independent samples, comparisons across multiple modeling approaches (beyond random forests), and longitudinal follow-up will be essential to determine whether the observed patterns generalize and whether they hold any relevance for future public health or educational initiatives.
