Evaluation of gabapentin administration on neurologic examination in 2 different age groups of healthy cats
Authors: Allison DuPont, Natalia Zidan, Lizabeth C Lueck, Starr Cameron
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGabapentin is often administered PO for preappointment or in‐hospital anxiolysis in cats. A previous study reported mild changes on the neurologic examination after administration.ObjectivesInvestigate the effects of gabapentin on anxiety, sedation, compliance, and neurologic examination in 2 age groups of cats.AnimalsThirty‐one young cats and 12 geriatric cats perceived by their owners to be healthy and neurologically normal.MethodsProspective double‐blinded clinical crossover study. Assessment of baseline sedation and anxiety was performed before initial neurologic examinations and after gabapentin administration (100 mg/cat). Assessments were repeated 90 to 120 minutes after administration. Ease of handling pregabapentin and postgabapentin was assessed in the younger cats. All examinations were performed by a board‐certified veterinary neurologist and scoring of examinations was performed by a different, masked board‐certified neurologist.ResultsSixteen cats (50%) in the younger cohort and 6 cats (50%) in the geriatric cohort exhibited an increase in their overall neurologic examination score postgabapentin administration, mainly through new or progressive postural reaction deficits and gait changes. Anxiety and sedation scores were significantly changed in the geriatric population (P < .01, P = .004, respectively); however, only sedation scores were significantly increased in the younger cats after gabapentin administration (P = .004).Conclusions and Clinical ImportanceAll study participants showed mild neurologic changes after gabapentin administration, most markedly noted in the geriatric population. Dose reduction of gabapentin for preappointment anxiolysis and neurologic examination in geriatric patients should be considered.Keywords: anxiety, behavior, feline, neurolocalization, neurology, sedation

INTRODUCTION

Handling domestic cats in a veterinary hospital setting for neurologic examination can be difficult because of anxiety and intolerance to handling, which then results in an abbreviated examination. Detecting subtle deficits during the neurologic examination is crucial because incomplete assessments may result in incorrect neuroanatomic localization. Accurate assessment of neurological patients is also critical for determining the best diagnostic tests and ultimately, recommending treatment options. Several strategies, including pheromone treatment, fear‐free handling, and adjunctive anxiolytic medications, often are used in cats to decrease the stress experienced in hospital and facilitate ease of complete examinations. 1 , 2 , 3

Gabapentin, an alpha2delta ligand, is a PO administered medication that often is administered to cats before routine appointments or as an adjunctive treatment for anxiety during hospitalization. 4 , 5 In cats, peak plasma concentrations are reached approximately 100 minutes after dosing, with the most commonly reported adverse effects being incoordination and sedation. 6 , 7 The mechanism behind the anxiolytic effects is unknown; however, it is postulated to be secondary to an inhibitory effect on calcium voltage gated channels, which ultimately leads to decreased release of neurotransmitters, such as the excitatory neurotransmitter, glutamate. 8 A single preappointment dose of gabapentin (100 mg per cat) has been shown to be well tolerated and highly efficacious in minimizing stress and fear‐related behaviors in healthy pet and community cats, improving ease of handling while in hospital. 4 , 9

Because some of the neuronal pathways tested during the neurologic examination rely on cortical input, administration of anxiolytic medications has the potential to produce false deficits or misleading findings, resulting in inaccurate neurolocalization. These effects can be particularly marked in older patients because of aging‐related decreases in organ function, resulting in higher bioavailability of these medications. 10

A recent study evaluated the effect of trazodone, a commonly prescribed anxiolytic medication, on the neurologic examination in healthy adult dogs and found that approximately 20% of dogs had new or worsening postural reaction deficits. 11 Similarly, another study performed in young cats reported postural reaction deficits during the neurologic examination after gabapentin administration. 12 These cats were assigned into 2 different groups (treatment and placebo) and older cats were not included in the study. 12

Therefore, our aim was to further evaluate the effects of gabapentin on anxiety, sedation, and compliance in cats, as well as to understand the influence of gabapentin administration on the neurologic examination using 2 populations: healthy young cats and geriatric cats with stable comorbidities. We hypothesized the following: (1) most cats in the study would exhibit new or worsening neurologic deficits after administration of gabapentin (mainly observed through postural reaction deficits and mentation changes) and (2) the geriatric group of cats (≥12 years of age) would exhibit more marked neurologic deficits than the younger population after gabapentin administration, possibly because of a combination of age and stable comorbidities.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This prospective, double‐blinded, clinical crossover study recruited healthy, neurologically normal cats owned by faculty, staff, and students at the University of Wisconsin‐Madison Veterinary Care (UWVC) hospital. The protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Wisconsin‐Madison, School of Veterinary Medicine (IACUC–V006637). Informed consent was obtained from all owners through an electronic questionnaire before enrollment in the study.

Animals

Enrolled cats were divided into 2 groups: young cats and geriatric cats.

Young cats

The inclusion criteria for young cats were as follows: (1) between 1 and 6 years of age, (2) never diagnosed with a chronic medical condition, (3) perceived to be healthy and neurologically normal by their owners, and (4) not receiving any chronic medications (including behavioral medications) other than monthly preventatives (flea, tick, and heartworm). Electronic questionnaires were distributed to owners, and gathered information regarding previously diagnosed medical conditions, indoor/outdoor status, feline leukemia virus/feline immunodeficiency virus (FeLV/FIV) status, and daily medications (including monthly preventatives or supplements).

Geriatric cats

For the geriatric subset of the study sample, cats 12 years of age or older were recruited. Cats with known neoplasia, infectious diseases, or chronic conditions that were not stable (eg, worsening chronic kidney disease [CKD]) were excluded. All age‐related comorbidities were required to be stable on laboratory testing and as perceived by their owners.

Study design

All cats were housed in individual cages in the same room for the study before the examinations. Examinations were performed in a separate environment (enclosed examination room) away from the other study participants to minimize distractions and variations in stimuli. Owners were not present during the assessments. Owners were instructed to fast their cat before administration of gabapentin (before drop‐off and initial neurologic examination); however, owner compliance was variable in the younger group. Despite the challenge in compliance, not all cats that were offered food in their respective kennels during the examination time period were observed eating, therefore an unknown quantity of food (if any) was consumed.

Before each neurologic examination (before and after PO administration of gabapentin), baseline anxiety and sedation levels were evaluated for each cat. Levels of sedation and anxiety were assessed using validated veterinary scales, including a sedation score and the cat stress score (CSS), respectively. 13 , 14 In the younger group, ease of handling also was assessed before each examination using a scale developed previously. 15 Anxiety, sedation, and ease of handling scoring was performed by the same investigators (A. Dupont [younger cats], L. Lueck [geriatric cats]) and neurologic examinations were performed by a single board‐certified veterinary neurologist (N. Zidan [younger cats], S. Cameron [geriatric cats]). Each neurologic examination (before and after PO administration of gabapentin) was filmed using a standard iPhone 12 video camera by 1 of the investigators (A. Dupont [younger cats], L. Lueck [geriatric cats]).

After the initial neurologic examination, each cat was given a single 100 mg gabapentin capsule PO and blood samples were collected for analysis. Thereafter, 3 mL of blood was collected from the medial saphenous vein using a 22‐gauge needle and submitted for analysis (PCV, total solids [TS] and serum biochemistry [young cats]; serum biochemistry, CBC and total T4 concentration [geriatric cats]). Additionally, the geriatric population had blood pressure, ophthalmologic examination, and complete urinalysis performed.

Within 90 to 120 minutes after administration of gabapentin, anxiety and sedation were reevaluated, and a 2nd neurologic examination was performed and recorded by the same investigators. Handling scores also were reassessed in young cats during the postgabapentin neurologic examination.

Neurologic examination video files were compiled and randomized. Audio was removed, as well as any evidence of name identification, date, and time. A numerical code was assigned to each video (before and after gabapentin examination videos) before being reviewed and blindly scored by a 2nd veterinary neurologist (S. Cameron [younger cats], N. Zidan [geriatric cats]). Each cat served as an internal control for before and after neurologic examination.

Neurologic examination

A complete neurologic examination was performed on each cat, assessing mentation, cranial nerves, postural reactions, spinal reflexes, gait, and pain on paraspinal palpation. Mentation was evaluated based on the 4 levels of consciousness (bright/quiet, alert and responsive; obtunded; stuporous; comatose). Cranial nerves were assessed using the following reflexes or responses: pupillary light reflex, palpebral reflex, menace response, facial/nasal sensation, and oculocephalic reflex. An external gag reflex was performed in most cats. Postural reactions were performed using paw replacement test; however, hopping, wheelbarrowing, and extensor postural thrust also were performed in cats where paw replacement results appeared inconsistent, or patient compliance with examination was lacking. The withdrawal reflex (in all 4 limbs), patellar reflex (hindlimbs), and perineal reflex were prioritized for assessment of the spinal reflexes; the cutaneous trunci reflex was attempted only if cats were amenable to handling during examination. Gait was assessed by allowing the cat to roam around the examination room freely, as well as using toys and treats to encourage exploration as needed. Spinal pain was assessed using gentle paraspinal palpation and evaluating cervical and tail range of motion.

The neurologic examination was assessed and scored in a sequential manner for each participant. Videos were scored by a masked board‐certified neurologist (Starr Cameron [younger cats], Starr Cameron [geriatric cats]) using previously determined criteria and scoring (Table 1). 11

Statistics

Findings were summarized using median and interquartile range for non‐normally distributed data and mean ± SD for normally distributed data. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro‐Wilk test. The results of each cat's sedation, anxiety, and handling scores were compared using a 2‐tailed paired t‐test to determine the level of effect on these variables. Nonparametric data (vital parameters) were compared using a Wilcoxon signed rank test to determine significance. The statistical program JMP Pro Version 17.0 was used for all calculations. A P‐value of <.05 was used to determine significance for all statistical analyses.

RESULTS

Study groups

Young cats

This cohort consisted of 31 young cats (15 male castrated, 12 female spayed, 4 female intact). Domestic shorthair cats were the most frequently encountered breed in this group (n = 22), followed by domestic long hair (n = 6), domestic medium hair (n = 2), and Siamese cat (n = 1). The mean age was 3.7 ± 1.7 years. The mean body weight was 4.82 ± 1.02 kg. The average dosage of gabapentin administered was 21.7 ± 4.9 mg/kg. Neurologic examinations were on average 3 minutes 35 seconds in duration (range, 51 seconds‐5 minutes 42 seconds) and the time between the 1st and 2nd neurologic examination ranged from 1 hour 20 minutes to 2 hours 9 minutes (mean duration, 1 hour 54 minutes). Baseline laboratory testing performed before gabapentin administration did not identify any clinically relevant abnormalities for any cat in this group.

Geriatric cats

The geriatric cohort consisted of 12 cats (8 male castrated, 4 female spayed). Breeds included domestic shorthair cats (n = 9), 2 domestic long hair and 1 domestic medium hair cat. The mean age of the geriatric population was 15.7 ± 2.5 years. The mean body weight was 5.0 ± 1.4 kg and the mean gabapentin dosage administered was 20.5 ± 6.5 mg/kg. Neurologic examinations were on average 5 minutes 37 seconds in duration (range, 3 minutes 53 seconds‐7 minutes 36 seconds) and the average time between the 1st and 2nd examination was 2 hours 5 minutes (range, 1 hour 40 minutes‐2 hours 40 minutes). Laboratory testing performed before study initiation detected comorbidities in 9 of the 12 geriatric cats, which included CKD, International Renal Interest Society (IRIS) Stage 2 (n = 5; 42%), bacteriuria with an active sediment and dilute urine specific gravity (USG; n = 1; 8%), and hyperthyroidism, defined as total serum T4 concentration >3.8 μg/dL (n = 3; 25%).

Vital parameters

Vital parameters (heart rate and blood pressure) before and after administration of gabapentin were measured in 7 of 12 geriatric cats. In 1 cat, vital parameters were successfully obtained only after administration of gabapentin. Before gabapentin administration, median heart rate was 200 beats per minute (bpm; range, 180‐240 bpm) and blood pressure before was 150 mm Hg (range, 101‐210 mm Hg). After administration of gabapentin, median heart rate was 200 bpm (range, 160‐205 bpm), and median blood pressure was 160 mm Hg (range, 140‐160 mm Hg). No significant difference was found in vital parameters (blood pressure and heart rate) measured before and after gabapentin administration (P = 1.5, P = 0.4, respectively).

Scales—anxiety, sedation, and compliance

Young cats

Based on the CSS, the level of anxiety before and after gabapentin administration of younger cats was not found to be significantly different (P = .2; Figure 1); however, level of sedation, using the previously mentioned sedation scoring system, was found to be significantly increased (P = .0001) when comparing scores of before and after administration of gabapentin (Figure 2).

Geriatric cats

Within the geriatric population, the level of anxiety was significantly lower after gabapentin administration when compared with the evaluation before gabapentin (P < .01; Figure 1). Furthermore, the level of sedation in geriatric cats was significantly higher (P = .004) after gabapentin administration when compared with their sedation scores immediately before gabapentin administration (Figure 2).

Neurologic examination before gabapentin administration

Complete neurologic examinations were performed before administration of gabapentin in all study participants, with the exception of 1 geriatric cat, wherein limited tolerance prevented complete assessment of spinal reflexes or paraspinal palpation.

Young cats

Nine of the 31 (29%) young cats exhibited at least 1 neurologic abnormality on assessment before gabapentin administration. Gait changes such as mild paresis or ataxia were noted in 4 cats, reaction to paraspinal palpation was elicited in 5 cats (pain was localized to the thoracolumbar spine in 4 of the 5 cats), and delayed postural reactions of at least 1 limb were observed in 3 cats.

Geriatric cats

On initial assessment of the geriatric group, 1 of the 12 cats (8.33%) was found to have neurologic deficits on examination, characterized by mildly delayed postural reactions in ipsilateral limbs.

Neurologic examination after gabapentin administration

Sixteen cats (50%) in the younger group and 6 cats (50%) in the geriatric group exhibited an increase (ie, worsening) in their overall neurologic examination score after administration of gabapentin (ie, an increase in neurologic deficits was detected). Despite gabapentin administration, 1 cat in the younger group and 1 cat in the geriatric group had a truncated examination because of temperament and decreased compliance after gabapentin administration.

Gabapentin was well‐tolerated by most study participants. Only 2 young cats exhibited adverse effects after administration, which included vomiting (n = 1) and hypersalivation (n = 1).

Young cats

One cat in the younger group was classified as obtunded after gabapentin administration. No cranial nerve deficits were observed on initial examination; however, 1 cat had an absent menace response in both eyes after receiving gabapentin.

Ten of the young cats (32%) exhibited a newly detected gait abnormality (mild to moderate ataxia or paresis or both) after gabapentin administration. Eleven of the 31 young cats (34.4%) exhibited postural reaction deficits during the postgabapentin examination. Of these 11 cats, 5 (45%) had all 4 limbs affected, 4 cats had 2 limbs affected, and 2 cats had 1 limb affected (Table 2).

Geriatric cats

Two cats in the geriatric group (16.7%) were considered obtunded after gabapentin administration. Similar to the younger group, only 1 cat had an absent menace response in both eyes after receiving gabapentin, despite an initially normal cranial nerve examination.

Within the group of geriatric cats, 5 cats (42%) exhibited a newly detected worsening of mild to moderate paresis and ataxia. Five of the 12 geriatric cats (41.7%) exhibited postural reaction deficits after gabapentin administration. All 5 cats were found to have absent paw placement in all 4 limbs. One of the 5 cats had progression of postural reaction deficits after gabapentin administration (only 2 limbs originally found to be affected on initial assessment); postural deficits for the remaining 4 cats were new developments after administration of gabapentin (Table 2).

In all study participants (young and geriatric), no changes in spinal reflexes or newfound development of pain on paraspinal palpation were noted after gabapentin. Geriatric cats with stable comorbidities detected on baseline laboratory testing, specifically those with CKD, did not exhibit more marked neurologic deficits in comparison to the geriatric cats that had normal laboratory test results.

DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, our study is the 1st to use a crossover study design to evaluate the anxiolytic effects and impact of gabapentin on the neurologic examination in young and geriatric cats. Our results indicate that, in young and geriatric cats, administration of gabapentin can result in the new development or worsening of previously determined neurologic deficits detected on the neurologic examination. As suggested in previously published studies, our findings support that these deficits are observed most markedly with postural reactions and gait abnormalities. 11 , 12

Half of the cats in our study exhibited newly detected deficits or abnormalities on the neurologic examination after gabapentin administration. Approximately 40% of all cats in our study showed new postural reaction deficits, in addition to new paresis and ataxia after receiving gabapentin. As suggested by previous studies, these changes potentially are explained by the cortical effects of this medication. 12 Similarly, gait disturbances (including ataxia) also are commonly reported adverse effects in humans receiving gabapentin. 16 Adverse effects of gabapentin reportedly have been more commonly observed in humans receiving high doses. 17 However, studies in domestic animals inconsistently have found a direct relationship between dosage (mg/kg) and severity of adverse effects. 9 , 12 Within our study population, a relationship between higher dose and neurologic signs was not observed.

Only 2 cats in our study (1 cat in each group), exhibited cranial nerve deficits characterized by an absent menace response; however, assessment of the menace response is subjectively challenging to interpret in cats. A recent study found that success of the menace response in ophthalmologically and neurologically normal cats varied with examination technique. 18 External factors including level of anxiety while in hospital, ambient noise, and outdoor status have the potential to impact the outcome of the menace response, making it difficult to attribute the administration of gabapentin to the diminished response as observed in these 2 cats.

A similar percentage of cats in both age groups exhibited worsening on the neurologic examination after gabapentin administration. However, when compared with the affected younger cats, the geriatric cats developed more severe additional deficits after gabapentin was administered, compared with their own scores on the pregabapentin examination. This effect is best observed through the changes found in postural reactions. Additionally, all geriatric cats found to have postural reaction deficits, after gabapentin administration, exhibited changes in all 4 limbs. Meanwhile, the younger cats experiencing new postgabapentin postural reaction deficits showed variable degrees of severity for postural reactions, with only 45% of those experiencing them in all 4 limbs.

Potential causes for the increased severity of neurologic deficits observed in the geriatric group when compared with younger cats may include underlying comorbidities commonly associated with aging, such as CKD. Forty‐two percent of the cats in the geriatric group showed changes on prescreening laboratory testing indicative of mild azotemia as characterized by the IRIS staging, 19 consistent with previous studies showing that the prevalence of CKD is between 30% and 40% in cats >10 years of age. 20 A recent study found that cats with stable CKD (IRIS Stages II and III) exhibited higher serum gabapentin concentrations, at 2 time points after administration, in comparison with healthy cats, despite dose reduction. 21 In humans and most species, gabapentin is cleared in its unchanged form by renal excretion. 22 Consequently, dose reduction in people with documented kidney disease is recommended to prevent adverse effects, including impaired consciousness, ataxia, and confusion. 23 Information and guidelines for dose adjustments in cats with CKD is limited, with predominant reliance on extrapolation of data from other species. 24 In our study population, cats that showed laboratory results consistent with IRIS Stage II CKD did not consistently exhibit more marked neurologic signs (moderate paresis or ataxia, obtundation) when compared with their geriatric counterparts.

Preappointment gabapentin historically has been administered for its presumed anxiolytic effect in veterinary medicine. In the literature, various studies have shown a decrease in perceived stress with administration in both cats and dogs. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 25 Our results show consistent significant findings in anxiolytic effects within the geriatric cats; however, no significant difference in anxiety was identified after administration of gabapentin in the younger group. In the literature, there remains a mixed consensus regarding the anxiolytic and sedation effects of gabapentin. A previous study with gabapentin in confined community cats showed that 60 minutes was adequate for sedation, whereas the highest stress reduction only was achieved after 2 hours. 5 In contrast, a study using client‐owned cats found that most did not exhibit signs of sedation after 70 minutes, whereas most of the study population achieved anxiolytic effects. 26 In our study design, participants were scored after removal from their kennel and within the examination room before handling. The 100 mg per cat PO dose of gabapentin was chosen because of previous consensus in the literature that it was well‐tolerated for preappointment management of fearful cats. Neurologic examinations were timed between 80 and 120 minutes after administration, which correlates with previously reported peak plasma concentrations. 8 The scoring performed in our study occurred during this timeframe before initiating the 2nd examination. Because postural reactions and coordination of gait have complex pathways relying heavily on cortical input, it is possible that the neurologic deficits detected in our study were within the time frame reflecting cortical concentrations that elicited peak sedation rather than anxiolysis.

Our study had some limitations. Because of challenges in owner compliance, some of the younger cats in our study were variably fasted with free choice kibble available in their kennel. In humans, it is known that the absorption kinetics of gabapentin are different in fed and fasted states as well as influenced by the type of meal consumed (eg, macronutrient composition). 27 , 28 Additionally, it has been shown that bioavailability is reliant on the presence and saturation of intestinal transporters. 29 Despite this limitation in the younger group, all cats in the geriatric group were reliably fasted throughout the study examination period and yet, even with this context, no direct linear relationship was identified between dosage (mg/kg) and severity of neurologic signs in any of the geriatric cats, including those cats with normal baseline laboratory test results (n = 3). In addition, although food was available in the kennels of some young cats, it is unclear if the available food was consumed while in hospital.

An additional known adverse effect of gabapentin administration is gastrointestinal upset, which was only observed in 2 cats in our study (vomiting and hypersalivation). The cat that developed vomiting was in the young cat cohort, and emesis occurred shortly after administration of gabapentin with access to food in the kennel. The dosage of gabapentin for this cat was 20.5 mg/kg. Despite repeated episodes of vomiting, this cat still exhibited substantial changes on neurologic examination after gabapentin administration. Although no appreciable trend between the severity of neurologic deficits and gabapentin dosage (mg/kg) was observed, the variation in fasting status among the young cats in our study may have resulted in late neurologic deficits that were not detected at the time of the examination.

Another study limitation was the small sample size of the geriatric population. Almost 60% of the geriatric cats our study had a concurrent comorbidity (CKD, hyperthyroidism). Although geriatric cats with an associated comorbidity did not consistently exhibit increased severity of neurologic signs, it is difficult to make definitive conclusions regarding the connection between comorbidities, specifically renal dysfunction, and neurologic severity using our results. The cats with CKD in our study were geriatric and diagnosed with mild to moderate azotemia (IRIS Stage II). The relationship between age, degree of renal dysfunction, and rate of gabapentin excretion is still under investigation, but a recent study found a correlation between gabapentin concentrations and serum creatinine concentrations, which may suggest that drug accumulation can worsen with progressive disease. 21 , 24 Furthermore, previous clinical trials in humans have shown significant variability in bioavailability of the medication among healthy individuals. 30 Future studies should focus on utilizing age‐matched controls with different stages of renal dysfunction as well as measurements of serum gabapentin concentrations to further understand and aid in the development of practical dosing guidelines for veterinary patients.

Our results showed that gabapentin exerts sedative effects on cats regardless of their age, likely contributing to the mild changes in the neurologic examination as observed with postural reactions and gait. However, geriatric cats have the potential to exhibit more marked adverse effects of gabapentin. Our findings support the potential for preappointment anxiolytic medications, such as gabapentin, to impact findings on the neurologic examination and, furthermore, highlight the need for practitioners to carefully consider dosing of these medications in geriatric patients or patients with known comorbidities, such as renal dysfunction, to minimize the increased risk of these associated known adverse effects.
