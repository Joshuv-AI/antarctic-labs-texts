Imported Schistosoma intercalatum/guineensis infection: a retrospective study from a referral Tropical Medicine Unit in Barcelona, Spain
Authors: Nicole Jeria-Visintini, Cristina Bocanegra, Fernando Salvador, Begoña Treviño, Francesc Zarzuela, Núria Serre-Delcor, Joan Martínez-Campreciós, Mª Luisa Aznar, Juan Espinosa-Pereiro, Diana Pou, Pau Bosch-Nicolau, Inés Oliveira, Lidia Goterris, Elena Sulleiro, Adrián Sánchez-Montalvá, Israel Molina
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSchistosomiasis due to the species Schistosoma intercalatum/guineensis, which had been traditionally classified as a single species, is one of the least studied. This work describes epidemiological, clinical and microbiological characteristics of patients with S. intercalatum/guineensis infection.MethodsA retrospective study was performed. The aim was to showcase a clinical overview of this rarely described disease All cases diagnosed of schistosomiasis by S. intercalatum/guineensis at the International Health Unit Vall d’Hebron-Drassanes (Infectious Diseases Department, Vall d’Hebron University Hospital, Barcelona, Spain) from January 2014 to April 2020 were included. The diagnosis was defined by the observation of S. intercalatum/guineensis eggs. Demographic, clinical, and microbiological data were analyzed.ResultsSeventy-four patients were included. Forty-seven (63.5%) of them were women, with a mean age of 24 (SD 12.8) years, and a mean time of residence in Spain of 9 (SD 20.7) months. All of them were migrants from Sub-Saharan Africa, 71 (95.9%) from Equatorial Guinea. Sixty-three (85.1%) patients had concomitant infections at the time of diagnosis and 50% of patients were symptomatic; abdominal pain and diarrhea being the more frequent. Anemia was detected in 25 (33.8%) patients, eosinophilia in 45 (60.8%), and hyperIgE in 48 (64.5%). Overall, 70 (94.6%) patients received treatment with praziquantel at 40 mg/kg/day in different scheme dosage. Post-treatment microscopic examination of the stools was negative in all the cases performed.ConclusionsPatients affected by S. intercalatum/guineensis infection come from a specific area of Central Africa. Co-infection with other parasites is very frequent, and symptoms are present in roughly 50% of patients. No cases of hepatic complications were detected but intestinal symptoms and anemia are frequent in these patients.

Background

Schistosomiasis is one of the most widespread parasitic disease in humans and is the most common parasite transmitted by contact with fresh water. More than 700 million people live in risk areas, including tropical and subtropical areas of African countries [1, 2], specifically in the center and west of the continent [3]. It is estimated that around 240 million people are infected, of whom 50% are symptomatic, and 20 million will suffer severe consequences from the disease [4–6]. It is considered by the World Health Organization as a neglected disease. It is estimated, that by 2018, 229 million people needed treatment, and only 92,2 million actually received it [1, 7].

There are currently nine species of Schistosoma. S. mansoni can be found in Africa and South America; S. intercalatum, S. matthei, S. bovis and S. guineensis in Africa; S. haematobium, in Africa and the Middle East, and finally S. japonicum, S. malayensis and S. mekongi can be found in Asia [1, 6].

The distribution of S. intercalatum has been restricted to certain countries in Central Africa [3, 8, 9]. Recently, on the basis of molecular studies, many strains that had been considered as S. intercalatum, have come to be classified as S. guineensis, morphologically indistinguishable. As a result, the geographic range of each species has recently been defined, namely Democratic Republic of Congo for S. intercalatum and a slightly broader area for S. guineensis in Cameroon, Equatorial Guinea, Gabon, Nigeria and Sao Tomé [10]. There are no epidemiological studies in non-endemic countries on this parasitic disease.

The clinical manifestations of S. intercalatum/guineensis are poorly reported, so there are doubts about its pathogenicity [11]. Similarly to S. mansoni, S. intercalatum/guineensis inhabits the portal and mesenteric venous systems of the host [12]. In contrast, S. intercalatum/guineensis is considered as having mild pathogenicity, causing mild liver damage, but rectal and sigmoid schistosomiasis [3, 13]. The aim of the present study is to describe epidemiological, clinical and microbiological characteristics of individuals with S. intercalatum/guineensis infection.

Methods

This is an observational retrospective study that included all patients with a diagnosis of schistosomiasis produced by S. intercalatum/guineensis and treated at the International Health Unit Vall d’Hebron-Drassanes (Infectious Diseases Department, Vall d’Hebron University Hospital, Barcelona, Spain) from January 2014 to April 2020. The diagnosis was defined by the observation of S. intercalatum/guineensis eggs. Due to the impossibility to perform molecular studies on the diagnosed samples, and therefore to differentiate both species, the denomination S. intercalatum/guineensis is used.

Most of diagnoses are made as part of the imported pathology screening program carried out in the unit. Some patients who were diagnosed with schistosomiasis based on different symptoms and were subsequently referred to the international health service were also included.

The screening strategy is adjusted by country of origin. In the case of Sub-saharan origin includes blood cell count, general biochemistry, hepatitis, syphilis, HIV serology and screening for latent tuberculosis infection and malaria, as well as parasitological examination of feces and urine. During the study period a schistosome serology was performed only in the case of eosinophilia.

The diagnostic technique used was the microscopic examination of feces, through the formalin-ether technique of Ritchie. An image of the egg is shown in Fig. 1. The serological test used for schistosomiasis was an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for IgG against S. mansoni (Novagnost S. mansoni IgG; Siemens Diagnostics, Marburg, Germany). Demographic, clinical, and microbiological data were collected through the electronic medical records.

Some definitions were established. Anemia was considered mild if hemoglobin was between 10 and 12 g/dL, moderate between 8 and 9.9 g/dL and severe less than 8 g/dL. Eosinophilia was considered mild when eosinophils were between 500 and 1500 cells per microliter, moderate between 1501 and 3000 cells per microliter and severe when the number of eosinophils was more than 3000 cells per microliter. In the case of hyper IgE, it was considered mild between 500 and 1000 U/mL, moderate between 1001 and 2500 U/mL and severe when it was greater than 2500 U/mL.

The data were processed in the SPSS 22.0 statistical system. Categorical variables were presented as absolute numbers and proportions, and continuous variables were expressed as means and standard deviation. Procedures were performed in accordance with the ethical standards laid down in the Declaration of Helsinki as revised in 2024. The protocol for this study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the Vall d’Hebron University Hospital (PR-AG-401/2020). A waiver of written informed consent was warranted considering the retrospective nature of the study and the expected large number of records to be screened.

Results

Seventy-four individuals were diagnosed with S. intercalatum/guineensis infection during the study period; 47 (63.5%) were women. The mean age was 24 years (SD 12.8). All of them were migrants from Sub-Saharan Africa: 71 (95.9%) from Equatorial Guinea, 1 (1.4%) patient came from Cameroon, 1 (1.4%) from Mali, and 1 (1.4%) from Senegal. The mean time of residence in our country was 9 months (SD 20.7). Regarding concomitant infections, 63 (85.1%) had at least one co-infection, the most common were trichuriasis in 38 patients (51.4%) and malaria in 13 patients (17.6%). Overall, 70 patients (94.6%) received treatment with praziquantel at 40 mg/kg/day in different scheme dosage. Post-treatment stool microscopy was performed in 44 cases (59.5%), and no eggs were detected in any of them. Additionally, five serological controls were conducted at least six months after treatment, of which 2 (40%) were negative and 3 (60%) positive. Among the 45 patients who initially had eosinophilia, 12 (26.7%) were checked about 6 months after treatment, with resolution of the blood alteration in 8/12 (66%) cases. IgE levels control were performed in 8 (20%) of those who initially presented hyperIgE, with normalization in three cases (37.5%).

Discussion

Few clinical studies have addressed specifically the clinical characteristics of S. intercalatum/guineensis [9, 11, 12, 14, 15], none of them in non-endemic areas. In this study, all patients were from the African continent, the most frequent being Equatorial Guinea, one of the countries with the highest prevalence of schistosomiasis due to S. intercalatum/guineensis [3, 8, 9]. Due to historical and cultural reasons, many people from this country reside in Spain. In relation to comorbidities, other parasitic diseases were very frequent in this group of patients, especially trichuriasis and malaria. All of them are diseases for which screening is routinely performed in our Unit, and very prevalent in their areas of origin [16]. Other pathologies that were part of the screening and that are detected in a significant percentage are latent tuberculosis infection, hepatitis B and HIV infection, two of them were in a severely immunocompromised state.

At diagnosis, 50% of patients were symptomatic. Although in most cases co-infections with other intestinal parasites were present, in 50% of the patients with abdominal pain, proctitis, hematochezia and anemia, schistosomiasis due to S. intercalatum was the only infection detected. Although S. intercalatum/guineensis has traditionally been considered a less pathogenic schistosome species, and does not seem to be associated with significant liver fibrosis, its pathogenicity is not very well known. Some case reports or small studies described manifestations similar to more aggressive intestinal schistosomiasis, such as S. mansoni and S. japonicum, or with genital involvement, usually associated with S. haematobium. Thus, S. intercalatum/guineensis has been associated with severe anemia [17], ulcerated intestinal polyposis [11], nodular colonic lesions [9], or hemoperitoneum due to tubal involvement [14]. It has also been associated with sepsis due to non-typhoidal Salmonella species in children [15]. The limited number of reported cases likely reflects the parasite’s restricted geographic distribution, frequent co-infections masking symptoms, and limited access to colonoscopy or histopathology in endemic areas.

Three patients presented ultrasound alterations, two of them including splenomegaly. Although splenomegaly may be the presentation of hepatosplenic schistosomiasis with periportal fibrosis, no liver involvement was detected in these cases; although at the time of diagnosis the presence of Plasmodium was not observed, in patients from endemic areas the most frequent cause of splenomegaly is tropical splenomegaly due to immunological overstimulation caused by repeated malaria infections [18]. Ultrasonography was performed in only 13% of all cases. Though imaging is recommended for patients diagnosed with schistosomiasis in non-endemic areas, this recommendation is controversial: in a recent series in a non-endemic area, ultrasound abnormalities were found in less than 3%, only in the urinary tract, and resolved after treatment [19]. However, in other series of patients with similar presenting characteristics, the presence of lesions both at the hepatosplenic level and in the urinary tract was approximately 50% [20]. For schistosomiasis caused by S. intercalatum/guineensis, the need of ultrasound is even more uncertain, given the limited association with periportal fibrosis, and because ultrasound is not ideal for detecting intestinal lesions [21]. Colonoscopy is more sensitive for the detection of lesions, but since it is an invasive method, it is reserved for particular cases. Other biomarkers, such as fecal occult blood detection and fecal calprotectin could be non-invasive markers of lesions at this level [22].

A particular case was diagnosed by colonic biopsy; this patient underwent surgery for a stage II-D urachal mucinous adenocarcinoma with extension to the cecum, with removal of the tumor and adjacent tissue. S. intercalatum/guineensis eggs were found in the colonic specimen; both serological and coproparasitological studies were negative. The patient had no immunosuppression or comorbidity, and was treated after surgery. Although possible, it is difficult to establish a causal relationship between the two processes, since there are no data on the relationship between S. intercalatum/guineensis and cancer, unlike other species such as S. haematobium. An isolated case of a woman from Cameroon with rectosigmoid carcinoma and S. intercalatum/guineensis infection was published in the Netherlands in 2008 [23].

Regarding blood abnormalities, hyper IgE and eosinophilia were the most common ones, followed by anemia. There are no specific studies for S. intercalatum, but in other species, hyper IgE is frequent, and does not correlate with disease severity [24, 25]. Eosinophilia varies with parasite maturation, peaking during migration and early egg deposition, and generally declining afterwards [26]. In relation to anemia, there are also no specific studies on S. intercalatum, but it is consistently associated with other species, mainly in relation to iron loss and chronic inflammation [27].

In this study, the diagnosis was made only by direct visualization of eggs, being identified as S. intercalatum. Considering the latest phylogenetic findings, it is possible that they are in fact S. guineensis [10]. Molecular studies must be performed to differentiate the two morphologically indistinguishable species. In addition, until very recently, there were sequences in GenBank incorrectly classified, with consequences in cataloging the epidemiology and even the clinical or immunology of these species [10]. Also, S. guineensis is genetically closer to S. haematobium, and many endemic areas have experienced a change in disease dynamics due to hybridization between the two. For example, in large areas of Cameroon, urogenital schistosomiasis due to S. haematobium / S. guineensis hybrids is now predominant over intestinal schistosomiasis due to S. guineensis [28].

While Schistosoma serology is nowadays included in routine screening, this was not the case during the study period. Thus, serology was performed in 13 patients, and 85% of them were positive. The serology used was specific por S. mansoni and yields positive results in the case of other species through cross-reactivity [29]. The one patient diagnosed with S. haematobium was through visualization of eggs in urine. No evidence of other Schistosoma species was found in the remaining patients, so positivity was attributed to S. intercalatum.

Regarding treatment, 5% of the patients did not receive treatment due to loss to follow-up, a frequent issue in newly arrived individuals with unstable socioeconomic conditions, also contributing to the low number of ultrasounds performed. To note, several different treatment schemes were performed in these patients. WHO and CDC recommend 40 mg/day praziquantel [1, 30], in one or two doses, while other treatment guidelines for imported schistosomiasis suggest the option of giving a second course of treatment two to four weeks later [31]. However, none of them is specific for S. intercalatum. In our study, 53% of patients received a regimen with a second dose of 40 mg/kg the day after the first dose, either in one or two doses. Five patients (6.8%) received a second dose after three days and 4 (5.4%) a second dose after 40 days.

A post-treatment coproparasitological control study was performed in 60% of the cases, with 100% showing egg clearance. In addition, five serological controls were performed at least 6 months post-treatment, of which 40% were negative and 60% positive. Schistosoma serology is not a marker indicating cure, as seroconversion may be present years later post treatment [32]. Of the 12 patients who initially had eosinophilia and underwent post-treatment control, 33% continued presenting eosinophilia. Among patients who initially had elevated IgE levels, it returned to normal values in 40% of cases during follow-up. For these particular cases, it would be advisable to continue the study, since it is possible the presence of other helminths, such as Strongyloides stercoralis or a filarial nematode [26].

The main limitations of the study derive from its retrospective nature and the small sample size. In addition, the absence of a molecular study does not allow us to reliably determine the species S. intercalatum or S. guineensis. This is a great challenge in non-endemic setting, and is a cause of underdiagnosis in migrants and travelers. Furthermore, there were notable gaps in follow-up. Some potential co-infections may also remain undetected. Its main strength derives from the possibility of gaining insight into the clinical characteristics of patients specifically infected with this species, which allows to increase the overall knowledge about it.

Conclusions

In summary, patients affected by S. intercalatum/guineensis infection come from a specific area of Central Africa and co-infection with other parasites is very frequent. No cases of hepatic complications were detected but intestinal symptoms and anemia are frequent in these patients. Treatment with praziquantel at the usual doses was highly effective. Molecular studies should be performed to differentiate the species S. intercalatum and S. guineensis.
