Association between Neutrophil-to-Lymphocyte ratio and left ventricular hypertrophy in chronic kidney disease patients: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Li Wang, Wen Zhang, Jun Ji, Fangfang Xiang, Lin Zhang, Xiaotian Jiang, Yi Fang, Xiaoqiang Ding, Wuhua Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLeft ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) is a common cardiovascular complication in chronic kidney disease (CKD), associated with increased morbidity and mortality. Systemic inflammation may contribute to LVH, but evidence remains limited in non-dialysis CKD populations. The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), a readily accessible inflammatory marker, may serve as a predictor of LVH.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, 514 hospitalized CKD patients were enrolled. LVH was defined by echocardiographic criteria based on left ventricular mass index. NLR was calculated from complete blood counts. Logistic regression models were used to evaluate the association between NLR and LVH after adjusting for potential confounders. Subgroup and restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses were conducted to assess consistency and non-linear relationships. Mediation analysis was performed to explore whether NLR mediated the relationship between CKD and LVH.ResultsPatients with LVH had significantly higher NLR levels than those without (3.14 vs. 2.20, p < 0.001). After full adjustment, standardized NLR remained independently associated with LVH (adjusted OR: 1.34, 95% CI: 1.05–1.72, p = 0.02). RCS showed a linear relationship between NLR and LVH risk (p for nonlinear = 0.229). Subgroup analyses confirmed this robust association across subgroups (p for interaction ≥ 0.05). Mediation analysis indicated that NLR partially mediated the relationship between CKD and LVH, accounting for 5.1% of the total effect.ConclusionElevated NLR was independently associated with LVH and may reflect underlying inflammatory processes involved in cardiac remodeling among patients with CKD. Given its simplicity and accessibility, NLR may serve as a potential marker for identifying individuals at increased cardiovascular risk, although further validation in prospective studies is warranted.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-025-05276-5.

Background

Left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) is highly prevalent among patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) and is a strong predictor of adverse cardiovascular outcomes, including heart failure and mortality [1, 2]. In CKD, volume overload [3], hypertension [4], and uremia [5] are major contributors to LVH, which affects more than half of CKD patients [4, 6]. LVH is associated with both systolic and diastolic dysfunction [7, 8], underscoring its importance in CKD-related cardiovascular complications [9].

CKD is marked by persistent low-grade inflammation [10], which accelerates renal and cardiovascular disease progression [11, 12]. This inflammatory state is driven by oxidative stress [13], gut dysbiosis [14], and uremic toxins [15], leading to elevated markers such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) [16], which have been linked to poor renal and cardiovascular outcomes [11, 17].

Inflammation contributes directly to cardiac remodeling in CKD. Elevated IL-6, C-Reactive Protein (CRP), and TNF-α are associated with increased left ventricular mass [18] and fibrosis [19–21]. Endothelial dysfunction further worsens vascular and cardiac outcomes [22].

The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) is a simple, accessible inflammatory marker used in CKD to assess prognosis [23, 24]. Neutrophils release cytokines and matrix-degrading enzymes [25, 26], while lymphopenia reflects impaired immune resolution—together indicating a pro-fibrotic state. NLR captures this imbalance, and elevated levels have been linked to LVH [27, 28]. However, its role in CKD-related LVH remains inadequately explored.

This study aims to examine the association between NLR and LVH in CKD patients. Specifically, we seek to: (1) determine whether higher NLR is associated with increased LVH prevalence, and (2) explore the potential mediating role of NLR in the CKD–LVH pathway.

Methods

Design and study sopulation

This study was a cross-sectional analysis conducted to explore the associations between NLR and left ventricular hypertrophy in patients with CKD. CKD was defined and staged according to the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines [29], which require evidence of kidney damage (e.g., proteinuria or hematuria) or decreased kidney function (eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m²) persisting for at least 3 months, as documented in outpatient or prior hospitalization records. The study population comprised patients admitted to the Division of Nephrology between July 2021 and December 2021. Ethical approval was obtained from the Zhongshan Hospital Ethics Committee (B2021-740), and all eligible participants provided written informed consent.

Exclusion criteria

(1) Patients receiving renal replacement therapy. (2) Recent use of antibiotics, lipid metabolism-altering drugs, or glucocorticoids/immunosuppressants within the last three months. (3) History of New York Heart Association class III/IV heart failure. (4) Acute infection or diagnosis of liver cirrhosis. (5) Severely elevated serum alanine aminotransferase or aspartate aminotransferase (exceeding 1.5 times the upper limit of normal). (6) Diagnosis of malignant tumors or HIV infection. (7) Recent major surgery or trauma in the past three months. (8) Severe anemia with hemoglobin levels below 60 g/dL. (9) Severe electrolyte imbalance. (10) Active autoimmune diseases (e.g., systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis) or chronic inflammatory conditions (e.g., Crohn’s disease, ulcerative colitis). (11) Recent substance abuse (drugs or alcohol) or recent vaccination within the past month. (12) Severe obesity with a body mass index (BMI) > 40 kg/m². (13) Pregnancy. (14) Absence of cardiac ultrasound results.

Definitions

The NLR was selected as the primary marker of systemic inflammation for this study due to its simplicity, cost-effectiveness, and widespread availability in routine clinical practice. NLR is derived from standard complete blood counts, which was obtained on the morning of the second day of hospitalization to ensure clinical stability, making it easily accessible in clinical settings. NLR were calculated using blood sample data, calculated as neutrophil count (109/L) divided by lymphocyte count (109/L). Blood specimens were collected in the morning after at least nine hours of overnight fasting. Full blood counts were obtained from BD EDTA-K2 samples and analyzed using a Sysmex XN9000 electronic counter.

BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m²).

Estimated Glomerular Filtration Rate (eGFR) was calculated using the CKD-EPI equation [30].

LVH was assessed using transthoracic echocardiography performed by experienced cardiologists following standard protocols [31]. Left ventricular mass (LVM) was calculated using the formula: LVM (g) = 0.8 × (1.04 × [(interventricular septal thickness + left ventricular internal diameter at end-diastole + posterior wall thickness)³ - (left ventricular internal diameter at end-diastole)³]) + 0.6. LVM was then indexed to body surface area to obtain the left ventricular mass index (LVMI). LVH was defined as an LVMI > 115 g/m² for men and > 95 g/m² for women, based on established guidelines [32].

The classification of glomerular disease as primary or secondary was based on kidney biopsy findings where available. For patients who could not undergo biopsy due to contraindications (e.g., small kidney size, solitary kidney) or personal refusal, diagnoses were inferred based on clinical history, serological tests, and genetic results when applicable.

Covariates

The selection of covariates was guided by their established associations with both systemic inflammation (as reflected by NLR) and LVH. Age, gender, BMI, smoking and CKD stages are recognized predictors of both cardiovascular outcomes and inflammatory status in CKD patients. Hypertension and diabetes mellitus, which are strongly associated with both LVH and inflammation, were also adjusted for to minimize confounding bias. Additionally, CRP was included as proxy for inflammatory, which could influence the inflammatory milieu and cardiovascular remodeling in CKD.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics

Data following a normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation, while those not normally distributed were shown as medians with interquartile ranges. Categorical data were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Differences between groups (LVH vs. no LVH) were assessed using the Student’s t-test or the Wilcoxon rank sum test for continuous variables and the Pearson’s chi-squared test for categorical variables.

Multivariable logistic regression and non-linear analysis

Due to the non-normal distribution of NLR, standardization was applied. Multivariable logistic regression model was used to examine the associations between standardized NLR and the presence of LVH. The model adjusted for gender, age, BMI, smoking, CKD stages, CRP, diabetes mellitus and hypertension. The results were expressed as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses were used to assess potential non-linear relationships between NLR and LVH.

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses were conducted to investigate the consistency of the associations across various strata, including age, gender, BMI, eGFR, smoking status, drinking status, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, hemoglobin and serum albumin.

Mediation effect analysis

The causal mediation analysis was conducted to identify the potential mediating role for NLR between CKD stages and LVH, using the ‘mediation’ package in R 4.2.2. Bootstrap method with 500 simulations was employed to estimate the total, indirect, and direct effects, as well as the proportion mediated.

Statistical software

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.2). Descriptive statistics and logistic regression analyses were conducted using the base and stats packages in R. RCS analyses were conducted using the rms package. A two-sided P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline clinical characteristics

Figure 1 illustrated the flow of patient enrollment. A total of 635 patients were initially screened, out of which 121 patients were excluded due to not meeting inclusion criteria. Finally, 514 CKD patients were included in the analysis. Table 1 presented the baseline clinical characteristics of the study population stratified by the presence of LVH. A significantly higher proportion of male patients had LVH (75.38% vs. 59.24%, p = 0.013). Patients with LVH also exhibited higher rates of hypertension (93.85% vs. 65.03%, p < 0.001), and valve disease (47.69% vs. 13.81%, p < 0.001), whereas there was no significant difference in the prevalence of diabetes mellitus (p = 0.526). Among the CKD stages, patients with LVH had a significantly higher proportion of advanced CKD. Specifically, 58.46% of patients with LVH were in CKD stage 5, compared to only 18.71% in the non-LVH group. In contrast, CKD stage 1 patients were only observed in the non-LVH group (27.62% vs. 0.00%). In terms of underlying kidney disease etiology, primary glomerular disease was more frequent in the non-LVH group (50.11%) than in the LVH group (16.92%). Notably, patients with LVH had lower hemoglobin levels (89 g/L vs. 122 g/L, p < 0.001) and lower lymphocyte counts (1.30 × 10^9/L vs. 1.70 × 10^9/L, p < 0.001). The NLR was significantly higher in patients with LVH compared to those without (3.14 vs. 2.20, p < 0.001) (shown in additional file, Supplementary Fig. 1). Additionally, patients with LVH exhibited worse renal function, as indicated by higher serum creatinine (474 µmol/L vs. 114 µmol/L, p < 0.001) and lower eGFR (10 mL/min/1.73 m² vs. 60 mL/min/1.73 m², p < 0.001).

Median N-terminal pro-B-type Natriuretic Peptide (NT-proBNP) levels were substantially higher in patients with LVH (1887.00 [511.18, 8285.25] pg/mL) than in those without (133 [41, 480] pg/mL, P < 0.001), and CRP levels were likewise elevated (3.70 [1.00, 7.70] mg/L vs. 1.3 [0.60, 2.90] mg/L, P < 0.001). Association between NLR and left ventricular hypertrophy

The logistic regression analysis revealed that standardized NLR is independently associated with LVH (Table 2). In the unadjusted model, the odds ratio (OR) for LVH per unit increase in standardized NLR was 1.42 (95% CI: 1.16–1.74, p < 0.001). After multivariable logistic regression analysis adjusting for confounding factors such as gender, age, BMI, smoking, CKD stages, diabetes mellitus, hypertension and CRP, NLR remained independently associated with LVH (adjusted OR: 1.34, 95% CI: 1.05–1.72, p = 0.02).

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore the consistency of the association between NLR and LVH across different patient characteristics (Table 3). The results showed that the association was stronger in patients younger than 60 years (OR: 1.59, 95% CI: 1.24–2.05, p < 0.001) and in females (OR: 1.58, 95% CI: 1.06–2.37, p = 0.026). Among patients with a BMI between 25 and 30 kg/m², the OR for LVH was 2.31 (95% CI: 1.37–3.89, p = 0.002), indicating a particularly strong association in this group. The association was also significant in patients with lower eGFR (< 60 mL/min/1.73 m²) (OR: 1.28, 95% CI: 1.02–1.59, p = 0.03) and in those without a history of diabetes mellitus (OR: 1.46, 95% CI: 1.16–1.84, p = 0.001). No significant interaction was found across most of the subgroups, indicating the robustness of the observed association.

Mediation effect analysis

The total effect of CKD on LVH was statistically significant, with a coefficient of 0.01010 (95% CI 0.00431, 0.01719) and a p-value of < 0.001. The indirect effect through NLR was also significant, with a coefficient of 0.00052 (95% CI 0.00002, 0.00150) and a p-value of 0.040. The direct effect of CKD on LVH remained significant after adjusting for NLR, with a coefficient of 0.00958 (95% CI 0.00417, 0.01615) and a p-value of < 0.001. The proportion of the effect of CKD on LVH that was mediated by NLR was 5.1% (95% CI 0.2, 11.5) (Table 4).

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study, we found that elevated NLR was independently associated with LVH in CKD patients, even after adjusting for key confounders including age, sex, BMI, and CKD stage. This relationship remained consistent across subgroups, and mediation analysis suggested that systemic inflammation, reflected by NLR, partly mediates the association between CKD and LVH.

The clinical implications are notable. Given that LVH is a strong predictor of cardiovascular events in CKD [33], early identification is essential. NLR, an inexpensive and widely available marker, may aid in risk stratification and early intervention in this high-risk population.

Our findings align with previous literature. Lano et al. [34] showed that elevated NLR predicted cardiovascular events and mortality in hemodialysis patients. Ao et al. [35] demonstrated in a meta-analysis of 116,709 CKD patients included both dialysis and non-dialysis populations, covering CKD stages 1–5D, that high NLR was associated with higher all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. Solak et al. [23] also found that NLR predicted CV events in CKD stage 3–5 patients, using a cutoff of 3.76 with good sensitivity and specificity.

While some prior studies have linked NLR to LVH, most were limited to hypertensive populations [28], reducing generalizability to CKD. Our study, focused on a CKD cohort, builds on prior work by specifically examining the inflammatory link to LVH in this population.

Nitta et al. [36] studied over 1,000 CKD patients and identified traditional risk factors like blood pressure and BMI as predictors of LVH, but did not assess NLR. Our study extends this by showing that NLR independently contributes to LVH risk, highlighting the role of systemic inflammation.

Importantly, our subgroup and mediation analyses provide additional insight. The modest mediation effect suggests inflammation is one of several contributors to LVH, alongside hypertension, fluid overload, and uremia [3–5].

Mechanistically, neutrophils release cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6, which are linked to cardiac hypertrophy and fibrosis [7, 16, 25, 26]. Lymphopenia, reflected in high NLR, may indicate suppressed immunity, perpetuating inflammation. This imbalance may contribute to maladaptive remodeling. Inflammation also worsens endothelial dysfunction [37], promoting vascular stiffness and increased afterload [38], further driving LVH.

As shown in Table 1, most LVH patients were in CKD stages 4–5, where renal biopsy is often not feasible. Consequently, many had unknown CKD etiology, underscoring the need for earlier detection and public education to enable diagnosis at earlier stages.

Despite the study’s strengths—such as robust adjustment and mediation analysis—several limitations exist. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Details on comorbidities, medication use (e.g., vitamin D), and CKD onset were incomplete, which may introduce residual confounding. This study was based on a relatively small sample size, which limited our ability to adjust for all potential confounding factors for LVH, such as proteinuria. The single-center design and modest sample size also limit generalizability. Lastly, while NLR is a useful inflammatory marker, direct comparisons with cytokines like TNF-α and IL-6 would have added depth.

In summary, our findings suggest that systemic inflammation, reflected by NLR, is independently associated with LVH in CKD patients. Future large-scale, longitudinal studies are needed to confirm this relationship and better elucidate the inflammatory mechanisms involved.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study identified a significant and independent association between elevated NLR and the presence of LVH in hospitalized patients with CKD, suggesting a potential role of systemic inflammation in adverse cardiac remodeling within this population. While NLR may serve as a convenient and accessible marker for identifying patients at higher cardiovascular risk, further prospective studies are needed to confirm its predictive utility. Additionally, mechanistic investigations are warranted to elucidate the inflammatory pathways underlying this association and to explore whether modulating inflammation could influence LVH progression in CKD.
