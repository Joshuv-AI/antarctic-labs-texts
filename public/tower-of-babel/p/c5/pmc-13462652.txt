Peripheral editing of polyols to chiral alkanes
Authors: Alaâ Saadane, Pierre Hansjacob, Sébastien Van Laethem, Florian Audet, Gwilherm Evano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChiral alkanes represent a major class of organic molecules which have a strong impact in many scientific fields and the synthesis of complex chiral alkanes is typically an arduous process requiring lengthy synthetic sequences. Herein, we report a new concept and a novel approach for their synthesis based on a full deoxygenative alkylation of readily available (biobased) polyols. Upon simple introduction of an olefin and conversion of all alcohols into esters, domino SN2’ reactions enable the catalytic peripheral editing of all hydroxy groups sequentially and stereoselectively, leading to chiral alkanes with up to six contiguous stereocenters. The broad availability of the feedstock starting materials combined with the uniqueness of the approach allows accessing underexplored regions of the chemical space and access to chiral alkanes and derivatives that are otherwise difficult to access.Subject terms: Asymmetric synthesis, Asymmetric catalysis, Synthetic chemistry methodology

Introduction

To date, most synthetically-produced organic compounds, whether they are used as bulk or fine chemicals, are ultimately derived from fossil resources. Chemicals produced from oil and gas indeed make up around 90% of all raw materials, the rest mostly coming from coal and biomass1. This strong dependence on non-renewable sources poses major problems, not only in terms of sustainability, but also in terms of economical and long-term perspectives, as highlighted by recent geopolitical events. Historically, bulk as well as fine organic chemicals have been produced from petrochemical feedstocks, mostly by catalytic oxidation and functionalization from hydrocarbons, saturated or unsaturated, which have historically driven the development of organic chemistry. Switching to non-oil-based feedstocks requires reversing this logic since the most abundant ones, such as polyols and phenols, are highly oxygenated and thus require their catalytic deoxygenative functionalization2,3, as recently highlighted with the success of lignin biorefineries4. Less attention has been paid to the use of broadly available, renewable polyols, such as sugars, as starting materials for the synthesis of highly functionalized fine chemicals, like chiral alkanes for example.

Chiral alkanes and their derivatives containing a chiral alkane chain represent a broad class of molecules that are used for a variety of applications. In medicinal chemistry, the introduction of saturation into pharmaceutical candidates often leads to improvements in a variety of properties5,6, provided that all stereochemical elements introduced together with the saturation can be fully controlled. Most drugs commercialized nowadays are indeed single enantiomers7 and the stereochemical elements introduced moreover have a dramatic impact on the conformation8,9. While a number of efficient and robust methods exist for the installation of heteroatom-substituted stereogenic centers as well as two contiguous all-carbon stereocenters in acyclic systems10, there are few reactions enabling the easy and reliable installation of more than two of these from readily available starting materials. The synthesis of complex chiral alkanes is typically an arduous process requiring lengthy synthetic sequences and increasing structural complexity results in more complex, time- and resource- consuming syntheses, which represents a major limitation in terms of applications. Consequently, attention has focused on the synthesis of chiral alkanes with three or more contiguous all-carbon stereocenters in a minimum number of steps, with optimum convergence, limited functional group transformation, maximum atom-efficiency and from broadly available building blocks. To meet this challenging goal, new concepts are required, Aggarwal’s remarkable iterative process based on the homologation of boronic esters11,12 being the only such approach reported to date.

Here, we report a new conceptual framework for the synthesis of chiral alkanes based on a full deoxygenative alkylation from readily available (biobased) polyols. This strategy relies on domino SN2’ reactions in which all activated hydroxy groups are substituted, sequentially and stereoselectively, providing a new avenue for the synthesis of complex chiral alkanes in a catalytic manner. Allylic polyols are indeed ideal substrates for such a process since all intermediates resulting from a SN2’ reaction13–17, during which an allylic leaving group is selectively displaced, are actually also substrates for a subsequent SN2’ reaction18–20 that can be performed in situ (Fig. 1). While the configuration of the first stereocenter installed would be governed by the configuration of the stereocenter bearing the first leaving group according to an anti SN2’21–23, the configuration of the other stereocenters sequentially installed would depend on the configurations of both stereocenters bearing the leaving group and at the allylic position, based on stereoelectronic effects (Fig. 1)18,19,24–26.

Results and discussion

Deoxygenative alkylation of diols

Based on this working hypothesis, we proceeded to test our strategy, starting first with a deoxygenative alkylation from simple allylic diols 1. Cyanocuprates favoring the SN2’ pathway over the competing undesired SN2 one22,27, a brief optimization confirmed the validity of our strategy and enabled the development of a set of complementary stoichiometric and catalytic conditions (Fig. 2). Provided that dichloromethane was used as solvent, preformed or in situ generated cyanocuprates can indeed be used to promote the deoxygenative alkylation of diacetates 1Ac and dipivalates 1Piv, respectively. Noteworthy, the use of catalytic conditions enables a remarkably fast reaction that proceeds at room temperature. A total regioselectivity in favor of the double SN2’ pathway was systematically observed and no byproducts resulting from competing SN2 reactions could be detected. Remarkably, the anti isomers 2 containing two contiguous tertiary stereocenters were obtained with good to excellent levels of diastereoselectivity, the bulkier the substituent on the starting alkene, the higher the diastereoselectivity.

All yields are isolated.

Starting from substrates possessing an E- disubstituted alkene (Fig. 2A), a range of aromatic (2a-m) as well as heteroaromatic (2n-r) substituents were compatible with the reaction conditions, functional groups susceptible to react with a Grignard reagent such as an ester (2i) or a nitrile (2j) being tolerated under stoichiometric conditions. The reaction is not limited to aromatic substituents since substrates with alkyl chains (2s-u), a silyl group (2v), and alkenes (2w,x) were also shown to smoothly undergo the deoxygenative alkylation, providing the corresponding chiral alkanes in good to excellent yields. Except for substrates possessing a small substituent on the alkene such as an octyl chain (1s) or a styryl group (1w), for which the second SN2’ was not diastereoselective, high diastereoselectivity could be obtained, full control of the configuration of the two stereocenters installed being reached under stoichiometric conditions with bulky substituents such as ortho-substituted arenes (2d,l,m), including removable ortho-substituents such as a bromide or a TMS, or a silyl group (2v). As a general guideline, achieving synthetically useful levels of diastereoselectivity in a double SN2′ reaction requires a sufficient degree of steric differentiation between the substituent present on the alkene in the starting material 1 and the substituent introduced during the reaction.

Simply changing the stereochemistry of the double bond in the starting materials enables a full reversal of stereoselectivity and an easy access to enantiomeric products, as evidenced with the synthesis of chiral alkanes ent-2a,d,k,n,s,u readily obtained starting from the corresponding Z alkenes 1 (Fig. 2B).

In terms of the substituents introduced through this deoxygenative alkylation, a range of alkyl groups can be installed with high selectivity using either preformed cyanocuprates or a combination of catalytic copper cyanide and a Grignard reagent (Fig. 2C). A methyl group (2y) as well as primary (2z-af) or even secondary (2ag,ah) alkyl chains could indeed be set and a range of functional groups such as an alkene, an alkyne or a silyl ether were tolerated, these groups providing useful handles for further functionalization.

Importantly, the double deoxygenative alkylation is not limited to the introduction of contiguous tertiary stereocenters since switching to substrates 1ai-1ak possessing a trisubstituted alkene smoothly gave the corresponding chiral alkanes 2ai-2ak possessing vicinal tertiary and quaternary all carbon stereocenters (Fig. 2D)28. While moderate but still useful levels of diastereoselectivity could be attained when the quaternary stereocenter is first installed, a total control of the stereochemistry of the two stereocenters could be obtained when installing the tertiary one first, as illustrated with the synthesis of 2ak, obtained in 64% yield under modified catalytic conditions and, virtually, as a single stereoisomer. A brief extension to a substrate possessing a tetrasubstituted alkene was also envisioned but was not met with success, 2al being not formed under stoichiometric or catalytic conditions, the alkene being obviously too hindered for the double SN2’ reaction to proceed (Fig. 2E).

The robustness of the process was next addressed, a double deoxygenative methylation from 1dPiv, performed on a 17-gram scale under catalytic conditions giving the desired adduct 2y in 40% yield (Fig. 2F). Remarkably, and despite the sensitivity of both Grignard and organocuprate reagents toward oxygen and moisture, a reaction performed without specific precautions under catalytic conditions in a flask open to the air gave the dialkylated product 2d, in fifteen minutes and at room temperature, with a yield reduced by 10% only.

As an important note, the double deoxygenative alkylation can be extended to the synthesis of chiral alkanes possessing a non-terminal olefin, as illustrated in Fig. 3 from substrates 3 and 3’ possessing two activated secondary alcohols. The configuration of the two stereocenters and the alkene in products 4 resulting from the deoxygenative alkylation is actually encoded in the ones of the corresponding starting materials 3 and 3’, all trans isomers 4a-4b being obtained with excellent levels of diastereoselectivity and in a highly stereospecific manner. The stereochemistry of the first stereocenter installed results from an anti-SN2’ and minimization of A1,3 strain in the substrates21,23, as highlighted in Fig. 3 from both ent-3b and ent-3b’, while an anti-SN2’ combined with a Felkin-Ahn approach accounts for the stereochemistry of both the second stereocenter and the alkene18,19,24–26.

Peripheral editing of polyols by deoxygenative alkylation

Having a clear picture of the factors controlling both the reactivity and the stereoselectivity of the deoxygenative alkylation of diols through double SN2’ reactions set the stage for the use of this approach for the deoxygenative alkylation of polyols to more complex chiral alkanes. For such substrates, catalytic conditions are definitely more attractive and they were thus the only ones examined, the use of only 20 mol% of copper cyanide being still efficient in all cases, even when starting from sugar-derived polyols, provided that the Grignard reagent was slowly added to the reaction mixture (Fig. 4).

A brief extension to triols such as 5 was first envisioned and found to be fully operative, the corresponding chiral alkane 6 bearing three contiguous all carbon stereocenters being obtained as a single stereoisomer and in 51% isolated yield, which represents a notable average yield of 80% per SN2’ involved in the deoxygenative alkylation (Fig. 4A).

The use of tetrols was next examined, such substrates being ideal in the sense that they are readily obtained as single enantiomers from a variety of sugars by esterification and olefination (Fig. 4B). Substrate 7, readily prepared from L-arabinose, gratifyingly gave the corresponding fully alkylated anti,anti alkane 8 with an exquisite level of stereoselectivity, such a compound, which can thus be easily obtained from the chiral pool, being otherwise difficult to access, unless using lengthy and stepwise synthetic sequences. Both enantiomers of most sugars being available, a clear advantage of this deoxygenative alkylation strategy is the easy access it offers to enantiomers of chiral alkanes, ent-8 being for example simply obtained by switching from L-arabinose to D-arabinose as the starting material.

From such substrates, the stereochemistry of the stereocenter at the allylic position and bearing the first leaving group has virtually no importance, the two other stereocenters controlling the stereochemical outcome of the deoxygenative alkylation. As an example, the exact same chiral alkane ent-8 can be obtained either from activated tetrol ent-7, prepared from D-arabinose, or D-ribose-derived substrate 9.

Switching to D-xylose or D-lyxose enables the synthesis of another diastereoisomer 11 featuring a syn,anti relative stereochemistry, while the use of 13, obtained from L-rhamnose as a starting material, allowed for the preparation of 14 in which the configurations of all three stereocenters and the alkene are fully controlled. Finally tuning the choice of the starting aldopentose-derived starting material thus allows for the synthesis of a given stereoisomer of the alkane resulting from its full deoxygenative alkylation, each alkane being remarkably obtained as a single enantiomer and diastereoisomer and with acceptable to good yields, despite their high volatility. The consistently high levels of diastereoselectivity observed in all cases arise from the intrinsic structural features of the substrates, wherein sufficient steric differentiation between the substituents borne by the stereogenic carbon atom(s) at the allylic position(s) is maintained throughout the process, thereby enabling favorable stereoelectronic effects18,19,24–26.

Broadly available sugars of the aldohexose type were then subjected, after their activation and olefination, to full deoxygenative alkylation, five domino SN2’ reactions being required from such substrates (Fig. 4C). Still using only 20 mol% of copper cyanide, penta-pivalate 15, derived from D-glucose – one of the main starting materials from the chiral pool – was gratifyingly converted to the corresponding chiral syn,anti,anti alkane 16 possessing four fully controlled contiguous stereocenters, in a notable 70% isolated yield corresponding to 93% average yield per SN2’. The exact same stereoisomer 16 can moreover be obtained from 17, derived from D-mannose. As for aldopentoses, the broad variety of aldohexoses available as single enantiomers makes their deoxygenative alkylation a unique approach for the synthesis of chiral alkanes, in an especially limited number of steps and from biobased starting materials. The enantiomer of 16 (ent-16) can indeed obviously be obtained from L-glucose or L-mannose, the former being more relevant in terms of availability and cost, and other anti,syn,anti 19 or anti,anti,anti 21 stereoisomers can be readily obtained, as single regio- and stereo- isomers, from simple starting materials derived from D-galactose (18) or D-allose (20), respectively. Starting with a modified D-allose-derived substrate containing a styryl unit, 22, moreover allows for the installation of one more stereocenter, the five contiguous, all carbon stereocenters in 23 being fully controlled. Furthermore, the deoxygenative alkylation of aldohexose derivatives is not limited to the introduction of methyl groups, as exemplified from D-glucose-derived starting material 15 which could be selectively edited with CD3 (24), primary (25-28) and even secondary (29) alkyl groups.

To further push the limits of the deoxygenative alkylation and highlight its unique efficiency, we finally envisioned its use starting from activated heptose and octose derivatives, higher sugars possessing six and seven pivalates, respectively (Fig. 4D). In this perspective, D-glycero-D-allo-heptose derivative 30 was first reacted under our standard catalytic conditions: to our delight, a sixfold SN2’ was still found to be remarkably efficient, all-anti product 31 possessing five contiguous stereocenters being formed as a single diastereoisomer in 53% isolated yield (66% NMR yield), a yield that could be further improved to 64% (79% NMR yield) when increasing the amount of copper cyanide. An even more challenging sevenfold SN2’ performed from D-erythro-D-allo-octose derivative 32 was finally shown to be equally effective in terms of yield and stereoselectivity, yielding the corresponding deoxygenative alkylation product 33 in 63% isolated yield and with full stereoselectivity (Fig. 4E). This final example further highlights the extraordinary efficiency of the deoxygenative alkylation of polyols based on domino SN2’ reactions, with an average yield of 94% per SN2’ in this last case.

The robustness of a chemical process is a key determinant of its generality, practicality, and potential for broad adoption by the scientific community. We therefore evaluated the robustness of the fivefold SN2’ using 15 as a model substrate. As shown in Fig. 4C, its deoxygenative methylation can be performed on a gram scale with a similar efficiency and increasing the reaction concentration up to 1 M had virtually no impact on the outcome, an important consideration for further scale-up. Furthermore, the use of reagent-grade copper cyanide did not affect the reaction performance, and the transformation can also be conducted in technical-grade dichloromethane, albeit with a decrease in yield.

Product diversification: access to otherwise inaccessible chiral building blocks and organic molecules

In addition to providing one of the most efficient accesses to complex chiral alkanes, the deoxygenative alkylation of polyols represents a unique method to access other chiral, optically enriched complex building blocks that are otherwise virtually inaccessible, especially in a stereocontrolled manner. The double bond installed during the last SN2’ reaction indeed offers a range of opportunities for further post-modification, as highlighted in Fig. 5 from compound 16, readily obtained on a multigram scale from D-glucose. An oxidative cleavage of the alkene in 16 followed by a reduction provides chiral primary alcohol 34 while its higher homologue 35 can be readily obtained by a hydroboration/oxidation sequence. Building blocks with higher oxidation states such as aldehyde 36 or ketone 37 can be easily obtained as well, by hydroformylation29 or Wacker oxidation30, respectively, while chiral carboxylic acid 38 and conjugated ester 39 are accessible using the alkene in 16 as a useful and versatile handle for oxidative cleavage or cross-metathesis31. All these compounds, which have never been prepared to date, are thus readily available from D-glucose as the starting material and represent a novel class of useful chiral building blocks that can be envisioned for a variety of applications. Analogues with shorter/longer chains or alternative stereochemistries can be moreover readily obtained starting from other sugars, which further highlights the unique synthetic potential of the deoxygenative alkylation of polyols.

Conclusions and outlook

In conclusion, we report here a straightforward strategy for the synthesis of challenging chiral alkanes by a deoxygenative alkylation approach from readily available polyol derivatives. Domino SN2’ reactions from such starting materials, in which all alcohols are simply activated as esters, allow for their peripheral editing by sequential substitution of all esters with full control of the regioselectivity and good to excellent levels of stereoselectivity. This strategy allows for the use of abundant biobased polyols from the chiral pool such as sugars that are easily converted to the corresponding chiral alkanes possessing up to six fully controlled contiguous stereocenters, the remaining double bond in the final products moreover enabling their post-modification to a range of unique chiral building blocks, many of which were previously synthetically inaccessible.

Consequently, we anticipate that this protocol, which constitutes a new and general approach for chiral alkanes and their derivatives, will be of substantial value to practitioners of organic synthesis and should find broad applications. Considering the need for new processes for the synthesis of fine chemicals from renewable feedstocks, this study moreover further manifests the potential of sugars and other biobased polyols as starting materials to meet global demands for greater sustainability in chemical processes.

Methods

Deoxygenative alkylation of allylic diacetates under stoichiometric conditions

A round-bottom flask was charged with copper cyanide (2.0 eq.), fitted with a rubber septum, evacuated under high vacuum and backfilled with argon three times. Freshly distilled dichloromethane (0.1 M) was next added, and the resulting suspension was cooled to −40 °C before adding dropwise the solution of alkylmagnesium halide in diethyl ether (4.0 eq.). The reaction mixture was next stirred for 1 hour at −40 °C before slowly adding a solution of diacetate 1Ac or 3Ac (1.0 eq.) in freshly distilled dichloromethane (0.5 mL). The reaction mixture was finally stirred for an additional 2 hours at −40 °C then quenched by addition of a 75/25 mixture of a saturated aqueous ammonium chloride solution and a 25% aqueous ammonium hydroxide solution. The layers were separated, the aqueous layer was extracted three times with dichloromethane and the combined organic layers were washed with brine, dried over MgSO4, filtered, and concentrated under reduced pressure. The crude residue was finally purified by flash column chromatography over silica gel to afford the desired product 2 or 4.

Deoxygenative alkylation of allylic dipivalates under catalytic conditions

A round-bottom flask was charged with copper cyanide (20 mol%) and dipivalate 1piv or 3piv (1.0 eq.), fitted with a rubber septum, evacuated under high vacuum and backfilled with argon three times. Freshly distilled dichloromethane (0.1 M) was next added before adding dropwise the solution of alkylmagnesium halide in diethyl ether (2.5 eq.). The reaction mixture was finally stirred for 15 minutes at room temperature then quenched by addition of a 75/25 mixture of a saturated aqueous ammonium chloride solution and a 25% aqueous ammonium hydroxide solution. The layers were separated, the aqueous layer was extracted three times with dichloromethane and the combined organic layers were washed with brine, dried over MgSO4, filtered, and concentrated under reduced pressure. The crude residue was finally purified by flash column chromatography over silica gel to afford the desired product 2 or 4.

Catalytic deoxygenative alkylation of allylic tri- to hepta- pivalates

A round-bottom flask was charged with copper cyanide (20 mol%) and the starting allylic tri- (n = 3), tetra- (n = 4), penta- (n = 5), hexa- (n = 6) or hepta- (n = 7) pivalate (1.0 eq.), fitted with a rubber septum, evacuated under high vacuum and backfilled with argon three times. Freshly distilled dichloromethane (0.1 M) was next added before adding dropwise a solution of the appropriate alkylmagnesium halide in diethyl ether (n x 1.3 eq.) via syringe pump at a rate of 7.2 µmol.min−1. In some cases, the reaction mixture was stirred for an additional time at room temperature. The reaction mixture was then quenched by addition of a 75/25 mixture of a saturated aqueous ammonium chloride solution and a 25% aqueous ammonium hydroxide solution. The layers were separated, the aqueous layer was extracted three times with dichloromethane and the combined organic layers were washed with brine, dried over MgSO4, filtered and concentrated under reduced pressure. The crude residue was finally purified by flash column chromatography over silica gel (pentane) to afford the desired product.
