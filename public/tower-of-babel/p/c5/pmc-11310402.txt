CASE PRESENTATION

A 72‐year‐old Chinese man presented with a 5‐day history of painful nodules on the perianal region to our emergency department. Due to the location of the lesion, he was immediately referred to the anorectal and dermatological department. On admission, a dark‐brown wingless alive parasite with four pairs of slim legs was observed on the perianal region, and its head adhered firmly into the perianal skin (Figure 1). The remainder of the detailed anal physical examination did not show any other abnormalities. His temperature was normal. The patient was a farmer. He lived and farmed in the countryside of China with his partner, and he recalled a history of wiping his anus with grass leaves after defecating in the wild. He reported no medical history of contact with animals or recent travel history. Laboratory examinations, including routine blood test, routine urine test, stool analysis test, hypersensitive C‐reactive protein, liver function, and renal function, were unremarkable. The neurological examination was negative. No evidences of other skin lesions were detectable. According to the above findings, a diagnosis of hard tick bite was made definitely after contacting the parasitology department and asking them to diagnose the genus of this specific tick. This patient was successfully treated with mechanical removal of the tick and adjacent tissues (Video 1). Topical tobramycin eye ointment was applied for the incision twice daily for 3 days. At a 2‐week follow‐up, the patient had resolution of symptoms (Figure 2). No systemic discomfort was observed. His family members or close contacts were asked for inspection, and no tick‐related symptoms were observed.

DISCUSSION

Ticks are reported to be the small blood‐feeding arthropod ectoparasites, and they can be divided into hard ticks and soft ticks. Several complications (e.g., encephalitis, rickettsioses, Lyme disease, Mediterranean fever) caused by tickborne pathogens have been described. 1 Tickborne diseases have become the threats to the health of people worldwide, accounting for nearly 80% patients with vectorborne diseases in the United States. 2 Diagnosis of tickborne diseases is based on the clinical characteristics and laboratory tests. Detecting ticks in the areas of the body has important benefits for confirming the diagnosis. Due to the concealed performance of ticks, sometimes it poses a huge diagnostic challenge for clinicians. Ticks may also be easily misdiagnosed as masses since patients often present with local nodules. For fever patients (especially people who work in tick infested areas) with unknown origin, highly suspicious of parasitic infection should be considered.

Untreated or delayed cases will result in life‐threatening consequences. Usually, patients with tickborne diseases should be screened and followed up by a multidisciplinary team. Early diagnosis and proper treatment play a significant role in the management. Reserving in the skin increases the risk of infection. 1 Manual removal of ticks with fine forceps is important for preventing the transmission of tickborne infections. 3 Several pesticides (e.g., trans‐p‐methane‐3,8‐diol, N,N‐diethyl‐3‐methylbenzamide) are recommended for patients with tickborne diseases. Trans‐p‐methane‐3,8‐diol is effective against ticks, but it should not be applied for children under the age of 3 years. N,N‐diethyl‐3‐methylbenzamide is a useful, safe, and widely used drug, however, the duration of effect is short. 1

People (who have been bitten by a tick) and close contacts should be screened for delayed symptoms associated with tickborne diseases in a relatively long period. Furthermore, clothing inspection is required for ticks because they may be hidden with clothing during a trip to tick infested areas. 1
