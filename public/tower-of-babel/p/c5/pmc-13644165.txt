Spatial heterogeneity of health care resources: a study of the socioeconomic, demographic, and natural geographic conditions of ethnic minority regions in Southwestern China
Authors: Zheng Wang, Huaxiang Lu, Qiuping He, Xi Liu, Qiuli Chen, JunJun Liu, Aihu Dong, Nannan Liu, Haoyu He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe inequitable distribution of health care resources causes disparities in access to health care services, impacting residents’ health status and quality of life. As a result, the allocation of health care resources has become a critical concern for governments when health care policies are formulated. China is a developing country with uneven development among cities and rural areas; thus, the problem of inequity in health care resources is noticeable, especially in less developed areas. Hence, we aimed to explore the spatial distribution of the average number of physicians and hospital beds in Guangxi and used spatial models to analyse the effects of economic, demographic and elevation factors on the distribution of health care resources. These findings may provide a basis for the government to improve health policies.MethodsWe described the spatial patterns of the number of hospitals (NH), the number of hospital beds per 10,000 people (NHB), and the number of practising physicians per 10,000 people (NPP) in Guangxi. Afterwards, we explored the economic and demographic factors that may influence the spatial distribution of the NHB and NPP through geographically weighted regression (GWR) and multiscale geographically weighted regression (MGWR) models.ResultsGuangxi’s NH (Moran's I = 0.127, p = 0.006), NHB (Moran's I = 1.363, p = 0.001), and NPP (Moran's I = 1.289, p < 0.001) exhibited significant autocorrelation and heterogeneity in terms of spatial distribution, significantly clustering and forming hotspots in Nanning, Liuzhou, Guilin, and Wuzhou. Spatial regression of the NHB with the proportion of the population ≥ 60 years old (beta = 0.337, p = 0.001), average years of schooling (beta = 0.409, p = 0.002), general government revenue (beta = 0.228, p = 0.023), and average elevation (beta = 0.225, p = 0.042) was performed, where NPP was related to population density (beta = 0.230, p = 0.009), the proportion of the population ≥ 60 years old (beta = 0.250, p = 0.003), and gross social consumption (beta = 0.241, p = 0.010). There were significant spatial regression relationships between gross domestic product per capita (beta = 0.324, p < 0.001) and the percentage of urban residents (beta = 0.172, p = 0.033).ConclusionsThe spatial heterogeneity of health care resources in Guangxi is related to the unqual socioeconomic, cultural, and population development and is influenced by topographical factors. In Guangxi, health care resources are clustered in areas with a relatively good degree of social development, such as Nanning, Liuzhou, Guilin, and Wuzhou, with both absolute and relative deficiencies and with different factors influencing the number of beds per capita and the number of physicians per capita. Moreover, feasible ways to meet the demand for health care resources in different regions should be explored.Keywords: China, Health care resources, Spatial distribution, Spatial heterogeneity, Socioeconomic factors

Background

Health care resources encompass the core components required to maintain a functional health system. They include human resources (e.g., physicians), infrastructure (e.g., hospitals and beds), and essential medical supplies and equipment, which collectively provide the foundation for medical service delivery and health promotion [1]. The inequitable distribution of health care resources causes disparities in people's access to health care services, impacting residents’ health status and quality of life [2, 3]. Therefore, the rational allocation of health care resources constitutes one of the key concerns for governments when health care policies are formulated, and it remains a long-standing research focus among scholars globally. For instance, Rahman reported that regions with lower economic status in the United States had fewer neurosurgeons per capita [4]. Ismail demonstrated a highly inequitable population-based distribution of health care resources across Sudan's 18 states [5]. A Japanese study revealed a more pronounced maldistribution among obstetricians than among general practitioners or paediatricians [6]. These findings reflect a global pattern of inequitable health care resource allocation, which may be exacerbated in developing nations.

China is a developing country with uneven development among cities and rural areas, and as the allocation of health care resources is closely associated with the economy, health care investment, education, and population size [7], the problem of inequity in health care resources is noticeable. Multiple studies have shown that there is inequality in the allocation of various health care resources in China, such as high-quality medical resources, traditional Chinese medicine health resources, the allocation of general practitioners, and emergency medicine beds [8–11]. In recent years, health care resources have tended to shift from the west to the east and central regions, mainly because the western region lags behind the central and eastern regions in terms of environmental conditions, economic level, and salary [12], which may exacerbate the shortage and inequality of health resources in the west. However, at present, there are more studies on the allocation of health care resources at the national level in China, while research focusing on the western region remains relatively scarce.

The terrain of western China is mainly mountainous and hilly. The terrain problems lead to inconvenient transportation, and the scarcity of sea access channels makes the economic development of the western region slower than that of the eastern and central regions. Several studies have focused on the issue of health care resource allocation in the western region [13, 14], but they broadly cover multiple provinces and autonomous regions as integrated research subjects. The proposed health care resource allocation strategies are insufficient at the subprovincial level. Therefore, research focused on intraprovincial allocation is essential. Given that western China is the primary settlement area for ethnic minorities, studying health care resource allocation in its ethnic minority autonomous regions is more representative.

In western China, there are several typical autonomous regions of ethnic minorities, including the Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region, the Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region, the Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region, the Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region, and the Tibet Autonomous Region. Guangxi, in southwestern China, has the largest population of Zhuang and Yao ethnic groups in the world. According to the seventh national census of China in 2020, the Zhuang ethnic group accounts for 31.36% (15.722 million people) of the population of Guangxi, and this group constitutes 80.34% of the total Zhuang population in China. The Yao ethnic group accounts for 3.36% (1.683 million people) of Guangxi's population, and this group constitutes 50.86% of the total Yao population in China. Guangxi is famous for its scenery of Guilin and the longevity of its Bama population [15, 16]. As an underdeveloped region, by 2020, the per capita gross domestic product (GDP) was approximately $6,833, which was far lower than that of neighbouring coastal Guangdong and inland Hunan, Guizhou, and Yunnan at $13,652, $9,638, $7,056, and $8,013, respectively [17–20]. The economic development gap between urban and rural areas was significant. Moreover, the topography of Guangxi is diverse and features karst, mountainous, hilly, and plain areas, and it also has a coastline. This may exacerbate imbalances in the geographical distribution of health care resources. Furthermore, there are large differences in elevation between cities and counties. Some counties and districts are located in high-elevation mountainous areas, and inconvenient transportation may restrict population mobility and economic development, which may directly or indirectly affect the distribution of health care resources. However, few studies have proven this hypothesis. In conclusion, this study of the allocation of health care resources in Guangxi and its influencing factors is highly representative and can provide reference data for similar research in other ethnic minority areas.

Public health researchers have focused on the remarkable heterogeneity in the spatial distribution of life expectancy [16, 21] and many diseases [22–24] in Guangxi. Given that disease diagnosis and treatment rely on health care resources, several scholars have focused on the rationality of the spatial allocation of health care resources in Guangxi. For instance, Junxu Zhou [25] and Yingying Lin [26] have respectively studied the fairness of the allocation of intensive care unit beds and medical resources for children with autism spectrum disorders in Guangxi. However, these studies focus primarily on specialized medical resources, while the spatial distribution patterns and associated factors influencing health care resources in Guangxi—including hospitals of various levels and categories—have received limited scholarly attention and remain insufficiently understood. The rational allocation of these resources determines the accessibility of basic health care services across different regions. Therefore, investigating the equity and efficiency of health care resource distribution is crucial for health care planning and policy-making.

Health resource allocation is often analysed using fairness research tools such as the Gini coefficient, Theil index, and concentration degree analysis [27]. The advantage of this approach is that it can numerically reflect the degree of fairness in resource allocation. However, some studies suggest that these methods are relatively general, lack intuitiveness, fail to fully capture the spatial distribution patterns of health resources, and have limitations in exploring the factors influencing allocation [28]. Thus, geographic information system (GIS) technology has been used in related studies. GIS not only visualises the aggregation of health resources more clearly but also further explores the factors influencing the distribution of health resources through spatial regression models, such as GWR and MGWR [29]. MGWR enables the detection of spatially varying relationships across multiple scales, thereby offering enhanced explanatory power and policy relevance. To a certain extent, it makes up for the limitations of traditional methods.

We aimed to explore the spatial distribution of the average number of physicians and hospital beds in Guangxi and used spatial models to analyse the effects of economic, demographic, and elevation factors on the distribution. These findings may provide a basis for the government to improve health policies.

Data source and methods

Data source

We took all the prefecture-level cities (cities that usually consist of several municipal districts), county-level cities, and counties within Guangxi as the research subjects. The digital maps were generated in shp format in ArcGIS with the coordinate system of the World Geodetic System 1984. A digital elevation model (DEM) was obtained from NASA's EARTHDATA. NHB and NPP were calculated using the data obtained from the Guangxi Health Commission and the 2021 Statistical Bulletin of Economic and Social Development.

Socioeconomic and demographic factors may influence the allocation of health care resources; thus, we used these factors as independent variables, including GDP per capita, total retail sales of consumer goods, general government revenue, disposable income per capita, density of the resident population, proportion of the population ≥ 60 years old, percentage of the urban residents, years of schooling per capita, and data from the 2021 Statistical Bulletin of Economic and Social Development.

Statistical analysis

The spatial analyses conducted in this study included spatial autocorrelation analysis, spatial clustering analysis, hotspot analysis, and trend analysis, as well as geographic GWR and MGWR models [30], which have been more widely used in recent years. To quantify the estimated statistics, spatial autocorrelation was assessed using Moran's I, along with z scores and p values for statistical validation. Spatial clustering analysis was performed using the general G statistic complemented by z scores and p values. Hotspot analysis was employed to identify statistically significant high-value hotspots and low-value cold spots within 90%, 95%, and 99% confidence intervals (CIs). Trend surface analysis, a part of geostatistical trend analysis, was used to illustrate the spatial distribution trends of the studied indicators and their crucial areas. Furthermore, we employed the GWR and MGWR models to explore the geographic variation in the relationships between the dependent and independent variables. These models provided estimated values of the regression coefficient (beta), standard errors, and the significance of the explanatory variables. Diagnostic indicators for model fit included the Akaike information criterion, the corrected Akaike information criterion, the Bayesian information criterion, R-squared (R2), and adjusted R-squared (adj-R2) [29]. Data management and statistical analysis were primarily conducted using software packages such as EXCEL, RStudio V402, ArcGIS Pro V3.0.1, and MGWR V2.2.

Results

Geographical, sociological, and economic status of Guangxi

We included 41 municipal districts from 14 prefecture-level cities, 10 county-level cities, 48 counties and 12 ethnic minority autonomous counties in Guangxi. The Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region is in southern China, located between 104°28′–112°04′ E and 20°54′–26°23′ N(Fig. 1a, b). The major secondary rivers are the Yu, Liu, Xiang, Hongshui, and Xun (Fig. 1c).

The location, neighbourhood, administrative border, tributaries (to improve the visualization, only the secondary tributaries of the Xun River and Yu River are marked), and typical karst terrain of Guangxi, South China are shown in Fig. 1a, b, c, and d, respectively

Guangxi has a typical kart landscape with a great difference in elevation, ranging from −76 m in the central basin to a maximum of 2093 m in the north. In the southwest, northwest, north, and northeast areas, mountains, hills, river valley plains, premountain plains, and other karst landforms account for 80% of the total area of Guangxi, with mountains at 400–900 m accounting for up to 50%, hills at 200–400 m accounting for 10%, and river valley plains, premountain plains, deltas, and other terraces at approximately 200 m accounting for nearly 30% (Fig. 1d). Spatial heterogeneity and patterns of health care resources in Guangxi

The number of hospital beds per 10,000 people (index1) was significantly autocorrelated in terms of spatial distribution (Moran's I = 1.363, z = 3.239, p = 0.001), with lower values ranging from only 8 to 27 beds per 10,000 people, such as in the Dongxing, Qinnan, Qinbei, Tieshangang, and Yinhai districts in the south; the Babu and Quanzhou districts in the east and northeast; and the Lingyun and Du'an districts in the northwest (Fig 3 - 2a). The highest values occurred in Nanning, Liuzhou, Guilin, Wuzhou, Yulin, and Baise in the upper reaches of the Liu and Hongshui Rivers upstream and downstream and the Xiang and Xun Rivers downstream. These areas formed critical aggregated hotspots (general G = 0.014, z = 3.074, p = 0.002) (Fig 3 - 2b), and the number of beds per capita in the major urban areas of Nanning, Liuzhou, Baize, Guilin, Wuzhou, and Yulin ranged from 129 to 214 (Fig 3 – 2a).

The spatial distribution of the number of physicians (index2) per 10,000 people showed significant autocorrelation and heterogeneity (Moran's I = 1.289, z = 4.947, p < 0.001). The low values were distributed in Qinnan, Yanshan, Babu, Bobai, Tieshangang, Linshan, Sanjiang, Jinxi, Lingyun, and Longlin, with only 6–11 physicians per 10,000 people, as shown in Fig. 3 - 3a. Nanning, Guilin, Liuzhou, and Wuzhou had many physicians per capita, resulting in the formation of significant aggregation hotspots (general G = 0.018, Z = 4.521, p < 0.001). The number of physicians per capita in these regions ranged from 52–123 per 10,000 people, as shown in Fig. 3 - 3a. Spatial trends of health care resources in Guangxi

The GWR and MGWR models were applied to evaluate the potential spatial associations among socioeconomic, demographic, and education variables with the per capita number of beds and the per capita number of physicians. The critical indicators identified in both the GWR and the MGWR models for the per capita number of beds and the per capita number of physicians were consistent. A slight difference was observed only in the standard error of the constant term for the per capita number of beds between the GWR (S.E. = 0.084) and MGWR (S.E. = 0.085) models. The diagnostic results indicated that the MGWR model outperformed the GWR model, as evidenced by the lower values of AIC, AICc, BIC, R2, and Adj-R2, suggesting a better model fit. Consequently, the results of the MGWR model were used for further analysis and subsequent spatial regression visualization, as presented in Table 3.

Geographically weighted regression (GWR) of health care resources and social, demographic, and economic variables

In the MGWR model, the number of beds per capita was spatially regressed with factors such as the proportion of the population aged ≥ 60 years (beta = 0.337, p = 0.001), average years of education (beta = 0.409, p = 0.002), general government revenue (beta = 0.228, p = 0.023), and average elevation (beta = 0.225, p = 0.042). Diagnostic indicators suggested that the model exhibited a good fit (AICc = 287.106, Adj-R2 = 0.330). The number of physicians per capita showed a significant spatial regression relationship with variables such as population density (beta = 0.230, p = 0.009), the proportion of the population aged ≥ 60 years (beta = 0.250, p = 0.003), gross social consumption (beta = 0.241, p = 0.010), GDP per capita (beta = 0.324, p < 0.001), and the percentage of urban residents (beta = 0.172, p = 0.033). Diagnostic indicators revealed a superior model fit for the per capita number of physicians (AICc = 232.166, Adj-R2 = 0.593) than the per capita bed number model (AICc = 287.106, Adj-R2 = 0.330), as shown in Table 4. Visualisation of the MGWR model for per capita bed numbers revealed that regions strongly associated with the per capita number of beds and population density were predominantly in central Nanning and most areas of eastern Wuzhou and Yulin. Areas strongly associated with the population aged ≥ 60 years were found in northern Guilin, northeastern Hezhou, central Nanning, the northern parts of Qinzhou, Beihai, Fangchenggang, and most areas of southeastern Wuzhou and Yulin. A large global association was noted with average years of schooling. The regions most strongly associated with general government revenue were southeastern Wuzhou, Yulin, and central Nanning. With respect to the terrain, strong associations were observed in central Nanning and the relatively flat terrains of southern Qinzhou and Beihai, as presented in Fig. 4. According to the visualisation of the MGWR model for the per capita number of physicians, there was a significant global association between the per capita number of physicians and population density. The regions strongly associated with the population aged ≥ 60 years included northeastern Guilin, Hezhou, central Nanning, Liuzhou, southeastern and southern Yulin, Wuzhou, Beihai, and Qinzhou, which were very similar to the patterns observed for the per capita number of beds. A significant deviation on both sides was found for total retail sales, with most areas in the western half and the northeastern region showing significance. Areas strongly associated with per capita GDP were in most regions, except for the southern region. The regions largely associated with the percentage of urban residents included central Nanning, Liuzhou, northern Guilin, southeastern Yulin, Wuzhou, and northwestern Hezhou, as depicted in Fig. 5.

Discussion

We explored the spatial distribution of the number of hospitals, beds and physicians in Guangxi. Spatial modelling was subsequently used to analyse the effects of economic, demographic, and elevational factors on the distribution of health resources.

The spatial heterogeneity and patterns of health care resources revealed that the numbers of beds and physicians per capita in Guangxi are unevenly distributed geographically. These resources are concentrated in cities such as Nanning, Liuzhou, Guilin, Wuzhou, Yulin, and Baise, whereas low-resource areas are primarily found in certain districts and counties of Guilin, Qinzhou, Beihai, Hezhou, and Hechi. Notably, even within relatively well-resourced cities such as Nanning, Liuzhou, and Guilin, some districts (e.g., Jiangnan, Liujiang, and Yanshan) have fewer hospitals and lower per capita numbers of beds and physicians. This suggests that health care resource inequality persists at both the regional and intracity levels. One contributing factor may be the governmental tendency to invest limited funds in high-quality medical institutions, aiming to advance development and meet public demand for complex disease care [28]. However, this may increase the cost and difficulty of accessing health care for residents in other regions [31]. Therefore, attention should be given to the rational allocation of health care resources at both the Guangxi and municipal levels to improve geographical accessibility. Leveraging technologies such as Internet + healthcare, artificial intelligence, big data, and telemedicine could also help expand the coverage of quality medical services [32].

In the spatial heterogeneity analysis, hotspots for per capita hospital beds and physicians were concentrated mainly in Nanning, Liuzhou, Guilin, Wuzhou, and Baise. Despite having higher economic levels and larger populations than Wuzhou and Baise, Yulin and Guigang have not formed hotspots. A likely explanation is that each of the hotspot cities hosts at least one medical university or several health colleges, attracting and training substantial numbers of medical students who often seek local employment after graduation, thereby reinforcing resource concentration. This pattern aligns with observations in cities such as Beijing, Shanghai, and Tianjin [33]. These results imply that regions with scarce health care resources could benefit from the establishment of local medical colleges to cultivate and retain health professionals. Furthermore, these findings suggest that health care resource distribution in Guangxi is not solely dictated by economic or population factors, indicating that more complex drivers exist that require further investigation.

Notably, even in mountainous or less developed regions such as Longlin and Xilin, no critical spatial cold spots emerged despite their fewer beds and physicians per capita. This is attributed to rational health care planning [34], wherein each county and township is ensured to have at least one medical institution (e.g., county central hospitals or township health centres), supported by state funding. Such planning has secured basic health care services and improved accessibility for rural populations in these poor and remote mountainous areas [35]. However, it should be noted that the allocation of rural health care resources remains unequal across the country [36, 37], accompanied by a trend towards gradual resource outflow. This is primarily reflected in the declines in hospital beds and health care technical personnel per 10,000 people in rural areas compared with the national average [38], as well as the continued migration of rural health professionals to counties with stronger economic development and higher levels of urbanization [39]. As a relatively underdeveloped province, Guangxi should focus on increasing fiscal investment to strengthen its rural health care resource system, thereby ensuring equity and accessibility in rural health care.

In the MGWR model, population density has a globally significant positive correlation with the number of physicians per capita. This indicates a synchronized development between them and supports previous findings of a relatively equitable physician-to-population distribution in China [40]. However, this correlation is not evident for hospital beds per capita in the western region. It is thus advised that western regions conduct surveys on health care resource utilization, measuring indicators such as bed occupancy rates [41] to evaluate whether current bed supplies meet local health care demands.

Population ageing is a critical issue globally and in China [42]. The results of the MGWR model reveal that in most regions, both beds and physicians per capita are positively correlated with the proportion of people ≥ 60 years old. These findings suggest that health care resource allocation in Guangxi proactively accounts for population ageing and adjusts distribution to better meet the needs of elderly people [43]. As ageing intensifies, shifts in disease patterns and rising demand will likely attract further health care investment. However, in less developed western areas, such as parts of Baise and Chongzuo, population ageing was not significantly correlated with the number of hospital beds or physicians per capita. This suggests that health care resources have not kept pace with ageing populations, possibly because of constrained economic and market investment [44]. In response, it is recommended that Guangxi direct resources towards expanding local health care capacity, prioritizing investments to ensure equitable medical access for its ageing population.

Education levels and general government revenue indicate regional development, with economically stronger cities concentrating more health resources [45]. Globally, the per capita number of hospital beds is positively correlated with education level. More educated individuals tend to have greater health awareness and higher incomes [46], making them more likely to seek hospital treatment, thereby indirectly increasing bed supply through demand. In China, publicly funded hospitals—responsible for basic care—hold far more beds than private ones do [47]. Their investment is linked to local fiscal revenue [48]. This linkage is evident in the significant positive correlation observed between general government revenue and per capita beds in the southeastern and central regions, indicating the potential allocation of fiscal resources to hospital infrastructure. Given that debt risk is a critical consideration in local government investment decisions [49], a balance must be struck between this investment and the efficient utilization of health care resources. Tailored planning that integrates population size and medical demand is needed to avoid overinvestment and inefficiency [50]. Underdeveloped regions should assess whether current bed capacity meets basic needs while also addressing imbalances through increased public funding or by incentivizing private capital for facility and bed expansion [51].

In this study, the terrain-related variable of elevation is innovatively incorporated. Interestingly, a significant spatial correlation is observed between the per capita number of hospital beds and topography in central and southern Guangxi, with fewer beds in lower-elevation areas than in resource-rich, low-elevation coastal cities such as Guangzhou, Hangzhou, and Shenzhen [52]. We speculate that the underlying cause is the lack of navigable river–sea corridors in Guangxi, which limits its maritime connectivity and economic growth despite its coastal location. The planned Pinglu Canal, designed to link inland waterways with seaports, is expected to establish a vital maritime corridor, potentially accelerating economic development and health care resource allocation in coastal cities. Northwards, the bed-elevation correlation weakens, primarily because of mountainous terrain, economic underdevelopment, and transport limitations that indirectly restrict the bed supply. Although the terrain data obtained in this study were limited, it demonstrates the impact of topography on health care resource allocation through elevation variables alone, offering a valuable reference for similar studies in topographically complex regions.

Gross social consumption, per capita GDP, and the percentage of urban residents are significantly positively spatially correlated with physicians per capita. These economic indicators reflect patient spending capacity. Regions with greater medical demand typically provide more employment opportunities for physicians, while those with higher consumption levels and urbanization tend to offer more attractive remuneration. Previous studies have reported that economic, social, and demographic factors influence physician mobility [49, 53]. It is recommended that regions with lower population density and spending power increase salary subsidies, lower the requirements for title evaluation, and provide housing concessions to attract physicians and improve local health care access, especially in townships. Notably, Tian'e, Donglan, Guiping, and Tengxian show no significant correlation between gross social consumption and physicians per capita, whereas Fangchenggang does not exhibit such a correlation between per capita GDP and physicians per capita. These areas do not have low numbers of physicians, which may indicate limited medical expenditure among residents. Future studies should examine whether this results from low health-seeking awareness or better overall population health.

In this study, the spatial distribution of health care resources (beds and physicians) in Guangxi was analysed, and spatial hotspots and trends were identified while associated social, economic, demographic, and topographic factors were examined. It further discusses the drivers and potential consequences of these associations. Given that few studies have addressed the spatial patterns of health care resources in Guangxi and their drivers, this work provides scientifically valuable evidence to support spatial resource planning by local health authorities.

However, there are several limitations to this study. First, as region-specific data on high-quality health care resources, population composition, and medical demand could not be obtained, this study could not analyse the allocation of premium health care resources or public access to medical services. Second, owing to the constraints in statistical calibre, periodicity, and public licensing, only eight commonly used social, economic, and demographic indicators were included, while other socioeconomic indicators were not, which may have led to limited extrapolation of our conclusions; thus, future studies will incorporate additional variables. Third, as preliminary models (including OLS and SEM) produced results that were very similar to the MGWR and GWR outputs, they are not included in this paper because of space limitations.

Conclusions

On the basis of our findings, we can conclude that the spatial distribution of beds per capita seems to be more strongly influenced by the level of urban development and general government revenue, whereas physicians per capita is more strongly influenced by demographic factors. These associations are most evident in economically advanced regions. Given the regional disparities in development and geography, tailored strategies grounded in comprehensive research are needed. These strategies may include increasing investment in health care infrastructure, establishing additional medical schools, providing incentives to attract external physicians, improving transportation, and enhancing public awareness of health. Such measures would support the attraction of high-quality medical personnel and contribute to an optimized spatial allocation of health care resources.
