Predictive modeling and network analysis uncover novel regulators of multi-stress tolerance in Saccharomyces cerevisiae
Authors: Zahra Zinati, Ali Niazi, Hanieh karimi, Batool Sardaripoor, Sima Sazegari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUnderstanding molecular responses to multi-stress conditions in Saccharomyces cerevisiae is crucial for optimizing industrial strains and enhancing stress tolerance. This study uses machine learning and data mining approaches to unravel the critical genes by which Saccharomyces cerevisiae responds to integrated stresses.ResultsRNA-seq analysis was performed on 48 sequencing runs; the subsequent results were analyzed by Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis, and a distinct red module consisting of 371 genes associated with stress response was identified. The Boruta feature selection algorithm was applied to this module, confirming 56 important genes while classifying others as tentative or unimportant. Subsequent Random Forest analysis refined this set by highlighting essential genes, achieving robust classification accuracy, and focusing intensely on the most relevant genes for stress adaptation. Our integrative computational approach, including Module Membership and Gene Significance tests and machine learning methods, identified known and unknown candidate genes linked to oxidative stress, osmotic stress, and glucose deprivation. Notably, PPN1, SAM3, PLN1, Icl1, CTA1, AMS1, GAC1, and ADH2 genes demonstrated significant roles in adaptation mechanisms under osmotic, oxidative, and acid stress.ConclusionThis preliminary study clarified the underlying molecular network responses to complex stress conditions in yeast, which can be applied for further efficient strain engineering to improve yeast robustness for industrial applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05191-5.

Introduction

Saccharomyces cerevisiae is the foremost broadly utilized eukaryotic organism in numerous industrial applications, particularly bioethanol production, because of its distinguished characteristics, including fast growth, high environmental stress tolerance, and consequently efficient ethanol production yield. The remarkable fermentation capacity and its endurance under environmental stress, such as osmolarity and low pH, make Saccharomyces cerevisiae one of the most common microorganisms in the fermentation industry [1]. Moreover, industrial strains such as BT0510 have developed remarkable abilities for adaptation to severe stresses [2, 3]. However, exposure to various environmental stresses inhibits reaching the maximum ethanol production capacity. For instance, by changing the osmotic potential, osmolar stressors impede structure and regular cellular function, affect metabolic pathway fluxes, and decrease ethanol yield [4]. Since Saccharomyces cerevisiae is exposed to multiple stress agents during industrial processes, improving yeast strains for cross-stress resilience and exploring the multifactorial stress combination responses is crucial.

During fermentation, cells may face various environmental stresses, primarily multi-faceted ones, including osmotic, oxidative, nutritional starvation, and ethanol accumulation [5]. These stressors negatively impact ethanol production yield, growth, and cell viability [6]. Initially, yeast cells may experience osmotic or oxidative stresses due to increased sugar levels, while as fermentation progresses, nutritional starvation combined with ethanol accumulation can restrict ethanol production [7]. To withstand these challenges, yeast cells undergo cellular and molecular adaptations at the transcriptome, proteome, and metabolome levels. In essence, the yeast’s stress-responsive mechanisms lead to the upregulation or downregulation of numerous genes and the corresponding enzymes and metabolites in response to stress conditions. Signal transduction pathways play a crucial role in detecting and modulating stress, resulting in a global modulation of gene expression regulated by genes such as transcription factors [8]. Understanding the genetic and molecular mechanisms that enable yeast cells to respond to stress stimuli is essential for developing high-tolerance strains with improved productivity. However, a comprehensive understanding of how yeasts respond to environmental stresses is lacking, and the connections between tolerance mechanisms across different environmental stresses remain unclear.

Transcriptomic analysis primarily facilitates the identification and functional characterization of genes and associated pathways that respond to environmental stress in yeast. Exploring the differentially expressed genes under hydrogen peroxide 30% (w/w) stress using RNA-seq analysis resulted in identifying MAP kinase and protein kinase A (PKA) signaling pathway genes in mutant yeast compared to the wild-type yeast strain. In addition, glycolysis-related genes were downregulated, while tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and fatty acid degradation ones were upregulated for efficient energy production and consumption [9]. Moreover, RNA-seq analysis resulted to the identification of ENA5 (a P-type ATPase) as the critical gene in Saccharomyces cerevisiae that is differentially expressed under osmotic and ethanol stress. In agreement, overexpression of the ENA5 gene confirmed its role in ethanol and osmotic stress tolerance, as the engineered strain exhibited enhanced growth under osmotic (400 g/L glucose and 3 mol/L sorbitol) and 13% (v/v) ethanol stress. Similarly, the fermentation efficiency of the modified strain was improved to 27.57% compared to the original strain under 8.0% (v/v) ethanol stress using batch fermentation [10]. Exploring the underlying molecular mechanism of stress tolerance and identifying the responsive genes and pathways are crucial for Saccharomyces cerevisiae strain improvement through biotechnology. As an outstanding genetic microorganism model, Saccharomyces cerevisiae transcriptomics analysis presents a deeper interpretation of stress response in other microbes using an outstanding genetic microorganism model.

Unravelling the distinct transcriptional signatures and identification of the principal genes obtained from an accurate RNA-seq-based bioinformatic analysis method could be firmly applied for a comprehensive understanding of the gene expression mechanism under stress conditions in yeast. In this regard, one of the most novel and efficient methods is to use Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA). This R-based package is a powerful tool for exploring the key modules and correlated genes at the biological system level with high sensitivity, even for low-expressed genes [11]. Even though analysis of high-throughput data is challenging and lacks an efficient clustering method for deciphering the association of co-expressed genes and multiple stressors, WGCNA could potentially facilitate the network-based analysis of RNA-seq data and provide better insight into Saccharomyces cerevisiaeʼs molecular response under stresses [12]. Although WGCNA has many advantages in obtaining information from expression data, this effective approach has been rarely employed for Saccharomyces cerevisiae expression analysis.

In addition, the Boruta algorithm serves as a robust and extensively utilized feature selection technique aimed at identifying all pertinent variables within high-dimensional datasets. It achieves this by iteratively comparing original features with their randomized shadow equivalents in a Random Forest (RF) framework [13]. Boruta offers a thorough methodology for distinguishing genuinely significant predictors from noise, which is particularly beneficial in intricate biological data, such as genomic profiles, where the quantity of variables (genes) greatly surpasses the number of samples [14]. Importantly, Boruta has shown considerable success in the field of plant stress genomics; for example [15], employed Boruta to pinpoint essential waterlogging-responsive genes in cucumber, thereby underscoring its effectiveness in uncovering critical molecular signatures in response to waterlogging stresses. While direct applications of Boruta in yeast stress genomics remain scarce, the algorithm’s impressive performance in various biological settings indicates its promising potential for clarifying stress response mechanisms in Saccharomyces cerevisiae.

In this context, numerous valuable raw RNA-seq gene expression datasets related to Saccharomyces cerevisiae are available in public databases derived under various stress conditions [16]. Biologists can apply these valuable datasets to unravel the underlying molecular basis of stress-induced transcriptome and metabolome changes through bioinformatics-based system-level analysis, such as WGCNA and machine learning tools. In this research, a detailed dataset comprising 48 sequencing runs was utilized to explore the transcriptional response of Saccharomyces cerevisiae to various sequential stressors. The yeast strain BT0510, known for its tolerance to multiple stress types, was exposed to different stress conditions, and its responses were evaluated through WGCNA and data mining methodologies. Furthermore, the Boruta feature selection algorithm and the RF model were employed to pinpoint the most significant genes involved in the stress response. This integrated approach facilitated a more precise and dependable identification of molecular markers linked to multi-stress responses and signifies an innovative use of machine learning techniques in the analysis of yeast gene expression.

Previous studies have predominantly characterized yeast stress responses using differential gene expression analysis [3]. However, the modular architecture of stress-associated co-expression networks, as well as the identification of intramodular hub genes, remains poorly defined. Given that differential expression alone does not capture network topology or module-specific relevance, the integration of systems-level network analysis with machine learning–based feature selection constitutes a more rigorous framework for identifying genes with central roles in stress-responsive transcriptional programs.

Methods

This study utilized a publicly available RNA-seq dataset deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA; accession PRJNA915019). In total, 48 sequencing runs corresponding to 27 biological samples were analyzed, originating from experiments examining the transcriptional response of yeast to sequential stress conditions. In our analysis, we treated each sequencing run as a technical replicate, which explains why 48 runs were included. We included them to capture all available sequencing data without discarding technical replicates. The yeast strain employed in the experiment was URM 6670 (also referred to as BT0510), a highly flocculent strain that exhibits ethanol stress, osmotic stress, and higher tolerance to elevated temperature. This particular strain was selected to investigate its adaptive and transcriptional responses to three distinct sequences of stressors. These stress treatments encompassed osmotic stress, oxidative stress, and glucose limitation, followed by ethanol stress, to better understand the strain’s capacity to manage sequential environmental challenges. RNA-seq analysis was performed using the Galaxy platform, which offers a comprehensive, open-source, and user-friendly environment for analyzing bioinformatics data. The subsequent steps delineate the procedure employed to analyze the 48 sequencing runs obtained from the transcriptional response of yeast to sequential stress treatments.

Data preprocessing

The raw RNA-seq data were uploaded to the Galaxy platform, where quality control was conducted using tools such as FastQC [17] to evaluate the quality of sequencing reads. Based on the quality control findings from FastQC, the RNA-seq data were processed with Trimmomatic [18] for trimming purposes. The trimming was executed with particular settings, including sliding window trimming that averaged four bases, necessitating a quality score 28. Furthermore, reads shorter than a defined minimum length of 30 bases were eliminated. In addition, the first 11 bases were trimmed from each read. These measures were implemented to guarantee high-quality data by discarding low-quality reads and enhancing the accuracy of subsequent analyses.

Read alignment

After the preprocessing phase, the alignment of high-quality reads to the Saccharomyces cerevisiae reference genome (R-64-1-1) was performed using the HISAT2 aligner [19]. This procedure guaranteed that the reads were accurately mapped to their respective locations within the genome for subsequent transcript quantification. During the alignment process, the strand-specific option “forward (FR)” was chosen in HISAT2, signifying that the first read corresponds to the forward strand. In contrast, the second read pertains to the reverse strand, in accordance with a stranded RNA-seq protocol.

Transcript quantification

The aligned reads were analyzed with featureCounts [20] to determine the number of reads that map to each gene. These counts were subsequently utilized to quantify gene expression levels across the 48 sequencing runs. During the featureCounts process, the “stranded (forward)” option was chosen to indicate the strand information.

Batch effect assessment

Each RNA-seq run originated from a single experiment (PRJNA915019) that employed standard sequencing and library preparation methods. We used Principal Component Analysis (PCA) on voom-normalized expression data to look for any technical batch effects. PCA plots were used to cluster the sequencing runs, and ANOVA was used to connect possible batch factors (like run) to the top five main components.

Differential expression analysis

The edgeR [21] was used for differential gene expression analysis under stress conditions compared to the control (pretreatment). Statistical significance was determined by implementing a False Discovery Rate (FDR) threshold of 0.05. No log-fold change threshold was applied.

WGCNA data collection and preprocessing

Following quality control and preprocessing, a total of 7127 genes were retained for downstream analysis. Raw count data were normalized using the variance stabilizing transformation (VST) available in the DESeq2 package. The resulting normalized expression matrix was then used for downstream WGCNA. In this study, we analyzed the dataset by integrating all available stress samples into a single “stress” group and all corresponding control samples into a unified “control” group. This integrative approach was applied to identify shared stress-associated transcriptional modules across different stress conditions. Genes and sequencing runs exhibiting high missing values (NAs) or zero variance were excluded during preprocessing. The good SamplesGenes function from the WGCNA package [22] was utilized to evaluate the data quality, and any problematic genes or sequencing runs were eliminated if deemed necessary.

Preprocessing outlier detection was conducted through hierarchical clustering. The sequencing runs were grouped using the hclust function, with distances computed using the Euclidean method. The dendrogram representing the sequencing runs was visualized, and a cut-off value was implemented to identify and eliminate outlier sequencing runs. The remaining sequencing runs were utilized for subsequent analysis.

Network construction and module detection

The module is a group of genes with similar patterns in their expression and is classified based on correlation calculation. Various soft-thresholding powers (1 to 40) were assessed to identify the optimal power for the co-expression network assembly. The pickSoftThreshold function was employed to analyze the scale-free topology fit index and mean connectivity for each power, leading to the selection of a power of 7 for network construction. The Supplementary File, sheet S1 contains the comprehensive scale-free topology fit statistics needed for soft-threshold selection. The soft-thresholding power β = 7 was chosen since it was the lowest power that maintained sufficient mean connection while providing a scale-free topology fit index (R² > 0.80).

The adjacency matrix was computed based on the selected power, and the Topological Overlap Matrix (TOM) was calculated to minimize noise effects and spurious associations. Hierarchical clustering was then applied to identify gene modules. Modules were detected using dynamic tree cutting, with the least module size comprising 50 genes.

Module merging and eigengene calculation

Highly similar modules were combined based on the dissimilarity of their module eigengenes. A threshold of 0.1 was established to determine the cut-off for merging. The module eigengenes were computed, and their dissimilarity was evaluated through hierarchical clustering. The final collection of modules was achieved after merging highly correlated modules.

Association of modules with traits

The connections between modules and traits were analyzed using correlation analysis. The module-trait correlation coefficients were calculated, and a heatmap was created to illustrate the strength and significance of these associations. The significance of module–trait correlations was evaluated using a Student t-distribution approximation of the Pearson correlation coefficient, as implemented in the WGCNA package.

Module Membership (MM) and Gene Significance (GS)

GS related to the trait of interest was determined for each gene in modules. Each MM was evaluated by correlating its expression profile with the module eigengene. The GS for the trait was established by correlating each gene’s expression with the trait data. Scatter plots were produced to visualize the association between MM and GS for each module.

Boruta feature selection

The Boruta algorithm was utilized to identify pertinent features linked to the gene expression data. The analysis was conducted using the Boruta package in R, an all-relevant feature selection method based on the RF algorithm. Crucially, Boruta was only utilized for feature reduction. In this study, the class variable refers to the sample phenotype (control and stress), which served as the target for classification. All gene expression variables were used as features (predictors) in the Boruta feature selection procedure. To guarantee methodological independence from Random Forest, the following predictive models were trained using separate methods (SVM and LASSO).

Data preprocessing and model setup

The dataset was divided into training and testing subsets with a 60 − 40 ratio, allocating 70% of the data for model training and 30% for testing purposes. A random seed (123) was established to guarantee reproducibility. The Boruta algorithm was implemented through the Boruta function, designating the class variable as the target while all other variables served as predictors. The dot trace parameter was configured to 2 to provide detailed trace output, and the analysis was permitted to run for a maximum of 100 iterations. Tentative attributes were resolved using TentativeRoughFix, and the final selected features were extracted for subsequent model building.

Feature selection and visualization

The outcomes from Boruta were retrieved using the attStats function, and the feature selection process was illustrated through various plots. These visualizations included a plot that depicted feature importance, a plot that showed the importance history, and a plot that represented the tentative feature selections after applying the Tentative Rough Fix. The identified features were obtained using the get Selected Attributes function, which included tentative features.

Final model training and evaluation

Support Vector Machine (SVM) and Logistic Regression with LASSO regularization (glmnet), two separate models, were trained using the dataset restricted to Boruta-selected characteristics in order to assess predictive performance. These models were trained on the training subset using 10-fold cross-validation (trainControl with method="cv”).

We examined the model’s performance on the training and testing datasets. For every model, we created confusion matrices and computed class-specific metrics such as TP, FP, FN, TN, Precision, Recall, Specificity, F1-score, Support, and Predicted. Additionally, metrics like Cohen’s Kappa and Accuracy were provided. Confusion matrices were displayed as heatmaps using the ggplot2 tool.

RF feature importance

To assess the significance of the features and provide more interpretability, a RF model was trained on them. The importance function was used to quantify the significance of the feature and bar plots were used to visualize it. The complete analysis code, including Boruta-based feature selection and machine learning models (RF, SVM, and LASSO logistic regression), is provided in the Supplementary File, sheet S2.

Gene ontology and pathway analysis of genes identified by Boruta algorithm

Biological processes statistically overrepresented in genes identified by the Boruta algorithm were identified through gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis. This analysis offers insights into the underlying biology implicated in stress response. We used a rather moderate p-value threshold of 0.1 to include all possible biological processes and pathways that might be related to the identified genes.

Identification of TF among critical genes

In order to explore transcription factors among the genes related to the highly correlated red module and the top-scored genes deduced from Bruta analysis, the YEASTRACT database was used, and the Regulatory Associations using the Search for Genes tool were utilized. https://www.yeastract.com/findregulated.php.

None of the remaining principal components showed significant associations with technical variables (Table 1). The reults show that there are no detectable technical batch effects, which means that the variances in gene expression are largely attributable to biological differences and not problems with sequencing or library preparation.

Modules associated trait analysis

Conducting a correlation analysis between module eigengenes and stress trait unveiled associations between modules and stress. In particular, a total of 19 modules were identified using WGCNA analysis. The heatmap representing trait-module correlations features traits on the horizontal axis and modules on the vertical axis. An increase in the expression of a module’s eigengene, whether positive or negative, within a given sample, signifies a close connection between the module and the stress. In other words, each cell quantifies the degree of correlation between a trait and a module. In the represented heatmap, deeper red hues show stronger positive correlations, while bluer tones suggest negative reverse associations. This analytical approach assists in identifying key modules associated with specific sequencing runs.

Regarding the successful trait-module correlations, we proceeded to analyze the associations. According to the results, of 19 identified modules, 11 modules were significant (p-value < 0.05) (Fig. 3a). In other words, eleven yeast-stress-associated gene clusters were distinguished in this study through WGCNA analysis. Among them, seven co-expression modules were probably correlated to stress since they showed a correlation above 0.4. One of the modules, as colored in blue, was negatively correlated, and the other five modules showed a positive relationship. From all the identified modules, the red module implied the highest correlation, -0.93, with the best significance 9e-22 (Fig. 3b). Conducting a correlation analysis between module eigengenes and the stress trait revealed associations between gene modules and a general stress response. In this study, the primary aim was to identify common stress-responsive gene modules shared across different stress conditions. Therefore, all stress-treated samples were combined into a single category (“Stress”) and analyzed against control samples. The resulting module–trait relationships thus reflect core stress-associated transcriptional signatures rather than stress-specific effects.

MM and GS analysis

Performing the statistical GS & MM analysis using the WGCNA package, the association between MM and GS was evaluated (Supplementary File, sheet S8). In this analysis, GS and the MM imply the correlation of each gene with a specific trait and individual module, respectively. This assessment aimed to validate the relationship between modules and traits [23]. In accordance with the result obtained from module trait analysis, the red module interestingly demonstrated the highest correlation and significance (cor = 0.95, p = 1.3e − 188) in GS & MM analysis (Fig. 3b and Supplementary File, sheet S8 and S9). The highest score obtained from both analyses confirmed that the key red module contains the most distinctive expressed genes under multi-stress treatment yeast exposure. We computed the correlation coefficients and p-values for each gene in order to investigate the correlation structure within the most important modules. Strong co-expression patterns within this module are highlighted by a heatmap of the 30 genes with the greatest significant correlations (Supplementary File, sheet S10).

Identification of critical genes using Boruta

Boruta is a Random Forest-based feature selection technique that compares real features to fictitious shadow features to identify the most significant features in a dataset. In this work, 48 sequencing runs were subjected to RNA-Seq analysis. After utilizing WGCNA to assess the processed count data, a critical module (the red module containing 371 genes) was selected for further investigation (Supplementary File, sheet S11). The most crucial genes were then identified using the Boruta method. Certain genes were deemed crucial by Boruta, whereas others were deemed uncertain or inconsequential (Fig. 4 and Supplementary File, sheet S12). Boruta ultimately verified 103 genes as noteworthy (Fig. 4 and Supplementary File, sheet S12), guaranteeing that only the most pertinent genes were kept for further examination.

A RF model was trained on the Boruta-selected features alone to assess feature relevance for interpretability. According to this research, 100 out of 103 genes made the biggest contributions to the model (Supplementary File, page S13). Crucially, all downstream predictive modeling was carried out independently utilizing different algorithms (SVM and LASSO), and this model was not utilized as a confirmatory or predictive analysis.

Model assessment using independent algorithms

After using Boruta to choose the best features, the chosen genes were used in predictive modelling with different independent methods, like SVM and LASSO. This was done to make sure the evaluation was strong and to lower the chance of overfitting. We trained a RF model using the same set of features just to see how important they were for interpretability.

100 out of 103 genes features made a significant contribution to the RF model, which showed great internal consistency among the chosen genes.

Independent models showed good predicting abilities for classification performance. Class-specific metrics were computed for both training and testing sets using the SVM classifier and LASSO models. The Supplementary File, sheet S14, contains performance metrics for the RF Model, SVM, and LASSO.

The findings show consistent performance across sensitivity, specificity, and F1-scores, as well as dependable distinction between sample groups. Similar results were found for SVM and LASSO models, indicating that the chosen features are useful and that the previous use of RF for feature selection does not affect predictive performance.

RF feature importance

The RF model was used only to assess the relative significance of each chosen gene in addition to feature selection (Fig. 5). Prioritizing important genes for subsequent biological interpretation and experimental validation is aided by this evaluation. Crucially, independent classifiers (SVM and LASSO) were used for all performance evaluations to guarantee objective evaluation; the RF model was not utilized as a predictive model for confirmation.

Differential expression analysis

In the Supplementary File, sheet S15, a list of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) that were found to be different between the stress conditions and the control conditions is provided. There was a total of 2,493 DEGs found, with 1,961 genes being upregulated and 532 genes being downregulated when compared to the control.

GO enrichment analysis

GO enrichment analysis facilitates the interpretation of complex datasets through categorizing in significant terms and describes the genes functions. Based on the GO analysis, significant biological processes (P-Value < 0.1) including biological process, regulation of clathrin-dependent endocytosis, sulfur compound transport, purine-containing compound transmembrane transport, cytosine transport and synaptonemal complex organization were dominated. On the other hand, the significant molecular function classes were consisting of actin monomer binding and sulfur compound transmembrane transporter activity terms. Considering all genes that were identified by Boruta (103 genes), distinguished significant pathways were assigned by KEGG Enrichment Analysis. They included Metabolic pathways, Riboflavin metabolism, Peroxisome, Endocytosis, Biosynthesis of secondary metabolites and Galactose metabolism (Supplementary File, sheet S16).

Transcription factors identification

Transcription factors are critical for regulating the environmental stress response (ESR) in Saccharomyces cerevisiae. According to the results, five transcription factors including NRG1 (systematic name YDR043C), Mal13p (systematic name YGR288W), Gal4p (systematic name YPL248C), Sut2p (systematic name YPR009W) and Fyv5p (systematic name YCL058C) were identified among the highly correlated red module (Supplementary File, sheet S17). However, no transcription factors were found through the top-scored genes sorted by Bruta algorithm.

Discussion

This investigation used powerful correlation-based methods, including WGCNA and GS-MM, to identify the significant multi-stress-associated gene clusters related to Saccharomyces cerevisiae. The reliability of co-expression modules and core gene identification is supported by PCA clustering and ANOVA results, which demonstrate that biological causes predominate in the observed variation. As a result, there was no need for batch correction, and all downstream studies (WGCNA, Boruta, RF, SVM, LASSO, and enrichment analyses) show real biological variations between treatments.

Moreover, to specify the responsive crosstalk genes expressed under integrated stress conditions, including osmotic, oxidative, glucose, and ethanol stresses, the Boruta algorithm was used for feature selection. The relative relevance of the chosen genes was then only assessed using an RF model; all predictive modeling and performance evaluation were conducted using independent classifiers (SVM and LASSO). Regarding the comprehensive analysis, we identified an interesting, highly correlated red module with a correlation of -0.93 and a p-value of 9e-22 from trait-module correlation analysis and validation by GS-MM analysis showing a correlation of 0.95 and a p-value of 1.3e-188, respectively. In addition, the feature selection algorithms analysis identified 56 discriminative genes between the control and integrated stresses. Among the genes prioritized by the Boruta method, 103 genes were also ranked highly based on importance scores evaluated by the RF algorithm, confirming the critical role of these genes under stress conditions.

The comprehensive exploration of expression data related to several stressors, including glucose limitation, osmotic changes, and oxidative stress, along with ethanol accumulation, frequently occurred during the fermentation process, via reliable machine learning algorithms, remarkably providing insight in to the identification of the most determining genes in complex stress conditions. Among all the discriminative genes, specific genes are recognized with distinguished contributions to stress conditions. However, it is important to highlight that the functions of some of the distinguished genes are yet to be identified, even though they may be expressed under stress. In other words, among these 103 genes, some are genes with known biological functions, while other genes were not known or they were uncharacterized based on Yeastgenome database (https://frontend.qa.yeastgenome.org/). We explored the association between these not yet functionally characterized genes and the stresses. One of the informative tools is SPELL (Serial Pattern of Expression Levels Locator) from Yeastgenome database and the data is based on microarray gene expression analysis. We found that some of the uncharacterized genes without any standard name have been expressed under target stress. So, we consider them as important genes, although their function has yet to be identified. For instance, in the case of the YKL030W gene, although the function is unknown and uncertain to produce a functional protein according to current experimental data, it is reported to be down-regulated under oxidative stress via H2O2 exposure [24](https://www.yeastgenome.org/). The involvement of the functionally unknown identified genes in stress responses, according to experimental evidence, has been shown in the Supplementary File, sheet S18. The experimental evidence regarding unknown genes’ involvement in stress adaptation responses was obtained from SPELL (Serial Pattern of Expression Levels Locator), a query-based search engine allocated for extensive gene expression microarray studies, which includes 752 datasets and 15,475 related arrays [25]. It is worthy to check the involvement of the genes originated from RNA-seq analysis under stress conditions with another experimental expression analysis technique and explore if they corresponded. However, this finding needs more empirical evidence.

Moreover, the contribution of known identified genes has been confirmed based on different research reports, including PPN1 (systematic name YDR452W) which contributes to phosphate metabolism, hydrolyzes the terminal phosphate of the multi-phosphate chain under DNA replication stress, and generates a shorter polymer. This gene was identified as the top-ranked gene with the highest score. In Saccharomyces cerevisiae, PPN1 functions as a dual endo/exopolyphosphatase, essential for maintaining phosphate homeostasis and responding to stress. It operates as an endopolyphosphatase by cleaving internal bonds within long polyphosphate (polyP) chains, while also acting as an exopolyphosphatase by releasing inorganic phosphate from the terminal ends of these chains. This dual functionality is vital for regulating polyP levels, which are crucial for various cellular processes, such as phosphate storage and the response to stress [26–28].

Exposure to nutritional and osmotic stress conditions leads to the accumulation of PolyP in Escherichia coli, confirming the role of polyphosphates in stress responses (Ault-Riché et al., 1998). Moreover, as evidence for the contribution of PPN1 in the adaptation of yeast cells to stress conditions, the PPN1 null mutant showed elevated tolerance to peroxide stress through the accumulation of long polyP chains in the Saccharomyces cerevisiae cell [29]. PPN1’s activity is linked to stress resistance in yeast since overexpression of PPN1 can increase resistance to specific stresses like manganese and peroxide [30].

SAM3 (systematic name YPL274W), one of the attributed genes, is involved in transporting S-adenosyl-L-methionine and is essential for S-adenosylmethionine utilization [31]. Based on our analysis, this gene was identified to be associated with multi-stress conditions. Supporting this finding, SAM3 expression was reported to be regulated under high ethanol stress during fermentation in yeast [32]. These results imply the importance of methionine biosynthesis and transport pathways during ethanol production. The PLN1 (systematic name YKR046C) gene contributed to lipid droplet (LD) formation, while the expression studies of this gene show its contribution to respiratory growth. Besides, bioinformatics studies exploring protein networks have identified their role in ATP/ADP exchange [33–37]. The lipid droplet-mitochondria contact sites, such as Pln1 protein, play a role in FA transfer to mitochondria, enabling the production of energy-related molecules through the TCA cycle and oxidative phosphorylation. Besides, during oxidative stress, energy stress, and nutrient starvation, the LD plays a crucial role in cell maintenance [38]. In Saccharomyces cerevisiae, the protein Pln1, located on LD surfaces, is vital for tethering LDs to the vacuole during lipophagy (the degradation of LDs) under different stress conditions. Specifically, Pln1 protein aids in transporting LDs to the vacuole, which functions similarly to a lysosome, for their degradation. This mechanism is critical for yeast survival during nutrient deprivation and other stressors [39].

Icl1 (systematic name YER065C), also known as isocitrate lyase, is a crucial enzyme in the glyoxylate cycle that facilitates the conversion of isocitrate into succinate and glyoxylate [40]. The glyoxylate cycle represents a unique variation of the TCA cycle, enabling the use of two-carbon compounds when glucose is not available [41]. The cycle plays a crucial role in developing two-carbon compounds like ethanol. The glyoxylate pathway and its associated genes are implicated and may be induced in response to oxidative stress and glucose deprivation [42, 43]. The CTA1 gene in Saccharomyces cerevisiae (systematic name YDR256C) encodes Catalase A, a peroxisomal enzyme responsible for breaking down hydrogen peroxide [44–46]. Expression of the CTA1 gene is tightly regulated by environmental factors and is sensitive to glucose repression. Mutants lacking CTA1 exhibit reduced catalase activity and increased sensitivity to oxidative stress [47, 48]. In detail, according to different studies, the expression of this gene is suppressed by glucose and is induced by ethanol that is in accordance with the results of this study, for its identification as a key gene involved in glucose and ethanol stress regulation responses [48].

The AMS1 gene in Saccharomyces cerevisiae (systematic name YGL156W) encodes vacuolar alpha-mannosidase, a critical enzyme involved in the degradation of free oligosaccharides (fOS) within the vacuole [49]. This is involved in mannose and oligosaccharide catabolism, and its expression is upregulated during glucose and nitrogen starvation [49]. Its role in facilitating the breakdown of complex sugars during nutrient limitation confirms its contribution to stress condition responses. The GAC1 gene in Saccharomyces cerevisiae (systematic name YOR178C) encodes Gac1p, a regulatory subunit of a particular protein phosphatase (PP1) complex that regulates glycogen production [50, 51]. This gene is essential for inducing heat shock factor mediated transcription of stress-inducible genes such as CUP1 (which encodes metallothionein) during heat shock, oxidative stress, and glucose deprivation [52]. Its relationship with stress could be interpreted via glycogen, which serves as a carbohydrate reserve that protects against stress [53] demonstrated that the genes responsible for encoding glycogen metabolic enzymes (including GSY2, which is the target of Gac1p) possess STRE (stress-responsive element) sequences and are transcriptionally activated in response to heat, osmotic, and oxidative stress. Furthermore, Zhang et al. (2023) illustrated that Glc7p dephosphorylates histone H3T11 during glucose deprivation, thereby initiating the transcription of autophagy-related genes [54]. Based on this evidence, GAC1 gene, which is distinctively determined in the current analysis, is involved through various routes when yeast are subjected to stress conditions.

One of the discriminative gene predicted here is ADH2. The ADH2 gene (systematic name YMR303C) in Saccharomyces cerevisiae encodes alcohol dehydrogenase II (ADH II), an enzyme with key role in the oxidation of ethanol to acetaldehyde when ethanol is used as a carbon source and is expressed solely when fermentable sugars are depleted [55, 56]. Under stress, such as acetaldehyde accumulation or glucose depletion, the expression of ADH2 is derepressed to enhance the metabolism and detoxification of ethanol [57]. Accumulation of hydrogen peroxide and superoxide in the null mutants of ADH2 gene indicates the importance of this gene in preserving redox balance and providing resistance to oxidative stress [57].

The pathways enrichment analysis provides informative results related to interactions and pathways that are dominated by identified genes. According to the results, the genes of the key red module were enriched in different significant pathways. Metabolic pathways, peroxisomes, biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, and galactose metabolism identified here coincide with the previous research findings related to biochemical pathways that were enriched by common upregulated DEGs under multi-stress conditions in Saccharomyces cerevisiae. Since galactose metabolism is rigorously inhibited by glucose and is induced solely when glucose is exhausted, the enrichment of galactose metabolism genes reveals a mechanism essential for sustaining vigorous gene expression under environmental stress. The representation of the secondary metabolites biosynthesis pathway under stress conditions indicates the reprogramming of the gene expression and corresponding metabolic networks to synthesize protective chemicals in response to stresses. Therefore, the highlighted pathways identified in these studies shed lights on how the yeast adapts to environmental constraints.

Altogether, the unique analytical methods utilized in this study facilitate the understanding and interpretation of complicated available expression data related to multiple stress conditions in Saccharomyces cerevisiae. The integrated stress response analysis performed here describes the involvement of significant crosstalk genes, which exhibit outstanding correlation to the multifactorial stress condition. Complementary transcriptome data analysis by an efficient method based on machine learning approaches, including RF and Boruta algorithms, rather than a primary differential analysis method provides accurate results and addresses the limitations, including ambiguities and false positive results and interpretation deficiencies. Nevertheless, more empirical evidence could confirm the association of identified responsive genes with stress tolerance. We found PPN1, SAM3, and PLN1, Icl1, CTA1, AMS1, GAC1, and ADH2 as strong candidates with possible regulatory functions in yeast stress response by combining WGCNA module analysis with Boruta feature selection and further verifying important genes using LASSO regression and SVM models. The convergence of several computational methods supports their stress-responsive role, even though experimental validation is required to prove their functional value. The transcriptome data from a single industrial strain is one of the study’s limitations. It is impossible to completely exclude strain-specific regulatory variations, even though several of the identified core genes have been reported in stress-response studies of different S. cerevisiae strains. The generalizability of these results would be further confirmed by future studies that include several industrial strains under similar multi-stress circumstances.

Conclusion

This research investigates the transcriptional response of Saccharomyces cerevisiae to combined stress conditions by employing dependable analytical methods such as WGCNA and GS-MM tests, yielding comprehensible and reliable results.

Furthermore, the most important genes linked to the major discovered module were chosen using machine learning and feature selection techniques, such as Boruta. The RF model was simply used to assess the relative significance of these chosen genes, but the Boruta method successfully identified critical genes. To ensure a reliable and objective assessment, independent predictive modeling using SVM and LASSO was then carried out to evaluate classification performance. The findings indicate the presence of both identified and unidentified genes among the results, and their involvement in stress conditions was evidently confirmed by different experimental reports. The feature importance analysis underscored genes that exert a more significant impact on classification. These findings could serve as a foundation for further investigating Saccharomyces cerevisiae transcriptional analysis. Overall, this study presents an efficient computational approach for identifying the critical genes that contribute to stress response and provides insight on how the yeast regulates itself under multi stresses at the transcriptome level.
