Experiences of researchers in navigating applied research in the MENA region: an exploratory online cross-sectional survey
Authors: Catherine El Ashkar, Tanya El Khoury, Berthe Abi Zeid, Stephen McCall, Jihad Makhoul, Rima Nakkash
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundResearch productivity has increased in the Middle East North Africa (MENA) region despite a generally non-supportive research environment. This study builds on existing scholarship by exploring researchers’ self-assessed skills, capabilities and the barriers they encounter in conducting research and applying research ethics in their contexts.MethodsWe conducted an exploratory cross-sectional online survey with 152 regional research networks from any discipline in six countries across the MENA region. Inclusion criteria were researchers over the age of 18 and having been actively engaged in research involving human participants within the past five years. Descriptive and bivariate analyses, Chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests were used to examine statistical differences in respondents’ characteristics, knowledge and perceptions of research ethics by gender and education level.ResultsA total of 117 researchers completed the survey; 64% were female, 55% had doctoral degrees, 43% worked at private universities, and 48% conducted research in low- and middle- income countries. The main fields of research were public health (28%), social sciences (18%), medicine (16%), and nursing (16%). Findings were observed at the intrapersonal and research environment levels. At the intrapersonal level, most respondents reported the presence of institutional ethics review processes (91%) and familiarity with ethics procedures (92%), however, only 46% felt confident resolving ethical dilemmas independently, with 83% seeking advice or consulting guidelines. Many expressed the need for more training and mentorship despite the high percentage (68%) reporting having received ethics training. Several challenges were reported at the level of the research environment, including limited time to conduct research (77%), insufficient funding and support (70%), lack of collaboration opportunities (63%), and restricted freedom in disseminating research findings (32%).ConclusionAlthough the vast majority of researchers had received training in research and research ethics, and many were familiar with relevant research ethics guidelines in their field and institution, gaps remained – particularly at the institutional level. Findings highlight the need for systemic improvements in research capacity, ethics support, and supportive institutional environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12910-026-01511-6.

Introduction

Research in the Middle East North Africa (MENA) region has increased over the recent years making it essential for researchers to be well-versed in ethics principles and guidelines, particularly when required to obtain approval from ethical review boards or committees [1–3]. This growing volume of research across the region has raised concerns regarding the capacity of researchers and institutions to uphold ethical research standards [4].

Gaps in the researchers’ knowledge of ethics guidelines and limited understanding of the role of research ethics committees have been reported as common barriers to conducting ethically sound research [5–7]. These gaps point to potential misconduct [8–10] and the need for better training [11, 12]. In addition, inconsistencies in the availability of contextually sensitive ethics guidelines, as well as structures and mandates of the authorities establishing and implementing them also poses a challenge to ethical research conduct [13, 14]. Commonly examined systems to assess research ethics include: oversight mechanisms, ethics review committees, building researchers’ capacity, availability of ethical standards for research, and responsible research conduct policies [13]. All these are often influenced by limited funding and growing demands and competition in the academic field [15]. A lack of consensus on when ethics approval is acquired [16], along with ambiguous requirements, bureaucratic procedures, and limited expertise among research ethics committee (REC) members [17] can have dire consequences, such as researchers modifying or abandoning their studies [16].

Although there is a growing body of literature on research ethics in the MENA region, those have been conducted only in a few countries, with data mainly drawn from a population of health sciences students and researchers (largely medicine or nursing) [2–4, 18–21].

Our research intended to expand the existing body of knowledge on applied research ethics in the MENA region through capturing insights from researchers across a variety of disciplines, health sciences and beyond, and from six country sites distributed across the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region. We conducted an exploratory cross-sectional survey to explore the self-reported barriers and facilitators which researchers encounter in conducting ethical research, considering the individual skillsets and training, as well as the broader institutional environment in which they operate.

Materials and methods

Study design, setting and study population

A cross-sectional survey was conducted between May 22, 2023 and August 28, 2023 as part of a larger, multi-component, multi-stakeholder study involving research teams across six country sites (Morocco, Tunisia, Egypt, Jordan, Oman, and United Arab Emirates), which aimed at assessing practices, resources, structures, and gaps in the ethical conduct of research. The larger study employed a phased methodology that included a desk review, country-specific reports, empirical data collection through focus group discussions and semi-structured interviews, and this online survey [22]. The survey covered the breadth of the research process. It goes beyond researchers’ knowledge and perceptions of guidelines and research ethics review processes to explore researchers’ self-rated capabilities, their knowledge in applied research ethics involving human participants, and their perception of the availability of resources to support research endeavors at the institutional.

The survey population consisted of researchers from all disciplines across the surveyed regions, affiliated with public and private research institutions, universities, research networks, and the networks of the country teams. The eligibility criteria included being 18 years of age or older and having been actively engaged in research involving human participants within the past five years. The countries that served as main sites for data collection included Morocco, Tunisia, Egypt, Jordan, Oman, and United Arab Emirates. This study followed the STROBE reporting guidelines for cross-sectional studies [23].

Sampling frame

In the absence of a registry of researchers in the region to serve as a sampling frame, a probability-based sampling frame could not be established. Therefore, the study employed a non-probability, network-based sampling strategy. Local country teams developed lists of networks and research centers in their respective countries that conduct research with human participants, while the research team in Lebanon compiled a list of research networks based on the findings of the larger study by Makhoul et al. [22]. A total list of 152 networks including research centers, academic institutions and relevant governmental research entities was compiled. An a priori sample size calculation was not feasible due to the absence of a comprehensive sampling frame of researchers. Therefore, the final sample size was determined by the number of eligible researchers who chose to participate during the data collection period.

Survey administration

The survey was distributed via email to the networks who were in turn asked to disseminate among their members. The email included an invitation script with details on the purpose of the survey, the structure of the questionnaire, the IRB approval and assurance of confidentiality. Networks were asked to confirm whether they conduct research with human participants and whether they are interested in disseminating the survey to their members. The email also provided network members with the eligibility criteria, contact details of the research team, and the survey link. Emails were sent by the research coordinator at the American University of Beirut to the regional networks, the networks in Morocco, and some networks in the United Arab Emirates as per discretion of the relevant country teams. Country teams in the rest of the countries sent the emails themselves. In addition to the emails, some country sites shared the survey with individual researchers (Jordan) and with WhatsApp and Facebook groups (Egypt and United Arab Emirates). The team sent two reminders at two-week intervals.

Questionnaire design and modality

The research team designed the survey questionnaire by building on the findings from the preceding qualitative phase which included focus group discussions and in-depth interviews [22], and after reviewing the relevant literature. The literature referred to employed surveys on similar topics or provided conceptual insights into themes of interest [5–7, 12, 20, 24–33]. The draft survey questionnaire was then shared with the 12 country team members and collaborators from the Arab Council for the Social Sciences and Birzeit University for comments. Two team members used the Questionnaire Appraisal System QAS-99 to evaluate the survey, which aims at evaluating survey questions as it utilizes a step-wise approach to help identify problems at various levels of wording and structure [34]. We developed the questionnaire in English, and professional translators translated it into Arabic and French. Three faculty members at the American University of Beirut pre-tested the questionnaire in Arabic, while the English version was pretested virtually on Kobo toolbox with 2 faculty members. Two team members from the Morocco team and a research fellow at their faculty pretested the French version.

The self-administered online survey was developed using the software Kobo Toolbox. The link first opened to eligibility requirements: (1) engagement in human subject research in the past five years and (2) age above 18 years. If the eligibility criteria were satisfied, participants were taken to the informed consent form for review and approval electronically. Upon approval to consent, participants were directed to the survey questionnaire in which no personal identifiers were captured. The survey tool consisted of five sections, and included a series of 43 closed-ended questions regarding demographic characteristics, research methods and research ethics training and guidelines, resources available for conducting research, challenges to research, and future implication to improve the research experience. Different question formats were used: multiple-choice questions, yes or no questions, and five-point Likert scale questions (for ex. to a great extent, somewhat, to a very little extent, to no extent at all, don’t know).

Ethics statement

The study obtained approval from the American University of Beirut Institutional Review Board (SBS-2022-0383) on May 16, 2023. Approval for the survey was also obtained by the Oman team from the Oman College of Health Sciences (OCHS/REC/PROPSAL-APPROVED /15/2023), Health Studies and Research Approval Committee (HSRAC), Ministry of Health Oman (Proposal ID: MoH/CSR/23/27230), and Sultan Qaboos University, the Jordan University of Science and Technology (Ref.:10/142/2021, date 11.07.2021) and United Arab Emirates University (Ref Number ERS_2022_8469).

Morocco, Egypt, and Tunisia did not have IRBs for social sciences within the country and relied on the AUB IRB approval. All researchers underwent research ethics training as stipulated by the AUB IRB which included CITI training where available or a free online training by the Office of Research Integrity/ University of Montana (OREC Section One–Ethical Issues.)

Statistical analysis

The demographic characteristics of respondents, along with their knowledge and perceptions of research ethics, were described and presented by gender and education level (PhD vs. non-PhD) in the tables. For the descriptive analysis, frequencies and percentages were reported for all quantitative variables. For the bivariate analysis, Chi-square tests and Fisher’s exact tests were used to identify any statistically significant differences in respondents’ characteristics, knowledge, and perceptions of research ethics by gender and educational level. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All analyses were conducted used Stata/SE 16.

Results

Of the 117 respondents, 64% were female, 55% had a doctoral degree, 43% worked in a public university, and 20% worked at private universities, with very few working at independent, government, or university research centers. Although nearly 60% of respondents graduated from universities in high income countries, almost half conducted research in low and middle-income countries (48%) (Table 1). The countries in which the most research was conducted were Oman (27%), Jordan (27%), United Arab Emirates (20%), and Egypt (18%). The main fields of research were public health (28%), social sciences (18%), medicine (16%), and nursing (16%). The majority were employed full-time (87%); 26% were involved in research for more than four days a week (Table 1).

Demographic characteristics of the participants

Findings emerged around two main themes, the intrapersonal level- composed of the researchers’ own knowledge, training and motivation- and the overall research environment they work in. At the intrapersonal level, these include limitations in researchers’ knowledge and skills. The overall research environment included barriers both at the institutional and the national/regional level. At the institutional level, barriers include the extent of the availability of training and funding opportunities, institutional research agendas, and the functioning of institutional review boards. At the national/regional level, these barriers manifest in censorship, lack of uptake of research findings by policy makers, and irrelevant research agendas. Intrapersonal-level competence

Knowledge of ethical review processes and research methods

The majority of respondents (91%) worked within institutions that require an ethics review process for research involving human participants. Around 49% reported being extremely familiar with the ethics procedures governing research involving human subjects/participants, and 46% reported being extremely familiar with the research ethics procedures practiced at their institution (Table 2). When faced with an ethical dilemma or question about applied research ethics, 83% of respondents reported they sought advice elsewhere (Table 2), for example from IRB (56%), colleagues/peers (54%), senior researchers (45%), and supervising professors (43%) or referred to guidelines, books, or other resources (76%) (S1 and S2 Figure, Additional File 1).

In relation to research methods, 82% of the respondents were very satisfied or satisfied with their research skills. More than half believed they had the necessary research skills to effectively lead a study (61%), follow ethical research practices (73%), and interact ethically with research participants (73%). However, this confidence dropped to 46% when participants were asked about their ability to resolve ethical dilemmas in their study if they arise (Table 2).

Knowledge of ethical review processes and research methodology by education and gender

Training in research methodology and research ethics

The majority of respondents received training in research methodology (91%). For instance, 24% attended a workshop or conference and 37% a course; 52% attended during their postgraduate studies. Only a third of the participants did not receive any training in research ethics. Of those who received training in research ethics, 29% were very satisfied (S1 and S2 Tables, Additional File 1).

Motivation for conducting research

The majority of the respondents reported they conduct research to produce knowledge to improve their field of research (80%) or to help their communities (75%), for personal motivation (66%), and as part of their job description (54%). Notably, 72% reported no requirement to publish to remain employed or get promoted, 85% report no pressure for obtaining external funding and 81% report no pressure to compete with other researchers (Table 3).

Research environment

Funding sources

Notably, government/national research funds (48%) and university internal funding (40%) were the primary main sources of funding available. In contrast, fewer respondents reported receiving funding from the private sector (12%), NGOs (10%) or multilateral/bilateral agencies (14%). Over a third of the researchers reported funding their research out of pocket (34.2%) (S3 Table, Additional File 1).

Non-financial resources

Almost half reported lack of technical and support staff (50%), supportive infrastructure (49.6%), and access to peer review journals (48%). In addition, respondents reported inadequate time to conduct research (77%), lack of clear and accessible research guidelines (82%), absence of training for scientific research (67.5%), and poor access to research ethics committee information (67%), as well as absence of active research networks or collaborations (63%) and incentives for doing research (82.9%) (S4 Table, Additional File 1).

Concerns over applied research ethical practices

A majority of respondents agreed or strongly agreed that there were insufficient funds to support publishing their research (76%). Many also indicated that they had valuable research ideas but were unable to find appropriate funding (70%), and that there was inadequate funding for their specific field of research (68%). Additionally, 66% believed that policymakers in their country rarely used research findings to inform decision-making. Over half of the respondents felt that those responsible for allocating research funds did not view their field as a priority (56%), and that local researchers were often excluded from conducting research in their own contexts (52%). Nearly half of the respondents believed that the quality of research in their country was suboptimal (49%), and that societal trust in scientific research was generally low (48%) (Table 4).

Contextual challenges

Respondents reported that country context impacts the choice of research topics (59%), where some are rejected for political reasons (51%), dissemination of findings censored (51%), and research priorities defined at national level limit their ability to conduct research aligned with their own interests (67%) (Table 5). Respondents who conducted research with international collaborators in the last five years (50%) strongly agreed or agreed that results are only published in international journals written in a foreign language (62%), research agenda is chosen by collaborators (62%), and that the research budget is controlled by external international collaborators (57%). A third strongly agreed or agreed that ethics guidelines adopted by collaborators are not suitable for the local context (33%), international research agendas are not relevant to the local context (43%), research ethics training requested by collaborators is not available in respondents’ native language (26%), and local researchers are not acknowledged as authors (26%) (Table 5).

73% of the respondents reported that limited time for research impacts research integrity, followed by pressure to publish (47%) and to secure external funding (42%). In contrast, only 20% reported artificial intelligence has an impact on research integrity (Table 6). Disagreement about authorship (49%) followed by plagiarism (31%) and selective dropping of data from outlier cases (30%) were the most frequent misconducts occurring in the workplace according to the respondents (S3 figure, Additional File 1). Coercive recruitment of respondents (32%), collecting sensitive information (46%), engaging with vulnerable populations (40%), conflict of interest (25%), and difficulties with consent (31%) were dilemmas encountered by the respondents while conducting research (Table 6).

Implications for practice

Seventy nine percent of the respondents emphasized the need to link research outcomes to policy, 74% need more research collaboration, 67% more time to conduct research and 62% report need to improve the publics’ awareness about research. Almost half indicated the need for independent research centers not affiliated with the government and 67% wanted more funding for social sciences and 64% want more engagement with local communities to develop research relevant to their needs (S5 Table, Additional File 1).

Discussion

Barriers to ethical research conduct which were revealed from this research both at the individual and institutional levels align with observations from the framework proposed by Brall et al. [15], which highlights the interconnectedness of researchers and the broader network of external and institutional influences shaping ethical practices. While a large percentage of our respondents reported confidence in their research ethics practice, less than half reported an ability to resolve emerging ethical dilemmas. Studies in the region have reported that while ethics training has been shown to increase awareness of ethics principles, it did not predict higher scores on knowledge-based questions, indicating a possible discrepancy between the content of training programs and their long-term effectiveness [1, 2].

The conceptual understanding of research ethics and the building of competence are closely linked to accessible quality training and mentorship. Similar gaps access to training have been reported in other studies from the MENA region, highlighting a lack of training for both researchers and ethics committee members, even among those conducting research involving human participants [1–3, 18–20, 35–37]. This issue also seems to extend beyond the region where Marss et al. [38] found that 54% of PhD or recent graduates in their sample had not received research methods training beyond what their doctoral programs offered.

Most respondents who indicated an interest in further training in research methodology and ethics specifically requested refresher courses in bioethics suggesting that research ethics may be narrowly perceived as primarily relevant to medical research, or alternatively, that there may be limited understanding of the broader scope and content of bioethics training.

In addition, hands-on training was the most requested mode of instruction, which is expected in collectivist societies where face-to-face and synchronous learning are often preferred due to the emphasis on community and interpersonal relationships in these formats [39, 40]. Findings also bring out the importance of providing regular training and embedding research ethics within curricula and educational programs, rather than relying on ad hoc or one-off sessions [41]. Among those who reported having received training in research ethics, 29% were very satisfied and 56% satisfied with the training.

When applying research ethics, a high proportion of respondents reported seeking guidance when confronted with ethical dilemmas. While such help-seeking behavior may be consistent with practices observed in collectivist contexts, it also underscores the importance of having resources and well-trained mentors. There is thus a need for institutions to invest in ongoing, comprehensive training in research ethics across career stages to ensure that those providing guidance are adequately equipped. Mentorship plays a crucial role in teaching responsible research conduct, as institutions and faculty members are responsible for training new investigators, and through this mentorship, students implicitly learn how responsible conduct operates in practice and how to make ethical decisions [42].

With regards to barriers in the research environment, lack of readily available guidelines, infrastructure, research support time and incentives to conduct research were noted. These deficiencies might pose a challenge to ethical research conduct. For example, Drolet et al. (2022) in their research with researchers, research ethics committee members, and research ethics experts in Canada describe how “systemic injustices” at various levels of the research environment can impact ethical practice as well as researchers’ morale. These barriers they report encompass the micro-level such as the lack of infrastructure to store confidential data, lack research assistant mentorship, inadequate guidance from research ethics committees and excessive bureaucratization, and lengthy cumbersome informed consent forms that deter real voluntary approval from participants. Another level is the macro level which can include injustice in the allocation of grants and awards, questionable requirements from funding agencies, hindrances to the dissemination of research in native languages, and the preference towards quantitative versus qualitative research. The urge and push to produce research while functioning in a resource deficient environment can also adversely affect the integrity of the research [43].

Worth noting was that despite concerns that Institutional Review Boards cause delays, researchers were generally satisfied with their oversight. More than half disagreed that the IRB process involves unclear guidelines, excess submissions, or incompetent communication, and did not see that IRBs interfere with research methods. This contrasts with findings from earlier focus group discussions and previous research in the region, where IRBs were described as “policing” research, and concerns were raised about IRB members’ knowledge of diverse research methodologies and disciplines, particularly in the social sciences [44]. In fact, research involving IRB members themselves has shown variability in expertise and knowledge [45]. The reasons why respondents in this survey reported no difficulties with IRBs may be due to the negatively phrased survey question, a reluctance to report shortcomings of IRB members within their institutions, or recent improvements in IRB practices and processes that have not been yet documented.

Respondents’ concern over censorship at the level of their institutions and governments aligns with observed practices in the region. Previous research has discussed this mostly in relation to Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries where some have reported academic freedom might be compromised due to the inability to critique governments, religion or the country’s economic plans [46]. In Egypt for example, some research topics are restricted and all research must receive security clearance [47]. Research across the region highlights such interventions by the state and other actors in universities across the region, including practices, such as controlling university curricula and restricting attendance at conferences and workshops, thereby distancing academic scholarship from important scholarly forums [48, 49].

Limitations

Due to the absence of a comprehensive registry of researchers in the Arab world, we relied on non-random snowball sampling through the networks of country team members. As a result, the response rate could not be calculated, and the sample is not representative of all researchers in the region. Only 117 researchers responded despite the survey being shared with over 152 research networks and thousands of researchers. The use of an online survey and electronic consent process likely contributed to the low response rate, consistent with previous findings on the limitations of online data collection [50–52]. These factors, combined with the sensitive nature of the topic-ethical research conduct, may have discouraged participation, as highlighted in the qualitative phase of the study [22]. Research on questionable practice may also prompt social desirability bias or non-response [53]. In several participating countries, researchers described an environment of censorship and the need for security approvals, which may have deterred participation. Survey administration challenges may have also impacted the study response rate. The survey was conducted during summer months with multiple holidays (e.g., Ramadan, Eid) which may have reduced responsiveness to emails. Additionally, we were unable to follow up with country teams closely which led in some instances to deviations from the planned dissemination strategy, such as the use of WhatsApp instead of email. We were also unable to track how many researchers or institutions received the survey and actually disseminated the questionnaire to their members. Notably, due to the nature of the research teams’ networks, the majority of respondents were from the field of health sciences. Given these factors, the findings cannot be generalized to all researchers in the Arab region. Nonetheless, this study offers valuable descriptive insights and sets the stage for future research on the challenges of conducting ethical research in the region.

Implications for practice and research

Fostering ethical research practice requires developing and institutionalizing contextually appropriate ethics training that promotes problem-solving and critical thinking, and equips researchers to conduct responsible research and navigate real world ethical dilemmas. In addition, research ethics education should be continuous, with refreshers offered at multiple stages of a researcher’s journey. This helps counter the limitations of one-off trainings by using dialogical, interactive approaches that build competence in responsible research conduct [54].

Training should be tailored to the researchers’ disciplines and roles (ex. IRB members or research mentors) rather than relying on a one-size-fits-all approach that overlooks the nuanced ethical issues specific to each filed and function. This also helps ensure that future generations of researchers receive adequate mentorship and training from senior researchers who serve as primary reference points. This tailoring and contextualization also apply to research ethics guidelines. Institutions should strive to create a supportive research environment with adequate funding, infrastructure, and collaboration opportunities. This will allow researchers to conduct research that is meaningful to them and rooted in the concerns of their communities instead of relying on foreign funding and agendas for research opportunities.

Ethical research conduct extends beyond training and ethics oversight processes; it also requires a supportive research environment that ensures academic freedom in choosing research topics that are relevant, provides adequate funding and infrastructural support, and prioritizes the use of research findings to impact policy and practice.

Follow up research can explore the required country or regional elements to sustain supportive research environment and the necessary investments in resources at individual and institutional level to elevate research practices in the region. Our survey did not investigate the content of the trainings received by respondents. Thus, an assessment of available research ethics trainings is warranted to understand the content of these trainings and what can be improved to equip researchers with more critical thinking skills and the ability to engage in responsible conduct of research. Research should be done with the institutions themselves to understand the challenges they are facing in offering a supportive research environment. Moreover, policy makers should be engaged in the discussion to understand what can enhance their uptake of research results in their policies. Such research at the institutional and governmental level will provide a vantage point that will complement that of the researchers’ and allow for developing solutions that are feasible and practical and acceptable to all involved stakeholders.

Conclusion

Despite the inability to generalize findings to all researchers across the region, this exploratory cross-sectional survey with researchers across the MENA region offers valuable insights on the multiple barriers facing researchers both at the micro-level factors, such as the researchers’ own skillsets, and macro-level dimensions, including the institutional research environment. Expanding this research is warranted both with individual researchers and with research institutions to address generalizability, validate findings across the studied contexts as well as understand how supportive research environments can be achieved.
