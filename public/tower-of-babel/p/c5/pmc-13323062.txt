The role of endothelial colony forming cells in the treatment of limb ischaemia: challenges in clinical translation
Authors: Christine Ayu Lagonda, Caomhán J Lyons, Yaqiong Liu, Michael Creane, Timothy O’Brien
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEndothelial colony forming cells (ECFCs) are emerging as a promising therapeutic option for treating various vascular and degenerative disorders. These cells, also known as endothelial progenitor cells, which play a crucial role in angiogenesis, may be a promising therapeutic in the management of chronic limb-threatening ischaemia (CLTI). CLTI requires the regeneration of surrounding niche cells through vascular repair, which is often compromised by oxygen and nutrient deprivation. ECFCs have been extensively studied for their unique endothelial properties and their capacity for vascular repair. Numerous preclinical studies have been initiated to explore the safety and efficacy of ECFCs in treating CLTI. However, translating ECFC based therapies into clinical practice faces significant challenges, and there has been very limited translation to therapeutic studies in humans. Chief among these challenges is the inherent heterogeneity within ECFC populations, shaped by factors such as donor characteristics, tissue origin, and underlying health conditions. Despite this challenge, in vivo studies in animal models have laid the groundwork for potential clinical translation of ECFCs. These studies underscore the successful application of ECFCs in vascular repair and regeneration, emphasizing the specific parameters used in in vivo studies that contribute to a broader understanding of ECFCs and paving the way for their future clinical use. This report will review the current status of preclinical studies of the use of ECFCs in limb ischaemia and the challenges associated with human translation.

This article provides an updated perspective on recent laboratory and animal studies exploring how specific types of cells that contribute to blood vessel repair and growth are defined and understood. In particular, it highlights new findings relevant to chronic limb-threatening ischaemia, a severe condition caused by poor blood flow to the limbs. By clarifying how these specialized cells differ from earlier definitions, this review helps refine scientific understanding and may guide the development of more effective cell-based therapies for restoring circulation and preventing tissue damage in patients with advanced vascular disease.

Introduction

Chronic limb threatening ischaemia

CLTI is a severe condition caused by the narrowing and blockage of major arteries leading to a major adverse limb event (MALE). According to Global Vascular Guidelines, CLTI patients experience persistent rest pain, ischaemic ulcers, and gangrene, which significantly impacts their quality of life.1 Factors such as smoking, diabetes mellitus, advanced age, and chronic renal dysfunction contribute to CLTI progression, of which diabetes and smoking are factors that exacerbating this disease. Around 5%-10% of PAD patients were affected with CLTI and present a critical medical concern due to their significant morbidity and mortality rates.2 The incidence ranges from 220 to 3500 cases per million annually, with a mortality rate of 50% within 5 years of diagnosis.2,3 Inadequate blood supply is associated with an ankle-brachial index (ABI) <0.4, ankle pressure <50-70 mm Hg, toe pressure <30-50 mm Hg, or a transcutaneous partial pressure of oxygen (TcPO2) <20-40 mm Hg.3

Recent CLTI treatments aim to enhance limb perfusion, primarily through arterial revascularization, either by angioplasty or stenting and bypass surgeries.3 Novel endovascular technologies include intravascular lithotripsy and the Tack endovascular stent, or surgical techniques like the distal vein patch technique aim to offering improved options for treating calcified plaques and tibial artery dissections or where autologous vein availability is limited.4 Some patients are ineligible for surgical revascularization due to underlying comorbidities or the poor state of distal blood vessels, which then may require repeat interventions and, in severe cases, amputation may be necessary.5

Various approaches using different cell and gene therapies have been explored as alternative treatments and ECFCs have emerged as a promising cell therapy for treating CLTI for their capabilities on enhancing blood flow restoration by promoting collateral revascularization and reperfusion.6 In this article, we deep delved on ECFCs dysfunction in ischaemic condition and the importance of ECFCs treatment for CLTI patients as well as the parameters of in vivo that can be considered for clinical translation of ECFCs.

Endothelial colony forming cells

ECFCs, first defined by Yoder et al.,7 are characterized by their ability to form colonies, have robust clonal proliferative capacity, and differentiate into endothelial cells. ECFCs are identified by their expression of CD31, CD105, and CD146 markers, while lacking hematopoietic markers CD45 and CD14.8 Functionally, ECFCs exhibit the ability to form vessels in vitro and in vivo and possess additional features such as Ac-LDL uptake and lectin binding.9

ECFCs were first thought to originate from bone marrow.10 However, single cell sequencing has since revealed that ECFCs originate from the endothelial lining, distinguishing them from the hematopoietic lineage.11 ECFCs are distinct from other pro-angiogenic cells, early endothelial progenitor cells (eEPCs), in their origin, cell morphology, and functional properties. eEPCs, which are coming from the hematopoietic lineage, express the leukocyte marker CD45 along with endothelial markers, display a fibroblastic morphology, have limited proliferative capacity, and primarily function through paracrine signaling and thus do not directly form vascular tubes in vitro or in vivo. ECFCs, on the other hand, showcase robust proliferative and vessel-forming abilities essential for vascular repair and display a cobblestone cell morphology with an absence of CD45 and CD14 expression.12

In healthy tissue, ECFCs exhibit high expression of key genes involved in angiogenesis (EGF, FGF, TIMP1, TIMP2, Ang2, Tie2, and VEGFR), which are crucial for growth and their interaction with surrounding cells.13 Upon ECFC administration into the pathological environment, ECFCs directly integrate into damaged vasculature and replace injured endothelial cells. Their strong proliferative capacity and strong responsiveness to pro-angiogenic stimuli, including VEGF, PDGF, and FGF enable ECFCs to contribute to tissue repair in an ischaemic environment (detailed mechanistic effects of ECFCs are reviewed in detail.14,15 Another critical pathway influencing ECFC vascular repair is mediated by endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS). eNOS catalyzes the production of nitric oxide (NO) that plays a vital role in various physiological processes, including regulating vascular tone, promoting vasodilation, and maintaining vascular integrity and homeostasis.16 Several studies have reported that ECFCs from peripheral blood or cord blood exhibit comparable levels of eNOS expression and NO production to HUVECs, indicating their similarity to mature endothelial cells from neonatal sources, while PB-ECFCs have been reported to have higher eNOS levels compared to adult mature endothelial cells like HAECs.17–19

Impaired functionality of ECFCs in ischaemia

Under pathological conditions, such as diabetes and ischaemia, ECFCs lose their functional integrity.20,21 During acute ischaemia, limb skeletal muscle may initiate angiogenesis as a natural angiogenic response due to the reduction of blood flow. This response is characterized by the upregulation of VEGF, VEGFR-2, and HIF-1 transcription and secretion. FGF-2 is also rapidly produced by inflammatory cells in ischaemic tissue shortly after the onset of ischaemia.22 Additionally, SDF-1 and VEGF play significant roles in recruiting ECFCs to the site of injury, supporting angiogenesis at the ischaemic site.23

On the other hand, chronic ischaemia presents a different challenge. The longer exposure to hypoxic conditions can trigger cell senescence and reduced tubulogenesis and migration capacity of ECFCs.24 In vitro studies investigating the functionality of ECFCs under hypoxic conditions have revealed notable dysregulation in gene expression, including the upregulation of ANKRD37, AK3L1, and PLAC8 and downregulation of ELMOD1 and LYVE1. PLAC8 gene was found to negatively regulate NOX4-mediated angiogenic signaling.25 NOX4, the most highly expressed isoform of NADPH oxidase, is essential for regulating ECFC migration and vascular network formation. In vivo studies showed that NOX4 knockdown in ECFCs reduced revascularization in ischaemic hindlimbs, whereas NOX4 overexpression enhanced reperfusion and vascular remodeling. This suggests that targeting NOX4 could improve the therapeutic potential of ECFCs in treating ischaemic diseases.26

The impaired functionality of ECFCs in ischaemic environments is further compounded by reduced activity of eNOS. Reduced or dysfunctional eNOS activity is associated with several pathological conditions, including atherosclerosis, hypertension, and pulmonary hypertension.27 In ischaemic tissue, the production of NO by eNOS decreases when oxygen levels are low (Figure 1). During ischaemia-reperfusion injury, oxidative stress increases, leading to excessive levels of reactive oxygen species, which causes eNOS uncoupling. This uncoupling prevents eNOS from producing NO, amplifying oxidative stress in the tissue.27 Deficiency of several essential cofactors for NO production, including heme, flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD), flavin mononucleotide (FMN), and tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4), has been reported to increase eNOS uncoupling that affects the wound healing capacity and angiogenic capacity of ECFCs.28 GTP cyclohydrolase 1 (GCH1) is an important protein in BH4 biosynthesis and reported to be downregulated by hyperglycaemia and ischaemia as well.28,29

On the other hand, ECFCs maintain their survival in the ischaemic condition through the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway, which plays a key role in increasing the secretion of VEGF.30 Under hypoxic conditions, the STAT3 signaling pathway is activated in ECFCs, which increases their proliferative capacity. In animal studies, the transplantation of hypoxic preconditioned ECFCs into mice with hindlimb ischaemia demonstrated significantly increased STAT3 activation in the ischaemic tissues compared to non-preconditioned ECFCs. This activation was accompanied by elevated levels of cell proliferation and tissue repair. Conversely, inhibition of the STAT3 pathway in hypoxic preconditioned ECFCs led to decreased proliferation in the ischaemic tissue, underscoring the critical role of the STAT3 pathway in mediating the therapeutic benefits observed.31

These findings underscore the complex interplay of molecular pathways, gene regulation, and environmental factors in determining ECFC functionality in ischaemic conditions. Targeting these pathways and addressing dysfunction in ECFCs offers promising strategies for advancing therapeutic interventions to improve vascular repair and treat ischaemic diseases effectively.

Autologous vs allogeneic approaches using ECFC therapy

Advances in understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying ECFC dysfunction in ischaemic conditions have brought significant attention to therapeutic strategies leveraging ECFCs. Two main approaches, autologous and allogeneic ECFCs, are being explored, each with distinct advantages and disadvantages. The autologous ECFC transplantation approach eliminates the risk of immune rejection. However, this approach can be challenging in cases where the patient has underlying conditions, such as diabetes or cardiovascular disease, which compromises ECFC functionality.20,21 In such instances, genetic or epigenetic modifications may be necessary to restore the cells’ reparative abilities.32 In contrast, allogeneic therapy involves using ECFCs from healthy donors. This approach is particularly beneficial in situations requiring immediate intervention or when the patient’s ECFCs are irreparably impaired. However, the use of allogeneic cells carries risks of immune rejection and potential graft-versus-host disease.33

Despite advancements, both approaches face challenges. Addressing the reduced angiogenic potential of dysfunctional autologous ECFCs is critical. This often involves tackling issues like impaired nitric oxide (NO) production, a common feature of endothelial dysfunction in ischaemic conditions. The regulation of endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) through pathways like PI3K/AKT and JAK2/STAT3 is a promising focus of research.34 On the other hand, strategies such as transient immunosuppression, hypoimmunogenic cell development, or cell licensing to enhance TNFR expression to reduce immune activation are investigated for allogeneic ECFCs.35–37 It has also been reported that allogeneic cord blood-derived ECFCs (UC-ECFCs) have been shown to have low immunogenicity properties, allowing them to integrate into vascular networks without triggering significant immune responses which makes it promising as an allogeneic ECFCs source, as demonstrated in in vivo xenogeneic transplantation in immunocompetent mice.38

Important aspects of in vivo preclinical studies using ECFCs

Influence of ECFC sources of origin

The source of ECFCs significantly influences their proliferative capacity and clinical outcomes (summary of Table S1).39 Although ECFCs were initially believed to originate from bone marrow,40 subsequent studies demonstrated that BM-MNCs don’t give rise to ECFC.41 Consequently, studies have predominantly focused on peripheral blood (PB) as the primary source for ECFC due to its accessibility and minimally invasive collection process.13,42,43 In addition, autologous sources of ECFCs, including white adipose tissue and other vascularized tissue, have been studied to be viable reservoirs that yield highly proliferative and vasculogenic ECFC.44,45 Umbilical cord blood has also emerged as ECFCs allogeneic sources, as abundant ECFCs can be obtained.46 ECFCs proliferative capacity was also heavily influenced by their sources, PB-ECFCs usually senesce at around passage 6, while UC-ECFCs remain proliferative up to passage 16, with senescence impacting negatively the cell functionality.47 Essaadi et al. found that percentages of PB-ECFCs that undergo senescence are more compared to UC-ECFCs at the same passage. This is particularly evident in HLI animal models, where UC-ECFCs show superior blood perfusion recovery.42

Impact of disease and aging on ECFC

The impact of disease and aging on ECFC functionality presents significant challenges on therapeutic benefits. Smadja et al. reported that the number of ECFCs isolated from the peripheral blood of CLTI patients is lower when compared to aged-matched healthy controls, whereas Lyons et al. reported the opposite trend. This variability highlights the heterogeneity among CLTI patients populations, for it may be attributed to differences in disease severity, inflammatory status, and comorbidity burden.48,49 In addition to CLTI, other pathological conditions such as pulmonary hypertension, chronic pulmonary obstructive disease, and smoking may also result in a complete absence of ECFC colonies “zero colonies” during isolation, despite the number of isolated cells being comparable to healthy aged-match controls.39,49

Moreover, obtaining high-quality ECFCs from patients with certain diseases, such as diabetes, can be particularly difficult. Elevated miR-139-5p levels in ECFCs from diabetic patients may impact the ECFC quality, hindering angiogenic function.50 Additionally, another study showed that diabetic patients excessive ROS production downregulates Nrf2 (stress-responsive transcription factor) and reducing ECFC survival and angiogenic capacity.43

Aging also affects the angiogenic ability of ECFCs. In the ISTH guidelines, ECFCs derived from young adult donors (20-40 years old) when compared with those from old adult donors (40-72 years old), the frequency of ECFCs emergence is observed to be higher in the latter group. Cord blood is the source that results in higher number of isolated ECFCs, followed by young donor aged 0-10 years old.39 Mauge et al. observed differences in ECFCs collected from young donors (20-30 years old) compared to those from older donors (60-70 years old) following ischaemic treatment in the forearm. Their findings revealed that although the quality of ECFCs from older donors was comparable to that of younger donors, structural changes in the tubules were evident in the ECFCs derived from older individuals.51 Another study by Wang et al. demonstrated reduced ECFC Skp2 levels during replication-induced senescence, which mimics the aging cells accompanied by increased p16INK4a and decreased eNOS levels, indicating impaired angiogenic function.52 Moreover, the downregulation of senescence markers like Panx1 restores ECFCs’ angiogenic potential and senescence status, suggesting therapeutic avenues for age-related vascular conditions.53 Additionally, antioxidant compounds such as fucoidan and resveratrol has been reported to reverse senescence in human and murine ECFCs, offering a potential means to mitigate cellular aging.54,55

Another potential source of ECFCs are derived from human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs) which has shown promise in overcoming the challenges posed by disease and aging. A study compared hiPSC-ECFCs from PAD patients and UC-ECFCs in improving blood flow and salvaging ischaemic limbs in nude mice. Results showed that NRP1+ hiPSC-ECFCs functioned similarly to UC-ECFCs, with both maintaining their endothelial phenotype up to passage 18 despite senescence. Neuropilin-1 (NRP-1) modulation emerged as a critical factor for improving hiPSC-ECFC generation. Enhancing NRP-1 activity increased KDR activation, which boosted angiogenic potential. Notably, treatment with Fc-NRP-1 significantly expanded late-passage hiPSC-ECFCs and reduced senescence markers, demonstrating NRP-1's role in improving proliferative potential and survival.56

The influence of delivery route on in vivo studies

The route of delivery for ECFCs is a critical factor influencing their therapeutic efficacy. Several studies have evaluated different administration methods to optimize outcomes. Gao et al. demonstrated long-term ECFC engraftment when locally injected intramuscularly. GFP/luciferin-labeled ECFCs in adult NSG mice showed sustained engraftment over 24 weeks post intramuscular injection, maintaining higher signal intensity compared to the Mesenchymal Stromal Cells (MSC)-only group. Similarly, in neonatal NSG mice, the ECFC-only group maintained elevated signal intensity throughout 26 weeks. Histological analysis confirmed successful engraftment of GFP-positive ECFCs in injected muscle tissues, with maintained endothelial marker expression indicating sustained functional activity in vessel formation.57 The optimized DiR labeling protocol tested with ECFCs administered either intramuscularly or intravenously to mice with HLI revealed localized DiR signal primarily around the injection area for IM administration and broader distribution to the abdomen, liver, and spleen for IV administration.6 There are a small number of other studies that have reported performing HLI study and injecting ECFC retro-orbitally.58,59 Retro-orbital delivery of ECFCs was expected to enter the animal’s bloodstream. One study showed that despite systemic entry, the homing or localization of exogenously injected ECFCs was in the limb of mice.23 It was also reported that post injection retro orbitally, cells were remained in the retro orbital plexus, and were detected in ischaemic muscle after 10 days.60 These studies suggest that intramuscular administration of ECFCs is the most effective approach (Figure S1B) for ensuring that ECFC localization in the ischaemic region of the limb, resulting in a higher rate of blood flow recovery as demonstrated by laser Doppler.

Dosage of cell therapy and time of observation

In numerous studies (referenced in Table S1), researchers typically injected between 5 × 105 to 106 cells and monitored the outcomes at specific time points. These studies mainly focus on assessing blood perfusion using laser Doppler and comparing ratios between ischaemic and non-ischaemic legs, as well as examining muscle histology and ECFC incorporation into existing arteries. We compared the numbers of successful blood perfusion recoveries in relation to the number of injected cells (Figure S1A) and concluded that higher dose of cells (106 ECFCs) resulted in greater recovery success. Researchers typically choose observation days such as pre-surgery (day 0) or before femoral artery ligation surgery. Different studies report various findings at different time points post-surgery, ranging from 24 hours to 4 weeks. Some find that ECFC showed increased blood perfusion at day 3, day 7, day 14, day 21, or day 28. However, not all studies observed significant differences between ECFC-treated groups and control groups receiving vehicle (buffer or saline) treatments.13,61 Patel et al. reported that at day 7 the UC-ECFC and PL-ECFC caused more rapid and superior recovery in limb perfusion compared to saline control on days 7, 14, and 21.62 These findings suggest that ECFC therapy improves blood perfusion in a time-dependent manner, with notable benefits as early as day 3, peaking around days 7 and 14, and tapering by day 21 or 28. While many studies demonstrated enhanced perfusion ratios in the ischaemic leg compared to controls, variability in outcomes exists, potentially due to differences in study design, dosage, and observation methods. Conversely, other studies found no statistically significant differences between ECFC and vehicle-treated groups, highlighting the need for further research to standardize protocols and identify factors that influence therapeutic efficacy, for instance, route of administration, effective dose range, timing of administration relative to disease onset, and dosing schedule.63

Challenges in clinical translation and proposed solutions

To date, no clinical trials using ECFCs alone have been conducted. However, one clinical trial study reported on a combination therapy of ECFC and MSC reporting accelerated healing in 12 patients with refractory foot ulcers.64 Nonetheless, ECFC pre-clinical in vivo studies showed promise for progression to clinical application. Progressing ECFCs to clinical trials faces significant challenges, primarily due to the donor variability, heterogeneity and cells identification. The quality of ECFCs and ease of manufacturing can be influenced by donor’s age and health condition. Moreover, since ECFCs are a heterogeneous cell population, their isolation and purity for clinical use should be considered.15 Scalability is another critical hurdle, as the production of ECFCs needs to be efficiently scaled up to generate sufficient quantities for therapeutic purposes while complying with GMP standards.10,65 To address these challenges, ISTH has implemented standardized protocols and guidelines for obtaining ECFCs, including factors like donor age, blood sources, culture medium, isolation and culture duration, quantification and characterization.47

To address the challenge of cell heterogeneity (Figure 2), exploration of advanced technologies such as single-cell sequencing is also valuable to establish standard marker gene panels and identify distinctive markers of ECFCs with high proliferative and angiogenic potential. Additionally, single-cell and spatial transcriptomics can uncover ECFC crosstalk with other cell types, further enhancing the therapeutic potential of ECFCs as well as to establish the markers for regulatory purpose.66

Effective delivery and integration of ECFCs into target tissues are also critical for their success, as this involves ensuring cell survival post-transplantation, homing to the desired location, and integrating into the existing vasculature to promote repair and regeneration.67 In addition to the advancements in ECFC integration, research has also explored various methods for delivering ECFCs to target tissues. One promising approach involves the use of hydrogels, which provide a supportive matrix for ECFCs and enhance their survival and function after transplantation.68 While few hydrogel experiments have been conducted to date, the results have been encouraging, demonstrating improved cell retention, viability, and therapeutic outcomes.69

Finally, multiple complementary strategies, including metabolic preconditioning with acidic conditions or glycomimetic compounds,58,70 genetic and epigenetic modulation,65 combination with other cell therapies (MSCs),71 have been shown to significantly enhance ECFC survival, angiogenic potency, and therapeutic efficacy. These approaches effectively restore dysfunctional autologous ECFCs and enable immunotolerant allogeneic applications.

Utilizing these complementary approaches may enhance the potential for developing more effective and safe ECFC-based therapies, offering new hope for treating various vascular diseases and advancing regenerative medicine.
