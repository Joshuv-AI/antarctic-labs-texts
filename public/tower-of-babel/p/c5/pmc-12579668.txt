Association of CPAP Prescription and Pneumonia Among Patients with Obstructive Sleep Apnea in the United States: A Retrospective Database Analysis
Authors: Maria J Tort, Birol Emir, Jennifer L Nguyen, Deepa Malhotra, Chai Hyun Kim, Vincenza T Snow
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionContinuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) is the first-line therapy for obstructive sleep apnea (OSA). We sought to understand whether CPAP prescription among adults diagnosed with OSA was associated with a higher likelihood of pneumonia diagnosis and inpatient hospitalization for pneumonia after adjusting for patient demographic characteristics and comorbidities.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study used de-identified data from the Optum Clinical Electronic Health Record (EHR) database for the time period January 2012 to December 2019. The study population comprised adults with an OSA diagnosis with and without CPAP prescription after the initial diagnosis. The primary outcome of interest was a pneumonia diagnosis in the first year following OSA diagnosis. Propensity score matching was used to balance the cohorts. Logistic regression was used to estimate the odds of developing pneumonia.ResultsThere were 328,340 patients with an OSA diagnosis and evidence of CPAP prescription and 964,199 patients with an OSA diagnosis without evidence of CPAP prescription. After 1:1 propensity score matching, 326,145 were included in each cohort. The risk of pneumonia in any setting and in the inpatient setting was higher among patients with CPAP prescription, odds ratio (OR), 1.06 (95% CI: 1.03, 1.09) and 1.24 (95% CI, 1.20, 1.29), respectively. The odds of developing pneumonia increased in any setting when CPAP was initiated on the date of OSA diagnosis (OR = 1.68, 95% CI: 1.62–1.72) among all patients, for patients aged 65 + years (OR = 1.68, 95% CI: 1.47, 1.92), patients with obesity (OR = 1.59, 95% CI: 1.47, 1.72), and patients with a history of pneumonia (OR = 1.68, 95% CI: 1.41, 2.00).ConclusionThis study indicates that among patients with OSA, CPAP initiation was associated with incident pneumonia, particularly inpatient pneumonia hospitalizations. Longer CPAP prescription, older age, obesity, and previous history of pneumonia further increased the risk of incident pneumonia associated with CPAP prescription.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12325-025-03343-z.Keywords: Continuous positive airway pressure, Electronic health records, Obstructive sleep apnea, Pneumonia, Real-world evidence

Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a relatively common chronic sleep disorder in the US, with an estimated prevalence ranging from 9 to 38% in the general adult population [1]. OSA is characterized by recurrent episodes of breathing cessation during sleep due to the narrowing and obstruction of the upper airway [2]. Moderate-to-severe OSA is typically diagnosed in patients in their 40s, with an estimated prevalence of 3.1% [3, 4]. Approximately 80% of all OSA cases remain undiagnosed, and untreated OSA has been associated with a greater risk of cardiovascular disease, metabolic disorders, depression, motor vehicle accidents, impaired work productivity, and diminished quality of life for both the patient and family members [5]. Healthcare spending to treat undiagnosed patients with OSA has been estimated to cost between $1950 and $3899 more per patient, per year than for patients without OSA [6].

Globally, approximately 45% of patients with OSA have moderate-to-severe OSA, for which the gold standard first-line therapy is continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) [7, 8]. CPAP devices deliver a stream of air to the upper airway through a nasal mask and act as a splint to prevent upper airway collapse [9]. In addition to the sleep-related benefits, regular use of CPAP therapy may also reduce the risk of premature death, cardiovascular disease, obesity, and depression [9–12]. However, CPAP devices can increase the likelihood of aspirating liquids from the throat into the lungs, which may transfer viruses and bacteria into the lower respiratory tract, increasing the risk of pneumonia [13].

Limited evidence is available in assessing the association between CPAP therapy and pneumonia risk [13, 14]. Sleep apnea has been found to be an independent risk factor for pneumonia, and patients with OSA on CPAP therapy have been found to be at an increased risk for upper airway infection, but the risk of pneumonia among patients with OSA on CPAP has yet to be explored or quantified [13–15]. Therefore, the aim of this observational study was to examine the hypothesis of whether evidence of CPAP therapy increased the risk of incident pneumonia diagnosis and hospitalization among adults diagnosed with OSA.

Methods

This was a retrospective cohort study utilizing Optum Clinical Electronic Health Record (EHR) de-identified database for the time period January 2012 to December 2019. Permission was obtained to access this database. Ethics approval was not applicable. This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Patients were included in the study if they were at least 18 years of age and diagnosed with OSA between January 1, 2012, and December 31, 2019. For inclusion in the analysis, patients had to be associated with an integrated delivery network and had at least 360 days of continuous enrollment both before and after the first OSA diagnosis (i.e., index date). A Subgroup of patients with OSA was evaluated, requiring 3 years of continuous enrollment following the index date.

Patients were excluded from the study if they had evidence of CPAP prescription before the index date, experienced pneumonia in the 30 days prior to and on the index date, or developed pneumonia between the OSA diagnosis and first CPAP prescription. Patients with evidence of pneumonia during the period of 360 days –31 days prior to the index date were included in the study to assess if the relationship between CPAP prescription and pneumonia among adults diagnosed with OSA was affected by a past history of pneumonia (Fig. 1).

Data Collection

Demographic and patient characteristic data such as age, gender, region, year of index date, past history of pneumonia, body mass index (BMI), and pneumococcal vaccination history, were collected from the databases. Behaviors and comorbidities of interest were those identified by the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP) to be associated with an increased risk of pneumococcal disease. The comorbidities were identified by the presence of ≥ 1 diagnosis, medication, or procedure code. Some of these comorbidities include immunocompromising conditions such as chronic renal failure, functional or anatomic asplenia, chronic comorbidities such as diabetes, behavioral factors, and risk behaviors such as alcoholism and smoking. (Table 1 includes the full list of comorbidities, and the corresponding ICD-9-CM and ICD-10-CM codes can be found in Table S1 in the supplementary material). In addition to analyzing comorbidities individually, overall comorbidity measures were assessed using the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), which assigns weights to each comorbidity based on the burden of disease and calculates an overall score by summing the individual weights of all comorbidities for each patient [16]. Patients were followed for up to 3 years after their first OSA diagnosis, at intervals of 1 and 3 years, depending on the length of post-index EHR activity observed.

Measurements

Evidence of CPAP therapy was identified by the presence of at least one of the following: ICD-9-CM diagnosis code V46.8, ICD-10-CM diagnosis code Z99.89, CPT procedure code 94,660, or a clinical term associated with CPAP identified through Optum’s proprietary natural language processing (NLP) algorithm.

The primary outcome was pneumonia diagnosis and inpatient hospitalization for pneumonia, within 1 year of the date of the first OSA diagnosis (index date). The first secondary outcome extended the follow-up period to 3 years and assessed pneumonia diagnosis for the following settings: (1) inpatient, (2) emergency room, (3) outpatient, (4) observation patient, (5) nursing home/skilled nursing facility, swing bed, and (6) all other (e.g., telephone/online visits, laboratory or imaging visits, inpatient psychiatric/rehabilitation visits). The other secondary outcome described and compared the characteristics between patients with OSA who did and did not experience pneumonia prior to CPAP prescription. Lastly, the exploratory outcome assessed whether the association of CPAP prescription and pneumonia among adults diagnosed with OSA varied by age, BMI, and history of pneumonia.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using SAS version 9.4 software (SAS institute, Cary, NC, USA). Continuous variables, age, BMI, and CCI scores, were summarized using mean (standard deviations) and median (interquartile range). Categorical variables, gender, region, year of index date, BMI, presence of comorbidities and those associated with pneumococcal infection, evidence of pneumonia diagnosis prior to the date of the first OSA diagnosis, and pneumococcal vaccination history, were presented using counts and percentages.

For patients with evidence of CPAP prescription, the data were stratified according to the amount of time exposed to CPAP: patients without CPAP prescription were classified as unexposed, whereas patients with CPAP exposure were divided into four groups based on quartiles according to the time between the date of first OSA diagnosis and first evidence of CPAP prescription: Q1 (0–0 days, i.e., on index date), Q2 (1–22 days), Q3 (23–100 days), and Q4 (101–359 days). These exposure strata, which examined CPAP use ordinally, were included as a covariate for the regression models. Pearson’s chi-square tests were used, as appropriate, for comparison between categorical variables, and t-tests were used for the comparison of continuous variables, such as age and comorbidity scores. In all analyses, results were considered significant if p < 0.05.

The probability that a patient would receive CPAP was assessed with multivariable logistic regression to create a propensity score, and then patients with OSA with and without CPAP prescriptions were matched 1:1 using propensity score matching. For the propensity model, matching was exact for gender, age, and past history of pneumonia. The covariates in the model included age, gender, region, year of index date, and CCI. Pneumonia outcomes of interest and patient characteristics were assessed between patients with OSA with and without CPAP prescription after propensity score matching. Logistic regression models adjusting for covariates such as age, gender, region, year of index date, BMI, past history of pneumonia, and CCI were constructed to determine the risk of pneumonia outcomes.

Time-based exposure definitions (time between OSA diagnosis and date of first evidence of CPAP prescription) may be affected by immortal time bias. Therefore, a sensitivity analysis was used to account for potential selection bias. Pneumonia outcomes between patients with and without evidence of CPAP prescription were assessed after both Kaplan-Meier and multivariable adjustment using Cox proportional hazards regression. The multivariate analysis classified person-time as unexposed (cumulative time between OSA diagnosis with no evidence of CPAP prescription) and exposed (first evidence of CPAP prescription and onward).

Results

Study Population Characteristics

Overall, a total of 2,628,541 patients were identified with OSA during the study period from January 2012 to December 2019 (Fig. 2). A total of 1,342,225 patients with OSA were included in the study after excluding patients < 18 years, those without the required enrollment (360 days pre- and post-index), and those with pneumonia diagnosis in the 30 days pre-index. After further exclusion of patients with evidence of CPAP in the year prior to index, a total of 1,303,099 patients were included. Of the study population, 328,340 patients presented with evidence of CPAP prescription and 964,199 patients without evidence of CPAP prescription and were included in the study. For the 3-year follow-up, 705,519 total patients were included: 168,201 with CPAP and 537,318 patients without evidence of CPAP. Patients with OSA and evidence of CPAP prescription did not differ regarding age, sex, CCI, comorbidities, and past history of pneumonia at baseline compared to patients with OSA without evidence of a CPAP prescription (Table 1). However, standardized differences in the distribution of BMI were statistically significant between the two cohorts (p > 0.1). Patients using CPAP were more likely to fall under the BMI category of obese compared to those not on CPAP (CPAP: 59.6%, no CPAP: 52.0%) (Table 1). The top three most common comorbid conditions associated with moderate/high risk for pneumococcal infection were diabetes (20.9% on CPAP, 19.7% no CPAP), coronary artery disease (CPAP: 12.0%, no CPAP: 11.7%), and asthma (CPAP: 9.7%, no CPAP: 8.7%) (Table 1).

A Cox proportional hazards regression model was used to model CPAP as a time-dependent factor to account for immortal time bias in the cohort exposed to CPAP. After multivariate adjustment for other confounders including age, gender, BMI, region, index year, history of pneumonia, and CCI, CPAP was independently associated with an 18% (any setting) and 32% (inpatient hospitalization) increased hazard of developing pneumonia compared with those with no CPAP (any setting HR = 1.18, 95% CI: 1.15, 1.20; inpatient HR = 1.32, 95% CI: 1.28, 1.36). Immortal time bias did not seem to be of concern since the magnitude and direction of the association measured by the hazard ratios of Cox regression were directionally consistent with that of the odds ratios measured by the logistic regression analysis (any setting: OR = 1.06, 95% CI: 1.04, 1.09; inpatient: OR = 1.24, 95% CI: 1.20, 1.28).

Association Between CPAP Exposure and Pneumonia Diagnosis (Main Analysis)

Following matching 1:1 on age, gender, history of pneumonia, region, index year, and CCI, 326,145 patients with and without CPAP were included in the analysis. In the logistic regression analysis, CPAP prescription was found to be independently associated with pneumonia diagnosis (any setting: OR = 1.06, 95% CI: 1.03, 1.09; inpatient: OR = 1.24, 95% CI: 1.20, 1.28) for up to 1-year follow-up. However, when stratified by the amount of exposure time to CPAP, only Q1 exposure time (0 to 0 days, i.e., on index day) was found to be significantly associated with an increased risk of pneumonia diagnosis (any setting: OR = 1.68, 95% CI: 1.62, 1.73; inpatient: OR = 2.19, 95% CI: 2.11, 2.28). There was a marginal association between Q2 (1 to 22 days) exposure time to CPAP and pneumonia diagnosis (any setting: OR = 1.03, 95% CI: 0.99, 1.07; inpatient: OR = 1.03, 95% CI: 0.97, 1.08), but this result was not statistically significant (Fig. 3). An increase in time between index date (OSA diagnosis) and evidence of CPAP prescription to Q3 (23 to 100 days) and Q4 (101 to 359 days) was found to be protective for pneumonia (OR < 1) for both any setting and inpatient hospitalizations (Fig. 3). Compared to patients who were followed for up to 1 year, patients followed for up to 3 years had a lower odds of pneumonia diagnosis for any setting or inpatient hospitalizations for Q1 exposure to CPAP but slightly increased odds for Q2, Q3, and Q4 exposure to CPAP (Figure S1, Table S2, supplementary material).

Association Between CPAP Exposure and Pneumonia Diagnosis by Effect Modifier

Subgroup analysis was performed to assess whether age, BMI, and pneumonia history modified the association between exposure to CPAP and pneumonia diagnosis and is presented in Fig. 3. As with the primary analysis, the association between CPAP prescription and pneumonia diagnosis was attenuated with increasing time between index date and evidence of CPAP prescription, and the odds ratio for pneumonia was higher for the inpatient hospitalization setting than for any setting (Fig. 3). Patients aged 65 + years with Q1 exposure to CPAP had the highest odds of pneumonia (any setting, OR = 1.68, 95% CI: 1.47, 1.92) (Fig. 3). Patients in the underweight or obese BMI category, with Q1 exposure to CPAP, had the greatest odds for pneumonia (any setting and underweight, OR = 1.56, 95% CI: 0.90, 2.73; any setting and obese, OR = 1.59, 95% CI: 1.48, 1.72), although the odds for those in the underweight BMI category were not statistically significant for patients diagnosed with pneumonia in any setting (Fig. 3). These odds increased for inpatient hospitalizations (underweight, OR = 2.63, 95% CI: 1.43, 4.83; obese, OR = 2.15, 95% CI: 1.95, 2.36), in which the odds ratio for the underweight category of BMI was the strongest effect modifier for the association between Q1 exposure to CPAP and pneumonia diagnosis (Fig. 3).

For the association between Q1 exposure to CPAP and pneumonia diagnosis for any setting, the strongest effect modifier was pneumonia history, in which patients with Q1 exposure to CPAP and with a past history of pneumonia were at a greater odds of pneumonia diagnosis (any setting, OR = 1.68, 95% CI: 1.41, 2.00; inpatient, OR = 2.33, 95% CI: 1.90, 2.86) compared to those without any previous history of pneumonia (Fig. 3). Compared to patients followed for up to 1 year, patients followed for up to 3 years had attenuated odds of pneumonia among all age groups, BMI, and pneumonia history subgroups for Q1 exposure to CPAP across both any setting and inpatient hospitalizations (Figure S1, supplementary material).

Discussion

This retrospective cohort study, using electronic health records data from 1,342,225 individuals diagnosed with OSA, found that CPAP prescription was associated with pneumonia in an exposure-dependent manner. Furthermore, CPAP prescription was independently associated with an increased risk of pneumonia diagnosis, with the greatest risk of inpatient hospitalization noted for patients with Q1 exposure to CPAP. Interestingly, an increase in time between index date (OSA diagnosis) and evidence of CPAP prescription was found to be protective for pneumonia for both any setting and inpatient hospitalizations, suggesting that delayed CPAP prescription following OSA diagnosis reduces odds of developing pneumonia after adjusting for confounding (Fig. 3). This may suggest that patients who started CPAP therapy later might have had different health profiles or circumstances that contributed to a lower risk of developing pneumonia.

These findings provide valuable insights into the timing and implications of CPAP therapy initiation. The observed delay between prescribing CPAP in Q3 (23–100 days) and Q4 (101–359 days) may be attributed to several factors. One potential reason is the severity of sleep apnea; patients with milder forms of OSA may not perceive an immediate need to start CPAP therapy. Additionally, logistical or administrative delays, such as insurance approvals or obtaining the necessary equipment, could contribute to this delay.

Among inpatient hospitalizations, the underweight and obese BMI subgroups had the largest influence on the association between Q1 exposure to CPAP and pneumonia diagnosis (Fig. 3). Previously published studies have shown a linear correlation between obesity and OSA, as well as indications that obesity is independently associated with an increased risk of acquiring pneumonia [17, 18]. Poor adherence to CPAP in patients with OSA has been associated with a BMI increase [19]. Although this study could not account for CPAP adherence, it is possible that some patients with OSA with poor adherence to CPAP are more likely to be obese, which can translate into an increased risk for pneumonia. Among inpatient hospitalizations, age Subgroups 18–44 and 45–64 years had the largest influence on the association between Q1 exposure to CPAP and pneumonia diagnosis, but ages 65 + had a slightly lower risk of pneumonia (Fig. 3). The prevalence of OSA has been reported to increase progressively with age but comes to a plateau after age 60; therefore, the higher risk of pneumonia among age groups 18–44 and 45–64 may be attributed to the higher prevalence of patients with OSA on CPAP for these age groups [2].

The precise pathophysiologic pathway that explains the causal pathway between upper airway obstruction in patients with OSA and pneumonia is not fully understood. Patients with OSA have been reported to be at a greater risk for pulmonary aspiration of pharyngeal contents during sleep, which could render patients more susceptible to pathogens and predispose them to the development of pneumonia [13, 20]. Patients with OSA have also been reported to have an impaired swallowing reflex, enhancing the likelihood of aspiration of regurgitated material into the airways [21]. Patients with OSA are chronically REM sleep-deprived, and sleep loss has been documented to alter immune system function, which could potentially make them more susceptible to infections and pneumonia events [22]. Daytime hypercapnia, diagnosed in 11% to 43% of patients with OSA, has been linked to suppression of lung neutrophil function, which is also important in defending against bacterial pneumonia [23]. Therefore, it is likely that the higher risk of pneumonia among patients with OSA on CPAP is multifactorial and may be attributed to higher susceptibility to pulmonary aspiration and impaired immunity [13, 24].

Evidence of CPAP prescription and increased risk of respiratory infections, such as pneumonia, is limited in the current literature. Mechanical ventilation has been found to be associated with increased risk for ventilator-associated pneumonia [25, 26]. One study found that patients using CPAP therapy either with or without heated humidifiers were at an increased risk of upper airway infections and pneumonia compared to those who received non-CPAP therapy, although the sample size was very small (only 1 pneumonia event observed vs. 0 events in controls) [14]. On the other hand, one study suggests that infants on CPAP equipment colonized with pathogenic microorganisms are not at a higher risk of pneumonia [27]. Another study found that patients receiving CPAP during postoperative care were also found to have lower rates of pneumonia compared to those receiving standard oxygen therapy [28]. However, these studies were not designed specifically to assess the effect of CPAP on pneumonia risk for patients with OSA, and some report pneumonia rate as a secondary outcome, being underpowered to detect significant differences in the pneumonia outcomes. The adjusted risk of pneumonia among patients with OSA on CPAP that we observed (inpatient: HR = 1.32, 95% CI: 1.28, 1.36) was similar to the results by Su et al., who reported the adjusted risk of pneumonia among patients with OSA who were indicated for needing CPAP treatment (HR = 1.32, 95% CI: 1.12, 1.55) [13]. Su et al. used the need for CPAP treatment as a surrogate marker for OSA severity, whereas this study assessed pneumonia incidence among patients with OSA with a confirmed CPAP therapy.

To our knowledge, this is the largest study to date that has assessed pneumonia risk for adult patients with OSA on CPAP. However, this study is not without limitations. This is an observational study, and even though the study cohorts were well matched, there may be unmeasured and residual confounding. There may also be heterogeneity in the study population of patients with OSA that was not accounted for. For example, because OSA severity data were not available from the Optum EHR database, we were not able to stratify the data by OSA severity level, despite severe OSA being associated with a higher risk of pneumonia than OSA of milder severity [13]. Since the propensity score matching performed does not completely eliminate biases, future research incorporating more comprehensive data on OSA severity and symptomatology will help to better understand these relationships. Furthermore, measures of CPAP compliance, which have been shown to vary substantially by age and sex [29], could not be assessed. While there is no established threshold for adherence to CPAP, one clinical benchmark of CPAP adherence that has been shown to improve health outcomes is 4 h/night [30]. Future studies should incorporate CPAP adherence metrics to support a better understanding of the relationship between CPAP use and the risk of pneumonia.

Moreover, the use of real-world data means that certain variables, such as detailed adherence metrics for CPAP use and the severity of OSA, were not available, potentially impacting the robustness of our findings. The lack of comprehensive vaccination records led us to use past history of pneumonia as a covariate instead of pneumococcal vaccination status. While pneumococcal vaccination is relevant to pneumonia risk, past history of pneumonia provides a more immediate and relevant measure of susceptibility to respiratory infections based on medical history. Vaccination status, while important, may not fully capture the patient's risk of pneumonia, as it does not account for factors such as vaccine efficacy, adherence to vaccination schedules, and other underlying health conditions. Additionally, real-world data often lack comprehensive and accurate records of vaccination status, which can introduce bias and limit the reliability of the analysis.

Furthermore, regarding the stratification of CPAP initiation into quartiles, quartile cut-offs were chosen based on the distribution of the data to ensure a balanced representation of different initiation times and were not based on clinical or empirical rationale, which may limit the interpretability and/or clinical utility of our results. Additionally, it is important to note that the division into quartiles could potentially result in the diagnosis of OSA and the dispensing of CPAP occurring on the same day. In some cases, patients could undergo a split-night sleep study, where the first half of the night is used to diagnose OSA, and if the diagnosis is confirmed, CPAP therapy is initiated during the second half of the night. Such a scenario would allow for both the diagnosis and the initiation of CPAP therapy to occur on the same day.

Variability in CPAP adherence, as well as in the type and care of CPAP machines used by patients with OSA, may have also impacted study findings. For example, some patients with OSA may have used CPAP machines with a humidifier and/or have improper cleaning, which can potentially increase a patient’s susceptibility to pneumonia [13, 24, 31]. CPAP accessory orders were not included in the analysis, and their inclusion in future evaluations may provide additional insights. Finally, this study relies partially on natural language processing to identify CPAP treatment. Therefore, it is possible that there was some misclassification of CPAP status due to potential errors in the NLP algorithm. However, we also used standard diagnosis and procedure codes to identify CPAP use, which may limit this bias.

Conclusions

This observational study suggests that patients with OSA and evidence of CPAP prescription have a greater risk for pneumonia diagnosis under any setting, particularly for inpatient hospitalizations. When exposure level to CPAP was stratified by time, the risk of pneumonia was even greater, particularly for incident exposures, especially when treatment starts the same day as OSA diagnosis exposure level to CPAP. Age group, BMI, and pneumonia history modified the association between CPAP prescription and pneumonia diagnosis.
