Graph transformer Q-network for collaborative governance and decentralized decision-making in multi-intersection networks
Authors: He Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCoordinated traffic signal control along urban corridors is critical for reducing stop-and-go waves, vehicle delay, and queue spillback, yet remains challenging under large-scale topology, partial observability, and stochastic demand. Existing MARL controllers often rely on dense or static inter-intersection communication and locally scoped rewards, which weakens corridor-level credit assignment and leads to attention diffusion and unstable coordination as network scale grows. We propose the Graph Transformer Q-Network (GTQN) that learns a state-dependent sparse interaction graph for coordination and couples it with centralized governance during training so that decentralized controllers can reliably form and sustain corridor progression. Concretely, GTQN (i) learns which upstream/downstream peers are causally influential at each decision step via a two-stage mechanism (discrete peer gating followed by soft relevance weighting), yielding sparse, interpretable coordination without attention diffusion; (ii) uses a unified graph-transformer backbone to jointly encode network topology and temporal history so delayed upstream-to-downstream effects that determine green-wave formation are represented within the value function; and (iii) introduces a Collaborative Governance Graph (CTDE) trained with a progression-oriented two-level reward that couples junction efficiency with a corridor hindrance penalty, improving credit assignment and learning stability. Experiments on synthetic and real-world urban networks, including a 100-intersection Chengdu network, show that GTQN consistently improves progression quality, achieving the lowest Average Number of Stops (e.g., ANS on SQ1 and on SQ3) while reducing waiting time and queues and increasing throughput. Ablations confirm that learned sparsity, unified spatiotemporal encoding, and centralized governance are each necessary for scalable and progression-aware coordination. Robustness study demonstrates that GTQN maintains acceptable performance under moderate communication perturbations such as message dropout and delays. Introduction

Urban traffic signal control remains a central operational component of intelligent transportation systems because signal timing directly influences queue growth, corridor travel time, fuel consumption, and spillback risk. In corridor settings, local optimization at each junction is typically insufficient: once a traffic convoy is released from an entry intersection, downstream signals must coordinate to preserve progression and reduce residual stopping; otherwise, stop-and-go waves re-emerge even when each intersection appears locally efficient1,2. This coordination problem is further complicated by partial observability and stochastic demand, as individual junctions only observe limited sensor snapshots and the impact of upstream control decisions propagates with time-varying travel times and dispersion3.

Recent reinforcement learning (RL) approaches have demonstrated strong potential for adaptive signal control by learning policies directly from traffic measurements and simulation feedback4,5. However, multi-intersection control is inherently multi-agent: each controller modifies a shared network state, creating non-stationarity under independent learning and making coordination difficult to stabilize6,7. Cooperative MARL frameworks address this via structured sharing, centralized critics, or value factorization, but practical deployments face two persistent issues. First, dense communication or global aggregation can introduce redundant or irrelevant information, particularly in corridor networks where influence decays with graph distance; this can dilute attention weights and destabilize training8,9. Second, corridor-level objectives such as progression require explicit mechanisms to connect local phase decisions to downstream outcomes across multiple time steps, which stresses temporal representation learning and credit assignment10,11. Our proposed GTQN learns a state-dependent sparse coordination structure (which intersections should exchange information at each step and with what strength) and couples it with centralized governance for corridor-level credit assignment, enabling decentralized controllers to sustain progression under partial observability. Unlike prior MARL signal control studies that (i) use dense/global communication, (ii) rely on static neighborhood rules (e.g., fixed distance-based neighbors), or (iii) optimize mainly local delay/queue rewards, GTQN explicitly learns a dynamic interaction graph through discrete peer gating and soft relevance weighting and trains this coordination with a governance value signal driven by a progression-oriented hindrance penalty. The remaining architectural choices (graph-transformer encoding, CTDE training, and reward structure) are introduced to operationalize this core idea under delayed upstream-downstream effects and large-scale topology.

Large-scale multi-agent systems inherently rely on structured interactions among neighboring decision units, yet such interactions introduce significant challenges in stabilizing and coordinating the learning dynamics of collaborative networks12. In particular, effective policy learning requires that locally optimized behaviors at individual agents evolve coherently toward a consistent network-level objective, rather than inducing conflicting or oscillatory dynamics. Achieving this balance between local autonomy and global coordination remains a central difficulty in multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL). Earlier studies primarily focused on improving learning efficiency in loosely coupled multi-agent systems by employing limited knowledge-sharing strategies and abstracted game representations13. These approaches typically assume that agents operate largely independently, with only minimal or infrequent information exchange. While such assumptions simplify learning and reduce communication overhead but restricts applicability in domains characterized by strong inter-agent coupling and frequent interaction, such as corridor-based traffic signal control where upstream decisions directly shape downstream performance.

More recent MARL frameworks introduce game abstraction mechanisms with predefined interaction rules, often based on static criteria such as physical distance or fixed neighborhood definitions14,15. Although these rules provide a structured means of limiting communication scope, they remain insufficient for capturing the dynamic, context-dependent nature of inter-agent influence. In practice, the relevance of an agent’s information depends not only on topological proximity but also on transient traffic states, temporal alignment, and evolving coordination objectives. Consequently, purely rule-based abstractions fail to fully characterize interaction dependencies in tightly coupled multi-agent networks. This limitation motivates learning-driven interaction modeling, where dependency structures and knowledge exchange pathways16 are inferred directly from experience. Concurrently, Transformer-based architectures have demonstrated superior capacity in modeling long-range temporal dependencies through self-attention, enabling parallelizable and context-aware sequence modeling17. In traffic signal control, temporal Transformers have begun to replace recurrent units to improve training efficiency and policy robustness18,19. Yet, most existing frameworks treat spatial and temporal modeling separately, missing opportunities for joint optimization of graph-structured relational reasoning and historical state evolution.

Graph representations provide a principled way to encode road-network topology and inter-junction dependencies by modeling intersections as nodes and physical/communication links as edges20,21. Graph neural networks (GNNs) and graph attention mechanisms enable message passing that fuses local observations with neighbor context22, but conventional attention can still remain effectively dense (assigning small non-zero weights to many neighbors) and may over-smooth node representations when stacked deeply23. In parallel, Transformer encoders have become attractive for traffic control because self-attention can model long-range temporal dependencies and supports parallel training compared to recurrent encoders17. These trends motivate architectures that jointly (i) encode network structure through graph operations, (ii) encode temporal history through Transformer-style self-attention, and (iii) maintain sparsity so that only materially influential neighbors contribute to coordination.

This paper proposes a Graph Transformer Q-Network for Collaborative Governance and Decentralized Decision-Making in Multi-Intersection Network Control (GTQN) as shown in Fig. 1. We formulate the corridor as a Distributed Interaction Graph (DIG) and cast the control process as a partially observable stochastic game in which each intersection selects from a finite phase-duration control set. The proposed architecture follows a two-tier design: (i) Distributed Junction Controllers (DJC) execute locally using an encoded local state and a sparse coordination context, while (ii) a Collaborative Governance Graph (CGG) supports centralized learning by integrating node attributes and topology into a graph-conditioned action-value function. In the DJC, we introduce a two-stage sparse attention mechanism (discrete peer selection followed by soft relevance weighting) to prevent attention diffusion and reduce redundant communication, consistent with recent evidence that selective neighborhood interaction improves scalability in large traffic networks9 and with emerging work emphasizing neighborhood-conditioned coordination and graph-structured MARL for multi-intersection control15,24. In the CGG, we train a centralized value-based controller with experience replay and mini-batch temporal-difference updates, while preserving decentralized execution consistent with CTDE principles11,25.

Problem formulation of the proposed Graph Transformer Q-Network for collaborative governance in multi-intersection traffic networks. The traffic network is modeled as a graph in which intersections are represented as nodes and their spatial-topological relationships as edges. Local traffic observations obtained from distributed sensors are first encoded into node features and processed through a graph neural network to capture inter-intersection dependencies. The resulting node embeddings are then enriched via transformer layers with self-attention, positional, and temporal encoding to learn spatiotemporal contextual representations. During training, a centralized governance module (CGG) aggregates graph-conditioned representations to compute a stabilizing action-value signal under CTDE; during execution, each intersection selects its action decentrally using its local encoding and the learned sparse coordination context.

GTQN is designed around four failure modes that commonly prevent scalable corridor coordination in MARL-based signal control. (i) Attention diffusion under dense communication: in large networks, aggregating messages from many intersections yields redundant and weakly relevant context, which spreads attention mass across marginal peers and destabilizes learning. We therefore hypothesize that state-dependent sparsification is necessary to focus coordination capacity on causally influential upstream/downstream peers. (ii) Delayed upstream-to-downstream effects: progression depends on travel-time-lagged interactions (e.g., upstream discharge influences downstream arrivals several decision steps later), which cannot be captured reliably by purely spatial encoders or short-horizon summaries. We thus hypothesize that joint spatiotemporal representation learning is required to model convoy propagation and phase alignment at scale. (iii) Credit assignment under decentralized learning: independent or weakly coupled learners experience non-stationarity and cannot attribute corridor-level outcomes (e.g., residual stops downstream) to coordinated action sequences. We therefore introduce centralized governance during training under CTDE to provide a stabilizing value signal while keeping execution decentralized. (iv) Objective mismatch between local efficiency and progression: minimizing local delay/queue alone does not consistently produce green-wave behavior and can even fragment platoons. We thus employ a two-level reward that couples local efficiency with a corridor hindrance term targeting residual stopping.

Literature review

Graph-based modeling of traffic signal control

The formalization of urban traffic signal control as a graph-structured problem has gained increasing traction in recent years, driven by the ability of graph representations to capture both spatial topology and dynamic interdependencies among road network components. In this paradigm, intersections are typically modeled as nodes, while road segments or communication links between adjacent intersections constitute edges20,21. This abstraction naturally aligns with real-world traffic corridors, linear sequences of signalized junctions, where upstream-downstream coordination is essential for forming uninterrupted progression bands1,2. Alternatively, in grid-like urban networks, dense interconnectivity demands more sophisticated message-passing mechanisms to avoid coordination bottlenecks and signaling conflicts26.

A critical aspect of graph-based modeling lies in the design of node and edge features that encapsulate actionable traffic states. Node-level descriptors commonly include queue lengths, cumulative vehicle delays, current phase status, and remaining green time8,23. More advanced models incorporate traffic convoy level information, such as estimated time of arrival at downstream intersections, convoy size, and speed deviation from nominal flow27. Edge attributes may encode inter-junction spacing, historical correlation of traffic flows, or communication latency that supports context-aware message routing26. Such feature engineering is pivotal for graph neural networks (GNNs) to learn meaningful relational inductive biases that reflect the causal structure of traffic dynamics.

Message passing over the interaction graph serves as the primary mechanism for propagating local observations into network-wide coordination signals. Traditional approaches leverage graph convolutional networks (GCNs) or graph attention networks (GATs) to aggregate neighbor states with uniform or attention-weighted schemes22,26. However, these models often assume dense or fully connected communication, which can introduce noise and scalability issues in large-scale networks8. Recent work has addressed this limitation through sparse communication protocols, where only top-K neighbors or those exceeding a learned relevance threshold participate in message exchange28,29. These methods enhance interpretability and reduce redundant information flow particularly beneficial in corridor settings where influence decays rapidly with graph distance. Our proposed DIG builds directly upon this line of research. Specifically, we formalize each intersection as a node equipped with multi-type descriptors (junction, flow, and road entities) and define edges based on physical adjacency and communication capability. The graph-mediated knowledge exchange in DIG is not merely aggregative but selective: a two-stage attention mechanism performs discrete peer gating followed by soft relevance weighting, ensuring that only upstream/downstream intersections with material impact on corridor progression contribute to local decision-making. This design mitigates the over-smoothing and attention diffusion issues observed in prior GNN-based controllers23,29, while remaining compatible with decentralized execution. Our model enables the graph transformer to reason about spatiotemporal coherence across the progression corridor, a capability absent in earlier static graph formulations.

The integration of graph structure with reinforcement learning has been further advanced by frameworks that combine centralized training with decentralized execution (CTDE)30, allowing global coordination to emerge from localized policies10,11. Recent studies have emphasized the role of topology-aware sparsification and temporal encoding in improving sample efficiency and transferability of graph-based traffic controllers4,31, both of which are explicitly incorporated into our architecture through sparse peer selection and position-enriched transformer encoders.

Multi-agent reinforcement learning for multi-intersection control

Multi-intersection traffic signal control constitutes a canonical multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) problem, wherein each signalized junction operates as an autonomous decision-making entity interacting with a shared, dynamic environment. Early approaches treated intersections as independent learners, each optimizing its local policy without explicit coordination32,33. While simple to implement and scalable, independent Q-learning suffers from environment non-stationarity, since each agent’s policy evolves concurrently, the perceived transition dynamics become non-Markovian from any single agent’s perspective, undermining convergence guarantees6,7.

To address this, cooperative MARL frameworks have been introduced that explicitly model inter-agent dependencies, either through shared value functions10, centralized critics34, or communication protocols35. In traffic control, such cooperation is essential: uncoordinated phase decisions at adjacent intersections often result in stop-and-go waves, excessive queuing, and degraded corridor throughput3,36. However, cooperative MARL introduces its own challenges. First, the credit assignment problem that determines each agent’s contribution to a global reward becomes acute when only sparse, delayed network-level feedback is available37. Second, scalability remains a concern: joint action spaces grow exponentially with the number of intersections, rendering naive joint-policy search infeasible beyond small networks38.

To mitigate these issues, recent studies adopt structured action spaces that balance expressiveness with tractability. The most common formulation treats each intersection’s action as a discrete selection from a finite set of phase-duration pairs. This event-based control paradigm, where decisions are triggered upon phase completion rather than at fixed intervals better aligns with real-world signal operations and reduces unnecessary switching8,35. Alternative formulations include continuous green split optimization within fixed cycles5, or hybrid schemes that jointly optimize cycle length and phase sequence39. However, these often require additional constraints to ensure safety and feasibility (e.g., minimum green times, pedestrian clearance), increasing the complexity of the action space.

Cooperative MARL formulation grounded in the partially observable stochastic game (POSG) model where each intersection functions as an autonomous decision unit with its own local observation space and discrete control set, consistent with event-based, phase-duration actuation. While execution is decentralized each controller selects its own action based on locally encoded state and coordination context, training leverages centralized information through the CGG. This follows the centralized training with decentralized execution paradigm10,11, which alleviates non-stationarity during learning while preserving scalability at inference time. Recent advances have further refined MARL for traffic control through topology-conditioned action factorization29 and adaptive horizon-aware credit assignment10. These works emphasize the importance of aligning agent interaction ranges with physical traffic propagation dynamics. Additionally, the use of graph transformers as the backbone for both local and global policies enables the simultaneous modeling of spatial dependencies (through graph structure) and temporal history (through positional encoding), a combination shown to improve robustness in stochastic, partially observable settings40. Our architecture thus integrates these cutting-edge insights while maintaining compatibility with real-world signal controller constraints, such as finite phase sets and minimum green durations.

Temporal representation learning for signal control

Accurate modeling of temporal dynamics is essential for effective traffic signal control, as intersection states evolve due to vehicle arrivals, departures, phase transitions, and traffic convoy propagation. Early deep reinforcement learning approaches for traffic control relied heavily on recurrent neural networks such as Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM)41 and Gated Recurrent Units (GRUs)42 to encode historical traffic measurements into fixed-length hidden states. While capable of capturing short- to medium-term dependencies, recurrent architectures suffer from sequential computation bottlenecks, limiting parallelization and increasing training time as sequence lengths grow8. Moreover, vanishing gradients and limited memory retention often impair their ability to model long-horizon patterns, such as traffic convoy formation over multiple signal cycles or demand surges during peak periods27.

To overcome these limitations, alternative temporal modeling strategies have emerged. Temporal convolutional networks (TCNs) use dilated causal convolutions to capture multi-scale temporal dependencies with parallelizable operations20. However, TCNs require careful design of kernel sizes and dilation factors to cover relevant horizons, and their fixed receptive fields may struggle with variable-length traffic phenomena. In contrast, Transformer-based architectures17 leverage self-attention mechanisms to dynamically weigh the relevance of all historical time steps, enabling direct modeling of long-range dependencies without recurrent state propagation. This property is important in traffic control, where the influence of a past event, such as a green phase at an upstream intersection, may manifest several cycles later at a downstream junction43. Self-attention also facilitates parallel training across time steps, significantly improving computational efficiency and scalability in multi-interaction settings where each agent must process its own temporal history. Recent studies44 confirm that transformers outperform LSTMs in both convergence speed and final policy performance for adaptive signal control, especially when combined with graph-based spatial modeling18,40.

A key enabler of Transformer effectiveness is the use of temporal encoding strategies that inject chronological or cyclical structure into otherwise permutation-invariant inputs. Positional encoding has been adapted in traffic applications to represent discrete decision steps or time-of-day context8. More sophisticated approaches incorporate temporal embeddings that encode absolute or relative timestamps, traffic cycle phases, or even periodic features (e.g., rush hour indicators)45. Additionally, history windows46 are often used to bound input sequences to a recent, task-relevant interval (e.g., the past 5-10 decision steps), balancing memory requirements with sufficient temporal context. Model19 demonstrates that hybrid spatio-temporal Transformers with adaptive history windows achieve state-of-the-art performance in corridor signal control by dynamically adjusting the temporal context based on traffic volatility. Similarly, GTQN framework40 show that integrating temporal self-attention with graph message passing improves robustness under partial observability and stochastic arrivals. Our model incorporates these insights by jointly learning temporal and relational representations through a unified Graph Transformer Q-Network, where node embeddings are enriched with both spatial neighborhood context and historical state evolution that enables the coordinated, progression-aware decisions that account for both where and when critical traffic events occur.

Centralized training with decentralized execution

Centralized training with decentralized execution (CTDE) has become a dominant paradigm in cooperative MARL, particularly in domains where agents must coordinate under partial observability while maintaining operational scalability at deployment time. The core principle of CTDE is to leverage global information during the training phase, such as the full joint observation, joint action, or centralized state, to stabilize policy updates and resolve credit assignment ambiguities, while restricting each agent to its local observation during execution. This separation mitigates the non-stationarity that plagues independent learners and enables the emergence of coordinated behaviors without requiring real-time access to global state during inference25,34.

CTDE frameworks can be broadly categorized into two main types: actor–critic and value-based. In actor–critic variants (e.g., MADDPG7, COMA34), a centralized critic evaluates the joint action-value function using global information, while decentralized actors generate local policies conditioned only on agent-specific observations. In contrast, value-based CTDE approaches (e.g., VDN47, QMIX10, QTRAN48) learn a centralized but factored action-value function that can be decomposed into agent-wise utilities during execution. These methods often impose structural constraints such as monotonicity in QMIX to ensure consistency between global and local value estimates. Both paradigms have been applied to traffic signal control, with value-based CTDE showing particular promise due to its compatibility with discrete phase-selection action spaces and its robustness in fully cooperative settings3,11.

A critical design consideration in CTDE is the nature of the shared information during training. While full state access is ideal for the critic, practical implementations often rely on constructed representations that approximate global context, such as concatenated local observations, graph-based summaries, or learned latent embeddings. Recent work emphasizes the role of communication in shaping this shared representation: during training, agents may exchange rich descriptors (e.g., embeddings, attention weights, or predictive features), but at execution time, communication is either eliminated or restricted to minimal, low-bandwidth messages to satisfy real-world constraints29,40. This distinction is especially relevant in traffic networks, where communication latency, bandwidth limits, and infrastructure heterogeneity necessitate lightweight execution protocols.

This CTDE instantiation aligns with structured communication protocols in large-scale traffic control. Model29 demonstrates that selective message passing, where only top-K neighbors contribute during training, improves generalization and reduces overfitting in corridor networks. Similarly, framework43 shows that integrating temporal graph representations into the centralized critic enhances robustness to stochastic demand and partial observability. Our model builds on these insights by coupling a sparse, two-stage attention mechanism (for selective neighbor influence) with a centralized Graph Transformer Q-Network that jointly encodes spatiotemporal context. This design ensures that training benefits from holistic corridor awareness, while execution remains decentralized, scalable, and compatible with existing traffic signal controller hardware.

Prior graph-based MARL controllers typically (i) communicate densely or with fixed neighborhoods, (ii) use soft attention that remains effectively dense, and/or (iii) optimize mainly local efficiency signals with limited corridor-level credit assignment. GTQN differs by explicitly learning a state-dependent sparse interaction graph and training it under a progression-oriented governance objective within CTDE (Table 1).

Reward and objective

We optimize a two-level utility that couples local efficiency with corridor progression. Corridor progression is not a direct consequence of minimizing local delay or queue length, because locally optimal phase extensions can fragment platoons and create residual downstream stopping. Conversely, a single global reward (e.g., total delay) yields weak and delayed credit signals for individual intersections. We therefore use a two-level utility: a junction-level term that preserves local efficiency and a corridor hindrance term that directly penalizes residual stopping of the coordinated stream after release from the entry junction. This reward design provides both frequent local feedback and a progression-specific shaping signal.

The corridor hindrance penalty is used only in the centralized governance reward provided to the CGG during training, thereby enforcing corridor-level credit assignment without requiring agents to explicitly compute non-local penalties during execution.

Corridor hindrance penalty

The corridor hindrance penalty is designed to quantify residual stopping-induced impedance experienced by the coordinated stream at downstream intersections after release from the corridor entry junction . To make the computation reproducible, we specify (i) which vehicles are included, (ii) which simulator signals are used, (iii) the spatial attribution to intersections, and (iv) the temporal aggregation horizon. For each intersection i, we define its influence region as the set of incoming lanes (or edges) that terminate at the stop line controlled by i. A vehicle is attributed to intersection i at time t if it is currently located on an incoming lane of i (within a fixed upstream distance threshold from the stop line) or is queued/halting on that incoming lane. This attribution avoids double-counting the same delay at multiple intersections and ensures that hindrance is intersection-specific. Within the influence region of intersection i, we measure stopping-induced impedance using per-vehicle incremental waiting time and stop incidence. At the simulator sampling rate (1 s in our setup), we query each attributed vehicle’s accumulated waiting time and detect whether it is effectively stopped using a speed threshold (consistent with the ANS definition). The hindrance contribution for a vehicle is defined as the increase in its waiting time while it remains in the influence region of i, optionally augmented by a binary stop indicator to emphasize stop-and-go events.

Scope and assumptions

This work focuses on the coordination of vehicular traffic signals for corridor progression under partial observability and stochastic demand. The formulation assumes standard vehicle detection and communication capabilities between intersections. While fundamental safety constraints–such as minimum green durations, intergreen (yellow/red) times, and kinematic clearance rules are enforced as hard constraints in the simulation environment (see Table 3), they are not explicitly modeled within the learning objective. Multimodal traffic participants (e.g., pedestrians, cyclists, public transit) and their associated safety-critical interactions (e.g., crosswalk clearance, transit signal priority) are considered out-of-scope for this study.

Proposed model

We represent the Graph Transformer Q-Network for Collaborative Governance and Decentralized Decision-Making in Multi-Intersection Network Control (GTQN) as a graph-structured system in which each controlled junction corresponds to a graph node (Fig. 2). The proposed GTQN framework is implemented as a two-tier architecture that separates junction-level actuation from network-level governance. The first tier, termed the Distributed Junction Controller (DJC), models the local decision process within each intersection, incorporating the available signal phases and the corresponding sensor-derived traffic descriptors. The second tier, termed the Collaborative Governance Graph (CGG), provides a coordination layer that links all intersections in the corridor and enables information fusion across the network to support progression-oriented objectives. Each junction controller therefore operates with a hybrid information set: (i) local observations extracted from its own approaches (e.g., queue and delay indicators associated with the available phases), and (ii) shared network context communicated through the governance layer, which summarizes the state of nearby (and, where required, farther) intersections. Internally, the local tier uses a dense intra-junction exchange (i.e., within-intersection coupling across phases) to capture dependencies among approaches, whereas the global tier uses the corridor connectivity to facilitate inter-junction message passing across the graph.

Graphical representation of the proposed collaborative traffic intersection network. The traffic intersection network is modeled as a graph-based system in which each intersection operates as a distributed junction controller (DJC) with access to local sensory observations, while coordination across intersections is achieved through a centralized collaborative governance layer. Local snapshot inputs capturing queue, delay, and signal-state information are processed at each junction to form latent descriptors that guide decentralized control decisions. In parallel, node attribute matrices and network topology information are aggregated within a collaboration graph and processed by a Graph Transformer Q-Network under a centralized learning paradigm. Knowledge sharing across intersections is facilitated through this collaborative network, enabling reinforcement learning with experience replay and mini-batch optimization. The framework supports progressive multi-agent training and centralized learning with decentralized execution that allows locally informed decisions to collectively achieve globally coordinated traffic signal control across the network.

We adopt progressive multi-agent training as a stabilization protocol to reduce early-stage non-stationarity, where the number of active junction controllers is gradually increased during training. This approach has been shown to simplify exploration and enhance learning stability in multi-agent environments49. Second, for the network tier, we follow the principle of centralized learning with decentralized execution, where global information is exploited during training to learn cooperative behaviors, while execution remains distributed across intersections50. In terms of model components, temporal representations are extracted using an attention-based sequence encoder (Transformer) rather than recurrent units, enabling efficient modeling of history-dependent traffic patterns. Inter-intersection dependencies are then integrated via a graph attention/transformer fusion module that produces node embeddings used for decision-making at each junction. Finally, the network-level decision process is realized using a value-based reinforcement learning objective, where the proposed Graph Transformer Q-Network estimates long-horizon utilities over the discrete control set to generate coordinated phase and timing actions.

Local intersection-level policy

Prior studies51,52 have investigated multi-agent communication by constructing a single, globally aggregated message that is broadcast to all agents. While such full aggregation simplifies the message-passing interface, it also forces every agent to ingest information from every other agent, yielding excessive and often redundant communication. In corridor-like traffic control, this dense information flow can make learning unstable and ambiguous because irrelevant dependencies are repeatedly introduced through unnecessary links. Moreover, conventional softmax-based attention typically assigns small but non-zero weights to many weakly related agents, which (i) prevents the communication graph from becoming sparse, (ii) dilutes attention mass away from the truly influential neighbors, and (iii) can encourage overfitting when deep networks process the resulting high-dimensional messages.

In corridor traffic, the set of influential peers changes with convoy position and travel-time dispersion: during discharge, the nearest upstream intersection is most predictive of imminent arrivals; during recovery or spillback risk, a different subset of upstream/downstream intersections becomes relevant. A purely distance-based neighborhood is therefore insufficient, and dense attention is inefficient because it assigns non-zero weights to many weakly related nodes. Hard Top-K selection reduces bandwidth but cannot represent graded and asymmetric influence among selected peers (e.g., one upstream junction dominates arrival timing while another provides secondary context). GTQN therefore combines (i) discrete gating to enforce a small, state-dependent peer set and (ii) soft relevance weighting to preserve continuous influence strengths within that sparse set.

Experiment and result

Dataset

To evaluate the proposed GTQN under both controlled and realistic simulation conditions, we adopt an experimental protocol widely used in recent multi-intersection studies, where synthetic networks57,58 are used for scalable training and ablation, and real-traffic networks extracted from an urban road map are used for generalization testing within the SUMO microscopic simulator4,59. This setting matches our GTQN formulation because it exposes the learning system to (i) structured corridor topologies that emphasize progression and upstream-downstream dependency and (ii) heterogeneous large-scale topologies that stress sparse interaction modeling in the DIG. We emphasize that all evaluations on synthetic RN layouts or real-traffic SQ1-SQ3 networks derived from Chengdu are conducted in simulation. Consequently, the reported improvements in progression quality, scalability to 100 intersections, and robustness to communication perturbations constitute evidence of algorithmic efficacy under high-fidelity simulation, not yet validation under field deployment conditions involving real sensor noise, actuator latency, legacy controller interoperability, or jurisdictional policy constraints

Synthetic-traffic networks

Following common practice4,59, we construct a family of synthetic networks denoted by , where controls the corridor depth and controls layout variants. In total, the set contains 20 network instances with diverse junction arrangements, enabling the proposed DJC-CGG architecture to experience multiple coordination regimes during learning. The arterial case is specifically included to encourage the emergence of “green-wave” behaviors, i.e., coordinated progression along a primary direction. The designed networks include common intersection geometries (e.g., “” and “+” configurations), which appear frequently in urban layouts and provide realistic turning and conflict patterns.

Evaluation metrics

All metrics are computed from microscopic simulator logs and reported at the network level for each scenario (synthetic RN and real-traffic SQ1–SQ3). For stochastic demand settings, we report the mean (and, where applicable, standard deviation) across multiple random seeds to ensure robustness, consistent with standard MARL evaluation practice in traffic control3,38. Together, AWT and AQL characterize intersection efficiency (delay and congestion), AF measures network service rate, and ANS explicitly quantifies progression quality that is the core objective targeted by the proposed DIG-enabled coordination and the CGG trained governance policy.

To quantify both local operational efficiency and corridor-level progression in the proposed GTQN setting, we evaluate all methods using four complementary metrics that are standard in learning-based traffic signal control studies. These metrics collectively capture (i) vehicle-level temporal cost, (ii) progression quality through stop minimization, (iii) spatial accumulation through queue growth, and (iv) network throughput. In particular, we follow the reporting practice adopted in prior multi-intersection RL literature, where average waiting time, average queue length, and stop-based measures are used to assess control policies under heterogeneous demand and partial observability3,38.

To train the proposed Graph Transformer Q-Network under diverse coordination regimes and to avoid over-specialization to a single corridor layout, we construct a Traffic Intersection Network (TIN) benchmark composed of parametrized synthetic road graphs. This design adheres to established protocols in multi-intersection reinforcement learning, where synthetic networks are utilized for scalable training and controlled ablations before transfer testing on real urban maps3,38. In our setting, each TIN instance is treated as a directed graph in which intersections are nodes and road segments are edges, while signal control is performed with the phase-duration action set defined in Eq. (4). The TIN benchmark explicitly contains both corridor-like and branched configurations so that the learning system experiences (i) strong upstream-downstream dependence that favors progression, and (ii) competing cross-street demands that require robust conflict resolution.

We define a synthetic family denoted by , where controls corridor depth (number of signalized stages along the primary direction) and indexes layout variants (e.g., branching patterns, merge/split structures, and cross-street connectivity). Each configuration instantiates a fixed number of signalized nodes, with a representative training-scale network containing 25 intersections (nodes) formed by composing two ubiquitous urban junction archetypes: three-leg junctions (“”) and four-leg junctions (“+”), consistent with commonly adopted synthetic benchmarks in recent work38. This mixture introduces heterogeneous conflict patterns and turning interactions while maintaining a consistent, feasible phase set at every controlled node.

All road segments in are multi-lane links with 3 lanes per direction to emulate moderate-capacity arterials. Each lane has a standardized width of m and links are configured to yield a total end-to-end arterial span of approximately 2.1 km for the representative training instance. This geometry ensures non-trivial travel time between adjacent intersections and enables the emergence (or degradation) of progression bands depending on coordinated signal timing1,2. Vehicle dynamics are simulated at a 1 s resolution (Table 3), and intergreen constraints are enforced through the amber-time rule in Eq. (12) to ensure operational feasibility.

To expose the learning system to both progression-friendly and adversarial coordination conditions, we generate two demand regimes: (i) unidirectional sequences emphasizing a dominant progression direction, and (ii) bidirectional sequences that introduce opposing flows and increase the likelihood of spillback. In each episode, the number of injected vehicles is sampled from a broad range (e.g., 60-460 vehicles per episode) to induce variability in saturation level and to improve robustness to stochastic arrivals3,5. We additionally impose an explicit priority asymmetry between the two principal axes: the North-South arterial is assigned higher priority with a target demand of 420 veh/hr/lane, while the West-East axis is assigned a lower priority with 140 veh/hr/lane. This asymmetric loading is designed to encourage the emergence of a near signal-free corridor along the dominant direction after vehicles are released from the entry intersection, directly matching our progression-oriented objective and hindrance penalty formulation.

To avoid learning degenerate, route-specific policies, trip origin and destination edges are randomized at the beginning of each episode, subject to graph connectivity constraints. Specifically, vehicles are injected at randomly selected boundary edges and assigned to feasible exit edges, producing a mixture of through and turning movements at both “” and “+” junctions. This randomized OD assignment yields diverse state transitions and prevents overfitting to fixed entry/exit patterns, which is critical under partial observability and decentralized execution6,7.

Implementation details

The proposed Graph Transformer Q-Network is implemented within a modular reinforcement learning framework that separates environment simulation, local controller execution, and centralized training. All experiments are conducted using a Simulation of Urban Mobility (SUMO) traffic simulator that supports event-driven signal control, lane-level vehicle dynamics, and per-vehicle trajectory logging. The simulator is interfaced with the learning framework through a synchronous step-based API, where environment transitions occur at a fixed temporal resolution of 1 s, while control decisions are issued at event-driven decision points corresponding to phase completions. The SUMO simulator provides lane-level vehicle dynamics, event-driven signal actuation, and per-vehicle trajectory logging that enable rigorous evaluation of coordination algorithms under reproducible demand patterns and topology variations. However, several field-relevant factors remain outside the current simulation scope: (i) sensor imperfections: loop detectors and connected-vehicle probes in practice exhibit noise, dropout, and calibration drift not modeled in our observation encoding; (ii) controller heterogeneity: real-world signal controllers vary in firmware, communication protocols, and timing resolution, whereas our DJC implementation assumes homogeneous PyTorch-based execution; (iii) operational constraints: jurisdictional policies (e.g., pedestrian clearance mandates, transit priority rules, emergency preemption) are not encoded in the action space or reward; and (iv) distribution shift: training and evaluation demands, while time-varying, are sampled from stationary parametric distributions, whereas real traffic exhibits non-stationary patterns driven by events, weather, and long-term behavioral shifts.

Each Distributed Junction Controller (DJC) is implemented as a lightweight neural module that shares parameters across intersections to ensure scalability and generalization across network sizes. Parameter sharing is applied to the local state encoder, sparse peer-selection mechanism, transformer encoder, and Q-value head. Junction identity is not encoded explicitly; instead, heterogeneity across intersections is captured implicitly through node attributes and graph connectivity, consistent with prior graph-based MARL designs20,21. This design allows a single trained policy to be deployed across synthetic and real-traffic networks with varying topologies and scales.

The Collaborative Governance Graph (CGG) is implemented as a centralized module that operates only during training. It aggregates node embeddings produced by the DJCs and processes them using a shallow graph transformer with topology-aware sparsification. The CGG outputs a global action–value estimate used solely for training updates under the CTDE paradigm. During execution, the CGG is disabled, and each DJC selects actions based on its local observations and learned coordination context, ensuring decentralized execution without reliance on access to global state.

All neural components are implemented in PyTorch. Transformer blocks follow the standard pre-normalization design with residual connections and layer normalization to stabilize deep training. Graph operations are implemented using sparse adjacency matrices to reduce memory overhead when scaling to large real-traffic networks (e.g., SQ3 with 100 intersections). Unless otherwise stated, all model parameters are initialized using Xavier initialization, and gradient norms are clipped to prevent instability during early training. Experimental setup

Experiments are designed to evaluate the proposed GTQN framework under both controlled synthetic conditions and realistic large-scale urban networks, with a focus on (i) corridor progression quality, (ii) robustness under stochastic and time-varying demand, and (iii) scalability with respect to network size and topology. All reported results are obtained using identical environment configurations and traffic demand profiles across methods to ensure fair comparison. For synthetic experiments, training is performed exclusively on the . During each training rollout, a network instance is sampled uniformly from the synthetic set, and traffic demand is generated according to the corresponding unidirectional or bidirectional regime. Episodes begin when vehicles are injected at the boundary edges and terminate once all vehicles exit the controlled region or when a predefined maximum simulation time is reached. This episodic formulation aligns with the progression objective, as rewards are accumulated over the complete traversal of coordinated streams.

Evaluation is conducted on both unseen synthetic layouts and real-traffic networks (SQ1-SQ3). For real-traffic evaluation, the trained model is directly transferred without fine-tuning to assess generalization across topology and scale. Each evaluation scenario is executed with multiple random seeds to account for stochastic arrival processes and vehicle interactions. Performance metrics are averaged across seeds, and standard deviations are reported where appropriate. Baseline methods follow their original implementations and hyperparameter settings as closely as possible, with adaptations made only where necessary to ensure compatibility with the event-driven phase-duration control formulation. All methods are evaluated under identical phase sets, minimum/maximum green constraints, and yellow-time rules (Table 3). No method is provided with privileged information beyond what is available through local observations and permitted coordination mechanisms.

All experiments are executed on a workstation equipped with an NVIDIA RTX-3080 GPU (10-GB VRAM), 128 GB system memory, and a multi-core CPU. A full training run requires approximately 140-150 hours wall-clock time, dominated by SUMO rollout generation rather than neural computation. This computational profile is consistent with prior large-scale MARL studies in traffic signal control3,5. To ensure experimental rigor and reproducibility, we established a strict fairness protocol governing baseline configuration. All methods, including GTQN and baselines, were restricted to the identical local observation feature set defined in Table 2 during execution; no method was granted access to privileged global state information unavailable to others. Baseline implementations and audit-ready fairness controls

To ensure that baseline comparisons are reproducible and audit-ready, we standardize the experimental pipeline across all learning-based methods and explicitly report (i) training budgets, (ii) hyperparameter tuning budgets, (iii) observation-feature parity, and (iv) communication assumptions. All baselines are evaluated using a shared SUMO environment wrapper and identical scenario configurations (network files, routing, demand schedules, signal timing constraints, and termination criteria). All methods including GTQN and all learning-based baselines operate under the same event-driven phase-duration action formulation in Eq. (4), with identical minimum/maximum green bounds and yellow/all-red constraints (Table 3). For baselines whose original implementations assume alternative timing parameterizations (e.g., fixed-cycle split control), we adapt their actuation interface to select from the same discrete phase-duration set to prevent implicit advantages from richer or different control authority.

All methods receive the same per-intersection observation signals extracted from SUMO at the same sampling rate (1 s) and aggregated at the same decision interval . Concretely, each intersection is provided the same raw traffic features used to construct the GTQN input descriptors (Table 2); methods that do not natively use heterogeneous graph inputs are provided a deterministic flattening of the same descriptors into a fixed-length vector (junction attributes + incident stream/link attributes) so that no baseline is deprived of information that is available to GTQN. No method is provided privileged information at execution time beyond the locally measurable signals and the permitted communication neighborhood described below.

To avoid confounding performance with asymmetric information access, we enforce the same permitted communication graph for all methods. Any baseline that uses inter-agent message passing, graph aggregation, or learned communication is restricted to exchanging information only with neighbors in (the same constraint used by GTQN through in Sec. ). Methods that do not use communication simply ignore peer features. Training parameters

Training follows a value-based reinforcement learning procedure with experience replay and target network stabilization, consistent with established practices in cooperative MARL11,25. A shared replay buffer stores transition tuples collected from parallel simulation rollouts. Mini-batches are sampled uniformly from the buffer to break temporal correlations and improve sample efficiency. The Graph Transformer Q-Network is optimized using the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of . The learning rate is held constant for the first of training iterations and then linearly decayed to to improve convergence stability. The discount factor is set to , reflecting the need to balance near-term junction efficiency with downstream corridor outcomes. Target network parameters are updated using a soft update scheme with update coefficient .

Each training run uses 20 parallel environment instances, resulting in approximately 1.2 million environment steps in total. This training budget of steps is applied uniformly to all methods, including GTQN and all baseline controllers, to ensure equitable comparison of sample efficiency and convergence. Each episode consists of up to 1020 decision steps, corresponding to multiple signal cycles across the corridor. To prevent rare deadlock conditions from dominating training time, episodes are forcibly terminated if the simulated duration exceeds 12 minutes, after which remaining vehicles are cleared without contributing further reward. Exploration is implemented using an -greedy strategy applied to the local DJC action selection. The exploration rate is linearly annealed from to over the first of training steps and held constant thereafter. During evaluation, exploration is disabled, and actions are selected greedily. Transformer encoders in both the DJC and CGG use 2 layers with 4 attention heads and an embedding dimension of 128. The sparse peer-selection mechanism restricts attention to the Top-K most relevant neighbors, with for synthetic networks and for real-traffic networks to account for increased connectivity. These values are selected based on validation performance and remain fixed across all experiments.

For discrete peer gating, we use Straight-Through Gumbel-Top-K with temperature linearly annealed from 1.0 to 0.2 over the first of training, transitioning from exploratory to near-deterministic selection. We set the gate-entropy regularization coefficient to in Eq. (22). During evaluation, Gumbel noise is disabled and gating is deterministic.

Ablation study

Each ablation is designed to test the corresponding design hypothesis, such as sparsity to prevent attention diffusion, unified spatiotemporal encoding to capture delayed effects, centralized governance for credit assignment, and two-level rewards for progression alignment. GTQN’s core contribution is learned relevance-aware sparse coordination trained under centralized governance. Our ablations are structured to isolate (i) whether learning sparsity (two-stage gating) is necessary beyond dense or random sparsification; (ii) whether centralized governance under CTDE is necessary for stable credit assignment; and (iii) whether joint spatiotemporal modeling is required to capture delayed upstream-to-downstream effects that drive progression.

Impact of joint spatiotemporal modeling

To evaluate the role of unified spatiotemporal reasoning in multi-intersection traffic control, we conduct an ablation study that isolates the contribution of graph-structured spatial modeling and Transformer-based temporal encoding. Four architectural variants are evaluated under identical training protocols, observation spaces, and reward structures on the real-traffic networks SQ1, SQ2, and SQ3 under time-varying demand. The Graph-only (GNN + MLP) variant captures spatial dependencies but compresses temporal dynamics into short-term averages, while the Temporal-only variant models historical dynamics at each intersection independently without inter-junction communication. The Separated Modeling variant stacks spatial and temporal modules sequentially but lacks joint attention or shared spatiotemporal encoding. In contrast, the proposed GTQN integrates spatial topology and temporal history within a unified Graph Transformer architecture.

However, this variant exhibits higher stopping frequency than GTQN (ANS 0.71 vs. 0.66), indicating less effective progression control. On SQ2, GTQN achieves the best overall balance, attaining the lowest AWT (35.7 s) and ANS (0.76) while maintaining competitive throughput. The proposed GTQN framework achieves genuine corridor-level progression rather than merely smoothing local delays. The clean green-wave patterns observed under Full GTQN, in contrast to the fragmented stop-and-go behavior of the ablated variants, substantiate the central claim that learned, relevance-aware sparse coordination is critical for scalable and progression-oriented multi-intersection traffic control.

Discussion

This work set out to address a fundamental limitation in learning-based multi-intersection traffic signal control: how to achieve robust corridor-level progression under partial observability, stochastic demand, and large-scale network topology without resorting to dense, impractical communication. GTQN demonstrates that progression-oriented coordination can be learned reliably when three design principles are jointly satisfied: (i) relevance-aware sparse interaction, (ii) unified spatiotemporal representation learning, and (iii) centralized governance during training coupled with decentralized execution. The experimental and ablation results collectively validate these principles and clarify the mechanisms through which GTQN achieves its performance gains.

A central finding of this study is that sparsity alone is insufficient for effective coordination; rather, sparsity must be learned and state-dependent. This distinction is evidenced consistently across the attention ablation (Table 7), the K sensitivity analysis (Fig. 7), and the qualitative space-time diagnostics (Fig. 6). Dense attention mechanisms degrade rapidly as network scale increases, particularly on SQ3, due to attention diffusion: weak but non-zero weights assigned to many marginally relevant neighbors dilute the influence of truly causal upstream intersections. This effect manifests as increased stopping frequency (ANS), higher queue accumulation (AQL), and fragmented progression bands.

Hard sparsification alone (Top-K Only) partially alleviates this issue by limiting communication scope, but it lacks the expressive capacity to model asymmetric and context dependent influence. In traffic corridors, the relevance of an upstream intersection depends not only on distance but also on convoy timing, residual green duration, and current discharge state. The two-stage sparse attention mechanism in GTQN, combining discrete peer selection with soft relevance weighting, captures this structure. The resulting coordination context concentrates representational capacity on the most influential peers while preserving graded influence among them, which explains both the lower mean ANS and the reduced variance observed across seeds.

The K-sensitivity study provides an important deployment-level insight: there exists a clear and consistent “knee” in the communication performance tradeoff around -4. Increasing K from 1 to this range yields substantial gains in progression quality, while further increases produce diminishing returns despite linear growth in communication cost. This effect is most pronounced on SQ3, where heterogeneous connectivity amplifies attention noise under dense aggregation. This finding has two implications. First, it refutes the argument that GTQN’s gains stem from arbitrary sparsification; instead, performance improvements arise from operating in a narrow, principled sparsity regime that aligns with physical traffic propagation constraints. Second, it introduces a practical scalability knob: K can be tuned to balance coordination quality and infrastructure constraints without retraining the model. This property is particularly relevant for real-world deployments where communication bandwidth, latency, or reliability may vary across jurisdictions.

Another key observation is that neither spatial nor temporal modeling alone is sufficient for robust progression control at scale. The spatiotemporal ablation (Table 8) shows that graph-only models fail to anticipate delayed downstream effects, while temporal-only models lack awareness of spatial causality. Sequentially stacking spatial and temporal modules improves performance on small networks but breaks down under large-scale, heterogeneous topologies where interaction relevance changes dynamically. GTQN’s unified graph transformer architecture resolves this limitation by allowing spatial and temporal dependencies to modulate each other through joint attention. This enables the model to learn when historical information from a specific upstream intersection remains relevant for a downstream decision several cycles later. The superior ANS and AQL performance on SQ3, together with lower variance, indicates that joint spatiotemporal reasoning is essential for stabilizing coordination under non-stationary demand.

The centralized governance ablation (Table 10) highlights that sparse local coordination alone does not resolve the non-stationarity inherent in multi-agent learning. Independent or loosely shared policies suffer from misaligned incentives and unstable learning dynamics, even when sparsity is enforced. CGG plays a critical role by providing a centralized value signal that aligns local DJCs with corridor-level objectives during training. Notably, centralized training alone is insufficient: dense aggregation within the CGG underperforms relative to GTQN, reinforcing that governance must be paired with structured sparsity. The combination of CTDE and sparse attention enables effective credit assignment without overwhelming agents with irrelevant global context, which explains both faster convergence and improved final performance.

The reward ablation confirms that progression does not emerge reliably from local efficiency objectives alone, nor from naive global rewards. Fixed linear weighting schemes improve performance but are sensitive to the choice of weighting parameter and exhibit trade-offs between delay minimization and stop suppression. The two level reward structure used in GTQN provides a more stable alternative by coupling junction-level efficiency with a corridor hindrance penalty that directly targets residual stopping. Importantly, GTQN does not always optimize a single metric most aggressively; instead, it consistently prioritizes progression robustness. This design choice is reflected in the lowest ANS across all networks and competitive (though not always minimal) AWT. Such behavior is desirable in real traffic systems, where eliminating stop-and-go waves often yields downstream benefits in fuel consumption, emissions, and driver comfort that are not fully captured by delay alone.

Practical deployment and computational cost profile

GTQN follows a standard offline-training/online-execution deployment pattern. Training is performed in a calibrated digital twin (SUMO) with CTDE and CGG enabled, while deployment executes only the decentralized DJC policies with sparse peer coordination; the CGG is disabled at inference time. This separation is operationally important: the centralized module contributes primarily to stabilizing credit assignment during learning, whereas run-time control reduces to lightweight forward passes at each junction plus sparse message exchange over a small peer set.

On the reference workstation (NVIDIA RTX-3080, 10-GB VRAM, 128-GB RAM), a full training run requires approximately 140–150 hours wall-clock time using 20 parallel simulation instances and environment interaction steps. This corresponds to an overall data-generation throughput of roughly 2.2–2.4 environment steps/s (computed from total steps and wall-clock), indicating that end-to-end training is dominated by simulator rollouts rather than neural network forward/backward passes. The primary driver of training time is therefore microscopic traffic simulation cost (vehicle interactions, routing, lane changes) which scales with network size and demand, while CGG/DJC updates add a comparatively smaller marginal overhead. In practice, training can be accelerated by (i) increasing the number of parallel rollout workers, (ii) distributing simulation across CPU cores/nodes, and (iii) reusing previously collected replay buffers for warm-start fine-tuning.

During deployment, each decision step executes a single DJC forward pass per active intersection and performs sparse coordination over the selected peer set. The dominant scaling terms are linear in the number of controlled intersections M and the peer budget K. In the CGG, topology-aware sparsification similarly avoids dense all-to-all attention. These properties are critical in large graphs (e.g., SQ3) where dense communication would induce quadratic growth in both runtime and noise sensitivity.

Given the reported training cost (140-150 hours on a single GPU), full retraining is best positioned as an offline operation triggered by substantial and persistent distribution shifts (e.g., infrastructure changes, long-term demand pattern changes, revised signal phase sets), rather than frequent day-to-day updates. In practical deployments, a feasible retraining schedule is typically on the order of weeks-to-months on a single workstation; more frequent updates require either (i) additional compute (multi-GPU/multi-node simulation), or (ii) warm-start adaptation where the learned policy is initialized from the previously deployed model and fine-tuned on a smaller set of targeted scenarios (e.g., the affected corridor subgraph and updated OD patterns). For short-lived regime shifts such as incidents or abrupt but transient surges, GTQN is intended to rely on its state-dependent peer selection (and optional adaptive-K extension) to adjust coordination scope without requiring retraining.

Limitations and scope delimitation

Despite its strong empirical performance within simulation, the proposed GTQN exhibits several limitations that merit careful consideration, and we explicitly delimit the scope of claims supported by the current evaluation.

Demonstrated in simulation: (i) Scalability: GTQN maintains coordination quality and computational tractability on networks up to 100 intersections (SQ3) under time-varying demand, with communication overhead scaling linearly in the peer budget K rather than quadratically in network size. (ii) Progression-aware credit assignment: The two-level reward and centralized governance mechanism reduce residual stopping (ANS) and improve green-wave formation relative to baselines, as validated through space-time trajectory analysis and ablation studies. (iii) Robustness to controlled perturbations: GTQN exhibits graceful degradation under simulated message dropout (), fixed transmission delays ( step), and asynchronous update cycles, supporting feasibility under moderate communication impairments.

Not yet validated for field deployment: (i) Sensor and actuation realism: The current observation encoding assumes noise-free, synchronized detector readings; real-world deployments must address sensor calibration error, communication latency, and actuator response variability. (ii) Controller heterogeneity and legacy integration: GTQN’s DJC implementation assumes homogeneous neural inference across intersections; interoperability with legacy controller hardware, proprietary communication stacks, and jurisdiction-specific timing constraints remains untested. (iii) Multimodal and safety-critical constraints: Pedestrian crossings, bicycle movements, transit priority, and emergency preemption are excluded from the action space and reward design; extending GTQN to multi-modal coordination requires explicit safety shielding and constrained optimization formulations. (iv) Distribution shift and long-term adaptation: Training and evaluation demands are sampled from parametric distributions; real-world deployment must handle non-recurrent events, seasonal demand shifts, and infrastructure changes, necessitating online adaptation or continual learning mechanisms not explored herein.

We therefore position GTQN as a simulation-validated algorithmic framework that establishes the feasibility of relevance-aware sparse coordination for corridor progression. Field deployment requires an additional validation pipeline incorporating: (a) domain randomization over sensor noise and demand uncertainty during training; (b) hardware-in-the-loop testing with representative controller platforms; (c) staged on-road trials with conservative safety constraints and human-in-the-loop oversight; and (d) post-deployment monitoring for distribution shift detection and policy adaptation. These steps constitute important directions for future work.

Future research directions

The above limitations motivate several promising directions for future research. A natural extension of GTQN is the incorporation of adaptive sparsity, where the peer set size K and gating thresholds are adjusted online based on traffic state, demand volatility, or coordination confidence. Instead of using a fixed K, the discrete gating mechanism could be augmented with uncertainty-aware or demand-aware criteria that expand coordination scope during congestion peaks and contract it during free-flow conditions, thereby improving robustness while preserving scalability. Another important direction concerns communication robustness. Future work could explicitly model communication delays, packet loss, or asynchronous updates within the DIG. This may involve training under stochastic communication dropout, integrating delay-aware temporal encoding, or leveraging predictive peer modeling so that controllers can anticipate missing or delayed information. Such extensions would improve the deployability of GTQN in heterogeneous infrastructure environments. The adaptive-K heuristic is a first step toward online adaptation; future work could explore more sophisticated reinforcement of the sparsity level itself, using meta-gradients or contextual bandits to adjust K in response to network-wide performance indicators.

This may involve training under stochastic communication dropout, integrating delay-aware temporal encoding, or leveraging predictive peer modeling so that controllers can anticipate missing or delayed information. Techniques from robust federated learning or adversarial training could be adapted to improve the policy’s resilience to unreliable links. Such extensions would significantly improve the deployability of GTQN in heterogeneous infrastructure environments with varying QoS guarantees.

Extending GTQN to support multi-modal and safety-aware control represents a critical step toward real-world adoption. Incorporating pedestrian phases, bicycle movements, emergency vehicle preemption, and public transport priority would require expanding the action space and modifying the reward structure to balance progression with safety and equity objectives. Graph-based modeling is well-suited to this extension, as different traffic participant types can be represented as heterogeneous nodes with distinct priorities and constraints. From a learning perspective, integrating online adaptation and continual learning remains an open challenge. While GTQN generalizes well across unseen networks, it is trained offline and deployed as a fixed policy. Future research could explore lightweight online fine-tuning, meta-learning, or federated learning schemes that allow intersections to adapt incrementally to long-term demand shifts, seasonal patterns, or infrastructure changes without retraining from scratch.

Finally, further investigation into explainability and interpretability would strengthen the practical utility of GTQN. Although sparse attention improves transparency relative to dense aggregation, the learned peer selection and relevance weights could be analyzed more systematically to extract interpretable coordination patterns, such as dominant upstream influence ranges or time-varying progression structures. Such insights would not only aid debugging and trust but could also inform human-designed signal plans and hybrid human-AI control strategies.

Conclusion

This paper presents the GTQN framework for multi-intersection traffic signal control. The central contribution is a learned, two-stage sparse attention mechanism that solves the attention diffusion problem inherent in scalable multi-agent coordination under high-fidelity simulation. By dynamically selecting only the most relevant upstream/downstream peers at each intersection, GTQN enables precise, scalable green-wave formation in SUMO-based evaluations, whereas prior methods suffer from noisy or inefficient communication. This core mechanism is effectively embedded within a unified graph-transformer architecture and trained under a CTDE governance scheme with a progression-oriented reward. Extensive experiments and ablations within simulation confirm that this capability for relevance-aware, sparse coordination distinguishes GTQN from existing MARL approaches and drives its superior performance in forming and sustaining progression corridors in synthetic and semi-synthetic urban networks up to 100 intersections. We emphasize that these results establish algorithmic efficacy under controlled simulation conditions; field generalization requires additional validation incorporating sensor realism, controller heterogeneity, and operational constraints, which we delineate as critical directions for future work. Ablation studies demonstrate that each component of GTQN is necessary within the simulated environment: dense or randomly sparse communication degrades coordination due to attention diffusion or exclusion of influential peers; decoupled spatial-temporal modeling fails to capture delayed causal effects critical for progression; and decentralized learning without centralized credit assignment yields unstable policies due to non-stationarity.
