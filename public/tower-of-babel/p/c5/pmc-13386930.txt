The knowledge-perception nexus: socio-linguistic disparities in surgical informed consent among patients at Ethiopian tertiary hospitals
Authors: Boka Dugassa Tolera, Endale Aschenaki Altaye, Tigistu Gebreyohannis Gebretensaye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSurgical informed consent (SIC), the ethical bedrock of patient autonomy is undermined by profound sociodemographic disparities in resource-limited settings. This study aimed to evaluate the knowledge-perception nexus and sociolinguistic determinants of SIC comprehension among surgical patients in Ethiopian tertiary hospitals.MethodsThis institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted among 412 surgical patients in Addis Ababa tertiary hospitals between March 8 to April 30, 2023. The validated interviewer-administered questionnaire was used to assess knowledge and perceptions. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics, and multivariable logistic regression identified predictors (P < 0.05).ResultsOnly 52.2% demonstrated adequate SIC knowledge; 57.8% had positive perceptions. strongest knowledge predictors were higher education (AOR = 5.21, 95% CI: 3.12–8.68), prior surgery (AOR = 4.58, 95% CI: 2.87–7.32), male sex (AOR = 2.28, 95% CI: 1.52–3.42), and native-language proficiency (AOR = 1.83, 95% CI: 1.09–3.07). Favorable perception associated with urban residence (AOR = 3.99, 95% CI: 2.34–6.80) and marriage (AOR = 1.79, 95% CI: 1.16–2.78). Adequate knowledge independently predicted positive perception (AOR = 1.64, 95% CI: 1.02–2.62), explaining 68% of perception variance.ConclusionSurgical patients in Ethiopia demonstrated inadequate knowledge and perceptions of informed consent, influenced by gender, education, residence, and language barriers. The association between knowledge and perception highlights the need for culturally appropriate interventions, including teach-back methods and shared decision-making to improve informed consent practices and surgical care outcomes in resource-limited settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-026-03872-6.

Introduction

Surgical informed consent (SIC) is a fundamental ethical requirement in global surgery with implementation that varies widely across countries [1]. High-income countries report high patient satisfaction with SIC, exceeding 90% in the USA and between 80 and 88% across various domains in European studies, often facilitated by standardized protocols that balance patient autonomy with medicolegal safeguards [2, 3]. In contrast, compliance with SIC in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) remains suboptimal, with rates as low as 49% in Uganda and 55% in Ethiopia due to the absence of national guidelines and resource constraints [4]. Consequently, many patients in the region have limited understanding and poor perceptions of surgical procedures, postoperative care, and their right to refuse treatment [5, 6].

Several studies have identified factors associated with patients’ understanding of and perceptions toward SIC, including low health literacy, limited educational access, language barriers, rural residence, and inadequate multilingual counseling [7–9]. Additionally, surgical urgency and prior surgical experience influence patients’ knowledge and attitudes toward the consent process [4, 6]. Communication gaps among providers, as well as institutional time constraints, are also important determinants of patients’ SIC knowledge and perceptions [10, 11]. These multilevel barriers may increase postoperative anxiety, reduce patient satisfaction, and undermine shared decision-making [4, 6]. Addressing these challenges requires targeted, culturally and linguistically appropriate interventions to enhance SIC implementation and improve patient outcomes in resource-limited settings [12].

Moreover, enhancing SIC requires understanding of the knowledge-perception nexus, wherein patients’ procedural comprehension directly shapes trust and voluntariness [5]. Ethically, informed consent is not merely a procedural formality but a tripartite requirement of disclosure, comprehension, and voluntariness [13]. When patients possess a clear understanding of surgical risks, benefits, and alternatives, their perception of the procedure shifts from passive compliance to active, autonomous engagement [14]. Conversely, limited comprehension has been shown to undermine the voluntariness of consent, as patients who do not fully grasp the implications of a procedure may feel coerced by the perceived necessity of the intervention rather than making an informed choice [15]. Furthermore, evidence suggests that transparent communication and verified comprehension are essential for fostering the trust necessary for successful shared decision-making, significantly reducing postoperative anxiety and dissatisfaction [11, 16].

Despite the clinical importance of surgical informed consent, no prior research has examined the sociolinguistic determinants of patient knowledge and perceptions in Addis Ababa’s tertiary hospitals—Ethiopia’s linguistically diverse hub of urban surgical care. Therefore, this study aims to address this gap by evaluating the association of knowledge and perceptions with linguistic proficiency, educational attainment, urban/rural residency, and sociodemographic factors among preoperative patients, thereby generating essential baseline data that is absent from the existing literature.

Methods and material

Study design, period, and settings

This institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted from March 8 to April 30, 2023, at three public tertiary teaching hospitals in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia: Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital (TASH), St. Paul’s Hospital and Millennium Medical College (SPHMMC), and Zowditu Memorial Hospital (ZMH). These largest national referral hospitals are serving diverse regional patient populations undergoing emergency and elective surgery across multiple specialties. This setting ensured a broad representation of socioeconomic, cultural, and linguistic backgrounds, which is critical for evaluating surgical patients’ knowledge, perceptions, and factors associated with a surgical informed consent process.

Population

The source population for this study included surgical patients seeking care at the selected tertiary hospitals in Addis Ababa. The study population specifically comprised elective surgical patients admitted to the surgical wards of the three selected institutions during the study period. Eligibility was restricted to adult postoperative patients (aged ≥ 18 years) who had undergone elective procedures. To ensure a broad and representative sample, inclusion was independent of sex, educational background, or the specific type of surgical procedure, focusing primarily on the patient’s cognitive capacity to provide informed consent. Conversely, patients were excluded if they had undergone emergency surgery, were critically ill, or lacked the mental capacity required for meaningful participation. Furthermore, individuals with profound hearing or speech impairments that precluded effective communication during the interview were excluded.

Sample size determination

The sample size for the study was determined using a single-population proportion formula:

An anticipated population proportion (P) of 50% was utilized, as there are no prior published studies regarding surgical informed consent among the target population in the study area. This proportion was chosen to ensure the maximum possible sample size. By adding 10% non-response rate, the final sample size was set at 422 patients.

Sampling procedure

To establish an appropriate sampling frame, a feasibility assessment was conducted using the surgical registries from both institutions. This review indicated a six-week average caseload of 195 elective procedures at Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital (TASH), 124 at St. Paul’s Hospital Millennium Medical College (SPHMMC), and 103 at Zowditu Memorial Hospital (ZMH). Accordingly, a consecutive sampling technique was employed to minimize selection bias and ensure representative capture of patient volume. All adult patients meeting the inclusion criteria were invited to participate in chronological order upon admission until a target sample size of n = 422 was attained.

Data collection instrument

A structured, interviewer-administered questionnaire was adapted from previously published Ethiopian SIC studies [9, 17] and translated into Amharic with back-translation verification (r = 0.92).

The instrument comprised three sections:

Section A: Sociodemographic/clinical variables (age, gender, educational level, occupational status, marital status, ethnic group, place of residence, surgical history, and language proficiency);

Section B: SIC knowledge (15 items; four domains: legal rights/voluntariness, information scope, roles/responsibilities, comprehension/participation); dichotomized scoring (Yes = 1; No/Don’t know = 0), range 0–15. Adequate knowledge defined as ≥ 12 (≥ 80th percentile; Cronbach’s α = 0.84).

Section C: Perceptions (10-item, 5-point Likert scale measuring comprehension, voluntariness, provider trust); continuous (0–10) or dichotomized scoring (Agree/Strongly Agree = 1; others = 0). Adequate perception was defined as ≥ 8 (top tertile: Cronbach’s α = 0.89).

Data quality assurance

The instrument was pretested on 5% of the sample (n = 20) at a similar facility outside the study sites. This yielded a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.85, with minor wording revisions implemented for clarity and cultural appropriateness. Three BSc-qualified nurses received standardized training from the principal investigator during a one-day session. Training covered study aims, instrument administration, ethical consent procedures, bias minimization strategies (e.g., scripted introductions for consistency), and query resolution protocols. Interviews were conducted after obtaining informed consent but before patient discharge to promote voluntary participation and minimize recall bias. All procedures adhered to ethical guidelines, ensuring data accuracy and patient comfort. Responses were double-entered into a secure electronic database, with 10% of entries randomly verified for discrepancies. This process achieved > 98% data completeness and consistency, with any errors resolved by consensus.

Data analysis

The data were screened for completeness, entered into Epi-Data version 3.1, and exported to SPSS version 27.0. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations, were used to summarize participant characteristics. Factors associated with patients’ knowledge and perceptions regarding informed consent for surgery were identified using binary logistic regression. Variables with a p-value < 0.25 in the bivariate analysis were entered into the multivariable model to control for confounding. The strength of associations was expressed using odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI), and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Model fitness was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (p > 0.05).

Results

Participants socio-demographic and clinical characteristics

Of the 422 eligible elective postoperative patients invited, 412 participated, yielding a response rate of 97.6%. As shown in Table 1, more than half, 52.7% (n = 217) were female; 35.4% (n = 146) were aged 18–33 years; 34.5% (n = 142) had completed primary education; 56.3% (n = 232) were married; and 52.4% (n = 216) resided in rural areas. The most common ethnic groups were Amhara 33.7% (n = 139) and Oromo (26.9% (n = 111); 41.0% (n = 169) identified as Orthodox Christians. Clinically, 61.2% (n = 252) had no prior surgical history; among those with prior surgery, orthopedic procedures were most common, 28.4% (n = 117). Notably, 45.9% (n = 189) required language interpreters during informed consent.

Patients’ perception of the SIC process

As illustrated in Table 3, a significant proportion of participants, 86.7% (n = 357), reported a clear understanding of the postoperative recovery process. A majority 83.0% (n = 342) of patients viewed the consent process as meaningful clinical dialogue rather than legal formality, 75.7% (n = 312) felt comfortable refusing surgery, and 73.1% (n = 301) understood and discussed risks/complications. Predictors of adequate SIC knowledge

As illustrated in Table 4, binary and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to identify predictors of patient knowledge regarding SIC. After adjusting for potential confounders, multivariable logistic regression identified sex, education, prior surgery, and language comprehension as predictors of adequate knowledge (all p < 0.05; Hosmer-Lemeshow p > 0.05). Males exhibited significantly higher odds of adequate knowledge regarding SIC compared to females (AOR = 2.28; 95% CI: 1.43–3.64; P < 0.001). Participants with a college education or above were five times more likely to possess adequate SIC knowledge than those with lower education levels (AOR = 5.21; 95% CI: 2.55–10.63; P < 0.001). Similarly, prior surgical experience (AOR = 4.58; 95% CI: 2.75–7.63; P < 0.001) and speaking Amharic as a primary language (AOR = 1.83; 95% CI: 1.08–3.08; P = 0.024) were significantly associated with higher knowledge scores.

Predictors of adequate SIC perception

As presented in Table 5, binary and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to identify predictors of positive patient perceptions toward the SIC process. After adjusting for potential confounders, parenthood, residency, and knowledge level remained significantly associated with positive perception (P < 0.05).

Multivariable logistic regression identified marital status, urban residency, and knowledge as predictors of favorable perception (all P < 0.05; Hosmer-Lemeshow P > 0.05). Married patients had 1.79 times higher odds than unmarried patients (AOR = 1.79; 95%CI 1.16–2.78; P = 0.009); urban residents had 3.99 times higher odds than rural residents (AOR = 3.99; 95%CI 2.58–6.20; P < 0.001); and those with adequate knowledge had 1.64 times higher odds than those with inadequate knowledge (AOR = 1.64, 95% CI 1.02–2.62; P = 0.040).

Discussion

Optimizing patient knowledge and perceptions of surgical informed consent (SIC) extends beyond legal requirements and serves as a key driver of surgical outcomes and patient-centered care [4]. This study evaluated patient knowledge, perceptions, and underlying predictors of the SIC process within tertiary hospitals in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. Findings indicate suboptimal knowledge and perceptions shaped by sociodemographic (gender, marital status, education, and residence) and clinical factors (prior surgery, linguistic barriers). These results illuminate the knowledge-perception nexus, where socio-linguistic disparities exacerbate the gaps between institutional protocols and patient understanding, necessitating tailored, culturally sensitive strategies.

In this study, only 52.2% of participants demonstrated adequate SIC knowledge, aligning with Ethiopian studies in Dessie (46.9%) [18], in Jinka (44.4%) [19], and international data from Egypt (50%) [20] and Brazil (59.6%) [21]. This global pattern reveals a disconnect between form signing and true comprehension, heightening the risks of decision regret and disputes. Our evidence supports the shift to comprehension-validated models over rote disclosure [12].

Our multivariable analysis revealed a significant gender disparity in SIC knowledge, with males showing over two-fold higher odds of adequate knowledge (AOR = 2.28; 95% CI: 1.43–3.64; P < 0.001). This finding aligns with Kebede et al. (2023), reporting 77.2% inadequate understanding among Ethiopian [7] and study from the Sudanese [6]. Sociocultural norms, gender-specific literacy gaps, and communication biases likely perpetuate these inequities, thereby hindering equitable information processing among female patients.

The correlation between educational status and consent comprehension underscores the critical role of health literacy in low-resource settings [22, 23]. In our study, educational attainment strongly predicted SIC knowledge (AOR = 5.21; 95% CI: 2.55–10.63; P < 0.001), affirming the literacy’s pivotal role in low-resource settings. Higher-educated patients outperformed those with limited schooling, consistent with previous Ethiopian and regional studies [5, 6, 18]. These results suggest that, while formal education facilitates the processing of complex medical information, a significant knowledge gap persists for patients with lower educational attainment. Consequently, clinicians must prioritize tailored communication strategies and simplified informational tools to ensure that informed consent is truly inclusive and accessible across all literacy levels. Furthermore, patients with prior surgery had significantly higher odds of adequate SIC knowledge (AOR = 4.58, 95% CI: 2.0–4.8, P < 0.001) than first-time patients, consistent with prior studies [5, 24]. This reflects experiential learning from repeated preoperative counseling and postoperative education.

In the present study, language proficiency was a significant predictor of SIC comprehension. Specifically, primary language speakers had nearly twofold higher odds of having adequate knowledge than those requiring a language interpreter (AOR = 1.83; 95% CI: 1.08–3.08; P = 0.024). This finding is consistent with prior literature [6, 7, 24], which highlights the critical role of linguistic concordance in effective informed consent communication. Furthermore, within the context of linguistically diverse and developing healthcare systems, these results underscore that providing SIC in a patient’s primary language is essential not only for enhancing comprehension and autonomy but also for ensuring equitable and valid consent [25].

This study also assessed patients’ perceptions of the SIC process. The findings showed that only 57.8% of participants reported favorable SIC perceptions. This finding is comparable to those from Northeast Ethiopia (66.8%) [18], Saudi Arabia (51%) [26] and another Ethiopian study (40%) [5]. This pattern signals shortfalls in achieving shared decision making, potentially due to provider communication gaps, time constraints, or low health literacy. Validated and standardized protocols, such as teach-back and visual aids, can enhance comprehension-based consent [12].

Analysis of sociodemographic factors identified marital status as significantly associated with adequate perceptions of the SIC process, with married patients exhibiting higher odds of good perception than unmarried counterparts (AOR = 1.79; 95% CI: 1.16–2.78; P = 0.009), potentially reflecting greater social support. This finding aligns with an Ethiopian study reporting widowed/divorced patients at nearly fourfold higher risk of poor SIC perception than married patients (AOR = 3.92; 95% CI: 1.71–9.01) [18]. Robust social support from marriage may underlie these associations, as suggested by prior work linking spousal networks to improved patient experiences [27]. These results highlight marital status as a key sociodemographic factor shaping SIC comprehension, supporting tailored education for unmarried or widowed patients.

In this study, urban residence strongly predicted favorable perceptions of the SIC process compared with rural residence (AOR = 3.99; 95% CI: 2.58–6.20; P < 0.001). This aligns with prior Ethiopian studies reporting urban patients’ 2-3-fold higher odds of good SIC understanding, attributed to better healthcare access and education [6, 18, 24], which suggests that urban populations often benefit from superior healthcare infrastructure, higher educational attainment, and greater economic opportunities. These urban-rural disparities highlight the need for targeted SIC interventions in rural settings to bridge comprehension gaps [13].

Patient knowledge is a critical factor in the positive perception of care [19]. This study revealed a significant positive association between procedure-specific knowledge and favorable perceptions of the SIC process, with high-knowledge patients showing higher odds of good perception than those with limited knowledge (AOR = 1.64; 95% CI: 1.02–2.62; P = 0.040). This aligns with prior findings linking higher health literacy to improved SIC comprehension (e.g., ≥ 70% good perception scores) [5, 6, 9]. These results support targeted education on surgical indications, risks, and alternatives to enhance both knowledge and perceptual outcomes in perioperative care [8]. Furthermore, these findings imply that when healthcare providers effectively educate patients regarding surgical indications, risks, and alternatives, they not only fulfill legal requirements but also foster greater patient trust and satisfaction with the perioperative experience.

Clinical implications

These findings suggest that tailored preoperative education addressing sociodemographic barriers (e.g., low literacy, rural residence) could enhance surgical informed consent (SIC) knowledge and perception, as evidenced by higher odds in urban residents (AOR = 1.64, 95% CI 1.02–2.62, P = 0.040). Validated strategies, such as teach-back methods and culturally adapted multimedia, improve comprehension in diverse settings [6, 12].Routine integration into clinical workflows in resource-limited environments merits further evaluation to promote patient autonomy and surgical outcomes [11].

Strengths and limitations

This study provides rare empirical data on the sociodemographic determinants of SIC comprehension in a resource-limited setting, addressing a critical evidence gap in Ethiopia. Strengths include the use of an adequate sample size and multivariable logistic regression to identify robust predictors—such as education and residence, while accounting for significant knowledge-perception interactions. Furthermore, the application of a validated questionnaire ensured reliable measurement across diverse surgical specialties [9, 17]. Despite these strengths, several limitations must be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design conducted across three urban centers precludes causal inference and limits broader generalizability to rural or primary care settings. Additionally, the reliance on self-reported postoperative data via face-to-face interviews may have introduced social desirability and recall biases. Furthermore, focusing exclusively on patient perspectives omits the influence of provider competency and systemic institutional factors. Future longitudinal, multi-site studies incorporating dyadic surgeon–patient assessments are warranted to inform scalable interventions and a more holistic understanding of the consent process.

Conclusion

This study identifies key sociodemographic and clinical disparities—notably gender, educational level, residency, and linguistic barriers—as primary drivers of suboptimal surgical informed consent in Ethiopia. The significant association between higher knowledge levels and favorable perceptions (AOR = 1.64; 95% CI: 1.02–2.62) underscores an urgent mandate for culturally validated interventions. Integrating structured teach-back protocols and shared decision-making frameworks is essential to mitigate these barriers. Future research should prioritize the evaluation of these strategies in resource-limited settings to bolster patient autonomy and standardize the quality of surgical informed consent.
