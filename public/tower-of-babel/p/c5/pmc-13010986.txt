Influence of stepped support on older adults' internet insomnia intervention engagement and outcomes
Authors: Kelly M Shaffer, Katharine E Daniel, Philip I Chow, Karen Ingersoll, Lee M Ritterband
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile delivering care by the Internet holds substantial potential to increase access to behavioral insomnia treatment, sustaining user engagement is considered a challenge. Minimal human support may enhance intervention engagement and efficacy, and older adults may particularly benefit from additional support for otherwise self-directed Internet interventions.ObjectivesTest whether a human-delivered stepped support protocol improves engagement and outcomes using a fully-automated cognitive-behavioral therapy for insomnia program tailored for older adults (Sleep Healthy Using the Internet-Older Adult Sufferers of Insomnia and Sleeplessness [SHUTi-OASIS]).MethodsAdults aged 55 and older with insomnia (N = 207) were randomized to receive SHUTi-OASIS alone or with stepped support (SHUTi-OASIS+SS). SS could be activated at intervention Core 1 or Core 2 (of six total Cores) if a participant had not completed the Core within two weeks of it becoming available. Engagement metrics were tracked by the Internet intervention platform. Participants self-reported insomnia outcomes by survey and prospective online sleep diaries at baseline, post-9 week intervention period, post 6-months, and post 12-months.ResultsThere was minimal activation of stepped support (14 of 102 SHUTi-OASIS+SS participants). There were no consistent differences in engagement or insomnia outcomes found when comparing SHUTi-OASIS versus SHUTi-OASIS+SS participants, nor when comparing only low-engaging participants across each condition (n = 15 SHUTi-OASIS versus n = 14 SHUTi-OASIS+SS).ConclusionsIn this trial, most older adults engaged with the Internet intervention as instructed without the need for human support. Findings highlight the utility of highly-engaging Internet interventions for addressing older adults' healthcare needs.Study preregistrationThis trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (Identifier NCT03213132; https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03213132; first registered July 6, 2017).Keywords: Insomnia, Sleep problems, Older adult, Digital health, Internet intervention, Cognitive-behavioral-therapy for insomnia, Engagement

Introduction

Far more people need psychological services than can realistically be helped by traditional one-on-one care, which is especially true for the behavioral treatment of insomnia (Thomas et al., 2016). Internet interventions increase the accessibility of psychological services, but maintaining user engagement with these interventions is a critical problem (Bennett and Glasgow, 2009; Eysenbach, 2005). Meta-analyses indicate that Internet interventions that incorporate minimal support from humans often obtain larger effect sizes than self-guided Internet interventions (Barak et al., 2008; Spek et al., 2007), although this effect was not found in a meta-analysis of insomnia therapies (Furukawa et al., 2023). The use of human support may be especially important to help older adults stay engaged with Internet interventions. Although the adoption of digital technologies among older adults continues to rise (Pew Research Center, 2021; Faverio, 2022), there remain unique motivational, cognitive, and health-related barriers that may influence how older adults use Internet interventions (Wildenbos et al., 2018). Therefore, as a secondary aim to our U.S. national trial of a robust Internet insomnia intervention for older adults (Sleep Healthy Using the Internet – Older Adult Sufferers of Insomnia and Sleeplessness, or SHUTi-OASIS) (Ritterband et al., 2025), we evaluated a stepped support (SS) protocol of human contact designed to help and encourage participants to log into and complete the online program. First, we tested whether differences emerged on engagement or sleep outcomes on average at the condition level between the SS condition versus the intervention alone condition. Second, because participants were only stepped up to receive support if they demonstrated low early engagement in the intervention, we tested whether there were differences between low-engaging participants who triggered SS versus those in the intervention alone condition.

Methods

Participants and procedures

Participants were enrolled in the SHUTi-OASIS trial (NCT03213132; approved by the University of Virginia Institutional Review Board for Social and Behavioral Sciences #2017-0089-00) from across the U.S. by the Internet from May 2018 to April 2019 (Ritterband et al., 2025). Adults aged ≥55 were eligible if they met DSM-5 criteria for sleep onset and/or maintenance insomnia and had regular Internet access. Exclusion criteria included having another uncontrolled sleep, medical, or psychiatric disorder; receiving ongoing psychological therapy for insomnia; and having a recent change in prescription sleep medication. Participants provided informed consent prior to data collection. After baseline completion, participants were randomized to either the SHUTi-OASIS intervention, SHUTi-OASIS+SS, or an online patient educational control. For this analysis, only the two SHUTi-OASIS intervention groups are compared. Participants received intervention access for nine weeks, then completed post-assessment. Follow-up assessments were completed at 6- and 12-months post-baseline.

Intervention

SHUTi is a fully-automated interactive Internet intervention delivering cognitive-behavioral therapy for insomnia through six “Cores,” or lessons (Ritterband et al., 2017; Thorndike et al., 2008). SHUTi-OASIS was tailored to meet the unique technical and clinical needs of older adults (Ritterband et al., 2025). All participants could contact the research team for technical support; no clinical or therapeutic support was provided.

Stepped support protocol

Participants randomized to either SHUTi-OASIS group (i.e., with and without SS) received the same intervention; however, SHUTi-OASIS+SS participants were eligible to receive engagement support if needed throughout the first two Cores. Considerations regarding the SS protocol design are discussed in the Supplement. The first SS checkpoint occurred at Day 15, or two weeks after the participant completed their baseline assessment and received initial access to the intervention. If at Day 15 the participant had not yet completed Core 1, the participant was randomized to receive SS by phone or email (see Supplement for details). Personalized SS contacts provided: (1) notice that the participant did not complete the Core; (2) encouragement to log in and complete the Core as soon as possible; and (3) support by asking if there were any concerns or obstacles to completing the Core. If the participant still had not completed Core 1 by 15 days after the initial SS contact (i.e., by Day 30), a second identical SS contact was made to the participant. For all participants who completed Core 1, SS contact procedures were repeated for Core 2. Participants could receive up to two SS contacts at each Core; therefore, participants could receive a maximum of four SS contacts total.

Measures

Engagement

The intervention platform logged the total number of Cores completed, intervention logins, and sleep diaries completed during the nine-week intervention period. Those completing ≥4 Cores are considered to have completed the program (Batterham et al., 2017).

Self-reported insomnia severity

Participants self-reported their insomnia symptom severity at each assessment time point using the seven-item Insomnia Severity Index (Bastien et al., 2001; Morin, 1993) (ISI); higher sum scores indicate more severe insomnia (range = 0–28).

Sleep diary metrics

Participants completed 10 daily sleep diaries (Carney et al., 2012; Shaffer et al., 2023a) over two weeks at each assessment. Sleep onset latency (SOL), wake after sleep onset (WASO), total sleep time (TST), sleep efficiency percentage (SE; i.e., TST / total sleep opportunity), nightly awakenings count, and sleep quality (1 = “Very poor” to 5 = “Very good”) were averaged from the 10 diaries at each assessment (Shaffer et al., 2023a, Shaffer et al., 2023b).

Statistical analysis

Analyses were completed in R (v4.2.0) (R Core Team, 2021). Engagement metrics and insomnia outcomes were compared using two-sided independent samples t-tests between the SHUTi-OASIS alone and SHUTi-OASIS+SS conditions overall (full group comparisons) and between only participants with low early engagement that activated (or would have activated) the SS protocol. Significance was set at p < .05.

Results

207 participants were assigned to SHUTi-OASIS (age M = 66.4 [SD = 7.8], 64.9% female, 91.3% white; see the supplement for full participant characteristics). The flow of participants through the first two Cores is depicted in Fig. 1; further details are reported in the Supplement. Of the 102 participants assigned to SHUTi-OASIS+SS, 7 (7%) received SS to prompt Core 1 completion (see Fig. 1.a, 1.c). Eight (8%) of the 97 participants who completed Core 1 received SS to prompt Core 2 completion. Only one participant received SS contacts at both Cores, meaning 14 (14%) of the 102 participants assigned to SHUTi-OASIS+SS received SS contact. Of the 25 total SS contacts across both Core 1 and Core 2, 17 (68%) resulted in no participant behavioral response, and three (12%) resulted in a participant withdrawing from the study. The remaining five (20%) contacts resulted in a participant re-engaging with the intervention, specifically, motivating four separate participants to complete a Core. Of these participants, two went on to complete the intervention; the remaining two participants passively dropped out after completing the Core at which they were contacted.

Of the 105 participants randomized to SHUTi-OASIS alone, there were 15 low-engaging participants (14%) who, had they been assigned to SHUTi-OASIS+SS, would have triggered at least one SS contact (see Fig. 1.b, 1.d). Six participants would have activated SS for Core 1 only, six for Core 2 only, and three for both cores. Of these 15 participants, five (33%) went on to complete the intervention.

There were no differences in engagement between those randomized to receive SHUTi-OASIS with or without SS on any engagement metric or insomnia outcome (see Table 1). Although statistical power is limited due to the small sample sizes (given the widely successful use of the program across participants), there were also no differences between low engagers who received SS (n = 14) versus those who did not receive SS (n = 15), with one exception regarding nighttime awakenings at 6-month follow-up (p = .04).

Discussion

The majority of the older adults with insomnia participating in this trial engaged with a robust, fully-automated Internet intervention for insomnia without need for supportive contact. Moreover, the SS human support protocol in which participants who had not completed Core 1 and/or Core 2 in a timely fashion were contacted did not appear to encourage better engagement or sleep outcomes. About twice as many participants went on to complete SHUTi-OASIS during the nine-week intervention period after initially falling behind when they did not receive a SS contact (i.e., 2/14 in SHUTi-OASIS+SS versus 5/15 in SHUTi-OASIS alone). While prior studies have demonstrated that added human support increased engagement (Spek et al., 2007) and clinical benefit (Barak et al., 2008; Spek et al., 2007) relative to fully self-guided programs, our findings corroborate meta-analytic findings that human encouragement does not influence outcomes in behavioral insomnia treatment (Furukawa et al., 2023).

Although the strong early engagement rates demonstrate the significant potential for fully-automated Internet interventions to increase access to psychosocial care among older adults, it did limit our ability to directly test the impact of human support on engagement and outcomes. Although we did not observe statistically robust effects of SS in the small resulting sample, this does not mean it has no positive effects or that certain older participants may not uniquely benefit from this support. It is also possible that a different operationalization of SS, or SS in the context of target disorders other than insomnia, may be more effective. However, overall, our findings contradict common misconceptions that Internet interventions are a poor fit for older adults. It also corroborates prior research suggesting that such interventions are actually both feasible and effective for addressing older adults' healthcare needs (Evangelista et al., 2019; Kim and Lee, 2017). Adding human support to fully-automated programs limits their scalability and increases their delivery costs, so it is important to minimize human support protocols that do not increase the efficacy of Internet interventions. Findings suggest that robust, highly-engaging Internet interventions that have been designed specifically for older adult users may not require human support to keep older adult users engaged and benefitting from the program.
