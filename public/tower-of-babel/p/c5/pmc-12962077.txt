Protocol for a multicentre target trial emulation comparing ketamine and propofol in critically ill adults undergoing emergency intubation
Authors: Rafael von Hellmann, Ryan Ruiyang Ling, Michael Toolis, Tapan Parikh Aka Parmar, Ashwin Subramaniam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEmergency intubation in the critically ill adults is frequently complicated by haemodynamic instability and severe hypoxaemia. Ketamine and propofol are widely used for induction, yet high-quality comparative effectiveness evidence in real-world emergency department, medical ward, and intensive care unit (ICU) settings remains limited.ObjectiveTo estimate the causal effect of ketamine versus propofol as the principal induction agent for emergency intubation in critically ill adults and using a target trial emulation framework with competing risks survival analysis.Design, setting and participantsA retrospective multicentre cohort across the Monash Health network, emulating a hypothetical randomised trial. Adults (≥18 years) undergoing unplanned endotracheal intubation in the emergency department, medical ward, or ICU with subsequent ICU admission will be included.InterventionInduction with ketamine or propofol as the principal sedative-hypnotic agent.Main outcome measuresPrimary outcome will be in-hospital mortality, assessed from induction until death, hospital discharge, or 28 days, whichever occurs first. The secondary outcomes will be a composite peri-intubation adverse event, defined as haemodynamic collapse or severe hypoxaemia within 30 min of intubation.ConclusionThis target trial emulation will provide real-world causal evidence comparing ketamine versus propofol induction for high-risk emergency intubation, with the potential to inform practice, protocols, and future randomised trials.

Introduction

Emergency orotracheal intubation in critically ill patients carries a high risk of adverse events. Approximately one-third of emergency intubations lead to major adverse events, most commonly haemodynamic instability (20 %) or severe hypoxaemia (12.5 %). Affected patients experience significantly higher 28-day mortality (57.6 % vs 39.2 %; adjusted hazard ratio = 1.43), emphasising the need to optimise intubation practices for improved safety and outcomes.1

A key modifiable factor during intubation is the choice of induction agent for rapid sequence induction. Propofol and ketamine are two widely used induction agents with markedly different pharmacologic profiles. Propofol is a potent sedative-hypnotic but is known to cause vasodilation and myocardial depression, often leading to hypotension, particularly in unstable patients. Ketamine, in contrast, is a dissociative anaesthetic that stimulates sympathetic output, which may help maintain blood pressure and cardiac output during intubation. Due to these properties, many clinicians prefer ketamine in hypotensive or patients with shock, assuming that it provides haemodynamic protection compared to propofol. However, robust evidence is limited. For example, a large emergency department (ED)-based study in haemodynamically unstable patients found ketamine use was associated with significantly lower post-intubation hypotension, supporting ketamine’s haemodynamic stability property.2 Despite the possibility of haemodynamic stability conferred by ketamine in comparison to propofol, a retrospective study on trauma patients reported no difference in 30-day mortality between those intubated with ketamine when compared with propofol.3 No consensus or high-level evidence (e.g. randomised control trials [RCTs]) currently exists to definitively guide the choice between ketamine and propofol for rapid sequence induction in critically ill adults, especially in the intensive care unit (ICU) setting. Given the frequency and importance of intubation-related hypotension and hypoxaemia and their association with mortality, it is crucial to determine whether ketamine is associated with improved haemodynamic and clinical outcomes were compared with propofol in this context.

Conducting a RCT to compare induction agents in emergency scenarios may be logistically challenging. Instead, we aim to leverage observational data using a target trial emulation approach, designing the study as if it were a hypothetical randomised trial of ketamine compared with propofol. By explicitly formulating the research question in trial terms (eligibility, treatment strategies, outcome, follow-up, and analysis) according to the hypothetical randomised trial framework proposed by Hernán and Robins,4 and using causal inference methods, we aim to obtain reliable evidence similar to an RCT.5 This approach, combined with advanced statistical adjustment, will help mitigate confounding and bias inherent in non-randomised comparisons.

Limitations in emulating the target trial

Although the emulation closely mirrors the ideal target trial, several discrepancies remain inherent to the observational nature of the data. Treatment assignment is not randomised; therefore, exchangeability must be approximated through weighting and diagnostic checks. Some covariates relevant to induction agent selection (e.g. clinician preference, precise haemodynamic trajectory in the minutes prior to induction) may be incompletely captured, introducing a potential for residual confounding. Finally, real-world variability in co-interventions (e.g. fluid boluses, vasopressors) may not be symmetric among treatment strategies, whereas these would be standardised in an RCT. These limitations will be addressed through prespecified sensitivity analyses and detailed reporting of balance metrics.

Methods and analysis

Trial design

A retrospective, multicentre, observational study analysed as a target trial emulation, with a priori specification of eligibility, exposure strategies, time zero, outcomes, and analysis to approximate a pragmatic RCT.

Operational definition of “principal induction agent”

To reduce exposure misclassification, the principal induction agent will be defined using two criteria: Sedative-hypnotic administered during induction as recorded in the medication administration timestamp and the agent contributing >50 % of the total sedative-hypnotic dose equivalence (using standardised potency conversion, e.g. ketamine-mg equivalents vs propofol-mg equivalents). However, if both agents were to be administered in a mixed fashion (“ketofol”), the patients will be excluded from primary analysis but included in an additional sensitivity analysis as a separate exposure category, classified either by (a) first-agent-dominant or (b) dose-dominant strategy.

Data collection and follow-up

Data will be abstracted from clinical information systems, intubation records, and the Electronic Medical Records (EMR) using standardised forms with random audit for quality. Follow-up for the secondary endpoint is 0-30 min post-intubation. For the primary endpoint, patients will be followed from time zero until death, hospital discharge, or 28 days after intubation, whichever occurs first. Statistical methods

The primary analysis will estimate the causal effect of ketamine versus propofol on time to in-hospital death using a competing risks framework. The primary estimand is the subdistribution hazard ratio and corresponding counterfactual cumulative incidence of hospital mortality, accounting for hospital discharge as a competing event.

Analyses will follow a causal inference approach to estimate the effect of the induction agent on outcomes. We will estimate propensity matching scores using all pre-treatment covariates. Although stabilised inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) will be used initially, the primary weighting approach will be overlap weighting, which has superior statistical stability, minimises the influence of extreme weights, and targets the population in clinical equipoise. Entropy balancing and IPTW with truncation (1st-99th percentile) will be used in sensitivity analyses. Weight diagnostics will include assessment of weight distribution, effective sample size, and post-weighting covariate balance (target Standardised Mean Difference (SMD) <0.10).

Primary survival analysis and competing risks

Hospital discharge will be treated as a competing event rather than simple censoring as discharge is likely to be informative. Accordingly, the primary survival analysis will use a weighted Fine–Gray subdistribution hazards model to estimate the effect of induction agent on hospital mortality.

Overlap weighting will be used in the primary analysis to address confounding, with robust (sandwich) standard errors. Results will be reported as subdistribution hazard ratios with 95 % confidence intervals, alongside counterfactual cumulative incidence curves for hospital mortality under each treatment strategy.

Sensitivity survival analyses

Cause-specific weighted Cox proportional hazards models will be used as sensitivity analyses to assess robustness of the primary findings to alternative hazard-based estimands. These models will treat hospital discharge as a censoring event and will report hazard ratios with robust standard errors.

Where proportional hazards assumptions are violated, prespecified alternatives will include weighted flexible parametric survival models and accelerated failure time models.

Counterfactual survival curves

Counterfactual cumulative incidence curves for hospital mortality will be derived from the primary Fine–Gray model using overlap weights. These curves will represent the estimated cumulative incidence of death had all eligible patients received ketamine versus propofol.

Secondary analysis

The composite peri-intubation adverse outcome will be analysed using a single weighted outcome regression model. A weighted logistic regression will be fitted, and counterfactual risks under each treatment strategy will be estimated using marginal standardisation. From these counterfactual risks, odds ratios, risk ratios, and absolute risk differences with 95 % confidence intervals will be reported. This approach avoids reliance on multiple outcome models while preserving interpretability of effect estimates.

Missing data

We will perform multiple imputations by chained equations (≥20 imputations) for covariates under Missing at Random (MAR) assumptions, using Rubin’s rules to combine estimates. We will perform sensitivity analyses for missing data that will include the following: complete case analysis to assess robustness of the primary findings and Delta-based sensitivity analysis to test departures from the MAR assumption. We will also perform scenario-based Missing not at random (MNAR) analysis for key physiologic variables (eg, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment [SOFA] components, pre-intubation vital signs); Compare imputed with unimputed distributions to detect systematic bias. These analyses will allow us to assess the extent to which potential MNAR mechanisms may influence the estimated treatment effect.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses will be conducted to assess the robustness of the primary findings to alternative assumptions regarding confounding adjustment, outcome definition, and modelling strategy. These analyses are explicitly secondary, do not redefine the primary estimand, and are intended to evaluate the stability of the primary competing risks analysis.

Cardiac arrest sensitivity analysis

Cardiac arrest occurring after induction will be considered part of the causal pathway and will not be used as an exclusion criterion. In the primary analysis, post-induction cardiac arrest will be incorporated within the definition of haemodynamic collapse.

As a sensitivity analysis, we will evaluate alternative outcome specifications in which cardiac arrest occurring within predefined early time windows (e.g. ≤5 min post-induction) is classified as an immediate severe peri-intubation adverse event, without excluding affected patients from the analysis. This approach preserves causal consistency while allowing assessment of outcome definition robustness.

Counterfactual framework

Our analyses explicitly follow a counterfactual approach. For each individual, we aim to estimate the potential outcome that would have been observed had the patient received ketamine and had the patient received propofol, even though only one treatment was observed. Using inverse probability weighting (and overlap weighting in sensitivity analyses), we reconstruct a pseudo-population in which treatment assignment is independent of measured confounders. This allows estimation of the average treatment effect by comparing counterfactual survival curves (for mortality) and counterfactual risks (for peri-intubation adverse events) under each treatment strategy, consistent with the target trial emulation framework.

Discussion

This protocol addresses whether induction agent selection during emergency intubation impacts patient-centred outcomes in critical illness.3,7 By explicitly emulating a pragmatic trial and using robust weighting and diagnostics, the design aims to minimise confounding inherent to agent selection while preserving generalisability from routine practice. The multicentre network, precise exposure and endpoint windows, and prespecified sensitivity/subgroup analyses strengthen internal validity; notwithstanding these measures, residual confounding from unmeasured factors and exposure misclassification are recognised limitations.8,9 The findings are expected to inform airway management protocols and identify subgroups most likely to benefit from specific agents, while demonstrating the practical utility of target trial emulation for time-critical ICU interventions.

Conclusion

This multicentre target trial emulation will deliver comparative effectiveness evidence on ketamine versus propofol-based induction for high-risk emergency intubation in the critically ill adults. Results may influence induction agent selection and protocol design and provide a reproducible methodological template for ICU questions where randomisation is impractical.
