The mechanism of how adolescents’ physical self-esteem and psychological resilience influence sports participation: the chain mediating roles of exercise motivation and social support
Authors: Minghuan Tang, Bo Peng, Weisong Chen, Yuxin Liu, Hongshen Wang, Ting Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to examine how physical self-esteem and psychological resilience influence adolescents’ sports participation, with a focus on the mediating roles of exercise motivation and social support. The goal was to clarify the interaction between individual psychological resources and contextual social factors in shaping youth physical activity.MethodA total of 2,588 adolescents from 15 provinces in China completed validated questionnaires assessing physical self-esteem, psychological resilience, exercise motivation, social support, and sports participation. Structural equation modeling (SEM) was used to test direct and indirect pathways. Multi-group analyses were conducted to examine model invariance across gender, grade level, and residential background.ResultsPhysical self-esteem and psychological resilience positively predicted sports participation both directly and indirectly. Exercise motivation and social support served as significant mediators, with a sequential pathway from psychological traits to behavioral outcomes via motivational and social mechanisms. Structural invariance was supported across all demographic subgroups, indicating the model’s robustness and generalizability.ConclusionThe findings underscore the dual importance of internal self-perception and external support systems in promoting adolescent engagement in sports. By elucidating a chain mediation process, this study contributes to a more nuanced understanding of youth physical activity and offers practical implications for developing targeted interventions in school, family, and community settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13102-025-01315-y.Keywords: Physical self-esteem, Psychological resilience, Sports participation, Exercise motivation, Social support, Adolescents

Introduction

Sports participation is not only a crucial avenue for adolescents to promote physical health but also a key platform for their psychological development and social adaptation. However, whether adolescents actively engage in sports often depends on intrinsic psychological factors such as physical self-esteem and psychological resilience [1, 2]. Physical self-esteem reflects individuals’ evaluations of their physical appearance and abilities, directly influencing their attitudes and confidence toward sports activities [3, 4]. Psychological resilience, on the other hand, equips adolescents with the ability to adapt to difficulties, helping them overcome challenges and failures in sports [5]. Although previous studies have highlighted the importance of these two variables for sports participation [6, 7], the mechanisms underlying their combined effects remain underexplored.

Self-determination theory provides a theoretical framework to unveil this mechanism. According to the theory, exercise motivation acts as the direct driving force for sports participation, and its formation is influenced by both internal psychological resources (such as physical self-esteem and psychological resilience) and external support (such as social support) [8, 9]. Additionally, social support theory suggests that encouragement and recognition from family, peers, and coaches can not only strengthen exercise motivation but also directly enhance the persistence of sports participation [10]. Therefore, exercise motivation and social support may serve as critical variables linking physical self-esteem, psychological resilience, and sports participation through a complex pathway. However, existing studies have insufficiently validated this chain mediation mechanism.

Most existing studies focus on the isolated effects of these variables. For instance, research has examined the independent impacts of physical self-esteem or psychological resilience on sports participation [11–13], but has not adequately considered their combined effects or the specific pathways through exercise motivation and social support. Similarly, while social support and exercise motivation are recognized as key factors [14–16], their roles as chain mediators have not been thoroughly investigated in an integrated model. This fragmented research perspective limits our ability to design comprehensive interventions addressing both internal and external determinants of sports participation.

This study aims to address these gaps by constructing and empirically testing a chain mediation model involving physical self-esteem, psychological resilience, exercise motivation, and social support. Specifically, the study explores how physical self-esteem and psychological resilience influence adolescents’ sports participation through the sequential mediating effects of exercise motivation and social support. This research makes several novel contributions. First, it emphasizes the interconnectedness of psychological and social factors, moving beyond traditional reductionist perspectives to provide a holistic framework for understanding adolescent sports behavior. Second, it conceptualizes exercise motivation and social support as dynamic processes, highlighting their roles not only as mediators but also as mutually reinforcing mechanisms, thus offering deeper insights into their contributions to sports participation. Lastly, the study provides actionable intervention recommendations for stakeholders (e.g., educators, parents, and policymakers) to foster a culture of sports participation among adolescents.

By revealing the specific pathways through which physical self-esteem and psychological resilience exert their influence, this study offers significant contributions to adolescent development research. It underscores the transformative power of sports as a vehicle for self-empowerment and social cohesion. In the face of increasingly complex challenges for modern adolescents, understanding and addressing the psychological and social determinants of sports participation is not only a scientific imperative but also a societal responsibility.

Literature review and research hypotheses

The relationship between adolescents’ physical self-esteem, psychological resilience, and sports participation

Adolescence is a period of rapid physical and psychological development, during which sports activities play a crucial role [17, 18]. However, whether adolescents actively participate in sports is not solely determined by external conditions but is also deeply influenced by intrinsic psychological traits [1]. Among these traits, physical self-esteem and psychological resilience are core psychological variables that play key roles in initiating and sustaining sports participation [2].

Physical self-esteem, as an individual’s subjective evaluation of their physical image and capabilities, directly determines adolescents’ performance in sports contexts [19]. Research has shown that adolescents with higher physical self-esteem exhibit greater confidence and enthusiasm in sports activities [20]. They tend to hold positive attitudes toward their appearance and physical abilities, which further stimulates self-efficacy, enhancing their drive and willingness to explore [21, 22]. In sports environments, adolescents can receive positive feedback through continuous physical performance, reinforcing their positive evaluations of their abilities and creating a virtuous cycle [23]. This cycle not only promotes sustained participation in sports but also enhances adolescents’ self-image recognition, encouraging them to take on new challenges and engage in team collaboration [24].

In contrast, psychological resilience focuses more on an individual’s ability to adapt when facing stress and setbacks [25, 26]. Sports activities inherently involve competition and challenges, during which adolescents inevitably experience failure and pressure [27]. Adolescents with higher psychological resilience can respond to these situations more positively [28, 29]. They can adjust strategies to adapt to their environment and grow from setbacks. This adaptive capacity provides strong psychological support for sports participation, helping adolescents maintain motivation and perseverance in the face of difficulties [30]. More importantly, psychological resilience enables adolescents to rebuild confidence and set new goals, making sports activities a critical vehicle for strengthening resilience and achieving psychological maturity [31].

It is worth noting that physical self-esteem and psychological resilience do not function independently but rather interact and complement each other. High levels of physical self-esteem often provide foundational support for psychological resilience, enabling adolescents to restore psychological balance more quickly under stress [20]. Conversely, psychological resilience reinforces the stability of physical self-esteem, preventing self-doubt when facing external evaluations or challenges [32]. This interaction not only enhances adolescents’ psychological support in sports activities but also helps them maintain their enthusiasm and persistence in the face of difficulties and failures.

The combination of physical self-esteem and psychological resilience offers a dual support mechanism for adolescents’ sports participation. Together, they influence the entire process of sports participation, from the formation of behavioral intentions to the maintenance of actual engagement [9]. Through this psychological support system, adolescents can face challenges more calmly in sports environments while deeply experiencing the growth and value brought by sports activities [33, 34].

Based on this understanding, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis (H1)

Physical self-esteem and psychological resilience positively predict adolescents’ sports participation.

The role of exercise motivation in the relationship between physical self-esteem, psychological resilience, and sports participation

Exercise motivation is a critical driving force for adolescents to engage in sports activities, and its formation is influenced by various psychological factors [35]. Based on self-determination theory, exercise motivation consists of two dimensions: intrinsic motivation and extrinsic motivation [6, 7]. Intrinsic motivation drives adolescents to participate in activities out of interest and enjoyment, while extrinsic motivation is related to external factors such as rewards or social recognition [36, 37]. This motivational mechanism not only affects adolescents’ choices in sports behaviors but is also closely linked to their psychological resources.

Studies have shown that physical self-esteem significantly influences adolescents’ exercise motivation. Positive evaluations of their physical appearance and abilities encourage adolescents to actively engage in sports activities [38]. When individuals are satisfied with their physical abilities, the sense of achievement and self-affirmation they gain from sports further reinforces their willingness to participate [39]. For instance, sports activities provide adolescents with opportunities to showcase their physical capabilities. Through these positive experiences, adolescents build stronger confidence, which stimulates higher levels of exercise motivation [40].

Psychological resilience also plays a key role in this process. Adolescents with high levels of psychological resilience possess the ability to cope with challenges and setbacks, enabling them to persist in their goals and maintain momentum in sports contexts [41, 42]. When faced with competition failures or physical exertion pressures, adolescents with strong psychological resilience can regulate their emotions and strategies, continuing to derive satisfaction from sports activities [43, 44]. This psychological trait not only helps individuals overcome difficulties in sports but also enhances their interest and enthusiasm for engaging in physical activities.

Moreover, the formation of exercise motivation is influenced by both physical self-esteem and psychological resilience. Physical self-esteem strengthens individuals’ sense of competence, providing intrinsic motivation for sports participation, while psychological resilience enhances their ability to cope with stress, ensuring the stability of exercise motivation in complex environments [45]. For example, studies have found that in competitive sports settings, individuals with high physical self-esteem are more likely to recognize their strengths, leading to greater engagement in activities, while psychological resilience helps them recover quickly from failures and continue participating [46, 47].

Based on this understanding, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis (H2)

Exercise motivation partially mediates the relationship between physical self-esteem, psychological resilience, and sports participation.

The role of social support in the relationship between physical self-esteem, psychological resilience, and sports participation

Social support is an indispensable external psychological resource in the process of sports participation, influencing multiple stages of adolescents’ sports behavior. Studies have shown that social support, derived from family, peers, and coaches within a social network, provides emotional encouragement, informational guidance, and practical assistance [8, 48]. In sports activities, social support not only enhances adolescents’ adaptability to the sports environment but also subtly influences their psychological state and behavioral tendencies [49, 50].

Physical self-esteem is closely related to social support in sports contexts. Positive evaluations of one’s physical appearance can increase the willingness to seek and accept social support [51]. Specifically, adolescents with higher physical self-esteem are more inclined to interact with others, make friends through sports, or gain recognition from coaches, further reinforcing their physical confidence in these social interactions [52, 53]. Research suggests that these positive interactions not only promote adolescents’ participation in sports but also provide ongoing emotional support and motivation [20].

Psychological resilience also relies on social support to cope with the stress and setbacks inherent in sports activities. Social support serves as a critical external safeguard for psychological resilience, enabling adolescents to better utilize external resources when facing challenges [54]. For instance, family encouragement and support can help adolescents overcome self-doubt, while peer recognition and coaching guidance provide actionable suggestions [55]. These forms of support create an environment in which psychological resilience can flourish, allowing individuals to demonstrate greater stability and persistence in adapting to uncertainties in sports contexts.

Moreover, social support serves as a bridge between physical self-esteem and psychological resilience. Research has found that adolescents with high physical self-esteem are more likely to gain recognition for their abilities from others, which, in turn, strengthens their psychological resilience [56, 57]. Similarly, adolescents with higher psychological resilience exhibit positive interactions within social networks, creating a feedback loop that ensures sustained support [58]. This bidirectional relationship indicates that social support not only influences adolescents’ sports participation behaviors but also strengthens the connection between physical self-esteem and psychological resilience, providing deeper psychological and behavioral support for sports engagement.

Based on this understanding, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis (H3)

Social support partially mediates the relationship between physical self-esteem, psychological resilience, and sports participation.

The roles of exercise motivation and social support in adolescents’ sports participation

Exercise motivation and social support are two critical factors driving adolescents’ participation in sports activities, exerting profound influences on the formation and maintenance of behavior. Research suggests that adolescents’ initiative and persistence in sports are often shaped by a combination of intrinsic psychological drivers and external environmental factors [59, 60].

Exercise motivation, as the direct driving force for individuals to engage in sports activities, is influenced by various psychological resources. Studies have shown that when individuals hold positive evaluations of their physical image and abilities, they are more likely to experience a sense of achievement and enjoyment during sports, thereby enhancing their interest in the activity itself [40]. Simultaneously, exercise motivation is supported by individuals’ ability to adapt to setbacks and challenges. Adolescents, when facing difficulties in sports activities, often need to adjust their goals and strategies to maintain continuity in their behavior, a process closely tied to their level of exercise motivation [61].

Social support, as an essential environmental factor for adolescents’ sports participation, provides external reinforcement for initiating and sustaining behavior. Studies indicate that emotional support and guidance from family, peers, and coaches can effectively alleviate stress and anxiety experienced during sports activities [8, 48]. This support manifests not only in emotional encouragement but also in providing resources and advice, helping adolescents better adapt to and engage in the sports environment [62]. For instance, peer recognition can boost confidence in participation, while coaching guidance offers specific assistance for skill improvement [63, 64].

Exercise motivation and social support do not function in isolation in sports activities. Research shows that the enjoyment and sense of achievement adolescents gain through sports often strengthen their need for social relationships, prompting more proactive interaction with peers [65]. This interaction, in turn, reinforces their willingness to participate, providing emotional support and behavioral feedback for sports engagement. Moreover, social support in sports also works by offering specific guidance and encouragement, creating conditions for adolescents to maintain motivation over time [49].

In sports activities, adolescents must overcome not only technical challenges but also multiple pressures within group environments. Studies have found that psychological motivation in sports helps adolescents take a more proactive approach to these pressures, while social support provides the necessary social networks and external resources to enable this coping process [66]. For example, when individuals experience failure during sports activities, a supportive social environment can help rebuild their confidence and reinvest in the activity [67]. The combination of exercise motivation and external support forms a comprehensive force that promotes the development of sports behaviors.

Based on this understanding, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis (H4)

Exercise motivation and social support serve as chain mediators in the relationship between physical self-esteem, psychological resilience, and sports participation.

Gender-based psychological mechanisms in adolescent sports participation

In the psychological mechanisms underlying adolescent sports behavior, gender plays a subtle yet profound moderating role. Biological differences, combined with socially constructed gender roles, shape how individuals perceive their physical selves, respond emotionally, and navigate social relationships. Studies have shown that boys generally display greater confidence and initiative in sports, with higher physical self-esteem making them more inclined to engage in competitive and physically intense activities [68, 69]. In contrast, girls’ physical self-esteem is more vulnerable to external influences such as societal beauty standards and peer evaluation, often resulting in heightened sensitivity about appearance and reduced motivation to participate in sports [70]. This not only affects their judgment of physical competence but also influences how they position themselves within groups and form behavioral intentions.

Moreover, gender-based differences in psychological resilience and social support are equally pronounced. When faced with failure or pressure in sports, boys tend to adopt problem-focused coping strategies, using behavioral adjustments to maintain goal-oriented stability. Girls, on the other hand, are more likely to rely on emotional regulation and interpersonal support to seek psychological comfort and emotional balance [71]. Within the social support system, girls place greater value on emotional connections—such as companionship from friends or encouragement from family—whereas boys are more reliant on structured feedback, including guidance from coaches or recognition through performance outcomes [72]. These gender-specific psychological patterns not only shape the subjective experience of sports but may also alter the pathways through which physical self-esteem and psychological resilience influence participation. Therefore, conducting gender invariance testing in the analysis of adolescent sports behavior is essential—not only for uncovering the diverse psychological mechanisms involved but also for informing more targeted strategies to promote participation across different gender groups.

Materials and methods

Sample estimation and data collection

To ensure the appropriateness of the sample, this study followed an empirical rule commonly used in social science research, which recommends that the sample size should be 5 to 10 times the total number of questionnaire items [73]. Referring to previous population-based studies [74, 75], a stratified sampling method was adopted. A total of 2,800 students from grades 7 to 12 across 15 provinces and cities in China were selected as participants. The survey was conducted using questionnaires, which included the Physical Self-Esteem Scale (30 items), Psychological Resilience Scale (10 items), Sports Participation Scale (3 items), Exercise Motivation Scale (28 items), and Social Support Scale (12 items). Teachers assisted students during the questionnaire completion process, and the time was strictly controlled to be within 15 min.

The survey lasted for seven months, from April 1, 2024, to November 1, 2024, during which 2,800 questionnaires were distributed. To ensure data accuracy and research rigor, all returned questionnaires underwent strict screening. Questionnaires with missing responses, logical errors, or clear response patterns were deemed invalid and excluded. In the end, 2,588 valid questionnaires were retained, resulting in a high effective response rate of 92.43%. During the screening process, predefined criteria were strictly applied to exclude incomplete or inconsistent responses, ensuring high data quality and integrity. Measurements

All measurement tools used in this study were based on validated scales with good reliability and validity. The details of the specific tools are as follows:

Physical self-esteem

Physical self-esteem was assessed using the scale developed by Xu Xia et al. [76]. This scale comprises 30 items across 5 dimensions, designed to evaluate individuals’ self-evaluation and attitudes toward their physical appearance, physical functions, and body acceptance. The scale adopts a 4-point Likert scoring system, ranging from 1 (“completely disagree”) to 4 (“completely agree”), with higher scores indicating stronger physical self-esteem. Previous studies have demonstrated good reliability (Cronbach’s α > 0.80) and validity for this scale across different cultures and populations [77]. In this study, the Cronbach’s α was 0.952.

Psychological resilience

Psychological resilience was measured using the Resilience Scale developed by Campbell-Sills and Stein [78]. This scale includes 10 items aimed at assessing individuals’ adaptability and recovery ability in stressful or adverse situations. The questionnaire adopts a 4-point Likert scoring system, ranging from 1 (“completely disagree”) to 4 (“completely agree”), with higher scores indicating higher levels of psychological resilience. The scale has shown high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α > 0.80) and construct validity in various age groups and cultural contexts [79]. In this study, the Cronbach’s α was 0.946.

Sports participation

Sports participation was evaluated using the scale developed by Liang Deqing et al. [80]. This scale consists of 3 items designed to measure the frequency and intensity of adolescents’ participation in physical activities. The scale uses a 5-point Likert scoring system, ranging from 1 (“very poor”) to 5 (“very good”), with higher scores indicating greater levels of sports participation. Previous studies have shown good reliability (Cronbach’s α > 0.80) and validity for this scale across different cultures and populations [81]. In this study, the Cronbach’s α was 0.830.

Exercise motivation

Exercise motivation was measured using the scale developed by Liu Shaoqi [82]. This scale includes 28 items across 3 dimensions, designed to evaluate individuals’ motivation for engaging in exercise or sports activities, revealing the psychological and behavioral factors driving physical activity. The scale uses a 7-point Likert scoring system, ranging from 1 (“completely disagree”) to 7 (“completely agree”), with higher scores indicating stronger exercise motivation. Previous studies have demonstrated good reliability (Cronbach’s α > 0.80) and validity for this scale across different cultures and populations [83]. In this study, the Cronbach’s α was 0.962.

Social support

Social support was measured using the Social Support Scale developed by Chen Wei et al. [84]. This scale comprises 12 items across 3 dimensions, designed to assess the level of support individuals receive in social interactions and their subjective perceptions of it. The scale uses a 5-point Likert scoring system, ranging from 1 (“completely disagree”) to 5 (“completely agree”), with higher scores indicating greater levels of support. Previous studies have shown good reliability (Cronbach’s α > 0.80) and validity for this scale across different cultures and populations [85]. In this study, the Cronbach’s α was 0.908.

Data analysis procedure

Based on previous studies [86, 87], to ensure the reliability and validity of the results, this study adopted a structured data analysis procedure to explore the mechanisms by which adolescents’ physical self-esteem and psychological resilience influence sports participation, with a particular focus on the chain mediating roles of exercise motivation and social support. The specific steps are as follows:

Common method bias

To assess potential interference from common method bias, Harman’s single-factor test was conducted using principal component analysis. The results showed that the first factor accounted for less than 40% of the total variance, indicating that common method bias was minimal and did not pose a significant threat to the study.

Descriptive statistics

First, descriptive statistics were conducted for key variables, including physical self-esteem, psychological resilience, sports participation, exercise motivation, and social support. By calculating the distribution, mean, and standard deviation of each variable, the study provided foundational insights into the overall characteristics of the sample, laying the groundwork for subsequent analyses.

Correlation analysis

Referring to previous population-based literature [88, 89], correlation analysis was conducted to examine the relationships among physical self-esteem, psychological resilience, sports participation, exercise motivation, and social support. The results showed significant correlations among these variables, providing preliminary support for the proposed chain mediation model and laying the foundation for subsequent structural equation modeling (SEM) analysis.

Differences in key variables across demographic backgrounds

Independent-sample t-tests were used to examine differences in physical self-esteem, psychological resilience, sports participation, exercise motivation, and social support between categories of demographic factors, including gender, grade level, and family location. This approach allowed for direct pairwise comparisons between groups on each key variable.

Model fit evaluation

Before testing the hypothesized paths in the chain mediation model, the overall model fit was evaluated using multiple recommended indices, including the chi-square to degrees of freedom ratio (χ²/df), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI), Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR), and Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA). According to widely accepted criteria [90], a good model fit is indicated by χ²/df < 5, CFI and TLI > 0.90, SRMR < 0.08, and RMSEA < 0.08, with CFI/TLI > 0.95 and RMSEA < 0.05 indicating excellent fit. These thresholds were used to guide the interpretation of model quality throughout this study.

Path analysis

Within the SEM framework, path analysis was conducted to examine the direct effects of physical self-esteem and psychological resilience on sports participation, as well as the chain mediating roles of exercise motivation and social support. In the model specification, all latent constructs were represented by their respective dimension-level mean scores derived from the corresponding validated scales. Physical self-esteem was indicated by the mean scores of its five dimensions (physical self-worth, sport ability, physical condition, physical attractiveness, physical fitness); psychological resilience was indicated by the mean score of the scale, reflecting its unidimensional structure; sports participation was indicated by the mean score of the scale, reflecting its unidimensional structure; exercise motivation was indicated by the mean scores of intrinsic motivation, extrinsic motivation, and amotivation; and social support was indicated by the mean scores of its three dimensions (family, friends, others). These aggregated scores served as observed indicators in the SEM, enabling estimation of the shared variance underlying each construct.

Effect size testing

To evaluate the strength of the chain mediation effects, standardized path coefficients were calculated. Bootstrapping methods were applied to obtain bias-corrected confidence intervals, further confirming the significant chain mediating roles of exercise motivation and social support.

Structural invariance testing across demographic groups

To examine whether the hypothesized chain mediation model holds consistently across different demographic subgroups, multigroup structural equation modeling (SEM) was conducted for gender, grade level, and family location. A series of nested models were compared by imposing successive equality constraints on measurement weights, structural paths, covariances, and residuals. Model fit was evaluated using multiple indices (χ²/df, CFI, TLI, SRMR, RMSEA), and changes in CFI and TLI (ΔCFI, ΔTLI) were used to assess invariance. Following the recommended threshold of ΔCFI ≤ 0.01 and ΔTLI ≤ 0.01 [90].

This indicates that common method bias is not a significant concern in this study, suggesting minimal interference with subsequent analyses.

These results indicate that the hypothesized measurement structures are well supported by the data and meet widely accepted criteria for model fit.

Differences in key variables among demographic groups of adolescents

To investigate whether adolescents’ physical self-esteem, psychological resilience, exercise motivation, social support, and sports participation varied systematically across demographic groups, a multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) was conducted with gender, grade level, and family location as fixed factors. The analysis revealed significant main effects for each factor (all p < 0.001), while all interaction terms—including gender × grade, gender × location, grade × location, and the three-way interaction—were statistically non-significant (all p > 0.05), indicating that the demographic variables exerted independent effects on the outcome variables without interactive influence. This finding justified the use of independent-sample t-tests to further examine pairwise differences within each demographic category.

As shown in Table 5, male adolescents reported significantly higher scores than females across all five key variables. Measurement and structural invariance across demographic groups

To assess the generalizability of the proposed structural model across different demographic populations, we conducted multiple-group structural equation modeling (MGSEM) to test for measurement and structural invariance across gender, grade level, and family location. Following the recommendations of Hair et al. (2019) [90], a series of nested models were compared for each grouping variable, including:

configural models (no constraints),

metric models (equal factor loadings), and.

structural models (equal structural paths).

structural models (equal structural paths).

Across all three demographic variables, the results consistently demonstrated invariance at both measurement and structural levels. Specifically, changes in Comparative Fit Index (ΔCFI) and Tucker–Lewis Index (ΔTLI) across constrained models were all within the acceptable threshold of ± 0.01, indicating that neither the factor structure nor the path relationships differed significantly by gender, grade, or location. The fit indices of the unconstrained and constrained models all exceeded conventional cutoffs (e.g., CFI > 0.95, RMSEA < 0.05), suggesting a robust and well-fitting model across subgroups.

These findings confirm that the latent constructs and structural pathways among physical self-esteem, psychological resilience, exercise motivation, social support, and sports participation are equivalent across demographic groups. Therefore, the proposed model demonstrates strong generalizability and conceptual stability across adolescents of different genders, grade levels, and residential contexts.

Discussion

This study systematically examined the psychological pathways through which physical self-esteem and psychological resilience influence adolescents’ sports participation, with a particular focus on the mediating roles of exercise motivation and social support, as well as structural consistency across demographic subgroups. The results offer both empirical clarity and theoretical advancement in understanding how individual and contextual resources jointly shape physical activity behavior in adolescence.

Notably, significant group-level differences emerged across gender, school grade, and family location. Male adolescents consistently demonstrated higher levels of self-esteem, psychological resilience, motivation, and engagement in sports compared to females, reflecting well-established gendered patterns in physical activity and psychological traits during adolescence [31, 47]. Additionally, rural adolescents showed advantages across all measured variables relative to their urban peers, underscoring the role of environmental affordances and community structures in facilitating participation. Middle school students also exhibited stronger social support and higher levels of participation than high school students, suggesting the constraining effects of academic burden and psychosocial transitions during later adolescence. These findings highlight the enduring impact of structural and developmental conditions on adolescents’ access to and engagement in physical activity contexts [15, 64].

Beyond mean differences, the present study provides novel insights into the underlying mechanisms that connect internal psychological resources with behavioral outcomes. Both physical self-esteem and psychological resilience were not only directly associated with sports participation but also exerted indirect effects through exercise motivation and social support. These results extend prior findings that have often examined these constructs in isolation [1, 2, 4, 10], and instead demonstrate how motivational and relational processes operate in tandem. Exercise motivation emerged as a proximal driver translating self-based psychological attributes into active behavioral intention and execution, consistent with self-determination theory. Simultaneously, social support functioned as a reinforcing external condition that amplifies and sustains this motivational influence. Importantly, the identification of a chain mediation pathway—whereby motivation facilitates the perception or utilization of social support, further promoting sports engagement—adds conceptual depth to existing models and provides a more nuanced account of how internal and external factors are dynamically intertwined.

Crucially, the structural equation model demonstrated full invariance across gender, school level, and family location. Despite clear mean-level differences in key variables, the pattern and strength of relationships among constructs remained stable across subgroups. This finding confirms that the proposed pathways operate equivalently regardless of demographic background, offering strong support for the model’s generalizability. Whereas prior literature has tended to emphasize demographic contrasts in trait levels or behavioral outcomes, the present study advances the field by establishing the robustness of the structural mechanisms underlying those outcomes. The consistency of psychological functioning across diverse groups reinforces the relevance of self-esteem, resilience, motivation, and support as universally applicable levers for promoting adolescent sports participation.

In summary, the current study delineates a comprehensive, theoretically grounded framework that integrates individual resources and social-contextual factors to explain adolescent sports participation. It further clarifies how demographic differences manifest at the level of trait expression rather than underlying mechanism, thereby offering both explanatory depth and practical relevance for broad-based interventions targeting youth physical activity.

Limitations and future directions

This study provides valuable insights into the mechanisms through which physical self-esteem and psychological resilience influence adolescents’ sports participation. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged, and directions for future research are proposed accordingly.

First, the cross-sectional design precludes definitive conclusions about causal relationships among the studied variables. Although the hypothesized sequential pathway—from physical self-esteem and psychological resilience to exercise motivation, then to social support, and finally to sports participation—is supported by relevant theories and empirical literature, the temporal ordering of these constructs cannot be firmly established based on cross-sectional data alone. To further examine the plausibility of the proposed directionality, we tested two alternative mediation models: one reversing the sequence of mediators (i.e., social support preceding exercise motivation), and another specifying exercise motivation and social support as parallel mediators. Neither model outperformed the hypothesized structure; the reversed chain model yielded unsatisfactory fit indices (χ²/df = 4.381, CFI = 0.902, TLI = 0.895, SRMR = 0.059, RMSEA = 0.063), and the parallel mediation model showed only moderate fit (χ²/df = 3.158, CFI = 0.943, TLI = 0.932, SRMR = 0.045, RMSEA = 0.048), with weaker indirect effects. These results support the structural integrity of the current model. Nevertheless, longitudinal or experimental designs are essential for rigorously testing temporal sequences and exploring potential bidirectional or recursive relations between psychological resources, motivation, social connection, and behavioral engagement.

Second, the exclusive use of self-report instruments may raise concerns about response bias, including social desirability and recall inaccuracies. Although validated measures were used, future research should consider integrating objective indicators—such as wearable devices to assess physical activity levels—and multi-informant or behavioral assessments for psychological resilience and social support. Such triangulation would enhance the robustness and ecological validity of the findings.

Third, although the study sample was sufficiently large for structural equation modeling and subgroup analyses, all participants were drawn from a specific regional and sociocultural context. As such, the generalizability of the results to broader or more heterogeneous populations remains limited. Future studies should recruit more demographically and culturally diverse samples to examine whether the observed mechanisms hold across different sociocultural systems, institutional settings, and socioeconomic conditions.

Fourth, while exercise motivation and social support were identified as key mediators in the proposed model, other potentially important contextual or psychological variables were not included. Future research could broaden the explanatory model by incorporating school-level factors (e.g., institutional physical activity climate, access to sports programs), family-level influences (e.g., parental modeling, home-based encouragement), and digital influences (e.g., fitness-related media exposure, screen time, or use of health apps). Exploring these multilevel determinants may yield a more comprehensive understanding of how adolescents’ physical activity behaviors are shaped by their social and technological environments.

Fifth, although structural invariance testing confirmed the stability of the model across gender, additional subgroup differences—such as those based on academic performance, age cohorts, or urban-rural background—were not examined in the multi-group SEM framework. Future work should test whether the identified pathways remain consistent across these dimensions to guide more nuanced and demographically sensitive intervention strategies.

Finally, although the present study was framed within an adolescent developmental context, several components of the proposed model—particularly the role of psychological resilience, exercise motivation, and social support—may have broader applicability beyond adolescence. As prior research has suggested, self-determined motivation and perceived social resources continue to influence physical activity engagement across the lifespan. Future research could therefore explore whether similar mechanisms apply to emerging adults or other age groups, either through developmental comparative studies or cohort-sequenced designs. This extension would not only expand the theoretical reach of the current framework but also inform life-course approaches to promoting sustained physical activity.

In sum, despite its limitations, this study advances a theoretically grounded and empirically supported model of how psychological and contextual factors jointly influence adolescents’ sports participation. Future studies are encouraged to adopt longitudinal designs, diversify measurement strategies, integrate additional mediators and moderators, and expand the model to broader populations. These efforts will contribute to the development of more comprehensive, contextually grounded, and developmentally informed interventions that promote lifelong engagement in physical activity and psychological well-being.

Conclusion

This study explored how physical self-esteem and psychological resilience affect adolescents’ sports participation, identifying exercise motivation and social support as key mediating variables. The findings demonstrated that both psychological traits and social-contextual factors jointly shape adolescents’ engagement in physical activity, with chain mediation further revealing their dynamic interplay. Although significant differences were observed across gender, grade level, and residential background in the levels of key variables, multigroup structural equation modeling confirmed the invariance of model structure and path coefficients across all demographic subgroups. These results underscore the robustness and generalizability of the proposed model and suggest that strategies aimed at enhancing physical self-perception, fostering motivation, and strengthening social support may be broadly applicable across adolescent populations.
