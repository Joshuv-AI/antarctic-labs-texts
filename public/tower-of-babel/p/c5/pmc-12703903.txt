Ultrasonographic evaluation of gastrointestinal wall thickness in Korean raccoon dogs (Nyctereutes procyonoides koreensis)
Authors: Yoonju Choi, Myeongsu Kim, Jae-Ik Han, Kichang Lee, Hakyoung Yoon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ImportanceUltrasonographic reference values for evaluating the gastrointestinal (GI) tract in Korean raccoon dogs (Nyctereutes procyonoides koreensis) have not yet been established.ObjectiveThis study aimed to establish baseline ultrasonographic parameters for GI wall thickness and assess differences based on sex, gastric distension, body weight, and the presence of GI symptoms.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 42 Korean raccoon dogs (35 healthy, seven symptomatic). Total and individual layer thicknesses (mucosa, submucosa, muscularis, and serosa) were measured for the stomach, duodenum, jejunum, ileum (folded and interfolded regions), and colon. Statistical analyses were performed to assess the effects of sex, body weight, gastric distension, and GI symptoms on wall thickness.ResultsAll Korean raccoon dogs exhibited a five-layer GI wall and echogenicity similar to that of domestic dogs. Mean total wall thickness values were: stomach (2.77 ± 0.74 mm), duodenum (2.19 ± 0.31 mm), jejunum (2.06 ± 0.35 mm), ileum (folded, 1.75 ± 0.35 mm, interfolded, 2.31 ± 0.24 mm), and colon (1.06 ± 0.37 mm). Stomach wall thickness was greater in collapsed (3.31 ± 0.37 mm) than in distended (2.15 ± 0.52 mm) states. No statistically significant differences in GI wall thickness were observed based on sex or body weight. Symptomatic Korean raccoon dogs exhibited significant thickening of the duodenum, jejunum, and colon, along with increased mucosal echogenicity, loss of normal wall layering, and intestinal corrugation.Conclusions and RelevanceThese findings provide essential reference values for GI ultrasonographic evaluation in Korean raccoon dogs, with implications for diagnostic accuracy.

INTRODUCTION

Gastrointestinal (GI) ultrasonography is a non-invasive, cost-effective, and sensitive diagnostic modality widely used across animal species to evaluate GI disorders [1234]. Studies on the normal ultrasonographic appearance of the GI tract in dogs and cats have significantly advanced diagnostic approaches to GI diseases [123].

The Korean raccoon dog (Nyctereutes procyonoides koreensis), an indigenous wild canid of South Korea, has recently attracted growing interest in veterinary research, particularly in diagnostic imaging [456]. Although GI diseases, including parasitic infections, have been reported in this species [78], baseline GI ultrasonographic reference values for the GI tract have not been established, complicating clinical evaluation.

This study aimed to define the typical ultrasonographic characteristics of the GI tract in Korean raccoon dogs, with a focus on total GI wall thickness and sex-related differences. It also sought to analyze the relative thickness of individual wall layers, including the mucosa, submucosa, muscularis, and serosa. In addition, this study examined the impact of gastric distension on gastric wall thickness and analyzed the correlation between body weight and GI wall thickness. Finally, both quantitative (e.g., wall thickening) and qualitative ultrasonographic changes were evaluated in animals exhibiting GI symptoms.

METHODS

Animals

This retrospective study analyzed GI ultrasonographic data from 42 adult Korean raccoon dogs rescued at the Jeonbuk Wildlife Center between November 2021 and February 2025. The most common reasons for admission were sarcoptic mange (21/42, 50.0%), anemia (17/42, 40.5%), pressure sores (8/42, 19.0%), and trauma-related injuries (9/42, 21.4%). Some animals presented with multiple conditions. This study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Jeonbuk National University (Approval No. NON2023-156).

Of the 42 animals, 35 were classified as clinically healthy based on the absence of GI symptoms, normal fecal examination results, and negative findings for parvovirus, distemper virus, and the SNAP 4Dx Plus assay (IDEXX Laboratories, USA). This group included 17 females (mean body weight: 3.93 ± 0.68 kg) and 18 males (3.92 ± 0.76 kg).

Seven Korean raccoon dogs were categorized as symptomatic based on GI signs and abnormal fecal examination results. Diagnoses included hemorrhagic gastroenteritis with hematochezia (n = 1), Toxocara canis infection (n = 1), bacterial enteritis (n = 2), Cystoisospora infection (n = 1), Tritrichomonas infection (n = 1), and peritonitis secondary to bladder rupture (n = 1).

Ultrasonographic evaluation of the GI tract

Ultrasonographic examinations were performed on conscious Korean raccoon dogs positioned in dorsal recumbency without the use of sedation. Two high-frequency ultrasound systems (Aplio 300 and Aplio i800; Canon Medical Systems, Japan) equipped with 12 MHz linear transducers were used. Examinations were conducted by a senior veterinary radiologist with 10 years of experience and a veterinary radiology resident with 2 years of training.

Transverse ultrasound images of the stomach, duodenum, jejunum, ileum, and colon were obtained. The gastric body was classified as fully collapsed or distended with gas or ingesta. The duodenum, jejunum, and ileum were evaluated in collapsed regions, while colonic images were captured from segments distended with fecal material.

Qualitative evaluations included assessment of GI echogenicity, wall layering, echotexture, motility, luminal corrugation, and peristaltic activity. Wall thicknesses of the total GI wall and individual layers (mucosa, submucosa, muscularis, and serosa) were measured using electronic calipers, following a trailing edge-to-leading-edge technique. Due to the characteristic wagon-wheel appearance of the ileum, both folded and interfolded regions were measured separately. Each segment was measured three times, and the median value was used for analysis.

Statistical analysis

The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess the normality of the data. Differences in GI wall thickness between sexes were analyzed using either an independent t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test, depending on data distribution. Normally distributed data were reported as mean ± standard deviation, whereas non-normally distributed data were presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs). Differences in total wall thickness across GI segments were evaluated using repeated-measures one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Bonferroni post hoc corrections. The thicknesses of collapsed versus dilated stomachs were compared using an independent t-test. The proportion of each GI wall layer relative to the total wall thickness was calculated and expressed as percentages (%). Differences among layers within a single segment, as well as differences in the same layer across different GI segments, were analyzed using repeated-measures one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni correction. The Greenhouse-Geisser correction was applied when the sphericity assumption was violated. Linear regression analysis was performed to assess the correlation between body weight and GI wall thickness. Differences in GI wall thickness between healthy and symptomatic Korean raccoon dogs were evaluated using either an independent t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 27.0; IBM, USA).

RESULTS

GI ultrasonographic findings in clinically healthy Korean raccoon dogs

In all Korean raccoon dogs, the GI wall displayed a typical five-layered structure on ultrasonography. The lumen, submucosa, and serosa were hyperechoic, whereas the mucosa and muscularis appeared hypoechoic (Fig. 1). A characteristic “wagon-wheel” pattern was consistently observed in the transverse view of the ileum (Fig. 2).

Impact of gastric distension on gastric wall thickness

Sixteen Korean raccoon dogs presented with a collapsed stomach, with a mean thickness of 3.31 ± 0.37 mm. Eighteen dogs had a dilated stomach with a mean thickness of 2.15 ± 0.52 mm. The walls of collapsed stomachs were significantly thicker than those of distended stomachs (p < 0.001, Bonferroni-adjusted).

Statistical differences in total wall thickness between different GI segments

The stomach exhibited the thickest wall thickness, and the colon the least, both with statistically significant differences (p < 0.001). Within the small intestine, the folded region of the ileum was significantly thinner than the duodenum (p = 0.01), jejunum (p = 0.02), and the interfolded region of the ileum (p < 0.001). No significant differences were found among the duodenum, jejunum, and interfolded ileum (p = 0.56–0.872).

Proportion (%) of individual wall layers relative to total GI wall thickness and statistical differences within each GI segment

The percentage of each wall layer relative to the total GI wall thickness is summarized in Tables 1 and 2 and Fig. 3. In the stomach, duodenum, and jejunum, the mucosa was the thickest layer, followed by the submucosa, muscularis, and serosa (p < 0.001 for all comparisons). In both interfolded and folded ileal regions, no significant differences were observed among the mucosa, submucosa, and muscularis (p = 0.263–0.812), although the serosa was significantly thinner than the other layers (p = 0.004–0.046). In the colon, the serosa was significantly thinner than the muscularis (p = 0.008); other comparisons among layers were not statistically significant (p = 0.089–1.00).

Correlation between body weight and GI wall thickness

Linear regression analysis revealed no statistically significant correlation between body weight and GI wall thickness for any segment: stomach (p = 0.858), duodenum (p = 0.547), jejunum (p = 0.352), ileum (folded; p = 0.438), ileum (interfolded; p = 0.434), and colon (p = 0.380).

Differences in GI wall thickness and ultrasonographic findings based on the Presence or Absence of GI symptoms

A comparison of gastric wall thickness between clinically healthy (n = 34) and symptomatic (n = 7) Korean raccoon dogs revealed no significant difference (p = 0.942). However, symptomatic Korean raccoon dogs exhibited significantly increased wall thickness in the duodenum (p = 0.012), jejunum (p < 0.001), and colon (p = 0.014). The mean duodenal wall thickness was significantly higher in symptomatic (2.60 ± 0.46 mm) compared to healthy counterparts (2.19 ± 0.31 mm, p < 0.001). Similarly, the jejunal wall thickness was significantly higher in symptomatic Korean raccoon dogs (2.51 mm, IQR, 2.41–2.90) than in healthy dogs (2.05 mm, IQR, 1.80–2.35, p < 0.001). Colonic wall thickness was also significantly greater in symptomatic dogs (1.33 mm, IQR, 1.02–2.44) compared to clinically healthy ones (1.10 mm, IQR, 0.90–1.21, p = 0.023).

In one case of bacterial enteritis, a segment of the jejunum exhibited marked muscular hypertrophy, with the muscularis layer nearly twice the thickness of the submucosa. Another case, involving enteritis secondary to uroperitoneum due to bladder rupture, demonstrated diffuse thickening of the small and large intestinal walls, increased mucosal echogenicity, partial loss of wall layering, jejunal muscular hypertrophy, and marked hypomotility with corrugation (Fig. 4).

DISCUSSION

Abdominal ultrasonography is widely recognized as a sensitive and non-invasive diagnostic modality for evaluating GI diseases in various animal species [9101112]. However, no baseline ultrasonographic parameters have been reported for the Korean raccoon dog (Nyctereutes procyonoides koreensis), despite the known occurrences of GI disorders in this species [78]. This study aimed to establish reference values for GI wall thickness and ultrasonographic features in Korean raccoon dogs, while also assessing the influence of sex, gastric distension, body weight, and GI symptoms.

All Korean raccoon dogs examined in this study demonstrated the characteristic five-layer GI wall structure, consisting of the luminal surface, mucosa, submucosa, muscularis, and serosa, similar to that in dogs [123]. No significant sex-related differences in GI wall thickness were identified, which may be attributed to the minimal variation in body weight between male and female Korean raccoon dogs. The stomach wall was significantly thicker than that of the small intestine, whereas the colon was the thinnest; these patterns are consistent with findings in dogs [123]. In contrast to domestic dogs, in which the duodenum is generally the thickest segment of the small intestine [2], Korean raccoon dogs exhibited duodenal thickness comparable to that of the jejunum and ileum. This finding may reflect species-specific anatomical traits, and further studies are needed to determine whether such differences have functional implications. Although no direct statistical comparison was conducted, the small intestine and colon in Korean raccoon dogs appeared thinner than those reported for domestic small-breed dogs in previous studies [2]. This disparity may be attributable to differences in body weight, as Korean raccoon dogs typically weigh around 4 kg, whereas small-breed dogs can weigh up to 15 kg.

During ultrasonographic examinations, the stomachs of Korean raccoon dogs were observed in distinct states—either fully collapsed or markedly distended—likely reflecting their feast-and-famine foraging behavior in the wild [1314]. Gastric wall thickness was significantly thinner in the distended state compared to the collapsed state, indicating that variations in gastric volume can influence the accuracy of wall thickness assessment, potentially leading to over- or underestimation [1516]. To minimize such variability, hydrosonography—a technique involving controlled gastric distension using a standardized volume of water or saline adjusted for body weight—could enhance measurement consistency and reliability. However, this technique was not utilized in the present study, and further research is necessary to evaluate its applicability and diagnostic value in Korean raccoon dogs [1718].

The characteristic “wagon-wheel” configuration of the ileum, commonly described in cats [1], was also observed in Korean raccoon dogs. Owing to this distinct morphology, measurements were performed separately for the folded and interfolded regions on transverse images. In Korean raccoon dogs, the ileum exhibited a greater proportion of submucosal and muscular layers compared to other segments of the small intestine. The submucosal thickness may reflect dense lymphoid aggregation, while the thickened muscular layer could be associated with intensified peristaltic activity near the ileocecal junction [1]. However, a more comprehensive understanding of these structural features requires further investigation, including histopathological analysis.

No significant correlation was observed between body weight and GI wall thickness, which may be attributed to the narrow range of body weight distribution among the Korean raccoon dogs evaluated in this study.

A comparison between clinically healthy and symptomatic Korean raccoon dogs revealed significantly increased wall thickness in the duodenum, jejunum, and colon. Gastric wall thickness remained unchanged. This lack of thickening may be attributable to luminal distension, which can lead to underestimation of wall thickness. Alternatively, the small number of symptomatic animals may have limited the statistical power of the analysis.

In cases of bacterial enteritis or inflammation associated with uroperitoneum, Korean raccoon dogs exhibited various ultrasonographic abnormalities, including muscular hypertrophy, generalized wall thickening, increased mucosal echogenicity, partial loss of normal wall layering, decreased peristalsis, and intestinal corrugation. These findings indicate that relying solely on wall thickness may be insufficient for diagnostic purposes; incorporating additional ultrasonographic features can improve diagnostic accuracy. However, the small number of symptomatic raccoon dogs limited the ability to establish definitive cut-off values for pathological wall thickening. Further large-scale studies are warranted to refine diagnostic criteria.

This study has certain limitations. Due to its retrospective nature, standardized transverse imaging of the ileum was not consistently achievable. Additionally, the absence of histopathological evaluation precluded confirmation of subclinical enteritis in asymptomatic animals and definitive diagnosis in symptomatic cases.

In conclusion, this study provides baseline ultrasonographic measurements of GI wall thickness in Korean raccoon dogs, establishing a reference framework for clinical assessments. The mean total wall thickness was 2.77 ± 0.74 mm for the stomach, 2.19 ± 0.31 mm for the duodenum, 2.06 ± 0.35 mm for the jejunum, 1.75 ± 0.35 mm for the folded ileum, 2.31 ± 0.24 mm for the interfolded ileum, and 1.06 ± 0.37 mm for the colon. Overall, the GI wall structure closely resembled that of domestic dogs, except that the duodenal thickness did not differ markedly from that of other small intestinal segments. In symptomatic animals, increased wall thickness and qualitative abnormalities, including altered echogenicity and partial disruption of wall layering, were observed. These findings underscore the importance of integrating both quantitative measurements and qualitative ultrasonographic features to improve diagnostic accuracy.
