Transmission network of Hepatitis C virus subtype 2a in Huazhou County, Shaanxi Province, China
Authors: Mengyan Zhang, Shaoqi Ning, Luqian Zhang, Gang Liu, Sa Chen, Yi Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHuazhou County has one of the highest rates of hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection incidence and prevalence in Shaanxi Province, northwest China. Understanding the characteristics of HCV transmission patterns in this area could help guide targeted prevention strategies. This study employed phylogenetic analysis and the construction of a molecular transmission network of HCV-infected people in Huazhou County to describe the predominant strains of HCV and identify factors associated with onward transmission.MethodsWhole blood samples were obtained from HCV RNA-positive individuals for sequencing of the non-structural protein 5B region. A maximum-likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree was constructed to determine HCV subgenotypes, and Bayesian phylogenetic analysis was employed to estimate the evolutionary history. The transmission network was constructed using the ML phylogenetic tree and pairwise distances. Logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with clustering in the transmission network.ResultsML phylogenetic analysis confirmed that the 61 sequences analyzed in the study belonged to subtype 2a. Bayesian phylogenetic analysis showed that the majority of subtype 2a sequences originated in the northwest of China and had descended approximately 8 to 20 years before sampling. Overall, 26.2% of participant sequences were grouped into phylogenetic network clusters. Multivariate logistic regression showed that individuals who had a history of blood transfusions and were living in Shi Village, Huazhou County, were more likely to form clusters within the transmission network.ConclusionHCV transmission in Huazhou County was predominantly associated with subtype 2a. Having a history of blood transfusions and living in residential Shi Village, Huazhou County, were factors associated with a high risk of HCV infection transmission. Background

Hepatitis C virus (HCV) is a bloodborne virus that is transmitted mainly through the reuse or inadequate sterilization of medical equipment, transfusions of unscreened blood and blood products, the sharing of injection equipment among injecting drug users and sexual activities that lead to blood exposure. In response to the global challenge posed by hepatitis C, the World Health Organization set an objective in 2016 to eliminate viral hepatitis as a public health threat by 2030. The Hepatitis C Elimination Action by 2030 was initiated by the National Health Commission of China in 2018, stressing comprehensive prevention interventions among key populations, nosocomial infection control, blood safety, and epidemiological investigations as its cornerstone strategies [1].

The epidemiology and drivers of hepatitis C infections vary across China’s provinces and regions. In certain provinces, the epidemic is driven by the sharing of needles and drug paraphernalia among injecting drug users, while poor injection safety and infection control practices in others have precipitated more widespread epidemics, affecting the general public and, notably, older patients [2–4]. Considering these regional disparities, the implementation of targeted interventions, tailored to the needs of each priority demographic and setting, is important for effective epidemic prevention and control.

Research into HCV genetic sequence data has enhanced our understanding of genotype distribution, migratory routes, and previously unreported transmission risk factors [4–6]. Analyzing clusters of highly similar HCV sequences allows for the identification of potential linkages that could uncover transmission routes among individuals with closely related viral strains. Moreover, the development of HCV transmission networks combining genetic and epidemiological data has been crucial for identifying transmission patterns and designing targeted interventions for high-risk populations, particularly among HIV/HCV coinfected patients [7–9]. However, such research has been limited in Shaanxi Province because of a lack of viral genetic surveillance data and underreporting of HCV infection risk factors. Conducting HCV phylogenetic and molecular research in Shaanxi Province therefore not only complements the understanding of regional HCV epidemiological characteristics, but may also guide public health strategies in comparable regions.

A spatial epidemiological analysis revealed geographical variations in the incidence and prevalence of HCV infections in Shaanxi Province between 2015 and 2021 [10], with a notable shift in the prevalence of cases across different regions. Surveillance data identified several high-prevalence areas, including Shangzhou District, Huazhou County, Zhouzhi County, Hi-tech District, and International Port District, necessitating specialized epidemiological investigations to uncover latent HCV transmission patterns. This study focuses on Huazhou County, Weinan Prefecture, which has a population of 265,200 and an urban per capita income of $3452. In contrast, Yanta District in Xi’an, the capital of Shaanxi and the largest district by population in the province, has over ten times the population (2.12 million) and more than twice the per capita income ($7820) of Huazhou County (Fig. 1).

A survey conducted prior to the COVID-19 pandemic in 2019 suggested that neither injection nor high-risk sexual behaviors were the main contributors to the local HCV epidemic. To understand the genetic diversity and transmission dynamics of HCV in this region, the non-structure protein 5B (NS5B) region of the HCV genome was selected for sequencing. This region is commonly used for HCV genotyping [11–13], allowing for the detection of both recent and older transmission events [14], and facilitating phylogenetic analysis to identify molecular clusters [7, 11, 13]. Through phylogenetic analyses and the construction of a transmission network among HCV infections, this study aims to understand the characteristics of the local HCV epidemic and develop targeted prevention and control strategies.

Materials and methods

Study subjects and sample collection

Research on the risk factors and prevention strategies for viral hepatitis C in Huazhou County, Weinan Prefecture was approved and initialed in 2019 by the Department of Science and Technology of Shaanxi Province. A case-control study was conducted between April 23 and 27, 2023 in Bei, Er, Yao, Ji, Dong, Nan, and Shi villages of Huazhou County. A total of 64 patients diagnosed with hepatitis C between January 2020 and April 2023 were selected from the China Information System for Prevention and Control. In addition, 194 local controls were selected from the same residential village as the patients. All participants provided written informed consent and were interviewed face-to-face to gather epidemiological data on demographic variables such as gender, age, and marital status. Whole blood samples were collected from both patients and controls.

Anti-HCV antibody and HCV RNA detection

A total of 258 samples collected from 64 patients and 194 controls were analyzed to detect hepatitis C (Fig. 2). Control plasma samples were pre-screened to detect anti-HCV antibodies using a commercial enzyme immunoassay kit (InTec Products, Xiamen, China). Plasma samples from patients and controls that were anti-HCV-positive were tested for HCV RNA in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions using standard controls (Sansure Biotech, Changsha, China). HCV RNA-positive samples were then sequenced and analyzed for genotyping.

HCV genotyping and phylogenetic analyses

HCV RNA was extracted from 200 µL plasma using an Ex-DNA/RNA kit (TIANLONG, Xi’an, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A nested-polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was employed to amplify the HCV NS5B fragment (H77: 8332–8673 nt, Fig. 3A) using a One-Step RT-PCR Kit and Taq (Takara Bio, Dalian, China), as previously described under the following conditions: 50 °C for 30 min, 94 °C for 2 min, followed by 35 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 51 °C for 40 s, and 72 °C for 45 s, then a final cycle at 72 °C for 10 min [11]. Positive PCR products were sent to TSINGKE Biotech Co Ltd (Xi’an, China) for purifying and sequencing using an ABI 3730XL Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Waltham, USA). The sequences were trimmed, assembled, and edited using Sequencher 4.9. The HCV NS5B sequences obtained in this study are available in GenBank (accession number: PP457742-PP457802).

(A) Maximum clade credibility tree estimated for subtype 2a sequences in the partial non-structural protein 5B (NS5B) regions, corresponding to nucleotides numbered 8332–8673 nt in the H77 genome. The tree includes three groups of sequences: 61 from the study individuals (red), 23 previously reported from Shaanxi province (magenta), and 25 similarity references. Branch length represents the evolutionary ages measured by the grids corresponding to a reverse timescale at the base of each tree, from the sampling time (right) to the past (left). Branches are colored according to their geographic origins, indicated in the upper left. Posterior probabilities of ≥ 0.70 are shown at the respective nodes in black. Node ages with 95% high posterior density intervals are shown at the lineages of the Huazhou sequence in blue. (B) Transmission network of HCV subtype 2a of Huazhou County, Weinan Prefecture

A BLAST search was performed against the HCV databases (https://www.HCV.lanl.gov/) to identify the top ten sequences with the highest homology to each of the Huazhou sequences. Seventeen subtype reference sequences representing the major HCV subtypes were thus downloaded from this database. HCValign (Los Alamos National Laboratory) was then used to align the NS5B sequences [15]. HCV genotypes were classified through phylogenetic analysis, for which a maximum-likelihood (ML) tree was constructed with 1000 bootstraps in FastTree v2.3 [16] using the general time reversible (GTR) model. The proportion of invariable sites and gamma plus invariant sites distributed rate heterogeneity (GTR + I + G) model was used, based on the Shimodaira-Hasegawa test. Sequences from this study clustered with reference sequences with good bootstrap support to determine subtypes.

Bayesian coalescent analysis was performed in BEAST (v1.10) [17]. The best-fit model GTR + G + I nucleotide substitution model was selected based on the Bayesian information criterion calculated by IQTREE2 [18]. Temporal signal evaluation was examined using root-to-tip regression in TempEst v1.5.3 [19], as well as phylogenetic molecular clock analysis by the Bayesian evaluation of the temporal signal [20]. An uncorrelated relaxed clock with a lognormal distribution under the Bayesian Skyline prior tree was chosen with the highest log marginal likelihoods (Table S1). Bayesian Markov chain Monte Carlo algorithm analyses were run sufficiently (200 million steps and every 10000 steps) to ensure stationary distribution and convergence of all parameters (effective sample size > 200). The evolutionary prior rate was set to 2.78E-03 substitutions per site per year (s/s/y) [11]. After discarding the 10% burn-in, a maximum clade credibility tree was generated using TreeAnnotator and visualized in FigTree program. The substitution rate per site per year was given in the text as the mean rate and 95% highest posterior density (HPD) interval. The most recent ancestor (tMRCA) was assigned the median of the estimated parameter.

HCV genetic transmission network

The transmission network was formed based on the phylogenetic clusters, where a phylogenetic cluster is defined as a group of sequences with genetically similar HCV strains. The construction of the network involved four steps. First, using the Huazhou HCV NS5B sequences, an ML tree was constructed using the method described above in FastTree v2.3. Second, transmission clusters were extracted from this ML tree using Cluster Picker v1.3 [21]. Transmission clusters were defined as those with node support criteria ≥ 90% and intra-cluster pairwise genetic distances ≤ 4 × 10− 2 nucleotide substitutions per site. Third, to establish linkages within a cluster, we calculated the pairwise genetic distances of all sequences within the identified clusters using the Tamura-Nei 93 model in Mega v7.0 [22]. The minimum pairwise genetic distances algorithm was used to define the linkage among individuals each other within a cluster [23]. Finally, the genetic transmission network was visually modified using the network package in R software. In the network, each node represents an individual or sequence, and links (edges) between nodes indicate possible transmission relations based on the genetic distances.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were carried out using SPSS v25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A multivariate logistic regression model was applied to determine the association between variables and phylogenetic clustering in the transmission networks. Being within the transmission network implies increased transmission and p ≤ 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Demographic characteristics and HCV subtype

Of the 64 patient samples, 29 tested positive for HCV RNA and were subsequently sequenced. Among the 194 controls, 46 samples were positive for anti-HCV antibodies, and 33 of these were HCV RNA positive. Ultimately, a total of 61 HCV NS5B sequences were successfully amplified and sequenced, comprising 29 sequences from patients and 32 from controls (Fig. 2). The demographic characteristics of participants having provided NS5B sequences are shown in Table 1. Women constituted 50.8% of the study population. The median age was 65 years (range: 45–83), with 68.9% of participants aged over 60 years. Most of them were married (88.5%, 54/61), of Han nationality (100%), and with a low educational level (defined as less than high school education; 68.4%, 57/61). Only one participant reported having had extramarital sex. The ML phylogenetic tree, which included 61 Huazhou sequences alongside 43 reference sequences for similarity, and 17 reference sequences for genotyping, identified all local NS5B sequences as belonging to subtype 2a (Figure S1).

Phylogenetic analysis

Time-scaled phylogenetic analysis was performed based on the similarity reference sequences, and subtype 2a sequences previously reported from Shaanxi [5] and Huazhou sequences. The root-to-tip regression analysis exhibited a positive correlation between genetic divergence and sampling time in TempEst (Figure S2). The estimated substitution rate was 3.49 × 10− 3 s/s/y (95% HPD interval: 2.19 × 10− 3-4.94 × 10− 3). The maximum clade credibility tree (Fig. 3A) showed sequences in group A originated mostly from northwest China, whereas those in group B came from various regions of the country, according to previous reports [11]. The analysis uncovered multiple independent introductions of subtype 2a into Huazhou County, with the earliest tMRCA estimated in 2003 in group B. In group A, Huazhou sequences formed six genetically distinct clades with tMRCA estimated dating between 2005 and 2015. It was noted that 14 sequences (93.3%) from Er village were concentrated in the two biggest clades, while sequences from Shi village were spread across all these clades. Three lineages were identified within the largest clade in Group A, each with a posterior probability ≥ 0.90, collectively comprising 14 Huazhou sequences.

Transmission network

Transmission network analysis was performed on 61 Huazhou subtype 2a NS5B sequences. Of these, 16 (26.2%) were connected to at least one other sequence in the four discrete transmission clusters (size ranging from two to nine sequences; Fig. 3B). Factors associated with increased odds of clustering in the transmission network included residence in the Shi Village (adjusted odds ratio = 5.308, 95% CI: 1.099–25.633) and a history of blood transfusion (adjusted odds ratio = 12.698, 95% CI: 1.123–143.580; Table 2). One large cluster comprising nine subjects, including 4 participants living at Shi Village, 3 participants living at Er Village, 1 participant living at Nan Village and 1 participant living at Yao Village was identified in the transmission networks. This cluster provided phylogenetic evidence for onward transmission with a high posterior probability of 1, as shown in Fig. 3.

Discussion

In this study, we characterized the molecular epidemiology of 61 HCV infections in Huazhou County, which has one of the highest HCV infection prevalence rates in Shaanxi Province, northwest China. All 61 strains belonged to HCV subtype 2a and formed distinct phylogenetic clades, indicating that HCV had been introduced independently during multiple events. NS5B sequence analyses suggest that this was followed by local transmissions approximately 8 to 20 years before sampling. Among the 61 HCV-infected patients, 26.2% were members of the Huazhou HCV transmission network. Patients with a history of blood transfusions and those living in Shi Village were more likely to form part of this transmission network. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first phylogenetic analysis of the HCV transmission network among the general population in Shaanxi Province. It provides helpful knowledge to better understand HCV transmission risk in high-prevalence regions and offers suggestions for public infection prevention strategies.

At present, the distribution of HCV genotypes in mainland China shows remarkable geographic and demographic differences. Subtype 2a, once predominantly transmitted through contaminated blood transfusions prior to the 1990s, has declined in prevalence after the 1992 ban on paid blood donations. However, it remains one of the most prevalent subtypes, alongside subtype 1b. Conversely, subtypes 3a, 3b, and 6a, which are associated with injecting drug use, have increased, according to recent studies [12]. The genotype distribution patterns in Shaanxi Province are less diversified than those in southern Chinese provinces, with subtype 2a being particularly prevalent [5, 12, 24]. Our study revealed that subtype 2a was independently introduced in Huazhou County in multiple events 8 to 20 years ago, with most strains originating in northwest China. These strains have contributed to the local epidemic over the last 20 years. Direct-acting antiviral treatments have shown over 90% efficacy in achieving sustained virologic response at 12 weeks in HCV subtype 2a infections [25]. Initiating timely direct-acting antiviral therapy for HCV subtype 2a-infected patients in Huazhou could effectively prevent further outbreaks and control the spread of HCV.

Subtype 2a has been identified in individuals engaging in intravenous drug use, unsafe medical practices, and high-risk sexual behaviors [11]. Reports have highlighted unsafe medical practices as the primary risk factor for HCV outbreaks across various Chinese provinces from 2011 to 2012 [14, 24]. Despite the government having significantly reinforced nosocomial infection control through the China Viral Hepatitis Prevention and Control Program over the last decade, the potential for unsafe medical practices in private clinics to contribute to the spread of HCV in Huazhou County cannot be ignored. It is suggested that relevant government agencies should enhance the regulatory oversight of healthcare facilities in rural areas to curb HCV transmission.

In our study, only 26.2% of participants from Huazhou County were grouped in subtype 2a phylogenetic clusters, a rate lower than those reported in similar studies among HIV/HCV coinfected patients in Guangdong (44.0%) [7] and Dehong (39.1%) [9], China, as well as among drug users in Vancouver, Canada (31.0%) [26]. Variations in these study populations may have accounted for the different results. Our study revealed an association between clustering in the transmission network and having a history of blood transfusions, which was previously recognized as a risk factor for acquiring hepatitis C [27, 28]. Although it is currently unlikely for individuals to contract hepatitis C through transfusions, our phylogenetic analysis indicates that individuals with a history of blood transfusions are at a heightened risk of transmitting HCV, emphasizing the necessity for targeted interventions for this demographic. Furthermore, the residence region was another factor associated with network clustering. Specifically, individuals from Shi Village exhibited a high degree of clustering within the transmission network, indicating close-knit genetic relationships and suggesting a potential social structure in closely related transmissions centered around Shi Village. Moreover, in the maximum clade credibility tree, sequences from Shi Village showed a notable spread potential among distinct subtype 2a phylogenetic clades in Huazhou. This finding emphasizes the urgent need for enhanced treatment and prevention in this community. Interventions that focus on traits linked to the potential risks of molecular transmission have proven effective in preventing further infections in settings where injecting drug use and sexual network data are unavailable [29]. Consequently, providing treatment as a preventive service to residents of Shi Village and individuals who have a history of blood transfusions could be an efficient way to prevent the spread of HCV.

This study has several limitations. First, the small sample size of the study limits the findings. The samples were collected for a case-control study on risk factors for hepatitis C infection. Approximately half of the patients’ genetic data could not be obtained because viral loads were low, likely because of prior antiviral treatment, which was not documented before blood sample collection. Consequently, some individuals diagnosed with HCV within the last three years may have recovered, affecting the availability of genetic data. Furthermore, the control group comprised more HCV RNA-positive samples than the patient group. People with chronic hepatitis C may not be aware of their infection status because it is usually asymptomatic. Expanding HCV testing and molecular epidemiology research is to the rest of the county is therefore recommended. Second, we aimed to investigate potential local phylogenetic relationships by including all previously reported HCV subtype 2a sequences from Shaanxi Province. However, because there has been limited reporting of HCV sequences over the past decade, our analysis did not identify clear local phylogenetic relationships. This highlights the need for more extensive monitoring and reporting of HCV sequences within Shaanxi Province to better understand regional transmission dynamics.

Conclusion

This study employed phylogenetic analysis and molecular transmission networks to investigate HCV transmission in Huazhou County, China. We identified subtype 2a as the predominant strain and showed that it was locally introduced 8 to 20 years ago. These findings suggest that comprehensive interventions should be strengthened for populations at a higher risk of transmission, including those living in Shi Village, Huazhou County, and those with a history of blood transfusions. Epidemiologic studies integration with molecular transmission networks would enable disease prevention and control agencies to better understand transmission of the HCV epidemic.
