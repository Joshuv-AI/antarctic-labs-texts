A deep-SVM hybrid framework with enhanced EEG feature engineering and SHAP-based explainability for Alzheimer’s classification
Authors: Frnaz Akbar, Yazeed Alkhrijah, Syed Muhammad Usman, Shehzad Khalid, Imran Ihsan, Mohamad A Alawad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate classification among Alzheimer’s Disease (AD), Fronto Temporal Dementia (FTD), and Cognitively Normal (CN) adults from EEG remains challenging. We propose a multi-class classification method that fuses interpretable spectral/connectivity biomarkers (band power, spectral entropy, -coherence) with compact temporal embeddings from a customized lightweight one Dimensional Convolutional Neural Network (1D-CNN). The fused features are reduced by Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and classified with Support Vector Machine (SVM). All data-dependent steps like Synthetic Minority Over Sampling Technique (SMOTE), z-scoring and PCA are fit strictly on training folds to prevent leakage. Hyperparameters including PCA dimensionality and SMOTE neighbors were selected via inner-loop grid sweeps that maximized macro-F1; full grids and class distributions before/after SMOTE (inner-train only) are reported in the Supplement. On OpenNeuro ds004504 (eyes-closed; ; 36 AD/23 FTD/29 CN) the model achieved 94.5% accuracy, macro-F1 , and AUC . Robustness was examined on two public datasets using the identical preprocessing/segmentation: (i) cross-condition testing on ds006036 (eyes-open recordings from the same participants) and (ii) zero-shot transfer to an independent OSF dataset with different instrumentation and demographics. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analyses provided global, class-wise, and subject-level attributions aligned with known electrophysiology (e.g., reduced -coherence, elevated ), supporting clinical interpretability. These results indicate that a transparent hybrid feature design can deliver accurate, leakage-safe, and explainable EEG-based differentiation of AD, FTD, and CN with preliminary external checks.Subject terms: Biomarkers, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Dementia is a pressing and growing global health concern, affecting an estimated 55 million individuals worldwide, a number projected to soar to 139 million by 20501,2. Alzheimer’s Disease (AD) and Fronto Temporal Dementia (FTD) are two prevalent dementia subtypes, each presenting unique clinical challenges. Early diagnosis is an important step to the process of determining the treatment plan, reducing the load on a caretaker and improving the overall quality of life of patients3. Classical methods of diagnostics like Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI), Positron Emission Tomography (PET) scans, or spinal fluid samples may yield useful data but are also generally expensive, invasive and are not suitable to large-scale or routine screening4,5. Electroencephalogram (EEG) signals unlike other methods enables the real-time monitoring of brain signals without invasive methods. Due to its fine time resolution it is able to record initial changes in the brain regarding the development and progression of dementia6,7. Such capability of the EEG in early dementia detection is illustrated in Figure 1 which highlights its applicability because it is an efficient tool with potential to offer scalable clinical neuro-diagnosis.

Significance of EEG signals for Alzheimer’s disease detection23–25.

A decrease in alpha and beta waves and an increase in theta and delta power disrupt brain connectivity, which is a key indicator of dementia8–10. EEG is considered a powerful diagnostic tool for the early diagnosis of dementia. But diagnosis through EEG signals remains challenging because of temporal variability, individual differences and these signals are easily affected by noise11. The last stage of dementia is easy to diagnose but separating the FTD and AD is important task. Graph Convolutional Transformers are utilized by Tigga et al.12 in order to distinguish between AD and FTD, but they utilized small and single-center dataset and reported only moderate accuracy ( 82–85%). Ghassemkhani et al.13 and Chetty et al.14 reported frontal–temporal asymmetry variations between AD and FTD, but their results showed limited consistency among different datasets. Current EEG based methods reported in literature have the following drawbacks (i) they mostly rely on spectral power features, (ii) they ignore the temporal aspects of neural signals, and (iii) they provide limited model interpretability. In order to mitigate the current challenges, our study suggests a hybrid Deep-SVM framework that incorporates both temporal and spectral representations into an explainable learning architecture.

For analysis of EEG signals, machine Learning (ML) based methods such as SVMs and Random Forests utilized handcrafted spectral features, producing limited improvements in accuracy15,16. Deep Learning (DL) based methods like CNNs and autoencoders can learn features automatically, but they require large datasets, and due to lack of interpretability makes clinical application challenging17–19. These limitations stress the need for methods that can balance interpretability and predictive performance. In this regard, hybrid frameworks that blend automated and handcrafted features are becoming increasingly popular among researchers. Recent studies have demonstrated performance gains in dementia and neurological disorder classification using such hybrid strategies20–22.

Conventional machine learning methods extracted spectral power, entropy, coherence, and Hjorth parameters as features and used SVM, RF, and LDA as classifiers. These methods achieved better performance but limited generalizability across different datasets8,15,16. Most works targeted AD vs. controls, with fewer directly contrasting AD and FTD, and often reported center-specific gains that did not transfer broadly. Convolutional Neural Network (CNN)/autoencoder models learn temporal–spectral representations directly from EEG, improving discrimination in some settings but typically requiring larger datasets and careful leakage control17,18. For multi-class dementia, small single-center dataset frequently lead to overfitting and unstable estimates. Methods that model spatial–functional structure e.g., Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs)/transformers over electrode graphs capture long-range dependencies and topology-aware features; however, reports on AD–FTD separation show only modest accuracy and limited replication in independent data12,26. Combining handcrafted biomarkers with deep embeddings has shown consistent, incremental gains by uniting domain priors with learned dynamics, but many pipelines remain opaque and inconsistently validated20–22. In this research, We target multi-class AD/FTD/CN differentiation (not only AD vs. controls). We fused interpretable spectral/connectivity biomarkers with compact 1D-CNN temporal embeddings in a single reproducible pipeline. Subject-wise, stratified nested cross-validation with all preprocessing, Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technnique (SMOTE), and Princpal Component Analysis (PCA) confined to the training folds has been performed. Furthermore, the proposed method provides multi-level interpretability including global, class-specific, subject-level SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) that maps to known physiology. These design choices directly respond to prior limitations in generalizability, clarity, and clinical interpretability.

EXplainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) techniques such as SHAP, Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM), and Layer-wise Relevance Propagation (LRP) have recently emerged as crucial bridges between algorithmic prediction and clinical reasoning27. In neurodiagnostic contexts, these methods allow clinicians to visualize which EEG components most strongly influence a diagnosis—offering not just a prediction but a rationale traceable to neurophysiological phenomena. With its connection to real neurological patterns, such as alterations in alpha band coherence and deviant theta activity, XAI is able to enhance clinician trust in technology and most importantly, facilitate the interdisciplinary usage of AI solutions in everyday clinical situations. Deep architectures that are mainly developed for interpretability i.e compact xEEGNet28, are a sign of the increased interest in making neuro-AI models explainable and trustworthy. Although advancement has been made, the majority of research to date still focuses on binary classification tasks (AD vs HC), rather than multi-class differentiation (AD vs FTD vs CN). Moreover, only a limited number of methods provide global, local, and temporal explanations–the three aspects recognized as key to clinical trustworthiness. Methodology

This study utilized a publicly available EEG dataset and proposed a robust and reliable methodology for automatically classifying subjects with FTD, AD, and CN. The pipeline integrates rigorous preprocessing, dual-path feature extraction (handcrafted and deep features), SVM classifier, and SHAP for post-hoc interpretability. Dataset description

We used the publicly available OpenNeuro ds004504 v1.0.8 dataset29, comprising resting-state, eyes-closed EEG recordings from 88 participants: 36 with AD, 23 with FTD, and 29 CN controls. Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores averaged 17.75 (SD = 4.5) for AD, 22.17 (SD = 8.22) for FTD, and 30 for CN subjects. The mean ages were 66.4 (SD = 7.9), 63.6 (SD = 8.2), and 67.9 (SD = 5.4) years, respectively. Median disease duration was 25 months (IQR 24–28.5). No dementia-related comorbidities were reported for AD participants. Recordings were acquired using a Nihon Kohden EEG-2100 system with 19 scalp electrodes (10–20 layout) and mastoid references A1–A2. The impedance was maintained below 5 k, signals sampled at 500 Hz, with sensitivity 10 V/mm, time constant 0.3 s, and a high-frequency filter cutoff at 70 Hz. Recording durations averaged 13.5 min (AD), 12 min (FTD), and 13.8 min (CN), totaling 485.5, 276.5, and 402 minutes, respectively. Data were available in raw and preprocessed formats; full details are in the original dataset descriptor29. Datasets’ description and comparisons with other public EEG datasets are provided in Table 1. For modeling, continuous EEG was partitioned into fixed-length epochs.

Cross-validation and external validation protocol

To ensure robust and unbiased performance evaluation, a nested five-fold cross-validation scheme was employed on the primary OpenNeuro ds004504 dataset. In this design, the inner loop performed hyperparameter tuning for the SVM (grid search over C and ) while the outer loop estimated final model performance. All preprocessing and data-dependent steps–including SMOTE oversampling, z-score normalization, and PCA dimensionality reduction–were applied strictly within each training fold to prevent data leakage.To prevent data leakage, each fold was structured to keep all epochs from a single participant within either the training or testing set. Accuracy, precision, recall, AUC, and macro-F1 metrics were then assessed to average the model’s performance across the outer folds. Furthermore, bootstrapping was used to determine 95% confidence intervals in order to evaluate the statistical robustness of final result.

Explainability with SHAP

To improve clinical trust, SHAP values42 were computed to attribute predictions to individual features:

This provided both global (dataset-level) and local (subject-level) interpretability, allowing neurologists to trace model decisions back to physiologically meaningful EEG patterns. For reporting, per-epoch SHAP values are (i) grouped by physiologically meaningful units (band region: frontal, central, parietal, occipital, temporal; and connectivity pairs for -coherence), (ii) averaged across an epoch set for the subject, and (iii) sign-preserved to indicate risk direction (positive pushes toward the predicted class, negative away). The clinician-facing report comprises three panels: (A) calibrated class probabilities (AD/FTD/CN); (B) a top-10 feature waterfall plot with band/region labels; and (C) a scalp map showing aggregated SHAP by band (e.g., reduced posterior power/coherence or increased frontal ). This design mirrors routine EEG review (band-specific, region-wise) and can be rendered as a one-page PDF (see Supplementary Fig. S1). Clinician-facing outputs include per-subject class probabilities and SHAP waterfall plots for the top-10 features, with scalp maps of band-specific SHAP attributions (see Supplementary Fig. S1). Overall, the proposed framework combines robust preprocessing, complementary handcrafted and deep features, and explainable classification, providing a reliable and interpretable tool for EEG-based dementia subtype detection.

Results and discussion

The experiments were conducted on the publicly available OpenNeuro dataset29, consisting of resting-state, eyes-closed EEG recordings from 88 participants: 36 diagnosed with AD, 23 with FTD, and 29 CN controls. Each recording was obtained using 19 scalp electrodes arranged according to the 10–20 system, sampled at 500 Hz, with session durations averaging 12–14 min per subject. This dataset offers clinically validated diagnostic labels and high-quality neurophysiological data, enabling a robust evaluation of the proposed framework (Figs. 5, 6, 7, 8).

When tested on this dataset, the suggested Deep–SVM hybrid model effectively and reliably distinguished between AD, FTD, and CN individuals. First, how signal preprocessing affected the quality of EEG data was investigated. Bandpass filtering and ICA-based artifact reduction were used to preserve physiologically significant brain oscillations while successfully suppressing noise and ocular artifacts. By lowering the possibility of mistakes brought on by lingering artifacts, these preprocessing techniques produced cleaner EEG data for feature extraction and classification. Every component in the suggested pipeline contributes differently, as seen by the ablation analysis in Table 4. Performance decreased when CNN-derived or handcrafted features were utilized separately, indicating that combining data-driven and domain-specific representations offers complementing discriminatory power. Due to class imbalance, removing SMOTE decreased performance, and leaving out PCA resulted in redundancy and poorer AUC values. The best overall performance was obtained by the full configuration, which combined CNN and handmade features, balanced sampling, and PCA before SVM classification. Even with a relatively small sample size, the hybrid model appears to generalize well to unseen data, as evidenced by the training and validation curves in Fig. 5, which show stable convergence without indications of overfitting. It is worth noting that the internal validation accuracy in Fig. 5 is slightly higher than the training accuracy. This pattern is expected in our setting due to regularization (dropout) and early stopping in the 1D-CNN encoder, together with strict nested subject-wise cross-validation. Moreover, SMOTE is applied only within training partitions, which can make the effective training distribution more challenging while improving generalization on held-out folds. The identified EEG patterns and their SHAP attributions align with widely recognised dementia biomarkers: reduced posterior power/coherence and increased frontal have been repeatedly linked to AD/FTD pathophysiology. We operationalise these findings into a clinician-facing report that includes: (i) calibrated class probabilities (assistive, not definitive); (ii) a top-features waterfall plot where each bar carries a band region label (e.g., “ power: frontal”); and (iii) a scalp map highlighting aggregated SHAP contributions per band. To aid actionability, we summarize interpretation rules in Table 7: SHAP sign indicates whether a feature increases the likelihood of a class (positive) or is protective (negative); band/region context provides clinical meaning (e.g., posterior or frontal ). In practice, this one-page output can be reviewed alongside conventional EEG traces and spectral summaries. We emphasize that the tool is decision support: it complements but does not replace clinical judgment, and it flags patterns for targeted review rather than issuing diagnostic labels in isolation.

While prior classifiers reported 76–91% accuracy under heterogeneous protocols, our hybrid model reached 94.5% accuracy with macro-F1 and sensitivity , with the largest gains on the minority FTD class. We attribute this to synergistic feature fusion, within-fold class balancing, and the RBF-SVM’s margin in a compact PCA space.

Overall, these findings demonstrate that combining handcrafted and CNN-derived features, balanced through SMOTE, dimensionally reduced by PCA, and classified with an SVM, results in a highly accurate and interpretable EEG-based dementia diagnostic framework. Through the integration of many SHAP analysis layers, such as global, local, temporal, and subject-specific visualizations, the framework provides profound understanding of the decision-making process and adds a remarkable degree of transparency to the model’s reasoning process. The suggested Deep-SVM framework is positioned as a therapeutically promising and explicable diagnostic tool for neurodegenerative illnesses due to its multi-layered interpretability and outstanding predictive performance. Future work should incorporate statistical significance testing on larger, multi-center datasets to further validate these results and strengthen claims of generalizability.

Conclusion and future work

We introduced an interpretable EEG pipeline that combines domain-informed spectral/connectivity biomarkers with lightweight 1D-CNN temporal embeddings, followed by PCA compression and RBF-SVM classification. Using subject-wise, stratified nested cross-validation with all preprocessing confined to training folds, the approach reached 94.5% accuracy (macro-F1 , AUC ) on OpenNeuro ds004504. Beyond internal validation, we examined robustness in two public datasets: (i) cross-condition testing on ds006036 (eyes-open) and (ii) zero-shot transfer to an independent OSF dataset differing in hardware and demographics. SHAP-based attribution consistently highlighted physiologically plausible patterns (e.g., -band coherence reduction and alterations), strengthening clinical interpretability.

These findings suggest that hybrid, leakage-safe EEG modeling can support reliable three-way differentiation among AD, FTD, and CN while providing transparent, clinician-readable rationales. Future directions include multi-center expansion to broaden demographic and hardware diversity, longitudinal analyses for progression tracking, and multi-modal fusion (EEG with MRI or fluid biomarkers). Translational work will focus on streamlined, real-time dashboards that surface per-patient SHAP summaries and uncertainty estimates to facilitate adoption in memory clinics.
