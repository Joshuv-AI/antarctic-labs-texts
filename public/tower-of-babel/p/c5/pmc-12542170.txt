The interplay of tumor suppressor p53 and oxidative stress in pancreatic β-cell apoptosis and insulin resistance in newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes mellitus
Authors: Amr Al-Baqeri, Molham AL-Habori, Ekram Al-Eryani, Riyadh Saif-Ali
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLoss of pancreatic β-cell function and increased β-cell death are central to the development of Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), and understanding the mechanisms behind β-cell failure is crucial for preventing or reversing the disease. While apoptosis and oxidative stress have been implicated in β-cell death, their precise roles remain under investigation. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the serum levels of the apoptotic factor tumor suppressor 53 (p53) and biomarkers of oxidative stress: malondialdehyde (MDA) and 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguansine (8-OHdG) in newly diagnosed T2DM, as well as determining the impact of p53, MDA and 8-OHdG and their combined interaction on both diabetic and metabolic syndrome parameters.MethodsThis case-control study included Yemeni male participants aged 30–45 years: 60 were normoglycemic subjects with fasting blood glucose < 100 mg/dL and BMI < 25 kg/m2 served as controls, and 52 patients with newly diagnosed (≤ 2 months), diet-controlled T2DM (fasting blood glucose > 126 mg/dL).ResultsCompared to control subjects, serum levels of p53, MDA, and 8-OHdG were significantly elevated in the newly diagnosed T2DM group (p < 0.001, < 0.001, and 0.002, respectively). Linear regression analysis revealed that β-cell dysfunction was associated with increased MDA (p = 6.3 × 10⁻⁶) and triglycerides (TG) (p = 2 × 10−4). Additionally, reduced insulin sensitivity, heightened insulin resistance, and elevated insulin levels were linked to p53 (p = 1.9 × 10−4, 0.001, 0.006), TG (p = 1.8 × 10−4, 0.007, 0.025), and BMI (p = 0.004, 3 × 10⁻⁵, 3 × 10−4). Fasting blood glucose and HbA1c levels were significantly influenced by MDA (p = 4.5 × 10⁻⁶, 3.6 × 10−4), p53 (p = 5 × 10−5, 2 × 10−4), and TG (p = 4.2 × 10⁻¹⁰, 2.7 × 10⁻⁹). The simultaneous interaction of p53, MDA, and 8-OHdG on diabetic traits. revealed synergy: the p53×MDA interaction amplifies insulin resistance, elevated insulin, reduced sensitivity, and higher FBG beyond individual marker contributions, while p53×MDA×8-OHdG elevates HbA1c.ConclusionThe results highlight the interplay of apoptosis and oxidative stress in inducing β-cell dysfunction and insulin resistance in newly diagnosed T2DM. Oxidative stress contributes to β-cell dysfunction, while p53 contributes to the development of insulin resistance, possibly by inducing oxidative stress.Keywords: Type 2 DM, Β-cell dysfunction, Apoptosis, Oxidative stress, P53, Malondialdehyde, 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguansine

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is the most prevalent multidimensional metabolic disorder in humans. It is estimated that over 537 million adults worldwide were affected by DM in 2021, and this number is projected to increase to 783 million by 2045 [1]. Type 2 DM (T2DM) is a metabolic disorder with multiple etiological factors, characterized by peripheral insulin resistance and progressive β-cell dysfunction, and its progression may increase the risk of various micro- or macrovascular complications [2]. β-cell dysfunction has been proposed as the primary pathogenic mechanism of DM and is critical for T2DM development [3–5]. Clinical and experimental studies infer that the pathogenesis of T2DM involves an initial phase of adaptation to insulin resistance, during which pancreatic β-cells expand their mass and increase insulin secretion. This is followed by a phase where glucose homeostasis is disrupted due to β-cell dysfunction, potentially β-cell death, and the onset and progression of DM [6–8]. Glucose plays a crucial role in both phases, initially enhancing insulin secretion and β-cell replication and later driving a vicious cycle of glucotoxicity that triggers β-cell failure and hyperglycemia [6, 9–11].

The ability to secrete adequate amounts of insulin is determined by the functional integrity of β-cells and their overall mass [12, 13]. It has been reported that there is an inverse association between β-cell mass and fasting plasma glucose, suggesting that glucose intolerance develops when the β-cell mass decreases by 50% of the normal level [13]. Several hypotheses for β-cell failure have been proposed, including cellular stress due to compensatory insulin overproduction, glucotoxicity due to chronic hyperglycemia, lipotoxicity due to chronic high dietary consumption, islet inflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and oxidative stress [5, 14–17]. Evidence suggests that apoptosis is pivotal in the decline of β-cell mass in T2DM [5, 17] and that β-cell apoptosis consequent to hyperglycaemia is one pathway responsible for the initiation and progression of DM [7, 14]. Apoptosis of human β-cells mainly occurs through the intrinsic or mitochondrial-dependent apoptosis pathway, in which pancreatic β-cells are activated by oxidative stress-induced cytokine production, triggering the activation of c-Jun N-terminal kinases (JNK) [18]. In addition, glucose-mediated apoptosis has also been associated with the activation of the extrinsic death pathway, such as an increase in cell surface Fas(CD95) expression.

Tumor protein 53 (p53) is a transcription factor that responds to various cellular stresses and regulates target genes involved in cell cycle arrest, apoptosis, DNA repair, and metabolism [19]. It exists bound up with the ubiquitin ligase Murine double minute 2 (MDM2), which inhibits its transcriptional activity and promotes its degradation. Under stress conditions, p53 activates a sequence of pro- and anti-apoptotic genes as well as mitochondrial alterations. Cellular stress induces the p53 protein phosphorylation and activates its transcriptional activity, leading to growth arrest or apoptosis. It has been postulated that p53 may play a significant role in metabolic diseases such as obesity and T2DM [20]– [21]. Moreover, adipose tissue from patients with T2DM also exhibited senescence-like features, confirming the role of p53 in regulating insulin resistance in T2DM [22]. p53 was reported to suppress insulin receptor gene expression and limit glucose intake by inhibiting the expression of GLUT1 and GLUT3, as well as insulin-dependent GLUT4 [23]. An earlier study has also reported that p53 promotes the expression of gluconeogenesis-related genes and enhances hepatic glucose production in human liver-derived cells with varying p53 statuses [24]. p53 was also reported to suppress glycolysis and promote oxidative phosphorylation in response to nutrient starvation and hypoxia [20].

Oxidative stress is a potential factor associated with β-cell destruction during the development of T2DM [25, 26]. Evidence has shown that oxidative stress is among the main factors responsible for insulin resistance, impaired insulin secretion and glucose utilization, abnormal hepatic glucose production, and ultimately T2DM [27, 28]. Oxidative stress, associated with increased metabolic activity, has been proposed as the driving force behind glucotoxicity. Hyperglycemia has long been considered a major factor in inducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) and increasing oxidative stress [29]. Obesity or excessive fat buildup within the pancreas also promotes oxidative stress, insulin resistance, and β-cell dysfunction in T2DM [30, 31]. Oxidative stress generally arises from excessive ROS production, affecting intracellular antioxidants’ neutralizing capacity [32]. Oxidative stress is associated with damage to many cellular processes that promote cell injury and impair their function. One prominent damaging consequence of ROS is lipid peroxidation that deteriorates cellular structure and function [26, 33], leading to the formation of malondialdehyde (MDA) (a product of polyunsaturated fatty acid peroxidation) [34, 35]. Moreover, ROS can directly attack DNA, causing mono- and double-stranded breaks, deletions, and nucleoside modifications, which leads to the production of an oxidation product, 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguansine (8-OHdG) [36, 37].

Loss of pancreatic β-cell function and increased β-cell death are central to the development of T2DM, and understanding the mechanisms behind β-cell failure is crucial for preventing or reversing the disease. While apoptosis and oxidative stress have been implicated in β-cell death, their precise roles remain under investigation. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the serum levels of the apoptotic factor tumor suppressor 53 (p53) and biomarkers of oxidative stress: malondialdehyde (MDA) and 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG) in newly diagnosed T2DM, as well as determining the impact of p53, MDA and 8-OHdG and their combined interaction on both diabetic and metabolic syndrome parameters.

Patients and methods

Study design, subjects and data collection

This case-control study was performed on 112 Yemeni male subjects aged 30–45 years, 60 healthy, normoglycemic subjects with fasting blood glucose < 100 mg/dL and BMI < 25 kg/m2 served as controls, and 52 newly diagnosed T2DM patients with FBG > 126 mg/dL were recruited during their visits to the Endocrine and Diabetic Clinic of Al-Thawra Hospital, Sana’a. All the enrolled T2DM patients were recently diagnosed within the preceding 2 months, controlled exclusively by diet. Participants with acute or chronic infections or severe medical conditions (malignancy, renal failure, liver cirrhosis, connective tissue disease, or chronic congestive heart failure) were excluded from the study. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Faculty of Medicine and Health Sciences, Sana’a University [MED310-15-02-2022]. Informed consent form was obtained from each individual after explaining the purpose and nature of the study.

Standard physical examinations, including clinical variables, were assessed as previously described [38]. The subjects’ height and weight were measured and BMI, defined as weight (kg)/height squared (m2), was calculated. Blood pressure (BP) measurements were taken from each patient’s right arm in the seated position using an Omron IntelliSense automatic blood pressure monitor after 10 min of rest. Two to three successive BP readings were obtained at 5 min intervals and averaged. Fasting venous blood (5 mL) was collected from each subject after an overnight fast of more than 10 h and divided into two vacuumed tubes: 4 mL into plain tubes for biochemical analysis and 1 mL into a K2EDTA tube for glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c). The serum from each sample was separated within 30 min and aliquoted into four Eppendorf tubes and immediately stored at −20 °C for biochemical analysis. Haemolysate was prepared immediately for HbA1c determination within 2 hours of blood collection.

Biochemical analysis

Fasting blood glucose (FBG), Glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c), triglyceride (TG), total cholesterol, HDL-cholesterol (HDL-c), and LDL-cholesterol (LDL-c) were measured on the Cobas c501 automated analyzer (Roche Diagnostic, Germany), using the respective Roche Diagnostic kits (Roche Diagnostic, Germany). Insulin was measured by an electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECL) on an Elecsys autoanalyzer e411 (Roche Diagnostics, Germany). Insulin resistance (HOMA-IR), insulin sensitivity (HOMA-S), and β-cell function (HOMA-β) were calculated using the Homeostasis Model Assessment (HOMA2) calculator v2.2, which is available from the Oxford Centre for Diabetes, Endocrinology, and Metabolism. The triglyceride-glucose index (TyG index) was calculated using the formula: Ln [TG (mg/dL) x FBG (mg/dL)]/2. Serum p53, 8-OHdG, and MDA were measured by enzyme-linked immunoassay (ELISA) kits (IBL International Company, Germany/Cell Biolabs Inc., USA).

Statistical analysis

The results were analyzed by the Statistical Product and Service Solutions (SPSS) for Windows, version 20 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The general linear model, univariate analyses (ANCOVA) adjusted for age were used to assess the differences of p53, 8-OHdG, MDA, diabetic parameters, and metabolic syndrome factors between T2DM and control subjects. Correlation analysis was conducted to identify contributing factors associated with serum p53 and oxidative stress (MDA and 8-OHdG) for inclusion in a linear regression model. We further assessed the impact of serum p53 and oxidative stress MDA and 8-OHdG (independent factors) on diabetic parameters (dependent variables) controlled for metabolic syndrome factors using hierarchical linear regression, first block stepwise (metabolic syndrome factors, BMI, age, TG, HDL-c) and the second block (8-OHdG, MDA, p53) using forward selection. Similarly, the combined interaction of p53, MDA, and 8-OHdG on diabetic parameters was analyzed. The significant differences were indicated if p-value was < 0.05.

Discussion

The results of our study showed that patients with T2DM exhibited significantly elevated serum p53 levels compared to non-diabetic controls, which aligns with a previous study reporting elevated p53 levels in T2DM [39]. In addition, Higher p53 corresponded with poorer insulin sensitivity, whereas insulin resistance, FBG, and HbA1c were positively associated with p53 levels. P53 has emerged as a pivotal regulator of DM and its severity in various cell types in both animals and humans with DM, and pharmacological inhibition of p53 exacerbates diabetic traits [20]. Earlier studies also linked p53 to obesity and T2DM pathogenesis [22, 40]. Like most cell types, β-cells are sensitive to activation of p53, as demonstrated by the development of β-cell dysfunction in transgenic mice overexpressing Δ40p53, a p53 isoform [41]. Moreover, p53-knockout animals display heightened susceptibility to streptozotocin-induced diabetes than control mice, implicating p53 in pancreatic β-cell death [42]. The recovery of β-cell populations has also been shown in p53 knockout mice, highlighting p53’s role in apoptosis-driven cell loss in pancreatic β-cell death and the development of DM [14, 41, 43]. Analysis of islets from T2DM patients revealed upregulation of multiple p53-pathway proteins [44]. In contrast, an earlier study in transgenic mice [45] and humans [46] argued against a direct role for p53 in pancreatic β-cell apoptosis.

Chronic hyperglycemia provokes programmed death of β-cells, leading to a marked loss of pancreatic β-cell mass [7]. The population of β-cells in human pancreatic tissues at autopsy reveal that β-cell numbers decline in proportion to glucose intolerance, from around 40% loss in those with impaired fasting glucose group to approximately 60% in T2DM patients compared with obese control subjects, implying that the β-cell deficit is an early process in the development of T2DM, primarily due to increase rate of apoptosis [7, 47]. In vitro and animal studies further show that sustained high glucose upregulates p53 in β-cells [14, 48]. The apoptosis in pancreatic β-cells due to hyperglycaemia has been attributed to several complementary mechanisms, including p53 translocation to the mitochondria for activation [49, 50], decreased glucokinase–mitochondrial binding [14], and shifts in mitochondrial permeability that release pro-apoptotic proteins and the onset of apoptosis [14, 49, 51]. Metabolic stress also triggers membrane depolarization via KATP channel closure, linking glucose toxicity to DNA-damage responses and apoptosis [52]; conversely, KATP openers can protect β-cells against several toxic insults [53, 54]. In pancreatic β-cells (RINm5F), exposure to chronic high glucose stabilizes p53 by suppressing Mdm2 expression and promoting p53-Mdm2 complex formation, which slows p53 degradation [55]. Other processes that have also been implicated in β-cell failure and hyperglycemia are endoplasmic reticulum stress and autophagy, which are regulated by p53 [56, 57]. This complex network was elucidated by linking DM-associated glucolipotoxicity with p53-mediated disruption of mitochondrial function [58]. This sequence of events highlights how the rate of intracellular glucose metabolism in β-cells, rather than hyperglycemia per se, orchestrates the apoptotic pathway [52].

Pancreatic β-cell failure in DM commonly stems from mitochondrial dysfunction coupled with excessive oxidative stress [59]. It is recognized that chronic hyperglycaemia drives multiple pathways, including mitochondrial dysfunction, advanced glycation end products (AGEs) formation, and protein kinase C activation, which all together promote ROS overproduction and oxidative stress [60, 61]. This undermines mitochondrial integrity and amplifies oxidative stress in β-cells. Elevated ROS not only disrupts energy production but also triggers p53 signaling, which regulates the expression of apoptotic, pro-inflammatory, and metabolic genes [25]. In cultured mouse β-cell-derived MIN6 cells, glucose-induced ROS accumulation activates pro-inflammatory mechanisms involving JNK and MAPKs, leading to accelerated apoptosis and reduced β-cell mass [62]. In pancreatic β-cell lines and insulin-producing islet cells, the pro-inflammatory cytokines IFN-γ and TNF-α further intensify this cycle by boosting ROS, stabilizing p53, and promoting apoptosis [14]. Several studies emphasize how both cytokines and oxidative stress act upstream of p53 to induce p53-mediated apoptosis of pancreatic β-cells [20, 63].

Excessive calorie intake in Ay transgenic mice precipitates oxidative stress within adipose tissue, increasing p53 levels and amplifying pro-inflammatory cytokine release; suppressing p53 in these models limits inflammatory cytokine expression and protects against developing insulin resistance [22]. Likewise, prolonged high-fat feeding in Wistar rats for 12 weeks elevates p53 in both adipose tissue and muscle, suggesting that p53 activation in the peripheral tissues of obese subjects under sustained hyperinsulinemia may serve as a protective homeostatic mechanism and represent a therapeutic target in T2DM [40, 64]. In another study, pancreatic β-cells exposed to excess free fatty acids (FFA), p53 activation triggers the upregulation of miR-34a, heightening β-cells’ apoptotic susceptibility and impeding insulin exocytosis, leading to impaired insulin secretion [65]. Furthermore, chronic FFA treatment diminishes AKT activity [66], normally responsible for phosphorylating MDM2 and limiting p53, thereby removing a critical obstruction to p53-driven apoptosis [67].

Recent experimental and clinical studies highlight oxidative stress as a central contributor to both the onset and progression of T2DM due to the inherent low expression of antioxidant enzymes in pancreatic islets [25, 27, 32]. The consequences of oxidative stress can have devastating effects on driving β-cell dysfunction during the development of DM. In our study, patients with T2DM exhibited significantly higher serum concentrations of 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguansine (8-OHdG), a marker of oxidative DNA damage, than healthy controls. Our results agree with several reports of elevated 8-OHdG in blood from T2DM patients [36, 68–70] and even prediabetic individuals, suggesting that oxidative DNA damage occurs with a minor elevation of blood glucose levels [68]. Similarly, our results showed that serum 8-OHdG was positively correlated with FBG and HbA1c, and negatively correlated with insulin sensitivity. Furthermore, several studies have shown that 8-OHdG levels are significantly elevated in urine samples from T2DM patients compared to healthy control subjects [71, 72]. Chronic hyperglycaemia can impair DNA repair pathways through enhanced poly(ADP) ribosylation, NF-κB activation, upregulated NADPH oxidase, and reduced heme oxygenase-1 expression [73, 74], thereby amplifying oxidative lesions. 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine has been commonly used as a sensitive biomarker of oxidative DNA base damage and overall systemic oxidative stress both in vitro and in vivo [75, 76]. Moreover, serum 8-OHdG was suggested to be a valuable indicator of the onset of DM and as an early biomarker for DM risk [69].

In our study, T2DM patients exhibited significantly higher serum malondialdehyde (MDA) levels than healthy controls, which is in agreement with earlier reports of up to a 6.8-fold increase in MDA in T2DM patients [77]. Similar elevations have been observed across multiple cohorts of T2DM [78–80] and in individuals with metabolic syndrome, suggesting that lipid peroxidation, and therefore oxidative stress, may precede overt T2DM [81]. We further found that rising MDA concentrations coincide with diminished β-cell function and are positively associated with both fasting blood glucose and HbA1c. Diabetes mellitus-related dyslipidaemia renders cell membranes rich in polyunsaturated fatty acids more susceptible to lipid peroxidation. Higher MDA levels reflect systemic oxidative damage and an increase in free radicals in the body [26, 80]. Despite methodological variations in its measurement across studies, MDA is considered a reliable biomarker of oxidative stress under various disease conditions [82].

Hierarchical regression analysis further distinguished the roles of each marker, revealed MDA to strongly contribute to β-cell dysfunction and elevated fasting hyperglycemia, consistent with studies showing that lipid peroxidation products disrupt islet cell membrane integrity and mitochondrial function, thereby impairing insulin secretion [83]. Conversely, p53 exerted an independent influence on insulin sensitivity, promoting compensatory hyperinsulinemia and insulin resistance through transcriptional regulation of cell cycle and apoptotic genes in insulin-responsive tissues, reinforcing its role in the metabolic dysregulation in T2DM pathophysiology [84]. Triglycerides and BMI amplified these effects across models, highlighting the interplay between adiposity, dyslipidemia, and oxidative/apoptotic pathways in T2DM. Moreover, the interaction analyses revealed synergistic exacerbation of metabolic dysfunction when p53 and MDA were simultaneously activated, resulting in amplified insulin resistance, hyperinsulinemia, reduced sensitivity, and elevated FBG beyond individual marker contributions. A three-way interaction, including 8-OHdG, further intensified HbA1c, suggesting a threshold-dependent mechanism where concurrent lipid peroxidation, DNA damage, and apoptotic stress accelerate chronic glycemic deterioration. Collectively, these findings support a two-stage model of diabetic progression. In the initial phase, lipid peroxidation impairs β-cell function and triggers hyperglycemia, while in the second phase, p53 activation affects peripheral insulin resistance, and cumulative DNA damage drives long-term glycemic deterioration. This echoes evidence that early T2DM involves reversible β-cell dedifferentiation under oxidative stress, with potential for restoring insulin secretion through alleviating metabolic stress [22]. It also emphasizes the potential of combined antioxidant and apoptosis-modulating interventions, targeting MDA, DNA repair mechanisms, and p53 activity, to preserve β-cell reserve, improve insulin sensitivity, and alleviate chronic hyperglycemia in early T2DM.

The limitations of the present study include: First, the study is cross-sectional and did not have a longitudinal design, which may not reflect the dynamic changes over time, limiting insight into how markers and β-cell function evolve. Second, the regional scope of the study may be affected by cultural and environmental factors since the study sample mainly consists of Yemenis, and hence the results cannot be generalized to other ethnicities. Third, all subjects were male; therefore, the findings may not be applicable to the overall population. Fourth, the sample size was small, limiting its statistical power for detecting associations. Fifth, the β-cell function was evaluated using the homeostasis model (HOMA-β), which primarily reflects β-cell secretory capacity rather than direct measures of cell health or pathology. Future studies should adopt a longitudinal design and expand the study to recruit a larger, gender-balanced, and multiethnic diverse population. Incorporating a broader panel of proinflammatory cytokines and employing sensitive biomarkers of early apoptosis (such as caspase activity assays and cytochrome c release) and oxidative stress markers (such as Nitrotyrosine and F2-isoprostanes) for more precise insights into the causative mechanisms of β-cell dysfunction and to help identify predictive markers of disease progression.

Conclusion

Our results emphasize the critical role of apoptosis and oxidative stress in inducing β-cell dysfunction and highlight the interplay between apoptosis and oxidative stress in newly diagnosed T2DM, which may have profound implications for understanding and managing the pathogenesis of T2DM. The observed elevations in p53 and lipid- and DNA-oxidation products (MDA and 8-OHdG) were closely correlated with hyperglycaemia, insulin resistance, and β-cell dysfunction, even at modest increases in blood glucose levels. Moreover, the association of p53 and MDA with FBG and HbA1c further suggests that hyperglycaemia may exacerbate the overall oxidative stress and cause apoptosis of pancreatic β-cells. Hierarchical regression revealed MDA to contribute to β-cell dysfunction and fasting hyperglycemia, while p53 independently impaired insulin sensitivity and promoted compensatory hyperinsulinemia. Interaction analyses further revealed a synergistic amplification of insulin resistance and glycemic deterioration when MDA and p53 were co-activated, with a three-way interaction involving 8-OHdG further elevating HbA1c. Collectively, these biomarkers may serve as potential predictors of T2DM onset, even with a slight increase in blood glucose levels, paving the way for more targeted prevention and management strategies in T2DM.
