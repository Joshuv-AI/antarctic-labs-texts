Mechanisms and clinical implications of radiation-induced endothelial dysfunction
Authors: Ka Hei Man, Shing Yau Tam, Mohan Huang, Helen Ka-Wai Law, Wen Zeng, Michael Tin-Cheung Ying, Yuanxi Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRadiation-induced endothelial dysfunction is a critical mechanism underlying long-term cardiovascular and multiorgan toxicity in cancer survivors after radiotherapy. This review integrates current evidence on the molecular pathogenesis, from initial endothelial apoptosis and senescence to the establishment of chronic pro-thrombotic and pro-inflammatory phenotypes involving various signalling pathways. Clinical data reveal that even low to moderate radiation doses can cause sustained endothelial injury, contributing not only to vascular diseases but also to common cerebrovascular and gastrointestinal complications. Advances in vascular imaging and circulating biomarkers facilitate earlier detection of subclinical damage. Promising protective strategies include statins, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, and pathway-targeted agents. Future research directions include the development of advanced preclinical models and the conduct of large-scale, prospective clinical studies to validate biomarkers and therapeutic interventions, ultimately aiming to integrate vascular preservation into personalized radiotherapy strategies.Keywords: Radiation, Radiotherapy, Endothelial dysfunction, Endothelial damage, Vascular damage

Background

Radiotherapy remains a cornerstone of modern cancer treatment and contributes substantially to improved long-term survival across multiple malignancies. However, as cancer survivorship continues to increase, delayed cardiovascular and multiorgan toxicities have emerged as major non-oncologic causes of morbidity and mortality among cancer survivors [1]. Increasing evidence suggests that endothelial dysfunction represents a central unifying mechanism linking radiation exposure to chronic vascular and tissue damage [2].

Endothelial cells constitute the inner lining of blood vessels and maintain vascular homeostasis by regulating vascular tone, blood fluidity, and permeability [3]. Given the endothelium’s essential role in vascular health and its documented radiation susceptibility, understanding the molecular mechanisms of radiation-induced endothelial dysfunction is critical for predicting and mitigating long-term vascular complications in cancer survivors. Endothelial dysfunction is characterised by impaired vasodilatory capacity, increased oxidative stress, and a pro-inflammatory and pro-thrombotic state, resulting in the disruption of vascular homeostasis.

The vascular response to radiation is dose- and time-dependent [4]. Acute effects (days to weeks), mediated primarily by endothelial apoptosis, manifest as increased vascular permeability, inflammation, and microvascular rarefaction. Chronic effects (months to years) involve sustained oxidative stress and endothelial senescence, leading to pro-thrombotic and pro-inflammatory phenotypes that accelerate atherosclerosis and fibrosis [2]. Understanding the mechanisms of radiation-induced endothelial dysfunction is essential for developing targeted preventive and therapeutic interventions to protect long-term health in cancer survivors [5].

While the biological effects of radiation are well described in the radiobiology literature, the impact of radiation on the vascular endothelium and its role in long-term complications remain relatively underexplored. This review focuses specifically on radiation-induced endothelial dysfunction as a key contributor to vascular injury, linking early cellular damage to later clinical outcomes. In addition, it summarises recent advances in diagnostic approaches and potential therapeutic strategies aimed at preserving vascular health. By bringing together current mechanistic insights with clinical evidence, this review highlights the importance of endothelial protection in improving long-term outcomes following radiotherapy.

Acute phase: oxidative stress and endothelial cell death

Apoptosis and autophagy

Apoptosis is a major mechanism of radiation-induced endothelial cell dysfunction and contributes directly to vascular injury. Ionizing radiation activates stress-response pathways, including p53 and caspase signalling, leading to endothelial cell loss, disruption of vascular barrier integrity, and reduced production of protective factors that maintain vascular homeostasis [6–9].

In response to radiation-induced stress, endothelial cells also activate autophagy, a cellular process that removes damaged proteins and organelles. Autophagy may initially serve a protective role by promoting cellular recovery. However, excessive or dysregulated autophagy can contribute to endothelial dysfunction [10]. Current evidence suggests that the balance between apoptosis and autophagy influences the severity of radiation-induced vascular injury and may vary according to radiation dose and the local tissue environment.

Clinically, several therapeutic targets against radiation-induced apoptosis and associated inflammatory diseases have been identified. DNA-dependent protein kinase (DNA-PK) and its component Ku86 can suppress radiation-induced apoptosis by modulating caspase activity and promoting anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 expression [11]. Basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF) protects endothelial cells from radiation-induced apoptosis in preclinical models, reducing pneumonitis in irradiated lungs and mitigating intestinal endothelial damage [12]. Angiopoietin-1 variants (COMP-Ang1) protect endothelial cells in multiple tissues, including the lung, intestine, and liver, from radiation-induced apoptosis [13]. These findings identify potential therapeutic targets for mitigating endothelial cell death.

Senescence

Senescence is a radiation-induced response in endothelial cells characterized by loss of proliferative capacity, reduced nitric oxide production, elevated inflammatory cytokines, and impaired angiogenic function [14]. Senescent endothelial cells express senescence markers (p16, p21) and exhibit β-galactosidase activity, reflecting an inability to form capillary-like structures [15]. Affected cells fail to support tissue regeneration and promote chronic vascular inflammation, promoting atherosclerosis and vascular dysfunction [16]. Minimizing radiation dose to normal tissues through optimized treatment planning remains the primary clinical strategy for reducing the burden of radiation-induced senescence.

Studies have identified microRNAs (miRs) as important regulators of endothelial senescence. miR-34a and miR-127 are upregulated in senescent endothelium and function as pro-senescent regulators that suppress sirtuin 1 (SIRT1) and forkhead box protein O1 (FoxO1) expression [17]. miR-34a is elevated in atherosclerotic lesions and induces senescence by inhibiting SIRT1, a class III histone deacetylase that normally protects against vascular aging [18]. miR-127 expression inversely correlates with SIRT1 and FoxO1 acetylation status, promoting senescence in atherosclerotic vascular disease [19]. These findings suggest epigenetic control mechanisms for radiation-induced senescence and identify potential targets for microRNA modulation or pathway activation.

Beyond DNA damage, ionizing radiation induces significant organelle dysfunction that contributes to endothelial injury. Mitochondrial damage leads to impaired respiration and excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, amplifying oxidative stress and promoting apoptosis [20]. Radiation also disrupts endoplasmic reticulum homeostasis, unfolded protein response pathways associated with inflammation and cell death [21]. Collectively, these subcellular changes play a key role in the initiation and progression of radiation-induced endothelial dysfunction. Intermediate phase: thrombosis, coagulation and endothelial activation

Platelet aggregation

Healthy endothelium maintains an antiplatelet and antithrombotic state through the release of nitric oxide (NO) and prostacyclin (PGI₂), which inhibit platelet aggregation by elevating cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) levels [22]. Upon radiation exposure, endothelial cells undergo a phenotypic shift toward a pro-thrombogenic state [23]. Activated endothelial cells secrete platelet-activating factor (PAF) and von Willebrand factor (vWF), promoting platelet adhesion and aggregation, particularly under shear stress conditions [24]. This pro-aggregatory state is further enhanced by thromboxane A2 (TXA2) production, resulting in microparticle formation and leukocyte recruitment, hallmarks of subclinical atherosclerosis and increased thrombotic risk [25]. Clinically, aspirin is often considered in the management of radiation-induced vascular diseases because it inhibits cyclooxygenase-1 and thus TXA2 production. A study demonstrated that high-dose aspirin promotes the formation of collagen-rich plaques which stabilize vascular lesions in irradiated mice [26].

Coagulation

Under normal conditions, endothelial cells maintain an anticoagulant environment through several protective pathways that limit excessive thrombin generation and clot formation [27, 28]. Following ionizing radiation exposure, endothelial cells undergo a phenotypic shift toward a pro-coagulant state, resulting in increased activation of coagulation pathways and a higher risk of thrombosis. Clinically, these changes can be detected using biomarkers such as thrombin–antithrombin (TAT) complexes and prothrombin fragment F1 + 2, which may assist in evaluating radiation-induced thrombotic risk in cancer patients [29].

Fibrosis

To maintain intravascular fluidity, endothelial cells facilitate fibrin clearance through tissue plasminogen activator (t-PA) and urokinase plasminogen activator (uPA) [30]. The Annexin A2/S100A10 complex on the endothelial surface serves as a co-receptor localizing t-PA and plasminogen, accelerating plasmin generation and fibrin degradation. Fibrinolysis is tightly regulated by plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1), the primary inhibitor of both t-PA and uPA [31]. Radiation and inflammatory signalling upregulate PAI-1 expression in endothelial cells, reduce Annexin A2 availability, and promote a shift toward a hypofibrinolytic, pro-fibrotic phenotype. Concurrent thrombin–thrombomodulin-mediated activation of thrombin-activatable fibrinolysis inhibitor (TAFI) further suppresses plasmin generation [32].

Fibrinolytic changes are closely associated with radiation-induced fibrosis observed in abdominal irradiation [33]. Elevated PAI-1 in irradiated tissues contributes to endothelial injury, monocyte recruitment, and extracellular matrix accumulation. Recent studies emphasize PAI-1/transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) crosstalk and the endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition as key mechanisms linking impaired fibrinolysis to post-radiation fibrosis [34]. Emerging therapeutic strategies targeting the PAI-1/TGF-β axis, including angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors, PAI-1 antagonists, and alpha-lipoic acid, show promise in preclinical models and warrant clinical translation [35, 36]. Chronic phase: VEGF, FAK and NO signalling pathways

Vascular endothelial growth factor

Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) is a potent pro-angiogenic mitogen acting through vascular endothelial growth factor receptors 1 and 2 [37]. VEGF regulates endothelial cell differentiation and proliferation in both physiological and pathological angiogenesis. Radiation impairs VEGF signalling and angiogenic recovery, contributing to tissue hypoxia and promoting a pro-fibrotic phenotype [38]. VEGF receptor activation initiates the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)-extracellular signal-regulated kinases (ERK) signalling cascade, which can support cell survival and potentially enhance radio-resistance [39]. Angiotensin II (Ang II) cooperates with VEGF in angiogenic signalling. Clinically, treatment with an Ang II receptor blocker reduces radiation-induced vascular proliferation and delays radiation-induced nephropathy in mouse models [40]. Combined anti-VEGF and angiotensin II receptor blockade strategies may restore normal angiogenesis and mitigate radiation-induced vascular injury and fibrosis, representing potential therapeutic approaches for reducing long-term radiation toxicity in cancer survivors.

Focal adhesion kinase

Focal adhesion kinase (FAK) is an important intracellular signalling protein that regulates endothelial cell survival, migration, and angiogenesis [41]. Activation of FAK promotes pro-survival pathways and helps endothelial cells adapt to cellular stress [42]. However, persistent activation of FAK-related signalling may contribute to abnormal vascular remodelling and treatment resistance after radiation exposure. Therefore, inhibition of FAK and its downstream pathways has emerged as a potential strategy for restoring normal angiogenesis and reducing radiation-associated vascular injury [43]. Emerging regulators of FAK signalling, including Semaphorin 7 A and apolipoprotein L3, may provide additional therapeutic targets, although their roles in radiation-induced endothelial dysfunction require further investigation [44].

Nitric oxide pathway

Nitric oxide (NO) is produced by endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) through L-arginine oxidation and serves as a hallmark of endothelial vascular health [45]. NO regulates vasodilation, platelet inhibition, and anti-inflammatory signalling. Under physiological conditions, physical stimuli (e.g., pressure and shear stress) and chemical stimuli (e.g., acetylcholine and serotonin) upregulate cytoplasmic Ca²⁺ in endothelial cells and activate Ca²⁺-calmodulin-dependent eNOS, generating NO [46]. NO diffuses into smooth muscle cells, activating guanylate cyclase to form cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP), resulting in vascular dilation and smooth muscle relaxation. Upon radiation exposure, oxidative stress driven by ROS elevation and protein kinase C-βII (PKC-βII) activation dysregulates eNOS-dependent NO production, shifting the endothelium toward a pro-constriction, pro-thrombotic, pro-inflammatory phenotype [47]. This NO depletion impairs early atherosclerosis resistance, promotes vascular ischemia, and accelerates long-term vascular disease. Restoration of NO signalling via eNOS modulation or exogenous NO donors represents a potential therapeutic strategy to mitigate radiation-induced vascular dysfunction [48].

Radiation-induced endothelial dysfunction is a dynamic process that evolves over time. Early events such as oxidative stress and DNA damage trigger signalling pathway alterations, leading to endothelial apoptosis, senescence, and inflammation. These changes progressively result in impaired vascular function and ultimately clinical manifestations such as fibrosis and cardiovascular disease. Understanding this temporal progression may help identify critical windows for intervention. Increasing clinical evidence demonstrates that radiation-induced endothelial dysfunction can be detected using advanced imaging and is associated with long-term multi-organ toxicities.

Clinical evidence and advancements

Radiation dose for endothelial dysfunction

Recent clinical studies have sought to establish radiation dose thresholds which induce endothelial dysfunction. A study of 16 patients with locally advanced rectal cancer receiving neoadjuvant radiotherapy revealed that low-dose ionizing radiation below 0.8 Gy activated peritumoral endothelial cells and significantly upregulated pro-angiogenic gene expression [49]. This pro-angiogenic phenotype was most prominent at 8 weeks after radiotherapy and correlated with increased microvascular density (MVD), indicating prolonged endothelial activation and dysfunction. A study involving 43 patients with non-small cell lung cancer assessed heart dose following radiotherapy [50]. The study suggested that a mean cardiac radiation dose of 9.7 Gy induced significant elevation of intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1) concentrations, indicating acute endothelial injury. Notably, 19 patients concurrently receiving statin therapy showed no post-radiotherapy increase in ICAM-1 or cholesterol levels immediately or at 3 months after radiotherapy, suggesting a protective effect of statins. An increase in ICAM-1 and total cholesterol concentrations was reported only in the non-statin group. Another study investigated 478 patients with non-small cell lung cancer [51]. Among the 283 patients who received at least one statin therapy course according to cardiovascular guidelines, improved overall survival was reported. The medium- and high-intensity statin subgroups were associated with significantly reduced mortality. Nevertheless, important questions regarding optimal patient selection, treatment timing, and duration of therapy remain unresolved. Patients with pre-existing cardiovascular risk factors or higher cardiac radiation exposure may derive the greatest benefit. Therefore, randomized clinical trials are required before routine statin therapy can be universally recommended for patients receiving radiotherapy [52].

Although radiation-induced endothelial injury is generally considered dose-dependent, reported dose thresholds vary considerably across studies. Endothelial activation has been observed following exposures below 1 Gy, whereas other studies have reported clinically significant vascular dysfunction primarily after substantially higher doses [2, 53]. Moreover, differences between photon and proton irradiation, as well as variations in fractionation regimens, may further influence endothelial responses. These findings suggest that a universal dose threshold for endothelial dysfunction has yet to be established.

Advancements in diagnosis of vascular endothelial dysfunction

The current standard approach for detecting vascular endothelial dysfunction is flow-mediated dilation (FMD) testing via ultrasound, which measures reactive hyperaemia-induced vascular dilation [54]. However, FMD is operator-dependent, time-consuming, and requires specialized training. Recent clinical studies have proposed computational advances in ultrasound-based diagnosis. One study compared carotid ultrasound findings from 107 post-radiotherapy nasopharyngeal carcinoma patients with those from 110 subjects with cardiovascular risk factors [55]. The study revealed that post-radiotherapy patients exhibited significantly larger carotid intima-media thickness (IMT) and plaque burden. Automatic segmentation algorithms for carotid intima-media assessment achieved 97.2% precision, enabling objective and operator-independent measurement [56]. Integration of computer-assisted ultrasound analysis with automated segmentation facilitated rapid and reproducible detection of radiation-induced vascular pathology, assisting clinicians in diagnosis and risk stratification among cancer survivors. In addition to ultrasound-based techniques, latest evidence suggested that non-invasive imaging such as single-photon emission computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was possible to indirectly assess the microvasculature of the heart and skin at multiple time points, detecting decreased perfusion and impaired hemodynamic [57]. These diagnostic advances complement traditional FMD testing and offer potential for large-scale surveillance of radiation-induced vascular complications. Beyond established ultrasound, MRI, and nuclear imaging-based approaches, photoacoustic imaging (PAI) has emerged as a promising non-invasive modality for assessing radiation-associated vascular and haemodynamic changes. Intrinsic or label-free PAI exploits endogenous absorbers, particularly oxyhaemoglobin and deoxyhaemoglobin, to quantify vascular morphology, haemoglobin content, oxygen saturation, and dynamic haemodynamic responses [58]. In the context of radiotherapy, preclinical studies have shown that PAI can detect early changes in normal-tissue haemodynamics after irradiation. For example, PAI detected altered salivary gland haemodynamic responses after head-and-neck irradiation, suggesting radiation-induced vascular damage [59]. Subsequent ultrasound-guided PAI studies further demonstrated that changes in salivary gland oxygenation and haemoglobin-related parameters correlated with functional impairment and histological evidence of radiation injury in small- and large-animal models [59, 60]. More recently, longitudinal PAI has also been explored in patients with head-and-neck tumors undergoing radiotherapy, supporting the feasibility of repeated non-invasive assessment of treatment-related oxygenation and haemoglobin changes [61]. However, direct clinical evidence supporting PAI for the diagnosis of radiation-induced carotid endothelial dysfunction remains limited.

In contrast to intrinsic PAI, extrinsic molecular probe-based PAI may provide higher molecular specificity but remains largely preclinical in the context of radiation-induced vascular injury. To date, targeted or activatable PAI probes have been developed for endothelial activation, vascular inflammation, oxidative stress, plaque activity, and extracellular matrix remodelling-related biomarkers, including VCAM-1, integrins, ROS/RNS-related signals, cathepsins, matrix metalloproteinases, and fibrillar collagen [62–66]. Although these probes have not yet been specifically validated for diagnosing radiation-induced vascular endothelial dysfunction after radiotherapy, they provide a conceptual framework for future molecular imaging strategies aimed at detecting early endothelial activation, inflammatory vascular remodelling, and microvascular injury before irreversible structural vascular disease becomes apparent. Major barriers to clinical translation include imaging depth, quantitative standardization, probe biocompatibility and pharmacokinetics, regulatory approval, and prospective validation in well-defined irradiated patient populations [58]. Therefore, PAI should currently be regarded as an emerging complementary tool for radiation-related vascular assessment rather than a replacement for established modalities such as FMD, carotid ultrasound or MRI.

Emerging evidence suggests that radiation-induced endothelial dysfunction is highly heterogeneous across tissues and vascular environments [67]. As endothelial cells perform specialized organ-specific functions, their molecular and cellular responses to radiation may differ substantially among vascular beds. Furthermore, endothelial responses are influenced by the local microenvironment, including differences in oxygenation, inflammatory signalling, and vascular architecture. For example, irradiation induces endothelial cell death in some settings while promoting endothelial activation and vascular remodelling in others [4]. These observations highlight the complexity of predicting vascular outcomes following radiotherapy and suggest that both tissue-specific and biological factors contribute to variability in radiation responses. Understanding the complex biological variability and protective interventions are crucial for optimizing treatments.

Associations with clinical diseases

Radiation-induced endothelial dysfunction is associated with increased cardiovascular disease risk in post-radiotherapy cancer survivors. One study investigated 15 patients with breast cancer receiving unilateral chest adjuvant radiotherapy with daily fractionated doses of 2.0–2.5 Gy [68]. The study showed significantly reduced peak acetylcholine-mediated endothelium-dependent vasodilation in the post-radiotherapy patients compared with controls, indicating radiation-induced impairment of microvascular endothelial function. Impairment in coronary vascular function is associated with diastolic dysfunction and heart failure in long-term follow-up [69]. Notably, radiation-induced heart disease (RIHD) is a leading non-oncological cause of morbidity and mortality in cancer survivors, often manifesting 10–15 years after radiotherapy [70]. Clinical studies revealed that the radiation dose delivered to the left anterior descending artery and left circumflex artery correlated with post-radiotherapy elevation of vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1) levels, a marker of endothelial damage [50]. In accordance with European Society of Cardiology guidelines, a mean cardiac radiation dose limit of no more than 15 Gy is recommended to reduce the risk of coronary heart disease [71]. These clinical findings highlight the importance of radiation dose minimization to normal cardiac tissue during cancer radiotherapy planning. Besides coronary and carotid complications, radiation-induced endothelial dysfunction is increasingly recognized as a fundamental mechanism underlying various radiation toxicities, including cerebrovascular morbidities and peripheral arterial disease. A case-control cohort study of 2,617 Hodgkin lymphoma survivors demonstrated excess risks of stroke and transient ischaemic attacks years after mediastinal or neck irradiation, supporting a diffuse vasculopathy rather than isolated large-vessel disease [72]. For irradiation of the head and neck regions, accelerated carotid atherosclerosis and plaque instability lead to a significantly higher risk of ischaemic cerebrovascular events, particularly when conventional cardiovascular risk factors coexist [73]. These observations highlight the need for systematic vascular risk assessment and management of hypertension, dyslipidaemia, diabetes, and smoking in post-radiotherapy patients, together with contemporary cardio-oncology recommendations [74].

Endothelial dysfunction also contributes to common post-radiation gastrointestinal and renal comorbidities. In the gastrointestinal tract, PAI‑1-driven endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition contributes to mucosal barrier breakdown and progressive fibrosis, which clinically presents as chronic diarrhoea, strictures, and malabsorption [75]. In the kidney, microvascular rarefaction and renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system activation have been implicated in radiation nephropathy, and early use of ACE inhibitors has shown mitigating effects [40]. Clinical evidence revealed that the use of ACE inhibitors significantly reduced the risk of grade 2 or above radiation pneumonitis in lung cancer patients treated with radiotherapy [76]. Additionally, the use of ACE inhibitors lowered the risk of proctitis, haematuria and rectal bleeding in prostate cancer patients followed by radiotherapy. ACE inhibitor users reported longer median survival in early-stage non-small cell lung cancer and glioblastoma. Nevertheless, evidence for other radiotherapy-related toxicity and survival outcomes remains inconsistent across cancer types and severities. Further research is recommended to control for confounders.

Future directions

Basic research

From a preclinical perspective, a key research priority is the development of advanced experimental models which more accurately replicate radiation-induced endothelial injury in humans. More refined models are required to thoroughly understand endothelial responses to clinically relevant fractionation schemes and combined-modality therapies [77]. Organoid and microfluidic “vessel-on-a-chip” platforms incorporating endothelial, smooth muscle, and immune components could better mimic shear stress, hypoxia, and inflammatory conditions present in irradiated tissues [78]. Integration of single-cell transcriptomics and spatial multi-omics will be essential to investigate endothelial heterogeneity across vascular beds and to identify vulnerable subpopulations that drive chronic inflammation, thrombosis, and fibrosis after irradiation [79]. Ultimately, translating these mechanistic insights into biomarker-guided, pathway-specific interventions can potentially reduce radiation-induced endothelial dysfunction and vascular morbidities in cancer survivors.

Clinical research

From a clinical perspective, an important research priority is determining how vascular surveillance can be effectively integrated into routine cancer survivorship care. Existing evidence recommends investigating risk-adapted vascular surveillance pathways embedded within oncologic follow-up [71, 74]. Dedicated clinics could systematically combine dosimetric information with serial assessment of circulating endothelial biomarkers, non-invasive vascular function tests such as FMD, and vascular imaging by carotid ultrasound or coronary computed tomography angiography, to detect and stratify patients at higher risk. Randomized trials are recommended to investigate preventive strategies such as statins and ACE inhibitors in high-risk populations, with standardized vascular endpoints [33, 36].

Conclusion

Radiation-induced endothelial dysfunction serves as a pivotal mechanism underpinning long-term vascular and organ toxicities after radiotherapy. This review integrates the mechanisms from endothelial apoptosis and senescence to chronic pro-thrombotic and pro-inflammatory remodelling, which extends beyond coronary and carotid beds to affect cerebral, gastrointestinal and renal vasculature. Existing evidence reveals that even low to moderate radiation doses can induce sustained endothelial injury, highlighting the importance of dose constraints and long-term surveillance. Emerging pharmacological strategies targeting specific pathways including VEGF and FAK show promise for endothelial preservation when combined with optimized treatment planning.

However, several limitations have to be acknowledged. Preclinical studies are often constrained by experimental models which do not fully replicate the complexity of the human vascular microenvironment, limiting translational applicability. In addition, substantial heterogeneity exists among in vitro and animal models, including differences in species, tissue types, radiation modalities, fractionation schedules, and dose regimens, making direct comparison between studies challenging. Clinically, much of the available evidence is derived from small, retrospective, and single-centre studies, which are susceptible to selection bias and limit generalizability. Diagnostic assessment of endothelial dysfunction also remains challenging because currently available biomarkers and vascular imaging techniques lack standardization and validation. Consequently, definitive dose-response relationships and clinically significant risk thresholds remain incompletely defined. Future research should prioritize advanced preclinical platforms, such as organoids and microfluidic systems, together with large-scale, prospective, multicentre studies to validate diagnostic biomarkers, establish standardized assessment strategies, and evaluate the efficacy of preventive and therapeutic interventions.

This review highlights that endothelial dysfunction is not only as a secondary consequence of irradiation, but a crucial target for intervention. By integrating current mechanistic understanding with clinical evidence, it provides a framework for future research and the development of vascular-sparing radiotherapy strategies. Addressing these knowledge gaps is important to reduce radiation-induced endothelial injury and improve long-term outcomes in cancer survivors.
