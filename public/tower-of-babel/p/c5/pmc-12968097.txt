Progressive endothelial coverage enhances hemocompatibility and prevents calcification in bioprosthetic valve tissue
Authors: Bastien Poitier, Ulysse Richez, Joseph Roux-de-Bezieux, Peter Ivak, Jeanne Rancic, Hugo Charrier, Christian Latremouille, Christophe Peronino, Jean Christian Roussel, Elisa Rossi, Salma El-Batti, Michel Kindo, Bruno Saubamea, Yuri Pya, Corinne Lesaffre, Antoine Capel, Paul Achouh, Ivan Netuka, David M Smadja
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExploring the hemocompatibility of bioprosthetic heart valves (BHVs) has been challenging due to the scarcity of non-degenerated material.ObjectivesThis study has two complementary objectives (1) To characterize the extent and temporal kinetics of endothelial coverage of BHVs implanted in humans; (2) To investigate, in a rat model, the impact of pericardial endothelialization on tissue calcification.MethodsWe employed histology and electron microscopy to assess cellular organization in non-degenerated BHVs and conducted hemodynamic simulations to evaluate shear stress fields in the ejection valves area. Furthermore, we investigated the impact of pericardium endothelial coverage on calcification using endothelial colony-forming cells (ECFCs) cultured on bovine pericardium discs implanted in athymic nude rats for 18 days. Calcium content was quantified through acetylene flame atomic absorption spectrophotometry.ResultsWe observed inflammatory cell infiltration within all explanted BHVs, as well as fibrin deposit on top of the leaflets. Endothelial coverage emerged in long-term implants (> 180 days) but remained incomplete in aortic valves, which could be linked to high shear stress levels in aortic position confirmed in hemodynamic simulations. Besides, the rat experiments revealed that the discs covered with fibrin + ECFCs were significantly less calcified than those covered with fibrin alone (respectively, median = 0.9 µg Ca/mg tissue; IQR: 0.7–1.1 vs median = 18.3 µg Ca/mg tissue; IQR: 9–34.9; p = 0.0003), and less extensively colonized by neutrophils.ConclusionsHuman explanted BHVs showed intact leaflets with a fibrin layer and organized endothelial coverage, without detectable calcification over the short observation period. In rats, endothelialization was associated with significantly reduced pericardial calcification, suggesting a potential protective effect. However, the limited follow-up in humans precludes conclusions on a causal role of endothelial coverage in hemocompatibility or protection against calcification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10456-026-10039-7.Keywords: Biomaterials, Bioprosthetic heart valve, Bovine pericardium, Hemocompatibility, Bioprosthetic total artificial heart, Endothelial cells, Calcification

Introduction

Bioprosthetic heart valves (BHVs) using glutaraldehyde-fixed xenogeneic pericardium were discovered by Professor Alain Carpentier in the late 1960s for aortic valve replacement [1]. The cross-linking of the collagenous fibers by glutaraldehyde results in increased in-vivo stability of the scaffold and a reduced antigenic immune response. More than 300,000 BHVs are implanted annually worldwide, and this number is predicted to reach 850,000 in 2050 [2, 3]. One of the main reasons for BHV success in contrast to mechanical heart valves is the absence of long-term oral therapeutic anticoagulation treatment [4]. This “acquired hemocompatibility” is a clinical and biological phenomenon that has been recognized over the last three decades: indeed, BHVs exhibit progressive compatibility with blood over time, unlike mechanical valves which require long-term anticoagulation therapy. In contrast, BHVs typically need only antiplatelet therapy or no antithrombotic treatment at all after an initial period. This clinical observation has been widely accepted despite the lack of detailed mechanistic understanding. However, BHV degeneration exists. This process is slow, gradual, and progressive, with 20–30% becoming dysfunctional within 10 years and more than 50% within 12–15 years due to tissue degeneration [5]. The primary cause of degeneration is calcification of the pericardium leaflets [5, 6]. We recently showed that fibrin deposition on bovine pericardial tissue led to its calcification in a rat model [7]. Fibrin network formation is the first physiological step that occurs after biological material implantation, allowing secondary cellular adhesion to its material [8]. Despite the number of implanted BHVs, the acquired hemocompatibility process through endothelial cells covering has never been studied extensively because of the impossibility of accessing non-degenerated material for in-depth exploration.

The Aeson Total Artificial Heart (A-TAH, Carmat SA, Vélizy, France) is a biventricular pulsatile pump made with bioprosthetic materials, mostly implanted as a bridge to transplant [9]. Its blood-contacting surfaces are made with expanded polytetrafluoroethylene and bovine pericardial tissue. Carpentier-Edwards BHVs (Edwards Lifesciences; Irvine, CA, USA) are positioned left and right at each inflow and outflow orifice. The good hemocompatibility of A-TAH allows use of only intermediate-dose prophylactic anticoagulation after implantation [9–11].

The aim of our study was to explore the surface of the BHVs explanted concomitantly with the A-TAH after varying implantation durations in humans, using histological and electron microscopy analyses to describe mechanism that give rise to BHVs acquired hemocompatibility. Furthermore, we explored the impact of the pericardium endothelial coverage by endothelial progenitor cells on its calcification in a rat model.

Methods

Patients included

Patients undergoing implantation of a A-TAH along with four BHV, from October 2017 to June 2018, in the context of the pivotal phase of the multicentric CARMAT clinical trials (NCT02962973), and for whom the device has been retrieved for histological analyses, were included for examination of the four explanted BHV. Characteristics of patients are presented in Table 1. The primary objective of this clinical trial was survival on a Carmat device at 180 days post-implant or survival to cardiac transplantation if occurring before 180 days post-implant. These patients had terminal biventricular heart failure, inotrope dependent or Cardiac Index < 2.2 L/min/m2 while not on inotropes, on optimal medical management as judged by the investigator based on current Heart Failure practice guidelines (ESC/AHA). All patients had signed the informed consent, and the study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Histologic examination of explanted BHV

The BHVs were collected at A-TAH explant and stored in 2.5% Glutaraldehyde (GTA) solution or 3.7% Formaldehyde (FA) solution. Four paraffin blocks were prepared in each case, one per valve, and were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Samples of valve were dehydrated with successive bath of increasing gradient of Ethanol solutions, then bath of Xylene substitute (Xylene substitute Hemo-De, Electron Microscopy Sciences) and included in paraffin (Surgipath Paraplast Plus, Leica Biosystems). Cross-sections of 4µm were trimmed using a microtome (Leica Biosystems RM2245). The sections were rehydrated using xylene substitute and successive bath of Ethanol with an increasing gradient of water until complete rehydration, then stained with Mayer’s Hemalum Solution (Millipore), differentiate in Ammonia Water 0.5% and stained with Eosin Y-Solution (0.5% alcoholic, Sigma-Aldrich). Then dehydrated with quick successive bath of Ethanol and Xylene substitute and mounted with Eukitt Mounting Medium (Sigma-Adrich).

Immunohistochemistry and fluorescence

The following markers were used: anti-CD144 (VE-cadherin Polyclonal Antibody, Catalog #36-1900, ThermoFisher Scientific) and anti-CD68 (Monoclonal Antibody [KP1], Ab955, Abcam). CD144: sections were rehydrated as previously described, incubated in target retrieval solution for 20 min at 90 °C (HIER sodium Citrate 10 mM pH6.0, Agilent), with inactivation of endogenous peroxidase in 0.3% H2O2 in Methanol for 20 min, and saturation of unspecific binding sites in 0.1% Triton goat serum PBS solution. Then incubation with primary antibody anti-CD144 was realized at 1/50 overnight at 4 °C. The next day, sections were incubated with a biotinylated secondary antibody (Goat anti-Mouse IgG1 Cross-Adsorbed Secondary Antibody, Biotin-XX, Catalog #A10519, Invitrogen) then with Streptavidin Alexa Fluor 488 Conjugate (ThermoFisher Scientific) and mounted in mounting medium with DAPI (Vectashield, Vector Laboratories). CD68: sections were rehydrated and target retrieval was performed as previously described so as endogenous peroxidase inactivation and saturation of unspecific binding sites. Then incubation with primary antibody anti-CD68 was realized at 1/100 overnight at 4 °C. The next day, sections were incubated with secondary antibody (VECTASTAIN Elite ABC-Peroxidase Kit, Vector Laboratories) and revealed with DAB (SIGMAFAST 3,3′-Diaminobenzidine, Sigma-Aldrich) for 5 min, then mounted with Eukitt Mounting Medium (Sigma-Adrich).

Electron microscopy

All steps were performed at room temperature unless otherwise indicated. Glutaraldehyde, Osmium Tetroxide (OsO4) and Epon resin were from Electron Microscopy Sciences. Hexamethyldisilazane (HMDS) and Propylene Oxide were from Merck.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The samples were fixed in 0.1M Phosphate Buffer (PB), pH 7.4 containing 2.5% glutaraldehyde overnight, washed in PB (3 × 15min) and fixed in PB + 1% OsO4 for 1h at 4 °C. After washing in PB (2 × 15min) and H2O (5min), the samples were dehydrated in an ascending series of EtOH (30%, 50%, 70%, 95%—15 min each), followed by 100% EtOH (2 × 20min), HMDS/EtOH (1/1: v/v) for 10min and 100% HMDS for 10min before being air-dried overnight. The samples were mounted on aluminium stubs with conductive glue, dried for 24 h and sputtered with Gold/Palladium. Images were acquired in a Jeol LV6510 scanning electron microscope operated at 15 keV.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

The samples were fixed and dehydrated with Ethanol as described for SEM. After the last step in 100% EtOH, they were incubated in a mixture of EtOH/Propylene Oxide (1/1:v/v) for 10 min, then in 100% Propylene Oxide (2 × 10 min). The samples were then immersed in Epon/Propylene Oxide (1/1:v/v) for 2 h, then in 100% Epon overnight. After several changes of Epon, samples were transferred molds containing fresh Epon. For resin polymerisation, the samples were left for 2 h at 37 °C, then for 3 days at 60 °C. Ultrathin (80 nm) sections were cut on ultramicrotome UC7 (Leica Microsystems, Nanterre, France). Ultrathin sections were post-stained in lead nitrate and 1% uranyl acetate prior to observation in a Jeol 100S transmission electron microscope equipped with a Gatan Orius SC200 camera and operated at 80 keV.

Hemodynamic simulations in the A-TAH

We performed hemodynamic simulations as previously described [12–14] to model the behavior of the A-TAH under several operating cases, in order to evaluate the shear stress fields in the blood cavities of both ventricles, especially at the ventricular side of the surface of the ejection valves leaflets. These cases were chosen as representative of various environmental physiological conditions in which the prosthesis was likely to operate (supplementary Table 1). Globally, the simulations allowed to estimate the blood velocity fields in the prosthesis, the gradient of which gave the shear rates at each point of the ventricle. More precisely, the simulations were made of two successive sub-simulations. The first simulation was a Fluid Structure Interaction (FSI) simulation modelling the behavior of the full ventricles, with the oil and blood cavities as well as the membranes and the valves. The meshes for the rigid and flexible structures, as well as the fluid domains, were built in Hypermesh and implemented in the finite element solver LS-DYNA using the immersed boundaries method. The rigid walls and the edges of the membranes and leaflets were fixed in all directions. As for the fluid boundary conditions, a constant pressure was applied at the blood inlets and outlets and a theoretical oil flow was prescribed at the oil inlets of both ventricles. The FSI simulations provided an accurate description of the membranes and valves movements during a cardiac cycle. These movements were extracted from the FSI simulations to be reinjected in Computer Fluid Dynamics (CFD) simulations modelling exclusively the blood compartment, providing a more precise description of the evolution of velocity and pressure fields in the fluid during a cardiac cycle. The finite element solver ANSYS Fluent was used to discretize the blood domains and solve the hemodynamics. The CFD simulations used moving boundaries, prescribing the movements of the membranes and valves at the fluid grid by means of a user-defined function implemented in the Dynamic Mesh algorithm. The evolutions of the velocity fields were extracted from the resulting data and used to compute the shear stress fields in the blood cavities of both ventricles, especially at the ventricular side of the surface of the ejection valves leaflets. More technical details regarding the implementation of the FSI model are available in previous studies [12–14].

Rat model of calcification

Following in vitro cellular coating, samples of pericardial tissue were implanted in athymic nude rats (Crl:NIH-Foxn1rnu; Janvier Labs, Le Genest-Saint-Isle, France). The nude phenotype results from a homozygous Foxn1rnu mutation (RRID:RGD_2312499, Rat Genome Database). The implants were explanted on day 18, and pericardial tissue calcification was subsequently measured and analyzed.

Tissue preparation

The animal study was reviewed and approved by APAFIS#17977-201705231635249. Tissue preparation of the samples lasted few days before being implanted in rats (Supplementary Table 1). Bovine pericardium from Neovasc® were used. The pericardial tissue was cut into discs of 0.8 cm in diameter. Discs were washed in cold tris-buffered saline (TBS) + fetal bovine serum (FBS) 10% for 5 days at 4°C with gentle shaking (2 discs/ml) for flushing glutaraldehyde. Three baths were realized the first day, and then rinsing baths were changed 3 times during the 5 days. Then, two groups were prepared: Group 1, discs covered with fibrin; Group 2, discs covered with fibrin + endothelial colony forming cells (ECFC). The fibrin was obtained by blending three reagents: Cryocheck™ Pooled Normal Plasma (4/6), tissue factor + phospholipids (Thrombinoscope BV) (1/6), and calcium 100 mM (1/6) as previously described [7]. ECFC were isolated from human umbilical cord blood as previously described [15], then grown in incubator before being put on pericardium discs. Once pericardium discs were washed according to the protocol, all of them were covered with fibrin. The solution of fibrin was put on both disc surfaces at 2 h intervals (20 µl/surface) [7]. The next day, ECFC were put on the discs from Goup 2. Three quantities were tested to completely recover the discs surface before planning animal implantation: 100 000, 200 000 and 300 000 ECFC were put on each disc surfaces at 6 h intervals.

Cells were grown on pericardial discs for 48 h.

Scanning electron microscopy

Before initiating the animal procedure, SEM was used to examine the surface aspect of the pericardium discs covered with ECFCs, to confirm that we obtained a complete covering. The surface aspect after fibrin recovery has already been described previously [7].

Surgical procedure

Twelve-day-old Athymic Nude rats (Janvier Labs, Le Genest St. Iles, France) were used for the experimentation as previously described [7]. Sedation was performed with isoflurane 2.5% and analgesia was allowed by a subcutaneous injection of buprenorphine (0.05 mg/kg). The skin was cleaned with betadine. Four subcutaneous incisions in the dorsal wall of the rats were performed and discs were placed (4 discs/rat; Supplementary Fig. 1). Incisions were closed by skin clips. The pericardium discs were explanted after 18 days. Rats were euthanized by an intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital after sedation. The discs were rinsed with 0.09% saline solution and stored at – 20 °C before analysis. The samples allocated for histology were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde.

Disc analysis

Calcium dosage

A first part of the discs was allocated to calcification assay. The discs were hydrolyzed in a 68% nitric acid bath at 60 °C for 20 min, allowing calcium dosage, performed by air-acetylene flame atomic absorption spectrophotometry (VARIAN AA240). Results are given in µg/mg of pericardium.

Histology

A second part of the discs was allocated to histology. The samples were dehydrated and paraffin-embedded as described in previous section regarding explanted BHV examination. Hematoxylin-eosine staining was carried out as previously described to examine cell morphology. Samples allocated to Von Kossa staining were incubated in Silver Nitrate Solution (5%) with exposure to ultraviolet light, then Sodium Thiosulfate Solution (5%) and Fast Red Solution. Then dehydrated with quick successive bath of Ethanol and mounted in synthetic resin. Slide staining were observed with an optical microscope (Leica DMRB (Leica Microsystems, Nanterre, France). Immunohistochemistry with Anti-CD68 (Polyclonal antibody, ab125212, Abcam), Anti-MPO (Monoclonal Anti-Myeloperoxidase antibody [EPR20257], ab208670, Abcam), and Anti-CD3 (Monoclonal antibody [SP7], ab16669, Abcam), were performed to highlight respectively macrophages infiltration, neutrophils infiltration, and T-cells infiltration, in explanted pericardium discs. Samples were observed with a confocal microscope (SP8, Leica Microsystems, Nanterre, France).

Biomarker profiling of ECFC

The functional state of ECFCs used in our rat model was assessed by quantifying soluble biomarkers in culture supernatants, using the same pericardial tissue preparation and culture system. Von Willebrand factor (vWF), reflecting endothelial integrity and hemostatic function were determined by an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) (Asserachrom vWf:Ag; Diagnostica Stago, France) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), a pro-inflammatory cytokine, were measured using a Human Magnetic Luminex Assay (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN). Data were assessed with the Bio-Plex 200 using the Bio-Plex Manager 5.0 software (Bio-Rad, Marnes-la-Coquette, France). Four groups were prepared: Group 1, ECFCs cultured on fibronectin (negative control); Group 2, ECFCs cultured on fibrin; Group 3, ECFCs cultured on pericardial discs; Group 4, ECFCs cultured on pericardial discs + fibrin.

Statistical analysis

We used a Mann–Whitney test to compare calcium dosages and inflammatory cells infiltration. Values were expressed as median and interquartile ranges (IQR). All analyses were 2-sided, and a P value was considered statistically significant for P < 0.05. Statistical analyses and curves were performed using Graphpad software.

Results

Explanted bioprosthetic heart valve have a cellularized deposit composition

This study included seven patients, from whom BHVs were explanted after implantation durations ranging from 14 to 270 days. Three patients underwent device explantation at the time of heart transplantation, while four patients died while on device support. More details regarding the exact implantation duration of each patient are given in Supplementary Table 2. Three groups were defined according to our observations to better describe the progressive steps of pericardium recolonization: short duration (< 60 days), medium duration (60–180 days), long duration (> 180 days). H&E staining, conducted on the four valves from each patient (Fig. 1), revealed an early deposit on both sides of the pericardial tissue without any difference between the smooth and the rough side. Over shorter durations (< 60 days), the deposit appeared predominantly composed of fibrin, with inflammatory-like cells infiltrating the superficial layers of the pericardium. During medium durations (between 60 and 180 days), an organization of this deposit became evident, characterized by a higher density of superficial inflammatory-like cells and minimal infiltration into deeper pericardial layers. The blood-contacting surface exhibited a smooth appearance, featuring sparse cells with flattened nuclei, indicative of endothelial cell colonization atop the biological deposit. In cases of longer implantation durations (> 180 days), a similar organization of stabilized inflammatory-like cells was observed, primarily infiltrating the superficial pericardial layers. However, these cells exhibited lower density and reduced quantity compared to medium-duration cases. The deposit surface was entirely covered with flattened cells displaying flattened nuclei, except for the aortic valve, which exhibited only a partial recovery process. Von Kossa staining was conducted on the four valves from each patient to investigate calcification formation in the explanted BHVs. No calcification was detected (Supplementary Fig. 2) within the pericardial matrix of any explanted BHVs, even after extended periods of implantation. In addition, macroscopic photographs of the explanted valves confirmed preservation of valve integrity after explantation (Supplementary Fig. 3). X-ray imaging of the explanted devices further demonstrated the absence of calcification in the membranes of the ventricular cavities (Supplementary Fig. 4).

The deposit on explanted BHV consists of fibrin, inflammatory cells and endothelial cells

During short durations of BHV implantation, a fibrin network becomes apparent on the surface of the pericardium, with embedded cells visible under SEM. In Martius Scarlet Blue staining (Supplementary Fig. 5A), the fibrin exhibits a light blue hue on both sides of the valve, embedded within the superficial layers of the bovine pericardium matrix, alongside cells within the network, and presents a smooth, compacted surface. The cells embedded within the fibrin display CD68 positivity in immunohistochemistry (Supplementary Fig. 5B). A very superficial infiltration into the external layers of the pericardium is evident, while the smooth surface of flattened cells does not show CD68 positivity. To assess the endothelial nature of this monolayer of squamous cells, comprehensive studies were undertaken. SEM provided a more precise estimation of the surface covered by these flattened cells across various implant durations. The results are outlined in a panel (Fig. 2), categorized into three durations similar to the H&E panel. During short durations (< 60 days), isolated elongated structures of cells are observed atop a fibrin deposit, featuring a visible network.

These structures could be in line with early pseudotubes as seen in the fibrin with ECFCs [16]. Red blood cells are observed on this fibrin deposit, probably due to post-mortem sedimentation, because they are not embedded in the fibrin network. The fibrin network for short durations lacks compactness, with visible reticulation and an unsmoothed surface texture. For medium durations (between 60 and 180 days), flattened cells become visible. Approximately 25–30% of the BHV surface is covered by these cells. In areas where cell coverage is incomplete, a noticeable difference in texture exists between the smoother cell surface and the rougher underlying fibrin network. In long-term durations (> 180 days), many areas exhibit a continuous layer of squamous cells with hexagonal morphology, covering around 75% of the surface. Some explanted valves are entirely covered by flattened cells at this stage, except for the aortic valve, which lacks continuous coverage and is not as extensively covered as the other valves. TEM confirmed the endothelial phenotype of these flattened cells, displaying a thin compacted structure at the interface between the cells and the extracellular matrix, indicative of a basal lamina (Fig. 3A). Additionally, electron-dense areas, characteristic of endothelial tight junctions, were observed at cell–cell contacts (Fig. 3B). Immunofluorescence sections (Fig. 3C) demonstrated the positivity of this surface cellular monolayer for CD144 (VE-Cadherin), a specific marker of endothelial cells.

Through hemodynamic simulations, we generated curves depicting the percentage of the aortic and pulmonary valves' surface exposed to specific shear stress thresholds (Fig. 4). Under nominal conditions, approximately 10% of the pulmonary valve surface experienced a shear rate of 5 s-1, whereas more than 50% of the aortic valve surface was affected. Irrespective of the operational state of the A-TAH, the aortic valve consistently endured higher shear stress levels compared to the pulmonary valve (Table 2) in line with relatively incomplete endothelial recovery observed in Figs. 1, 2 and 5, suggesting that shear stress is a limiting factor for endothelial adhesion and retention.

ECFC-mediated endothelialization blocks bovine pericardium calcification in a preclinical implantation model

To evaluate the behavior and functional integration of endothelial colony-forming cells (ECFCs) on pericardial scaffolds, we first assessed their adhesion, coverage, and phenotype maintenance when seeded onto fibrin-coated pericardium. First, we used SEM to examine pericardium discs coated with fibrin and endothelial colony-forming cells (ECFCs) to validate the success of the fibrin and cellular coverage (Supplementary Fig. 6). When 100,000 ECFCs were deposited, the coverage remained incomplete. However, deposition of 200,000 ECFCs resulted in a continuous cellular covering visible across the entire surface of both faces of the pericardium, completely concealing the fibrin network beneath. This observation was consistent with a deposition of 400,000 ECFCs. Consequently, we determined that the deposition of 200,000 ECFCs was the most effective and utilized it for the animal implantation. We wanted then to address the functional competence of the endothelial coverage on top of pericardium, so we evaluated ECFCs secretion of von Willebrand factor (vWF), a marker of Weibel-Palade body activity and endothelial hemostatic function, along with interleukin -6 (IL-6) as a surrogate of inflammatory activation, under various culture conditions. We analyzed conditioned media after 48 h of culture on different substrates, quantifying vWF and IL-6 levels. ECFCs were cultured on four conditions: fibronectin (control), bovine pericardium alone, fibrin-coated plastic, and fibrin on top of pericardium. Quantification of vWF secretion revealed that ECFCs cultured on fibrin-based substrates secreted significantly higher levels of vWF than those on fibronectin or pericardium alone (Fig. 6A). Specifically, vWF levels were 1.14 ± 1.04 pg/mL on fibronectin and 1.33 ± 1.49 pg/mL on pericardium, with no significant difference between these two conditions. However, vWF secretion was significantly elevated when fibrin was present: 2.18 ± 0.74 pg/mL on fibrin alone and 2.27 ± 1.16 pg/mL on fibrin over pericardium (p = 0.04 vs. pericardium only). Notably, the presence of fibrin enhanced vWF secretion regardless of whether it was applied to plastic or pericardium, whereas the pericardial substrate itself did not appear to influence vWF release. In contrast, IL-6 concentrations remained consistently low across all four conditions (Fig. 6B), indicating that none of the tested environments induced a pro-inflammatory response from ECFCs. These findings confirm that endothelial coverage with ECFCs maintain a functional endothelial phenotype on pericardial substrates and that fibrin plays a key role in promoting vWF secretion without inducing inflammatory activation.

Finally, we evaluated ECFCs cultivated on fibrin on top of bovine pericardium implanted in a rat calcification model. A total of seven athymic nude rats (3 males and 4 females) underwent surgical implantation of 28 pericardial discs, with each experimental condition equally distributed between males and females. Among these, twenty discs were designated for calcification assessment. We observed that discs covered solely with fibrin exhibited elevated levels of calcification (median = 18.3 µg Ca/mg tissue; IQR: 9–34.9), whereas discs coated with fibrin + ECFCs displayed significantly lower levels of calcification (median = 0.9 µg Ca/mg tissue; IQR: 0.7–1.1) (p = 0.0003) (Fig. 7A). Von Kossa staining revealed a central calcification area in discs covered only with fibrin, whereas those covered with fibrin + ECFCs showed no evidence of calcification (Fig. 7B and C). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) sections allowed us to examine the composition of immune infiltrates in the pericardium discs post-implantation in rats. We observed a higher infiltration of neutrophil cells in discs coated solely with fibrin compared to those coated with fibrin + ECFCs (20.5 cells/mm2; IQR: 15.37 vs 8.2 cells/mm2; IQR: 7.17; p = 0.0048) (MPO detection in Fig. 7D–F). Significant infiltration of monocytes and macrophages was noted in both groups without discernible differences. No T-cell infiltration was detected in any section, as anticipated given the athymic nature of Nude rats.

Discussion

This study presents, for the first time, an in-depth examination of the acquired hemocompatibility of bovine pericardium valves implanted in humans. This hemocompatibility is the result of an evolutionary process involving the deposition of fibrin and inflammatory cells, followed by a progressive and complete coverage by endothelial cells. Bioprosthetic valves made with bovine pericardium fixed in glutaraldehyde have been widely used for more than 30 years in patients [1]. The clinical success encountered was explained by the low anticoagulation requirement. However, the mechanism of this acquired hemocompatibility has never been clearly studied and demonstrated. The fates of the bovine pericardium biomaterial after implantation were not extensively described due to the lack of non-degenerated explanted material available for in-depth studies. However, one case-report described four explanted aortic bioprostheses made of porcine pericardium in histology, showing an early phase of fibrin deposit and inflammatory response after implantation, and a late neointimal coverage with progressive regression of the inflammatory response over time [17]. A case-report of bovine pericardium patches (CardioCel, Admedus Regen Pty Ltd, Perth, Western Australia) used for congenital heart disease in children also showed a neointimal layer of varying thickness developed on the visceral surface on 5 of 6 CardioCel explants with endothelial coverage of the longest duration implant [18]. These findings are in line with the results presented in our study. The first step of fibrin deposit prior to hemocompatibility acquisition is counterintuitive. A wide surface covered by fibrin should be the site of an active coagulation process leading to thrombi formation with a risk of thromboembolic events. BHV thrombosis is indeed an increasingly recognized entity that can affect valve function and cause thrombo-embolic complications [19]. However, a smoothed fibrin deposition was already described in the first implanted artificial heart [20] (Liotta-Cooley artificial heart) in 1969 without thromboembolic consequences. The same observation was made in the A-TAH, where all the membrane blood-contacting surfaces presented an adherent fibrin cellular network, while no thrombotic events occurred in the patients [21]. Multiple observational studies support the use of Vitamin-K antagonists to reduce the risk of thromboembolism during the first post-operative months [22, 23]. An analysis of the Danish National Patient Registry in a study of 4,075 patients with bioprosthetic aortic valve replacements showed that warfarin discontinuation was associated with increased thromboembolic events and cardiovascular deaths in the first 1 to 6 months after surgery [24]. Interestingly, although the stroke rate was increased in the first 3 months in the treatment group, the number of strokes between 3 and 6 months was no different between patients who continued or discontinued warfarin. Even more interestingly, the group without any antiplatelet therapy nor Warfarin exhibits very high rates of cardiovascular death within the first 90 days, but after 90 days the rates of cardiovascular death are comparable between warfarin, antiplatelet or both therapy groups and the no treatment group. Moreover, even if leaflet thrombosis causing valve failure is relatively uncommon, subclinical thrombosis has been reported in up to 30% of transcatheter valves at one year and in 28% of surgically implanted valves across multiple valve types [25, 26]. However, optimal antithrombotic strategy early after surgical implantation of an aortic BHV remains controversial, due to a lack of high-quality evidence. ESC/EACTS and AHA guidelines recommend, in patients with no baseline indications for anticoagulation, either low-dose aspirin (75–100 mg/day) or oral anticoagulation using a Vitamin-K antagonist for the first 3 months after surgical implantation of an aortic BHV [27, 28]. The acquired hemocompatibility is assumed to be achieved within this duration, with the initial fibrin deposition being progressively recovered by endothelial cells.

The A-TAH has an overall surface area some 24 times that of a biologic valve [29]. In our reported cases, between 3 and 6 months, no patient presented complete endothelial coverage of the BHV surface. However, no thrombotic events occurred since the patients were treated with low molecular weight heparin (either therapeutic or intermediate dose) [11]. As we do not know if the acquired hemocompatibility requires a complete endothelial covering of the biomaterial, or a significant amount of endothelialized area ensures sufficient hemocompatibility to dispense with anticoagulation treatment without a major thrombotic risk, we cannot be sure yet how long the anticoagulant regimen is needed in patients supported by the A-TAH.

In our case of A-TAH implant, the conditions are different compared to the isolated surgical valve replacement. First, the shear stress induced by the device seems to modulate BHV endothelialization. We previously demonstrated that the shear stress provided by the A-TAH is at a physiologic rate, which does not cause hemolysis or acquired Von Willebrand syndrome [10]. However, we demonstrate here that the aortic valve is subjected to higher shear stress compared with the pulmonary valve. Importantly, valves exposed to high shear stress were found to exhibit reduced endothelialization, suggesting that elevated shear stress may represent a limiting factor for endothelial cell adhesion and retention, as previously reported in studies investigating the influence of shear stress on endothelial mechanobiology [30–34]. Then, in an isolated surgical valve replacement, the proximity between the biomaterial and the native endothelium is very likely to trigger the covering of the biomaterial by endothelial cells with a hyperplasia mechanism observed in vascular graft models [35]. In the A-TAH implant, the valves are distant from the anastomosis [29, 36], thereby impairing covering by a hyperplasia mechanism, possibly causing a delay in endothelial coverage compared to the usual BHV implant. Endothelial covering has already been described on the ventricular membranes of the Carmat TAH [21], also made with bovine pericardium. These membranes are not in direct contact with the native endothelium of the recipient either. Therefore, the endothelial cells may come from the bloodstream, and their nature could be circulating endothelial cells, endothelial progenitor cells, or more immature stem cells [15, 37–39]. The progressive and smooth fibrin deposit probably ensures increased endothelial cell adhesion and proliferation since these cells can stabilize and recanalize fibrin deposit [16, 40] which finally ensures hemocompatibility and prevents thrombo-embolic complications.

Surprisingly, calcification within explanted BHV leaflets has previously been found to mainly occur in the central section of the leaflets [41], as described in our rat model. Furthermore it seems to colocalize predominantly with regions of thrombus formation, raising the hypothesis that BHV thrombosis might be a trigger for valve degeneration [41]. Furthermore, we recently showed that fibrin deposition on a bovine pericardial tissue treated with glutaraldehyde used for BHV led to calcification in a rat model [7]. We demonstrate here that bovine pericardium tissue coated with human endothelial colony-forming cells (ECFCs) exhibits significantly lower levels of calcification compared to bovine pericardium tissue coated solely with fibrin following implantation in athymic Nude rats. Our results suggest that endothelial coverage may serve as a protective factor against pericardial calcification. In 1981, Ishihara et al. suggested that the covering of the surface of bioprosthetic valves by endothelial cells might present a barrier against the penetration of plasma proteins and other components that may contribute to the formation of calcific deposits [8]. Thus, we can propose that the calcification degenerative process of the implanted BHV is influenced by the endothelial covering of its surface. Initial anticoagulation or antiplatelet therapy might restrict fibrin formation to a network conductive to endothelial cell adhesion and prevent the formation of effective thrombus. Conversely, inadequate BHV thrombus could lead to early structural valve deterioration due to insufficient initial endothelial covering. This hypothesis has indeed been corroborated by findings from the GALILEO-4D study, offering insights into the distinction between anticoagulation and antiplatelet agents following BHV implantation during transcatheter aortic-valve replacement. Although rivaroxaban had a significantly higher risk of major bleeding compared to antiplatelet therapy [42], its effectiveness was demonstrated by showing less frequent subclinical leaflet-motion abnormalities than the antiplatelet-based strategy [43, 44]. We recently observed the same finding in a cohort of patients undergoing surgical aortic valve replacement with the INSPIRIS RESILIA bioprosthesis (Edwards Lifesciences). At the 3-month mark, subclinical leaflet thrombosis occurred exclusively in patients under antiplatelet therapy rather than anticoagulation [45]. Finally, Bozso et al. demonstrated that recellularization of bovine pericardium by autologous mesenchymal stem cells results in reduced immune response after co-incubation with autologous blood. They suggested that autologous mesenchymal stem cells recellularization of BHV before implantation might potentially lead to a reduction of structural valve deterioration [46]. This is in line with our study, since we found that recellularization of bovine pericardium tissue by ECFCs results in a lower neutrophil infiltration as well as a lower calcification after implantation in athymic Nude rats.

Endothelial cells are known to contribute for hemostasis through their physiological synthesis and constitutive secretion of VWF, which is stored in Weibel-Palade bodies and released both basally and in response to stimuli. Impaired synthesis, trafficking, or release of VWF, has been recently implicated in the pathogenesis of acquired von Willebrand syndrome [47]. We therefore sought to confirm the functional competence of the ECFCs used to seed the pericardial discs by assessing VWF secretion into the culture supernatant. The cells effectively secreted VWF, while the absence of IL-6 release indicated a non-activated, non-inflammatory state.

However, we suggest that in the longer term, later endothelial dysfunction could lead to endothelialized BHV degeneration through atherosclerosis-like phenotypes, with the same related triggers [48, 49]. Of note, Kostyunin et al. have notably described the differences but also the similarities in physiopathology of atherosclerosis, calcific aortic valve disease, and BHV degeneration [50].

We acknowledge several limitations for our study. Indeed, we aimed to describe the acquisition of hemocompatibility of the BHV from A-TAH through endothelial covering. However, we were able to study only 7 prostheses that were explanted at different times. Due to the low number of patients included, we could not evaluate the potential impact of interindividual status on the endothelial covering rate. Furthermore, the pericardium we used for the rat experimentation was from Neovasc®. Several differences in terms of hemocompatibility processes can be expected between the different bovine pericardium tissues that are available on the market. A set of experiments on different bovine pericardium tissues from different BHV should be tested to confirm our findings. In addition, the relatively short duration of BHVs implantation in our cohort does not allow assessment of whether the degree of endothelial coverage impacts tissue calcification in humans.

All in all, our investigation into BHVs incorporated in the Aeson bioprosthetic Total Artificial Hearts marks an unparalleled advance in the field, offering the first opportunity to scrutinize non-degenerated BHVs within a human context. This research has elucidated a critical pathway to biomaterial passivation, highlighting the emergence of a structured fibrin layer and the subsequent establishment of an endothelial cell monolayer. Moreover, our findings on the potential of endothelial recovery to guard against calcification introduce a novel dimension to the pathophysiology of valve diseases. The significant impact of endothelial restoration on the acquired hemocompatibility of BHVs opens new avenues for research and underscores the imperative for further investigative work. This pivotal study not only broadens our understanding but also lays the groundwork for the development of supremely hemocompatible materials, setting a new benchmark for future innovations in valve technology.
