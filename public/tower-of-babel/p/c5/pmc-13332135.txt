Introduction

Gastric abscess is a rare and often overlooked clinical entity that can present with signs and symptoms mimicking gastric malignancy. This diagnostic overlap can pose significant challenges, particularly when standard diagnostic tools such as imaging studies and endoscopy yield inconclusive results. The rarity of gastric abscesses is largely attributed to the stomach's rich vascular supply and the bactericidal effect of gastric acid, which act as protective mechanisms against infection [1]. However, when gastric abscesses do occur, they most commonly involve the pyloric or antral regions, although they can arise anywhere within the stomach [2]. Even with advanced diagnostic modalities, including endoscopic ultrasound (EUS), it remains difficult to distinguish gastric abscesses from malignant tumors reliably [3].

Several case reports have described patients initially presumed to have advanced gastric cancer who were later found, often after surgical intervention, to have benign abscesses [4, 5, 6]. These diagnostic pitfalls emphasize the limitations of non‐invasive methods and highlight the need for clinicians to maintain a high index of suspicion for gastric abscess, particularly when clinical and histologic findings are discordant.

Here, we present a case of a patient who was initially diagnosed with suspected gastric cancer based on radiologic and endoscopic findings. Despite multiple biopsies and EUS‐guided fine needle aspiration (FNA), a definitive diagnosis remained elusive until surgical resection revealed the lesion to be a gastric abscess. This case underscores the diagnostic challenges associated with this rare condition and the importance of considering gastric abscess in the differential diagnosis of suspicious gastric masses.

Case Presentation

History and Physical Examination

Our patient is a 58‐year‐old female patient with a past medical history of peptic ulcer disease (PUD), confirmed by endoscopy 15 years prior, with no available records for endoscopy. The patient experienced a three‐week history of fever (documented at 38.8°C) accompanied by a productive cough, sore throat, and hoarseness. Symptoms partially improved with antibiotics and acetaminophen. 10 days prior to admission, she self‐administered ibuprofen for musculoskeletal pain, subsequently developing progressive epigastric pain, nausea, and black tarry stool suggestive of melena. There was no history of jaundice, no weight loss, no bleeding from other sites, no sick contact, and no family history of chronic gastrointestinal diseases or malignancy. Her past medical history was significant for Type II diabetes mellitus and hypertension. She was taking Metformin and Amlodipine. Her past surgical history was significant for uncomplicated cholecystectomy and hysterectomy more than 5 years before presentation.

On admission, she was hemodynamically stable. Her general examination was remarkable for conjunctival pallor. Her abdomen was soft and lax with tenderness on deep palpation of the epigastric area. No hepatosplenomegaly and no stigmata of liver disease were appreciated. Chest, cardiac, and neurological examinations were non‐revealing.

She was transfused with multiple units of packed red blood cells (PRBCs) as she continued to have melena with further drops in her hemoglobin. The persistent melena, in the absence of an active bleeding source identified at endoscopy, is most likely explained by the passage of oxidized residual blood through the gastrointestinal tract—a well‐recognized phenomenon when bleeding has ceased or is intermittent. She underwent an upper endoscopy that showed a large pedunculated, ulcerated polypoid mass in the gastric body (greater curvature) (Figure 2) suggestive of a neoplastic process, including gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST) or gastric adenocarcinoma. However, the biopsy result showed severe active chronic gastritis with ulceration and reactive glands, and it was negative for Helicobacter pylori , intestinal metaplasia, dysplasia, or malignancy.

Given the patient's age, clinical presentation with persistent melena while in hospital, and highly suspicious radiologic and endoscopic findings, malignancy was still considered in the differential diagnoses despite benign biopsy results. After a multi‐disciplinary team (MDT) meeting discussion, endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) with fine needle aspiration (FNA) was done with limited submucosal sampling. Although EUS‐guided aspiration was considered as a potential diagnostic and therapeutic approach, per the 2023 ACG Clinical Guideline (Jacobson et al. [7]), EUS imaging alone carries only 64%–80% sensitivity and 77%–80% specificity for differentiating benign from malignant subepithelial lesions, with fine‐needle sampling improving accuracy to 73%–84%—still leaving meaningful diagnostic uncertainty. Given the combination of worrisome imaging characteristics, clinical urgency, and discordant non‐invasive results, the multidisciplinary team determined that surgical resection was the most appropriate path to definitive diagnosis and treatment [1].

The FNA biopsy was remarkable for a superficial fragment of antral‐type gastric mucosa with reactive gastritis and microscopic perivascular spindle cell proliferation with background fibrin and inflammatory cells. Findings were reviewed again, and the recommendation was to go for surgical resection. While the perivascular spindle cell proliferation raised GIST as a differential diagnosis, comprehensive immunohistochemical staining performed on the surgical specimen was negative for CD117 (c‐KIT), DOG1, and CD34—the defining markers of GIST—as well as negative for S100, desmin, and smooth muscle actin, effectively excluding GIST and other mesenchymal tumors. The final pathology, confirmed by expert review at Mayo Clinic, was consistent with myofibroblastic proliferation in the context of abscess formation.

Robotic laparoscopic gastric wedge resection was performed, and a localized gastric mass without peritoneal spread was found. The initial histopathology report for the mass biopsy reported a benign mesenchymal lesion along with 14 benign reactive lymph nodes.

The sample was sent to Mayo Clinic for expert pathology review. After 2 weeks, the report returned with a finding of myelofibroblastic proliferation with associated abscess. The lesion was reclassified as a gastric abscess, avoiding unnecessary oncologic treatment.

The patient was seen in the clinic after 3 months of surgery. She was doing well, with no recurrence of bleeding and no other concerning symptoms.

Discussion

Gastric abscess is defined as a pyogenic inflammatory condition of the gastric wall. It is a rare condition, and its rarity could be attributed to adequate gastric blood supply as well as the bactericidal effect of gastric acidity [1]. The pylorus and antrum are the most common sites of gastric abscess. However, they can develop anywhere in the stomach [2], and pathophysiology includes either direct invasion by microorganisms or hematogenous spread from distant infection [8]. Multiple risk factors have been identified for gastric abscess development, including diabetes mellitus, alcoholism, immunosuppression, and decreased gastric acidity [8, 9]; however, many cases have been reported without predisposing factors [8, 10, 11].

A gastric abscess can develop inside gastric subepithelial lesions, including mesenchymal tumors [12, 13, 14, 15]. The diagnosis could be challenging given the rarity of the condition and findings that resemble gastric cancer, and even with advanced diagnostic methods such as endoscopic ultrasound (EUS), it would be difficult to distinguish them from tumors [3]. Many cases have been reported with provisional clinical diagnoses of advanced gastric cancer with pathologic results compatible with gastric abscess; in all of these cases, surgical resection was the safest approach to reach the definitive diagnosis of benign pathology [4, 5, 6]. Our case, as well as the review of all reported cases, suggests the limited capability of available non‐invasive diagnostic methods, including endoscopy, EUS, and MRCP, in differentiating benign gastric abscess from gastric cancer, so physicians should keep a low threshold to keep gastric abscess as a differential diagnosis of suspected gastric tumor seen by non‐invasive investigations.

Will et al. reported the first successful endoscopic treatment of a gastric abscess in 2003 [16]. The diagnosis was evident, as the mucosa was broken and visible pus was draining. Few cases with successful endoscopic treatment have been reported since then [3, 17, 18]. However, surgical resection remains the standard treatment given the high mortality rate, and it is often misdiagnosed as gastric cancer [2, 6].

Conclusion

Gastric abscess is a rare and often misdiagnosed condition that can closely mimic gastric malignancy on imaging and endoscopic evaluation. Despite advances in diagnostic techniques, including EUS and tissue sampling, non‐invasive methods may be insufficient to achieve a definitive diagnosis. Our case highlights the importance of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis when evaluating gastric masses, especially when clinical, radiologic, and histologic findings are discordant. Surgical resection, while invasive, may be necessary in select cases to establish the correct diagnosis and avoid inappropriate oncologic treatment. Clinicians should consider gastric abscess in the differential diagnosis of suspected gastric tumors to prevent unnecessary interventions and optimize patient outcomes.
