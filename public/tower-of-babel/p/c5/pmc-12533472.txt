Altered expressions of CGRP, SULT1A1, HMGB1, and HIF-1α in the trigeminal ganglion in medication overuse headache in female rats
Authors: Elif Gulcicek Abbasoglu Topa, Doga Vuralli, Duygu Deniz Usta, Aysen Calikusu, Zeynep Yigman, Hayrunnisa Bolay
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/AimMedication-overuse headache (MOH) is a female-predominant secondary headache generally associated with nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug overuse and chronification of migraine. MOH is associated with elevated serum levels of inflammatory and nociceptive molecules and intestinal leak in migraine patients. We aimed to characterize the changes in expression patterns of high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) and hypoxia-inducible factor 1 alpha (HIF-1α) in the trigeminal ganglion cells, thereby offering new insights into the molecular mechanisms contributing to MOH.MethodsMOH was induced by oral piroxicam in female Sprague Dawley rats, and pain-related behaviors were assessed via periorbital mechanical withdrawal thresholds, head-face grooming, freezing, and head shaking during the metestrus and diestrus phases. The levels, and the cell type preference and nuclear or cytoplasmic localization for the expression of HMGB1, HIF-1α, calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), and SULT1A1 in the trigeminal ganglia were examined using immunohistochemistry and Western blot analyses.ResultsChronic piroxicam administration decreased periorbital withdrawal thresholds and increased nociceptive behaviors. CGRP-positive neuron number and CGRP levels are increased in the TG of the MOH group. HMGB1 immunoreactivity was observed in both neurons and satellite glial cells (SGCs), with enhanced cytoplasmic translocation and elevated total protein levels in the MOH group. HIF-1α was present in neurons and SGCs, with increased nuclear localization and expression in the MOH group. SULT1A1 was localized to the cytoplasm of trigeminal ganglion neurons, not detected in satellite glial cells, and was significantly downregulated in the MOH group.ConclusionOur study showed for the first time that NSAID overuse headache is associated with a robust increase in CGRP, HMGB1, and HIF-1α and reduced SULT1A1 expression in the trigeminal ganglion. Increased nociceptive, inflammatory, and mitochondrial stress signaling in the trigeminal ganglion cells may drive sustained trigeminal nociceptive activity in MOH. Besides, reduced SULT1A1 expression in the trigeminal ganglion suggests reduced capacity of detoxification and catecholamine metabolism in MOH. Introduction

Medication-overuse headache (MOH) can be induced by the overuse of acute analgesics in patients with migraine and is frequently associated with chronic migraine by increasing the frequency and severity of headaches. NSAIDs are the most commonly used over-the-counter analgesics worldwide. MOH affects approximately 1–2% of the global population and is 3–4 times more frequently seen in females [1, 2]. The pathogenesis of MOH, however, remains to be fully elucidated. Persistent activation of the trigeminovascular system (TVS) and dysregulation of central pain-modulatory circuits drive MOH by lowering pain thresholds [3, 4]. Stimulation of the TVS triggers release of neuropeptides such as calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), which interacts with neighboring neurons and satellite glial cells (SGCs) to maintain peripheral sensitization and amplify central nociceptive signaling [5]. CGRP also contributes to headache development via vasodilation, increased vascular permeability, glial cell activation, and the release of inflammatory mediators [6, 7].

High Mobility Group Box-1 (HMGB1) is a common non-histone nuclear DNA-binding protein expressed in various cell nuclei, including neurons and microglia [8, 9]. HMGB1 is the most mobile protein in the nucleus and can reach the cytosol within 1–2 s. The location of HMGB1 in the cell varies depending on the cell type, tissue, and stress signals [10–12]. HMGB1 is an essential extracellular protein in inflammation and is actively secreted from stressed cells, including neurons, astrocytes, neuroblastoma cells, and other tumor cells after translocation to the cytoplasm [8, 13]. The transport of HMGB1 from the nucleus to the cytoplasm regulates cell stress response and inflammation [8]. Extracellular release of HMGB1 acts as a damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) to stimulate the innate immune system, and it forms the molecule “Alarmin” together with pathogen-associated molecular pattern protein (PAMP) against the disturbances of homeostasis [14–16]. The animal MOH models show elevated brain HMGB1 levels in both male and female rats [17, 18]. Increased serum HMGB1 levels have been demonstrated in chronic migraine patients with MOH [19].

Hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF-1) is an important transcription factor related to cellular survival in response to the oxygen level in the cell. Activation of HIF-1 depends on the stabilization of the oxygen-sensitive “α” subunit. The functional alpha (α) subunit is found in the cell cytoplasm. It is sensitive to oxygen, while the beta (β) subunit is constitutively expressed in the nucleus and is resistant to oxygen [20–22]. Under hypoxic conditions, the alpha subunit (HIF-1α) translocates to the nucleus. It forms a heterodimer with the beta subunit, regulating the transcription of hundreds of genes related to erythropoiesis, angiogenesis, metabolic reprogramming, apoptosis, or survival, in response to hypoxia [23, 24]. In addition to hypoxia, there is evidence that HIF-1 is regulated by various proinflammatory cytokines under normoxic conditions using different signaling pathways. Many studies have shown that the regulation of inflammatory mediators such as nitric oxide (NO), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and interleukin-1β (IL-1β) is associated with HIF-1α, and the activity of HIF-1α plays a vital role during the initiation and possibly termination of inflammation. High serum HIF-1α levels have been demonstrated in chronic migraine patients with MOH [19].

Sulfotransferase (SULT) enzymes, particularly the isoforms SULT1A1 and SULT1A3, detoxify numerous drugs, metabolize hormones and neurotransmitters [25–27], and are expressed in the liver [22], brain [28–30], and gut [28, 31]. Reduced SULT1A1 enzyme activity in the brain was revealed in an MOH animal model induced by mefenamic acid [32]. The distribution and function of SULT1A1 in the trigeminal ganglion remain to be defined.

The present study employs an experimental MOH model induced by chronic NSAID administration to (i) characterize HMGB1 and HIF-1α expressions in the trigeminal ganglion and (ii) delineate cell-specific alterations in SULT1A1 expression, thereby providing new insight into the molecular mechanisms underlying MOH.

Materials and methods

Animals and experimental groups

This study used 32 female Sprague Dawley rats weighing 200 to 250 g. The animals were divided into two groups: a control group of 16 rats and an experimental group of 16 rats administered oral piroxicam (10 mg/kg/day). Piroxicam was administered daily by gavage for 5 weeks to induce MOH. The control group received an equal volume of vehicle (water, 1 ml/kg/day) for the same duration. Throughout the experimental period, the animals were housed in a controlled environment with a 12-hour light/dark cycle and had ad libitum access to food and water. Behavioral testing was conducted according to the estrous cycle stages of the female rats. The study was carried out in accordance with national guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals, and the Local Ethics Committee for Animal Experiments of Gazi University approved the protocol (Approval No: GÜ-ET-23.007).

Behavioral assessments

Estrous cycle staging in female rats was performed using vaginal lavage. There is an enhanced sensitization of the trigeminal system during the later halves of proestrus and estrus, and pain thresholds are reduced during the proestrus and estrus phases; therefore, we conducted behavioral testing during the metestrus and diestrus phases [33, 34]. All behavioral evaluations were carried out within the same time frame of the day to minimize circadian variability. All behavioral tests were conducted during the light phase (between 9:00 a.m. and 6:00 p.m.). Rats were habituated to the testing room for at least 30 min before the assessments. The testing room was soundproof, and rats were returned to their home cages after each trial. The experimental apparatus was cleaned with 70% ethanol to eliminate odor cues that might affect behavior. Baseline (BL) values were recorded before testing.

Pain-related spontaneous behaviors, including head and facial grooming, freezing, and head shakes, were monitored for 10 min in a plexiglas testing cage equipped with a video recording system [32]. Mechanical pain thresholds were assessed using calibrated von Frey filaments (ranging from 0.008 g to 15 g; Aesthesio® Precision Tactile Sensory Evaluators). The “up-and-down” method was applied, starting with the 2.0 g filament, and the mid-rostral region of the eyes was targeted for stimulation [35]. A positive response was defined as head withdrawal, head shaking, or grooming behavior. The 50% withdrawal thresholds were calculated using the online tool available at https://bioapps.shinyapps.io/von_frey_app/.

Sacrification and tissue collection

Rats were anesthetized using a combination of ketamine (Ketalar, Pfizer, 60 mg/kg) and xylazine (Rompun™, Bayer, 8 mg/kg) administered intraperitoneally. A hind leg pinch followed the injections to ensure sufficient sedation. Animals were euthanized via terminal intracardiac blood collection under anesthesia, and the trigeminal ganglia (TGs) were quickly harvested and stored at − 80 °C for Western blot analysis.

For immunohistochemical analyses, animals were weighed to calculate the accurate anesthetic dosage. They were deeply anesthetized with intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (Ketalar, Pfizer, 60 mg/kg) and xylazine (Rompun™, Bayer, 8 mg/kg). Transcardial perfusion was then performed using heparinized saline, followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Trigeminal ganglia (TG) tissues were post-fixed in 4% PFA for 48 h.

Western blotting

For western blot analysis, frozen TG tissues were homogenized and lysed using homogenization buffer (T-PER, Thermo Fisher Scientific, US) that contained a cocktail of protease inhibitors with a tissue homogenizer cooled to 4 °C (Bertin Technologies, France). Each sample’s protein concentration (µg/µl) was measured using a fluorometric method (Qubit 4 Fluorometer, Thermofisher Scientific, USA).

Equal amounts (20 µg) of protein samples were loaded onto 4–12% gradient Bolt Bis-Tris SDS-PAGE gels (Invitrogen, USA). Proteins were then transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. After blocking with % 10 normal goat serum (NGS), the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti–HIF-1α (1:500; Elabscience, US), anti–HMGB1 (1:500; Elabscience, US), anti–SULT1A1 (1:500; Elabscience, US), and anti–CGRP (1:500; Santa Cruz, US). An anti-β-actin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, US) antibody was used as a loading control. Then, the primary antibody was removed, and the membranes were washed three times and further incubated for two hours at room temperature with secondary anti-rabbit IgG-HRP antibody and anti-mouse IgG-HRP (1:5,000; Thermo Fisher Scientific, US). Protein detection was performed using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL), and protein bands were visualized and quantified using the iBright FL1500 imaging system (Invitrogen, US). The intensity of each target protein band was normalized to the corresponding β-actin band.

Immunofluorescence

After fixation, the tissues were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, embedded in paraffin, sectioned at five µm thickness, and mounted on positively charged glass slides. Tissue sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through a graded series of ethanol solutions, and heated to 95 °C for 20 min in 10 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for antigen retrieval. After cooling to room temperature and rinsing three times with PBS, non-specific binding was blocked by incubation in PBS with 0.05% Tween 20 and 10% normal goat serum (NGS, Calbiochem) for 1 h at room temperature (RT). Sections were then incubated overnight at + 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted in blocking solution: HIF-1α (1:1000, rabbit polyclonal, Elabscience, US), HMGB1 (1:1000, rabbit polyclonal, Elabscience, US), SULT1A1 (1:1000, rabbit polyclonal, Elabscience, US), and CGRP (1:1000, mouse monoclonal, Santa Cruz, US).

Double immunohistochemical staining was carried out with antibodies against neural cell marker β III-tubulin, glutamine synthetase (GS), which is specific to satellite glial cells, to investigate the cell type-specific expressions of these proteins. The following day, sections were washed three times in PBS and incubated with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies: Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG and Cy3-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (both from Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA; 1:200) for 2 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1:5000), and sections were mounted using Fluoromount-G (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and coverslipped. Prepared specimens were visualized using an upright fluorescence microscope (Axio Scope A.1, Zeiss, Germany) with an AxioCam 503 camera (Zeiss, Germany). Images were captured using HBO100 fluorescence filter sets at 20× and 40× magnifications, specifically from the trigeminal ganglion’s ophthalmic (V1) division due to its relation to the headache.

Image analysis

To analyze the HMGB1 or HIF-1α expressions within the V1 division of the trigeminal ganglia (TG), each of the four sections labelled with either HMGB1 or HIF-1α was investigated from each animal (n = 6 from each group). Images of the neurons were obtained from five randomly selected, non-overlapping fields of V1 division per section and were captured under 40× objectives for analysis. Since cytoplasmic translocation of HMGB1 and nuclear translocation of HIF-1α were considered for immunopositivity, corrected total cell fluorescence (CTCF) analysis was used to measure mean fluorescence intensity to reveal the increased cytoplasmic or nuclear expression of HMGB1 and HIF-1α, respectively, in individual ganglionic neurons (number of neurons evaluated per section was approximately 100 ± 10 for HMGB1 and HIF-1α individually). Mean fluorescence intensity of each nucleus was measured in HIF-1α and HMGB1 images, and total cell fluorescence was obtained by combining nuclear and cytoplasmic signals. Both nuclear and cytoplasmic intensities were normalized to the total fluorescence intensity of each cell. To that end, by using freehand tool selection, the boundary of the nucleus of each neuron in the field was drawn to determine the ROI using the DAPI channels of images. Then, the area of each nucleus and the HMGB1 or HIF-1α mean fluorescence density were measured in these designated nuclear areas in the relevant channels of images. An integrated density per nucleus was calculated by multiplying the area of the nucleus by the mean fluorescence density of the HMGB1 or HIF-1α. Mean fluorescence of the background was measured in the same images. Afterwards, CTCF was calculated using the following formula: CTCF = Integrated Density – (Area of selected cell × Mean fluorescence of background readings) [36]. The same procedure was applied to measure the CTCF values of the entire cell (nucleus + cytoplasm), and the CTCF value of the entire cell was proportioned to the CTCF value of the nucleus.

To analyze CGRP expression within the V1 division of the trigeminal ganglia (TG), four sections per animal (n = 6 animals from each group) labelled with CGRP antibody were observed, and images of 3 fields per section under 20× objectives were captured. The number of neurons positive for the CGRP signal was counted in these micrographs. The percentage of CGRP-positive neurons was calculated, and the average proportion of neurons was designated, which is expressed as “CGRP (+) neurons %”.

Image acquisition and analysis for CTCF were performed using Zen Blue Edition 3.9 software, and image analysis for the CGRP-positive cell number was performed using the ImageJ software (1.54f, National Institutes of Health, USA). All analyses were carried out in a blinded manner to prevent bias.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted and analyzed in a blinded manner. Sample size was calculated using G*Power software. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, CA, USA). Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). The normality of the data was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Continuous variables were analyzed with Student t-tests, or repeated measures analysis of variance, and post-hoc multiple comparisons were carried out using Šidák’s multiple comparisons test. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Effect of MOH on nociceptive behaviors: increased pain responses and decreased mechanical withdrawal thresholds were observed

A significant increase in pain-related behaviors was observed in the MOH group subjected to chronic piroxicam administration compared to the control group. The total duration of freezing behavior (p = 0.03), head-face grooming duration (p < 0.001), and the number of head shakes (p < 0.001) were significantly higher in the MOH group than in controls. Moreover, the periorbital mechanical withdrawal thresholds were markedly decreased in the MOH group compared to the control group and baseline values (p < 0.0001, both) (Fig. 1).

Statistical analysis was performed using the Student’s t-test in figures A, B, C; while repeated measures ANOVA was used in figure D (n = 8 animals for each group). In MOH, pain-related molecule CGRP and inflammation-related mediators (HMGB1, HIF-1α) are increased in the trigeminal ganglion, while SULT1A1 is decreased

CGRP immunostaining revealed a distribution throughout the whole cytoplasm of the neurons, with a much more intense staining around the nucleus. CGRP immunostaining appeared in a granular pattern confined to the cytoplasm of small and medium-sized neurons and in thin fibers presumed to be C fibers (Fig. 2A). Quantitative analysis revealed a significant elevation in the percentage of CGRP-positive neurons in the MOH group compared to controls (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2B). These immunohistochemical findings were corroborated by western blot analysis, which demonstrated increased CGRP protein expression levels in trigeminal ganglion tissue lysates from the MOH group relative to controls (p = 0.002) (Fig. 2C).

Discussion

This study is the first to demonstrate the association between TG neurons, SGCs, and neuroinflammatory mediators such as HMGB1 and HIF-1α in the pathogenesis of MOH. We investigated the expression and distribution of key inflammatory and nociceptive mediators, CGRP, HMGB1, and HIF-1α within TG neurons and SGCs during the development of MOH. We have demonstrated increased expression of the innate immune response and alarm protein HMGB1 and the mitochondrial stress-related inflammatory response protein HIF-1α in TG neurons. As a result of further analysis conducted on immunohistochemistry, this upregulation was correlated with increased cytoplasmic translocation of HMGB1 and nuclear translocation of HIF-1α in neuronal cells, suggesting a possible role of neuroinflammatory signaling in MOH. Immunohistochemistry and western blot analyses demonstrated that CGRP, the most crucial neuropeptide in the pathogenesis of migraine, was increased in TG neurons in MOH. Additionally, this study was the first to show the cell-specific localization of SULT1A1 in the trigeminal ganglion and its alteration in MOH. SULT1A1, localized exclusively in TG neurons, decreased with MOH, suggesting impaired drug and neurotransmitter metabolism. Our findings indicate that neuroinflammation, compromised detoxification capacity, and impaired catecholamine homeostasis in the TG may contribute to the chronicity of headache in MOH.

In our MOH model, female rats were preferred, and piroxicam, an NSAID, was administered orally to enhance clinical compatibility. In addition, animals were examined in the metestrus and early diestrus phases of the cycle to eliminate the effects of the menstrual cycle on pain threshold changes. Our findings show that five-week oral administration of piroxicam led to increased pain-related behaviors—such as head grooming, head shaking, and freezing—and a significant reduction in periorbital mechanical pain thresholds. In previous studies, mechanical pain thresholds were shown to be altered similarly in MOH models. In one of these studies, repeated administration of sumatriptan induced both periorbital and paw mechanical allodynia in mice [37], and two-week administration of either lasmiditan or sumatriptan resulted in cutaneous allodynia in both periorbital and hind paw regions in another study [38]. While previous studies primarily focused on triptan-induced models, we have previously shown comparable behavioral and sensory alterations in an NSAID-induced MOH model using intraperitoneal administration of mefenamic acid [32]. We advanced this model by utilizing oral administration of piroxicam to increase the translational relevance of our findings [17]. Repeated exposure to piroxicam reproduced hallmark features of MOH, supporting its validity as a novel oral NSAID-based MOH model.

The trigeminal ganglion and its ophthalmic branch, which play a pivotal role in headache pathophysiology, were central to our investigation. The trigeminal ganglion comprises pseudo-unipolar sensory neurons enveloped by SGCs, structurally and functionally resembling astrocytes of the central nervous system. Despite the well-known importance of TG in migraine and headache disorders, this is the first study to evaluate changes in TG concerning expressions of mediators related to pain and inflammation in the context of MOH.

At the molecular level, our immunohistochemical analyses revealed that the ophthalmic branch of TG—closely associated with headache processing—exhibited increased expression of HMGB1 and HIF-1α in TG neurons. These findings suggest activation of innate immune responses and mitochondrial stress pathways in MOH. Notably, these proteins’ nuclear or cytoplasmic translocation patterns indicated intracellular signaling changes that may be attributable to inflammation-related cellular processes. Furthermore, Western blot analyses demonstrated elevated levels of CGRP in TG. This finding was also consistent with an increased number of ganglionic neurons expressing CGRP, which was revealed by immunohistochemistry.

This study covers a multifactorial model of MOH implicating neuroinflammatory mechanisms, glial-neuronal interactions, and impaired detoxification processes within TG. By integrating clinically relevant factors such as oral NSAID administration and female sex, the model gains translational relevance and may reflect key aspects of MOH in patients. The findings underscore the presumptive contribution of TG-localized molecular and cellular alterations—including dysregulated neuroinflammatory signaling and reduced detoxification capacity—to the pathogenesis of MOH. As such, this model provides a valuable insight for further investigation into the peripheral mechanisms underlying headache disorders and may guide the development of therapeutic strategies to restore metabolic and inflammatory homeostasis in sensory ganglia.

Many clinical and preclinical studies have demonstrated that increased levels of CGRP, a potent nociceptive neuropeptide synthesized in TG neurons and released from peripheral and central terminals of the trigeminal nerve, occur during headache episodes [3, 18, 19, 39]. Chronic administration of medications frequently overused by patients with MOH—such as triptans, opioids, and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs—has been shown to alter the levels of key pain-related molecules, including CGRP, substance P, nitric oxide, and serotonin [40–42].

In the current study, CGRP immunoreactivity was predominantly observed in the cytoplasm of small- and medium-sized TG neurons, particularly around the perinuclear cytoplasm. The number of CGRP-positive neurons and total CGRP protein expression significantly increased in the TG of animals exposed to chronic piroxicam treatment. Notably, no CGRP immunoreactivity was detected in SGCs under either control or MOH conditions, suggesting that CGRP expression in MOH remains neuron-specific within the TG.

The observed increase in CGRP levels was accompanied by behavioral indicators of headache—such as increased head grooming, shaking, freezing—and decreased periorbital mechanical pain thresholds, indicating that trigeminal nociceptive activation may stand as a key component in the development of MOH in this model. Furthermore, CGRP expression with accompanying nuclear translocation of HIF-1α in TG neurons provides novel evidence drawing attention to possible mitochondrial stress and an associated inflammatory response in nociceptive sensory neurons. To our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate the co-expression of CGRP and HIF-1α within the TG in the context of MOH.

HMGB1, a chromatin-binding protein implicated in DNA replication, transcription, and repair, has recently emerged as a pivotal mediator of neuroinflammatory signaling in various pathological contexts, including seizures, ischemia, and chronic pain conditions [43]. Elevated serum HMGB1 levels have also been associated with inflammatory responses in COVID-19-related headaches [44]. In our previous studies, we showed that brain HMGB1 levels increased in both male and female rats in the MOH model established by the use of two different NSAIDs and that serum HMGB1 levels also increased in chronic migraine patients with MOH [17–19]. However, the role of HMGB1 in MOH, particularly within the trigeminal ganglion, had not been explored until now. We showed that chronic piroxicam administration increased HMGB1 expression in TG neurons. HMGB1 was primarily localized to the nucleus in control animals, consistent with its canonical function. In contrast, animals subjected to MOH conditions exhibited cytoplasmic translocation of HMGB1, indicating its activation. This translocation pattern was not dependent on neuronal size and was uniformly observed across small, medium, and large TG neurons.

The upregulation of HMGB1 and its cytoplasmic redistribution support the hypothesis that HMGB1 serves a dual role in MOH pathophysiology. On one hand, its cytoplasmic accumulation may represent an endogenous attempt to protect neurons from chronic stress and damage, as suggested by its role in initiating repair mechanisms [45]. On the other hand, its potential extracellular release and proinflammatory activity may contribute to the sustained neuroinflammation and sensitization observed in chronic headache conditions, thereby promoting the persistence of MOH.

Our findings are consistent with previous work showing elevated HMGB1 levels in the brain in a mefenamic acid-induced MOH model [17], and together, these results highlight HMGB1 as a potential mediator linking peripheral inflammation in TG with central sensitization mechanisms in MOH. Future investigations are warranted to clarify whether HMGB1 acts primarily as a component of an intracellular protective mechanism or as a secreted proinflammatory mediator during MOH.

In addition to systemic inflammation, which may originate from leaky gut mechanisms and has been previously associated with NSAID overuse [17, 18], the current study provides evidence that HMGB1 increase in TG neurons may also constitute a local source of probable inflammation that may contribute to the persistence of headache symptoms in MOH. These findings may suggest that the trigeminal ganglion is an active participant in the neuroinflammatory process of MOH, beyond being a passive relay center.

HIF-1 is a key transcription factor involved in cellular adaptation to hypoxic stress. Its activity depends on the stabilization and nuclear translocation of its oxygen-sensitive α-subunit; HIF-1α [46, 47]. In the present study, HIF-1α immunoreactivity was observed in both TG neurons and SGCs. While its expression in the control group was weak and primarily cytoplasmic, chronic piroxicam exposure in the MOH group induced strong nuclear translocation, indicating activation. Quantitative analysis confirmed a significant increase in HIF-1α expression in the MOH group. This suggests a potential role of mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress in the trigeminal system during MOH, with HIF-1α acting to regulate metabolic adaptation, mitochondrial integrity, and cellular survival mechanisms. Since CGRP is involved in pain, HIF-1α translocation in CGRP-positive TG supports that HIF-1α is a potential contributor to persistent nociception besides being a marker of mitochondrial energy stress.

SULT1A1, the predominant sulfotransferase isoform in the rodent central nervous system, plays a crucial role in the sulfonation and detoxification of xenobiotics and endogenous compounds and the metabolism of neurotransmitters [48]. This study’s novel and notable finding is the significant reduction of SULT1A1 in the TG during MOH development. Its downregulation may reflect impaired detoxification capacity within the TG microenvironment, potentially exacerbating neuroinflammatory responses, pain sensitivity, and altered metabolism of catecholamines. Our findings revealed that SULT1A1 was exclusively expressed in TG neurons, with no detectable expression in surrounding SGCs. Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated homogeneous cytoplasmic distribution of SULT1A1 across all neuronal subtypes -small, medium, and large. Although no change in subcellular localization was observed in MOH by immunohistochemistry, a significant reduction in total SULT1A1 protein levels was detected, consistent with our earlier findings from a mefenamic acid-induced MOH model [32]. In that previous study, decreased brain SULT1A1 activity was associated with increased cortical excitability and reduced cortical spreading depression (CSD) thresholds, suggesting a role in central sensitization. Reduced SULT1A1 expression could impair neurotransmitter homeostasis, potentially contributing to sustained nociceptive signaling in MOH. These findings may point to a novel mechanism by which impaired enzymatic detoxification within TG may facilitate peripheral sensitization and maintenance of headache chronification.

One of the key strengths of our study is the use of a clinically relevant MOH model involving the administration of an oral NSAID in female rats. In this model, we showed a significant upregulation of HMGB1 and HIF-1α in TG neurons, suggesting a role of neuroinflammatory signaling in TG in the pathogenesis of MOH. Additionally, we were the first to identify the cell-specific localization of SULT1A1 in the trigeminal ganglion and to show its downregulation in MOH, suggesting impaired drug detoxification capacity and disrupted neurotransmitter metabolism.

The study has several limitations that need to be acknowledged. We did not include a comprehensive panel of inflammatory markers, which should have provided a broader understanding of the inflammatory response in TG in MOH. We only used female rats to enhance clinical compatibility, preventing us from evaluating sex differences in our MOH model. We only evaluated the animals during the metestrus and diestrus phases instead of assessing them across all cycle stages. Another limitation of the current study was the lack of assessment of serum levels for the parameters investigated. Our analysis was restricted to TG, and we did not examine the cerebral cortex, which may also be involved in MOH pathophysiology. Also, a quantitative analysis of immunoreactivity in SGCs was not performed.

Conclusions

In this study, we demonstrated for the first time that chronic NSAID exposure leads to molecular alterations that sustain neuroinflammatory processes and are involved in pain and drug and neurotransmitter metabolism within TG in a female rat model of MOH. The increased expression of HMGB1 and HIF-1α, elevated CGRP levels, and reduced SULT1A1 expression in TG neurons reflect the presence of mitochondrial stress, sustained neuroinflammatory signaling, compromised detoxification capacity, and impaired catecholamine homeostasis. These findings suggest that both neuroinflammatory response and metabolic dysregulation in the trigeminal ganglion neurons may contribute to the persistence of trigeminal sensitization and pain in MOH. Future studies should further investigate the potential interaction between systemic inflammation and trigeminal neuroinflammation in the pathogenesis of chronic headaches.
