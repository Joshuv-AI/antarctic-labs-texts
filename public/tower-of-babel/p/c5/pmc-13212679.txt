Radioanatomical assessment of the subtympanic sinus and its relationship to facial nerve position and temporal bone pneumatization in adults: CT study
Authors: Costanza Campostrini, Tomasz Wojciechowski, Kazimierz Szopiński, Matteo Fermi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe subtympanic sinus (STS) is a recess of the inferior retrotympanum of various morphology and depth. Its shape and dimensions may depend on the configuration of surrounding bony ridges and the degree of temporal bone pneumatization. Despite increasing interest in retrotympanic anatomy, quantitative radiological descriptions of STS variability in adults remain limited. Our aim was to describe the morphology, dimensions, and distribution of the subtympanic sinus in adults and to analyze its anatomical relationships with the facial nerve, the subcochlear canaliculus, and pneumatization of the temporal bone.Materials and methodsComputed tomography (HRCT and CBCT) scans of 82 adults (164 temporal bones) without otologic disease were retrospectively analyzed. The STS was classified according to its extension relative to the mastoid segment of the facial nerve. Sinus depth and width were measured, and the presence of facial nerve–adjacent pneumatization, subcochlear canaliculus, and pneumatization within the vascular compartment of the petrous bone were assessed.ResultsType A STS configuration was most prevalent (80.5%), followed by type B (16.5%) and type C (3.0%). STS depth increased progressively from type A to type C, with statistically significant differences among groups (p < 0.001), whereas sinus width did not differ significantly. A connection with the subcochlear canaliculus was present in 62.8% of temporal bones and was significantly associated with greater STS depth. Facial nerve–adjacent pneumatization was frequent (85.4%) and correlated with increased STS depth.ConclusionsThe subtympanic sinus exhibits substantial morphological variability in adults, primarily expressed through differences in depth. Its configuration may reflect broader patterns of temporal bone pneumatization and may support the concept of an integrated retrotympanic and infracochlear anatomical system.

Introduction

The retrotympanum represents one of the most anatomically complex regions of the middle ear, formed at the interface between the otic capsule, the facial canal, and the styloid complex [6, 7, 13]. It comprises several recesses and sinuses, the configuration of which is determined by variable bony ridges, including the ponticulus, subiculum, and finiculus. Among these structures, the subtympanic sinus (STS) is located inferior to the sinus tympani and posterior to the round window niche, forming a recess with highly variable morphology [2, 13, 17].

The STS received limited attention in classical anatomical descriptions, largely due to its restricted visualization using traditional microscopic techniques [2, 6, 13]. Advances in endoscopic approaches and high-resolution imaging have renewed interest in this region, allowing improved characterization of its spatial relationships and anatomical variants [6]. Anschuetz et al. proposed classification systems based on the extension of the STS relative to the facial nerve, emphasizing its limited visualization, and therefore high clinical relevance in case of cholesteatoma removal [2]. However, available studies have been conducted on relatively small cohorts or using ex vivo techniques such as micro-CT, which, although highly precise, may not fully reflect clinically encountered anatomical conditions [2, 8].

From an anatomical perspective, the STS should not be regarded as an isolated cavity. Its proximity to the round window niche, the facial nerve canal, and the infracochlear pneumatized region suggests that it may participate in a broader system of air spaces within the temporal bone [17]. In particular, the presence of a patent or deeply developed subcochlear canaliculus raises questions regarding structural continuity between the inferior retrotympanum and the petrous apex [13]. Moreover, the relationship between the morphology of the vascular compartment—namely the jugular bulb and carotid canal—and the configuration of the STS remains insufficiently understood [17]. A high-riding jugular bulb may protrude into the hypotympanum, subtympanic sinus, or retrocochlear recess, thereby altering their morphology and warranting careful preoperative evaluation [6, 17, 18]. Despite these observations, quantitative data on STS morphology, dimensions, and distribution in adults remain scarce, particularly in relation to surrounding pneumatization patterns.

To date, no study has systematically evaluated the morphology of the subtympanic sinus in a large cohort of healthy adults using a standardized and reproducible radiological protocol.

The aim of the present study is therefore to provide a detailed radioanatomical assessment of the subtympanic sinus in adults, focusing on its morphology, depth, and relationships with adjacent anatomical structures. This study also aims to establish a reproducible framework for STS assessment that may facilitate comparisons across future studies and support preoperative clinical, particularly in the context of endoscopic ear surgery and cochlear implantation in well-pneumatized temporal bones.

Materials and methods

This was a retrospective, multicenter observational study based on an anonymized set of Computed Tomography (HRCT and CBCT) scans of the temporal bone. All examinations had been acquired for routine clinical indications and were subsequently retrieved from institutional radiological databases. In accordance with national and institutional regulations, the retrospective analysis of fully anonymized imaging data did not require approval by an ethics committee or informed consent. The Ethics Committee of Medical University of Warsaw accepted the use of CT scans for morphometric studies of the temporal bone with no additional conditions (decision number AKBE/185/2019). The Ethics Committee of University of Bologna granted an IRB exemption due to the retrospective nature of the study and the analysis of anonymized CT scans.

The study material consisted of CT scans obtained from 82 adult individuals, yielding a total of 164 temporal bones. The mean age of the patients was 46 years (± SD 16), with the youngest being 17 and the oldest 83. 62.2% of patients (51/82) were female, compared to 37.8% of males (31/82). Inclusion criteria comprised age ≥ 17 years and the absence of radiological evidence of middle or inner ear pathology, previous otologic surgery, or destructive temporal bone disease. Only scans showing normally aerated mastoid compartments were included in the analysis (Tan classification grade 3 or 4).

The HRCT examinations were performed using a 64-slice multidetector CT scanner with a standardized temporal bone protocol. Acquisition parameters included a tube voltage of 120 kV, tube current of 225 mA, collimation and slice thickness of 0.6 mm, and image reconstruction using a high-resolution bone algorithm. The CBCT examinations were performed using Planmeca Promax 3D Mid CBCT scanner (Planmeca USA, INC, Roselle, Illinois, USA), source voltage 90 kV, current 12 mA, voxel dimensions 400 × 400 × 400 μm. Multiplanar reconstructions were generated for all of the used sets of images in the axial, coronal, and sagittal planes. Half of the scans were obtained with the HRCT device, the other half – with CBCT scanner.

Image analysis was carried out using dedicated DICOM-viewing software (RadiAnt DICOM Viewer 2022.2 (64-bit; Medixant, Poznan, Poland)). A strict and reproducible analysis protocol was applied as proposed in the literature [17]. The window level was set to 500 Hounsfield Units (HU), and the window width was 3500 HU. The axial plane parallel to the lateral semicircular canal served as the reference plane for all measurements. The facial nerve was first identified in its tympanic segment and subsequently followed along its course to the mastoid segment to allow accurate spatial orientation.

At the level of the round window niche, the subtympanic sinus (STS) was identified and classified according to its extension in relation to the mastoid segment of the facial nerve, as follows: type A, The excavation of the sinus corresponds to the medial border of the third portion of the FN in the mastoid; type B, the STS extends to the medial limit of the FN but without posterior extension; and type C, medial and posterior expansion of the sinus from the FN [2]. To avoid misinterpretation of adjacent retrotympanic structures, multiple consecutive sections above and below the reference level were evaluated as proposed in the literature [17]. CT images were evaluated jointly by two researchers (CC, TW) using a standardized analysis protocol. Measurements and classifications were performed in consensus rather than independently. In cases of uncertainty, consensus was achieved through discussion, with additional consultation from a co-author of the applied classification system (MF) or an experienced radiologist and head of the department (KSz), particularly for the assessment of pneumatization. This approach was adopted to ensure consistency and reliability of the measurements across all cases.

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (version 29.0.2; IBM Corp.). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as percentages. Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Depending on distribution, comparisons of continuous variables were performed using the unpaired Student’s t-test, Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis or while categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test with Yates’ correction where appropriate. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Maximum depth and width of the STS were measured following protocol proposed in the literature [17]. The details of the measurements are presented in Fig. 1.

In addition, the presence of pneumatization adjacent to the facial nerve canal was recorded, as well as a radiologically identifiable connection between the STS and the subcochlear canaliculus. The pneumatization adjacent to the facial nerve was defined and assessed between mastoid portion, posterior semicircular canal and posterior wall of the petrous part of the temporal bone (the so called retrofacial tract) [1, 4]. Pneumatization within the vascular compartment of the petrous bone, defined as the presence of air cells between the internal carotid artery and the jugular bulb, was also assessed (Fig. 2.).

Additional analyses included comparison of STS measurements between imaging modalities (CBCT vs. HRCT) using the Mann–Whitney U test and assessment of the correlation between age and STS depth using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient.

Results

A total of 82 CT scans, corresponding to 164 temporal bones, were analyzed. Regarding the STS configuration, 132 temporal bones exhibited a type A STS (80.5%), 27 bones showed a type B STS (16.5%), and 5 bones presented a type C STS (3.0%).

The mean width of the STS entrance was 2.4 mm (± SD 0.6), and the mean sinus depth was 2.0 mm (± SD 1.1). A radiologically identifiable connection between the STS and the subcochlear canaliculus was observed in 103 cases (62.8%). When analyzed by STS subtype, the STS depth measured 1.6 mm (± SD 0.7) in type A, 3.1 mm (± SD 1.0) in type B, and 4.7 mm (± SD 0.9) in type C sinuses (Table 1). Corresponding mean STS width was 2.3 mm (± SD 0.6), 2.5 mm (± SD 0.5), and 2.0 mm (± SD 0.6) for types A, B, and C, respectively. A statistically significant difference in depth was observed among STS types (Kruskal-Wallis test, p < 0.001), whereas no significant association was found between STS width and STS type (Kruskal-Wallis test). No significant differences were observed between sexes (chi-squared test).

A bilateral analysis was performed to assess the distribution of symmetrical versus asymmetrical STS configurations. Symmetry was observed in 65 out of 82 patients (79.3%), while asymmetry was present in 17 patients (20.7%). This distribution differed significantly from an equal distribution (chi-square test, p < 0.001), indicating that symmetrical STS configuration predominates.

No statistically significant association was found between STS type and the presence of a subcochlear canaliculus connection (chi-squared test, p = 0.055), despite a higher prevalence of this connection in deeper STS types (81.5% in type B and 80.0% in type C) compared with type A sinuses (58.3%). However, when STS depth was analyzed as a continuous variable, a significant association emerged (Mann-Whitney U test, p = 0.017). Specifically, the mean depth was 2.1 mm (± SD 1.1) in cases with a connection, compared with 1.7 mm (± SD 0.9) in cases without a connection.

No significant association was observed between STS depth and the presence of air cells in the vicinity of the facial nerve (Mann-Whitney U test, p = 0.063). Facial nerve–adjacent pneumatization was present in 85.4% of temporal bones, whereas air cells between the internal carotid artery and the jugular bulb were identified in 125 bones (76.5%).

Facial nerve-adjacent pneumatization was significantly associated with greater STS depth (Mann-Whitney U test, p = 0.004), with a mean depth of 2.0 mm (± SD 1.1) in pneumatized cases compared with 1.6 mm (± SD 0.6) in non-pneumatized specimens. No significant association was observed between facial nerve-adjacent pneumatization and STS type or width (chi-squared test, p > 0.05). A statistically significant sex-related difference was found, with facial nerve-adjacent pneumatization present in 90.2% of female specimens compared with 77.4% of male specimens (chi-squared test, p = 0.025).

In contrast, pneumatization within the vascular compartment was not associated with STS type, width, or depth ( p > 0.05). However, it showed significant associations with both facial nerve-adjacent pneumatization (chi-squared test, p = 0.026) and the presence of a subcochlear canaliculus connection (chi-squared test, p < 0.001).

Mean STS depth was greater in the CBCT subgroup than in the HRCT subgroup (2.2 ± 1.1 mm vs. 1.8 ± 0.9 mm, p = 0.026), whereas STS width did not differ significantly between modalities (2.4 ± 0.6 mm vs. 2.3 ± 0.5 mm, p = 0.147) (Table 2.). No significant correlation was found between age and STS depth (Spearman’s ρ = −0.068, p = 0.390).

Discussion

The subtympanic sinus (STS) was initially named and described by Marchioni et al. [13] as a well-defined recess located inferior to the sinus tympani, at the junction of the inferior retrotympanum and the hypotympanum. A formal classification based on the spatial relationship of the STS to the facial nerve was introduced in 2018 by Anschuetz et al. [2]. The feasibility of exploring the STS using this classification was also assessed by the same authors as well as by Bonali et al. [2, 6, 7, 12], with comparable results. However, the available literature remains limited in defining normal morphometric parameters—particularly depth and width—and their relationship with surrounding pneumatization patterns.

In this context, the present study provides, to our knowledge, the first comprehensive radioanatomical assessment of the subtympanic sinus in a relatively large cohort of healthy adult temporal bones. In contrast to previous studies based on smaller samples or ex vivo micro-CT imaging, our analysis is based on clinically acquired CT data and follows a standardized and reproducible measurement protocol, allowing for more direct applicability in everyday surgical practice. The study by Anschuetz et al. [2] primarily focused on the qualitative distribution of STS types in relation to the facial nerve, without providing absolute morphometric measurements such as depth or width, which limits direct quantitative comparison with our results. Similarly, the micro-CT–based study by Geneci et al. [8] reported a mean STS depth of 2.376 mm (range 1.428–6.429 mm), which is comparable to the values observed in our cohort. However, their analysis did not demonstrate a clear correlation between STS type and depth, despite using a classification system based on the relationship to the facial nerve.

The present study demonstrates that the subtympanic sinus is a variable anatomical structure whose morphology in adults is primarily determined by depth rather than width. The predominance of the type A configuration confirms that a shallow STS represents the most common anatomical arrangement, whereas deeper sinuses extending toward or beyond the facial nerve are less frequent. The progressive increase in depth from type A to type C sinuses supports the anatomical validity of classification systems based on spatial extension. Depth appears to be the principal morphometric parameter distinguishing STS configurations, suggesting that posterior and inferior excavation plays a greater role in sinus development than transverse expansion.

Our findings are consistent with previously reported data in both children and adults. Wojciechowski et al. reported that in children, type A sinuses are most prevalent (59%), followed by type B (30%) and type C (10%) [17]. Similarly, Anschuetz et al. demonstrated that in adults, type A is the most common configuration (72%), followed by type B (21%) and type C (7%) [2]. Comparable distributions have also been described in adults with chronic otitis media (type A: 69%, type B: 24%, type C: 7%) [12]. Study by Hool et al. [12] was also based on a heterogeneous dataset of CT scans with variable slice thickness (0.3–1 mm), which may influence the accuracy of morphometric assessment but does not put in doubt the objective value of the report – it is even more priceless to have contribute to literature on such rarely chosen topic. Additionally, no absolute measurements of STS depth or width were reported, further limiting direct comparison with our results.

The predominance of bilateral symmetry, confirmed by statistical analysis, suggests that STS morphology may be influenced by shared developmental factors. However, the presence of asymmetry in a subset of patients indicates that local anatomical or environmental factors may also contribute to its variability.

Interestingly, this pattern contrasts with observations in the superior retrotympanum, specifically regarding the sinus tympani and facial sinus, where shallow configurations are more frequently found in diseased temporal bones than in well-pneumatized, healthy subjects [15, 16]. Our findings suggest however that the relatively low prevalence of deep STSs may reflect intrinsic characteristics of hypotympanic pneumatization rather than being associated with disease or impaired aeration of the temporal bone.

We also identified statistically significant differences in STS depth among all three types, further supporting the classification proposed by Anschuetz et al. [2]. In contrast, Geneci et al. [8] reported significant depth differences only between type B and type C sinuses, with comparable measurements between types A and B, possibly due to a smaller sample size. Moreover, methodological differences, including the use of micro-CT imaging and less standardized measurement approaches, may further contribute to discrepancies between the studies. No significant differences in STS width were observed between types, indicating that entrance width alone is unlikely to constitute a limiting anatomical factor unless modified by additional variants, such as a high-riding jugular bulb encroaching on the hypotympanum or retrotympanic recesses [2]. These observations are consistent with findings previously reported in pediatric populations [17, 18].

Facial nerve–adjacent pneumatization was frequently observed and showed a significant association with increased STS depth, although no correlation with categorical STS type was identified. This suggests that facial nerve pneumatization reflects a broader tendency toward enhanced temporal bone pneumatization rather than determining a specific STS configuration. The higher prevalence of facial nerve–adjacent pneumatization observed in females is consistent with previously reported sex-related differences in temporal bone morphology [2, 4, 7, 14].

Analysis of the subcochlear canaliculus further supports the concept of an anatomical continuum linking the inferior retrotympanum with the infracochlear pneumatization compartment. Deeper STSs were more frequently associated with continuity of the subcochlear canaliculus, reinforcing the view that these spaces are developmentally and morphologically related. From an embryological perspective, this variability may reflect differences in the timing and extent of ossification and fusion of bony elements derived from the otic capsule and surrounding mesenchyme. Incomplete fusion of the subiculum, finiculus, and fustis may result in persistent recesses or channels, manifesting as a deep STS or a patent subcochlear canaliculus [13].

The observed correlation between STS depth, subcochlear canaliculus continuity, and pneumatization of the vascular compartment further suggests coordinated development of pneumatized regions within the temporal bone. Although Han et al. [11] did not find a significant correlation between pneumatization around the ascending carotid artery and total temporal bone pneumatization volume, they did report an association between hyperpneumatized petrous apices and overall temporal bone pneumatization. Other studies have yielded divergent results with use of air cell volume rather than qualitative analyses [9, 10]. Analyses focusing on correlations between specific pneumatization subgroups remain limited [1]. In the present study, the presence of air cells within the vascular compartment was significantly associated with both facial nerve pneumatization and subcochlear canaliculus continuity. It should be noted, however, that pneumatization was assessed qualitatively rather than quantitatively, with even minimal air cell presence classified as pneumatization. This approach may have led to an overestimation of correlation strength and underscores the need for future studies employing volumetric or graded assessment methods. Although the association between STS type and subcochlear canaliculus entrance presence did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.055), a clear trend toward higher prevalence in types B and C was observed. Given the low number of type C sinuses in our cohort, this finding may reflect insufficient statistical power and the possibility of a type II error rather than a true lack of association. Therefore, this relationship should be interpreted with caution and warrants further investigation in larger cohorts.

Pneumatization of the vascular compartment may also be conceptually related to recent findings describing the presence of a so-called retrocochlear recess in children [18]. However, confirmation of this relationship requires further investigation in adult populations. Further studies are also needed to explore potential age-related changes in temporal bone pneumatization in adulthood, as this aspect remains insufficiently characterized and may provide additional clinically relevant insights; notably, the currently applied Schillinger classification assumes stability of pneumatization patterns in adult life as proposed in our previous works [16, 17].

Our results may increase awareness of the possible configurations of recesses within the tympanic cavity and, consequently, improve the safety of otologic procedures. This is particularly relevant in adult patients with well-pneumatized temporal bones, in whom detailed knowledge of the inferior retrotympanum may influence surgical strategy, including endoscopic transcanal approaches and cochlear implantation. Thorough preoperative assessment of temporal bone CT scans enhances procedural safety and increases the likelihood of complete and successful disease removal, thereby reducing the risk of residual pathology, particularly in the management of cholesteatoma [6, 7, 13].

We believe that our morphometric data may be useful in the development of new surgical instruments dedicated to dissection of the inferior retrotympanum, analogous to the specifically angled or double-angled instruments currently used for the sinus tympani and facial sinus.

In addition, the subtympanic sinus may serve as a potential site for electrode placement for intraoperative hearing monitoring due to its close proximity to the promontory and the round window niche [5]. During cochlear implantation, particular caution is required when introducing the electrode array through the round window to avoid inadvertent displacement or migration into the subtympanic sinus or even into the subcochlear canaliculus.

Overall, our findings support the view that the subtympanic sinus is not an isolated anatomical recess but rather an integral component of a broader, interconnected pneumatized system within the temporal bone. The present study may therefore be considered a step toward systematizing current knowledge of STS morphology and establishing a foundation for future comparative and clinically oriented investigations. STS morphology appears to be shaped by coordinated developmental and morphogenetic processes influencing the retrotympanum, infracochlear region, facial canal, and vascular compartment.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The low prevalence of type C STS limits detailed statistical analysis of this subgroup. The retrospective design, as well as the use of mixed imaging modalities (HRCT and CBCT) with differing spatial resolutions, may introduce variability and potentially affect measurement consistency. A statistically significant difference in STS depth was observed between the CBCT and HRCT subgroups, which may be related to differences in spatial resolution between the two imaging modalities. In contrast, STS width did not differ significantly between modalities. This finding should be considered when interpreting morphometric comparisons based on mixed imaging datasets.

Additionally, reliance on CT imaging may lead to underestimation of very thin bony structures due to inherent resolution constraints. Presumable interobserver variability, although minimized through a standardized analysis protocol, cannot be entirely excluded. Finally, the clinical utility of our findings requires further validation in studies incorporating endoscopic or microscopic visualization, particularly in patients with chronic otitis media. Also, the inclusion of only temporal bones with Tan grades 3 and 4 pneumatization may limit the generalizability of our findings, particularly to patients with reduced pneumatization, such as those with chronic otitis media or cholesteatoma [2].

Conclusions

The subtympanic sinus exhibits substantial morphological variability in adults, predominantly expressed through differences in depth rather than width. Its configuration is closely related to facial nerve position, subcochlear canaliculus continuity, and certain air cells tracts of the temporal bone pneumatization. These findings highlight the importance of considering the STS as part of an integrated retrotympanic and infracochlear anatomical system and provide an anatomical framework for future morphologic and developmental studies.
