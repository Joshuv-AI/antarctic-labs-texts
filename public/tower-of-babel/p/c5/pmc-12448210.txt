Editorial: Accountability, voice, and trust - responsible use of GenAI in scientific publishing
Authors: Judith E Mank
Published: 2025

Over the last few years, the development and rapid advancement of Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) and Large Language Models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT and Gemini have upended many industries, and academic publishing is no exception. At Evolution Letters, we have been grappling with changes to data analysis and scientific writing from these new powerful tools, and trying to balance reasonable use and professional ethics. It has been interesting!

As of yet, LLMs are not sophisticated enough to generate an entire paper that would survive the rigorous assessment and peer review process at Evolution Letters. That has not prevented some contributors—I hesitate to call them authors since they have done little more than writing a single chatbot prompt—from submitting papers composed entirely by LLMs. These are, at the moment, easy to detect as what is often referred to as AI Slop, low-quality output requiring little effort on the part of the contributor and serving little purpose.

Although they are not yet capable of writing high-quality scientific papers, GenAI and LLMs are for many a very useful tool that enhances their scientific work. I have asked people in my lab and beyond how they use these tools, and their answers have revealed just how quickly they have become embedded in the research and writing process. For those writing code for data analysis, ChatGPT has replaced simple web searches as the best route to find scripts for specific tasks. When writing, many people I spoke with use LLMs to find a specific turn of phrase, shave words off applications with strict limits, or generate short texts on specific topics that they then edit. For others, LLMs are remarkably effective at helping reduce the cognitive burden of writing in a language with which they are less comfortable. This latter use is perhaps the most promising application of LLMs as long as English remains the primary medium of scientific discussion.

The expansion of LLMs into our enterprise has generated a wide array of opinions. Some have argued that their use should be entirely banned. There are indeed worrying aspects of LLMs that should be thought through carefully, including the way that some have been trained on texts that were scraped without permission, the potential for embedded bias, and substantial energy requirements. However, banning the use of LLMs and GenAI is, at least in my opinion, neither reasonable nor enforceable. Many of us find these tools effective, and a natural progression of previous methods. For example, LLMs represent an advance on web searches for appropriate code for data analysis, or on autocorrect functions in text editors for writing. In both cases, the human doing the work, not the algorithm, is ultimately responsible for verifying that the suggestions are appropriate and correct. More practically, most uses of LLMs cannot be detected, and therefore banning their use simply cannot be policed. Like it or not, LLMs and GenAI are part of our world.

At the same time, there has been little discussion, at least within the scientific communities I engage with, about where the boundaries of LLM use should be. Over the past few months, a few discussions have occurred with authors and reviewers at Evolution Letters that have helped clarify acceptable use of LLMs in scientific publishing. There are three things that we must preserve as LLMs and GenAI tools pervade our professional endeavors.

Accountability. Ultimately, the authors of a paper are accountable for the data, analysis, and manuscript itself. If the authors use ChatGPT, Gemini, or another LLM to help write code for data analysis, they must ensure that this code is correct and appropriate, just as they would for code they found through a traditional internet search engine or public repository, such as GitHub. LLMs can speed up the process of writing scripts for data analysis, particularly for those who have a less developed code bank at their disposal, but it is not a substitute for human expertise. ChatGPT or any other LLM cannot be an author of a manuscript, and they make mistakes, sometimes spectacular ones. The human writing the prompts and doing the analysis is ultimately responsible for checking that anything they implement from LLMs is appropriate and correct.

It will be increasingly tempting to rely on LLM and GenAI tools as they improve to make fewer mistakes and respond to more complex prompts, making accountability all the more important. Accountability includes transparency about the use of AI tools, which can be done in the acknowledgments if related to the writing process, or the methods if related to data analysis. Some may feel there is a stigma in acknowledging the use of LLMs, and so let me help alleviate that by acknowledging that I used ChatGPT to generate text, which I then edited and integrated into the first paragraph of the section on Voice, below. I will let you decide whether I was able to successfully maintain my own voice in that segment.

It could be argued that there is some ambiguity about what level of LLM use should be acknowledged. For those using these tools to polish text that they have written or to search out specific lines of code, perhaps acknowledgment is less necessary than those who rely on LLMs to a greater extent. However, because the line is exceedingly fuzzy and likely to vary from person to person, we indicate in our instructions to authors that any use be acknowledged, which has the benefit of reducing future stigma.

We at Evolution Letters are working hard to keep pace with new developments in this area, as are many other journals. Our instructions to authors and reviewers offer advice and guidelines about acceptable LLM use, and if our contributors are still unsure how to acknowledge their use of LLMs, they are welcome to ask us directly. Several have suggested that we might need some sort of checklist for authors and reviewers at submission about GenAI use. I am hesitant to implement this for several reasons. First, it is difficult in a checklist to anticipate all possible uses, and so the list is likely to be vague and less than helpful. Second, we already offer instructions about acceptable use, and it feels redundant to add another step about this specifically when there are so many other important aspects of publishing. Finally, I personally find these bothersome as an author or reviewer, and prefer to limit barriers to submission. However, it may be that members of our community are more willing to check a box than they are to note use in the acknowledgments, and so it is worth further consideration.

Voice. For authors and reviewers, the use of LLMs can be helpful in writing, however, we cannot allow them to override our own voice in manuscripts and reviews. Ultimately, LLMs are tools, not co-authors. Their utility comes from enhancing our writing process, but they are not a replacement for human judgment. Maintaining our individual voice in writing is necessary for preserving clarity, priorities, and purpose. This is particularly important in academic writing, where credibility and originality matter.

Many people, myself included, have been pleasantly surprised by how effective LLMs are at finding a particular turn of phrase or revising short sections of text for clarity and flow. Several people have told me that LLMs are remarkable tools that make writing in English more accessible and comfortable. However, in all these cases, writers must engage critically with LLMs and revise outputs to align with their own voice to maintain coherence, meaning, and authenticity.

I enjoy the process of scientific writing, and use it to order and clarify my thoughts. However, I recognize that it is a skill that requires extensive time and practice to develop—in my case, numerous revisions with my very patient PhD supervisor. For many, LLMs present a tempting shortcut, and I genuinely do not know where we are headed with regard to LLMs and writing. At the moment, there are characteristics of LLM output that mark it clearly as such, for example, the awkward turns of phrase, vague text, and frequent em-dashes of ChatGPT text. However, LLMs and GenAI are improving rapidly and newer models minimize these quirks. It is not beyond the realm of possibility that we will soon be able to train LLMs to write compelling text with unique voices, or even in the style of our favorite writers. However, as we are ultimately accountable for our writing, it will remain necessary to engage with any LLM output to ensure the intent and priorities remain true. It may even be that the ability to write original text will become more important as everyone employs the same AI tools.

Trust. This last category comes largely from recent instances of reviewers relying on LLMs, leading to two major issues. First is confidentiality. Sharing a manuscript with an LLM for the purposes of generating a review breaks confidentiality. This is particularly true for manuscripts that have not been deposited on preprint servers prior to peer review (and I am not taking a side here about whether that should be required or not), but is also the case when a reviewer uses ChatGPT to revise their entire review. We have absolutely no control over how LLMs use the information we enter, and sharing information about a manuscript’s content is a violation of the confidentiality assumed by authors when they submit a paper.

Additionally, peer review is the foundation of all scientific publishing, and at Evolution Letters, we work particularly hard at ensuring that we have a robust and vigorous peer-review process that authors can trust. That trust is shaken when there is clear evidence of LLMs in reviews. When the reviewer submits unrevised LLM output, with the clear hallmarks of such, authors are rightly concerned. They may assume that the reviewer simply fed their paper into an LLM and asked it to generate the review directly, even if this is not the case, and will likely not trust the suggestions as valid, or even salient.

Some have argued recently that we should use LLMs for reviewing manuscripts en masse, almost like triage, and I would caution against this. Although this could reduce the burden of finding suitable reviewers and speed up the process of review, it comes with a serious cost in terms of trust. That is not to say that peer review by humans is infallible—far from it! However, we seek multiple reviews in hopes of generating a balanced opinion of a manuscript. As LLMs become more embedded in scientific writing, using them to mass review manuscripts presents an unappealing vision of a world in which humans are reduced to prompting an LLM for a paper, which another human feeds into an LLM for review. More importantly, as many GenAI training algorithms exhaust input written by humans and shift to simulated training materials, the risk of errors becoming deeply embedded in these networks grows. To maintain trust and accountability, the human element must somehow remain to check for accuracy and preserve integrity.

The three points above may be obvious to some, but I have realized through interactions with reviewers and authors that many have not thought through these issues sufficiently as they incorporate GenAI tools into their daily work. These are the principles we will use at Evolution Letters as we chart a path forward, and I hope that reviewers and authors will think carefully about how to balance the benefits of these new tools with the need for accountability, personal voice, and trust.
