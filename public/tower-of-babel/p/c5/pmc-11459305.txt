Leveraging investments, promoting transparency and mobilising communities: a qualitative analysis of news articles about how the Ebola outbreak informed COVID-19 response in five African countries
Authors: Lauren P Courtney, Manon Billaud, Alex Paulenich, Rob Chew, Zainab Alidina, Meredith Pinto
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe WHO declared the novel COVID-19 outbreak a pandemic in March 2020. While the COVID-19 pandemic was unprecedented, prior experiences with diseases such as Middle East respiratory syndrome, severe acute respiratory syndrome and Ebola shaped many countries’ preparedness and response strategies. Although lessons learnt from outbreak responses have been documented from a variety of sources, news media play a special role through their dissemination of news to the general public. This study investigated news media to explore how lessons learnt from the West African Ebola outbreak in 2014–2016 informed the COVID-19 responses in several African countries.MethodsWe conducted qualitative analysis on a dataset of previously compiled COVID-19-related news articles published from 1 March 2020 to 31 August 2020. This dataset included 34,225 articles from 6 countries. We filtered the dataset to only include articles with the keyword ‘Ebola’. We used a machine-learning text classification model to identify relevant articles with clear and specific lessons learnt. We conducted inductive and deductive coding to categorise lessons learnt and identify emergent themes.ResultsOf the 861 articles containing the word ‘Ebola’, 18.4% (N=158) with lessons learnt from Ebola were included across five of the countries: Ethiopia, Ghana, Kenya, Liberia and Sierra Leone. News articles highlighted three emergent themes: the importance of leveraging existing resources and past response system investments, promoting transparency in public health messaging and engaging community leaders in all phases of the response.ConclusionsFindings suggest fostering trust prior to and throughout an outbreak facilitates timely implementation and compliance of mitigation strategies. Trust can be built by leveraging existing resources, being communicative and transparent about their funding allocation and decision-making and engaging communities.Keywords: COVID-19, Liberia, Kenya, Health systems, Qualitative study

Introduction

The WHO declared COVID-19 a pandemic in March 2020.1 Although the COVID-19 pandemic was unprecedented, prior experiences with disease outbreaks, like Middle East respiratory syndrome (MERS), severe acute respiratory syndrome (SARS) and Ebola, shaped many countries’ preparedness and response strategies.2,5 Research suggests that these countries were better prepared to manage public health emergencies more effectively.4 5 Their investments in national and community health systems and their public health workforce strengthened their response coordination and supported innovation and infrastructure development.4 5

In Southeast Asia, lessons learnt from MERS and SARS informed numerous countries’ COVID-19 response. The SARS outbreak in 2003 led the Hong Kong government to adopt a command structure to respond to future outbreaks, establish stronger point-of-entry policies and invest in research.4 Consequently, Hong Kong was able to implement mitigation measures quickly and had a very low number of cases and fatalities per capita during the first year of the COVID-19 pandemic.6 Taiwan created an ad hoc Central Epidemic Command Center for coordination and logistics at the national level, which was quickly activated in January 2020 to respond to the COVID-19 outbreak.5 It immediately implemented a series of mitigation measures, resulting in a few positive cases.4 In the Republic of Korea, the MERS outbreak in 2015 led to a change in the legislature for rapid response, enhancement of the epidemic investigation workforce, increased laboratory capacity and the establishment of an emergency operation ccentre (EOC) in the Korea Centers for Disease Control and Prevention.7 These systems allowed the country to quickly respond to the COVID-19 pandemic and successfully reduced cases between February and March 2020.7

Similarly, in Africa, countries’ responses to previous Ebola outbreaks offered valuable lessons that guided the COVID-19 response. Directly following the 2014–2016 Ebola outbreak, Guinea created the National Agency for Health Security and prioritised investment in health personnel availability and capacity leading to a successful COVID-19 response.8 Likewise, the Nigerian government developed emergency preparedness and biosecurity policies, strengthened laboratory capacity, implemented a robust surveillance system and trained key staff, which allowed the country to respond promptly to the COVID-19 pandemic.9 In Liberia, the Ministry of Health launched the National Community Health Assistant Programme that recruited, trained and deployed more than 3500 community health workers during and after the Ebola epidemic. These workers were then rapidly trained and deployed during COVID-19.4 In Sierra Leone, community engagement and social mobilisation strategies assisted in case detection and effective reduction of Ebola transmission.10 The government used a similar response structure to establish a COVID-19 Emergency Response Centre at national and district levels.11 Liberia, Sierra Leone and Guinea also made investments during and after the Ebola epidemic to strengthen their Integrated Diseases Surveillance and Response Systems and laboratory capacities, which enabled them to quickly mobilise for COVID-19.12

News media has been used by public health researchers as an alternate data source for disease monitoring and surveillance.13,15 News media plays a central role in raising public awareness and communicating risk, shaping public opinion and understanding of health issues and influencing policy formulation during national security and health emergencies.16,20 Internet-based sources and online news media are a primary source of health information connecting the public to health experts and policy-makers, influencing public trust in science and attitudes towards specific policies or politics, cultivating social norms and connectedness and ensuring accountability for civic engagement and government decisions.16,18 For these reasons, and because news media played a major role in disseminating information during COVID-19,21 22 we chose to analyse news media. Compared with other data sources and methodologies already explored in current literature, we believed news media would provide us with a new and unique perspective from the public, health experts and decision-makers on how previous outbreaks informed the COVID-19 response.

We used news articles to explore how the Ebola outbreak informed countries’ responses to the COVID-19 pandemic. Specifically, our research question asked: what were the lessons learnt from the 2014–2016 West Africa Ebola outbreak and how were they leveraged during the COVID-19 response?

Methods

Dataset

We conducted qualitative analysis on a dataset of previously compiled COVID-19-related news articles published from 1 March 2020 to 31 August 2020. This dataset included 34 225 articles from 6 countries (Ethiopia, Georgia, Ghana, Kenya, Liberia and Sierra Leone). These countries were included in the dataset because they met three criteria, as follows: country receives support from the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s Division of Global Health Protection, English is a predominant language and World Press Freedom Index score of 65.0 or above.23 The time period was selected to focus on the early response efforts and initial reactions to the COVID-19 pandemic. Articles contained the keywords ‘COVID’, ‘COVID-19’, ‘SARS-COV-2’ and ‘novel coronavirus’ and their full text was available online, regardless of the original medium (ie, print, electronic, radio or television). Articles were compiled from the Common Crawl archives,24 an open-source database of historical web pages across local, regional, and international news outlets, extracted using the Python ‘news-please’ library25 and translated into English using Google Translate, when necessary.

For our analysis, we filtered the original dataset of articles to include only those that contained the keyword ‘Ebola’. This resulted in 861 articles across 5 countries—Georgia did not have relevant articles. We used a machine-learning text classification model to screen out articles not applicable to the study. We trained the model by having two reviewers manually code a sample of 49 (~5%) articles as ‘relevant’ or ‘not relevant’ based on the predetermined inclusion/exclusion criteria and provide justifications for their decision. Using the sample of coded articles, the model assigned the remaining articles as ‘relevant’ or ‘not relevant’. The model attempted to learn which articles were relevant by determining what vocabulary was unique or relatively more common in the articles that met the inclusion/exclusion criteria discussed below and those that did not. Due to the limited number of labelled examples, we used the SetFit algorithm26 when training the text classification model to accentuate the differences between articles labelled as ‘relevant’ and ‘not relevant’. Prior work suggests that SetFit can achieve comparable results to other methods for limited labelled data that require orders of magnitude more parameters. We used the gradient boosted tree algorithm27 on the SetFit text embeddings to achieve a 71% accuracy (sensitivity of 0.73 and specificity of 0.67) in identifying Ebola articles relevant to the study aims. This resulted in 442 articles.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

For this analysis, we included articles with clear and specific lessons learnt directly attributable to the Ebola response efforts and discussed within the context of the COVID-19 pandemic. We excluded articles if (1) it was a duplicate article; (2) there were no lessons learnt described or the lessons learnt were too general to code; (3) the lesson learnt was not directly attributable to Ebola response efforts or multiple disease outbreak responses were discussed without specifying which lessons learnt were from Ebola; (4) the lesson learnt was not described in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic; (5) the lesson learnt occurred in a different country from the article’s country of focus or (6) the lesson learnt was about two or more countries.

Qualitative analysis

We conducted qualitative analysis to examine how the Ebola outbreak response informed the COVID-19 response.28 During the manual review of the sample of articles, referenced above, we generated an initial set of codes and draft codebook. Two independent reviewers coded the same 22 (~5%) articles to validate the codebook; this included confirming a common understanding of code definitions and building consensus on code application to ensure coding consistency between reviewers moving forward. Through this process, we identified new codes and updated the codebook. Each reviewer independently evaluated the remaining articles. Reviewers determined whether the article met the inclusion/exclusion criteria and, if so, coded. A second reviewer then independently assessed the first reviewer’s inclusion/exclusion decision and coding. When there were disagreements, the two reviewers met to discuss and come to a consensus. If needed, a third reviewer was brought in to provide input. The codebook was iteratively refined and updated throughout this review and coding process. We conducted informal inter-rater reliability tests throughout the process to confirm agreement and consistency among reviewers. Once coding was completed, all reviewers met to discuss the findings and identify emergent themes.

Software

We imported articles into MAXQDA to conduct the qualitative content analysis.29 We used the Python ‘news-please’ library to extract articles.25

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design and conduct of this review.

Leveraging existing resources and past investments

News articles in all five countries discussed how governments, health facilities, businesses and other institutions leveraged resources developed and investments made during Ebola for the COVID-19 response.

Specifically, news articles from three of the five countries (Ghana, Liberia and Sierra Leone) referenced how policies, procedures and response systems developed during Ebola were used and reinstituted at the start of the COVID-19 pandemic, primarily by governments, businesses and hospitals. For example, government policies and response systems, including mandatory quarantine, airport screening, and testing at points-of-entry were reimplemented with the goal of curbing transmission early and minimising cases. In Liberia, for example, ‘when news of a new coronavirus reached [the country], the government responded swiftly. Systems set up to contain infectious diseases like Ebola were harnessed to confront the coronavirus. This includes rapid response teams of contact tracers, border controls, and other measures that helped Liberia overcome Ebola’ (Record ID 268, Liberia). Articles also described how businesses reinstated hand washing, sanitization, and masking procedures as requirements for entry and demanded strict adherence. At the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, hospitals reinstituted policies and rapidly reprioritized the provision of personal protective equipment for their staff to prevent them from falling ill or dying, as had commonly occurred when treating Ebola patients. Articles discussed how adapting and adopting these already developed policies, procedures, and response systems meant that countries could easily initiate response and mitigation efforts early in the pandemic when case counts were still low.

In addition, articles from four of the five countries (Ghana, Kenya, Liberia and Sierra Leone) outlined how governments invited front-line healthworkers, experts and leaders with experience responding to the Ebola outbreak to support the management of the COVID-19 response nationally and locally by providing guidance and sharing their experiences. During the Ebola outbreak, public health workers, including contact tracers, community health workers and medical staff, took charge of response efforts. At the start of the COVID-19 pandemic, articles discussed how these Ebola-trained health workers were, again, called to action to assist in mitigating the spread of the disease. In Kenya and Ghana, this included health personnel who volunteered in Sierra Leone and Liberia during Ebola. One Kenyan article stated that the country ‘was banking on lessons from the Ebola outbreak in West Africa to deal with the current pandemic… ‘We have incorporated 42 healthcare officials who volunteered in the fight against Ebola in 2014/2015. They will help in the war against COVID-19,’ [the county health official] told the Nation… ‘They will apply what they learned to combat the coronavirus in Kenya’ (Record ID 21, Kenya). Articles also highlighted how leaders who oversaw the national-level response structures during Ebola were appointed to reactivate response structures and systems during COVID-19, expecting them to use their experience to manage the pandemic effectively. One example was from Sierra Leone where ‘the President had directed the use of the Ebola Response experience to strengthen the government’s Coronavirus enhanced preparedness, adding that the Ebola Response team was meeting the President to share their experience’ (Record ID 695, Sierra Leone).

Articles from four of the five countries (Ghana, Kenya, Liberia and Sierra Leone) underscored how investments in healthcare systems during and post Ebola resulted in stronger systems that were better equipped to respond to COVID-19. Prior to the Ebola outbreak, healthcare systems suffered from underfunding and unsustainable reliance on foreign aid. The Ebola outbreak exposed this fragility, overwhelming treatment centres and leading to increased death counts and healthcare workforce attrition. Ebola’s rapid spread and devastation stemmed from these vulnerabilities. In Liberia, ‘[t]he Ebola outbreak … between 2014 and 2016 uncovered the country’s healthcare challenges. Among the challenges, there was inadequate funding. While the country aims to promote universal and affordable healthcare, low investment and the unsustainable dependency on aid for many essential primary healthcare interventions are two of the paramount contributors to weak healthcare services in Liberia,’ (Record ID 839, Liberia) and Sierra Leone ‘lost as much as over four-thousand lives to the 2014 Ebola outbreak primarily because the healthcare system lacked capacity and treatment centers were overwhelmed’ (Record ID 650, Sierra Leone). During the Ebola crisis, systems and infrastructure were established, alongside international investments. As a result, after Ebola, there was a renewed emphasis on bolstering healthcare and community structures to improve access. These investments in strengthening the healthcare systems allowed countries to respond more quickly and effectively to COVID-19. Articles highlighted how much of the success of the COVID-19 response was attributed to countries’ established systems and trainings with an emphasis on using, adapting and leveraging existing resources, people, facilities and systems that were built during Ebola. For example, in Liberia ‘most of the successes scored in the fight against the virus can be credited to the lessons learned from the Ebola virus disease in 2014/2015… ‘Ebola helped Liberia prepared [sic] for COVID-19 in many ways…. It helps[sic] us to build the infrastructures. Prior to Ebola, we did not have any structure in place for surveillance [systems]. We did early warning and surveillance system and all of these were lessons we learned from Ebola’ (Record ID 736, Liberia).

Articles from four of the five countries (Ethiopia, Ghana, Liberia and Sierra Leone) discussed preparedness and response infrastructure developed during Ebola and their successful use during COVID-19. Most articles focused on how infrastructure, such as health centres, community treatment centres and laboratory networks, were leveraged. For example, Sierra Leone used a ‘Rapidly Deployable Isolation Treatment Facility (RDTIF) [consisting] of 36 beds, 4 ambulances and other equipment (a leftover from the Ebola epidemic). This is the set of equipment the military will quickly deploy along with their well-trained men and women should we have a sudden surge of infections in a region’ (Record ID 415, Sierra Leone). However, notably, not all infrastructure built during Ebola was maintained, which had an impact on COVID-19 response efforts. Lack of maintenance was noted, for example, in Sierra Leonean laboratories and Ghanian Emergency Operations Centers (EOC). In Ghana the EOC and other response facilities set up during the Ebola outbreak were dismantled after the outbreak; the situation was attributed to Ghana’s ‘poor maintenance culture and government’s failure to provide resources for the creation of special health facilities’ (Record ID 67, Ghana).

Finally, articles from four countries (Ethiopia, Ghana, Liberia and Sierra Leone) discussed how the countries relied on established international partnerships to access resources, research and funding. Examples of these partners included other countries USA, including Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, China and France), non-governmental organisations, and international bodies (United Nations, WHO, World Bank, African Union). These partnerships focused on mass mobilisation and donation of resources including equipment, medicine and personnel. There was also an emphasis on sharing scientific discoveries and supporting economic recovery. These articles highlighted the importance of international cooperation and coordination. Yet, articles also discussed how international support during COVID-19 was limited as countries were struggling with their own COVID-19 responses, leading to shortages of personal protective equipment and other supplies. For example, ‘for the Ebola outbreak in Sierra Leone, there was massive collaboration and coordination of financial and technical support from developed nations, international organizations and non-governmental organizations to collectively combat the coronavirus in West Africa. On the other hand, however, the COVID-19 pandemic has seen limited support from these bodies because they themselves [were] barely grappling to contain the deadly virus in their regions and right across the world’ (Record ID 405, Sierra Leone).

Promoting communication and transparency

News articles from three of the five countries (Ghana, Liberia and Sierra Leone) discussed communication and transparency, including how the government disseminated information to the public and whether there was transparency in decision-making processes.

Articles described the widespread damage caused by countries experiencing a lack of effective transparency and communication between the governments and their communities during the Ebola outbreak with examples of how lack of communication led the police to open fire on people disregarding the Ebola lockdown in Liberia. However, since the onset of COVID-19, these governments have prioritised establishing strong and open communication channels to ensure transparency in decision-making and communication of mitigation strategies. Commentary from a Sierra Leonean journalist states, ‘It is widely accepted that in a disease epidemic or pandemic, effective communication is key to controlling the spread of the disease. The Ebola epidemic taught us that this is true.… Ebola taught us that it is effective communication and social mobilization that will limit the spread of the disease, so I urge [the] government to invest time and resources heavily in this area’ (Record ID 256, Sierra Leone). In addition, the recency of the Ebola outbreak helped foster acceptance of response efforts and justification for communities on the necessity of taking immediate action. ‘[T]he Ebola outbreak, which killed almost 4000 people in Sierra Leone, is still fresh in the minds of people, so they are being extremely careful. Counter-measures readily accepted ‘these are measures that went into place basically overnight, and nobody really bats an eye at them because we remember it from Ebola—this is what happened not too long ago’ (Record ID 31, Sierra Leone).

News articles from three of the five countries (Ghana, Liberia and Sierra Leone) also discussed funding transparency. During the Ebola outbreak, articles discussed issues concerning corruption and the lack of transparent allocation of funds. In response, during COVID-19, articles showed that there was an emphasis on financial transparency and responsible use of allocated funds. For example, in Liberia, ‘the massive corruption that occurred during the Ebola epidemic in Liberia has put the General Auditing Commission on its heels to caution the Executive Committee on COVID-19 to be very cautious of how funds coming in for the fight against coronavirus are disbursed’ (Record ID 507, Liberia).

Mobilising local leaders and community engagement

Articles across all five countries discussed community engagement, including the importance of involving local communities in the decision-making and enforcement of mitigation measures, leading to better compliance and cooperation among community members. Articles emphasised the need to empower communities to take leadership and ownership of their local COVID-19 response efforts, including involving trusted members of the community, such as traditional and religious leaders, who could encourage adherence and cooperation with mitigation efforts. For example, Liberia’s former president, Madam Sirleaf, was quoted reflecting on Ebola, ‘What we did was to put our communities in charge of their lives and their livelihoods. In that, we're getting to community leaders and workers, keeping them informed [sic], training them as much as possible to understand the virus, and the preventive measures that need to be taken’ (Record ID 462, Liberia). She also ‘named the lack of public confidence as one of the greatest challenges of her government’s efforts in combating Ebola in Liberia’ (Record ID 462, Liberia).

At the same time, articles discussed how communities took the initiative to be more active during the COVID-19 pandemic because of their experience with Ebola. Communities were still fearful about how their governments handled the Ebola response, especially the mismanagement of quarantine and lockdown measures by police. This fear and a desire to avoid making the same mistakes made during Ebola prompted communities to take on more responsibility in responding to COVID-19. In Liberia, ‘Roland Weah, the commissioner of the West Point neighborhood, where police opened fire during the Ebola outbreak to enforce a quarantine, said authorities were working closely with communities to avoid a repeat. ‘We have put into place measures to ensure there is not a recurrence of that situation’ (Record ID 41, Liberia). Articles reported that during Ebola anyone with a fever was feared to have the virus, whether they did or not and sent to isolation centres, where they may have then contracted the virus. Some communities even burned down isolation centres meant to treat victims of the disease.

Community engagement was also found through community education campaigns. Prevention messages across communities were shared during the COVID-19 response, as they had been during the Ebola response. For example, articles highlighted songs shared in multiple countries; in Sierra Leone, a musician wrote a song about COVID-19 to encourage prevention in the community based on personal Ebola experiences. Other articles noted the need to mimic the strong educational community awareness campaigns from Ebola, including launching radio public information campaigns. Early in the pandemic, news articles shared suggestions for prevention messages to be shared through various leaders; traditional leaders and politicians were encouraged to play a lead role. Articles highlighted communications within Muslim and Christian religious communities as being successful in sharing key messages.

Discussion

This is the first study to use news articles published during the first 6 months of the COVID-19 pandemic to investigate how the West African Ebola outbreak in 2014–2016 informed the COVID-19 response in several African countries. Overarching themes uncovered in these news articles include how investments made and resources developed during the Ebola outbreak were leveraged for COVID-19; how communication and transparency were promoted throughout the pandemic response; and how local community leaders were mobilised for the enforcement of COVID-19 mitigation strategies.

We found that investments relied on and capitalised during COVID-19 included policies, procedures, response systems, public health and medical workers, leaders, experts, healthcare systems, infrastructure and international partnerships. Although it was clear that using these investments and existing resources allowed countries to implement response activities faster, some articles reported that not all investments or resources could be leveraged. For example, some resources that were abundant during Ebola were limited during COVID-19, given that this was a global pandemic rather than a regionally centralised outbreak. International partner countries, donors and international organisations had limited funding and support to provide as they were managing their own response efforts. Some physical infrastructures built during Ebola were not maintained and even torn down prior to COVID-19, making these investments unusable for the pandemic.

Communication and transparency focused on information dissemination, decision-making and funding allocation. This contrasted with the lack of transparency and funding corruption that characterised the Ebola outbreak, which caused distress, fear and mistrust among the countries’ citizens, particularly noted by the Ebola-affected countries. Traditional and religious community leaders were mobilised to engage communities in enforcing mitigation strategies, which opposed the top-down approach of response efforts that were dictated during Ebola. The findings extracted from these news articles complement the available evidence around Ebola response lessons learnt and their application to COVID-19 response activities.

News coverage also identified leveraging investments, transparency and community engagement as being key to a faster response to COVID-19 relative to the Ebola experience. In addition, news media seem to suggest that each component leads to a sense of trust, either through familiarity or openness, which further facilitates the timely implementation and adoption of mitigation measures. News media discourse emphasised that leveraging existing resources, such as policies, procedures and response systems, builds trust because people are familiar with them. Also, people feel more secure with healthcare workers that have experience. For example, governments or response efforts that chose to hire front-line workers and government leaders with resumes that included managing and mitigating the spread of past infectious diseases, like Ebola, showed communities that the experience of these workers was respected and trusted.

Articles also observed that relying on international partners that have collaborated in the past builds trust because people are familiar with the partners’ work, how they operate, their values and how they have supported and can support the country and its people. This familiarity seen throughout the leveraging of investments and existing resources seems to be a precondition of trust. In addition, transparency fosters trust because demonstrating goodwill in being open, sharing decisions and why they were made demonstrates to citizens that there is nothing to hide or be fearful of, which was the case during Ebola.

Finally, articles highlighted that community engagement built trust because it empowered communities to steer the response in a way that fit their needs. There was a sense of ownership of local response efforts because it provided the opportunity for members to partake in the decision-making process, giving them a voice in the response. In all, the news articles highlighted how leveraging investments, communication and transparency, and community engagement facilitated trust, which in turn enabled a faster response because people knew what to do, how to do it, and why they were doing it. Communities also felt a sense of confidence and security because they had ownership over response activities that directly affected them.

Additionally, our results highlight that news articles, even from countries that did not directly experience the Ebola outbreak (Ethiopia, Ghana and Kenya), contain lessons learnt applicable to COVID-19. Articles from these countries focused primarily on leveraging existing resources and past investments, showing the enduring impact of the Ebola response investments across a broader geographic scope. This underscores that outbreak-related news and lessons learnt resonate as topics of interest to neighbouring countries.

These findings suggest that journalists, experts and other independent writers (both from public health and non-public health backgrounds) came to the same conclusion as other research about how the previous Ebola outbreak experience supported a faster COVID-19 response. This implies that countries can leverage past outbreak experiences to support the implementation of future responses.

The study and data source have several limitations. First, there may be inherent biases in news media. Whereas some articles may present all the facts in neutral tones as informational pieces, other articles may be sensational in that writers report on events from personal or organisational perspectives with the motivation to persuade their audience of an argument. We accounted for this bias in the study by analysing the entire collection of articles and capturing themes that were presented across articles, countries and news sources. Second, we conducted a qualitative analysis, which is subjective by its nature. Although independent coauthors may have had different interpretations of the same text, we attempted to reduce this discrepancy by engaging in coding discussions to ensure coding consistency and consensus in the application. Third, we translated some articles into English and recognise that certain terms, phrases or expressions may not translate well, and therefore, our interpretation of translated text may have inherent biases. Fourth, we did not conduct additional background research about these countries’ Ebola and COVID-19 response efforts that could generate clearer links between themes. Finally, we did not specifically analyse articles published from Ebola outbreak countries against articles published in non-Ebola outbreak countries because we did not have enough articles to generate a true comparison. Such analysis may have given us insight into whether there were differences in how and to what extent investments were leveraged between these groups of countries, and to what extent transparency and community engagement played a role. Future research should consider conducting such analysis with this data source. Also, future research should consider triangulating news media data with other formally collected data for a more robust validation of findings and interpretations.

Conclusion

COVID-19-related news articles describing lessons learnt from the Ebola response focused on the importance of leveraging existing resources and past investments; promoting transparency in public health messaging and engaging communities. Study findings suggest that fostering trust prior to and throughout an outbreak facilitates timely implementation of and compliance with mitigation strategies. Trust can be built by leveraging existing resources, being communicative and transparent about their funding allocation and decision-making and engaging communities.
