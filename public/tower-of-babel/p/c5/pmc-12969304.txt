An augmented evidence base for the interpretation of forensic text-based communications materials as intelligence and evidence
Authors: RM Morgan, E Levin, M Biriotti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThere are many different materials and traces that are now used routinely as intelligence in investigations or evidence in court. Traces remain important for many investigations and crime reconstructions, and text-based materials are growing in significance, especially in cases where the critical questions focus on intent and consent which in many situations cannot be answered by traces alone.Traces generally have a so-called ‘single object of study’ and focussed questions to answer, which is reflected in the established evidence base that underpins the interpretation of these traces. For text-based materials, there are usually ‘multiple objects of study’ with a broad range of potential questions to be considered. Those questions tend to be less defined, and it is not possible for one discipline or specialism to provide the full picture. Therefore, it is not possible to simply transpose the approach for interpreting single object of study traces to multiple objects of study materials.Four rapid reflexive thematic analyses were undertaken of samples of literature from four different disciplinary discourses that address text-based communications materials. The findings illustrate the range of contrasting underlying assumptions across different disciplines that address the multiple objects of study relevant to evaluating and interpreting text. An augmented evidence base to the traditional evidence base that underpins trace evaluation and interpretation is proposed for text-based materials that is able to bring together insights from multiple disciplinary traditions that may have contrasting philosophical foundations to address the different objects of study of text-based communication materials.Keywords: Digital communications evidence, Text, Context, Interpretation, Evidence base, Reflexive thematic analysis

Introduction

The increasing prevalence of digital and text-based materials in crime reconstruction

Digital materials are increasingly important in forensic investigations [1]. In the UK, it is estimated that digital materials are present in 90% of the cases investigated in England and Wales [[2], [3], [4]], and data extracted from mobile phones are playing an increasing role in cases which are presented in court [1,5].

Digital forensic materials represent a class of trace [6], which has resulted in a growing prevalence of relevant text-based materials. As such, it is important to consider the practical and theoretical ramifications of this increasingly prevalent evidence type. As digital text-based materials have become more widespread there has been considerable discussion focussed on aligning digital evidence collection, preservation, analysis, and evaluation with the approaches established for trace materials [[7], [8], [9]]. At the same time, it is possible to observe differences between traces and text-based materials.

First, historically, traces or marks have been identified, recovered, and analysed to

Derive as much intelligence, and potentially evidence, as possible [10]. For a specific and individual trace, such as trace biological materials, particles, or finger marks, this has tended to involve dealing with a scarcity of material, where the identification, preservation, and recovery of that material are salient considerations, and finite amounts of materials traditionally meant that finite physical methods of analysis could be conducted [11]. As a result, the importance of combining the insights from different types of trace in the crime reconstruction process is well understood [[12], [13], [14]]. With text-based materials, and especially digital communications evidence (DCE) it is possible to observe that often there is a shift from dealing with a scarcity of material to dealing with an abundance of material, where one device may contain many thousands of text messages, social media messages, and emails in addition to associated metadata. This shift means that the challenge for investigators is often less about being able to find a trace, and more about identifying which traces are relevant in a large amount of material and attempting to “sift through electronic haystacks” [15] to identify materials relevant to the questions under investigation. This is especially important where the sheer quantity of information overwhelms the capacity for analysis [5]. In addition, in comparison to many methods employed to analyse physical traces, most analyses of digital text-based materials are non-destructive, whereby analysing materials using one method does not preclude the use of other methods.

Second, the analysis and interpretation of traces in crime reconstruction has tended to seek to establish what may have happened within a relatively discrete moment or moments in time. For example, establishing whether contact has been made with a particular object from trace DNA analysis [16] or a particular place using environmental traces [17], or whether gunshot residue has been transferred through direct or indirect transfer onto garments [18]. While text-based materials can also be used to investigate whether an offence was committed at a specific moment in time, investigations which utilise Digital Communications Evidence (DCE) may also focus on attempting to establish whether a pattern of behaviour has occurred or has been sustained over an extended period. This is the case with, for example, Digital Communications Evidence used to support investigations of Coercive Control offences.

Third, it is possible to observe a distinction between traces and text-based materials in terms of the objects of study (what is intended to be investigated). For traces, while there are often multiple forms of analysis that can be undertaken (for example blood can be considered by blood pattern analysis and DNA analysis) there is generally a single object of study (the blood substrate), which poses defined questions to answer (what led to the blood deposit? Who does the blood come from?). The analysis of that blood sample utilises established knowledge bases derived from the ‘parent disciplines’ of fluid mechanics (for pattern analysis) and molecular biology, population genetics, and statistics (for DNA analysis). Similarly, for text-based communications materials the meaning of each word can be explored from multiple analytical perspectives derived from parent disciplines (psychological, sociologically, literary). At the same time text-based materials are comprised of a collection of words that form a text representing multiple words (objects) that can be analysed from different perspectives, as a collection of words that can also have meanings that are different or greater than the sum of its parts, and as a series of messages (collections of words) which may also reveal additional meaning to the text(s). As such, for text-based communication materials, there are usually multiple objects of study, and a broad range of potential questions asked to derive as much evidence and/or intelligence as possible from the material. For example, in the analysis of a series of text-based messages, the question posed might be ‘What does this term mean in this context?’ ‘Do these messages substantiate controlling and/or coercive behaviour?’. To answer such questions might involve analysis and interpretation of the metadata of the corpus of messages (providing insight into who sent and received it, when, and where), the content of the message (what is stated as text and what is unstated as subtext), and the broader contexts and intentions of the messages, and the modes of communication. However, in addition, the analysis of specific words, phrases within a text, or the series of texts that form interactions between different parties is also necessary. To address these multiple dimensions of text-based materials, and questions which arise, it is possible to argue that a broader evidence base is needed to interpret the text-based material.

The importance of achieving a robust, transparent, and reproducible evaluative interpretation of what the trace means in a specific context remains a core challenge across different materials. However, this paper suggests that while there are many resonances, there are also differences in the interpretation challenge for traces with a single object of study in comparison to text-based communications materials that have multiple objects of study.

The interpretation of digital communications evidence (DCE)

The forms of expertise required to inform the interpretation of DCE

The interpretation of forensic science evidence is normally underpinned by expertise derived from relevant parent disciplines (genetics for DNA, fluid mechanics for blood pattern analysis etc.), Traces such as fingerprints, DNA, residues, fibres have all been scrutinised in the published literature and there is broad consensus about what type of expertise is required to analyse each trace [19,20], how inferences can be presented in appropriate ways through proposition based reasoning [[20], [21], [22]], and where the threshold for admissibility lies [[20], [23]]. Text-based materials represent an allied but distinctive challenge because while many parent disciplines have established traditions and methods for decoding the meaning of text, these disciplines may have different underlying assumptions and philosophical frameworks that mean that there are often tensions and seeming incompatibilities when they are considered together (this is explored further in sections 1.2.3, 3.4). As materials such as text-based DCE become increasingly prevalent in cases presented at court [1,5], it is vital to consider what disciplinary insight and expertise is needed to underpin the interpretation of text-based material transparently, reproducibly, and robustly as part of the wider discourse that acknowledges that many aspects of the treatment of text-based DCE currently require broader harmonisation and formalisation [see 4].

The differences between the interpretation of text-based DCE and other forms of writing

Although there are hundreds of years of scholarship on the interpretation of texts [24], emerging forms of text-based DCE are formally different. For example, text-based DCE have multimodal texts [25] which are rich in subtext, irony and nuance, rendering meaning highly dependent on context [19]. It is presently unclear, and a matter of considerable academic debate, to what extent they represent continuity or discontinuity with older forms of text, the extent to which they might be categorized as public or private, ephemeral or permanent, spontaneous or planned [25]. Messages are normally intended to be ephemeral [19] and, as with the use of transcripts of intercepted phone communications as evidence, they may carry cultural nuances that render generic protocols for text analysis problematic [26]. Digital materials also bring new practical challenges for interpretation, in terms of privacy considerations [15,27] and the volume of material that can be pertinent to an enquiry [5].

The anatomy of the evidence base for the interpretation of traces

As discussed in section 1.2.1, there is a depth of literature addressing the interpretation of traces in crime reconstructions [19,20]. Over time a general understanding has been reached in terms of the importance of the knowledge base and forensic science evidence base (made up of a consideration of activity level questions and also the extrinsic and intrinsic factors that impact evaluation) that is needed to underpin evaluative interpretation of traces (as articulated by Morgan [28]; see Fig. 1).

The two core elements of interpretating science evidence for forensic applications; the knowledge base and the evidence base (reproduced from Ref. [22]).

When considering a trace with a single object of study it is possible to develop this understanding of the contributing attributes (the knowledge base and evidence base as presented in Fig. 1) to reach a deeper understanding of the anatomy of the evidence base that underpins trace evaluation and interpretation (outlined in Fig. 2).

A model of the anatomy of the evidence base for traces.

In this model (Fig. 2) a trace (for example, a sample of DNA, or a bloodstain) is considered by examiners alongside relevant context (both generic to the analysis technique and specific to the sample). The ‘evidence base’ brings together four components that address the singular object of study (the trace). First, there is the data (1) derived from analysis (e.g. establishing the characteristics of a trace) which is usually achieved on the basis of established insight from parent disciplines (2) (such as genetics for DNA, or fluid mechanics for blood pattern analysis). Then it is necessary to situate those data in the specific context of the specimen in the crime reconstruction. This is achieved by incorporating case-specific context (3) (such as the age of a complainant in a sexual assault case) along with a consideration of activity level (4) (the broader understanding of transfer and persistence of the trace) and human decision making (both intrinsic cognitive processing factors and the extrinsic impact of external factors on that decision making). Bringing these four aspects together provides a way to interpret what that trace may mean (in the context of probabilistic reasoning and assessment of appropriate thresholds of uncertainty). This model has been developed in the context of traces with a single object of study where there is clarity about which knowledge base and evidence base(s) are required to address the relevant questions. By identifying the multiple components of the evidence base the differences between single object of study traces and multiple objects of study traces becomes more apparent.

For materials with multiple objects of study there are potentially multiple justifiable foci and therefore multiple relevant knowledge bases and evidence bases that are relevant. Where there is a single object of study, such as addressing a blood mark, to assess the circumstances of that blood being deposited it is necessary to engage with the parent discipline of physics and fluid dynamics along with the evidence bases developed in forensic blood pattern analysis. To assess the source of that blood it is necessary to engage with the parent discipline of biology and genetics along with the evidence bases developed in forensic DNA analysis. These forms of knowledge are compatible, operating with shared foundations and assumptions. In contrast to address a corpus of text it is plausible that there are many parent disciplines that address the decoding of text (history, linguistics, literary, studies psychology, sociology) but not all of the frameworks, foundational assumptions and methodologies are logically compatible or philosophically coherent. As such, it is proposed that a broader evidence base is needed for materials with multiple objects of study such as text-based digital communications evidence.

Study aim

To explore the possibility of a broader evidence base to support the evaluation and interpretation of materials that have multiple objects of study such as text-based digital communications evidence (DCE) this study sought to identify the characteristics of a broader evidence base. To achieve this, a reflexive thematic analysis of relevant samples of disciplinary literature were analysed in order to ascertain the current insights from different disciplines regarding the analysis and interpretation of text-based digital communications and to establish whether it is possible using a sample of literature from different disciplinary perspectives as a starting point, to juxtapose and combine the different disciplinary insights in useful ways, to access and interpret multiple facets of text-based digital communications materials. In so doing, this study addressed how the anatomy of a broader evidence base for materials with multiple objects of study may differ to the evidence base for traces with single objects of study.

For the purposes of this study, the literature sampling included a specific focus on the analysis and interpretation of text-based digital communications evidence (DCE) within the investigation of violence against women and girls (VAWG) offences. VAWG is a highly pervasive challenge globally with one in three women experiencing physical or sexual violence in their lifetime [29] and text-based DCE is highly relevant in these investigations. In VAWG cases there is a frequent occurrence of alleged victims and alleged perpetrators cohabiting, and so there are usually highly plausible explanations for the transfer of physical trace evidence, and as a result there is increased attention directed towards digital text-based communications evidence [9]. In addition, due to the high prevalence of pre-existing relationships between victims and alleged perpetrators, there is often a large and accessible corpus of messages from before, after, and sometimes during the alleged offence(s) [15] which can be critical for establishing intent and consent, which often means that there is a need to understand patterns of behaviour over time [30].

Method

Literature sampling method

Four disciplinary perspectives were selected given the insights they could offer in relation to text-based DCE in cases of VAWG; forensic science and policing, linguistics, ethics/philosophy and psychology (Fig. 3). Forensic science and policing was selected as digital forensic examiners have a track record of analysing digital text-based communications evidence in both investigative and legal contexts, with analysis and interpretation including both the textual content of the message, and the technical metadata of such communications (e.g. when and where it was sent and received) [3]. Linguistics was chosen because linguists have developed schema, frameworks, methods, and techniques for analysing written communications and conversations [31]. Psychology was explored as the discipline has developed insights into the analysis and interpretation of written text with the intention of inferring mood, intent, and pathologies [32]. Ethics and philosophy was also considered given the focus on the admissibility issues raised in the use of DCE and the consideration within the discipline of ethical, moral, and philosophical challenges that arise in the interpretation of text [33].

A diagrammatic representation of the four disciplinary ‘lenses’ of literature surveyed in this study.

For each of these disciplinary lenses, a five-stage process was followed in order to identify appropriate samples of recent literature since 2006 (Fig. 4). Step 1 was to identify sub-fields, prominent themes and key authors within each discipline. Step 2 involved undertaking a stratified search to ensure that all the relevant established sub fields of a discipline were represented in the literature sample. In step 3 a snowballing search was undertaken to identify any papers that had been missed through the search in step 2. At this stage (step 4) a longlist of articles was selected, and then from this longlist a short list was selected for review (step 5). This resulted in samples of approximately 10 papers for each disciplinary rapid review (Fig. 5).

An overview of the literature sampling method.

The fields, sub-topics, and publication years and titles of the sampled literature in each disciplinary review.

Reflexive thematic analysis

Reflexive Thematic Analysis [[34], [35], [36]] was employed to identify the key themes which appeared within the samples of literature selected (Fig. 5). This method was selected as it is suitable for inductive research, where themes are not known a priori [37,38]. This analysis followed the six-stage process proposed by Braun and Clarke [28,29], and used by others [33]; namely, (1) familiarisation with the data, (2) initial code generation (3) generation of initial themes, (4) reviewing those themes, (5) refining, defining and naming themes, and (6) the production of a report.

Variations between disciplines: what is text?

At a foundational level, there was variation between the different disciplinary lenses about what ‘text’ represents or could represent. Within the literature drawn from Forensic Science and Policing, there was a sense that intercepted texts may represent factual content, which can be analysed to verify whether an alleged offence may have taken place [15], the context for the offence [9], used as “admissions of guilt” by the prosecution [41:578], or used “to infer […] motive [and] intent” [5:1096]. In contrast, within the Linguistic literature sample, text was generally framed as the product of a set of underlying rules and frameworks, which underpin conversation, and which can be rendered legible and interpretable [e.g. 26,32]. In tension with the lenses of Forensic Science and Policing interpreting text as potentially factual content, within Linguistics, the framework of Relevance Theory [31,52,77], includes a concept of ‘implicature’; that there exists a difference between what an interlocutor says (explicates), and what they actually mean (implicate) [31]. Within the literature sample drawn from the Ethical and Philosophical aspects of text analysis, text was presented as an ethical minefield, raising issues around privacy [64], ethical standards [63], and the ‘ideal’ witness or victim [66,72]. Within the Psychology literature sample concerning the Psychological analysis of text, text was presented as a reservoir of data relating to the personality traits, mood, and consumer preferences of the language-producer [32,56,58,75,76].

Variations between disciplines: assumptions which underpin the enterprise of text analysis

Within the Forensic Science and Policing literature sample, a prominent theme that emerged was an assumption of continuity with older forensic science evidence types – that the ‘accuracy’, ‘transparency’ and ‘robustness’ of analysis should be valued (revealing an underlying positivist view that there is a ‘true’ meaning, and that high-quality interpretation should accurately access that meaning) [39]. Within the Linguistic literature sample, a different assumption surfaced as prominent; that one can, and must, select a level of analysis for a text; e.g. defining analysis at a lexical, semantic, or pragmatic level [31]. Within the literature sample drawn from the Ethical and Philosophical aspects of text analysis, a conspicuous and predominant assumption was that the analysis of text-based DCE is currently a ‘protoscience’, requiring further formalisation of methods, theories, and legal boundaries [64]. Within the Psychology literature sample concerning the psychological analysis of text, the most prominent assumption was that (automated) text analysis is a viable and valuable research opportunity [32,56,58,75,76].

Variations between disciplines: key challenges articulated

Within the Forensic Science and Policing literature sample, key challenges were identified included the volumes of digital evidence available compared to capacity [5,41], the funding and competence of personnel [3,15,41,54], the legislative and regulatory environments in which analysis must occur [e.g. 3,35], and the scope for error in the process of interpretation [15,40]. Within the Linguistic literature sample, different challenges to the analysis interpretation of digital text communications were articulated. These included disagreement as to the nature of the medium of digital communications, with uncertainty expressed as to whether they should be considered public or private, ephemeral or permanent, and spontaneous or planned [25], and uncertainty over whether digital media constituted a significant change, or whether existing methodologies remained appropriate (e.g. ibid), where digital communications were likely to contain multimodal and iconographetic elements [25,50,51]. Issues around objectivity were also identified, that when interpreting digital communication, parallels between sampled conversations and the researchers' own experiences might make objectivity impossible [46]. Within the literature sample drawn from the Ethical and Philosophical aspects of text analysis, challenges discussed included an ‘expectation gap’ between what community and victims see as proportionate violation of privacy and the level of intrusion and scrutiny which victims may be asked to experience [64,65], alongside the lack of standardisation in ethical codes, training, and regulation [49], and the interpretable nature of text-based DCE [66,72], combined with societal expectations of victims [66,65]. Within the Psychology literature sample concerning the psychological analysis of text, authors highlighted a variety of technical challenges specific to different methods of automated text analysis [57,59,75], and more generally, limitations to language as a form of communication, since “people are inconsistent” [57] and language is imprecise, with phenomena such as colexification [see 46] complicating attempts at interpretation.

Whether the medium of the text (in electronic communications) matters

Within the literature sample from the lens of Linguistics, one theme which was explicitly discussed was whether the medium of a text being analysed was significant, with debate over whether existing protocols for text analysis and linguistic frameworks can be readily applied to the new forms of short, multimodal communication [25]. This strand of discussion was also discussed explicitly within the Psychology literature sample, with authors discussing the potential significance of medium [75], modality [76], channel (ibid.), and device (ibid.) in shaping the style and meaning of language used (ibid.). Within the Forensic Science and Policing literature sample, much of the discussion was implicit, with an assumption that digital communications represented continuity with older forms of intercepted communication evidence, but with explicit mention that “officers need to be careful to interpret this evidence in the context of the online/digital forms of expression” [16: 509].

Whether it is possible to infer the mental state and intentions of an author from a text

Within the literature sample from Forensic Science and Policing, it was explicitly stated that the analysis of text messages might “[provide] investigators with insight through which to infer internal mental states” [5: 1096]. This discourse is aligned with some of the statements observed in the Psychology sample of literature, where it was proposed that the analysis of text could be used to infer the personality traits, mood, and consumer preferences of an author [32,56,58,75,76]. At the other end of the spectrum, there was literature within the Linguistic sample that implicitly expressed the sentiment that there will always be a gap between what is said and what is meant as implied in ‘The Inference Model’ and ‘implicature’ outlined by Grice [77], referring to the difference between what a speaker says (or explicates), and what they mean (or implicate) [31].

Whether the analysis of text is an art or a science, and is or should be underpinned by positivist or constructivist assumptions

Within the four literature samples, there was a range of stances on whether the analysis of digital text is a factual, scientific exercise in observation, or more of an art, and a process of decoding possible meanings with nuance. Relatedly, the former stance exhibited underlying positivist assumptions; the idea that if an examiner were to clinically dissect a text, any examiner would robustly, repeatably, and accurately access the ‘true’ meaning (e.g. as implied in discussions of ‘accuracy’ and ‘rigour’ in Ref. [39]). Conversely, other authors expressed underlying constructivist assumptions; that it is possible to ‘read’ meaning into a text, but that this meaning will be constructed by the examiner and would vary between examiners and over time and space (as seen in discussions of human bias in the Psychology literature, as in Ref. [57], and in Linguistic discussions of human examiners qualitatively analysing text messages in Ref. [53]). Positions in between those two ends of the spectrum were also observed, with some suggesting implicitly that while attendance to reflexivity is required, ‘robust’ and ‘transparent’ methods could be adopted (e.g. as expressed implicitly in the psychology and linguistics literature).

The anatomy of a broader evidence base for text-based digital communications evidence

As discussed in section 1.2.3, the framework developed for understanding what a specific trace means in a particular case context is predicated on there being a single object of study, and thus a focussed evidence base informed by knowledge from the relevant parent discipline(s) which underpins analysis and interpretation (Fig. 2). The themes identified in the reflexive thematic analysis of the four disciplinary literature samples identified themes that suggest that the analysis of text-based materials can take different units of text as units of analysis (e.g. words, phrases, entire messages, series of messages) and apply different forms of analysis (such as linguistic or semantic) which in many cases have different underlying assumptions and frameworks (such as contrasting positivist or constructivist assumptions about whether there is a ‘true’ interpretation or whether all analyses constructed, along with different assumptions about the potential stability of meaning(s) of iconographetic elements). As such, what has been identified in this study is that the anatomy of the evidence base of text-based DCE (an example of a material that has multiple objects of study) is broader that the evidence base underpinning the consideration of traces with a single object of study and is presented in Fig. 6.

Anatomy of an augmented evidence base for text-based digital text communications evidence.

For text-based materials with multiple objects of study, the evidence base is made up of two parts each with multiple components. In the first part, the analysis of each object of study results in the generation of insight resulting in multiple forms of data. For example, for text-based DCE it is possible to analyse the text with a linguistic lens to consider what is implied, linguistically, by the text, or a psychological lens to consider what is implied about the nature of the psychological characteristics of the communicator. Each of these analyses is underpinned by the parent discipline (in a similar manner to the analysis of a trace with a single object of study as outlined in Fig. 2). In addition, much like the analysis of a trace, it is necessary to consider the case specific context, such as the attributes of the relationship between the two parties, the size of the body of messages, and the metadata from the messages to establish time, location, device, server involved in the creation, sending and receiving of the text.

In the second part of the evidence base the multiple forms of insight generated in part 1 need to then be considered in order to develop an understanding of the text from a multifaceted perspective and offer inferences as to what the text may mean in a specific crime reconstruction. In a similar manner to the evaluation of a trace with a single object of study, this will not necessarily result in compromise between the different perspectives, it may result in holding multiple and seemingly incompatible attributes together. However, for text-based materials this is meaningfully different from the evidence base that has been articulated for traces (Fig. 2) where a broad consensus is reached through the consideration of proposition-based reasoning by the triers of fact. For text-based materials insights from multiple objects of study can offer clues to the patterns of behaviour that may have developed over time that offer important context when considering the inferences reached through analysis of the text by different disciplinary approaches. This contrasts with traces with a single object of study where source attribution or indications of contact in a discrete moment relevant to the crime being reconstructed is often the predominant focus. While there are similarities between traces and text-based materials in terms of analysis being undertaken that generates multiple forms of insight, there is also an observable distinction. For a trace such as blood, there may be a difference in opinion about the meaning and relevance of the results generated from high quality and reproducible DNA analysis in a specific case. For a text-based material the nature of the material is distinctive (for example it is not possible to run a linguistic analysis against known standards). As such a linguistic or sociological analysis of the text may result in multiple evidence-based inferences about the psychological state of the sender or the nature of the relationship which may be underpinned by different philosophical assumptions as outlined above. Therefore, for text-based materials with multiple objects of study the evidence base requires the additional consideration of plurality (i.e. a plurality of insights contributing to the evidence base resulting in a plurality of interpretations that are distinctive to the range of insights resulting from the analysis of a trace).

Discussion

Forensic science is a synthetic discipline, incorporating insights from diverse intellectual traditions and multiple disciplines [28,78,79] and these disciplines have different disciplinary framings, assumptions, ontologies, epistemologies, and methods. Historically, it has taken time to establish an evidence base for the interpretation of a particular material or trace [[80], [81], [82], [83]]. However, it is clear that the importance and significance of text-based DCE is growing, particularly in specific cases where physical traces are not generally appropriate for questions at the core of a case such as where physical contact is not in dispute, but where the prosecution and defence offer different interpretations of intent and consent (as is often the case in VAWG offences).

Through conducting Reflexive Thematic Analyses of samples of literature from four disciplines, it was possible to identify variations in disciplinary perspectives on the analysis of text. There were three themes identified across these disciplinary approaches of text analysis which were in tension.

These themes indicate that the nature of text-based materials pose related by different challenges when bringing multiple disciplinary perspectives to consider the meaning of those materials in a crime reconstruction context. As such, the disciplinary reviews illustrate that the anatomy of the evidence base that has been established to support the interpretation of traces (that usually have a single object of study) (presented in Fig. 2) can be differentiated from the anatomy of the evidence base for text-based materials that have multiple objects of study. Forensic Science brings together insights from so called ‘parent disciplines’ to support the analysis and evaluation of relevant materials. For traces with a so called ‘single object of study’ these disciplines while distinct are generally predicated on common philosophical assumptions which means that different disciplines can be applied together. When it comes to text-based materials, relevant parent disciplines have established traditions for decoding meaning and implications of text that are often predicated on contrasting philosophical foundations and assumptions which can result in tensions and seeming incompatibility. Therefore, it is not feasible to simply transpose the approach for traces with one object of study to text-based materials such as text-based DCE that have multiple objects of study. Achieving this represents a challenge. The findings from this study indicate that it is possible to build on the established evaluative interpretation approaches for traces and complement the endeavours that seek a harmonised approach to evaluative interpretation of materials in crime reconstructions [9]. At the same time, text-based materials such as text-based DCE represent a distinctive challenge, and require an evidence base that is able to accommodate a broad range of evidence-based insights that are derived from different disciplinary traditions, yet still offer a means for transparent and robust evaluation and interpretation (Fig. 6).

The broader evidence base proposed in this paper (Fig. 6) will require a broader approach to the traditional multidisciplinary proposition-based approach that is prevalent in crime reconstruction that addresses traces [21]. A multidisciplinary approach brings different disciplines to address different parts of the reconstruction challenge (such as insight from DNA, CCTV and footwear impressions) so that when those insights are brought together the insights can be weighed up and key factors that are relevant to the crime reconstruction in a particular case established. For the broader evidence based proposed here for text-based materials this approach needs to find a way to explore a broader range of different disciplinary perspectives that address different objects of study in concert to achieve intelligence and evidence that preserves the different insights reached from different disciplines (which may be contradictory or mutually incompatible), examines resonance and tensions, and offers insights that can be utilised by the triers of fact within the justice system.

The research undertaken in this paper suggests that alongside the traditional disciplines that have developed forensic applications, linguistics, ethics and philosophy, psychology, and law are key disciplines that bring valuable additional insight to materials that have multiple objects of study. Additional disciplines that should be considered include literary studies, which has a long tradition of interpreting text, subtext, and nuance, and which have developed a suite of methodologies for attempting to decode meaning and implications. Further research will be necessary to assess whether a core of required disciplines can be articulated as a general standard with additional disciplinary inputs brought in as the evidence materials or case necessitates, or whether a more fluid case sensitive approach is needed.

Conclusion

This paper presents the findings of four rapid reflexive thematic analyses that considered samples of literature from the disciplines of Forensic Science and Policing, Linguistics, Ethics and Philosophy and Psychology that addressed the analysis of text as a form of insight and/or evidence. Different disciplines identified key themes and issues that were in resonance with each other and with traditional forensic science approaches such as the importance of context. Three key areas of tension were also identified through the analysis in terms of the medium of the text, the degree to which it is possible to infer the mental state and intention of the author from the text, and whether text analysis is an art or a science.

These findings suggest that there is value in broadening the evidence base that underpins the evaluative interpretation of traces that have a single object of study for text-based digital communications evidence that have multiple objects of study. This paper presents a proposal for a broader evidence base for text-based digital communications evidence that accommodates multiple disciplinary perspectives which may have incompatible underlying assumptions and frameworks) that address the multiple objects of study (Fig. 6). It is proposed that identifying where materials have multiple objects of study is critical, and that the model presented here for text-based DCE (Fig. 6) may have wider applications to other forms of evidence that have multiple objects of study.
