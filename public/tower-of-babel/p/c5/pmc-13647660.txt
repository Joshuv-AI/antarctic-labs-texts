Impact of ultra-low tidal volume ventilation on 1-year functional outcome in COVID-19 ARDS patients. A long-term follow-up analysis of a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Jean-Christophe Richard, Fatima Chorfa, Nicolas Terzi, Hodane Yonis, Florent Wallet, Claire Dupuis, Laurent Argaud, Bertrand Delannoy, Guillaume Thiery, Gaël Bourdin, Thomas Rimmele, Michel Muller, Florian Sigaud, Guillaume Rigault, Emilie Joffredo, Mehdi Mezidi, Bertrand Souweine, Loredana Baboi, Hassan Serrier, Muriel Rabilloud, Laurent Bitker

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUltra-low tidal volume ventilation (ULTV) aims to minimize ventilator-induced lung injury in acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), but is associated with an increase in arterial carbon dioxide (PaCO2). This follow-up study aimed to evaluate whether ULTV impacted long-term pre-specified secondary outcomes in the VT4COVID trial.MethodsVT4COVID was an open-label multicenter randomized controlled superiority trial performed in ten French intensive care units. Eligible patients were COVID-19 ARDS patients with arterial oxygen pressure (PaO2) over inspired oxygen fraction (FiO2) ≤ 150 mmHg, randomized to ULTV with tidal volume (VT) 4 mL.kg− 1 predicted body weight (PBW), or low VT ventilation (LTV) with VT 6 mL.kg− 1 PBW. Participants, investigators, and outcome assessors were unblinded to group assignment. The following prespecified secondary outcomes were assessed at day-365: MoCA 5-minute, SF-36 and IES-R scores; mortality at day-365 was added a posteriori as an outcome. Scores were compared between arms with the Mann-Whitney test, and mortality was compared between groups with a Cox proportional-hazard model. Seven patients were lost to follow-up and 34 surviving patients had missing data for at least one score. Mortality at day-365 was not significantly different between ULTV and LTV arms (47/102 [46%] vs. 44/106 [42%], hazard ratio 1.19 (95% confidence interval (CI95%):0.79–1.80)). The other secondary outcomes were evaluated in 39/55 [71%] and 44/62 [71%] patients in the ULTV and LTV arm, respectively. The MoCA 5-minute score was significantly lower in the ULTV arm (median difference: -2 [CI95%:-4–0] points, p < 0.05). There was no significant difference in SF-36 and IES-R scores between groups. Background

Permissive hypercapnia is used in ARDS since the early 1990s following the seminal studies of Hickling et al. [1, 2], and protective ventilation (i.e., ventilation with low tidal volume (VT)) is a standard of care of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). However, barotrauma is still observed in 7% of the patients [3], and its prevalence is even higher in the context of COVID-19 ARDS [4], suggesting that protective ventilation is not protective enough. Ultra-low tidal volume ventilation (ULTV), by reducing VT at or below 4 mL.kg− 1 predicted body weight (PBW) may minimize ventilator-induced lung injury but may be associated with an increase in arterial carbon dioxide partial pressure (PaCO2) with potential deleterious consequences across multiple organ systems [5, 6]. ULTV was not associated with improved outcomes when combined with extracorporeal CO2 removal (ECCO2R) in patients with acute respiratory failure in the REST randomized controlled trial [7]. This may be related to a high rate of serious adverse events related to the ECCO2R technique, and/or inadequate patient selection as ULTV may be most relevant in patients with severe ARDS and elevated standardized elastance [8]. We recently published the short-term results of the VT4COVID randomized controlled trial comparing ULTV without extracorporeal respiratory support with low tidal volume ventilation (LTV) in COVID-19 ARDS [9]. The VT4COVID study did not identify any impact of ULTV on the composite primary outcome of short-term mortality and ventilator-free days and on all other secondary outcomes, but was associated with an 11 mmHg elevation of PaCO2 in the ULTV arm [9]. As changes in PaCO2 levels induced by ULTV may have altered cerebral perfusion [10], and as the ULTV strategy may have led to higher sedation dose with adverse potential effects, the consequences of ULTV on long-term functional outcomes (i.e., cognitive function, health-related quality of life (HRQL) and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD)) need to be assessed to evaluate the long-term safety of this strategy.

Methods

Study aim

The aim of the present study was to evaluate whether ULTV without extracorporeal respiratory support impacted long-term secondary outcomes at day-365 from inclusion.

Study design and setting

The VT4COVID study was a multicenter open-labelled, randomized superiority trial, with two parallel groups, and enrolled hospitalized participants from 10 intensive care units (ICU) in France. This study is a pre-planned 1-year follow-up of the VT4COVID participants. The VT4COVID study was approved by a research ethics committee (CPP-IDF-VII) on April 14, 2020, under the reference IDCRB-2020-A00869-30. The trial protocol has been previously published [11], and is provided with the statistical analysis plan in Additional file 1. The trial was monitored by an independent data safety monitoring board and registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04349618) on April 16, 2020. This manuscript is reported according to the CONSORT 2025 statement [12]. We obtained written informed consent from all patients and/or representatives and vouch for the accuracy and completeness of the data and for the fidelity of the trial to the protocol.

Patients

Eligible patients were 18 years or older, under invasive mechanical ventilation for COVID-19 confirmed by RT-PCR, fulfilled ARDS criteria according to the Berlin definition [13], had an arterial partial oxygen pressure to inspired oxygen fraction ratio (PaO2/FiO2) ≤ 150 mmHg with a VT ≤ 6 mL.kg− 1 PBW and were under continuous intravenous sedation. Major exclusion criteria are reported thereafter, while the full list is reported in Additional file 2: onset of invasive or non-invasive ventilation more than 48 h before inclusion, arterial pH < 7.21 despite respiratory rate (RR) set to 35 min− 1 at the time of inclusion [14], patient under extracorporeal CO2 removal or extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, suspected intracranial hypertension, severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease defined by a GOLD score ≥ 3, chronic respiratory failure requiring long-term oxygen or non-invasive ventilation, and lack of written informed consent by patient or next of kin.

Patients included and randomized in the study with eligibility criteria and without withdrawal of consent defined the intention-to-treat (ITT) population.

Randomization and masking

We randomly assigned patients in a 1:1 ratio to the ULTV or the LTV arm. The random allocation sequence was generated by the statistician in charge. Randomization was performed through a web-based software using a balanced randomization scheme by center and random blocks of size 4 and 6. Study enrollment and assignment of participants to interventions was ensured by attending physicians, who had no access to the random allocation sequence. Care providers, outcome assessors and data analysts were not blinded regarding group allocation.

Procedures

In the ULTV arm, VT was decreased, starting within the first hour after randomization, by stepwise reduction of 1 mL.kg− 1 PBW steps at intervals ≤ 2 h down to 4 mL.kg− 1 PBW, and RR increased up to 35 min− 1 to maintain minute-ventilation constant. In the LTV arm, VT was set at 6 mL.kg− 1 PBW [15].

In both arms, positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) was set using a PEEP-FiO2 Table [15] aiming for a plateau pressure < 30 cmH2O under deep sedation (i.e., Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RASS) score [16] between − 3 and − 4). Sedative agents were left at the discretion of the attending physician. RR was further adjusted targeting a pH range between 7.20 and 7.45, and VT could be increased if pH remained below 7.15, despite RR increase to 35 min− 1 and minimization of instrumental dead space.

Both ventilatory strategies were applied until the success of a deep sedation withdrawal trial performed daily from day-2 of the study in patients with PaO2/FiO2>150 mm Hg in the supine position (Additional file 3). This trial was performed by decreasing PEEP to 5 cmH2O, and by setting FiO2 to 50% and VT to 6 mL.kg− 1 PBW during a 30-min test. Patients were deemed weanable from deep sedation if SpO2 remained greater than 87% and RR lower than 36 min− 1 during the test. After a successful deep sedation withdrawal trial, patients in both arms were ventilated with VT 6–8 mL.kg− 1 PBW, sedation was titrated to target a RASS score between − 2 and 0, and a spontaneous breathing test was performed daily until successful extubation. Deep sedation could be resumed (along with resumption of the assigned ventilatory strategy) in the following situations: respiratory distress, hypoxemia, ventilator asynchrony, and RR > 35 min− 1 despite ventilatory adjustments. All other co-interventions were standardized to ensure treatment arm comparability (the reader may refer to the original publication for a complete description [9]).

Outcomes

The following pre-specified secondary outcomes of the VT4COVID trial were assessed at 365 ± 10 days from inclusion by phone call: 1- the median value of the French version of the Montreal Cognitive Assessment 5-minute (MoCA 5-minute) score evaluating cognitive impairment [17, 18]; 2- the median values of both physical (PCS) and mental (MCS) summary scores of the Short Form-36 Health Survey (SF-36) score evaluating HRQL [19]; and 3- the median value of the Impact of Event Scale-Revised (IES-R) score evaluating subjective distress caused by traumatic events [20].

Mortality at day-365 was added a posteriori as a secondary outcome and analyzed as a time-to-event variable.

Cognitive impairment was deemed present when the MoCA 5-minute score was below 28, in accordance with the validation of the French version of the MoCA 5-minute score [18]. MCS was deemed altered for values lower than 40 [21]. PTSD was deemed present if the IES-R score was greater than 32 [22].

Data collection

Information on sex was obtained from the administrative section of electronic health records. The following respiratory parameters were assessed daily from inclusion to withdrawal of deep sedation or 14 days after inclusion whichever came first : PaO2/FiO2, pH, PaCO2, PEEP, VT, total PEEP, plateau pressure, driving pressure, RR, and mechanical power (MP). Total PEEP and plateau pressure were measured at the end of 3-s end-expiratory and end-inspiratory pauses, respectively. Driving pressure was defined as the difference between plateau pressure and total PEEP. In case of missing total PEEP, driving pressure was computed as (Pplat-PEEP)/VT. Total mechanical power was computed as previously described [23].

Primary investigators at each trial site collected the date of death of non-survivors from medical records and/or through phone calls to relatives or next of kin. Survivors were contacted by phone by trained research personnel, and several attempts were made to minimize loss to follow-up.

Statistical analysis

All analyses (except mortality at day-365) were conducted according to a modified intention-to-treat (mITT) principle. The mITT population corresponded to all randomized patients with long-term follow-up and at least one pre-specified outcome available. MoCA 5-minute, SF-36 and IES-R populations were defined as all randomized patients with at least one corresponding assessment performed.

Unless indicated otherwise, quantitative variables were described by median [interquartile range (IQR)] and compared between arms with the Mann-Whitney test.

Qualitative variables were described by frequencies and percentages, and compared between arms using the chi-square or the Fisher exact test.

For the one-year functional outcomes, complete-case data were used without replacement for missing data, except for the IES-R outcome. For this measure, a sensitivity analysis was conducted to account for one missing IES-R item (item#15) out of the 22 items composing the scale, due to a pagination error in some printed questionnaires. These values were assumed to be missing completely at random (MCAR), meaning that the probability of missingness was unrelated to both observed and unobserved data. They were replaced by multiple imputation, and descriptive statistics were estimated by Rubin‘s method [24].

Adjusted linear models were used for modeling the MoCA 5-minute score, the SF-36 summary scores and the IES-R score, respectively. Each multivariable model were adjusted for age, simplified acute physiology score 2 (SAPS2) [25] and SOFA [26] scores at inclusion, and ventilatory ratio at inclusion. The following variables were added in the multivariable model if their p-value was below 0.2 in univariable analysis: duration of deep sedation; minimum value and area under the curve (AUC) of PaO2/FiO2 standardized per day, maximum value and AUC of PaCO2 standardized per day. Minimum value and AUC of PaO2 standardized per day, and benzodiazepine dose were also tested, but were not prespecified. Minimum, maximum and AUC values were computed during the first fourteen days after inclusion or weaning of deep sedation whichever came first. Selection of variables was performed backwards, and restricted cubic spline form for continuous covariates was used for relaxing predictors shape and examining linearity assumptions [27].

A secondary analysis of the MoCA 5-minute score was performed by categorizing the scores according to the cut-off value of 28. Logistic regressions were used for modeling the probability of cognitive impairment. The discriminant capacity of the final model was assessed using the estimation of the c-statistic, corrected for optimism bias with a bootstrap method. An optimal predicted probability was obtained by maximizing the Youden index [28, 29]. At this probability level, a cut-off value of a continuous covariate of interest was estimated using a probit model [30], with the other covariates in the model set to their mean values. The 95% confidence limits were also computed.

A Cox proportional-hazard model was performed to test the intervention effect on mortality. Proportionality of hazards was assessed by examining log-log survival plots and Schoenfeld residuals.

All the analyses were performed using SAS statistical software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Role of the funding source

The funders had no role in study design; in the collection, analysis, and interpretation of data; in the writing of the report; and in the decision to submit the paper for publication.

Results

Between April 15, 2020, and April 13, 2021, 220 patients were included in the study, and 5 patients lacking eligibility criteria or withdrawing consent were subsequently excluded. Two hundred and fifteen patients were enrolled in the ITT population: 106 patients allocated to the ULTV arm, and 109 to the LTV arm (Fig. 1). Four patients in the ULTV arm and 3 patients in the LTV arm were lost to follow-up before day-365. One hundred and seventeen patients were alive at day-365, and the mITT population comprised 83 patients who underwent at least one long-term prespecified secondary outcome on day-365 (39/55 patients [71%] in the ULTV arm and 44/62 [71%] in the LTV arm). Patients included in the mITT population differed from those not included regarding baseline characteristics (Additional file 4). Compared with patients included in the mITT population, those not included had a higher BMI and a higher AUC of PaCO2 per day during the first 14 days after inclusion, received a higher cumulative dose of benzodiazepine and were more likely to receive neuromuscular blocking agents during the first 14 days after inclusion, and had a longer hospital stay. Similar imbalances were observed between patients included in and not included in the MoCA 5-minute, SF-36, and IES-R populations, respectively (Additional files 5–7), with the exception of more severe hypoxemia and hypercapnia after inclusion in patients not included in the IES-R population (Additional file 7).

Day-365 mortality

At day-365, 47/102 [46%] and 44/106 [42%] patients had died in the ULTV and in the LTV arms, respectively, and mortality was not significantly different between arms (hazard ratio: 1.19 [CI95%: 0.79–1.80], Additional file 8).

Characteristics of the mITT population

Both arms were well-balanced regarding characteristics at inclusion (Table 1), except that the SAPS2 score was slightly higher in the ULTV arm (41 [32–53] vs. 37 [28–47]). The maximum value of PaCO2 was substantially higher in the ULTV arm (66 [53–82] vs. 50 [44–58] mmHg), and the area under curve (AUC) of PaCO2 standardized per day was also higher in this arm (47 [42–60] vs. 41 [38–47] mmHg).

Functional outcomes at day-365

The MoCA 5-minute score was assessed in 74 patients, including one patient of the LTV arm with one missing item. The MoCA 5-minute score was significantly lower in the ULTV arm (26 [19–28] vs. 27 [25–29], median difference: -2 [CI95%: -4–0], p < 0.05) (Fig. 2). This 2-point median difference is the minimal value defined as clinically relevant [31, 32], and was related to a significant decrease in the memory-learning component (4 [3–4] vs. 5 [3–5], median difference: -1 [CI95%: -1–0], p < 0.05, Additional file 9). Cognitive impairment was present in 42/73 patients [58%] and was not significantly different between arms (21/33 patients [64%] in the ULTV arm, and 21/40 patients [53%] in the LTV arm (absolute risk difference: 11 [CI95%: -11–34]%, p = 0.34).

IES-R denotes Impact of Event Scale-Revised score; LTV; low tidal volume ventilation; MoCA 5-minute, French version of the Montreal Cognitive Assessment 5-minute score; SF-36 Short Form-36 Health Survey score; and ULTV ultra-low tidal volume ventilation. The MoCA 5-minute score is a validated telephone version of the Montreal Cognitive Assessment score. It comprises MoCA items not requiring pencil and paper or visual stimulus and consists of 4 subtests including memory-learning (ranging 0 to 5), language (ranging 0 to 9), orientation (ranging 0 to 6), and memory (ranging 0 to 10). Total scores of the MoCA 5-minute protocol range between 0 and 30. Scores of 28 or higher are deemed normal with the French version [18]. The IES-R is a 22-items score that assesses subjective distress caused by traumatic events. Each item is rated from 0 to 4, and the total score ranges from 0 to 88. The IES-R score also comprises three subscale scores evaluating intrusion, avoidance, and hyperarousal. Post traumatic stress disorder is deemed present if the IES-R score is greater than 32. The physical and mental summary components of the SF-36 score are standardized to a mean of 50 and a standard deviation of 10, with summary components scores above 50 representing better than average and below 50 poorer than average function

The SF-36 score was assessed in 81 patients, including two patients of the ULTV arm with missing domains of the score. There was no significant difference between groups regarding the 9 domains of the SF-36 score (Additional file 9 and 10), although domain scores in the ULTV arm were all virtually numerically lower. Both PCS and MCS were not significantly different between groups (Fig. 2). The rate of patients with MCS below 40 was not statistically different between groups (18/36 [50%] in the ULTV arm vs. 14/43 [33%] in the LTV arm, absolute risk difference: 17 [CI95%: -4–39], p = 0.11).

The IES-R score was assessed on 75 patients for at least one item, and 59 patients with no missing items. It was not statistically different between groups (19 [9–35] in the ULTV arm vs. 15 [8–36] in the LTV arm, median difference 4 [CI95%: -4–12], p = 0.34, Fig. 2, Additional file 9). PTSD was present in 7/26 patients [27%] in the ULTV arm, as compared to 9/33 patients [27%] in the LTV arm, absolute risk difference: 0 [CI95%: -23–23]%, p = 0.98).

A sensitivity analysis was performed, with imputation of missing IES-R items and 71 patients could be compared (31 in the ULTV arm and 40 in the LTV arm). The IES-R score remained not statistically different between groups (19 [9–36] in the ULTV arm vs. 12 [6–34] in the LTV arm, median difference 5 [CI95%: -2–12], p = 0.17). PTSD was present in 9/31 patients [30%] in the ULTV arm, as compared to 11/40 patients [28%] in the LTV arm (p = 0.80, Additional file 11).

Post-hoc univariable and multivariable analyses

The only variables associated with the MoCA 5-minute score in univariable analysis were the maximal PaCO2 (R2 = 0.12) and AUC of PaCO2 standardized per day (R2 = 0.14) with a negative linear coefficient indicating lower MoCA 5-minute scores for higher values (Table 2). In multivariable analyses, those two variables remained independently associated with the MoCA 5-minute score, after adjustment on age, ventilatory ratio [33] at inclusion, and SAPS 2 (Table 2; Fig. 3) or SOFA scores at inclusion (Additional file 12, Fig. 3).

Cognitive impairment at day-365 was significantly associated with maximal PaCO2 and SOFA score at inclusion in univariable analyses (Additional file 16). Sensitivity analyses

The first set of sensitivity analyses was performed in the per-protocol population, which comprised 65 patients (24 in ULTV arm and 41 in the LTV arm). The MoCA 5-minute score was significantly lower in the ULTV arm (26 [19–28] vs. 28 [25–29], p < 0.05), with a median difference amounting to -2 [CI95%: -6–0]. There was no difference between the ULTV and the LTV arm regarding both MCS (36 [28–43] vs. 41 [29–50], p = 0.09) and PCS (41 [31–51] vs. 50 [39–55], p = 0.07) total SF-36 scores, nor in the IES-R score (16 [15–27] vs. 12 [6–26], p = 0.27) in the per-protocol population.

The second set of sensitivity analyses performed in the 215 patients of the ITT population with missing values treated either as worst-case values or as best-case values did not find any statistically significant difference in any score between groups, but maximal PaCO2 during the first 14 days after inclusion remained an independent predictor of the MoCA 5-minute score on day-365 only in both worst-case and best-case scenarios (Additional file 17).

The third set of sensitivity analyses was performed on all patients alive at day-365, including the 34 patients who did not undergo all functional tests on day-365 (Additional file 18), treated either as worst-case or best-case scenario. These analyses did not find any statistically significant difference in any score between groups, but maximal PaCO2 during the first 14 days after inclusion remained an independent predictor of the MoCA 5-minute score on day-365 only in the worst-case scenario (Additional file 18).

In the fourth set of sensitivity analyses performed on all patients alive at day-365 under varying MNAR assumptions, multiple imputation of missing MoCA 5-minute data at day-365 yielded median differences between the ULTV and LTV arms ranging from − 2.18 [CI95%: -4.84–0.47] to -1.27 [CI95%: -3.44–0.9] (Additional file 19). Maximal PaCO2 during the first 14 days after inclusion remained an independent predictor of the MoCA 5-minute score at day-365 in virtually all MNAR scenarios (Additional file 20).

Discussion

In this trial comparing ULTV with LTV in patients with moderate-to-severe COVID-19-related ARDS, there was no significant difference in mortality at day-365. However, the MoCA 5-minute score of the survivors at day-365 was significantly lower in the ULTV arm. Finally, PaCO2 (either maximal or AUC standardized per day), was the sole variable significantly associated with the variability and decrease in MoCA 5-minute score at day-365.

Prevalence of long-term functional outcome

The prevalence of cognitive impairment after ARDS amounted to 36% [CI95%: 26–46] in a recent meta-analysis [34], with a high heterogeneity between studies likely stemming from differences in study designs, cognitive assessment methods, follow-up durations, and patient characteristics. Of note, the rate of cognitive impairment was similar between COVID-19 and non-COVID-19 ARDS [34]. In the present study, the rate of cognitive impairment was substantially higher in both arms than in this meta-analysis, which may be related to greater ARDS severity (see below). Regarding HRQL, the median value of the PCS of the SF-36 score was similar in the present study and in a recent meta-analysis on 2286 patients one year after ARDS (i.e. 40 [CI95%: 38–43]) [35]. Regarding the MCS in our study, it was also in the range of the value identified in this meta-analysis (i.e., 44 [CI95%: 42–47] [35]). Of note, no difference was found in this meta-analysis between COVID and non-COVID-19 ARDS. Finally, the rate of PTSD in our study was in the upper range of the value computed at one year from ARDS diagnosis in a meta-analysis on 2298 patients (i.e., 22 [CI95%: 18–27]) [35]. Heterogeneity in the scale used to assess PTSD in this meta-analysis (namely IED-6, IES-R, and PCL-5), and in the diagnostic cut-off of a given scale may explain this finding.

Risk factors for impairment in long-term functional outcomes

In our study of mechanically ventilated patients with moderate to severe ARDS, PaCO2 was the only tested variable associated with cognitive impairment. A longer duration of delirium [36], hypoxemia and conservative fluid management during ICU stay [37] are known independent risk factors of long-term cognitive impairment after ARDS, while hypercapnia was either not tested [36] or not identified as a risk factor [37]. The lack of association between cognitive status and PaCO2 in Mikkelsen et al. study [37] may be explained by a normal PaCO2 mean value during ARDS, suggesting that few patients were severely hypercapnic. We did not observe a significant relationship between PaO2/FiO2 nor PaO2 and cognitive impairment in our study, and this may be related to the severity of patients who were all severely hypoxemic by design, or to a lack of power.

On the other hand, the long-term analysis of the REST trial [38], which compared ULTV with ECCO2R to LTV in patients with acute hypoxemic respiratory failure, did not demonstrate any significant difference between groups in terms of cognitive dysfunction (nor in HRQL and PTSD). Several factors could explain this apparent discrepancy with the VT4COVID trial. First, in the REST trial, the ULTV group had only slightly higher PaCO2 levels than the LTV group during the first week after inclusion (by 3 to 6 mmHg) [7], whereas the between-groups difference was substantially greater (around 10 mmHg) in the VT4COVID trial [9]. Second, while mean PaCO2 levels in the control group of the VT4COVID trial remained close to normal after inclusion (i.e., 46 ± 1 mmHg) [9], patients in the control group of the REST trial were also exposed to high PaCO2 (i.e., mean daily values from 49 to 56 mmHg) [7]. This relatively high level of hypercapnia in both groups may have limited the ability to detect an effect of PaCO2 on cognitive outcomes in the REST trial. Likewise, in a long-term analysis of the ARMA trial, there was no difference in HRQL between groups [39] (cognitive status was not assessed in this study), while PaCO2 was significantly higher in the low tidal volume arm of the study from day 1 to 7 (although remaining within the normal range).

The mechanisms by which severe hypercapnia adversely impacted cognitive status in our study remain unclear. In healthy volunteers, mild acute hypercapnia (i.e., 48 mmHg) increases cerebral blood flow and cerebral blood volume by approximately 30% and 13%, respectively [10], while maximal PaCO2 levels were considerably higher in our study (i.e., 66 [53–82] mmHg), suggesting greater impact on cerebral hemodynamics. It is however unclear from our data whether acute PaCO2 increase at inclusion, or acute decrease after the deep sedation weaning trial were related to cognitive impairment. As hypoxia also increases both cerebral blood flow and cerebral blood volume [10], both phenomena could have acted synergistically in our study, although we were unable to identify any impact of minimal PaO2 during ARDS management on the MoCA-5 min score.

Study limits and strengths

Our study has several limitations. First, it was underpowered to identify variables with a modest impact on functional outcomes, and several potentially important variables were not assessed. Notably, the lack of ICU delirium assessment is a major limitation when evaluating the impact of the ULTV strategy on long-term cognitive function. Second, the competing risk of death was not accounted for in the analysis of functional outcomes, which may have led to an overestimation of favorable outcomes. However, the absence of a significant difference in mortality between groups suggests that this should not have substantially influenced the results. Third, death and missingness were not accounted for in the complete-case primary analysis. The potential MNAR mechanism was addressed post hoc in a sensitivity analysis performed in the ITT population, which confirmed that maximal PaCO2 during the first 14 days after inclusion was independently associated with the MoCA 5-minute score. Fourth, attrition was substantial as only 71% of the patients alive at day-365 could be assessed, and this could have biased the results as the assumption of randomized balance between groups may no longer hold. Although the attrition rate was similar between study arms, baseline characteristics differed between patients with and without missing score values, suggesting that missing data did not follow an MCAR pattern. Indeed, missingness was probably related to the unobserved score itself as patients with severely impaired cognitive function were more likely to be unable to perform any functional test, suggesting an MNAR pattern. Under this assumption, the significant between-group difference observed in the complete-case analysis of MoCA 5-minute scores may have been biased. However, our sensitivity analysis under various MNAR scenarios showed that the median between-arm differences in MoCA 5-minute score remained within a similar range. Furthermore, maximal PaCO2 during the first 14 days after inclusion remained independently associated with the MoCA 5-minute score in virtually all scenarios, reinforcing its potential key association with long-term cognitive status. Fifth, the relationship between PaCO2 and cognitive status does not imply causality and may be related to residual confounding. Among other causes, heterogeneity in sedation management could have impacted cognitive status, although sedation drugs and doses were similar between arms in the mITT population during the period of deep sedation (Table 1). Sixth, all analyses of this follow-up study, although most were prespecified, were performed on secondary outcomes of the VT4COVID study and should be considered as exploratory. Finally, the study was performed in patients with COVID-19 ARDS, and extrapolation of its results to non-COVID-19 ARDS should be made with caution.

Nevertheless, our study has the following strengths. First, data were collected during a randomized controlled trial, in which all ventilatory co-interventions were protocolized, which should limit bias. Second, to our knowledge, this is the first study to assess the long-term impact of ULTV and hypercapnia on functional outcomes.

Implication for clinical practice

As ULTV was not associated with any improvement in short-term clinical outcome in our study [9], this strongly pleads against the systematic use of ULTV in ARDS. Furthermore, clinicians should be aware of the potential deleterious impact of acute hypercapnia on long-term cognitive function during ARDS management, even under LTV. We provide here an estimate of the threshold of PaCO2 over which the rate of cognitive dysfunction increases significantly, which may be used to adjust mechanical ventilation and/or trigger indication for ECCO2R or extracorporeal membrane oxygenation. It should however be stressed that patients with chronic lung disease and most at risk for chronic hypercarbia were excluded from the present study, and hence the impact of acute on chronic hypercarbia on long-term cognitive function remains to date unknown.

Conclusions

Ultra-low tidal volume ventilation without extracorporeal respiratory support compared to low tidal volume ventilation has no detectable impact on mortality at day-365 in COVID-19 ARDS patients but is associated with a small but significant decrease in cognitive status possibly related to increased PaCO2.
