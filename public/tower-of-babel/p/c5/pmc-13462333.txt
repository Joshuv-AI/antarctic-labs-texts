SRM-CSR: unsupervised aspect category detection based on semantic-aware relevance modeling and contextual sentence representation
Authors: Yao Xu, Xian Mu, Ketong Liu, Dagang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnsupervised aspect category detection aims to identify the underlying aspect categories discussed in a given sentence without any annotated labels. Recent studies typically generate pseudo-labels from review corpora and subsequently train models in a supervised manner. However, during pseudo-label generation, existing methods either fail to capture aspect discriminability within sentences or rely solely on pre-trained models derived from general corpora, which can mislead the training process and degrade model performance. To mitigate the limitations, we propose a novel framework (SRM-CSR) that integrates lexical-level aspect-relevant information and sentence-level contextual representations for generating high-quality pseudo labels. Specifically, in the lexical-level stage, we extract Aspect-Relevant Terms (ARTs) based on two properties: (1) domain specificity, measured by word frequency divergence between the review corpus and a general corpus; and (2) semantic stability, reflected by semantic consistency across different sentences. We further introduce an entropy-driven discriminability mechanism that can assign higher weights to aspect terms within sentences based on their similarity distributions with the extracted ARTs. In the sentence-level stage, we leverage Sentence-BERT to encode sentences into contextual representations and compute the similarity between seed sentences and sentences from the review corpus. Pseudo-labels are generated independently in both stages. We construct the training set based on the consistent results produced by the two stages, which is subsequently used to train a neural classifier based on a post-trained Domain Knowledge BERT. Extensive experiments on three real-world datasets demonstrate the effectiveness of the pseudo-labeling strategy in SRM-CSR, with the proposed method achieving an average improvement of 2.9 percentage points in macro-F1 over the strongest baselines.Keywords: Aspect category detection, Aspect-relevant terms, Contextual sentence representation, Pseudo-labelingSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Aspect category detection (ACD), a fundamental task in Aspect-Based Sentiment Analysis (ABSA), aims to identify the aspect categories discussed in a given sentence, where the categories belong to a predefined category set1,2. Reviews often contain explicit or implicit expressions related to aspect categories3. As illustrated in Figure 1, the Citysearch dataset4 includes multiple aspect categories (e.g., Food, Staff, Ambience). Some reviews mention the aspect categories explicitly, while others refer to them implicitly through semantically related terms. For instance, “quiet” in the sentence “The place was quiet and delightful” indicates the Ambience category. ACD is essential, as it provides the basis for subsequent sentiment analysis5.

Examples of review sentences containing aspect terms and their corresponding aspect categories. The aspect terms are highlighted in red.

ACD approaches are generally divided into supervised and unsupervised methods6. Supervised methods, such as LICD7 and GML8, have achieved promising results, but they are less effective in settings with limited annotated data, such as recommendation systems (e.g., Amazon Reviews9 ). This limitation has motivated increasing interest in unsupervised approaches.

Unsupervised aspect category detection (UACD) has been widely studied, with early approaches primarily relying on probabilistic topic models such as Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA)10–12. However, LDA assumes that words are independent given a latent topic, ignoring contextual dependencies. This weakens the semantic relatedness among words within the same topic, thereby reducing topic coherence and impairing the accuracy of aspect category detection. Subsequently, some clustering-based methods13–15 are proposed. These methods leverage word embeddings16 as semantic representations. They typically employ an autoencoder framework to maximize the similarity between sentence representations derived from word embeddings and reconstructed representations based on cluster centroid. However, these methods perform clustering over the entire embedding space and are therefore susceptible to noise introduced by aspect-irrelevant words.

In recent years, pseudo-labeling-based methods have demonstrated strong performance in UACD and emerged as the state-of-the-art techniques17–20. These methods generate pseudo-labels for sentences from the review corpus, and subsequently feed the pseudo-labeled sentences into pre-trained language models (e.g., BERT21, RoBERTa22) to train neural networks for aspect category detection. However, pseudo-labels are often noisy, and directly using them for subsequent training may substantially impair model performance. For example, CASC19 regards the nouns associated with adjectives in a sentence as potential aspect terms. However, some nouns refer to general concepts and do not correspond to any specific aspect category. In ASeM18, sentences are represented by summing word embeddings, and the resulting representations are used to measure similarity to seed words. However, aspect-discriminative words within a sentence should be assigned greater importance. In PCCT17, the masked language modeling mechanism of BERT is utilized to enrich the vocabulary of each aspect category, while SB-ASC20 adopts Sentence-BERT23 to measure similarities between unlabeled sentences and seed sentences. However, as pre-trained models are typically trained on large-scale general corpora, relying solely on them is insufficient to capture aspect-relevant information specific to the target corpus.

To tackle these issues, we propose a novel unsupervised method, SRM-CSR (Semantic-Aware Relevance Modeling and Contextual Sentence Representation), for aspect category detection. In the lexical-level stage, we first extract Aspect-Relevant Terms (ARTs) from the review corpus to mitigate the interference from aspect-irrelevant words. ART is defined as a word that is relevant to a particular aspect category within a corpus. Typically, ARTs are specific to a particular domain and frequently occur in semantically consistent contexts. Therefore, we integrate both properties to evaluate aspect relevance. Domain specificity is measured by the divergence in word frequency between the review corpus and a general corpus, while semantic stability is reflected in semantic consistency across different sentences and quantified by the norm of the corresponding word embeddings. Furthermore, we calculate the similarity between sentence words and the extracted ARTs. Based on the similarity distributions, we introduce an entropy-driven mechanism that assigns higher weights to aspect terms according to their discriminability quantified by information entropy. Pseudo-labels in this stage are generated based on the similarity between the weighted aggregation of word embeddings in a sentence and the embeddings of predefined seed words for aspect categories. In the sentence-level stage, following the approach in20, we employ Sentence-BERT to derive contextual sentence representations and compute similarities between seed sentences and unlabeled sentences from the review corpus. The aspect category of the most similar seed sentence is selected as the pseudo-label for the target sentence. We construct the training set based on the consistent results produced by the two stages, which jointly leverage aspect-relevant information from ARTs and the contextual meaning of sentences captured by Sentence-BERT. Finally, the pseudo-labeled sentences are fed into a post-trained Domain Knowledge BERT24 to train a neural classifier for aspect category detection. Related works

Unsupervised Aspect Category Detection (UACD) has been studied extensively over the past decades. Early rule-based methods25,26 rely on part-of-speech patterns and dependency relations, but are limited by predefined rules and domain knowledge. Subsequently, a series of methods adopt probabilistic topic models, particularly variants of Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA), for aspect category detection. SAS27 integrates seed-based topic modeling and maximum-entropy priors to guide aspect and sentiment extraction. MaxEnt-LDA28 jointly identifies aspect and opinion words by integrating a discriminative maximum-entropy classifier into a generative LDA framework. AKL11 proposes a two-stage method that first mines cross-domain aspect knowledge using LDA and then incorporates it during inference through a noise-tolerant mechanism. W2VLDA12 integrates WordNet with LDA to guide topic generation based on synonym similarity. However, these LDA-based methods exhibit limited ability to maintain topic coherence, as they ignore contextual dependencies and assume the independence of words given latent topics.

With the advancement of deep learning, some approaches employ an autoencoder framework to maximize the similarity between sentence representation derived from word embeddings and reconstructed representation based on cluster centroids. These methods adopt similar frameworks while employing different strategies for generating sentence representations. For example, ABAE13 derives sentence representations through an attention mechanism, where attention weights are computed by combining each word embedding with the global context representation. AE-CSA14 first generates an initial sentence representation as in13. It then employs an RNN to model sequential dependencies and concatenates the hidden vector with the initial representation to produce the final sentence representation via MLP. CMAM15 applies depthwise convolutions with multiple channels to capture aspect-specific representations, which are then aggregated across channels to generate the final sentence representation. However, the performance of these methods tends to degrade when clustering coherence is impaired by noise. Moreover, the centroids obtained by averaging high-dimensional word embeddings may obscure semantic information29. The lexical-level stage of our method is also based on word embeddings, but it mitigates the influence of aspect-irrelevant words by extracting ARTs. In addition, it identifies aspect terms through an entropy-driven mechanism rather than clustering, thereby further avoiding semantic information loss.

There are also several approaches based on seed words, which focus on learning a shared embedding space for sentences and seed words, enabling the measurement of semantic similarity between sentences and aspect categories. JASen30 learns joint sentiment–aspect embeddings guided by seed words in the word embedding space and employs a CNN classifier to generalize discriminative word-level information via self-training on unlabeled data. UCE31 learns a shared embedding space for segments and aspects by approximating seed-word-based similarity using an uncertainty-aware loss, thereby enhancing robustness to noisy supervision. However, relying solely on seed words to represent aspects while ignoring contextual representations limits the effectiveness of these methods.

In recent years, with the power of pre-trained language models, pseudo-labeling-based methods have demonstrated strong performance and become the state-of-the-art techniques. These methods typically use pseudo-labeled sentences to train neural networks based on pre-trained language models for aspect category detection. CASC19 employs a pre-trained BERT with masked language modeling to expand aspect vocabularies. Pseudo-labels are assigned by computing normalized overlap scores between MLM-generated contextual candidates and the corresponding vocabularies. PCCT17 expands each aspect name into a category-specific vocabulary using a pre-trained language model with masked prompts. A sentence is assigned a pseudo-label by matching the top predictions at the masked position against the aspect vocabularies. ASeM18 introduces a seed-word enhancement strategy that expands the initial seed set by extracting informative keywords from sentences with uncertain predictions. Each sentence is then assigned a pseudo-label according to its similarity to aspect representations. SB-ASC20 leverages Sentence-BERT to obtain contextual sentence representations and assigns pseudo-labels based on the similarity between unlabeled sentences and seed sentences. However, during pseudo-label generation, these methods either ignore differences in the aspect-discriminative ability of individual words or rely solely on pre-trained language models with seed words or sentences. As a result, the generated pseudo-labels tend to be noisy, thereby adversely affecting subsequent model training. Aspect-relevant terms extraction

Typically, each review corpus contains domain-specific terms. These terms tend to be frequent in one domain but uncommon in others. For instance, in restaurant reviews, words like pizza, soup, and waiter are common, whereas in movie reviews, words such as performance, acting, and plot are more prevalent. Such domain-specific terms are crucial because they provide important cues for identifying the underlying aspect categories discussed in reviews. For example, pizza indicates the Food category, while performance indicates the Actor category.

Entropy-driven discriminability mechanism

Different words in a sentence contribute unequally to aspect category detection. Since each extracted ART typically corresponds to a specific aspect category, aspect terms in a sentence can be identified by measuring the similarity between sentence words and the ARTs. If a word is highly similar to the ARTs of one aspect category but dissimilar to those of other categories, it should be assigned a higher weight due to its strong discriminative power. Conversely, if a word is similar to many ARTs across different aspect categories (e.g., restaurant) or to very few ARTs (e.g., everyone), it contributes little to distinguishing aspect categories and should receive a lower weight.

Contextual sentence representation

Sentence-BERT (SBERT)23 is a framework designed specifically for sentence-level encoding and similarity computation. Implementation details

The proposed SRM-CSR is implemented in Python. The optimal hyperparameters are selected on the validation set taken from the SemEval43,44 training set of the corresponding domain.

In the lexical-level stage, we preprocess the review texts by tokenizing each sentence using the tokenizer provided by CountVectorizer47 and filtering the resulting tokens with the NLTK English stopword list48 via list comprehension. The remaining tokens are then lemmatized using WordNetLemmatizer49 and used as ART candidates. We train word embeddings using the Word2Vec implementation in the Gensim library50, with a vector dimension of 200, a context window size of 5, the Skip-gram architecture, and negative sampling with 5 negative samples. We compute the divergence between word frequency distributions of the review corpus and those of the BNC (British National Corpus)51, which serves as the general corpus. To handle zero-frequency words in the BNC, we apply Laplace smoothing when estimating the probability distribution for KL divergence computation. In this stage, the hyperparameter is the proportion () of top-ranked ART candidates. Since different aspect categories may exhibit different distributions in the review corpus, a small may provide insufficient coverage for some categories, whereas a large may introduce terms with weak aspect relevance, thereby reducing the quality of the generated pseudo-labels.

In the sentence-level stage, we use the all-mpnet-base-v252 SBERT model to compute similarities between review sentences and seed sentences. The hyperparameters in this stage include the confidence thresholds for aspect category and sentiment polarity (i.e., and , respectively). The thresholds play an important role in balancing sample quality and coverage. Specifically, overly low thresholds may retain too many low-quality samples and introduce noise, whereas excessively high thresholds may filter out too many samples and result in insufficient coverage.

During model training, we use post-trained Domain Knowledge BERT24 models, specifically BERT-DK_rest53 for the Restaurant and Citysearch datasets, and BERT-DK_laptop54 for the Laptop dataset. The hyperparameters of the neural classifier include the batch size, learning rate, and focal loss parameters (i.e., and ).

We adopt a staged hyperparameter tuning strategy as follows: (1) We first tune the ART selection ratio () from 10% to 15% with a step size of 1%, while fixing the hyperparameters of the sentence-level stage and model training to the settings reported in20. (2) With the optimal , we tune the hyperparameters in the sentence-level stage using the TPE hyperparameter optimization algorithm55, where the confidence thresholds are tuned within the range from 0.4 to 0.75, while still keeping the model-training hyperparameters fixed to the settings reported in20. Performance analysis

According to the comparison results, ABAE and CAt show the weakest performance. This is because they rely solely on static word embeddings, which fail to capture contextual semantics and limit the ability of these methods to distinguish aspect categories across different contexts. SSCL consistently outperforms ABAE and CAt mainly because it leverages BERT-based knowledge distillation, which enables effective learning from both soft and hard pseudo-labels under noisy supervision. By distilling rich contextual representations from a teacher model, SSCL captures fine-grained aspect semantics that static embedding–based methods fail to model. CASC, JASen, and UCE all rely on seed words to guide model learning toward meaningful aspect semantics, thereby improving aspect detection. They achieve substantially better performance than ABAE and CAt. JASen uses seed words to initialize aspect semantic and transfer seed-based knowledge to unlabeled data via self-training, enabling robust aspect category detection. UCE explicitly leverages seed words to define aspect representations and induce segment–aspect similarity signals, which can guide the model toward learning more discriminative aspect semantics. CASC leverages seed words and a pre-trained BERT with masked language modeling to expand aspect vocabularies, which are used with POS tags to generate pseudo-labels for training a noise-robust neural network. SB-ASC outperforms other methods by leveraging SBERT to encode contextual sentence representations and further refining aspect category detection with a BERT-based model trained on pseudo-labels induced from sentence-level similarity. Our method, SRM-CSR, consistently outperforms SB-ASC on both the Restaurant and Laptop datasets in terms of macro-F1. Moreover, it achieves better performance than the strongest baselines in most aspect categories in terms of category-wise F1. This is because SRM-CSR not only leverages contextual representations to capture overall sentence semantics, but also incorporates Aspect-Relevant Terms that jointly encode domain specificity and semantic stability. As a result, it alleviates the interference of aspect-irrelevant words and improves pseudo-label quality.

Complexity analysis

The computational cost of pseudo-label generation in SRM-CSR mainly comes from the lexical-level and sentence-level stages. In the lexical-level stage, for a corpus of P sentences with an average sentence length of M, N extracted ARTs, and I seed words, the complexity is O(P(MN+I)), where O(PMN) is required for computing similarities between sentence words and ARTs, and O(PI) is required for matching sentence representations with seed-word embeddings. In the sentence-level stage, matching P review sentences with L seed sentences requires O(PL) similarity computations. Since these operations mainly involve similarity computations, the overall computational cost remains manageable in practice. The subsequent training of the neural classifier based on Domain Knowledge BERT follows the standard fine-tuning process and computational cost of Transformer-based models.

Ablation study

To validate the contribution of each component in SRM-CSR, we design three ablation variants: (1) removing the lexical-level pseudo-labeling stage and relying solely on the SBERT-based approach to generate pseudo-labels (denoted as “w/o PLLS”); (2) extracting ARTs solely based on domain specificity, without incorporating semantic stability (denoted as “w/o Sta”); and (3) extracting ARTs solely based on semantic stability, without incorporating domain specificity (denoted as “w/o Spe”). We compare the performance of SRM-CSR and its ablation variants on the Restaurant and Laptop datasets in terms of macro-F1. The results are presented in Figure 11.

Performance comparison of SRM-CSR and its ablation variants in terms of Macro-F1 on the Restaurant and Laptop datasets.

The experimental results reveal the following findings: (1) SRM-CSR consistently outperforms all the variants. (2) Removing the lexical-level pseudo-labeling process leads to a performance decline. This is mainly because SBERT is pre-trained on general corpora and does not incorporate aspect-relevant information. As a result, the quality of the generated pseudo-labeled training data is substantially degraded in domain-specific settings. (3) The inferior performance of the other two variants is mainly due to their reliance on a single property during ART extraction. Taking the Restaurant dataset as an example, focusing solely on domain specificity tends to overlook words that are highly related to specific aspects but exhibit lower domain distinctiveness relative to the general corpus (e.g., beef, chicken). On the other hand, relying solely on semantic stability tends to emphasize general words with stable semantics but weak aspect relevance (e.g., review, tip). As a result, aspect-irrelevant words are introduced into ARTs, degrading the quality of pseudo-labels and impairing model performance.

Analysis of word embedding

In the Methodology section, we discussed the importance of word embeddings in terms of semantic stability. Here, taking the Citysearch dataset as an example, we conduct a quantitative analysis of the correlation between word embedding norms and word frequency. As illustrated in Fig. 12, logarithmic scaling and bin-wise mean aggregation are used to better reveal the underlying trend.

Correlation between word frequency and word embedding norm on the Citysearch dataset. Word frequency is shown on a logarithmic scale, and blue points denote bin-wise means.

As shown in the figure, we make the following observations: (1) Low-frequency words generally exhibit small vector norms because they receive limited updates during training. (2) High-frequency words are more likely to be used as negative samples in the Skip-gram model, leading to unstable updates and relatively small vector norms. (3) Medium-frequency words that occur in semantically consistent contexts tend to receive stable and sufficient updates, resulting in relatively large vector norms.

Intuitively, words with different frequency levels exhibit distinct roles within a domain-specific corpus. Low-frequency words (e.g., seeker) rarely occur in the reviews and are often unrelated to any aspect category. High-frequency words (e.g., restaurant) tend to represent general concepts and occur in a large number of reviews, thereby contributing little to distinguishing specific aspect categories. By contrast, medium-frequency words (e.g., dessert) are often used in the domain to express aspect-related concepts and more likely to be relevant to particular aspect categories.

Therefore, since the vector norms of word embeddings not only reflect semantic stability but also convey word frequency information, it is meaningful to incorporate them into ART extraction.

This section further demonstrates the effectiveness of ART extraction in mitigating the influence of aspect-irrelevant words. As discussed earlier, some methods rely on all nouns in a sentence or high-frequency nouns from the corpus during pseudo-label generation. Contribution and discussion

In this section, we summarize the contributions and limitations of this work and outline directions for future research.

Contributions

In this paper, we propose SRM-CSR, a novel unsupervised approach for aspect category detection that generates high-quality pseudo-labels by jointly leveraging lexical-level aspect information and sentence-level contextual representations. In the lexical-level stage, we mitigate interference from aspect-irrelevant words by extracting Aspect-Relevant Terms based on domain specificity and semantic stability. Moreover, we introduce an entropy-driven mechanism to highlight aspect-discriminative words within sentences. In the sentence-level stage, we employ Sentence-BERT to generate contextual sentence representations and compute similarities between seed sentences and sentences from the review corpus. Pseudo-labeled training data are constructed through cross-stage consistency, resulting in high-quality training samples that integrate domain-specific aspect information with the contextual semantics of sentences. Extensive experiments on real-world datasets verify the effectiveness of the pseudo-labels generated by SRM-CSR, and demonstrate that the proposed method consistently outperforms competitive baselines.

Discussion

Despite its effectiveness, SRM-CSR has some limitations. First, the proposed method applies the same threshold to all aspect categories in the sentence-level stage. Since different categories often exhibit different distributions in the review corpus and may differ in learning difficulty, applying the same threshold to all categories in combination with consistency-based integration may not be optimal for pseudo-label selection. Second, since BNC is used as the general corpus, the current framework is primarily designed for English. In addition, limited domain data in low-resource settings may reduce the reliability of distribution estimation. Finally, the current experiments are mainly conducted on review-oriented datasets. Although these datasets cover different domains, the general applicability of the proposed method to other types of text corpora remains to be further verified.

In future work, we will explore category-specific and dynamically adaptive thresholds to improve pseudo-label quality and training effectiveness for each category. We will also investigate more robust strategies for data-scarce settings and extend the framework to multilingual scenarios by incorporating suitable general corpora in the target languages. In addition, we will further evaluate the proposed method on other types of text data, such as social media texts and forum posts, to examine its broader applicability.
