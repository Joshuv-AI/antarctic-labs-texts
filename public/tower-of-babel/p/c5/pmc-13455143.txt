Clinic practitioner and support staff perspectives on implementation strategies to increase patient referrals to diabetes self-management education and support services
Authors: Aaron J. Kruse-Diehr, Borsika A. Rabin, Jessica Elliott, Vance Drakeford, Laura Wright, Brent McKune, Russell E. Glasgow, Key C. Douthitt, James W. Keck, Mary E. Lacy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe prevalence of diabetes in Appalachian Kentucky is among the highest in the United States. Diabetes self-management education and support (DSMES) is a cost-effective, evidence-based intervention that improves patient outcomes yet is underutilized due to multilevel barriers. Throughout 2023–2024, we implemented a 9-month learning collaborative with two regional healthcare systems to increase DSMES referrals and documented their clinical strategies. At study conclusion, we assessed clinical perceptions of each strategy’s feasibility, acceptability, appropriateness, as well as likelihood of sustainment of the clinical DSMES referral program.MethodsAfter 6 months of participation and at study conclusion, participants (N = 13) described timing and intended outcomes for activities, which were then mapped to relevant Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) domains. Intended outcomes were coded to Practical, Robust Implementation and Sustainability Model (PRISM) contextual factors and implementation outcomes, and refinements to implementation strategies were documented iteratively throughout the study. At study end, participants (n = 9) individually assessed each strategy’s acceptability, appropriateness, and feasibility, as well as its potential for sustainment. Assessments used 5-point unidimensional Likert scales that were summed and averaged, with higher numbers indicating greater favorability.ResultsTo help facilitate DSMES referrals, clinic learning collaborative participants most frequently employed implementation strategies within the ERIC stakeholder interrelationshipsand supporting clinicians domains. Specific activities included creating a new electronic health record referral system, utilizing morning huddles to identify eligible patients, and creating patient face sheets for providers to review during visits. Strategies were refined minimally, primarily to increase provider and staff participation and patient and provider acceptability of activities. Across all strategies, mean scores for acceptability, appropriateness, and feasibility were mostly high, as was the overall likelihood of sustainment.ConclusionsThe process of developing and refining implementation strategies to fit context was associated with positive clinical perceptions of acceptability, appropriateness, feasibility, and sustainability. Systematically documenting strategies along with their intended outcome targets throughout an intervention can help contextualize the dynamic nature of implementation and inform future scale-up.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s43058-026-00978-z.

Diabetes is a major cause of morbidity and mortality in the US, affecting over 37 million Americans, or 11.6% of the population [1]. In Kentucky, the prevalence of diabetes has more than doubled over the past 20 years, from 6.5% in 2000 to 14.8% in 2022 [2]. Rural Kentucky faces the highest burden of disease, with 16% of the adult population having been diagnosed with diabetes, making disease management a critical regional priority [3]. Diabetes self-management education and support (DSMES) services are an integral component of disease management. Patient participation in DSMES is cost effective and is associated with improved glycemic control [4, 5], reduced mortality [6], better quality of life [7], and lower costs of care [8, 9]. Nevertheless, despite the overwhelmingly positive evidence base, DSMES remains vastly underused, with some of the lowest rates of engagement found in rural regions of the United States, a trend reflected across Kentucky [10–12].

As of 2019, Kentucky offers 90 nationally recognized and accredited DSMES programs, providing in-person classes in 80 of Kentucky’s 120 counties and virtual access for residents in other counties [13]. The Healthy Living With Diabetes Program though the Kentucky Department of Public Health provides DSMES at no cost to participants throughout the state. While DSMES services nearly tripled in Kentucky since 2012, patient engagement decreased and eventually stagnated throughout this same period (Fig. 1). Although DSMES is covered by most insurance plans, patients need a written referral from the provider who treats their diabetes to participate [14]. Having providers as gatekeepers to the referral process is a major barrier to DSMES [15], especially when providers report a lack of knowledge and awareness of its benefits and value, have insufficient information about availability of local DSMES services, and receive unclear guidance on specific coverage details for DSMES [16]. Given that patient-level barriers to DSMES engagement, such as cost and access, have been reduced throughout the state, it is important to address these provider and clinic-level barriers. Indeed, lack of a provider referral has been identified as a significant barrier to patient uptake of DSMES services [17], and a recent retrospective study found a nationwide DSMES referral rate of only 12% [18]. Since 90% of patients receive diabetes care from their primary care physicians [19], a logical next step is to partner with primary care practices to implement contextually relevant and feasible strategies to promote and support DSMES referrals for patients living with diabetes.Fig. 1DSMES geographic access and utilization in Kentucky, 2012–2019. The Chronic Care Model (CCM) provides a framework for primary care clinics to identify opportunities to improve care for patients living with chronic diseases [20–22]. Within this framework, the Institute for Healthcare Improvement’s Model for Improvement outlines a quality improvement approach using Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) cycles to identify barriers, develop activities to address care gaps, and rapidly measure change [23]. This approach has been used successfully throughout Kentucky via clinic participation in facilitated learning collaboratives, processes through which healthcare organizations come together to learn from one another and from experts in the field. Specifically, they undertake small tests of change by applying and refining implementation strategies to address contextual barriers specific to their clinical practices and ultimately meet diabetes care goals, including reduced patient A1c levels, increased clinician DSMES referrals, and improved patient engagement with DSMES services. Data from previous collaboratives in Kentucky show the utility of the model, with notable improvements across all three targets; clinics participating in these learning collaboratives increased the number of eligible patients referred to, and engaging with, DSMES [3, 24].

To understand strategies used by clinics that may have supported these positive changes, we partnered with two rural healthcare systems in Appalachia Kentucky to implement a 9-month facilitated learning collaborative using a cluster randomized trial design, wherein two clinics from each rural healthcare system were randomized to participate in the intervention (4 total intervention sites) and one clinic per system served as a control site (Fig. 2). While clinical outcome targets remained the same as in previous collaboratives (i.e., DSMES referrals, patient A1c levels, and DSMES attendance), in this iteration, we added control clinics to assess the rigor of our model and applied implementation science frameworks to better understand implementation processes and outcomes. To accomplish the latter goal, we had three objectives: (1) to document strategies used by clinics throughout the collaborative, as well as their intended implementation outcome targets; (2) to assess clinical perceptions of each strategy’s acceptability, appropriateness, and feasibility; and (3) to evaluate the potential for clinics to sustain their respective DSMES programs over time. The long-term goal of this approach is to identify contextually relevant approaches that are likely to be scalable, effective, and sustainable at reducing disparities in diabetes outcomes throughout rural Kentucky and in other health systems with underserved patient populations. Herein, we describe findings from these implementation assessments.Fig. 2Cluster randomized trial design for the 9-month facilitated learning collaborative

Methods

Participants and procedures

Study participants (N = 22) were comprised solely of members of each quality improvement (QI) team. Teams were formed at the healthcare system level and represented members from each system’s two respective clinics (16 from system 1, 6 from system 2). Each of the two QI teams were comprised of clinic practitioners, support staff, managers, and technical assistance (TA) personnel who worked directly with our study team to learn about and develop PDSAs which they then implemented in their clinics. These QI team members were then responsible for diffusing implementation strategies and overall intervention approach to the remainder of their clinic teams. For the facilitated interviews with the QI teams, one occurred after 6 months of the learning collaborative and one at the end.

At two separate timepoints—(1) approximately 6 months after the start of the collaborative and (2) at the end of the learning collaborative—we met separately with each QI team and asked them to describe activities they used during the collaborative to increase DSMES referrals, identify the intended targets of those strategies, and document any ways in which strategies were refined, if applicable, using facilitation guides (see Supplementary Material). At the 6-month timepoint, 11 members of the two QI teams (health system 1 n = 7, health system 2 n = 4; 50%) participated in data collection, and 9 members (health system 1 n = 4, health system 2 n = 5; 41%) participated at the end of the collaborative. These individuals held a wide variety of mostly leadership team roles, including performance improvement coordinators, senior leaders, office managers, practice managers, and administrators, but also represented patient-facing roles, such as DSMES educators and dietitians. Data were collected using Qualtrics, and conversations were facilitated by members of the research team in a Zoom-based group format using breakout rooms for each respective QI team during two regularly scheduled collaborative learning session meetings; this data collection process took approximately a half hour of the 4 total hours in each learning session meeting. These discussions were consensus-based; for example, participants iteratively discussed intended implementation outcome targets in real time during these meetings, a process that ultimately resulted in agreement among QI team participants. Finally, upon study conclusion, all participants were invited to complete individual summative surveys via Qualtrics, one of which assessed the acceptability, appropriateness, and feasibility of each defined strategy and the other of which explored their perceived potential for sustained success of their overall clinic DSMES referral program at meeting RE-AIM implementation outcome targets. A total of 9 participants (41%) completed these surveys. All methods and materials were approved by the University of Kentucky Institutional Review Board (protocol #75928) prior to engaging in any study activities, and participants provided oral consent to participate in all study activities and data collection procedures.

Measures

The Practical, Robust Implementation and Sustainability Model (PRISM) [25] is a broadly utilized implementation science framework that includes a set of contextual domains to assess multilevel perspectives about the context of implementation (i.e., recipient perspectives of the intervention, recipient characteristics, implementation and sustainability infrastructure, and external environment) that can then be used to guide the selection of implementation strategies and, ultimately, their impacts on RE-AIM implementation outcomes (i.e., reach, effectiveness, adoption, implementation, and maintenance) [25–27]. Questions that asked participants about their QI teams’ respective implementation strategies (and refinements to those strategies, when applicable) and their intended RE-AIM implementation outcomes were adapted from methods described in Hall et al. [28] and McCarthy et al. [29], as well as questions from the PRISM interview guide [30, 31] and iterative PRISM (iPRISM) webtool assessment. [32] The Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) [33] compilation of implementation strategies to map clinic activities to a standardized set of implementation strategies categorized within one of nine discreet conceptual domains [34]. Finally, acceptability, appropriateness, and feasibility were assessed via a validated instrument (12 total questions, 4 per domain) developed by Weiner et al. [35], and sustainability questions (17 total) were refined for the context of this study from an instrument described in Rabin et al. [36]

Data analysis

Implementation strategies developed by QI team participants in their PDSA cycles were mapped to relevant ERIC strategies and conceptual domains by two members of the research team (AK-D and BR) and presented descriptively (e.g., what, when, where, why, how), with minimal coding discrepancies resolved via iterative discussions among the coders and study PI (MEL). Clinic strategies were mapped to multiple ERIC implementation strategies (and domains) based on their perceived “match” with ERIC operational definitions, a decision the team made to ensure that the complexity of clinic strategies were adequately reflected in the mapping process. We then categorized those strategies based on what RE-AIM implementation outcome(s) they were intended to improve (e.g., effectiveness, sustainability). Lastly, the individual surveys assessing each strategy’s acceptability, appropriateness, feasibility, and potential for sustainment were measured using validated 5-point unidimensional Likert scales that were summed and averaged, with higher numbers indicating greater favorability. Questions regarding sustainment explored participant perceptions of both (a) how well their respective final DSMES programs might address RE-AIM outcomes moving forward and (b) overall alignment of their DSMES programs in terms of adequately addressing their unique contextual determinants (i.e., PRISM domains).

Results

Implementation strategies

At the 6-month timepoint, QI team participants listed activities that mapped primarily to implementation strategies within the ERIC domains of stakeholder interrelationships and supporting clinicians and noted that most activities occurred either after the first learning session, corresponding with the first PDSA cycle, or after the second learning session, corresponding with the second PDSA cycle. Beyond the primary clinical effectiveness outcome of increasing DSMES referrals, participants noted multiple intended implementation outcome targets for these strategies (Table 1). For example, to encourage stakeholder interrelationships, participants involved staff during morning huddles, designated a resource contact/clinic champion, and provided DSMES-specific education during clinician meetings; additionally, one clinic initiated the process of getting one of their own staff members certified to provide DSMES services. These activities were intended primarily to increase clinician and staff adoption of learning collaborative activities, as well as to encourage maintenance of program activities, in the case of initiating the DSMES certification process. To support clinicians, strategies included developing a new electronic health record (EHR) DSMES referral system and creating a face sheet for each patient that included diabetes diagnosis and a reminder to screen for whether they had been previously referred to DSMES. By project end, QI teams made small refinements to only a few implementation strategies but did not add any new implementation strategies. For example, the EHR reminder system was modified to include information about external DSMES providers for patients whose insurance did not cover the cost of DSMES within the health system, a change made proactively by that system’s implementation QI team approximately one month after initial implementation that resulted in increased patient reach and overall effectiveness, as measured by number of DSMES referrals. QI team participants noted that this strategic alignment between external and internal partners promoted greater equity and more consistency in the DSMES referral process. Other changes to initial implementation strategies were minor and intended primarily to increase patient reach or provider acceptability. Acceptability, appropriateness, and feasibility of each clinic’s three implementation strategies had high-to-very high mean scores across each construct, except for initiating DSMES certification, which likely was rated lower in terms of feasibility due to the lengthiness of the certification process and that it was still not complete at project end (Table 2). Discussion

In a continuing effort to better understand how participation in the learning collaborative model yields clinical improvements, we applied PRISM and the ERIC taxonomy of implementation strategies to categorize and contextualize implementation strategies used by participants throughout the 9-month active intervention. We found that QI team activities to increase DSMES referrals (i.e., implementation strategies) primarily fell within the ERIC domains of stakeholder interrelationships and supporting clinicians. These domains are logical focal points for clinic-wide activities given that (1) the collaborative model itself emphasizes group-based planning and implementation via small tests of change [37], and (2) stakeholder buy-in has been identified as a priority both for increasing DSMES referrals [38, 39] and, more generally, for overcoming barriers to implementing diabetes research into clinical practice [40]. Our findings reflect the pragmatism of these activities: participants not only rated them as highly feasible, appropriate, and acceptable, but they also perceived them to be effective and that their DSMES referral program, overall, would be sustainable beyond the active implementation period.

Our findings are also novel given that participants were asked to consider the intended implementation outcomes of their strategies, including any ways in which they refined the strategies throughout the collaborative, and document them according to RE-AIM dimensions after 6 months and at project conclusion. This iterative use of RE-AIM is particularly useful within the context of rapid improvement frameworks, such as the learning collaborative model, in that it can guide timely adaptations in response to quickly evolving contextual concerns that often arise in real world settings [41, 42]. Furthermore, using the PRISM framework to monitor an intervention’s dynamic context and to assess progress on RE-AIM outcomes can help promote implementation equity [43] in disparate populations, such as those found in underserved rural environments. In our study, during the course of implementation, participants in one healthcare system noticed that patients whose insurance did not cover “in-house” DSMES were not being referred consistently to DSMES, and they responded by refining their EHR reminder strategy to include information about external, free-of-charge DSMES providers to increase the equity of their intervention’s reach, a change that resulted in a more consistent referral process. Indeed, this type of flexibility, in which an intervention’s core functions are preserved while the forms of delivering those functions can shift to meet evolving implementation needs, is not only useful, but likely necessary, in heterogeneous environments [44] such as primary care clinics.

Moving forward, it is important that future study designs include regular sustainability check-ins to determine whether implementation strategies are sustained or modified over time and to document reasons for any changes (i.e., to address specific implementation outcome targets), as well as any differences in contextual determinants for healthcare systems that sustain their chosen implementation strategies vs. those that have difficulty doing so. For example, for other clinics that address sustainability of their DSMES referral program by having one of their staff members pursue certification of DSMES, it might be worthwhile to initiate this implementation strategy early given the lengthiness of the certification process. Another potential way to ensure sustainability of project activities might be via the development of unique action plans that address each clinic or healthcare system’s contextual needs and guide clinical workflows. The clinical sustainability assessment tool (CSAT) [45], for example, might be an effective tool to guide this type of assessment and identify organizational factors [46] perceived as more important to promote sustainment beyond the active intervention period. This question is salient to our own findings, in which we found that sustainability of project activities was perceived to be high across all PRISM and RE-AIM dimensions except for adoption at the provider or staff level. As we continue to develop and refine our implementation approach, it will be important to identify differences between adopters and non-adopters and how they might impact sustainability.

Finally, our findings revealed that participants primarily selected implementation strategies that fell within the supporting clinicians and stakeholder interrelationships ERIC domains. Whereas supporting clinicians seems relatively straightforward as an implementation strategy approach for primary care teams, an interesting finding is the equal importance of stakeholder interrelationships. In the original ERIC concept mapping study, Waltz and colleagues [34] found that the greatest proportion of implementation strategies fell within the stakeholder interrelationships domain, and Bartley et al. further analyzed the ERIC strategies and determined that a greater proportion of implementation strategies were relational (i.e., bi-directional and power sharing), rather than transactional (i.e., unidimensional), in nature [47]. It may be worthwhile to investigate whether strategies that promote these types of bi-directional relationships are perceived as more valuable in general across settings or are unique to specific settings such as rural environments or primary care.

Strengths and limitations

While our findings should be interpreted as to consider some limitations, our results also highlight a few important methodological strengths, including (1) linking strategies to intended RE-AIM outcomes, (2) including levels of perceived effectiveness at impacting those outcomes, and (3) assessing the final set of strategies and their likelihood of sustainment in relation to PRISM contextual domains and RE-AIM outcomes. While the generalizability of our findings is limited by the inclusion of only four clinics (two each from two different healthcare systems) in our study design—a necessary budgetary limitation—it is nevertheless likely that many of the approaches, strategies, and concerns are similar across other rural healthcare clinics and systems. Relatedly, because the learning collaborative focused on quality improvement initiatives among existing clinicians and support staff in participating health systems, we did not collect demographic data of clinic practitioners and staff, allowing for the possibility that success of the learning collaborative model might be impacted by demographic trends of QI team members. Another limitation is that we did not collect data on patient perspectives of the intervention. Our team plans to address this omission in future study designs, where we plan to expand on the learning collaborative model to incorporate community linkages and adjunctive interventions, such as the inclusion of community health workers, to promote patient DSMES utilization. Similarly, although our QI team participants clearly focused on equitable reach of intervention activities, as evidenced by the responsive change to the EHR referral system to address the needs of patients whose insurance did not cover the cost of in-system DSMES services, our study design was focused primarily on clinic implementers and activities and thus did not collect data on patient demographics to determine actual equitable reach across subgroups, such as socioeconomic status, educational attainment, or race/ethnicity. It is also possible that participant ratings of high-to-very-high favorability for each strategy is suggestive of potential bias; however, given the PDSA cycle and iterative development of strategies, it is also quite logical that the QI teams would ultimately end with strategies they would find highly feasible, acceptable, appropriate, equitable and sustainable beyond the active intervention period, which is also suggested by the high scores provided by participants across RE-AIM dimensions. Our relatively small samples at both 6 months and project end, as well as the fact that only approximately half of the QI team members participated in data collection, are also important limitations that may have also contributed to the high perceptions of favorability, though it should be noted that the QI team members who participated in data collection tended also to be the ones who most consistently attended the learning sessions, owing often to scheduling conflicts of other members who either held leadership positions (e.g., healthcare system CEOs) or were needed in their respective clinics (e.g., clinicians, TA specialists) when learning sessions were scheduled. While these quantitative scores may not necessarily be generalizable outside of their own unique contexts, they are nevertheless helpful when considering the sustainability of project activities within these particular healthcare systems. Finally, analyzing the ultimate impact of implementation strategies beyond their hypothesized outcomes is a continuing challenge in implementation science [48], and our data analysis plan—partly due to our study’s limited scope and size—was not developed as to identify these mechanisms. Similarly, while “best practice” in implementation science recommends specifying implementation strategies across multiple domains [49] (e.g., dose, justification, actor), both the learning collaborative model itself and pragmatic time constraints (i.e., we were only able to utilize 30 minutes each time during two of the regularly scheduled 4-hour learning sessions) did not allow for comprehensive specification. Future studies should specify implementation strategies as descriptively as possible using established best practices [49] and consider utilizing effectiveness-implementation hybrid designs [50] to better elucidate the specific pathways from implementation strategy design/selection to improvements in implementation outcomes.

Conclusions

Our study contextualized determinants of successful implementation by documenting implementation strategies and categorizing them according to both their ERIC domain and intended PRISM/RE-AIM targets, a process conducted iteratively across the course of the intervention, which yielded important data about the ways in which clinics refined their project activities to meet clinic and patient needs. These data provide important context for the dynamic nature of interventions in clinical settings and inform project activities for scale-up of our research design. Our next step is to assess the specific mechanism(s) driving clinical change, i.e., whether a bundle of activities that fall within these specific implementation strategy domains (i.e., supporting clinicians and stakeholder interrelationships) or participation in the learning collaborative model itself (i.e., the quality improvement process) is a stronger driving factor in increasing DSMES referrals. Nevertheless, the findings from this suggest that our facilitated learning collaborative approach can yield acceptable, appropriate, and feasible implementation strategies with high likelihood for sustaining increased DSMES referrals.
