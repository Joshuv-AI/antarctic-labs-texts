ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiabetes mellitus (DM) is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by persistent hyperglycemia due to impaired insulin secretion, action, or both. As a major global health concern, its prevalence is rapidly increasing, particularly in low- and middle-income countries like Pakistan. Despite treatment availability, awareness and adherence remain low, with notable disparities between knowledge and practice.ConclusionPakistan is facing an escalating diabetes epidemic influenced by both behavioral and systemic factors. Immediate action is needed to implement targeted public health interventions, improve healthcare accessibility, and promote awareness and lifestyle modification. Diabetes in Pakistan: an epidemiological perspective

Diabetes mellitus (DM), a chronic metabolic disorder marked by persistent hyperglycemia due to impaired insulin secretion, action, or both [1]. Pakistan, the fifth most populous nation globally, grapples with significant health challenges [2], particularly the rising prevalence of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) [3]. Diabetes mellitus (DM) has become a critical public health issue [4], driven with its increasing incidence and the associated health and economic burdens [5]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), NCDs were responsible for 58% of all deaths in Pakistan in 2016 [6]. Among these, cardiovascular diseases, cancers, chronic respiratory illnesses, and diabetes are the most prevalent [7].

The epidemiological landscape of diabetes in Pakistan reveals a sharp upward trend [8]. In 2000, around 5.2 million adults were diagnosed with diabetes [4, 9], a number that skyrocketed to approximately 33 million by 2021 [10]. This alarming rise highlights the pressing need for targeted interventions [11]. Although diabetes directly contributed to 3% of total deaths in 2016 [4, 12], its influence extends further as a risk factor for other NCDs, such as heart disease and hypertension, exacerbating its overall impact on mortality rates [13].

Given the escalating prevalence of diabetes mellitus and its profound health and economic consequences, there is an urgent need for a comprehensive understanding of its epidemiological trends, contributing risk factors, and management challenges in the Pakistani context. This review was conducted to address this gap by synthesizing current data on the burden of diabetes in Pakistan, with a particular focus on disparities between urban and rural populations, diagnostic standards, and the role of emerging technologies. The primary objectives are to enhance early detection through evidence-based insights, support the development of culturally appropriate public health campaigns, and improve patient education on diabetes prevention and self-management. Additionally, the findings aim to serve as a valuable resource for healthcare professionals and policymakers in designing targeted interventions, allocating resources more effectively, and formulating national strategies to curb the growing diabetes epidemic in Pakistan.

Research strategy and databases

The literature search was conducted using major academic databases including PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar. Additional region-specific sources such as the Pakistan Journal of Medical Sciences, Journal of the College of Physicians and Surgeons Pakistan (JCPSP), and relevant grey literature were also reviewed to capture localized insights.

Search Terms and Boolean Operators: The search strategy employed a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and keywords, including:

"Diabetes Mellitus" OR "Type 2 Diabetes" AND "Prevalence" AND "Risk Factors" AND "Pakistan" AND ("Management" OR "Complications" OR "Technology" OR "Diagnosis"). Boolean operators were applied to refine the results and ensure relevance.

"Diabetes Mellitus" OR "Type 2 Diabetes" AND "Prevalence" AND "Risk Factors" AND "Pakistan" AND ("Management" OR "Complications" OR "Technology" OR "Diagnosis"). Boolean operators were applied to refine the results and ensure relevance.

Time frame

Articles published between January 2000 and March 2024 were considered to capture both historical trends and the most recent developments in diabetes prevalence, management, and policy evolution in Pakistan.

Inclusion criteria

Peer-reviewed journal articles, reports, and reviews related to diabetes mellitus in the Pakistani population. Studies focusing on epidemiology, diagnostics, health behaviors, complications, and technological interventions.

English-language publications

Both quantitative and qualitative studies, including cross-sectional surveys, cohort studies, and health policy reviews.

Exclusion criteria

Case reports, editorials, and commentaries without empirical data.

Studies focusing on populations outside Pakistan unless they provided comparative insights relevant to the national context, and Non-English language publications.

Screening and selection process

Titles and abstracts were screened for relevance, followed by full-text reviews. Data were extracted thematically, focusing on: (i) prevalence trends, (ii) risk factors and complications, (iii) diagnostic standards (ADA/WHO), (iv) urban–rural disparities, (v) use of technology in diabetes care, and (vi) awareness and management practices.

We have integrated these methodological details into the manuscript to enhance its rigor and transparency. We hope this addition meets the standards of methodological reporting expected for literature reviews and welcome any further suggestions for improvement.

Understanding the diabetes and contributing factors

The rising burden of diabetes mellitus (DM) in Pakistan is deeply influenced by two primary pathways: unhealthy lifestyles and limited healthcare access [14]. The first pathway begins with unhealthy lifestyle choices, such as poor dietary habits and physical inactivity [14, 15], which are increasingly common due to urbanization and socioeconomic changes [16]. These behaviors often lead to obesity and weight gain, which are significant risk factors for insulin resistance [17]. Over time, insulin resistance progresses to type 2 diabetes, creating a cycle of metabolic dysfunction that further exacerbates the disease burden [18]. This pathway highlights the critical role of behavioral and biological factors in driving the diabetes epidemic [19] (Table 1).

Contributing factors leading to increased diabetes prevalence and complications

The systemic challenges in healthcare access, particularly in rural and underserved areas. Limited availability of healthcare facilities, diagnostic tools, and trained professionals often results in delayed diagnosis of diabetes [26]. Without timely detection, individuals are unable to manage their blood sugar levels effectively, leading to poor disease control [27]. This lack of management increases the risk of severe complications, such as cardiovascular diseases, kidney failure, and neuropathy, which further strain the healthcare system and reduce the quality of life for patients [28].

Both pathways underscore the interconnected nature of individual behaviors and systemic barriers in contributing to the diabetes crisis. Unhealthy lifestyles and limited healthcare access not only increase the prevalence of diabetes but also amplify its complications [29], creating a dual burden on individuals and the healthcare system [18]. Addressing these issues requires a multifaceted approach that combines public health interventions, improved healthcare infrastructure, and policies promoting healthier lifestyles.

To mitigate the diabetes burden, it is essential to implement targeted strategies that address both pathways. Public health campaigns can raise awareness about the importance of healthy eating and regular exercise [30], while healthcare reforms can improve access to diagnostic and treatment services, especially in rural areas. By tackling the root causes and systemic barriers, Pakistan can reduce the prevalence of diabetes and its associated complications, ultimately improving the overall health and well-being of its population.

Diagnostic criteria for DM comparison of American Diabetes Association (ADA) and World Health Organization (WHO)

The diagnostic criteria for diabetes mellitus (DM) as outlined by the American Diabetes Association (ADA) and the World Health Organization (WHO). Both organizations emphasize the use of key biomarkers, including HbA1c, fasting plasma glucose (FPG), 2-h post-load glucose during an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), and random plasma glucose [33, 34]. However, there are subtle differences in their guidelines. For instance, the ADA requires two abnormal test results from the same or separate samples for confirmation if there is no unequivocal hyperglycemia [35], whereas the WHO includes an additional criterion for capillary plasma glucose during the 2-h post-load test [36]. These differences reflect variations in clinical approaches but share the common goal of accurate and early diagnosis of diabetes.

The diagnostic criteria highlight the importance of standardized testing methods and clinical context. For example, random plasma glucose is used by both organizations only in the presence of classic hyperglycemia symptoms or a hyperglycemic crisis. The inclusion of HbA1c as a diagnostic tool by both the ADA and WHO underscores its reliability in reflecting long-term blood glucose levels. However, the WHO provides more specific details, such as the requirement for an 8–14 h fast for FPG testing and the distinction between venous and capillary plasma glucose measurements. These guidelines are essential for healthcare providers to ensure consistent and accurate diagnosis, enabling timely intervention and management of diabetes (Table 3).

Role of technology in diabetes management, particularly in Pakistan, where healthcare access is often limited, especially in rural areas. Technologies such as telemedicine, mobile health apps, and wearable devices offer innovative solutions for remote consultations, real-time glucose monitoring [42], and patient empowerment. However, challenges like high costs, limited internet connectivity, and cultural resistance hinder widespread adoption. Artificial intelligence and digital health initiatives can further enhance diabetes care through predictive analytics and public–private partnerships [43], but require significant investment and local adaptation. Prevalence of DM and complications

Microvascular and macrovascular complications of diabetes significantly increase the disease burden on patients and the healthcare system. A study conducted by Zia et al. in the capital city reported the prevalence of diabetic macrovascular complications as follows: ischemic heart disease at 28.2%, stroke at 8.5%, and peripheral vascular disease at 5.4% [44]. Similarly, Uddin et al., in a national multicenter observational study across 20 cities in Pakistan, documented the prevalence of microvascular and macrovascular complications at 68.6% and 9.0%, respectively [45]. The most commonly observed macrovascular complications included angina (5.2%), myocardial infarction (3.3%), peripheral arterial disease (2.2%), and stroke (2.0%) [45, 46]. Additionally, Basit et al. (2004) reported hypertension as the most prevalent macrovascular complication in Karachi, affecting 50.4% of patients, followed by coronary artery disease at 15.1% [46, 47]. These findings highlight the substantial impact of diabetes-related complications on public health in Pakistan.

Diabetes awareness

The awareness and knowledge about diabetes among the diabetic population in Pakistan remain generally poor, as highlighted by various studies. In a survey (2022) in rural and urban areas of Faisalabad district involving 1,180 diabetic patients, women demonstrated better knowledge of diabetic complications compared to men [47]. However, overall awareness levels were still insufficient. Shams et al. identified several barriers to diabetes knowledge, particularly among elderly diabetics, including illiteracy, poverty, poor glycemic control, inadequate dietary management, and reliance on alternative therapies. These factors contribute to a lack of understanding about the disease and its management.

Further insights come from a KAP study by Uthman et al., which revealed that while 63.0% of respondents had good knowledge about diabetes [47], only 7.0% demonstrated good practices toward managing the condition [47]. This gap between knowledge and practice underscores the need for more effective education and intervention programs. Additionally, Kumar et al. found that 42.5% of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) patients lacked adequate knowledge about hypoglycemia symptoms and self-management, further emphasizing the critical need for improved patient education [48].

Standard treatments for managing glucose levels include oral hypoglycemic agents and insulin. In Pakistan, Metformin is the most commonly prescribed medication, with a prescription rate of 51.3%, followed by insulin/analogues at 34.6% [49]. A recent multicenter cross-sectional study by Butt et al. revealed that the majority of patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) receive triple therapy (26%), while 34.7% are on monotherapy [46]. These findings highlight the diverse treatment approaches used to manage diabetes in the country, reflecting the need for tailored therapeutic strategies based on patient-specific factors.

Discussion

The epidemiological landscape of diabetes mellitus (DM) in Pakistan reveals a dramatic upward trajectory in prevalence over the past two decades [50]. In 2000, approximately 5.2 million adults were living with diabetes, a figure that surged to an estimated 33 million by 2021 [51], this sharp increase underscores the growing burden of DM and reflects the broader rise in non-communicable diseases nationwide. Age-specific data indicate that the prevalence of DM is highest among adults aged 45–64 years, although recent studies also report an alarming increase in younger age groups, particularly those between 25–44 years [52, 53], likely due to changing dietary and lifestyle patterns. High-risk behaviors contributing to this rise include physical inactivity, prolonged screen time, and the widespread consumption of fast food and ultra-processed food products. These lifestyle shifts, especially prevalent in urbanized settings, are strongly associated with rising rates of obesity and metabolic syndrome, both of which are major risk factors for type 2 DM.Complications of uncontrolled diabetes are widespread and severe, macrovascular complications such as ischemic heart disease, stroke, and peripheral artery disease are increasingly common [54], while microvascular complications—including diabetic nephropathy, retinopathy, and neuropathy—pose substantial challenges to patient quality of life and healthcare systems [55]. These complications not only elevate morbidity and mortality but also place significant economic and logistical strain on Pakistan’s already overstretched healthcare infrastructure. The confluence of demographic vulnerability, behavioral risk factors, and insufficient healthcare access amplifies the urgency for targeted public health interventions.

The rising prevalence of diabetes mellitus (DM) in Pakistan presents a significant public health challenge, exacerbated by both lifestyle factors and systemic healthcare barriers [56]. The study highlights the dual pathways contributing to the diabetes epidemic: unhealthy lifestyle choices, such as poor diet and physical inactivity, and limited access to healthcare, particularly in rural areas. These factors not only increase the risk of developing diabetes but also lead to delayed diagnosis and poor management, resulting in severe complications such as cardiovascular disease, kidney failure, and neuropathy. The findings underscore the urgent need for comprehensive public health interventions that address both behavioral and structural determinants of diabetes. By promoting healthier lifestyles and improving healthcare infrastructure, particularly in underserved regions, Pakistan can mitigate the growing burden of diabetes and its associated complications.

The disparities in diabetes prevalence and management between urban and rural populations further complicate the situation. Urban areas, characterized by better healthcare access and greater exposure to risk factors like sedentary lifestyles, report higher diabetes prevalence due to better detection rates [4, 57]. In contrast, rural areas face significant barriers, including limited healthcare facilities, low awareness, and inadequate resources, leading to underdiagnosis and poor disease management. These disparities highlight the need for tailored interventions that address the unique challenges faced by each population. Policymakers must prioritize healthcare infrastructure development and awareness campaigns in rural areas while continuing to focus on preventive measures and lifestyle modification programs in urban settings. Such targeted strategies can help bridge the gap in diabetes care and reduce the overall disease burden.

To operationalize these findings, several policy actions are recommended. These include integrating routine diabetes screening into primary care services at BHUs and RHCs, implementing nationwide awareness campaigns in regional languages, subsidizing essential diagnostics and medications, and expanding telemedicine platforms for remote management. Additionally, training programs for diabetes educators and frontline workers should be prioritized. These measures align with WHO PEN guidelines and are suitable for integration into Pakistan’s provincial and national NCD programs.

Finally, the study emphasizes the importance of standardized diagnostic criteria and patient education in managing diabetes effectively. While both the American Diabetes Association (ADA) and the World Health Organization (WHO) provide guidelines for diabetes diagnosis [37, 58], the subtle differences in their criteria reflect the need for context-specific approaches [59]. Moreover, the low levels of diabetes awareness and knowledge among patients, particularly in rural areas, highlight the critical role of education in improving disease management. Effective public health campaigns and patient education programs can empower individuals to adopt healthier behaviors and adhere to treatment regimens, ultimately reducing the risk of complications. By addressing these multifaceted challenges, Pakistan can make significant strides in combating the diabetes epidemic and improving the overall health outcomes of its population.

Conclusion

The rising prevalence of diabetes mellitus (DM) in Pakistan is a significant public health challenge, driven by unhealthy lifestyles and limited healthcare access. Addressing this crisis requires comprehensive strategies, including public health campaigns, improved healthcare infrastructure, and tailored interventions for urban and rural populations. Enhancing diabetes awareness, early diagnosis, and effective management are crucial to reducing the disease burden and its complications.
