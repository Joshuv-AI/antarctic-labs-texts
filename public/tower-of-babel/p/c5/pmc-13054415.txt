Stenting for symptomatic left renal vein compression in patients with complex pelvic venous disease is safe on long-term follow-up
Authors: Hind Anan, Yashashwini Sudina, Natalie Sridharan, Eric Hager, Raymond Eid, Efthymios Avgerinos, Michael J Singh, Rabih A Chaer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe ideal treatment for symptomatic left renal vein (LRV) compression continues to be debated, with several available open and endovascular options. Concerns about percutaneous stenting have been raised due to risk of stent migration, fracture, and erosion. This study aims to assess the long-term safety, patency, and effectiveness of LRV stenting.MethodsA retrospective institutional chart review of all patients undergoing LRV stenting for symptomatic LRV compression between 2010 and 2024 was conducted. Baseline characteristics, operative details, complications, reinterventions, and symptom resolution were collected. Complete resolution was defined as full relief of presenting symptoms; partial resolution as patient-reported improvement without full relief; and no relief as no change from preoperative symptoms.ResultsA total of 44 patients were identified. Forty-three patients with complete follow-up were included in the analysis. A total of 13 patients underwent LRV transposition for symptomatic compression at our institution in the same time frame; of which six required stenting due to symptom recurrence and were included in the analysis. The mean age was 42.6 years (range, 17.6-79.1 years). Body mass index ranged from 15.2 to 42.5 kg/m2. Most of the patients were female (42/43) and White (41/43). Thirty patients (69.8%) had a concomitant diagnosis of pelvic congestion syndrome. Seven patients underwent stenting for recurrence after LRV transposition. Pelvic or lower abdominal pain was the most common presenting symptom (35/43), followed by flank pain (25/43) and hematuria (18/43). Thirty-nine patients received a single stent, while four had two stents at index procedure. The stent size ranged from 8 to 16 mm in diameter (median, 14 mm) and 30 to 60 mm in length (median, 40 mm). Dedicated venous stents were used in 42% of patients after 2019. Intravascular ultrasound was used in the majority of patients (32/43; 74.4%). Sixteen patients (36.4%) underwent concomitant gonadal vein embolization. The median clinical follow-up was 46.5 months (interquartile range, 23.7-82.2 months). No major perioperative complications were reported. No stent migration, embolization, or vein pseudoaneurysm occurred. One case of complete stent occlusion was reported. The primary patency was 89.9% at 1 year and 82.8% at 4 years, while the primary-assisted patency remained at 100%. Symptom resolution was achieved in 90.7% of patients (39/43; 24 complete, 15 partial). Two patients eventually underwent auto-transplantation, three underwent nephrectomy (one for cancer), and one had a stent explant due to persistent pain despite patent stents.ConclusionsLRV stenting for symptomatic compression offers a minimally invasive approach and results in high symptom resolution and patency with no major stent complications at long-term follow-up. It can continue to be a treatment option where appropriate expertise is available.Keywords: Left renal vein compression, Left renal vein stenting, Left renal vein transposition, Long-term outcomes, Nutcracker syndrome

The prevalence of symptomatic left renal vein (LRV) compression, also known as Nutcracker syndrome, is unknown.1 It is likely underdiagnosed due to the absence of a standardized diagnostic criteria and variations in presenting symptoms.2 Most patients present with hematuria and/or flank/pelvic pain,3 although presenting signs or symptoms can include proteinuria, varicosities, weight loss, and vague gastrointestinal symptoms.4

Due to the ill-defined pathophysiology of this condition, there are no guidelines on diagnosis and management of symptomatic LRV compression. The most recent expert consensus agrees that it is diagnosis of exclusion and requires symptoms to be present for at least 6 months with confirmed compression of the LRV between the aorta and superior mesenteric artery on imaging.2

The same expert panel agreed that LRV transposition is the preferred operative management for symptomatic LRV compression over stenting.2 Concerns about stenting have been raised primarily due to several reported cases of stent migration.5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13 Nevertheless, over the past years, LRV stenting has become more common, with encouraging results.4,14,15

In 2019, the United States Food and Drug Administration approved dedicated venous stents.16 However, these current dedicated venous stents were not designed or tested for the treatment of LRV compression, with concerns related not only to catastrophic cardiac migration but also to stent erosion requiring surgical conversion.

Given that the majority of patients treated are young and otherwise healthy,2,17 consideration for durability and long-term complications are even more paramount. This study aims to assess the long-term safety and effectiveness of LRV stenting for the treatment of symptomatic LRV compression, specifically focusing on acute and long-term complications related to dynamic stent compression and impact on migration, stent patency, and renal vein erosion.

Methods

Study design

Following the approval from the University of Pittsburgh Institutional Review Board, we retrospectively reviewed our institutional electronic health records for all the patients undergoing LRV stenting for symptomatic LRV compression between 2010 and 2024 across several UPMC hospitals within our multihospital health care system. Patients who underwent stenting after failed LRV transposition and persistent or recurrent symptoms were also included.

Preprocedural protocol

Patients were referred for LRV compression after excluding or addressing other non-vascular etiologies. In cases with concomitant pelvic congestion, management was performed either concurrently or in a staged manner to target the symptoms. In patients where symptoms were predominantly pelvic or pubic in nature, pelvic congestion or symptomatic pubic and vulvar varicosities were treated first; when symptoms were predominantly due to LRV compression (eg, flank pain), LRV compression was treated first.

All patients evaluated for LRV compression underwent computed tomography venography of the abdomen and pelvis and duplex ultrasound imaging of the LRV. Venography and intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) have also become routine in our practice to confirm LRV compression before stenting.

Operative technique

Under local anesthesia and conscious sedation, percutaneous access of the right common femoral vein is established and selective catheterization of the LRV is performed. After heparinization, a stiff wire is advanced into the renal vein branches (or the left ovarian vein) to provide sufficient purchase for sheath delivery. Following balloon predilation, a self-expanding stent is deployed and post dilated. In our practice, we have moved away from distal branch landing, and partial protrusion of the stent into the inferior vena cava (IVC) (<50% encroachment of the IVC) is generally advocated.

Our detailed preprocedural diagnostic protocol and procedural techniques have been previously published.4 Since 2019, the use of dedicated venous stents has gradually increased in our practice.

Data collection

Data collected included baseline characteristics, preoperative comorbidities, presenting symptoms, prior interventions, and operative details. Outcomes assessed include perioperative results, symptom relief, reinterventions, stent patency, and complications.

Symptom resolution was classified as complete, partial, or no relief based on clinical follow-up. Complete resolution refers to full relief of the patient’s presenting symptoms; partial resolution refers to patient-reported improvement in those symptoms without reaching full relief; and no relief indicates no change from the preoperative symptom status. Long-term follow-up was defined in line with the Vascular Quality Initiative definition as at least 9 months.18

Patency was evaluated based on the most recent imaging follow-up.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data. Variables were presented as frequency (percentage), mean ± standard deviation, or median (interquartile range [IQR]) as appropriate. Kaplan-Meier analysis was used to assess patency rates during follow-up. Dashed lines represent time points where the standard error exceeds 10%. Data was analyzed using Stata (StataCorp LLC, 2025; Stata Statistical Software: Release 19)

Results

A total of 44 patients underwent stenting for symptomatic LRV compression, some of which were done to salvage a failing renal vein transposition. During that time period, 13 patients underwent LRV transposition, six of whom later required adjunctive stenting and were included in the analysis, whereas the seven patients who had only LRV transposition were excluded.

One patient had no follow-up and was excluded from the symptom resolution analysis. The mean age was 42.6 ± 16.3 years, with a median of 39.4 years (range, 17.6-79.1 years). Almost all patients were female (42/43; 97.5%) and White (42/43; 95.3%). The body mass index ranged from 15.7 to 42.5 kg/m2.

Most patients were referred by their primary care provider (45.2%) or gynecologist (16.2%). Patients commonly presented with more than one symptom, with several having overlapping complaints. Pelvic/lower abdominal pain was the most common presenting symptom (35/43; 81.4%) followed by flank pain (25/43; 58.1%) and gross hematuria (18/43; 41.9%). Other patients presented with vague gastrointestinal symptoms (11/43; 25.6%), recurrent dysuria (7/43; 16.3%), lower limb varices/swelling (6/43; 14.0%), or pelvic varicosities/varicocele (5/43; 11.6%).

Seven patients had recurrent symptoms after LRV transposition and underwent adjunctive stenting for recurrent symptomatic stenosis at the mid-renal vein. The median time to stenting after transposition was 7.8 months (IQR, 3.6-11.1 months). Thirty patients (69.8%) had a concomitant diagnosis of pelvic congestion syndrome. Five patients (11.6%) underwent ovarian vein embolization prior to LRV stenting, whereas 16 (37.2%) were treated simultaneously. Three patients had May-Thurner syndrome, two of whom underwent iliac stenting prior to LRV stenting.

Vascular surgeons performed 76.7% of the procedures, whereas interventional radiologists performed the remaining. Thirty-nine patients received a single stent, whereas four had two stents at the index procedure. Protégé EverFlex stents (Medtronic) were commonly used in this series. Dedicated venous stents accounted for 42.3% of the stents after 2019. The stents diameters ranged from 8 to 16 mm (median, 14 mm) and 30 to 60 mm in length (median, 40 mm). All stents but one were self-expanding. The patients treated with an 8-mm stent and a balloon expandable stent were an exception to our practice, treated by another service. IVUS was used in the majority of patients (32/43; 74.4%). The average diameter of the non-compressed LRV ranged from 7.7 to 16.1 mm on IVUS, but IVUS was available for review in only 19 patients (44.2%). Most patients (81.4%) were treated on an outpatient basis, and eight were admitted for pain control. No major postoperative complications occurred. One patient developed a small access site retroperitoneal hematoma that was treated conservatively.

At a median clinical follow-up of 46.5 months (IQR, 23.7-82.2 months), symptom resolution was achieved in 90.7% of patients (39/43; 24 complete, 15 partial). All patients presenting with gastrointestinal symptoms or recurrent dysuria experienced resolution of their symptoms. Of the patients presenting with pelvic pain, 60% had symptom resolution after LRV stenting, compared with 77% of those presenting with hematuria and 76% of those presenting with flank pain.

Four patients experienced ongoing symptoms after stenting. Among them, one required nephrectomy, but the flank pain persisted, and the patient was referred for a nerve block. Another patient continued to have flank pain and hematuria, despite an auto-transplant, and was diagnosed later with loin pain hematuria syndrome. The third patient underwent stent explant, renal artery denervation, and nephrectomy, but remained undiagnosed and symptomatic. The fourth patient achieved complete symptom resolution after gonadal vein embolization and multiple venoplasties. One patient with a patent stent required nephrectomy for malignancy, unrelated to LRV compression.

When excluding patients who underwent concomitant gonadal vein embolization, symptom resolution occurred in 85.2% of patients (23/27; 12 complete and 11 partial).

The median surveillance imaging follow-up was 32.3 months (IQR, 11.4-61.1 months). Seven patients underwent a secondary stent intervention for symptom recurrence or stent restenosis (Table). No stent migration, erosion, embolization, or vein pseudoaneurysm occurred. One patient had complete stent occlusion at 1.5 months follow-up but was asymptomatic and did not require any reintervention. There were no open surgical conversions for stent-related complications. Patency assessment was done using duplex ultrasound, with the addition of computed tomography angiography with a venous phase in patients with inconclusive studies. The last available follow-up imaging modality was duplex ultrasound in 56% of patients, computed tomography angiography in 34%, and venogram in 10%. The primary patency was 89.9% at 1 year and 82.8% at 2, 3, and 4 years, whereas the primary-assisted patency remained at 100% (Fig). Primary-assisted and secondary patency were achieved with repeat balloon angioplasty with or without over-stenting. Due to the limited sample size and event rate, reliable predictors for reintervention or loss of patency could not be established.

Discussion

Since the first case of LRV stenting in 1996,19 stenting has become increasingly reported for the treatment of LRV compression, with the majority of published series from Asia.4,15,20 In our previous smaller series, LRV stenting was found to be safe and effective at midterm follow-up.4 In the current series, stenting resulted in high symptom resolution, good safety outcomes, and acceptable patency on long-term follow-up.

Despite favorable outcomes and a less invasive approach of LRV stenting, LRV transposition remains the preferred treatment for symptomatic LRV compression.2 Both procedures have demonstrated comparable rates of symptom resolution in approximately 90% of patients.15,21,22 In our patients, stenting resulted in a similarly high symptom resolution rate at 90.7%. However, LRV transposition remains an invasive surgical procedure that can be associated with major adverse events,22, 23, 24 and typically requires a longer hospital stay of around 5 days20,25 compared with 1 to 2 days following renal vein stenting.4,20 Our practice slowly shifted from LRV transposition to outpatient stenting over the years, and this was mostly driven by our institutional experience.4

Our study demonstrated a primary patency rate of 82.8% and a primary-assisted patency rate of 100% at 4 years. Consistent with these findings of high long-term patency, two large retrospective studies conducted in China reported no significant cases of restenosis, with follow-up periods of 66 and 36 months.14,15 For LRV transposition, primary and primary assisted patency has been reported to be 74% and 97% at 2 years26 with a reintervention rate of 20% to 30%.20,26 Almost one-half of these reinterventions are endovascular procedures.26 Of our 13 patients who underwent LRV transposition, six subsequently required stenting. Restenosis in the transposed vein may result from persistent external compression, tension from the aorta, intimal hyperplasia, or thrombus formation, ultimately necessitating stenting.20 In this context, stenting constitutes a durable and effective long-term treatment option for symptomatic LRV compression.

There has been skepticism and major reluctance to use stenting for LRV compression due to the risk of stent migration. Stent migration could be mitigated with oversizing, with concerns related to stent erosion, given the ongoing stent compression, ultimately requiring surgical conversion. In our case series, we had no instances of stent migration. Wu et al reviewed a series of 75 patients from 2004 to 2014 and reported a stent migration rate of 6.7%, with a mean follow-up of 55 months.13 However, the majority of these migration cases were linked to stent sizing issues and operator experience. In recent years, several case reports have also documented instances of LRV stents migrating to critical locations such as the tricuspid valve, pulmonary artery, and right ventricle, necessitating either open surgical or endovascular retrieval procedures.5,7, 8, 9 These cases predominantly involved small or balloon-expandable stents.

Our operative technique of regularly ballooning before and after stent placement may have reduced the migration incidence. Additionally, IVUS was frequently used in most of our interventions. IVUS allowed direct measurements of the LRV diameter along its entire length, and the relationship to the IVC was obtained,27 allowing for accurate stent sizing and placement. Currently, our practice is to oversize the stent by 20% of the average vein diameter, aiming to reduce the risk of migration Although a small stent and balloon-expandable stent were used in this series, this was not in line with our practice and is not recommended. To date, we have not encountered any cases of stent erosion or migration.

Since 2019, 42% of the stents used in our cases have been dedicated venous stents. These stents offer flexibility that adapts to venous anatomy, resistance to compression and recoil, and are available in larger diameters and longer lengths.28 However, they were not designed or optimized for treating LRV compression, and arterial stents remain a common choice in our practice. The latter have lower radial force,28 and they are more susceptible to compression between the superior mesenteric artery and aorta. There is a growing theory that such stents are less likely to migrate as they are anchored in place at the compression point at the aortomesenteric angle, and that stiffer stents are more likely to migrate, although this remains to be proven. Additionally, arterial stents are the only option when smaller stents are needed after renal vein transposition.

This study had several limitations, particularly due to its retrospective design and small sample size. Variability in stent selection and differences in procedural techniques between interventional radiologists and vascular surgeons may have influenced outcomes. Symptom assessment also relied on subjective patient reporting without the use of standardized measurement tools, which may introduce variability related to individual interpretation and recall.

A substantial number of patients underwent other venous interventions in addition to LRV stenting. Gonadal vein embolization for pelvic congestion syndrome and/or iliac vein stenting for May-Thurner syndrome was performed either prior to LRV stenting or concurrently in nearly one-half of the cohort, which limited our ability to isolate the clinical effect of LRV stenting alone.

This is clinically reflective of the frequent co-existence of LRV compression with other complex pelvic venous pathologies. Prior imaging-based studies, in asymptomatic populations, suggest that overlapping anatomic findings are common, with concomitant left common iliac vein compression reported in up to 30% of people with LRV compression29 and gonadal vein dilatation reported in up to 22%.30

Our treatment strategy and sequence were guided by the dominant presenting symptoms, which may vary. Existing pelvic venous disease was treated either as a first-stage procedure or concurrently with LRV stenting. In patients whose symptoms were predominantly pelvic in nature, gonadal vein embolization was done first. Conversely, when symptoms were predominantly attributable to LRV compression, LRV stenting was performed first. Concurrent LRV stenting and gonadal vein embolization were performed in patients with mixed clinical presentations. This resulted in a heterogeneous cohort, which reflects the complexity and intertwined nature of LRV compression and pelvic venous disease.

Conclusions

Symptomatic LEV compression is uncommon and typically presents in healthy young female patients. LRV stenting provides a minimally invasive approach for the treatment of symptomatic compression. This series shows high rates of symptom resolution and stent patency, with no stent-related complications observed in long-term follow-up. LRV stenting can be considered a primary treatment option for symptomatic LRV compression where appropriate expertise is available. Given the sustained favorable long-term outcomes of stenting for symptomatic LRV compression, consideration should be given for dedicated stents to treat this condition.
