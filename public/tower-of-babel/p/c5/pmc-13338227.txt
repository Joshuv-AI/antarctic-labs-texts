Joule-Heated direct writing: an electrically-driven additive manufacturing paradigm for space fabrication
Authors: Suli Li, Zhuang Gao, Jie Xiong, Heng Zhang, Laixia Yang, Bingheng Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe imperative for in-space manufacturing drives the development of compact, energy-efficient metal additive manufacturing (AM) technologies. This work introduces and investigates a novel Joule-Heating Additive Manufacturing (JHAM) process, inspired by the Joule–Lenz effect, for the direct writing of metallic structures. A fully coupled thermal-electrical-structural finite element model is developed to simulate the multilayer deposition of 304 stainless steel wire, elucidating the unique "tusk-shaped" temperature field evolution and the effects of current, speed, and contact width. Single-bead, five-layer walls are fabricated experimentally, and the influence of process parameters on macrostructure, geometry (width, height, overlap rate), surface roughness (Ra, Rz), and cross-sectional hardness is systematically characterized. The results demonstrate that optimal macroscopic quality is achieved at 120 A, 200 mm/min, and a 1.0 mm contact width. The forming width and overlap rate increase with current, while the forming height decreases; conversely, increasing speed reduces width and overlap but increases height. Surface roughness and hardness exhibit non-monotonic trends with varying parameters, with a minimum Ra of 0.142 µm attained. Simulated and experimentally measured voltages show good agreement, validating the multiphysics model.Validation results indicate a voltage discrepancy of less than 10% between simulation and experiment. The JHAM process, with its low power consumption (<1 kW) and simplified architecture, presents a promising candidate for on-orbit fabrication of metallic components.Keywords: Additive manufacturing, Direct writing, Joule heating, Multiphysics simulation, In-space manufacturing, Stainless steelSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Physics

Introduction

The advancement of long duration space exploration and the establishment of extraterrestrial habitats necessitate a paradigm shift from ground-based manufacturing to on-orbit, on-demand fabrication1–4. This transition is crucial in order to overcome the severe constraints of payload mass, volume, and associated launch costs. Additive manufacturing (AM) is widely recognized as a key enabling technology for this shift, as it offers the potential for in-situ repair, recycling, and production of strategic components5–9.

Global space agencies, including NASA, ESA, and the China Manned Space Agency, have active research programs focused on adapting AM for the space environment10,11. The overarching goal is to evolve the supporting infrastructure from “transporting everything” to "manufacturing what is needed" on-site12,13. Various studies have explored specific space AM applications, ranging from electron beam freeform fabrication and micro-droplet jetting to variable-gravity forming of polymers and space trusses14–20.

Current mainstream metal AM heat sources for terrestrial use—such as lasers21–26, arcs27–32, electron beams33–38, and plasma arcs39,40—present challenges for space applications, including high power demand, complex optics, and vacuum requirements. To address these limitations, we propose a Joule-Heating Additive Manufacturing (JHAM) technique. Based on the fundamental Joule-Lenz law, JHAM utilizes the electrical resistance of the metal wire itself as the heat source for melting, offering inherent advantages of simplicity, low energy consumption, compactness, and gravity-independent operation.

Previous research on resistance-based AM has explored mechanisms of wire melting, droplet transfer, and single-layer formation. However, a comprehensive understanding of the multi-physics phenomena governing multi-layer deposition-encompassing dynamic heat generation, complex thermal history, and their resultant effects on geometry, surface integrity, and mechanical properties remains limited. Accurate numerical simulation of this process is challenging due to the moving contact zone and strong coupling between electrical, thermal, and mechanical fields.

Although our previous research41 demonstrated the feasibility of using Joule heating for single-layer droplet transfer, it did not address the cumulative thermal effects and structural stability required for 3D manufacturing. To accurately capture the melting and solidification of the 304 stainless steel wire, the phase change process and the associated latent heat were incorporated using the equivalent heat capacity method. The latent heat of fusion () is distributed over a solidus-liquidus temperature range (from to ). This ensures that the energy balance during the solid-to-liquid transition is correctly conserved in the thermal field calculations.

The contact resistance at the roller-wire and wire-substrate interfaces plays a critical role in Joule heat generation. As shown in Table 3, the electrical contact resistance is modeled as a temperature-dependent variable. It progressively decreases as the local temperature increases, which physically reflects the material softening, the increase in actual contact area under constant pressure, and the eventual melting at the interface.

Given the simulated in-space vacuum environment, convective heat transfer is entirely neglected. Thermal dissipation is governed solely by thermal conduction within the solid structures and thermal radiation to the ambient environment. The radiation boundary condition is applied to all free surfaces governed by the Stefan-Boltzmann law, with an assumed ambient temperature of . The surface emissivity () is set to for the stainless steel and for the copper roller. Mechanically, the bottom surface of the substrate is fully fixed, while a dynamic mechanical pressure is applied by the top roller.

To ensure numerical accuracy while maintaining computational efficiency, a mesh convergence test was conducted prior to the primary simulations. Three different mesh densities were evaluated for the deposition zone. The results indicated that refining the minimum element size below yielded a peak temperature variation of less than . Consequently, this optimal mesh density was adopted for the deposition region to capture the localized steep temperature gradients accurately.

Results and discussion

Temperature field evolution

The simulated temperature field evolution reveals a characteristic "tusk-shaped" profile (Fig. 2). During the roller movement step, the highest temperature region is not static; it initiates at the contact interface between the newest (fifth) and the previous (fourth) layer, then shifts rearward relative to the roller’s motion. The formation of this asymmetric profile is governed by competing heat transfer mechanisms: the copper roller acts as a significant heat sink, inducing rapid quenching at the contact front.Under vacuum conditions, the absence of convective heat transfer fundamentally alters the thermal dissipation characteristics. At the contact front, the copper roller serves as a primary heat sink, inducing rapid quenching. Conversely, heat dissipation at the rear is governed exclusively by thermal radiation and conduction through the built wall. This imbalance leads to a localized heat accumulation behind the moving roller, which is the primary driver for the asymmetric tusk-shaped temperature field.This directional heat flow helps stabilize the deposition by preventing the re-melting of previously solidified layers. A parametric study quantified the effects of current (I), speed (V), and contact width (W) on the peak temperature and melt zone extent (Fig. 6, Table 4). Within the studied range, every 10 A increase in current raised the peak temperature by approximately 300°C. Increasing the speed by 100 mm/min reduced the peak temperature by about 150°C, as the heating time per unit length decreased. Increasing the contact width by 0.2 mm reduced the peak temperature by roughly 200°C, due to a decrease in current density for a fixed current. Critically, the simulations identify parameter windows that achieve sufficient melting for bonding without excessive heat input that causes collapse or excessive remelting of lower layers.

Macrostructure and geometry analysis

Figure 7 presents the macroscopic top-view morphologies of the single-bead five-layer walls fabricated under varying process parameters (current: 100–140 A, speed: 100–300 mm/min, and section width: 0.6–1.4 mm). The results demonstrate that the optimal macroscopic quality, characterized by a flat surface devoid of deformation or gaps, is achieved at a current of 120 A, a speed of 200 mm/min, and a section width of 1.0 mm. Deviations from these optimal values led to significant degradation in forming quality. Specifically, fluctuations in current or section width altered the degree of wire melting, shifting the morphology from under-melted (poor adhesion) to over-melted (collapse). Similarly, variations in deposition speed disrupted the stability of the layer formation, resulting in inconsistent surface quality.

Macroscopic morphology of single-bead multi-layer walls under different process parameters: (a) varying current. (b) varying speed. (c) varying section width.

The impact of varying current, speed, and section width on the cross-sectional morphology, forming size, and overlap rate of single-bead multilayer walls is depicted in Fig. 8a, b, and c. Within a certain range, a gradual increase in current enhances the forming width of a single-bead multilayer wall while simultaneously reducing the forming height. However, the change in width is significantly less pronounced than that in height, and the overlap rate progressively increases due to the heightened heat production associated with larger currents.Physically, the increase in current leads to higher Joule heat generation (), which raises the melt pool temperature. This elevated temperature reduces the viscosity and surface tension of the molten metal, thereby enhancing its fluidity and wettability. Consequently, the molten metal spreads more readily across the substrate, resulting in a wider bead and a reduced layer height. On the other hand, increasing the speed significantly reduces the bonding rate, leading to a decrease in the forming width and an increase in the forming height. This shift is attributed to the reduction in linear heat input (Joules per mm) as speed increases. With less thermal energy delivered per unit length, the volume of the molten pool decreases, and the solidification rate accelerates. The molten metal solidifies before it can fully spread outwards, leading to a narrower width and a piled-up morphology. Additionally, increasing the section width solely increases the forming height, with both the forming width and overlap rate decreasing. Notably, a shift from a 0.9 mm to a 1.0 mm section width resulted in the most significant decrease in the overlap rate, decreasing by 38.71% compared to the preceding period. This decrease is due to the increased section width and decreased current density, leading to dispersed heat production and reduced wire melting.

Surface roughness and hardness analysis

The surface quality of the contact surface is crucial in JHAM, as it directly impacts heat production within the system. Fig. 9a, b, c, and 9d illustrate the measurement positions and results of surface roughness for a single-bead multilayer wall. Due to significant size variations on the side of the single-bead multilayer wall caused by the step effect, analyzing surface roughness on this surface is not critical. Consequently, measurements were conducted solely on the top surface, revealing that the surface roughness Ra could reach as low as 0.142 μm.It is critical to clarify that the minimum Ra of 0.142 μm reported here is measured strictly on the top surface. This exceptional smoothness is not a natural result of wire melting but is achieved through the in-situ hot-burnishing (ironing) effect. The copper roller applies continuous mechanical pressure to the semi-molten metal during deposition, flattening the surface irregularities before final solidification, effectively acting as an integrated finishing process. The side surfaces, however, naturally retain the step-effect typical of layer-by-layer manufacturing. Fig. 9e and f elucidate the influences of different process parameters on the surface roughness Ra and Rz of a single-bead multilayer wall, as well as the section hardness. Both Ra and Rz initially decrease and then increase with increasing process parameters across the three groups. Conversely, the section hardness exhibits a non-monotonic trend. Initially, the hardness increases as the process parameters optimize the layer density. However, with excessive heat input, the hardness demonstrates a declining trend. This reduction in hardness (softening) under high heat input is attributed to the grain coarsening effect, governed by the Hall-Petch relationship. Prolonged residence at elevated temperatures and slower cooling rates in the vacuum environment promote grain growth, which reduces the density of grain boundaries and consequently lowers the mechanical resistance. Although detailed EBSD analysis is beyond the scope of this work, the observed hardness trends are consistent with these thermally-driven microstructural changes. Future microstructural investigations are required to definitively correlate these hardness variations with grain size evolution via the Hall-Petch relationship.Moreover, as depicted in Fig. 9(i), the three-dimensional perspective surface scan of the single-bead multilayer wall exhibits a continuous and flat surface morphology without obvious macroscopic surface defects such as un-melted gaps or severe distortion. This macroscopic integrity further substantiates the technological feasibility of the JHAM process.

Model validation

A key validation of the multiphysics model is the comparison of the electrical response. Fig. 6h plots the simulated and experimentally measured voltage across the roller and substrate during a deposition run at I = 120 A, V = 200 mm/min. The simulated voltage remains stable at ~600 mV, reflecting the steady-state contact resistance in the model. The experimental voltage fluctuates around this value (e.g., 550-650 mV), which is expected due to minor variations in real-time contact pressure and surface conditions.The fidelity of the multiphysics model is corroborated by both electrical and geometric responses. While the average voltage error remains below 10%, a comparative analysis of the simulated melt pool dimensions and the experimental bead cross-sections (as shown in Fig. 4 and Fig. 5) reveals a strong correlation in geometric trends. The model successfully predicts the expansion of the fusion zone with increasing current and its contraction with increasing speed, thereby validating its predictive capability for the JHAM process. Beyond the electrical response, the model’s fidelity is further corroborated by geometric trends. As demonstrated in both the simulated melt pool dimensions and the experimental cross-sections (Fig. 4 and Fig. 5), the predicted trends—where increasing current widens the fusion zone while increasing speed narrows it—show strong qualitative and quantitative agreement. This confirms that the model reliably captures the thermal-structural coupling during the deposition process.

Conclusion

This work presents a comprehensive investigation into Joule-Heating Additive Manufacturing (JHAM) through integrated multiphysics simulation and experimental validation. The main conclusions are:

The numerical model successfully predicts the formation of a distinctive "tusk-shaped" temperature field in multi-layer deposition, arising from the dynamic balance between localized Joule heating, heat conduction to the roller, and accumulation in the track.
