A 3-dimensional modeling workflow to guide left pulmonary artery reimplantation
Authors: Dominic P Recco, Shannen B Kizilski, Noah E Schulz, Lauren E Marshall, Nao Sasaki, Aditya K Kaza, Benjamin Zendejas, Peter E Hammer, Christopher W Baird, David M Hoganson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivePatients with a left pulmonary artery (LPA) sling often require surgical reimplantation to address pulmonary flow maldistribution and tracheal compression. We developed a virtual modeling workflow to determine what length of LPA should be retained for reimplantation and provided these data for intraoperative guidance.MethodsFrom March 2024 to May 2025, 5 patients diagnosed with an LPA sling with (n = 3) or without (n = 2) tracheal stenosis underwent surgical LPA reimplantation with or without slide tracheoplasty at median age 1.8 years [range, 0.4-3.6 years]. All patients had preoperative surgical planning using measurements from a segmented 3-dimensional model using a digital workflow. Model measurements included distances from the main pulmonary artery reimplantation site to nearby landmarks and the length of pressurized, prestretched LPA that should be retained for reimplantation based on the distance between the reimplant site and anticipated extent of distal LPA mobilization. These measurements were used as intraoperative guidance.ResultsThe planned main pulmonary artery reimplant site varied across surgeons, and recommended LPA length ranged from 42% to 71% of the total native length. Model recommendations were closely followed in all patients with LPA sling. Median hospital length of stay was 7 days [6-15]. At discharge, 3 patients had no/trivial LPA stenosis and 2 had mild (peak gradient 23 mm Hg [20, 26]). At median follow-up of 17.6 months [6.5-22.1], all patients had no/trivial stenosis and were asymptomatic with 100% freedom from reintervention. Postoperative lung-perfusion and computed tomography scans demonstrated left/right split of 40/60% ± 2% (n = 3) and LPA diameter z score of −0.23 [−0.82, +0.31] (n = 2).ConclusionsThree-dimensional models provide a platform for quantitative surgical planning to help achieve targeted reconstruction with good initial clinical follow-up.Key Words: cardiovascular surgery, congenital heart surgery, left pulmonary artery sling, aberrant pulmonary arteries

Virtual model measurements guide length of aberrant LPA to retain for MPA reimplantation.

Central Message

Five pediatric patients with aberrant LPA underwent surgical reimplantation with targeted LPA length and MPA reimplant site measurements determined preoperatively using a 3D modeling workflow.

Perspective

Digital 3D models allow surgeons to plan and follow precise geometric targets for congenital heart surgeries. Models based on cross-sectional imaging detail the anatomy under physiologic pressure, enabling surgical plans to account for tissue stretch and surrounding structures. Thoughtful development of modeling workflows may promote more effective surgical strategies for complex cases.

Left pulmonary artery (LPA) sling (also referred to as aberrant LPA) is a rare congenital vascular anomaly with an incidence of 0.14% in patients with congenital heart disease and prevalence of 6 in 100,000 school-aged children.1,2 LPA sling is often associated with airway anomalies such as congenital tracheal and/or bronchial stenosis resulting in respiratory compromise; notably, complete cartilaginous rings are reported in 50% to 100% of patients with PA sling.3, 4, 5, 6 Patients who are asymptomatic may be followed clinically, but symptomatic presentation usually dictates surgical intervention, given unlikely spontaneous improvement.7

Seventy years after Potts performed the first LPA sling repair,8 current surgical management involves division of the LPA at its right pulmonary artery (RPA) origin and reimplantation onto the main pulmonary artery (MPA) along with concomitant repair of tracheobronchial stenosis and malacia and/or intracardiac structural defects.5,9, 10, 11 Patient prognosis is significantly impacted by associated tracheobronchial and intracardiac anomalies; reported mortality is 11% to 48% in patients with tracheal involvement and 0% to 12% without.1,5,9,10,12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18 Reintervention rates for isolated LPA sling repair are up to 25%,14 whereas patients undergoing concomitant tracheal or intracardiac surgery present increased risk, ranging from 15% to 96% for airway and from 0% to 41% for PA reintervention.5,9,10,12,15, 16, 17, 18 Correction of ductal origin of branch pulmonary artery (PA) has similarly poor outcomes, with 45% to 69% of patients requiring postoperative surgical or interventional pulmonary arterioplasty.19, 20, 21 High reintervention rates are unsurprising because previous studies have shown that 6% to 79% of patients undergoing PA reimplantation demonstrate postoperative PA stenosis.5,16,17,19,21 Furthermore, on lung-perfusion scans, the percentage of blood flow to the left lung varies from 25% to 41%9,10,20 and 7% to 59% of patients continue to display symptoms.5,12, 13, 14,21

We suspect that the moderately high morbidity associated with aberrant LPA management is partially attributed to technical aspects related to LPA reimplantation, resulting in vascular redundancy or tethering. LPA reimplantation is performed in an unpressurized state intraoperatively, and prediction of the postoperative size and tortuosity of the repressurized vessel is challenging. In this study, we present a workflow using preoperative cross-sectional imaging to generate digital 3-dimensional (3D) models, from which we quantify the length of LPA that should be included in the reimplanted segment to preserve the native prestretch. Using these models, surgeons were provided with measurable intraoperative targets for the reconstructed anatomy to reduce morbidity associated with aberrant LPA repair.

Methods

Study Design

The study was approved by Boston Children's Hospital IRB-P00053104 (November 11, 2025), with the need for patient consent waived. A single-center retrospective review was conducted of all pediatric patients undergoing LPA sling repair between March 2024 and May 2025. All patients underwent preoperative cross-sectional imaging, 3D model creation, and preoperative surgical planning to determine the MPA reimplantation site and length of the LPA segment needed to preserve native prestretch.

Preoperative 3D Modeling Workflow

A digital 3D model of the pertinent anatomy, representing the surface at the interface between tissue and the blood pool, was created from preoperative computed tomography (CT) imaging using Mimics Medical 26.0 (Materialise). An LPA centerline was defined and extended from the LPA takeoff, proximally, to the left upper pulmonary artery (LUPA) branching point, distally. The distal extent of the centerline served as the “pivot point” around which the LPA was anticipated to rotate upon reimplantation (Figure 1, A). Under a surgeon’s guidance, a virtual marker was placed at the center of the targeted MPA reimplantation site (Figure 1, B). For intraoperative reference, surface measurements were taken from the virtual marker to the (1) medial aspect of the LPA takeoff and to the (2) lateral aspect of the patent ductus arteriosus/ductal ligament. The distance from the pivot point to the center of the MPA reimplant site was measured (Figure 1, C). This same distance was then measured from the pivot point along the LPA centerline, and a perpendicular cut plane was created to represent the portion of LPA to retain for reimplantation (Figure 1, D). However, because surgeons are only able to make surface measurements intraoperatively, the distance from pivot point to cut plane was remeasured along the superior-anterior surface of the LPA (Figure 1, E). Surgeons were provided with 3 key surface distances along the pressurized native vasculature: (1) LPA takeoff to MPA reimplantation site center, (2) patent ductus arteriosus/ductal ligament to MPA reimplantation site center, and (3) LUPA branch to location of LPA transection. To ensure appropriate sizing of the LPA at the neobifurcation, diameters at the proximal LPA cut plane were taken and converted into z scores. If the reimplanted LPA diameter z score was hypoplastic (ie, less than −2), then patch augmentation was recommended.

Intraoperative Surgical Workflow

The repair of LPA sling was performed as previously described22 (Video 1). To summarize, the patient was positioned close to the head of the table to facilitate bronchoscopy, if applicable. Sternotomy was performed. The aorta was dissected from the RPA to expose the aberrant LPA. The ligamentum (or ductus) arteriosus was ligated and divided, if applicable. The recurrent laryngeal nerve was identified and preserved. The LPA lobar branches were mobilized to prevent kinking or distortion with reimplantation. In cases of left superior vena cava, the vena cava was sufficiently mobilized to allow reimplantation of the LPA in approximately the “normal” anatomic origin on the MPA. With the PAs still pressurized, the surgeon used pre-cut silks of lengths matching measurements from the modeling workflow to mark the MPA reimplantation site and the LPA length on the basis of the anatomic references. Using partial occlusion clamps, the LPA was transected from its aberrant origin. The stump was closed with a double-layered running 7-0 polypropylene suture. Aneurysm clips were placed on the LPA branches to prevent back bleeding. The LPA was carefully dissected away from the trachea and esophagus and pulled through this space to a position anterior to the aorta. The depressurized LPA was transected at the location marked in the pressurized, intact state. Depending on the surgeon’s preference, the proximal end was spatulated to both widen the orifice at the planned anastomosis and to create a more natural curvilinear neobifurcation. Cardiopulmonary bypass was initiated. An elliptical opening was excised from the MPA centered on the marked reimplant site. The LPA was anastomosed to the MPA with running fine polypropylene suture. Patch augmentation of the LPA and/or MPA was performed if needed, and concomitant slide tracheoplasty was executed as needed along with intracardiac repairs.

Results

Preoperative Patient Characteristics and Echocardiographic Measurements

Five patients met inclusion criteria (Table 1). All 5 patients had a primary cardiac diagnosis of LPA sling. Secondary diagnoses included tracheal stenosis (n = 3), left superior vena cava to coronary sinus (n = 2), right aortic arch with mirror image branching (n = 1), and tetralogy of Fallot (ToF) (n = 1). One patient had undergone LPA translocation with slide tracheoplasty previously. Most patients (4/5) presented with obstructive respiratory symptoms, 2 with reduced left lung perfusion, and 1 with dysphagia. Preoperative lung-perfusion scans were completed in 2 patients with left lung perfusion measured at 16% (patient 1) and 36% (patient 3). On preoperative echocardiogram, pulmonary valve and biventricular function were normal in 4 of 5 patients, with patient 2 having severe pulmonary stenosis (PS) in the setting of ToF. The RPA diameter z score was normal (ie, between −2 and 2) in all patients, whereas the MPA was mildly hypoplastic in patient 2 (ToF/PS) but normal sized in all others. The LPA was not visualized by echocardiogram in 3 of 5 patients but was normal sized in all on CT.

3D Model Recommendations

The planned LPA length measured from the LUPA branching point ranged from 11.1 mm to 19.0 mm (Figure 2), corresponding to 42% to 71% of the total native LPA length measured from anomalous origin (Table 1). The LPA diameter z scores at the planned proximal cut plane ranged from −1.62 to +0.66. No patients had a predicted cut-plane diameter z score less than −2, and, therefore, patch augmentation was not advised. Targeted MPA reimplant sites were defined preoperatively by the surgeon and were measured using landmarks noted in Table 1.

Operative Details

Five patients underwent LPA reimplantation at median age 1.8 years [range 0.4-3.6 years], weight 11.2 kg [6.8-13.4 kg], and body surface area 0.51 m2 [0.36-0.60 m2] (Table 2). Concomitant procedures included tracheo/bronchoplasty in 3 patients, patent foramen ovale closure in 1 patient, and ToF repair in 1 patient. Recommendations on MPA reimplant site were followed within 1 mm over all 5 cases. Across the patients with an LPA sling, recommendations for preserved LPA length were followed within 2.3 mm, with guidance exactly followed in 2 patients (patients 2 and 5) and slightly more LPA preserved in 3 patients (patients 1, 3, and 4). In patient 1, the LPA was marked according to 3D model guidance but was subsequently transected 2.5 mm proximal to this location because of concern for inadequate length. There was 1 case of patch augmentation from the proximal MPA into the LPA neo-origin (patient 2). For patient 2, given the mildly hypoplastic nature of the MPA in the setting of ToF, it was opened longitudinally from the annulus to just past the target LPA insertion site. The LPA proximal end was spatulated to achieve an anatomically normal position, given the curvature of the neobifurcation, and then an autologous pericardial patch was used to augment the MPA and LPA origin at the reimplantation site. All patients had unobstructed laminar LPA flow on postbypass epicardial echocardiogram, although patient 3 demonstrated mild stenosis at the distal LPA with a peak gradient of 20 mm Hg.

Postoperative and Follow-Up Outcomes and Echocardiographic Measurements

Median postoperative hospital length of stay was 7 days [6-15 days] (Table 2). Predischarge echocardiogram demonstrated normal pulmonary valve and biventricular function, except in patient 3, in whom PS was decreased to mild. The LPA measured normal size with no/trivial stenosis in 3 of 5 patients, mild stenosis in patient 3 (peak gradient 20 mm Hg), and slightly hypoplastic with mild stenosis in patient 2 (diameter z score −2.48, peak gradient 26 mm Hg) (Figure 3). At follow-up echocardiogram 13.4 months [2.5-22.1] from surgery (n = 4), the LPA measured normal size with trivial stenosis in all patients. Two patients underwent postoperative CT, which demonstrated normal-sized LPA diameter z scores of +0.31 (patient 4) and −0.82 (patient 5). Three patients underwent postoperative lung-perfusion scans, which demonstrated left lung perfusion ranging from 38% to 42%. At median follow-up of 17.6 months [6.5-22.1], all patients were alive and asymptomatic, and no patient required reintervention.

Discussion

Herein, we report a case series of LPA reimplantation for LPA sling using a preoperative digital workflow of 3D modeling and virtual surgery to inform the length of LPA needed to maintain native prestretch and prevent vascular mis-sizing after reconstruction. In summary, 5 patients underwent LPA reimplantation guided by a workflow in which the MPA reimplant site was defined by measurements from nearby anatomic landmarks, and the required pressurized, prestretched LPA length was measured from the LUPA takeoff to the selected site. Targeted LPA lengths ranged from 42% to 71% of the total distance from the proximal LPA origin to the LUPA, and all surgeries followed the plan with minor discrepancies between the preoperative targets and intraoperative measurements. After surgery, median length of stay was 7 days and all patients had normal sized LPAs (between z score −1.00 and + 0.49) and trivial stenosis at latest imaging. Furthermore, postoperative ventilation-perfusion scans demonstrated relatively normal LPA flow distribution (38%-42%, n = 3) compared with preoperative (16%, 36%, n = 2). At median follow-up of 1.5 years, all patients were alive and asymptomatic, and no patient required reintervention.

This work exemplifies the need for patient-specific surgical planning and the advantages of using modeling to inform intraoperative targets. Patient anatomy was highly variable—distances from the anatomic landmarks to the planned MPA reimplant site ranged drastically as did the percentage of total LPA length preserved. This anatomic variability is best accounted for with a patient-specific approach, challenging the traditional practice of simply transecting the LPA at its aberrant origin and using the entire length. Furthermore, the course of the LPA could not be visualized on echocardiogram in 3 patients, and 3 patients had concomitant tracheobronchial disease requiring intervention and/or vascular anomalies (eg, bilateral superior vena cava, right aortic arch with mirror image branching, etc). Cross-sectional imaging is often necessary to fully define the anatomic relationships of such anomalies and can be exploited to generate high-resolution 3D models for both spatial visualization and surgical planning.

Although there are several potential benefits of the described workflow, successful implementation requires diligent coordination between the surgical and engineering teams. In our practice, engineers reviewed the MPA reimplant site with the surgeons preoperatively and relayed essential elements of the workflow including the targeted preserved LPA length from relevant anatomic references. Intraoperatively, the surgeons performed measurements in a pressurized, prestretched state (to mimic the 3D model) and communicated any changes in the MPA reimplant site to the engineer to allow for real-time adjustments and an updated LPA target length. Attention to such details was required for accurate measurements and translation of the virtual surgery steps into clinical practice. In fact, the MPA reimplant site was followed within 1 mm of the suggested target and the largest LPA length discrepancy was 2.5 mm across the 5 sling cases. Lastly, this workflow can provide guidance on the need for LPA augmentation at the maintained length depending on the measured diameter z score at the anticipated cut plane. If the diameter at the predicted cut-plane is abnormal (ie, less than z score −2), recommendations for LPA augmentation can be provided based on the difference between the measured preoperative and targeted postoperative diameters. The technical information of the computed surgical plan and 3D model imaging is provided to the surgeon as adjunct information in their clinical decision making during the operation. The final implementation of the surgical plan and any adjustments depending on the intraoperative findings (tissue quality, adequacy of mobilization, etc) rests with the surgeon. One example may be in smaller patients with tracheobronchial stenosis who may not tolerate extensive dissection before bypass initiation. In this cohort, it may be appropriate to mark the MPA reimplant site initially and then approximate the LPA length on bypass. However, in this scenario, one must be cognizant that the LPA may not be in its native pressurized state, which will impact the overall accuracy of the workflow.

Overall, the mortality and morbidity of LPA sling and ductal origin of the LPA repair is high with standard techniques. In isolated LPA sling repair, mortality is up to 12% and the need for PA reintervention up to 25% with rates approaching 50% with associated tracheobronchial and intracardiac anomalies.1,5,9,10,12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18 Previous studies have shown that up to 80% of patients undergoing PA reimplantation demonstrate postoperative PA stenosis,5,16,17,19,21 supported by postoperative perfusion scans demonstrating flow to the left lung as low as 25%9,10,20 and up to 60% of patients continuing to display symptoms.5,12, 13, 14,21 In fact, we have previously reported on our institution's experience with LPA sling repair from 1983 to 2018.10 Forty-two patients underwent either isolated LPA sling repair (n = 16), LPA sling/intracardiac repair (n = 10), or LPA sling/tracheal repair (n = 16). At 2 years postoperatively, survival rates were 100% (isolated LPA sling repair), 65% (concomitant intracardiac repair), and 64% (concomitant tracheal surgery); freedom from isolated LPA intervention was 100% after isolated LPA sling repair, 93% after LPA sling/tracheal surgery, and 69% after LPA sling/intracardiac repair; and freedom from isolated tracheal intervention was 92% after isolated LPA sling repair, 78% after LPA sling/intracardiac repair, and 73% after LPA sling/tracheal surgery. At a median follow-up of 1.5 years, none of the 5 patients in this series have died or have undergone an LPA or tracheal reintervention. In the historic cohort, standard practice was to transect the LPA at its RPA origin and reimplant the entire segment onto the MPA. Our modeling efforts suggest that only a fraction of the LPA length is needed for the repair, and we hypothesize that retaining excessive length may predispose patients to recurrent stenosis of the LPA or trachea. We suspect that the moderately high morbidity associated with traditional LPA implantation techniques is partially attributed to technical aspects resulting in vascular mis-sizing as the procedure is performed in an unpressurized state intraoperatively and prediction of the postoperative size and tortuosity of the repressurized vessel is challenging. With preoperative 3D modeling, surgeons are provided with measurable intraoperative targets for the reconstructed anatomy to help reduce morbidity associated with aberrant LPA repair. The use of virtual surgical planning tools, focused primarily on model visualization and interactive manipulation for anatomic reconstruction, is gaining momentum in the field of congenital cardiac surgery. These tools have been already used for airway reconstruction, intracardiac baffles for double-outlet right ventricle repair, unifocalization in the setting of major aortopulmonary collateral arteries, biventricular and Fontan planning, ventricular septal defect closure, and ductal stenting.23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28 Even without formal virtual surgery calculations, preoperative visualization of the segmented anatomy can help the surgeon to plan their approach. Furthermore, these tools have demonstrated a role in patient education and reduction in preoperative anxiety.29,30 The integration of 3D modeling and virtual surgery in cardiothoracic surgery continues to expand as prospective planning tools become increasingly sophisticated and clinically practical.

Conclusions

Three-dimensional modeling enables preoperative, patient-specific planning of LPA reimplantation, offering precise intraoperative guidance to preserve native prestretch and address challenges related to postoperative vessel size and tortuosity after re-pressurization. This workflow is feasible and may facilitate more effective surgical strategies for LPA reimplantation to reduce reinterventions and residual lesions.
