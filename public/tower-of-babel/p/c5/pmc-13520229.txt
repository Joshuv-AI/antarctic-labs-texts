Mentorship perception and experience among pediatric surgical trainees: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Abdullah F. Alshehri, Salman Theeb Alwadani, Yasser Alfraih, Tariq Altokhais
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMentorship is essential in surgical education and fosters professional growth, skill development, and career advancement. Despite its recognized importance, structured mentorship remains underdeveloped in pediatric surgical residency programs, particularly in small and specialized fields where limited faculty availability may restrict access to mentorship. This study aimed to measure mentorship prevalence, assess satisfaction, identify barriers to effective mentorship, and explore residents’ preferences for structured mentorship models in the Saudi National Pediatric Surgery Residency Program.MethodsA cross sectional survey was distributed to all residents of the Saudi National Pediatric Surgery Residency Program. The 27 question survey assessed mentorship relationships, satisfaction, perceived benefits, communication preferences, and barriers. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0, with significance set at p < 0.05.ResultsOf the 41 respondents (80% response rate), 60% were female, and 56% were under 30 years of age. Although 98% recognized the importance of mentorship, only 17 of 41 (41.5%) reported having a mentor. Key barriers included the absence of a structured mentorship program with clear objectives (61%), mentor time constraints (58.5%), and a lack of scheduled meetings (53.6%). Among mentored residents, 12 of 17 (70.5%) were satisfied, and 11 of 17 (64.7%) felt that their mentors provided strong skill development opportunities.ConclusionSignificant gaps exist between the perceived value and availability of mentorship in pediatric surgical residency programs. Formalized and structured mentorship programs with clear objectives and regular meetings are crucial for improving mentorship effectiveness. These findings support the development of more structured mentorship frameworks within pediatric surgery training programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09814-3.

Introduction

Mentorship is a fundamental component of medical training that facilitates knowledge transfer, professional development, and personal growth across generations [1, 2]. In this context, mentorship refers to a developmental relationship in which a more experienced surgeon supports a trainee’s professional, academic, and personal growth. In surgical education, mentorship represents more than passive role modeling; it is an active, structured relationship in which experienced surgeons guide trainees toward achieving technical competence, academic productivity, and career advancement [3, 4]. Within the demanding environment of surgical training, mentorship provides psychological support, fosters confidence, and mitigates the effects of stress and burnout that are common among residents [5–9]. Despite extensive literature emphasizing the benefits of mentorship in surgical programs [10–12], little is known about mentorship practices in pediatric surgery, a small and highly demanding specialty with limited training positions and faculty. Pediatric surgical trainees often face unique challenges, including fewer mentors with specialty expertise, higher patient complexity, and increased emotional workload, all of which may hinder the establishment of effective mentorship relationships. Previous reports from other surgical disciplines have demonstrated that structured mentorship programs enhance satisfaction, skill acquisition, and academic output, whereas unstructured or informal systems can lead to inconsistent access and inequitable experiences [8, 10–12]. However, the current status of mentorship within pediatric surgery residency training, particularly in Saudi Arabia, remains unclear. The Saudi pediatric surgery residency program is a structured six-year training program. Unlike systems in which pediatric surgery training is completed after a full general surgery residency and subsequent fellowship, the Saudi pathway provides dedicated pediatric surgery training through a national residency structure. Residents rotate through different training sites during the program, which broadens clinical exposure but may also limit continuity in mentor-mentee relationships. Understanding the perceptions and experiences of residents is essential to identify existing gaps and inform evidence based improvements in mentorship structures. Therefore, this study aimed to measure the prevalence of mentorship, assess satisfaction among mentored residents, identify key barriers to effective mentorship, and explore residents’ preferences for structured mentorship models within the Saudi National Pediatric Surgery Residency Program, thereby providing a preliminary gap assessment to inform future mentorship development.

Methods

After obtaining approval from the King Saud University Institutional Review Board (KSU-IRB. No. 24/0965/IRB), a cross-sectional study was conducted between July and September 2023 to evaluate pediatric surgery residents’ perceptions and experiences of mentorship within the Saudi National Pediatric Surgery Residency Program. The 27-item survey was distributed electronically via WhatsApp, the official communication channel for residents. One reminder message was sent after one week. Participation was voluntary, no incentives were offered, and no identifying information (names, emails, or IP addresses) was collected to ensure anonymity.

The questionnaire was developed specifically for this study after reviewing and adapting items from previously published instruments on mentorship [12–15]. Items reflected four domains identified in prior literature: career guidance, psychosocial support, operative skill development, and academic engagement. Content validity was reviewed by a panel of three pediatric surgery faculty members and two senior residents for relevance and clarity. Because all pediatric surgery residents were invited to participate, pilot testing was conducted with four nonparticipant general surgery residents to assess clarity, comprehension, and timing. The final version (Supplementary File 1) included sections on demographics, mentorship experience, goals and benefits, communication and satisfaction, and barriers and preferences. Training level was categorized as junior residents, defined as postgraduate years 1–3, and senior residents, defined as postgraduate years 4–6.

Responses were recorded using a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). Likert-scale items were treated as ordinal categorical variables, and agreement was defined as scores ≥ 4 (“agree” or “strongly agree”). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize frequencies and percentages. Exploratory chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests were applied to examine associations between mentorship status and categorical variables (gender, training level, and perceived barriers), with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. Informed consent was obtained electronically from all participants before completing the survey, and submission implied voluntary participation. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Mentorship Status and Resident Characteristics

There were no significant demographic differences between residents with and without mentors regarding age, gender, or training level. Although this difference did not reach statistical significance, a higher proportion of female residents reported not having access to a mentor compared with male residents (63% vs. 43.7%, respectively).

Resident mentorship characteristics

Although nearly all respondents 40/41 (98%) recognized the value of mentorship for success in their training and future careers, only 17 (41.5%) reported having a mentor. Nearly two-thirds of those without a mentor expressed the desire to have a mentor. All residents with mentors (n = 17) sought mentors independently, with 8/17 (47%) being mentored by a senior faculty member, 5/17 (29.4%) by their program director, and 4/17 (23.5%) by their department heads (Table 1).

Preferences and goals for mentorship

When asked about their preferred mentorship structure, 17/41 (41.5%) preferred a structured and mandatory model, 11/41 (27%) preferred a structured but optional model, 8/41 (19.5%) favored an informal yet encouraged program, and 5/41 (12%) believed that it should be the residents’ responsibility to initiate mentorship (Table 1). Most residents viewed mentorship as an essential tool for professional and personal development. The most frequently identified mentorship goals were career guidance and operative skill improvement 37/41 (90.2%), followed by professional development 36/41 (87.8%), clinical confidence building 35/41 (85.4%), exam performance support and conflict resolution 29/41 (70.7%), research involvement 25/41 (61%) and personal life guidance 16/41 (41.5%) (Table 2).

Perspective and Factors in Selecting a Mentor

When selecting a mentor, the most important factors for residents were involvement in training 36/41 (87.8%), availability 31/41 (75.6%), relatability 27/41 (65.9%), and specialty alignment 26/41 (58.5%). Additional considerations included mentors’ age 11/41 (26.8%), research involvement 8/41 (19.5%), and gender 7/41 (17.1%) (Table 2).

Mentor-mentee interaction

Communication methods with mentors varied, with 9/17 (53%) reporting in person interactions and 8/17 (47%) using phone or text. The frequency of communication varied: daily 2/17 (11.7%), weekly 3/17 (17.6%), monthly 5/17 (29.4%), biannually 4/17 (23.5%) and annually 3/17 (17.6%) (Table 3). Discussions primarily focused on clinical training 17/17 (100%), career planning 13/17 (76.5%), research 11/17 (64.7%), work life balance 9/17 (52.9%), networking 5/17 (29.4%), and financial matters 2/17 (11.7%).

Outcomes of mentoring

Among those with mentors (n = 17), satisfaction levels were generally positive, with 12 (70.5%) being either very satisfied or satisfied, while 5 (29.4%) remaining neutral. Regarding feedback on performance and progress, 10 (58.8%) reported that their mentors provided feedback extremely well, 5 (29.4%) provided feedback somewhat well, and 2 (11.7%) were neutral. The comfort levels for approaching mentors with questions or concerns were as follows: 10 (58.8%) extremely comfortable, 6 (35.3%) somewhat comfortable, and 1 (5.9%) neutral. Additionally, 11 (64.7%) indicated that their mentors provided opportunities to develop and enhance their skills extremely well, 5 (29.4%) somewhat well, and 1 (5.9%) were neutral (Table 3).

Barriers to mentorship

The most commonly reported barriers were the lack of formalized objectives 25/41 (61%), lack of time among mentors 24/41 (58.5%), lack of formalized meeting time 22/41 (53.6%), lack of personal connection with their mentors 21/41 (51%), and lack of mentors with similar personal and professional goals 18/41 (44%). Additional concerns included the lack of skilled mentors 13/41 (31.7%), lack of mentors with the same background 12/41 (29%), and lack of mentors of the same gender 3/41 (7%) (Table 4).

Discussion

This study provides the first national assessment of mentorship experiences among pediatric surgery residents in Saudi Arabia. Although nearly all respondents recognized mentorship as essential to professional growth, fewer than half reported having a mentor, a rate lower than that described in several other surgical programs [13, 14, 16, 17]. These findings suggest that pediatric surgery trainees may face structural barriers to mentorship, including smaller faculty pools, geographically dispersed rotations, and high clinical demands.

In the absence of a formal mentorship structure, residents relied entirely on self-initiated relationships. This may unintentionally favor more assertive or well-connected individuals and contribute to inequitable access to guidance and support.

It is also possible that informal mentorship occurs within the training environment but is not consistently recognized by residents. Faculty may provide clinical guidance, operative feedback, role modeling, or career advice without these interactions being explicitly labeled as mentorship. Therefore, the observed gap may reflect not only limited access to mentors but also a lack of shared definitions, expectations, and visibility around mentorship.

While mentorship is widely considered beneficial, its impact depends on program quality and intentional design. Poorly structured mentorship has been associated with minimal or even negative influence on resident wellbeing and professional satisfaction [3]. Our findings, particularly the reported lack of structured objectives and scheduled meetings, support the need for clear expectations, protected mentorship time, and administrative oversight. Although this study did not measure burnout or academic productivity directly, prior work has linked structured mentorship with reduced burnout and improved job satisfaction among surgical trainees [9, 17–20]. These associations should be explored in future longitudinal studies.

Gender differences in mentorship access also warrant attention. A higher proportion of female residents reported lacking a mentor compared with male residents, although this difference was not statistically significant. This pattern is consistent with broader surgical literature [14] and may reflect institutional or cultural factors influencing mentor selection, approachability, and comfort. Programs should therefore consider proactive strategies to ensure equitable mentorship opportunities for all trainees.

Residents expressed a strong preference for structured mentorship models, although views differed regarding whether participation should be mandatory. The most commonly cited barriers, including lack of formal objectives, mentor time constraints, and irregular meetings, reflect both organizational limitations and the workload typical of small surgical subspecialties. Addressing these barriers requires institutional recognition of mentorship as a core educational responsibility rather than an optional activity.

The goals identified by residents suggest that mentorship needs may differ across domains. Career guidance, operative skill improvement, research involvement, psychosocial support, and professional development are related but distinct areas of support. Operative skill improvement and performance feedback may align more closely with coaching, whereas career planning and professional identity formation are more consistent with traditional mentorship. Research guidance may require academic advising, while career advancement may benefit from sponsorship.

Recognizing these distinctions may help future programs match residents with appropriate faculty support rather than relying on a single mentorship model for all needs.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The cross-sectional design captures perceptions at a single point in time, limiting causal interpretation. Data were self-reported and may be influenced by social desirability bias. The survey was confined to one national training program with a small specialty cohort, and despite a high response rate, nonresponse bias cannot be excluded. Subgroup analyses were underpowered to detect small differences.

Burnout, academic productivity, operative performance, and other objective outcomes were not measured. Additionally, the survey did not include qualitative free-text items, limiting deeper exploration of residents’ motivations and experiences. The survey also may not have fully captured informal or unstructured mentorship, as residents may not identify clinical teaching, role modeling, operative feedback, as mentorship unless these interactions are explicit and longitudinal. In addition, the study reflects only the trainee perspective and does not capture mentor perspectives, mentor characteristics, or faculty-level barriers.

Finally, the findings are most directly applicable to the Saudi pediatric surgery training context, and mentorship expectations may differ across countries, cultures, and residency structures. Nevertheless, this study provides valuable foundational insight into how pediatric surgical residents perceive and experience mentorship and identifies clear priorities for programmatic improvement.

Implications and future directions

This study highlights a gap between the perceived importance of mentorship and its availability within pediatric surgical training. While the data do not demonstrate direct adverse outcomes associated with limited mentorship, the findings support structured mentorship approaches that align with trainee preferences and evidence from other surgical disciplines.

In a program with rotations across multiple sites, one feasible approach would be a tiered mentorship model. Each resident could be assigned a longitudinal primary mentor throughout residency, supplemented by site-specific faculty or near-peer mentors during individual rotations. The longitudinal mentor could provide continuity in career guidance, professional development, and academic planning, whereas site-specific mentors could provide immediate clinical feedback and operative coaching.

This structure may balance the benefits of a sustained mentor-mentee relationship with the practical need for accessible support at each training site.

Faculty development initiatives that train mentors in communication, feedback delivery, and goal setting may further enhance mentorship quality and consistency [21, 22]. Given residents’ interest in academic and research guidance, programs may also consider research-focused mentorship tracks that pair trainees with active clinical investigators [23].

Future studies should evaluate structured mentorship programs through longitudinal and multicenter designs, focusing on objective indicators such as academic productivity, clinical competence, wellbeing, and career progression. Qualitative studies exploring mentor-mentee dynamics across genders and institutions could also provide valuable insights into how cultural and institutional factors influence mentorship success [24–27].

Conclusions

Mentorship is highly valued yet remains insufficiently structured among pediatric surgical trainees. Residents identified important barriers, particularly the lack of structured objectives, limited mentor availability, and irregular meetings. These findings support the development of more structured mentorship approaches within pediatric surgery training, with attention to residents’ differing needs for career guidance, clinical coaching, academic advising, and professional support. Future studies should evaluate mentorship models longitudinally and include both trainee and mentor perspectives.
