Unexpected elevation of β-hCG in EGFR-mutant lung adenocarcinoma: a case study and implications for clinical practice
Authors: Xiaomei Zeng, Xiaoyan Qing, Taiguo Liu, Ping Chen, Zhaoyou Jiang, Min Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBeta-human chorionic gonadotropin (β-hCG) is frequently overexpressed in germ cell and trophoblastic tumors as well as various solid tumors; however, the presence of high levels of serum β-hCG is very rare in patients with non-small cell lung cancer.Case DescriptionThis report serves as a case study of an 84-year-old, non-smoking female who was diagnosed with advanced lung adenocarcinoma with bone and pleural metastases. The serum and pleural fluid β-hCG levels were significantly increased (2,618 mIU/ml and > 10,000 mIU/ml, respectively). Next-generation sequencing of the cancerous lung tissue revealed a mutation in the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) gene, consistent with a deletion of exon 19. The patient was treated with gefitinib, an EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor, which resulted in a partial response. Unfortunately, during treatment, the patient developed interstitial lung disease and a pulmonary infection. Despite receiving treatment with steroids and antibiotics, the condition worsened, and the patient ultimately succumbed to the illness and passed away. The overall survival was 77 days.ConclusionsThis is the first report of pulmonary adenocarcinoma with both high serum β-hCG levels and an EGFR mutation.

Introduction

Human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) is a glycoprotein hormone consisting of one α and one β subunit that is produced by trophoblastic cells in the placenta. Women and infants shortly after childbirth as well as individuals with germ cell tumors are typically hCG-positive; however, the carcinogenic effects of hCG are not fully understood [1]. Currently, the majority of the hCG subtypes associated with cancer are hyperglycosylated hCG (hCG-H), free hCG β subunit (β-hCG), and a large variant of hCG β (hCG β-large). Tumor cells can produce hCG-H, β-hCG, or hCG β-large to promote tumor growth, invasion, and metastasis [2]. Elevated serum β-hCG levels have been detected in certain epithelial tumors, such as head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, gastric cancer, ovarian cancer, and bladder cancer [3–5]. Typically, high levels of serum β-hCG are often an indicator of poorer prognosis for cancer patients. Despite increased levels of serum β-hCG in patients with various solid tumors, ectopic expression of β-hCG is not observed in patients with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), although certain isolated cases have been reported [6–26].

Epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) gene mutations in NSCLC cells were initially described in 2004; however, the etiology, clinicopathological features, prognosis, and treatment paradigms of NSCLC harboring EGFR mutations are different compared with those of other NSCLC patients. The frequency of EGFR mutation in lung adenocarcinoma among Asian patients ranges from 40% to 60% [27]; however, the incidence of NSCLC cases with both an EGFR mutation and high levels of β-hCG have not been previously reported.

In the present study, the case of an elderly female with advanced lung adenocarcinoma accompanied by trophoblastic differentiation is reported. This patient featured an EGFR mutation with a deletion of exon 19 and high serum β-hCG expression. The patient was treated with gefitinib, an epidermal growth factor receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitor (EGFR-TKI); however, despite its effectiveness in reducing the size of the adenocarcinoma and its associated symptoms, she experienced several complications that caused her to succumb to the illness.

To our knowledge, this report of a pulmonary adenocarcinoma patient with both high serum β-hCG levels and an EGFR mutation is very rare. This report aims to provide clinicians and researchers with clinically relevant data that can assist in the diagnosis and treatment of patients with similar pathology.

This case report is written following the CARE reporting checklist.

Case presentation

The patient in this case was an 84-year-old nonsmoking female with a performance status(PS) score of 3, who presented with dyspepsia, chest and back pain, and difficulty sitting or standing comfortably. She experienced intermittent cough and chest tightness, but no significant shortness of breath. In the one month prior to the visit, the patient has been a decrease in appetite, weight loss is not significant. Body mass index(BMI) was 18.6 kg/m2. The patient had no history of chronic lung conditions or abortion, and had a history of pregnancy. A chest computed tomography (CT) scan revealed a 4.7 × 4.8 cm solitary mass in the right upper lobe of the lung, along with enlarged lymph nodes in the right hilum and mediastinum (Fig. 1A, D). The patient also had concurrent obstructive pneumonia and pleural effusion. Multiple areas of bone destruction were observed in the thoracic spine and ribs. Unfortunately, we did not conduct pulmonary function tests. Abdominal and pelvic CT examination revealed the possibility of uterine fibroids. However, a PET/CT scan was not performed at our hospital. The patient, who had vertebral metastasis, was in poor general condition with pain, which prevented her from sitting or standing. Therefore, it was difficult for her to visit another hospital for this test. Enhanced CT indicated a uterine fibroid. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans of her head showed no signs of a brain tumor; however, whole-spine MRI indicated the presence of tumor metastasis in the 8th thoracic vertebra. We conducted serum tumor marker testing including β-HCG differentiate between reproductive system tumors because of the uterine mass. Tumor marker testing revealed that her serum β-hCG levels were 2,618 mIU/ml, which were significantly higher compared with the normal upper limit of 7 mIU/ml in nonpregnant women. Blood tests indicate that liver and kidney function are normal, and plasma albumin was 31.8 g/L. A thoracentesis was performed. Due to the elevated serum β-HCG levels, we also conducted β-HCG testing on the pleural effusion, and the pleural fluid analysis revealed β-hCG levels > 10,000 mIU/ml; however, cytological examination of the pleural fluid did not reveal the presence of tumor cells. Based on the findings from this patient’s head, chest, and abdominal examinations, gastrointestinal tumors, liver cancer, reproductive system tumors, and pituitary tumors were currently not under consideration. Additionally, the patient had no recent history of relevant medication use. Given the patient’s lung mass, pleural effusion, and vertebral bone destruction, the clinical assessment suggested a high likelihood of a primary lung tumor.

A CT-guided biopsy of the right lung mass was performed. The tissue was negative for other immunomarkers, including placental alkaline phosphatase, P40, anaplastic lymphoma kinase-V (D5F3), and ROS proto-oncogene 1. Some of the malignant cells were also positive for hCG (Fig. 2). As illustrated in Fig. 3, histopathological examination of the specimen revealed the presence of a poorly differentiated carcinoma with necrosis. The tissue was positive for cytokeratin (CK) 7, occasionally positive for transcription termination factor 1(TTF-1), focally positive for napsin A, and minimally positive for CK5/6 by immunohistochemistry. The immunohistochemical markers related to lung adenocarcinoma, CK7, TTF-1, and napsin A, were positive. The immunohistochemical markers associated with choriocarcinoma and germ cell tumors were as follows: PLAP was negative, and HCG was positive. Combining these findings with the patient’s HE morphology, it was considered to be poorly differentiated lung adenocarcinoma with trophoblastic differentiation. Next-generation sequencing was conducted by Berry Oncology Corporation, Beijing, 102,200, China, which encompassed 457 cancer-related genes. The results indicated the presence of the following mutations: EGFR gene with a deletion of 15 nucleotides at position c.2236–2250 (EGFR19 c.2236-2250del), TP53 gene with a C deletion at exon 3 (TP53 3-exon c80delC), EPHA3 gene with a G-to-A substitution at position c.1543 in exon 7 (EPHA3 7-exon c.1543G > A), and the NAV3 gene with a C-to-T substitution at position c.1895 in exon 8 (NAV3 8-exon c.1895 C > T). The tissue was positive for Programmed Death-Ligand 1 expression with a Tumor Proportion Score of 50% and a Combined Positive Score of 50% The Tumor Mutation Burden was 3.5 mutations per megabase and it was considered microsatellite-stable.

Before the diagnosis, considering the patient’s poor nutritional status and back pain, we provided nutritional support, pain relief, and other palliative treatments. The patient was finally diagnosed with stage Ⅳ right lung adenocarcinoma cT3N2M1c. She was prescribed an oral dose of 250 mg of gefitinib once daily. Because of the presence of metastasis in the thoracic spine and severe cancer-related pain, palliative radiation therapy was performed on the metastatic lesion in the thoracic vertebrae to alleviate symptoms after 6 days of gefitinib. Following 20 days of treatment with gefitinib, the patient developed a cough and fever. Blood tests indicated an increased white blood cell count and neutrophil percentage, whereas the sputum culture results and blood cultures were negative. A follow-up chest CT scan revealed a bilateral lung infection, although the mass in the right lung, the right hilum and mediastinal lymph nodes had decreased in size (Fig. 1B, E), which was considered a partial response (PR). In addition, serum β-hCG levels decreased by 518.5 mIU/ml and the β-hCG levels in the pleural fluid decreased to 1,484 mIU/ml. The patient was suspected of having gefitinib-induced interstitial lung disease along with a concurrent bacterial lung infection. Although the patient underwent thoracic vertebral radiotherapy with a total dose of 27 Gy/3Gy/9d, the lung involvement dose was V20 < 5%, which was a safe dose for the lungs. In addition, the lung injury did not occur within the radiation field. Therefore, radiation pneumonitis was not considered. In the present study, an echocardiogram, electrocardiogram, cardiac enzymes, and brain natriuretic peptide were conducted, and the results did not indicate the presence of any underlying cardiac disease in the patient. Therefore, cardiogenic disease was excluded. Based on CT findings, the patient was diagnosed with grade 3 interstitial pneumonia according to the CTCAE criteria. Gefitinib treatment and radiation therapy were discontinued. The patient was administered 40 mg of methylprednisolone in combination with antibiotics for 2 weeks. During this period, her symptoms were relieved and follow-up chest CT scans indicated improvement of the infection as well as a continued reduction in the size of the lung tumor lesions (Fig. 1C, F). Then, oral prednisone was administered at a dosage of 40 mg for 5 days, 30 mg for 5 days, and 20 mg for 5 days. 1 week after the antibiotics were discontinued, the patient’s cough and dyspnea worsened, and antibiotics were added for treatment. There was an increase in right-sided pleural effusion, a thoracentesis was performed again. Pleural effusion was drainaged intermittent to relieve dyspnea. The patient’s cough and dyspnea were no obvious relieved, combined with hypoproteinemia and electrolyte imbalance, subsequent CT images indicated worsening of the infection in both lungs (Fig. 2C, D). Therefore, the antibiotic treatment was intensified. However, the patient’s condition soon deteriorated again, leading to a transfer to the intensive care unit (ICU). The patient was administered meropenem for infection management and received non-invasive ventilatory support in the ICU. The patient’s symptoms alleviated for four days; however, the patient subsequently experienced a recurrence of fever and a decrease in blood oxygen saturation. Blood gas analyses indicated that the patient was suffering from Type I respiratory failure. During this time, the patient developed left-sided pleural effusion and underwent thoracentesis. The patient refused invasive ventilator support. Furthermore, three days before death, sputum culture results confirmed the presence of Candida albicans. Despite the administration of antifungal, antibacterial, and supportive care, the patient eventually succumbed to respiratory failure and her overall survival was 77 days.

Discussion

Elevated serum β-hCG levels have been noted in approximately 20–40% of patients with epithelial-derived tumors, including gastric, prostate, renal, and lung cancer [28]. Positive serum β-hCG levels are associated with poorly differentiated and high-grade tumors [29], indicating a more aggressive tumor and a poorer prognosis [28]; however, increased serum β-hCG levels in NSCLC is not commonly observed and has primarily been reported in individual cases. Specifically, 24 related cases have been published over the last 20 years which are summarized in Table 1. The median age of these patients was 49 years (range: 28–80 years), with a higher prevalence among female patients (16 cases) compared with male patients (8 cases). Most of the cases involve stage Ⅳ patients, and serum β-hCG levels ranged from 6.14 to 55,1100 mIU/ml, with an average value of 27,319 mIU/ml. Most NSCLC patients with elevated serum β-hCG levels did not exhibit initial symptoms that were distinct from those of typical lung cancer patients. Common symptoms included dyspnea, cough, chest pain, and decreased appetite. Four young female patients presented with vaginal bleeding or amenorrhea [7, 10, 12, 26], whereas three male patients presented with gynecomastia [8, 13, 16]. These symptoms may be associated with high β-hCG levels; however, the validity of these data is unclear. These 24 NSCLC cases included 11 adenocarcinomas, 4 large cell carcinomas, 4 pleomorphic carcinomas, 2 giant cell carcinomas, 2 squamous cell carcinomas, and 1 undifferentiated carcinoma. Sixteen exhibited the presence of poorly differentiated tumor cells. Following immunohistochemical analysis, the majority (19/24) of the tumors were hCG-positive. Szturmowicz et al. [30] found that elevated serum β-hCG levels occurred in 12% of NSCLC patients. Of these, adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and an undefined subtype accounted for 57.9%, 36.8%, and 5.3% of the cases, respectively. We found that elevated serum β-hCG values were more frequently observed in stage IV patients compared with those with stage I–III disease. Patients with elevated serum β-hCG levels also had a relatively poorer response to chemotherapy compared with patients with exhibiting normal β-hCG levels [30]. A similar finding was reported by Szturmowicz et al. [31] in small-cell lung cancer. The mechanism underlying the secretion of β-hCG in NSCLC is currently unclear. Szturmowicz et al. [30] found that the expression of β-hCG in tumor tissues did not correlate with its serum levels; however, in a study by Yokotani et al. [32], the β-hCG gene was detected in lung cancer tissues by reverse transcription PCR (RT-PCR) and Southern blot analysis, whereas it was not detected in normal lung tissues. Furthermore, patients with significantly elevated β-hCG levels in their blood had high levels of β-hCG gene expression in their tumor tissues, indicating that β-hCG is a tumor-derived product.

It is unclear how β-hCG promotes tumor growth. In breast cancer, it has been suggested that the β-hCG/luteinizing hormone receptor may be selectively upregulated in invasive tumors, which increases the sensitivity of ductal cells to hormones that target β-hCG/LHR, thereby promoting carcinogenesis [1]. In other tumor types, hCG-H and β-hCG have been shown to antagonize the TGFβ receptor, which results in an antiapoptotic and proliferative effect [33]. Another study suggested that β-hCG acts as a growth factor that inhibits tumor cell apoptosis, possibly by inhibiting the transforming growth factor beta receptor complex [28].

Patients with NSCLC and elevated β-hCG levels are often treated according to the guidelines for NSCLC. As shown in Table 1, systemic chemotherapy was used for these patients in advanced stages. However, in most cases, the disease progresses rapidly following chemotherapy. Among the nine patients who underwent surgical treatment (stage IA–IIA), one with poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma received one cycle of methotrexate before surgery, resulting in overall survival (OS) of > 28 months [26]. In another case of lung pleomorphic carcinoma, carboplatin and etoposide were administered postoperatively and resulted in a PR with an OS of eight months [16]. A patient with lung giant cell carcinoma had an OS of greater than four years following surgery [13]. Notably, only one case mentioned the use of genetic testing, and no EGFR mutations were detected [22]. The other cases did not provide any genetic testing results.

The correlation between lung adenocarcinomas and EGFR mutation is strong. In the Iressa Pan Asian Survival Study of Asian patients with non- or never-smoking lung adenocarcinoma, 80% of the patients were female and 437 patients underwent genetic testing, with 60% of the subjects harboring EGFR mutations [34]. Since the discovery of EGFR signaling in the context of tumorigenesis, many studies have been conducted to determine the efficacy of targeting this receptor as an anticancer therapy [35], which resulted in the development of tyrosine kinase inhibitors [36]. EGFR-TKIs are an important class of anticancer drugs that effectively target EGFR receptors on the surface of tumor cells, leading to the inhibition of tumor growth and metastasis. Specifically, gefitinib was the earliest EGFR-TKI developed and approved for cancer treatment. The ongoing development of EGFR-TKIs continues to result in significant advancements in cancer treatment.

Genomic characteristics of pulmonary choriocarcinoma and lung cancer with choriocarcinoma features have not been clearly defined. EGFR L858R, TP53 C275G [37], KRAS G13R and GNAS R201H [38] have been reported previously. To explore the genomics of this rare type of tumor and provide a basis for subsequent treatment, comprehensive genetic testing was conducted. In the present case study, elevated β-hCG levels concurrent with an EGFR mutation were observed in the blood and pleural effusion of a patient. The tumor cells were poorly differentiated, and the overall tumor burden was extensive. Enhanced CT indicated a uterine fibroid. A previous study indicated that enhanced CT is optimal for the diagnosis of uterine fibroids [39]; therefore, a biopsy was not performed. During treatment with gefitinib, the patient developed interstitial lung disease. Although the patient underwent thoracic vertebral radiotherapy, regardless of the radiation dose and the affected areas, we did not consider it to be radiation pneumonia. In a real-world study by Borghetti et al. [40], the combination of TKIs and radiation to the brain, bone, and other sites was well-tolerated in patients with advanced NSCLC. The incidence of interstitial lung disease associated with gefitinib is less than 5% with a median onset time of 31 days (range: 18–50 days) and a mortality rate of 1.6%. Male patients, most with a history of smoking, were considered to be at a higher risk for developing interstitial lung disease [36]. The radiological images obtained from the patients indicated the following changes, which were similar to gefitinib-induced interstitial lung disease: ground-glass attenuation, airspace consolidation, and interlobular septal thickening [41]. Based on the changes observed in the patient’s lung CT (Fig. 2A, B) and the hematological tests, the lung lesions were likely the result of a combined effect of gefitinib-induced interstitial lung disease and bacterial infection. Disease progression and eventual death of this patient may have been exacerbated by various factors, such as advanced age, poor performance status, bone marrow suppression during radiation treatment, and concurrent bacterial infection. Based on the treatment response, however, EGFR-TKI therapy was considered an effective treatment for the lung adenocarcinoma as the mass was reduced by nearly 40% and the β-hCG levels were significantly decreased following 20 days of once-daily treatment. The patient’s chest CT scan still indicated controlled lesions one month following the discontinuation of gefitinib. Therefore, the treatment response was characteristic of driver gene-positive NSCLC. The decrease in serum β-HCG levels also suggests effective treatment. Hopefully, the present case study will provide useful data for future studies. EGFR-TKI therapy provides a significant advancement in the management of this type of lung cancer. It offers an alternative and promising option for patients, improves their chances of successful treatment, and potentially extends survival.

Conclusions

Elevated serum β-hCG levels in patients with NSCLC are relatively rare compared with most NSCLC cases. This particular subtype is often characterized by poor differentiation, advanced disease stage, and poor response to conventional chemotherapy. Therefore, genetic testing is particularly important for such cases. We also recommend plasma β-HCG testing for lung cancer patients. Based on the present study, targeted therapy appears effective for NSCLC patients with elevated serum β-hCG levels with an EGFR mutation, and it provides an effective treatment approach for this type of lung cancer. But future studies are required to assess the efficacy of targeted therapy in patients with elevated level of β-HCG. Although the median age of patients with lung cancer with elevated plasma β-hCG expression is 49 years, it is necessary to first exclude the possibility of concurrent pregnancy in reproductive-age women with elevated serum β-hCG levels. In addition, pathological diagnosis should be used to determine whether it is a primary pulmonary trophoblastic tumor. The reason why the description of β-HCG elevation and EGFR mutations in the literature is so low may be related to the fact that, generally, nobody would test for β-HCG in an NSCLC patient, so this find could have a strong bias.
