A method validation of determination of melamine in marketed infant formula and milk powder of Iran
Authors: Yeganeh Mazaheri, Maryam Jafari, Hadi Eghbaljoo, Ghazal Mirzaei, Parisa Sadighara, Tayebeh Zeinali
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMelamine is a recognized food contaminant that can arise incidentally or intentionally in specific categories of food. This study aimed to validate the melamine detection and quantification in infant formula and milk powders and also analyzed 40 samples consist of infant formula and milk powders from commercially available food products, from different geographic regions of Iran. The approximate content of melamine in samples was measured by High-performance liquid chromatography-ultra violet (HPLC–UV) system. A calibration curve (R2 = 0.9925) was established for detection of melamine in the range of 0.1–1.2 μg mL−1. Limit of quantification and limit of detection were 1 μg mL−1 and 3 μg mL−1, respectively. The presence of melamine in infant formula and milk powdered was investigated and it was observed that the amount of melamine in samples of infant formulas and milk powders was 0.001–0.095 mg kg−1 and 0.001–0.004 mg kg−1, respectively. These values were found to be within the prescribed limits by the European Union and Codex Alimentarius Commission legislation. It is important to note that the consumption of these dairy products, which contain low content of melamine, does not pose any significant threat to consumer health. Furthermore, the results of the risk assessment confirmed this issue.Keywords: Melamine, Contamination, Infant formula, Powder milk, AdulterationSubject terms: Biochemistry, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Melamine is an organic compound with molecular formula of C3H6N6 and obtains from cyanamide. It’s solubility in water is very low and about 66% of this compound is made up of nitrogen. Melamine is a commonly utilized compound in the industry and has various and wide legal uses in the plastic manufacturing, chemical fertilizers, making food-related equipment, including food packaging and kitchen utensils1,2. Melamine is also used in pharmaceuticals as a drug carrier to treat diseases. The high percentage of nitrogen in melamine can cause misuse of this compound and give the properties of protein molecules to the food composition3,4. As a result, adding melamine to foods, including dairy products, enhances the nitrogen content. Therefore, the amount of protein in milk is falsely inferred to be higher than its actual amount.

Adding one gram of melamine increases the amount of protein in food by 0.4%, on the other hand, the solubility of melamine in water causes more abuse, because when 1.3 g of melamine is added to liquid foods such as milk, the protein content of milk increased up to 30%5,6. Although melamine is mixed with animal food and even human food to enhance the percentage of protein7, it has not been approved as a food additive in the Codex Alimentarius commision (CAC) and by national authorities, and has been introduced as a hazard in case of ingestion, inhalation or skin absorption. The International Agency for Research on Cancer of the World Health Organization (WHO) added Melamine to the list of 2B carcinogens in 2012 due to its potential to damage human health8. Chronic exposure to melamine can cause cancer or kidney damage2. Melamine in food can form yellow crystals insoluble in water by forming a cyanuric acid complex and cause tissue damage to kidney and bladder and cancer including urinary tract cancer and weight loss9,10. This substance can acutely cause food poisoning and in high concentrations can cause death, especially in infants and children11. WHO has considered the tolerable daily intake (TDI) of melamine for humans at 0.2 mg kg−1 of body weight per day12 also based on the CAC. According to Food and Drug Administration of the United States (US FDA), the maximum of melamine residue level in infant formula is 1 mg kg−1 and for other food is also 2.5 mg kg−12,7. In September 2008, it was revealed that several companies producing infant formula have added melamine to infant formula as a fraud to increase the protein content of their products, which led to an epidemic of melamine poisoning initiated by the consuming of contaminated infant formula in China that leads to affection of more than 294,000 children in China and hospitalization of more than 50,000 people13.

Feeding babies with breast milk is not always possible due to various conditions such as challenges related to urban life and diseases of the mother or the baby, and these factors leads to nourish the baby by infant formula. Therefore, factories have been developed with the aim of producing infant formula with the most similar formulation to mother's milk composition14. The infant formulas available in the market are often prepared from cow's milk and are generally produced using special mixtures of fat, protein, carbohydrates, vitamins, minerals and other compounds, and for being close to breast milk formula, the amount of protein and fat in them changes to some extent, and depending on the type of milk, they are enriched with compounds such as vitamins and minerals like iron15. Due to the placement of babies in sensitive groups and the possibility of poisoning in them, the health and safety of the consumed powdered milk is very essential. After the occurrence of melamine poisoning in Chinese babies, there was a great concern in all the countries of the world and the sensitivities in this field increased, so the need to control the infant formulas produced in order to maintain the health of babies increases. Melamine in food measures by various methods, the main of which include high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), electrophoresis, sensors, spectrophotometry, and immunoassay based on antigen–antibody16. FDA designed and published the HPLC method to measure the amount of melamine and cyanuric acid contamination in foods in 2007, which is most useful in determining the amount of melamine17.

The melamine concentration was determined from 0.33 to 0.96 (mg kg−1) in infant milk formula by New Infrared Spectroscopy Technique18. A study conducted in Sri Lanka have displayed that the melamine levels in full fat milk powders ranged as 0.39–0.84 mg kg−1. Additionally, the highest amount of melamine was 0.96 and 0.94 mg kg−1 in imported infant formula samples. These were lower than the regulatory limit (1 mg kg−1), but a surveillance program is necessary for safety of consumer19.

Melamine content in infant milk formula in Iran was detected in various studies. About 65% of samples had contained melamine with the average of 0.73 mg kg−1 and maximum amount of 3.63 mg kg−120. Another study reported that melamine levels in infant formula was from 0.35 to 3.40 μg g−1, with a mean value of 1.38 μg g−121. Overall, the presence and levels of melamine in infant milk formula in Iran varied across different studies, with some samples exceeding the maximum level set by regulatory authorities (2.5 mg kg−1 feed).

Considering the high direct and indirect consumption of powdered milk in the food industry, as well as the special importance of nourishing babies with infant formula, this study aimed to validate the melamine determination in powdered milk and infant formula. In fact, the first purpose of this study was to develop a rapid, easy, and accurate method for the quantitative detection of melamine adulteration in infant formula and powdered milks using HPLC with ultra-violet (UV) detector and the second one was to measure the melamine content in infant formula and powdered milks marketed in Iran.

Results and discussion

Method validation

The instrumental equipment for analysis of melamine depending on food production sites can be very different. For measurement of melamine residual in milk and infant formula, a sensitive and reliable HPLC–UV assay was employed. Dairy products consist of numerous proteins and fats that may interference with melamine measurement. So, appropriate and efficient purification strategies are crucial before instrumental analysis, as identified by Sun et al.22. In this study, disposable syringe filters were utilized for this purpose. In the present study, separation of melamine in infant formula and powdered milk was carried out by a C18 column. Figure 1 shows the chromatogram of melamine detection. Additionally, spiked samples with 0.1–1.2 mg kg−1 of melamine demonstrated recoveries ranging from 95 to 109% with a regression equation, y = 1.2487x − 0.005 (r = 0.9925), and relative standard deviations % (RSD) values from 0.8 to 2%. This evidence suggests that the procedure was reliable over the studied concentration range (Table 1). The instrument limit of detection (LOD) and limit of quantification (LOQ) for melamine was 1 and 3 μg mL−1, respectively. Additionally, the UV spectrum of melamine shows absorption bands at 242 nm. The proposed approach for the detection of melamine content was sensitive, dependable, and accurate. This technique can be used to determine melamine on a regular basis.

Similar results have been published by several authors. A HPLC-photodiode array (PDA) method was established for analyzing melamine in dairy products, achieving LOQs of 340 μg kg−1 for milk powder and 280 μg kg−1 for infant formula at a wavelength of 240 nm22. Filazi et al., 2012 reported that melamine was undetectable by HPLC in infant formulas. However, 8% of milk powder samples contained melamine at 0.505–0.86 mg kg−1. The same study has been conducted by Tittlemier et al.23 that evaluated melamine content by HPLC–mass spectrometry/MS (HPLC–MS/MS) in infant formula (sample number: 72) around 0.0431–0.346 mg kg−1. In the study conducted by Venkatasami et al., 2010 a green chemistry approach (acetonitrile-free) and reverse phase HPLC (RP-HPLC) was employed for the evaluation of melamine in dry and liquid infant formula. A linear response (r > 0.999) was obtained for samples ranging from 1.0 to 80 g mL−1. The method showed recovery percent of 97.2–101.2 in the level range of 5–40 g mL−1, reproducibility lower than 1.0% RSD. Also, LOD and LOQ were observed 0.1 g mL−1 and 0.2 g mL−1, respectively24. Lutter et al., 2011 determined the melamine contamination in cow’s milk and milk-based powdered infant formula using HPLC–UV. Melamine concentration ranged from < 0.2–2.52 mg kg−1. The HPLC–UV method encompasses a linear dynamic range extending from 0.05 to 2.5 mg kg−1 for cow’s milk, 0.13–6.25 mg kg−1 for infant formula with a protein mass fraction of less than 15%, and 0.25–12.5 mg kg−1 for infant formula with a protein mass fraction of 15%. The determination of LOD (and LOQ) yielded values of 0.03 mg kg−1 (0.09 mg kg−1) for cow’s milk, 0.06 mg kg−1 (0.18 mg kg−1) for infant formula with a protein content below 15%, and 0.12 mg/kg (0.36 mg kg−1) for infant formula with a protein content of 15%, using a signal-to-noise ratio of 3 and 10 for LOD and LOQ, respectively25. Deabes et al., 2012 studied the melamine content in infant milk formula and milk powder samples using HPLC/DAD. The lowest and the highest levels were observed to be 9.49 and 258 mg kg−1, respectively in infant milk formula. They illustrated a LOD of 0.05 mg kg−126.

Jawaid et al. indicated melamine residue by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-MIR) (LOD = 1 mg kg−1; LOQ = 3.5 mg kg−1) in infant formula at the range of 0.002–2 mg kg−1. Rezai et al.27 proposed an HPLC–PDA (λ = 220 nm) technique for melamine evaluation, achieving a LOQ of 0.08 μg mL−1 for powdered milk, that was lower than the present study. Sun et al. developed RP-HPLC–DAD for detection of melamine in liquid milk with solid phase extraction (SPE). They had a LOD and LOQ of 18 and 60 μg kg−1, respectively28, that was more sensitive than current study. Montesano et al. validated a HPLC–DAD method for the evaluation of melamine in protein supplements and reached to a LOQ of 0.05–3 mg kg−1, that had lower sensitivity than the current study29.

Green chemistry aspects

Analytical laboratories undeniably play a crucial role in environmental protection by monitoring pollutants in variety samples. Conversely, the utilization of numerous reagents and solvents in analytical processes leads to the production of hazardous residues. Therefore, green analytical chemistry (GAC) emerged in 2000 with the aim of mitigating or eliminating the adverse impacts of analytical procedures on both operators and the environment26. Conventional techniques for detecting melamine, including chromatography, electrophoresis, capillary electrophoresis, and the enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), have been employed for the identification of melamine. However, electrochemical sensors have attracted significant attention in detection amidst various methods, owing to their notable sensitivity, selectivity, rapid analysis duration, and user-friendly nature30,31. Green nanotechnology, utilizing biological pathways for nanomaterial synthesis, decreases hazardous waste generation and energy consumption, enhancing sustainable practices. Nanocomposites, such as those made from eco-friendly materials, can be applied in biosensors for determination of substances like melamine32–34.

In a study, solid-phase microextraction (SPME) was utilized efficiently due to its superior energy efficiency and sustainability in comparison to traditional extraction methods. SPME's environmentally friendly and energy-conserving characteristics establish it as a prominent substitute for conventional extraction techniques in the field of analytical chemistry, presenting a more sustainable and effective method for sample preparation35.

Wu et al., 2013 stablished a high sensitive and selectivity surface plasmon resonance (mini-SPR) biosensor for rapid detection of melamine in infant formula by immunoassay based on connection between melamine and anti-melamine antibody. The SPR biosensor with immunoassays (via bovine serum albumin conjugated melamine) revealed the easy to use and cost-saving technique with low detection limit of 0.02 μg mL−136.

Nasiri and Abbasian used the high potential portable sensor coupled with graphene oxide-chitosan (GOCS) composite for melamine detection in commercial samples37. Ultra-selectivity and accuracy, as well as responsiveness have been shown through this approach. The GOCS sensor demonstrated a notable level of sensitivity (239.1 μM−1), a linear range between 0.01 to 200 μM, an affinity constant of 1.73 × 104, and a remarkable LOD of 10 nM. In addition, in the study conducted by Chandrasekar et al., 2024 the environmentally friendly and cost-effective approach was utilized. Zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO-NPs) were synthesized through a green approach employing Carica papaya peel extract as a reducing agent. Subsequently, a unique Micro Raman Spectroscopy technique was established for the identification of melamine in infant milk powder. The utilization of ZnO-NPs, prepared from agricultural by-products, shows potential as a valuable diagnostic instrument and reliable, low-cost technique for monitoring and detecting melamine38.

Alizadeh et al., 2024 employed the high sensitive fluorescent platform based on a metal–organic framework (MOF) to determine melamine in milk powder. The sensor's linear range and lower limit of detection, determined using 3σ/S, ranged from 40 to 396.45 nM (equivalent to 25 µg kg−1 to 0.25 mg kg−1) and 40 nM (equivalent to 25 µg kg−1), respectively. This range is notably lower than the maximum residual level (MRL) established for the identification of melamine in infant formula (1 mg kg−1) and other food/feed samples (2.5 mg kg−1). Fluorescence sensor (terbium (Tb)@NH2-MIL-253 (Al) MOF) showed more accuracy and more precise measurement capability for the detection of melamine in milk powder than the HPLC method39. Biosensors and nanocomposites in green chemistry not only enhance detection capabilities but also minimize environmental harm, aligning with the principles of sustainable development.

Green chemistry principles were applied in various methods for the determination of melamine. One approach involved the development of a green dispersive solid phase microextraction method using a natural polar polymer, ß-cyclodextrin, cross-linked with citric acid for efficient extraction of melamine from samples like infant formula and hot water40. Another method utilized a Mannich reaction for melamine determination in milk samples, offering a low-cost and environmentally friendly technique with good precision and a linear range of 0.1–2.5 ppm and a low limit of detection41. Additionally, a cost-effective and eco-friendly method was established using transmission Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy for quantifying melamine in liquid milk and infant powder, achieving high accuracy and excellent detection limits of 1 ppm and 3.5 ppm, respectively42. These methods showcase the application of green chemistry principles in developing efficient and sustainable techniques for melamine determination.

Various studies have proposed innovative approaches to detect melamine, such as the use of solid-phase extraction and HPLC43, as well as a rapid HPLC procedure that eliminates the need for complex pretreatment or ion-pair reagents, thus reducing chemical waste44. These methods not only provide accurate results for melamine content in dairy products but also align with green chemistry principles by minimizing the use of hazardous chemicals and reducing the overall environmental impact of the analysis process.

Obtained results were lower than the limit set by FDA which is less than 1 and 2.5 mg kg−1. Moreover, the results of the study were found to be within the limits prescribed by the CAC (2010) and the EU45,46, which have set the maximum permissible limits of 1 mg kg−1 for infant formula and 2.5 mg kg−1 for dairy products.

According to Ghanati et al.47, 2023 study, the melamine content in different type of Iranian packaged milk ranged from 50.7 to 790 µg kg−1. Their findings were lower than allowable limits of FDA. Our findings were lower than results observed by Schoder et al.48 and Rima et al.49. Schoder, 2010 seen that the range of melamine levels detected in milk powders (n = 49) by ELISA were between 0.5 and 5.5 mg kg−1. Rima et al. analyzed melamine residue in powdered milk by Spectrofluorometric and realized that milk powder contained melamine content at 0.72–5.76 mg kg−1. The study was carried out in Canada in 2011 to monitor melamine level in infant formula (n = 94) using liquid chromatography (LC/MS). Melamine concentration was determined as lower than the permissible limit (interim standard: 0.5 mg kg−1). The quantity of melamine discovered as a result of deception was found to be improbable as a means of increasing the protein content. However, this cannot be attributed to the use of fertilizer or the movement from containers or similar factors. Moreover, the source of the melamine imported into Canadian milk powder has not been disclosed50.

Hassani et al., 2013 determined melamine in powder and liquid milk in market of Iran and similar results have been achieved. The results indicated that, apart from one milk powder brand and liquid milk, all other samples were found to be tainted with melamine, ranging from 1.50 to 30.32 μg g−1 for milk powders and 0.11 to 1.48 μg mL−1 for milk. Notably, there was no detection of cyanuric acid in any of the samples, thereby reducing the likelihood of toxicity of melamine for consumers51. In a previous investigation, the melamine concentration in chocolate products contained milk powder was evaluated. Approximately 94% of the imported samples and 77% of the Iranian samples were found to contain melamine. The melamine content ranged from 0.032 to 2.692 mg kg−1 in the imported samples, whereas it was detected at levels between 0.013 and 2.600 mg kg−1 in the Iranian samples. All in all, melamine was detected in 85% of the samples, but only one specific brand exceeded the permissible limit44. Tittlemier et al. reported contamination range of melamine in milk powder as 0.00528–0.0122 mg kg−1.

Conclusion

Considering the critical importance of food safety management in the dairy industry, especially when it comes to protecting public health, it is essential to develop and validate a technique for assessing and comparing levels of melamine and its residual presence in powdered milk and infant formula. A HPLC–UV method, which is both simple and precise, has been well developed to determine melamine in infant formula and milk powder. The method has been validated to ensure its reliability and accuracy. The LOD and LOQ of this method have demonstrated sufficient sensitivity to measure melamine content in infant formula and milk powder. According to the outcomes of our investigation, melamine was detected in the majority of Iranian samples. All of the identified quantities of melamine were lower than the Maximum Residue Limit established by CAC, signifying that the consumption of these types of dairy products does not present a health hazard to individuals.

Methods

Chemicals

Analytical grade purified chemical reagents were used as follows: Melamine (2,4,6-triamino-1,3,5-triazine) with 99% purity (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO); HPLC grade acetonitrile (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany); Ultrapure water that was obtained from a Millipore system (Millipore, Molfheim, France). Disposable syringe filters (Chromafil Xtra PVDF-45/25 pore size 0.45 μm, membrane diameter of 25 mm) (Macherey–Nagel, Düren, Germany).

Instrumentation

An ultrasonic bath (Elma, Germany), centrifuge (Beckman Coulter, Krefeld, Germany), and HPLC (KNAUER, Germany) were utilized for sample preparation.

Equipment and chromatographic conditions

High-performance liquid chromatography (KNAUER, Germany) equipped with a UV detector was used. The HPLC analytical condition was performed on a UHPLC ultimate system with ODS-3 C18 analytical column (4.6 mm × 250 mm, 5 μm particle size) (MZ, Germany). The HPLC eluents (mobile phase) were TFA/methanol (450:50 mL) pumped at 1 mL min−1 flow rate. At the wavelength of 242 nm detection was performed. Injection volume was 100 μL and 20 °C was column temperature. The retention time was longer (15 min), therefore, the next injection followed 25 min later. Retention time and the peak of UV spectral of melamine standards were compared for identification of melamine.

Standards and stock solution

Melamine’s stock standard solution was prepared in water (10 μg mL−1), kept in a cold and dark refrigerator (4 °C). Mobile phase was used for dilution of stock solution and preparation of working standard solutions. Injection of each standard solution in the HPLC was performed 7 times. By regression of a certain average peak area with a certain level the calibration equation was calculated10.

Sampling and sample preparation

Commercial cow powdered milk (20 samples) and milk-based powdered infant formula samples (20) of different brands designed to feed babies and infants for different type of ages (0–6 month, 6–12 month and more than 12 month) were purchased from local supermarkets and pharmacies in Iran and kept in the refrigeration temperature (4 °C) until the analysis. Then, 1 ± 0.01 g of each homogenized milk powders was weighed and was mixed with acetonitrile: water (50:50, V/V; 5 mL). The mixture was stirred for 1 min, and then in an ultrasonic cleaning bath was sonicated for 30 min, and finally vortexed for 1 min. Then it was centrifuged at 9000 × g for 10 min at room temperature, and a 0.45-μm syringe filter was used for the filtration of supernatant into a 2-mL auto sampler vial. Then, the filtrate (250 μL) was mixed with water (750 μL) and injected into the HPLC system10,42.

Method validation

In order to validate the method, recovery, accuracy, LOD, LOQ, and precision were performed in the optimum conditions. The content of the sample that results in a peak three times higher than the baseline noise level is defined as the LOD. On the other hand, the LOQ is determined as the sample content that generates a peak with a height 10 times the signal-to-noise ratio.

The response of the apparatus was determined through a calibration curve consisting of seven data points. The different content of melamine (0, 0.2, 0.3, 0.5, 0.8, 1, and 1.2) was employed. The linearity of the technique for calculation of melamine was determined. Moreover, blank samples were spiked with several content of melamine. Calibration curve was diagrammed by the various succeeding injections of melamine’s standards solutions from 0.1 to 1.2 μg mL−1 in infant formula and powdered milk samples with R2 = 0.9925. Precision was evaluated by repeatability and reproducibility of the procedure and was obtained by injections (in triplicate) of the sample on the first day and three succeeding days. The estimation of the repeatability of the method was obtained by calculation of RSD % of three different spiked concentrations of melamine. The recovery studies were performed to determine the accuracy. The extraction method’s recovery was calculated at three spiked concentrations of melamine (0.1, 1.2, 2) in infant formula and powdered milk samples9,11,15.

Risk assessment for melamine in infant formula

Milk consumption for infants is defined as follows53.

Infants (0–6 months): 120 g with a mean body weight of 5.9 kg.

Infants (0–6 months): 120 g with a mean body weight of 5.9 kg.

Children (6–12 months): 160 g with a mean body weight of 9.3 kg.

Children (6–12 months): 160 g with a mean body weight of 9.3 kg.

Children (12–24 months): 200 g with a mean body weight of 12.2 kg.

Children (12–24 months): 200 g with a mean body weight of 12.2 kg.

The estimation of daily intake (EDI) was determined using the following formula: EDI = Ci × Cc/BW.

where Ci represents the mean content of melamine, Cc signifies the ingestion rate of milk, and BW denotes the mean body weight of children.

The ADI (acceptable dairy intake) was considered as 0.2 mg/kg bw/day.

Hazard quotient (HQ) is calculated as EDI/ADI.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 24 was utilized for data analyses. Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was employed for normality tests; all data were nonparametric (p = 0). Consequently, to determine significant differences between groups, Kruskal–Wallis and Mann–Whitney tests were used.
