The miR-8/miR-200 family: from functional diversification to translational applications
Authors: Ximei Yuan, Yiqing He, Chen Wang, Yuejun Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The miR-8/miR-200 family is a phylogenetically ancient microRNA regulator conserved across bilaterian animals, originating as a single miR-8 isoform in invertebrates and expanding to miR-200a/b/c, miR-141, and miR-429 in vertebrates through gene duplication. This review summarizes the biogenesis, genealogical details, and regulatory roles of the miR-8/miR-200 family. Furthermore, it presents the first comprehensive analysis of its evolutionary trajectory from invertebrates to vertebrates, thereby establishing a foundational understanding that underscores its significant potential for translational applications. Phylogenetic analyses confirm its sequence conservation and lineage-specific distribution, indicating that miR-8 originated in Platyhelminthes, with subsequent isoforms emerging in chordates. Functionally, this family plays pleiotropic roles: in invertebrates, miR-8 regulates immune defense, reproduction, and biosynthesis; in vertebrates, miR-200 subtypes modulate stress responses, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), tumor suppression, and neurodevelopment via conserved pathways (e.g., Wnt, Notch, PI3K). Cross-species conservation underlies core functions in cell differentiation, apoptosis, and growth, while vertebrate-specific adaptations drive roles in cancer (e.g., targeting ZEB1 in metastasis) and metabolic diseases (e.g., diabetes via β-cell apoptosis). This work explores the translational potential of this family, including nucleic acid pesticides in agriculture (targeting insect miR-8) and diagnostic/therapeutic tools in medicine (miR-200 as cancer biomarkers). Key challenges remain in delivery specificity and addressing functional pleiotropy. Future research should elucidate regulatory networks in non-model species and refine targeted delivery systems for clinical and agricultural applications.Graphical abstract

Introduction

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are single-stranded RNA molecules of approximately 21-23 nucleotides that regulate gene expression at the post-transcriptional level by binding to the 3′ untranslated region (3′UTR) of target mRNAs [1, 2]. A single miRNA can target multiple mRNAs, and individual mRNAs may be regulated by several miRNAs, enabling miRNAs to participate in complex regulatory networks [2, 3]. Through these networks, miRNAs modulate essential biological processes such as immune response, cell proliferation, differentiation, metabolism, and apoptosis [3]. Consequently, dysregulation of miRNA expression is implicated in various diseases, including cancer, neurodegenerative disorders, and cardiovascular conditions, highlighting their potential as therapeutic targets.

As shown in Fig. 1, the synthesis of miRNA in vivo begins with the transcription of primary miRNA (pri-miRNA) by RNA polymerase II [4]. This pri-miRNA is then processed into a miRNA precursor (pre-miRNA) through the combined action of the Drosha enzyme and its cofactor Pasha. Following this, exportin-5 facilitates the transport of pre-miRNA from the nucleus to the cytoplasm, aided by the Ran protein [5]. Once in the cytoplasm, pre-miRNA undergoes a second cleavage by the RNase III enzyme, Dicer, resulting in the production of double-stranded RNA [6]. The passenger strand of this double-stranded RNA is degraded, freeing the guide strand. The miRNA, now serving as the guide strand, associates with the argonaut-1 (Ago1) protein to form the miRNA-induced silencing complex (miRISC), which pairs with the target mRNA and inhibits its translation [7, 8]. If the mRNA of the target gene perfectly complements the miRNA, the silencing complex will specifically degrade the mRNA, a process primarily observed in plants [9]. In contrast, in animals, miRNAs typically regulate protein expression by obstructing mRNA translation through incomplete complementary pairing with the 3′UTR of the mRNA.Fig. 1The biogenesis and function of miRNAs. The miR-8/miR-200 family is highly conserved across bilaterians, from Drosophila to humans [10, 11], and regulates diverse processes including cell differentiation and renewal [12], cell survival and epithelial maintenance [13], neural development and movement [14], growth and development [15], biosynthesis [16], innate immunity [17], stress response [18], cancer and disease [19], and reproduction [20]. While functional studies on individual members of the family are extensive, comprehensive reviews are largely confined to the vertebrate members. This work fills this gap by providing the first integrated analysis of the family’s evolutionary trajectory and conserved regulatory functions across both invertebrates and vertebrates. The miR-8/miR-200 family represents an ancient and phylogenetically widespread miRNA cluster that exhibits both functional conservation and lineage-specific expansion. Particularly noteworthy is its unique capacity to form bidirectional feedback loops with target genes, enabling it to function as molecular switches within regulatory networks—a feature that distinguishes it from other conserved miRNA clusters. We systematically analyze these regulatory mechanisms while highlighting the family’s broad functional spectrum and translational potential across different biological contexts.

This review delineates the roles of the miR-8/miR-200 family across species, elucidates its conserved functions, and contrasts miR-8 in invertebrates with its vertebrate subtypes. We present phylogenetic and sequence analyses to trace functional gains and losses during evolution. This study represents the first comprehensive integration of the family’s evolution from Platyhelminthes to Mammalia, revealing its expansion from a basic immune regulator (miR-8) to a central hub governing multiple cancers (miR-200). We also discuss applications in agriculture and medicine, current challenges, and future directions.

The miR-8/miR-200 family has six members: miR-8, miR-200a, miR-200b, miR-200c, miR-141, and miR-429. These six miRNAs are highly conserved during evolution; Among them, miR-8, miR-200b, miR-200c, and miR-429 share an identical seed sequence. This seed sequence differs from that of miR-200a and miR-141 by only a single nucleotide [19, 21]. However, the terminal sequences of their mature miRNAs exhibit lower conservation [19]. Here, we summarize the distribution of these six members in most species with the miR-8/miR-200 family. As shown in Fig. 3, invertebrate species only have one homolog of this family, miR-8. Except for Schmidtea mediterranea, other invertebrates only have one copy of the miR-8 precursor. S. mediterranea has two copies of the miR-8 precursor, and the corresponding mature miR-8 sequences have slight differences. In addition, five subtypes of miR-8 are present in vertebrate species, namely miR-200a, miR-200b, miR-200c, miR-141, and miR-429 [22]. Each vertebrate with the miR-8/miR-200 family has multiple copies of this family member, and the distribution of members varies among different species. There are only miR-200b and miR-141 in Urochordata. However, Cephalochordata lacks miR-141 and miR-429. Most fish in Osteichthyes lack miR-200c and miR-141. Interestingly, multiple copies of miR-200a, miR-200b, and miR-429 appear in Osteichthyes. Multiple copies of miR-200a and miR-200b have the same mature sequence, while multiple copies of miR-429 have different mature sequences. miR-200c and miR-141 are absent in Amphibia, Aves, and Reptilia. This finding is consistent with a 2014 study, which detected their precursor sequences only in cephalochordates, teleosts, and mammals [22]. In Mammalia, different species have different degrees of deletion of miR-8/miR-200 family members. The increase in family member abundance from lower to higher animals reflects evolutionary progression. Sequence evolution analysis of the miR-8/miR-200 family

To elucidate evolutionary relationships, we retrieved precursor and mature sequences of miR-8/miR-200 members from miRBase for 54 species across eight phyla (Platyhelminthes, Aschelminthes, Annelida, Mollusca, Arthropoda, Urochordata, Cephalochordata, and Vertebrata). We employed Clustal W within the Molecular Evolutionary Genetics Analysis X (MEGA X) software to conduct multiple sequence alignment analyses on the precursor and mature sequences of the six member genes of the miR-8/miR-200 family across all examined species. Phylogenetic trees were reconstructed for each member using the neighbor-joining method on the basis of genetic distances, with branch support assessed from 1000 bootstrap iterations. The sequence logos for miR-8, miR-200a, miR-200b, miR-200c, miR-141, and miR-429 were generated using TBtools software. Except for the miR-8 sequence for Meloidogyne incognita [23], which was obtained from miRNA sequencing data reported in literature, all other sequences were sourced from miRBase.

The biological functions and pathways regulated by the miR-8/miR-200 family

These results demonstrate that the miR-8/miR-200 family is highly conserved in bilaterians, suggesting its pivotal role in regulatory functions. The specific functions and regulatory mechanisms in invertebrates

The miR-8/miR-200 family and immune defense

miR-8 is a key regulator of insect innate immunity, primarily regulating the Toll pathway. Studies demonstrate that Toll and Dorsal, two central components of this pathway, are direct targets of miR-8 [11]. Furthermore, miR-8 targets ush, a transcriptional co-regulator of the GATA binding transcription factor, which is involved in antimicrobial peptide (AMP) expression. It is hypothesized that ush induces expression of the AMP drosomycin by directly regulating a GATA factor [11]. In addition, miR-8 positively regulates the transcription level of Serpin27 in Plutella xylostella. Serpin27 negatively regulates phenoloxidase (PO) and serine proteases, which inhibit Spätzle processing. These miR-8-targeted pathways play essential roles in the cascade regulation of AMP expression and prophenoloxidase (proPO) activation [24].

Critically, miR-8 functions as a safeguard against chronic immune activation. Drosophila melanogaster mutants lacking miR-8 exhibit ectopic melanization and lethality [25]. The role of miR-8 in antiviral defense is evident across species. In Bombyx mori, miR-8 downregulates the expression of the ie1 and vp39 genes of the Bombyx mori nucleopolyhedrovirus (BmNPV), inhibiting viral genome replication [26]. In Arma chinensis Fallou, miR-8 is upregulated during early Autographa californica multiple nucleopolyhedrovirus (AcMNPV) infection, suppressing viral ie1 and vp39 genes; conversely, miR-8 downregulation in the late phase promotes the expression of host Toll family and Defensin genes [17]. Functional conservation is evident in Apostichopus japonicus, where miR-200 (homolog) regulates antibacterial activity via Tollip in the TLR pathway [27].

The miR-8/miR-200 family and reproduction

miR-8 in insects regulates the development and occurrence of the host’s germ cells. In Aedes aegypti female mosquitoes, a blood meal is essential for mosquito reproduction. The fat body, which is vital for immunity, reproduction, and metabolism, plays a crucial role in promoting ovarian development in adult female mosquitoes after they have fed on blood [28]. After blood feeding, female mosquitoes promote the synthesis and secretion of yolk protein precursors (YPP) in the induced fat body, which is then absorbed by developing oocytes [29]. In A. aegypti females, blood-meal-induced miR-8 in the fat body is crucial for YPP secretion and ovarian development by targeting swim, which affects Wingless (Wg) signaling [30]. miR-8 also regulates male fertility. Mitoferrin is a member of the mitochondrial carrier family (SLC25) and is conserved across eukaryotic species. Mitoferrin is necessary for spermatid elongation and individualization, and its homolog in D. melanogaster is needed for male fertility [31]. In Bactrocera dorsalis, miR-8 targets mitoferrin (bmfrn) in testes, reducing sperm number and motility, indicating a potential for insect sterilization techniques [20].

The miR-8/miR-200 family and biosynthesis

The miR-8/miR-200 family plays a crucial role in regulating the biosynthesis of diverse molecules in organisms, including pigments, hormones, and lipids. Following a blood meal in female mosquitoes, significant alterations in miRNA and gene expression occur within the fat body. Notably, miR-8 exhibits the highest expression level during this process and is activated by the 20-hydroxyecdysone (20E) pathway. It targets choline/ethanolamine kinase, which catalyzes the phosphorylation of choline and ethanolamine to generate phosphocholine and phosphoethanolamine, thereby potentially participating in the biosynthesis of phospholipid components for cell membranes [32].

miR-8 interacts with both juvenile hormone (JH) and 20E signaling pathways. In Helicoverpa armigera, it targets the BrZ2 gene (a downstream signal of JH and 20E, essential for high expression of the cuticle protein gene BMWCP10) to influence baculovirus-induced climbing behavior [33, 34]. Furthermore, miR-8-3p coordinates the regulation of V-ATPase subunits in the prothoracic gland via prothoracicotropic hormone (PTTH) signaling to control ecdysone biosynthesis in D. melanogaster [35]. Within the Drosophila ring gland (comprising the corpus allatum (CA), prothoracic gland, and corpus cardiacum), overexpression of miR-8 in CA promotes CA cell growth, and is associated with enhanced JH enzyme expression, suggesting a potential regulatory role in JH biosynthesis [16].

Adult cuticle pigmentation serves as a key model for studying the genetic basis of insect morphological diversity. In D. melanogaster, miR-8 acts as a positive regulator of both pigmentation and eclosion. Its loss results in defective pigmentation responses to high temperature during eclosion and disrupts the correct spatial patterning of abdominal pigmentation in adult females [36]. miR-8 also contributes to neuromotor regulation. In Blattella germanica, miR-8-3p, along with miR-8-5p, Ago1, and Ago2, maintains appropriate mRNA levels of atrophin, a transcriptional corepressor critical for neuromotor coordination [37]. In A. chinensis, miR-8 overexpression alters metabolite levels (e.g., reduces eicosanoic acid, increases erucamide), though mechanisms require further study [15].

The conserved functions across invertebrates and vertebrates

Stress response mediated by the miR-8/miR200 family

miR-8 regulates stress responses to adverse environments in both invertebrates and vertebrates. Litopenaeus vannamei exposed to excessive ammonia-N experiences disrupted physiological functions, leading to increased oxidative stress and apoptosis. In L. vannamei under ammonia-N stress, miR-8-3p targets IKKβ, thereby negatively regulating the IKKβ/NF-κB pathway, inducing apoptosis, and reducing antioxidant defense [18]. The miR-8/miR-200 family regulates the response of embryos to osmotic stress in Danio rerio. NHERF family PDZ scaffold protein 1 (Nherf1) is a regulator of the apical trafficking of transmembrane ion transporters and also acts as an adaptor protein to the actin cytoskeleton. In D. rerio embryos, the miR-8 family, expressed in ionocytes, regulates osmotic stress by targeting Nherf1, which controls apical trafficking of ion transporters. Silencing these miRNAs disrupts responses to osmotic stress. This response is coordinated with planar cell polarity (PCP) signaling, as PCP mutations alleviate the phenotype caused by Nherf1 deletion [38, 39]. Thus, correct apical patterning may depend on input and coordination between PCP signaling and the stress response. During ovarian tumorigenesis, miR-141 and miR-200a regulate the oxidative stress response by inhibiting p38α, thereby affecting tumorigenesis and chemotherapy sensitivity [40].

The miR-8/miR-200 family and cell differentiation and renewal

miR-8 plays a crucial role in neural development and stem cell differentiation. In D. melanogaster, the Notch signaling pathway, together with the polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2) and its enzymatic subunit E(z), coregulates miR-8 activity to balance intestinal stem cell (ISC) renewal and differentiation [12]. In D. melanogaster muscle tissue, two isoforms of the zfh1 mRNA exist. The long zfh1 mRNA contains a target binding site for miR-8 in its 3′ untranslated region (3′UTR), while the short isoform lacks this target site. Consequently, Notch protein activity in muscle progenitor cells can evade differentiation by promoting the production of the short isoform of zfh1 mRNA that is insensitive to miR-8 regulation [41]. In neuroblasts, miR-8 promotes glial growth via autocrine signaling and inhibits neuroepithelial over-proliferation by targeting Spitz (epidermal growth factor receptor ligand) paracrinally, ensuring normal neurogenesis [42]. miR-8 suppresses Wnt signaling by inhibiting Wntless, TCF, and the Wnt pathway’s positive regulator, CG32767. However, other members of the miR-8/miR-200 family induce myeloid stromal cell differentiation into adipocytes by inhibiting Wnt signaling [43]. By targeting esg gene during epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), miR-8 enables intestinal blast cells to differentiate into intestinal epithelial cells promptly upon damage to the intestinal epithelium [44]. Serum response factor (SRF), a ubiquitous transcription factor that transactivates actin-related genes, promotes stem cell differentiation into oligodendrocytes. miR-200 family members inhibit the translation of srf mRNA, thereby regulating the oligodendrocyte progenitor differentiation process [45]. The miR-200 family in the mouse brain directly targets the pluripotency factor Sox2 and the cell cycle regulator E2F3 in neural stem/progenitor cells, while miR-200 expression is, in turn, directly regulated by Sox2 and E2F3 to jointly control neural progenitor cell cycle exit and differentiation [46].

During late embryogenesis or upon terminal differentiation of stem cells, the expression of miR-8 is upregulated, promoting the acquisition of somatic cell fates [44]. Upon initiating terminal differentiation, miR-8 also triggers endoreplication and promotes polyploidization by regulating target genes [42, 44]. Endoreplication increases ploidy (nuclear DNA content) without subsequent cell division, often resulting in enlarged cells and organs [47]. Consistent with this role, cells overexpressing miR-8 exhibit similar phenotypes, including expanded cell volume and enlarged nuclei [15, 42]. A key reason why miR-8 remains inactive during early embryonic and undifferentiated stem cell stages is the PRC2-mediated repression via trimethylation of histone H3 at lysine 27 (H3K27me3). PRC2 maintains the epigenetic suppression of miR-8 and pro-endoreplication/anti-proliferation genes such as zfh1, klu, Sox21a, pdm1, and GATAe, thereby preserving stem cell potency [12]. Before the onset of terminal differentiation, Notch signaling relieves this PRC2-maintained repressive chromatin state and is essential for miR-8 expression [12]. Subsequently, upregulated miR-8 promotes endoreplication. In glial cells, miR-8 overexpression leads to increased cell size without proliferation, suggesting that miR-8 may deplete negative regulators of endoreplication [42]. In muscle and intestinal tissues, miR-8 represses stemness-maintaining genes zfh1 and esg, with zfh1 acting upstream of esg [41, 44]. The esg gene encodes a C2-H2-type zinc finger protein that suppresses DNA endoreplication and maintains diploidy [48]. This regulatory network likely explains the strong expression of miR-8 in tissues and cells undergoing endoreplication.

Mechanistically, Notch signaling alleviates the repressive effect of E(z)/PRC2 on miR-8 expression. This Notch activity, in turn, antagonizes the pro-differentiation effect of miR-8 by regulating zfh1 isoform selection. The Wnt pathway is a key downstream effector, whose inhibition by this miRNA family drives differentiation. Both miR-8 (targeting esg) and miR-200 (targeting srf) promote terminal differentiation by repressing factors maintaining an undifferentiated state. Moreover, the family engages in evolutionarily conserved antagonistic interactions with key regulators of the EMT pathway, thereby ensuring the precision of the differentiation process. Furthermore, Sox2/E2F3 forms a feedback loop with the miR-200 family to control neural progenitor cell cycle exit and differentiation.

The miR-8/miR-200 family and cell survival and epithelial maintenance

The functions of the miR-8/miR-200 family are crucial for apoptosis and the maintenance of epithelial tissue. In Drosophila wings, miR-8 targets actin cytoskeleton regulators; its inhibition enhances resistance to apoptosis, while overexpression activates the JNK pathway and disrupts epithelial tissue [25]. Ammonia-N (NH3/NH4+) is a common abiotic stress factor in water, which can induce oxidative damage and cellular apoptosis at high concentrations in animals. miR-8 in L. vannamei negatively regulates the host’s antioxidant defense under ammonia nitrogen stress. Overexpression of miR-8 induces vacuolations and increased edema in the gill lamellae of L. vannamei, ultimately leading to gill apoptosis [18].

In a mouse model of diabetes, the miR-200 family is strongly induced in islets. Specifically, overexpressing miR-200 in β-cells induces β-cell apoptosis and type 2 diabetes (T2D) by regulating tumor suppressor Trp53, thereby promoting the expression of pro-apoptotic genes [49]. miR-200b, miR-200a, and miR-429 are essential in maintaining mammary epithelial identity; their genes are clustered together in mice. The miR-200b/200a/429 cluster maintains mammary epithelial identity by downregulating mesenchymal markers (Vim, Snai1, Twist1/2, and Zeb1) in a murine mammary tumor cell line, resulting in an epithelial phenotype [13]. EMT plays a critical role during tumor metastasis. Negative regulation, as an EMT inhibitor, is performed by the miR-200 family, which can inhibit transforming growth factor TGF-β-induced EMT by targeting the E-cadherin transcriptional repressors ZEB1 and SIP1 [50]. Epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) is overexpressed in most anaplastic thyroid cancers (ATC), and the activation of EGFR by its ligand, epidermal growth factor (EGF), promotes the migration of thyroid cancer cells and enhances their invasiveness. Targeting the EGFR pathway by the miR-200 family in SW1736 human thyroid cancer cells restores the EGF-induced epithelial phenotype [51].

The miR-8/miR-200 family and nerves and movement

The miR-8/miR-200 family participates in an organism’s neurogenesis, development, and neurotrophic functions. The conserved member of the Ena/VASP (Enabled/vasodilator-stimulated phosphoprotein) family, namely Ena, exerts its function within the subsynaptic reticulum (SSR) surrounding the presynaptic terminal. miR-8 targets the conserved sites in the 3′UTR of Ena, which is indispensable for SSR morphogenesis [52]. miR-8 targets atrophin to prevent neurodegeneration; both increased atrophin activity and excessive miR-8-mediated suppression are detrimental, indicating precise level control is essential in D. melanogaster [53]. Furthermore, miR-8 governs the initial assembly of synaptic sites in the embryo. The expression of synaptic cell adhesion molecules Fasciclin III (FasIII) and Neuroglian (Nrg) is subject to the regulation of miR-8. At the embryonic stage, miR-8 controls synaptic formation by coordinating the regulation of cell adhesion molecules FasIII and Nrg during synaptic development [54]. During neurogenesis, precise regulation of the renewal and differentiation of stem cells is necessary to guarantee the correct ratio and quantity of neural cells. miR-8 inhibits neuroblast transformation while promoting neuroepithelium proliferation, ensuring correct neurogenesis in the early larval stages [42].

The miR-200 family is specifically induced at the late stage of olfactory bulb neuron differentiation in newborn mice. The loss-of-function and gain-of-function studies of the entire miR-200 family determined that miR-200 targets the transcription factor Zeb2 to regulate neuronal maturation during postnatal neurogenesis [14]. Human embryonic stem cells (hESCs) can differentiate into neural ectodermal precursors (NEP) and epidermal (EPI) cells. By analyzing the miRNA expression profiles of hESCs in different differentiation directions, it was found that the miR-200 family shows decreased expression in NEP cells. Zinc finger E-box binding homeobox (ZEB) transcription factors play significant roles in neural induction, and the miR-200 family targets ZEB to regulate neural induction [55].

The miR-8/miR-200 family and growth and development

During insect growth and metamorphosis, the miR-8/miR-200 family promotes cell growth and proliferation in the fat body by inhibiting USH/FOG2 and enhancing PI3K activity, thereby activating the insulin/insulin-like growth factor signaling (IIS) pathway [10]. miR-8 expression is downregulated by ecdysone early-response genes, leading to increased activity in its target ush. The gene ush controls the size of insects’ bodies by inhibiting IIS signaling [56]. Furthermore, miR-8 regulates multiple hormonal peptides in Drosophila fat bodies that activate the IIS pathway [57]. Beyond metabolic regulation, miR-8 represses Yorkie to reduce tissue size via the Hippo pathway [58]. Chitin biosynthesis is essential for insect molting and growth. In Sogatella furcifera, miR-8 targets soluble trehalase (Tre1), resulting in pre-molting and molting lethality [59]. In A. chinensis, miR-8 modulates the IIS and mTOR pathways via ush and bub1b/lst8 and promotes proliferation via mcm7 and cdc27 [15].

miR-8 plays a pivotal role in organogenesis. In Tribolium castaneum, the Wingless, Eyg, Fpps, and Sema-1a genes are involved in organism development, such as wing formation, eye development, leg formation, and embryonic development [60]. Acyrthosiphon pisum exhibits wing dimorphism, producing winged or wingless offspring depending on environmental cues. Wnt signaling acts as a key regulator for wing morph determination. Knockdown of miR-8 induces the winged aphid phenotype by alleviating its negative regulation of the Wnt pathway [61].

The miR-200 subfamily members (homologous to miR-8) exert significant effects in vertebrates. miR-200b inhibits PI3K/Akt signaling while enhancing matrix metalloproteinase 2 (MMP2) expression, thereby promoting cell proliferation and migration [62]. In addition, miR-200b suppresses the angiogenic ability of human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) by targeting Notch1 [63]. During taste organ development in D. rerio, FGF signaling regulates Delta–Notch signaling and miR-200 activity to promote cell differentiation [64]. In mouse embryonic submandibular gland (SMG) development, miR-200c participates in organ morphogenesis by inhibiting FGFR-dependent epithelial proliferation via targeting the very-low-density lipoprotein receptor (Vldlr) [65]. Furthermore, miR-200c and miR-141 are essential for mouse tooth development, where they induce E-cadherin and amelogenin expression while regulating BMP signaling and the cell differentiation of dental epithelial cells [66].

The miR-8/miR-200 family and tumor metastasis

Tumor metastasis, which involves the invasion, migration, survival, and proliferation of tumor cells within new tissue microenvironments, is a pivotal factor in cancer progression and treatment. The evolutionarily conserved miR-8/miR-200 family exerts inhibitory effects on multiple stages of this complex process in both invertebrates and vertebrates. A key mechanism involves the regulation of EMT, a process intimately linked to tumor cell invasion and metastasis. miR-8 regulates the EMT pathway by targeting the zfh1 and esg genes [41, 44]. The miR-200 family and the transcriptional repressor Zeb1 engage in a bidirectional regulatory loop that critically controls EMT. The miR-200/Zeb1 axis inhibits the CRKL-mediated activation of the FAK/Src signaling pathway, which is directly implicated in tumor cell invasion and metastasis [67]. Consequently, the miR-200b/200a/429 cluster promotes an epithelial state by downregulating EMT markers (Vim, Snai1, Twist1/2, and Zeb1), reducing metastatic potential [13].

The miR-8/miR-200 family also acts as a potent negative regulator of oncogenic signaling pathways. By suppressing the Notch signaling pathway, this miRNA family controls cell proliferation. In advanced tumors, the loss of miR-8/miR-200 expression coupled with excessive Notch activation leads to uncontrolled proliferation and enhanced invasiveness, driving metastasis [68, 69]. Furthermore, the family directly targets regulators of the cytoskeleton and cell adhesion. In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), overexpression of miR-200b/200c/429 impedes cell migration by targeting RhoA and ROCK2, key mediators of cytoskeletal reorganization and cell–matrix adhesion. Modulation of Rho/ROCK activity underlies the inhibition of HCC metastasis by these miRNAs [70]. Similarly, expression levels of miR-200a/200b/200c/429 are significantly downregulated in cholangiocarcinoma. miR-200b/c targets SUZ12/ROCK2 directly to inhibit tumorigenesis and migration, and invasion of cholangiocarcinoma cells in vitro and in vivo [71].

The tumor-suppressive role extends to targeting critical factors involved in cell cycle progression and signaling. In non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), miR-200b/c targets the 3′UTR of RhoE, a pivotal factor in NSCLC development, inducing G0/G1 phase arrest. This results in the inhibition of proliferation and invasion while altering EMT-related transcript expression [72]. High mobility group box 3 (HMGB3), implicated in tumorigenesis across various cancers, is negatively regulated by miR-200b/c. Targeting HMGB3 suppresses MAPK signal transduction by inhibiting MAPK phosphorylation, ultimately reducing glioblastoma (GBM) tumorigenic potential, proliferation, and metastatic capabilities [73].

The unique functions and regulatory pathways in vertebrates

The miR-8/miR-200 family and cancer and disease

The miR-200 family is downregulated in various cancers, where it serves as diagnostic/prognostic markers and playing key roles in disease pathogenesis. Within the mammary gland, the miR-200 family maintains an epithelial phenotype and suppresses breast tumorigenesis by suppressing the expression of mesenchymal markers. In triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), miR-200c is expressed at significantly low levels and targets genes involved in EMT to repress their activity [74]. Overexpression of miR-200b/200a/429 in mouse mammary tissues inhibits Spp1, Saa1, and Saa2 to prevent mammary tumorigenesis [75]. In addition, miR-200b and miR-429 target the LIMK1/CFL1 pathway, downregulating cellular actin and cyclin expression while inhibiting the proliferation and motility of TNBC cells [76]. In gastric cancer cells, miR-200c targets CXCL12 and CXC chemokine receptor 7 (CXCR7) to inhibit cell proliferation while inducing cell cycle arrest in vitro. Conversely, targeting NRG1 via ERBB2/ERBB3 signaling prevents gastric cancer development through regulation by miR-200b [77, 78]. Knockdown of all members of the miR-200 family induces senescence within gastric cancer cells, along with the recruitment of stromal cells within the tumor microenvironment. This activation leads to EMT induction alongside TGF-β/TNF-α pathway activation [79]. Chemokine receptors CX3CR1 and CXCR1 regulate metastasis as well as invasion potential within lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD); modulation by members of the miR-200 family reduces overall proliferative capacity within these tumors located within their respective microenvironments [80].

Hirschsprung’s disease (HSCR) is a developmental disorder caused by defective migration of enteric neural crest cells (ENCCs). Studies reveal that miR-200a and miR-141 are significantly downregulated in HSCR colon tissues, leading to phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) upregulation, which inhibits ENCC migration and proliferation. Functional experiments demonstrate that restoring miR-200a/141 expression or knocking down PTEN significantly enhances migration and proliferation in 293T and SH-SY5Y cells, suggesting that targeting the miR-200a/141-PTEN axis represents a novel therapeutic strategy for HSCR [81]. Delivery of miR-200a/141 mimics or PTEN-inhibiting small molecules may restore normal ENCC development. In addition, miR-200a/141 expression levels could serve as noninvasive diagnostic biomarkers for early HSCR screening. This study highlights the central regulatory role of noncoding RNAs in HSCR and provides a theoretical foundation for miRNA-based precision therapies.

The miR-200 family is aberrantly overexpressed in diabetic mouse islets, where its upregulation directly triggers β-cell apoptosis and lethal T2D. Mechanistically, miR-200 regulates apoptosis via dual mechanisms: (1) suppressing key anti-apoptotic factors, such as Dnajc3 (chaperone p58IPK) and Xiap (caspase inhibitor), thereby weakening cellular defenses against oxidative and inflammatory stress, and (2) dose-dependently activating the tumor suppressor Trp53 and its pro-apoptotic target Bax, inducing mitochondrial dysfunction and programmed cell death. Genetic knockout of miR-200 significantly reduces β-cell apoptosis and improves T2D phenotypes, underscoring its role as a key regulator of T2D progression. The pathological role of miR-200 positions it as a promising therapeutic target. Inhibiting miR-200 could restore Dnajc3/Xiap-mediated cytoprotection and block the Trp53–Bax axis, preserving β-cell viability and delaying T2D [49]. Antisense oligonucleotides or small-molecule inhibitors targeting miR-200 may offer novel approaches to reverse β-cell failure. Furthermore, miR-200 expression levels could serve as biomarkers for assessing β-cell damage and guiding personalized therapies.

Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is a chronic liver condition linked to metabolic syndrome, with approximately 20% of patients with NAFLD progressing to non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH). The miR-200 family is markedly downregulated in patients with NAFL/NASH, with its expression inversely correlating with liver injury severity and metabolic dysregulation [82]. Compared with controls, patients with NAFLD/NASH exhibit reduced exosomal miR-200 and miR-298 levels alongside elevated pro-inflammatory miRNAs, a signature detectable via real-time quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) and validated by high diagnostic specificity in receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses. Unlike invasive biopsies or low-sensitivity imaging, miR-200 family members serve as noninvasive biomarkers to distinguish NAFL/NASH stages and predict progression. While current research focuses on miR-200’s regulatory roles, its involvement in metabolic diseases is gaining traction. Elucidating its specific targets and mechanisms in NAFL/NASH, combined with miR-200’s therapeutic potential, may pave the way for miRNA-based multitarget interventions to advance early diagnosis and precision treatment.

Diabetic retinopathy (DR), a chronic microvascular complication of diabetes, arises from prolonged hyperglycemia-induced retinal vascular leakage, ultimately causing vision loss. Aberrant activation of the TGF-β2/Smad pathway plays a pivotal role. Clinical studies confirm that elevated TGF-β2 levels in the vitreous correlate positively with VEGF (an angiogenic factor) expression in patients with DR, highlighting this pathway as a therapeutic target. The miR-200 family, particularly miR-200a-3p, is significantly downregulated in diabetic retinal tissues. Mechanistically, miR-200a-3p targets TGF-β2, inhibiting Smad2/3 phosphorylation, reducing inflammatory cytokines and angiogenic factors, and promoting endothelial cell survival, thereby counteracting DR progression [83]. Future studies elucidating the functional divergence of miR-8/miR-200 family members in regulating the TGF-β pathway may advance miRNA-based gene therapies to reverse early DR lesions or mitigate advanced pathology.

The miR-8/miR-200 family and immunosuppression

Tryptophan catabolism contributes significantly to tumor progression. A key metabolite, kynurenine (Kyn), binds to the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) in CD8+ T cells in a paracrine manner, suppressing their antitumor activity. Tryptophan-2,3-dioxygenase (TDO2) catalyzes the rate-limiting step of this pathway. Its activation increases intracellular expression and extracellular secretion of Kyn. In triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), inflammatory signals and loss of cell adhesion can induce TDO2 expression. miR-200c emerges as a critical regulator within this immunosuppressive axis. It directly inhibits TDO2 expression, thereby reducing Kyn production in TNBC. Furthermore, miR-200c post-transcriptionally suppresses several other immunosuppressive factors, including programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1/CD274), heme oxygenase 1 (HO-1), and growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF-15). Collectively, miR-200c exerts broad post-transcriptional immune regulation, impacting tryptophan metabolism, allograft rejection, complement and coagulation pathways, and cytokine/cytokine receptor signaling [74].

The infiltration of lymphocytes, particularly CD8+ tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), into tumor tissue represents an antitumor immune response. However, TILs are often functionally suppressed within the tumor microenvironment (TME), which comprises various immunosuppressive cells such as tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and regulatory T cells (Tregs). Blocking the interaction between the co-inhibitory receptor PD-1 and its ligand PD-L1 has shown therapeutic promise. A critical molecular link exists between epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), a driver of cancer progression, and CD8+ TIL immunosuppression. miR-200 acts as a negative regulator of EMT and directly targets PD-L1 for inhibition. Conversely, the transcriptional repressor ZEB1, a potent activator of EMT, represses miR-200 expression. This alleviates miR-200-mediated inhibition of PD-L1 in tumor cells. Thus, miR-200 and ZEB1 reciprocally regulate tumor cell PD-L1 expression and intratumoral immunosuppression, influencing CD8+ T-cell function and metastasis [84].

The evolutionary conservation and translational applications of the miR-8/miR-200 family

The miR-8/miR-200 family represents a paradigm of evolutionary conservation and functional innovation. As a phylogenetically ancient regulator originating in early-diverging bilaterians, this family has undergone lineage-specific expansion and functional repurposing from governing fundamental physiology in invertebrates to orchestrating complex disease pathways in vertebrates. This section synthesizes its evolutionary trajectory across taxa and examines emerging translational applications in agroforestry, diagnostics, and therapeutics. It evaluates how conserved molecular mechanisms enable cross-species functionality while highlighting adaptations that drive species-specific targeting. Critically, the discussion addresses the dual potential of miR-8/miR-200 members as both biological tools (e.g., nucleic acid pesticides and diagnostic biomarkers) and therapeutic targets, alongside key challenges in delivery specificity and functional pleiotropy that require resolution for successful clinical and agricultural implementation.

The miR-8/miR-200 family: evolutionary trajectory from invertebrate physiology to vertebrate disease regulation

The miR-8/miR-200 family, a highly conserved miRNA family in bilaterian animals, exemplifies adaptive evolution from simple to complex gene functionality. Phylogenetic analyses indicate that miR-8 serves as the ancestral gene of this family, first emerging in Platyhelminthes and subsequently diversifying into multiple isoforms—miR-200a, miR-200b, miR-200c, miR-141, and miR-429—through gene duplication during vertebrate evolution. This genomic expansion is closely associated with increased organismal complexity. For instance, in mammals, miR-200 family members maintain epithelial cell phenotypes and suppress tumor metastasis by targeting EMT-related genes such as ZEB1, SIP1 [50], RhoE [72], and CD274/CD273 [74]. However, invertebrates possess only a single miR-8 member, which engages a substantially smaller repertoire of target genes during EMT. Such functional diversification not only reflects an evolutionary strategy of gene repurposing but also provides a model for studying complex regulatory networks in higher animals. Although miR-8/miR-200 family members exhibit minor sequence variations across species (1 nt differences in seed sequences), their core target genes (particularly within the Wnt and Notch pathways) remain highly conserved. For example, Drosophila miR-8 regulates the Wnt signaling pathway by suppressing Wntless [43], while the human miR-200 family achieves analogous functions by targeting β-catenin [85]. This conservation underscores the family’s central role in maintaining cellular homeostasis. miR-8 modulates osmotic stress responses via NHERF1 in Danio rerio (zebrafish) [39], a mechanism strikingly similar to human miR-200c’s function in renal diseases [86], where both regulate host stress responses by targeting transmembrane ion transporters. Such cross-species functional conservation highlights its evolutionary significance.

In invertebrates, miR-8 primarily governs immune defense and reproductive development, whereas in vertebrates, the miR-200 family has expanded its roles to tumor suppression and disease pathogenesis. For instance, miR-8 promotes yolk protein synthesis by targeting swim to activate the Wingless pathway in Drosophila [30], whereas human miR-200 enhances antitumor immunity by suppressing PD-L1 [84]. This functional divergence reflects species-specific adaptations to environmental pressures. Recent studies reveal that the miR-200 family has acquired novel roles in regulating tumor microenvironments in mammals. For example, a study demonstrated that miR-200a/141 inhibits immune evasion in lung adenocarcinoma by targeting CX3CR1/CXCR1 [80], a function absent in invertebrates, suggesting that post-duplication functional innovation drives complex disease mechanisms in higher organisms. The evolutionary conservation of the miR-8/miR-200 family provides a natural model for studying gene function evolution. Furthermore, cross-species comparisons emphasize miRNAs’ pivotal role in adaptive evolution: insect miR-8 regulates juvenile hormone synthesis to adapt to environmental changes [16], while the mammalian miR-200 family enhances drug sensitivity by suppressing EMT.

The application prospects and challenges of the miR-8/miR-200 family

Agricultural and forestry production

Insects, as key agricultural ecological factors, support the sustainability of agriculture and forestry through biological control of natural enemies (such as A. chinensis, which preys on pests of multiple orders), which reduces chemical inputs, and product manufacturing (such as B. mori, which supplies most of the world’s silk, and T. molitor, which provides-high protein livestock feed and may help remediate organic and plastic waste by converting it into resources), thereby combining pest management with a circular bioeconomy. miR-8, a highly conserved miRNA in insects, regulates host immunity, development, and reproduction. Given its functional versatility, miR-8 holds significant application potential in agricultural and forestry production, particularly for pest control, targeting beneficial insects, pests, and economically important species.

In pest management, miR-8 could serve as a nucleic-acid-based pesticide to reduce diseases and pests in agriculture and forestry. For immunity, miR-8 can reduce immune activity in pests, rendering them more susceptible to pathogen infection and mortality [24]. For development, through its involvement in insect molting and organ development [56], dysregulation of miR-8 expression in pests may lead to molting failure, developmental deformities, increased mortality, or reduced activity range. For reproduction, by affecting the development of germ cells, miR-8 can diminish the reproductive capacity of pest populations [20]. In summary, miR-8 shows significant potential as a nucleic-acid-based pesticide that can control pest populations by disrupting multiple biological processes, thereby protecting crop yields.

However, the practical deployment of miR-8 faces several limitations. As a small RNA, miR-8 exhibits inherent instability in complex environments and is susceptible to nuclease degradation. Nucleic acid pesticides primarily function through foliar spraying or root immersion, necessitating delivery vehicles or chemical modifications to maintain miR-8 efficacy. Nanomaterials are promising carriers for such green pest control strategies. Nanocarriers can enhance pesticide stability, improve foliar adhesion and systemic transport, and prolong the duration of action. Crucially, they effectively protect nucleic acids from enzymatic degradation [87].

In addition, off-target effects of miR-8 remain a significant challenge throughout drug development, including preclinical and clinical stages. Beyond utilizing delivery systems to mitigate side effects from off-target activity, chemical modifications can enhance the stability of the miRNA, promote its cellular uptake, increase target affinity, and improve specificity to reduce off-target effects [88]. Efficient delivery of miR-8 to target pests, enabling targeted release into the cytoplasm, represents another critical hurdle. The high conservation of the miR-8/miR-200 family underscores the necessity of ensuring product specificity during practical application to minimize impacts on nontarget organisms. Furthermore, within the same insect, miR-8 mediates a trade-off between reproduction and growth [30, 31]. Concurrently, miR-8-mediated suppression of the innate immune system increases the susceptibility of miR-8-overexpressing insects to pathogen-induced mortality [17]. This substantially alleviates concerns that the growth benefits of miR-8 overexpression might outweigh the reproduction costs. This functional pleiotropy, however, underscores the need to develop tissue-specific delivery systems.

Baculoviruses, with their inherent insecticidal properties, host specificity, and favorable safety profile, serve as natural delivery vectors for the specific delivery of miRNAs [89]. Tandem repetition of miRNA precursor sequences within baculoviruses may represent a strategy to enhance insecticidal efficacy [90]. Insect sex pheromones share similar advantages in host specificity and mammalian safety. These species-specific, complex natural compounds are nontoxic to mammals and environmentally benign, enabling large-scale pest trapping and mating disruption [91]. Combining miR-8 application with sex pheromones presents a potential strategy to reduce the quantity of miR-8 required for large-scale use while potentially improving efficacy, although this hypothesis urgently requires validation through specific studies. Sex pheromones can also be combined with nanomaterials to reduce volatility and extend their duration [91]. Therefore, the combined application of miR-8 with baculoviruses, nanomaterials, or sex pheromones offers a feasible concept for facilitating the safe and practical implementation of miR-8-based pest control strategies. It is important to note that while addressing the hurdles impeding the practical application of miRNAs, establishing a rational regulatory framework is equally crucial. Currently, regulatory frameworks for nucleic acid pesticides are underdeveloped, necessitating the establishment of risk assessment standards and the acceleration of standardized production and quality control protocols across all stages.

Medical diagnostics

In addition, the miR-200 family’s ability to target cancer hallmarks and its dysregulation in diseases have spurred diagnostic and therapeutic advancements. miR-200 family members are now recognized as liquid biopsy biomarkers for multiple cancers, with their expression levels post-treatment serving as recovery indicators. Beyond cancer, the miR-200 family exhibits dysregulated expression in other diseases. miR-200a shows a strong correlation with pathological markers of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) [92]. miR-200a and miR-141 are significantly downregulated in colon tissues of patients with Hirschsprung’s disease (HSCR) [81]. Exosomes from patients with non-alcoholic fatty liver (NAFL) or non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) display reduced miR-200 expression levels [82]. miR-200a-3p is significantly downregulated in diabetic retinal tissues [83]. The miR-200 family is aberrantly overexpressed in the pancreatic islets of diabetic mice [49].

The dysregulation patterns of the miR-200 family are disease-specific. Consequently, pathological detection requires precise localization to specific organs, tissues, or even cell types, imposing stringent and demanding requirements on detection technologies and standards. Current miRNA detection has evolved from tissue biopsies to liquid biopsies, enabling noninvasive and highly sensitive analysis [93]. However, clinical implementation still necessitates standardized and streamlined workflows, alongside enhanced detection capabilities for heterogeneous populations with unique genetic backgrounds or high drug resistance. Monitoring miR-200 expression holds significant importance for tracking tumor progression and assessing the efficacy of various therapeutic interventions. As such, these miRNAs possess substantial clinical potential as biomarkers to guide personalized treatment selection.

Furthermore, miRNA profiling could potentially be integrated into routine health screenings. By comparing an individual’s miRNA levels against both their own historical baseline and population averages, this approach could enable the prediction of disease onset or facilitate early diagnosis. Establishing large-scale databases for population-wide miRNA expression profiles, coupled with personalized longitudinal miRNA tracking for individuals, could refine personalized medicine. Comprehensive data analysis of such repositories may also uncover novel associations between miRNAs and a broader spectrum of diseases.

Disease treatment

As a core EMT suppressor, the miR-200 family is frequently downregulated in cancers, with its loss directly correlating with tumor aggressiveness. The miR-200/ZEB1 axis reduces kynurenine (Kyn) production by inhibiting TDO2, thereby reversing the immunosuppressive microenvironment in TNBC [84]. Recent studies show that restoring miR-200 expression in PANC-1 pancreatic cancer cells suppresses tumor viability, invasion, and migration. The expression of miR-200 is also correlated with the prognosis of pancreatic cancer, making it a promising therapeutic strategy to halt disease progression [94]. A 2025 study shifted focus from cytotoxic therapies to preventive approaches in breast cancer. Using transgenic mice with 100% tumor incidence, researchers demonstrated that elevating miR-200 family expression in myoepithelial cells completely abrogates Neu-driven mammary tumorigenesis [95].

Researchers are also exploring combination therapies and targeted delivery systems. The miR-200 family holds therapeutic potential, particularly through its inhibition of the EMT pathway. For instance, combining miR-200 mimics with chemotherapeutic agents can counteract tumor drug resistance by suppressing EMT [96]. In addition, a 2020 study combined miR-200c-loaded nanoparticles with immune checkpoint inhibitors, significantly enhancing efficacy in gastric cancer treatment and synergizing with radiotherapy [97]. In 2021, co-administering miR-200c with adoptive T-cell therapy (ACT) improved T-cell survival in solid tumors and boosted antitumor activity mediated by T-cell receptor (TCR) and chimeric antigen receptors (CARs) [98]. A 2023 breakthrough showed that oral delivery of miR-200-loaded lipid nanoparticles restores intestinal stem cells (ISCs) and promotes regeneration in mice with acute injury, improving the safety and efficacy of gut disease treatments [99]. However, caution is warranted owing to the family’s dual regulatory roles. For instance, miR-200 overexpression in mouse pancreatic islets induces β-cell apoptosis and type 2 diabetes [49], underscoring the need for precise modulation.

Currently, achieving safe and effective targeted delivery remains a major obstacle for clinical translation. Several strategies have been developed to address this challenge: first, the conjugation of miRNA therapeutics with maleimide for covalent binding to endogenous albumin has demonstrated potent antitumor activity while avoiding systemic toxicity in models of solid and metastatic tumors [100]. Second, recombinant baculoviruses engineered to display ligands for specific tumor surface antigens enhance delivery precision; their inherent inability to replicate in mammalian cells mitigates risks from sustained transgene expression, with improved transduction efficiency validated in human liver and pancreatic cancer cells [89]. Third, apolipoprotein-mediated fusion technology enables the conjugation of tumor-targeting antibodies with lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) encapsulating miRNAs, showing potential to reduce systemic toxicity arising from the functional pleiotropy of this family [101]. Fourth, utilizing exosomes derived from mesenchymal stem cells for the expression and delivery of target miRNAs also presents considerable therapeutic potential [102]. Off-target effects remain the most significant technical barrier. Current approaches involving the combination of miRNAs with chemical modifications, biomolecular conjugates, or delivery vectors can enhance precise cellular targeting and improve efficiency [103]. Particularly noteworthy is the necessity for comprehensive risk assessment before miRNA-based therapies to mitigate off-target effects and avoid either subtherapeutic efficacy due to insufficient dosage or adverse health effects from excessive concentrations. From a regulatory perspective, it is crucial to establish evaluation standards specifically tailored for miRNA therapeutics, particularly concerning their in vivo persistence and long-term biological effects in oncology treatments.

In conclusion, the miR-8/miR-200 family, recognized as an evolutionarily conserved multifunctional regulator, represents an ideal model for investigating gene function evolution and species adaptation. This family also offers essential targets for exploring disease mechanisms and advancing precision medicine (Fig. 9). Future research should prioritize the elucidation of its regulatory networks in non-model organisms and the optimization of therapeutic strategies for clinical application, ultimately bridging the gap between basic research and practical implementation.

Discussion

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) regulate the expression of numerous genes, influencing essential biological processes including host development, immunity, metabolism, and reproduction. Their dysregulation is implicated in diverse disease states [104]. Although miRNAs play a critical role as gene regulators, their evolutionary origin remains unresolved. Throughout organismal evolution, miRNA repertoires have expanded in both quantity and diversity, accompanied by increasing regulatory complexity [105]. These evolutionary adaptations enable miRNAs to fulfill fine-tuned regulatory requirements for gene expression control. The miR-8/miR-200 family exists in various organisms, with more evolutionarily advanced organisms possessing more abundant family members. Its functional repertoire transitions from invertebrate-specific roles in immune defense [17], insect reproduction [20], and biosynthesis [35] to core physiological processes, such as stress response [40], cell differentiation [41], cell survival and epithelial maintenance [13], neural development [53], growth and development [10], and tumor metastasis [68], and further to vertebrate-specific functions in cancer and disease [83] and immunosuppression [74]. Our analysis demonstrates that the family’s evolutionary trajectory, from a single ancestral miR-8 in invertebrates to a multi-member system in vertebrates, has created a functional continuum that directly informs its application potential in agriculture and medicine.

The phylogenetic conservation and functional evolution

The remarkable sequence conservation of miR-8/miR-200 members across bilaterians, combined with their strategic genomic expansion in vertebrates, reflects an evolutionary adaptation to increasing organismal complexity. This deep conservation implies the maintenance of essential biological functions through evolution, potentially via conserved regulatory mechanisms. While core seed sequences and fundamental regulatory principles remain largely unchanged, duplication and diversification into miR-200a/b/c, miR-141, and miR-429 isoforms have enabled functional diversification and network stabilization. Under distinct selective pressures, lineage-specific adaptations likely drive functional divergence across species. This genomic and functional expansion correlates with the emergence of sophisticated physiological systems in vertebrates, particularly in epithelial maintenance, immunosuppression, and disease pathogenesis. The conservation of target pathways—Notch, Wnt, PI3K, and EMT—across evolutionary boundaries demonstrates the fundamental importance of these regulatory circuits, while species-specific modifications reveal adaptive fine-tuning to different physiological requirements. Consequently, owing to variations in target genes across species, practical applications shift from pest control in agriculture (targeting invertebrates) to medical diagnosis and treatment in humans (targeting vertebrates).

The conserved core pathways with translational implications

The functional conservation of miR-8/miR-200 regulation across species provides strong validation for its translational relevance. In invertebrates, miR-8’s regulation of innate immune pathways [24, 106] and reproductive processes [30] establishes fundamental principles of immune homeostasis and developmental control that find application prospects in agricultural pest management. The demonstrated ability of miR-8 to modulate insect immunity, reproduction, and development through conserved pathways offers attractive opportunities for nucleic-acid-based pesticides that can disrupt multiple biological processes in target pests while potentially minimizing environmental impact through species-specific delivery systems.

In vertebrate, the miR-200 family’s regulation of epithelial–mesenchymal transition and cell differentiation pathways reveals conserved mechanisms that have been repurposed for mammalian pathophysiology, such as EMT in development and cancer metastasis, while retaining core immunoregulatory principles, albeit through a broader set of targets such as PD-L1 [74, 84]. The family’s role in maintaining epithelial integrity through targeting ZEB transcription factors [50] and its involvement in tumor suppression [68–75] provide mechanistic insights bridging evolutionary biology and cancer therapeutics. This evolutionary perspective not only validates these pathways as biologically fundamental but also identifies them as high-value targets for therapeutic intervention.

The translational applications informed by evolutionary principles

The evolutionary trajectory of the miR-8/miR-200 family directly informs its practical applications. In agriculture, the insect-specific functions of miR-8 in immunity and reproduction suggest strategies for developing targeted pest control measures that exploit these conserved vulnerabilities (avoiding side effects on vertebrates in the environment). In medical applications, the vertebrate miR-200 family’s conserved role in epithelial maintenance and its dysregulation in carcinomas establishes it as both a diagnostic biomarker and therapeutic target [68–75, 107]. The family’s context-dependent functions, acting as a tumor suppressor in most carcinomas while promoting β-cell apoptosis in diabetes [49], highlight the importance of tissue-specific delivery systems, a challenge that mirrors the specificity requirements in agricultural applications. The recent demonstration that MSC-derived exosomes can deliver therapeutic miRNAs [102] represents an approach leveraging natural biological processes, similar to the baculovirus delivery systems explored in agricultural contexts [90].

The inherent functional trade-offs observed in miR-8/miR-200 family members represent one factor complicating translational applications. For instance, miR-8 in invertebrates exhibits an antagonistic relationship between regulating insect reproduction and development. In mammals, miR-200 members can exert opposing regulatory effects on normal cell proliferation and malignant tumor growth. Therefore, therapeutic strategies must carefully select the specific miR-8/miR-200 family member best suited to the target organism and the desired therapeutic outcome. Consequently, focusing on translating single, well-defined functions of these miRNAs could significantly enhance application efficiency and safety.

Current challenges and future directions

The dual challenges of delivery specificity and functional pleiotropy represent the next frontier in harnessing this miRNA family’s potential. In agricultural applications, the development of nanocarriers [87] or species-specific delivery systems using baculoviruses [89] must balance efficacy with environmental safety. Similarly, clinical translation requires sophisticated delivery platforms capable of achieving tissue-specific miRNA modulation while avoiding off-target effects and systemic toxicity that might arise from the family’s pleiotropic functions. Developing engineered exosomes [102], nanocarriers [101], viral vectors [90], or delivery systems for antibodies targeting specific surface antigens [108] represents promising future directions.

Future research should leverage evolutionary insights from comparative studies to identify optimal intervention points. The conservation of pathways across species suggests that mechanisms validated in invertebrate models may have direct relevance to vertebrate systems, potentially accelerating therapeutic development while reducing research costs. Simultaneously, lineage-specific adaptations revealed through phylogenetic analysis can guide the development of highly specific interventions that minimize off-target effects in both agricultural and clinical contexts. In conclusion, the miR-8/miR-200 family demonstrates how evolutionary biology can directly inform translational applications. The family’s deep conservation provides validation for targeting specific pathways, while its functional diversification across species offers insights for developing context-specific interventions.
