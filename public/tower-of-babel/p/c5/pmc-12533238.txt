Impact of neoadjuvant and adjuvant chemotherapy on breast cancer prognosis in a propensity score matched population
Authors: Ying Xu, Bowen Liu, Chang Chen, Feng Mao, Songjie Shen, Xiaohui Zhang, Xin Huang, Yan Lin, Yidong Zhou, Qiang Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBreast cancer is the most common cancer among women worldwide. To evaluate the real-world effectiveness of Neo-adjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) and adjuvant chemotherapy (ACT), we conducted a retrospective study comparing their impact on breast cancer prognosis. We retrieved data from the SEER database. We applied propensity score matching (PSM) to minimize confounding factors. Breast-cancer specific survival (BCSS) and overall survival (OS) were compared between the two groups. We also developed a nomogram prognostic model using clinicopathological parameters to predict survival rates. We identified 46,447 eligible patients in the SEER database. After PSM, 9,025 patients were included in each group, with 11,762 patients receiving NACT and 34,685 undergoing ACT before PSM. In the PSM cohort, the 5-year BCSS and OS were 88.3% (95% CI: 87.6–89.0%) and 85.7% (95% CI: 84.9–86.5%) for the NACT group, respectively. Notably, patients in the ACT group showed significantly better survival prognosis compared to those in the NACT group. We also developed nomograms to predict the 3-year, 5-year, and 8-year OS and BCSS rates for patients receiving NACT or ACT. Introduction

Breast cancer stands as the most prevalent form of cancer among women globally, ranking second as the leading cause of cancer-related fatalities. In 2024, the United States will witness a staggering 310,720 new cases of breast cancer, with a projected 42,250 individuals succumbing to this disease1. Due to the advancements in early detection for breast cancer and the improvement of treatment, there has been a decline in breast cancer-related mortality rates on an annual basis1. Adjuvant chemotherapy has been shown to significantly increased both overall survival (hazard ratio 0.75, 95% CI 0.69–0.83) and disease-free survival (HR 0.82, 95% CI 0.75–0.90) compared to surgery alone2. Breast cancer patients who are at a heightened risk of recurrence often require chemotherapy following surgical intervention. This therapeutic approach aims to eradicate any residual tumor cells that may remain in the bloodstream, thereby reducing the likelihood of disease recurrence and improving patient outcomes. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) emerged in the 1970 s as a novel approach to managing breast cancer. Its primary objective is to downstage locally advanced, potentially inoperable tumors, rendering them amenable to surgical intervention3. Furthermore, NACT aims to shrink the tumor size, thereby facilitating breast-conserving surgery as an option for patients. Some experts contend that NACT may offer a superior capacity to eradicate micro-metastatic diseases compared to adjuvant chemotherapy administered post-surgery, underscoring its potential significance in the comprehensive treatment of breast cancer3.

Previous studies have indicated that patients who exhibit a complete clinical response after neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) generally have a more favorable prognosis compared to those who show a partial response or little to no response4,5. Consequently, some experts argue that neoadjuvant therapy may be more efficacious than adjuvant therapy, and suggest that tumor complete response can serve as a criterion for identifying patients with a good prognosis, while allowing for intensified treatment strategies for those with a poor response. In recent years, the scope of neoadjuvant therapy applications has continued to broaden. For patients with T2 stage disease or higher, guidelines now recommend NACT as an alternative to adjuvant chemotherapy, particularly for those with HER2-positive or triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC)6. However, despite trials demonstrating high rates of complete remission, NACT has not demonstrated significant superiority over adjuvant chemotherapy in terms of distant recurrence or cancer-related mortality7.

To assess the effectiveness of two types of chemotherapy in real-world settings, we conducted an exhaustive review of the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database from the US National Cancer Institute. Our analysis aimed to compare the influence of neoadjuvant chemotherapy and adjuvant chemotherapy on the prognosis of breast cancer patients.

Compared to patients undergoing adjuvant chemotherapy, those receiving neoadjuvant therapy were younger and presented with more advanced disease at diagnosis, characterized by later disease stages and more lymph node metastasis. In terms of pathological features, the NACT group was predominantly comprised of high-grade tumors, with a higher incidence of HR-negative and HER2-positive tumors. Notably, the proportions of triple-negative and HER2-positive breast cancers were significantly elevated in this group compared to the ACT group. Regarding treatment, a greater number of patients in the NACT group underwent total mastectomy and received radiotherapy. (Table 1)

Following PSM, 9,025 patients from each treatment group were included in the following analysis. In the PSM cohort, patients younger than 55 years old comprised 51.4% of the population, slightly outnumbering those older than 55. Regarding tumor Characteristics, high-grade tumors constituted 59.4% of cases, while stage II patients exhibited the highest proportion, ranging between 61.8% and 61.9%. Additionally, HR-positive patients accounted for 68.7%, and HER2-negative patients represented 68.1% of the cohort. In terms of breast cancer molecular subtypes, Luminal A&B, HER2, and Basal-like subtypes comprised 68.7%, 8.2%, and 23.3% respectively. The PSM cohort demonstrated well-balanced patient Characteristics between the two treatment groups in terms of age, tumor-related features, and radiation therapy status. However, notable differences in surgical treatment were observed. Specially, The proportions of patients undergoing mastectomy were 59.5% and 57% in the two groups, respectively. (Table 1)

Survival outcomes of selected patients

Before PSM, the median follow-up duration for patients in the NACT group was 60 months, while for those in the ACT group, it was 68 months. The interquartile range (IQR) for the follow-up duration was 35 months in the NACT group and 36 months in the ACT group. Kaplan–Meier curves that compare the survival between the two groups are shown in Fig. 2. Patients in ACT group had a better prognosis according to BCSS and OS (p < 0.0001).

Specifically, the 5-year BCSS and OS for patients in the ACT group were 94.1% (95% CI 93.9–94.4%) and 91.4% (95% CI 91.1–91.7%), respectively. In contrast, the corresponding rates for patients in the NACT group were 86.7% (95% CI 86.0-87.3%) and 84.0% (95% CI 83.3–84.7%). When compared to the ACT group, the unadjusted Hazard Ratios (HRs) for BCSS and OS in the NACT group were 2.27 (95% CI 2.14–2.42) and 1.86 (95% CI 1.76–1.96), respectively. Even after adjusting for factors such as age, stage, and tumor treatment status in a multivariate sensitivity analysis, the survival difference between the two groups remained significant (Table 2).

In the PSM cohort, the 5-year BCSS for the NACT and ACT groups were 88.3% (95% CI: 87.6–89.0%) and 92.2% (95% CI: 91.6–92.8%), respectively. Correspondingly, the 5-year OS were 85.7% (95% CI: 84.9–86.5%) for the NACT group and 89.6% (95% CI: 89.0-90.3%) for the ACT group. After PSM, patients in the ACT group exhibited significantly superior survival prognosis compared to those in the NACT group (Fig. 2). Specifically, the unadjusted hazard ratios (HRs) for BCSS and OS in the NACT group, relative to the ACT group, were 1.52 (95% CI: 1.39–1.66) and 1.40 (95% CI: 1.29–1.52), respectively. Even after conducting a multivariate sensitivity analysis, a statistically significant difference in survival between the two groups remained evident (P < 0.001). (Table 2)

We conducted a comprehensive subgroup analysis of clinical and pathological factors within the PSM cohort. As depicted in Supplementary Fig. 1, the results revealed that in the majority of subgroups, patients in ACT group exhibited better prognosis in BCSS and OS compared to those in NACT group. However, specifically among Grade I breast cancer patients (n = 1042) and patients with HR-negative, HER2-positive breast cancer subtypes (n = 1478), no statistically significant difference in BCSS was observed between NACT group and ACT group. Additionally, in the survival analysis of patients with stage IV breast cancer (n = 459), no significant difference in BCSS or OS was noted between the two groups.

We developed nomogram models within the training cohort to predict BCSS (Fig. 3A) and OS (Fig. 3B) for patients undergoing neoadjuvant and adjuvant chemotherapy, respectively. These models were constructed based on patient age, clinical and pathological characteristics of the tumors, and treatment status with hazard ratios. The nomogram determines the probability of survival by summing the scores corresponding to each variable on the point scale. The cumulative score, when projected onto the bottom scale, provides an indication of the likelihood of BCSS and OS.

Nomograms predicting 3-, 5‐, and 8‐year BCSS (A) and OS (B) rates of breast cancer patients.

The Harrell’s C-index for the nomogram model predicting BCSS was 0.748(95% CI 0.734–0.761). Similarly, the C-index for the nomogram model predicting OS was 0.718 (95% CI 0.706–0.731). These indicated that both nomogram models demonstrate good discriminative capabilities. ROC curves for the prediction nomogram for BCSS indicated that the 3-, 5- and 8-year AUCs were 0.742, 0.736, and 0.728 in the training cohort and for OS were 0.713, 0.707 and 0.699, respectively (Fig. 4A and D).

Discussion

Since the widespread adoption of neoadjuvant chemotherapy for the treatment of breast cancer, a persistent debate has emerged regarding which approach—neoadjuvant or adjuvant chemotherapy—demonstrates superior efficacy. We conducted a comparative analysis of the prognosis of patients undergoing neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by surgery versus those undergoing surgery followed by adjuvant chemotherapy, utilizing data sourced from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database. In the real-world setting, patients who undergo surgery followed by adjuvant chemotherapy exhibit significantly improved prognosis compared to those who receive neoadjuvant chemotherapy. In the initial cohort prior to PSM, patients who underwent neoadjuvant chemotherapy demonstrated more advanced tumor staging, as well as a higher incidence of local lymph node involvement and distant metastasis, compared to those who received adjuvant chemotherapy. This is in line with clinical practice, where, for patients presenting with advanced stages of the disease, doctors are more prone to opting for neoadjuvant chemotherapy. This approach aims to shrink the tumor and manage metastases effectively prior to surgical intervention. Previous studies have demonstrated that neoadjuvant chemotherapy is efficacious in reducing tumor burden and mitigating adhesions between tumor tissue and neighboring tissues. Consequently, this treatment approach diminishes surgical complexity and associated risks, while concurrently inhibiting tumor progression and metastasis3,8. In our research, we utilized the Cox proportional hazards model to adjust for patient age, tumor stage, and tumor treatment. Nevertheless, a statistically significant survival difference persisted between the two patient groups even after these adjustments. This outcome suggests that the survival disparity between the groups is influenced not only by tumor stage but also potentially by the sequence of chemotherapy and surgery, highlighting the potential importance of different types of chemotherapy (neo-adjuvant or adjuvant chemotherapy) in determining patient outcomes.

To ensure a robust comparison of the long-term survival outcomes between patients in the neoadjuvant chemotherapy group and those in the adjuvant chemotherapy group, we conducted propensity score matching(PSM) on the original cohort. This method allowed us to align the baseline data of the two groups more closely, facilitating a more accurate assessment of the efficacy of each treatment approach. After PSM, no significant differences were observed in tumor staging, receptor status, and tumor molecular typing between the two patient groups. Notably, the adjuvant chemotherapy group exhibited significantly better long-term survival compared to the neoadjuvant chemotherapy group. This finding challenges our conventional understanding, suggesting that the benefit of neo-adjuvant chemotherapy for breast cancer patients may not be as significant as previously anticipated. In fact, it may even fall short of the long-term survival outcomes achieved with the more traditional adjuvant chemotherapy. The efficacy of neoadjuvant chemotherapy is intimately tied to its response rate. The prognosis for patients who achieve a complete clinical response following NACT is superior to that of patients who exhibit a partial response, and significantly better than for those who demonstrate little or no response to NACT4,5. However, even in clinical trials with high frequency of complete remission, NACT group did not show significant superiority over adjuvant chemotherapy group in terms of distant recurrence or cancer mortality7. For the general patient population, a complete response to neo-adjuvant chemotherapy can serve as an indicator for identifying breast cancer patients who are likely to have a favorable prognosis. However, neoadjuvant chemotherapy may not provide superior clinical outcomes compared to adjuvant chemotherapy across all patient populations.

When compared to neo-adjuvant chemotherapy, direct surgical intervention was found to more rapidly reduce the tumor burden of patients. Following surgery, postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy effectively targeted and eliminated any residual tumor cells that may have persisted in the bloodstream, while mitigating the risk of tumor progression associated with potentially ineffective neoadjuvant chemotherapy. According to existing literature, the side effects associated with NACT chemotherapy have been reported to potentially result in surgical complications. These complications, in turn, may cause further delays in the administration of adjuvant systemic therapy and radiotherapy, ultimately impacting the overall prognosis of patients9. A recent study further indicated that a delay in surgery following NACT chemotherapy was correlated with inferior survival outcomes. Specifically, a delay exceeding 8 weeks after the completion of systemic therapy is associated with reduced survival rates10. The surgical scope typically tended to be more limited in patients who have undergone NACT chemotherapy compared to those who have received adjuvant chemotherapy. Additionally, there may be challenges in accurately locating the tumor after NACT, which can increase the risk of local recurrence2,3. The clinical trial on radiotherapy indicated that a substantial reduction in local recurrence of breast cancer was associated with a decrease in cancer-related mortality. Specifically, for every four instances of local recurrence that were averted, one cancer death was prevented11. This finding may partially explain the superior efficacy observed in the adjuvant chemotherapy group.

In prior research, it has been observed that women with small tumors, tumors that are negative for ER, and tumors that are PR + or high-grade are more inclined to attain a pathological complete response (PCR) following neoadjuvant chemotherapy2. The subgroup analysis of this study revealed no statistically significant difference in BCSS between the NACT and ACT groups among patients with stage I breast cancer and those with HR negative status. This observation may be explained by the high rate of PCR and the favorable prognosis associated with these patient subgroups. In prior clinical trials, the rates of pCR among patients with Basal-like breast cancer and HER2-positive breast cancer were approximately 50% and 30%, respectively12. In this study, no statistically significant difference was observed in BCSS among HER2-positive patients who underwent neo-adjuvant or adjuvant chemotherapy. However, for patients with basal-like breast cancer, those who received adjuvant chemotherapy exhibited improved BCSS and OS. These findings suggested that different molecular subtypes may influence the prognosis of patients based on the chemotherapy approach they choose. Although stage IV breast cancer is generally considered incurable, breast cancer has a relatively favorable prognosis. Previous studies have shown that oligometastatic breast cancer patients may achieve long-term survival or clinical cure through a combination of local therapy and systemic chemotherapy13–15. The NCCN Guidelines also recommend that oligometastatic breast cancer be considered for local therapy combined with systemic treatment16. In clinical practice, we have observed that patients with bone metastases or single liver/lung metastases can achieve cure through surgical resection combined with systemic chemotherapy. In this study, referring to the definition of oligometastasis from previous clinical research, we included a small number of stage IV breast cancer patients with metastases confined to 1–2 organs13–16, all of whom received both surgery and chemotherapy. For these patients, the sequence of chemotherapy and surgery did not affect their prognosis.

A key advantage of neoadjuvant chemotherapy is its capacity to reduce tumor size, thereby facilitating breast-conserving surgery. However, in the PSM cohort, the breast-conserving rate in the NACT group was not higher than that in the ACT group. Conversely, a higher percentage of patients opted for total mastectomy following neoadjuvant chemotherapy. While it is true that patients eligible for breast-conserving surgery may opt out due to other factors—including reluctance to undergo postoperative radiotherapy, financial constraints, or concerns that breast conservation might increase the risk of local recurrence—this finding may suggest that the role of neoadjuvant chemotherapy in achieving the goal of breast conservation may not be as effective as we initially anticipated. While neoadjuvant chemotherapy offers several advantages, including allowing surgeons additional time for decision-making, facilitating potential downgrades in surgical procedures, and providing guidance for subsequent treatment through drug sensitivity testing, the formulation of treatment decisions requires a comprehensive consideration of patients’ benefits and potential harms.

To enhance the accuracy of assessing the prognosis of breast cancer patients following their choice of neo-adjuvant or adjuvant chemotherapy, we utilized PSM cohort data to develop and validate a nomogram prediction model. The excellent differentiation and calibration of the model indicated its potential utility in assessing the prognosis of breast cancer patients who had undergone different chemotherapy options, thereby facilitating personalized treatment decisions. Notably, older age (≥ 55 years) at diagnosis emerged as a significant risk factor for patient prognosis, aligning with the findings of the model previously established using the SEER database17. A separate SEER-based study18 demonstrated that women under 40 years had a significantly higher breast cancer-specific mortality in unadjusted analysis (HR 1.8, 95% CI 1.6–1.9). However, after adjusting for confounding factors, younger age remained independently associated with worse outcomes only in the hormone receptor (HR)-positive/low-grade disease subgroup (HR 1.7, 95% CI 1.4–2.1), but not in high-grade/HR-positive, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-positive, or triple-negative subtypes. Notably, women over 75 years showed increased mortality risk across all subtypes. In the context of modern molecular classification, younger age (< 40 years) persists as an independent adverse prognostic factor exclusively in the hormone receptor-positive/low histological grade disease subset.This finding is complemented by a TNBC-specific study19, which reported that elderly patients with advanced disease had significantly poorer cancer-specific and overall survival compared to younger patients. Further evidence from a multinational European-Asian cohort analysis20 revealed that patients over 75 years had the poorest prognosis despite a higher prevalence of HR-positive tumors, potentially due to immunosenescence and diminished DNA repair capacity that may offset the prognostic advantage of HR positivity. Intriguingly, data from the Korean Breast Cancer Society21 showed that patients over 60 years had the lowest breast cancer-specific survival rate and worst prognosis, highlighting potential ethnic variations in age-related prognostic patterns. A higher T stage, N stage, and grade were consistently associated with poorer outcomes, whereas HR-positivite emerged as a protective factor for both BCSS and OS, suggesting that patients with HR-positive tumors have a more favorable prognosis. These findings were in line with our understanding. Due to the rapid advancement and widespread utilization of targeted therapy medications directed at the HER2 receptor, positivity for the HER2 receptor has emerged as a favorable prognostic indicator17. Patients who had undergone breast-conserving surgery may experience an improved prognosis, owing to the adjunctive radiation therapy administered post-surgery. In clinical practice, the nomogram model could serve as a valuable tool for estimating patient prognosis across diverse treatment options, enabling clinicians to make informed treatment decisions that ultimately enhance patient outcomes.

This study possesses several limitations that should be noted. Firstly, it is a retrospective study, which inherently limits the completeness and accuracy of the data. Consequently, incomplete data were excluded from the analysis, potentially introducing selection bias. Secondly, the SEER database lacks specific information regarding targeted therapy and endocrine therapy, including details on the selection of specific therapeutic agents, which hinders our ability to assess their impact. Additionally, there are no detailed records on whether patients in both groups received intensified postoperative treatments. These limitations further restrict the generalizability of our study findings. Additionally, the SEER database does not provide detailed information on chemotherapy regimens, making it Challenging to analyze the effects of different chemotherapy drugs on prognosis. In this study, during the data analysis, it was assumed that all cases marked as receiving systemic chemotherapy had received standard chemotherapy regimens that conformed to the treatment guidelines at that time. Furthermore, the median follow-up time of this study was 60 months, which is insufficient to evaluate longer-term survival risks. This is particularly problematic for patients with favorable prognoses, such as those with HR+/HER2 breast cancer or stage I breast cancer, as the follow-up time may be too short. Notably, previous meta-analysis results have indicated that there is no significant difference in the 15-year distant recurrence risk and mortality rate between the NACT and ACT groups, with only a variation in local recurrence rates.[3] Lastly, the time interval (2013–2018) for the inclusion of patients in this study is in a crucial transitional period of the systemic treatment paradigm for breast cancer. During this period, milestone studies such as the CREATE-X study (2015), the KATHERINE study (2018), and the MONARCH-E study (2020) have not fully transformed clinical practice, which may have an impact on the study results and endow this study with limitations related to the time period. In the absence of options for intensified treatment, the value of pathological assessment in neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) has not been translated into survival benefits. Furthermore, the presence of intact tumor during neoadjuvant chemotherapy may provide a more favorable immunological environment for immunotherapy. The KEYNOTE-522 trial22 showed that incorporating pembrolizumab into neoadjuvant chemotherapy regimens significantly improved overall survival compared to conventional neoadjuvant chemotherapy alone in patients with early-stage, high-risk TNBC. In contrast, the IMpassion03023 and SWOG S1418(NCT02954874) trials investigating adjuvant immunotherapy in TNBC patients failed to show significant clinical benefits24. Although no head-to-head clinical trials have directly compared neoadjuvant versus adjuvant immunotherapy combinations, the available evidence suggests potential superiority of the neoadjuvant approach when combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors. However, as more intensive treatment strategies and novel therapeutic agents emerge, whether neoadjuvant chemotherapy can sustain long-term survival advantages requires further validation through extended follow-up.

Method

Data source

We obtained the data from the SEER database using SEER*State software (version 8.4.2) based on November 2021 submission (2000–2019). The SEER database covered cancer incidence and survival records of over 30% of the US population25. The variables of data included age at diagnosis, tumor grade, tumor stage, node stage, TNM stage, HR status, HER2 status, molecular subtypes, surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy and survival data.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Peking Union Medical College Hospital (PUMCH), Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (No. I-23PJ1439). This retrospective study analyzed existing medical records and data, adhering to ethical regulations that allowed for exemption from informed consent, without direct involvement or interaction with participants.

Patient selection

We collected data from SEER database according to the following inclusion criteria: (a) January 2013 to December 2018; (b) female patients aged over 18 years old; (c) diagnosed with primary breast cancer; (d) underwent chemotherapy and surgery. Exclusion criteria include: (a) incomplete follow-up data; (b) follow-up time < 6 months; (c) not invasive carcinoma; (d) without chemotherapy or surgery; (e) unknown sequence of therapy; (f) with unknown clinical characters; The flowchart of participant inclusion and exclusion is shown in Fig. 1. Considering that the 2013 St.Gallen International Breast Cancer Conference redefined the molecular typing of breast cancer and this definition has been in use ever since, in order to ensure the consistency of the molecular typing of the included data, we chose 2013 as the starting time for the inclusion of patients. At the same time, to ensure an adequate follow-up period, the data of the included population was cut off at 2018.

We used the time-to-event outcomes as relevant outcomes of prognosis. Overall survival (OS) and breast cancer-specific survival (BCSS) were calculated from the date of diagnosis until the death of any cause and death due to breast cancer, respectively.

Statistical analyses

For demographic and clinicopathological data, continuous variables were presented as mean value and standard deviation whereas categorical variables as numbers and percentages. Continuous variables were compared using t test. Binary categorical variables were compared using Pearson’s chi-squared test. The comparison of multi-category ordered variables was performed using Wilcoxon test.

We used the propensity score matching (PSM) analytic approach to reduce confounding risk. Specially, we performed a 1:1 “nearest-neighbor” matching method to match patients between neo-adjuvant chemotherapy group and adjuvant chemotherapy by adjusting for age, tumor grade, tumor stage, molecular subtypes, HR status, HER2 status, surgery and radiotherapy. Survival outcomes were reported both before and after PSM. The Kaplan-Meier method was utilized to construct the survival curve for breast cancer patients, and comparisons were performed using the log-rank test. Cox’s proportional hazards regression models were constructed to analyze the factors associated with survival in PSM cohort and also subgroup analyses.

Nomogram prognostic model was developed with the clinicopathological parameters to predict 3-year, 5-year and 8-year OS and BCSS in PSM cohort. The Survival Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve and C-index were computed to assess the discriminatory ability of the nomogram model. Calibration curves were employed for visual inspection of model calibration. Additionally, decision curve analysis (DCA) was conducted to evaluate the potential clinical utility of the model.

All p-values were two-sided, and the statistical significance was set at 0.05. All analyses were performed using R software (Version 4.2.2).

Conclusion

This retrospective study demonstrated that breast cancer patients who received surgery followed by postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy had a more favorable prognosis compared to those who received neoadjuvant chemotherapy before surgery. The retrospective analysis of the SEER database for breast cancer patients treated between 2013 and 2018 indicated that neoadjuvant chemotherapy did not improve the prognosis of patients. With the emergence of new treatment modalities and research evidence, clinical decision has changed. It is necessary to conduct future studies with more comprehensive data on emerging treatment strategies to further clarify the role of neoadjuvant chemotherapy in the treatment of breast cancer.
