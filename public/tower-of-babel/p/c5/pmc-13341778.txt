Exercise-associated changes in leptin and irisin relate to cognitive function in older adults stratified by cognitive impairment
Authors: Sami A. Gabr, Mohamed E. Salama, Abdullah H. Alzahrani, Hadeel Alsirhani, Maysa A. Aljohany, Masood Khan, Ahmad H. Alghadir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Age-related cognitive decline and cognitive impairment are associated with metabolic dysfunction, oxidative stress, inflammation, and disrupted neurotrophic signaling; however, the mechanisms linking exercise-induced molecular changes to cognitive improvement remain incompletely understood. This study investigated whether a 12-week moderate-intensity aerobic exercise program modulates circulating irisin, leptin, and brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and their associations with cognitive performance in older adults stratified by cognitive status. Sixty participants were classified into normal cognitive function (NCF), mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and moderate cognitive impairment (MoCI) groups according to Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) scores and completed supervised aerobic training for 12 weeks. Anthropometric and metabolic parameters together with oxidative stress markers (malondialdehyde, total antioxidant status, and superoxide dismutase), inflammatory cytokines (IL-6 and TNF-α), and circulating irisin, leptin, and BDNF were evaluated before and after intervention. Exercise significantly improved metabolic indices, antioxidant capacity, and inflammatory profiles across all groups (p < 0.05 to p < 0.001). Circulating irisin and BDNF increased, whereas leptin levels decreased following training. Improvements were also observed in global cognitive performance and specific domains including executive function, attention, and memory, with greater responses in participants with MCI and MoCI. Changes in irisin and BDNF showed positive associations with MoCA domains, whereas leptin demonstrated inverse relationships. These findings suggest that aerobic exercise improves cognitive function through coordinated regulation of myokine and neurotrophic signaling pathways alongside reductions in oxidative stress and inflammation, particularly in older adults with cognitive impairment. These findings also, support further controlled studies to clarify causal relationships and underlying mechanisms.

Introduction

Aging is a natural process marked by the gradual deterioration of physical functions. It reflects a diminishing ability of an organism to adjust to its surroundings and a reduced regenerative capacity of tissues and organs across the body. These changes significantly contribute to the risk of various human diseases, such as cancer, diabetes, cardiovascular conditions1,2. In addition, aging is frequently accompanied by a progressive decline in cognitive function, leading to neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer’s disease and age-related dementia3.

Aging often brings about various cognitive changes, with normal aging typically resulting in mild cognitive decline. Such changes may include noticeable yet manageable impairments in memory and other mental functions that do not severely disrupt daily life4,5. Nonetheless, the transition from mild cognitive impairment (MCI) to more serious forms of cognitive deterioration, like dementia, poses a substantial concern for older individuals and those who care for them6.

Cognitive deterioration is strongly influenced by both metabolic and hormonal dysregulations. Among the hormones implicated, leptin and irisin have emerged as critical players in the regulation of neuroplasticity, synaptic function, and overall cognitive performance7.

Leptin, primarily secreted by adipose tissue, is well known for its role in energy homeostasis. Beyond metabolism, leptin exerts neuroprotective effects, influencing hippocampal synaptic plasticity, learning, and memory8.

Dysregulation of leptin signaling has been associated with cognitive impairment in obesity and aging9. Leptin, besides its anti-obesity action as regulating hormone in the maintenance of body weight10, there is growing evidence supporting the significant influence of leptin on both glycemic control11, and cognitive function12.

Irisin, a myokine cleaved from the FNDC5 protein during exercise, has gained attention for its neurogenic and neuroprotective properties. Irisin enhances brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) signaling, which supports synaptic function and cognitive resilience13. Previous, irisin has revealed to exerts effects beyond muscle tissue, playing an important role in synaptic regulation within the brain. Significantly, its influence on the nervous system demonstrates an impressive ability to boost cognitive function14,15.

Emerging evidence suggests that leptin and irisin share overlapping signaling pathways that may converge on neuroinflammatory processes, insulin signaling, and synaptic plasticity, thus contributing to cognitive health in aging populations16–19. However, the exact mechanisms underlying their hormonal crosstalk remain poorly understood.

Exercise is a well-established non-pharmacological intervention known to improve metabolic health and cognitive performance. It not only increases circulating irisin but also modulates leptin sensitivity, thereby providing a potential synergistic regulatory effect on brain function19–21.

Although aerobic exercise has consistently demonstrated beneficial effects on cognitive performance and has been proposed as a non-pharmacological strategy for delaying cognitive decline among aging individuals and those with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), the biological mechanisms underlying these improvements remain incompletely understood. Particular attention has recently focused on exercise-associated myokines, especially irisin, because of their proposed involvement in neuroprotection, synaptic plasticity, regulation of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), and metabolic homeostasis. However, evidence regarding the role of irisin in human cognitive impairment remains limited and heterogeneous, with inconsistent findings concerning circulating irisin concentrations, cognitive performance, and disease severity. Furthermore, data describing exercise-associated irisin responses across different stages of cognitive impairment, particularly moderate cognitive impairment, remain scarce. Therefore, additional studies are required to determine whether exercise-associated changes in irisin are linked with cognitive responses in aging populations and whether these associations differ according to cognitive status7–22.

Current evidence suggests independent roles of leptin and irisin in metabolic regulation, neuroprotection, and cognitive function during aging; however, limited information is available regarding their potential interaction and association with cognitive status in older adults, particularly in relation to exercise-associated physiological changes19–23. Therefore, the present study was designed to address this knowledge gap by evaluating the relationship between leptin and irisin signaling and their association with cognitive health in aging individuals. We hypothesized that exercise-associated changes in leptin and irisin levels would be related to cognitive function and neuroprotective markers among older adults and that these relationships may differ according to cognitive status.

Accordingly, this study aimed to investigate the interrelationship between leptin and irisin hormonal signaling and their association with cognitive health among aging individuals. Specifically, the study evaluated older adults exposed to a supervised 12-week moderate-intensity aerobic exercise program and examined exercise-associated changes in serum leptin, irisin, brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), antioxidant status, and inflammatory cytokines. In addition, the study explored whether these changes were associated with cognitive performance and markers related to neuroprotection and age-associated cognitive impairment.

This study was designed using a PICO-oriented framework in which the Population (P) consisted of older adults stratified according to cognitive status based on MoCA scores (NCF, MCI, and MoCI groups); the Intervention (I) involved participation in a supervised 12-week moderate-intensity aerobic exercise program; the Comparator (C) was limited to within-group baseline versus post-intervention assessment because no non-exercise control group was included; and the Outcomes (O) included changes in cognitive performance, circulating leptin, irisin, BDNF levels, oxidative stress biomarkers, inflammatory cytokines, metabolic indices, and the observed associations between these biomarkers and cognition.

Materials and methods

Sample size estimation

The sample size was estimated a priori using global cognitive performance assessed by the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) as the primary outcome variable because cognitive improvement represented the principal endpoint of the study. Sample size calculation was performed using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7) under a two-tailed significance level of α = 0.05, 90% statistical power (1 − β = 0.90), and a 95% confidence interval, assuming a moderate effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.65) derived from previously published exercise-based interventions reporting significant improvements in cognitive performance among older adults and individuals with mild cognitive impairment assessed using MoCA and related neurocognitive measures22,25–26,56−62. Based on these assumptions, the minimum required sample size was estimated to be 56 participants, which was considered sufficient to detect meaningful exercise-associated changes in cognitive outcomes and related biochemical parameters including leptin, irisin, BDNF, oxidative stress, and inflammatory biomarkers. To compensate for potential attrition, incomplete datasets, participant withdrawal, or non-response during follow-up, the recruitment target was increased and a total of 60 participants were finally enrolled. Participants were subsequently stratified according to cognitive status into Normal Cognitive Function (NCF; n = 20), Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI; n = 20), and Moderate Cognitive Impairment (MoCI; n = 20) groups.

Ethical considerations

The study took place between June 2024 and December 2025, focusing on 100 individuals who residing in their homes and were randomly invited to participate in this study. The ethics sub-committee at King Saud University assessed and approved this study in accordance with ethical guidelines, as recorded under file reference RRC-2024-08 dated 15/4/2024. The research adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki (2010). Prior to commencing the study, a signed informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Study participants

A total of sixty adults aged 50 to 80 years (30 men and 30 women) who fulfilled the eligibility criteria and provided written informed consent were enrolled in this study. Participants were classified according to cognitive status using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) as follows: normal cognitive function (NCF; MoCA ≥ 26), mild cognitive impairment (MCI; MoCA score 19–25), and moderate cognitive impairment (MoCI; MoCA score < 19). To reduce the influence of educational background on cognitive assessment outcomes, only participants with an educational level exceeding 12 years were included, this criterion was selected to reduce educational bias and improve interpretation of MoCA scores, as educational attainment may influence cognitive screening performance. Participants with obesity (BMI ≥ 35 kg/m²), physical disabilities, mobility limitations, chronic hepatic or renal diseases, severe diabetes-associated complications (neuropathy, retinopathy, neuromuscular disorders), cardiopulmonary diseases, endocrine disorders, immune dysfunction, psychiatric illnesses, eating disorders, or current glucocorticoid treatment were excluded because these conditions may influence circulating leptin, irisin, BDNF levels, oxidative stress markers, inflammatory cytokines, and cognitive function. All participants had a sedentary lifestyle characterized by little or no regular physical activity during work, transportation, or leisure activities. Participants were instructed to maintain their habitual dietary patterns throughout the study period without modifications. Demographic and clinical data of the participants are shown in Table (1).

Assessment of cognitive function

Cognitive function was assessed using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), a brief, standardized screening instrument developed to detect mild cognitive impairment and early cognitive decline across multiple clinical populations22–26. The MoCA evaluates several cognitive domains, including visuospatial/executive function, attention, language, memory, abstraction, naming, and orientation. Total scores range from 0 to 30, with higher scores indicating better cognitive performance. The MoCA was administered by trained assessors in a quiet clinical setting according to standardized administration guidelines.

For participants with 12 years of education or fewer, one point was added to the total MoCA score to correct for educational bias, as recommended in the original validation study. In older adults, the MoCA was used as a sensitive tool to identify age-related cognitive decline and mild cognitive impairment (MCI).

A cutoff score of < 26 was applied to indicate cognitive impairment, consistent with previous validation studies in geriatric populations. Scores between 26 and 30 were considered indicative of normal cognitive aging, while lower scores reflected varying degrees of cognitive dysfunction.

The MoCA was employed to assess global cognitive status and domain-specific impairments, particularly executive function and memory. Cognitive impairment was defined as a MoCA score < 26, with severity further categorized as mild (19–25), moderate (11–18), or severe (< 10), in line with prior neurocognitive research, whereas, subjects with Normal cognitive function comprises MoCA score of (26–30).

Thus, participants were classified according to cognitive performance using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA). Individuals with scores between 26 and 30 were classified as having normal cognitive function (NCF), scores between 19 and 25 were categorized as mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and scores between 11 and 18 were classified as moderate cognitive impairment (MoCI). These cutoffs were selected to allow stratification of participants according to progressive cognitive decline while maintaining clear separation between normal cognition and increasing impairment severity. The classification was adapted from previously reported MoCA-based cognitive stratification approaches and modified to fit the distribution of cognitive scores observed in the present cohort22–26.

Training procedure

Based on Karvonen’s formula, participants completed a supervised moderate-intensity aerobic exercise program performed three times weekly for 12 weeks. Exercise intensity was individualized according to resting and maximal heart rates to achieve the target training heart rate (THRmax; 60–70%). Each session included a 5–10 min warm-up period followed by 45–60 min of aerobic interval exercise using a treadmill, cycle ergometer, or stair climber. Exercise intensity corresponded approximately to 30–45% of maximal oxygen consumption (VO₂max). Heart-rate responses were continuously monitored throughout training sessions using wearable automatic heart-rate monitors (Polar Electro, Kempele, Finland).

All participants completed the exercise intervention, resulting in a 100% adherence and retention rate with no reported withdrawals or dropouts during the 12-week training period. Attendance and exercise compliance were monitored throughout the intervention to ensure adherence to the prescribed exercise intensity and duration.

Leisure-time physical activity (LTPA)

Leisure-time physical activity (LTPA) was assessed using the validated Baecke Habitual Physical Activity Questionnaire, which evaluates habitual physical activity patterns with particular emphasis on occupational activity, sports participation, and leisure-time exercise. The questionnaire has demonstrated acceptable validity and reproducibility in adult populations, with reported reliability coefficients and intraclass correlation values generally exceeding 0.70. In accordance with previous studies, weekly energy expenditure was expressed as total leisure-time physical activity in metabolic equivalent hours per week (T-LTPA-MET-h/week)26,31–33.

Biochemical analysis

Blood serum samples were collected from all participants in the early hours after a night of fasting, both before and after the exercise training regimen, to analyze biochemical parameters related to diabetes, inflammatory, oxidative stress, and antioxidant profiles as well as Leptin, Irisin, and brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) hormones.

Assessment of glycemic control parameters

All participants underwent assessment of glycemic control parameters before and after a 12-week moderate exercise program. After overnight fasting, venous blood samples were collected. Plasma glucose was measured using an enzymatic colorimetric method (glucose oxidase–peroxidase or hexokinase) on a calibrated spectrophotometric analyzer, according to the manufacturer’s instructions32. HbA1c was determined from EDTA whole blood/hemolysate using an IFCC-standardized, DCCT/NGSP-traceable turbidimetric inhibition immunoassay (TINIA) (Roche Tina-quant® HbA1c Gen.3) on a compatible chemistry analyzer, with internal quality controls applied. Fasting plasma insulin was quantified using a sandwich ELISA (Abcam Human Insulin ELISA Kit, ab100578, or equivalent), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Immunoassay analysis of Irisin, Leptin, and BDNF

Plasma concentrations of irisin, leptin, and brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) were measured before and after the 12-week moderate exercise intervention. Following overnight fasting, venous blood samples were collected into EDTA tubes, centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C, and plasma aliquots were stored at − 80 °C until analysis.

Biomarker concentrations were quantified using commercially available human enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Plasma irisin levels were measured using a Human Irisin ELISA Kit (Catalog No. E-EL-H5735; Elabscience Biotechnology Inc., Houston, TX, USA). Leptin and BDNF concentrations were determined using validated human ELISA kits obtained from R&D Systems (Minneapolis, MN, USA) using Human Leptin Quantikine ELISA Kit (Catalog No. DLP00) and Human BDNF Quantikine ELISA Kit (Catalog No. DBD00), respectively.

For the irisin assay (E-EL-H5735), the manufacturer-reported analytical sensitivity was 0.19 ng/mL, with a detection range of 0.31 to 20 ng/mL, and intra-assay and inter-assay coefficients of variation maintained below 10%. All samples and standards were analyzed in duplicate, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader with wavelength correction according to assay recommendations. Hormone concentrations were calculated from standard curves generated using four-parameter logistic (4-PL) regression analysis. To minimize inter-assay variability, pre- and post-exercise samples obtained from the same participant were analyzed within the same assay run. Laboratory personnel performing biomarker analyses were blinded to participant grouping and intervention outcomes. Quality control procedures included duplicate measurements for all samples, use of manufacturer-provided standards and controls, and analysis of paired pre- and post-intervention samples of the same participant within the same analytical batch to reduce inter-assay variability34–41.

Immunoassay analysis of TNF-α and IL-6

Serum concentrations of tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) were measured in samples collected before and after the 12-week moderate exercise intervention using commercially available Quantikine Human Immunoassay ELISA kits obtained from R&D Systems (Minneapolis, MN, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. IL-6 concentrations were determined using the Human IL-6 Quantikine HS ELISA Kit (Catalog No. HS600B), whereas TNF-α levels were measured using the Human TNF-α Quantikine HS ELISA Kit (Catalog No. HSTA00E). The manufacturer-reported minimum detectable concentrations were approximately 0.7 pg/mL for IL-6 and 1.6 pg/mL for TNF-α. All samples and standards were analyzed in duplicate, and absorbance was measured using a microplate reader at 450 nm, with wavelength correction according to assay recommendations. Cytokine concentrations were calculated from standard curves generated using four-parameter logistic (4-PL) regression analysis. To minimize analytical variability, pre- and post-exercise samples obtained from the same participant were assayed within the same analytical run. Laboratory personnel performing cytokine analyses were blinded to participant classification and intervention outcomes. Given the established role of chronic inflammation in aging and cognitive decline, IL-6 and TNF-α were selected as representative pro-inflammatory biomarkers to evaluate exercise-associated immunomodulatory responses42–46.

Immunoassay analysis of MDA, SOD, and TAC

Plasma levels of malondialdehyde (MDA), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and total antioxidant capacity (TAC) were determined before and after the 12-week moderate exercise intervention as markers of oxidative stress and antioxidant status47–51.

MDA concentrations were measured using a Human Malondialdehyde (MDA) ELISA Kit (Catalog No. E-EL-0060; Elabscience Biotechnology Inc., Houston, TX, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The assay is based on enzyme-linked immunosorbent detection for quantitative determination of plasma MDA levels. Samples and standards were analyzed in duplicate, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. MDA concentrations were calculated from standard curves generated by four-parameter logistic (4-PL) regression analysis.

SOD activity was evaluated using a Human Superoxide Dismutase (SOD) ELISA Kit (Catalog No. E-EL-H1113; Elabscience Biotechnology Inc., Houston, TX, USA). The assay quantitatively determines SOD activity based on inhibition of superoxide-mediated oxidative reactions according to the manufacturer’s protocol. All samples were assayed in duplicate, and absorbance measurements were obtained at 450 nm.

Total antioxidant capacity (TAC) was measured with a commercial colorimetric assay kit (K274-100; BioVision, Milpitas, CA, USA), that assesses overall plasma antioxidant capacity against a defined oxidant. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 17.0). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Normality of data distribution was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Within-group comparisons (pre versus post exercise) were performed separately for the Normal Cognitive Function (NCF), Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI), and Moderate Cognitive Impairment (MoCI) groups using paired Student’s t-tests. Cohen’s d effect sizes were additionally calculated to estimate the magnitude of exercise-induced changes and were interpreted as small (d = 0.2), moderate (d = 0.5), and large (d ≥ 0.8). Between-group differences at baseline and post-intervention were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test to determine whether differences among cognitive severity groups persisted after exercise training. Variables that did not satisfy normality assumptions were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Associations between biochemical, hormonal, neurotrophic, and cognitive parameters, as well as exercise-induced changes in biomarkers and MoCA cognitive domains, were evaluated using Pearson’s or Spearman’s correlation coefficients according to data distribution. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

This study explored the modulation of the levels of Leptin, Irisin, BDNF hormones, antioxidant status, and inflammatory cytokines by participating in supervised aerobic exercise of moderate type for a 12-week. In addition, this work tried to scope on whether these modulations potentially enhancing neuroprotection and mitigating age-related cognitive impairment among healthy adults. Thus, the interrelationship between leptin and irisin hormonal signaling in the regulation of cognitive health among aging individuals were identified.

Participant characteristics and cognitive classification

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were comparable among the normal cognitive function (NCF), mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and moderate cognitive impairment (MoCI) groups, with no significant differences in age, sex distribution, anthropometric indices, or blood pressure parameters (P > 0.05). In contrast, cardiorespiratory fitness (VO₂max) and leisure-time physical activity (LTPA) progressively declined with worsening cognitive status (P < 0.01–0.001). Mean MoCA scores differed significantly among groups (P < 0.001), confirming the validity of cognitive stratification (Table 1).

Effects of exercise training on metabolic, biochemical, and cognitive parameters

Following 12 weeks of supervised moderate-intensity aerobic exercise, significant improvements were observed across all study groups. Exercise training led to reductions in BMI, waist-to-height ratio, fasting blood glucose, HbA1c, and HOMA-IR, indicating enhanced metabolic and glycemic control, with the greatest relative improvements noted in the MCI and MoCI groups (Table 2). Aerobic capacity (VO₂max) and physical activity levels increased significantly in all groups.

Markers of oxidative stress and inflammation were favorably modulated after exercise, as evidenced by significant decreases in MDA, IL-6, and TNF-α levels, alongside marked increases in total antioxidant status and SOD activity. Concomitantly, circulating irisin and BDNF levels increased significantly, while leptin levels decreased, indicating enhanced myokine–neurotrophic signaling following exercise training (Table 2).

Exercise-induced changes in global cognitive function and MoCA domains

Exercise training resulted in significant improvements in total MoCA scores across all cognitive groups (P < 0.05–0.001), with the largest gains observed in participants with MCI and MoCI (Table 2). Domain-specific analysis revealed baseline impairments in memory, executive function, attention, language, visuospatial ability, and orientation in MCI and MoCI participants compared with normal controls. Post-exercise, all groups demonstrated significant within-group improvements across most cognitive domains.

Notably, executive function, attention, and memory domains showed the greatest responsiveness to exercise, particularly in MCI and MoCI participants, whereas language and orientation domains exhibited smaller but significant gains. Between-group differences persisted after exercise but were attenuated, indicating partial restoration of cognitive function with increasing benefit proportional to baseline impairment severity (Table 2).

Moreover, within-group analysis demonstrated significant exercise-associated improvements across metabolic, oxidative stress, inflammatory, neurotrophic, and cognitive parameters in NCF, MCI, and MoCI participants. Cohen’s d analysis indicated predominantly moderate to large intervention effects, with greater magnitudes observed in the cognitively impaired groups. Between-group analysis performed at the post-exercise time point using one-way ANOVA showed that several variables remained significantly different among NCF, MCI, and MoCI groups after training, indicating that although all groups improved following exercise intervention, the magnitude of response differed according to baseline cognitive severity.

The results in Table 2, showed that exercise intervention produced moderate to large effects within-group effects across metabolic control, oxidative stress, inflammatory biomarkers, myokines, neurotrophic markers, and cognitive performance. Post-intervention ANOVA further demonstrated persistent between-group differences after training, supporting differential responsiveness according to baseline cognitive severity.

Associations Between Myokines/Neurotrophic Markers and Cognitive Domains

Correlation analysis demonstrated domain-specific associations between exercise-induced changes in circulating irisin, leptin, and BDNF levels and MoCA cognitive domains across the NCF, MCI, and MoCI groups (Table 3). Significant correlations were predominantly observed for moderate-to-strong coefficients, whereas weaker associations were re-evaluated and considered non-significant according to the sample size (n = 20 per group). Positive associations were mainly identified for irisin and BDNF, particularly with executive function, attention, language performance, visuospatial ability, and orientation, with stronger relationships generally observed in participants with cognitive impairment, especially within the MCI group. In contrast, leptin levels showed predominantly inverse associations with several cognitive domains, most notably executive function, attention, and language. Exact P-values were additionally included in Table 3 to improve statistical transparency and interpretation of the observed associations.

Overall, these findings suggest that exercise-induced modulation of myokines and neurotrophic factors may contribute to cognitive improvements while preserving biological responsiveness even in participants with moderate cognitive impairment.

Figure 1 diagram is intended only to illustrate potential biological pathways and observed associations between exercise-related physiological responses and cognitive outcomes. These systemic changes were associated with significant improvements in global cognitive performance and domain-specific MoCA scores, particularly in executive function, attention, and memory. The findings support a mechanistic link between exercise-driven metabolic–neurotrophic signaling and enhanced cognitive function, with the most pronounced benefits observed in individuals with mild and moderate cognitive impairment. These changes collectively promote synaptic plasticity, antioxidant defense, and neuroprotection, leading to improvements in executive function, attention, memory, and overall cognitive performance, particularly in individuals with mild and moderate cognitive impairment.

It does not imply confirmed mechanistic interactions, coordinated hormonal regulation, or causal relationships because the study lacked a non-exercise comparator group and the proposed pathways were not directly tested experimentally.

Discussions

In this controlled exercise intervention, 12 weeks of moderate-intensity aerobic training was associated with significant improvements in global and domain-specific cognitive function across normal cognition (NCF), mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and moderate cognitive impairment (MoCI) groups.

These improvements occurred in parallel with favorable shifts in circulating biomarkers, including increased irisin and BDNF, reductions in leptin, attenuation of oxidative stress (↓ MDA, ↑ TAS and SOD), and decreased inflammatory cytokines (↓ IL-6 and TNF-α). Notably, exercise-induced increases in irisin and BDNF correlated positively with enhancements in MoCA domains such as executive function, memory, and attention, while reductions in leptin were associated with better domain performance, suggesting biologically coherent muscle–brain and metabolic–neurotrophic mechanisms for cognitive enhancement.

Although the present study demonstrated reduced circulating leptin levels following the exercise intervention, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because previous studies have reported considerable variability in leptin responses across different populations and experimental settings. While several investigations observed exercise-associated reductions in leptin concentrations, other studies reported minimal changes or context-dependent responses influenced by adiposity status, metabolic profile, energy balance, exercise intensity, intervention duration, age, and baseline health conditions. Furthermore, the relationship between leptin and cognition remains complex because both leptin deficiency and leptin resistance have been implicated in age-related cognitive alterations. Therefore, the current findings support an exercise-associated reduction in leptin within our cohort; however, these observations should not be generalized as a universal response pattern and may reflect broader interactions among metabolic regulation, inflammation, and neurocognitive function rather than direct mechanistic effects of leptin alone56–58.

Exercise, cognitive function, and aging

Our findings are consistent with evidence that aerobic exercise improves cognitive function in older adults, especially in individuals with MCI. Recent meta-analyses have shown that aerobic exercise interventions significantly improve global cognition and executive function in older adults with MCI, with moderate-intensity and optimized frequency yielding the greatest benefits59–63.

These results extend the literature by supporting the utility of exercise as a non-pharmacological intervention that benefits both global and domain-specific cognition across different levels of cognitive impairment. For individuals with MCI and MoCI, domain improvements were most pronounced in executive function, attention, and memory, which aligns with prior reports that aerobic training preferentially enhances these functions, potentially through improved neural efficiency and neuroplasticity63–66.

Myokines and the FNDC5/Irisin–BDNF Pathway

We observed significant post-exercise increases in irisin and BDNF, supporting the role of muscle–brain signaling in cognitive outcomes. Irisin, a myokine released during exercise, has emerged as a mediator of neuroplasticity and brain health66–69. Intracellular signaling through FNDC5/irisin upregulates hippocampal BDNF expression, promotes synaptic plasticity, and supports dendritic growth, providing a mechanistic basis for improvements in cognitive domains66–69. Though the precise impact of irisin on specific cognitive domains remains an active area of research, recent systematic reviews suggest positive associations between circulating irisin and global cognitive function, as well as correlations between irisin and BDNF levels64–76. These interactions may underlie the stronger correlations observed in MCI and MoCI groups in our data.

Oxidative stress, inflammation, and cognition

Exercise facilitated reductions in MDA, indicating decreased lipid peroxidation, and enhanced antioxidant defense via increased TAS and SOD activity. Oxidative stress has been implicated in age-related cognitive decline, as reactive oxygen species disrupt neuronal integrity and synaptic functioning47,48,70,71. The reduction in oxidative stress following exercise may therefore contribute to preserved or improved cognitive performance. Similarly, reductions in pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and TNF-α align with evidence that chronic inflammation accelerates cognitive deterioration in aging and neurodegenerative conditions and that physical activity can mitigate this inflammatory burden72–76. Mechanistically, chronic exposure to pro-inflammatory cytokines has been shown to exert direct adverse effects on neural systems relevant to cognition75–78.

Integrated mechanistic perspective

Taken together, the present findings demonstrate correlations between exercise-associated improvements in cognitive function and changes in myokine, neurotrophic, metabolic, oxidative, and inflammatory markers. Specifically, improved cognitive outcomes were accompanied by increased circulating irisin and BDNF levels, reduced leptin concentrations, attenuation of oxidative stress reflected by lower MDA levels and enhanced antioxidant status, as well as reduced inflammatory markers including IL-6 and TNF-α. These observations are consistent with proposed biological pathways involving skeletal muscle–brain communication and systemic metabolic regulation that may contribute to exercise-associated cognitive responses. However, the present findings are based on observed associations and correlation analyses and therefore do not establish causal relationships or confirm mechanistic interactions between these pathways and cognitive improvement. Nevertheless, the results support the possibility that these interconnected physiological responses may participate in the exercise-related cognitive changes observed in older adults77–84.

Finally, the data showed that, a 12-week moderate aerobic exercise program improved cognitive performance across multiple MoCA domains and was accompanied by favorable changes in circulating biomarkers indicative of enhanced muscle brain signaling, reduced oxidative stress, and lower inflammation. Domain-specific cognitive gains were most pronounced in executive function, attention, and memory, particularly in individuals with MCI and MoCI, reinforcing the therapeutic potential of exercise for cognitive impairment.

Strengths and limitations

The present study provides important insights into the relationship between exercise intervention, cognitive performance, and biomarker modulation among older adults with different cognitive states. Several methodological strengths enhanced the scientific value of the work; however, certain limitations should also be acknowledged to ensure appropriate interpretation of the findings and to guide future investigations.

Conclusion

Among older adults stratified according to cognitive status based on MoCA scores, participation in a supervised 12-week moderate-intensity aerobic exercise program was associated with improvements in cognitive performance accompanied by changes in circulating irisin, leptin, BDNF, oxidative stress indices, and inflammatory cytokines. The study design did not include a non-exercise comparator group; therefore, exercise-specific effects cannot be definitively established. However, moderate to large effect sizes observed across several biochemical and cognitive outcomes support the presence of meaningful exercise-associated changes. Correlation analyses further demonstrated significant associations between post-exercise changes in irisin, leptin, BDNF, and selected cognitive domains across normal cognition, mild cognitive impairment, and moderate cognitive impairment groups, suggesting possible biological pathways linking exercise-associated biomarker responses with cognitive health rather than confirming coordinated hormonal modulation or causality. These findings support further controlled studies to clarify causal relationships and underlying mechanisms.

Future directions

Future studies should employ larger, longitudinal designs to determine whether exercise-induced changes in myokines, neurotrophic factors, oxidative stress, and inflammation causally mediate cognitive improvement. Comparative trials evaluating different exercise modalities and intensities, alone or combined with cognitive training, are needed to optimize intervention strategies. Incorporation of neuroimaging and advanced neurodegenerative biomarkers will further clarify brain-level mechanisms and support personalized exercise prescriptions for cognitive health.
