Unraveling the Enterococcus enigma in ICU peritonitis: a multicenter cohort study
Authors: Melchior Bardoul, Charly Angebault, Pierre Fillâtre, Pierre Bouju, Agathe Delbove, Guillaume Rieul, Yannick Fedun, Yoann Launey, Florian Reizine
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe role of Enterococcus spp. and the need for specific anti-Enterococcus therapy in Intensive Care Unit (ICU) patients with peritonitis remain debated.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective multicentre cohort study including all consecutive adults admitted to the ICUs of four hospitals in western France with peritonitis between 2020 and 2022. Outcomes were compared according to Enterococcus spp. isolation and, among Enterococcus-positive cases, according to early administration (< 48 h) of active antimicrobial therapy. Propensity-weighted Cox models were used to estimate 90-day survival.ResultsAmong 392 patients, Enterococcus spp. were isolated in 161 (41.1%). Enterococcus-positive patients were older and more frequently had postoperative and diffuse peritonitis. ICU mortality rates were 29.8% vs 28.1% in patients with and without Enterococcus infection (p = 0.805). In multivariable analysis, postoperative peritonitis (aOR 2.56 [1.67–3.95], p < 0.001), diffuse peritonitis (aOR 1.78 [1.10–2.89], p = 0.020) and solid organ transplantation (aOR 5.82 [1.18–28.76]; p = 0.031) were independently associated with Enterococcus isolation. Among the 155 patients with documented Enterococcus peritonitis and available antimicrobial treatment data, 95 (61%) received early active therapy. Early anti-Enterococcus treatment was not associated with improved 90-day survival either in the raw population (HR 0.71 [95% CI 0.39–1.27]; p = 0.241) or after weighted Cox regression (HR 0.66 [95% CI 0.37–1.18]; p = 0.160).ConclusionsEnterococcus spp. isolation appears common in ICU peritonitis but not independently associated with higher mortality. Introduction

Peritonitis is a life-threatening condition frequently encountered in intensive care units (ICUs), associated with significant morbidity and mortality [1–3]. Its management hinges on a combination of adequate source control and timely, appropriate antimicrobial therapy. The microbial ecology of community-acquired and postoperative peritonitis is typically polymicrobial, involving enterobacterales, anaerobic bacteria, and streptococci [2, 4, 5].

Among isolated pathogens, Enterococcus spp. plays a controversial and incompletely understood role. Its presence in peritoneal cultures is common, yet its clinical significance as a true pathogen rather than a bystander in mixed infections remains a subject of intense debate [5–10]. This uncertainty directly impacts therapeutic decisions, particularly the need for early, targeted anti-enterococcal therapy [11, 12]. While some guidelines and expert opinions recommend such coverage, especially in specific high-risk settings like healthcare-associated infections or immunocompromised patients, the evidence supporting a mortality benefit is inconsistent [1, 3, 7, 10]. International guidelines acknowledge this uncertainty, recommending empiric anti-enterococcal coverage only in selected high-risk situations, while a large meta-analysis of randomized and observational studies found no consistent survival benefit, leaving its role in critically ill ICU patients unresolved [11–13].

The analysis by Morvan et al. using the OUTCOMEREA database contributed to this ongoing discussion by examining species-specific effects and treatment impacts in critically ill patients with enterococcal peritonitis reporting a signal toward benefit of anti-enterococcal therapy in unadjusted analyses, which was not confirmed after multivariable adjustment [7]. However, despite this and other investigations, fundamental questions about the independent prognostic impact of Enterococcus isolation and the true therapeutic benefit of targeted anti-enterococcal therapy remain unresolved.

While early empiric antibiotic therapy is essential in severe peritonitis, unnecessary exposure to broad-spectrum agents (particularly anti-pseudomonal beta-lactams and glycopeptides in the absence of clear risk factors) may increase selective pressure for antimicrobial resistance and drug-related toxicity, underscoring the importance of a stewardship-oriented approach [14–16]. Balancing timely effective coverage with antimicrobial stewardship is therefore essential in the management of ICU peritonitis. Therefore, we conducted a large, retrospective multicenter cohort study with two primary objectives: first, to investigate whether the isolation of Enterococcus spp. from peritoneal fluid is independently associated with 90-day mortality in critically ill patients with peritonitis, using rigorous statistical methods to account for confounding; and second, to assess the impact of early, active anti-enterococcal therapy on survival among patients with culture-confirmed Enterococcus spp. peritonitis.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a retrospective multicentre cohort study including all consecutive adults admitted to the intensive care units (ICUs) of four hospitals (one university-affiliated hospital and three general hospitals) in western France with secondary or postoperative peritonitis between January 2020 and December 2022. Patients were initially identified using hospital administrative databases based on predefined ICD-10 discharge codes related to peritonitis (K65, N73.5, K35.2, K35.3, and K66.8) (Supplementary Table 1). All potentially eligible cases were subsequently reviewed at the individual chart level to confirm the diagnosis of peritonitis, defined as an intra-abdominal infection requiring surgical or radiological source control and associated with microbiological sampling. Patients identified through ICD-10 coding who did not meet this predefined clinical definition were excluded. Patients with missing microbiological data were excluded. The study protocol was approved by the French Ethics Committee for Research in Anaesthesia and Intensive Care (IRB 00010254–2023-017), which waived the need for individual informed consent in accordance with French law. The study followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and complied with STROBE reporting guidelines for observational studies [17].

Data collection and definitions

Peritonitis was defined as an intra-abdominal infection confirmed by surgical or radiological findings associated with microbiological sampling of peritoneal fluid. Postoperative peritonitis was defined as any intra-abdominal infection occurring within 30 days after abdominal surgery. Peritonitis was also categorized based on the timing of onset into two distinct groups: community-acquired peritonitis, occurring before hospital admission or within the first 48 h or nosocomial (i.e., developing after day 3 of hospitalization). Clinical, microbiological, and therapeutic data were extracted from electronic medical records using a standardized case report form. Comorbidities included obesity (body-mass index > 30 kg/m2), diabetes mellitus, cirrhosis, chronic kidney disease, and immunosuppression (active malignancy, solid organ transplantation (SOT), autoimmune disease, or long-term corticosteroid or immunosuppressive therapy). Severity at ICU admission was assessed by the Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II) [18]. Septic shock was defined according to Sepsis-3 criteria [19]. Clinical and radiological features were carefully noted, including the source of peritonitis (upper vs. lower gastrointestinal tract), the presence of diffuse peritonitis, and evidence of organ perforation confirmed by pneumoperitoneum on imaging. Diffuse peritonitis was defined as generalized intra-abdominal contamination involving more than one abdominal quadrant, as documented by intraoperative findings and/or preoperative radiological imaging.

Source control was considered successful when no additional surgical or radiological intervention for the initial infectious focus was required within seven days and when there was no persistent inflammation as previously described [2]. Antimicrobial therapy was considered appropriate according to the antibiogram of isolated species when available. Additionnaly, antimicrobial therapy was considered active against Enterococcus spp. when it included at least one agent with recognized in vitro activity against the isolated species, administered within 48 h after the index surgery. This operational definition was adapted from the framework previously proposed by Morvan et al. [7]. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (antibiograms) of isolated pathogens was systematically reviewed when available and used to guide treatment classification. Aminopenicillins (amoxicillin), glycopeptides (vancomycin), and linezolid were systematically considered active. In addition, piperacillin–tazobactam and imipenem were considered active when the isolated strain was susceptible. Fluoroquinolones and daptomycin were considered active when in vitro susceptibility were documented, irrespective of the Enterococcus species. When susceptibility data were incomplete, classification relied on the most likely activity profile based on species identification and local epidemiology, reflecting real-world empiric practice. All patients received immediate empiric antimicrobial therapy at the time of peritonitis diagnosis in accordance with sepsis guidelines. The 48-h window was used to define early enterococcal-active therapy specifically, reflecting the timing of addition or maintenance of agents with activity against Enterococcus spp. during the initial empiric treatment phase. Patients were followed until day 90 after peritonitis onset or hospital discharge, whichever occurred first, with censoring of survivors at day 90.

Objectives

The primary objective was to assess the association between Enterococcus spp. identification and 90-day survival after peritonitis onset. Ninety-day mortality was chosen as the primary outcome to capture both early and delayed deaths related to severe peritonitis and its complications in critically ill patients. This time horizon also limits potential bias related to early ICU or hospital discharge while allowing appropriate time-to-event analyses accounting for the front-loaded nature of mortality in peritonitis. Secondary outcomes included the assessment of early clinical response at day 7, persistence of systemic inflammation, need for reoperation or radiological drainage, organ-support requirements, ICU or hospital length of stay, and the requirement for additional surgical or radiological intervention within 28 days after peritonitis onset. We also aimed to assess risk factors for Enterococcus spp. peritonitis and survival among Enterococcus spp. peritonitis patients. Finally, among Enterococcus-positive cases, we aimed to assess the impact of early administration (within 48 h) of active antimicrobial therapy on survival up to day-90 after peritonitis onset.

Statistical analysis

All continuous variables were expressed as medians (interquartile ranges (IQR)), while categorical variables were presented as counts (percentages). Comparative analyses between groups were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test or Student’s t-test for continuous variables depending on their distribution, and the Chi2 test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables as appropriate.

To address potential confounding due to baseline imbalances between patients with and without Enterococcus peritonitis, we performed a stabilized inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) approach. We first estimated non parsimonious propensity scores (PS) using multivariable logistic regression incorporating all clinically relevant baseline characteristics. These included age, sex, postoperative status, presence of diffuse peritonitis, community-acquired peritonitis, upper gastrointestinal source of peritonitis, the SAPS II, timing variables including delay before surgery, time between hospital admission and peritonitis onset, immunosuppression status, obesity, malnutrition, cirrhosis, chronic renal failure, congestive heart failure, diabetes mellitus, sepsis or septic shock at ICU admission and previous antimicrobial exposure along with Gram negative Bacilli and Candida spp. isolation from peritoneal cultures. Post-baseline variables reflecting the subsequent clinical course (e.g., source control success at day 7) were not included in survival models to avoid overadjustment and collider bias. Stabilized weights were then calculated by dividing the marginal probability of Enterococcus spp. isolation by the predicted probability of isolation given the covariates for patients with Enterococcus spp., and similarly for those without isolation using the complementary probabilities. For the secondary analysis focusing on the effect of early anti-Enterococcus therapy among patients with confirmed Enterococcus peritonitis, we developed a second PS model. This model specifically included variables associated with either early anti-Enterococcus therapy or survival in preliminary univariate analyses with p-values < 0.2 (i.e., including age, diabetes mellitus, cirrhosis, obesity, postoperative peritonitis, diffuse peritonitis, septic shock at ICU admission and the SAPS II). The effectiveness of these weighting approaches in achieving balance was systematically evaluated using standardized mean differences (SMD) for each covariate before and after weighting, with values below 0.1 considered indicative of adequate balance.

The primary outcome of 90-day mortality was analyzed using weighted Cox proportional hazards models, with results expressed as hazard ratios (HR) with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI). The proportional hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residuals, and survival probabilities were graphically represented through weighted Kaplan–Meier curves.

Factors associated with Enterococcus isolation were identified using univariate then multivariable logistic regression with backward stepwise selection (entry p < 0.2, retention p < 0.05), reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% CI. Additionally, for patients with confirmed Enterococcus peritonitis (n = 161), we conducted a survival analysis to identify prognostic factors associated with 90-day mortality. We first performed univariate Cox regression analyses. Variables with p-values < 0.2 in univariate analysis were then included in a multivariable Cox proportional hazards model. Then, variable selection was performed using a backward stepwise method.

Additionally, we conducted a sensitivity analysis of baseline characteristics along with clinical course in the subgroup of patients with post- operative peritonitis according of Enterococcus spp. isolation.

To handle baseline missing values as potential confounders, and assuming that missing data were randomly missing, missing data were imputed using the “MICE” R package (Monte Carlo Markov chained equations, five datasets combined using Rubin’s rules). Only baseline missing at random variables were used for the imputation process.

Statistical significance was defined by a two-sided p-value threshold of 0.05. All analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.0.4).

Results

Study population

During the study period, 392 patients were admitted to the participating ICUs for postoperative or secondary peritonitis (Supplementary Fig. 1). Among them, 161 (41.1%) had Enterococcus spp. isolated from peritoneal cultures. Enterococcus faecalis accounted for the majority of isolates (104/161, 64.6%), followed by Enterococcus faecium (55/161; 34.2%). Patients with Enterococcus-positive peritonitis were slightly older (median age 70 [63–75] vs 67 [57–74] years; p = 0.041). Comorbidities were generally comparable, although SOT was more frequent in the Enterococcus group (5.0% vs 0.9%; p = 0.027). The Enterococcus group presented more often with postoperative peritonitis (49.7% vs 27.7%; p < 0.001) and diffuse peritonitis (72.2% vs 58.0%; p = 0.009). Conversely, Enterococcus peritonitis were less commonly community-acquired (32.9% vs 48.9%; p = 0.002). Time from hospital admission to peritonitis onset was longer among Enterococcus-positive patients (median 4 [0–11] days vs 1 [0–7] days; p = 0.001) (Table 1). Vancomycin-resistant enterococci (VRE) were rare, with only three cases identified.

To address potential confounding due to baseline imbalances, we implemented a stabilized IPTW approach. After weighting, all covariates were well balanced (SMD < 0.1 for all variables; Supplementary Fig. 3). In the IPTW-weighted cohort, several important outcomes remained comparable between groups: duration of mechanical ventilation (3 [1–9] vs 2 [1–7] days; p = 0.086), hospital length of stay (26 [14–47] vs 27 [12–48] days; p = 0.928), and the requirement for additional interventions within 28 days (35.3% vs 25.5%; p = 0.076). Survival analysis of Enterococcus peritonitis and effects of early antimicrobial therapy

Among the 155 patients with documented Enterococcus peritonitis and antimicrobial data available, 95 (61.3%) received early antimicrobial therapy active against Enterococcus spp. within 48 h after index surgery. Before IPTW adjustment, baseline characteristics appeared well balanced between groups (all p > 0.05) as presented in Supplementary Table 2. Of note, early antimicrobial regimens differed between groups. Patients who did not receive early active anti-enterococcal therapy more frequently received a third-generation cephalosporin combined with metronidazole (56.7% vs. 14.7%, p < 0.001), whereas piperacillin–tazobactam was more commonly used in patients receiving early active anti-enterococcal therapy (64.2% vs. 30.0%, p < 0.001). The use of carbapenems, vancomycin, linezolid, and amoxicillin–clavulanate was low and did not significantly differ between groups.

Of those 155 patients, non-survivors were older (median age 73 [67–77] vs 69 [62–73] years; p = 0.016) and more frequently presented with diffuse peritonitis (88.6% vs 65.1%; p = 0.006), septic shock at ICU admission (64.6% vs 40.2%; p = 0.008), and higher SAPS II scores (61 [54–67] vs 45 [36–58] years; p < 0.001) (Supplementary Table 3). In multivariable Cox analysis, independent predictors of 90-day mortality were cirrhosis (aHR 2.49, 95% CI 1.18–5.29; p = 0.018) and higher SAPS II score (aHR 1.04 per point, 95% CI 1.02–1.06; p < 0.001) (Supplementary Table 4).

Survival curves did not show any difference in survival up to day 90 after peritonitis onset between treated and non-treated Enterococcus spp. patients in the raw population (Log Rank test, p = 0.23) (Supplementary Fig. 4).

After application of stabilized IPT weights, all baseline characteristics were balanced between groups (Supplementary Table 5 and Supplementary Fig. 5). Early anti-Enterococcus therapy was not associated with improved 90-day survival (adjusted HR 0.71, 95% CI 0.39–1.27; p = 0.241) as depicted in Fig. 2. Similarly, clinical success at day 7 and other secondary outcomes were similar between groups (Supplementary Table 6). Finally, when conducting a sensitivity analysis on survival according to Enterococcus species we did not observe any significant differences in 90-day mortality (Log-Rank test, p = 0.7) (Supplementary Fig. 6). The remaining isolates grouped as ‘Other Enterococcus spp.’ consisted of low-frequency species, mainly Enterococcus durans (n = 9), Enterococcus casseliflavus (n = 4), and Enterococcus avium (n = 11).

Sensitivity analysis

The subgroup analysis of patients with post-operative peritonitis revealed no significant difference in ICU mortality between Enterococcus-positive and Enterococcus-negative patients (28.7% vs. 29.7%, p = 1.000), nor in successful source control at day 7 (41.8% vs. 54.7%, p = 0.171). However, Enterococcus isolation was associated with a higher rate of early surgical reintervention within 7 days (38.4% vs. 15.8%, p = 0.008), a greater need for additional interventions within 28 days (42.5% vs. 18.8%, p = 0.004), and a longer ICU length of stay (12 [6–20] vs. 6 [3–16] days, p = 0.014) (Supplementary Table 7).

Discussion

In this large multicenter cohort of critically ill patients with peritonitis, we report two principal findings. First, the isolation of Enterococcus spp., while common (41.1%), was not independently associated with increased 90-day mortality after adjustment for confounders using IPTW. Second, among patients with culture-confirmed Enterococcus peritonitis, the early administration of targeted anti-enterococcal therapy was not found to be associated with improved survival or other key clinical outcomes.

The high prevalence of Enterococcus spp. in our cohort aligns with recent literature highlighting its increasing role in healthcare-associated and postoperative peritonitis [2, 5, 8]. Our study confirms that specific clinical settings are strong independent risk factors for its isolation, namely postoperative peritonitis, diffuse peritonitis, and SOT. This profile reinforces the concept of Enterococcus as an opportunistic pathogen thriving in complex clinical scenarios characterized by breached intestinal integrity, prior surgical intervention, selective pressure from prior antibiotics, and compromised host immunity. The association with postoperative peritonitis is particularly salient and has been consistently reported, suggesting iatrogenic inoculation or translocation following surgical trauma [6, 20].

In the present study, we defined “active” anti-enterococcal therapy according to the operational criteria used by Morvan et al. [7], as this is the clearest previously published framework addressing this specific clinical question. In this approach, therapy is considered active when an antibiotic with recognized in-vitro activity against Enterococcus spp. is administered within 48 h of the index surgery.

However, this definition carries important limitations that must be acknowledged. First, complete antimicrobial susceptibility testing was not available for all isolates. Second, the assumption that amoxicillin susceptibility reliably predicts susceptibility to ureidopenicillins (e.g., piperacillin–tazobactam) is imperfect. While this assumption has been applied in previous work, including Morvan et al., recent microbiological data show that discordant susceptibility patterns do occur, particularly among E. faecalis strains [21].

As a result, our exposure definition is subject to a potential misclassification bias, with possible over- or underestimation of true antimicrobial activity in individual patients. Such nondifferential misclassification would tend to bias effect estimates toward the null, potentially contributing to the absence of a measurable benefit of early anti-enterococcal therapy in our study. Importantly, these limitations also reflect real-world clinical practice, where therapeutic decisions often rely on incomplete susceptibility profiles and heterogeneous laboratory testing protocols across centers.

The absence of an independent association between Enterococcus isolation and 90-day mortality, even after employing a robust propensity-weighted analysis, is a pivotal finding. It suggests that the prognosis of critically ill patients with peritonitis is driven more by the severity of the acute illness and the patient’s baseline comorbidities than by the presence of Enterococcus per se. This finding challenges the historical perception of Enterococcus as a primary driver of mortality in polymicrobial peritonitis [6, 20]. Our results are consistent with several previous studies that, after multivariate adjustment, found no independent mortality effect [5, 8]. Our data thus contribute to a growing body of evidence downplaying the role of Enterococcus as an independent "killer" in mixed infections, instead portraying it as a marker of a more complex and severely ill patient [22]. Along these lines, previous studies have suggested that the isolation of Enterococcus spp. in peritoneal cultures might predispose to persistent or secondary intra-abdominal infections. In particular, Seguin et al. reported an increased rate of postoperative abscesses and reinterventions in patients with enterococcal peritonitis, suggesting a potential role of this pathogen in impaired local bacterial clearance and delayed healing [23]. In our cohort, although we observed a trend toward more reoperations by day 28 among patients with Enterococcus isolation, this did not reach statistical significance. This discrepancy may reflect improvements in surgical techniques, earlier and more effective source control, or differences in patient selection. Altogether, our findings do not support a direct causal link between enterococcal isolation and secondary abscess formation, but rather suggest that Enterococcus may serve as a marker of initial infection complexity.

Our second key finding, that early active anti-enterococcal therapy was not found to be associated with improved survival. This result was consistent across both unadjusted and adjusted analyses and was corroborated by the lack of improvement in secondary outcomes. Our data do not support the routine, empiric use of anti-enterococcal coverage in all critically ill patients with peritonitis. This aligns with the growing body of evidence questioning the universal necessity of such coverage [13, 24]. A recent analysis by Morvan et al. using the OUTCOMEREA database also found no significant association between adequate anti-enterococcal therapy and 30-day mortality after multivariable analysis, although they suggested a potential species-dependent effect [7]. Our study, with its focus on early therapy and 90-day mortality in a dedicated peritonitis cohort, strengthens the conclusion that a strict recommendation for empiric coverage is not evidence-based. Nonetheless, although early anti-enterococcal therapy was not statistically associated with improved survival, the wide confidence intervals around the effect estimate indicate that a moderate benefit or harm cannot be definitively excluded, suggesting that this study may be underpowered to detect smaller but clinically relevant effects. Larger, prospective studies would therefore be needed to more definitively determine whether specific subgroups of critically ill patients may benefit from early targeted anti-enterococcal therapy.

Instead, our multivariable survival analysis of Enterococcus-positive patients identified that mortality was predominantly determined by factors reflective of disease severity and host reserve: the presence of cirrhosis and higher SAPS II scores. Another crucial determinant of outcome in peritonitis is the timeliness and adequacy of source control. Delays in surgical or radiological intervention have been associated with increased mortality in intra-abdominal infections [25]. In our cohort, although delayed source control (> 6 h) was not independently associated with Enterococcus isolation or mortality after adjustment, patients with unsuccessful source control at day 7 exhibited markedly poorer outcomes. These findings underscore that, beyond microbiological factors, the prompt removal of the infectious focus remains a cornerstone of management. Along these lines, while the concept of Enterococcus spp. acting as a bystander organism in polymicrobial peritonitis is not new, our study strengthens this hypothesis by testing it in a large, contemporary multicentre ICU cohort using robust adjustment methods to limit confounding by severity, immunosuppression, and polymicrobial burden.

Beyond its methodological interest, the sensitivity analysis restricted to post-operative peritonitis provides important insight into the interpretation of our findings. In this setting, microbiological processing is usually more standardized and exhaustive, thereby limiting the risk of under-reporting low-burden co-pathogens such as Enterococcus. Despite this more reliable microbiological ascertainment, Enterococcus isolation remained unassociated with ICU mortality, although it was associated with a more complicated postoperative course. This consistency across analyses reinforces the robustness of our main results and strongly suggests that Enterococcus acts primarily as a marker of infection complexity rather than as an independent driver of mortality.

Our study must be interpreted in light of its limitations. Its retrospective and observational design precludes definitive causal inferences, despite our robust methodological approach to control for confounding. The decision to administer anti-enterococcal therapy was at the discretion of the treating physician, potentially introducing unmeasured confounding, although we attempted to account for this with a second propensity model (Noteworthy, direct microscopic examination of peritoneal fluid was not available and therefore could not be incorporated into the propensity score models. This information could have influenced early empirical anti-enterococcal therapy). Furthermore, our findings are derived from an ICU population with a low prevalence of vancomycin-resistant enterococci (1.9% of isolates) and few solid organ transplant recipients (4.5%). Consequently, they may not be fully generalizable to settings with different patient profiles, such as high-prevalence VRE centers [2] or institutions managing larger populations of profoundly immunocompromised patients. Additionnaly, we must also acknowledge a potential misclassification bias related to heterogeneous microbiological reporting of Enterococcus, particularly in community-acquired peritonitis, where low-burden co-isolates may not be systematically reported. Such nondifferential misclassification would tend to bias associations toward the null and reduce statistical power. To address this issue, we performed a sensitivity analysis in the subgroup of post-operative peritonitis, where microbiological identification is usually exhaustive. The absence of any mortality signal in this setting, despite improved ascertainment, further reinforces the robustness of our main results. In addition, despite careful review of species identification and available antimicrobial susceptibility testing, some degree of exposure misclassification cannot be entirely excluded, particularly during the early empiric phase of treatment. Such non-differential misclassification would be expected to bias effect estimates toward the null. Furthermore, data on concomitant Enterococcus bloodstream infection were available but infrequent (n = 10, 6.5%). Its prevalence did not differ significantly between treatment or outcome groups (all p-values > 0.2). Therefore, it was not included in the adjusted analyses. Along these lines, the prevalence of VRE was very low in our cohort, and empirical use of linezolid within the first 48 h was uncommon, limiting confounding related to systematic use of broad anti-enterococcal agents in more severe patients.

In conclusion, this large contemporary cohort study demonstrates that in critically ill patients with peritonitis, Enterococcus spp. is frequently isolated but is not an independent predictor of mortality. The prognosis appears to be dictated primarily by the severity of peritonitis, the adequacy of source control, and the patient’s underlying comorbidities. The routine addition of early, targeted anti-enterococcal therapy was not associated with a statistically significant improvement in survival in this cohort, although a moderate benefit or harm cannot be definitively excluded. These findings support a more nuanced approach, while highlighting the need for larger studies to identify potential subgroups of patients who may benefit from early anti-enterococcal therapy.
