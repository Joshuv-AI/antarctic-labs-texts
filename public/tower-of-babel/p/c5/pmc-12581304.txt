Quantitative profiling of bacterial communities via full length 16S rRNA gene sequencing with internal controls: optimization and validation across diverse human microbiomes
Authors: Ezra Valido, Alessandro Bertolo, Jivko Stoyanov
Published: 2025

Background

Controlling bacterial load is crucial for the prevention and management of infections in healthcare settings. Patients in these environments are often vulnerable to opportunistic pathogens due to compromised immunity, invasive procedures, or underlying health conditions. Bacterial load plays a major role in the clinical setting in determining whether thresholds for specific diseases are met, as observed in the diagnosis of urinary tract infections [1] and can set the basis for starting antibiotic treatment. Traditionally, bacterial load estimation has relied on microbial cultures. However, this approach is limited by its inability to grow all organisms, the requirement for specific growth conditions, and long incubation times [2–5]. Furthermore, serial dilutions are required when the number of bacteria is elevated, which adds time and effort to the procedure. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) offers a promising alternative, allowing direct identification and quantification of microorganisms based on their genomic content, potentially saving time and improving accuracy [6, 7].

Among the various NGS approaches, full-length 16S rRNA gene sequencing has emerged as a powerful tool for microbial monitoring and quantification [7, 8]. The 16S rRNA gene is a highly conserved region present in bacterial genomes with variable sequences which differ between species and is often targeted for taxonomic classification [9, 10]. While previous sequencing technologies were limited to targeting specific variable regions of the 16S rRNA gene, emerging technologies such as nanopore sequencing now enable full-length sequencing of the entire gene. This sequencing method captures electrical signals from ionic current changes derived from nucleic acid molecules that pass through a nanopore in a membrane [11] and are used to decode the base sequence. To optimize this sequencing method, protocols need to carefully consider quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) settings and analytical frameworks, as some analytical tools are incompatible with long-read sequence data [12].

High-throughput sequencing data of microbial communities produce compositional or relative data [13]. Compositional data are scale dependent and have limitations in data analysis. For example, when comparing the absolute abundance of similar organisms in diverse environment, large differences in magnitude may not be reflected in their relative proportions, thus leading to distorted conclusions. Therefore, developing methods to estimate absolute abundance in microbial load using sequencing techniques is of critical importance for improving healthcare-associated infection control and patient outcomes.

In this study, we aimed to address these challenges by quantifying bacterial community profiles using full-length 16S rRNA gene sequencing with the incorporation of spike-ins as internal control. We sought to optimize PCR conditions, initial DNA input, and the percentage of spike-in relative to DNA input. Moreover, we aimed to extend the taxonomic classification to the species level, improving upon our previous method [12]. Finally, we investigated whether our quantification method could be applied to human samples with varying microbial loads and how it compared to estimates from culture methods.

Mock microbial community standard and internal controls

For our experiments, we utilized three distinct mock community standards. The first was the ZymoBIOMICS™ Microbial Community DNA Standard (Zymo, D6305) and the second was the cell-based ZymoBIOMICS™ Microbial Community Standard (Zymo, D6300). Both bacterial control contained the following bacterial strains: Pseudomonas aeruginosa (NRRL Acc. No. = B-3509), Escherichia coli (NRRL Acc. No. = B-1109), Salmonella enterica (NRRL Acc. No. = B-4212), Lactobacillus fermentum (NRRL Acc. No. = B-1840), Enterococcus faecalis (NRRL Acc. No. = B-537), Staphylococcus aureus (NRRL Acc. No. = B-41012), Listeria monocytogenes (NRRL Acc. No. = B-33116), and Bacillus subtilis (NRRL Acc. No. = B-354). The third mock community standard is a cell-based, designed to more closely mimic the human gut microbiome, was the ZymoBIOMICSTM Gut Microbiome Standard (Zymo, D6331). This bacterial control contained the following strains: Faecalibacterium prausnitzii (14%; Strain ID = AP34BHI), Veillonella rogosae (14%; Strain ID = AC2811 ANNA2), Roseburia hominis (14%; Strain ID = OB EAV1 11 DCM), Bacteroides fragilis (14%; Strain ID = OB EAV1 11 D6 FAA), Prevotella corporis (6%; Strain ID = OB21 FMU 4), Bifidobacterium adolescentis (6%; Strain ID = LMG 10502), Fusobacterium nucleatum (6%; Strain ID = 2/1/50A), L. fermentum (6%; Strain ID = B-1840), Clostridioides difficile (1.5%; Strain ID = P4D3A1-1), Akkermansia muciniphila (1.5%; Strain ID = OB21 FAA NB 28), Methanobrevibacter smithii (0.1%; Strain ID = DSM 861), S. enterica (0.01%; Strain ID = B-4212), E. faecalis (0.001%; Strain ID = IP101412 AER FAA2), Clostridium perfringens (0.0001%) Strain ID = OB21 TSA 19), E. coli (14%; Strain IDs = JM109, B-3008, B-2007, B-766, B-1109). As an internal control, we used the ZymoBIOMICS™ Spike-in Control I (High Microbial Load) (Zymo, D6320). The spike-in comprised of the following bacterial strains: Allobacilus halotolerans (BCCM/LMG Accession NO. = LMG 26483) and Imtechella halotolerans (BCCM/LMG Accession NO. = LMG 24826) at a fixed proportion of 16 S copy number of 7:3.

DNA extraction and 16S rRNA gene amplification

DNA from the ZymoBIOMICS™ Microbial Community Standard and the ZymoBIOMICS™ Gut Microbiome Standard were extracted using QIAamp PowerFecal Pro DNA Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) according to manufacturer’s instructions. The DNA concentration was measured by Qubit dsDNA BR Assay Kit using Qubit 4.0 fluorimeter (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Q33238).

DNA samples from ZymoBIOMICS™ Microbial Community DNA Standard (1.0 ng) were used for 16 S amplification reactions as previously described [12] for 25 cycles using an adapted protocol from Oxford Nanopore technology (ONT, Protocol PCR barcoding amplicons, SQK-LSK109). To assess the method’s performance with a more complex microbiome, extracted DNA from ZymoBIOMICS™ Gut Microbiome Standard was processed similarly.

Extracted DNA from ZymoBIOMICS™ Microbial Community Standard were tested with various total template amounts (0.1 ng, 1.0 ng and 5 ng) with spike-in comprising 10% of the total DNA. 16 S amplification reactions were performed as previously described [12] for 25 or 35 cycles.

Barcoding and DNA sequence analysis

After 16 S amplification, DNA was barcoded, pooled, purified, and processed for end repair and dA-tailing, as previously described [12]. This was followed by a purification step using SPRIselect magnetic beads and Adapter Bead Binding buffer was added to the DNA library. Quality control and priming of the flow cell (Flow Cell Mk I, R9.4, FLO-MIN106D) were performed with 50 fmol of purified DNA library, and a standard sequencing protocol was initiated using the MinION Mk1C device (ONT, MIN-101 C). Basecalling was performed with the Guppy agent (version 6.3.7) at high accuracy. Barcodes were trimmed, and sequences were filtered to include only those with a q-score ≥ 9. Reads shorter than 1,000 bp and longer than 1,800 bp were discarded. The output FASTQ was analyzed with Emu [14]. We then compared their correlation to the reported composition of the microbial community standards.

Human samples collection, DNA extraction microbial culture and qPCR

To validate our quantification method across various human microbiomes, we collected four sample types with known varying microbial loads (skin of antecubital fossa, nasal cavity, saliva, and stool) from five volunteers. Details of the collection method are described in Supplement 1. To estimate the microbial load in each sample, we cultured the collected samples on blood agar plates (Becton Dickinson) and incubated them under aerobic conditions at 37 °C for 24 h. Nasal swabs (up to 1/104) and saliva samples (up to 1/106) were serially diluted, whereas no cultures were conducted on the stool samples. Colony-forming units (CFU) were counted and recorded.

Sample DNA was extracted with QIAamp PowerFecal Pro DNA Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) according to manufacturer’s instructions. We anticipated that nasal and skin swabs would have a low microbial load. To better gauge the total bacterial DNA in these samples, we conducted a qPCR. We used the ZymoBIOMICS™ Spike-in Control I (High Microbial Load) (Zymo, D6320) as internal controls (5.0 ng, 1.0 ng, 0.2 ng, 0.04 ng, and 0.008 ng). Each sample had 5.0 ng DNA template, 12.5 µL iQ SYBR® Green Supermix (iTaq DNA Polymerase, Bio-Rad, 1708880) ready-to-use master mix and 2.0 µL of primer set (forward primer 27 F (5’-AGAGTTTGATCCTGGCTCAG-3’) and reverse primer 1492R (5’-CGGTTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3’) [15]) to a total volume of 25.0 µL. The mixture was amplified to 35 cycles with the following settings: 5 min at 94 °C for polymerase activation (1 cycle); 20 s at 94 °C for denaturation, 30 s at 48 °C for annealing and 90 s at 60 °C for extension and a final step of 5 min at 65 °C. Using the estimated total bacterial DNA in the samples, we amplified the samples as previously described [12], with starting input of total DNA of 0.01 ng including 10% spike-in for 35 cycles. Positive (spike only) and negative (DNA-free water) controls were included simultaneously for each run. Organism found in the positive and negative controls were were grouped and labelled as “Others”. In the case of Cutibacterium acnes, a well-documented contaminant in sequencing [16, 17] but can be found in multiple body parts, the data was kept.

Statistical analysis

The data was analyzed using R (version 4.2.2). We identified sequences at both the genus and species levels. Descriptive analyses were performed using proportions. The Pearson correlation coefficient (r) was used to determine the linear correlation, and to measure the strength and direction of the relationship between the analyses and the theoretical composition of the bacterial community. We estimated the quantified values using the proportions of the spike-ins and obtained the means of the two organisms. We then accounted for the 16S rRNA gene copies, based on the rrnDB (the Ribosomal RNA Operon Copy Number Database) of the University of Michigan [18], to convert to cells/mL or cells/g. Whenever possible, species-level 16S rRNA copies were used; however, if unavailable, the immediate upper taxa were used. The estimated quantification is presented as log10. Pearson’s r was used to compare the estimated quantification by sequencing and culturing. Sample computation is provided in the supplement. We refer to estimation based on cultures as colony forming units (CFUs) per mL and those on the sequencing methods as estimated bacterial abundance (cells/mL or cells/g). Quantified results for each sample type are presented with the common organisms shared across all donors. The proportion of shared bacteria, the number of shared species over the total number of identified species, for each sample type were computed. Contaminants were not included in the total number of identified species. Beta diversity was compared by generating a distance matrix by Bray-Curtis and ordinated by principal component analysis (PCA). The closer the points indicate compositional similarity while the lack of overlap among groups indicate compositional difference between the groups. Figures were generated with Biorender.

Results

The use of Emu in elucidating the bacterial profile in mock communities

Emu achieved a Pearson r of 0.94 at the genus level (Fig. 2a). At the species level, the Pearson r was at 0.93 (Fig. 2b). Notably, the workflow underestimated proportion of Pseudomonas (below 0.5% instead of the expected 4%). We further evaluated Emu’s performance using the ZymoBIOMICS™ Gut Microbiome Standard. At the genus level, the Pearson r was 0.83 (Fig. 2c), but at the species level, the Pearson r was lower at 0.28 (Fig. 2d). At species level, Emu could not identify Veillonella rogosae and instead identified as Veillonella parvula or Veillonella dispar. Likewise, Bifidobacterium adolencentis was identified but was underestimated in abundance (below 0.1%, expected 8%).

Impact on bacterial quantification of initial DNA amount, number of PCR cycles and percentage of spike-in

The Pearson r ranged from 0.90 to 0.93 for the 25 PCR cycle and 0.87–0.88 for 35 PCR cycles (the differences were not statistically significant) (Fig. 2a). The estimated bacterial abundance remained within the expected range, regardless of the amount of input DNA or PCR cycles used. The error percentages per parameter are summarized in Supplementary 2. Overall, the 25 PCR cycles performed better than the 35 cycles in terms of the quantified log total estimate and relative abundance of microbiomes.

We then investigated whether varying the percentage of spike-ins had an impact on the estimated total quantity (Fig. 3b). The Pearson r ranged from 0.76 to 0.90 but the decrease in the percentage of spike did not change the estimation. An estimated value for 0.01% spike was absent. This is because one of the spike-in species was not identified at this low concentration.

Different sample types and their microbiome quantification

To validate our bacterial quantification method in real-world scenarios, we applied it to samples from four body sites (skin, nasal cavity, saliva, and stool) collected from five healthy individuals. We used bacterial cultures with samples derived from nasal swab, salivary, and skin swab samples to compare CFU with our 16S rRNA sequencing counts. The comparison of the log10 of CFUs and the log10 of estimated bacterial counts showed a strong correlation, with a Pearson r of 0.94 (Fig. 4a).

Our method revealed distinct bacterial abundances across different sample types (Fig. 4b). The estimated bacterial abundance was at 109−1010cells/g in stool samples, 108−1010 cells/mL in saliva samples, 104−106 cells/mL in nasal swab samples and 102−104 cells/mL in skin swab samples. Beta-diversity analysis of the samples demonstrated a distinct compositional difference among the samples tested (Fig. 4c). The analysis demonstrated distinct clustering of samples based on their origin, highlighting the method’s ability to capture site-specific microbial signatures. We further analyzed the bacterial species identified in all samples and quantified the proportion of shared bacteria among all donors for each sample type (Fig. 4d). The proportion of shared bacteria was highest in saliva at 59%, followed by skin at 29%, nose at 23%, and stool at 18%. C. acnes was only seen in the skin and nasal samples and the rate of contamination range from 1 to 4% for each (Supplement 3).

Discussion

Quantifying the microbiome profiles particularly in the human body provides more insights in investigating the association of microbial population to disease [19, 20]. Efforts in developing methods in quantifying high throughput microbiome data has focused in introducing spike-in controls, qPCRs and flow cytometry [19–21] however there is limited data with nanopore sequencing, a low-cost sequencer. Moreover, comparison to culture data, the most widely used method in identifying and quantifying clinical samples, are currently not available. In this study, we demonstrated full-length 16S rRNA gene sequencing with nanopore sequencing could reliably identify the microbiome at the species level using Emu and the inclusion of a spike-in provided a bacterial estimation at the expected magnitude. The bacterial estimation was unaffected by the amount of input DNA, the number of PCR cycles, or the percentage of spike-ins relative to the input DNA. However, there is the risk of not being able to sequence the spike-in if the amount added is very small. Rather, the estimation of quantity depends on the precise identification of bacterial taxa. Testing the method on a range of human body derived samples with varying microbial loads showed high concordance between the culture and sequencing estimates.

The emu workflow for community profiling, combined with full-length 16S rRNA gene sequencing, provided superior identification at the genus and species levels even when compared to previous workflows in our previous publication [12]. Emu optimizes the increased sequence length and error rate of full-length 16S rRNA by employing an expectation–maximization algorithm to generate taxonomic abundance profiles [14]. Similarly to our previous result, Pseudomonas was underrepresented but this could be due to high GC content and would require choosing the appropriate DNA polymerase if this the organism of interest [12]. Moreover, our use of Emu failed to reliably identify Bifidobacterium and had difficulty differentiating species within Veillonella genus in more complex microbiomes. The inability to identify Bifidobacterium is a limitation arising from the primers set used rather than from Emu and has been previously reported [22]. On the other hand, Veillonella species have high similarity in their 16S rRNA, thus making it difficult to distinguish one from the other when targeting the 16S rRNA gene for identification [23]. This emphasizes the need to use appropriate primers for specific sample sites and the limitations of reference-based classification of microbiomes, wherein some organisms may not be well represented and thus can be misidentified.

Quantification of microbiome profiles depends on the accuracy of identification of bacterial taxa. Our results indicated no significant difference between 25 and 35 PCR cycles, though fewer cycles showed a higher Pearson r correlation with the microbial standard and less deviation from the expected quantity. As we have shown previously, the use of more PCR cycles increases the presence of false positive and misclassified reads [12]. Similarly, the amount of spike-in did not influence the estimate of the bacterial load, as long as the spike-in and bacterial composition were identified. Adjusting the proportion of spike-in to the sample is critical to ensuring that the sample is sequenced preferentially over the spike-in. Our spike-in contained two organisms, and we used the mean of the estimates from both organisms to quantify the bacteria. There is a fixed ratio of spike-in organisms, and the use of both estimates balances the quantification; thus, the importance of sequencing both organisms. It is important to note that this study used log10 estimates to measure abundance; thus, the accuracy is in magnitude differences, which is representative of the difference observed in the clinical setting or in biological systems.

Quantification of the microbial community by comparing cultures and sequencing was highly concordant. However, quantifying low-to-high microbial loads remains challenging. Low microbial load samples, particularly swabs and biological liquids like urine often contain significant amounts of host DNA [24–26], which complicates microbial DNA extraction and spike-in targeting. Estimating the bacterial DNA by qPCR allowed us to determine in advance the precise amount of spike-in required for organism identification, so as to maximize the sequencing of the bacterial sample rather than the spike-in itself. In this study, we did not use our qPCR products for sequencing and amplified them again with another polymerase. Our previous research demonstrated that the choice of the polymerase could significantly impact bacterial identification accuracy [12]. Additionally, we used low amounts of spike-in, alongside negative and positive controls, to differentiate contaminants from low-abundance bacterial organisms. Caution is needed in interpretating C. acnes presence particularly in low microbial load and non-skin microbiomes, especially if it is the suspected driver of the disease, as they could be a contaminant and present in negative controls [16, 17].

Despite these findings, this study has several limitations. First, the Emu workflow struggled to identify certain genera and species due to issues like primer design and the high similarity of 16S rRNA gene sequences. These challenges highlight a broader problem with reference-based classification systems wherein underrepresented and highly similar organisms in reference databases can lead to misidentifications. This limitation can be improved by using newer nanopore library preparation chemistries, flow cells and basecalling algorithms which can provide higher accuracy at species level classification [27]. We also encountered difficulties quantifying microbial loads in samples with low microbial content or high levels of host DNA, such as swabs, where the spike-in can dominate sequencing. While 16S rRNA gene sequencing bypasses the issue of having high host DNA since human DNA is not amplified. Lastly, by relying on log10 estimates for measuring bacterial abundance, we may have overlooked fine-scale differences in low-abundance bacterial populations, limiting the precision of our quantification in some cases.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that full-length 16S rRNA gene sequencing using Emu can accurately identify microbiomes at the species level, and the inclusion of a spike-in provides correct magnitude of bacterial quantification regardless of DNA input, PCR cycles, or spike-in proportion. Accurate quantification depended on reliable identification of taxa and proper spike-in proportion, ensuring balanced sequencing. The use of an internal control such as spike-ins can be applied reliably for quantifying of various sample types with varying microbial loads.
