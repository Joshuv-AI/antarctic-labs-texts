Development of a LiDia-SEQ™ platform compatible breast cancer panel and testing with metastatic breast cancer patient samples
Authors: Mohammadali Yavari Ramsheh, Raquel L Fraccari, Rossitza Christova, Jarrett Killpack, Jean-Rene Acquah, Georgios Nteliopoulos, Evie Wren, Farah Rehman, Karen Page, Jacqueline A Shaw, Atreyee Saha, Ryan Wong, Subhas Pradhan, Peter Novotny, Marie Peras, Beth Arnold, Michele Wisniewski, Hrishikesh Joshi, R Charles Coombes, Samuel Reed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPlasma circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA) analysed using next generation sequencing (NGS) reflects the evolving tumour molecular landscape. However, barriers to routine adoption of NGS ctDNA analysis in clinical settings to detect breast cancer recurrence and therapy selection and monitoring include high costs, the need for highly trained personnel to perform complex workflows and specialised laboratories. DNAe is addressing this with its sample-to-result NGS-based LiDia-SEQ™ platform. This benchtop platform is simple to operate, rapid, fully automated and likely cost-effective.Materials and methodsA clinically relevant panel (DNAe panel) covering 127-point mutations on three key genes – ESR1, PIK3CA and TP53 was designed and optimised with commercially available reference material. Once sufficiently optimised, the panel was validated using 32 clinical samples from metastatic breast cancer patients in a side-by-side comparison with the Oncomine™ Breast cfDNA Assay. Results from the Oncomine assay were analysed with the Oncomine standard pipeline while results from the DNAe panel were analysed using DNAe's custom bioinformatics pipeline.ResultsOut of the 32 clinical samples tested with the two panels, 12 samples had no mutations, and 20 samples contained mutations. Fifteen samples had mutations detected by both panels and five had a single mutation detected by one or the other panel.ConclusionUsing clinical samples, we show that the DNAe panel with analysis using DNAe's pipeline detects mutations comparably to the commercial Oncomine-Assay. This paves the way for development of the DNAe panel into a test for the LiDia-SEQ platform.Keywords: Liquid biopsy, Circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA), Breast cancer, Next generation sequencing

Introduction

Circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA) is the fraction of cell free DNA (cfDNA) that originates from tumours and is found in plasma. It offers a minimally invasive route to characterise the molecular landscape of tumours from a simple blood draw or “liquid biopsy”. The European Society of Medical Oncology (ESMO) and American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) have recommended its implementation into routine clinical care for therapy selection when clinical relapse has been identified [1,2]. The advantages of liquid biopsy and ctDNA testing compared to tissue biopsy include being minimally invasive, more reflective of multiple subclones, amenable to repeat testing if fast and cost-effective, and thus guiding therapy selection. However, current barriers to their widespread adoption in clinical cancer care include cost, infrastructure requirements (if running in-house), and turnaround times [[3], [4], [5]]. Commercially, next generation sequencing (NGS) liquid biopsy tests cost ∼£2500 per test with a turnaround time often of several weeks [6,7]. Consequently, this often translates into liquid biopsy-based NGS tests not being used enough to guide treatment decisions to the detriment of cancer patient care.

Research and clinical trials have shown that tumour NGS information is beneficial to breast cancer (BC) patients. It can be used to tailor treatment to a patient's tumour profile and/or to aid in prognosis. For example, approximately 40% of patients with hormone receptor (HR)- positive and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-negative breast cancer have PIK3CA mutations, with the SOLAR-1 trial showing that these patients benefitted from the selective PI-3K inhibitor, Alpelisib [8]. Furthermore, patients with ESR1 mutations, which are known drivers of acquired resistance to aromatase inhibitors, have been shown to benefit from the selective oestrogen receptor degraders (SERDs), such as Elacestrant in certain cases [9,10]. Moreover, TP53 gene mutations are the most frequent, occurring in approximately 30% of BC patients but unfortunately for now, it remains an undruggable target [[11], [12], [13], [14]].

These targeted BC drugs are enabling a shift from the traditional ‘one size fits all’ to personalised medicine. However, barriers such as cost and turnaround time are limiting the adoption of personalised medicine into routine clinical practice. DNAe is addressing this by developing a rapid, sample-to-result NGS-based platform, ‘LiDia-SEQ™’ that will fully automate the entire sequencing workflow into a single benchtop platform and provide same-day results. The underlying sequencing technology relies on Ion-Sensitive Field Effect Transistors (ISFETs), a technology that DNAe co-developed and is well established [15]. A range of diagnostic tests will be amenable to running on the platform, for example, for the detection of bloodstream infections (BSI) and earlier detection of BC recurrence or BC therapy selection and monitoring.

Here we present our work developing a clinically relevant panel (DNAe panel) that will run on the Lidia-SEQ platform. The DNAe panel is a targeted panel focusing on three key genes – ESR1, PIK3CA and TP53, as mutations in these three genes account for 93% of all variants across ten BC driver genes [16]. Within these three key genes, the panel targets frequent mutations in metastatic BC (mBC) as per the Catalogue Of Somatic Mutations in Cancer (COSMIC) [17,18]. The DNAe panel has 16 amplicons covering 127-point mutations on the PIK3CA, ESR1 and TP53 genes, while the Oncomine™ Breast cfDNA Assay (ThermoFisher Scientific) used for comparison as method validation covers ten genes with ∼150 hotspots and detects single-nucleotide variants (SNV) and small insertions and deletions. Use of NGS for targeted gene panel sequencing facilitates high sequencing depth, allowing sensitive detection of mutant sequences at low allele frequencies, such as 0.1%.

Library preparation for the DNAe panel was optimised with reference material. We then compared the DNAe panel to the established Oncomine assay using blood plasma samples from mBC patients [16,19]. In addition, we have developed a custom bioinformatics pipeline (DNAe pipeline) for the NGS analysis of DNAe panel libraries. Importantly, this clinically relevant DNAe panel and DNAe pipeline will be LiDia-SEQ platform compatible. This will help to bring rapid (same-day) NGS testing into routine clinical care.

Materials and methods

Reference material testing

To develop the DNAe panel and establish its sensitivity, we used gDNA (Promega). Library preparation was performed with different amounts of SeraCare ctDNA, ranging from 100 ng to 0.5 ng per reaction. Each amplicon was quantified using qPCR with gene-specific primers, and the number of copies was calculated using a standard curve with gDNA (Promega). Seraseq® ctDNA Complete™ Reference Material (SeraCare), covering mutations in 28 genes including PIK3CA and TP53, has established % variant allele frequency (VAF) which was used as a calibrator for DNAe pipeline analysis of the specific % VAFs prior to clinical sample testing.

Patient sample collection

40 blood samples were collected from mBC patients recruited at routine clinical appointments at Charing Cross Hospital. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients conforming to the ethics committee, which enables access to the Imperial College Tissue bank (REC reference number: 22/WA/0214). Blood was taken into K2 EDTA tubes (BD Biosciences), processed to plasma within 2 h of collection and centrifuged at 1000g (Jouan CR422; Jouan) for 10 min at 4 °C. Plasma was carefully removed, leaving 3-5 mm above the buffy coat, added to a 15 ml Falcon tube (BD Biosciences) and re-centrifuged at 2000g for 10 min at 4 °C. Plasma was carefully removed and stored at −80 °C in 1 ml aliquots in 1.5 ml tubes (Starlab) for subsequent cfDNA isolation.

DNAe panel design

This was designed from human genome reference assembly GRCH37 to cover actionable mutations with high prevalence in metastatic disease. The design covers 127 single-nucleotide variants (SNV) hotspot mutations, described in the Catalogue Of Somatic Mutations In Cancer (COSMIC) database, across 3 key BC genes: ESR1, PIK3CA and TP53 (Supplementary Table S1).

Cell-free DNA (cfDNA) extraction

cfDNA was purified using MagMAX Cell-Free DNA isolation kit (ThermoFisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer's recommendation. It was then quantified using dsDNA High sensitivity assay using Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer (ThermoFisher Scientific) as described previously following the manufacturer's instructions [16].

Oncomine library prep and sequencing

Oncomine libraries for each patient were prepared using Oncomine Breast cfDNA Assay (ThermoFisher Scientific) as described previously according to manufacturer instructions [16]. Eight samples were multiplexed, pooled and sequenced on a single Ion 530™ Chip (ThermoFisher Scientific) on GeneStudio S5 Ion Torrent instrument (ThermoFisher Scientific). Variant calling for Oncomine samples was determined using the Oncomine standard pipeline in Torrent Suite Software running on the Torrent Server.

DNAe library prep and sequencing

The DNAe libraries for each patient were made following Oncomine Breast cfDNA Assay library preparation with some modifications. Unique molecular identifiers (UMI) and barcodes were annealed to the patient DNA during PCR1 (2 cycles), using Platinum SuperFi Master Mix (ThermoFisher Scientific). The unused primers were removed with AMPure beads (Beckton Dickinson) purification. The purified libraries were amplified with 30 cycles of PCR2 using Platinum SuperFi MM (ThermoFisher Scientific). Eight DNAe panel libraries were pooled together and sequenced according to identical protocols as Oncomine libraries. Library reactions were set up on the Ion Chef™ system according to the manufacturer's protocol, using the Oncomine™ Breast cfDNA v1 Assay and run on a 540 chip on the Ion S5 sequencing platform. DNAe panel sample analysis was performed using an in-house bioinformatics pipeline (DNAe pipeline).

Bioinformatic processing

Oncomine Breast cfDNA Assay sequencing data: The processing from the raw data to the variant detection was done locally by the Ion Torrent Suite Software version 5.18.1, using preconfigured settings.

DNAe panel sequencing data: For the in-house developed DNAe pipeline, after adapter trimming by the fastp version 0.23.2 software [20], the molecular tag was extracted and filtered by base quality with the umi_tools version 1.1.2 software [21]. A round of quality control was then performed, where the reads were filtered by length and quality, respectively by the software version 2.2.0 [22] and bbmap version 38.18 [23]. After alignment by the BWA-MEM2 software version 2.2.1 using the default parameters, the consensus reads were built following the best practices for the software fgbio version 2.1.0 [24,25]. After a second alignment of the consensus reads with BWA-MEM2, all the variants with frequency >0.01% were called by the VarDict software version 1.8.3 [26]. All those variants were further filtered according to various metrics (VarDict variant filtering metrics, frequency, molecular coverage, variant type) by an in-house developed Python script.

Statistical analysis

The data was analysed using Microsoft® Excel and Python. Spearman's rank correlation coefficient and linear regression were calculated to determine whether the % VAF detected by each panel was correlated, while agreement in terms of gene mutation detection was explored using Cohen's kappa coefficient.

Results

DNAe panel selection

The DNAe panel targets were carefully selected to meet the needs of patients and clinicians and fill the diagnostic gap that exists in the market. Specifically, we have focussed on ESR1 and PIK3CA mutations because patients with these mutations respond with greater frequency to Elacestrant and Alpelisib respectively.

Limit of detection (LOD) of the DNAe assay

We first determined the LOD of the panel using gDNA. The number of molecule (copies) per amplicon was determined using qPCR and calculated relative to a standard curve. At the clinically relevant cfDNA amount of 25 ng per sample, the DNAe panel produces an average of 6.35E+8 copies/amplicon specific libraries. Even at 0.5 ng per sample, the library preparation contains an average of 7.94E+6 molecules/amplicon (See Supplementary Figure S1).

To develop the DNAe panel and improve the library preparation chemistry, we tested the panel on reference material with well-characterised VAFs, such as SeraCare. SeraCare reference material covered two mutations in the PIK3CA gene and three mutations in the TP53 gene. We prepared the libraries, containing our 16-plex assay, including the ESR1 amplicons with WT, 1%, 0.5% and 0.125% reference material (n = 1). Ion Torrent platform-specific adaptors and IonXpress barcodes were incorporated into the libraries and sequenced on the Ion Torrent S5 instrument. The analysis was performed using the DNAe pipeline and enabled us to determine which pipeline parameters influenced the results the most, as well as which parameters worked best with each gene mutation. These were then used to determine a ‘universal’ set of DNAe pipeline parameters to blindly analyse the clinical sample data.

Testing of clinical samples

To test the validity of the DNAe panel on patient samples, we collected blood from 40 women with mBC. This is a valuable checkpoint to ensure the assay developed in a research and development setting will apply to real-life clinical samples.

Having determined the LOD of the assay, we analysed clinical samples containing ≥∼40 ng cfDNA and did not use the 8 samples containing less than this. Thirty-two samples, with cfDNA amounts ranging from 44 ng/sample to 1206 ng/sample, with a median of 103.7 ng/sample, were split into two groups with 20 ng cfDNA each. These were then analysed in parallel using the two assays. One tube was used for preparing libraries with the Oncomine Breast cfDNA Assay (ThermoFisher Scientific), and the other was used for the DNAe panel (Fig. 1). The starting amount of 20 ng was chosen as it allows the detection of ∼0.1% VAF using the Oncomine assay with minimum amplicon coverage of 25,000.

There was poor concordance at the protein sequence position 1047 on the PIK3CA gene for the histidine to leucine and histidine to arginine mutations. These are located on an amplicon, which we know from testing on reference material tends to perform worse than other amplicons. The PIK3CA p.H1047R mutation was present in the reference material, so we were able to re-analyse the data using the mutation-specific DNAe pipeline parameters identified using the reference material. The improved coefficient of variation for the re-analysed PIK3CA p.H1047R mutation becomes 0.052 and 0.088 for samples 1 and 30, respectively (Fig. 3). Discussion

In this study we show the performance of our clinically relevant panel using reference material to begin with. This allowed us to optimise the DNAe pipeline parameters so that the 32 clinical samples could be analysed in a blind manner. The analysis of the 32 clinical samples with the DNAe pipeline was performed using universal parameters informed by testing reference material. Once this analysis was complete, the results were compared to the Torrent Suite Software, ensuring that the Oncomine Breast cfDNA Assay results did not influence the DNAe pipeline results. A moderate to strong correlation was found for the % VAF detected by the two panels. This was lower than another study reporting a Spearman's rank correlation coefficient of 0.88 across 47 variants from 30 samples when comparing the Oncomine Breast cfDNA Assay_v1 and InVisionSeq™ ctDNA Assay [27]. However, this study used two commercially available and optimised panels, whereas we have compared the commercially available Oncomine Breast cfDNA Assay to a newer panel that has not been fully optimised, so a weaker correlation is to be expected.

The PPA, NPA, and OPA demonstrate the very good performance of our assay and DNAe pipeline, with only two samples (6.3%) positive with the Oncomine-assay, not detected with the DNAe panel. As additional plasma was not available, we were unable to determine whether the nine samples (28.1%) positive with the DNAe panel only were false positives using an orthogonal method such as digital PCR. A 92% PPA and 99.74% NPA is clinically important as it means that with the DNAe panel, few BC positive patients would miss treatment due to not being diagnosed (PPA), and extremely few patients would have unnecessary treatment due to being over-diagnosed (NPA). These results are consistent with others who have reported 81% sensitivity and 99.9% specificity for the Oncomine Breast cfDNA assay [28].

For mutations detected by both panels, the poorest concordance was for the PIK3CA gene at protein sequence position 1047 in samples 1, 30, and 34. The PIK3CA p.H1047R mutation was present in the reference material, so we were able to re-analyse the data with DNAe pipeline parameters optimised to that specific mutation. This gave % VAF results which were much closer to those of Oncomine. A possible explanation for the large improvement observed for sample 30 when using universal vs. specific parameters could be the low number of reads, making the universal parameters unreliable. Nevertheless, this gives us confidence that the DNAe pipeline could be improved further with optimisation. For example, different parameters, known to work best for each mutation or group of mutations, could be used in future improved DNAe pipeline versions. Also, cell lines could be used to generate reference material that is not commercially available. Although poorer concordance is seen at VAFs less than 1%, this is consistent with the literature, which have also reported poorer concordance between assays and platforms for <1% VAF [29,30].

Clinical samples can be difficult to obtain without a formal clinical trial, and thus only a limited number of samples were tested (n = 32) to give us confidence in the performance of our library preparation and DNAe pipeline detecting mutations down to low VAF (<0.5%). These results should not be interpreted as definitive assay performance but rather as a successful checkpoint in the prelude to full assay validation with a clinical trial.

At ultra-low VAF (≤0.1%) the absolute number of target molecules present in the sample is scant and presents a common problem for clinical diagnostic assays—stochasticity in sampling. This problem can be improved by increasing the assay's input sample volume, but this will only improve sensitivity linearly. Therefore, diagnostic assays should be designed to make the best use of those low abundance molecules (i.e., by maximizing yield). While we have demonstrated detection of mutations as low as 0.1% VAF in our dataset, further assay development work will require comparator assay testing with both commercially available and in-house developed assays to prove this low level LOD to obtain approval from the regulatory bodies. Furthermore, we are developing a ctDNA enrichment method upstream of this library preparation assay with the aim of maximizing the yield of these scant target variant molecules for use in the fully integrated assay workflow.

We will continue to optimise the library preparation step alongside our DNAe pipeline. Once the library preparation step is optimised we will integrate it with the other parts of the workflow (sample preparation, clonal amplification, ISFET-based sequencing and analysis with the DNAe pipeline) to create the world's first integrated and automated NGS-based BC test that will provide rapid results in a clinically useful turn-around time. Once the workflow is fully integrated, we plan to test the complete workflow on DNAe's platform with a larger number of clinical samples. Importantly for now, we have designed this first panel-based test that covers around 95% of hot spot mutations in 3 key breast cancer genes and that can be run on the LiDia-SEQ platform which will provide rapid NGS-based sequencing results at the point-of-need.

Furthermore, liquid biopsy also has the potential of targeting and analysing circulating cell-free RNA (cfRNA), which has distinct advantages over cfDNA analysis. cfRNA can be highly abundant in plasma and other bodily fluids, with levels greater than the corresponding gene in cfDNA, resulting in increased detection sensitivity. Tumour gene expression can be measured by quantitative analysis of cfRNA, with tissue-specific expression profiles allowing for tumour tissue of origin identification. Detecting gene fusions in cfRNA, which are often expressed at high levels in tumours but difficult to detect via cfDNA analysis, is another advantage. While there are currently no validated cfRNA biomarkers in breast cancer, as the field develops this is almost certain to change. We are interested in and have begun expanding our NGS technological capabilities to include cfRNA from liquid biopsy to ensure future compatibility with these additional biomarkers as their clinical utility is demonstrated [31,32].

Conclusions

The results shown here are a key enabler to developing a clinically relevant rapid turnaround BC test on the LiDia-SEQ platform for routine clinical care. We now plan to make improvements to the panel design and library preparation protocol, then repeat this to increase our n and validate our previous results. Alongside this, the work on platform development will continue so that this panel can run on it. This will require process updates to make the protocol cartridge compatible. For example, DNAe has developed cartridge-compatible library preparation chemistry for its BSI test, which can now be leveraged and applied to oncology panels. In addition, the platform runs on whole blood for bloodstream infections but will require plasma to run liquid biopsy tests. A novel cartridge-compatible and centrifugation-free method has been developed and will be integrated with the platform. This will allow the user to load whole blood onto the instrument rather than having to isolate plasma beforehand [33].
