A macrophage-related efferocytosis-based two-gene prognostic model for acute myeloid leukemia identified by multi-omics and machine learning
Authors: Xiaoning Li, Yujie Zhang, Xiaoying Wei, Rui Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) remains a lethal hematologic malignancy with high heterogeneity. Macrophage-mediated efferocytosis in the tumor microenvironment is implicated in immune suppression and disease progression. Methods We integrated single-cell and bulk transcriptomic data from public cohorts to identify genes associated with macrophages and efferocytosis in AML. Candidate genes were screened for prognosis using univariate Cox regression. A comprehensive machine learning framework, evaluating 117 algorithm combinations, was employed to construct a robust prognostic model. The optimal LASSO and random survival forest approach identified CD52 and S100A4 as core prognostic genes. The resulting two-gene model was rigorously validated using Kaplan-Meier analysis, time-dependent ROC curves, and calibration plots across multiple independent cohorts. The associations of the risk score with the immune microenvironment and drug sensitivity were further analyzed. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis was applied to interpret the model’s decision-making. Results The two-gene signature demonstrated stable and powerful predictive performance for overall survival in both training and external validation sets. The risk score was an independent prognostic factor and showed significant correlations with immune cell infiltration patterns and response to chemotherapeutic agents. SHAP analysis confirmed the consistent and biologically plausible contributions of CD52 and S100A4. Single-cell resolution analysis revealed their specific enrichment in distinct AML-associated macrophage subpopulations. Conclusions We developed a novel macrophage efferocytosis-based prognostic model using a multi-omics and machine learning approach. This model provides valuable insights into the immune microenvironment of AML and offers a potential tool for risk stratification and therapeutic guidance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00277-026-07044-7.

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a highly heterogeneous malignant hematological disorder that originates from hematopoietic stem cells. It is characterized by impaired differentiation and clonal proliferation of myeloid lineage cells, leading to the accumulation of immature progenitor cells (blast cells) in the bone marrow, which subsequently suppresses normal hematopoiesis. Clinically, AML presents with manifestations such as severe infections, anemia, and hemorrhage [1]. Epidemiological data indicate that the annual incidence rate of AML is approximately 4.3 per 100,000 individuals, with a median age at diagnosis of 68 years, and the incidence increases markedly with advancing age [2]. Despite advancements in chemotherapy, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, molecularly targeted therapies, and immunotherapies, which have improved complete remission rates and five-year disease-free survival, most patients experience relapse following initial remission, resulting in an overall poor prognosis. The five-year overall survival rate remains as low as 28.3% [3–5]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to identify novel and effective prognostic biomarkers to better monitor disease progression, assess risk stratification, and enhance understanding of AML pathogenesis.

Macrophages, as critical components of the innate immune system, play essential roles in processes including pathogen elimination, regulation of inflammatory responses, and tissue regeneration. Based on their activation status and functional properties, macrophages are broadly categorized into two phenotypes: classically activated M1 macrophages and alternatively activated M2 macrophages. M1 macrophages predominantly exhibit pro-inflammatory and anti-tumor activities, whereas M2 macrophages demonstrate anti-inflammatory properties, promote tissue repair, and may facilitate tumor progression [6]. Recent studies have revealed that macrophages constitute a significant component of the bone marrow microenvironment. In the context of AML, these cells undergo reprogramming into leukemia-associated macrophages with tumor-promoting characteristics, particularly exhibiting a tendency toward M2-like polarization, thereby supporting leukemic cell survival and chemoresistance [7, 8].

Efferocytosis refers to the process by which apoptotic cells (ACs) are engulfed and cleared by professional or non-professional phagocytes [9]. This mechanism is crucial for maintaining tissue homeostasis and preventing excessive inflammation. Within the tumor microenvironment (TME), dysregulated efferocytosis may contribute to immune tolerance, tumor immune evasion, and the malignant transformation of the surrounding milieu [10, 11]. Existing evidence suggests that the efferocytic process can induce tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) to adopt an M2-like phenotype, impair T-cell function, and foster tumor progression [12–15]. In AML, inhibition of the efferocytosis pathway has been shown to reverse macrophage-mediated immunosuppression, indicating that this mechanism may represent a promising target for modulating the immune microenvironment in AML [16]. However, systematic investigations into the synergistic effects of macrophages and efferocytosis-related genes in AML, as well as their prognostic implications, remain limited.

The emergence of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has provided a powerful tool for dissecting tumor heterogeneity, enabling high-resolution transcriptomic profiling at the individual cell level and facilitating the identification of rare cellular populations. This technology allows for the elucidation of tumor evolutionary trajectories and the dynamic remodeling of the immune microenvironment. In AML research, scRNA-seq has been extensively applied to identify key cellular subpopulations, analyze intercellular communication networks, and uncover potential therapeutic targets, thereby contributing valuable insights for precision medicine [17].

In summary, this study aims to systematically investigate the prognostic significance of efferocytosis- and macrophage-related genes in AML to enhance the accuracy of diagnosis and treatment strategies for affected patients. Initially, candidate genes were selected through the integration of single-cell and bulk transcriptomic datasets, followed by in-depth analysis of associated biological pathways and regulatory mechanisms to clarify their roles in AML pathogenesis. Subsequently, a risk scoring model based on key prognostic genes was developed, and differences across risk groups in terms of immune microenvironment composition, drug sensitivity, and response to immunotherapy were evaluated to inform clinical decision-making. In addition, this study explored the expression patterns of prognostic genes in different macrophage subsets at the single-cell level, verified their expression through public databases, and evaluated their reliability and stability as potential prognostic biomarkers for AML.

Materials and methods

Data sources and participants

Publicly available datasets were used in this retrospective bioinformatics study. Training data were obtained from 132 acute myeloid leukemia (AML) samples in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA-LAML) [18] and 70 normal bone marrow samples from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) project [19].From the original 173 TCGA-LAML cases listed in the GDC portal, 151 samples with STAR-FPKM RNA-seq quantification were retrieved from UCSC Xena. After excluding samples with missing or invalid survival time (NA or ≤ 0), 132 patients with complete expression and clinical data were included in the training cohort. Single-cell RNA sequencing data from GEO dataset GSE116256—after quality control consisting of 16 AML patients and 4 healthy controls—was used for macrophage subset annotation. Transcriptomic data from GSE114868 (194 AML, 20 controls) was used exclusively to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and for intersection gene set analysis.

Three GEO datasets with survival information (GSE37642-GPL96, n = 417; GSE12417-GPL96, n = 157; GSE71014, n = 104) were employed during model development for machine learning–based feature selection. Among them, only GSE71014 was used for external validation of the final risk model via time-dependent ROC curve analysis.

Single-cell data processing and macrophage subset identification

To comprehensively dissect macrophage heterogeneity and function in AML, we integrated and analyzed the GSE116256 single-cell RNA sequencing dataset [20]. The original dataset comprised bone marrow samples from five healthy donors (BM1 to BM5). Sample BM5 was excluded due to inconsistencies in sampling and processing protocols. Consequently, the analysis included 16 AML patients and 4 healthy controls. Using the Seurat standard workflow, we constructed and integrated expression matrices for all samples [21]. In the single-cell RNA-seq analysis, strict quality control (QC) criteria were applied prior to downstream analyses, including retaining cells with > 500 detected genes, > 1,000 UMI counts, and mitochondrial gene content < 40%, removing potential doublets using the scDblFinder algorithm, and excluding ribosomal and mitochondrial genes to minimize technical noiseCell cycle effects were regressed out by using the ScaleData function with cell cycle scores as covariates. Batch effects were corrected with the Harmony algorithm [22]. Based on canonical marker genes and original annotations, cell populations such as “Mono-like” and “ProMono” were unified as monocyte/macrophage (Mono/Mac) clusters; other immune cell types including T and B cells were also categorized accordingly. Mono/Mac cells were extracted and further processed by removing cell cycle noise followed by dimensionality reduction and clustering. Principal component analysis (PCA) and uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP), combined with multi-resolution clustering and clustree analysis, identified multiple subclusters within the Mono/Mac population [23]. Macrophage marker gene expression and module scoring confirmed clusters as macrophages (Macro). After another round of cell cycle noise correction and dimensionality reduction, six functional macrophage subsets (Macro1 to Macro6) were delineated.

Identification of macrophage- and efferocytosis-related genes

We compiled a list of efferocytosis-related genes (ERGs) through keyword searches (“efferocytosis”) in GeneCards and KEGG, combined with literature-reported gene sets, and after removing duplicates, a total of 501 unique ERGs were retained [24, 24, 25, 25].DEGs in macrophages (DEGs‑1) were identified from single‑cell data using the Wilcoxon rank‑sum test (|avg_log2FC| > 0.25, ≥ 10% expression, adjusted p < 0.05). Bulk transcriptome DEGs (DEGs-2) were identified using a dual-platform integrative strategy. Microarray data from GSE114868 were analyzed with the limma package, and RNA-seq data from TCGA-LAML combined with GTEx normal bone marrow samples were analyzed using DESeq2. Genes meeting |log2FC| > 1 and adjusted P < 0.05 in both datasets were retained as robust DEGs-2 [26, 26, 27, 27]. The intersection of DEGs‑1, DEGs‑2, and ERGs defined candidate genes related to macrophage function and efferocytosis in AML. To explore functional associations, a PPI network was built with the 41 candidate genes using the STRING database [28]. Functional enrichment analysis was concurrently performed in STRING, and the network was visualized in Cytoscape with node size scaled to connectivity [29].

Prognostic analysis and model construction

We performed univariate Cox regression (R survival package) to evaluate associations between candidate genes and overall survival (OS), selecting genes with p < 0.05 and HR ≠ 1.We then applied LASSO-Cox regression (glmnet package, 10-fold cross-validation, α = 1) to select prognostic genes with non-zero coefficients [30]. These genes were modeled using random survival forests (RSF) to determine variable importance, ranking the top features. A final multivariate Cox regression model was built from core genes selected by LASSO and RSF, with hazard ratios (HR), 95% confidence intervals (CI), and p-values reported [31].

Model explanation using SHAP values

SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values were calculated using fastshap (100 simulations) to assess gene contributions to predictions [32].

Risk score calculation and grouping

(S100A4 and CD52) weighted by their multivariate Cox regression coefficients (log HR): Patients were dichotomized into high- and low-risk groups using the median risk score as the cutoff.

Statistical analysis and model evaluation

Survival differences between risk groups were analysed using Kaplan–Meier curves and log-rank tests (survival, survminer). Model discrimination was evaluated by Harrell’s concordance index (C-index, survcomp) and time-dependent ROC curves at 1, 2, and 3 years (timeROC) [33].A nomogram for 1-, 3- and 5-year survival probabilities was constructed using the rms package. Calibration was assessed via 1,000 bootstrap iterations. Clinical utility was evaluated by decision curve analysis (DCA) and clinical impact curves (CIC) using rmda and ggDCA packages [34].Overall model accuracy was quantified by the integrated Brier score (IBS, pec).

Functional enrichment and tumor microenvironment characterization

Pathway disparities between risk groups were assessed using Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) and Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) [35, 36].The tumor microenvironment was evaluated by calculating StromalScore, ImmuneScore, and ESTIMATEScore with the ESTIMATE algorithm [37].Differences in therapeutic sensitivity were predicted by comparing the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of common chemotherapeutic agents between risk groups using the pRRophetic R package [38].

In vitro induction of tumor-associated macrophages and qRT-PCR validation

In vitro macrophage polarization and qRT-PCR validation were performed to investigate the expression of prognostic genes in a tumor-associated microenvironment. Murine Raw264.7 macrophages were seeded at a density of 5 × 10⁵ cells per well in 12-well plates. After adherence, cells were cultured in the presence or absence of C1498 leukemia cell–conditioned medium to induce a tumor-associated macrophage (TAM)-like phenotype. After 24 h of stimulation, total RNA was extracted and subjected to quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) to assess the mRNA expression levels of CD52 and S100A4. Relative gene expression was normalized to internal controls and analyzed using the 2^−ΔΔCt method.

Identification and functional characterization of prognostic efferocytosis-related genes in AML

To characterize the AML bone marrow microenvironment, we analyzed the GSE116256 single-cell RNA sequencing dataset, which included 18,938 high-quality cells from 16 AML patients and 4 healthy controls. UMAP-based clustering revealed seven major cell types, with a notable expansion of hematopoietic progenitor cells (HPCs) and a reduction in lymphoid and erythroid populations in AML patients compared to healthy controls (Supplementary Fig. 1A-B). Within the monocyte/macrophage population, we identified six distinct subclusters (Macro1–Macro6), each with unique transcriptional profiles linked to functions such as inflammation, antigen presentation, and metabolic reprogramming (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Fig. 1C–D). Building on this cellular framework, we sought to identify prognostic efferocytosis-related genes (ERGs). We performed differential expression analysis across three distinct datasets: our single-cell macrophage data (AML vs. normal, DEGs-1), bulk RNA-seq data from TCGA-LAML and GTEx bone marrow (DEGs-2), and the GSE114868 microarray dataset (Fig. 1b). By intersecting the overlapping differentially expressed genes with a curated list of ERGs from the literature, we identified a core set of 41 candidate genes (Fig. 1e). A protein-protein interaction (PPI) network constructed using the STRING database confirmed strong functional connectivity among these 41 candidates, suggesting they operate as a coordinated module (Fig. 1f).

Similarly, KEGG pathway analysis highlighted enrichment in the complement and coagulation cascades, phagosome pathway, and Staphylococcus aureus infection (Fig. 2b). Prognostic performance and clinical utility of the two-gene risk model

We first evaluated the prognostic power of our two-gene model in the TCGA-LAML training cohort (n = 132). Patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups based on the median risk score (cutoff = 2.6922). Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrated that the high-risk group had significantly shorter OS (P < 0.001) (Fig. 4a). The model showed strong predictive accuracy, with time-dependent ROC AUCs of 0.724, 0.688, and 0.764 at 1, 2, and 3 years, respectively, and a Harrell’s C-index of 0.653 (Fig. 4b). A nomogram was developed to predict individual survival probabilities, and calibration plots confirmed its accuracy (Fig. 4c, d). Furthermore, decision curve analysis (DCA) and clinical impact curves revealed that the model provided substantial net clinical benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities (Fig. 4e, f). Importantly, after adjusting for clinical covariates including age, sex, WBC count, and cytogenetic risk, the two-gene risk score remained a significant and independent prognostic factor for OS (HR = 4.723, P < 0.001) (Fig. 4g). Biological pathways, immune microenvironment, and therapeutic implications of risk stratification

To understand the biological mechanisms underlying the risk stratification, we compared pathway activities between the high- and low-risk groups. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) revealed that the high-risk group was enriched for pathways related to cell proliferation (E2F targets), metabolic dysregulation (heme and fatty acid metabolism), and genomic instability (P53 pathway, apoptosis). In contrast, the low-risk group showed enrichment in immune-related pathways, including interferon response, TNF-α/NF-κB signaling, and complement activation, suggesting a more immunologically active state (Fig. 6a). We next investigated differences in the tumor immune microenvironment. CIBERSORT analysis revealed that the low-risk group had a significantly higher abundance of adaptive immune cells, such as B cells and various T cell subsets. Conversely, the high-risk group was characterized by increased infiltration of monocytes and neutrophils (Fig. 7a, b). Correlation analysis showed that S100A4 expression was positively correlated with monocytes (r = 0.75) and negatively with cytotoxic immune cells, while CD52 expression was positively correlated with regulatory T cells, suggesting their roles in shaping an immune-evasive microenvironment (Fig. 7c). Consistent with this, ESTIMATE scores were significantly higher in the high-risk group, indicating a more complex stromal and immune cell infiltrate that is often associated with immunosuppression (Fig. 7d).

Finally, we explored the therapeutic implications of our model by predicting drug sensitivity. The low-risk group showed higher predicted sensitivity (lower IC50) to conventional chemotherapies like Cytarabine and Bortezomib. In contrast, the high-risk group exhibited potential sensitivity to tyrosine kinase inhibitors such as Dasatinib and Imatinib (Fig. 7e).

Expression validation and cellular localization of S100A4 and CD52 in AML

To validate the relevance of S100A4 and CD52 in the AML microenvironment, we returned to our single-cell data. This analysis confirmed that both genes were predominantly expressed in the AML-associated macrophage subclusters (Macro1, Macro4, Macro5, and Macro6), directly linking them to the cell type of interest (Fig. 8 a). Analysis of bulk transcriptomic data showed that S100A4 expression was significantly higher in AML samples (TCGA-LAML) compared to normal bone marrow (GTEx), while CD52 expression was not significantly different (Fig. 8b). To explore their expression dynamics in a tumor context, we performed an in vitro macrophage polarization experiment. qRT-PCR analysis demonstrated that CD52 expression was significantly upregulated in M2-like tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), while S100A4 expression remained unchanged, suggesting that CD52 may play a key role in the pro-tumoral functions of macrophages in AML (Fig. 8c). Discussion

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a clonal hematologic malignancy characterized by the accumulation of immature myeloid progenitor cells due to blocked differentiation and uncontrolled proliferation [39, 40]. These blasts impair normal hematopoiesis, leading to complications such as anemia, infection, and hemorrhage. Despite advances in chemotherapy, targeted therapy, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, and immunotherapy, high relapse rates and poor overall prognosis persist, necessitating more effective prognostic models and therapeutic strategies.

In the bone marrow microenvironment of AML, macrophages—crucial components of innate immunity—are reprogrammed into M2-like, tumor-supportive phenotypes, which contribute to immune suppression and leukemia progression [41]. Efferocytosis, the process by which apoptotic cells are cleared, plays a dual role in tissue homeostasis and immune modulation [42]. Although recent studies have proposed the prognostic value of efferocytosis-related genes (ERGs) in AML [43], these investigations have not systematically integrated single-cell heterogeneity or modeled macrophage subtype–specific effects. Our study addresses this gap by incorporating both single-cell and bulk transcriptomic datasets to identify macrophage- and efferocytosis-related genes associated with AML prognosis. Using a multi-method machine learning framework spanning 117 algorithmic combinations, we constructed and validated a robust two-gene prognostic model comprising CD52 and S100A4, with the LASSO + RSF combination yielding the highest C-index across datasets. Drug response prediction based on GDSC cell line data revealed distinct in silico sensitivity patterns between the low- and high-risk groups (Fig. 6e). Consistent with established clinical paradigms, the low-risk group exhibited lower predicted IC50 values for cytarabine, the cornerstone chemotherapeutic agent in AML induction and consolidation therapy, suggesting that the low-risk signature may capture intrinsic chemosensitivity. In contrast, the high-risk group showed potential sensitivity trends toward several tyrosine kinase inhibitors, including dasatinib and imatinib, as well as the proteasome inhibitor bortezomib. Although these agents are not currently standard therapies for unselected AML populations, emerging evidence supports their context-dependent efficacy in specific molecular subsets. It is important to note that these findings are computational predictions intended for hypothesis generation and require further experimental and clinical validation.

CD52: A validated immune target in AML

CD52 is a glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchored surface protein expressed on most mature lymphocytes and myeloid cells, including nearly all normal and malignant B and T lymphocytes, monocytes, and macrophages [44]. While its expression is notably low or absent in the leukemic blasts of most AML subtypes [45]—suggesting limited utility as a bulk diagnostic marker—this finding masks critical cell-type-specific dynamics within the tumor microenvironment. Indeed, bulk RNA-seq analysis can obscure localized alterations, and our supplementary in vitro experiments using qRT-PCR revealed a significant upregulation of CD52 in tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) compared to control macrophages, highlighting its enrichment in the immunosuppressive niche.

Beyond surface expression, soluble CD52 (sCD52) exerts functional immunoregulatory effects by inhibiting Toll-like receptor and tumor necrosis factor receptor signaling pathways, thereby restricting NF-κB activation and suppressing the synthesis of inflammatory cytokines by macrophages, monocytes, and dendritic cells [46]. Despite its low abundance in AML blasts, CD52 has been clinically validated as an immunotherapeutic target in specific AML contexts, including subtypes with high EVI1 expression [47] and myelodysplastic syndromes/AML with 5q deletions [48].Consistent with these therapeutic implications, CD52 was highlighted as an important feature in SHAP analysis and retained independent prognostic value in multivariate Cox regression, underscoring its relevance as a microenvironment-associated biomarker and potential target for immune modulation.

S100A4: Linking immune suppression and metabolism

S100A4, a calcium-binding protein implicated in metastasis and inflammation, was the second gene selected for the prognostic model. Increasing evidence suggests that S100A4 plays a multifaceted role in AML progression: S100A4 has emerged as a multifaceted contributor to AML pathogenesis through its involvement in nuclear signaling, immune regulation, and metabolic adaptation. Alanazi et al. (2020) identified S100A4 as a nuclear signaling regulator and potential therapeutic target in AML by integrating nuclear proteomic and transcriptomic analyses [49]. Building on this, Peng et al. (2025) demonstrated that S100A4 facilitates the expansion and immunosuppressive activity of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) via the GP130/JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway, thereby promoting tumor microenvironment (TME) remodeling and immune evasion [50]. Furthermore, Chen et al. (2024) utilized single-cell metabolomic profiling to reveal that S100A4 is enriched in glutamine metabolism–active AML subpopulations, implicating it in metabolic reprogramming processes essential for leukemic cell survival [51]. These findings collectively underscore the central role of S100A4 at the intersection of immune suppression, metabolism, and leukemic progression.

Collectively, these findings support S100A4 as a central node linking immune dysfunction, metabolic adaptation, and disease progression in AML. Our results, including SHAP-based importance ranking, immune cell correlation, and differential expression validation, further substantiate its prognostic and functional significance.

Limitations and considerations

While our study integrates multi-omics data and leverages advanced machine learning algorithms, certain limitations must be acknowledged:

Despite the strengths of our study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, model bias and data heterogeneity may arise from the retrospective nature of public datasets, differences in treatment regimens, and technical variability across platforms, potentially limiting the generalizability of our findings. Second, sample imbalance in certain cohorts, including small sample sizes and unbalanced case-control ratios, may affect the robustness and statistical power of the analysis. Third, although previous studies support the roles of CD52 and S100A4 in AML, the precise mechanisms through which these genes contribute to efferocytosis, macrophage polarization, and leukemic progression remain to be elucidated, warranting further functional assays. Finally, this study is mainly based on multi-omics bioinformatic analyses without experimental validation. Although the findings were consistently observed across multiple public datasets, further confirmation in independent clinical cohorts and functional experiments will be necessary to substantiate the biological and translational significance of these efferocytosis-related biomarkers in AML. In addition, although the model demonstrated stable prognostic performance in several independent cohorts, its predictive ability was not consistently reproduced across all external datasets. In particular, in certain cohorts characterized by distinct clinical backgrounds and technical platforms, the model showed limited discriminative capacity. This variability may reflect the inherent biological heterogeneity of AML, as well as differences in patient characteristics, treatment strategies, and data acquisition methods across studies. Such inconsistencies highlight the challenges in developing universally applicable biomarkers. Future studies incorporating larger sample sizes, standardized data processing pipelines, and prospective clinical validation will be essential to further improve the robustness, reproducibility, and clinical utility of the proposed model.

Conclusion and future perspectives

This study proposes a novel two-gene risk model based on macrophage- and efferocytosis-related biomarkers, CD52 and S100A4, which reliably stratifies AML patients into high- and low-risk groups. The model not only demonstrates robust predictive performance across multiple datasets but also reveals distinct immune landscapes and drug sensitivities between risk groups. By highlighting the dual roles of these genes in immune modulation and leukemia biology, our findings provide a rationale for targeted immunometabolic interventions.

Future studies should aim to integrate spatial transcriptomics, longitudinal immune profiling, and in vivo perturbation experiments to further dissect the cellular context and therapeutic potential of these markers. As the understanding of efferocytosis in hematological malignancies continues to evolve, our work adds to the growing body of evidence that these pathways are not merely bystanders but active participants in AML pathogenesis and treatment response.

Conclusions

In this study, we integrated multi-omics data, combining single-cell and bulk transcriptomic differential expression analyses to identify AML prognostic genes related to macrophage function and efferocytosis. By systematically evaluating 117 machine learning algorithms and their combinations, the LASSO combined with random survival forest (RSF) model was identified as the best-performing approach, selecting the key prognostic genes CD52 and S100A4. SHAP analysis was employed to interpret the model, elucidating the contribution of these genes to the risk score and their potential underlying mechanisms. A robust two-gene prognostic model was further constructed using multivariate Cox regression, effectively stratifying AML patients into high- and low-risk groups. This model demonstrated strong predictive performance and revealed close associations between the prognostic genes, immune microenvironment, and drug sensitivity. Although the current findings are derived from integrative bioinformatic analyses, further validation in larger independent clinical cohorts and experimental studies is warranted to confirm the clinical utility of the model and to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms.
