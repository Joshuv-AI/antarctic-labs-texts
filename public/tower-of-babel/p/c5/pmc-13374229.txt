Carbon (charcoal) tattoo localization of biopsy-proven metastatic axillary lymph nodes for targeted axillary dissection after neoadjuvant therapy in breast cancer: a systematic review and single -arm meta-analysis
Authors: Wajahat Mirza, Alishbah Khan, Hania Iqbal, Mehak Ejaz Khan, Hadi Mohammad Khan, Muhammad Bilal Moeen-Ud-Din
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTargeted axillary dissection (TAD) using carbon tattooing aims to localize and excise biopsy-proven metastatic axillary nodes after neoadjuvant systemic therapy (NAST), potentially improving post-NAST axillary staging while enabling the de-escalation of axillary surgery.MethodsWe systematically searched Scopus, Embase, PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane CENTRAL, and ClinicalTrials.gov, supplemented by screening reference lists. Prospective and retrospective clinical studies evaluating carbon tattooing–guided targeted tattooed lymph node (TLN) excision in initially node-positive breast cancer patients treated with NAST were included. Random-effects generalized linear mixed models (GLMM) were used to pool the proportions of dichotomous outcomes. Continuous outcomes were pooled using a random-effects inverse variance model. The risk of bias was assessed using the ROBINS-I tool. Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses and a pre-specified clinical subgroup analysis (completion-ALND verification cohort vs. feasibility/no-mandatory-ALND cohort) were also performed.ResultsNine studies were included in this review. The pooled TLN identification rate was 0.94 (95% CI: 0.88–0.97). The pooled sentinel lymph node (SLN) identification rate was 0.754 (95% CI 0.541–0.888), with substantial heterogeneity between studies. TLN–SLN concordance was 0.554 (95% CI 0.333–0.755), indicating a moderate overlap between the tattooed node and SLN. In the completion-ALND verification cohorts, the pooled false-negative rate (FNR) of the targeted procedure versus ALND for residual nodal disease was 0.068 (95% CI 0.042–0.108). The pooled mean number of retrieved nodes was 3.25 (95% CI 2.54–3.96), and the pooled tattoo-to-surgery interval was 153.0 days (95% CI 131.9–174.2). Sensitivity analyses showed that no single study unduly influenced pooled estimates.ConclusionCarbon tattooing-guided targeted axillary procedures after NAST demonstrated high TLN retrieval and low verification-cohort FNR, supporting procedural feasibility for post-NAST axillary staging in initially node-positive breast cancer patients. However, variable SLN identification, moderate TLN–SLN concordance, and heterogeneity in technique and reporting support implementation within standardized multidisciplinary workflows with local audits. Further prospective comparative studies are required.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-026-04430-3.Keywords: Breast cancer, Targeted axillary dissection, Carbon tattooing, Neoadjuvant chemotherapy, Sentinel lymph node biopsy, Axillary staging

Introduction

Axillary lymph node involvement remains a cornerstone of prognostication and treatment tailoring in invasive breast cancer, directly influencing systemic therapy decisions, radiotherapy fields, and locoregional control strategies. With the expanding use of neoadjuvant systemic therapy (NST), a substantial proportion of patients with biopsy-proven node-positive disease achieve clinical node-negative status at the time of surgery. However, microscopic residual nodal disease may persist and is often heterogeneously distributed within the axillary basin. This creates a critical clinical tension: the imperative for accurate post-NST axillary staging to avoid undertreatment must be balanced with the need to reduce morbidity associated with routine axillary lymph node dissection (ALND). Carbon tattoo localization has emerged as a pragmatic strategy to mark the biopsy-proven metastatic node before NST and enable targeted axillary surgery afterward, with prospective data demonstrating its feasibility and clinically meaningful diagnostic performance in post-NST targeted workflows [1, 2].

Carbon-based marking is particularly attractive because it is radiation-free, low-cost, visually identifiable, and can be placed at the time of diagnostic needle sampling without requiring an additional preoperative localization procedure. A multicenter prospective feasibility program demonstrated that biopsied nodes can be successfully tattooed and subsequently identified during surgery, with frequent overlap between tattooed and sentinel nodes. This supports the simplicity of the workflow while highlighting that the target node is not reliably synonymous with the sentinel node [3]. The prospective multicenter TATTOO trial further evaluated targeted lymph node biopsy after primary systemic therapy and framed carbon tattooing as a scalable alternative to more resource-intensive marker technologies [4]. In parallel, charcoal tattooing at diagnosis followed by sentinel lymph node biopsy after neoadjuvant chemotherapy has been reported to be technically feasible and potentially advantageous for staging performance, reinforcing the biological premise that the retrieval of the originally metastatic node may mitigate sampling error after NST [5].

Despite this rationale, the carbon-specific evidence base remains fragmented across study designs, patient selection criteria, and operative pathways, including targeted node biopsy alone, targeted axillary dissection combining target node plus sentinel nodes, and protocols incorporating completion ALND. Innovations such as perilymph node tattooing with carbon nanoparticle suspension aim to optimize dye retention and intraoperative visibility, thereby expanding the technical spectrum of carbon-based targeting [6]. Retrospective series have evaluated carbon tattooing prior to NST and explored the intraoperative correspondence between tattooed and sentinel nodes, underscoring the substantial variability in concordance and the need for standardized endpoint reporting [7]. Additional prospective and comparative studies have reported accuracy metrics and false-negative events under different reference standards, emphasizing that performance is closely linked to node yield, retrieval success of the marked node, and pathology protocols [8, 9].

Accordingly, a rigorous synthesis specifically focused on carbon localization is timely and clinically necessary. Individual studies are often underpowered for uncommon but consequential failures, report non-uniform outcomes, and differ in definitions (e.g., target node versus tattooed node versus targeted lymph node), limiting direct comparability across cohorts [1–9]. To address this gap, the present study is the first systematic review and single-arm meta-analysis dedicated to carbon tattoo localization of biopsy-proven metastatic axillary nodes for targeted axillary procedures after NST, including contemporary feasibility data from additional cohorts [10]. Critically, it uniquely incorporates prespecified sensitivity analyses, subgroup analyses, and a GRADE certainty of evidence assessment to quantify performance benchmarks, interrogate heterogeneity, and provide clinically actionable guidance for response-adapted axillary de-escalation therapy.

Methods

Study design

This systematic review and single-arm meta-analysis were conducted according to the PRISMA 2020 guidelines. The protocol was prospectively registered with the PROSPERO database (CRD420261307110). We aimed to evaluate the feasibility and performance of carbon/charcoal (including carbon nanoparticle suspension) tattoo localization of biopsy-proven metastatic axillary lymph nodes to enable targeted axillary procedures after neoadjuvant systemic therapy (NST) in breast cancer.

Literature search strategy

A comprehensive search was performed from database inception to February 11, 2026, in PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science Core Collection, Cochrane CENTRAL and ClinicalTrials.gov. The strategy combined controlled vocabulary (where applicable) and free-text terms for breast cancer, axillary lymph nodes, carbon/charcoal tattooing, carbon nanoparticles, and targeted axillary dissection/lymph node biopsy. All records were exported and managed in Covidence, where duplicates were removed before screening. The complete search strategies and database yields are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

We included full-text, peer-reviewed clinical studies enrolling breast cancer patients with biopsy-proven metastatic axillary lymph node(s) at baseline who underwent carbon/charcoal (or carbon nanoparticle) tattoo localization of the target node before or during NST, followed by attempted targeted axillary surgery at definitive operation (e.g., targeted lymph node biopsy, targeted axillary dissection with or without concurrent sentinel lymph node biopsy, with ALND as a reference when performed). Eligible designs included prospective and retrospective cohorts and feasibility studies, provided that extractable outcome data were available.

The exclusion criteria were as follows

case reports, conference abstracts, editorials/letters, narrative reviews, protocols without extractable outcomes, animal studies, and non–breast cancer populations. Studies using non-carbon localization strategies alone (e.g., clip/seed/wire without carbon/charcoal) were excluded unless carbon-specific outcomes were extracted separately. Mixed-method cohorts were excluded when carbon/charcoal data could not be disaggregated. Studies without biopsy confirmation of baseline nodal metastasis or without post-NST surgical attempts to retrieve tattooed/target nodes were excluded.

Data extraction and outcome measures

Two reviewers (W.M. and M.E.K.) independently screened the titles/abstracts and full texts in Covidence, with disagreements resolved by discussion and adjudication by a third author (M.B.M.D.). Data were extracted in duplicate using a piloted standardized form, including study characteristics, neoadjuvant systemic therapy (NAST) context, carbon/charcoal tattooing technique and timing, axillary surgical approach, sentinel lymph node mapping method, and all extractable numerators/denominators for outcomes. The primary outcome was the identification (detection) rate of tattooed/target lymph nodes (TLN) at surgery after NAST. Secondary outcomes included the SLN identification rate (when SLNB was performed), TLN–SLN concordance, diagnostic accuracy measures when a pathological reference standard was available (FNR), and procedural outcomes (number of nodes retrieved and tattoo-to-surgery interval). Outcomes that were not amenable to pooling were summarized narratively.

Quality assessment

Because the included evidence was predominantly nonrandomized, the methodological quality and risk of bias were assessed using the ROBINS-I tool (Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies—of Interventions) tool. Two reviewers independently assessed bias across the ROBINS-I domains (confounding, participant selection, intervention classification, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, outcome measurement, and selective reporting), with consensus resolution and third-author arbitration, when required. Risk-of-bias judgments were incorporated into the interpretation of pooled estimates and the certainty of the evidence appraisal.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing) in RStudio IDE version 2026.01.2 + 418. Because the included studies reported common core outcomes as proportions across clinically heterogeneous cohorts, we performed a single-arm random-effects meta-analysis to generate pooled estimates with corresponding 95% confidence intervals, while accounting for between-study heterogeneity. For dichotomous outcomes reported as counts (TLN identification, SLN identification, TLN–SLN concordance, and diagnostic metrics when extractable), pooled proportions were calculated using variance-stabilizing transformations and back-transformed for presentation. Where required, continuity corrections were applied to zero-event cells. Between-study variance (τ²) was estimated using the restricted maximum likelihood method, and heterogeneity was quantified using I² and τ². For continuous outcomes reported with sufficient consistency across studies, pooled means were calculated using inverse variance random-effects models. When medians with ranges or interquartile ranges were reported, established conversion methods were used to approximate the means and standard deviations. Outcomes that could not be harmonized for quantitative synthesis were summarized.

Sensitivity analysis, subgroup analysis, and GRADE certainty of evidence

Sensitivity analyses were prespecified using a leave-one-out approach for the primary outcome and any secondary outcomes eligible for pooling, repeating the meta-analyses after omitting each study sequentially to evaluate the influence of individual studies on the pooled estimates and heterogeneity. Subgroup analyses were prespecified using a clinically relevant stratification based on the reference-standard strategy, recognizing its direct impact on interpretability of diagnostic-performance outcomes: (i) ALND-mandated/reference-standard cohorts, Boniface [1], Chen [2], Hartmann [4], and Khallaf [8]; and (ii) non-mandated ALND/selective ALND or de-escalated cohorts, Goyal [3], Li [6], Park [5], Porpiglia [7], and Natsiopoulos [9]. Subgroup pooling was performed only when at least two studies per subgroup reported harmonizable denominators; otherwise, subgroup findings were summarized narratively. The certainty of evidence for each pooled outcome was assessed using GRADE, considering the risk of bias (ROBINS-I), inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias/small-study effects where assessable, and evidence profiles were generated for the primary and key secondary outcomes.

Results

Across six databases and trial registries, 166 records were identified, and an additional six records were identified through citation searches. After the removal of 54 duplicates, 118 titles/abstracts were screened, leading to the exclusion of 106 records from the review. Twelve full-text articles were assessed, with no reports unretrieved, and three were excluded for predefined reasons (incorrect outcomes, incorrect comparator, and incorrect study design). Ultimately, nine studies met the eligibility criteria and were included in the qualitative synthesis and single-arm meta-analysis (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of included studies

Across the included studies, carbon/charcoal localization was evaluated predominantly in single-arm cohorts spanning Europe and Asia, with experimental sample sizes ranging from 20 to 254 participants. The most consistent primary outcome was successful TLN identification, while the key secondary outcomes included SLN–TLN concordance and diagnostic accuracy metrics, such as FNR, when reference pathology (often ALND) was available. Only one of the included studies provided a direct comparative framework between the targeted approaches and SLNB alone (Table 1).

Across the included cohorts, the sample sizes ranged from 20 to 254, and when reported, the patients were typically in their late 40s to mid-50s (Table 2). Most studies enrolled patients with biopsy-proven clinically node-positive disease, frequently cN1-predominant, with ductal histology as the dominant tumor type (Table 2). Methodological quality and study integrity

Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2 summarize the ROBINS-I assessments across the included non-randomized studies. The overall risk of bias was predominantly moderate, driven mainly by bias due to confounding (D1) and bias in the selection of the reported result (D7), which were consistently judged as moderate across studies, reflecting the inherent limitation that carbon-tattoo–guided targeted axillary approaches are typically evaluated in observational settings where residual confounding (e.g., baseline nodal burden, response to neoadjuvant therapy, and treatment selection) and selective reporting of outcomes cannot be fully excluded. In contrast, most studies demonstrated a low risk for classification of interventions (D3) and deviations from intended interventions (D4), indicating a clear definition and faithful delivery of carbon tattooing/TAD procedures in clinical pathways. Missing data (D5) were largely low but showed moderate concerns in a subset, consistent with the incomplete reporting of some variables/outcomes in the clinical cohorts. The measurement of outcomes (D6) was consistently low-risk, suggesting that key endpoints (e.g., detection metrics and nodal pathology outcomes) were ascertained using objective surgical/pathological standards. The selection of participants (D2) varied between studies (low-to-moderate), aligning with the differences in enrollment approaches (prospective vs. retrospective designs and eligibility pathways). Taken together, the figures indicate that while procedural conduct and outcome measurement are generally robust, the certainty of causal inference is constrained primarily by confounding and reporting-related limitations typical of non-randomized evidence (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2).

Primary outcome

Targeted Tattooed Lymph Node (TLN) identification rate

The pooled targeted tattooed lymph node (TLN) identification rate was 0.940 (95% CI 0.880–0.971), indicating the high overall feasibility of TLN retrieval after tattooing in the included cohorts. Heterogeneity was substantial (I² = 75%), suggesting clinically important differences between studies (e.g., patient mix, timing, surgeon experience, and localization adjuncts). Despite heterogeneity, the CI remained relatively tight around a high point estimate, supporting a consistently strong detection performance in most settings (Fig. 2).

Forest plot of the pooled TLN identification rate using a random-effects GLMM

Secondary outcomes

Sentinel Lymph Node (SLN) identification rate

The pooled sentinel lymph node (SLN) identification rate was 0.754 (95% CI: 0.541–0.888), reflecting a moderate-to-high overall SLN detection rate. However, heterogeneity was very high (I²=91%), indicating marked between-study variability, and the CI was comparatively wide, implying less certainty in the pooled values. This suggests that SLN identification is more sensitive to study-level factors than TLN identification in this dataset (Fig. 3).

Forest plot of the pooled SLN identification rate using random-effects GLMM

TLN–SLN concordance rate

The pooled TLN–SLN concordance rate was 0.554 (95% CI: 0.333–0.755), indicating that only approximately half of the tattooed nodes coincided with the sentinel nodes. Heterogeneity was substantial (I² = 82%), consistent with the variable overlap between the targeted and sentinel mapping across techniques and populations. Clinically, this supports the concept that TLN retrieval may provide non-redundant staging information relative to SLN biopsy in many patients (Fig. 4).

Forest plot of the pooled TLN–SLN concordance rate using a random-effects GLMM model

False-Negative Rate (FNR) of the targeted procedure.

The pooled FNR of the targeted procedure was 0.068 (95% CI 0.042–0.108), with no observed heterogeneity (I²=0%), suggesting consistent diagnostic performance across the contributing verification cohorts. Importantly, this FNR is anchored to a pathological reference standard for residual nodal disease, meaning that the denominator represents patients with residual axillary disease on verification, rather than the full study cohort. Accordingly, this outcome provides the most clinically relevant pooled estimate for the subgroup with persistent nodal disease after neoadjuvant therapy. However, the included studies did not consistently report whether residual ypN+ disease was identified uniquely because of tattoo-guided retrieval versus concurrent sentinel lymph node mapping methods (Fig. 5).

Forest plot of the pooled false-negative rate of the targeted procedure versus the reference standard for residual nodal disease using a random-effects GLMM

Number of nodes retrieved (targeted/removed nodes)

The pooled mean number of nodes retrieved was 3.250 (95% CI 2.536–3.964), suggesting that targeted approaches typically yield approximately three nodes per procedure. Heterogeneity was extreme (I²=95%), implying large differences in surgical approach and definitions (e.g., whether counts reflect targeted nodes only or targeted plus additional nodes). Therefore, while the pooled mean provides a useful benchmark, it should be interpreted as highly context-dependent (Fig. 6).

Forest plot of the pooled mean number of retrieved nodes using an inverse-variance random-effects model

Tattoo-to-surgery interval (days)

The pooled mean tattoo-to-surgery interval was 153.024 days (95% CI: 131.898–174.151). Heterogeneity was maximal (I²=100%), which is expected because timing varies substantially by neoadjuvant regimen duration, local workflows, and whether tattooing occurred at diagnostic biopsy or a later date. Thus, this pooled estimate mainly summarizes the overall distribution rather than representing a stable “typical” interval applicable to all programs (Fig. 7),

TLN identification was high across outcomes, with a pooled rate of 0.94, despite the substantial heterogeneity (Fig. 2). SLN identification was moderate-to-high but showed very high variability across studies (Fig. 3). TLN–SLN concordance was moderate (~ 0.55) and heterogeneous, supporting the notion that targeted nodes are frequently distinct from sentinel nodes (Fig. 4). The FNR of the targeted procedure was low (~ 0.07), with excellent consistency (I²=0%) when evaluated against the reference standard for residual nodal disease (Fig. 5). The procedural yield was approximately three nodes, and the tattoo-to-surgery timing averaged ~ 153 days; however, both continuous outcomes showed extreme heterogeneity (Figs. 6 and 7).

Sensitivity analysis

In the leave-one-out analyses, the pooled TLN identification rate showed the most meaningful heterogeneity reduction when Goyal 2021 was omitted, lowering I² from 75% to 42% and rendering heterogeneity statistically non-significant. For SLN identification, omitting Chen 2025 produced only a modest improvement (I² 91%→88%), indicating persistently high between-study variability. TLN–SLN concordance heterogeneity decreased the most after omitting Goyal 2021 (I² 82%→66%); however, moderate heterogeneity remained. In contrast, FNR demonstrated stable findings with no heterogeneity (I²=0%), whereas both continuous outcomes (nodes retrieved and tattoo-to-surgery interval) remained highly heterogeneous, even after removing the most influential study (Table 2) (Table 3).

Subgroup analysis

Across the completion ALND verification cohort (Group A), the pooled TLN identification rate was 0.962 (95% CI 0.939–0.976) with no observed between-study heterogeneity (I²=0%), indicating highly consistent TLN detection when TLN retrieval was evaluated in cohorts undergoing completion ALND (Supplementary Fig. 9). In contrast, in the no mandatory ALND/feasibility cohort (Group B), the pooled TLN identification rate was 0.912 (95% CI 0.781–0.968) with substantial heterogeneity (I²=77%), reflecting greater variability in TLN detection across feasibility-oriented cohorts with non-uniform axillary verification and/or broader clinical practice conditions.

When all studies were pooled, the overall TLN identification rate remained high (0.940; 95% CI 0.880–0.971) but with considerable heterogeneity (I²=75%). Importantly, the test for subgroup differences was not statistically significant (χ²=2.16; p = 0.1418), suggesting that, despite numerically higher identification rates in the completion ALND verification cohorts, the available evidence does not confirm a robust between-subgroup effect on TLN detection performance (Supplementary Fig. 9).

GRADE certainty of evidence

Overall, the certainty of evidence for all pooled outcomes was rated very low (Supplementary Table 2), primarily because the body of evidence comprised non-randomized designs with at least serious risk of bias (moderate ROBINS-I concerns), and for most outcomes, substantial heterogeneity driving serious to very serious inconsistency. In particular, identification and process outcomes (TLN identification and SLN identification) demonstrated high pooled proportions; however, the confidence in these estimates was reduced by marked between-study variability for several endpoints and by imprecision for outcomes with wide confidence intervals. The FNR estimate was comparatively consistent across studies (low heterogeneity) but was downgraded for risk of bias and imprecision due to the limited number of verification cohort events against the residual nodal disease reference standard. Collectively, these ratings indicate that the pooled point estimates should be interpreted as indicative rather than definitive and that future well-designed prospective verification studies could materially change the magnitude and confidence in these effects (Supplementary Table 2).

Discussion

This systematic review and single-arm meta-analysis provides the most focused synthesis to date of carbon tattoo localization of biopsy-proven metastatic axillary lymph nodes to facilitate targeted tattooed lymph node (TLN) excision after neoadjuvant systemic therapy (NAST) in patients with initially node-positive breast cancer. The principal clinical message is that carbon tattooing yields a high pooled TLN identification rate (0.94), supporting feasibility across heterogeneous clinical pathways, while accuracy endpoints, where a robust pathological reference standard was available, suggest encouraging performance but remain limited by the study design and verification patterns [1]. Our pooled feasibility signal is directionally consistent with the extended prospective TATTOO experience and subsequent prospective accuracy cohorts, which collectively demonstrate that durable marking can enable post-NAST retrieval of the originally involved node in contemporary systemic therapy settings [1, 2]. Importantly, the feasibility is not uniform across centers. Multicenter feasibility studies have demonstrated that TLN retrieval can be lower in some workflows, reinforcing that tattoo placement, imaging confirmation, and operative targeting must be treated as a multidisciplinary process rather than a single marker choice [3]. When a structured protocol is applied, multicenter prospective evidence indicates that carbon tattooing can achieve reliable target retrieval, providing a pragmatic non-radioactive pathway for TAD implementation [4]. Newer carbon nanoparticle strategies have technical advantages in terms of visibility and persistence, although they also introduce additional procedural variables that may become sources of heterogeneity unless standardized and validated prospectively [6].

A second clinically important message from our pooled secondary outcomes is that TLN excision should not be considered interchangeable with sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB) after NAST. SLN identification in post-NAST and initially node-positive cohorts is known to vary by mapping technique, patient selection, and treatment response, and our pooled SLN identification rate demonstrated substantial variability, consistent with real-world differences in post-NAST lymphatic mapping [5]. Likewise, the pooled TLN–SLN concordance was only moderate, indicating that the tattooed originally metastatic node frequently did not overlap with the sentinel node basin. However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously because several included studies used concurrent SLN mapping agents and did not consistently report whether retrieval of the tattooed node was attributable specifically to carbon tattooing, blue dye/radioisotope-based sentinel mapping, or the combined targeted axillary workflow. Accordingly, our results support the feasibility of carbon-guided targeted node retrieval within multimodal axillary staging pathways; however, they do not definitively establish the independent incremental contribution of tattooing over conventional SLN mapping alone. This approach is aligned with targeted axillary strategies that emphasize the removal of documented positive nodes to reduce the risk of residual disease being missed when ALND is de-escalated [8, 9].

For clinical decision-making, diagnostic accuracy outcomes must be interpreted in the context of the reference standard for residual nodal disease. Our pooled false-negative rate (FNR) estimate reflects cohorts in which residual axillary disease could be verified pathologically, typically through completion of ALND, which represents the most clinically defensible denominator for assessing whether a targeted procedure risks missing residual disease [10]. However, verification approaches differ across studies, and this reference standard variability is a major reason why targeted axillary evidence can appear inconsistent across marker types and institutions. This is also why feasibility studies that do not incorporate systematic verification cannot be overinterpreted as accurate evidence, even when TLN identification rates appear high [11]. Although residual ypN+ disease is the subgroup of greatest clinical interest, the available studies did not consistently distinguish whether such disease was detected uniquely by tattoo-guided retrieval or whether it would also have been captured by concurrent SLN mapping, limiting the inference on the incremental value of tattooing in ypN+ patients specifically. The evolving literature increasingly favors standardized hybrid strategies, such as clipping plus carbon dye tattooing combined with SLNB, designed to mitigate failures of any single localization modality and maximize the retrieval of the originally positive node [12]. Radiology-oriented guidance reinforces the same principle: successful targeted surgery depends on correct marker deposition, image confirmation, communication of node location, and workflow integration, and failures often arise at the imaging–surgery interface rather than from the intrinsic properties of the marker [13]. The broader targeted axillary dissection literature also demonstrates substantial variability in marker type and timing, supporting our interpretation that heterogeneity is driven not only by biology but also by methodological and pathway diversity [14].

Sensitivity and subgroup analyses are central to establishing the credibility of these findings. In the leave-one-out sensitivity analysis, removing influential studies reduced heterogeneity for some outcomes without reversing the overall feasibility direction (i.e., TLN identification remained high), which supports the robustness of the primary feasibility signal and suggests that heterogeneity is concentrated in specific workflows or cohorts rather than representing a fundamental inconsistency of carbon tattooing itself [15]. In the prespecified clinical subgroup analysis comparing completion ALND verification cohorts versus feasibility or no mandatory ALND cohorts, TLN identification was numerically higher and far more consistent in verification cohorts, whereas feasibility cohorts retained substantial heterogeneity. Clinically, this pattern is intuitive: standardized verification pathways tend to impose stricter operative targets and uniform assessments, whereas feasibility cohorts often reflect broader practice variability, mixed axillary approaches, and differing thresholds for additional axillary surgery. A similar pathway dependence is observed in tailored axillary surgery programs using clips and point marker systems, where performance is closely tied to protocolization and radiology–surgery integration [16]. Adjacent nanoparticle-assisted staging approaches further illustrate that technical innovation must be paired with reproducible workflows; otherwise, new sources of variability can be introduced despite the theoretical advantages [17]. Importantly, as selective and targeted axillary strategies expand, the field is moving beyond feasibility toward oncologic endpoints and safety frameworks, including early outcome reporting with carbon-based marking approaches, which is essential for validating the de-escalation strategies [18].

Comparing carbon tattooing with the broader targeted axillary evidence base strengthens the clinical interpretation. Alternative targeted localization methods, such as radioguided occult lesion localization and other radioguided techniques, demonstrate that high diagnostic performance is achievable, but they require distinct infrastructure, regulatory, and resource requirements [19]. Real-world surveys have confirmed a wide variation in neoadjuvant marking practices, reinforcing the need for local protocols and auditing, regardless of marker choice [20]. The preclinical development of melanin-based and polymer-encapsulated carbon particle systems is scientifically compelling and may eventually improve marker stability and histologic handling; however, these data remain translational until they are linked to clinical retrieval performance and patient-centered outcomes [21–23]. Isolated case-level innovations illustrate feasibility but should not be conflated with generalizable evidence [24]. Likewise, emerging oncologic outcome reporting of selective axillary dissection with carbon marking provides valuable signals, but cohort overlap and design heterogeneity mean that these data must be interpreted cautiously and ideally validated prospectively [25]. Encouragingly, the wider field is now generating prospective cohort data evaluating non-radioactive targeted axillary strategies after NAST, including feasibility and oncologic safety signals that provide a comparative backdrop for carbon tattooing approaches [26, 27]. High-quality diagnostic accuracy evidence exists for radio-guided seed placement and structured response-guided marking protocols, and these studies illustrate what best-in-class standardization can achieve, representing benchmarks that carbon tattooing pathways should emulate in terms of workflow rigor, reporting transparency, and verification standards [28, 29]. Accordingly, carbon tattooing-guided TAD can be positioned as a scalable, low-cost, nonradioactive option that performs well when embedded in standardized multidisciplinary workflows; however, it should be implemented with clear quality metrics and prospective outcome capture to ensure safe de-escalation [14].

Therefore, the clinical implications are practical and actionable. First, in initially node-positive patients treated with NAST, carbon tattooing enables a pathway in which the originally metastatic node is intentionally retrieved, addressing a known limitation of SLNB alone after NAST, given only moderate TLN–SLN overlap. Second, because heterogeneity is driven by workflow factors, centers adopting carbon tattooing should standardize (i) tattoo placement technique and imaging confirmation, (ii) operative targeting and specimen verification, and (iii) criteria for completion of ALND or additional axillary treatment. Third, the GRADE assessment, predominantly of very low certainty, should be framed as a caution against overconfident de-escalation in low-standardization environments: high pooled feasibility does not equate to universally reproducible accuracy, and future prospective verification and oncologic outcome studies may still meaningfully change effect estimates. In summary, carbon tattoo-guided TAD appears clinically useful for axillary staging after NAST, especially where non-radioactive, cost-conscious strategies are needed; however, the safest adoption model is protocolized implementation with ongoing audit and linkage to oncologic outcomes.

Although carbon tattooing is an attractive low-cost, non-radioactive localization strategy, its broader adoption is likely to depend on rigorous workflow standardization, multidisciplinary coordination, and local performance validation. This may be particularly relevant in resource-constrained settings, where the economic advantages of carbon tattooing are meaningful, but the infrastructure required to ensure reproducible retrieval and acceptably low false-negative rates may still be difficult to implement consistently in these settings. Accordingly, carbon tattooing may be best positioned as a pragmatic option within protocolized pathways or in combination with complementary targeting approaches rather than as a universally preferred standalone technique.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths

We restricted the inclusion to carbon tattooing-based targeted approaches, synthesized feasibility, concordance, verification-cohort accuracy, and workflow outcomes, and tested robustness using sensitivity and subgroup analyses, providing clinically actionable pooled estimates aligned with practical implementation.

Limitations

Most of the included studies were non-randomized and, therefore, subject to confounding and selection bias. The reporting of several outcomes was incomplete or inconsistent across studies, and procedural heterogeneity was substantial, including variations in mapping methods, operative workflows, and verification strategies. Heterogeneity was particularly marked for SLN identification and tattoo-to-surgery interval, limiting the precision and generalizability of pooled estimates. Moreover, the included studies did not consistently distinguish whether retrieval of the tattooed node or detection of residual nodal disease was uniquely enabled by carbon tattooing or also facilitated by concurrent sentinel lymph node mapping methods.

Conclusions

Carbon tattooing-guided targeted axillary procedures after NAST demonstrated high tattooed-node identification and low verification-cohort false-negative rates, supporting their feasibility in carefully selected and well-audited settings. However, substantial heterogeneity in technique, reporting, and workflow limits the certainty in the pooled estimates and may constrain broader adoption as a standalone preferred method. Future prospective comparative and ideally randomized studies should standardize procedural workflows, minimize missing data, and directly compare carbon tattooing with newer localization strategies, including combined method approaches.
