Association between prognostic nutritional index and mortality in patients with sepsis-associated delirium: a retrospective cohort study using the MIMIC-IV database
Authors: Sijia Liu, Zimeng Zhu, Zhize Yu, Yuxiu Liu, Zhiling Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivePatients with sepsis-associated delirium (SAD) exhibit poor prognosis and elevated mortality risk. As a comprehensive indicator reflecting nutritional status and inflammatory responses, the prognostic significance of the prognostic nutritional index (PNI) in patients with SAD has not been fully elucidated. This investigation aimed to examine the relationship between PNI values and all-cause mortality risk in SAD patients.MethodsA retrospective analysis of data from SAD patients in the MIMIC-IV database was conducted, with 28-day all-cause mortality as the primary endpoint. Additional assessments included 90-day, 365-day, in-hospital, and ICU mortality. Kaplan-Meier curves, the log-rank test, and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to examine the relationship between PNI and prognosis. Subgroup analysis was performed to assess the robustness of the results. Moreover, RCS models were employed to assess the presence of a linear relationship between PNI and mortality risk.ResultsAnalysis incorporated 5394 patients diagnosed with SAD. Kaplan-Meier analysis showed that the survival curves of the four PNI groups were significantly separated (P<0.001), indicating a positive correlation between PNI levels and survival probability, while lower PNI levels suggested an increased risk of mortality. Multivariate Cox regression analysis revealed that when PNI was considered as a categorical variable (quartiles), patients in the highest PNI group (Q4) had a 42% significant decrease in 28-day mortality risk compared to the lowest PNI group (Q1) (HR=0.58, 95% CI: 0.48-0.69). Protective associations were consistently observed for 90-day, 365-day, in-hospital, and ICU mortality, with all P-values less than 0.001. RCS analysis indicated a notable non-linear "L"-shaped association between PNI and mortality risk across all endpoints (P for nonlinear <0.001), showing a steep decline in mortality risk as PNI increased below 35.7645, followed by a stabilization of the protective effect beyond that threshold.ConclusionPNI is an indicator that has an impact on patients with SAD. It can reduce their short-term and long-term mortality rates and exhibits a significant threshold effect. A PNI value <35.7645 may serve as a key clinical cutoff to identify high-risk patients with SAD and guide the initiation of nutritional interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40795-026-01376-3.

Introduction

Sepsis represents a critical challenge in global intensive care medicine, with its pathological core characterized by a dysregulated systemic inflammatory response and multi-organ dysfunction induced by infection. Epidemiological data indicate that over 31 million cases of sepsis occur globally each year, with a mortality rate of up to 40% among critically ill patients [1]. Against this backdrop, sepsis-associated delirium (SAD), the most common form of acute brain dysfunction, has garnered increasing attention. The incidence of SAD in intensive care unit (ICU) sepsis patients can reach 30%–60%, with clinical manifestations including acute attention deficits, confusion, and a rapid decline in cognitive function [2]. More importantly, the 28-day mortality rate of sepsis patients with delirium is significantly higher, and the risk of long-term cognitive sequelae is substantially increased, making SAD a key factor influencing the overall prognosis of sepsis [3]. The etiology of SAD is characterized by a multifaceted interplay of pathological events. While the amplification of neuroinflammatory cascades, compromise of the blood-brain barrier, and oxidative insult, coupled with neurotransmitter dysregulation, form the traditional mechanistic framework, contemporary evidence increasingly highlights nutritional metabolic disturbances as a pivotal driver of the disease’s progression [4]. The hypermetabolic state induced by sepsis accelerates protein breakdown and increases energy expenditure, often accompanied by insufficient intake and absorption dysfunction, leading to rapid malnutrition. This malnutrition not only weakens systemic immune defense and exacerbates the inflammatory response, but may also directly impair brain energy metabolism, aggravating neuroinflammation and creating a vicious cycle of infection-malnutrition-brain dysfunction, which significantly impacts patient prognosis. However, limitations still exist in clinical practice regarding the early identification of high-risk SAD patients and the prediction of their outcomes. On one hand, SAD diagnosis is often delayed, and there is a lack of sensitive biomarkers for the early identification of high-mortality-risk subgroups. On the other hand, existing sepsis prognostic scoring systems, such as Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) and Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II(APACHE II), lack sufficient risk stratification capability for SAD patients, making it difficult to guide individualized neuroprotection and enhanced monitoring.

The Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI), a composite parameter integrating immunological and nutritional dimensions, not only reflects the nutritional status of sepsis patients but also serves as a key biomarker for assessing immune-nutritional status [5, 6]. Changes in PNI levels are closely related to systemic inflammatory responses, immune dysfunction, protein catabolism, and blood-brain barrier damage. Therefore, The PNI is poised to serve as a comprehensive surrogate marker that encapsulates the triad of nutritional exhaustion, immunological impairment, and neuroinflammatory activity. By integrating these disparate pathological axes, the index facilitates the early identification and clinical stratification of SAD patient cohorts at elevated risk for adverse outcomes. Recent evidence suggests that PNI is an independent predictor of mortality risk in sepsis patients, with low PNI levels significantly increasing the 28-day mortality risk [7]. Its predictive value surpasses that of individual markers such as albumin or lymphocyte count. Whether the PNI possesses sufficient discriminative power to function as a definitive prognostic surrogate in SAD warrants further scrutiny. Subsequent research must address the current gap in defining standardized clinical cut-offs, thereby ensuring that the PNI effectively supports early risk stratification and the optimization of therapeutic interventions for these vulnerable patients.

Leveraging the MIMIC-IV database, the present investigation employs a retrospective cohort design to systematically quantify the prognostic efficacy of the PNI in predicting SAD-related mortality. The aim is to provide a new, convenient indicator for prognosis assessment and offer both theoretical and practical references for early nutritional interventions and the improvement of clinical outcomes.

Materials and methods

Data sources

Clinical data were extracted from the MIMIC-IV repository (hosted on physionet.org), utilizing an expanded dataset that appends records from 2020 to 2022 to the previous version 2.2. This retrospective framework allowed for a systematic evaluation of robust, high-granularity parameters in a contemporary cohort of critically ill patients. As a publicly available, large-scale database, MIMIC contains anonymized health-related records for over 40,000 patients admitted to the intensive care unit at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston, Massachusetts. Therefore, in accordance with the protocols of the review committee overseeing research using anonymous public datasets, this study does not require ethical review or participant informed consent. Approval. Data access and extraction were performed by an authorized investigator (S.J. Liu), who successfully completed the mandatory Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative (CITI) program or equivalent institutional certification (Certificate No. 15077497). This accreditation ensures that all data handling procedures adhere strictly to the ethical guidelines required for the use of the MIMIC-IV repository.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion Criteria: (1) Sepsis-3 diagnosis (SOFA score ≥ 2 with confirmed or suspected infection). (2) Age ≥ 18 years. (3) First ICU admission with length of stay ≥ 24 h. (4) Delirium confirmed by CAM-ICU. Exclusion Criteria: (1) Failure to meet the Sepsis-3 diagnostic criteria; (2) Age < 18 years with first ICU admission and duration < 24 h; (3) History of cerebrovascular events (stroke, cerebral infarction, cerebral hemorrhage) or metabolic encephalopathy; (4) Absence of delirium assessment; (5) Patients with positive delirium prior to SAD onset or outside the ICU; (6) Lack of lymphocyte and albumin measurements. Delirium was diagnosed using the CAM-ICU, which was routinely administered daily by trained critical care staff during the entire ICU stay. The first incident delirium episode was defined as the first positive CAM-ICU result occurring after the onset of SAD and within the ICU period. Patients with pre-existing delirium before SAD onset or delirium diagnosed outside the ICU were excluded to avoid prevalent cases and ensure temporal relevance.

Data extraction and management

Structured Query Language (SQL) and PostgreSQL (version 9.6) were utilized to retrieve data from the MIMIC-IV database. The extracted datasets were organized into the following core components: demographic characteristics, anthropometric measurements, initial vital signs and laboratory test results upon admission, disease severity scores (SOFA, APACHE II), comorbidities, and initial treatment interventions.

Both albumin and lymphocyte values were defined as the worst values (lowest levels) documented within the first 24 h following ICU admission.

For all continuous variables, we predefined reasonable numerical ranges. Outliers exceeding these ranges or values with obvious contradictions were treated as missing data. According to established criteria, any variable with a data missing rate exceeding 30% was excluded from subsequent analyses. For missing values in retained variables, multiple imputation was performed using the “randomForest” package in R via the random forest (RF) algorithm. Detailed procedures are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Endpoints of interest

The primary clinical endpoint was defined as the 28-day mortality rate. Secondary objectives focused on extended survival outcomes—specifically 90-day and 365-day mortality—alongside acute healthcare-related metrics, including in-hospital and ICU fatality rates.

Statistical analysis

Statistical processing and graphical rendering were executed via R (version 4.2.2). The distributional characteristics of continuous variables were ascertained using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Accordingly, normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and analyzed using Student’s t-tests. In contrast, non-parametric variables were reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), utilizing Mann-Whitney U or Kruskal-Wallis tests for dual or multi-group comparisons, respectively. For categorical data, the Pearson chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was implemented based on the expected frequency distributions. Longitudinal survival trajectories across PNI strata were visualized using Kaplan-Meier curves, with the significance of inter-group disparities determined by the log-rank test. We constructed Cox proportional hazards models to calculate hazard ratios (HR) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) between PNI (as a continuous variable and as a categorical variable grouped by quartiles) and clinical outcomes. Confounding factors were progressively adjusted in each model to control for potential bias. In order to prevent overfitting when adjusting the model, the adjusted variables are diagnosed for collinearity and the variance inflation factor of the variables is calculated.

We utilized four progressively adjusted models to mitigate the influence of potential confounders. Following the unadjusted analysis (Model 1), we accounted for baseline demographics in Model 2. Model 3 built upon this by adjusting for a multifaceted array of clinical variables, encompassing comprehensive metabolic panels, coagulation profiles (INR, PT, PTT), and life-support interventions. The final fully adjusted model (Model 4) accounted for the underlying burden of chronic illness (comorbidity profiles) and the acute physiological insult at admission, as quantified by standardized severity scores (APACHE II, SOFA, GCS), while also considering the confounding effects of sedative therapy.

RCS analysis

To investigate whether a nonlinear relationship exists between PNI and all-cause mortality, this study employed a Restricted Cubic Spline(RCS) model [8]. By generating RCS curves, the model visually depicted the association patterns between PNI level changes and fluctuations in mortality risk.

Forest plot

To validate the stability and reliability of key findings, this study employed subgroup analysis, stratifying patients based on predefined characteristics potentially influencing clinical outcomes. Stratification variables were selected considering two factors: confounding factors identified in the analysis and interaction effects supported by existing literature. Subgroup divisions were based on clinically practical grouping criteria or median values of variables. Included subgroups comprised: AMI, CKD, COPD, hypertension, DM, AKI, and stroke.

Results

Patient characteristics

Our final analysis comprised 5,394 SAD patients (selection process in Fig. 1). The cohort was partitioned into quartiles (Q1–Q4) based on PNI scores, with respective ranges defined as: Q1 (first quartile): ≤ 30.35; Q2 (second quartile): 30.35–35.7645; Q3 (third quartile): 35.7645–41.8; Q4 (fourth quartile): >41.8. These thresholds were utilized to investigate the independent prognostic weight of the PNI across diverse patient profiles. Detailed baseline characteristics for each PNI group are presented in Table 1. Our analysis demonstrated that patients in the lowest PNI quartile (Q1) exhibited significantly lower levels of laboratory markers including albumin, as well as significantly higher disease severity scores (SOFA and APACHE II; all P < 0.05). Notably, however, a novel and clinically relevant finding was that patients in Q1 were not the oldest (P < 0.05), nor did they carry the highest burden of chronic comorbidities.

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis of PNI and mortality

Based on the quartile levels of PNI, patients with SAD in this study were divided into four subgroups. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were plotted for each subgroup, and intergroup comparisons were conducted using the log-rank test. As shown in Fig. 2, PNI levels were significantly associated with survival outcomes in SAD patients, with the survival curves of all observed endpoints exhibiting significant separation (all log-rank test P < 0.001). Specifically, PNI levels were positively correlated with survival probability, meaning higher PNI levels were associated with higher survival rates. The survival curve of the highest PNI group (Q4) consistently remained at the top, while the curve for the lowest PNI group (Q1) declined the fastest, indicating that low PNI is a significant risk factor for mortality.

Multivariate cox regression models

To quantify the independent prognostic value of PNI and control for potential confounding factors, we further constructed multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models. When PNI was treated as a continuous variable, its association with 28-day mortality did not reach statistical significance after adjustment for multiple variables (Table 2). However, when PNI was analyzed as a categorical variable based on quartiles, a significant and consistent dose-response relationship was observed. Using the Q1 group as a reference, in the fully adjusted model (Model 4), the 28-day mortality risk of patients in the Q4 group was significantly reduced by 42% (HR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.48–0.69, P < 0.001); the risks in the Q3 and Q2 groups were also significantly reduced in sequence (HR = 0.66 and 0.75, both P < 0.001). This protective association remained robust across all secondary outcomes: the 90-day mortality risk in the Q4 group was reduced by 35% (HR = 0.65, 95% CI: 0.55–0.76), in-hospital mortality risk by 41% (HR = 0.59, 95% CI: 0.48–0.73), and ICU mortality risk by 33% (HR = 0.67, 95% CI: 0.51–0.87) (all P < 0.001). The 365-day mortality risk was also significantly reduced (HR = 0.77, 95% CI: 0.67–0.89, P < 0.001). From the crude to the fully adjusted model, this inverse correlation remained stable, confirming that PNI is related to the short-term and long-term mortality rates of SAD patients. To prevent overfitting, we calculated the VIF values of the variables and found that the maximum VIF of the variables in Model 4 was 3.517, which was below the conventional threshold of 5. This indicates that there is no serious multicollinearity phenomenon in our final model. The details can be found in Supplementary Material Table 1.

RCS analyses

RCS regression was employed to examine the dose-response relationship between PNI and mortality (Fig. 3). Analysis revealed that PNI is linked to all-cause mortality through a significant nonlinear association across all assessed time points (P for nonlinearity < 0.001), indicating that the survival benefit of higher PNI follows a threshold-dependent rather than a purely linear pattern. A distinct “L-shaped” curve relationship was observed between PNI and mortality risk. When PNI levels were below 35.7645, the mortality risk decreased sharply with increasing PNI; however, when PNI surpassed this inflection point, the risk curve plateaued, and the protective effect of further increases in PNI diminished. This nonlinear pattern was highly consistent across all five clinical outcomes (28-day, 90-day, 365-day, in-hospital, and ICU mortality). Our data demonstrate that the protective influence of the PNI on survival is governed by a distinct threshold effect. This non-linear behavior provides a robust empirical justification for operationalizing the PNI as a categorical stratification tool in clinical practice.

Subgroup analysis

To assess the robustness of the association between PNI and mortality, we performed subgroup analyses based on different clinical characteristics (Fig. 4). The protective association between higher PNI levels and lower mortality risk (HR < 1.00) was consistent across nearly all predefined subgroups, demonstrating the universality of this relationship. Specifically, interaction analyses showed that for 28-day mortality, in-hospital mortality, and ICU mortality, the interaction P-values for all stratification variables were greater than 0.05, indicating no statistical difference in the strength of the protective effect of PNI across these subgroups. Notably, for 90-day mortality, CKD status showed a significant interaction (P for interaction = 0.012). For 365-day mortality, a significant interaction was found with a history of stroke (P for interaction = 0.008). These findings suggest that the protective effect of PNI on long-term mortality may differ between CKD patients and non-patients, as well as between stroke patients and non-stroke patients.

Discussion

Our analysis indicates that PNI has an impact on both the acute phase and long-term survival in the SAD study population, and its prognostic signal exhibits a significant nonlinear threshold effect. When modeled by quartiles and RCS, a consistent dose-response gradient was observed; notably, the highest PNI quartile (Q4) was associated with a 42% attenuation in 28-day mortality risk relative to the lowest quartile (Q1). These findings suggest that higher immuno-nutritional indices serve as a critical buffer against the systemic insults of sepsis-associated delirium. This protective association remained robust after multivariate adjustment, consistently observed across 90-day, in-hospital, ICU, and 365-day mortality outcomes. The RCS analysis revealed that the association between PNI and mortality risk followed a nonlinear L-shaped pattern. Below a threshold of 35.7645, mortality risk declined sharply with increasing PNI, however, beyond this point, the survival benefit plateaued, suggesting diminishing returns in prognosis with further PNI elevation. This inflection point provides a clinically meaningful cutoff for risk stratification.

PNI is a composite score integrating serum albumin and absolute lymphocyte count, reflecting both nutritional reserves and immune function [9]. Albumin, as a negative acute-phase reactant, is synthesized at a reduced rate during the inflammatory storm of sepsis and is lost in large amounts due to capillary leakage [10, 11]. Hypoalbuminemia thus reflects not only malnutrition but also the severity of the inflammatory response. Lymphocytes, critical mediators of adaptive immunity, undergo apoptosis, mitochondrial dysfunction, and metabolic exhaustion (e.g., ATP/NAD+ depletion) during sepsis, contributing to immunosuppressione [12]. In the pathophysiology of sepsis, a bidirectional interplay between inflammation and malnutrition creates a vicious cycle: pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α suppress albumin gene expression via the NF-κB pathway while accelerating its catabolism [13]. Concurrently, stress-induced corticosteroids and other immunosuppressive mediators inhibit lymphopoiesis, leading to lymphopenia [14]. Our findings demonstrated that patients in the Q1 group not only had significantly lower albumin levels and lymphocyte counts, but also exhibited higher SOFA and APACHE II scores, which is highly consistent with previous literature reporting that lower PNI is associated with elevated SOFA and APACHE II scores [15]. Emerging evidence supports the prognostic utility of PNI in critically ill populations. For instance, a PNI below 33.4 has been associated with increased mortality in patients with COVID-19. In oncology, PNI has shown superior prognostic performance compared to 13 other inflammatory and nutritional indices [15, 16]. While recent research has demonstrated a negative association between PNI and delirium incidence, that study was limited to non-mortality outcomes and did not examine dose-response dynamics or threshold effects [17].

PNI showed significant prognostic utility in SAD patients as a categorical variable, with multivariate Cox regression demonstrating a 42% lower 28-day mortality risk in the highest quartile (Q4) versus the lowest (Q1). Consistent protective associations were observed across 90-day, 365-day, in-hospital and ICU mortality rates. There is a nonlinear relationship between PNI and mortality risk, which manifests as an ‘L’-shaped curve. Mortality risk rises sharply within the extremely low PNI range. Beyond a certain nutritional-immunological threshold, the survival benefit of further PNI elevation plateaus. This result aligns with the biological characteristics of the inflammatory response, where, under severe malnutrition and immunosuppression, the body’s reserves for combating sepsis are severely depleted [18, 19]. In such cases, even small improvements in nutritional status can result in significant survival benefits. Once the baseline nutritional-immune status improves to a certain level, other clinical factors, such as organ dysfunction and secondary infections, will become more prominent in influencing prognosis. The apparent discrepancy between the non-significant continuous-variable association and the significant quartile-based effects is biologically and statistically consistent with the L-shaped relationship identified by RCS. Because a standard Cox model assumes a proportional log-linear relationship across the entire distribution, it can obscure a threshold-dependent association where mortality risk declines steeply below a PNI of 35.7645 but stabilizes above this point. Thus, the prognostic value of PNI in SAD should be interpreted as a critical threshold effect—identifying a distinctly high-risk subgroup below 35.7645—rather than a uniform linear gradient, and its detectability depends on appropriately modeling this non-linear form.

The underlying pathophysiological mechanisms of SAD remain incompletely elucidated, although current evidence implicates neuroinflammatory cascades as central contributors [20, 21]. Systemic inflammation increases blood-brain barrier (BBB) permeability, enabling peripheral inflammatory mediators to infiltrate the central nervous system, thereby activating microglia and astrocytes and inducing neurotoxicity [22, 23]. This process is frequently accompanied by mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and dysregulation of key neurotransmitter systems—including glutamate and acetylcholine. Our study demonstrates that patients with low PNI exhibit a higher prevalence of comorbidities such as acute myocardial infarction and chronic kidney disease, which may further compromise BBB integrity through microcirculatory disturbances and chronic inflammation, thus contributing to a “second hit” phenomenon. The influence of PNI on SAD outcomes occurs through multiple interrelated pathways. On the one hand, albumin serves not only as a marker of nutritional status but also exerts antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and microcirculatory-stabilizing effects [24]. At physiological concentrations, albumin inhibits TNF-α-induced expression of vascular cell adhesion molecules, thereby reducing endothelial injury. Hypoalbuminemia exacerbates capillary leakage, facilitating the translocation of inflammatory mediators into the brain and directly intensifying neuroinflammation. On the other hand, lymphopenia reflects impaired adaptive immune function, particularly a reduced proportion of CD14+CD16− monocytes, which has been associated with an increased risk of SAD [25]. Dysregulation of lymphocyte subpopulations may compromise pathogen clearance, prolonging the duration of sepsis and indirectly extending delirium episodes and increasing the likelihood of neuronal injury. Additionally, a decrease in PNI is typically accompanied by an elevation in markers of microcirculatory dysfunction, such as D-dimer, suggesting that a low PNI state may increase the risk of cerebral microthrombus formation, leading to focal cerebral ischemia, which plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis of SAD [26, 27]. Notably, our subgroup analysis revealed an interaction effect of CKD and stroke history on the long-term prognostic value of PNI. CKD patients may experience a reduction in PNI’s protective effect due to mechanisms like prolonged microinflammation, protein-energy wasting, and the accumulation of uremic toxins [28, 29]. Similarly, in individuals with a history of stroke, pre-existing BBB disruption, ongoing neuroinflammation, swallowing dysfunction leading to inadequate nutrient intake, and muscle atrophy secondary to prolonged immobilization collectively amplify the adverse impact of low PNI on long-term clinical outcomes [30, 31]. We acknowledge that some adjusted variables—particularly markers of organ dysfunction and acute laboratory derangements—may lie on the causal pathway between nutritional depletion and mortality rather than serving as pure confounders. However, as this observational study aimed primarily at risk stratification rather than causal mediation, treating these variables as confounders ensures that the prognostic value of PNI remains independent of disease severity. The attenuation of continuous PNI in the fully adjusted model likely reflects mathematical coupling with its constituent parameters (albumin and lymphocyte count), further supporting the clinical utility of quartile-based threshold analysis. Finally, despite extensive multivariable adjustment, residual confounding cannot be entirely ruled out. Moreover, because acute inflammation, fluid resuscitation, and capillary leakage can rapidly reduce serum albumin independent of chronic nutritional state, the use of the worst (nadir) albumin value may not purely reflect baseline nutritional status, potentially overestimating acute nutritional risk in critically ill patients.

This study provides novel insights into risk stratification and potential clinical interventions for patients with SAD. The PNI is straightforward to calculate and cost-effective, making it highly feasible for routine monitoring in intensive care unit settings and demonstrating strong clinical applicability. Based on our RCS analysis, a PNI threshold of 35.7645 appears to be a reasonable cutoff for initiating nutritional support interventions. For SAD patients with PNI below this level, early enteral nutrition, immunomodulatory agents, and anti-inflammatory strategies should be prioritized. Evidence indicates that early total enteral nutrition significantly improves prognosis in sepsis patients [32], and our findings suggest that such interventions may disrupt the vicious cycle between systemic inflammation and malnutrition by effectively elevating PNI levels, ultimately enhancing clinical outcomes.

Limitation

Despite comprehensive confounder adjustment, several limitations exist. First, as a single-center retrospective study, these findings require multicenter prospective validation. Second, MIMIC-IV lacks longitudinal PNI measurements, precluding assessment of temporal trends and extensive exclusion for missing albumin or lymphocyte data. Third, the absence of detailed delirium subtypes or validated severity scores limited evaluation of differential PNI effects across distinct phenotypes. Fourth, although CAM-ICU is validated, retrospective documentation may misclassify delirium—particularly in deeply sedated or paralyzed patients—and introduce non-differential misclassification that attenuates observed associations. However, CAM-ICU high specificity (98%) minimizes false positives, and excluding unassessable patients and pre-sepsis delirium aligns with current MIMIC-IV standards. Future prospective studies should incorporate serial RASS-guided CAM-ICU assessments and sedation interruption protocols to reduce ascertainment bias.

Conclusion

In summary, PNI, as a comprehensive indicator integrating nutritional and immune status, demonstrates strong independent prognostic value in SAD patients. Its “L”-shaped dose-response relationship provides a clear target for clinical decision-making. Future research should focus on whether PNI-guided nutritional interventions can improve neurological outcomes in SAD patients and explore the development of more precise multidimensional prognostic models by integrating other inflammatory biomarkers.
