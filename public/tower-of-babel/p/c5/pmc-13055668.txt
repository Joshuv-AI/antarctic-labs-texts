Different Characteristics of Incidental Diffusion‐Weighted Imaging‐Positive Lesions and Recent Small Subcortical Infarct in Patients With Cerebral Small Vessel Disease

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe mechanisms underlying covert versus symptomatic acute infarcts in cerebral small vessel disease are unclear.MethodsBased on the multicenter Cerebral Small Vessel Disease Disability and Outcome Cohort Study, we consecutively enrolled patients presenting with an acute lacunar stroke within 30 days or patients without acute stroke symptoms but with moderate to severe white matter hyperintensities. Between August 2016 and June 2019, 1710 patients (mean age 61.87±11.28 years, 37.25% female) were recruited across 30 hospitals in China. We compared the clinical and imaging characteristics between individuals with incidental diffusion‐weighted imaging (DWI)‐positive lesions and those with recent small subcortical infarcts (RSSI).ResultsThe prevalence of incidental DWI‐positive lesions and RSSI was 2.28% and 31.64%. Patients with incidental DWI‐positive lesions were older, had lower blood pressure and lipid levels, and more prior lacunar stroke. Incidental DWI‐positive lesions were typically smaller and round/ovoid, more often involved the subcortical white matter and cerebral cortex. RSSI more frequently affected perforating artery territories, 39.9% of RSSI cases were consistent with branch atheromatous disease. Incidental DWI‐positive lesions patients had a higher risk of 90‐day recurrent stroke (hazard ratio [HR], 8.28 [95% CI, 1.96–34.90], log‐rank P<0.001). They also had lower Mini‐Mental State Examination scores after adjustment for age and sex.ConclusionsAtherosclerosis and branch atheromatous disease appear to be predominant causes of RSSI among Chinese patients with cerebral small vessel disease. Incidental DWI‐positive lesions likely reflecting intrinsic small vessel injury constitute a cerebral small vessel disease marker and carry prognostic value for stroke recurrence and cognitive decline.

Cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) accounts for 20% to 30% of ischemic strokes and contributes substantially to physical disability and cognitive impairment. 1 Recent small subcortical infarct (RSSI), a core imaging marker defined by the Standards for Reporting Vascular Changes on Neuroimaging (STRIVE), represents an acute infarction within the territory of a single perforating artery. 2 On the other hand, diffusion‐weighted image (DWI) positive lesions in CSVD are increasingly recognized to occur without overt clinical symptoms. 3 , 4 , 5 The updated STRIVE2 criteria emphasize that RSSI should be clearly linked to corresponding clinical manifestations, while acknowledging incidental DWI‐positive lesion as an emerging CSVD marker. 6

It remains unclear why some acute infarctions in CSVD lead to symptomatic stroke, often prompting earlier diagnosis, whereas others accumulate covertly. Due to the limited understanding of the nature and clinical significance of these covert lesions, incidental DWI‐positive lesions are not yet considered as a core feature of CSVD. 6 Both RSSI and incidental DWI‐positive lesions may evolve into white matter hyperintensity (WMH), lacunes, cortical cerebral microinfarct, or they may resolve, thereby contributing variably to CSVD progression. 3 , 4 , 5 This wide spectrum of evolutions underscores CSVD heterogeneity and highlights the need to elucidate the distinct pathophysiological mechanisms underlying these lesion types.

We aimed to compare the clinical and imaging characteristics of RSSI and incidental DWI‐positive lesions, analyze their associations with chronic CSVD markers, and evaluate their predictive value for 90‐day stroke recurrence. Additionally, we sought to identify their most common anatomical locations and explore potential mechanistic differences, which may inform targeted therapies.

METHODS

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Population

We analyzed participants from the CSVD DOC (Disability and Outcome Cohort Study), a multicenter cohort with CSVD in China. To reflect the spectrum of patients seen by neurologists in routine clinical practice, we prospectively and consecutively enrolled 2 groups of patients across multiple hospitals according to the following criteria: (1) patients with acute lacunar stroke within 30 days, presenting clinical features consistent with a lacunar syndrome and no cortical symptom, and with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) confirmed acute lesions <20 mm in maximum axial diameter within the territory of 1 single perforating artery. Patients with >50% stenosis of the ipsilateral internal carotid artery, middle cerebral artery, or other major arteries supplying the infarct territory, or with cardioembolic stroke were excluded; and (2) patients without acute lacunar symptoms but with WMH of presumed vascular origin, having Fazekas score ≥2 on MRI (2 out of 6 points, range 0–3 for periventricular WMH and 0–3 for deep WMH). Exclusion criteria were age <18 years; severe comorbidities or life expectancy <2 years; psychiatric conditions precluding assessment; and contraindications to MRI. From August 2016 to June 2019, a total of 1879 patients were enrolled from 30 hospitals, among whom 541 had acute lacunar stroke and 1338 met the WMH criterion. Patients in the WMH group sought medical attention for reasons including cognitive impairment, gait disturbance, or nonspecific symptoms such as dizziness and headache; in some, WMH were incidental findings during routine checkups. For the present analysis, 169 individuals lacking DWI and apparent diffusion coefficient scans were excluded, yielding a final sample of 1710 participants. Figure S1 shows the flow chart of study population. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study adhered to the ethical principles of the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethical Committee of Peking Union Medical College Hospital (JS‐1280).

Brain MRI Acquisition

MRI was performed using 3‐T or 1.5‐T scanner (Siemens, Germany; Philips, Netherland; General Electric, USA; Toshiba, Japan; United Imaging Healthcare, China). The sequences included T1‐weighted, T2‐weighted, fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery, DWI, apparent diffusion coefficient, susceptibility‐weighted or T2*‐weighted gradient recalled echo images and time‐of‐flight MR angiography. Detailed parameters of different MRI machines are listed in Table S1.

Clinical Evaluations

Cognition was assessed via baseline scores of Mini‐Mental State Examination and Montreal Cognitive Assessment scale (MoCA). All participants were followed up 90 days after baseline via outpatient visits or telephone interviews to ascertain incident stroke. Recurrent stroke was determined using established diagnostic criteria and confirmed by stroke clinics or study team consensus.

Evaluations of DWI‐Positive Lesions

All CSVD imaging markers were defined according to STRIVE2 criteria. 6 In brief, DWI‐positive lesions were required to be as hyperintense on DWI with corresponding hypo‐ or isointense on apparent diffusion coefficient. RSSI was defined as a recent infarct within the territory of a single perforating artery, with imaging features and clinical symptoms consistent with an event in the preceding few weeks. Incidental DWI‐positive lesions were small lesions meeting the DWI/apparent diffusion coefficient requirements, but lacking a corresponding acute focal neurological deficit; they could be asymptomatic or accompanied atypical neurobehavioral symptoms or subtle, gradual neurological decline. 6 Following L. R. Caplan's description, each DWI‐positive lesion was evaluated for branch atheromatous disease (BAD) features, 7 defined by (1) a single, deep (subcortical) infarct; (2) supplied by a lenticulostriate artery (LSA) or paramedian pontine artery (PPA), with characteristic DWI patterns: (A) LSA: comma‐like lesion with fan‐shaped superior extension on coronal plane or involvement of ≥3 continuous axial slices (slice thickness 3–6.5 mm); (B) PPA: a lesion extending from the deep pons to the ventral pons on axial images; and (3) absence of >50% stenosis in the parent artery of the culprit perforator. 8 All DWI‐positive lesions were assessed and marked by a neurologist with 5 years' experience and cross‐checked with clinical information to classify each as RSSI or an incidental DWI‐positive lesion (Z.P.). Ambiguous cases were resolved by consensus with 2 senior neurologists (F.H. and Y.Z.).

DWI‐positive lesions were manually labeled on DWI using MRIcroGL (https://www.nitrc.org/projects/mricrogl/). To visualize spatial distributions, each lesion underwent bias‐field correction, was coregistered to the participant's T1 weighted image, normalized to the standard Montreal Neurologic Institute 152 space, and then aggregated to generate lesion probability maps stratified by lesion type (incidental DWI‐positive lesions versus RSSI) using Statistical Parametric Mapping 12 (SPM 12, fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/).

Evaluations of Other CSVD Markers

Lacune was defined as round or ovoid, subcortical, fluid‐filled cavity with cerebrospinal fluid‐like signal, with a diameter ≤15 mm, located in the basal ganglia, subcortical white matter, brainstem, or cerebellum. WMH were graded using Fazekas scores (0–3 for periventricular WMH and 0–3 for deep WMH, total 6 scores). Cerebral microbleeds (CMBs) were defined as small areas of signal void with blooming on T2*‐weighted gradient‐echo or susceptibility weighted imaging. Trained neurologists who were blinded to all clinical data independently evaluated lacunes (Z.P.), WMH (T.B.) and CMBs (Y.W.). Intrarater agreement was assessed in a randomly selected sample of 50 individuals, with an interval of >1 month between the first and second readings. Interrater verification was performed by 3 additional neurologists (C.L. for lacunes, S.T. for WMH, M.S. for CMBs). The kappa coefficients were (1) lacunes: intrarater agreement 0.82, interrater agreement 0.71; (2) WMH: intrarater agreement 0.91, interrater agreement 0.82; (3) CMBs: intrarater agreement 1.00, interrater agreement 0.84.

Evaluations of Intracranial Large Artery

Large arterial stenosis was assessed via MR angiography at the site with the most severe degree of stenosis by a 5‐year experienced neurologist (M.S.). Intracranial artery stenosis (ICAS) was defined as any degree of stenosis in at least 1 of the following intracranial arteries: bilateral internal carotid arteries, middle cerebral arteries, anterior cerebral arteries, intracranial segment of the vertebral arteries, posterior cerebral arteries, and basilar artery. 9 Intrarater agreement was assessed as same as mentioned. Interrater verification was performed by another 5‐year experienced neurologist (Y.W.). Intrarater agreement was 0.78, interrater agreement was 0.91.

Assessments of Covariates

Baseline demographics and vascular risk factors were estimated using structured interviews, physical examinations, and laboratory testing. Hypertension was defined as self‐reported hypertension, systolic blood pressure ≥140 mm Hg, or diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mm Hg. Diabetes was defined as self‐reported diabetes, fasting plasma glucose ≥7.0 mmol/L, or hemoglobin A1c≥6.5%. Hyperlipemia was defined as self‐reported hyperlipemia, total cholesterol ≥5.20 mmol/L, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol ≥3.4 mmol/L, or triglyceride ≥1.70 mmol/L. Coronary heart disease and atrial fibrillation were diagnosed by experienced cardiologist based on self‐reported history, clinical manifestation, medication use, prior investigations, and baseline ECG findings.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are summarized as mean±SD or median (interquartile range), and categorical variables as number (percentage). We compared the characteristics of participants with RSSI and incidental DWI‐positive lesions using independent‐samples t test, Mann–Whitney U test, chi‐square test, or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. All analyses and comparisons were conducted at the per‐person level. We used multivariable logistic regression to compare the risk of coexisting MRI markers between incidental DWI‐positive lesions and RSSI, and multivariable linear regression to compare the differences in cognitive scores. For models examining MRI markers, outcomes included: lacunes present, lacunes ≥5 lacunes, CMBs present, strictly lobar CMB, any deep or infratentorial CMB, and WMH Fazekas score ≥4.

Model 1 adjusted for age and sex; Model 2 additionally adjusted for vascular risk factors (body mass index, hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipemia, coronary heart disease, atrial fibrillation, current smoking, and prior lacunar stroke). For models assessing cognitive outcomes, Model 1 adjusted for age and sex. Model 2 additionally adjusted for years of education and vascular risk factors. Model 3 further adjusted for lacunes burden (≥5 or <5), and WMH Fazekas sum score ≥4. Kaplan–Meier curves with the log‐rank test and Cox regression were used to compare stroke‐free survival between groups during the follow‐up period. Statistical significance was set at a 2‐sided P<0.05. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 19.0 (IBM Co., USA).

Thirty‐nine participants (2.28%) had incidental DWI‐positive lesions.

Clinical Differences Between Incidental DWI‐Positive Lesions and RSSI

As shown in Table 2, compared with the group with RSSI, patients with incidental DWI‐positive lesions were older (mean age 63.31±9.63 versus 59.34±10.08 years, P=0.018) and had lower systolic blood pressure (134.80±17.27 versus 143.54±19.35 mm Hg, P=0.016) and DBP (78.97±11.19 versus 87.59±11.86 mm Hg, P<0.001). They also exhibited lower levels of triglyceride (1.23 [0.94–1.43] versus 1.45 [1.09–2.07] mmol/L, P=0.021), total cholesterol (3.92 [3.37–4.80] versus 4.38 [3.76–5.08] mmol/L, P=0.024), and LDL‐cholesterol (2.21 [1.72–2.72] versus 2.56 [2.07–3.04] mmol/L, P=0.012). A prior history of lacunar stroke was more common in the incidental DWI‐positive group than in the group with RSSI (43.59% versus 25.88%, P=0.016).

MRI Differences Between Incidental DWI‐Positive Lesions and RSSI

MRI features of incidental DWI‐positive lesions and RSSI were summarized in Table 2. First, incidental DWI‐positive lesions were substantially smaller than RSSI (4.09 [3.37–5.62] versus 9.24 [5.80–12.60] mm, P<0.001). Second, the shape of incidental DWI‐positive lesions was typically round or ovoid, whereas RSSI were more likely to appear irregular and patchy (Figure 1). Third, incidental DWI‐positive lesions more frequently involved the cortex (5/39 [12.82%] versus 0, P<0.001) and subcortical white matter (28/39 [71.79%] versus 284/541 [52.50%], P=0.034) and were not observed in the brainstem (0 versus 89/541 [16.45%], P=0.005). In contrast, RSSI were predominantly located along primary motor and sensory tracts, particularly within territories supplied by the LSA, followed by the PPA (Table 2, Figure 2).

Associations With MRI Markers

Compared with RSSI, incidental DWI‐positive lesions were more frequently associated with chronic CSVD markers, including lacunes (34 [87.18%] versus 344 [63.59%], P=0.003), CMBs (28 [80.00%] versus 171 [58.76%], P=0.015), and a WMH Fazekas score ≥4 (36 [92.31%] versus 332 [61.37%], P<0.001). No significant difference was observed between 2 groups in the prevalence of mild ICAS (≤ 50%), nor in the prevalence of stenosis anatomically corresponding to the lesion‐supplying artery. (Table 3). After adjusting for age and sex, incidental DWI‐positive lesions remained significantly associated with a higher risk of lacunes; these associations persisted in fully adjusted models. Specifically, incidental DWI‐positive lesions were more likely to coexist with lacunes in the basal ganglia (odds ratio [OR], 2.29 [95% CI, 1.06–4.97], P=0.037), subcortical white matter (OR, 4.72 [95% CI, 1.83–12.21], P<0.001), and cerebellum (OR, 5.63 [95% CI, 1.85–17.17], P=0.002). They were also associated with a greater likelihood of having ≥5 lacunes (OR, 5.07 [95% CI, 1.48–17.35], P<0.001) and a WMH Fazekas score ≥4 (OR, 6.03 [95% CI, 1.36–26.66], P=0.018) (Table 3). No significant differences were found regarding CMBs prevalence in multivariable analyses.

Comparison of Cognitive Scores Between Incidental DWI‐Positive Lesions and RSSI

Patients with incidental DWI‐positive lesions had lower Mini‐Mental State Examination scores than those with RSSI (26 [23–29] versus 29 [26–30], P=0.004). After adjusting for age and sex, incidental DWI‐positive lesions remained independently associated with lower Mini‐Mental State Examination scores (β±SE: −0.094±0.683, P=0.025) (Model 1). However, the association was no longer statistically significant after additional adjustment for educational years and vascular risk factors (Model 2), nor after further adjustment for the presence of ≥5 lacunes, and WMH Fazekas score ≥4 (Model 3). No significant difference was observed in Montreal Cognitive Assessment scores between 2 groups (Table S2).

Comparison of 90‐Day Recurrent Stroke Risk Between Incidental DWI‐Positive Lesions and RSSI

Over a median follow‐up of 3.00 months (P25–P75: 3.00–3.02), no case missed. Recurrent symptomatic stroke occurred in 3 patients with incidental DWI‐positive lesions and in 5 patients with RSSI. Kaplan–Meier curves demonstrated significantly lower stroke‐free survival in the incidental DWI‐positive group, corresponding to a hazard ratio (HR) of 8.28 (95% CI, 1.96–34.90, log‐rank P<0.001) (Figure 3).

DISCUSSION

In this multicenter cohort with CSVD study, we found that (1) incidental DWI‐positive lesions were substantially smaller, typically round/ovoid, and more often located in the subcortical white matter and cerebral cortex, whereas RSSI predominantly involved perforator territories, especially those supplied by the LSA and PPA; and (2) compared with RSSI, incidental DWI‐positive lesions were associated with a heavier lacune burden, higher WMH grades, a higher risk of 90‐day recurrent stroke, and lower Mini‐Mental State Examination scores.

The proposal to address incidental DWI‐positive lesions in STRIVE2 was motivated by uncertainty regarding their prevalence and mechanism. 6 In our cohort, the overall prevalence was 2.3%, lower than the 4.8% reported in the European cohort with CSVD from the RUN DMC (Radboud University Nijmegen Diffusion Tensor and Magnetic Resonance Imaging Cohort) study (N=503, mean age 65.6 years). 4 Prior studies indicate that the proportion of incidental DWI‐positive lesions rises with increasing CSVD severity. 10 Despite a greater CSVD burden in our sample than in RUN DMC, incidental DWI‐positive lesions were less frequent. The detection frequency may depend on MRI timing and repetition. The RUN DMC study organized 3 follow‐ups so each participant underwent multiple DWI scans, which could substantially influence the observed prevalence. In addition, the discrepancy may reflect differences in the symptom composition of the patients, nearly one third of our participants were inpatients enrolled for acute lacunar stroke. Moreover, the pathophysiology of these acute infarctions may differ between Chinese and Western populations. To better characterize lesion evolution process and determine its prognostic implications, regular follow‐up with DWI sequences during asymptomatic periods is necessary.

To elucidate potential mechanistic differences, we compared clinical and imaging characteristics between the 2 groups. Patients with RSSI exhibited higher blood pressure and lipid levels than those with incidental DWI‐positive lesions, aligning the RSSI risk factor profile with atherosclerosis. RSSI frequently occurred in the basal ganglia and pons and displayed a larger median diameter of 9.24 mm (5.80–12.60), features consistent with BAD, which may be common in Asian populations. Although STRIVE2 classifies most RSSI (85%) as manifestations of intrinsic small vessel disease, 6 our findings suggest that RSSI are not solely attributable to lipohyalinosis of small arterioles. Rather, they may arise from occlusion of perforator origins by parental‐artery atherosclerotic plaque, or from arteriosclerotic disease affecting proximal perforating arteries. 11 High‐resolution vessel wall MRI supports these mechanisms even in cases where small plaques are not visible on conventional MR angiography. 12 , 13 Another Chinese study demonstrated that superiorly distributed middle cerebral artery plaques at the LSA origin are closely associated with LSA morphological changes in symptomatic middle cerebral arteries, 14 providing pathogenetic insight into BAD in RSSI. 15 A recent multicenter study in China further confirmed the feasibility of identifying BAD‐related stroke using conventional MRI. 8 Although conventional imaging rarely allows for the direct visualization of perforating arteries, it is somewhat reliable to infer atherosclerotic infarction of perforating arteries based on the location, size, and shape of the infarct. In essence, these observations suggest that the pathogenetic spectrum of RSSI in Asian populations may differ from that in Western people, but a direct comparison between Asian and Western populations is needed to fully clarify this issue.

Compared with patients with RSSI, those with incidental DWI‐positive lesions exhibited a greater CSVD burden, poorer global cognitive performance, and a higher 90‐day risk of recurrent stroke. Collectively, these results suggest that incidental DWI‐positive lesions may indicate diffuse, active small vessel damage. Our findings are consistent with the results of previous studies, which similarly reported a higher prevalence of incidental DWI‐positive lesions among individuals with cognitive impairment than among cognitively normal individuals. 16 The Rotterdam study linked such lesions to worse performance on a reading subtask. 3 In terms of recurrence risk, these asymptomatic lesions are more likely to represent the intrinsic SVD. CSVD burden may explain a larger proportion of stroke‐risk variance than large artery stenosis. 17 Furthermore, when RSSI is attributable to atherosclerosis (eg, BAD), antiplatelet and lipid‐lowering therapies may confer greater benefit for secondary prevention, particularly in reducing early recurrence, compared with lesions driven by intrinsic small vessel pathology. Additionally, most incidental DWI‐positive lesions were located in the subcortical white matter, a common site for lacunes, supporting previous evidence that covert infarctions might be important source of lacunes. 4 , 5 A subset also involved the cerebral cortex, consistent with a potential embolic mechanism (eg, artery to artery), raising concern for coexisting large artery pathologies alongside CSVD. Given that incidental DWI‐positive lesions are subclinical and DWI hyperintensity is transient (typically visible for only 2–4 weeks), detecting just a few on opportunistic MRI likely underestimates their cumulative occurrence over time. 18 These easily overlooked lesions warrant heightened vigilance and targeted secondary prevention, as they reflect the active cerebrovascular injury and are associated with adverse prognoses.

Based on the first multicenter cohort with CSVD in China, we provide robust evidence highlighting differences in acute CSVD markers between Asian and Western populations and underscore the need for refined classification of incidental DWI‐positive lesions in the future. However, our study has some limitations. First, the imaging assessments were cross‐sectional and the prevalence of incidental DWI‐positive lesions is relatively low, which may limit statistical power; larger and longitudinal MRI are needed. Second, MRI field strengths varied across centers, and use of 1.5 T scanners at some sites is less sensitive for detecting small DWI‐positive lesions. Third, the assessments relied primarily on conventional imaging, preventing direct verification of small parent‐artery atherosclerotic plaques at perforator origins.

CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, the pathogenetic profile of RSSI in Chinese patients with CSVD appears to differ from that reported in Western populations. RSSI are more closely associated with atherosclerosis, with BAD a common mechanism. In contrast, incidental DWI‐positive lesions likely reflect intrinsic, diffuse small vessels injury, and are associated with higher short‐term stroke recurrence and worse cognitive performance. These covert infarctions warrant recognition as a core CSVD marker in future research and clinical practice.
