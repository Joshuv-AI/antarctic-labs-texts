Peroxynitrite regulates ER stress-mediated Ca2+ flux to mitochondria characterizing cardiac microvascular ischemia–reperfusion injury associated with hyperhomocysteinemia
Authors: Haipeng Liu, Siyang Yu, Shansong Gao, Xiaoming Liu, Chengpeng Qiu, Ning Wang, Xinyu Tan, Kaiyang Zhao, Qiuyu He, Wan Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHomocysteine (Hcy) is not only associated with the development of chronic cardiovascular diseases like atherosclerosis, but may also participate in the acute cardiovascular events. However, the exact mechanism of the latter remains elusive. The present study aims to further investigate the mechanism of cardiac microvascular endothelial cells (CMECs) death after I/R induction in the presence of Hcy and explore new therapeutic strategies.MethodsBy generating the hypoxia/reoxygenation (H/R) human cardiac microvascular endothelial cell (HCMEC) model and the I/R models in rats with hyperhomocysteinemia (HHcy), the mechanisms of endothelial cell injury associated with HHcy were investigated.ResultsWe demonstrated that ONOO−, generated by the combination of Hcy and Cu2+ during I/R, induces ER stress and the subsequent ER-mitochondria Ca2+ transfer via IP3R-mediated Ca2+ release in CMECs. The cytosolic/mitochondrial Ca2+ oscillations and mitochondrial Ca2+ overload promote mROS generation, provoke LMP, and ultimately drive CMEC necroptosis. Our study further demonstrates the IP3R inhibitor 2-APB (5 mg/kg) significantly reduced infarct size by 29.14%, and improved cardiac function in HHcy rats (HHcyR), as evidenced by increased LVEF (35.71% → 55.32%), elevated LVFS (31.44% → 48.54%), and reduced LVEDd (6.98 mm → 5.80 mm).ConclusionsAltogether, our results reveal the pathological role of Hcy in acute cardiovascular events. We show that HHcy aggravates cardiac microvascular I/R injury via ONOO−-driven ER stress that triggers IP3R-mediated Ca2+ mis-handling, culminating in mitochondrial dysfunction and necroptosis. These data identify IP3R-dependent Ca2+ transfer as a tractable pathway for HHcy-complicated reperfusion injury.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07263-y.

Introduction

Elevated plasma homocysteine (Hcy) is a recognized biomarker and risk factor for cardiovascular diseases [1, 2]. While hyperhomocysteinemia (HHcy) contributes to chronic vascular pathology such as atherosclerosis and hypertension [2], it’s mechanistic role in acute cardiovascular events, including cardiac ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI), atherosclerotic plaque rupture dynamics et al., remain controversial.

IRI remains a major obstacle to successful myocardial infarction (MI) therapy, as most pharmacological strategies that show promise preclinically fail to translate clinically [3–6]. Beyond cardiomyocyte injury, post-reperfusion microvascular dysfunction is now acknowledged as a key determinant of recovery, with prognostic value independent of epicardial patency [7, 8]. Defining microcirculatory mechanisms, especially in patients with comorbid risks such as HHcy, is therefore essential for developing effective therapies.

Cardiac microvascular endothelial cells (CMECs) are highly susceptible to IRI [9, 10]. Endothelial nitric oxide (NO) bioavailability, vital for vascular homeostasis, is impaired by oxidative stress [11, 12]. We recently reported that reperfusion mobilizes copper (Cu2+), which synergizes with Hcy to increase peroxynitrite (ONOO⁻), promote CMEC necroptosis, and worsen cardiac dysfunction [13]. We further proved that antioxidant nanozymes attenuates this injury in HHcyR [14], yet the proximal link from ONOO⁻ to endothelial necroptosis remained unclear.

Calcium (Ca2+) signaling coordinates cell survival and death [15]. Cellular Ca2+ primarily enters cells through transmembrane channels [16]. The endoplasmic reticulum (ER) is the major intracellular Ca2+ store, releasing Ca2+ primarily through inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate receptors (IP3R) and, to a lesser extent, ryanodine receptors (RyR) [17, 18]. Mitochondria take up Ca2+ mainly via the mitochondrial Ca2+ uniporter (MCU) [17]. Physiological mitochondrial Ca2+ supports oxidative phosphorylation, whereas overload is detrimental to mitochondria function, and finally induces cell death [19]. Although cardiomyocyte Ca2+ dysregulation in IRI is well studied, Ca2+ homeostasis in CMECs during IRI, particularly under HHcy, remains poorly defined.

Mitochondria are both a major source and a primary target of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [20]. During IRI, mitochondrial ROS can arise from electron transport chain (ETC) damage induced by exogenous ROS [21, 22] or from mitochondrial Ca²⁺ overload [17]. Importantly, both Ca²⁺ and ROS are key activators of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP), whose opening leads to irreversible cell and tissue injury during IRI [17, 23, 24]. Therefore, elucidating the interplay between mitochondrial Ca²⁺ and ROS, and the mechanisms leading to cell death, is crucial-particularly in the setting of HHcy, which exacerbates cardiac IRI.

Here, we investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying Hcy-exacerbated microvascular IRI to identify potential therapeutic targets. We show that ONOO⁻-induced ER stress activates IP3R-dependent ER-mitochondria Ca2+ transfer, leading to mitochondria Ca2+ overload, oxidative amplification, lysosomal membrane permeabilization, and CMEC necroptosis. In vivo, inhibiting IP3R signaling limits microvascular injury and improves cardiac function under HHcy conditions. These findings clarify the pathological role of Hcy in acute cardiovascular events and highlight a promising strategy for treating cardiac IRI, particularly in patients with additional risk factors.

Materials and methods

Materials

Homocysteine, copper (II) chloride dehydrate, 2,3,5-Triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC), Evans blue (EB), EGTA, L-NAME, MnTBAP were purchased from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). VAS2870, BAPTA-AM, Xestospongin C, DS16570511 and Nec-1 were obtained from GLPBIO (Montclair, CA, USA). Necrosulfonamide (B7731) was purchased from APExBIO (Houston, USA). Fluo-4, MitoTracker Green, ER Tracker Red, MitoSOX Red, LysoTracker Green, Acridine Orange (AO) and Calcein-AM were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). Rhod-2, AM (40776ES72) was from Yeasen (Shanghai, China). Anti-RIP3 (phospho-S227) (ab209384), Alexa Fluor®594 (ab150116) and Alexa Fluor®488 (ab150077) and Anti-IP3R (ab108517) were from Abcam (Cambridge, MA, UK). Anti-RIP (phospho-S166) (31122), anti-CHOP (2895) and anti-ATF4 (97038) were from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA). Anti-VE-cadherin (sc-9989) was obtained from Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Inc. (Santa Cruz, CA, USA). Anti-albumin (16475-1-AP), anti-MCU (26312-1-AP) and anti-CD31 (11265-1-A P) were from Proteintech (Wuhan, China). Anti-eIF2α (phospho-Ser51) (YA203), 2-Aminoethyl diphenylborinate (2-APB), ML204, mitoTEMPO, ER000444793 and Cyclosporin A (CsA) were from MCE (New Jersey, USA). DAPI was from Southern Biotech (Birmingham, AL 35260 USA). G-CEPIA1er (58215) was from Addgene. The ONOO− assay kit (BB-460652) was obtained from BestBio (Shanghai, China).

Cell culture and hypoxia/reoxygenation (H/R) induction in vitro

Human cardiac microvascular endothelial cells (HCMECs) were cultured in endothelial cell culture medium (ECM) with 5% fetal bovine serum, 1% endothelial growth factor, and penicillin (100 U/mL) + streptomycin (100 µg/mL). For the H/R preconditioning, the ECM was replaced with DMEM without serum, glucose, glutamine and pyruvate, and Hcy, CuCl2 and other inhibitors or drugs required for the experiment were added immediately. The cells were rapidly placed in the hypoxia chamber contained 95% N2 and 5% CO2 and incubated for 2 h. After the completion of hypoxia, a new complete medium of ECM was added. Then, Hcy, CuCl2 and other inhibitors or drugs were added according to the experimental requirements, and then incubated for 24 h under normal culture conditions to induce reoxygenation injury.

Cytotoxicity assay

To determine the cytotoxicity and cell viability under different treatment conditions, we used the MTT assay kit to detect cell viability. Briefly, the same number of HCMECs were cultured in 96-well plates and different inhibitor treatments were given 1 h prior to H/R treatment. Then the cells were treated with 150 µM Hcy and 5 µM CuCl2 under H/R condition for 24 h. In addition, an in situ Calcein AM/PI assay was performed to assess the efficiency of the different treatments. HCMECs were stained with 0.5 µM Calcein AM and 10 µg/mL propidium iodide (PI) and observed with a Leica TCSSPE confocal microscope. In situ survival/death status was observed, and cell fluorescence images were captured randomly. All assays were performed at least 3 times.

Detection of ROS levels, Ca2+ levels, ONOO− levels, and mitochondrial transmembrane potential

The fluorescent dye Fluo-4/AM, Rhod-2/AM, MitoSOX Red, ONOO− probes, and TMRM were used to measure the cytoplasmic Ca2+, mitochondrial Ca2+, mitochondrial ROS levels, intracellular ONOO− levels, mitochondrial oxidative damage and mitochondrial transmembrane potential, respectively. Briefly, the HCMECs cultured under various experimental conditions were incubated with Fluo-4/AM (2 µM), Rhod-2/AM (2 µM), MitoSOX Red (2 µM), ONOO− probe (2 µM) and TMRM (20 nM) at 37℃ for 45 min in the dark. After washing twice with PBS, the samples were analyzed by flow cytometry, and the results were analyzed by the instrument’s built-in software.

3-Nitrotyrosine detection

3-Nitrotyrosine (3-NT) was determined using the 3-Nitrotyrosine ELISA Kit (Abcam, USA, ab116691) according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 800 000 /well and incubated routinely for 12 h. The cells were treated as indicated under H/R conditions. Cells were harvested after 12 h of reoxygenation, lysed on ice for 20 min and centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 20 min at 4 ℃. The supernatant was transferred to a clean tube for detection. The specific operation method was carried out according to the instructions.

Western blot

After the whole-cell lysate denaturation with SDS-PAGE loading buffer for 10 min at 100 ℃, proteins were loaded on 10% or 12% SDS-PAGE gels and then the separated protein bands were transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. The protein blots were blocked with 5% nonfat milk for 1 h at room temperature, and then incubated with the following primary antibodies: p-RIP (phospho-S166), p-RIP3 (phospho-S227), MCU, IP3R, ATF4, CHOP, and p-eIF2α (phospho-S51), all at 1:1000 dilution, and β-actin at 1:10,000 dilution. After incubation overnight at 4℃, the membranes were washed three times for 10 min in TBS containing 0.1% Tween-20 and then incubated for 1 h at room temperature in the corresponding secondary antibodies. β-actin detection at 1:10,000 dilution, all the others detection at 1:3000 dilution. The blots were washed three times with the same TBST buffer and analyzed with an enhanced chemiluminescence detection kit (GE Healthcare Life Sciences, Chalfont, UK), followed by imaging with the gel imaging system (Gel Doc XR+, BIORAD).

SiRNA transfection

siRNA targeting MCU, IP3R, eNOS, or non-targeting sequence control siRNA (NC) was transfected to HCMECs with Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The transfected HCMECs were washed with PBS, and then incubated in new culture media for additional time for Hcy and CuCl2 treatment, followed by western blot and cell viability analysis as indicated in the figure legends.

SgRNA transfection

HCMECs were plated in 6-well plates, and cells were transfected with sgRNA-containing lentivirus and Polybrene reagent (Beyotime) for 6 h. After 3 days of screening with puromycin, the effect of knockout was verified using western blot.

mPTP

Briefly, HCMECs subjected to various treatments were stained with 20 nM Calcein, AM at 37℃ for 45 min. The samples were divided into two and the FITC fluorescence intensity of the first sample was detected using flow cytometry or laser confocal microscopy. The second sample was bleached with 1 mM CoCl2 for 15 min at 37℃. The FITC fluorescence intensity of the second sample was detected using the same assay as the first sample. In the absence of CoCl2, fluorescent calcein is present in the cytosol as well as the mitochondria, resulting in a bright signal. In the presence of CoCl2, calcein in the mitochondria emits a signal, but the cytosolic calcein fluorescence is quenched; the overall fluorescence is reduced compared to calcein alone. When mPTP was activated, the fluorescence signals from both the cytosol and mitochondria are largely abolished.

Change of LMP

LysoTracker Green and acridine orange (AO) was used to assess lysosomal membrane stability. In the experiment, HCMECs seeded in a 12-well culture plate were cultured under various experimental conditions, and then incubated with AO working solution (2 µg/ml) (Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA)) or LysoTracker Green (2 µM) (Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA)), and then incubated at 37 ℃ for 30 min. After washing twice with PBS, the fluorescence intensity of AO staining was observed under a laser confocal microscopy and images were captured. The samples stained with LysoTracker Green were analyzed by flow cytometry, and the results were analyzed by the instrument’s built-in software.

Animals and treatment

4-week-old male Sprague-Dawley rats (100 ± 10 g) were purchased from Beijing Sibiu Biotechnology Co., LTD. After passing quarantine, rats were housed in the Animal Laboratory of the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University. All animal experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Nanchang University (CDYFY-IACUC-202212QR002) on December 6th, 2022, and the care and use of experimental animals adhered to the guidelines. The hyperhomocysteinemia rat (HHcyR) model was established according to the methods described previously [13, 14]. Briefly, Hcy was dissolved and buffered to pH 7.4 by 0.9% sodium chloride. From day 1 to day 21, 4-week-old male SD rats (100 ± 10 g) were intraperitoneally injected with Hcy twice daily. The rats were given 0.3 µmol Hcy/g body weight in the first week, 0.4 and 0.6 µmol Hcy/g body weight in the second and third weeks. Plasma Hcy levels in rats receiving this treatment were similar to those in HHcy patients (Figure S1). HHcyR with similar weights were equally randomized to the following groups with five or six rats per group: sham-operated plus saline treatment, sham-operated plus 2-APB treatment (5 mg/ kg, i.v.), cardiac I/R plus saline treatment, and cardiac I/R plus 2-APB treatment (2.5-5 mg/ kg, i.v.). For IP3R small-interfering RNA (siRNA) treatment, 100 µg of siRNA (or a control siRNA) from Genepharma was administered by intravenous injection on day 4 and day 8 of Hcy injection and 50 µg on day 12. The siRNA was complexed with a polymer from TransIT in vivo gene delivery system and delivered according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Three rats in each group were used to verify the knockdown effect, and immunofluorescence assay was used to detect the knockdown effect. The remaining rats were used for subsequent experiments. The si-NC and si-IP3R HHcyR were randomized to the following groups with five rats per group: si-NC sham-operated, si-NC cardiac I/R, si-IP3R sham-operated, si-IP3R cardiac I/R. The HHcyRs were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg). The heart was visualized subsequent to a thoracotomy through the left anterior chest. The left anterior descending coronary artery was ligated with 8 − 0 nylon thread, and the same position was ligated each time. And a slipknot was tied with 30 min of ischemia followed by 20 h reperfusion. The knot was released at the end of the ischemic time to allow reperfusion of the blood in the heart. The rats were resuscitated and fed for another 20 h. Except for coronary artery ligation, other surgical procedures were similar between the sham-operated group and the operation group. All procedures were performed by the same person. 2-APB was resuspended in DMSO and further diluted by saline at a final concentration of 10 mg/ml. Different concentrations of 2-APB were administered by tail vein injection 10 min before ischemic. The control groups received i.v. injection of an equivalent volume of saline. At the end of the reperfusion period, the hearts were stained with 2% EB and 2% TTC to evaluate the area at risk (AAR) and infarct area (IA). Images of the stained slices were captured using a digital camera, quantified by Image J, and presented as a percentage of the heart area.

Cardiac microvascular imaging by gelatin-ink perfusion

Ink and gelatin were mixed together to make a 3% gelatin-ink mixture. This was then put into the jugular vein and kept at a temperature of between 25 and 30 °C. As the rat’s extremities (limbs) went dark, the large vessels at the base of the heart and the superior and inferior vena cava were occluded. The heart samples were extracted, stored at 4 °C for 1 h, and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde before cry sectioning. Images were taken straight away using a Zeiss Axio Lab A1 microscope.

Histomorphometric analysis

Briefly, rat heart specimens were extracted after treatment with different experimental protocols and saline perfusion. Fresh hearts were laterally divided into sections of approximately 2–5 mm thickness and subsequently fixed in 4% cold paraformaldehyde. After thorough dehydration, the specimens were embedded in paraffin. The specimens were then cut into 5 μm thick sections and stained with hematoxylin-eosin (HE), toluidine blue, and Masson trichrome. Images were acquired by an Axio Lab A1 biomicroscope (Carl Zeiss). Fresh hearts were cut into 2–5 mm thick tissue blocks and embedded with OCT. Tissues were made into 5 μm thick frozen sections for immunofluorescence analysis using a cryosectioner (HM525NX, Thermo Fisher). After washing the sections with buffer to remove OCT, they were permeabilized with 3% Triton X-100 for 30 min at room temperature. After further washing with buffer, the sections were closed with 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C in 5% BSA containing CD31, eNOS, VE-cadherin, p-RIP1 and p-RIP3 (1:200). Subsequently, samples were incubated with Alexa 488- or 594-labeled anti-mouse and rabbit IgG diluted 200-fold with 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. DAPI (0100 − 20, Southern Biotech) was used to stain and block the nuclei. Fluorescence images were taken by a Leica TCS SPE confocal microscope. Immunohistochemical staining for plasma albumin was performed on 8 μm-thick sections of cardiac tissue using a 1:1000 dilution of plasma albumin overnight at 4 °C. The sections were then incubated with goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h. Images were taken with an Axio Lab Model A1 biomicroscope (Carl Zeiss).

Biochemical parameters

Serum concentrations of creatine kinase (CK), creatine kinase MB (CK-MB), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and cardiac troponin T (cTnT) were measured to assess myocardial damage. Blood was collected 2–4 h after the end of the reperfusion period and obtain serum. The assay kits from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, P.R. China, were used to detect the above parameters, according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Cardiac echocardiogram

The rats were anaesthetized and depilated following the administration of the experimental treatment. Transthoracic two-dimensional M-mode echocardiograms were obtained using a Vivid S70N ultrasound system from General USA. The left ventricular diastolic diameter (LVEDd), left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), and left ventricular short axis shortening (LVFS) were measured and recorded in the left ventricular long axis view. All measurements were performed over a minimum of 5 consecutive cardiac cycles to ensure reliable results. All echocardiographic examinations were performed by the same operator.

Statistical analysis

The data are expressed as the mean ± SD unless otherwise indicated. Differences were assessed with independent-samples t tests for comparison of two groups or one-way ANOVA for comparison of more than three groups. Differences were considered significant if P < 0.05. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (La Jolla, CA, USA). All experiments were performed at least three times.

Result

The combination of Hcy and Cu2+ induces Ca2+ dyshomeostasis and mitochondrial damage in HCMECs after IR injury induction

Our recent study has confirmed that Cu2+ was released during cardiac I/R, and have interactive toxic effect with Hcy to enhance cardiac dysfunction [13]. Consistently, in the presence of 5 µM CuCl2, Hcy induced HCMEC death under hypoxia/reoxygenation (H/R) conditions in a concentration-dependent manner, whereas Hcy alone has limited toxicity even at concentration as high as 300 µM (Fig. 1A). Concomitantly, we observed the homeostatic Ca2+ disruption in HCMECs after H/R. As shown in Fig. 1B-C, the cytoplastic Ca2+ levels, as detected by the intracellular calcium indicator Fluo-4/AM ester [25], increased significantly as early as 1 h after reoxygenation, which was followed by a substantial rise in mitochondrial Ca2+ at a later time point, indicating by a rhodamine-based mitochondrial Ca2+ sensitive probe Rhod-2/AM [26, 27]. The mitochondrial Ca2+ influx was further visualized by confocal fluorescent imaging in HCMECs. HCMECs were co-loaded with Rhod-2/AM and the mitochondrial specific probe Mitotracker green FM. As presented in Fig. 1D and Figure S10, under H/R condition, Hcy and Cu2+ induced an increase in Rhod-2/AM fluorescence 3 h after reoxygenation, corresponding to mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake. At 6 h after reoxygenation, we observed severe mitochondria fragmentation in HCMECs, whereas the pretreatment of Ca2+ chelator BAPTA-AM largely preserved the mitochondrial morphology (Fig. 1E, Figure S10). Moreover, the combination of Hcy and Cu2+ induced the loss of mitochondrial transmembrane potential of HCMECs under H/R conditions in a time-dependent manner, and finally led to the cell death at 24 h after reoxygenation (Fig. 1F-G). However, the pretreatment of BAPTA-AM almost completely prevented the loss of mitochondrial transmembrane potential, from 42.80% to 92.25%, and increased the cell viability from 42.38% to 90.3% (Fig. 1F-G). Altogether, these data suggest that mitochondrial Ca2+ dyshomeostasis leads to mitochondrial dysfunction and cell death of HEMCEs after H/R in the presence of Hcy and CuCl2.

ER–mitochondria Ca2+ transfer via IP3R is involved in the increased mitochondrial Ca2+ levels

The elevation of intracellular Ca2+ can either result from the activation of cell surface ion channels like NMDAR [28] or LTCC [27], or from intracellular remobilization [29]. To investigate the source of intracellular Ca2+, HCMECs were pretreated with a variety of calcium channel inhibitors for 1 h, and followed by the treatment of Hcy and CuCl2 under conditions of H/R. As shown in Fig. 2A, IP3R inhibitor 2-APB exhibited a substantial protective effect against cell death of HCMECs, whereas other inhibitors of cell surface Ca2+ transporters mildly affected the survival rate. Meanwhile, 2-APB completely mitigated both cellular and mitochondrial Ca2+ overload (Fig. 2B-C). It is noteworthy that Hcy-Cu2+ complex could trigger the cellular uptake of Ca2+ via transient receptor potential canonical (TRPC) channels 4/5 [30], and 2-APB has been reported to inhibit TRPC as well [31, 32]. To specify the effect of 2-APB in our system, we used ML204, a specific inhibitor of TRPC4/5, and EGTA, an extracellular Ca2+ chelator, and found these interventions neither influenced intracellular Ca2+ overload nor prevented HCMEC death (Figure S2A-B). Moreover, IP3R knockdown inhibited the upregulation of both the intracellular and mitochondrial Ca2+ (Fig. 2D, Figure S10), and prevented the cell death of HCEMCs in the presence of Hcy and CuCl2 under conditions of H/R (Fig. 2E). To further determining the dynamics of intra-ER Ca2+ in HCMECs, we used the genetically encoded ER Ca2+ indicators, green calcium-measuring organelle-entrapped protein indicators 1 in the ER (G-CEPIA1er) [33]. As shown in Fig. 2F and Figure S10, G-CEPIA1er colocalized with the ER tracker, and with the treatment of Hcy and CuCl2 under H/R conditions for 1 h, a large reduction in fluorescence intensity of G-CEPIA1er was noted, indicating a substantial decrease of ER Ca2+ in HCMECs. However, knockdown of IP3R greatly prevented the loss of ER Ca2+, suggesting that it is the IP3R that mediated Ca2+ release from ER. Moreover, we observed the elevated expressions of ER stress-related proteins as early as 1 h after the treatment (Fig. 2G). Collectively, these observations suggest that ER stress and intracellular Ca2+ overload via IP3R-mediated ER Ca2+ release, may contribute to cell death of HCMECs with the treatment of Hcy and CuCl2 under H/R conditions.

Bearing in mind that the overload of mitochondrial Ca2+ was secondary to the increase of intracellular Ca2+ (Fig. 1B-C), we hypothesized that the augmented ER Ca2+ in HCMECs via IP3R may lead to enhanced mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake. The Ca2+ uniporter complex, which includes the protein MCU, mediates mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake and regulates mitochondrial metabolism [34]. As shown in Fig. 2H, the expression of MCU was upregulated in HCMECs by Hcy and CuCl2 under H/R condition. Inhibition of MCU or IP3R by inhibitors or siRNA largely prevented the upregulation of mitochondrial Ca2+ of HCMECs (Fig. 2C-D, I, Figure S3A-B, S4, S10), and the followed mitochondrial dysfunction (Fig. 2J-K) and cell death (Fig. 2L-M, Figure S5). Altogether, these results indicated that ER-mitochondrial Ca2+ transfer induces mitochondrial dysfunction and cell death of HCMECs under H/R condition in the presence of Hcy and CuCl2.

ER-originated mitochondrial Ca2+ contributes to mROS generation and LMP in HCMECs after IR injury induction in the presence of Hcy and CuCl2

During physiological process, mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake is required to match energy supply, whereas mitochondrial Ca2+ overload under pathological conditions gives rise to oxidative stress [24, 29]. As shown in Fig. 3A, the levels of mitochondrial ROS (mROS) exhibited a time-dependently upregulation in HCMECs with the treatment of Hcy and CuCl2 under H/R condition. Scavenging mROS with mitoTEMPO partially attenuated mitochondrial Ca2+ overload (Fig. 3B), and mitigated the cell death of HCMECs with the treatment of Hcy and CuCl2 under H/R conditions (Fig. 3C). Moreover, intracellular Ca2+ chelation or pharmacological inhibition of MCU or IP3R prevented mROS generation to a great extent (Fig. 3D). These findings suggested that IP3R-mediated ER-mitochondria Ca2+ transfer is the primary cause of mROS generation induced by Hcy and CuCl2 under H/R conditions, and mitochondrial Ca2+ and mROS mutually regulates each other.

High levels of mROS released through mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) may trigger a ROS burst and disrupt the membranes of other organelles in the cytosol. As shown in Fig. 3E, the mPTP opening in HCMECs was proven by the decreased Calcein fluorescence induced upon Hcy and CuCl2 treatment under H/R condition, which was also visualized by confocal microscopy (Fig. 3F, Figure S10). Importantly, inhibition of mPTP by ER000444793, but not CsA that relying on Cyclophilin D (CyPD) [35], substantially prevented the cell death of HCMECs (Fig. 3G), indicating that the treatment of Hcy and CuCl2-induced cell death of HCMECs under H/R condition is associated with CyPD independent-mPTP opening. Moreover, lysosomal membrane lipids are particularly prone to mROS damage, and thus affect membrane permeability that can lead to lysosomal membrane permeabilization (LMP) and the following cell death [36, 37]. Firstly, we used lysotracker green, which accumulates inside acidic organelles and diffuses after LMP, to assess LMP. As shown in Fig. 3H, a great decrease of lysotracker green fluorescence was detected in Hcy and CuCl2-treated HCMECs at 8 h after reoxygenation, whereas inhibition of mPTP or mROS almost completely prevented LMP. Considering that weakened lysotracker fluorescence may also be attributed to an increase in lysosomal pH, we further used acridine orange (AO), which could shift from red to green fluorescence when lysosomal alterations occur, to check LMP. We consistently found that Hcy and CuCl2-treated HCMECs under H/R condition displayed increased green fluorescence and reduced red fluorescence, which was prevented by mROS scavenger or mPTP inhibitor (Fig. 3I, Figure S10). ROS-initiated LMP could be suppressed by iron chelator DFO, we found that DFO pretreatment only partially protected HCMECs from cell death (Fig. 3J). This observation, together with the fact that LMP occurs late after reoxygenation, suggests that LMP might not the initiator of cell death, but more likely be involved in amplifying the cell death signaling induced by Hcy and CuCl2 under H/R condition.

Peroxynitrite generation promotes ER to mitochondria Ca2+ flux and eventually leading to HCMEC necroptosis

Subsequently, we embarked on the upstream mechanisms underlying ER-mitochondrial Ca2+ transfer and mitochondrial impairment. Our previous studies have demonstrated that the combination of Hcy and CuCl2 induces cardiac microvascular IRI by causing oxidative stress in the reperfusion treatment of ischemic cardiopathy [14]. Moreover, our recent study has specified the oxidative stress is mainly peroxynitrite (ONOO−)-related, producing from simultaneous activation of NADPH oxidase (NOX) and endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) [13]. In this study, we consistently found that intracellular levels of ONOO− were significantly elevated in HCMECs cells treated with Hcy and CuCl2 under H/R conditions (Fig. 4A, Figure S6). However, the inhibition of ONOO− generation, either by the inhibitors of NOX, eNOS, and ONOO− scavenger, or by eNOS siRNA, significantly diminished the upregulated expressions of ER stress-related proteins in HCMECs induced by Hcy and CuCl2 under H/R conditions (Fig. 4B, Figure S7A-B); meanwhile, the augmented levels of cytoplasmic and mitochondrial Ca2+ were substantially prevented by ONOO− inhibition (Fig. 4C-D, Figure S7C). These findings indicate that ONOO−, produced by Hcy and CuCl2 under H/R conditions, might induce ER stress and influence mitochondrial Ca2+ homeostasis.

Our previous studies have confirmed that HCMEC death by Hcy and CuCl2 treatment under H/R conditions is mediated by necroptosis, and ONOO− is involved in the process [13]. To solve the jigsaw puzzle of necroptosis in cardiac microvascular IRI, we finally explore the connections of necroptosis and Ca2+ hemostasis. As shown in Fig. 4E, the phosphorylation levels of the key effector molecules RIP1/3 were elevated in HCMECs subjected to Hcy and CuCl2 under H/R condition; however, inhibiting IP3R-mediated ER Ca2+ release or chelating the intracellular Ca2+ led to a significant dephosphorylation of RIP 1/3. Furthermore, our studies demonstrated that the inhibitors of RIP 1 or MLKL had no effect on the levels of intracellular Ca2+ (Fig. 4F), but greatly prevented the upregulated mROS in HCMECs induced by Hcy and CuCl2 under H/R condition (Fig. 4G). These findings were further confirmed by the knockout of RIP 1 in HCMECs (Fig. 4H, Figure S3C), indicating that necrosomes may function upstream of mROS generation and downstream of intracellular free Ca2+. Notably, the inhibitors of RIP1 or MLKL also partially prevented the increase of mitochondrial Ca2+ in HCMECs in the presence of Hcy and CuCl2 under H/R condition (Fig. 4I), suggesting that, besides MCU transporter, necrosomes may also contribute to mitochondrial Ca2+ overload.

Pharmacological intervention targeting intracellular Ca2+ dyshomeostasis inhibits microcirculation malfunction during IRI in HHcy rats

To further confirm the mechanism in vivo and assess the significance and clinical relevance of intracellular Ca2+ dysregulation, we investigated necroptosis in cardiac microvessels and evaluated the effect of the IP3R inhibitor 2-APB on microvascular injury following ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) in HHcyR. An ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) model in rats with HHcy was established previously (denoted as HHcyR I/R) [13]. With the reference to the CD31-labeled fluorescence signal of endothelial cells, IRI in the heart of HHcyR induced a significant decrease of the red fluorescence signal of p-RIP1 and p-RIP3 (Figure S8). These findings confirm that necroptosis occurs in CMECs during I/R injury in the presence of Hcy. The effect of 2-APB was compared between the sham HHcyR groups and the I/R HHcyR groups, which were i.v. injected with saline or 2-APB 10 min before ischemia (Fig. 5A). Firstly, we employed the gelatin ink perfusion method to observe the general morphology and perfusion of cardiac microvessels after I/R. As shown in Fig. 5B, the number of gelatin ink-positive microvessels in I/R heart of HHcyR treated with saline was significantly reduced compared with those in the hearts of sham-operated rats, indicating the sparse of vascular beds. However, the application of 5 mg/kg 2-APB greatly diminished the degree of microvascular obstruction (Fig. 5B). We next performed hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining in the heart and found the accumulation of red blood cells (RBC) in the myocardium and microvessels in the HHcyR I/R group treated with saline. The treatment of 5 mg/kg 2-APB, however, effectively decreased the RBC deposition (Fig. 5C), indicating the improved microvascular integrity and blood flow. Considering the importance of integrity and barrier function of microvasculature for microcirculation function, and VE-cadherin is a component of endothelial intercellular junctions that is imperative for maintaining vascular integrity, we further detected the effect of 2-APB on the expression of VE-cadherin, using CD31 as a label of microvascular in the heart. As shown in Fig. 5D, there is a significant decrease of the red fluorescence signal of VE-cadherin in CD31⁺ microvessels of the heart following I/R injury in HHcyR rats compared to sham controls, whereas 5 mg/kg 2-APB administration significantly restored the expression of VE-cadherin and thus prevented the damage of the microvascular barrier function.

We then investigated the effect of 2-APB on the inflammatory response in the cardiac microvascular IRI. The toluidine blue staining demonstrated that cardiac IRI in HHcyR induced significant infiltration of mast cells in the heart, whereas 5 mg/kg 2-APB treatment massively reduced the mast cell infiltration when compared with the saline treatment, suggesting the dampened inflammatory response in the heart of the HHcyR I/R group (Fig. 5E). It is well established that vascular inflammation and endothelial barrier damage typically result in increased vascular permeability, a phenomenon that can be discerned through immunohistochemical analysis of plasma albumin in the myocardial tissue. As shown in Fig. 5F, cardiac I/R in HHcyR triggered enhanced leakage of plasma albumin from the vasculature into the myocardial tissues, signifying heightened microvascular permeability. However, 5 mg/kg 2-APB application prevented the diffusion of plasma albumin beyond the vessel wall in the heart of HHcyR I/R group. Taken together, preventing intracellular Ca2+ dyshomeostasis by IP3R inhibitor can attenuate cardiac microvascular IRI in HHcyR.

2-APB reduces infarct size and improves cardiac function following IRI in HHcy rats

We further examined the effect of 2-APB on the recovery of cardiac function after I/R in HHcyR. Evans blue (EB)/TTC staining was performed to evaluate the extent of cardiac infarction. As shown in Fig. 6A-C, the area placed at risk (AAR) by ligation, expressed as a percentage of the left ventricle (LV), showed similar magnitudes‌ in HHcyR I/R treated with saline versus 2-APB, ranging between 48.01% and 51.17%. However, the infarcted area (IA) normalized to the area at risk (IA/AAR) was significantly smaller in the 2-APB group, decreasing from 51.64%±9.247% in saline controls to 23.30%±5.113% with 5 mg/kg 2-APB, corresponding to a 28.34% relative reduction (P < 0.0001). To corroborate the histologic findings, myocardial damage was further evaluated by an assessment of the cardiac enzyme profile. Serum levels of myocardial injury markers -including CK (↑by 14.66-fold), CK-MB (↑by 10.38-fold), cTnT (↑by 113.00-fold), LDH (↑by 7.97-fold), and AST (↑by 2.51-fold) -were markedly elevated in the saline-treated HHcyR I/R group. In contrast, treatment with 2-APB (5 mg/kg) significantly reduced these elevations by approximately 42.32–53.55% (Fig. 6D), ‌suggesting a cardioprotective role of 2-APB after I/R in HHcyR. In addition to the pharmacologic IP3R blockade in vivo, we have complemented 2-APB with a transient siRNA approach in HHcy rats subjected to I/R. As shown in Figure S9, tail-vein delivery of si-IP3R lowered IP3R in CD31⁺ microvessels, reduced IA/AAR, and prevented the elevation of serum heart injury markers in HHcy I/R rats. Effects paralleled pharmacologic IP3R inhibition. Moreover, cardiac function in HHcyR after I/R was further assessed via echocardiography. Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and fractional shortening (LVFS), derived from M-mode echocardiographic imaging, were quantified as key functional parameters. In the cardiac IRI model, administration of 5 mg/kg 2-APB significantly enhanced cardiac function in HHcyR compared to the saline-treated controls, as demonstrated by elevated LVEF (35.71% → 55.32%), increased LVFS (31.44% → 48.54%), and reduced LVEDd (6.98 mm → 5.80 mm) (Fig. 6E-F), indicating improved ventricular mechanics and attenuated pathological remodeling by 2-APB administration. Collectively, these findings underscore the therapeutic potential of 2-APB as an adjunctive strategy to mitigate the exacerbation of cardiac IRI in HHcyR.

Discussion

Ischemic disorders remain the leading cause of morbidity and mortality [38, 39]. Although HHcy is increasingly prevalent [40], the mechanisms linking HHcy to cardiac IRI have been incompletely defined. Here, we uncover a molecular mechanism underlying the microvascular dysfunction during cardiac IRI complicated with HHcy. In particularly, we demonstrate that in HCMECs, Hcy associated with the mobilized Cu2+ during I/R induces the ONOO- formation and enhances IP3R-dependent ER-mitochondrial Ca2+ transfer. This, together with cytosolic Ca2+-activated RIP 1/3, results in mitochondrial Ca2+ overload, mROS generation, LMP and CMEC necroptosis. In vivo, interventions that restrain this Ca²⁺ dyshomeostasis preserve microvascular integrity, reduce infarct size by 28.34%, decreased the serum injury markers by 33.02–52.26%, and improve cardiac function (Fig. 6G).

HHcy is present in roughly one-quarter to one-third of MI patients [41]. Men and elderly patients tend to have higher Hcy levels, contributing to increased prevalence in these groups. HHcy is widely regarded as an independent risk factor for MI. Elevated Hcy levels in MI patients are associated with worse short- and long-term outcomes, including higher risk of recurrent MI, heart failure, and cardiovascular mortality [42]. B-vitamin and folate supplementation can lower Hcy levels, but large trials have shown limited benefits on major cardiovascular outcomes [43, 44]. Our study unravels the mechanism contributing to Hcy-aggravated acute cardiac IRI, and may open avenues for targeted therapies beyond just Hcy reduction.

Among the ions involved in cardiac function, Ca²⁺ is the most critical. As a ubiquitous second messenger, Ca²⁺ regulates both cardiomyocyte physiology and pathology [29]. Under normal conditions, cytosolic Ca²⁺ oscillations drive excitation-contraction coupling [45], while mitochondrial Ca²⁺ fluxes coordinate oxidative phosphorylation with ATP demand [46]. This balance is tightly controlled in both the cytosol and mitochondria [29]. However, during IRI, mitochondrial Ca²⁺ overload triggers permeability transition and cell death [47]. Although less studied, Ca²⁺ overload also occurs in endothelial cells, largely mediated by excessive activation of sodium-hydrogen exchanger 1 (NHE-1). Increased intracellular sodium via NHE-1 drives Ca²⁺ entry through the sodium–calcium exchanger (NCX), elevating cytosolic Ca²⁺ [48, 49]. In porcine heart models, the NHE-1 inhibitor cariporide preserved cardiac function during IRI [50]. However, in the context of cardiac IRI with HHcy, the role of Ca²⁺ in the microcirculation remains poorly defined. Previous study shows that Hcy-copper complexes activate T-type Ca²⁺ channels in endothelial cells, enhancing Ca²⁺ uptake and inducing apoptosis [30]. Consistent with this, we observed mitochondrial Ca²⁺ overload in HCMECs during I/R in the presence of Hcy. Notably, this dysregulation was not mediated by NHE-1 or L-type voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels but instead by ER-mitochondrial Ca²⁺ transfer. These findings refine the mechanistic understanding of mitochondrial Ca²⁺ overload in HHcy-associated cardiac IRI and highlight the need to consider additional risk factors when identifying therapeutic targets.

Our data reveal that ER stress-mediated Ca²⁺ dyshomeostasis disrupts mitochondrial function and activates necroptosis in endothelial cells during IRI complicated by HHcy. Cellular stress often overwhelms protein-folding capacity, triggering ER stress, which may restore homeostasis or induce cell death-either by necroptosis [51] or more commonly through Bcl-2-regulated mitochondrial apoptosis [51, 52]. While ER stress-induced apoptosis is well established, its role in necroptosis remains less understood. Previous studies have shown that RIP3 upregulation in cardiomyocytes subjected to IRI evokes ER stress, elevates intracellular Ca²⁺ and xanthine oxidase (XO) expression, and ultimately drives necroptosis through the Ca²⁺/XO/ROS/mPTP axis [53].

In our study, Hcy, in association with mobilized Cu²⁺ during I/R, induced ER stress in HCMECs via RNS. This stress triggered Ca²⁺ release through IP3R into the cytosol. On one hand, ER-derived Ca²⁺ transfer to mitochondria via upregulated MCU promoted mROS generation, sustained mPTP opening, lysosomal membrane permeabilization (LMP), and subsequent necroptosis. On the other hand, cytosolic Ca²⁺ activated RIP1 and RIP3, which further exacerbated mitochondrial Ca²⁺ overload, likely via MCU, ultimately amplifying necroptosis in HCMECs.

Because the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) is sensitive to both ROS and Ca²⁺ overload, disturbances in either pathway create a feedback loop that promotes mPTP opening and further amplifies ROS or Ca²⁺ release, namely “ROS-induced ROS release”, or “Ca2+-induced Ca2+ release” [17]. Our recent work demonstrates that Hcy, together with mobilized Cu²⁺, stimulates NOX and eNOS activity in HCMECs, producing ROS and NO and leading to excessive ONOO− generation [13]. Previous study has shown that ONOO− pathologically inhibits reversible S-glutathiolation, thereby preventing SERCA activation and impairing Ca²⁺ clearance [54]. In this study, ONOO− overproduction imposed ER stress, promoting ER Ca²⁺ release and elevating both cytosolic and mitochondrial Ca²⁺ levels. ER-mitochondrial Ca²⁺ transfer further increased mROS generation, which in turn exacerbated mitochondrial Ca²⁺ overload.

This bidirectional interplay of ROS and Ca²⁺ drives mPTP opening, amplifies ROS release, and ultimately triggers necroptosis of HCMECs during I/R under HHcy. Notably, mPTP opening was only modestly delayed by CsA but was strongly inhibited by ER000444793, indicating CyPD-independent pore activation. Moreover, our findings show that reactive nitrogen species (RNS) amplify mROS formation in HHcy-associated IRI, supporting crosstalk between RNS and ROS sources. Although intracellular antioxidant systems normally buffer ROS [55], overall SOD activity was markedly reduced in HCMECs exposed to Hcy and CuCl₂ during I/R [14]. Therefore, the vicious cycle of RNS-mROS generation together with the defect of antioxidant system evoke necroptosis of HCMECs and thus deteriorate cardiac IRI. Thus, the combination of excessive RNS/mROS production and impaired antioxidant defense forms a vicious cycle that promotes endothelial necroptosis and aggravates cardiac IRI.

Cell fate is closely linked to the extent of lysosomal membrane permeabilization (LMP), which contributes to multiple cell death pathways [56, 57]. Partial and selective lysosomal leakage promotes apoptosis [58], whereas extensive rupture with rapid protease release induces necroptosis [56]. Lysosomes can degrade MLKL and thereby suppress necroptosis, but ischemia impairs autophagic flux and instead activates this pathway [56]. Thus, alterations in LMP play a pivotal role in necroptosis. Lysosomal membranes are particularly vulnerable to ROS-mediated damage, with destabilized mitochondria serving as an early ROS source [36]. Persistent mPTP opening allows mROS to diffuse into lysosomes, where they react with lysosomal iron via the Fenton reaction to generate highly reactive hydroxyl radicals [59]. These radicals drive lipid peroxidation of lysosomal membranes, thereby promoting LMP and necroptosis. Our findings indicate that Hcy, together with mobilized Cu²⁺ during cardiac I/R, induces mPTP opening, mROS-dependent LMP, and necroptosis in HCMECs. Moreover, lysosomes also regulate metal homeostasis and are known to store Cu²⁺ as well as iron [60]. However, it remains unclear whether mobilized Cu²⁺ during cardiac I/R is sequestered in lysosomes via specific transporters. This raises the possibility that lysosomal Cu²⁺ could react with mROS through a Fenton-like process, amplifying hydroxyl radical production, exacerbating LMP, and driving necroptosis in HHcy-associated cardiac I/R.

Beyond ischemic conditioning, pharmacological interventions remain essential for cardioprotection after IRI. Most candidate drugs are derived from pathways implicated in cardiac IRI and aim to either suppress injury or activate protective mechanisms. However, a persistent gap exists between promising preclinical results and poor clinical translation [4]. Many interventions reported to reduce infarct size, such as adenosine, nitrite, PKCδ inhibitors, or mitochondrial-targeted agents like CsA, failed to consistently improve outcomes in STEMI patients when administered at reperfusion [4–6]. Thus, the development of effective cardioprotective therapies remains a major unmet clinical need. Our results provide mechanistic proof-of-concept that targeting the IP3R by 2-APB can restore intracellular Ca²⁺ homeostasis, limit Ca²⁺-mediated mitochondrial damage, and improve both microcirculation and cardiac function during IRI complicated by HHcy.

For successful clinical translation, it is critical to account for common comorbidities and focus not only on reducing infarct size but also on alleviating coronary microvascular obstruction. Our study suggests that the targeting IP3R by 2-APB is a promising strategy for acute MI with HHcy, as it prevents microvascular dysfunction, reduces infarct size, and enhances cardiac function. Consistent with other reports, 2-APB mitigates I/R-induced organ injury (heart, kidney, ovary, and testis) by suppressing cytosolic Ca²⁺ elevation and exerting antioxidant and anti-apoptotic effects [61–63]. By simultaneously inhibiting necroptosis and apoptosis, 2-APB significantly reduces infarct size in preclinical studies and warrants further investigation.

Conclusion

Altogether, our findings pinpoint ER stress-mediated Ca2+ flux to mitochondria to amplify ROS generation as a key regulator of intracellular Ca2+ events engaged in cardiac microvascular ischemia–reperfusion injury associated with HHcy. Importantly, we demonstrate that inhibition of intracellular Ca2+ mobilization by IP3R inhibitor 2-APB has great potential to prevent the cardiac microvascular IRI complicated with HHcy.

Limitation

Our previous studies have compared the cardiovascular damage in HHcyR and normal rats [64]. In this study, the in-vivo pharmacology was performed only in HHcyR without a parallel normal control. Future studies will adopt a factorial design, and develop more strategies that target IP3R-MCU to strengthen translational inference. The development of more selective IP3R or MCU modulators, as well as strategies that specifically limit ONOO- accumulation, will be necessary to translate these mechanistic insights into safe and effective therapies.
