Dual-layer biphasic calcium phosphates/tantalum pentoxide coating on titanium for concurrent biocorrosion resistance and enhanced biocompatibility
Authors: Ying-Sui Sun, Kai-Yi Lin, Wei-Cheng Chen, Her-Hsiung Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/purposeTitanium (Ti) dental implants must resist biocorrosion while maintaining biocompatibility. This study developed a dual-layer biphasic calcium phosphates/tantalum pentoxide (BCPs/Ta2O5) coating on Ti to improve both biocorrosion resistance and biological performance.Materials and methodsA dense Ta2O5 inner layer was fabricated via a hydrolysis —condensation process to act as a corrosion barrier. Subsequently, a porous BCPs outer layer was deposited by electrochemical cathodic deposition to provide a bioactive interface. Surface morphology, functional groups, crystalline structure, wettability, surface free energy, coating thickness and nanostructure, protein adsorption, and in vitro bioactivity were characterized. Biocorrosion behavior was evaluated using polarization curve measurements in simulated blood plasma and artificial saliva with and without fluoride ions. Cellular responses, adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation of human bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells, were assessed. Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test.ResultsThe Ta2O5 inner layer significantly improved biocorrosion resistance, evidenced by increased corrosion potential and reduced anodic current density, even under fluoride-containing conditions. The porous BCPs outer layer increased wettability, surface free energy, protein adsorption, and in vitro bioactivity, thereby promoting cell spreading, cytoskeletal organization, and proliferation. The bioactive BCPs coating on Ta2O5/Ti surface significantly enhanced alkaline phosphatase activity, suggesting a favorable early osteogenic response, without compromising the corrosion protection provided by the underlying Ta2O5 layer.ConclusionDual-layer BCPs/Ta2O5 coating on Ti surface combines enhanced biocorrosion resistance with osteogenic bioactivity and represents a promising surface modification strategy for Ti dental implants, although studies are required to assess long-term stability and durability.Keywords: Titanium dental implant, Biphasic calcium phosphate, Tantalum pentoxide, Surface characterization, Biocorrosion resistance, Biocompatibility

Introduction

Titanium (Ti) is widely used for dental implants owing to its excellent mechanical properties, corrosion resistance, and clinical outcomes.1 Its biocompatibility arises from a stable titanium dioxide (TiO2) passive layer;2 however, this layer can be compromised in the oral environment.3 Fluoride exposure is clinically relevant because fluoride-containing products, including toothpastes, mouth rinses, and topical agents, are routinely used in dental care, at concentrations from 1000 to 1500 ppm in conventional toothpastes to 5000 ppm in prescription dentifrices containing 1.1 wt% NaF. Under acidic conditions, fluoride may destabilize the TiO2 passive film and accelerate Ti corrosion.4–7 Although fluoride exposure is generally intermittent in vivo, transient peri-implant conditions, including biofilm accumulation, inflammation, and acidic microenvironments, may increase corrosion risk.

Beyond fluoride ions, oral fluids may also destabilize the passive TiO2 layer.8,9 Corrosion-induced Ti ion release has been linked to inflammation,10 hypersensitivity,11 and peri-implant tissue damage,12 and may exacerbate peri-implantitis by promoting microbial dysbiosis and bone resorption.13 Consequently, surface coatings have been developed to improve Ti biocorrosion resistance and biological performance; however, achieving both remains challenging.

Tantalum (Ta) exhibits excellent corrosion resistance because of the spontaneous formation of a stable Ta2O5 surface passive layer.14 Previous studies found that Ta2O5 coatings protect Ti substrates from corrosion while maintaining a porous surface morphology.15–17 Although porous tantalum-based structures support bone ingrowth, Ta2O5 exhibits limited bioactivity and does not actively promote osteogenic differentiation.18 Consequently, implant surfaces based solely on porous Ta2O5 often face a trade-off between corrosion protection and biological integration.

Biphasic calcium phosphates (BCPs), consisting of hydroxyapatite (HA) and β-tricalcium phosphate (β-TCP), are known for their osteoconductivity and bioactivity.19 The complementary dissolution behaviors of HA and β-TCP in BCPs balance biodegradability and stability, facilitating apatite formation and enhancing bone regeneration.20–22 Therefore, a dual-layer coating comprising a corrosion-resistant Ta2O5 inner layer and a bioactive BCPs outer layer represents a promising strategy to improve both biocorrosion resistance and osteogenic performance.

Various techniques are developed for fabricating calcium-phosphate layers, including spray pyrolysis, sol—gel processing, ion implantation, and electrochemical deposition.23–25 Among these, electrochemical deposition is attractive in biomaterials applications owing to its controllability, cost-effectiveness, and suitability for complex implant geometries.26,27

In our previous work, we created a dual-layer BCPs/Ta2O5 coating with a 600–700 nm Ta2O5 inner layer and a bioactive BCPs outer layer containing a trace amount of β-TCP.22 Because a thicker Ta2O5 inner layer may enhance corrosion resistance and a more balanced HA/β-TCP composition may improve bioactivity and biocompatibility, 20–22 this study optimized the coating by increasing the tantalum(V) butoxide precursor concentration during Ta2O5 formation. This modification aimed to produce a thicker Ta2O5 inner layer and a BCPs outer layer with a clear β-TCP phase. The optimized coating was evaluated for biocorrosion resistance and osteogenic performance relative to the previous coating and control Ti surfaces.

Materials and methods

Specimen preparations

Commercially pure grade IV Ti discs (15 mm diameter, 1 mm thickness) were used as substrates and ground with SiC papers up to 1200 grit. A Ta2O5 coating was prepared by immersing the discs in 100 mM tantalum(V) butoxide in ethanol/toluene (50/50, v/v) for 3 min, followed by hydrolysis—condensation in ultrapure water for 10 min. After air-drying, the specimens were designated as Ta2O5/Ti. A BCPs layer was then deposited on Ta2O5/Ti (designated BCPs/Ta2O5/Ti) via electrochemical cathodic deposition at 37 °C under an applied cathodic current of −0.1 A for 3 h using an electrolyte containing 0.6 mM Ca(NO3) 2·4H2O, 0.4 mM NH4H2PO4, and 0.1 M KCl. A platinum sheet and a Ta2O5/Ti specimen were used as the anode and cathode, respectively. Additional procedural details have been reported previously.22

Surface characterizations analysis

Surface morphology and cross-sectional thickness were analyzed by field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM; JSM-6500F, JEOL, Tokyo, Japan), while crystal structure, functional groups, and nanocrystalline feature were characterized using X-ray diffraction (XRD; D8 Discover, Bruker, Karlsruhe, Germany), Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR; Vertex 80v, Bruker, Ettlingen, Germany), and transmission electron microscopy (TEM; JEM-2100 LaB6, JEOL), respectively. Cross-sectional TEM specimens were prepared by focused ion beam milling (FIB; GAIA3, TESCAN, Brno, Czech Republic), with coating thickness verified by energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS; Oxford Instruments, Abingdon, UK). Surface wettability, including distilled water and diiodo-methane contact angles and surface free energy, was determined using a contact angle goniometer (100SB, Sindatek, New Taipei City, Taiwan) with build-in MagicDroplet software.

Surface bioactivity for CaP formation23 was assessed by immersing specimens in simulated body fluid (SBF) at 37 °C for 7 d, followed by FE-SEM and EDS analyses of morphology and Ca/P ratio. Protein adsorption was evaluated using bovine serum albumin (BSA; Sigma—Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA); specimens were incubated in 5 mg/mL BSA in saline at 37 °C for 1 h, and adsorbed BSA was semi-quantified by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS; ESCA-003800, ULVACPHI, Kanagawa, Japan) based on C1s and N1s intensities.

Corrosion resistance analysis

An electrochemical workstation (model 5600, Jiehan, Taichung, Taiwan) was used to evaluate corrosion resistance by measuring potentiodynamic polarization curves. The specimens served as working electrodes, with a saturated calomel electrode (SCE) and a platinum sheet as the reference and counter electrodes, respectively. Polarization curves were recorded from −0.8 to +2.0 V relative to the open-circuit potential at a scan rate of 1 mV/s. Measurements were conducted at 37 °C in simulated blood plasma (SBP, pH 7.4),22 acidic artificial saliva (AS, pH 5.4),7 and AS containing 0.5 wt% NaF to assess fluoride-induced corrosion at a clinically relevant NaF concentration.

Cellular responses analysis

Human bone marrow—derived mesenchymal stem cells (hMSCs; Bioresource Collection and Research Center, Hsinchu, Taiwan) were cultured in low-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (Sigma—Aldrich). All cellular assays were performed in three independent experiments conducted on different days using independently prepared cell cultures. For adhesion assays, hMSCs were seeded at 5 × 104cells/specimen and adhesion morphology was examined using FE-SEM after 2 and 24 h. The 2 h time point was selected to evaluate early cell attachment, and the 24 h time point to assess cell spreading and interactions. Cytoskeletal organization (F-actin arrangement) after 2 h was evaluated by immunofluorescence staining and observed under a fluorescence microscope (Nikon AZ-100; Nikon Instruments, Inc., Tokyo, Japan). For proliferation and osteogenic assay, hMSCs were seeded at 1 × 104cells/specimen. Cell proliferation was assessed by MTT assay (Sigma—Aldrich) over 10 d, with absorbance measured at 570 nm. The 10 d period was selected to monitor cell proliferation. Higher optical density (OD) values indicated greater cell numbers. Osteogenic differentiation was assessed by measuring alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity, normalized to total protein content (nM/mg), over 14 d using an ALP detection kit (BioVision, Milpitas, CA, USA). The 14 d period was selected because ALP activity is a marker of early osteogenic differentiation.

Statistical analysis

Cellular data were derived from three independent experiments (biological replicates, n = 3), with three specimens per group analyzed at each time point in each experiment as technical replicates. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and analyzed using one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test when statistical significance was detected (P< 0.05).

Results

Surface characterizations

Fig. 1A presents the surface morphologies of test specimens. Bare Ti exhibited a smooth surface, whereas Ta2O5/Ti showed a rough coating with micro- and submicron pores formed by aggregated spherical particles. After BCPs deposition, the surface was covered by nanoscale dendritic crystals, forming an interconnected three-dimensional morphology. FTIR analysis (Fig. 1B) confirmed Ta—O—Ta bonds for Ta2O5 and PO43-/P-OH groups after BCPs coating. XRD results (Fig. 1C) indicated an amorphous Ta2O5 layer and crystalline HA/β-TCP phases in the BCPs layer.

Surface characterizations, including morphology, functional group, and crystalline structure, of test specimens.

(A) Field emission scanning electron microscopy analysis results showed a smooth surface on Ti, a rough surface with micro- and submicron-sized pores on Ta2O5/Ti, and a rough surface with nano-sized dendritic deposits on BCPs/Ta2O5/Ti. The Ta2O5/Ti and BCPs/Ta2O5/Ti surfaces revealed the complex three-dimensional porous morphologies. (B) Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy analysis results showed Ta—O—Ta groups on Ta2O5/Ti, while PO43- and P—OH groups on BCPs/Ta2O5/Ti. (C) X-ray diffraction analysis results showed amorphous structure on Ta2O5/Ti, while crystalline hydroxyapatite (HA) and β-tricalcium phosphate (β-TCP) structures on BCPs/Ta2O5/Ti.

Cross-sectional FIB—EDS analysis (Fig. 2) showed a Ta2O5 inner layer of ~ 0.6–1.0 μm and a BCPs/Ta2O5 coating thickness of 1.1–1.6 μm. TEM/EDS results (Fig. 3) revealed an amorphous Ta2O5 inner layer with ~5 nm nanodomains and a nanocrystalline BCPs outer layer composed of HA (Ca/P ~ 1.69) and β-TCP (Ca/P ~ 1.52) with grain sizes of ~ 5–10 nm.

Cross-sectional characterizations, analyzed using focused ion beam milling process accompanied with energydispersive spectroscopy elemental mapping, of Ta2O5/Ti and BCPs/Ta2O5/Ti specimens.

The Ta2O5 coating on Ta2O5/Ti had a thickness of approximately 0.6–1.0 μm, while the overall thickness of BCPs/Ta2O5 coating on BCPs/Ta2O5/Ti ranged from 1.1 to 1.6 μm.

Cross-sectional characterizations, analyzed using transmission electron microscopy accompanied with selected area electron diffraction and energy-dispersive spectroscopy, of Ta2O5/Ti and BCPs/Ta2O5/Ti specimens.

The Ta2O5 layer was amorphous, containing nano-sized domains of about 5 nm. The BCPs outer layer exhibited nanostructured crystalline phases composed of hydroxyapatite (HA) (Ca/P ratio ~ 1.69) and β-tricalcium phosphate (β-TCP) (Ca/P ratio ~ 1.52), with grain sizes of 5–10 nm.

Wettability measurements (Fig. 4) showed that ground Ti was moderately hydrophilic (water contact angle ~45°), whereas Ta2O5/Ti (~24°) and BCPs/Ta2O5/Ti (~10°) exhibited markedly enhanced hydrophilicity, with similar trends for diiodo-methane. Correspondingly, surface free energy increased from Ti (~59 mN/m) to Ta2O5/Ti (~76 mN/m) and BCPs/Ta2O5/Ti (~80 mN/m), indicating progressive improvement in surface wettability.

Wettability, analyzed using contact angle goniometer, of test specimens and their corresponding surface free energies.

Ti surface had moderate wettability, Ta2O5/Ti and BCPs/Ta2O5/Ti surfaces exhibited enhanced wettability, particularly the latter. The corresponding surface free energy followed the order: Ti < Ta2O5/Ti < BCPs/Ta2O5/Ti, representing the improved wettability.

Fig. 5A shows that no Ca/P precipitates formed on Ti or Ta2O5/Ti after 7 d in SBF, whereas apatite (Ca/P ~1.77) rapidly deposited on BCPs/Ta2O5/Ti within 4 d. As shown in Fig. 5B, Ta2O5/Ti and especially BCPs/Ta2O5/Ti exhibited significantly higher protein adsorption, in terms of C1s and N1s intensities, than Ti.

In vitro bioactivity and protein adsorption of test specimens.

(A) Bioactivity ability, in trems of apatite formation ability during 7 d immersion in simulated body fluid (SBF), analysis results showed that BCPs/Ta2O5/Ti surface had apatite (Ca/P ratio ~ 1.77) deposition after only 4 d immersion in SBF, while the others did not present apatite deposition even after 7 d immersion. (B) Protein adsorption ability, in terms of X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy C1s and N1s intensities after 1 h immersion in albumin-containing saline, analysis results showed that higher C1s and N1s intensities were detected on Ta2O5/Ti and BCPs/Ta2O5/Ti surfaces, especially the latter.

Corrosion resistance

Fig. 6 presents polarization curves in SBP, AS, and AS + 0.5% NaF solutions. In SBP and AS, Ta2O5/Ti and BCPs/Ta2O5/Ti exhibited more noble corrosion potentials and lower anodic current densities than Ti. In AS + 0.5% NaF, the coated-surfaces maintained corrosion potentials above 0.7 V and anodic current densities more than two orders of magnitude lower than those of Ti, indicating superior biocorrosion resistance. Because the current densities were normalized to the projected geometric area, they should be interpreted comparatively rather than as absolute area-corrected electrochemical values. In addition, because a BCPs/Ti single-layer control could not be prepared without a Ta2O5 interlayer, the results support the favorable performance of the dual-layer BCPs/Ta2O5 architecture rather than definitive evidence of synergy.

Biocorrosion resistance, in terms of polarization curve, of test specimens in different environments, including simulated blood plasma (SBP), artificial saliva (AF), and artificial saliva containing 0.5% NaF (AF + 0.5% NaF).

In SBP, Ta2O5/Ti and BCPs/Ta2O5/Ti surfaces had similar corrosion potentials which were more noble (~ 0.25 V) than that of Ti surface, while BCPs/Ta2O5/Ti surface showed lower anodic current densities than Ti surface at potentials below 0.3 V. In AS, Ta2O5/Ti and BCPs/Ta2O5/Ti surfaces had similar corrosion potentials which were significantly more noble (~ 0.8 V) than that of Ti surface, while Ta2O5/Ti and BCPs/Ta2O5/Ti surfaces showed lower anodic current densities than Ti surface at potentials below 0.25 V. In SA + 0.5% NaF, Ta2O5/Ti and BCPs/Ta2O5/Ti surfaces had similar corrosion potentials which were significantly more noble (~ 0.7 V) than that of Ti surface, while Ta2O5/Ti and especially BCPs/Ta2O5/Ti surfaces showed significant lower (even less than 1/100) anodic current densities than Ti surfaces in the anodic polarization regions.

Cellular response

Fig. 7 summarizes hMSCs responses to the surfaces. FE-SEM images (Fig. 7A) showed enhanced cell spreading on Ta2O5/Ti and BCPs/Ta2O5/Ti compared with bare Ti after 2 h, with compact cell—cell interactions and pore-penetrating pseudopodia after 24 h. Immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 7B) revealed ordered and elongated F-actin organization on the coated surfaces, particularly on BCPs/Ta2O5/Ti. MTT assay results (Fig. 7C) indicated higher cell proliferation on BCPs/Ta2O5/Ti over 10 d (*P< 0.05; **P< 0.01). ALP activity (Fig. 7D) was significantly elevated on BCPs/Ta2O5/Ti over 14 d (*P< 0.05; **P< 0.01; ***P< 0.001), suggesting a more favorable early osteogenic response.

Responses of hMSCs to test specimens.

(A) Adhesion morphology, analyzed using FE-SEM, showed the cell spreading area tended to increase on Ta2O5/Ti and BCPs/Ta2O5/Ti surfaces after 2 h; the intercellular interactions on the coating surfaces, especially BCPs/Ta2O5/Ti, were more compact after 24 h with the penetration of filamentous pseudopodia into the surface pores, as indicated by arrows. (B) Cytoskeleton arrangement, analyzed using immunofluorescence staining, showed the cytoskeleton arrangement on Ta2O5/Ti and especially BCPs/Ta2O5/Ti surfaces after 2 h was more ordered and elongated, as indicated by arrows. (C) Proliferation, analysized using 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay, showed cells on BCPs/Ta2O5/Ti surface exhibited the strongest proliferation during 10 d of incubation. (D) Early osteogenic differentiation, in terms of alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity, showed the ALP activity on BCPs/Ta2O5/Ti surface was significantly higher than that on Ti and Ta2O5/Ti surfaces during 14 d of incubation. Data are presented as mean ± SD from three independent experiments (biological replicates, n = 3), with three technical replicates per group at each time point in each experiment. *P< 0.05, **P< 0.01, and ***P< 0.001 represent statistical differences between the two groups.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that a dual-layer surface-engineering strategy improved corrosion resistance and biological performance of Ti dental implants. The enhanced corrosion resistance was attributable to the amorphous Ta2O5 inner barrier, whereas the improved biological responses were associated with the combined effects of BCPs surface chemistry, nanoscale morphology, and wettability. Compared with our previous work,22 the thicker Ta2O5 layer and more balanced HA/β-TCP composition may have contributed to these improvements, although their individual effects could not be distinguished.

The hydrolysis—condensation process produced a dense amorphous Ta2O5 coating on Ti (Fig. 1). Owing to the absence of grain boundaries and limited ion transport, amorphous oxides exhibit superior corrosion resistance.28 Accordingly, Ta2O5/Ti showed a nobler corrosion potential and lower anodic current density than bare Ti in all test media (Fig. 6). Fluoride ions in AS markedly increased the anodic current density of Ti, indicating degradation of its native TiO2 passive layer, whereas Ta2O5/Ti and BCPs/Ta2O5/Ti remained much more corrosion resistant, confirming the Ta2O5 layer as an effective barrier against fluoride-induced degradation. Because the porous coatings had a larger exposed surface area than bare Ti, the current densities should be interpreted comparatively rather than as absolute area-corrected values. SEM images (Fig. 1A) showed complex three-dimensional porous morphologies in both Ta2O5/Ti and BCPs/Ta2O5/Ti, indicating that conventional roughness measurements would provide only limited insight. In this system, corrosion protection is attributed to the dense oxide near the Ti/Ta2O5 interface, while the outer porous layer primarily contributes to bioactivity.

The FTIR spectra for Ta2O5/Ti surface (Fig. 1B) showed characteristic Ta—O—Ta stretching at ~600 cm−1,29 along with broad bands at 3200–3600 cm−1 assigned to surface hydroxyl groups. The coexistence of Ta—O—Ta bonds and hydroxyl-rich chemistry indicated the formation of a dense yet chemically active Ta2O5 layer. The absence of Tirelated bands confirmed complete substrate coverage, limiting ionic transport and accounting for the superior biocorrosion resistance of Ta2O5/Ti and BCPs/Ta2O5/Ti compared with bare Ti (Fig. 6).

After formation of the Ta2O5 inner layer, a BCPs coating was deposited to form a hierarchical dual-layer structure. FTIR and XRD confirmed the presence of PO43-/P-OH groups and the coexistence of HA and β-TCP phases (Fig. 1B and C). In addition, the absence of Ta—O—Ta signals in the FTIR spectrum suggests that the BCPs coating uniformly covered the rough Ta2O5 surface, forming a continuous bioactive interface. The BCPs coating comprised nanoscale dendritic crystallites (Fig. 1A) despite deposition at low-temperature 37 °C, suggesting a kinetically driven formation process distinct from conventional high-temperature synthesis (>700 °C).30 A key concern for multilayer implant coatings is whether a bioactive outer layer compromises the corrosion resistance of the underlying barrier. Previous studies have shown that calcium phosphate—based coatings enhance corrosion resistance of Ti, Mg, and NiTi alloys.30,31 In this study, electrochemically deposited BCPs on Ta2O5/Ti did not deteriorate the protective function of the Ta2O5 inner layer. Under all tested conditions (Fig. 6), BCPs/Ta2O5/Ti exhibited lower anodic current densities and comparable corrosion potentials relative to Ta2O5/Ti, indicating that the BCPs outer layer did not compromise, and may further enhance, the corrosion protection provided by the Ta2O5 inner layer.

The enhanced biological performance of BCPs/Ta2O5/Ti is likely multifactorial. Increased protein adsorption and subsequent cell adhesion on BCP/Ta2O5/Ti surfaces (Fig. 5B; Fig. 7A and B) partially resulted from elevated wettability, surface free energy, and nanoscale porosity. 32–34 In addition, apatite formed on BCPs/Ta2O5/Ti within 4 d, whereas no apatite was detected on Ti or Ta2O5/Ti even after 7 d (Fig. 5A). This rapid apatite formation may be related to the coexistence of the more soluble β-TCP phase and the more stable HA phase, which together facilitate ionic exchange and apatite nucleation.19,20,35 These findings support the superior in vitro bioactivity of the BCPs/Ta2O5/Ti surface.

Integrin—extracellular matrix interactions are essential for regulating cellular activities.36 Accordingly, enhanced F-actin accumulation on BCPs/Ta2O5/Ti was associated with greater cell spreading, pronounced filopodia, and more organized F-actin than on Ti and Ta2O5/Ti (Fig. 7A and B), indicating stronger early cell anchorage and mechanotransduction. Consistent with previous reports on BCPs coating,37–39 BCPs/Ta2O5/Ti exhibited the highest cell viability and significantly increased ALP activity (Fig. 7C and D), suggesting a favorable early osteogenic response. However, these findings are not conclusive evidence of osteogenic differentiation because evaluation was limited to early-stage in vitro assays.

Limitations should be acknowledged. Corrosion testing was limited to potentiodynamic polarization in simulated media and did not assess long-term degradation. Adhesion, scratch resistance, and fatigue durability were not evaluated. Osteogenic performance was assessed only by early cellular responses and ALP activity; studies on mineralization, osteogenic markers, and in vivo osseointegration are needed. These findings should be considered as preliminary in vitro evidence.

In summary, a dual-layer BCPs/Ta2O5 coating was fabricated on Ti via hydrolysis—condensation and electrochemical cathodic deposition. The dense amorphous Ta2O5 inner layer enhanced biocorrosion resistance in simulated physiological and fluoride-containing environments, while the bioactive BCPs outer layer improved wettability, protein adsorption, apatite-forming formation, and hMSCs adhesion, proliferation, and early osteogenic response. These results suggest the BCPs/Ta2O5 architecture is promising surface modification strategy for Ti dental implants, although further studies are needed to evaluate its long-term degradation, mechanical durability, and broader osteogenic performance.
