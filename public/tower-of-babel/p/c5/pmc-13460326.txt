Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Allergic Contact Dermatitis from 2-Octyl Cyanoacrylate Adhesives
Authors: Daniel S Rouhani, Ashton Rosenbloom, Steven Zeng, Alexander Sun, Saba H Seradj, Chloe Moshrefi, Kimberly Khoo, M Mark Mofid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:For surgical wound closure, 2-octyl cyanoacrylate (2-OCA) topical skin adhesives are widely used but are increasingly associated with allergic contact dermatitis (ACD). We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to define the incidence, clinical features, and risk factors for 2-OCA–associated ACD.STUDY DESIGN:A systematic review conducted according to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines identified studies from PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science (2008 to 2025) reporting cutaneous hypersensitivity to 2-OCA in human wound closure. Randomized, observational, and case-based reports were included. Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane ROBINS-I and RoB-2 tools. Incidence from analytic cohorts was pooled using a random-effects model with prespecified subgroup analyses by surgical specialty.RESULTS:Seventy-four studies comprising 26,330 exposed patients were included; 20 analytic cohorts (25,442 patients) contributed to the meta-analysis. The pooled ACD incidence was 4% (95% CI 3% to 5%) with substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 94.5%; prediction interval 0% to 12%). Incidence was 4% in orthopaedic cohorts and 8% in plastic surgery cohorts, with lower rates in dermatology and OB/GYN (p = 0.015 for subgroup differences). Reexposure markedly increased risk, with reaction rates rising from 1% to 3% after initial exposure to >20% in staged or repeat procedures in several cohorts. Earlier adhesive contact allergy and cosmetic acrylate exposure were also strong risk factors. Diagnosis was primarily clinical, with selective patch testing. Management typically involved adhesive removal and topical corticosteroids; systemic therapy was reserved for severe cases.CONCLUSIONS:ACD to 2-OCA is a clinically meaningful and likely underrecognized complication of surgical wound closure. Reexposure is strongly associated with increased postoperative reaction rates, supporting preoperative risk assessment and caution in repeat adhesive use.

Cyanoacrylates are widely used as topical skin adhesives in medicine, yet they are increasingly recognized as a cause of allergic contact dermatitis (ACD), leading to erythema, irritation, and impaired wound healing.1 Cyanoacrylates were first introduced into clinical practice in the 1960s with n-butyl-2-cyanoacrylate, which was valued for its rapid hemostatic properties.2 In 1998, the US FDA-approved Dermabond, made from 2-octyl cyanoacrylate (2-OCA), for topical skin closure.3 Although introduced to improve the closure time of superficial incisions, data from the initial 510(k) submission documented suspected wound infection rates of 3.6% in Dermabond-treated patients, compared with 0.9% and 1.2% in 2 suture control groups, prompting early questions about its risk profile. In 2012, Dermabond Prineo was introduced as a 2-part skin closure system that combines 2-OCA with a polyester mesh to facilitate the closure of longer incisions.4 Several additional cyanoacrylate-based adhesives, including Exofin, Histoacryl, and LiquiBand, are now used across diverse surgical specialties.5-8

All cyanoacrylates share a CH2=C(CN)CO2R backbone and undergo rapid polymerization to harden when exposed to moisture.9 The resulting polymer gradually degrades through hydrolysis, releasing cyanoacetate and formaldehyde.10 Formaldehyde is classified by the International Agency for Research on Cancer as a group 1 carcinogen and is designated by the European Union as a category 1 skin sensitizer and irritant.11 Even at concentrations as low as 0.03 μg/cm2, formaldehyde has been shown to provoke sensitization and ACD, providing a biologic rationale for the allergenic potential of cyanoacrylate-based adhesives.12,13

The incidence of these reactions ranges in the literature from under 1% in large, retrospective cohorts to 24% in randomized controlled clinical trials (RCTs).14,15 Earlier exposure appears to be a key contributor to this variability. In a pediatric orthopaedic surgery cohort of 234 patients, Koritz and colleagues16 found reaction rates increasing from 12% after a first exposure to 21% with reexposure, nearly doubling the risk. In staged bilateral knee arthroplasty involving 250 patients, Ali and Makhdom17 reported rates rising from 1% after the first procedure to 22% after the second, a 22-fold increase that reflects the cumulative sensitization characteristic of type IV delayed hypersensitivity. Despite these reports, the true population-level incidence of ACD to surgical cyanoacrylate adhesives remains unknown, as these reactions often go unrecognized or underreported.18

This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluate current evidence on ACD from 2-OCA skin adhesives by summarizing reaction incidence across surgical fields and identifying patient risk factors to better define the clinical burden and risk profile of these adhesives in modern surgical practice.

METHODS

Study design and reporting

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses guidelines. The objective was to characterize hypersensitivity reactions and ACD associated with 2-OCA-based topical skin adhesives used for surgical and procedural wound closure.

Search strategy

A structured electronic search of PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science was performed on January 10, 2025, to identify studies reporting cutaneous hypersensitivity reactions to 2-OCA in human wound closure. Search strategies combined terms for 2-OCA and common trade names (including Dermabond, Dermabond Prineo, Exofin, and LiquiBand) with terms related to ACD, hypersensitivity, allergy, and cutaneous adverse reactions (database-specific search phrases are provided in Supplemental Digital Content 1, https://links.lww.com/JACS/A654). A subsequent manual search was conducted on November 7, 2025.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they reported human patients undergoing surgical or procedural wound closure with a cyanoacrylate-containing topical adhesive, with or without a mesh component or additional cyanoacrylate monomers, and described a cutaneous hypersensitivity reaction, ACD, or other adverse skin reaction attributed to the adhesive. Eligible study designs included RCTs, prospective and retrospective cohort studies, case–control studies, case series, and single-patient case reports. Studies were excluded if they evaluated non-wound-closure uses of cyanoacrylates (eg internal or intravascular applications), preclinical or animal models, in vitro work, systematic reviews, or if the article was not available in English.

Study selection and data extraction

All search results were imported into Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia) for deduplication, screening, and data management. Titles and abstracts were screened independently in Covidence by 3 reviewers (DSR, AR, and SHS) for each record, and articles that met criteria underwent full-text review using the same paired-review process. Any discrepancies at either stage were flagged in Covidence and resolved through discussion, with consensus reached among all 3 reviewers. For each included study, extracted variables included publication year, country, clinical specialty and procedure type, study design, sample size, patient demographics (age, sex, and BMI where available), adhesive formulation, number of exposed patients, number and type of adverse cutaneous reactions and ACD, diagnostic approach (clinical assessment, patch testing, and histopathology), treatment and time to resolution, earlier adhesive exposure or reexposure, and other reported risk factors such as a history of contact allergy or cosmetic acrylate use.

Risk of bias for nonrandomized studies was assessed at the study level using the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies of Interventions tool, and for randomized trials using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool instrument. Reviewers performed these assessments independently, with disagreements resolved by consensus.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize study, patient, and procedural characteristics. For analytic cohorts that reported both the number of patients exposed to 2-OCA and the number who developed clinically significant ACD, we calculated the study-level incidence as the number of events divided by the total number of patients exposed. These proportions were pooled using a random-effects inverse-variance model in Review Manager (RevMan, The Cochrane Collaboration; version 7.2.0). We report pooled incidence with 95% CIs, along with the between-study variance (τ2), Cochran’s Q (χ2), and I2 statistics to quantify heterogeneity. A 95% prediction interval was derived to estimate the expected range of incidence in a new study. Prespecified subgroup meta-analyses were conducted by surgical specialty (orthopaedic surgery, plastic surgery, dermatology, and OB/GYN). Case series and single-patient case reports were summarized descriptively and excluded from the quantitative meta-analysis.

RESULTS

Study selection

The database search identified 986 records (PubMed n = 233, Embase n = 488, and Web of Science n = 265). Before screening, 18 duplicate records were removed manually and 376 records were excluded using automated tools, leaving 592 records for title and abstract screening. Of these, 226 were excluded, and 366 reports were sought for retrieval. After full-text screening, 283 reports were excluded, leaving 73 studies. An additional article identified through manual searching was then included, resulting in a total of 74 studies in the final review (Fig. 1).

Study characteristics

Seventy-four studies met the inclusion criteria, comprising 26,330 patients exposed to 2-OCA-based topical skin adhesives (Supplemental Digital Content 2, https://links.lww.com/JACS/A654). Publication years ranged from 2008 to 2025, with 36 of 74 studies (48.6%) published between 2021 and 2025, indicating a growing recognition of these reactions in recent years. With respect to study design, 32 studies (43.2%) were single-patient case reports5,10,19-48 and 19 (25.7%) were case series.4,6,7,49-64 Twenty-one studies (28.4%) were retrospective cohort or chart review designs,1,8,14,16,17,65-80 and there was 1 RCT15 and 1 prospective cohort81 (each 1.35%). Forty-five of 74 studies (60.8%) originated from the US, followed by the UK (7 of 74; 9.46%), Australia (5 of 74; 6.76%), Japan (4 of 74; 5.41%), and South Korea (3 of 74; 4.1%). Smaller numbers of studies came from Austria, France, Germany, Saudi Arabia, Switzerland or Belgium, and Colombia (each approximately 1% to 3%; Supplemental Digital Content 3, https://links.lww.com/JACS/A654).

Patient and procedural characteristics

Across the 74 studies that reported the total number of exposed patients, the study size ranged from single-patient reports to a cohort of 13,271 patients.65 The mean sample size across all studies was 356 patients. The mean age was reported in 67 studies at 43.4 years, with a median of 45 years and a range of 7 to 82 years, indicating inclusion of both pediatric and older adult populations. The mean BMI was available in 12 studies; the mean of the reported mean BMIs was 29.1 kg/m2 (median 28.0 kg/m2, range 22.9 to 33.2 kg/m2).

Sex distribution could be quantified in a subset of 4,004 patients for whom both male and female counts were available. Within this group, 910 patients (22.7%) were men and 3,094 (77.3%) were women, reflecting a predominance of female patients in the published series, likely related to breast procedures in several cohorts. Orthopaedic surgery accounted for 34 of 74 studies (45.9%),1,5,8,14,16,17,19,22,23,26,33,35,37,38,41,44,49,52,53,58,60-62,65,66,68-70,72,74,77,78,80,81 plastic surgery for 17 of 74 studies (23.0%),4,15,21,24,28,45-47,50,57,59,64,67,71,75,76,79 and dermatology for 11 of 74 studies (14.9%).7,10,25,27,30,31,36,40,42,48,54 Five studies (6.76%) were in general surgery,6,29,43,55,63 3 studies (4.05%) were in OB/GYN,20,34,73 and additional studies were performed in cardiothoracic surgery (2.7%),32,56 vascular surgery (1.35%),39 and endocrine surgery (1.35%).51

Adhesive formulations

Most studies evaluated 2-OCA formulations marketed as Dermabond or Dermabond Prineo. Forty-six of 74 studies (62.2%) used Dermabond alone, and 22 of 74 studies (29.7%) reported the use of Dermabond Prineo. A small number of studies evaluated alternative cyanoacrylate formulations and commercial products. A retrospective study on 281 patients (160 Dermabond Prineo and 121 Exofin Fusion) by Michalowitz and colleagues8 found that the rate of superficial wound complications after total joint arthroplasty with Dermabond Prineo was 19.2% compared with 18.0% for Exofin Fusion, with no statistically significant difference in complication rates between the 2 groups (p = 0.489). Another study by Park and colleagues74 compared the 2-OCA products Surgiseal and Dermabond Prineo (739 patients) with the n-butyl cyanoacrylate product LiquiBand (406 patients), and similarly found no statistically significant difference in the incidence of ACD between both groups (2.7% for Surgiseal and Dermabond Prineo and 2.2% for LiquiBand, p = 0.62). In addition, a study conducted by Bianchetti and colleagues,54 evaluating multiple cyanoacrylate formulations, including Dermabond Propen, Dermabond Prineo, Histoacryl-Octyl, and the dual-monomer adhesive Indermil Flexifuze, demonstrated that all of these commercial products can elicit confirmed allergic reactions. In that series, 15 of 16 patients (94%) showed positive patch test reactions to the specific product used, underscoring that ACD can occur across a range of cyanoacrylate-based formulations.

Meta-analysis of the incidence of allergic contact dermatitis

All 74 studies reported the total number of exposed patients, and 71 reported counts of patients with ACD or other adverse cutaneous reactions. Among the 20 analytic cohorts that reported both the number of exposed patients and ACD events (n = 25,442), random-effects meta-analysis yielded a pooled incidence of 4% (95% CI 3% to 5%; Fig. 2). Between-study heterogeneity was substantial (τ2 = 0.0048; χ2 = 345.9, df = 19; p < 0.0001; I2 = 94.5%). Across these analytic cohorts, study-level incidence ranged from 0.5% to 24% (individual percentages: 0.5%,14 0.7%,65 1.5%,78 1.6%,8 1.8%,75 2.1%,73 2.3%,69 2.7%,74 2.8%,77 2.8%,72 3.8%,1 4.1%,81 4.8%,17 6.4%,80 6.6%,67 7.0%,76 9.9%,70 14.0%,16 14.0%,71 24%15; Table 1). Rates were consistently lowest in large orthopaedic arthroplasty series where ACD was captured as 1 component of broader wound-complication surveillance (0.5% to 2%), and higher in smaller breast/plastic surgery cohorts and targeted allergy studies where ACD was a primary, actively ascertained endpoint with clinical or patch-test confirmation (reported incidences 6.6% to 24%). Among the 51 case reports and case series, 107 patients were described, and by definition, all had some form of reaction.

Subgroup analysis by surgical specialty and risk of bias

Subgroup meta-analysis by surgical specialty is shown in Figure 3. Pooled ACD incidence was 4% (95% CI 2% to 5%) in orthopaedic cohorts (13 studies, n = 24,166), 2% (95% CI 1% to 3%) in the single dermatology cohort (n = 577), 8% (95% CI 3% to 16%) in plastic surgery cohorts (5 studies, n = 511), and 2% (95% CI 0% to 4%) in the OB/GYN cohort (n = 188). The test for subgroup differences was statistically significant (χ2 = 10.5, df = 3; p = 0.015), suggesting that procedure type and specialty account for some of the observed heterogeneity. However, heterogeneity within several subgroups remained high (I2 ≥ 80% in the orthopaedic and plastic surgery cohorts).

Of the 19 observational cohorts evaluated with the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies of Interventions risk of bias tool, 8 (42%) were judged at overall low risk of bias, 10 (53%) at moderate risk, and 1 (5%; Chalmers and colleagues14) at serious risk, largely due to concerns about participant selection and outcome ascertainment (Supplemental Digital Content 4, https://links.lww.com/JACS/A654). The single RCT was rated as low risk of bias in all Risk of Bias 2 tool domains (Supplemental Digital Content 5, https://links.lww.com/JACS/A654). In a sensitivity analysis stratified by Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies of Interventions overall risk of bias, pooled ACD incidence estimates were similar in the low- and moderate-risk cohorts (both 4% [95% CI 2% to 7%]), whereas the single serious-risk study reported a markedly lower incidence (0% [95% CI 0% to 1%]). Heterogeneity remained high within each stratum (I2 = 80% to 96%), and the test for subgroup differences was statistically significant (χ2 = 46.6, df = 2; p < 0.0001; Supplemental Digital Content 6, https://links.lww.com/JACS/A654).

Risk factors, reexposure, and sensitization

Several analytic studies quantified how reexposure and individual patient risk factors modify the incidence of adhesive-related dermatitis and ACD. In the total knee arthroplasty (TKA) series by Ali and Makhdom,17 ACD occurred in 1.0% of patients (2 of 205) after unilateral TKA but in 22% (10 of 45) after the second knee in staged bilateral TKA (p = 0.001), representing more than a 22-fold increase in reaction rates. Koritz and colleagues16 reported a similar pattern in pediatric orthopaedic surgery using Dermabond alone. Skin reactions occurred in 12% of children (17 of 144) at their first documented Dermabond exposure and in 21% of children (27 of 128) at the time of any repeat Dermabond exposure (p = 0.03), representing an approximately 75% relative increase in risk. Among the subset of 51 children who had not reacted after their initial exposure but later underwent a second Dermabond exposure, the reaction rate was 24% (12 of 51). In a prospective hip and knee arthroplasty cohort, Jones and colleagues81 found ACD to Dermabond Prineo in 1.9% (3 of 159) of dressing-naive procedures compared with 8.1% (7 of 86) of procedures with earlier exposure (p = 0.030), representing a 6.5-fold increase in adjusted odds (odds ratio 6.48, 95% CI 1.64 to 25.43).

Preexisting adhesive or contact allergy was also strongly associated with ACD. Ojo and colleagues,1 studying Dermabond Prineo after shoulder arthroplasty, found Prineo-associated ACD in 3.8% (9 of 236) overall, including 50% of patients (2 of 4) with a documented history of adhesive or contact allergy compared with 3.0% (7 of 232) without such a history, yielding an odds ratio of 38.5 (95% CI 2.3 to 667; p = 0.01) on multivariable analysis.

Patch-testing series and dermatology cohorts frequently identified sensitization to multiple cyanoacrylates and related acrylates. In a case series by Bianchetti and colleagues,54 patients underwent European baseline and extended acrylate series testing with application of 2-OCA and n-butyl cyanoacrylate glues, revealing frequent sensitization to ethyl cyanoacrylate and, in some, additional methylacrylates such as ethylene glycol and triethylene glycol dimethacrylate. Earlier cosmetic exposure, especially artificial nail products, was common in certain cohorts, and nail and eyelash adhesives were highlighted as underrecognized sources of sensitization.78

Diagnostic workup and treatment

Diagnostic approaches to 2-OCA-associated ACD varied across studies. After grouping similar descriptions, 31 of 74 studies (41.9%) relied on clinical diagnosis alone, 26 of 74 (35.1%) used patch testing as the primary diagnostic tool, and 9 of 74 (12.2%) explicitly combined clinical assessment with patch testing. One study (1.4%) used biopsy or histopathology alone,42 whereas 7 of 74 (9.5%) did not clearly specify how the diagnosis was established.

Treatment of ACD was reported in 64 of 74 studies and topical corticosteroids were used in approximately two-thirds of studies with treatment information, frequently combined with oral antihistamines in about one-third. Around one quarter of reports described the use of systemic corticosteroids and/or antibiotics, typically in more extensive or severe cases or when secondary infection was suspected. Where time to resolution was reported, most reactions improved over days to a few weeks, with prolonged courses generally associated with ongoing exposure, delayed recognition of the allergen, or more severe dermatitis.

DISCUSSION

Principal findings

In this systematic review of 74 studies and 26,330 exposed patients, ACD to 2-OCA medical adhesives emerged as a recurrent and clinically important complication of surgical wound closure across multiple specialties. In analytic cohorts, ACD incidence ranged from 0.5% in arthroplasty series to 24% targeted allergy studies, with a pooled incidence of 4% (95% CI 3% to 5%) and substantial heterogeneity. Taken together, these findings may suggest that such reactions are not uniformly captured in routine care, potentially contributing to underreporting and variability in reported incidence.18 Across the various studies, several risk factors were consistent. Reexposure to the same or related 2-OCA product, a history of adhesive or contact allergy, and earlier cosmetic acrylate use were associated with several-fold higher reaction rates.

Patient-level risk factors and sensitization pathways

Reexposure was one of the strongest predictors of ACD in this review. Multiple cohorts showed reaction rates exceeding 20% in staged bilateral procedures or repeat mesh-glue applications, compared with rates of 1% to 3% in patients having a first-time application. This pattern mirrors classic type IV sensitization, in which an initial exposure primes T cells and subsequent exposures elicit more rapid and severe reactions. Preexisting adhesive or contact allergy also consistently increased risk by an order of magnitude in available case–control analyses. Other comorbidities, such as atopic dermatitis, obesity, and diabetes, were frequently reported among affected patients but were rarely evaluated in adjusted models.69,74

Cosmetic acrylates represent an increasingly important sensitization pathway. Numerous case reports and dermatology series documented patients who developed ACD to 2-OCA after earlier exposure to artificial nail or eyelash glues, with patch testing confirming cross-reactivity to ethyl cyanoacrylate and other methylacrylates.48,78 Given the widespread use of these products, many initial surgical reactions likely occur on a background of earlier, unrecognized sensitization. Beyond cosmetics, acrylate and cyanoacrylate monomers are also present in a wide range of consumer products, including household “super glues,” model cements, fast-drying primers and paints, and wearable medical devices, broadening opportunities for unrecognized background sensitization.82 Taken together, these findings present the potential for a public health concern due to the possibility of continued growth of population-level sensitization.

Procedure-related exposure helps explain the variation in ACD rates across specialties. Breast and body-contouring procedures require the broad application of 2-OCA and higher adhesive volumes, which correspond to the higher incidence of ACD observed in several plastic surgery cohorts.71,83 Larger treated areas increase total monomer exposure and the extent of skin contact with degradation products, making sensitization and elicitation more likely. Adhesive may also seep between sutures into the interstices of the incision, where it can contact the underlying dermis. Once polymerized in these spaces, the rigid adhesive can form occlusive ridges that interfere with wound-edge approximation and delay reepithelialization.15

Environmental conditions further modulate this risk by altering polymerization and degradation kinetics. Case reports describing ACD after Dermabond use in rooms heated with space heaters, as well as clusters reported in drier geographic regions, support the hypothesis that dry conditions facilitate incomplete curing and extended exposure.39,63 Cyanoacrylate adhesives can also generate airborne exposure. Reports of visible fumes and transient mucosal irritation suggest that vapors reach the breathing zone and may contribute to ocular, respiratory, or systemic sensitization.84 When these adhesives are covered with occlusive bandages or tight-fitting garments, evaporative clearance of these vapors is reduced, allowing reactive byproducts to also accumulate at the skin surface.85

Clinical implications

Treatment of ACD from 2-OCA adhesives first requires early identification and removal, using an adhesive remover or warm water and soap to prevent skin stripping. Most studies describe topical corticosteroids as the primary therapy to reduce peri-incisional inflammation and oral antihistamines for symptomatic relief of pruritus, with the majority of reactions improving over days to a few weeks after treatment, without long-term sequelae. More severe allergic reactions are often managed with a short, supervised course of systemic corticosteroids and closer follow-up to prevent secondary complications.14 Because the inflammatory eruption can resemble early infection and impaired barrier function may increase susceptibility to secondary bacterial involvement, careful clinical assessment for objective signs of infection remains important as part of postoperative surveillance, where antibiotics may be warranted.72

In a retrospective cohort of 3,932 lower limb arthroplasty procedures, TKAs closed with 2-OCA and sutures demonstrated higher surgical site infection (SSI) rates (4.6%) than those closed with sutures alone (1.0%) or skin clips (1.9%; p = 0.003), highlighting 2-OCA use as an independent risk factor for SSI.86 In procedures involving implanted hardware, such as wound disruption and superficial contamination, raises concern for progression to deep infection and prosthetic joint infection.60-62,77 SSIs are among the most expensive hospital-acquired complications, accounting for an estimated $3.5 to $10 billion in annual US healthcare costs. Per SSI costs can exceed $90,000 when a prosthetic implant becomes infected, and reducing the incidence of these reactions remains an important consideration for future research.87

Alternatives to topical 2-OCA include avoiding rapid-polymerizing adhesives altogether and using standard primary skin closure (sutures or staples based on anatomic site and wound tension), with non-2-OCA topical adjuncts applied after closure (paper tape, skin closure strips, or mechanical devices). The literature indicates that omission of topical adhesives does not worsen surgical outcomes. A Cochrane review of 33 RCTs found a higher risk of wound dehiscence with tissue adhesives compared with sutures (risk ratio 3.35, 95% CI 1.53 to 7.33).88 Similarly, a retrospective study of 532 patients (1,048 breasts) undergoing reduction mammoplasty reported higher rates of wound dehiscence (7.6% vs 0.4%; 19-fold increased risk) and major complications (11% vs 2.5%; 4.4-fold increased risk) with Dermabond compared with conventional dressings, with Dermabond remaining an independent risk factor for dehiscence and hypertrophic scarring.79 Postoperative wound coverage ranges from simple bandages and gauze to advanced antimicrobial or silver-containing dressings; however, a Cochrane review of 29 RCTs found no clear evidence that any particular dressing type consistently reduced the risk of SSI in clean surgical wounds.89

Limitations and future directions

This review has several limitations. The included studies used differing diagnostic approaches, outcomes definitions, and follow-up intervals, which limits direct comparability and likely contributed to the wide prediction intervals observed in our pooled estimates. Many of the larger cohorts relied on retrospective chart review, introducing the potential for underreporting of reactions. Methods and outcome definitions varied considerably across studies, limiting direct comparison of incidence and contributing to the heterogeneity seen in pooled estimates. Many reports also lacked detail on key exposure factors, such as adhesive volume, surface area of application, perioperative conditions, and history of earlier use, making it difficult to evaluate dose response relationships or fully characterize reexposure risk. Long-term outcomes, including scarring, recurrent reactions, and the impact on implant-related complications, were also inconsistently reported. Subgroup analyses were limited by the small number of high-quality analytic cohorts within some specialties. Future studies should use prospective designs with standardized outcome definitions, predefined surveillance for postoperative skin reactions, detailed reporting of adhesive use conditions, and comparative evaluation of alternative closure approaches for patients.

CONCLUSIONS

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, ACD to 2-OCA emerged as a frequent, underrecognized complication of surgical wound closure, with a pooled incidence of 4% in analytic cohorts and much higher rates among reexposed patients and those with earlier adhesive or cosmetic acrylate allergy. Across included studies, reexposure increased the likelihood of ACD to as high as 22-fold, reflecting the potent sensitization potential of repeated 2-OCA contact. Our findings highlight the potential for cumulative sensitization in the setting of repeated 2-OCA exposure and raise awareness of the intersection between nonmedical acrylate exposures in the public and subsequent surgical adhesive reactions. Given the consistency of allergic reactions reported across specialties, procedures, and adhesive formulations, 2-OCA-associated ACD should be recognized as an inherent risk of topical cyanoacrylates used in wound closure.
