Association between occupational stress and mental health of nurses during the COVID‐19 pandemic: A cross‐sectional research
Authors: Huiying Wu, Kewen Qi, Bonan Luan, Zhengwen Liu, Qingyun Zhao
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThis study is aimed to explore the association between the occupational stress and mental health among Chinese nurses during the COVID‐19 pandemic.DesignCross‐sectional study.MethodsThere were 2913 nurse participants enrolled in the study. Demographic characteristics and other data were measured by a self‐reported questionnaire. Occupational stress was measured by using the effort–reward imbalance (ERI) questionnaire. Mental health was measured by the Patient Health Questionnaire‐9 and the General Anxiety Disorder‐7.ResultsAmong the participants, 14.32% (417) experienced ERI; 8.1% (237) and 7.2% (210) reported depressive and anxiety symptoms, respectively. Compared with a reference group at the lowest quartile of the ERI, participants in the highest quartile had an adjusted odds ratio (OR) (95% CI) of 11.17 (4.42, 28.25) for incident depressive symptoms. For anxiety symptoms, the OR and 95% CI was 2.28 (1.21, 4.29). The ERI was shown to have a detrimental effect on mental health, especially depressive symptoms.Keywords: anxiety symptoms, COVID‐19, depressive symptoms, nurses, occupational stress

INTRODUCTION

According to a report by the World Health Organization (WHO), about 322 million and 264 million people suffer from depression and anxiety disorders worldwide, respectively (WHO, 2017); these mental health disorders show rising trends in many countries (Edwards et al., 2023). For nurses whose professional responsibilities place them at the frontlines of patient care, increasing job‐related demands or requirements will likely lead to more serious psychological problems, including depression and anxiety (Maharaj et al., 2018). A systematic review study of 22 Chinese provinces showed that 52,592 nurses suffered from depression, accounting for 43.83% (Xie et al., 2020), much higher than Australia (32.4%), Iran (22%) and Vietnam (13.2%) (Maharaj et al., 2018; Saeedi et al., 2017; Tran et al., 2019).

Distress, self‐accusation and sleep disorders are typical symptoms of depression (Tolentino & Schmidt, 2018); anxiety often accompanies muscle exhaustion and distracted minds (Pashazadeh et al., 2021). Poor mental health may adversely affect an individual's professional performance and can lead to serious safety incidents (Loukzadeh & Mazloom, 2013). Nurses play an important role in a healthcare system by providing excellent patient care. Patient care relies heavily on nurses' professional performance (Gatchel, 2018).

Since the 1990s, the effort–reward imbalance (ERI) model has been one of the most widely used theoretical models to assess potential health hazards in the conceptual psychological‐social work environment (Siegrist, 1996). The model assumes a lack of interrelationship between effort and reward in the workplace. More effort to receive less reward produces occupational stress, triggers stress responses and negatively affects mental health (Siegrist, 1996).

In a study that included 1077 nurses from Shandong Province, China, 26.5% reported ERI (Kong et al., 2020). A systematic review found that ERI at work predicted a 1.5‐fold increase in the risk of depression (Rugulies et al., 2017). A cross‐sectional study of 406 nurses found that a higher effort‐to‐reward ratio was significantly associated with depression (Kikuchi et al., 2010). However, another population‐based prospective cohort study demonstrated that exposure to ERI at baseline was not related to the risk of antidepressant treatment in the Danish workforce (Nielsen et al., 2016). The risk factors of poor mental health in nurses vary; studies focusing on the association between ERI and mental health are few and their conclusions are inconsistent. Of special note is that no such study has been conducted among nurses.

The COVID‐19 global pandemic has made concerns about a shortage of healthcare resources quite prominent (Chen et al., 2020). Much research among healthcare workers during the COVID‐19 pandemic found high incidents of mental health problems (Luo et al., 2020; Salazar et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2021). In a case–control study that enrolled 324 participants of healthcare workers, Etesam et al. (2021) found that job stress had a significant relationship with the main dimensions of occupational burnout regardless of where participants worked, either in COVID‐19 wards or general wards. This cross‐sectional study aimed to investigate the association between Chinese nurses' ERI and mental health during the COVID‐19 pandemic.

METHODS

Design and sample

This cross‐sectional study was conducted at Shengjing Hospital of China Medical University from March 2022 to April 2022. It is a tertiary hospital, the third‐largest hospital in China, with more than 6700 beds. Three thousand four hundred and fifty nurses (all nurses working in this hospital) were enrolled in the present study. The nursing department organized this study. A self‐administered questionnaire in the form of an on‐site investigation was adopted. Participants completed a structured questionnaire within 20–25 min. Investigators were responsible for distributing, retrieving and managing the questionnaires, and they explained any unclear questionnaire items to participants. The survey was anonymous, and the confidentiality of information was assured. Finally, 2913 effective questionnaires were obtained (effective response rate: 84.43%). Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: active professional nurses working in hospital. The following exclusion criteria were used: nurses who had been in post for less than 3 months and those who did not provide a completed questionnaire.

Measurement of characteristics

The main contents included the participants' gender, age, body mass index, smoking status, alcohol consumption, coffee consumption (current vs. never vs. former); lifestyle‐related factors, such as monthly household income, history of chronic diseases, etc.; and work‐related factors, such as years of working experience, profession, etc.

A short version of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ), developed by Craig and his team in 2003, was used to measure participants' physical activity levels over the last week (Craig et al., 2003). The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) was applied to assess participants' sleep over a 1‐month period (Buysse et al., 1989), Cronbach's α coefficients was 0.857.

The Chinese version of the Psychological Capital Questionnaire (PCQ) (Luthans et al., 2007) was used to assess participants' levels of psychological capital. The questionnaire has four dimensions, including self‐efficacy, hope, resilience and optimism and the Cronbach's α coefficients for each dimension were 0.921, 0.936, 0.920 and 0.900, respectively. We needed to focus on the interaction between the individual and the work environment, therefore chose the ERI questionnaire for the study (Siegrist et al., 2004). The criterion was that an ERI was indicated when the ERI ratio was >1. The Cronbach's α coefficients for effort and reward were 0.917 and 0.898. Participants' depressive symptoms and anxiety symptoms were measured using the clinically validated Patient Health Questionnaire‐9 (PHQ‐9) (Kroenke et al., 2001) and Generalized Anxiety Disorder‐7 (GAD‐7) (Spitzer et al., 2006). Major depressive disorder was defined when the PHQ‐9 score was ≥10 (Kroenke et al., 2001). Anxiety symptoms were defined when the GAD‐7 score was ≥10 (Spitzer et al., 2006). The Cronbach's α coefficients for PHQ‐9 and GAD‐7 were 0.951 and 0.928, respectively. The Chinese version of the IPAQ, PSQI, PCQ, ERI, GAD‐7 and PHQ‐9 has been validated and used to assess various professional populations (Bai et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2021).

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 22.0 (SPSS Inc.). Data descriptions were chosen as median (interquartile range) or number (percentage) depending on the type of variable. Independent samples Student's t‐test were used for two continuously normally distributed variables; the Mann–Whitney U‐test was used when comparing two continuously non‐normally distributed variables; the χ 2 test or Fisher's exact test was used when categorical variables were present.

The quartiles were classified on the basis of ERI points based on the distribution of all participants' points and were used for further analysis. The relationship between the quartile f ERI and mental health status was studied using binary unconditional logistic regression analysis. Mental health status was used as a dependent variable and ERI as an independent variable.

Raw samples were used to calculate the approximate OR value and model 1 was corrected for age, gender and BMI. Model 2 was further adapted to baseline variables considered clinically relevant or p‐value <0.10 in univariate analysis, including BMI, alcohol habit, sleep quality (PSQI scores), religions, experienced major events (included separation/divorce, death or serious illness of closely family members, serious injury/traffic accident, violence, unemployment, natural disasters, death or serious illness of partner, serious conflict with family, medical disputes, or income decrease/debt.), visiting friend, years of employment, speciality, worktime duration, exposure to the COVID‐19 and PsyCap scores for depressive symptoms; and age, BMI, sleep quality (PSQI scores), physical activity, marital status, siblings, experience of major events, history of chronic disease, visiting friend, speciality, worktime duration, exposure to the COVID‐19 and PsyCap scores for anxiety symptoms. Model 3 adjusted for all baseline variables. Adjusted odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were determined after adjusting for potential confounders by binary unconditional logistic regression. A linear trend across increasing tertiles was tested using the median value of each tertile as a continuous variable. All p values were two‐tailed, and the difference was statistically significant when p < 0.05.

Ethical issues

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shengjing Hospital Affiliated China Medical University (2022PS753K). Informed consent was obtained from the participants prior to the investigation and a commitment to absolute confidentiality by not disclosing information about the subjects. The study protocol complies with the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki 1975.

FINDINGS

Finally, 2913 registered nurses were included in this study, with a median age of 35 years and a median BMI of 21.83 kg/m2; 94.2% of participants were women. Among participants, 14.32% (417) experienced ERI; 8.1% (237) and 7.2% (210) reported depressive and anxiety symptoms, respectively.

In a univariate analysis of depressive symptoms, the participants who developed depressive symptoms have higher BMI and ERI, poor sleep quality and less physical activity, and lower scores of perceived organization efficacy, hope, resiliency and optimism. A higher ratio of participants with depressive symptoms had current alcohol habits, visited with friends rarely, was exposed to the COVID‐19 pandemic, working more than 40 h a week. A lower ratio of participants had experienced major events and believed in religion; almost half of the depressed people worked in surgery and worked for more than 10 years. All the above‐mentioned factors were statistically significant (p < 0.05, except for exposed the COVID‐19, belief in religion and years of employment, which were less than 0.10) and then included in multivariate analysis.

In a univariate analysis of anxiety symptoms, participants with older age, higher BMI, lower ERI, poor sleep quality, lower weekly physical activity and lower scores of perceived organization efficacy, hope, resiliency and optimism were more likely to suffer from anxiety symptoms. A higher ratio of participants with anxiety symptoms had siblings, experienced major events, had a history of chronic disease, visited with friends rarely, worked duration >60 h a week, and exposure to the COVID‐19 pandemic. Most of them were surgical nurses and worked 40–60 h per week. All the abovementioned factors were statistically significant (p < 0.05 except for exposure to COVID‐19, and have siblings, which were less than 0.10) and then included in multivariate analysis, see detailed in Table 1.

DISCUSSION

This large cross‐sectional study investigated the association between nurses' ERI and mental health in China. The study found that 14.32%, 8.1% and 7.2% of nurse participants suffered from ERI, depressive symptoms and anxiety symptoms, respectively. ERI was shown to have a positive association with poor mental health, especially depressive symptoms.

There were a considerable number of nurses who experienced poor mental health. Numerous evidences have shown that during the COVID‐19 pandemic, most healthcare workers suffered from high ERI, depression and anxiety (Kang et al., 2020; Rossi et al., 2020; Sun et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2021). Occupational stress is a worldwide epidemic experienced by a large proportion of the working population. In a multi‐centre cross‐sectional study that recruited 1988 medical workers, 42.5% (845/1988) of medical workers experienced occupational stress; the risk features for occupational stress included the nurse population (Sun et al., 2022). During the COVID‐19 pandemic, healthcare workers in Italy have been exposed to unprecedented pressure and traumatic events. In total, 24,050 respondents completed an online survey. Rossi et al. (2020) found that both depressive symptoms (28.35%) and anxiety symptoms (20.55%) were more frequent in front‐line healthcare workers. In addition, in a cross‐sectional study that included 994 medical and nursing staff working in China, 22.4% and 6.2% had moderate and severe depressive symptoms, respectively. The noted burden fell particularly heavily on young women (Kang et al., 2020).

We found that ERI was shown to have a positive association with poor mental health, especially depressive symptoms. In line with this, a systematic review and meta‐analysis included eight cohort studies and 84,963 employees, Rugulies et al. (2017) indicated that employees exposed to ERI were at increased risk of depressive disorder (1.49, 95% CI 1.23–1.80, p < 0.001). Another multi‐centre cross‐sectional study included 4850 front‐line public health workers from the Center for Disease Control or public health institute during the COVID‐19 pandemic in China, Zhang et al. (2021) also confirmed that the ERI was positively associated with depression and anxiety.

On the contrary, a longitudinal cohort enrolled 4541 participants from Denmark, Nielsen et al. (2016). found that ERI did not predict incident antidepressant treatment (hazard ratio: 0.91, 95% CI 0.81–1.03), which contradicted previous evidence on the association between ERI and depression. However, there were several limitations of this study; first, antidepressants are also used to treat diseases other than depression, and a substantial portion of people with depression do not receive antidepressant medication (Gardarsdottir et al., 2007), thereby it may have resulted in misclassification (Thielen et al., 2009).

The mechanisms underline the apparent harmful effect of occupational stress on poor mental health remains unclear. However, the following reason may be attributed to the observed results that psychosocial stressors at work may increase low‐level inflammation (Duchaine et al., 2021), which plays an essential role in the development of depression (Troubat et al., 2021).

Limitations

The current study had some limitations. First, because of the limited number of male nurses included in this study, gender subgroup analysis was not performed. Second, psychological variables were measured using self‐report questionnaires, which may have led to recall or reporting bias. Therefore, we recommend further prospective studies on ERI and the risk of mental disorders using non‐self‐reported measures and clinical assessments of depression and anxiety.

CONCLUSION

This study demonstrated that occupational stress was significantly associated with poor mental health, particularly depressive symptoms. These findings suggest that healthcare organizations should offer interventions targeting occupational stress to prevent or minimize mental health problems in nurses.
