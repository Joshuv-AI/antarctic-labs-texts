Activation of Nerve Growth Factor signaling limits the response to lenvatinib in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Minghao Xu, Yimin Zheng, Longtao Zhao, Siwei Wang, Junbo Chen, Zhongchi Fu, Aying Ma, Bugang Liang, Yilan Huang, Haihan Xu, Yue Wang, Yingjie Ai, Haiying Zeng, Chao Gao, Jiabin Cai, Aiwu Ke, Jia Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Resistance to lenvatinib remains a major barrier in the treatment of advanced hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), underscoring the urgent need to elucidate the underlying mechanisms and identify actionable therapeutic targets. In this study, we identified a neurosecretory factor derived from HCC cells, Nerve Growth Factor (NGF), as a critical mediator of lenvatinib resistance. Utilizing an innovative in vivo-in vitro cross-circulated strategy, we established a phenotypically stable lenvatinib-resistant HCC cell line (LenR-cells). Through proteomic screening of conditioned media and subsequent functional validation, we demonstrated that NGF secretion progressively increases with the acquisition of resistance. Mechanistically, we uncovered that the SRPK1-SRSF1 axis drives enhanced NGF production by regulating alternative splicing of its precursor transcript, specifically promoting the expression of a shorter, translationally efficient isoform (proNGF-B). Elevated NGF subsequently activates the non-canonical MAPK pathway (MEK5-ERK5) via its high-affinity receptor TrkA, thereby sustaining tumor cell viability and proliferation under sustained tyrosine kinase inhibitor pressure. Critically, pharmacological co-targeting of TrkA with the clinically approved inhibitor larotrectinib restored lenvatinib sensitivity in both patient-derived organoids and xenograft models, producing marked synergistic anti-tumor effects without evidence of exacerbated toxicity. Clinical analyses of two independent patient cohorts further confirmed that elevated NGF expression is significantly associated with poor response to lenvatinib, shorter recurrence-free survival, and worse overall survival. Our findings unveil a critical and previously underappreciated role for tumor-derived NGF in orchestrating adaptive signaling through a precise post-transcriptional regulatory circuit and propose a readily translatable, biomarker-guided combination strategy to overcome lenvatinib resistance in HCC.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), accounting for 80%-90% of primary liver cancer, is the sixth most prevalent malignancy and the fourth leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, representing a substantial global health burden.1 For patients with intermediate to advanced HCC, systemic therapies have become essential, offering significant survival benefits.2 The introduction of molecular-targeted therapies has revolutionized HCC management, with sorafenib being the first agent to demonstrate a survival advantage in unresectable cases.3 Lenvatinib has since emerged as an effective alternative to sorafenib, providing comparable efficacy with an improved safety profile and gaining widespread clinical adoption.4 However, despite these advances, the median overall survival (mOS) has not significantly improved, largely due to the development of acquired resistance. Resistance not only diminishes therapeutic efficacy but may also accelerate tumor progression, ultimately leading to poorer clinical outcomes.5 Thus, elucidating the molecular mechanisms of lenvatinib resistance and developing effective countermeasures are of urgent clinical and public health importance. In our previous research, the activation of kinase pathways and lineage transitions in HCC cells have been shown to induce varying degrees of lenvatinib resistance in HCC.6,7 However, it remains unclear whether resistance arises from tumor cell-derived products through autocrine or paracrine mechanisms.

Nerve Growth Factor (NGF), a prototypical neurotrophic factor, orchestrates neuronal growth, survival, and differentiation via its two receptors: tropomyosin receptor kinase A (TrkA) and the pan-neurotrophin receptor p75NTR (also known as NGFR).8,9 In contrast, proNGF can engage a receptor complex of p75NTR and sortilin, often initiating pro-apoptotic signaling, highlighting a critical balance between trophic and degenerative pathways within the nervous system.10 This intricate receptor interplay governs NGF’s diverse physiological roles. Beyond its established role in the nervous system, emerging evidence implicates NGF signaling in the pathogenesis of several non-neural malignancies, including HCC. NGF has been shown to promote tumor proliferation, invasion, metastasis, and therapy resistance in various cancers.11–14 Elevated NGF expression has been observed in early hepatocytic lesions, with its spatial redistribution correlating with increased tumor size and proliferative capacity during HCC progression.15–17 Despite these observations, the role of NGF-mediated signaling in lenvatinib resistance remains largely unexplored.

Another layer of complexity in the regulation of oncogenic pathways, including those involving growth factors like NGF, is post-transcriptional control. Alternative splicing (AS) is a fundamental and ubiquitous mechanism that greatly expands the diversity of the transcriptome and proteome from a limited set of genes. Through the differential inclusion or exclusion of exons, introns, or parts thereof, a single gene can generate multiple mRNA isoforms that may encode protein variants with distinct functions, stabilities, or subcellular localizations.18 The execution of precise splicing is governed by the spliceosome, a massive, dynamic ribonucleoprotein complex composed of five small nuclear ribonucleoproteins (snRNPs) and numerous auxiliary proteins. It recognizes specific sequences at exon-intron boundaries: the 5’ splice site, the branch point sequence (BPS), the polypyrimidine tract, and the 3’ splice site. The selection of these sites is not deterministic but is regulated by a complex “splicing code.” This code integrates information from cis-regulatory RNA elements—which include exonic and intronic splicing enhancers (ESEs/ISEs) that promote splice site recognition, and silencers (ESSs/ISSs) that inhibit it—and their cognate trans-acting RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) such as the SR protein family and hnRNP family. The combinatorial and often competitive binding of these activators and repressors fine-tunes the inclusion or exclusion of exons, giving rise to context-specific splicing outcomes.19 In recent years, dysregulation of the splicing machinery and resultant aberrant AS patterns have been firmly established as hallmarks of cancer, contributing to tumor initiation, progression, metastasis, immune evasion, and drug resistance.20,21 In the specific arena of tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) resistance, altered splicing can modify the expression or activity of drug targets, activate compensatory survival pathways, and remodel the tumor microenvironment to foster a resistant niche.22 Understanding the contribution of AS dysregulation to drug resistance may uncover novel therapeutic targets and enhance treatment efficacy.

In this study, we embarked on a multi-dimensional investigation to identify secreted factors driving lenvatinib resistance in HCC. Through an innovative modeling approach and rigorous screening, we identified NGF as the most significantly upregulated autocrine factor associated with lenvatinib resistance. We further delineated a precise molecular cascade wherein the SRPK1-SRSF1 axis drives elevated NGF production through alternative splicing, specifically favoring a translationally efficient isoform. The secreted NGF then activates its high-affinity receptor TrkA, leading to a critical rewiring of downstream MAPK signaling from the canonical ERK1/2 pathway to the non-canonical ERK5 branch, thereby sustaining tumor cell survival under TKI pressure. Importantly, we validated the clinical relevance of this axis in patient cohorts and demonstrated that pharmacological co-inhibition of TrkA with the clinically approved agent larotrectinib potently restores lenvatinib sensitivity in preclinical models. Our work not only unveils a previously underappreciated role for tumor-derived NGF in orchestrating a sophisticated adaptive resistance program but also proposes a mechanistically rational and readily translatable combination strategy to overcome a major limitation in the current management of advanced HCC.

Results

Screening for resistance-associated secretory factors

Conventional in vitro models for generating TKI resistance often fail to fully recapitulate the complex tumor microenvironment, particularly the role of angiogenesis. To better model this, we employed an innovative in vivo-in vitro cross-circulated strategy and successfully established a phenotypically stable lenvatinib-resistant HCC cell line (LenR-cells). Briefly, an orthotopic tumor model was generated by implanting MHCC97H cells into the livers of nude mice, followed by daily oral administration of lenvatinib (20 mg/kg), a dose validated as appropriate by our previous studies.7 Tumor progression was monitored weekly using bioluminescence imaging. After approximately four weeks, the largest tumors were excised, dissociated, and cultured in vitro, with lenvatinib added to the medium to maintain selection pressure. This process was repeated for four cycles; wherein resistant cells were re-implanted into new mice to form subsequent generations of orthotopic tumors (Fig. 1a). By the fourth generation (G4), tumors derived from LenR-cells under lenvatinib treatment exhibited comparable sizes to vehicle controls, which indicates that the G4 cells have developed extreme resistance to lenvatinib. (Fig. 1b). Additionally, the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of G4 was only marginally higher than that of G3 (Fig. 1c). During stepwise increase of lenvatinib concentration in culture, drug precipitation was observed at 80 µM, beyond which the LenR-cells’ viability was severely compromised, and stable expansion became unfeasible. These findings suggest that the IC50 of G4 (71.65 µM) approaches the upper limit of lenvatinib resistance. Interestingly, during the gradual induction of resistance, we observed that LenR-cells exhibited transiently increased apoptosis following replacement of the culture medium with fresh medium containing the same lenvatinib concentration. This was evidenced by an increased number of floating apoptotic cells the next day. To quantitatively validate that these floating cells were apoptotic, we performed Annexin V/PI staining and flow cytometry. The results confirmed a significant increase in the apoptotic cell population upon fresh medium change compared to conditioning with the resistant cell-derived supernatant (Fig. 1d). These observations led us to hypothesize that autocrine secreted factors in the supernatant might confer lenvatinib resistance by inhibiting apoptosis. Conditioned media (CM) were collected from G0 and G4 after 48 h and applied reciprocally to the opposite cell populations. Long-term clonogenic assays revealed that CM from G4 enhanced the lenvatinib resistance of G0, whereas CM from G0 decreased the resistance of G4 (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 1a), indicating the presence of key resistance-promoting factors in the G4 supernatant. Given that CM contain both soluble proteins and extracellular vesicles such as exosomes, we evaluated the role of exosomes by repeating the supernatant exchange in the presence of GW4869, an exosome inhibitor. Exosome inhibition had minimal impact on resistance (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Fig. 1b), suggesting that soluble secretory proteins are the principal mediators. To identify these factors, we performed proteomic profiling and differential expression analysis of G0 and G4 supernatants. Among the proteins most upregulated in G4 CM, six candidates (PSPN, NGF, CDNF, MANF, NRTN, and TGFB1) were selected for further validation (Fig. 1g). Immunoblot and ELISA analysis across different resistance generations showed that while MANF and VEGFA peaked at G1 and then stabilized or declined in later generations, NGF expression progressively increased with resistance progression (Fig. 1h-i). Moreover, exogenous administration of NGF alone, rather than VEGFA or MANF, significantly conferred lenvatinib resistance in HCC in vitro. (Fig. 1j and Supplementary Fig. 1c). Genetic ablation of NGF restores lenvatinib sensitivity

To determine whether NGF secreted by LenR-cells is biologically active, we established a non-contact co-culture system, placing G0 or G4 in the upper chamber and PC12 cells in the lower chamber. Co-culture with G4 induced morphological changes in PC12 cells consistent with neuronal differentiation, including reduced proliferation and neurite outgrowth, changes absent in PC12 cells co-cultured with G0 (Fig. 2a). We then generated NGF-knockout (NGF-KO) G4 cells using CRISPR/Cas9 and NGF-overexpressing (NGF-OE) G0 cells via lentiviral transduction (Supplementary Fig. 1d–e). Functional assays showed that NGF knockout or exogenous α-NGF significantly inhibited the proliferation of G4 and reduced its lenvatinib IC50, and vice versa (Fig. 2b–d and Supplementary Fig. 1f). Dramatically, NGF knockout significantly suppressed tumor growth under lenvatinib treatment, while tumor growth rates in untreated mice were comparable between NGF-KO and control groups. Conversely, NGF overexpression accelerated tumor growth regardless of lenvatinib administration (Fig. 2e–f). Serum NGF levels did not differ significantly among groups (Fig. 2g), suggesting that NGF acts primarily within the tumor microenvironment rather than systemically. Immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis further revealed that NGF overexpression promoted tumor proliferation and angiogenesis, whereas NGF knockout enhanced lenvatinib-mediated tumor suppression (Fig. 2h). To investigate the clinical relevance of NGF in lenvatinib resistance, we analyzed tumor specimens from two independent cohorts of HCC patients. Cohort 1 included twelve matched pairs of primary tumors (T1) and recurrent tumors (T2) from patients who had received postoperative adjuvant lenvatinib therapy following surgical resection (Fig. 2i). IHC analysis revealed a significantly elevated NGF expression in recurrent tumors compared to their corresponding primary lesions (Fig. 2j and Supplementary Fig. 2a). Cohort 2 comprised 55 treatment-naive tumors (NT) from patients who had not received any preoperative therapy and 55 residual active tumors (RT) from patients who underwent preoperative lenvatinib treatment (Fig. 2k, Table 1). Additionally, fresh tumor tissue and peripheral blood samples were collected from 20 patients in each group for further analysis. Consistently, NGF expression was significantly higher in RT compared to NT, whereas serum NGF levels did not differ significantly between the two groups (Supplementary Fig. 2b). These findings suggest that NGF exerts its effects primarily within the tumor microenvironment, rather than through systemic circulation. Subsequent IHC analysis of a tissue microarray from Cohort 2 further confirmed the significant upregulation of NGF in RT compared to NT (Fig. 2l–m). Besides, patients with high NGF expression had a higher proportion of stable disease (SD), while partial responses (PR) were more common in those with low NGF expression (Fig. 2n–o). High NGF expression was associated with shorter overall survival (OS) in cohort 2 and an external validation cohort (Fig. 2p and Supplementary Fig. 2c). Single-cell transcriptomic sequencing was performed on 6 RT and 3 NT,23 supplemented by established HCC subpopulation markers,24 leading to the identification of eight major cell types: T cells, hepatocytes, macrophages, monocytes, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, plasma cells, and B cells (Supplementary Fig. 2d). Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) visualization showed similar overall cellular composition between NT and RT (Supplementary Fig. 2e), validating the comparability of the samples. CytoTRACE analysis of tumor cells revealed that cells derived from RT exhibited a more undifferentiated and malignant phenotype compared to those from NT (Fig. 2q). Notably, NGF-associated transcriptional signatures and genes related to angiogenesis were predominantly enriched in tumor cells from the RT, indicating a strong link between NGF upregulation and lenvatinib resistance (Fig. 2r–s). These single-cell insights further demonstrate that lenvatinib-resistant tumors are characterized by enhanced malignancy and angiogenic potential, with aberrant activation of NGF signaling pathways contributing to the resistant phenotype. ERK5 pathway activation by NGF/TrkA signaling confers lenvatinib resistance

NGF exerts its biological functions through two distinct receptors: the high-affinity receptor TrkA and the low-affinity receptor NGFR.9 To identify the predominant NGF receptor involved in HCC, we analyzed TrkA and NGFR expression in the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database. TrkA was found to be significantly more highly expressed than NGFR in HCC tissues (Supplementary Fig. 3a). This observation was validated by IHC in four paired tumor samples with the highest NGF expression from Cohort 1, where TrkA expression markedly exceeded NGFR (which was nearly undetectable). Furthermore, recurrent tumors under postoperative lenvatinib treatment showed increased phosphorylated TrkA (p-TrkA) without significant changes in total TrkA (t-TrkA), indicating enhanced NGF/TrkA pathway activation in lenvatinib-resistant HCC (Supplementary Fig. 3b). To further elucidate the mechanism by which NGF contributes to lenvatinib resistance, phospho-proteomic profiling of G0 and G4 was analyzed, revealing that MAPK and neurotrophin signaling pathways were significantly enriched in G4 (Fig. 3a, b). To simulate the resistant tumor microenvironment, cells were exposed to low-dose lenvatinib and stimulated with increasing concentrations of NGF. Western blotting showed dose-dependent activation of MEK1/2-ERK1/2 cascade in both G0 and G4, though G4 cells displayed a dampened phosphorylation response (Fig. 3c), suggesting involvement of compensatory signaling mechanisms. Given that NGF/TrkA signaling is known to activate multiple downstream pathways, including classical MAPK, non-classical MAPK, PI3K-AKT, and PLC-PKC,8,25,26 we systematically examined these pathways in cells from G0 to G4. While activation of PI3K-AKT and PLC-PKC remained unchanged (Fig. 3d–e), MEK5-ERK5 signaling showed a progressive increase corresponding with resistance development, and MEK5/ERK5 knockout significantly enhanced the therapeutic efficacy of lenvatinib in resistant cells (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Fig. 3c–e). These findings led us to hypothesize that lenvatinib resistance is associated with a shift within the MAPK signaling network, from canonical to non-canonical MAPK pathways. To test this hypothesis, we analyzed MAPK pathway activity across G0 to G4 under lenvatinib treatment. In drug-free conditions, differences between generations were minimal: G4 cells showed slightly higher ERK5 and lower ERK1/2 activity compared to G0. However, under lenvatinib exposure, resistant cells exhibited a marked switch from ERK1/2- to ERK5-dominant signaling (Fig. 3g and Supplementary Fig. 3f). Functional blockade of NGF/TrkA with neutralizing antibodies or the TrkA inhibitor larotrectinib preferentially inhibited MEK5-ERK5 activity over ERK1/2, and combining these agents with lenvatinib synergistically suppressed MAPK activation (Fig. 3h). These data indicate that the development of resistance is characterized by the constitutive upregulation of the NGF/TrkA axis, which pre-adapts cells to rapidly rewire their signaling output. Upon lenvatinib challenge, which attenuates the canonical MEK1/2-ERK1/2 cascade, this pre-existing NGF/TrkA signaling node is preferentially routed to activate the MEK5-ERK5 pathway, which serves as a critical bypass survival mechanism under TKI pressure. To functionally validate these findings, we performed long-term clonogenic survival assays in G0 and G4 cells treated with lenvatinib alone or in combination with pathway-specific inhibitors. MEK5 (BIX02189), ERK5 (XMD8-92), and TrkA (larotrectinib) inhibitors showed strong synergy with lenvatinib in G4 cells but had limited effects in G0 (Fig. 3i–k and Supplementary Fig. 3g–i). Notably, ERK1/2 inhibition (SCH772984) synergized with lenvatinib in early resistance stages (G1-G2), but its efficacy diminished in more advanced resistance (G3-G4). Conversely, ERK5 inhibition demonstrated increasing synergy as resistance progressed (Fig. 3l and Supplementary Fig. 3j). These results suggest that, as HCC cells acquire increasing resistance to lenvatinib, the signaling pathways essential for their survival shift from the canonical MAPK pathway to non-canonical MAPK pathways. In drug-sensitive cells, the autocrine level of NGF is low; therefore, once the canonical MAPK signaling is suppressed by lenvatinib, no alternative bypass pathway is activated, leading to apoptosis. When HCC cells elevate their autocrine NGF production, TrkA receptors undergo autophosphorylation and activate downstream signaling. Consequently, the canonical MAPK pathway—initially inhibited by lenvatinib—can be reactivated through TrkA, and HCC cells can additionally obtain survival signals from non-canonical MAPK bypass pathways, conferring partial resistance to lenvatinib. As the concentration of lenvatinib in the microenvironment continues to increase, the canonical MAPK pathway becomes profoundly suppressed. However, the non-canonical MAPK pathway, having been engaged during the earlier stages of resistance evolution, becomes the major survival mechanism. Previous studies have shown that activation of EGFR signaling limits the efficacy of lenvatinib in HCC.5 To further explore the involvement of the EGF/EGFR pathway during the development of resistance, we examined its activity across different stages of acquired resistance. In early-stage resistance (G1), both EGF secretion and EGFR phosphorylation peaked, but declined progressively in later generations (G2-G4) (Fig. 3n). Consistently, the EGFR inhibitor erlotinib synergized with lenvatinib in G1 but not in G4 (Fig. 3o and Supplementary Fig. 4a). IHC staining of xenograft tumors from G0-G4 also showed elevated EGF expression and p-EGFR levels in early-resistant tumors (G1-G2), which diminished in advanced resistance (G3-G4) (Supplementary Fig. 4b). These observations suggest that EGF/EGFR signaling plays a predominant role in the initiation and early progression of lenvatinib resistance. However, its contribution appears to wane as resistance intensifies and becomes sustained over time, during which NGF/TrkA signaling may progressively assume a dominant role (Fig. 3p).

Alternative splicing enhances the expression potential of NGF

To investigate the molecular mechanism underlying elevated NGF expression in Len-R cells, we first assessed NGF protein stability. Cycloheximide (CHX) chase assays and leupeptin treatment experiments revealed no significant differences in NGF degradation rates between G1 and G4 cells (Fig. 4a–b). Moreover, NGF mRNA levels remained relatively stable from G0 to G4 (Fig. 4c). These results suggest that the increase in NGF protein levels is not due to enhanced transcription or impaired degradation but likely results from post-transcriptional regulation. Given the central role of RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) in post-transcriptional control,27,28 we performed RNA pull-down assays coupled with mass spectrometry, identifying a specific interaction between NGF mRNA and the splicing factor SRSF1 in G4 (Fig. 4d–e). RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) confirmed that this binding was prominent in G4 but negligible in G0 (Fig. 4f–g). As SRSF1 is a well-characterized regulator of alternative splicing (AS), we hypothesized that it may influence NGF splicing isoforms. The NGF gene produces two major alternatively spliced transcripts: a long isoform (proNGF-A, 4077 nt) and a short isoform (proNGF-B, 1061 nt)29,30(Fig. 4h). qPCR and agarose gel electrophoresis revealed a dominant expression of proNGF-A in G0, whereas proNGF-B predominated in G4 (Fig. 4i–k). SRSF1 knockdown in G4 significantly increased proNGF-A and decreased proNGF-B levels, with no effect observed in G0 (Fig. 4l and Supplementary Fig. 5a), supporting that SRSF1 facilitates a resistance-associated switch toward proNGF-B. Deletion of the promoter upstream of exon 1 abrogated proNGF-A expression (Supplementary Fig. 5b), confirming that both isoforms are derived from alternative splicing of a single pre-mRNA. Although proNGF-A is approximately four times longer than proNGF-B, the proteins encoded differ by only 55 amino acids and are ultimately processed into the same mature NGF form in the Golgi (Fig. 4h). This structural economy suggests that proNGF-B may be translated more efficiently, conferring a survival advantage under therapeutic stress. Supporting this, our RNA pull-down data revealed increased association of NGF mRNA with ribosome-associated proteins (RPS2, RPS6, RPL11, RPL18A) in G4 (Supplementary Fig. 5c), and a progressive increase was also observed in their binding across G0 to G4 (Supplementary Fig. 5d). In vitro transcription assays confirmed that proNGF-B produced significantly more NGF protein than proNGF-A in both LenR-G4 and HEK293T cells (Fig. 4m). Additionally, dual-luciferase reporter assays showed markedly higher luciferase activity driven by proNGF-B compared to proNGF-A (Fig. 4n). These findings demonstrate that the proNGF-B isoform possesses superior translational efficiency, contributing to elevated NGF protein levels in resistant cells. Furthermore, RBPmap analysis predicted three potential SRSF1 binding sites near nucleotides 31, 644, and 883 of proNGF-B (Supplementary Fig. 5e). UV cross-linking immunoprecipitation (CLIP) assays identified SRSF1-mediated protection at nucleotide 31, confirmed by Sanger sequencing (Fig. 4o–p). Previous studies have shown that SRSF1 shuttles between the nucleus and cytoplasm, with its splicing function being confined to the nucleus.31,32 Although SRSF1 expression remained unchanged from G0 to G4 (Supplementary Fig. 6a), immunofluorescence and subcellular fractionation revealed increased nuclear accumulation of SRSF1 in resistant cells (Supplementary Fig. 6b–c), consistent with enhanced splicing activity. Functionally, SRSF1 knockdown in G4 significantly reduced lenvatinib resistance, as evidenced by decreased IC50 values and impaired proliferation (Supplementary Fig. 6d–e).

SRPK1/SRSF1 axis regulates the alternative splicing of proNGF-B

To elucidate the upstream regulatory mechanism of SRSF1, transcriptome sequencing was performed on G0 and G4. The analysis revealed a significant enrichment of all five major types of alternative splicing (AS) events in G4 cells, with the NGF gene specifically undergoing mutually exclusive exon (MXE) splicing (Fig. 5a). Notably, the serine-arginine protein kinase 1 (SRPK1) was markedly upregulated in G4 (Fig. 5b). SRPK1 encodes a serine/threonine kinase with a conserved C-terminal catalytic domain that mediates interactions with nucleotides, Mg²⁺, protein substrates, and ATP.33 As a key regulator of pre-mRNA splicing, SRPK1 phosphorylates serine/arginine-rich (SR) proteins, including members of the SRSF family, thereby modulating AS.34 Dysregulation of SRPK1 has been implicated in various cancers.35,36 Consistent with transcriptomic data and elevated NGF expression, SRPK1 levels were higher in both previously reported lenvatinib-resistant HCC cell lines5 and in Len-R cells (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 7a–b). We hypothesized that SRPK1 promotes lenvatinib resistance in HCC by mediating NGF alternative splicing through SRSF1 phosphorylation. To test this, we established stable SRPK1-overexpression and knockout cell lines in G0 and G4 backgrounds, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 7c). SRPK1 deletion significantly reduced phosphorylation of several SRSF proteins, including SRSF1, while SRPK1 overexpression had the opposite effect (Fig. 5d–e). Importantly, SRPK1 loss had minimal impact on NGF protein stability (Supplementary Fig. 7d–f), suggesting its regulatory effect occurs upstream of protein degradation. Functionally, SRPK1 overexpression enhanced NGF production and promoted neurite outgrowth in PC12 cells, whereas SRPK1 knockout or pharmacological inhibition using SPHINX31 significantly reduced NGF expression (Fig. 5f). Molecular analyses confirmed that SRPK1 specifically promotes the proNGF-B isoform and subsequent NGF protein expression (Fig. 5g). Phenotypically, SRPK1 overexpression in G0 cells markedly increased the IC₅₀ for lenvatinib, while its knockout in G4 cells restored drug sensitivity (Fig. 5h). In orthotopic xenograft models, SRPK1 overexpression promoted tumor growth, with the advantage becoming more evident under lenvatinib treatment (Fig. 5i). These in vitro and in vivo results collectively support a strong association between SRPK1 expression and lenvatinib resistance in HCC. Notably, re-expression of SRSF1 in SRPK1-deficient cells failed to restore proNGF-B levels, indicating that SRPK1-mediated phosphorylation is essential for SRSF1’s splicing activity. Conversely, SRSF1 knockdown in SRPK1-overexpressing G0 cells significantly reduced both proNGF-B transcript levels and NGF protein expression, supporting a model in which SRPK1 enhances NGF production via SRSF1-dependent splicing (Fig. 5j). Additionally, SRPK1 knockout impaired nuclear translocation of SRSF1 in G4 cells (Fig. 5k). Consistently, SRPK1 overexpression preferentially activated the TrkA-MEK1/2-ERK1/2 axis under lenvatinib treatment, whereas SRPK1 knockout suppressed the TrkA-MEK5-ERK5 pathway in resistant cells (Fig. 5l), highlighting signaling plasticity in response to NGF pathway modulation. Moreover, exogenous NGF supplementation reversed the effects of SRPK1 knockout, while NGF-neutralizing antibody abrogated the resistance conferred by SRPK1 overexpression (Supplementary Fig. 7g–h). Crucially, SRSF1 overexpression could not rescue NGF expression in the absence of SRPK1, and SRSF1 knockdown significantly attenuated SRPK1-driven lenvatinib resistance (Fig. 5m). IHC results of orthotopic tumors further validated the pivotal role of the SRPK1/SRSF1/NGF signaling axis in mediating lenvatinib resistance in HCC. (Supplementary Fig. 7i). Liver-specific overexpression of SRPK1 was achieved through adenoviral delivery (Fig. 6a). The combination treatment, however, resulted in the most significant tumor suppression. To extend these findings to clinical samples, we performed IHC analysis on tissues from our previously established patient cohorts. Tumors classified as resistant (RT) exhibited significantly higher SRPK1 expression compared to their matched non-resistant (NT) counterparts (Supplementary Fig. 8a–b). Analysis of six paired fresh tumor samples confirmed upregulation of both SRPK1 and NGF in RT compared to NT tissues (Supplementary Fig. 8c). Furthermore, tissue microarray IHC results indicated a positive correlation between NGF and SRPK1 expression levels in lenvatinib-resistant HCC (Fig. 6d and Supplementary Fig. 8d). Clinically, stable disease (SD) was more prevalent in the high-SRPK1 group, partial response (PR) was more common in patients with low SRPK1 expression (Fig. 6e and Supplementary Fig. 8e). Notably, patients with high SRPK1 expression who received pre-operative lenvatinib treatment exhibited significantly poorer OS and RFS (Fig. 6f). scRNA-seq analysis further validated these findings. Using the AddModuleScore algorithm, pathway activity scores were calculated and visualized via bubble plots. Compared to lenvatinib-sensitive cells, resistant cells exhibited strong activation of the NGF signaling pathway, the non-canonical MAPK/ERK5 pathway, and MAPK-Trk axis (Supplementary Fig. 8f). Mapping these scores onto a tSNE plot of malignant cell populations revealed that sensitive cells primarily relied on canonical MAPK signaling (ERK1/2), while resistant cells exhibited a shift toward non-canonical MAPK signaling through ERK5 (Fig. 6g), consistent with our earlier observations. Patient-derived- xenografts (PDXs) and organoids (PDOs) were established using primary tumors with low NGF expression (sensitive) and residual tumors with high NGF expression following lenvatinib treatment (resistant) (Supplementary Fig. 8g–h). Therapeutic responses varied significantly between PDXs: in NGF-low tumors, lenvatinib monotherapy robustly inhibited tumor growth, with no added benefit from combination therapy. Larotrectinib alone had negligible effects. However, in NGF-high resistant tumors, lenvatinib monotherapy was largely ineffective, while larotrectinib exhibited superior anti-tumor activity. The combination treatment produced a synergistic effect, achieving maximal tumor suppression in PDXs and PDOs without severe toxicity profiles (Fig. 6h–k and Supplementary Fig. 8i–k). These findings demonstrate that inhibition of the NGF-TrkA signaling axis effectively overcomes lenvatinib resistance in HCC.

Discussion

Although lenvatinib has offered renewed hope for patients with advanced HCC, its clinical efficacy is severely limited by drug resistance. To better model this resistance trajectory, we established a novel orthotopic tumor-derived cell model. Unlike traditional in vitro systems that rely on chronic drug exposure, this model recapitulates the tumor microenvironment under therapeutic pressure more accurately, incorporating factors such as angiogenesis and cell-cell interactions. Within the tumor microenvironment, numerous growth factors operate within intricate signaling networks to promote tumor initiation, progression, and neovascularization.37 Neurotrophic factors have emerged as important modulators of tumor biology, influencing growth, metastasis, and immune evasion. For example, brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and its receptor TrkB are associated with tumor grade and patient prognosis, exerting pro-tumorigenic effects via PI3K/Akt and Ras/MAPK signaling pathways.38 Similarly, neurotransmitters such as norepinephrine promote HCC progression by enhancing angiogenesis and suppressing anti-tumor immunity.39 Moreover, the density of intratumoral nerve fibers inversely correlates with tumor stage and patient survival, highlighting the relevance of neurotrophic signaling in HCC pathogenesis.40 Despite the relatively low innervation of liver tumors, previous studies have shown that both murine and human HCC cells are capable of secreting NGF.15 In this study, we confirmed that multiple human HCC cell lines express and secrete NGF, and that its high-affinity receptor TrkA is broadly expressed in clinical HCC specimens. This bidirectional communication via both autocrine and paracrine mechanisms promotes tumor proliferation and survival,41,42 offering a rationale for targeting neurosecretory signaling in HCC.43–45 Notably, we observed significant upregulation of NGF in lenvatinib-resistant HCC tissues.

Activation of compensatory or bypass signaling pathways is a well-established mechanism of TKI resistance. Here, we identified the NGF/TrkA axis as a critical driver of lenvatinib resistance, accompanied by reprogramming MAPK pathway, specifically a shift from canonical ERK1/2 to non-canonical ERK5 activation. ERK1/2 and ERK5 are distinct branches of the MAPK cascade with differing activation mechanisms and biological outputs. ERK1/2 is classically activated via the Raf-MEK1/2 axis in response to growth factors and cytokines, regulating proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis.46 In contrast, ERK5, activated by MEK5, plays key roles in stress response, cell survival, and migration.47 Our findings support a model in which lenvatinib resistance evolves in stages: First, lenvatinib treatment itself acts as the initial trigger, creating a selective pressure by inhibiting its primary kinase targets and disqualifying the canonical MEK1/2-ERK1/2 pathway as a robust survival signal. Second, the SRPK1-SRSF1-mediated upregulation of NGF provides the specific driver that fulfills this compensatory need. The constitutively elevated NGF/TrkA axis in resistant cells is poised to respond to this pressure by preferentially engaging the MEK5-ERK5 branch. In this paradigm, NGF/TrkA is the indispensable engine for ERK5 activation, while lenvatinib is the ignition key that forces the cell to depend on this alternative pathway for survival. This model clarifies that while lenvatinib creates the conditions for the switch, it is the tumor cell’s pre-adaptation through NGF overexpression that determines the specific compensatory pathway deployed in this setting. However, tumor resistance is a dynamic evolutionary process, and it is plausible that under prolonged or varying selective pressures, further mechanistic adaptations could emerge.

Both animal models and patient cohort analyses confirmed a strong correlation between SRPK1 and NGF expression. Given SRPK1’s pleiotropic roles in normal physiology, direct inhibition may be associated with off-target toxicity. Instead, targeting TrkA, the receptor abnormally activated by NGF in tumors, may offer a more selective and safer therapeutic option. Larotrectinib, the first clinically approved TRK inhibitor, has demonstrated robust efficacy in NTRK fusion-positive solid tumors, including lung, thyroid, and salivary gland cancers.48,49 Notably, its use is biomarker-driven, guided by TRK activation rather than tumor type. The latest version of the NCCN liver cancer diagnosis and treatment guidelines (2024.v2) has recommended it as a Class 2 A regimen for NTRK fusion-positive patients. Although NTRK fusions are rare in HCC ( ~ 0.17%, 2/1133 cases),50 their frequency post-TKI treatment remains unclear. It is critical to emphasize that our strategy does not rely on the presence of these genomic alterations, nor do we posit that lenvatinib induces them. Instead, we identify a ligand-driven, non-genomic mechanism of TrkA activation that serves as a functional surrogate for an NTRK fusion. Both mechanisms-constitutive activation by fusion and hyper-activation by autocrine/paracrine NGF signaling—converge on the same critical node: the ligand-independent or ligand-sustained dimerization and trans-autophosphorylation of TrkA, leading to persistent downstream MAPK/ERK5 pathway activation. This paradigm, where the state of pathway activation rather than a specific genomic alteration predicts response, has a strong precedent in oncology, as seen with EGFR-directed therapies in lung cancer that are effective in contexts beyond classic activating mutations.51 Therefore, our work argues for a redefinition of the predictive biomarker for TRK inhibitor sensitivity in lenvatinib-resistant HCC from the presence of an NTRK fusion to the evidence of NGF/TrkA pathway activation, potentially assessable by IHC for p-TrkA or NGF overexpression. This conceptual shift could significantly expand the population of HCC patients eligible for this targeted strategy. We speculate that NGF-mediated TrkA activation in lenvatinib-resistant HCC mimics the downstream signaling of NTRK fusions, suggesting a novel therapeutic niche for TRK inhibitors. Furthermore, larotrectinib, as a highly selective TRK inhibitor, is generally well-tolerated, with its most notable side effects being dizziness, weight gain, fatigue, and transient elevations in liver enzymes.52 Its side effect profile does not show significant overlap with the toxicities associated with lenvatinib. Our preclinical models also suggest that the combination therapy did not lead to a significant increase in toxicity. Although preclinical models have limitations in predicting all human adverse events, this preliminary observation is reassuring. In a clinical trial setting, the management of this combination regimen would involve proactive monitoring and management of the known class effects of each drug. This would include regular blood pressure monitoring and management for lenvatinib, as well as monitoring for neurological effects (e.g., dizziness) and liver function tests for larotrectinib. Besides, the timing of therapeutic intervention is a critical consideration to maximize benefits and reduce the risk of adverse events. We envision two complementary, biomarker-guided approaches. First, for a subset of patients identified as high-risk for rapid resistance—potentially based on high baseline NGF/p-TrkA expression in initial biopsies or adverse clinical features—an upfront combination of lenvatinib and larotrectinib could be explored to preempt the emergence of resistance. Second, and likely more immediately applicable within current clinical workflows, is a resistance-triggered strategy. In this scenario, upon confirmation of radiological progressive disease (PD) during lenvatinib monotherapy, a tumor biopsy (or potentially a liquid biopsy) would be performed to assess for activation of the NGF/TrkA pathway (e.g., via IHC for NGF/p-TrkA). For patients with evidence of pathway activation, larotrectinib would then be initiated, either as a switch or an add-on therapy. This latter approach directly targets the identified resistance mechanism at the time it becomes clinically dominant. The flexibility of these strategies highlights the potential for a personalized, biomarker-driven application of TRK inhibition in managing lenvatinib resistance in HCC. To validate this, future clinical trials such as a Phase Ib/II study in patients progressing on lenvatinib, stratified by NGF/TrkA pathway activation are warranted. The combination of lenvatinib and larotrectinib appears feasible given their non-overlapping toxicity profiles, though vigilant monitoring remains essential. Finally, integrating this targeted combination into the current treatment landscape, potentially in sequence or in combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors in light of emerging neuro-immune interactions, represents a critical next step in optimizing therapy for advanced HCC.

In conclusion, our study uncovers an SRPK1/SRSF1-dependent alternative splicing mechanism that enhances NGF translation via production of the proNGF-B isoform. NGF, in turn, drives lenvatinib resistance in HCC by activating the non-canonical MAPK/ERK5 signaling pathway. Pharmacologic inhibition of activated TrkA using larotrectinib effectively reverses resistance, providing a promising therapeutic strategy for patients with TKI-refractory HCC.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and cell culture

The lenvatinib-resistant cell line, derived from the wild-type MHCC97H cell line, served as the primary tool cell in our study. All the HCC cell lines utilized in this research were sourced from the Liver Cancer Institute of Fudan University or purchased from National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures (Shanghai). Mycoplasma contamination was excluded via a PCR-based method. The cell lines were authenticated by short-tandem-repeat DNA profiling. These cell lines were chosen based on their varying levels of drug resistance and NGF expression. The cell culture media was prepared according to the supplier’s recommendations and supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. The cells were then incubated in a chamber maintained at 37 °C with an atmosphere of 5% CO2 and 95% air. Stable NGF-overexpressing cell lines were generated using a lentiviral system. The lentiviral particles and the corresponding packaging services were provided by Genomeditech Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). For transduction, target cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded in 6-well plates. When the cells reached 30–50% confluence, they were incubated with the lentiviral supernatant supplemented with 6 μg/mL polybrene (Sigma-Aldrich) to enhance infection efficiency. After 24 h, the virus-containing medium was replaced with fresh complete medium. To select stable polyclonal populations, cells were cultured under puromycin (1–2 μg/mL) selection for 5–7 days. The overexpression efficiency of NGF was confirmed by quantitative RT-PCR and Western blotting.

In vivo-in vitro cross-circulated strategy for Len-R cell line

First, MHCC97H cells transfected with luciferase and blasticidin resistance genes were orthotopically injected into the livers of mice. After tumor formation, the mice were treated with lenvatinib via oral gavage at a dose of 20 mg/kg/day. At the end of each treatment cycle, the largest orthotopic tumor was identified through bioluminescence imaging. Primary cells were isolated, selected using blasticidin, and expanded in vitro. The concentration of lenvatinib in the culture medium was then gradually increased. The treated cells were re-injected into the livers of mice, and the entire process was repeated for four cycles. Ultimately, lenvatinib-resistant cell populations from generation G0 to G4 were established.

Patients and tissue samples

This study constituted a retrospective analysis of clinical data and tissue samples obtained from patients with HCC who received treatment at Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University. The patients involved in this study were not prospectively enrolled or actively involved in the design, conduct, or reporting of this specific research project. All clinical management, including the administration of lenvatinib and subsequent therapies, was performed as part of routine standard of care, independent of this laboratory investigation. HCC was diagnosed by classical imaging characteristics, regardless of whether there were elevated serum tumor markers. Prior to the commencement of systemic therapy, patients were deemed inoperable if they exhibited advanced-stage HCC, insufficient remnant liver volume (less than 40% of the standard liver volume for those with liver cirrhosis, or less than 30% for those without), or did not meet the up-to-seven criteria. Tumor response was assessed using abdominal contrast-enhanced MRI or CT scans, adhering to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1. All imaging evaluations were performed independently by two board-certified abdominal radiologists who were blinded to the patient’s clinical data, laboratory results, and NGF expression status. Any discrepancies in assessment were resolved by consensus or, if necessary, by a third senior radiologist. Patients achieving a partial response (PR) or complete response (CR) were classified as lenvatinib-sensitive, while those with stable disease (SD) or progressive disease (PD) were deemed clinically resistant to lenvatinib.

All human samples were coded anonymously in strict adherence to the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was signed from each patient, and the study protocol received approval from the Review Board of Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University (Approval No.:B2025-115).

Xenograft tumor model

All mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free facility at the Laboratory Animal Center, Fudan University. The animal experiment protocol received approval from the Animal Care and Use Committee of Fudan University (approval number: DSF-2020-064). Mice were euthanized if they exhibited signs of distress or when the maximum tumor diameter exceeded 1.5 cm. For the orthotopic tumor model, a suspension of 15 µL of HCC cells mixed with Matrigel was prepared and injected into the liver using a microinjector. Adequate analgesia was provided both during and after the surgical procedure. In the case of the Patient-Derived Xenograft (PDX) model, tumors from HCC patients were minced into small pieces (3 × 3 × 3 mm) and subcutaneously implanted into the flanks of 4–5 week-old NSG mice.

Lenvatinib treatment on patients, cells and mice models

Lenvatinib was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, patients with a body weight less than 60 kg received 8 mg/d lenvatinib, while patients with a body weight greater than 60 kg received 12 mg/d lenvatinib. For CDXs and PDXs, lenvatinib was administered at a dosage of 20 mg/kg/d by gavage based on our preliminary experiments. For cell cultures, the concentration was defined as the IC50 value of the corresponding cell line with indicated treatment.

Library preparation and sequencing

Single-cell RNA sequencing libraries were prepared using the 10× Genomics Chromium Single Cell 3ʹ Reagent Kits v3 according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, approximately 5000 FACS-sorted cells were washed three times with 0.04% BSA DPBS and resuspended to a concentration of 700–1200 cells/μL, with cell viability exceeding 85%. Cells were encapsulated into droplets for targeted recovery. After reverse transcription, emulsions were disrupted and barcoded cDNA was purified with Dynabeads, followed by PCR amplification. The amplified cDNA was used to construct 3ʹ gene expression libraries through fragmentation, end-repair, and double-size selection with SPRIselect beads. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq platform to generate 150 bp paired-end reads.

Sequencing and quality control

Sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq platform by Shanghai Applied Protein Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China. Raw BCL files were converted to FASTQ format using bcl2fastq. Quality control included filtering sequences with more than three N bases, regions where >20% of bases had quality scores below 5, and adapter contamination. Only high-quality data were retained for downstream analysis.

Single-cell transcriptome generation and analysis

Raw reads were demultiplexed and aligned to the reference genome using the 10× Genomics Cell Ranger pipeline. Digital expression matrices were generated after unique molecular identifier (UMI) counting. Secondary filtering in Seurat required genes to be detected in at least five cells and cells to express a minimum of 300 genes. After removing foreign cells, Cell Ranger processed FASTQ files through alignment, filtering, barcode assignment, and UMI counting to produce feature-barcode matrices. Dimensionality reduction, clustering, and gene expression analysis were performed with default parameters. Data integration used Seurat’s canonical correlation analysis (CCA). For clustering, highly variable genes were used to derive principal components, and graph-based clustering was performed at a resolution of 0.2.

Cell type identification

Highly variable genes were selected based on normalized expression (0.125–3) and a quantile-normalized variance above 0.5. Principal component analysis (PCA) reduced dimensionality, and the top 20 principal components were used for t-SNE and UMAP visualization. Cell clusters were preliminarily annotated using the GPTCelltype R package and validated using canonical marker genes. Gene signature scores were computed with the Seurat AddModuleScore function. Gene sets were sourced from MSigDB, enabling evaluation of specific expression patterns across cells and subpopulations. CytoTRACE (v0.3.3) was applied to infer stemness based on transcriptional diversity. Higher CytoTRACE scores indicate greater stemness, reflecting increased transcriptional diversity in stem-like cells.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were carried out using SPSS software (version 23.0; IBM), R software (version 3.6.3), and GraphPad Prism (version 8.0). Overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) were plotted using the Kaplan-Meier method and evaluated with the log-rank test. Continuous variables were compared using Student’s t-test or ANOVA, while categorical variables were analyzed using the Pearson chi-square test. Pearson’s correlation analysis was conducted to assess the relationship between two variables. Each experiment was independently performed three times. Statistical significance was considered at a two-tailed p value of < 0.05. The p values are denoted as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001.
