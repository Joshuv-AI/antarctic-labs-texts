Real-World Clinical Outcomes of Sulbactam-Durlobactam Against Carbapenem-Resistant Acinetobacter baumannii with Diverse β-Lactamase Profiles: A Multicenter Study in China
Authors: Chao Liu, Ying Chen, Lu Wang, Junwei Wang, Dong Xu, Zhongju Chen, Chengren Shu, Yan He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSulbactam-durlobactam (SUL-DUR) was approved in China in 2024 for the treatment of carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB) infections. However, real-world clinical data—particularly in critically ill patients and against isolates harboring diverse β-lactamases—are limited.MethodsWe conducted a multicenter, retrospective case series across three hospitals in Hubei Province, China (January 2025 – March 2026), to evaluate the clinical efficacy, safety, and preliminary pharmacoeconomic profiles of SUL-DUR in patients with CRAB infections.ResultsTwenty-one patients were included; SUL-DUR was administered as primary (n=12) or salvage (n=9) therapy. Infections primarily involved the respiratory tract (n=20) and bloodstream (n=3). Genetic profiling of available isolates (n=18) identified OXA-23 (94.4%) as the predominant β-lactamase, alongside the co-production of KPC and ESBLs in a subset of strains. Despite critical illness—with 38.1% of patients requiring continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) and 14.3% requiring extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO)—the overall clinical improvement rate was 57.1%. Notably, both patients receiving SUL-DUR as salvage therapy for bacteremia survived. Mild, reversible renal or hepatic laboratory abnormalities occurred in 28.6% of cases. Pharmacoeconomic analysis indicated that although SUL-DUR carries a higher defined daily cost, patients achieving clinical improvement had a shorter median length of stay and lower total daily treatment costs compared to those experiencing clinical failure.ConclusionSUL-DUR, administered exclusively as part of combination regimens, was associated with favorable clinical outcomes and a manageable short-term safety profile in this small cohort, including in patients requiring extracorporeal life support and those infected with strains co-harboring multiple β-lactamases. These descriptive findings are hypothesis-generating and, given the small sample size, the absence of a comparator group, and the use of combination therapy in all patients, should be interpreted with caution; any apparent pharmacoeconomic advantage associated with early clinical improvement requires confirmation in controlled studies.Keywords: sulbactam-durlobactam, carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii, safety, effectiveness, treatment cost analysis

Introduction

Carbapenems have long served as the backbone for treating critical clinical infections. However, their extensive use has led to a rapid surge in antimicrobial resistance. Carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB) has emerged as a formidable pathogen in healthcare-associated infections (HAIs) and was designated by the WHO as a “critical priority” pathogen for the research and development of new antibiotics.1–4 CRAB primarily causes hospital-acquired bacterial pneumonia (HABP), ventilator-associated bacterial pneumonia (VABP), bloodstream infections (BSI), wound infections, and meningitis, presenting with alarmingly high morbidity and mortality rates in intensive care units (ICUs).5,6 Among these, HABP and BSI are the most common, with mortality rates reaching 45%–70%.7 The intrinsic environmental resilience of CRAB, coupled with multifaceted resistance mechanisms—such as biofilm formation, efflux pumps, and outer membrane modifications—has left clinicians with extremely limited therapeutic options.7,8

Currently, the mainstay treatment for CRAB infections often involves high-dose ampicillin-sulbactam combined with at least one other active agent, typically colistin, tigecycline, or minocycline.9,10 In clinical practice, however, CRAB isolates frequently exhibit extensive drug resistance (XDR), remaining susceptible to only a single agent or a narrow combination, which poses an immense challenge to anti-infective management.11,12

Sulbactam-durlobactam (SUL-DUR) was approved in China in 2024 for the treatment of HABP and VABP caused by susceptible strains of the Acinetobacter baumannii-calcoaceticus complex (ABC). Based on the favorable clinical outcomes from the landmark ATTACK trial, SUL-DUR is now recommended as a first-line agent for CRAB infections.13–16 Nevertheless, real-world evidence regarding the use of SUL-DUR in Chinese patients remains extremely limited. While recent clinical consensus strongly advocates for SUL-DUR combination therapy (particularly with carbapenems) over monotherapy to prevent treatment failure and relapse,17 real-world evidence validating these optimal administration strategies and their pharmacoeconomic implications in Chinese patients remains extremely limited. In this study, we review our clinical experience utilizing SUL-DUR for CRAB infections, with a specific focus on indications, clinical efficacy, safety, and pharmacoeconomic implications.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This was a real-world, multicenter, retrospective, observational case series conducted at medical centers in Hubei Province, China, between January 2025 and March 2026. The study cohort comprised patients with confirmed CRAB infections who received SUL-DUR therapy. Exclusion criteria were: (1) SUL-DUR treatment duration < 3 days; (2) age < 18 years; and (3) incomplete or poor-quality clinical records. The study also aimed to describe the incidence of adverse events documented in the electronic medical records. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of the participating centers prior to data collection. Patient consent was waived due to the retrospective and observational nature of the study.

Ethics Approval and Informed Consent

This multicenter retrospective study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of all participating institutions, including the Medical Ethics Committee of Tongji Hospital (Approval No. [TJ-IRB202602106]), the Medical Ethics Committee of Renmin Hospital (Approval No. [WDRY2025-K180]), and the Union Hospital (Approval No. [2025–1052]). Regarding study participant consent, written informed consent was officially waived by all the Medical Ethics Committees prior to study commencement. This waiver was granted due to the retrospective and observational nature of the study, which involved no more than minimal risk to the subjects. To ensure patient confidentiality, all clinical data were strictly de-identified and anonymized prior to analysis.

Definitions of Clinical Outcomes and Safety

Clinical Improvement at Discharge

Defined as survival to discharge with the improvement or resolution of baseline symptoms (eg., fever, cough, sputum production), supported by radiological or laboratory evidence of infection clearance. Because standardized test-of-cure time points (eg, 14 days post-treatment) were not uniformly available in this retrospective cohort, we utilized clinical improvement at discharge as a pragmatic endpoint. We acknowledge, however, that non-infectious variables may also influence discharge readiness.

Clinical Failure

Defined as a lack of clinical improvement, worsening of symptoms necessitating a change in antibiotic therapy, or all-cause mortality during the index hospitalization.

Safety Evaluation

Renal and hepatic functions were evaluated at baseline and following SUL-DUR therapy. Nephrotoxicity was classified using the RIFLE criteria, while hepatotoxicity was defined according to Hy’s Law (ALT or AST > 3× the upper limit of normal [ULN] combined with total bilirubin > 2× ULN).

Microbiological Identification and Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing

Initial identification of ABC clinical isolates was performed using the VITEK-2 Compact system. Due to the retrospective, real-world design of this study and the recent introduction of SUL-DUR, baseline in vitro minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) for this agent were not routinely measured.

Because SUL-DUR lacks activity against Class B metallo-β-lactamases (MBLs), available isolates were retrospectively screened for major β-lactamase genes (including blaOXA-23, blaKPC, and ESBL determinants) to characterize their underlying carbapenem resistance mechanisms. These assays were conducted using multiplex polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) following standard laboratory protocols. Additionally, mNGS served as an adjunctive diagnostic tool in complex cases where conventional cultures remained negative despite high clinical suspicion, enabling both rapid pathogen identification and resistance profiling.

Economic Evaluation Definitions

To objectively assess the economic impact of SUL-DUR therapy, we defined two primary cost metrics based on electronic billing records:

Defined Daily Cost of SUL-DUR (DDC)

Calculated as the total expenditure on SUL-DUR during the hospital stay divided by the actual days of SUL-DUR therapy.

Daily Treatment Cost (DTC)

Calculated as the total hospitalization expenditure (including all medications, ICU bed charges, CRRT, and supportive care) divided by the total length of stay (LOS) in days.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as medians with ranges (minimum-maximum). Categorical variables are presented as absolute numbers and percentages (n, %). For inferential statistics, continuous variables (such as LOS and costs) were compared between the clinical Improvement and failure groups using the non-parametric Mann–Whitney U-test. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant Given the small sample size and the absence of a comparator group, all inferential comparisons should be regarded as exploratory and interpreted with considerable caution.

Main Demographic and Clinical Characteristics of the Study Patients

The median age was 62 years (range: 21–85), and the median weight was 70 kg (range: 55–111). Males accounted for 81.0% (n=17) of the cohort. The most frequent site of CRAB infection was the lung (20/21, 95.2%), followed by the bloodstream (3/21, 14.3%). Comorbidities were highly prevalent and complex: 8 patients (38.1%) had chronic kidney disease and required continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT); 3 patients (14.3%) required extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO). Among the 18 CRAB isolates that underwent retrospective β-lactamase genotyping (3 isolates were discarded prior to genetic analysis), 17 isolates (94.4%) carried the OXA-23 enzyme. Strikingly, 1 isolate co-harbored ESBL and KPC enzymes, and another isolate simultaneously co-expressed OXA-23, ESBL, and KPC enzymes. The cohort presented with high disease severity, reflected by a median APACHE II score of 18 and a SOFA score of 8 at the onset of SUL-DUR therapy.

SUL-DUR Treatment and Efficacy

SUL-DUR was administered as a primary therapy in 12 cases (57.1%) and as a salvage therapy in 9 cases (42.9%), the latter initiated due to the failure of prior regimens. The standard maintenance dose was 2g every 6 hours, with a prolonged infusion strategy (≥3 hours) utilized in the majority of patients (18/21). All patients received SUL-DUR in combination with at least one other antimicrobial agent, predominantly carbapenems (16/21, 76.2%), including meropenem, imipenem-cilastatin, and biapenem. Because SUL-DUR was never given as monotherapy, the outcomes reported below reflect the combined regimen and cannot be attributed to SUL-DUR alone.

Overall, 12 patients (57.1%) achieved Clinical Improvement and were discharged. Nine patients (42.9%) experienced Clinical Failure, which included 6 cases of symptom non-resolution and 3 in-hospital deaths. Regarding safety, mild, reversible adverse events potentially attributable to SUL-DUR were observed in 6 patients (28.6%), consisting of mild hepatotoxicity (n=3) and mild nephrotoxicity (n=3). None of these events required the discontinuation of SUL-DUR therapy.

SUL-DUR Treatment Costs

Cost analysis revealed an overall median DDC of $1,435.98 and an overall DTC of $1238.03. When stratified by clinical outcomes, the Clinical Improvement group exhibited a shorter median LOS compared to the Clinical Failure group (27.5 days vs 39 days, P = 0.042). Consequently, despite a slightly higher DDC in the Success group ($1,482.67 vs $1,373.73), their DTC was lower ($1,109.34 vs $1,409.60, P = 0.038), as shown in Figure 2. Discussion

The clinical availability of SUL-DUR in China in 2024 provided a critical therapeutic option for managing CRAB infections. This study addresses a notable scarcity of real-world data among East Asian populations. By evaluating highly complex cases—particularly patients with bacteremia or those requiring extracorporeal life support—our findings offer practical evidence regarding the drug’s performance in high-risk clinical settings.

The microbiological profile of our cohort highlighted clinically significant shifts in resistance patterns. Consistent with global epidemiology,18 OXA-23 was the predominant β-lactamase among the tested isolates (94.4%). However, the detection of KPC and ESBL co-production in a subset of CRAB isolates is noteworthy. While A. baumannii resistance traditionally relies on intrinsic efflux pumps and OXA-type carbapenemases, KPC enzymes are typically associated with Enterobacterales. Their presence in our CRAB isolates strongly points to interspecies horizontal gene transfer (HGT) via mobile genetic elements (eg, plasmids or transposons), a phenomenon increasingly documented in multidrug-resistant Acinetobacter species.19,20 This dissemination of resistance determinants complicates empiric treatment and underscores the therapeutic value of broad-spectrum β-lactamase inhibitors like durlobactam, which maintains potent in vitro activity against Ambler Class A, C, and D enzymes. However, because baseline sulbactam-durlobactam MICs were not available for these isolates, the microbiological interpretation linking resistance genotype to treatment response remains speculative and should be regarded as exploratory.

The optimal combination strategy for SUL-DUR remains a subject of ongoing debate. In our cohort, all 21 patients received combination therapy, primarily with carbapenems (16/21, 76.2%; predominantly imipenem-cilastatin or meropenem). This approach aligns with the recent consensus by Bonazzetti et al,17 which recommends against SUL-DUR monotherapy—particularly in critically ill or polymicrobial infection settings—due to the risk of treatment failure. The favorable responses observed in our carbapenem-combination group are supported by established mechanistic synergies: while SUL-DUR targets penicillin-binding proteins (PBP) 1 and 3 in A. baumannii, imipenem provides complementary inhibition of PBP 2.

This dual-target blockade produces a potent bactericidal effect.17,21 Furthermore, five patients treated with non-carbapenem combinations (eg, polymyxin B, tigecycline) achieved a comparable clinical improvement rate to the carbapenem group (60.0% vs 56.3%). Although limited by sample size, this finding suggests that alternative combination partners for SUL-DUR may be viable when carbapenems are contraindicated.

SUL-DUR demonstrated a favorable safety profile in this cohort. We observed only mild, reversible hepatic or renal laboratory abnormalities (28.6%), with no adverse events requiring treatment cessation. The all-cause mortality rate (14.3%) was numerically lower than the 19.0% reported in the SUL-DUR arm of the ATTACK study; however, given the small sample, the absence of a comparator arm, and differences in case mix, this comparison is descriptive only and should not be interpreted as evidence of superior efficacy. This numerical difference is noted only in the context of13 the observed tolerability in this critically ill population. However, given the pharmacokinetic variability in patients undergoing CRRT or ECMO, routine TDM for SUL-DUR should be considered in future practice to ensure target attainment and prevent sub-therapeutic exposure.22–26

From a pharmacoeconomic perspective, although the defined daily cost (DDC) of SUL-DUR is relatively high, patients in the clinical improvement group had a shorter median length of stay compared to those in the clinical failure group (27.5 vs 39 days). This reduction in hospitalization days—which offsets indirect costs such as ICU bed fees and prolonged nursing—contributed to a 21.3% lower daily treatment cost (DTC) in the improvement group ($1,109.34 vs $1,409.60).27 However, these cost differences are inherently confounded by underlying disease severity. Patients experiencing clinical failure likely incurred higher overall daily costs due to prolonged ICU requirements and maximal supportive care. Therefore, definitive cost-effectiveness cannot be established without a severity-matched comparator group.

This study has several limitations inherent to its retrospective design. First, the small sample size (N=21), resulting from the recent approval of SUL-DUR in China, limits statistical power and generalizability. The findings should be interpreted as an early descriptive series rather than a definitive efficacy trial. Second, the absence of a comparator group receiving alternative therapies (eg, colistin-based regimens) precludes definitive conclusions regarding comparative efficacy or cost-effectiveness. Third, baseline in vitro susceptibility testing (MIC values) for SUL-DUR, along with whole-genome sequencing (WGS) or multi-locus sequence typing (MLST), were not routinely performed due to financial constraints and the drug’s recent market entry. Consequently, the correlation between MIC values, clonal lineages, and clinical outcomes could not be established. Future large-scale, prospective surveillance studies are warranted to better map the molecular epidemiology of SUL-DUR susceptibility.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this multicenter real-world case series suggests that sulbactam-durlobactam (SUL-DUR), used as part of combination therapy, may be a useful option for CRAB infections, although the small sample size, lack of a comparator group, and absence of baseline MIC data preclude definitive conclusions regarding its independent efficacy. Notably, clinical improvement was observed even among critically ill patients with bacteremia or those requiring extracorporeal life support (CRRT and ECMO). Furthermore, our findings highlight the emergence of CRAB isolates co-expressing OXA-23, KPC, and ESBL determinants in the ICU setting, against which SUL-DUR retains potent activity. Economically, the observation that early clinical improvement was accompanied by a shorter length of stay is hypothesis-generating; because these cost comparisons are uncontrolled and confounded by disease severity, they should not be interpreted as establishing cost-effectiveness.

A key strength of this study is the provision of early real-world evidence from an East Asian cohort following the recent market approval of SUL-DUR. By capturing a highly heterogeneous and vulnerable patient population—rather than a strictly selected clinical trial cohort—and by integrating clinical outcomes, microbiological resistance profiles, and preliminary pharmacoeconomic data, this study offers valuable insights for utilizing SUL-DUR as both a targeted and salvage therapy for extensively drug-resistant infections.
