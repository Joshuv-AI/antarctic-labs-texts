Pediatric lung abscesses: a 13-year journey from diagnosis to treatment
Authors: Asena Ünal, Özlem Özgür Gündeşlioğlu, Ferhat Can Pişkin, Kamuran Tutuş, Fatma Tuğba Çetin, Emel Bakanoğlu, Gökçe Oğuz, Ümmühan Çay, Orkun Tolunay, Derya Alabaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLung abscess is a relatively rare but potentially serious condition in childhood. It is characterized by a localized collection of pus within the lung parenchyma resulting from infectious necrosis. Although its incidence is low, it presents significant clinical challenges due to the risk of severe complications and the prolonged treatment course. This study aims to evaluate the clinical characteristics of pediatric lung abscesses. We also describe diagnostic methods and treatment approaches to contribute to improved disease management.MethodsThis study included patients aged 1 month to 18 years who were diagnosed with and treated for lung abscess at our center between 2012 and 2025. Data on demographic characteristics, as well as clinical, laboratory, and radiological findings and treatment outcomes, were obtained from patient files and hospital electronic medical records.ResultsA total of 33 pediatric patients with lung abscess were included; the median age was 8 years, and 66.7% were male. The most common symptoms were fever (78.8%), cough (78.8%), and dyspnea (39.4%). The median length of hospital stay was 34 days. Primary lung abscesses accounted for 36.4% of cases, whereas 63.6% were secondary and associated with underlying conditions such as immunodeficiency or bronchiectasis. Most abscesses were uniloculated. The most frequently isolated pathogens were Pseudomonas spp. and Staphylococcus aureus. All patients received antibiotic therapy, with a median treatment duration of 34 days. Invasive procedures were required in 30.3% of patients, most commonly percutaneous abscess drainage. The overall mortality and survival rates were 12.1% and 87.9%, respectively; however, all deaths were attributable to underlying diseases rather than lung abscess–related complications.ConclusionsUnderstanding the clinical spectrum and appropriate management of pediatric lung abscess is essential for reducing morbidity and preventing long-term complications. This study contributes to the current literature by providing an up-to-date overview of the diagnostic and therapeutic approaches used in this rare patient population.Keywords: Child, Infection, Lung abscess

Introduction

Lung abscess is a rare complication of pneumonia that can be seen in all age groups. It is defined as a thick-walled, pus-filled cavitary lesion [1, 2]. Clinically, lung abscesses are categorized into two groups: primary and secondary. Cases occurring in otherwise healthy individuals with no known chronic disease are classified as primary, whereas those arising due to underlying conditions such as congenital malformations, cystic fibrosis, immunodeficiencies, neurological disorders, or foreign body aspiration are considered secondary [2]. The distinction between primary or secondary abscesses is crucial for predicting the underlying disease status and the likely causative microorganisms, thereby guiding appropriate treatment selection. The time to onset of clinical and radiological manifestations also varies according to these factors. The most common causative agents of lung abscess include Staphylococcus aureus, Group A Streptococcus species, Escherichia coli, and Klebsiella pneumonia [3]. Primary lung abscesses may be caused by gram-positive cocci (e.g., Streptococcus pneumoniae and Staphylococcus aureus), gram-negative bacilli (e.g., Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Klebsiella pneumoniae), anaerobic bacteria, or fungi [4]. Fever and cough are the most frequently observed symptoms. Tachypnea, tachycardia, decreased breath sounds on auscultation, and crepitant rales may also accompany the condition [5]. Clinical signs and symptoms alone are insufficient to differentiate between primary and secondary abscesses. Chest X-ray, thoracic ultrasonography (USG), and thoracic computed tomography (CT) are used as imaging modalities for diagnosis (Figs. 1 and 2). Peripheral abscesses can be visualized with thoracic USG. Contrast-enhanced thoracic CT may be required for a more detailed distinction from necrotizing pneumonia, pneumatocele, empyema, bronchogenic cysts, and congenital malformations such as pulmonary sequestration [6]. Imaging also guides anatomical localization for interventional procedures and surgical planning [7]. Mediastinal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) may also be helpful [8].

Antibiotic treatment should be initiated promptly at the appropriate dose and continued for an adequate duration once the diagnosis is established [4]. Treatment is generally completed over a total of 4–6 weeks, beginning with 2–4 weeks of parenteral therapy, followed by outpatient oral antibiotic therapy [9]. Approximately 90% of lung abscesses resolve with systemic antibiotic treatment [4]. Antibiotic therapy should be initiated in accordance with national guidelines, considering any underlying diseases and the local antibiotic resistance profile. Initial treatment must target Streptococcus pneumoniae and Staphylococcus aureus. Penicillins, ceftriaxone, clindamycin, and metronidazole are commonly used for the treatment of community-acquired infections. If methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) is suspected, vancomycin should be added to the treatment regimen [4]. Secondary lung abscesses should be managed according to the underlying disease. Antifungal therapy may be added, when necessary, in patients with immunodeficiency. In patients who do not respond to medical therapy, abscess drainage guided by interventional radiology is preferred [10]. If the interventional procedure fails, surgical treatment may be required [11]. If not treated appropriately, a lung abscess can lead to empyema, pyothorax, or pneumothorax [4]. With antibiotic treatment administered at the appropriate dose and duration in primary lung abscesses, the prognosis improves and mortality decreases to below 5% [12, 13]. Even children who undergo surgery do not experience long-term respiratory complications [14].

Most available evidence on pediatric lung abscesses is derived from small case series or studies including mixed adult and pediatric populations. As a result, comprehensive data addressing clinical presentation, radiological findings, microbiological characteristics, and treatment outcomes in children—particularly with respect to differences between primary and secondary lung abscesses—remain limited. This lack of pediatric-focused data may hinder early recognition and evidence-based clinical decision-making. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the clinical, radiological, and microbiological characteristics of pediatric patients with lung abscess managed at a tertiary care center over a 13-year period and to describe the management strategies and treatment outcomes from diagnosis to recovery.

Methods

This study was designed as a retrospective, single-center observational case series and included patients aged 1 month to 18 years who were diagnosed with and treated for lung abscess at our center between 2012 and 2025. The study was conducted at Çukurova University Faculty of Medicine Hospital, a tertiary referral university hospital that provides comprehensive care for pediatric patients. The hospital serves as a regional referral center, has a capacity of 1,200 beds (150 pediatric beds), and offers a wide range of pediatric subspecialty services. As a tertiary center, it receives patients with complex and severe conditions from across the region, including cases requiring advanced diagnostic and interventional procedures. Demographic data, clinical findings, laboratory results, radiological images, and treatment-related information were obtained from patient files and the hospital’s electronic medical database.

The diagnosis of lung abscess in pediatric patients was based on the following criteria: (1) the presence of an air‒fluid level on chest radiographs; (2) thick-walled cavitary lesions containing purulent material and signs of parenchymal destruction on thoracic CT; and (3) confirmation of a lung abscess by a radiologist at the time of hospitalization for patients lacking a chest radiograph or CT image in the medical records. Patients were classified as having primary or secondary lung abscesses. Primary lung abscess was defined as an abscess occurring in previously healthy children with no underlying chronic disease. Secondary lung abscess was defined as an abscess occurring in children with predisposing conditions such as immunodeficiency, chronic pulmonary disease, neurological disorders, or a history of prior pulmonary infections [1, 3]. Follow-up imaging was performed using chest X-ray or thoracic CT based on the clinical course and the physician’s judgment. Chest X-rays were typically used for routine follow-up, whereas CT scans were reserved for patients with persistent symptoms, complicated abscesses, or unclear findings on X-ray. Radiological resolution of the abscess was defined as the disappearance of the cavity or significant reduction in its size, with resolution of the air–fluid level and surrounding inflammatory changes.

Microbiological evaluation was performed according to institutional practice. Blood cultures were obtained from all patients at the time of hospital admission. Sputum cultures were collected from patients who were able to expectorate. In patients who underwent interventional procedures, additional samples including bronchoalveolar lavage, pleural fluid, and abscess drainage material were obtained for microbiological analysis. This approach prioritized non-invasive sampling at admission, with invasive specimens obtained only when clinically indicated. Anaerobic cultures were routinely requested; however, due to limitations related to sample transport and culture conditions, reliable anaerobic growth could not be consistently achieved. Blood culture results were recorded, but blood culture positivity was not used to define the etiology of lung abscess, as no positive blood culture results were observed. Patients diagnosed with pneumonia, necrotizing pneumonia, or empyema without definitive radiological evidence of an abscess, as well as patients whose with incomplete or inaccessible medical records were excluded from the study.

Ethical approval

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Çukurova University.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), version 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Whether the numerical measurements in the study group met the assumption of normal distribution was tested with the Shapiro Wilk test. Descriptive statistics for parametric numerical variables are presented as the means ± standard deviations (SD), whereas nonparametric variables are expressed as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR). Categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages. The Mann-Whitney U test was applied to compare the distributions of numerical measurements (median). In addition to univariate analyses, a restricted multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to assess whether abscess classification (primary vs. secondary) was independently associated with the need for invasive intervention. Given the limited sample size and number of events, the model included abscess type and age as covariates, which were considered clinically relevant potential confounders. Results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 33 patients who met the study criteria were included. Of these, 22 (66.7%) were male, and 11 (33.3%) were female. The median age was 8 years. The demographic and clinical characteristics of the patients are summarized in Table 1. The most common presenting symptoms were fever (78.8%), cough and sputum production (78.8%), and dyspnea (40.6%) (Table 2). The median length of hospital stay was 34 days. Among the patients, 21 (63.6%) were classified as having a secondary abscess, while 12 (36.4%) were evaluated as having a primary abscess. The underlying conditions among patients with secondary abscesses included hematological-oncological diseases in 8 patients (38.1%), immunodeficiency in 7 patients (33.3%), endocrine disorders in 2 patients (9.5%), neurological conditions in 2 patients (9.5%), a history of previous tuberculosis in 1 patient (4.8%), and a rheumatological disease in 1 patient (4.8%) (Table 1).

Clinical manifestations of the patients (n = 33)

In patients classified as having secondary lung abscess, the underlying conditions were identified either as pre-existing diagnoses or as diagnoses established during hospitalization. Of these patients, underlying conditions were known prior to admission in 19 patients (90.5%), whereas 2 patients (9.5%) were diagnosed during the evaluation and treatment of lung abscess. Newly identified conditions included Hyper-IgE syndrome (HIES) (n = 1) and granulomatosis with polyangiitis (n = 1). Most abscesses were uniloculated (87.5%) (Table 1). Regarding localization, the abscesses were predominantly located on the right side, followed by the left side and bilateral involvement (50%, 46.9%, and 3.1%, respectively). The patient with bilateral involvement was followed with a diagnosis of chronic granulomatous disease. All four patients with multilocular abscesses had secondary abscesses: one patient had cerebral palsy, one patient had HIES, one had concomitant tuberculosis, and one was being followed for acute myeloid leukemia (AML).

When comparing patients with primary and secondary lung abscesses, there were statistically significant differences in white blood cell count, absolute neutrophil count, absolute lymphocyte count, and procalcitonin (PCT) levels (Table 3). Among the demographic and clinical variables, only age differed significantly between the groups. The median age was 5.5 years (range: 0.25–16) in patients with primary abscesses and 12 years (range: 1–17) in those with secondary abscesses (p = 0.041, 95% CI: 0.35–0.42).

Microbiologically, aerobic microorganisms were isolated more frequently than anaerobic microorganisms (n = 9 vs. n = 2). The most commonly isolated aerobic pathogens were Staphylococcus aureus and Pseudomonas spp., while Enterobacter spp. were the most frequently detected gram-negative facultative anaerobes. Culture samples were obtained from various sources, including abscess material, blood, sputum, pleural fluid, and bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluid, and microbial growth observed in 31.25% of the cases. Growth was detected in 5 patients with primary abscesses (methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA) in 2 patients, Enterobacter hormaechei in 1 patient, Haemophilus influenzae in 1 patient, and Streptococcus viridans in 1 patient) and in 6 patients with secondary abscesses (Pseudomonas spp. in 2 patients, Enterobacter cloacae in 1 patient, MRSA in 1 patient, MSSA in 1 patient, and Acinetobacter baumannii in 1 patient). Radiological findings in pediatric lung abscess patients

During follow-up, chest X-rays were performed in all patients and CT scans in 21 (63.6%) patients. Radiological resolution was observed in the majority of patients, with the cavity either disappearing completely or showing a significant reduction in size. Persistent air–fluid levels or other complications were further evaluated with CT as indicated by the clinical course.

With respect to treatment, all patients received medical therapy; the most commonly used agents were intravenous antibiotics (ceftriaxone, clindamycin, vancomycin, teicoplanin, and meropenem). Empiric antifungal therapy was administered in 36.4% of the cases; however, no fungal growth was detected in any of these patients. All patients who received empiric antifungal therapy had primary or secondary immunodeficiency. The median duration of intravenous antibiotic therapy was 34 days.

In this study, 4 patients (12.1%) underwent a diagnostic BAL procedure. Invasive interventions were performed in 10 patients (30.3%). Among these, percutaneous abscess drainage guided by interventional radiology was the most common and was performed in 7 patients. Surgical excision and video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) were each performed in 1 patient, and pleural effusion drainage was performed in 1 patient. Invasive procedures were performed in 5 patients with primary abscesses and 5 patients with secondary abscesses. In multivariable logistic regression analysis adjusting for age, abscess classification (secondary vs. primary) was not independently associated with the need for invasive intervention (OR 0.61, 95% CI 0.12–3.12, p = 0.55). Age was also not significantly associated with invasive intervention (OR 0.91 per year, 95% CI 0.78–1.07, p = 0.26).

One patient had a history of previously treated tuberculosis. Two patients were diagnosed with tuberculosis during the treatment of their lung abscess and were receiving quadruple antituberculosis therapy. While Pseudomonas fluorescens grew in the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid culture of one patient diagnosed with tuberculosis, no growth was detected in the sputum or blood cultures of the other patient. At the end of the follow-up period, 29 patients survived, and 4 patients died. The cause of death in all deceased patients was attributed to their underlying primary disease. Three of these patients had undergone bone marrow transplantation, and one patient had AML.

Discussion

Consistent with previous studies, fever and respiratory symptoms were the most common presenting features in our cohort, although symptom patterns may vary across age groups and clinical settings [1, 3, 14, 15].

In our cohort, patients with secondary lung abscesses tended to be older than those with primary lung abscesses, a finding that aligns with previous reports suggesting age-related differences between these two groups [14]. In our cohort, most clinical characteristics—including sex distribution, length of hospital stay, radiological localization, and the need for invasive procedures—were comparable between primary and secondary lung abscesses. Nevertheless, the distinction between these two entities has important clinical implications, particularly in guiding empirical antimicrobial therapy. Patients with secondary lung abscesses more frequently required broader-spectrum empirical antibiotic regimens and adjunctive antifungal therapy, reflecting the presence of underlying immunosuppressive conditions. In contrast, empirical treatment in primary lung abscesses was mainly directed toward community-acquired pathogens. Although overall treatment duration was similar between groups, secondary lung abscesses were associated with a longer duration of oral antibiotic therapy. The predominance of secondary lung abscesses in our cohort is consistent with previous pediatric studies emphasizing the role of underlying chronic or immunocompromising conditions in abscess development [3, 14]. Prior reports have shown that secondary lung abscesses are frequently associated with respiratory, neurological, and congenital disorders, as well as tuberculosis-related lung disease [3, 14]. In our series, tuberculosis-associated lung abscesses demonstrated heterogeneous clinical presentations, ranging from patients with a history of treated tuberculosis to those with active pulmonary tuberculosis and structural lung involvement, which may increase susceptibility to abscess formation.

In our study, hematological–oncological diseases and immunodeficiency states were the predominant underlying conditions, reflecting a patient population with a high burden of immune compromise. Although the specific distribution of comorbidities varies across studies, the available pediatric literature consistently underscores that chronic systemic disease, structural lung abnormalities, or immune dysfunction substantially increase susceptibility to lung abscess formation in children [3, 14]. These findings highlight the clinical importance of systematically evaluating pediatric patients with lung abscess for underlying conditions, as such factors may influence disease severity, antimicrobial selection, treatment duration, and overall clinical outcomes.

Despite differences in patient characteristics and treatment approaches, abscess classification was not significantly associated with the need for invasive procedures. Although univariate analyses demonstrated differences between primary and secondary lung abscesses, multivariable analysis adjusting for age showed that abscess classification was not independently associated with invasive intervention. These findings suggest that decisions regarding invasive management in pediatric lung abscesses should be guided primarily by clinical course and radiological characteristics rather than abscess etiology alone. Consequently, abscess classification may be more relevant for antimicrobial selection and follow-up strategies than for procedural decision-making.

The higher inflammatory markers observed in patients with primary lung abscesses may reflect a more robust systemic inflammatory response in immunocompetent hosts. In contrast, lower laboratory values in secondary lung abscesses may be influenced by underlying immunodeficiency or chronic disease, potentially masking disease severity. Consequently, laboratory parameters alone may not reliably reflect disease burden in secondary lung abscesses and should be interpreted in conjunction with clinical and radiological findings when guiding management decisions.

Previous studies have reported variable distributions of lung abscess localization, with some suggesting a predominance of right-sided involvement, while others report more frequent left lung involvement [3, 16]. In our cohort, right and left lung involvement occurred at similar rates, and most abscesses were unilocular, with multilocular abscesses observed exclusively in patients with secondary lung abscesses. Although bilateral lung abscesses are rare, isolated cases similar to ours have been described in the literature [17]. In our study, thoracic CT consistently demonstrated cavitary lesions with air–fluid levels, whereas chest radiography frequently failed to detect these findings. These observations support the clinical value of thoracic CT in patients with pneumonia who show persistent fever, clinical deterioration, or inadequate response to treatment, consistent with previous recommendations [3, 18].

Previous studies have reported variable lengths of hospital stay for pediatric lung abscesses, often describing longer hospitalization in secondary cases, although findings have been inconsistent [4, 14, 19–21]. In our cohort, the length of hospital stay did not differ significantly between primary and secondary lung abscesses. These discrepancies across studies may be related to small sample sizes and heterogeneity in underlying or coexisting conditions, which can substantially influence disease course and hospitalization duration.

Consistent with previous studies, the most commonly identified pathogens in pediatric lung abscesses include Staphylococcus aureus and gram-negative organisms such as Pseudomonas spp [1, 3, 14]. In our cohort, microbiological confirmation was achieved in approximately one-third of cases, a finding comparable to prior pediatric series. The relatively low culture positivity rate may be attributed to prior antibiotic exposure and variability in sampling methods, which are well-recognized limitations in retrospective studies of pediatric lung abscess. Importantly, culture negativity does not exclude active infection, and microbiological findings should be interpreted in conjunction with clinical, laboratory, and radiological features. Given the potential for colonization in respiratory specimens, particularly in children, isolated organisms were considered clinically relevant only when supported by compatible clinical findings and response to targeted antimicrobial therapy, as previously emphasized in the literature [3].

The duration of antibiotic therapy in our cohort was generally prolonged and comparable between primary and secondary lung abscesses, consistent with previous pediatric studies [1–3, 14, 21]. Variations in treatment length across reports are likely influenced by factors such as underlying disease, host immune status, and clinical response to therapy. Empirical antibiotic regimens were selected in accordance with national guidelines and tailored to individual patient characteristics and local antimicrobial resistance patterns. In patients with secondary lung abscesses, treatment strategies were further individualized based on underlying conditions, including the use of adjunctive antifungal therapy in selected immunocompromised patients. Importantly, antibiotic therapy alone may be insufficient in certain cases, particularly when abscess size is large or additional complicating factors are present, highlighting the need for a multidisciplinary and individualized treatment approach [22].

Surgical or interventional procedures may be required in selected pediatric lung abscess cases when medical therapy alone is insufficient. These interventions can range from image-guided percutaneous drainage to more invasive surgical approaches such as video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery or lobectomy [23]. In our cohort, invasive management was reserved for a limited number of patients with inadequate clinical response or associated complications. Similar experiences have been reported in previous pediatric case series, in which a minority of patients required surgical intervention, underscoring that most lung abscesses can be successfully managed with conservative treatment [1–3].

In our study, the overall mortality rate was 12.1%; however, all deaths occurred in patients with severe underlying diseases and were not directly attributable to lung abscess itself. Previous pediatric studies have shown that abscess-related mortality has markedly decreased with advances in antimicrobial therapy and is generally reported to be below 5% [12, 13]. Consistent with these reports, mortality in our cohort appears to reflect the severity of underlying comorbid conditions rather than complications directly related to lung abscess.

Our study has several limitations. The most important limitation is its single-center, retrospective design. Due to limited available data, the exact timing of symptom resolution and the onset of clinical improvement could not be fully assessed. In addition, there was a lack of standardization in culture timing and sample type, and some longitudinal laboratory data were missing. The relatively low detection rate of microbial growth may have been influenced by prior antibiotic use and variations in sampling methods. Antibiotic regimens varied among patients, and the small sample size within each subgroup restricted the depth of statistical analysis and may limit the generalizability of our findings. Although a restricted multivariable analysis was performed, the relatively small number of events limited the inclusion of additional covariates such as comorbidity burden or laboratory parameters; therefore, residual confounding cannot be completely excluded and the findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Conclusion

Pediatric lung abscess is a rare but clinically significant condition associated with substantial morbidity, particularly in children with underlying diseases. Delays in diagnosis may result in prolonged treatment courses and an increased need for invasive interventions. Both our findings and previous reports underscore the importance of early recognition and timely management to optimize clinical outcomes. Increasing physician awareness of pediatric lung abscess is therefore essential to reduce complications. However, optimal antimicrobial selection and treatment duration remain uncertain. Larger, multicenter studies are needed to better define evidence-based treatment strategies, including empirical antibiotic choices, duration of therapy, and indications for invasive management.
