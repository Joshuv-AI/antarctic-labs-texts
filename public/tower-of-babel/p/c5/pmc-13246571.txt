Quadruple reinforcement technique for pancreatic stump during robotic distal pancreatectomy
Authors: Satoru Abe, Yoshikuni Kawaguchi, Kyoji Ito, Sho Kiritani, Yusuke Kazami, Yujiro Nishioka, Akihiko Ichida, Takeshi Takamoto, Kiyoshi Hasegawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostoperative pancreatic fistula (POPF) remains a significant challenge in robotic distal pancreatectomy (RDP). We developed a novel “quadruple reinforcement technique” to mitigate this risk and evaluated its clinical efficacy compared to conventional techniques.MethodsIn this prospective study of 44 consecutive RDP patients (March 2020–September 2025), outcomes were compared between a quadruple reinforcement group (n = 27) and a control group (n = 17). The reinforcement technique involved: (1) staple line reinforcement; (2) absorbable reinforcement felt; (3) U-shaped sutures using a 3–0 non-absorbable double-armed monofilament; and (4) sprayed fibrin sealant. Clinically relevant POPF (CR-POPF) was defined per 2016 ISGPS criteria.ResultsPancreatic texture was comparable between the groups. The quadruple reinforcement group demonstrated a significantly lower incidence of CR-POPF (3.7% vs. 41.2%, P = 0.002) and fewer complications ≥ Clavien-Dindo grade IIIa (0% vs. 23.5%, P = 0.004) compared to controls. Median hospital stay was significantly shorter in the reinforcement group (5.0 vs. 7.0 days, P = 0.007). There were no 30-day reoperations or 90-day mortalities in either group.ConclusionsThe quadruple reinforcement technique is a safe and feasible strategy that significantly reduces CR-POPF and major complications. This systematic combination effectively optimizes postoperative outcomes in RDP.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00464-026-12744-2.Keywords: Robotic distal pancreatectomy, Pancreatic fistula, Delayed gastric emptying

Distal pancreatectomy (DP) is a standard and well-established procedure for the resection of tumors located in the pancreatic body and tail [1]. In recent years, the adoption of minimally invasive surgery, including laparoscopic DP (LDP) and robotic DP (RDP), has increased in parallel with advancements in surgical technology [2, 3]. Compared to open distal pancreatectomy (ODP), LDP and RDP offer benefits such as early postoperative recovery and improved cosmetic outcomes [4–7]. Studies reported that RDP was associated with a lower rate of open conversion [5, 6, 8], shorter hospital stay [5–7], and less blood loss [7], when compared with LDP.

Despite these technical advancements, postoperative pancreatic fistula (POPF) remains a persistent and unresolved complication. A systematic review of 43 studies showed a 20.2% incidence of clinically relevant POPF (CR-POPF) [9], with no significant difference in rates among RDP, LPD, and OPD [5, 10]. POPF causes increased morbidity and mortality, prolonged hospital stay, and increased healthcare costs [11, 12]. Various surgical techniques for pancreatic transection and stump reinforcement have been proposed to reduce POPF after DP [13, 14]. Nonetheless, achieving a low incidence of CR-POPF remains a significant challenge. To address this clinical need, we developed a quadruple reinforcement technique for the pancreatic stump during RDP.

In this study, we describe our RDP approach in detail, with particular emphasis on the method of pancreatic division and the reinforcement of the pancreatic stump. We compared surgical and postoperative outcomes between patients who underwent our new reinforcement technique and those treated with conventional techniques.

Methods

Patients

From a prospectively maintained database, we identified consecutive patients who underwent RDP at The University of Tokyo from April 2022 to October 2024. During this period, patients eligible for minimally invasive surgery were prioritized for RDP unless specific contraindications existed (e.g., highly advanced pancreatic cancer requiring vascular resection, lack of robotic slots). Open or laparoscopic surgeries performed during this period were excluded from this analysis to focus on the robotic platform. All operations were performed after obtaining informed consent from each patient. The Institutional Review Board of The University of Tokyo approved the current study (no. 2158–11).

Definitions

The American Society of Anesthesiologists classification[15] was used to determine patients’ preoperative physical fitness and recorded based on anesthesiologists’ preoperative assessment. CR–POPF was defined based on the 2016 updated International Study Group of Pancreatic Surgery (ISGPS) definitions [16]. Drainage tubes were typically removed during outpatient clinic visits. In cases where patients were unable to attend the clinic before POD 21, drainage tube removal was deferred. When drains were removed between PODs 22 and 27, the delay was attributed to scheduling constraints rather than the clinical suspicion of pancreatic fistula. Therefore, these patients (i.e., patients with drain removal during POD 22–27) were categorized as having Grade BL CR-POPF. The definition of postoperative complications was as follows: any deviation from the normal postoperative course within 90 days after resection. The Clavien-Dindo (C­D) classification was used to grade the significance of complications [17]. The diameter of the main pancreatic duct (MPD) was measured on a preoperative contrast enhanced CT image.

Pancreatic texture was categorized as either soft or hard by three independent surgeons (S.A, K.I, and Y.K). The assessment was based on several features identifiable in the surgical videos, including the presence of pancreatic atrophy, the degree of pancreatic deformation during surgical manipulation, and the depth of indentation after a pre-compression using intestinal clips. The final texture categorization was determined through consensus among the evaluators.

Setting of robotic distal pancreatectomy

Patients are placed in a split-leg supine position (head–up 10 degrees). A LapShield™ and LapBase™ (Pro–seed Corporation, Japan) were used at the 5–cm umbilicus incision, and two robot trocars and an assistant trocar were inserted [18]. Two other robot trocars were placed to shape hemi–circle to the object. A robotic camera was inserted into port 3, and two Maryland bipolar forceps were set as the left (port 2) and right arms (port 3), and Cadiere forceps were set as the retraction arm (port 1) (Fig. 1). Depending on the console surgeon’s preference, Fenestrated bipolar forceps was set as the left (port2).

Setting of robotic distal pancreatectomy: 5-cm umbilicus incision + 2 trocars M, Maryland bipolar forceps; C, Cadiere forceps; A, Assistant trocar A single 5-cm vertical incision is made at the umbilicus, into which a wound retractor (LapShield™: Pro-Seed Cooperation, Japan) and a seal cap (LapBase™: Pro-Seed Cooperation) are placed. Through this single 5-cm access platform, we insert two robotic trocars and one assistant trocar. This allows for simple trocar placement, instrument clash reduction, and easy specimen retrieval, as detailed in our previous report [18]

Surgical procedures of robotic distal pancreatectomy

Following incision of the greater omentum, the stomach was retracted for exposure of the pancreas. Regional lymph nodes (#7, #8a, #9) were dissected for malignant cases. The splenic artery (SPA) was identified by incising the suprapancreatic retroperitoneum, and divided after confirming adequate intrahepatic blood flow by temporal clamping. The splenic and/or superior mesenteric veins were identified and dissected inferiorly. The pancreas was taped at the planned transection line and compressed for at least 15 min using intestinal clips (Fig. 2a). After marking the transection line (Fig. 2b), the pancreas was divided over at least 5 min using a 60-mm linear stapler with staple line reinforcement (Technology Reloads Black 60-mm and ECHELON ENDOPATH™ Staple Line Reinforcement, used with Powered ECHELON FLEX® 3000, ECHELON™ Gripping Surface; ETHICON, Johnson & Johnson, Cincinnati, OH, USA, or Tri-Staple™ 2.0 Black 60 mm Reinforced Reload, used with Signia™ Stapling System; Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA) (Fig. 2c). The choice of two staplers and reinforcement materials was primarily based on surgeon’s preference and institutional product availability, rather than a deliberate selection based on patient characteristics. Finally, the pancreatic body and tail were dissected from the posterior tissues, including or excluding retroperitoneal tissue based on tumor invasion.

Quadruple reinforcement technique of the pancreatic stump

After the specimen was removed from the abdominal cavity, the pancreatic stump dividied using staple line reinforcement (Single reinforcement) (Fig. 3a) was further reinforced to prevent pancreatic fistula after the 18th patient of our consecutive experiences. First, the pancreatic stump was covered with an absorbable reinforcement felt made of polyglycolic acid (PGA) (NEOVEIL™ SHEET; GUNZE MEDICAL LIMITE, Osaka, Japan) (Double reinforcement) (Fig. 3b).

Pancreatic stump reinforcement. a Single reinforcement: The pancreas was divided using a 60-mm linear stapler with staple line reinforcement. b Double reinforcement: The pancreatic stump was covered with an absorbable reinforcement felt made of polyglycolic acid. c–d Triple reinforcement: The location of the MPD was confirmed by intraoperative ultrasonography, and the pancreatic stump was sutured to form a U-shape surrounding the MPD and ligated with a 3–0 non-absorbable double–armed monofilament. e Quadruple reinforcement: An absorbable reinforcement felt was covered, and a fibrin sealant was splayed on the pancreatic stump and the splenic vessels

Second, the location of the MPD was verified by intraoperative ultrasonography. Importantly, the MPD was identifiable in all cases, including those involving a soft pancreas. The pancreatic stump was sutured to form a U-shape surrounding the MPD with a 3–0 non-absorbable double–armed monofilament (3–0 ASFLEX™; Kono Seisakusho Co.,Ltd., Chiba, Japan) over a width of approximately 15 mm and ligated to gently make a tie (Triple reinforcement) (Fig. 3c, d). A microporous polysaccharide hemosphere-based hemostat (Arista™ Absorbable Hemostat; Becton, Dickinson and Company, New Jersey, USA) was spread around the pancreatic stump and splenic vessels for hemostasis. Finally, we covered the splenic vessels with an absorbable reinforcement felt and sprayed a fibrin sealant on the pancreatic stump and the splenic vessels (Quadruple reinforcement) (Fig. 3e).

We termed our technique as the quadruple reinforcement technique because the pancreatic stump was reinforced with (1) a staple line reinforcement, (2) an absorbable reinforcement felt, (3) a U-shaped suture, and (4) a sprayed fibrin sealant. After completion of the distal pancreatectomy, two drainage tubes were placed at the pancreatic stump.

Postoperative management

We implemented our Enhanced Recovery After Surgery program, termed as the Fast–Track Return to Normal Activity (FASTOL) program in patients undergoing minimally invasive surgery [18–20]. The target of hospital discharge after RDP was set on PODs 4 to 6. The following standardized protocols were used. Oral intake and mobilization exercises were initiated on postoperative day (POD) 1. Supportive peripheral parenteral nutrition was subscribed to target 20 kcal/kg and 0.8 g/kg of amino acid per day during PODs 1–4. Epidural anesthesia up to POD 3 and/or intravenous and oral analgesia were used. Hydrocortisone was administered immediately after surgery, 100 mg on PODs 1–2 and 50 mg on PODs 3. Criteria for determining hospital discharge were as follows: postoperative complications were not observed, intravenous analgesia was not needed, and the patients could conduct activities of daily living. The drainage tube placed at the pancreatic stump was removed up to POD 4 if patients did not have an elevated amylase level upon discharge from the drainage tubes. If patients showed grade BL POPF based on ISGPS definition, one of the two drainage tubes placed was cut short and left inserted, and the patient was discharged. If the nature of the discharge was not infection and blood tests showed no evidence of inflammation, the drainage tube was removed gradually at two separate outpatient clinics appointed before PODs 21.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables, expressed as numbers and percentages, were compared using Fisher’s exact test or χ2 test as appropriate. Continuous variables are presented as median (interquartile range, IQR), and were compared using Wilcoxon’s rank-sum test. Statistical analysis was performed using R version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Patient characteristics

From March 2020 to September 2025, 44 consecutive patients underwent RDP. The quadruple reinforcement technique was introduced after the 17th patient in our consecutive experience. Patients who underwent the quadruple reinforcement technique (the 18th patient and later) were termed the quadruple reinforcement group (n = 27). The remaining patients (the first 17 patients) were termed the control group (n = 17). Patient demographics and clinical characteristics are shown in Table 1. The common diagnoses were IPMN (37.0%) and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) (22.2%) in the quadruple reinforcement group, and IPMN (52.9%) and MCN (35.3%) in the control group.

Surgical and postoperative outcomes

A total of 9 surgeons, including 5 attending surgeons and 4 fellows, acted as console surgeons. The rate of fellows for a console surgeon role (with instruction from experienced console surgeons) was 70.4% in the quadruple reinforcement group and 23.5% in the control group. The operation time and estimated blood loss did not differ significantly between the groups. The rate of soft pancreatic texture was not significantly different between the quadruple reinforcement and control groups: 18 (66.7%) vs. 10 (58.8%), P = 0.599. There were no significant differences regarding the pancreatic parenchymal thickness (12 mm [7–16] vs. 11 mm [8–17], P = 0.845) and the main pancreatic duct diameter (1.0 mm [1.0–2.0] vs. 1.0 mm [1.0–2.0], P = 0.551) between the quadruple reinforcement and control groups. The incidence of capsule injury at stapler transection was higher in the quadruple reinforcement group than in the control group (42.9% vs. 17.7%), without significance difference (P = 0.096) (Table 2).

The incidence of CR-POPF was significantly lower in the quadruple reinforcement group than in the control groups: 1 (3.7%) vs. 7 (41.2%), P = 0.002. For the quadruple reinforcement group, a drainage tube was removed at PODs 34 in one patient, and no patient underwent percutaneous, endoscopic, or surgical interventions. For the control group, seven patients experienced CR-POPF: removal of drainage tube at PODs 36, 38, and 67 (n = 3), endoscopic intervention (n = 1), percutaneous ultrasound-guided transhepatic drainage (n = 1), drainage tube replacement under fluoroscopy (n = 2).

The rate of complications ≥ Clavien-Dindo classification 3a was significantly lower in the quadruple reinforcement group than in the control group: 0 vs. 23.5%, P = 0.004. The median hospital stay (IQR) was significantly shorter in the quadruple reinforcement group than in the control group: 5.0 (5.0–6.0) days vs. 7.0 (7.0–11.0) days, P = 0.007. The 30-day readmission rate (3.7% vs. 5.9%, P = 0.739) did not differ significantly between the quadruple reinforcement and control groups. Neither group experienced the 30-day reoperation nor the 90-day mortality. Discussion

We detailed the procedural approach and clinical outcomes of patients undergoing RDP using the quadruple reinforcement technique for the divided pancreatic stump. Our technique incorporates four distinct layers of reinforcement–(1) staple line reinforcement, (2) absorbable reinforcement felt, (3) U-shaped suturing, and (4) fibrin sealant spray–to enhance stump integrity and reduce the risk of POPF. Among 27 consecutive patients treated with our technique, the incidence of CR-POPF was low at 3.7%. No postoperative interventions were required, and the median hospital stay was 5 days, with the 30-day readmission rate of 3.7%. Although these findings are based on a single-center experience, our quadruple reinforcement technique, combined with structured perioperative management, may contribute to improved clinical outcomes in patients undergoing RDP.

Although the quadruple reinforcement group predominantly comprises cases from the later phase following the introduction of RDP, making historical bias unavoidable, we believe the influence of the surgeon’s learning curve is minimal. This is primarily because the surgical procedures in this later phase involved a higher proportion of fellows acting as the cosole surgeon role. Furthermore, regarding the potential impact of expanding the surgical indication to include PDAC in the later period (a factor potentially contributing to lower fistula rates), our analysis suggests otherwise. As demonstrated in the Supplementary Table 1, the incidence of CR-POPF remains significantly lower in the quadruple reinforcement group even when PDAC cases are excluded from the comparison. This robust finding strongly indicates that the quadruple reinforcement technique itself is the principal contributor to the improved surgical outcomes, particularly the reduction in CR-POPF.

Since 2003, RDP has gained increasing recognition as a feasible and effective minimmaly invasive approach for pancreatic surgery [3, 21]. The robotic platform offers enhanced visualization, improved instrument dexterity, and greater precision, particularly in complex anatomical reconstructions [4, 8]. These advantages have led to growing interest in RDP as a potential alternative to LDP. Although robotic surgery demonstrated a reduction in CR-POPF rates in pancreaticoduodenectomy,[22, 23] the incidence of CR-POPF in RDP remains comparable to that observed in ODP and LDP [5, 10]. As such, CR-POPF remains a serious and unresolved complication in patients undergoing DP. Various clinical and anatomical factors have been identified as predictors for POPF after DP. Previous studies highlighted smoking, diabetes, BMI, pancreatic texture, a thick pancreas, ODP, operative time, intraoperative blood loss, and blood transfusion as significant risk factors.[9, 24–26] In the present study, no significant differences were observed in the POPF across known risk factors, including pancreatic texture, BMI, blood transfusion, intraoperative blood loss, operative time, smoking, and diabetes. This suggests that these established risk factors were comparably distributed among the study cohort, leading to the inference that the efficacy of the present procedure is not confounded by high-risk patient subsets, thereby supporting its clinical utility. To prevent POPF, technical strategies have been proposed to optimize pancreatic division and stump protection. Pre-compression of the pancreas for more than 10 min prior to pancreatic division was recommended [25, 27]. Regarding the method of pancreatic division, linear staplers are generally preferred compared to hand-sewn techniques, with reinforced staplers shown to be particulary effective [13, 28, 29]. Additional measures, including continuous suturing of the staple line[29] and coverage with an absorbable reinforcement felt and fibrin glue [30], have also demonstrated benefit. We adopted a comprehensive approach to pancreatic stump management, combining pre-compression with intestinal clips and a quadruple reinforcement technique for the pancreatic stump. The single U-shaped suture tecunique offers a simple and time-efficient alternative to continuous suturing, with the potential to reduce intraductal pressure and leakage. While the quadruple reinforcement technique involves four steps, the added actions are relatively simple and time-efficient. The application of the absorbable felt and fibrin sealant takes only a few minutes. The U-shaped suture is a single stitch, unlike the more time-consuming continuous suture techniques often described in other protocols. Furthermore, the standardization of the procedure allowed the surgical team to perform these steps seamlessly, preventing significant prolongation of the total operative time, although the quadruple reinforcement group mainly included less experienced console surgeons.

Our current surgical strategy incorporates a U-shape suture around the MPD, which represents a procedural variation on the technique described by Jang et al.[30] The method involved applying fibrin glue followed by tight wrapping of the pancreatic stump with Polyglycolic Acid (PGA) mesh, secured with stitching to the peripancreatic soft tissue. It should be noted that the use of an explicit U-shape suture around the MPD was not part of their published protocol. While direct comparison is limited by these differences and the variation in sample sizes, the results of both studies provide context on reinforcement strategies. The previous study reported a CR-POPF rate of 11.4% (5 out of 44 patients) in their PGA group, which represented a significant reduction compared to their control group (28.3%, P = 0.04). In our current series, the adoption of a U-shape suture resulted in a CR-POPF rate of 1 out of 27 cases (3.7%). This observed low rate suggests that the combined approach, including the MPD suture, may be a beneficial technique for preventing CR-POPF following distal pancreatectomy.

To mitigate the risks associated with fluid spread and ensure effective management of pancreatic secretions, we devised a strategy utilizing two peripancreatic drains positioned adjacent to the stump. Typically, one drain is placed near the pancreatic stump, and the other is placed in the splenic fossa or adjacent area, using the two trocar holes. This methodology facilitates reliable drainage and localized absorption of pancreatic fluid. Recent randomized controlled trials explored the non-inferiority of a no-drain policy over routine drain placement in patients undergoing DP and showed postoperative intervention rates of 10–27.3% in the no-drain groups, with no significant differences compared to routine drain placement [31–33]. In our study, abdominal drains were placed in all cases. Notably, the postoperative intervention rate was 0% in the quadruple reinforcement group. These findings suggest that the routine drain placement remains a reasonable option, most likely when combined with meticulous stump management.

Our study should be understood in the context of the following limitations. First, it reflects the experience of a single institution, where consistency in surgical technique and perioperative management may limit the generalizability. Second, the sample size was relatively small (44 consecutive patients), which restricts the external validity of our findings. The quadruple reinforcement group predominantly comprises cases from the later phase following the introduction of RDP, making historical bias unavoidable. We acknowledge that institutional learning and the maturation of the robotic program over time likely contributed to improved overall surgical proficiency. The surgical procedures in this later phase involved a significantly higher proportion of fellows acting in the console surgeon role (70.4% vs. 23.5% in the control group). As such, the influence of the individual surgeon’s learning curve on the specific outcome of pancreatic fistula was mitigated by the standardized nature of our technique. The fact that the incidence of CR-POPF was significantly lower in the quadruple reinforcement group, despite the higher participation of less experienced operators, suggests that the quadruple reinforcement technique is a highly reproducible and standardized procedure. This technique likely provides a robust safety margin that reduces operator-dependent variability. Furthermore, regarding the potential impact of expanding the surgical indication to include PDAC in the later period (a factor potentially contributing to lower fistula rates), our analysis suggests otherwise. As demonstrated in the Supplementary Table, the incidence of CR-POPF remains significantly lower in the quadruple reinforcement group even when PDAC cases are excluded from the comparison. This finding indicates that the quadruple reinforcement technique with standardized surgical and postoperative management may contribute to the improved surgical outcomes, particularly the reduction in CR-POPF. Although our technique and FASTOL management showed promising results–demonstrated by a low rate of CR-POPF and no need for postoperative interventions, short hospital stays, and minimal readmission–data from a larger patient cohort would be necessary to confirm these outcomes.

In conclusion, we showed our RDP techniques, emphasizing the quadruple reinforcement technique for pancreatic stump closure: (1) stapler with staple line reinforcement, (2) an absorbable reinforcement felt, (3) U-shaped suture, and (4) fibrin sealant spray. Our technique, combined with structured perioperative management, effectively reduced the incidence of CR-POPF and may serve as a valuable contribution to improving outcomes in DP.
