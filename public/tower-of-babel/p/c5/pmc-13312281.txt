Tumorigenic p53N236S balances aging and tumorigenesis via regulating DREAM/MMB and downstream telomere DNA replication pathways
Authors: Qianqian Wang, Haili Li, Quan Zheng, Jun Yang, Kailong Hou, Liangxia Jiang, Shuting Jia, Xiaoming Wu, Juhua Dan, Ying Luo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Werner syndrome (WS) is characterized with both premature aging and tumorigenic phenotypes. In this study, we introduced a tumorigenic mutation p53N236S (referred to as p53S later), which is found in immortalized WS mouse embryo fibroblasts, back into WS mice to investigate its impact on the telomere dysfunction-induced aging process. Intriguingly, the introduction of p53S rescued the aging phenotypes of WS mice, showing the extension of the lifespan and the delay in organ degeneration. Further studies revealed that the introduction of p53S transcriptionally upregulated the DREAM/MMB pathway and downstream DNA helicases and telomere maintenance proteins, facilitated the recruitment of these proteins to G-quadruplex (G4) DNA structures proximal to DNA replication forks, and promoted the unwinding of G4. By comparing the cellular responses to pyridostatin and hydroxyurea, respectively, we confirmed that p53S specifically regulates G4-related DNA replication stress. Thus, p53S compensates the loss of Wrn and telomerase function, solves the DNA replication, telomere lengthening, and cell proliferation problems in WS cells, and ultimately rescues the aging phenotypes of WS. Together, our data indicate that certain tumorigenic features can be applied to balance with premature aging, rescuing the aging phenotype without tumorigenic risk. This study suggests a new mechanism in aging regulation and provides the possibility of developing a tumor-free longevity strategy and targeting G4 and DNA replication in aging-related tumor therapy.

Introduction

Aging process is well known as the agonistic or antagonistic factor for tumorigenesis (de Magalhaes, 2013; Montegut et al., 2024), but how tumorigenesis influences aging process is not well understood. Among the well-established aging hallmarks (Lopez-Otin et al., 2013, 2023), telomere dysfunction and its induced DNA damage response and cellular senescence are at the intersection of cancer and aging (Bernardes de Jesus and Blasco, 2013; Campisi, 2005). Is it possible to control this turning point at the intersection, by applying the tumorigenic features to anti-aging regulation, to achieve the goal of tumor-free longevity? Here, with telomere dysfunction-induced Werner syndrome (WS) mouse model and a p53 mutation interfering with DNA replication, we investigate how the tumorigenic features influence aging.

The smooth DNA replication progress is essential for genomic DNA, especially telomere DNA maintenance. However, special DNA secondary structure like G-quadruplex (G4) structure needs extra effort to unwind before the DNA replication fork can move on. This process may slow down the progression of DNA replication forks, which become the potential obstacles for DNA replication and telomere lengthening (Lezaja and Altmeyer, 2021). The function of DNA helicases ensures the unwinding of G4 and the progression of DNA replication (Singh et al., 2012). Maintaining proper level of DNA helicase activity is essential for cell proliferation, cell survival, as well as stem cell self-renewal (Lynch et al., 2024). In organisms, the genetic defect of DNA helicase results in various types of progeroid syndromes, such as WS caused by Wrn mutation (Epstein et al., 1966).

The regulation of Drosophila Myb–E2F2/RBF transcriptional repressor (DREAM/MMB) complex might be the key to maintain proper cellular level of DNA helicases. The DREAM/MMB complex is downstream of the p53–p21 or p16–Rb pathway and essential for balancing aging and cancer (Walston et al., 2021; Engeland, 2022).

To understand the connection of DREAM/MMB, DNA replication, and telomere lengthening, the WS mouse model was generated by double knockout of the DNA helicase Wrn and the RNA template of telomerase (mTR) (Chang et al., 2004). It has been reported that WS mouse embryo fibroblasts (MEFs) could escape from senescence status and become immortalized spontaneously (Laud et al., 2005). Our previous study showed that some of the immortalized cell lines have acquired the point mutation of p53N236S (p53S) and become tumorigenic (Jia et al., 2012). We then established the p53S knock-in mouse model (Zhao et al., 2018) and crossed p53S mice with WS mice to introduce p53S into WS background and investigate its impact on the telomere dysfunction-induced aging process and underlying mechanisms.

Results

The introduction of p53S in WS mice promotes tumorigenesis with medium telomere length but rescues aging phenotypes with critically shortened telomere length

We crossed the p53S mice with WS mice (double gene mutations, DM, genotype: mTR–/–Wrn–/–) and eventually got the 1st–5th generations (G1–G5) of mice with triple gene mutations (TM, genotype: p53S/SmTR–/–Wrn–/–) and gradually shortened telomere length (Figure 1A). With the introduction of p53S, the tumor incidence was 33.33% in G1TM, 24.13% in G2TM, 64.10% in G3TM, 13.33% in G4TM, and 6.67% in G5TM, but remained 0% in their matched DM mice (Figure 1B), indicating that p53S promoted the tumorigenesis in WS background. Notably, the tumor incidence first increased, reached a peak in G3TM mice when the telomere was at medium length, and then decreased (Figure 1B). The tumors derived from TM mice were mostly sarcomas and lymphomas (Figure 1C), similar to the tumor spectrum in mice with p53S single mutation (Zhao et al., 2018).

The testes from G5DM mice lacked spermatogonium, while this aging phenotype was rescued in G5TM mice. Interestingly, after the introduction of p53S in WS, TM mice looked younger than their DM controls, showing better hair condition and body shape, even the G3TM mice with high tumor incidence (Figure 1D). We then compared the Kaplan–Meier survival curves of G1, G3 and G5 mice. The median survival time was 702 ± 96 days for G1DM mice versus 282 ± 38 days for G1TM mice, 303 ± 33 days for G3DM mice versus 227 ± 15 days for G3TM mice, and 130 ± 96 days for G5DM mice versus 185 ± 38 days for G5TM mice (Figure 1E), suggesting that p53S significantly impacted the lifespan of WS mice. The introduction of p53S shortened the lifespan of G1TM or G3TM mice, due to the increase of tumorigenesis, but conferred a significant survival advantage in G5TM mice, probably due to the improvement of aging phenotypes (Figure 1E).

We next compared the aging phenotypes in G5DM and G5TM mice. Hematoxylin–eosin (HE) staining demonstrated that the testes were exhausted and lack of spermatogonium in G5DM mice, which was rescued in G5TM mice, showing abundant spermatogonia in the testes (Figure 1F, HE). The BrdU incorporation assay showed that almost no BrdU incorporation occurred in the testes of G5DM mice (also due to the lack of spermatogonium), while the introduction of p53S restored the BrdU incorporation (Figure 1F and G, BrdU). The SA-β-Gal staining showed that only a few cells were positive for SA-β-Gal in G5TM mice, while in G5DM mice, due to the lack of spermatogonium, the SA-β-Gal staining could not work (Figure 1F and G, SA-β-Gal). Furthermore, the microCT results revealed the obviously loss of bone mass in G5DM mice and the restoration of bone mass in G5TM mice compared to wild-type (WT) mice (Figure 1H).

These data revealed that the introduction of p53S in WS background antagonized the aging phenotypes induced by DNA helicase loss and telomere dysfunction, with the price of tumorigenesis when telomere length was not critically shortened.

The introduction of p53S transcriptionally upregulates the DREAM/MMB pathway and downstream DNA helicases and telomere proteins

To understand how p53S rescues the aging phenotypes in WS, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) with the MEFs derived from G1, G3, and G5 mice (both DM and TM), and the WT and p53S MEFs were used as control. Single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) of RNA-seq data revealed the differential pathways. The classical p53 signaling pathways were downregulated, while the Rb and related DREAM, E2F, and Myc pathways were all upregulated in p53S-containing MEFs (Figure 2A). Consistently, the downstream pathways of Rb–DREAM/MMB–E2F, such as DNA replication, DNA helicase, cell cycle regulation, and telomere lengthening and maintenance pathways were also upregulated (Figure 2A).

(B) GSEA analysis of RNA-seq data confirmed the enrichment of the DREAM/MMB pathway in G5TM cells compared with G5DM cells. (C) RNA-seq data indicated that the introduction of p53S upregulated genes associated with the DREAM/MMB pathway. (E and F) Western blot analysis confirmed the elevated expression levels of DREAM/MMB-related proteins in G5TM cells compared with G5DM and G5TKO cells. (G and H) Western blot analysis confirmed the elevated expression levels of DNA helicases and telomere maintenance proteins in G5TM cells compared with G5DM and G5TKO cells. To further dissect the genes involved in individual pathways, we performed GSEA analysis for the DREAM target pathways. The data revealed nice enrichment of genes in G5TM cells compared with G5DM cells (Figure 2B). The genes consistently upregulated in cells with p53S (TM vs. DM) included DREAM/MMB core components (Foxm1, Mybl2, and E2f1), DNA helicases (Mcm2, Mcm7, Mcm9, Recql4, Fancd2, Gins2, Ddx11, and Timeless), the DNA replication gene Pcna, and the p53-related protein Wrap53 (Figure 2C). Some genes were upregulated in p53S, G1TM, and G3TM cells but not in G5TM cells compared to their controls, such as Pot1a, Pot1b, Terf1, Rbl1, Rbl2, Blm, Lin9, Lin54, and Hist2h2be (Figure 2C). As positive and negative controls, the Trp53 gene was upregulated and the Cdkn1a gene was downregulated due to the introduction of p53S (Figure 2C). These data suggested that the introduction of p53S upregulated DREAM/MMB and the downstream DNA helicase activity, thus promoting DNA replication, telomere maintenance, and cell cycle progression.

To exclude that the gene expression change after the introduction of p53S may be due to the loss of wild-type p53 function, we also included the G5TKO (p53null in WS background, genotype: p53–/–mTR–/–Wrn–/–) MEFs as a control. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed to verify the RNA-seq results. Recql4, Fancd2, Mcm7, Ddx11, Timeless, Mybl2, Foxm1, and Wrap53 genes were significantly upregulated in G5TM cells compared with G5DM and G5TKO cells (Figure 2D). Different from RNA-seq data, Blm, Rbl2, Terf1, and Pot1a genes were also significantly upregulated in G5TM cells (Figure 2D).

We then applied western blotting to test the expression change of key proteins in the DREAM/MMB pathway (Figure 2E and F). The positive control p53 protein (here is mutant p53S) obviously overexpressed in G5TM cells due to the introduction of p53S, and the negative control p21 protein had lower expression levels in G5TM and G5TKO cells due to the loss of wild-type p53. Notably, the protein level of phosphorylated Rb, rather than non-phosphorylated Rb, was upregulated in G5TM cells. The E2f1 protein level was also upregulated in G5TM cells. The level of p130, a key protein in DREAM, was upregulated in G5TM and G5TKO cells, though not significantly, compared with G5DM cells. Meanwhile, levels of Foxm1 and B-Myb, two key proteins in MMB, were significantly upregulated in G5TM cells compared with G5DM and G5TKO cells. Lin54 and Lin9, common key proteins of DREAM/MMB, were also upregulated in G5TM cells compared with either G5DM or G5TKO cells. These data indicated that the DREAM/MMB pathway was upregulated in G5TM cells.

Moreover, protein levels of DNA helicases Blm, Fancd2, Recql4, Timeless, Ddx11, Mcm7, and Gins2, the telomere binding protein Pot1a, the DNA replication protein PCNA, and histone H2B were all significantly upregulated in G5TM cells compared with G5DM and G5TKO cells (Figure 2G and H), suggesting that the introduction of p53S rescued the loss of these key proteins in G5DM cells.

To understand the protein binding pattern of the DREAM/MMB complex, we performed the co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) assay with anti-Lin9 antibody, followed by immunoblotting for p130, B-Myb, and Lin9. We observed that more p130 and B-Myb proteins were co-immunoprecipitated with Lin9 in G5TM cells than in G5DM cells, suggesting enhanced DREAM/MMB complex formation in G5TM cells (Figure 2I).

To examine whether p53S is the upstream transcription factor for the DREAM/MMB pathway in G5TM background, we applied the chomatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay with anti-p53 antibody in WT, G5DM, and G5TM cells. Results showed that in G5TM cells, p53S abundantly bound to the promoter regions of DREAM/MMB component genes Foxm1, Lin9, Lin54, Mybl2, and Rbl2 (Figure 3A). Interestingly, p53S could also directly bind to the promoters of DNA helicase genes Blm, Fancd2, Recql4, Timeless, Ddx11, Mcm7, and Gins2, as well as telomere maintenance genes Terf1 and Pot1a (Figure 3B).

p53S in G5TM cells could transcriptionally regulate the DREAM components and downstream DNA helicase and telomere maintenance genes. (A) ChIP assay indicated that in G5TM cells, p53S could bind to the promoters of DREAM components Foxm1, Lin9, Lin54, Mybl2, and Rbl2. (B) ChIP assay indicated that in G5TM cells, p53S could directly bind to the promoters of DNA helicase and telomere maintenance genes. Furthermore, we performed ChIP assay with anti-B-Myb antibody in G5DM and G5TM cells, with WT and G5TKO cells as control. Indeed, B-Myb directly bound to the promoters of DNA helicase genes Blm, Fancd2, Recql4, and Mcm7, as well as telomere maintenance genes Terf1 and Pot1a, thus promoting their transcription and expression (Figure 3C). The binding of B-Myb to the promoters was significantly enhanced in G5TM cells compared with G5DM cells (Figure 3C), confirming that p53S together with the elevated MMB protein complex transcriptionally upregulates DNA helicases and telomere proteins in G5TM cells.

The upregulated DNA helicases promote G4 unwinding, DNA replication, and cell survival

Next, we investigated whether the upregulated DNA helicases could promote the unwinding of G4 DNA structure. Immunofluorescence staining demonstrated much more G4 DNA in G5DM cells than in WT cells, might due to the loss of Wrn function (Figure 4A). The introduction of p53S promoted the unwinding of G4 DNA and thus reduced the abundance of G4 DNA in G5TM cells, which was not found in G5TKO cells, suggesting that the promoted G4 unwinding resulted from the gain of function of p53S (Figure 4A and C). We also exogenously introduced the G4-binding protein G4P (Zheng et al., 2020) into the cells to directly display the abundance of G4 DNA. Consistently, p53S promoted the unwinding of G4 DNA and reduced the G4 DNA abundance in G5TM cells, but this reduction of G4 DNA was not found in G5TKO cells (Figure 4B and D).

p53S promotes the colocalization of DNA helicases with G4 DNA and DNA replication forks and facilitates the unwinding of G4 DNA structure. (A and C) Immunofluorescence staining of G4 DNA indicated the reduced abundance of G4 DNA in G5TM cells compared with G5DM and G5TKO cells (n = 200). (B) Immunofluorescence staining of exogenously expressed G4P showed the reduced abundance of G4 DNA in G5TM cells compared with G5DM and G5TKO cells (n = 200). (E) Immunofluorescence staining showed the elevated colocalization of DNA helicases Blm, Fancd2, Ddx11, and Recql4 with G4 DNA in G5TM cells. (F and G) SIRF assay indicated the elevated colocalization of DNA helicases Blm, Fancd2, Ddx11, and Recql4 with EdU-labeled DNA replication forks in G5TM cells (n = 50). ns, no significance; ***P < 0.001.

Immunofluorescence staining also demonstrated the increased abundance of DNA helicases Blm, Fancd2, Ddx11, and Recql4 and their enhanced colocalization with G4 DNA in G5TM cells compared with G5DM or G5TKO cells (Figure 4E). To examine whether these G4 structures with elevated DNA helicases are proximal to active replication forks in G5TM cells, we applied the in situ analysis of protein interactions at DNA replication forks (SIRF) assay (Roy et al., 2018). Blm, Fancd2, Ddx11, and Recql4 were all connected with newly incorporated EdU in G5TM cells, while only few SIRF foci were observed in G5TKO cells (Figure 4F and G), suggesting the gain of function of p53S in facilitating the recruitment of these DNA helicases to G4 structures proximal to DNA replication forks.

To determine whether the function of p53S on DNA replication is specifically through regulating G4 DNA structure or generally on DNA replication process, we treated cells with the G4 DNA stabilizer pyridostatin (PDS) (Rodriguez et al., 2012) or the general DNA replication stress inducer hydroxyurea (HU). After 10 μM PDS treatment, G5TM cells showed significantly higher cell viability compared with G5DM or G5TKO cells (Figure 5A), suggesting that p53S endowed cellular resistance to PDS-induced DNA replication stress. The apoptosis assay also revealed much lower percentages of apoptotic cells in G5TM cells than in G5DM and G5TKO cells (early apoptosis, 1% vs. 6.53% and 5.89%; late apoptosis or necrosis, 0.39% vs. 2.14% and 1.5%) after 10 μM PDS treatment (Figure 5B).

(C and D) Western blot analysis confirmed more upregulated expression of DNA damage response proteins in G5TM cells than in G5DM cells. (I and J) Immunofluorescence staining and quantification by Manders’ coefficients indicated the elevated colocalization of γH2AX with G4 DNA structures in G5DM and G5TKO cells but not in G5TM cells (n = 200). ns, no significance; *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001.

Western blot analysis of DNA damage response protein ATR, phosphorylated RPA (RPA-s33), and γH2AX before and after 10 μM PDS treatment revealed lower background levels of ATR, RPA-s33, and γH2AX, as well as less elevated γH2AX level and not obviously changed ATR and RPA-s33 levels in response to PDS-induced DNA replication stress, in G5TM cells compared with G5DM cells (Figure 5C and D). Immunofluorescence staining further confirmed the significantly lower levels of ATR, RPA-s33, and γH2AX in G5TM cells than in G5DM and G5TKO cells, either before or after PDS treatment (Figure 5E–H). We also observed that more γH2AX foci were colocalized with G4 DNA in G5DM and G5TKO cells, but not in G5TM cells, compared with WT cells (Figure 5I and J), suggesting that p53S could prevent G4 block-induced DNA damage.

Interestingly, G5TM, G5DM, and G5TKO cells showed comparable cell viabilities right after 1 mM HU treatment, but after 6 h DNA damage repair, WT and G5TM cells showed significantly higher cell viability compared with G5DM and G5TKO cells (Figure 6A), suggesting that although p53S did not affect the initial DNA damage induced by HU, it rescued the dysfunctional DNA replication damage repair caused by the loss of Wrn function.

In addition, G5TM, G5DM, and G5TKO cells showed comparable percentages of apoptotic cells right after HU treatment (early apoptosis, 6.72%, 6.04%, and 7.4%; late apoptosis or necrosis, 1.56%, 1.93%, and 1.35%) (Figure 6B). Western blot analysis showed that right after HU treatment, ATR, RPA-s33, and γH2AX were all upregulated to the comparable levels in G5DM, G5TM, and G5TKO cells, but after 6 h repair, the ATR, RPA-s33, and γH2AX levels were much lower in G5TM cells than in G5DM and G5TKO cells (Figure 6C and D), suggesting that p53S promoted the repair of DNA damage induced by HU. Immunofluoresence staining of ATR, RPA-s33, and γH2AX confirmed the similar DNA damage responses induced by 1 mM HU treatment in G5DM, G5TM, and G5TKO cells but more efficient DNA damage repair in G5TM cells than in G5DM and G5TKO cells after 6 h repair (Figure 6E–H). Consistently, 1 mM HU treatment did not result in differential colocalization of G4 DNA with γH2AX foci in G5DM, G5TM, and G5TKO cells (Figure 6I and J), confirming that the HU treatment was not G4 DNA-specific.

The upregulated DNA helicases promote telomere lengthening and facilitate cell cycle progression

Subsequently, we investigated the impact of p53S on telomere lengthening. TRF Southern assay indicated the elongated telomere length and the enhanced telomere signal in p53S cells compared with p53null and WT cells (Figure 7A), suggesting that p53S per se promoted telomere lengthening. In WS background, we could also observe the enhanced telomere signal and elongated telomere length in TM cells compared with DM cells (Figure 7A). In particular, the telomere signal was critically decreased in G5DM cells but rescued by the introduction of p53S in G5TM cells (Figure 7A). We further compared telomere lengths in G5DM and G5TM cells by qPCR and found that telomere length in G5DM cells was shortened significantly compared with WT cells, which was rescued by the introduction of p53S (G5TM) but not p53null (G5TKO) (Figure 7B).

(B) qPCR data confirmed the restoration of telomere length in G5TM cells. 36B4 served as the reference gene. (C and D) IF-FISH showed the enhanced colocalization of DNA helicases Recql4, Blm, Fancd2, Timeless, Ddx11, Mcm7 (C) and telomere maintenance proteins Terf1 and Pot1a (D) with telomere signals in G5TM cells. (E) IF-FISH showed the enhanced colocalization of DNA damage markers with telomere signals in G5DM and G5TKO cells but not in G5TM cells. (F and G) EdU incorporation assay indicated that p53S rescued cell proliferation in G5TM cells. (H) FACS analysis revealed that p53S promoted cell cycle progression in G5TM cells. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ****P < 0.0001.

We then examined whether the elevated DNA helicases in G5TM cells could be recruited to G4-riched telomere DNA. The immunofluoresence staining with fluorescent in situ hybridization (IF-FISH) assay indicated that the signals for telomere DNA and DNA helicases Recql4, Fancd2, Timeless, and Mcm7, as well as the colocalization signals, all increased in G5TM cells (Figure 7C). The signals for telomere binding proteins Terf1 and Pot1a and their colocalization with telomere DNA were also enhanced in G5TM cells compared to G5DM and G5TKO cells (Figure 7D). To verify whether these elevated DNA helicases reduce the replication stress induced by telomere dysfunction, we compared ATR, RPA-s33, and γH2AX levels and their colocalization with telomere DNA in G5TM and G5DM cells. The background levels of ATR, RPA-s33, and γH2AX were upregulated in G5DM cells compared with WT cells, and the foci were colocalized with telomere signals (Figure 7E). The introduction of p53S, but not p53null, rescued the DNA replication stress and reduced the levels of ATR, RPA-s33, and γH2AX (Figure 7E).

Furthermore, EdU incorporation assay showed that the introduction of p53S rescued the reduced cell proliferation (Figure 7F and G). The cell cycle analysis by FACS showed that a great portion of G5DM cells (53.1%) stayed at G1 phase, while the introduction of p53S promoted the big portion of G5TM cells (51.57%) shift to G2/M phase, but only a small protion of G5TKO cells (26.28%) were in G2/M phase (Figure 7H).

Since various p53 mutations could occur during cell culture and impact DNA replication, cell proliferation, etc., we sequenced full-length p53 cDNAs derived from three independent p53S-containing TM cell lines. Only p53S single mutation was detected (Supplementary Data S1), confirming that p53S mutation is sufficient to rescue the WS phenotypes.

These data suggest that the introduction of p53S rescues telomere lengthening in WS cells and endows the cells with proliferation capacity, which contributes to the rescue of aging phenotypes in WS mice.

Discussion

It has been extensively studied how aging process influences tumorigenesis, while how tumorigenesis affects aging is still under investigation. Restoration of telomerase has been shown beneficial for longevity in the context of cancer resistantance by enhanced expression of tumor suppressors p53, p16, and p19ARF (Tomas-Loba et al., 2008). However, in the context of telomere dysfunction and cancer prone to lack of tumor suppressors, telomerase reactivation promotes aggressive carcinogenesis with metastases (Ding et al., 2012).

We have identified a unique p53 mutation p53S from spontaneously immortalized tumorigenic WS MEFs and found the new gain of function of p53S in facilitating the resolving of G4 DNA structures, which secures the progression of DNA replication and telomere lengthening (Wang et al., 2018, 2023). This gain of function is not found in the human cancer hot spot mutation p53H (p53R172H in mice, p53R175H in humans) (Lang et al., 2004), strongly supporting that the regulation of helicases and telomere lengthening via the regulation of G4 structure may be specific to p53S mutation (Wang et al., 2023). Thus, p53S could be a genetic factor that interferes DNA replication and telomere lengthening. Since p53S is not a common sense cancer-related hot spot mutation in humans, we speculate that its actual biological significance might be in anti-progeroid with its tumor-like features.

In this study, we introduced p53S back into the WS background. In WS mice, along with the increase of generation, the telomere length was gradually shortened, and the aging phenotypes gradually became obvious. With the introduction of p53S in WS, the tumor incidence first increased, reached the peak with the medium telomere length (G3TM), and then decreased along with further telomere shortening. Interestingly, in late generation (G5) mice, p53S balanced the aging phenotypes with its tumor potential, rescuing the aging phenotypes of WS and elongating the lifespan of G5TM.

These data perfectly display the relationship between tumor incidence and telomere length and model the trend of tumor incidence in aged human population, in which tumor incidence increases dramatically around 50 years old, reaches the peak around 80 years old, and then declines (Siegel et al., 2024). This model will benefit the study of tumorigenesis occurred in elder population, which is known to be challenging in clinic.

We revealed that WS cells utilized the tumorigenic features of p53S to elevate and balance the DREAM/MMB pathway and downstream DNA helicases and telomere maintenance proteins, solving the DNA replication stress and telomere maintenance problems. The gain of function of p53S also specifically endowed the cellular resistance to DNA replication stress caused by PDS, but not HU. Thus, the tumorigenic characteristics of p53S in regulating G4-riched DNA replication progression balanced with the loss of Wrn and telomerase function and rescued aging phenotypes in WS.

It has been shown that the overexpression of Claspin and Timeless increases replication stress tolerance by protecting replication forks in cancer cells, and primary fibroblasts could adapt to oncogene-induced replication stress by spontaneously overexpressing Claspin and Timeless (Bianco et al., 2019). A recent study revealed that the sensitivity of PARP1 inhibitors might be affected by Timeless (Petropoulos et al., 2024; Tarsounas, 2024). In addition, FANCM, BRCA1, and BLM are actively recruited to the telomeres with alternative lengthening of telomere (ALT) that are experiencing replication stress and cooperatively resolve the replication stress at the ALT telomeres (Pan et al., 2017). Our TRF Southern data revealed the typical ALT pattern in G5TM cells, probably activated by p53S via compensating DNA helicase activity and facilitating telomere DNA replication.

This kind of functional compensation should be considered in clinical treatment for cancer as side effect but also could be a beneficial strategy for clinical treatment of aging-related diseases. The peak of tumor incidence in G3TM mice also suggests the essential role of the balance between telomere dysfunction and proper maintenance of telomere function (DNA helicase level, G4 structure level, etc.). Aging-related tumors might be resistant to chemotherapy due to the aging phenotypes, while DNA replication-related proteins, such as Wrn and Blm, could be the ideal targets for tumor therapy.

The key regulator connecting DNA damage response and DNA replication and mitotic regulation is p53 downstream DREAM/MMB pathway (Fischer et al., 2016; Hoareau et al., 2024; Sadasivam and DeCaprio, 2013). Interestingly, the DREAM/MMB pathway regulates genes associated with DNA replication and repair, later phase cell cycle regulation, and cell division, such as Timeless, the topoisomerase-binding protein Topbp1, Wrn, Fancj, and Fancc (Engeland, 2022). In this study, we further confirmed that p53S could directly regulate DREAM/MMB component genes, as well as the downstream DNA helicase and telomere maintenance genes, thus connecting the DREAM/MM pathway with the secure DNA replication and telomere lengthening regulation, which is essential for non-tumorigenic immortalization or organism longevity.

This study also reveals that maintaining the proper level of DNA helicases is the key to balance cell growth and cell senescence, raising a new concept in preventing the progress of aging or tumorigenesis.

Materials and methods

Mice and MEFs

All mouse protocols have been approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Guizhou Medical University. All mouse strains are on a C57BL/6 background. We crossed the WS mice (DM, mTR–/–Wrn–/–) with p53S (p53S/S) mice and obtained the first generation of triple mutation (G1TM, p53S/SmTR–/–Wrn–/–) mice. The mice were then inbred to get G2TM, G3TM, G4TM, and G5TM mice. The WT, p53S, p53null (p53–/–), DM, or G5TKO (p53–/–mTR–/–Wrn–/–) mice were used as controls. All mice were maintained in a specific pathogen-free mouse facility. Adult mice of both sexes aged 2–3 months were randomly collected according to their genotypes and used for experiments. The MEFs derived from these mice were harvested on embryonic day 13.5 and cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium with 10% fetal bovine serum at 37°C with 5% CO2 and 3% O2. All experiments with MEFs were performed with cells cultured for < 5 passages.

RNA-seq and bioinformatics analysis

The MEFs derived from G1TM, G3TM, and G5TM mice and the control DM, WT, p53S, and p53null mice were harvested to isolate total RNA for RNA-seq. The RNA-seq data were analyzed by ssGSEA to identify the differentially regulated pathways in response to the introduction of p53S (Subramanian et al., 2005). The C2 gene sets from Molecular Signatures database (Liberzon et al., 2011) were used as the pathway database for ssGSEA analysis. The GSEA was applied on RNA-seq data to further analyze the individual gene sets involved in the related pathways.

Bone density measurement

Mouse bone density was measured by microCT. Prior to microCT scanning, the mice were sacrificed, and the femur samples were collected and fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin and then transferred to 70% ethanol. Excised femurs were scanned on a Latheta LCT-200 scanner (Hitachi-Aloka) using high resolutions.

G4P overexpression

G4P was exogenously expressed in WT, G5DM, G5TM, and G5TKO MEFs by transiently transfection of pNLS-G4P-IRES2-EGFP plasmids (Zheng et al., 2020) with Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher), and the nuclear green fluorescent protein (GFP) fluorescence was used to estimate the amount of G4 structures.

PDS or HU treatment and CCK8 assay

The cells were counted and cultured in 96-well plates for 12 h and treated with 10 μM PDS (MedChemExpress) for 48 h or 1 mM HU (Sigma) for 24 h. Then, the culture medium with PDS or HU was replaced with fresh medium, and the CCK8 working solution was applied and cultured for 3 h. The OD value were measured with a microplate reader (Multiscan GO; Thermo) at 450 nm.

Quantification and statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was processed and analyzed with Graphpad prism (9.1.0) by unpaired two-sided t-test for most quantification data. The Fisher’s exact test was applied to analyze the significance of tumor incidence. The Log-rank (Mantel–Cox) test was applied to analyze the differential survival between TM and DM mice. Statistical significance is indicated as follows: ns, no significance; *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001.
