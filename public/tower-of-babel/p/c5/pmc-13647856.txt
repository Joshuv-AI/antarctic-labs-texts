Conflicts of interest as a barrier to the development of health technology assessment in Iran: a qualitative study
Authors: Meysam Behzadifar, Saeed Shahabi, Ahad Bakhtiari, Mariano Martini, Masoud Behzadifar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHealth Technology Assessment (HTA) is vital for evidence-based decision-making and efficient resource allocation. Although HTA has gained importance in Iran’s health policy agenda, its development faces major barriers. Among these, conflicts of interest (COI) remain a critical yet understudied challenge that can undermine the integrity of HTA processes. This study explored how COI manifest within Iran’s HTA environment and hinder its development.MethodsA qualitative design was used, employing purposive sampling to select key informants involved in HTA and health policymaking. Twenty-eight semi-structured interviews were conducted with policymakers, academics, healthcare professionals, and representatives from regulatory and insurance bodies. Data were analyzed thematically using an inductive approach to identify the types, sources, and impacts of COI.FindingsParticipants described multiple personal, institutional, and systemic COI influencing HTA. Key themes included the lack of formal disclosure and management mechanisms, strong industry influence, overlapping roles of decision-makers, and weak regulatory oversight. These factors distorted priority-setting, delayed the adoption of cost-effective technologies, and eroded public trust. Cultural norms favoring informal relationships and political patronage further reinforced these problems.ConclusionsCOI threaten the credibility and transparency of HTA in Iran. Strengthening governance frameworks, ensuring institutional independence, and promoting ethical accountability are essential to enhance HTA integrity. The findings provide insights for other low- and middle-income countries seeking to institutionalize HTA within complex governance systems. This study highlights that managing COI requires not only technical solutions but also interdisciplinary approaches, particularly drawing on legal frameworks and organizational management theories, to address governance failures in Iran’s health system.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s41043-026-01383-x.Keywords: Conflicts of interest, Health technology assessment, Health policy, Iran, Qualitative study

Introduction

Health policy plays a pivotal role globally in addressing critical healthcare challenges, including promoting equity, ensuring access, optimizing cost-effectiveness, and strengthening the resilience of health systems [1]. These challenges have become increasingly pronounced due to demographic shifts such as aging populations, the rapid pace of technological innovation, and emergent threats like pandemics [2]. Within this complex landscape, Health Technology Assessment (HTA) has emerged as a vital tool for guiding evidence-based decision-making and resource allocation, thereby supporting the sustainable development of health systems [3].

Despite its recognized importance, the development and institutionalization of HTA remain highly variable across countries, particularly in low- and middle-income settings [4]. In Iran, where recent health reforms emphasize cost containment and value-based care, HTA has gained increased policy salience. However, significant obstacles continue to hinder its full integration into health decision-making processes [5]. Among these, conflicts of interest (COI) represent a critical yet insufficiently addressed barrier that undermines the objectivity, transparency, and credibility of HTA processes [6]. The World Health Organization (WHO) has emphasized that effective COI management is essential for ensuring trustworthy health governance. Nevertheless, empirical evidence regarding the influence of COI within Iran’s HTA ecosystem remains sparse and fragmented [7].

COI arise when individuals or institutions involved in policymaking or evaluation processes have competing personal, professional, or financial interests that may compromise their judgment or actions [8]. In the context of HTA, COIs can manifest through relationships with pharmaceutical companies, medical device manufacturers, or other stakeholders whose interests may diverge from public health priorities [9]. These conflicts, when undisclosed or poorly managed, can bias the selection, appraisal, and recommendation of health technologies, leading to suboptimal or even harmful decisions [10]. In health systems with weak regulatory oversight or limited transparency mechanisms such as Iran’s COIs may be pervasive yet inadequately addressed, posing a substantial threat to the integrity of HTA processes [5].

The influence of COI presents a significant obstacle to the institutionalization of robust HTA systems globally [11]. Evidence demonstrates that industry ties can bias evaluation outcomes, resulting in a distorted evidence base and delayed adoption of equitable health technologies [12]. These COI-related risks are often exacerbated by systemic barriers, including political aversion to centralized HTA bodies, a lack of independent funding, and resistance from key stakeholders [13]. Particularly in resource-limited settings, consistently identifies COI as a fundamental impediment to developing transparent and accountable HTA frameworks [14]. In response, prevailing international recommendations advocate for structural reforms such as mandatory disclosure protocols, stringent ethical guidelines, and inclusive multi-stakeholder engagement to mitigate these barriers and foster credible HTA processes [15].

Although the technical aspects of HTA are well-discussed, the governance and regulatory dimensions, particularly the management of COI, remain underexplored in the Iranian context. Addressing these challenges requires moving beyond a purely clinical or economic perspective and adopting an interdisciplinary lens that integrates insights from public administration, organizational behavior, and health law. Such an approach is essential for understanding how institutional structures, legal loopholes, and managerial cultures facilitate or hinder transparent decision-making.

This study investigates the role of COI as a key impediment to the development of HTA in Iran, using a qualitative approach that draws on insights from key stakeholders across the health system. By examining structural, cultural, and regulatory dimensions, this research sheds light on context-specific factors that limit the effectiveness and integrity of HTA. To the best of our knowledge, no prior study in Iran has specifically examined the influence of COI on the development and institutionalization of HTA, thereby addressing a critical gap in the existing literature. The findings offer novel insights into how entrenched interests and opaque decision-making processes distort evidence-informed policymaking and propose actionable recommendations to strengthen transparency, accountability, and governance.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a qualitative descriptive design to explore the role of COI as a barrier to the development of HTA in Iran. Data were collected through in-depth semi-structured interviews, enabling rich and detailed exploration of participants’ experiences and perspectives. To ensure methodological rigor and transparency, this study adhered to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist [16] (Supplementary File 1). A schematic diagram summarizing the overall research process from participant recruitment and data collection to thematic analysis and interpretation is provided in Fig. 1 to enhance methodological clarity. This study is one component of a larger qualitative research program exploring barriers to the development and institutionalization of HTA in Iran. The broader project comprised several complementary studies investigating different aspects of HTA implementation, including governance, financing, conflicts of interest, and evidence-informed decision-making. Although the studies were conducted within the same overarching research program, each addressed a distinct research objective, used an independent analytical framework, and generated unique findings. Therefore, the results have been reported in separate publications to allow comprehensive and in-depth examination of each topic while avoiding unnecessary overlap. Related publications from the overall project are cited to provide the context for the present study [4, 11, 17, 18].

Participant selection

A purposive sampling strategy was implemented to recruit participants with relevant expertise and direct involvement in Iran’s health system and HTA processes. Inclusion criteria included: [1] substantial experience in health management, policy-making, or clinical practice related to HTA; [2] active or prior involvement in HTA-related decision-making or implementation; [3] senior or influential positions within their organizations; and [4] representation across diverse sectors, including public health, research, clinical practice, and health policy. Invitations were extended to 40 potential participants via email and telephone, providing detailed information about the study objectives and procedures. Ultimately, 28 individuals consented and participated in the study. Non-participation was mainly due to scheduling conflicts, perceived lack of relevance, organizational restrictions, or personal reasons. To ensure an interdisciplinary perspective, we also specifically included individuals with backgrounds in health law, public administration, and organizational management, alongside clinicians and health economists.

Interviewer characteristics and relationship with participants

Interviews were conducted by two experienced researchers holding PhDs in health policy and with extensive qualitative research expertise. Both interviewers were external to the participants’ organizations and had no prior relationships with them, which helped minimize potential biases. At the outset of each interview, participants received an overview of the study’s aims and the interviewers’ backgrounds to foster openness and rapport.

Data collection

Data collection occurred from September 2024 to May 2025. A semi-structured interview guide (Supplementary File 2) was developed through a comprehensive literature review and expert consultation, and pilot-tested with five health professionals to ensure clarity and relevance. All interviews were conducted in Persian, the native language of both interviewers and participants. To ensure translation accuracy, the transcribed Persian texts were translated into English by a professional bilingual translator and verified by the research team through back-translation and cross-checking for conceptual equivalence. Interviews were conducted either face-to-face at participants’ workplaces, including health offices, hospitals, and academic institutions, or via secure online video calls, based on participant preference and logistical considerations. Each interview lasted between 45 and 75 min, was audio-recorded with consent, and supplemented by field notes capturing contextual details and non-verbal cues. Data saturation was determined when no new codes or themes emerged from successive interviews, and information became repetitive. Saturation was achieved after 24 interviews; however, four additional interviews were conducted to confirm the stability and completeness of themes. Data collection continued until thematic saturation was achieved, whereby no new significant themes emerged.

Data analysis

Thematic analysis was performed according to the six-phase framework described by Braun and Clarke [19]: [1] familiarization with data through repeated readings; [2] generation of initial codes; [3] searching for themes by collating codes; [4] reviewing themes to refine coherence and distinctiveness; [5] defining and naming themes; and [6] producing the final report integrating analytic narratives with illustrative quotes. Coding was conducted iteratively and independently by two authors (MaB and AB), followed by a collaborative review and consensus-building process involving two additional authors (SS and AB). To enhance analytical rigor, methodological triangulation was employed through joint discussions among interviewers, co-authors, and participants. An expert qualitative methodologist (MY) supervised and audited the process. Discrepancies in coding or theme interpretation were resolved through deliberation until consensus was reached. Data management and analysis were facilitated using MAXQDA 10 software.

Trustworthiness and rigor

To enhance the credibility, dependability, transferability, and confirmability of the findings, several strategies were applied: (a) member checking through sharing preliminary findings with participants for feedback and validation; (b) prolonged engagement of lead researchers with the data and iterative expert review; (c) inclusion of researchers with diverse academic and professional backgrounds in data interpretation to minimize bias; (d) extensive use of direct participant quotations throughout the manuscript to demonstrate authenticity; and (e) purposive sampling ensuring diversity in participants’ roles, expertise, and sectoral affiliation.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Lorestan University of Medical Sciences (IR.LUMS.REC.1402.310). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before interviews, and confidentiality was strictly maintained throughout the research process. All identifying information, including names, organizational affiliations, and job titles, was removed or anonymized during transcription and reporting to protect participant anonymity and privacy.

Results

A total of 28 participants were included in this study (Table 1). The majority were male (n = 18), and participants’ ages ranged from 34 to 66 years. Most participants held doctoral or specialized degrees in fields related to health policy, economics, or medicine. Regarding the employment sector, 19 participants were affiliated with the public sector, while 9 were from private institutions or NGOs. Fifteen interviews were conducted in person, and thirteen were conducted online. Participants’ years of experience ranged from 6 to over 30 years, with an average of approximately 18 years, indicating a highly experienced sample. Based on the analysis of interview transcripts, five major themes emerged that reflected participants’ perceptions of how COI acts as a barrier to the development of HTA in Iran. Each theme includes one or more sub-themes that further elaborate on the specific manifestations or mechanisms through which COI influence HTA processes. Lack of institutional and structural transparency

Absence of formal mechanisms for disclosure of interests

A prominent barrier identified was the absence of formal, binding mechanisms requiring individuals to disclose their financial or professional interests. This lack of transparency allows decision-makers to act without accountability, often prioritizing personal interests.

“In our health system, there is no formal or clear structure that obliges individuals to disclose their financial interests, professional ties, or relationships. This allows decision-makers to make choices without any accountability, often hiding their personal interests during policymaking processes. Such lack of transparency has led to decisions being influenced by hidden motives, which harms the system.” (P3).

“From my experience, most decision-makers in this field have no interest in disclosing their personal interests, and even informally, such discussions are uncommon. This has become institutionalized, creating an environment ripe for conflicts of interest, without anyone being held responsible.” (P12).

Overlapping roles and responsibilities of policy-makers

Participants described overlapping roles as another structural issue, where individuals simultaneously hold several key positions across organizations, blurring the lines between personal, organizational, and public interests.

“The main problem is that some individuals hold multiple key roles simultaneously; for example, they might be members of the Ministry of Health, insurance organizations, and even boards of related companies. This overlap makes it easy for personal or organizational interests to override the public good, complicating decision-making processes.” (P7).

“When someone occupies several important positions at the same time, it becomes very difficult to determine whether decisions are made for the system’s benefit or for the personal and organizational interests of that individual or entity.” (P19).

Lack of transparent reporting and accountability systems

The absence of clear reporting and accountability mechanisms further deepens the problem, leading to a culture where misconduct or COI go unaddressed.

“When people have conflicts of interest or misconduct occurs, usually no organization takes responsibility, and there are no effective accountability mechanisms. This has created an atmosphere of distrust among staff and stakeholders.” (P15).

“Trying to follow up on these issues often leads to dead ends. The system is designed in a way that people can keep passing the responsibility along, and no one is clearly answerable.” (P26).

Influence of powerful stakeholders in decision-making

Intervention of pharmaceutical and medical device industries

Participants highlighted the strong influence of pharmaceutical companies and medical device manufacturers, which frequently lobby to sway HTA decisions in their favor, often at the expense of scientific evidence.

“Pharmaceutical and medical device companies actively participate in HTA-related meetings and usually can apply their interests without significant restrictions. These lobbying efforts heavily influence outcomes and decisions, causing many evidence-based decisions to shift towards specific interests.” (P5).

“We still have not established effective mechanisms to control the influence of these industries. Their lobbying power is very strong, and in the end, most decisions are made in their favor, even when they are not scientifically or economically justified.” (P22).

Role of physicians with financial interests against HTA evidence

Physicians with financial incentives were reported to resist HTA recommendations that might reduce their income from prescribing costly drugs or devices.

“Many physicians whose income depends on prescribing specific drugs or expensive equipment are reluctant to reduce the use of these items. They oppose HTA findings and try to undermine the scientific results to protect their financial interests.” (P9).

“Financial ties between some physicians and pharmaceutical companies have turned HTA, which should be a scientific and impartial process, into a battleground for personal interests.” (P18).

Lack of mechanisms to counter non-scientific influence

The study found limited mechanisms to identify and manage COI within HTA decision-making committees, enabling biased individuals to influence outcomes.

“In expert committees making HTA decisions, many members have financial or organizational interests and no serious system exists to identify and manage these conflicts. These individuals influence decisions despite their conflicts of interest.” (P2).

“Independent and impartial members in these committees are very few, and their voices are rarely heard, so most decisions are shaped by special interests.” (P27).

Weakness in policy-making and regulatory frameworks

Absence of comprehensive guidelines for managing conflicts of interest

A critical weakness is the lack of binding laws and guidelines to effectively identify and manage conflicts of interest in HTA.

“The lack of clear and binding laws and guidelines means there is no real commitment to identify and manage conflicts of interest in HTA, and this problem is growing day by day.” (P6).

“Unless we establish a transparent legal and executive framework for managing conflicts of interest, we will never be able to eliminate this problem.” (P11).

Dependency of HTA organizations on stakeholder entities

The dependency of HTA bodies on ministries or organizations with vested interests undermines their independence.

“Organizations responsible for HTA usually operate under ministries or bodies that themselves have financial or organizational interests in health technologies. This dependency undermines their independence and impartiality.” (P14).

“As long as the HTA organization is not independent, its results cannot earn public or professional trust, and decisions are made under non-scientific pressures.” (P21).

Inability of regulatory bodies to detect hidden conflicts

Regulatory bodies lack the necessary resources and tools to detect hidden COI.

“Many conflicts of interest occur covertly, and regulatory bodies lack the tools, resources, or capacity to detect and confront them.” (P4).

“The absence of strong monitoring technologies and mechanisms means that hidden conflicts remain unnoticed and unaddressed, pushing the system toward corruption and distrust.” (P28).

Institutionalization of conflicts of interest in organizational culture

Normalization of self-interested and group-oriented behaviors

COI have become normalized within the organizational culture, and pursuing personal or group benefits is often seen as acceptable behavior.

“In our organizational culture, conflicts of interest have become so accepted that pursuing personal or group benefits is seen as normal behavior, and few consider it problematic.” (P8).

“Many people believe conflicts of interest are an unsolvable problem and therefore simply accept them, which normalizes self-interested behaviors.” (P17).

Preference for personal and political relationships over merit and transparency

Appointments to key positions often rely more on political or personal connections than on expertise, worsening conflicts of interest.

“Appointments to key positions in the health system are usually based more on political and personal relationships than on competence and scientific expertise, which significantly increases conflicts of interest.” (P10).

“I have seen unqualified individuals appointed to important roles simply because of their close relationships with powerful figures, which severely undermines scientific decision-making.” (P20).

Organizational indifference to the risks of conflicts of interest

There is widespread organizational indifference toward the risks posed by COI, resulting in a lack of effective policies.

“Many organizations do not consider conflicts of interest as a serious threat, and therefore, no effective policies or actions are developed to address it.” (P13).

“This organizational indifference has allowed conflicts of interest to persist in the health system without meaningful change.” (P25).

Negative impact of conflicts of interest on HTA performance

Shifting HTA research priorities toward specific interests

HTA research tends to prioritize projects with financial backing rather than addressing the most urgent health system needs.

“Most HTA research and projects are directed towards technologies with financial backing, while less attention is paid to the real and urgent needs of the health system. This diverts limited resources toward specific interests.” (P1,)

“Important and priority issues in the health system receive less attention due to lack of financial independence and external pressures, which weakens HTA’s effectiveness.” (P23).

Ignoring HTA results in resource allocation

HTA findings that question the cost-effectiveness or necessity of certain technologies are often disregarded due to political or economic pressures.

“HTA findings that indicate inefficiency or high costs are often ignored, and ineffective technologies continue to receive support due to short-term interests or political pressures.” (P16).

“In practice, budget and insurance decisions are mostly influenced by the economic interests of stakeholders rather than HTA’s scientific evidence.” (P24).

Erosion of public and professional trust in HTA

The influence of vested interests and lack of transparency have undermined trust in HTA among both professionals and the public.

“Due to the influence of special interests and lack of transparency, trust in HTA among the scientific community and the public has declined, with many perceiving it as a power game rather than an impartial evaluation process.” (P19).

“Unfortunately, many health professionals view HTA as a political rather than a scientific process, serving the interests of particular groups instead of improving service quality.” (P7).

Discussion

This study provides a critical examination of how COI poses a significant barrier to the development of HTA in Iran. Through in-depth interviews with key stakeholders, we explored how structural, cultural, and regulatory shortcomings perpetuate hidden interests that compromise the objectivity and credibility of HTA processes. Participants highlighted a lack of institutional mechanisms to disclose and manage COI, which in turn erodes trust in HTA outputs and creates room for undue influence by powerful actors in both public and private sectors.

Our findings resonate with broader international concerns regarding COI in health policymaking. Although Iran represents a specific case, the challenges observed here, opaque decision-making structures, overlapping roles of experts and stakeholders, and insufficient accountability are not unique to one country. Similar challenges have been reported in other LMICs, where weak regulatory oversight and politicized appointment systems impede transparent HTA implementation [10, 20]. Similar patterns have been documented in other low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), as well as in high-income contexts, indicating that COI is a global governance challenge that requires systemic responses [21, 22].

Importantly, our study adds to the global literature by contextualizing these governance issues within a semi-centralized health system characterized by limited pluralism in decision-making. Unlike systems where independent HTA agencies have greater autonomy, Iran’s structure allows vested interests to dominate discussions around technology evaluation and prioritization [23]. In comparison, middle-income countries like Thailand and India have made progress by establishing independent HTA agencies and adopting structured COI management frameworks supported by legal mandates, demonstrating feasible pathways for reform in similar contexts [24, 25]. This limits the capacity of HTA to serve as an impartial mechanism for evidence-based policymaking [3, 26].

Our findings align with the WHO’s 2022 guidance on managing conflicts of interest in health policy, which emphasizes the establishment of transparent disclosure systems, periodic review of declared interests, and institutional separation between advisory and decision-making roles [27]. The absence of these mechanisms in Iran’s HTA system highlights a crucial implementation gap relative to international standards.

Our analysis further suggests that managing COI is not merely a technical task, but a deeply political process requiring institutional reform, legal safeguards, and cultural change [28, 29]. In countries such as Iran, where informal networks and political affiliations often override formal processes, addressing COI demands a shift toward transparency, public accountability, and the depersonalization of decision-making roles [30, 31]. Legal instruments such as mandatory asset declarations, public registries of interests, and independent oversight committees are essential tools that could strengthen governance and limit undue influence [32].

This has global relevance for HTA to fulfill its potential as a decision-support tool in national health systems; mechanisms for identifying and mitigating COI must be integrated into governance frameworks [33, 34]. Oortwijn et al. emphasize that structured stakeholder deliberation and transparent governance can reduce uncertainty and bias in HTA decision-making principles directly applicable to COI management [35]. The WHO and other international bodies increasingly emphasize the need for such systems, particularly as countries adopt value-based healthcare models and expand access to new health technologies [36–38].

The findings of this study underscore that COI in HTA is not merely a technical or ethical lapse but a symptom of deeper governance and managerial deficits. Therefore, mitigating these conflicts requires interdisciplinary interventions. From a legal perspective, there is an urgent need to codify binding disclosure laws and establish independent judicial oversight mechanisms [39]. From a management perspective, reforming organizational culture, redefining performance evaluation metrics, and depoliticizing appointments are critical [40]. The intersection of these fields, law, management, and public health, offers a more robust framework for understanding and addressing the systemic nature of COI than any single discipline could provide alone.

Moreover, our study illustrates how COI can distort health priorities and resource allocation. In Iran, technologies are sometimes prioritized not based on cost-effectiveness or need but due to lobbying by interest groups, a phenomenon echoed in other LMICs [7, 41, 42]. This finding highlights the urgent need to safeguard HTA from politicization and ensure that it aligns with public health goals rather than market-driven incentives [4, 43, 44]. Compared with countries that have integrated explicit COI disclosure mechanisms into their HTA frameworks [45, 46], Iran’s absence of enforceable regulations contributes to fragmented and inconsistent COI management. Insights from these experiences could guide the development of governance arrangements aligned with Iran’s institutional context.

From a policy perspective, this study supports the development of independent, legally mandated HTA bodies with clearly defined protocols for COI disclosure and management. These institutions should be shielded from political and commercial pressure and include diverse stakeholder representation to reduce monopolization of influence. Policymakers should institutionalize COI management by embedding disclosure and review mechanisms into HTA legislation, creating independent oversight boards, and aligning national policies with WHO’s 2022 recommendations [27]. Policymakers should institutionalize interdisciplinary committees for HTA oversight, comprising not only epidemiologists and economists but also legal experts and organizational behavior specialists, to ensure that COI management is embedded in both the regulatory framework and the day-to-day administrative culture. As HTA continues to globalize, driven by cross-national collaborations, donor influence, and shared evaluation frameworks, global governance mechanisms must ensure that COI standards are not only defined but enforceable across diverse settings [47, 48]. The experiences of countries like Iran provide crucial insights for international actors seeking to support equitable and transparent health systems through HTA.

While this study provides qualitative insights, it also reveals a significant gap in interdisciplinary research in Iran that combines health policy with administrative law and strategic management. Future empirical studies should investigate how legal sanctions, managerial accountability, and political economy factors interact to either perpetuate or mitigate COI within health technology governance.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, as a qualitative study, the findings are not statistically generalizable to all stakeholders involved in HTA in Iran. The purposive sampling strategy aimed to ensure diversity in participant backgrounds, yet the perspectives may still reflect a subset of views shaped by individual experiences and institutional contexts. Second, despite efforts to establish trust and neutrality, participants may have withheld or moderated their opinions on sensitive topics such as COI, particularly given the political and hierarchical nature of Iran’s health system. Although the interviewers had no prior relationships with participants, social desirability bias could not be fully eliminated. Third, the interpretation of findings relies on thematic analysis, which is inherently shaped by the researchers’ perspectives and prior assumptions. While multiple analysts, triangulation, and expert oversight helped reduce this bias, complete objectivity is unattainable in qualitative research. Fourth, data were collected over several months, during which policy dynamics and organizational structures may have evolved, potentially influencing participants’ views. This temporal variation could affect the consistency of responses across interviews. Lastly, while the study focused on Iran’s health system, the complex sociopolitical and institutional context may limit the transferability of the findings to other countries with different governance structures and HTA maturity levels. Although we included professionals from legal and managerial backgrounds, the depth of interdisciplinary analysis was constrained by the qualitative nature of the study. A more comprehensive interdisciplinary investigation would require quantitative surveys and legal document analysis, which were beyond the scope of this research. Future research employing mixed-methods approaches and cross-country comparisons could provide broader insights into the systemic influence of COI on HTA development.

Conclusion

This study highlights conflicts of interest as a critical and under-recognized barrier to the development and effective implementation of HTA in Iran. The findings demonstrate how entrenched personal, institutional, and political interests undermine the objectivity, transparency, and credibility of HTA processes, limiting its ability to guide rational health policy and resource allocation. Without effective mechanisms to identify, disclose, and manage COI, HTA risks becoming a tool for advancing individual or organizational agendas rather than serving public health goals. The structural absence of regulatory safeguards, combined with a lack of accountability and a culture of informal influence, allows conflicts to persist unchecked across different levels of the health system. Given the importance of governance tools, the introduction of clear legal and regulatory frameworks for COI disclosure, monitoring, and enforcement is essential to strengthen HTA credibility. Policymakers should prioritize the establishment of independent HTA agencies supported by binding COI management legislation, transparency registers, and routine audits of declared interests. Furthermore, integration of WHO’s 2022 COI management guidance into national HTA frameworks can promote global alignment and ensure public accountability. Engaging civil society, academia, and international partners can support these efforts and help ensure that HTA fulfills its role in improving health system performance and equity. By shedding light on the multidimensional nature of COI within the Iranian HTA context, this study contributes to a growing body of evidence calling for governance reforms. Future research should adopt a mixed-methods interdisciplinary design, incorporating legal analysis, organizational behavior surveys, and political economy models to comprehensively map the drivers of COI. Collaborative studies involving faculties of law, management, and public health are urgently needed to translate these findings into actionable regulatory and administrative reforms. Future strategies must address these challenges if HTA is to realize its potential as a trusted decision-support tool in Iran’s evolving health landscape.
