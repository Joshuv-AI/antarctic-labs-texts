Synergistic metal halide perovskite@metal-organic framework hybrids for photocatalytic CO2 reduction
Authors: Saandra Sharma, Noah Jacob, G. Krishnamurthy Grandhi, Mahendra B. Choudhary, Swathi Ippili, Venkatesha R. Hathwar, Paola Vivo, Rabindranath Lo, M. Motapothula, Kolleboyina Jayaramulu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe photocatalytic reduction of carbon dioxide (CO2) into multi-electron carbon products remains challenging due to the inherent stability of CO2 and slow multi-electron transfer kinetics. Here in, we synthesized a hybrid material, cesium copper halide (Cs3Cu2I5) intercalated onto two-dimensional (2D) cobalt-based zeolite framework (ZIF-9-III) nanosheets (denoted as Cs3Cu2I5@ZIF-1) through a simple mechanochemical grinding. The synergy in the hybrid effectively reduces CO2 to carbon monoxide (CO) at 110 μmol/g/h and methane at 5 μmol/g/h with high selectivity, suppressing hydrogen evolution. Further, we have investigated additional Cs3Cu2I5@ZIF hybrids with varying ZIF-9-III amounts, confirming their selective CO2 reduction to methane over hydrogen. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations reveal a non-covalent interaction between Cs3Cu2I5 and ZIF-9-III, with electron transfer suggesting potential for improved photocatalysis.

Introduction

The urgent need to reduce increasing carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions from fossil fuels underscores the critical importance of sustainable energy solutions.1 Converting CO2 into hydrocarbon fuels through photocatalysis offers a promising solution, addressing environmental concerns and meeting future energy needs.2,3,4 However, this conversion demands significant energy because of the strong C-O bond, coupled with complex electron transfer processes, necessitating highly efficient and selective photocatalysts.5,6,7,8,9,10,11,12 Recently, metal halide perovskites (MHPs) have shown promise in the photocatalytic reduction of CO2 (CO2 reduction reaction [CO2RR]) into multi-electron carbon products. However, challenges remain, including inadequate CO2 capture, slow reaction rates, and sensitivity toward moisture, UV light, oxygen, and high temperature.13,14

Moreover, there are numerous research reports focused on photocatalytic CO2RR by incorporating MHPs into different polymers, two-dimensional (2D) materials, and porous materials.15,16 The requirement for effective catalytic sites presents a critical obstacle to maximizing the potential of MHP-based hybrids for CO2RR. Recently, metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) have emerged as promising photoactive CO2 catalysts due to their crystalline nature and coupled with tailored functionalities, including selective CO2 adsorption/activation and the presence of highly active metal centers.15,17,18,19,20,21,22 The ordered and functional pores of MOFs can accommodate a variety of guest molecules, enabling a range of photophysical and technological applications.23,24,25 Considerable effort has been devoted to creat various MOF hybrids using different perovskites as guests, leading to hybrid materials with superior properties compared to the individual components.26,27,28,29,30 The confinement effect of MHPs within MOF templates and their improved stability facilitate intimate contact between MHPs and the catalytic active centers within MOFs, thereby enhancing the efficiency of CO2 conversion.13,31,32,33,34,35,36,37,38 In addition to the conversion rate challenge, the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) competing with the CO2RR plays a crucial role in determining the efficiency and selectivity of the photocatalytic process. While engineering heterojunction composites by adjusting band edge positions can improve photocatalytic conversion, it may not always control the selectivity of both reduction reactions, as they typically occur on the same surface. Since these reactions happen on the surface, modifying the surface properties of photocatalysts becomes crucial. In this work, we have developed a metal halide perovskite-based metal-organic framework (MHP@MOF) by integrating cesium copper iodide (Cs3Cu2I5) particles onto 2D cobalt-based zeolite framework (ZIF-9-III) nanosheets.39 This is achieved using a simple, one-pot mechanochemical grinding method, as depicted in Scheme 1, resulting in a hybrid material denoted as Cs3Cu2I5@ZIF-1. In order to ensure the high structural and optical quality of the MHP@MOF hybrid material, it is crucial to select an appropriate MHP candidate whose high-quality properties are retained even when subjected to a simple room temperature grinding process. The chosen lead-free perovskite, Cs3Cu2I5, is ideal due to its significant structural flexibility and bright luminescence. In addition, its synthesis is straightforward and involves a simple hand grinding process.40 The hybrid material, Cs3Cu2I5@ZIF-1 shows excellent performance in converting CO2 into CO and CH4, while also preventing other HER. Due to their capacity to improve electron-hole pair separation and increase surface active site density, these halide perovskite-MOF hybrid structures mark a significant advancement in solar photocatalytic technology. Furthermore, our innovative approach includes light-induced surface reconstruction, generating hydroxyl groups on Cs3Cu2I5, accelerating CO2 reduction to CO and CH4 while quashing competing reactions like HER. Therefore, we also prepared other MHP@MOF hybrids (Cs3Cu2I5@ZIF-0.5) and (Cs3Cu2I5@ZIF-2) using varying amounts of ZIF-9-III with different photophysical properties. Further, these hybrid materials obtained by varying the ratio between Cs3Cu2I5 and the Co(II)-based MOF reveal that the exceptional selectivity for methane over hydrogen stems from the improved separation of electron-hole pairs and the increased density of surface-active sites. Furthermore, our density functional theory (DFT) calculations reveal a non-covalent interaction between the Cs3Cu2I5 and ZIF-9-III, suggesting a plausible CO2 reduction reaction pathway to produce CO and methane. In addition to that, we carried out an analysis of electron-trapping and photocatalytic activity of perovskite. Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of Cs3Cu2I5@ZIF hybrids

Low-toxicity and lead-free MHPs have attracted significant attention for various optoelectronic applications, including light-emitting diodes and X-ray detectors, due to their bright emission intensities and impressive stability in the air.41,42,43,44 In this study, we utilized a simple, cost-effective, and environmentally friendly method to synthesize zero-dimensional cesium copper iodide (Cs3Cu2I5) particles by simple grinding of cesium iodide (CsI) and copper iodide (CuI) in the desired molar ratio of 3:2 for 5 min in the presence of acetone solvent (see supporting information for complete synthesis information and Table S1).40 The powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) pattern of Cs3Cu2I5 powder sample confirms their formation in a zero-dimensional crystal structure and structural rigidity, which is in consistent with the literature. Le-Bail profile fitting was conducted on the PXRD pattern to confirm the phase purity of the Cs3Cu2I5 particles, as shown in Figures S1, using the FullProf suite, and the resulting lattice parameters are listed in Table S2. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was conducted on the Cs3Cu2I5 powder, and the XPS survey profile confirms the presence of Cs, Cu, and I in this sample and copper in monovalent nature (see Figure S2). Among porous MOFs, cobalt-based zeolitic imidazole frameworks (ZIFs) stand out due to their superior photocatalytic efficiency.45,46,47,48 This is attributed to their catalytically active Co(II) centers, remarkable thermal stability, and intense purple color, making them excellent light-harvesting species. Herein, we prepared ZIF-9-III through a simple and scalable mechanochemical method.39 The ZIF-9-III exhibits a (4,4) square planar grid structure, wherein Co(II) ions are coordinated by benzimidazole groups, linked at corners to form Co4(bIm)16 units (see Figures S3 and S4).49 The phase purity of the synthesized compound was determined using PXRD. The PXRD pattern also confirms all crystal planes corresponding to the ZIF-9-III phase. Additionally, Le-Bail profile fitting was conducted on ZIF-9-III, as shown in Figures S5, using the FullProf suite, and the resulting lattice parameters are listed in Table S2.50 The XPS survey spectrum shows the presence of cobalt, carbon, and nitrogen elements (see Figure S6). In the high-resolution spectra, the N 1s peak exhibits a single peak at 398.97 eV, corresponding to pyrrolic nitrogen, while the C 1s peak appears at 284.2 eV, indicative of sp2 carbon. The Co 2p3/2 peaks are observed at 780.40 eV and 781.7 eV, with two satellite peaks at 783.8 eV and 789.9 eV, suggesting the presence of CoII centers. We have engineered MHP-based ZIFs by grinding the precursor materials that are necessary for the production of both Cs3Cu2I5 and ZIF-9-III. The process was carried out in an ethanol environment, which produced a hybrid of Cs3Cu2I5 and ZIF-9-III, denoted as Cs3Cu2I5@ZIF-1 (please see supporting information for complete details). The phase purity of the dried Cs3Cu2I5@ZIF-1 sample was confirmed through PXRD, as shown in Figure 1A. The PXRD pattern shows all peaks corresponding to both cobalt imidazole framework ZIF-9-III and Cs3Cu2I5 particles. Moreover, the Le-Bail profile fitting was conducted on Cs3Cu2I5@ZIF-1 using the FullProf suite to confirm the absence of impurity phases during the synthesis of these hybrids, and the resulting lattice parameters are listed in Table S1. The XPS survey spectrum of Cs3Cu2I5@ZIF-1 reveals distinct peaks corresponding to cesium, copper, cobalt, carbon, nitrogen, and iodine, as shown in Figure S7. The C 1s signal can be resolved into two peaks, indicating distinct carbon bonding states: C-C (sp2) at 283.8 eV and nitrogen-bonded carbon at 285.1 eV (Figure 1B). In the high-resolution N 1s spectrum shows two peaks at 398.4 and 400.1 eV corresponding to C=N-C and pyrrolic, nitrogen atoms, respectively (Figure 1C). Further, high resolution Co 2p shows Co 2p3/2 and Co 2p1/2 peaks at 780.39 and 795.3 eV, respectively. They confirm the presence of Co (II) centers in CoZIF-9-III (Figure 1D). High resolution deconvolution spectrum of Cu 2p shows two peaks at ∼932.4 and 952.1 eV corresponding Co 2p1/2 and Co 2p3/2 to characteristic of Cu(I) (Figure 1E). The high-resolution iodine spectrum exhibits two prominent peaks at binding energies of 618.8 and 630.2 eV, attributed to the I 3d spin-orbit doublet of I 3d5/2 and I 3d3/2, respectively (see Figure S8). The high-resolution deconvolution Cs 3D spectrum shows Cs 3d3/2 and Cs 3d5/2 peaks with the respective binding energies of 724.4 and 738.6, attributable to Cs3Cu2I5 particles (Figure 1F). The combined XRD and XPS analysis suggests that there is not a significant shift in peaks, revealing the absence of noncovalent interactions between Co-ZIF-9(III) nanosheets and zero-dimensional Cs3Cu2I5 particles, as illustrated in Scheme 1. Further, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) image of Hybrid of Cs3Cu2I5@ZIF-1 shows a homogeneous distribution of bright spots corresponding to Cs3Cu2I5 particles interspersed among micron-sized crystals of cobalt-based ZIF-9-III (refer to Figures 2A and S9). Additionally, high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) images of the hybrid structure with varying magnifications reveal bundles of ZIF-9-III nanosheets intercalated with Cs3Cu2I5 particles (Figures 2B–2F). The colloidal solution of nanosheets was dispersed onto a flat substrate for characterization using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and atomic force microscoy (AFM). AFM images revealed structures with typical lateral dimensions of 80–100 nm and thicknesses of approximately ∼1.3 nm (see Figure S10). High-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) mapping confirms a homogeneous distribution of cesium, copper, cobalt, carbon, nitrogen, and iodine throughout the sample (Figures 2G–2N). To highlight the impact of ZIF-9-III content in Cs3Cu2I5@ZIF, we synthesized two additional hybrids (Cs3Cu2I5@ZIF-0.5) and (Cs3Cu2I5@ZIF-2), using varying amounts of ZIF-9-III (0.5 mmol and 2 mmol) while keeping the Cs3Cu2I5 source constant at 1 mmol. Figures 3A and 3B shows the PL (photoluminescence) and PLE (photoluminescence excitation) spectra of Cs3Cu2I5.40,51 The PL peak wavelength of around 445 nm aligns well with previous studies on this material. This zero-dimensional, lead-free, perovskite-inspired material has a high photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY) of about 90%, due to the strong confinement of the excitons. However, the strong blue luminescence of Cs3Cu2I5 was substantially lower when mixed with Co-ZIF-9(III), indicating suppressed luminescence intensities in the Cs3Cu2I5@ZIF hybrid samples. As shown in Figure 3C, the PLQY of Cs3Cu2I5 decreases from 90% to 1.5–3.0% in these hybrid samples. This substantial PL reduction, visible even to the naked eye (Figure 3), suggests an effective electron transfer from Cs3Cu2I5 to the Co-ZIF-9(III). This is further supported by the favorable alignment of the conduction bands between the two materials (Cs3Cu2I5: −2.03 eV and Co-ZIF-9(III): 1.95 eV).52 Time-resolved PL decay curves of the pure Cs3Cu2I5 and a Cs3Cu2I5-MOF hybrid sample are shown in Figure 3D. The PL lifetime of Cs3Cu2I5 is 1.43 μs, which is within the broad range of exciton lifetimes reported for Cs3Cu2I5. However, the lifetime decreases to 1.04 μs for the Cs3Cu2I5@ZIF sample in the presence of Co-ZIF-9(III). While this marginal decrease in the excitonic lifetime may not completely explain the significant PL quenching (from 90% to 1.5%), the accelerated decay at the start of the PL decay curve in the case of Cs3Cu2I5@ZIF (Figure 3D) compared to pure Cs3Cu2I5 indicates that the major changes related to electron transfer between the two materials in the hybrid samples occur in the sub-nanosecond regime. Photocatalytic activity of the samples was tested using a custom-made reactor with a fused silica window through which illumination was provided to the sample dispersed in water using AAA class a solar simulator calibrated to 1 Sun (100 mW cm−2) (see Scheme S1). The reactor after being purged with Argon gas was pressurized to 2 bar against atmosphere and the reaction products were monitored using an online gas chromatography (GC) at regular intervals for a period of 12 h. The primary reduction products observed for all the evaluated by passing the resultant photocatalysts as pristine Cs3Cu2I5, ZIF-9-III, and their hybrids, namely Cs3Cu2I5@ZIF-0.5, Cs3Cu2I5@ZIF-1 and Cs3Cu2I5@ZIF-2 are H2, CO, and CH4. The photocatalytic CO2 reduction reactions of Cs3Cu2I5@ZIF hybrids

These findings strongly indicate the efficient execution of the CO2RR within this composite material. Intriguingly, during this period, there was a notable absence of the HER. In stark contrast as shown in Figures 4A and 4B, both the individual perovskite and ZIF-9-III components independently exhibited hydrogen (H2) production, devoid of any discernible reduction of CO2 into CH4, with rates falling below our detection limits of 0.1 μmol/g (Figures 4C–4F). However, Cs3Cu2I5@ZIF-0.5 heralded a significant transformation in activity, initiating CO2RR to CH4. Within this composite material, CO was generated at a rate of approximately 60 μmol/g/h, accompanied by the production of CH4 at a rate of 4 μmol/g/h. Therefore, the synergistic effect of incorporating MOFs and perovskites demonstrates possibilities toward reshaping the reactivity and selectivity of composite materials. Upon further amplification of the ZIF-9-III proportion in the perovskite matrix, as evidenced in Cs3Cu2I5@ZIF-2, a fascinating temporal pattern is visible in which, no hydrogen was produced within the first 6 h, directing photoelectrons preferentially toward the reduction of CO2 into CH4, excluding the formation of CO. However, after this initial duration, hydrogen evolution rapidly ensued at an impressive rate of 340 μmol/g/h; reaching up to the production of the precursor ZIF-9-III. Therefore, there exists a dynamic nature and adaptability of the catalytic system of these hybrids which can be engineered to selectively inhibit the competition of the highly efficient HER exhibited by the parent compound and produce desired CO2RR products. This selectivity toward the CO2 reduction reaction in Cs3Cu2I5@ZIF-1 is attributed to the strong affinity of CO2 molecules of cobalt based ZIFs in the literature.53,54 It should be noted that the stability of the catalyst Cs3Cu2I5@ZIF-1 after post-CO2 reduction after 12 h long photocatalysis measurements was confirmed through powder XRD, TEM, and elemental analysis. The powder XRD shows all significant planes corresponding ZIF-9-III and TEM images show a layered morphology (see Figure S19). Indeed, energy-dispersive X-Ray spectroscopy (EDS) analysis also confirms the presence of cobalt, carbon, nitrogen, iodine, cesium, and copper (see Figure S20), Further our photocatalytic CO2 Reduced CO values comparable with literature as shown in Table S3. We performed 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) measurements to detect liquid products from CO2RR but observed none. No peaks were found for formic acid or hydrocarbons (see Figure S21). Additionally, efficient photoelectron transport from perovskite onto ZIF-9-III, as indicated by time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) lifetime data together with the PLQY loss, contributes to this selective behavior and plausible mechanism depicted at Figure 5. The composite’s success in promoting long photocarrier lifetimes, efficient photoelectron transfer, and minimal hydrogen dimerization on surfaces underscores its potential for real-world applications. Nevertheless, our composite material addresses a fundamental rule for achieving selective CO2 reduction reactions. A portion of the catalyst should repel water molecules and strongly favor the adsorption of CO2 molecules, exhibit strong photo-absorption with long lifetimes of photocarriers, and enable easy transport to surface-adsorbed CO2 molecules selectively. DFT calculations for understanding Cs3Cu2I5@ZIF hybrids

To enhance our comprehension of the interaction between Cs3Cu2I5 and CoZIF-9-III, we performed DFT calculations. Initially, we explore the interactions between perovskite and water concerning the pathway for the HER (see Figure S22). We consider the binding of a water molecule on the surface of Cs and Cu sites and the results show that the water molecule preferentially binds at Cs site. The adsorption energy ΔEads(H2O) is define as,H2O(g)+perovskite→H2O(ads)ΔEads(H2O)=E[H2O(ads)]−E[H2O(g)]−E(perovskite)where, E[H2O(ads)] is the total energy of the perovskite with an adsorbed water molecule and E[H2O(g)] and E(perovskite) are those of the isolated water molecule and of the perovskite, respectively. Similarly, the adsorption energy ΔEads(2H2O) of two water molecules on the surface of Cs sites is calculated using the following equation:ΔEads(2H2O)=E[2H2O(ads)]−2∗E[H2O(g)]−E(perovskite)

The calculated values of adsorption energies indicate the increased probability to find water molecules adsorbed on Cs sites. The ability of Cs3Cu2I5 surface to dissociate water molecule is studied here. For hydrogen evolution, the process occurs either in the ground or in the excited state of perovskite. The dissociation energy for various charged states, ΔEdisn(H2O) (neutral, n = 0 and under photo charging conditions, n = 1, 2) is calculated using the following equation:H2O(ads)+ne−→OH(ads)−+H(ads)1−nΔEdis(H2O)=E[OH(ads)−+H(ads)1−n]−E[H2O(ads)+ne−]where, E[OH(ads)−+H(ads)1−n] is the total energy of a system in which a H2O(ads) is replaced by a OH(ads)− with the dissociate H being adsorbed on Cu site. E[H2O(ads)+ne−] is the energy of the water adsorbed perovskite with n extra electrons. The calculated results show that the dissociation of water is considerably disfavored in the ground state of perovskite. However, under electron-rich conditions, the dissociation energy is significantly decreased when one electron is captured (see Figure S23). Thus, the trapping of electrons at a perovskite is an essential step for hydrogen evolution step. This process is mediated by the movement of two Cu atoms toward each other and the out-of-plane shift of I atoms. To understand the reactivity of perovskite toward the HER, we consider the overall reaction,2H2O(ads)+2e−→2OH(ads)−+H2(g)

The possible pathway of hydrogen evolution involves various intermediates starting with the adsorption of two H2O molecules (Figure 6A). The reaction proceeds through one and two electrons trapping at Cu sites and there is a dissociation of H2O into OH− + H+. The dissociation of water is energetically favored, making the hydrogen evolution step feasible. In order to acquire a deeper understanding of the interaction between Cs3Cu2I5 and the ZIF-9-III, DFT calculations have been carried out. The relaxed geometry indicates the presence of a non-covalent interaction between the perovskite and the ZIF-9-III. The charge distribution of the composite system is illustrated in Figure 6B, with an accumulation of electrons in the vicinity of the phenyl ring and a reduction in the regions containing I species from Cs3Cu2I5. This observation suggests a relatively weak interaction between the perovskite and the ZIF-9-III. Moreover, it implies that during the interaction, there is a potential for electron transfer from the perovskite to the ZIF-9-III. This electron transfer process has been elucidated based on the positions of the conduction band (CB) and the valence band (VB) of perovskite and ZIF-9-III. The CB and VB positions of ZIF-9-III are found to be more negative compared to those of Cs3Cu2I5 (see Figure S24). This indicates that incorporating Cs3Cu2I5 and ZIF-9-III can form a type II heterojunction photocatalyst. Under ultraviolet light irradiation, electrons in the VB of both Cs3Cu2I5 and ZIF-9-III are excited to their respective CBs, creating holes in the VBs. Following this, electrons generated in the higher CB of Cs3Cu2I5 are more prone to transfer to the CB of ZIF-9-III. These electrons, along with the photogenerated electrons on ZIF-9-III, are involved in the subsequent reduction reaction of CO2 molecules adsorbed within the ZIF matrix. Simultaneously, the photogenerated holes in the VB of ZIF-9-III can transfer to the VB of perovskite, facilitated by the potential difference between their respective energy levels. Such photogenerated holes in perovskite were neutralized by H2O molecules, resulting in the generation of O2. Cs3Cu2I5 exhibits a direct bandgap at the Γ point. The projected density of states (PDOS) depicted in Figure S24A illustrates that the valence band maximum (VBM) primarily consists of Cu 3d orbitals, whereas conduction band minumum (CBM) originates from a combination of Cu 4s and I 5p orbitals. In contrast, Cs+ ions do not contribute to either the CBM or VBM. In ZIF-9-III, the unpaired d-electrons in Co contribute to the VBM. The CO2 reduction process occurs at Co site of ZIF-9-III. This is possible due to the interface between the perovskite and zeolite imidazole framework (ZIF-9). Photoelectrons transfer pathway occurs from perovskite to the organic linker of ZIF-9. Within the MOF, the active Co center serves as an electron reservoir, receiving electrons for activation and subsequent utilization in the CO2 activation and reduction reaction.50 Initially, CO2 molecule adsorbed at the MOF surface and held in place by the weak hydrogen bonding of the organic linker, providing stability (see Figure S25). Figure 7 illustrates the energy profile diagram representing the preferred paths for CO2 reduction on the MOF surface. The energy calculations were based on the assumption that the chemical potential of the electron-proton pair matches half of that of hydrogen at equilibrium.55 In the first step of hydrogenation, CO2 is primarily transformed into HCOO∗, which then binds to Co through an oxygen atom at a distance of 1.871 Å. Previous studies have demonstrated that the adsorption of HCOO∗ is stronger than that of C∗OOH, leading to enhanced catalytic performance.56 In the second hydrogenation step, an additional proton is adsorbed onto the O atom, resulting in the formation of HCO∗OH. The reaction profile indicates that the step is energetically favorable. In the HCO∗OH intermediate, the Co-O bond length undergoes a slight elongation, extending to a distance of 1.937 Å. Additionally, a non-covalent interaction involving O-H…π occurs in this intermediate. Subsequently, HCO∗OH dissociates to release water and gives rise to C∗O. This particular step has a slightly uphill energy barrier. After this step, a proton is added to the C atom, forming HC∗O with an energetically favorable pathway. Then an extra proton attaches to the O atom, resulting in the intermediate HC∗OH. This intermediate then acquires one more proton to produce C∗H2OH, followed by a downhill process that generates C∗H2 while releasing H2O. In summary, we have successfully synthesized a hybrid material, Cs3Cu2I5@ZIF-1, through a one-pot mechanochemical grinding process, combining cesium copper halide (Cs3Cu2I5) with two-dimensional cobalt-based zeolite framework (ZIF-9-III) nanosheets. This hybrid material exhibits exceptional photocatalytic activity, particularly in the reduction of CO2 to CO and CH4, with high selectivity and suppression of competing processes such as hydrogen evolution. The hybrid shows remarkable selectivity for methane production over hydrogen, entirely eliminating hydrogen evolution and selectively generating CO and CH4 at rates of 109 μmol/g/h and 5 μmol/g/h, respectively. The superior selectivity of is attributed to enhanced separation of electron-hole pairs and increased density of surface-active sites. Our DFT-relaxed geometry of the hybrid structures reveals a non-covalent interaction between Cs3Cu2I5 and ZIF-9, suggesting potential electron transfer from Cs3Cu2I5 to ZIF during this interaction. Overall, our research introduces synthesizing low-dimensional perovskite-like materials in photocatalysis, especially when combined with synergic porous templates, offering promising prospects for sustainable energy conversion and environmental remediation.

Limitations of the study

In this study, we synthesized a hybrid material consisting of MHP and two-dimensional ZIF-9 nanosheets using a straightforward mechanochemical grinding approach. It is important to note that achieving complete purity of the sample remains a challenge with this method, which may affect the CO2 reduction reaction yields and hinder our understanding of the interactions between MHP and 2D ZIF. Additionally, one of the main limitations of this work is the need for isotopic labeling experiments to accurately identify the origin of the products in CO2 reduction to CO and CH4, which we were unable to perform in this study.

Method details

Synthesis of bulk Co-ZIF-9 (III)

The synthesis of Co-ZIF-9-III adopted from the literature. Co(NO3)2⋅6H2O (0.30 mmol), bIm (0.60 mmol), and sodium bicarbonate (0.56 mmol) were combined in a mortar with 2 mL of ethanol and ground for approximately 5 min. The resulting mixture was washed with water to remove any remaining NaHCO3 and NaNO3. An indigo color CoZIF-9-II powdered product was obtained.

Mechanochemical synthesis of Cs3Cu2I5 particles

Solid-state synthesis of Cs3Cu2I5 particles CsI and CuI were taken in a mortar in 3:2 M ratios, respectively and were mixed and ground by hand using a pestle for 5–10 min. A few mL of acetone was used for mixing the precursors before the grinding in the case of larger quantities of precursors.

Preparation of Cs3Cu2I5@ZIF-9 hybrids

The synthesis of Cs3Cu2I5@ZIF-9 hybrids using a simple one pot synthesis of Co(NO3)2⋅6H2O, bIm, sodium bicarbonate CsI and CuI were combined in a mortar with 2 mL of ethanol and ground for approximately 5 min. The obtained product washed three times with water and three times with methanol. Methanol was decanted and replaced once per day during the course of three days and later it was removed under vacuum. We prepared two more hybrids of ZIF-9-III content in the hybrid as Cs3Cu2I5@ZIF-1, Cs3Cu2I5@ZIF-0.5 and Cs3Cu2I5@ZIF-2, using varying amounts of ZIF-9-III (1, mmol, 0.5 mmol and 2 mmol) while keeping the Cs3Cu2I5 source constant at 1 mmol. The complete synthetic information provided in the Table S1.

Photocatalysis measurements

The photocatalytic CO2 reduction reactions were conducted inside a customized 30 mL chamber. The chamber’s outer casing is constructed from aluminum, and illumination is achieved through a 10 mm thick fused quartz plate, which selectively allows the transmission of UV-Vis-NIR light. During the experimental investigations, a catalyst and 5 mL of Type 1 water (18.2 MΩ cm LABJAL) were placed in a circular glass Petri dish with a 3.8 cm inner diameter. An AAA-class solar simulator, featuring a 300 W Xenon lamp and an AM 1.5 G filter from Sciencetech, Canada, was used to provide 1 SUN (100 mW cm−2) illumination. The calibration and verification of the solar simulator were ensured using the SSIVT-REF (125–9007) single-element silicon detector, calibrated to the NIST-traceable reference cell for wavelengths ranging from 190 nm to 1100 nm. The reactor was purged with 99.999% argon gas at 2 bar pressure against atmospheric conditions, and the reactor outlet was connected to the GC (Trace 1310, Thermo Scientific) injection port online, equipped with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD) and flame ionization detector (FID) with Methanizer. The TCD channel was used to detect H2 while the FID was used to quantify CO and CH4. The reactor was purged with 99.999% argon gas at 2 bar pressure against ambient conditions. A 5 mL gas sample was injected every 3 h of light exposure using a Mass Flow Controller (Alicat) into the GC. To get rid of any leftover gases, the system was flushed three times with argon gas prior to being exposed to light and was confirmed with a GC injection.

Characterization techniques

XRD measurements were performed using X’Pert PRO MPD diffractometer (PANalytical) in the Bragg–Brentano geometry equipped with a Co X-ray tube. Samples were placed on a zero-background Si slide, gently pressed, and scanned with a step size of 0.0334°, and the 2θ range of 5°–120° was used to record the pattern. The Le-Bail profile fitting was performed on all materials using the FullProf suite and the lattice parameters determined are listed in Table S2. Infra-red spectra were acquired using an iS5 FTIR spectrometer (Thermo Nicolet) with the Smart Orbit ATR accessory featuring a ZnSe crystal. A drop of sample dispersion in ethanol or water was placed on a ZnSe crystal, left to dry forming a film in ambient conditions, and spectra were recorded by summing 50 scans. XPS was conducted using a PHI VersaProbe II (Physical Electronics) spectrometer with an Al Kα source (15 kV, 50 W, spot size 100 μm). Binding energies were referenced to the C1s core level of the C-C bond at the nominal value of 284.8 eV. Steady-state Photoluminescence (PL) spectra of the powder samples were collected using a Hitachi F-7000 fluorescence spectrophotometer. The absolute PL QYs of the powder samples were measured by exciting them at their absorption maxima wavelength in an integrated sphere (Biorad/C10027/C9920-20) at the Korea Photonics Technology Institute in Gwangju, Seoul. The variation of PL intensity during the heating-cooling cycles was measured using a connection to a Hitachi F-7000 fluorescence spectrometer with an integrated heater, temperature controller, and thermal sensor. TEM images were obtained using a JEOL 2100 TEM with an emission gun of LaB6 type operating at 160 kV. High-resolution TEM images were obtained using an FEI Titan electron microscope operating at 80 kV. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was performed using a Hitachi SU6600 instrument with an accelerating voltage of 5 kV. For these analyses, a small droplet of a material dispersion in ultrapure water (concentration approximately 0.1 mg mL−1) was placed on a carbon-coated copper grid and left to dry.

Computational details

Periodic calculations are performed using the VASP (Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package) implementation of DFT in the projected augmented plane-wave scheme. The plane-wave cutoff energy is set to 400 eV. The Monkhorst-Pack scheme using a 4 × 4 × 3 k-point mesh is used for Cs3Cu2I5 to generate the k-point sampling grids. The crystal structure of Cs3Cu2I5 (unit cell dimensions: a = 10.1743 Å, b = 11.6532 Å) was taken in this study. The band gap and density of states calculations are well-reproduced by the methods employed. A highly symmetrical Gamma k-point with the meshes of 2 × 2 × 2 and 1 × 1 × 1 are used for ZIF-9(III) and Cs3Cu2I5@CoZIF-9-III heterostructures, respectively. The calculated band gap of 3.151eV for ZIF-9(III) aligns well with the reported band gap of 3.2 eV. This indicates the appropriateness of the model and methods utilized in this study. The atomic positions are thoroughly optimized until reaching an electronic energy convergence threshold of 10−6 eV.
