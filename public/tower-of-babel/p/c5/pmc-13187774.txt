Great Saphenous Vein Availability in Lower-Extremity Arterial Disease: Analysis of a Prospectively Mapped Nonselective Cohort
Authors: Sangho Lee, Jihye Kim, Yumi Jeon, Hyung-Kee Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aimed to quantify the real-world availability of a single-segment great saphenous vein (GSV) in a nonselective Korean cohort with peripheral arterial disease and to identify patient-level predictors that may limit the adoption of a vein-first strategy.Materials and MethodsThis retrospective analysis of prospectively collected data included 115 consecutive patients admitted for evaluation and planning of lower-extremity revascularization (53 with claudication and 62 with chronic limb-threatening ischemia [CLTI]) from 2023 to 2024. All patients underwent mandatory bilateral duplex mapping before treatment by registered vascular technologists under a standardized protocol (reverse Trendelenburg position, temperature 25°C-26°C, inner-to-inner measurements). A suitable single-segment conduit was defined as a continuous, thrombus-free GSV from the saphenofemoral junction to the proximal calf without duplex-detected reflux and was categorized by minimum diameter as ideal (≥3.0 mm), acceptable (≥2.5 mm), or borderline (≥2.0 mm).ResultsIn the ipsilateral limb, the GSV met the ideal, acceptable, and borderline thresholds in 23/115 (20.0%), 39/115 (33.9%), and 57/115 (49.6%) patients, respectively. When the contralateral limb was included, overall availability (either limb) increased to 28/115 (24.3%), 58/115 (50.4%), and 75/115 (65.2%), corresponding to absolute gains of 4.3, 16.5, and 15.6 percentage points, respectively. Ipsilateral and overall ideal availability tended to be lower in CLTI than in claudication (14.5% vs. 26.4%, P=0.112 for ipsilateral; 17.7% vs. 32.1%, P=0.074 for overall). On multivariable analysis, older age and female sex were independently associated with reduced overall ideal availability (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] per year, 0.932; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.887-0.979; female sex: aOR, 0.201; 95% CI, 0.046-0.869).ConclusionIn this consecutive real-world cohort, ideal single-segment GSV availability (≥3.0 mm) was low and was further limited by older age and female sex. These findings highlight an important limitation of a vein-first strategy in routine practice and support standardized bilateral mapping to clarify conduit availability and guide individualized revascularization planning.Keywords: Great saphenous vein, Peripheral arterial disease, Infrainguinal bypass, Duplex ultrasonography, Chronic limb-threatening ischemia

INTRODUCTION

An autogenous great saphenous vein (GSV) graft is widely recommended as the preferred conduit for infrainguinal bypass, particularly when durable limb outcomes are prioritized and distal targets are anticipated [1-3]. Notably, in the Best Endovascular versus Best Surgical Therapy in Patients with Critical Limb Ischemia (BEST-CLI) trial, surgical bypass achieved superior limb-related outcomes compared with endovascular therapy among patients with an adequate single-segment GSV, reinforcing the clinical importance of conduit availability at the time of strategy selection [2]. Although the relationship between vein caliber and graft performance is continuous, smaller preoperative GSV diameters and unfavorable vein characteristics have been associated with reduced patency; accordingly, many centers use pragmatic diameter thresholds, often around 3.0 mm, to guide planning and patient counseling [4].

Despite this emphasis on a vein-first approach, the real-world availability of a suitable single-segment GSV at the point of decision-making remains incompletely defined. Trial populations may overestimate availability because conduit adequacy is commonly assessed before enrollment. In routine practice, preprocedural mapping is implemented variably, and reported suitability rates differ substantially across institutions and protocols [5,6]. Moreover, contemporary data from Asian populations are limited, and potential differences in body habitus and duplex assessment practices may further complicate cross-study comparisons [7,8]. Accordingly, a key practical question is what proportion of patients evaluated for lower-extremity revascularization actually have a suitable conduit for bypass, because this determines the real-world feasibility of a vein-first strategy.

Therefore, this study aimed to quantify the real-world availability of a single-segment GSV in a consecutive, nonselective cohort of patients evaluated for lower-extremity revascularization using mandatory bilateral duplex mapping under a standardized protocol. The specific objectives were to determine conduit availability at prespecified diameter thresholds, assess the incremental yield of contralateral mapping, and explore patient-level factors associated with reduced suitability.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1) Study design and population

We performed a retrospective analysis of prospectively collected data from consecutive patients evaluated for lower-extremity revascularization at a tertiary vascular center between January 2023 and December 2024. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Kyungpook National University Chilgok Hospital (IRB No. 2025-11-017), and the requirement for informed consent was waived because this was a retrospective analysis of prospectively collected clinical data.

Eligible participants were adults admitted with lower-extremity peripheral arterial disease (PAD) for diagnostic evaluation and revascularization planning, including those with intermittent claudication and chronic limb-threatening ischemia (CLTI). CLTI was defined clinically by ischemic rest pain and/or ischemic tissue loss (ulceration or gangrene) attributable to PAD. Patients with isolated aortoiliac disease without infrainguinal involvement were excluded. All eligible patients who underwent bilateral duplex mapping during the study period were included in the final analysis, and no patients were excluded after vein mapping.

2) Clinical variables

Baseline demographics and clinical characteristics were prospectively recorded, including age, sex, PAD presentation (claudication vs. CLTI), hypertension, diabetes, coronary artery disease, dyslipidemia, chronic kidney disease, dialysis dependence, and current smoking status. Prior GSV use or treatment was also documented, including previous GSV harvest (for coronary artery bypass grafting or prior lower-extremity bypass) and prior GSV stripping or endovenous ablation for varicose vein disease.

3) Duplex vein mapping protocol

All patients underwent mandatory bilateral GSV duplex ultrasonography before selection of a definitive treatment strategy. Examinations were performed by registered vascular technologists using a standardized protocol in a temperature-controlled room (25°C-26°C), with the patient in the reverse Trendelenburg position. The GSV diameter was measured inner-to-inner with minimal probe compression. The GSV was assessed from the saphenofemoral junction (SFJ) to the distal calf at eight predefined levels (Fig. 1).

Schematic illustration of bilateral GSV mapping. Diameters were measured along the entire length of both limbs, and the smallest diameter at each of eight predefined levels was recorded. Anatomical segment labels correspond to the standardized mapping sites used in the study protocol. Blue segments represent nonrefluxing veins, and red segments indicate refluxing veins (retrograde flow >0.5 seconds on spectral Doppler). The smallest recorded diameter at each level was used to represent that segment, ensuring a standardized assessment of the single-segment conduit. Reflux was assessed by spectral Doppler and defined as retrograde flow lasting >0.5 seconds.

4) Conduit definition and suitability thresholds

For the primary analyses, a suitable single-segment conduit was defined as a continuous, thrombus-free GSV extending from the SFJ to the proximal calf without duplex-detected reflux. Duplex-detected reflux was excluded as part of our institutional, relatively conservative definition of conduit suitability. We used the SFJ-to-proximal calf segment because it provides a practical and reproducible duplex endpoint that generally corresponds to a usable conduit length for many infrainguinal bypass configurations, including most below-knee bypasses. However, more distal targets, such as tibial bypasses, may require a longer conduit length. Distal calf measurements are also more variable and less consistently obtainable; therefore, they were retained as descriptive data to identify where diameter most commonly becomes limiting, rather than being incorporated into the primary suitability definition. Suitability was categorized according to the minimum measured diameter along the SFJ-to-proximal calf segment as ideal (≥3.0 mm), acceptable (≥2.5 mm), or borderline (≥2.0 mm).

Conduit suitability was assessed first in the ipsilateral limb (the index limb evaluated for revascularization) and then overall, defined as suitability in either limb (ipsilateral or contralateral) based on bilateral mapping.

5) Outcomes

The primary outcome was the prevalence of a suitable single-segment GSV at three prespecified diameter thresholds (ideal, acceptable, and borderline), assessed in (1) the ipsilateral limb and (2) either limb after bilateral mapping. Secondary outcomes were (1) the incremental yield of contralateral assessment, expressed as the absolute percentage-point increase (overall minus ipsilateral suitability), and (2) patient-level factors associated with ideal suitability. As a sensitivity analysis, we also summarized diameter-only availability (without incorporating reflux status) at the same thresholds for ipsilateral and overall assessments.

6) Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are reported as mean±standard deviation, and categorical variables are reported as numbers and percentages. Comparisons between claudication and CLTI were performed using the Student t-test for continuous variables and the χ2 test or Fisher exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate.

To explore patient-level factors associated with ideal conduit suitability, multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed using the ideal threshold (≥3.0 mm) as the outcome for (1) ipsilateral suitability and (2) overall suitability. Multivariable analyses were limited to the ideal threshold; corresponding analyses were not performed for the acceptable or borderline thresholds. Candidate covariates were selected on the basis of clinical relevance and a univariable P-value of ≤0.20. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) are reported; age was modeled per year. A two-sided P-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics ver. 25.0 (IBM Co.), and complete-case analysis was performed (no imputation).

RESULTS

A total of 115 patients were included (mean age, 71.2±11.3 years; 87 [75.7%] were male); 62 (53.9%) had CLTI and 53 (46.1%) had claudication. Comorbidities were frequent and included hypertension in 76/115 patients (66.1%), diabetes in 78/115 (67.8%), coronary artery disease in 32/115 (27.8%), chronic kidney disease in 41/115 (35.7%), and dialysis dependence in 30/115 (26.1%); 39/115 (33.9%) were current smokers (Table 1). A total of 17 patients had no usable GSV in at least one limb because of prior vein harvest or varicose vein treatment (coronary artery bypass grafting, n=4; prior lower-extremity bypass, n=8; prior GSV stripping or endovenous ablation, n=5).

Ipsilateral ideal suitability tended to be lower in CLTI than in claudication (9/62 [14.5%] vs. 14/53 [26.4%], P=0.112). Differences at the acceptable and borderline thresholds were not significant (P=0.232 and P=0.635, respectively). For overall ideal suitability after bilateral mapping, the prevalence was 11/62 (17.7%) in CLTI vs. 17/53 (32.1%) in claudication, showing a trend toward lower suitability in patients with CLTI (P=0.074) (Table 2). In the diameter-only sensitivity analysis, ideal suitability was significantly lower in CLTI than in claudication for both ipsilateral (14.5% vs. 34.0%, P=0.014) and overall (21.0% vs. 39.6%, P=0.029) assessments (Table 3).

The results of univariable and multivariable analyses are summarized in Table 4. For the ipsilateral ideal threshold, each 1-year increase in age was associated with a 4.7% reduction in the odds of ipsilateral ideal suitability (aOR, 0.953; 95% CI, 0.912-0.995; P=0.030). Female sex showed directionally lower odds of ipsilateral suitability (aOR, 0.341; 95% CI, 0.087-1.331; P=0.121). In an additional multivariable logistic regression analysis using overall ideal conduit suitability as the outcome, both older age and female sex were independent predictors of reduced availability. Each 1-year increase in age was associated with a 6.8% reduction in the odds of overall ideal suitability (aOR, 0.932; 95% CI, 0.887-0.979; P=0.005), and female sex was independently associated with lower availability (aOR, 0.201; 95% CI, 0.046-0.869; P=0.032).

Among the 115 patients, 25 underwent bypass surgery, of whom 20 (80.0%) received a GSV-based bypass. Of these 20 patients, 6 underwent bypass using the contralateral GSV, and 11 underwent GSV bypass despite having mapped GSVs classified as acceptable or borderline rather than ideal according to the study criteria. Of the remaining five patients, three underwent non-autogenous or composite graft bypass despite having an ideal mapped GSV, including two femoral-to-above-knee popliteal bypasses using polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) grafts at the surgeon’s discretion and one distal posterior tibial artery bypass using a sequential composite graft, with PTFE to the above-knee popliteal artery and GSV to the distal posterior tibial artery, because of the need for extended conduit length. The remaining two patients, who had no adequate GSV, underwent prosthetic femoral-to-below-knee bypasses.

DISCUSSION

In this consecutive, nonselective cohort of 115 patients undergoing evaluation for lower-extremity revascularization with mandatory bilateral duplex mapping, the availability of a single-segment GSV meeting commonly used diameter thresholds was limited. Using our primary definition, namely a continuous, thrombus-free, nonrefluxing GSV from the SFJ to the proximal calf, only 23/115 patients (20.0%) met the ideal threshold (≥3.0 mm) in the ipsilateral limb; when either limb was considered, 28/115 (24.3%) met the ideal threshold. Notably, suitability remained modest even after relaxing the diameter criteria: 39/115 (33.9%) ipsilateral and 58/115 (50.4%) overall met the acceptable threshold (≥2.5 mm), whereas 57/115 (49.6%) ipsilateral and 75/115 (65.2%) overall met the borderline threshold (≥2.0 mm). These findings indicate that strict single-segment GSV suitability, particularly at ≥3.0 mm, is uncommon in routine practice within this Korean cohort and may limit the straightforward implementation of a vein-first strategy in routine clinical decision-making.

This study also quantified the incremental contribution of contralateral mapping under a standardized protocol. Compared with ipsilateral assessment alone, bilateral mapping increased overall suitability by 4.3, 16.5, and 15.6 percentage points at the ideal, acceptable, and borderline thresholds, respectively. These findings suggest that contralateral assessment can meaningfully expand conduit options, particularly when acceptable or borderline diameters are considered. From a practical standpoint, standardized bilateral mapping may help clarify conduit availability before treatment is chosen, while recognizing that this approach is unlikely to substantially increase the number of patients with an ideal single-segment GSV.

Our results also provide context for implementing vein-first concepts. Contemporary guidelines and randomized trials highlight the value of autogenous bypass when an adequate vein is available, and the BEST-CLI trial further reinforced that conduit quality is central to strategy selection [1,2,9-11]. In the BEST-CLI trial, 78.4% of randomized patients were enrolled in the adequate-vein cohort on the basis of preoperative ultrasound mapping, which typically requires a healthy, thrombus-free, single-segment GSV with a diameter categorized as ideal (≥3.0 mm) [2,11]. However, because prescreening and protocol differences affect the denominator, trial participants and selected cohorts may overestimate real-world suitability. In our consecutive, admission-based cohort with mandatory bilateral mapping, we observed substantially lower ideal suitability (24.3% overall). Consistent with our results, two recent real-world studies also showed markedly limited GSV availability: a CLTI cohort reported ipsilateral adequacy of only 11.4% (14.3% including the contralateral limb), while only 44% of mapped patients had an adequate conduit in a multicenter BEST-CLI–eligible cohort [5,6].

Several factors may explain the lower ideal single-segment GSV suitability in our cohort compared with BEST-CLI and other selected series. First, the denominator differed: trials and selected cohorts may overestimate real-world suitability because patients are often prescreened or enrolled after conduit assessment, whereas our admission-based design mandated bilateral mapping in consecutive revascularization evaluations. Second, definitions of suitability and mapping protocols vary across studies; our primary definition required a continuous SFJ-to-proximal calf segment and excluded duplex-detected reflux, which may have yielded a lower prevalence under stricter criteria. Third, Fig. 2 offers an anatomic explanation for the low prevalence of ideal single-segment suitability. While proximal GSV segments frequently met the ≥3.0 mm threshold, adequacy consistently declined toward the calf, indicating that distal segments commonly act as the practical bottleneck when continuity is required for a single-segment conduit [6]. Fourth, patient-level factors also contributed within our cohort, as older age and female sex were independently associated with reduced ideal suitability. Finally, prior vein harvest or varicose vein treatment was common in routine practice: 17/115 patients (14.8%) had no usable GSV in at least one limb for these reasons, further limiting conduit availability at the time of strategy selection.

Although our cohort was not designed to test differences by indication, ideal conduit suitability tended to be lower in CLTI than in claudication according to our primary definition (ipsilateral, 14.5% vs. 26.4%, P=0.112; overall, 17.7% vs. 32.1%, P=0.074). In the diameter-only analysis without incorporating reflux status, this difference was more pronounced and reached statistical significance (ipsilateral, 14.5% vs. 34.0%, P=0.014; overall, 21.0% vs. 39.6%; P=0.029) (Table 3). These findings should be interpreted cautiously, as the primary analysis did not reach statistical significance and the study may have been underpowered to detect subgroup differences of this magnitude. Nevertheless, the consistent direction of the association suggests that conduit suitability may be lower in CLTI, which is clinically relevant given the importance of durable revascularization in this population. Additionally, older age and female sex were associated with reduced ideal suitability, particularly for overall suitability (age, aOR 0.932 per year; female sex, aOR 0.201), suggesting that patient characteristics may help anticipate conduit limitations early in the evaluation pathway. These associations may reflect, at least in part, differences in body habitus and baseline vein caliber, although this study was not designed to determine the underlying mechanisms.

These findings have direct implications for planning and counseling. First, an ipsilateral-only assessment may miss a potentially usable contralateral conduit, and standardized bilateral mapping can provide actionable information when considering treatment strategies. In our cohort, the incremental gain from contralateral assessment was greater at the acceptable threshold (+16.5 percentage points) than at the ideal threshold (+4.3 percentage points), underscoring the practical value of bilateral mapping for identifying a usable conduit. The use of contralateral GSV in 6 bypasses further supports the practical value of bilateral mapping, as it can identify a more suitable conduit in the opposite limb when the ipsilateral GSV is suboptimal. Second, because an ideal single-segment GSV (≥3.0 mm) is uncommon, clinicians should consider alternative strategies when an ideal ipsilateral conduit is unavailable, particularly in older and female patients. Importantly, the lack of an ideal segment does not necessarily preclude autogenous bypass: selectively used 2.0-3.0 mm conduits may still be reasonable in favorable anatomy with strict selection and surveillance [12,13], although many series continue to associate a diameter of <3.0 mm with inferior outcomes, especially for long or infrapopliteal grafts [4,14-16]. Third, when a suitable single-segment GSV is unavailable, treatment should follow a structured approach [1,17]. Options include endovascular-first revascularization; hybrid approaches combining limited endovascular inflow/outflow optimization with shorter autologous bypass using the best available segment [18,19]; and alternative autologous conduits (spliced vein, arm vein, or small saphenous vein) or composite grafts [20-23].

In our cohort, 20 of the 25 patients who underwent bypass surgery received a GSV-based bypass, including 11 whose mapped GSVs were classified as acceptable or borderline rather than ideal according to the study criteria. Conversely, three patients underwent non-autogenous or composite graft bypass despite having an ideal mapped GSV. These findings suggest that actual conduit selection is not determined solely by diameter-based vein mapping results. Vein mapping provides useful anatomic guidance, but its findings should be interpreted within the broader clinical context.

This study has several limitations. First, it is a single-center analysis with a modest sample size, which may limit generalizability. Second, although we included descriptive data on actual bypass conduit use, the study was not originally designed to evaluate the relationship between mapped conduit suitability and treatment selection or longitudinal bypass outcomes. Therefore, this research cannot determine how the observed limitations in suitability translated into conduit choice, patency, or limb salvage. Third, our availability estimates depend on the distal endpoint used to define a single-segment conduit. Although the SFJ-to-proximal calf segment reflects a practical conduit length for many infrainguinal bypasses, including most below-knee bypasses, it may overestimate suitability for more distal targets, such as tibial bypasses. More proximal endpoints would be expected to yield higher availability. Fourth, our definition of suitability incorporated duplex-detected reflux as an exclusion criterion; alternative definitions could yield different prevalence estimates. In a diameter-only sensitivity analysis, overall ideal availability increased modestly (29.6% vs. 24.3%), suggesting that excluding reflux alone did not account for the limited ideal suitability. Finally, diameter-based categorization cannot capture all intraoperative factors that influence conduit usability (e.g., wall quality or prior segmental injury), and duplex measurements may not perfectly reflect intraoperative findings.

CONCLUSION

In this consecutive real-world cohort, ideal single-segment GSV availability (≥3.0 mm) was low, with further reductions associated with older age and female sex. These findings suggest that conduit availability should be assessed early during evaluation for lower-extremity revascularization and that bilateral vein mapping can help define available conduit options before treatment strategy selection. In routine practice, limited availability of an ideal GSV should prompt individualized planning that considers acceptable or borderline autogenous conduits, contralateral vein use, and alternative revascularization strategies when needed.
