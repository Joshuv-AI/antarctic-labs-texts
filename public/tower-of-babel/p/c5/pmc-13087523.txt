Utilizing and Optimizing Forecasting Models for Nursing Demand: A Narrative Review
Authors: Kalpana Singh, Liyan Ajit D. Souza, Ananth Nazarene, Amane Mounia, Mahmoud Abdelwahab Khedr, Ahmad Mousa, Rebecca Rejo George, Abdulqadir J. Nahwan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAccurately forecasting nursing demand is essential for effective workforce planning in the context of increasing patient volumes and the growing complexity of healthcare systems. Reliable forecasting supports optimal staffing, enhances patient care quality, and reduces operational risks. However, traditional forecasting approaches are increasingly challenged by evolving clinical demands, rising chronic disease burden, and rapid technological advancements.AimThis narrative review aimed to (1) map existing models used for predicting nursing demand, (2) identify key predictive methodologies, data inputs, and evaluation metrics, and (3) highlight existing gaps and implications for healthcare policy and workforce management.MethodsA comprehensive narrative literature synthesis was conducted, examining a range of forecasting approaches used in healthcare settings. The review included time‐series models (e.g., ARIMA), machine learning techniques (e.g., Random Forest and Long Short‐Term Memory [LSTM]), and hybrid modeling frameworks. Relevant studies were analyzed to compare methodologies, performance, and applicability in nursing workforce prediction.ResultsThe findings indicate that advanced forecasting models, particularly machine learning and hybrid approaches, improve prediction accuracy and enable proactive workforce planning. However, significant limitations were identified, including inconsistencies in data quality, lack of standardized validation methods, and limited contextual adaptation. Additionally, many models fail to incorporate dynamic factors such as patient acuity, seasonal variations, and unit‐specific characteristics, which restrict their real‐world applicability.ConclusionForecasting models hold substantial potential to enhance nursing workforce planning, but their effectiveness depends on methodological rigor, high‐quality data infrastructure, and organizational readiness. Future efforts should focus on integrating dynamic healthcare variables and improving model validation to ensure reliable and context‐sensitive predictions. This review provides guidance for researchers, healthcare administrators, and policymakers in developing evidence‐informed and sustainable nursing workforce strategies.

Introduction

Factors Impacting Nursing Demand

Accurate prediction of the demand for nurses plays a crucial role in the strategic planning of the healthcare workforce, enabling healthcare managers to ensure appropriate staffing levels for providing high‐quality care to patients [1]. The intricate and diverse nature of contemporary healthcare systems and the continuously expanding patient population have significantly heightened the need for precise and sophisticated forecasting models to anticipate staffing needs effectively. The complexity of healthcare delivery has recently escalated owing to advancements in medical technology, the implementation of novel treatment protocols, and the increasing prevalence of chronic illnesses [2]. These elements have made the accurate estimation of nurse staffing requirements more challenging through conventional means. Consequently, there is an urgent need for innovative and resilient forecasting methodologies capable of considering these variables and delivering dependable predictions.

Role of Forecasting Models in Nursing Demand

Numerous studies have investigated forecasting techniques, including time‐series analysis, machine learning, and simulation‐based methods. Time‐series analysis involves using historical data to detect patterns and trends that can guide future staffing requirements. Conversely, machine learning models employ intricate algorithms to examine extensive data sets, reveal concealed patterns, and generate precise forecasts [3]. Simulation‐based approaches enable healthcare administrators to simulate diverse scenarios and evaluate the potential impact of different factors [4], which can be applied to nursing demand. Each of these methodologies presents distinct benefits and offers valuable insights into nurse workforce planning. While many forecasting models integrate this aspect to enhance accuracy, the discussion of seasonal influences has been consolidated in this section to avoid redundancy and improve coherence. Simulation‐based strategies offer a versatile framework for testing various assumptions and assessing their influence on staffing needs [4].

Moreover, incorporating these forecasting techniques into healthcare workforce planning procedures has exhibited encouraging outcomes. By utilizing sophisticated forecasting models, healthcare administrators can optimize nurse staffing levels, mitigate the risk of inadequate or excessive staffing, and ultimately enhance patient care quality [5]. Additionally, precise forecasting can help alleviate the financial burden associated with staffing inefficiencies and ensure the efficient allocation of resources [2]. This review is intended to serve both healthcare professionals interested in applying forecasting tools for workforce planning and Biostatisticians or data scientists interested in domain‐specific applications of predictive techniques. The review balances conceptual clarity with technical relevance and contextualizes methodological choices within real‐world nursing operations. As compared to earlier reviews such as Squires et al. [6], which had strategic workforce planning as the focal point, this review offers methodological synthesis focused on hybrid forecasting frameworks and AI‐based methods. It offers new comparative insights into model strengths, weaknesses, and implementability, aiming to link predictive modeling with pragmatic nursing demand scenarios.

Literature Review

Previous Research on Forecasting Models

Several studies in the literature have focused on developing and evaluating different forecasting models for nursing demand in contemporary healthcare systems. To begin with, time series analysis is a forecasting technique employed to examine data on account of time, and its primary goal is to forecast future values based on the inherent characteristics of the data input [7]. The successful prediction of this forecasting technique relies highly on understanding its patterns, which allows the user to observe forecasts closely aligning with the actual outcomes [7]. Time series analysis differs from other forecasting techniques primarily in its reliance on temporal ordering and historical patterns within a single variable or small set of variables over time. It typically assumes that past patterns such as trends and seasonality will continue into the future. In contrast, other forecasting techniques like machine learning models (e.g., Random Forest, Support Vector Machines, LSTM) and simulation‐based methods incorporate a wider range of input variables, can handle complex nonlinear relationships, and often do not rely on strict assumptions about data stationarity or continuity over time. For example, Machine Learning models use large, multidimensional data sets and can automatically detect interactions among variables without predefined model structures. And simulation‐based models allow users to test different scenarios and evaluate how changes in inputs (e.g., patient admission rates, nurse availability) affect outcomes, providing a more dynamic and exploratory approach.

Furthermore, Wu and Kang [8] recently introduced a forecasting model termed Gray GM (1,1) designed to predict the demand for the nursing workforce for the next decade, with a particular emphasis on specialized geriatric nurses. This model aims to provide a scientific foundation for policy development within healthcare institutions and subsequently address the anticipated requirements of the nursing workforce [8]. To effectively assess the future demand for nursing personnel, it is essential to project the total population over the coming decade and then analyze the current population‐to‐nurse ratio, a critical metric for evaluating healthcare workforce adequacy. Combining the population forecast with the projected population‐to‐nurse ratio leads to an estimate of future nurse demand. This analysis provides insights into the number of nurses required to ensure adequate healthcare services for the projected population. The results indicate a significant increase in nurse demand [8]. However, this forecasting model assumes that interest in nursing careers, entry into the profession, and retirement patterns will remain relatively stable over the next decade [8]. Thus, significant disruptions or unmeasured factors could result in inaccuracies in the forecast [9].

Moreover, healthcare managers frequently require accurate and reliable forecasts to inform decisions on nurse staffing, procurement, and service delivery; hence, the ability to analyze data and anticipate future trends is crucial. While qualitative methods can aid in strategic planning within dynamic environments, quantitative techniques may suit certain situations. In addition, a foundational understanding of forecasting models can lead to significant savings in time and resources [1]. For instance, a study conducted by Dedeoglu and Çeti̇n [10] indicated that the Holt–Winters method of forecasting yielded the most accurate predictions when assessed by criteria set by the Mean Absolute Deviation (MAD), Mean Squared Error (MSE), and Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE). In contrast, it was commonly observed in time series forecasting for data to exhibit trend patterns, indicating either an upward or downward trend. The Holt–Winters exponential smoothing method is a forecasting technique that utilizes exponential smoothing, drawing on the outcomes of prior period forecasts to make predictions [7].

Additionally, there is a global issue of nursing shortage, and amidst this, AI offers a solution to align patients' needs with nursing expertise, hence improving care and workload distribution. However, challenges to its implementation, such as ethical integration, data security concerns, and job stability, must be addressed through effective, efficient nurse involvement, training, and strong oversight. Prioritizing ethics and positioning AI as a collaborative tool is the key to successfully transitioning AI implementation to combat the rising demand for nurses in healthcare [11]. Machine learning extracts patterns and makes repeatable decisions in classification, regression, and clustering tasks. It uses supervised or unsupervised learning based on the problem and data, making it a widely used predictive technology across various fields [12]. By including relevant factors such as patient load, seasonal trends, and bed occupancy rates, Random Forest effectively predicts nurse demand, supporting optimal staffing decisions. Finally, simulation models introduce significant innovations in workforce planning. While static models estimate healthcare workforce demand and supply simultaneously, dynamic models project future estimates by considering changes in planning parameters over time [13].

Thematic Categorization and Patterns in Nursing Workforce Forecasting

Through a critical synthesis of the literature, three dominant themes were identified in the forecasting of human health resources, particularly nursing:

Theme: Data‐Driven Time Series Modeling

Studies in this category focus on historical trends using ARIMA or exponential smoothing methods. These models are most effective in detecting recurring demand patterns but often lack adaptability in sudden demand shifts (e.g., pandemics).

Theme: Intelligent Algorithms and Machine Learning

This includes models such as Random Forest, SVM, and LSTM, which provide high accuracy and can handle complex interactions. However, they often require large data sets and suffer from a lack of transparency and generalizability across hospital systems.

Theme: Simulation and Scenario‐Based Forecasting

These models, while flexible and suitable for planning under uncertainty, are often conceptual or not integrated into real‐world decision‐making systems.

Forcasting Models

Model Selection and Development

When creating a forecasting model for nurse demand, selecting the appropriate methodology is essential for generating accurate predictions that can guide staffing decisions. Various approaches are available, including Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA), Long Short‐Term Memory (LSTM) networks, and hybrid models that integrate statistical and machine learning methods. The choice of ARIMA is based on its proven effectiveness for analyzing time series data, especially when such data display trends and seasonal patterns. As a well‐recognized statistical technique, ARIMA facilitates the modeling of temporal dependencies. It is appropriate for forecasting nurse demand using historical data on staffing levels and patient admissions [16]. This approach is especially useful in healthcare environments where seasonal variations, such as higher patient admissions during winter, may affect demand patterns. This is a new review of time series forecasting methods as applied to nursing staff forecasting. While many of the methods mentioned such as ARIMA, LSTM, and hybrid models are well‐established in other domains, prior systematic reviews have examined workforce planning in nursing, this review provides a novel contribution by specifically focusing on hybrid models and AI‐integrated forecasting techniques, which remain underexplored in the current literature. The intention is to introduce these advanced forecasting techniques to the nursing workforce planning context, proposing them as promising tools for future implementation. This should be clarified in the manuscript to highlight its novelty and contribution to the field. Conversely, LSTM networks, a recurrent neural network, are specifically designed to identify long‐term dependencies in sequential data. This feature is particularly beneficial for healthcare forecasting, as patterns may not be readily observable and can change over time [14]. LSTM models are capable of handling large data sets. They can learn intricate relationships among input features, making them well‐suited for forecasting nurse demand influenced by patient demographics, hospital policies, and external events like pandemics. On the other hand, hybrid models that combine ARIMA and LSTM harness the strengths of both approaches. By merging the interpretability of ARIMA with the predictive capabilities of LSTM, these models can deliver more reliable forecasts. For example, ARIMA models linear relationships and seasonal trends effectively, while LSTM is adept at capturing nonlinear patterns and interactions among multiple variables [17].

This approach is especially relevant for forecasting nurse demand, where various factors can affect staffing requirements. For examples: Patient acuity levels, which fluctuate and directly impact nurse‐to‐patient ratios; Seasonal disease surges (e.g., influenza, respiratory illness); Policy changes or accreditation requirements affecting staffing minimums; Staff turnover and burnout rates, which are disproportionately high in nursing.

For instance, during the winter season, emergency departments may see a spike in respiratory illness admissions, requiring higher nurse staffing levels. A model that integrates historical admission data, seasonal illness patterns, and real‐time occupancy rates can more accurately predict such surges.

The model development process consists of several key stages: training, validation, and testing. Initially, the data set is divided into training, validation, and testing, where the training set is utilized to build the model. In contrast, the validation set is used to adjust hyperparameters and avoid overfitting. This step is vital, as overfitting can result in models that perform well on the training data but fail to generalize to new, unseen data [18]. After training the model and fine‐tuning it using the validation set, the next step is to evaluate its performance on the testing data set. This evaluation process provides insights into the model's ability to generalize and its accuracy when applied to new, previously unseen data, which is crucial for real‐world applications. Furthermore, cross‐validation methods are utilized to assess further the model's robustness by verifying its consistent performance across various data subsets [19].

Evaluation Metrics

Several metrics are utilized to evaluate the performance of forecasting models, including Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), and MAPE. The MAE measures the average magnitude of errors in a set of predictions without considering their direction. It is calculated by averaging the absolute differences between the predicted values and the actual results. MAE is especially valuable for gauging the average error magnitude, making it a simple yet effective metric for evaluating forecast accuracy [20].

In nurse demand forecasting, MAE offers valuable insights into the model's ability to accurately predict staffing requirements, facilitating scheduling and resource management adjustments. The RMSE measures the model's performance in predicting actual outcomes. It calculates the square of the errors, averages them, and then takes the square root, which emphasizes larger errors more. RMSE is particularly sensitive to outliers and is commonly favored when minimizing significant errors, which is especially important [16]. In nurse demand forecasting, reducing RMSE is essential because substantial differences in staffing levels can result in negative patient outcomes and heightened nurse burnout. The MAPE represents the error as a percentage of the actual values, simplifying the forecast accuracy interpretation in relative terms. MAPE is particularly useful for comparing forecast performance across various data sets or scales, as it offers a standardized measure of accuracy [21].

In nursing, MAPE assists stakeholders in gauging the reliability of demand forecasts, which supports informed decision‐making about staffing and resource allocation. Each of these metrics is vital in the context of demand forecasting. MAE is useful for assessing the average error, RMSE is crucial for evaluating the significance of larger errors, and MAPE enables comparisons across various forecasting situations. These metrics offer a thorough assessment of the forecasting model's effectiveness, ensuring that nurse demand predictions are precise and actionable for healthcare managers and policymakers.

Implementation and Results

Model Training and Testing

The first step in development is to train and test the model, which begins with data preparation. This data should include information from hospital administrative records on nurse staffing levels, patient admissions and discharges, and seasonality, among other relevant variables, as this helps track trends in demand for potential nurses. For example, a data set from a large tertiary hospital may include daily records from 2019 to 2023, capturing variables such as number of nurses on duty per shift, total patient admissions, discharge rates, average patient acuity scores, bed occupancy, day of the week, and public holiday indicators. These variables provide the temporal and operational context needed to forecast future staffing demands accurately. Standard steps in data preparation for the task include [1]: cleaning of data by filling in missing data or removing instances [2], scaling or normalizing data to avoid model input bias toward a few variables, and [3] splitting the data between the training set (70%–80%) and test set (20%–30%) [22]. Since the data are time series in nature, random splitting is avoided to preserve chronological order. For example, data from 2019 to 2021 can be used as the training set, and 2022–2023 as the test set. This setup ensures that the model is trained on past data and tested on future, unseen trends. Furthermore, if the hospital has multiple departments (e.g., intensive care unit [ICU], general ward, pediatrics), splitting can be stratified by department to account for differences in staffing needs and patterns across units.

After data preparation, the next step is to train the prediction model. Forecasting algorithms are selected based on the data and the primary objectives for time series forecasting. Various machine learning algorithms such as ARIMA, Random Forest, Support Vector Machine, or more complex methods can be used for this step [22]. Although Random Forest is not inherently designed for time series, it can still be used by restructuring the data into a supervised learning format. For instance, to predict nurse demand on a given day, one could use lagged values such as patient admissions over the past 3 days, current day of the week, previous nurse staffing levels, and holiday indicators as input features.

The training set recognizes formulas like information analysis. The training process adjusts the model parameters to rectify errors made during prediction. The best parameter combination can be found through grid search or a random search method [23]. A K‐fold cross‐validation ensures the model interpolates well for new data. This is achieved by dividing the training data into several sets, training the model over some of these sets, and then evaluating to summarize model skill [24]. Alternatively, nursing demand can be framed as a classification problem instead of a regression task. For example, based on historical patterns, the hospital may categorize each day as “low,” “moderate,” or “high” demand for nursing staff (e.g., < 50, 50–75, > 75 nurses needed). Random Forest can then be used as a classification model to predict these categories based on recent trends, supporting operational decisions like activating surge staffing protocols or reallocating float staff.

Metrics for Results Evaluation

Measuring the accuracy of a predictive model is done with metrics such as MAE and RMSE, as they consider the typical error size of model predictions and how well those predicted values converge to actual nurse demand [1]. Precision and recall performance metrics are also used to evaluate the model's ability to correctly detect an observation of interest or a positive prediction in an instance, reducing false positives. Recall determines the model's ability to identify positives from the data set, differentiating between true positives and false negatives. These metrics can help develop an accurate and reliable forecasting model that recognizes true demand without over‐ or under‐estimation.

The F1 Score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall that allows them to become a single metric accounting for both factors to be used as a form of measurement [25]. This score is valuable in imbalanced data sets and guarantees reliable performance [26]. Issue Matrix is used to visualize the model's performance, determining true positives, false positives, or negatives. This matrix facilitates the projection of error types, that is, where features were misclassified or classified correctly, indicating what aspects remain strong and identifying those with clear weaknesses [27]. The receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) and AUC are used as metrics to evaluate how well a model forecasts distinctive class. To illustrate, AUC values can range from 0 to 1, where values closer to 1 mean that the model would better distinguish between high versus low nurse demand scenarios [27].

Analysis of Results

Performance metrics of nurse demand forecasting models, such as MAE and RMSE, indicate how closely the predicted demand approximates actual nursing demand. Lower values of these metrics reflect better predictive accuracy and support effective hospital resource planning, including workforce scheduling. High precision and recall further indicate that the model reliably identifies true demand without overestimation or underestimation. This is essential for maintaining adequate staffing levels, reducing burnout, and improving patient care quality. As a combined indicator of precision and recall, a high and balanced F1 score suggests that the model effectively predicts both high‐demand and low‐demand periods. This measure is particularly useful in imbalanced datasets, where certain periods require significantly more nurses than others. Additionally, the confusion matrix provides a detailed assessment of model performance by identifying correct and incorrect predictions, thereby highlighting areas for improvement. Understanding these errors can support model refinement and enhance predictive accuracy. Finally, a high AUC value from the ROC curve indicates strong discriminative ability in distinguishing between different levels of nurse demand, which is critical for proactive staffing and resource allocation.

Discussion

Implications for Healthcare Policy and Management

Healthcare systems worldwide face significant challenges in balancing the supply of nurses with fluctuating demands driven by aging populations, disease outbreaks, and changes in healthcare delivery models [32, 33]. Accurate forecasting models for nursing demand have profound implications for healthcare policy and management. From a policy perspective, these models provide a robust foundation for making informed decisions regarding workforce planning and resource allocation. A reliable forecasting model allows policymakers to anticipate these changes, develop strategies to ensure an adequate and responsive nursing workforce and ensure that healthcare facilities are neither understaffed nor overstaffed [6]. Understaffing can lead to burnout among nurses, increased patient wait times, and compromised quality of patient care while overstaffing can impose unnecessary financial burdens on healthcare institutions [31, 34, 35, 36]. Moreover, precise nurse demand forecasting supports the development of long‐term strategies for nursing education and training. This proactive approach is essential in addressing the healthcare industry's evolving needs and ensuring the continued provision of high‐quality care [1].

Hospital management and staffing also stand to benefit significantly from accurate demand forecasting. By aligning staffing levels with forecasted demand, hospitals can optimize their operational efficiency, improve patient outcomes, and enhance the work environment for nurses [35]. By integrating sophisticated forecasting models—such as machine learning algorithms and time‐series analysis into the workforce planning process, healthcare administrators can make data‐driven decisions that optimize staffing levels. This optimization enhances patient outcomes by ensuring that the right number of skilled nurses is available to meet patient needs at all times [37]. Furthermore, accurate forecasting contributes to more effective budgeting and financial planning, as it anticipates staffing needs and associated costs. This staffing alignment with actual demand can lead to significant cost savings and promote more sustainable healthcare delivery [2].

Our thematic review suggests that nursing forecasting models fall into three key categories: time‐series, machine learning, and simulation. While these models offer distinct advantages, they are rarely integrated or tested together. Comparative evidence suggests that time‐series models (e.g., ARIMA) are highly interpretable and perform well when demand patterns are stable and seasonal, but less so when nonlinear or exogenous effects predominate [38]. Machine learning methods (e.g., Random Forest, SVR, etc.) better capture complex interactions than simple models but may require large, clean data sets and suffer from lack of interpretability and the risk of overfitting [39].

A hybrid, context‐aware forecasting framework that incorporates both historical patterns and real‐time decision‐making inputs may offer a more robust solution. Furthermore, a noticeable gap remains in applying these models beyond pilot stages or in evaluating their policy‐level impact. This calls for more pragmatic research that links forecasting accuracy with real‐world staffing improvements and patient outcomes.

Limitations and Challenges

Despite the advancements in forecasting methodologies, several limitations and challenges remain. One of the primary challenges in developing and implementing forecasting models is data availability and quality. Accurate predictions require comprehensive data sets that capture various variables, including patient demographics, disease prevalence, healthcare policies, and economic factors [40]. However, data collection in healthcare is often fragmented, with information spread across different systems and institutions, leading to potential gaps or inconsistencies in the data used for forecasting [41]. Additionally, forecasting models are inherently limited by the assumptions on which they are based. For example, time‐series models like ARIMA assume that future demand solely depends on past patterns, which may not always hold, particularly during unprecedented events such as pandemics or sudden policy changes [1, 35]. Many forecasting models, such as ARIMA, assume consistent temporal patterns, which may not hold in dynamic healthcare environments. Despite their predictive power, machine learning models such as Random Forest can be prone to overfitting, particularly when historical data are limited or contain high variability. Moreover, models like Random Forest are not inherently designed for time‐series data and may struggle with temporal continuity unless specifically adapted. Similarly, ARIMA models rely on assumptions of stationarity and linearity, which may not hold in complex real‐world healthcare data sets. A critical understanding of these methodological constraints is essential when selecting or implementing forecasting tools. Machine learning models like Random Forest and LSTM, while powerful, are prone to overfitting and require substantial amounts of high‐quality, labeled data.

Furthermore, model design and assessment technicalities deserve diligence. For example, Random Forest algorithms are not designed intrinsically for time‐dependent or sequential data and therefore need data transformation methods—like feature engineering with lag variables or sliding time windows—to prepare them for time‐series prediction [42, 43]. Similarly, recurrent models such as LSTM or GRU are particularly well adapted to temporal dependencies but may need more data and computational resources. On top of this, the metrics for evaluation to be employed need to be aligned with the kind of forecasting problem. Regression‐type measures such as MAE, RMSE, and MAPE are appropriate for continuous demand forecasting [44]. In contrast, classification‐based metrics—e.g., Precision, Recall, F1‐score, or ROC–AUC—are only applicable when the forecasting problem is recast as a categorical prediction problem (e.g., predicting whether nurse demand is in excess of some threshold or within specific risk bins) [45]. A clear definition of these appraisal frameworks ensures methodological rigor and appropriate interpretation of model performance in nursing workforce studies.

Simulation models may lack real‐time adaptability. Additionally, during unpredictable events like pandemics, all models face limitations due to reliance on historical trends. Additionally, most research does not rigorously validate models for forecasting with prospective or out‐of‐sample data, which limits confidence in their performance when applied in extreme or varying environments. Finally, trade‐offs among model complexity, interpretability, computational complexity, and the size of the required data are underexplored in most nursing demand forecasting research.

Future Research Directions

The field of nurse demand forecasting is ripe for further research, particularly in areas that address the limitations and challenges discussed above. One promising direction for future research is the integration of additional data sources, such as electronic health records, real‐time patient monitoring systems, and socioeconomic data, into forecasting models. These data sources can provide more granular and up‐to‐date information, enhancing the accuracy of predictions. Another area for future exploration is the development of hybrid models that combine the strengths of different forecasting techniques. Hybrid models that integrate time‐series analysis with machine learning algorithms could offer a more robust approach to forecasting by capturing both historical patterns and complex, nonlinear relationships between variables [46]. There is a need for more case studies that demonstrate the practical application of forecasting models in real‐world settings. These case studies can provide valuable insights into the challenges and successes of implementing forecasting models and offer guidance for institutions and organizations looking to adopt similar approaches. By documenting and sharing these experiences, the field can continue to evolve and improve, ultimately leading to more effective and efficient nurse workforce planning.

Conclusion

The conclusions drawn reflect the strengths and limitations identified throughout the review. While forecasting models present a valuable opportunity to optimize nursing workforce planning, their real‐world impact depends heavily on the availability of quality data, model validation across settings, and institutional readiness. Generating an accurate predictive model involves selecting the appropriate methodology, and the development process includes training, validation, and testing, which assess the model's generalization and accuracy when applied to unseen data, thereby implicating its real‐world applications. Evaluating metrics assesses the model's effectiveness in accurately predicting nurse demand for healthcare managers and policymakers and, thus, in developing strategies to achieve adequate staffing, as over‐ or under‐staffing could negatively impact nursing staff and the quality of patient care. For example, predictive models can forecast nursing shortages in rural areas, helping to guide targeted recruitment efforts and resource distribution to underserved communities. The challenges in implementing forecasting models include the availability and quality of data, as well as the inherent limitations of the assumptions on which they are based, which may not always remain true. Innovative and resilient forecasting methodologies, such as integrating additional data sources, could enhance prediction accuracy and develop hybrid models that offer a more robust approach. Similarly, hospitals can use short‐term forecasting tools to predict patient surges related to flu seasons or public health emergencies, allowing for timely staffing adjustments and better patient care. There is also a growing need for case studies, as they provide insights into the practical application of forecasting models in real‐world settings, including guidance on their implementation in institutions, leading to more effective planning of nursing demand. Future research should focus on developing hybrid approaches, integrating diverse data sources, and conducting implementation studies to assess practical outcomes. These insights emphasize the importance of developing hybrid forecasting systems and conducting implementation‐focused research that bridges the gap between predictive accuracy and operational impact in nursing workforce management.
