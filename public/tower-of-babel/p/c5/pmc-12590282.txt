Patient Perspectives on Virtual vs In-Person Posttreatment Care for Brain Metastases
Authors: Bardia Amanirad, Chinmay M Potdar, Matthew Ramotar, Anna T Santiago, Janet Papadakos, David B Shultz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aims to explore perspectives of patients with brain metastasis on posttreatment care, comparing virtual and in-person visits, and identifying factors shaping those views.Methods and MaterialsA cross-sectional survey assessed patient perspectives on posttreatment care. We offered the survey to English-fluent patients with internet access who received posttreatment care at a Brain Metastases Clinic (n = =140). One hundred twenty-three participants returned the survey, and 112 completed at least 80% of it, a criterion for inclusion. Patients received posttreatment follow-up care either virtually, in-person, or both. Nonparametric data were analyzed using Mann-Whitney U and Chi-Square tests, with a modified linear regression model evaluating factors related to visit satisfaction. Our hypothesis was that virtual care would be rated higher based on doctor punctuality, but lower on personal connection, communication, and overall satisfaction.ResultsParticipants who experienced both visit types rated in-person visits higher for personal connection (χ²(df = 1) = 19.703, P < .0001), ability to demonstrate physical problems (χ²(df = 1) = 18.778, P < .0001), and confidence in addressing health concerns (χ²(df = 1) = 16.941, P < .0001). Overall satisfaction did not significantly differ between visit types (U = 3607.5, z = 1.613, P = .107). Doctor punctuality (t = –2.328, SE = 0.32, P = .025) and communication effectiveness (t = –3.166, SE = 0.608, P = .003) were significant correlates to visit satisfaction.ConclusionsSimilar levels of satisfaction with virtual and in-person visits suggest that virtual care is a viable alternative to in-person visits. Higher ratings of personal connection felt with the physician, ability to demonstrate physical problems, and having health concerns properly addressed, within in-person visits, underscore their importance within a health care setting. Additionally, a doctor punctuality and communication skills are the most significant factors affecting visit satisfaction in this population, highlighting key areas for improvement in health care delivery.

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic catalyzed the rapid adoption of virtual cancer care, which was widely implemented across health care systems.1 Virtual care consists of either phone or video consultations and, in the context of the pandemic, was used to minimize person-to-person physical contact and reduce microbial spread.2 There are several reasons to support virtual care for patients with cancer, beyond pandemic circumstances. First, patients with cancer are often immunocompromised, which makes in-person visits riskier from the standpoint of common, hospital-acquired infections.3,4 Second, patients with cancer endure excessive physical, emotional, and financial strains, and virtual care offers greater convenience and lower costs compared to in-person visits.5, 6, 7 In particular, people who received a diagnosis of brain metastases demonstrate a markedly increased psycho-oncological burden.8 That said, virtual care is not necessarily ideal for all patient populations with cancer. Assessing patient experiences is essential for the effective implementation of virtual care in order to directly assess patient satisfaction and overall well-being9 Given the significant challenges faced by people with cancer, evaluating the adequacy of virtual care from the perspective of patient experience is critical.10,11 The authors are not aware of any reports of patient perspectives of posttreatment virtual care for patients with brain metastases. Therefore, in this study, patients at the Brain Metastases Clinic at the Princess Margaret (PM) Cancer Centre were surveyed to evaluate their experiences with both virtual and in-person visits, as well as how the COVID-19 pandemic affected their care.

Methods and Materials

Study design

The study employed a cross-sectional, observational design. A health care professional from within the circle of care initially approached each potential participant, invited them to participate, and explained the details of the study to them. Interested participants were asked to complete a survey emailed to them, which included questions about their experiences with virtual and in-person care. The survey was designed using Research Electronic Data Capture, a software used to build and securely manage online surveys and data bases.12 This study was approved by the University Health Network Cancer Research Ethics Board.

Measures

The start of the questionnaire asks participants about the type of follow-up visit they received (ie, virtual, in-person, or both). Based on their answer, if participants had virtual visits only or in-person visits only, the survey branched accordingly to 46 questions, whereas if participants had both in-person and virtual visits, it branched to 53 questions (see Supplement 1). In each version of the survey, there were 4 main sections.

The first section of the survey collected demographic information (19 questions for in-person, 16 for virtual, and 18 for participants who had both visit types). Demographic details included age, gender, highest level of education, first language, race/ethnic group, country of birth, work status, income, mode of transportation to the cancer center, cost of visit, distance to, and site of primary cancer in the body. The second section focused on health status and social activity (the same 4 questions regardless of visit type). Symptoms related to brain metastases, included in this section, were assessed using the PM Brain Metastases Symptom Checklist.13 The third section focused on experience with different visit types (9 questions for in-person, 10 questions for virtual, and 16 for participants who had both types). The fourth section pertained to the effects of COVID-19 on participant care and psychological well-being. It included 15 questions irrespective of visit type.

Subjects

The study population consisted of patients with brain metastases who had previously received posttreatment care for brain metastases through the PM Brain Metastases clinic. Prior treatments included radiation therapy, surgery, targeted systemic central nervous system active therapies, or any combination of the above. The brain metastases clinic from which the study cohort was drawn is administratively located within the Department of Radiation Oncology and is jointly staffed by radiation oncologists and neurosurgeons. Patient visits are conducted by a range of health care providers, including staff physicians, fellows, residents, and physician assistants, depending on clinical circumstances and scheduling. Although the clinic functions within a multidisciplinary framework, the vast majority of patients receive radiation therapy at some point during their treatment course, with the exception of those managed with central nervous system-penetrant systemic therapies who are under surveillance. Patients requiring a new course of radiation therapy during a given visit are evaluated specifically by a radiation oncologist. Eligibility criteria required participants to be fluent in English, as Toronto’s demographic profile reflects a predominantly English-speaking population. Additionally, participants needed internet access and a valid email address, as the surveys were distributed via email between September 2022 and November 2023. Email addresses were obtained from a list of brain metastasis patients recorded within the Epic health care platform, used by health care professionals at the cancer center. The entirety of the study was conducted between September 2022 and June 2024.

Data and statistical analysis

Demographic data were analyzed using the sum of the responses for each option, including the relative percentages. The demographic data were not included in the comparative analyses between virtual and in-person visits as these questions did not assess visit experience. The Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare values between 2 groups across a set of ordinal categories. The Chi-Square test was used to compare values within a single category (in-person vs virtual). For the regression model, visit type (in-person or virtual) was not included as a factor. All other factors assessed in the survey were included in the regression model. The model took into consideration mixed effects and was used to identify factors that correlated with visit satisfaction rating.

Results

Demographics and participant information

A total of 140 participants were invited to participate in the study, of which 123 returned the surveys (response rate = 87.86%). Of the 123 returned surveys, those deemed incomplete or partially complete (defined at the authors' discretion as having fewer than 80% of questions answered or missing responses for the “visit type.”) were excluded from the analysis. Ultimately, 112 surveys were included in the analysis after applying the aforementioned filtration criteria. The number of participants remaining after each filtering step is displayed in Table E1 in Supplement 2. Of the included surveys, 21 were from participants who had in-person visits only, 73 were from participants who had both virtual and in-person visits (mixed), and 18 had virtual visits only. Most participants were native English-speakers (75.73%), Caucasian (71.17%), and college/university graduates (66.07%). A majority of the participants were in their 60s (37.86%). More than one-third were born outside of Canada (36.27%). Most participants lived within 25 km of the PM Cancer Centre (70.79%). A majority of the participants reported the cost of attending a clinic visit as less than $60 CDN (65.17%). Additional participant demographic data are shown in Table 1, Table 2.

Impact of COVID-19 on participant care and psychological well-being

Of 112 participants, 106 responded to the questions regarding COVID-19 and its impact on participant care and psychological well-being. Most (62.26%) reported that their treatment was not affected by COVID-19. The plurality of participants “disagreed” that they experienced a fear of COVID-19 affecting their future cancer care (n = 34, 34.34%), had a fear of their cancer returning and not being managed properly because of COVID-19 (n = 30, 30.61%), had difficulty sleeping because of COVID-19 (n = 44, 44.90%), felt irritable because of COVID-19 (n = 34, 34.69%), or had anxiety about financial concerns because of COVID-19 (n = 38, 39.18%). The plurality of participants felt “neutral” about having difficulty focusing on tasks because of COVID-19 (n = 33, 33.33%), fear of getting COVID-19 (n = 34, 34.34%), or experiencing a sense of isolation because of COVID-19 (n = 30, 30.93%). Finally, most “agreed” with experiencing fear of a family member getting COVID-19 (n = 48, 48.48%), fear of friends getting COVID-19 (n = 43, 43.43%), confidence that the COVID-19 pandemic would be successfully controlled (n = 51, 52.52%), and having a sense of knowing how to protect themselves from COVID-19 (n = 68, 61.26%) (Table E2a and Table E2b in Supplement 2).

Correlates to visit rating

Birthplace (Bulgaria) (statistic = 2.467, SE = 2.042, P = .021), being a homemaker (statistic = 2.117, SE = 2.938, P = .044), being on disability (t = –2.190, SE = 1.14, P = .038), confidence that COVID-19 could be successfully controlled (t = –2.951, SE = 0.45, P = .007), doctor punctuality (t = –2.328, SE = 0.32, P = .025), and the extent to which the doctor was easy to understand (t = –3.166, SE = 0.608, P = .003) most significantly correlated to visit satisfaction rating (Table E3 in Supplement 2).

Discussion

With the rise of the COVID-19 pandemic, many health care settings adopted a virtual model of patient visits to prevent the spread of the disease to vulnerable patients and the general population.14 Patients with brain metastasis are among these vulnerable groups, requiring frequent medical follow-ups and face unique challenges in accessing care. Virtual care allowed for convenient doctor-patient follow-ups while protecting patients against the highly contagious SARS-CoV-2 virus.15 However, the transition to virtual care raised questions about its impact on patient experience and the quality of health care in patient with brain metastasis. Our study explored patients’ experiences with different types of visits (in-person, virtual, or both) and factors that influence patients’ follow-up visit ratings while keeping a record of their demographic data. A multidisciplinary team, including radiation oncologists, neurosurgeons, fellows, and physician assistants, was involved in patient management. Physician assistants and fellows were more commonly involved in virtual visits, particularly for follow-up patients, whereas staff physicians were more likely to conduct in-person consultations. The majority of participants in our study identified English as their first language and reported being Caucasian, which is consistent with the demographic profile of Toronto as a predominantly English-speaking city with a majority Caucasian population.16 A significant proportion of the participants were retired (48.21%), which parallels the plurality of participants falling within the age bracket of 60 to 69 (37.86%). The majority of participants (60.75%) arrived at the cancer center accompanied by a family member or friend, which is consistent with the fact that cancer participants often require assistance with transportation, as the majority of them face hardships when traveling on their own.17 The majority of participants resided within 25 km of the cancer center, which is expected, as individuals referred to hospitals are typically those living in its vicinity.18 Our study found that the most common primary cancer site for participants with brain metastases was the lung (52.81%).19 We found that brain metastasis participants with a history of both virtual and in-person visits reported a stronger personal connection with their doctor during in-person compared to virtual visits. Multiple studies support this finding, which is likely because of a lack of shared space between doctor and participant in a virtual setting.20, 21, 22 In a study conducted by Andreadis et al,20 participants with chronic disease reported that the absence of shared physical space in a virtual setting reduced the sense of personal connection between the participant and their doctor. Participants who had both types of visits also reported a greater ability to show physical problems during their in-person visits. Likewise, multiple studies support this finding.23, 24, 25 Finally, participants who experienced both types of visits felt that their health concerns were better addressed in-person compared to virtually. A study by Gordon et al26 similarly found that in participants with type 2 diabetes, in-person visits were more effective at addressing health care concerns compared to virtual visits. Our study respondents who experienced both types of visits reported a similar level of comfort disclosing personal information in virtual and in-person visits. A study by Moulaei et al,24 conducted among patients referred to medical centers and hospitals in western Iran, reported that study participants perceived a higher level of confidentiality during in-person visits, resulting in greater comfort in disclosing personal/sensitive information. This difference in results may reflect differences in participant population, including cultural and health status differences, as many cultures have different perceptions and regard for privacy.27

In contrast to the above, we found that, among participants who had either in-person or virtual visits only, there was no significant difference in ratings of personal connection felt with the doctor, the ability to show a physical problem, the extent to which they felt their health concern could be addressed, or the comfort level felt sharing personal information during their visits. Given that these were indirect comparisons, it is difficult to interpret these findings. They may reflect the increased potential for confounding effects in indirect comparisons, arising from inherent differences between the subjects themselves.

In the survey, participants were asked whether they would recommend virtual visits to individuals in their social circles. Among those who participated in a virtual visit, only 4% indicated that they would not recommend this option to friends or family. A study by Chu et al,28 conducted among ambulatory care participants who attended at least 1 virtual visit, reported favorable participant experiences with virtual visits, with 80% of participants rating video visits an 8 of 10, and 72% rating telephone visits similarly for recommendations to family and friends. In line with multiple prior studies, there was no significant difference detected between overall participant satisfaction ratings for virtual compared to in-person care among participants who had virtual visits only, in-person visits only, or both virtual and in-person visits.29, 30, 31

The survey was administered to participants late in the COVID-19 pandemic. Survey distribution and data collection occurred in September 2022 and ended in November 2023. According to multiple studies, during this time, participants remained wary about the risks of in-person interactions within a health care setting and appreciated the use of virtual care.32, 33, 34 A study by Lou et al35 found that the stress, uncertainty, or trauma experienced by many participants during the COVID-19 pandemic may have altered their overall views on hospital care. It was also found that participants were most concerned about their family members or friends contracting COVID-19, a risk that may be obviated by virtual care.36 Despite this, participants reported high levels of confidence that the pandemic would be successfully controlled, and a large portion of the participants also reported that they knew how to best protect themselves against COVID-19. A study by Ugas et al37 obtained similar results.

Although the initial widespread adoption of virtual care was largely driven by the need to minimize COVID-19 transmission, the ongoing use of virtual modalities must now be evaluated in the context of an endemic phase. While COVID-19 remains a relevant consideration for immunocompromised patients, including those with brain metastases, decisions regarding appointment modality have increasingly shifted toward patient convenience.38 As such, understanding patient satisfaction with different appointment types remains important for informing sustainable, long-term care models in oncology beyond the acute phase of the pandemic.

Regardless of visit type, 2 factors that correlated with visit satisfaction ratings were doctor punctuality and the comprehensiveness of doctor communication. These elements are pivotal in shaping participant perceptions of care and present clear opportunities for improvement. Interestingly, the findings suggest that among all participants, doctor punctuality was rated significantly higher during virtual visits compared to in-person visits. This may reflect the logistical advantages of virtual care, where a study by Hensel et al39 showed that doctors felt that their timeliness for participant visits improved. Furthermore, among participants, a significant difference was not detected in the rating of comprehensiveness of communication between virtual and in-person visits, which suggests that virtual platforms can deliver similarly effective doctor-participant interactions in terms of information exchange. Brain metastases can constitute an oncologic emergency, often presenting with significant symptoms such as cognitive impairment, headaches, motor deficits, and seizures, all of which may severely impact their quality of life. These symptoms not only affect their quality of life but can also impair communication, comprehension, and decision-making capacity, making virtual care particularly challenging.40 Compared to patients with noncentral nervous system metastases, those with brain metastases may require more nuanced, multidisciplinary support, including neurology, neurosurgery, and urgent imaging services that often necessitate in-person coordination.41 Consequently, in-person visits are recommended for participants with brain metastases, as virtual care may fall short in addressing the complexities and specific needs of their condition. Conversely, once treated, routine follow-up often entails a straightforward review of imaging and symptoms, making these patients excellent candidates for virtual care. Furthermore, given additional health care commitments they may have, such as chemotherapy, further imaging, and physical examinations, patients benefit from virtual follow-ups in terms of time, energy, and cost savings.5 Taken together, our findings underscore the potential of posttreatment virtual care to not only maintain but, in some respects, improve the patient experience.

The majority of patient appointments in this study were conducted within the context of radiation oncology, and therefore, the findings have important implications for optimizing care delivery in this specialty. Specifically, adopting a more judicious use of virtual and in-person visits may lead to more efficient resource allocation and reduce the travel burden for patients. For instance, in-person visits could be prioritized for newly referred or less well-established patients requiring comprehensive assessment, while virtual visits may be more appropriate for well-established patients in busy clinics where scheduling punctuality and minimizing wait times significantly influence patient satisfaction. This tailored approach could improve triage and enhance the overall quality of care delivery in radiation oncology clinics managing patients with brain metastases.

Limitations

The results of this study reflect self-reported responses from patients, which can be prone to response bias and are influenced by the patients’ psychological condition at the time of survey completion.42 The Likert scale used for several survey questions included only 3 or 4 options, which may have affected the accuracy of results because participant ratings could be somewhere between or outside the range of what our Likert scale suggests.43 Because of the unranked nature of categorical variables included in the regression model, correlation with visit satisfaction was calculated by comparing the frequency of participant selection for each categorical question choice with the participant’s respective visit satisfaction rating. This method of analysis by the model splits most of the demographic variables by their answer options to allow them to be included in the correlation calculations. The use of this method identified a significant positive correlation between participants who selected Bulgaria as their place of birth and visit satisfaction, but this is anomalous and likely a type I error because of only 2 participants selecting Bulgaria as their place of birth. Similarly, the selection of “homemaker” as an occupation appears anomalous, as it was chosen by only 1 participant and is likely attributable to a type I error. Both of these variables reflect very small sample sizes (n = 2 for Bulgaria as country of birth and n = 1 for homemaker occupation), which limits the generalizability of these findings to broader patient populations. As such, these results should be interpreted with caution and may warrant further investigation in future research with larger, more representative samples. Our survey did not capture the reasons that specific follow-up visits occurred on a participant-specific basis, in other words, whether they were scheduled visits or done because of a participant concern, such as new symptoms, which may affect participant experience. Likewise, we did not capture what new information was shared by the physician during follow-up visits, such as adverse findings on a recent scan. This is especially relevant because, in our practice, virtual visits most often occur for well-participant and stable-scan follow-ups, whereas in-person follow-up visits primarily occur because of a participant concern or on the basis of adverse findings seen on recent follow-up scans. These associations could strongly influence participant experiences and retrospective visit ratings. This is because patients with more severe symptoms, often accompanied by greater daily pain, are more likely to give lower ratings for their visits.44 However, it was observed that nearly all patients reported low symptom severity. Additionally, the cross-sectional design of the study and single-center setting limit the generalizability of the findings. Data were collected at a single point in time, and the survey used was not previously validated. Moreover, the involvement of multiple provider types across appointment modalities presents a potential source of provider bias. For example, virtual visits were more often conducted by physician assistants and fellows, while in-person visits frequently involved staff physicians. Differences in provider experience, communication approach, and patient familiarity may have influenced patient satisfaction independently of appointment modality. This represents an important limitation in interpreting the observed associations. Lastly, the location of where the participants took their virtual visits, whether in a public or private place, was not reported. This could influence the participant's perception of comfort in sharing personal/confidential information.

Conclusion

In summary, there were no significant differences found between visit satisfaction rating for virtual and in-person visits by indirect comparison. That said, participants with a history of both virtual and in-person visits reported that in-person visits elicited a stronger personal connection with their doctor, a greater ability to show physical problems, and a higher perception that their health concerns were better addressed. Participants who experienced both virtual and in-person visits also reported feeling equally comfortable in either setting, disclosing personal information. Finally, doctor punctuality and the effectiveness of doctor communication are modifiable factors that significantly correlated with visit satisfaction rating.
