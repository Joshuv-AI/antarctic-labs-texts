Reconstruction of Acromioclavicular and Coracoclavicular Joints Augmented With Semitendinosus Autograft and Tensionable Cerclage System and K-Wire Supplementation
Authors: Amyn M Rajani, Anmol RS Mittal, Vishal U Kulkarni
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcromioclavicular (AC) joint separations are commonly seen in athletic settings, as well as road traffic injuries. A wide range of surgical interventions are proposed for the treatment of high-grade AC separations, from hook plates, wires, screws, Endobuttons, dog-buttons, to anatomical and nonanatomical reconstruction using autografts, biological augments, and cerclage systems. However, reconstruction of merely the coracoclavicular ligaments caused recurrent deformity, citing the need to address the AC ligaments concurrently, without compromising on the mobility of the AC joint. This Technical Note and the accompanying video describe AC joint reconstruction and augmentation of the coracoclavicular ligaments along with anatomical reduction, repair, and additional stabilization using a combination of K-wire, tensionable suture cerclage system, and autograft.

Acromioclavicular (AC) joint separation is seen primarily in athletes and road-traffic accidents, with a reported incidence of 2.0 per 1,000 person-years and 9% of all shoulder injuries in the general population, and 30–50% of all shoulder injuries in athletes.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 Most commonly used classification system for these injuries is the Rockwood classification. Type 1 and 2 injuries are typically conserved, whereas surgical intervention is deemed necessary for type 4-6. Management of type 3 injuries remains a dilemma.1,6,7 Despite the high incidence and potentially disabling effects of an insufficient therapeutic technique, a consensus over the gold standard for surgical management is still pending. The proposed surgical techniques have evolved from using a hook plate, to repair or reconstruct coracoclavicular (CC) ligaments.3,5,8, 9, 10 However, owing to the translational and rotational role of the AC joint in shoulder biomechanics, the recurrence rate of deformities with these techniques was as high as 47%.3 This warranted addressing the torn AC ligaments too. The biomechanical, clinical, and radiographic evidence of superior outcomes with biological augmentation, and better strength and stability of a tendon graft construct, especially in chronic AC joint separations, have been growing in the literature.6,9,11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18 Despite the advances, technical challenges, such as complete supero-inferior reduction, hardware failure and need for removal, clavicular and acromion fractures, and progressive loss of reduction, continue to raise questions over an ideal technique.15,19, 20, 21 This technique aims to describe an open, hardware-free approach, by combining advantages of autograft reconstruction for CC ligaments, augmented by a tensionable, cerclage, high-strength suture tape for a robust vertical fixation. This is combined with constant horizontal stabilization (AC ligaments) using the same autograft and cerclage tails, both biologically around the AC joint soft tissue, and further lateral to the acromion, with supplemental stabilization using an old-school 1.6-mm K-wire (Video 1).

Surgical Technique

Indications

Preoperative assessment includes clinical examination for AC joint instability, tenderness, pain with cross-body adduction in acute injuries and reducibility in chronic (≥1 year) injuries. Zanca and axillary lateral views are used to assess anteroposterior and superoinferior instability. This surgical technique is indicated for high-grade AC joint separations (Rockwood 4-6) in acute or chronic, reducible setting, with an extended indication in type-3-injury to the dominant side in young, high-demand individuals. Written, informed consent was obtained from all individual participants.

Operative Setting

Under general anaesthesia, and semi-beach-chair position, the patient is positioned with adequate padding to the head, face, and bony prominences. After establishing a sterile field, anatomical landmarks (clavicle, acromion, AC joint, and coracoid) are marked (Fig 1). Harvest the semitendinosus tendon graft (∼270 mm length) from ipsilateral knee; whip-stitch it with a 3-0 Monocryl suture, dry-drape it in sterile gauze, and close donor-site incision in layers (Fig 2).

The semitendinosus graft is prepared and whipstitched at both ends.

Surgical Approach

A ∼7-cm incision is made from the AC joint to the coracoid. Deltopectoral fascia is split to enter the interval. Superior surface of lateral end of clavicle is exposed using sharp dissection, and soft-tissue attachments are reflected until the AC joint, torn coracoclavicular ligament, and acromioclavicular ligament are visible. Palpate the tip of the coracoid process and bluntly dissect between the deltoid and pectoralis minor to expose it. Pectoralis minor can be dissected subperiosteally, and coracoid base can be cauterized for better exposure.

Suture Management and Reduction

A looped Ethibond (Ethicon) is passed around the clavicle (anterior-to-posterior) using a Mixter forceps, keeping the loop posteriorly for later passage of cerclage tape and secure by cinching. Next, pass the Mixter forceps under the coracoid (medial-to-lateral). Loop another number 2 Ethibond around the coracoid and cinch the loop laterally on the coracoid (Fig 3). Attention is then turned to the clavicle. Using a malleable ruler, mark points 2 cm and 4 cm medial from the lateral end of the clavicle. Place a scoop under the clavicle to prevent iatrogenic neurovascular or pulmonary injury. Two 4.0-mm tunnels are drilled at these points directed toward the coracoid. Pass nitinol wire loops through each clavicular tunnel and secure them. Under C-arm guidance, the AC joint is manually reduced and fixed using a 1.6-mm K-wire passed from the lateral aspect of the acromion, angled toward the clavicle (Fig 4). Confirm the reduction in the coronal and sagittal plane.

Reconstruction Using Autograft

The whipstitched semitendinosus autograft is relayed and hooked around the undersurface of the coracoid, such that the medial limb is longer. Using the nitinol loops, medial limb is relayed to the superior surface of the clavicle from the predrilled medial (conoid) tunnel, followed by the lateral limb through the lateral (trapezoid) tunnel (Fig 5). Medial limb is reconfirmed to be longer, and the two ends are parked separately.

Intraoperative image of the superior surface of clavicle after passage of the autograft through the two clavicular tunnels using nitinol wire loops showing a longer medial and shorter lateral limb (left shoulder, semi-beach-chair position, as viewed from head-end). Orientation guide: left-superior, right-inferior, top-lateral, bottom-medial).

Augmentation With Cerclage Tensioner

Open an Arthrex FiberTape cerclage device. Pass its free end first through the superior clavicle via the Ethibond loop. The same free end is then hooked around the undersurface of the coracoid by relaying it through the TigerWire loop (Fig 6). Once the relay of the FiberTape around the clavicle and coracoid is confirmed, thread the free end through the cerclage loop to form a sliding knot. The sliding knot is seated and secured on the superior surface of the clavicle. The cerclage tensioner is applied to the sliding knot and is advanced to a tension of 60 on the device, securing the AC joint reduction. The knot is further secured by throwing multiple half-hitches over it (Fig 7).

Final Fixation

The medial and lateral ends of the autograft are tied to each other over the superior surface of the clavicle. The graft and remainder of the cerclage FiberTape is sutured together, which is, in turn, sutured to the deltopectoral fascia using a number 0 FiberWire (Arthrex) (Fig 8). This arrangement provides robust vertical stability of the coracoclavicular ligaments. A small incision is made next to the entry point of the K-wire. The long medial limb of the autograft and the remaining cerclage FiberTape is exteriorized from this incision using an artery forceps (Fig 9). With an assistant holding them under tension laterally, the primary surgeon sutures the medial limb of the graft and the cerclage tape to the soft-tissue fascia over the AC joint to reconstruct the horizontal component of the AC ligament complex (Fig 10). Autograft and cerclage FiberTape are tied over one another and pushed onto the lateral end of the acromion using a knot-pusher (Fig 11). Excess graft and FiberTape are cut. K-wire is left in situ for supplemental stability.

Postoperative Care

The operated side is immobilized in a sling for 6 weeks, after which the K-wire is removed. When the patient is released from the hospital (Fig 12), formal physiotherapy is commenced. The pearls and pitfalls of the procedure are elucidated in Table 1.

Discussion

The technique demonstrated in this technical note provides superior vertical and horizontal stability at the AC joint in cases of acute and reducible, chronic separations, using a combination of autograft, high-strength suture tape cerclage, and K-wire, resulting in a robust biomechanical fixation from day 1. Reconstruction of the CC ligaments using the autograft and augmentation with cerclage system—and its continuation laterally followed by double point fixation at AC joint and further laterally—ensures precise fixation without the need for any hardware in situ, circumventing removal and failure in the long term.

With better techniques and understanding of the AC joint biomechanics, there has been a rapid progression from traditional procedures addressing only vertical stability of CC ligaments by repair, interference screws or buttons, resulting in failures as high as 35%.1,10,16,22, 23, 24, 25, 26 Milewski et al.27 and Millet et al.19 reported 80% coracoid and 6.5% clavicle fractures when using 6-mm coracoid tunnel for fixation with GraftRope (Arthrex), while subsequent corrective methods proved to be technically challenging. No coracoid tunnels circumvent the possibility of iatrogenic fractures in this technique. Fixation using graft has been widely documented to provide reliable superoinferior and anteroposterior stability.18,23

The outcomes further improve with augmentation of the graft, as shown by Borbas et al.28 and Shin et al.,29 and multipoint fixation, both of which is incorporated in this technique through cerclage augmentation, fixation at the AC joint, lateral to acromion, and K-wire. Use of cerclage system for augmentation is not unexplored. Kim et al.,30 Makhni et al.,31 and Youn et al.,32 showed good outcomes with open technique. Ardebol et al.33 demonstrated an arthroscopy-assisted technique. However, the use of tensioner over knot-tying to ensure good anteroposterior stability and precise, controlled tensioning, and to avoid under- or over-reduction was advocated in these studies.30,33

Our proposed technique combines advantages of reconstructing the CC ligaments with an autograft (reduced cost and allograft-associated-failure); augmenting the fixation with tensionable SutureTape cerclage construct for precise, controlled reduction without the need for coracoid tunnels or hardware (circumventing hardware failure32, and coracoid fracture); restoring the horizontal stability by fixing the autograft and cerclage tails at the AC joint and lateral to acromion (preventing anteroposterior translation and acromion fracture secondary to acromion tunnels); and supplementing the fixation with a K-wire to avoid loss of reduction in both planes due to accidental loading in the immediate postoperative period. The technique has potential limitations: (1) The use of an autograft requires an additional incision, which carries the potential for donor site morbidity, graft failure, neurovascular damage due to manipulation around the coracoid, and other complications related to K-wire in situ for 6 weeks; (2) and the K-wire needs to be removed later. Adept suture management is necessary and can be challenging. However, advantages of this technique are that it is not technically challenging, it is economical, and it restores biomechanics without any long-term metal implant. Clinical studies are encouraged to establish reproducibility and good clinical outcomes of this technique. Advantages and disadvantages are outlined in Table 2.
