Engineering yeast chromosomal telomeres with a bacteriophage system
Authors: Weiqin Deng, Yanyan Li, Yangyang Shao, Ronglin Zhao, Fan Zhou, Li Zhong, Wang Liu, Tao Wang, Ting He, Siying Zhang, Huilin Chen, Xin Man, Can Hu, Ping Fang, Yongping Huang, Xiangzhou Meng, Jin-Qiu Zhou, Zixin Deng, Xiaoli Xue, Zhongjun Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAll eukaryotes, including yeast, plants, animals and humans, possess linear chromosomes. The conserved eukaryotic telomere-telomerase systems, originated and evolved over 1 billion years, protect the chromosomal ends and regulate critical physiological functions through complex networks. In this study, we replace the endogenous eukaryotic telomeres in the single-chromosome yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae with the prokaryotic telomere system TelN/tos from the Escherichia phage N15, which forms a closed hairpin structure, by interrupting the MRX/Sae2 pathway. The prokaryotic telomeres effectively protect linear chromosomal ends and prevent genetic instability. Through adaptive evolution, we identify yeast strains harboring additional mutations (TEL1 and CYR1) that restore functional MRX/Sae2 activity, thereby improving host fitness and meiotic capacity. Interestingly, the two-associated TelN/tos telomeres position deeper into chromosomes and exhibit increased interactions with their adjacent regions. The successful replacement of a complex eukaryotic chromosomal telomere with a simple bacteriophage system demonstrates functional equivalence between these divergent systems, implying possible natural origins of such stochasticity (e.g., horizontal gene transfers). Furthermore, these engineered strains facilitate development of a tos-YAC system that enabled iterative assembly and stable maintenance of megabase-level heterogeneous DNA (1.23-2.77 Mb), providing a robust platform for large-scale DNA manipulation.Subject terms: Fungal genetics, Genome evolution, Genetic engineering

Introduction

Prokaryotes, including bacteria and archaea, typically possess circular chromosomes, whereas eukaryotes (e.g., fungi, plants and animals) have linear chromosomes. Evolutionary studies suggest that telomeres emerged approximately 1 billion years ago, facilitating the transition from ancestral circular to linear chromosomes1,2. Canonical eukaryotic telomeres, characterized by conserved telomerase and repetitive sequences at the ends of chromosomes, protect linear chromosomes from degradation and fusion3. To counteract telomere length-shortening during cell divisions, hundreds of telomere-binding proteins regulate telomere replication and length maintenance, preserving genomic integrity and preventing cellular aging4,5. However, some eukaryotes, such as Drosophila melanogaster, employ telomere-specific retrotransposons for end protection6, highlighting the diversity of telomere maintenance strategies.

Certain prokaryotes, such as Streptomyces, utilize a terminal protein-capped telomere system to protect linear chromosomes and plasmids7. The Bacillus linear phage phi29 employs a protein-primed strand-displacement mechanism for end protection8. The simplest telomere system, reported in Escherichia phage N159, relies on the protelomerase TelN to cleave the telomerase-occupancy-site (tos), forming a protective hairpin structure10. Through molecular engineering, the TelN/tos system from N15 has been used to convert the circular chromosome of E. coli into a linear form, demonstrating that the phage-derived end-protection system can safeguard linear prokaryotic chromosomes11,12. The successful use of the TelN/tos system to create mammalian vectors further supports its functional integrity13. Intriguingly, linear plasmids with prokaryotic-type telomeres have been identified in the cytoplasm of some eukaryotes, such as the terminal protein-capped pGKL2 plasmid in Kluyveromyces lactis14. This raises an intriguing question: could the conserved eukaryotic telomere system be replaced by an evolutionarily distinct mechanism, such as a simplified prokaryotic telomere system, to develop an alternative telomere maintenance strategy?

In this study, we evaluate three prokaryotic telomere systems and demonstrate that the simplified TelN/tos from Escherichia phage can functionally replace the eukaryotic telomere system in the single-chromosome yeast S. cerevisiae.

Results

Rationale for creating a simplified eukaryotic telomere system

To engineer an effective telomere system that maintains chromosome stability while preserving cellular fitness and meiotic functions, we established three key principles: (1) Model selection: we chose Saccharomyces cerevisiae as our experimental system due to its well-characterized telomere biology15. Using engineered single-chromosome strains16,17 minimized telomere complexity and facilitated system construction. (2) System autonomy: the selected telomere system needed to function independently, avoiding interference with native cellular networks. (3) Adaptive optimization: We implemented competitive co-cultivation and genome-wide screening to identify beneficial mutations that enhance functional integration post-replacement.

Functional replacement of yeast chromosomal telomeres with prokaryotic systems

Phylogenetic analysis of replication protein domains involved in telomere maintenance across 118 species (Source Data 1) identified five distinct telomere maintenance mechanisms (Fig. 1). To determine whether evolutionarily distinct prokaryotic telomerase systems could function in eukaryotic cells, we examined representative systems from three clades (Fig. 1b) in the single-chromosome yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae. Remarkably, the simplest system (TelN/tos) successfully replaced endogenous conserved eukaryotic telomeres in S. cerevisiae, demonstrating its functional compatibility (Fig. 2).

Consistent with previous reports18–20, the MRX complex (Mre11-Rad50-Xrs2), aided by Sae2, recognizes and cleaves hairpin DNA. Deletion of SAE2, MRE11, RAD50, or XRS2 prevented cleavage of TelN/tos-formed hairpin ends, converting the circular single chromosome into a linear form (Fig. 2a). However, this approach resulted in remarkably low telomere replacement efficiency (only a few hundred viable clones per 10⁷ cells). These engineered strains (DW1- sae2Δ, DW1- mre11Δ, DW1- rad50Δ, and DW1- xrs2Δ) showed distinct circular vs. linear chromosome patterns by pulsed-field gel electrophoresis (PFGE) (Fig. 2b). Denaturing Southern hybridization showed double-sized bands compared to neutral conditions, confirming hairpin-sealed linear ends (Fig. 2c). MRX gene deletions impaired yeast growth, while linearization restored growth in all strains except DW1- sae2Δ, which grew slowest (Fig. 2d). Genome sequencing of DW1-sae2Δ (designated DW1) revealed high collinearity with the parental SY14 (Supplementary Fig. 1, Supplementary Table 1). DW1 maintained linear chromosomal stability over 800 generations, with unchanged PFGE band patterns (Fig. 2e). Comparative genomic (DW1-0 G vs. DW1-800 G) identified only 3 single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and 1-bp insertion identified, demonstrating exceptional genome stability conferred by the TelN/tos system (Fig. 2f, Supplementary Table 2).

Adaptive evolution of yeast chromosome with a bacteriophage telomere

Starting from sae2Δ yeast strains with a circular or linear single-chromosome, we created the telomeres using the bacteriophage TelN-tos system. A few large colonies were selected (Fig. 3a). Genome sequencing of the fastest-growing colony, C-1 (designated DW2), revealed a tel1-L1562P point mutation (Supplementary Table 3). Introducing this mutation into the slow-growing DW1 to yield strain DW4, which exhibited faster growth, improved cell morphology (Fig. 3b), and normal cell cycle progression (Fig. 3c). Tel1 and Sae2 proteins activate the MRX complex through distinct pathways to facilitate DNA double-strand break (DSB) repair, demonstrating functional synergy21,22. Accordingly, the SAE2 gene could be complemented in DW2 and DW4 strains carrying the Tel1 mutation, though this slowed yeast growth.

Through competitive co-culturing of DW2 (SAE2-complemented) with fast-growing wild-type yeast, we obtained strain DW3, a mutant with enhanced growth fitness. Genome sequencing revealed mutations including cyr1-H1695N (Supplementary Table 3), a mutation in adenylate cyclase (CYR1), which modulates metabolism, stress response, and meiosis via cAMP signaling23. Introducing cyr1-H1695N into DW4 (tel1-L1562P) generated DW5, which exhibited enhanced growth, normal morphology, and increased genotoxic resistance (Fig. 3a–d). Although DW5 remained less competitive than the linear-chromosome strain SY14, it outperformed the circular-chromosome strain SY15 (Fig. 3e). DW5 stably maintained its linear chromosome (Supplementary Fig. 2, Supplementary Table 4).

Notably, DW5 diploids achieved sporulation efficiency similar to SY14 (Fig. 3f), albeit with reduced spore viability (Fig. 3g). Transcriptomics revealed that cyr1-H1695N upregulated energy production, stress response, and sporulation genes (Fig. 3h). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) linked this mutation to enhanced oxidative phosphorylation but suppressed steroids/amino acids biosynthesis (Supplementary Fig. 3). Key sporulation regulators (KIN82 and EMI2) and their interaction networks were upregulated (Fig. 3i), explaining DW5’s restored meiosis.

Strains with the prokaryotic TelN/tos telomeres (DW1, DW4 and DW5) abolished eukaryotic telomere position effect (TPE), derepressing sub-telomeric genes (Supplementary Table 5).

Impact of bacteriophage telomere on chromosomal 3D structure

To assess how TelN/tos telomeres affect chromosome organization, we performed Hi-C analysis, which combines High-Throughput Sequencing (HTS) and Chromosome Conformation Capture (3 C) methods, to compare wild-type SY14 (native telomeres) with engineered strains DW1 and DW5. Global interaction patterns remained largely unchanged (Fig. 4a), maintaining distance-dependent contact probabilities. However, differential interaction analysis revealed weakened contacts between prokaryotic telomeres related to native ones (Supplementary Fig. 4).

While both telomere types maintained spatial proximity, their nuclear positioning differed substantially: native telomeres clustered at the chromosome periphery, whereas TelN/tos telomeres localized internally (Fig. 4b). The prokaryotic telomeres also showed enhanced interactions with chromosomal arms (Fig. 4c), likely due to loss of nuclear membrane anchoring (Fig. 4d). Topologically Associating Domain (TAD) conservation in DW1 and DW5 ranged from 69.6–78.6% related to SY14 (Supplementary Table 6), indicating moderate structural preservation.

Cloning and stable maintenance of megabase-scale linear tos-YAC in yeast

Yeast artificial chromosomes (YACs) are widely used for cloning and assembling ultra-large DNA fragments exceeding 1000 kb (megabase-scale). They can exist either in a linear form protected by eukaryotic telomeric sequences or in a circular form24–26. Here, we developed a linear tos-YAC assembly system (Supplementary Fig. 5) using engineered strain DW6 (derived from DW5 by removing terminal selection markers), featuring bacteriophage telomeres that replace natural yeast telomeres. As shown in Fig. 5, this system successfully achieved iterative assembly and stable maintenance of silkworm chromosomal DNA fragments (1.23–2.77 Mb). PFGE analysis confirmed assembly positive rates of 40–100% (Supplementary Table 7). The 1.23 Mb pY8 showed near-perfect fidelity (1 SNP), while the 2.77 Mb pY15 accumulated just 6 variants across 12 assembly cycles (Supplementary Table 8), demonstrating DW6’s capacity for megabase DNA maintenance. Remarkably, pY15 maintained unchanged PFGE profiles throughout 14 days of daily subculturing (Supplementary Fig. 6) and exhibited native-like cellular functions including growth kinetics, genotoxic resistance, and meiosis competence comparable to its natural telomere counterpart (Supplementary Fig. 7). At 2.77 Mb, this represents the largest heterologous DNA stably cloning and maintained in yeast, exceeding both standard YAC capacity (<2 Mb)27 and the previous reported 2.3 Mb record28.

Discussion

Synthetic genomics and chromosome engineering have emerged as powerful tools for investigating genome architecture, function, and evolution. The Synthetic Yeast Genome Project (Sc2.0) has pioneered this approach through systematic chromosome redesign, yielding functional insights into genome organization and evolutionary adaptability29–33. Similarly, chromosome engineering has enabled metabolic pathway optimization for industrial applications while serving as a platform for evolutionary studies34–37. Investigation into yeast long terminal repeats (LTRs) and telomere-related sequences38 and centromeres39 have further illuminated the dynamic nature of genome evolution and cell division mechanisms. Together, these advances establish synthetic genomics as a transformative paradigm for exploring life’s diversity and adaptability.

Our study advances this paradigm by engineering a minimal prokaryotic telomere system in the single-chromosome yeast, revealing three key findings (Supplementary Fig. 8): (1) we demonstrate that evolutionarily complex eukaryotic telomeres can be functionally replaced by a simple prokaryotic system, suggesting functional equivalence of structurally divergent systems. This finding parallels the natural occurrence of hairpin-ended chromosomes in Borrelia species40, where we propose the telomere system likely originated from linear plasmids via horizontal gene transfer; (2) we show that prokaryotic hairpin telomeres effectively protect linear chromosome ends in yeast, with engineered strains maintaining long-term genetic stability; (3) yeast genomes rapidly evolve compensatory mutations (e.g., in TEL1 and CYR1) to restore critical functions like meiosis, demonstrating remarkable plasticity in accommodating foreign telomeres.

The integration of prokaryotic telomeres into eukaryotic chromosomes requires either disruption of core DNA repair pathways (MRX/Sae2) or acquisition of compensatory mutations. While DW1 (sae2Δ) and DW4 (tel1-L1562P) strains exhibit impaired growth and sporulation compared to SY14 (with natural telomeres), these phenotypes likely result from both their specific genetic modifications and inherent constraints of the tos-system. This interpretation is supported by two key observations: (1) partial phenotypic restoration in DW5 (cyr1-H1695N, tel1-L1562P), and (2) comparable performance between tos-YAC and natural telomere-YAC constructs in DW6 background (derived from DW5).

The closed-end hairpin structure of tos-telomeres imposes unique topological constraints for DNA replication and chromosome pairing. While bacterial systems like phi29 phage and yeast pGKL2 plasmids employ terminal protein-primed strand-displacement for telomere replication8, our preliminary experiments revealed that codon-optimized phi29 or pGKL2 telomere systems failed to achieve stable episomal replication of plasmids with prokaryotic telomeres in the nucleus of S. cerevisiae. Similarly, while Streptomyces replicates linear chromosomes bidirectionally with opened-end terminal “patching”7, the complete set of proteins involved in Streptomyces telomere replication remains unidentified. While we synthesized three known genes (tpg, tap and dinB1)41–43 associated with Streptomyces telomere replication, this system did not function in S. cerevisiae chromosomes. Future implementation of such open-ended protelomere systems may prove to offer advantages over hairpin structures, pending identification of additional required factors.

Methods

Construction of plasmids

The pCas9 plasmid44 contained a CRISPR/Cas9 system component with galactose-inducible guide RNA targeting 2μ replicon. Guide RNA plasmids (pgRNA1-6) were generated using psgRNA plasmid template, incorporating 25–35 bp homologous overlaps between adjacent fragments and Cas9 recognition sites targeting TLC1, telN integration vicinity, KanMX4/NatMX6 markers, MATα locus, and CYR1. The plasmid pgRNA3-NatMX6 was constructed by substituting the LYS2 marker with NatMX6 (from pNatMX6). The plasmid pSAE2-1 consists SAE2 gene (S. cerevisiae S288C), NatMX6 (pNatMX6), and 2μ replicon (pgRNA), while pSAE2-2 replaced this replicon with CEN6/ARS4. All plasmids were constructed using the One Step Cloning Kit (C117, Vazyme Biotech) and purified via the Plasmid MaxiPrep Kit (EM122, TransGen Biotech) for transformation.

Construction of yeast strains

The parental strain SY15 was modified by integrating the tos sequence (a 519-bp TelN recognition sequence from Escherichia phage N15) between COS5 and PAU22 via homologous recombination, flanked by URA3 and HIS3 markers. The TLC1 (yeast telomerase RNA template) was knocked out using CRISPR/Cas9, followed by KanMX4 replacement of MRX-SAE2 complex genes (MRE11, RAD50, XRS2, SAE2), generating SY15-tos- tlc1Δ- mre11Δ/ rad50Δ/ xrs2Δ/ sae2Δ. The codon-optimized TelN gene (under ADH1 promoter, encoding protein with 631 amino acids) was inserted between the SGE1 and ARR1 genes in circular chromosome strains via CRISPR/Cas9, yielding DW1-mre11Δ /rad50Δ /xrs2Δ /sae2Δ. DW4 derived from DW1-sae2Δ or SY15-tos- tlc1Δ- sae2Δ incorporated a TEL1 mutation (L1562P) and TelN-mediated chromosome linearization. The KanMX4 gene was targeted for cleavage using the CRISPR/Cas9 system, and the SAE2 gene was reintroduced into the genome via yeast homologous recombination. The DW5 strain was obtained by introducing a point mutation in the CYR1 gene of the DW4 strain, substituting His for Asn at the 1695th codon.

Phylogenetic tree of telomere systems

Total 42 Prokaryotic terminal proteins and 76 Eukaryotic terminal proteins (Source Data 1) corresponding to key components of telomere systems were retrieved from NCBI, covering phylogenetically diverse representative species. Multiple sequence alignment was performed using Clustal W45. The resulting alignment was used to construct a maximum-likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree in MEGA1146 under the Whelan and Goldman (WAG) evolutionary model, with rate heterogeneity modeled using a discrete gamma distribution (20 categories). The final tree was midpoint-rooted for visualization and further annotated using FigTree v1.4.4.

Pulsed-field gel electrophoresis (PFGE) analysis of yeast genome

Restriction enzyme-digested yeast genomic DNA was analyzed by PFGE using the following protocol: logarithmic-phase yeast cells (108 cells/plug) were embedded in 2% low-melting-point agarose with Zymolyase-20T (8320921, MP Biomedicals), lysed with Lyticase buffer (37 °C, 3 h), and treated with Proteinase K (CW2572L, CWBIO) (50 °C, 36 h). After sequential washes with nuclease-free water (BU011-01, Belong Biotech) and Tris EDTA (TE) buffer, plugs were digested with FseI (R0588V, NEB) (37 °C, 2 h). PFGE separation was performed using a CHEF-DR system (BIO-RAD) under 12 °C with voltage gradients: 90 V (19 h) followed by 120 V (14 h), 6 V/cm, 120° switch angle. CHEF DNA Size Marker (1703605, BIO-RAD) was used for molecular weight determination.

Competitive co-cultivation of yeast cells

DW2 and wild-type BY4742 were cultured overnight in YPD medium at 30 °C, diluted to OD600 = 1.0, and mixed (50 μL each) in 5 mL fresh YPD for 24-h co-cultivation at 30 °C. The co-culture was subcultured (initial OD600 = 0.02) in fresh YPD for another 24 h at 30 °C, with each competitive cycle lasting 3 days. After each cycle, diluted cultures were plated on SC-UH medium (selecting URA3, HIS3-containing DW2), incubated for 2–3 days at 30 °C, and surviving colonies were propagated in SC-UH liquid medium as inoculum for subsequent cycles. Adaptive mutant DW3 with enhanced fitness was isolated through similar iterative co-cultivation and selection.

Screening of yeast mutants

The CRISPR/Cas9 system was used to integrate TelN into the genome of SY15-tos-tlc1Δ-sae2Δ, yielding ~10³ PCR-verified transformants retaining linear chromosomes (pgRNA2-retained). (1) For every 25 clones pooled, 1 μg pSAE2-1/pSAE2-2 was transferred, and the mixture was plated on SC-LUH+nourseothricin (100 μg/mL) to screen SAE2-complemented clones with linear chromosomes. Subsequent elimination of pgRNA2 and pSAE2-1 was achieved via galactose/raffinose SC-LUH cultivation, while pSAE2-2 removal required serial passaging. KanMX4 replacement with SAE2 via CRISPR/Cas9 enabled genetic complementation, with PCR-confirmed absence of chromosomal circularization preceding pgRNA3 elimination. (2) Each group of 25 clones was transformed with 1 μg pgRNA3-NatMX6 and 2 μg SAE2- targeting fragments. The transformed clones were plated onto SC-LUH+nourseothricin (100 μg/mL) plates to screen for positive clones. CRISPR/Cas9 was used again to replace KanMX4 with SAE2. After screening clones with linear chromosomes, both pgRNA2 and pgRNA3-NatMX6 were simultaneously eliminated in galactose/raffinose medium, ultimately yielding SAE2-complemented strains harboring linear chromosomes.

Sample preparation for scanning electron microscopy

Yeast cells cultured overnight were washed with PBS, fixed in 50% FAA (50% ethanol, 5% acetic acid, 5% formaldehyde) at 4 °C for 6 h, then dehydrated through an ethanol gradient (30–100%, 10–15 min per step) followed by three times of 100% ethanol washes. After CO₂ critical-point drying, samples were dispersed on conductive adhesive, sputter-coated with gold, and imaged using a scanning electron microscope (Zeiss) under optimized parameters to resolve surface ultrastructure47.

Yeast cell cycle synchronization

The yeast strain was inoculated into YPAD liquid medium and cultured overnight at 30 °C, followed by subculturing to the logarithmic growth phase (OD600 = 1.0). Cells (150 μL) were centrifuged, resuspended in pre-chilled sterile water, and fixed with 700 μL pre-chilled absolute ethanol (70% final concentration) as a pre-synchronization control. The remaining culture was treated with 0.2 M hydroxyurea (V900323, Sigma) at 30 °C for 2.5 h, washed repeatedly with sterile water, and transferred to pre-warmed YPAD medium for recovery. Samples (150 μL) were collected at 15-min intervals and fixed in 70% ethanol. After overnight fixation at 4 °C, cell cycle dynamics were analyzed by flow cytometry.

Flow cytometry analysis of yeast

Inoculated the yeast strain into 3 mL YPAD medium and cultivated overnight at 30 °C. Diluted the culture to an OD600 of 0.2 and grew to an OD600 of 1.0. Centrifuged 200 μL of the culture, discarded the supernatant, and washed the pellet with sterilized water. Resuspended the cells in pre-chilled sterile water (4 °C), then slowly added 700 μL of pre-chilled absolute ethanol (final concentration 70%) for overnight fixation at 4 °C. Centrifuged to remove the ethanol, washed with FACS buffer, and digested with 20 mg/mL RNaseA (DE111-01, Vazyme Biotech) at 37 °C for 2 h. Re-centrifuged, stained in the dark for 30 min with FACS buffer containing PicoGreen dye (P7581, Thermo), filtered through a 40 μm filter (YB-40UM, Yueyibio), and analyzed cell cycle progression using a Flow Cytometer (Beckman). A total of 105 cells were analyzed for each strain. Data were analyzed with Summit 5.2. Cells were gated by FSC‑A vs. SSC‑A to remove debris and large aggregates. Single cells were gated using SSC‑A vs. SSC‑H to exclude doublets. FL1‑A histograms (PicoGreen) were used to analyze DNA content and cell cycle phases. At least 100,000 events were acquired per sample.

Measurement of yeast cell growth

At least three individual colonies of each strain were inoculated into YPD liquid medium for overnight cultivation at 30 °C. Diluted and transferred 1 mL of the overnight cultures (OD600 = 0.1) into deep-well plates and cultivated at 30 °C with 1000 rpm for 24 h in the Microbial Growth Real-Time Analysis System (Jieling MicroScreen). The optical density of cells was automatically measured every 5 min.

Growth fitness and genotoxin sensitivity assay

Incubated the test strains in YPD liquid medium at 30 °C overnight. Measured the OD600 of each culture and adjusted to an OD600 of 1.0. Performed serial dilutions of the yeast suspension to 1/10, 1/100 and 1/1000 of the initial concentration. Spotted 4 μL of each dilutes onto YPD plates supplemented with genotoxic agents (CPT, MMS, Phl). After incubating at 30 °C for 3 days, we photographed the plates (MINI Space 1000, Tanon) and evaluated the drug sensitivity of each strain. For temperature sensitivity assays, spotted serially diluted samples onto YPD/YC plates and incubated at 24 °C, 30 °C, and 37 °C for 2–3 days. Photographed the results to assess thermal tolerance.

Mating and sporulation assays

The CRISPR/Cas9 system facilitated yeast mating-type switching by replacing the 807 bp sequence at the MATα locus with a 703 bp MATa-specific fragment through lithium acetate-mediated co-transformation of pgRNA5 and the MATa targeting fragment. The engineered MATa strain and the original MATα strain were cultured to early logarithmic phase (OD600 = 1.0) and mixed in equal volumes, and co-cultured at 30 °C for 4 h to promote mating. The mated culture was diluted, plated onto YPD agar, and diploid colonies were selected via PCR screening. Diploids were induced to undergo meiosis using a two-step protocol: pre-sporulation in 1% YPA medium (30 °C, 28–30 h), followed by sporulation in 1% potassium acetate (KAc) medium (25 °C, 4–7 days). Spore formation was confirmed microscopically. The sporulated culture was treated with Zymolyase-20T to digest tetrad spore cell walls, and at least 40 tetrads were dissected microscopically to assess spore viabilities.

Genetic stability assay

The initial strain was individually isolated on YPD plates, with three clones undergoing continuous liquid subculturing (culture duration adjusted by generation time). Yeast cultures were transferred to fresh YPD medium every 24 h (initial OD600 = 0.1, 30 °C, 220 rpm). At 100-generation intervals, cell survival rates were calculated by streaking 100 clones onto SC-UH plates, confirming intact hairpin telomeres and stable marker retention. Genomic samples collected at generational intervals (0, 200, 500, 800) underwent FseI digestion and PFGE to verify chromosomal linearity and assess telomere system stability.

Telomere Southern hybridization

For each sample, collect 100 mL of yeast culture in the logarithmic growth phase (OD600 = 0.8). The genomic DNA for neutral and alkaline agarose gel running was extracted using bead beating and cetyl- trimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) protocol, respectively. For bead beating, yeast cells were disrupted by bead beating and genomic DNA was extracted by Phenol-chloroform and precipitated by EtOH. For CTAB48, yeast cells were treated with zymolyase to produce spheroplasts. DNA was extracted and precipitated by CTAB. Extracted DNA (~35 μg) was digested by XhoI at 37 °C for 16 h and separated by either neutral gel (1 × TAE, 100 V, 2 h) or alkaline agarose gel49 (1 × alkaline buffer, 46 V, 7 h). DNA was then transferred onto Biodyne B Membrane. Hybridization was performed with a telomere-specific probe, comprising a biotin-labeled 566-bp DNA fragment corresponding to the tos sequence. Signal detection is ultimately performed using a chemiluminescence detection kit50 (89880, Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Genome sequencing and assembly

Yeast genomic DNA was extracted using the CTAB method with modifications quantified by Qubit Fluorometer (Invitrogen, USA), and assessed for quality using NanoDrop Spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). Sequencing libraries were prepared using TruSeq DNA Kit (Illumina, USA) and Template Prep Kit (Pacific Biosciences, USA). Paired-end sequencing was conducted on PacBio Revio and Illumina Novaseq platforms (Personal Biotechnology, Shanghai). Data processing involved adapter removal (AdapterRemoval51) and error correction (SOAPec51), followed by hybrid assembly using SPAdes52 (short reads), A5-miseq53 (contig construction), and Flye54 (PacBio long reads). Final chromosomal sequences were polished using Pilon software55 (sequence polishing).

Variation detection of chromosome sequence

The high-quality filtered data were aligned to the reference genome using bwa-mem56 (default parameters), followed by local realignment of reads near InDels using GATK’s Realigner Target Creator and Indel Realigner tools with known InDel data to enhance SNP calling accuracy. Variant calling was performed using GATK v3.8’s Unified Genotyper (stand_call_conf = 30; stand_emit_conf = 10 for SNPs; stand_call_conf = 10; stand_emit_conf = 10 for InDels), with select variants applied to extract mutations specifically occurring on single mutant chromosomes for downstream analysis. Long variant regions were analyzed by aligning the assembled chromosome sequence of DW1 with the reference sequence SY14. PCR and Sanger sequencing were used for further verification of those differences.

Hi-C experiments for yeast

Hi-C libraries were prepared according to established protocols57 as following: yeast cultures were fixed with 3% formaldehyde for 20 min at ambient temperature, followed by neutralization with 0.375 M glycine for 5 min. Cellular structures were mechanically disrupted through cryogenic grinding in liquid nitrogen. Subsequent steps included chromatin digestion inactivation with 0.1% SDS, restriction digestion using 100 units of MboI enzyme (New England Biolabs), followed by biotin-14-dCTP (Invitrogen) labeling and T4 DNA ligase (50 units, NEB)-mediated intermolecular ligation. Crosslink reversal preceded DNA purification using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen) per manufacturer's protocols. Purified DNA was fragmented to 300–500 bp sizes, then subjected to blunt-end repair and A-tailing before adapter ligation. Biotinylated fragments underwent streptavidin affinity enrichment followed by PCR amplification. Final library quantification and sequencing were performed using either the Illumina Nova-seq platform (San Diego, CA) or the MGI-seq platform (BGI, China).

RNA-seq analysis

The yeast strain to be tested was streaked on YPD agar plates for single colony isolation. Three clones were selected for liquid culture. Early log-phase cells (OD600 = 1.0) were collected, and RNA was extracted using the TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, USA). Paired-end sequencing (150 bp) was performed using the Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform, yielding 6 Gb of high-quality data per sample. The HISAT2 (v2.1.0)58 was employed to build an index for the S. cerevisiae S288C reference genome (GCF_000146045.2_R64), followed by alignment of paired-end clean reads to the reference genome. HTSeq (v0.9.1)59 was used to quantify read counts mapped to each gene, which served as raw expression values. Expression levels were normalized using FPKM. Differential expression analysis between comparison groups was conducted with DESeq2 (v1.38.3)60. Differentially expressed genes were identified using the following criteria: absolute log2-fold change > 1 (|log2 Fold Change| > 1) and statistical significance (P-value < 0.05).

The tos-YAC assembly and efficiency determination

The circular plasmids carrying tos sites and YAC backbone were separately transformed into the respective mating-type strains MATα DW6G and MATa DW6N, where they were linearized to form tos-YAC plasmids. Mating of MATα × MATa combined both tos-YAC plasmids into a diploid cell. Then, a gRNA1 expression plasmid (targeting the shared markers like HIS3) was transformed via lithium acetate, directing Cas9 to create double-strand breaks in both plasmids. Subsequent homologous recombination yields the assembled construct. Transformants were selected on SC-Leu-Lys-URA plates for 3 days. To quantify assembly efficiency, 6-8 random colonies were processed into agarose plugs. PFGE analysis verified plasmid assembly. The percentage of clones with the correct size defines the assembly success rate.

Iterative assembly of the tos-YAC system

Haploid strains carrying distinct linear plasmids and opposite mating types are mated to generate diploids. Under the action of the CRISPR/Cas9 system, both linear plasmids are induced to undergo double-strand breaks simultaneously (for each assembly event, different plasmid selection markers and target sites are used; the guide RNA-expressing plasmids are typically from the pSgRNA1‑9 series). Successfully assembled plasmids are then obtained via homologous recombination and selection. For pSgRNA1-9 plasmid curing, cells were grown in SC medium with 2% galactose and 3% raffinose at 30 °C for 24 h with shaking. Subsequently, 10 µL of culture was plated on SC medium (supplemented with the appropriate auxotrophic nutrients) and incubated at 30 °C for 3 days. Successful plasmid curing was verified by replica plating. For haploidization, the strain-specific pgRNA3 or pgRNA4 plasmid targeting either the DW6G (site G) or DW6N (site N) chromosomal Cas9 sites was introduced. CRISPR/Cas9-mediated chromosome breaks induced mitosis instability, driving haploidization. Resulting MATα or MATa haploid cells containing the assembled plasmid were isolated for subsequent assembly cycles.

Genetic stability analysis of the assembled tos-YAC plasmids

A single colony of the test strain was inoculated into 4 mL of liquid SC medium. The cell culture was then incubated at 30 °C for 24 h, with continuous shaking. Subsequently, a 100/200 μL aliquot of the culture was transferred into a fresh 4 mL SC medium, and incubated for another 24 h. This process was repeated on the third and fourth days. Cell cultures were sampled every 24 h. The collected cells (OD600 = 4/5) were then embedded in low-melting-point agarose to form plugs for PFGE.

Sequencing and integrity analysis of the assembled tos-YAC plasmids

High-throughput sequencing of 21 original BAC plasmids was performed using the Illumina NovaSeq Xplus platform. De novo sequencing of pY8, pY12, pY13, pY14, and pY15 was conducted using both the Illumina NovaSeq Xplus platform and the PacBio Revio platform. The filtered high-quality data were aligned to the reference genome using the bwa (0.7.17–r1188) mem program, with all alignment parameters set to the default values of bwamem. SNP detection was carried out using the GATK software. The UnifiedGenotyper program was used to obtain all mutation sites. SelectVariants was then used to extract InDel mutation information.

Alignment of the consensus sequences (pY8, pY12, pY13, pY14, pY15) with the reference was performed using SnapGene’s alignment tool to identify large insertions and deletions. Variations present in both the original BACs and the assembled plasmids were excluded, as they were not attributable to the assembly process.
