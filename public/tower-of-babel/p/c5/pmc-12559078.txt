Electroacupuncture for chemotherapy-induced gastrointestinal symptoms in colorectal cancer: protocol for a multicenter, three-arm, randomized controlled trial
Authors: Zi-Wen Chen, Tao Xu, Zi-Han Yin, Qi-fu Li, Yun-Jie Shu, Xue-Li Zhou, Ling Zhao, Ming-Sheng Sun, Fan-Rong Liang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) is the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality globally. While chemotherapy remains a cornerstone of CRC treatment, gastrointestinal (GI) toxicity—manifesting as abdominal pain, diarrhea, constipation, and reflux—affects 40%–100% of patients, severely impairing quality of life. Despite the prevalence of these symptoms, there is a lack of effective non-pharmacological interventions. This study aims to evaluate the efficacy of electroacupuncture (EA) in alleviating chemotherapy-induced GI dysfunction in CRC patients.MethodsIn this multicenter, three-arm, randomized controlled trial (RCT), 231 CRC patients with post-chemotherapy GI symptoms will be centrally randomized to EA plus standard care, sham acupuncture (SA) plus standard care, or standard care alone. The EA and SA groups will receive three 30-min sessions over consecutive days, delivered by certified acupuncturists (EA: LI11, PC6, ST36 and ST37 with electrical stimulation; SA: non-acupoints with blunt-tip needles without stimulation). The primary outcome is the change in Gastrointestinal Symptom Rating Scale (GSRS) total scores from baseline to post-treatment (day 3). Secondary outcomes include GSRS subscale scores, Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy-Colorectal (FACT-C) scores, and Self-Rating Anxiety Scale/Self-Rating Depression Scale (SAS/SDS) scores. All outcomes will be assessed at baseline (T0), post-intervention (T1: day 3), and follow-up (T2: day 10). Statistical analysis will employ intention-to-treat linear mixed-effects models.ConclusionThis multicenter RCT is designed to generate high-quality evidence on the comprehensive efficacy of EA in addressing chemotherapy-induced GI toxicity in CRC patients. If proven effective, EA could provide a safe, non-pharmacological adjunct to standard care, helping to reduce symptom burden and improve patient adherence to chemotherapy.Trial registration ChiCTR2200062317. Background

Colorectal cancer (CRC) accounts for approximately 1.9 million new cases and 900,000 deaths annually, with rising incidence rates linked to aging populations and westernized lifestyles [1–3]. While advancements in cytotoxic regimens (e.g., FOLFOX, FOLFIRI) and targeted therapies have improved survival [4–6], chemotherapy-induced gastrointestinal (GI) toxicity persists as a major clinical challenge [7, 8]. About 40%–100% of patients experience GI symptoms such as nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and abdominal pain during treatment, with CRC patients being especially vulnerable owing to the anatomical proximity of the tumor to the GI tract [9], often necessitating dose reductions or delays that compromise oncological outcomes [10–12]. Current management relies heavily on antiemetics, antidiarrheals, and opioids, which provide incomplete relief and carry risks of adverse effects (e.g., constipation from 5-HT3 antagonists, sedation from anticholinergics) [13, 14]. This therapeutic impasse has spurred interest in complementary therapies like acupuncture [15, 16], which modulates visceral hypersensitivity and gut motility via somato-autonomic reflexes [17, 18], and reduces intestinal inflammation through vagally mediated cholinergic pathways [19, 20]. Meta-analyses suggest acupuncture reduces chemotherapy-induced nausea/vomiting (CINV) compared to sham controls, though evidence for broader GI symptom clusters remains limited [21–23].

Notably, existing trials focus narrowly on isolated symptoms (e.g., CINV), overlooking the interconnected nature of GI dysfunction in CRC patients receiving chemotherapy. Furthermore, inadequate blinding and heterogeneous acupuncture protocols plague prior studies, limiting clinical translation [23]. This multicenter trial addresses these gaps through three key innovations: (1) evaluation of electroacupuncture’s (EA) comprehensive impact on GI symptom clusters using validated multidimensional scales, (2) implementation of a web-based central randomization system (CRS) to ensure balanced allocation across multiple arms and centers, and (3) incorporation of a non-penetrating sham control to isolate EA-specific effects.

By elucidating EA’s role in CRC supportive care, this study aligns with the NIH’s strategic priority to advance non-pharmacological symptom management in oncology [24]. Findings may establish EA as a scalable adjunct to standard therapies, potentially reducing healthcare costs associated with prolonged hospitalization and polypharmacy.

Objectives

The objective of this proposed study is to evaluate the efficacy and safety of EA versus sham acupuncture (SA) and standard care in alleviating chemotherapy-induced GI dysfunction among CRC patients.

Methods

Study design

The study will be a three-arm, parallel-group randomized controlled trial (RCT) investigating the effects of EA on chemotherapy-induced GI dysfunction in CRC patients. The study protocol followed the SPIRIT guidelines [25].

Six tertiary hospitals across China—including Sichuan Cancer Hospital, Chongqing Traditional Chinese Medicine Hospital, Yunnan Cancer Hospital, Changsha Traditional Chinese Medicine Hospital, and the First and Third Affiliated Hospitals of Henan University of Chinese Medicine—will jointly enroll 231 CRC patients with post-chemotherapy GI symptoms. Using a web-based central randomization system (CIMS V5, Chengdu Symex), participants will be stratified by center and allocated 1:1:1 to:

EA group: three daily 30-min EA sessions + standard care;

SA group: three daily non-penetrating SA sessions + standard care;

Control group: standard care alone.

Outcomes will be assessed at baseline (T0), post-intervention (T1: day 3), and follow-up (T2: day 10). The 3-day intervention and 10-day follow-up were designed to align with the expected trajectory of chemotherapy-induced GI toxicity and real-world treatment logistics in China. Randomization and blinding

An independent statistician will implement block randomization (block size = 6) via the CIMS V5 platform, ensuring allocation concealment. Participants in the EA and SA groups, outcome assessors, and data analysts remain blinded throughout the trial, while acupuncturists are not involved in the outcome assessment or data analysis. Blinding success will be quantitatively assessed post-intervention using the Blind Evaluation Scale. To minimize performance bias, the SA is designed to closely mimic EA in terms of needle appearance, device sounds, and practitioner interaction. Additionally, treatment rooms are soundproofed to prevent cross-group communication.

Informed consent and withdraw from the trial

This trial will be conducted under the requirements of the Declaration of Helsinki. This project has been approved by the Sichuan Regional Ethics Review Committee on TCM (ethical approval number: 2021KL-079) and has been registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (registration number: ChiCTR2200062317). All participants will receive detailed information about the study’s purpose, procedures, potential risks, benefits, and the randomization process. Written informed consent will be obtained from each participant before enrollment. Participants have the right to withdraw from the study at any time without providing a reason and without facing any penalties. The reasons for withdrawal will be documented, and participants will be encouraged to continue the study if possible.

Interventions

All patients will receive standard care for CRC patients after chemotherapy. During the enrollment period, all medications used to treat GI dysfunction resulting from CRC chemotherapy will be recorded in detail on the Case Report Form (CRF), including drug name, dosage, frequency, route of administration, and start and end times. Any changes in medication during the study period will also be recorded. An abdominal oncology specialist will assess the feasibility of continuing acupuncture treatment for patients with GI dysfunction during chemotherapy.

The acupuncture prescription has been developed based on our pilot study and expert consensus meetings, and it includes bilateral Quchi (LI11), Neiguan (PC6), Zusanli (ST36), and Shangjuxu (ST37). SA will be performed at non-acupuncture points, located 10 mm away from LI11, PC6, ST36, and ST37 [26]. Detailed information for each acupoint is provided in Table 1 and Figs. 2 and 3. All acupuncturists must hold a valid license and have at least 3 years of clinical experience. Prior to the trial, acupuncturists will receive training on acupoint location and needle manipulation. Each study site will assign one acupuncturist to treat all patients at that center. Both the EA and SA groups will use the same needle insertion device on the acupoints to ensure better blinding.

EA group

During treatment, the patient will remain seated or in a supine position for ease of needle insertion. The acupuncturist will disinfect the skin around the acupoints with 75% alcohol. A needle insertion device, consisting of a sticky pad, needle tube, and guide tube, will be used to insert the needles at the specified acupoints. For Quchi (LI11), Zusanli (ST36), and Shangjuxu (ST37), needles (0.25 × 40 mm; Suzhou Hwato, China) will be inserted to a depth of approximately 25–30 mm. For Neiguan (PC6), needles (0.25 × 25 mm; Suzhou Hwato, China) will be inserted to a depth of approximately 15–20 mm. A Deqi sensation will be elicited at each acupoint, and the needles will be retained for 30 min. An EA device (Hwato, SDZ-V, Suzhou, China) will be connected to the needles, with a frequency of 2 Hz and an amplitude range of 0.1–10 mA until the patients felt comfortable.

SA group

The SA group will receive placebo acupuncture at bilateral “non-acupoints” (10 mm away from LI11, PC6, ST36, and ST37) [26]. After routine skin disinfection, a sticky pad will be adhered to the “non-acupoint,” the same as used in the EA group. A disposable blunt needle (0.25 × 40 mm; Suzhou Hwato, China) will be used to pierce the base of the pad, fixing the needle body vertically on the patient’s skin. The patient will feel mild pain but without puncturing the skin or producing a Deqi sensation. Additionally, the EA device will also be used in the SA group, but the current output will be set to 0 mA. The needles will be left in place for 30 min. Considering the ethical requirements, a compensatory acupuncture treatment will be provided to patients at the end of the trial.

Control group

The control group will receive guideline-directed pharmacotherapy for chemotherapy-induced GI symptoms. This includes antiemetic agents (e.g., 5-HT3 receptor antagonists, corticosteroids), antidiarrheals (e.g., loperamide, adsorbents), and laxatives when indicated, without any acupuncture intervention. All medications administered for symptom management will be meticulously documented in electronic CRFs, including indications, administration routes, and temporal patterns of use. Participants in this group will undergo identical outcome assessments at the designated timepoints and will be offered optional EA treatment upon trial completion as ethical compensation.

Outcome measurement

Primary outcome

The primary endpoint is the change in total GSRS score from baseline (day 0) to post-intervention (day 3). The Chinese version of the GSRS has demonstrated proven reliability and effectiveness [27]. This 15-item instrument rates five symptom clusters (abdominal pain, indigestion, diarrhea, reflux, constipation) on a 4-point Likert scale (0 = no discomfort; 3 = severe symptoms). Higher total scores (range 0–45) reflect greater GI dysfunction severity.

Outcomes will be measured at baseline (T0), immediately post-intervention (T1: day 3), and follow-up (T2: day 10). Trained assessors blinded to group allocation will administer all patient-reported instruments.

Safety assessments

Any adverse events (AEs) related to acupuncture treatment (e.g., bleeding, hematoma, pallor, sweating, dizziness, fainting serious pain, local infection) will be monitored and reported throughout the study. When AEs occur, clinicians will assess whether to discontinue the trial based on the condition and report severe cases to the Project Management Office of Chengdu University of Traditional Chinese Medicine and the Ethics Committee within 24 h. All AEs will be assessed and managed by the investigators.

Sample size

The sample size was determined through a priori power analysis using data from our registered pilot trial (ChiCTR2100050821, n = 90). Based on observed post-treatment reductions in GSRS scores (EA = 4.90, SA = 2.13, control = 1.98) and a pooled standard deviation of 5.19 across groups, we conducted a one-way ANOVA power analysis with PASS 15.0 (NCSS LLC), setting α = 0.05 and β = 0.10 (90% power). This yielded a required sample size of 65 participants per group. To account for a 15% dropout rate, we inflated each group to 77 participants, resulting in a total enrollment target of 231.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses will follow the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle, including all randomized participants regardless of whether they completed the intervention or any post-baseline assessments. Missing data will be imputed using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE package in R). The primary outcome and continuous secondary outcomes will be analyzed using a linear mixed-effects model adjusted by baseline GSRS values, with time, group, and interaction between time and group as the fixed effects, and individual subjects as random effect. Bonferroni correction will be applied for pairwise comparisons of the primary outcome to control for multiple testing. Secondary outcomes will be analyzed without multiplicity correction to explore potential trends. Categorical variables, including baseline variables, blinding outcome, and the incidence of AEs, will be analyzed using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. Continuous variables, including baseline characteristics and treatment satisfaction scores, will be compared between groups using t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests.

Results will be presented as least squares means with 95% confidence intervals, with a two-sided significance level of 0.05. All data analyses will be conducted by statisticians independent of the research team, who will be blinded to group assignments. Statistical analyses will be performed using R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing), version 4.4.2.

Data management and quality control

The trial protocol was reviewed and revised by experts in acupuncture, abdominal oncology, methodology, and statistics. Standard operating procedures (SOPs) will be developed in advance, and training sessions will be held before the start of the study to provide standardized training for researchers at each center. These sessions will ensure that all researchers are familiar with the research process and the specific implementation details. To ensure consistency, researchers at each center will be fixed, and the methods for data recording and judgment criteria will be standardized. This will guarantee that scale assessments, data management, and the completion of the CRF are conducted under uniform standards. Data will be collected via both paper and electronic CRFs. A web-based system (CIMS V5) will be used for data entry and cross-validation to ensure data accuracy and consistency.

Data will be monitored regularly to ensure completeness and accuracy. A data monitor will conduct quality control checks at each research center every 3 months to ensure compliance with the study protocol, verify the correctness of the completed information, and generate quality control analysis reports. If any modifications are made to the study protocol, they must be approved by the ethics committee.

Discussion

This multicenter RCT protocol is designed to generate high-quality evidence regarding the comprehensive efficacy of EA in addressing chemotherapy-induced GI toxicity in CRC patients. As a widely utilized non-pharmacological intervention for GI disorders, EA may exert its therapeutic effects by modulating gastrointestinal motility, reducing inflammatory biomarkers [28, 29], and enhancing neuro-enteric signaling pathways [30–32]. While previous studies have demonstrated EA’s efficacy in alleviating isolated symptoms, such as CINV, its impact on broader GI symptom clusters remains underexplored. Our preliminary data identified the five most prevalent GI toxicity symptoms in post-chemotherapy CRC patients as nausea/vomiting, incomplete defecation, urgent defecation, abdominal distension, and increased flatulence. This study seeks to address this gap by using the GSRS scale, which allows for the comprehensive assessment of multiple GI domains. Another strength of this trial lies in its robust design, with a large-scale, multicenter approach involving six tertiary hospitals, ensuring a diverse and highly representative sample of CRC patients, which enhances the generalizability and external validity of the results. Furthermore, the randomization process is independently conducted by third-party statisticians, minimizing the risk of allocation bias and ensuring methodological rigor.

However, some limitations of this study must be acknowledged. First, the use of a standardized acupuncture protocol, including fixed acupuncture points and electrical stimulation parameters, may not account for individual variability in treatment response. Second, the number of acupuncture sessions is constrained by the chemotherapy schedule, which may restrict the overall therapeutic potential of EA. Third, the relatively short follow-up period prevents the assessment of differences in gastrointestinal dysfunction across multiple chemotherapy cycles. Fourth, the exclusion of patients with poor performance status may reduce the applicability to frailer populations who might benefit more from non-pharmacological interventions. Moreover, our exclusive focus on CRC patients was intended to enhance internal validity—given their high susceptibility to chemotherapy-induced GI toxicity [9] and relatively homogeneous symptom profiles [10, 33]—but this design inevitably limits the generalizability of the findings to other cancer types. Future studies should consider expanding the study population to include patients with other cancer types and broader performance statuses to enhance generalizability. In addition, longer intervention durations and extended follow-up periods would help assess the sustained effects of EA and its potential benefits across multiple chemotherapy cycles.

Despite these limitations, this trial represents one of the first multicenter, randomized controlled efforts to evaluate EA for chemotherapy-induced GI toxicity in patients with CRC. The findings will contribute valuable clinical evidence regarding the feasibility, safety, and short-term efficacy of EA as an adjunctive supportive care strategy. These results may serve as a foundation for future large-scale trials and inform integrative approaches to symptom management in oncology settings.

Trial status

Due to the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic, the progress of this study was delayed in its early stages. However, recruitment resumed gradually in 2023, and as of now, a total of 125 patients have been enrolled in the trial. Recruitment is ongoing, and further updates will be provided as additional participants are included (Protocol version: V3.0, dated 25 July 2021).
