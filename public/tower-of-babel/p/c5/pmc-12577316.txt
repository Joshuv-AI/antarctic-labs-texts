Is stress different in infertile women and men? A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Masoumeh Simbar, Fahimeh Rashidi, Reza Taherian, Vida Ghasemi, Mehri Kalhor, Zahra Kiani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStress is gender-specific and differs between in infertile women and men Therefore, according to the importance of this issue, this systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted to investigate the difference in stress in infertile women and men.MethodsThis a systematic review and meta-analysis in accordance with PRISMA guidelines was conducted without time limit until August 20, 2024 through searching in the international database (PubMed, Web of sciences, Scopus), and the Google Scholar search engine using the keywords of infertility and stress. The words were combined using AND and OR, and the specific search strategy for each database was used. All articles retrieved from the databases were imported into EndNote 7 software. Duplicate records were removed, and studies were then screened according to the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. The quality of the articles was assessed by two reviewers independently using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI). The analysis was performed using STATA 18.0. Cohen’s d index was used to examine the effect size.ResultsThe systematic part of the study included 53 studies with a total sample size of 21,355 infertile people, 12,913 men and 8,442 women. Nine questionnaires including the Fertility Problem Inventory (FPI), the Perceived Stress Scale (PPS), the Perceived Stress Questionnaire (PSQ), Booklet contains several questions related to reproductive health, stress, social relations, coping, and well-being (The COMPI questionnaire), Depression Anxiety and Stress Scale (DASS), the Psychological State of Stress, Specific stress symptom scale, Treatment related Stress Scale (TTS), and the Infertility-Related Stress Scale (IRSS) were used in this study. Given the fact that at least 3 studies are needed to perform a meta-analysis, it was only possible to compare the stress of infertile men and women on the FPI questionnaire and its dimensions. The difference in the total stress score of infertile men and women was 0.33 (95%CI: 0.23–0.44, P˂0.001), and in the dimensions of the FPI questionnaire, it was 0.35 (95%CI: 0.12–0.59, P < 0.001) for social concern, 0.35 (95%CI: 0.08–0.61, P = 0.005) for sexual concern, and 0.31 (95%CI: 0.19–0.43, P < 0.001) for need for parenthood. The difference between women and men was significant, and women’s stress score was higher than men’s.ConclusionThe stress in infertile women and men is an important issue that requires attention and planning and affects both sexes. It is suggested that couples’ stress be assessed in infertility evaluations using standard tools, and that appropriate planning for screening and counseling in this area be designed, implemented, and evaluated in accordance with the culture of each country.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24964-7.Keywords: Infertility, Stress, Women, Men, Meta-analysis, Systematic review

Introduction

Infertility, an important social and public health problem, is a disorder related to the reproductive system of women and men and refers to the failure to conceive after one year of regular and unprotected intercourse [1, 2]. According to the latest report published by the World Health Organization (WHO) in 2023, the global prevalence of infertility lifetime and period was 17.5% and 12.6%, respectively. In this report, the prevalence of primary infertility lifetime and period was estimated at 9.6% and 9%, respectively, and of secondary infertility at 6.5% and 4%, respectively [3].

Infertility, as a lifelong stressor, is associated with a wide range of social, psychological, physical, and financial problems for couples at the individual, social, and family levels [4]. Infertile people can experience many psychological reactions such as shock, discomfort, anxiety, depression, guilt, low self-esteem, low worth, and stigma [5–8]. These people may limit their relationships and social activities or hide their infertility for fear of being judged by others [9]. Moreover, considering that in many societies, especially traditional ones, having children is considered a value, infertile people may experience social stigma under the social pressure of those around them [6, 10]. Infertility can also be a threat to the stability of married life, even leading to emotional and legal divorce [11].

All these factors along with other causes such as high number of visits to infertility centers, long-term treatment process, failure of treatment methods, high cost of treatment, lack of insurance coverage, plan for effective intercourse and psychological side effects of drugs exposes infertile people to more mental pressure and stress compared to fertile people [9, 12].

This stress not only negatively affects the quality of life, marital relationships, and sexual satisfaction of infertile couples, but can also affect treatment success and positive fertility outcomes [13–15]. Based on the results of a 2019 cohort study in China, stress level of infertile women was directly related to FSH levels and inversely related to AMH hormone levels and endometrial thickness. High stress levels had also a negative effect on some semen parameters, such as sperm density and rapid progressive motility, and finally, the number of high-quality and transferable embryos was lower in infertile couples with high stress levels [16]. These factors highlight the importance of assessing the mental health of both men and women during infertility to increase treatment success [17, 18].

Studies have shown that although both men and women experience fertility-related stress during the infertility process, women may be more affected than men [19, 20]. This may be due to the fact that in many societies, especially traditional ones, fertility and childbearing are women’s responsibilities, and infertility is usually associated with a strong sense of guilt, stigma, and social pressure, leading to more stress for women [6, 10]. However, it should not be overlooked that infertile men are also affected by psychological distress, as they are less likely to seek psychiatric services compared to infertile women [6]. Moreover, men are often the main source of family income, and the high cost of infertility may place more stress on them. In some countries, fertility has affected men’s perceptions of masculinity, causing psychological distress [21, 22].

There is a difference in stress levels between infertile women and men; generally, studies show that women experience higher levels of stress. However, an important point is that men have been studied less frequently and are less likely to express their problems, highlighting a gender difference in this issue [23]. One of the main reasons for this difference is the influence of social and cultural factors, as well as family pressure to have children. In most societies, infertility is often viewed as a female issue, and the stigma associated with it tends to affect women more than men, resulting in higher levels of stress among women [24, 25].

Considering the negative impact of stress on various aspects of the lives of infertile individuals and the gender differences in stress levels between infertile men and women, as well as the lack of systematic reviews and meta-analyses on this topic, this study was designed to compare stress in infertile women and men through a systematic review and meta-analysis in order to provide new insights in this field.

Methods

Search strategy

This systematic review and meta-analysis was reported according to the preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses (PRISMA) [26], and was registered in PROSPERO with the code CRD42024545953. Our main question in this study was whether stress is different in infertile women and men. In this systematic study, a search was performed in English in the international databases (PubMed, Web of sciences, Scopus) and the Google scholar search engine without time limit until August 20, 2024. Keywords of infertility and stress, as well as mesh words and their synonyms, were used for searching. The search strategy of each database was followed and AND and OR operators were used (Search Strategy, Appendix 1).

PICO

In this study, “P” refers to infertile women or men who have not achieved pregnancy after at least one year of unprotected sexual intercourse. “I” and “C” are not applicable, as this research does not involve an intervention or comparison group. Finally, “O” pertains to the assessment of stress in infertile women and men using standardized instruments, such as questionnaires or clinical interviews.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: studies that reported the mean stress score in infertile individuals using valid and reliable questionnaires or clinical interviews; participants who had not achieved pregnancy after at least one year of unprotected intercourse; and studies with a cross-sectional design or longitudinal data. Exclusion criteria included studies involving participants with mental or physical illnesses, lack of access to full-text articles, non-English language publications, systematic reviews and meta-analyses, case reports, and animal studies.

Outcome measures

The mean stress score of infertile people (women or men) reported in studies using standard tools (valid and reliable questionnaires or clinical interviews) and then the comparison of their mean differences by using meta-analysis were examined. The following methods are commonly used to assess infertility-related stress:

The fertility problem inventory (FPI)

Newton et al. in 1999 [27], developed this inventory for measuring perceived infertility-related stress. This 46-item inventory includes five dimensions of social concern (10 items about changing attitudes of family and friends, difficulty spending holidays, feeling lonely, feeling longing when seeing the child, not being around family and friends), sexual concern (8 items about losing interest in sex, decreasing attraction, sexual failure, and having sex only for the purpose of having children), relationship concern (10 items about not understanding your spouse, not expressing your feelings to your spouse, anxiety, disappointment, worry about separation, and arguments related to infertility) negative view of child-free lifestyle (8 items about not having enough time to do things without the child, imagining a good life, doubting whether having children is necessary, believing that not having children does not mean lack of happiness, and feeling freedom without children) and need for parenthood (10 items about considering having children as an important fact of life, anxiety about the future, emptiness in life, and the desire to become a parent). The Likert scale of this questionnaire ranges from 1 strongly disagree to 6 strongly agree, and the minimum and maximum scores are 46 and 276 [27]. Higher scores indicate higher stress, and the total stress score is the sum of the 5 dimensions. This questionnaire has been translated into several languages around the world and has validity and reliability [28–31]. In different studies, the internal consistency with the total Cronbach’s alpha and the dimensions of the questionnaire is above 0.8 and it is one of the most reliable and widely used tools to study stress in infertile people [28–31].

The perceived stress scale (PPS)

PPS was developed by Cohen et al. in 1993 [32]. This questionnaire examines people’s stress in various life situations and measures thoughts and feelings about reactions to stressful situations, control, coping and overcoming psychological pressure, and experienced stress The scale has three versions: the original 14-item version (PSS-14), a shorter 10-item version (PSS-10), and a brief 4-item version (PSS-4). It is based on a 5-point Likert scale with the following response options: 0 = never, 1 = low, 2 = average, 3 = high, and 4 = very high. The scale’s ICC and its Cronbach’s alpha in most studies have been reported to be more than 0.7 [33], and has a good validity [33, 34].

The perceived stress questionnaire (PSQ)

This 30-item questionnaire was developed by Levenstein et al. in 1993 [35], to assess perceived stress over the past four weeks in four dimensions of worries, tension, joy and demands. The questionnaire is based on a 4-point Likert scale including almost never = 1, sometimes = 2, often = 3, and usually = 4. An individual’s total stress is the sum of the four dimensions, with higher scores indicating greater stress [36]. The Cronbach’s alpha of this questionnaire has been reported in various studies to be between 0.8 and 0.94 and has a good validity [37–39].

The COMPI questionnaire

This questionnaire was developed by Schmidt in 2006 and the booklet contains several questions related to reproductive health, stress, social relations, coping, and well-being [40]. The stress-related part of the questionnaire includes 14 questions in three dimensions of personal stress (6 items), social stress (4 items), and marital stress (4 items). Other parts of the questionnaire include the COMPI Marital Benefit Measure (Partner communication and Infertility-related Communication Strategies), the COMPI Coping Strategy Scales, attitudes to treatment, and evaluation of treatment. Its Cronbach’s alpha for the dimensions and the whole questionnaire has been reported to be between 0.7 and 0.8 [41, 42].

The Depression, anxiety and stress scale (DASS)

The DASS was developed by Lovibond and Lovibond in 1995 [43]. This scale measures depression, anxiety, and stress in 21 questions (7 questions for each part) based on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from never (0) to almost always [3]. The 21-item questionnaire is a shortened version of the 42-item version, which is doubled after the questions are answered [44]. The scale has been translated to different languages its validity and reliability have been confirmed [45–47].

The psychological state of stress

This questionnaire was developed by Lemyre and Tessier in 1998 [48], to assess the degree to which subjects experience stress. Patients rate behavioral, cognitive, or affective signs of stress on the Specific Stress Symptom Scale using 27 questions on an 8-point Likert scale, with scores ranging from 27 to 216. This scale has validity and reliability [48, 49].

Specific stress symptom scale

The questionnaire was derived from the validated Copenhagen Psychosocial Questionnaire [50], which included 14 items related to psychological stress symptoms experienced during the previous 4 weeks. The 14 items relate to somatic (four items), behavioral (four items), burnout (three items), and cognitive stress symptoms (three items) [51]. The 5-point scale of the questionnaire includes all the time (100 points), most of the time (75 points), sometimes (50 points), rarely (25 points), and never (0 points). The overall stress score is the mean of all 14 questions and ranges from zero to 100, with higher scores indicating more stress. This questionnaire has validity and its Cronbach’s alpha and ICC have been reported above 0.8 [52].

The treatment related stress scale (TTS)

This scale was developed by Lee’s et al. in 2001 [53], to assess treatment-related stress with 13 questions on a 0–3 Likert scale with a score range of 0–39 and scores standardized to 100. The reliability and validity of the questionnaire have been confirmed [53, 54].

The Infertility-Related stress scale (IRSS)

Developed by Casu and Gremigni [55] in 2016, this scale examines infertility-related stress. This 12-item self-report scale measures the impact of infertility in the intrapersonal and interpersonal domains of life. The Likert scale of this questionnaire ranges from 1 (none at all) to 5 (a great deal), and the range of scores is from 12 to 60, with higher scores indicating more stress. This questionnaire has adequate construct validity and its Cronbach’s alpha is greater than 0.91 [56].

Data extraction

Considering the specific search strategy for each database, the search was performed by two reviewers independently and all articles were entered into the EndNote 7 software. EndNote 7 software was then used to remove duplicate items. The title, abstract and full text of the articles were screened by two reviewers independently based on the inclusion criteria, and then data extraction was performed. The information extracted by the reviewers was reviewed in an in-person meeting, and the agreement coefficient between them was k = 1. Required information such as the name of authors, year of publication, place of research, sample size, total score and the scores of stress dimensions, type of infertility, mean age, duration of infertility, and type of tool were extracted from the studies. The review stages are described in flowchart 1.

Quality assessment

The Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) tool for cross-sectional studies was used to assess the quality of the studies. This checklist included eight questions addressing clarity of inclusion criteria, identification and management of confounders, definition and measurement of variables, completeness of data, reporting of results, and relevance of results to the study objectives. Responses to the questions were “yes,” “no,” “Unclear”, “not applicable,” with higher scores indicating a lower risk of bias [57, 58]. Overall appraisal can be categorized into three sections: include, exclude, and seek further information. The quality assessment in this study was conducted independently by two reviewers (ZK and MK), and a third reviewer was consulted in case of disagreement. The inter-rater agreement coefficient between the reviewers was K = 0.90.

Statistical analysis

In this study, the I2 index was used to assess heterogeneity among studies. The Cohen’s d index was used as the main effect size to quantify the standardized mean difference between women and men. The Cohen’s d index is often used in meta-analyses because it provides a standardized measure of effect size that is independent of sample size and allows comparisons between studies of different scales. The Cohen’s d value is interpreted based on conventional thresholds: d = 0.2 is considered a small effect size, d = 0.5 is considered a medium effect size, and d = 0.8 is considered a large effect size [59]. Egger’s test was used to test for publication bias. Subgroup analysis was performed based on gender (women and men) and questionnaire dimensions, with the condition of at least 3 studies that would allow comparison of women and men. Analysis was performed using STATA, version 18.0. The significance level for statistical tests was set at 0.05.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

Ethics approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee, Faculty of Pharmacy and Nursing Midwifery, Shahid Beheshti University (Ethical code: IR.SBMU.PHARMACY.REC.1403.014). All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Results

The results of this study were represented in two parts of systematic review and meta-analysis.

The results of the systematic part

Of 42,951 primary articles, after excluding duplicate articles, the title and abstract of the articles were reviewed. After removing the articles unrelated to the purpose of our study and based on the inclusion criteria, the original text of 1,488 articles was further reviewed and then 53 articles were included in the study. All studies were of good quality and were included. Considering the possibility of conducting a meta-analysis and comparing the average stress difference between infertile women and men, 18 articles were used in the meta-analysis (Fig. 1).

Flowchart for selection of studies

Of the 53 studies reviewed, the largest number were conducted in China and Iran (seven studies each), followed by the United States and Italy (five studies each), and Canada (four studies). Thirty studies included both male and female participants, 22 studies focused exclusively on women, and one study focused exclusively on men. The total sample size of the studies was 21,355 infertile people of whom 12,913 were women and 8,442 were men. The smallest sample size was 27 for women and 26 for men, and the largest sample size was 1009 for women and 818 for men. Various standard tools had been used to check stress in infertile people (Table 1).

The results of the meta-analysis part

According to the results of the systematic part and the possibility to compare the mean difference between infertile women and men, the analysis was done only using the FPI tool and its dimensions. In the COMPI questionnaire only the average results of infertile women were reported.

FPI

A total of 18 studies were included in the meta-analysis comparing stress total score between infertile women and men using FPI tool. The pooled Cohen’s d in stress scores, based on a random-effects model using the REML method, was 0.33 (95%CI: 0.23–0.44, P < 0.001). The forest plot (Fig. 2) illustrates the individual study estimates and their confidence intervals. Most studies report a positive effect size favoring higher stress in women, and the overall summary estimate (green diamond) is clearly above zero. This indicates that infertile women report significantly higher stress levels than infertile men.

The overall difference in the mean stress score of infertile women and men

There was substantial heterogeneity across studies (I2 = 76.20%). This suggests that variation in effect sizes is more than would be expected by chance alone.

To assess the presence of potential publication bias, a funnel plot and Egger’s regression test were conducted. The funnel plot appears generally symmetrical, although a few smaller studies lie slightly outside the funnel (Fig. 3). Egger’s test showed (Intercept = 0.11, t = 0.78, P = 0.447). Since the p-value is not statistically significant, there is no evidence of funnel plot asymmetry, suggesting that publication bias is unlikely in this meta-analysis.

Discussion

This systematic review aimed for the first time to investigate how stress differs between infertile women and men. The results of this meta-analysis based on the FPI tool showed a significant difference between the stress of infertile women and that of infertile men, such that the stress of infertile women was greater than that of men.

Infertile women tend to experience higher levels of stress than infertile men due to a combination of biological, psychological, and social factors. Biologically, women are more affected by hormonal fluctuations and disorders, which can influence both their stress levels and fertility. Psychologically, women are more likely to experience anxiety, depression, and feelings of blame or guilt in relation to infertility. Socially, cultural expectations and pressures surrounding childbearing are often placed more heavily on women in most societies. As a result, the interplay of these factors leads infertile women to experience significantly greater stress compared to infertile men [97, 98].

Social concern, as part of infertility stress, is caused by the placement of women in social situations and verbal and nonverbal reprimands and comparisons. This social concern is associated with the social stigma of infertility attributed to a woman [24, 99, 100], and is not specific to developing societies [101]. The pressure caused by these social stresses may be such that it leads to social withdrawal [72, 102]. Infertility is also a stigma for men [103], leading to social concern for men as well. However, as the present meta-analysis showed, the social concern of infertile women seems to be higher than that of men, because in addition to social expectations and labels, part of the social concern is related to women’s own beliefs, as they believe that the responsibility for fertility is more related to them. Accordingly, more psychological consequences will be imposed on women [24].

Another part of infertility stress is related to sexual concerns, because infertility affects women’s sexual function. Thus, this concern leads to disturbances in lubrication, orgasm, and dyspareunia [104, 105]. Infertility also has a negative impact on men’s sexual function [106], and the sexual problems of infertile couples increase their psychological distress [107]. Meanwhile, infertile women are doubly concerned as they may think that they are no longer sexually attractive to their husbands [27].

Infertility not only affects the sexual relationship, but it can also affect the emotional relationship of couples and cause relationship concerns. Among the relationship concerns, we can mention the inability of couples to talk about infertility, the lack of understanding and acceptance of gender differences, the feeling of losing one’s partner, and the feeling of being disappointed in him or her [27]. Although this factor was not significant between women and men in the present study, more relationship concern was observed in women, which may be related to the difference in attachment style reported in infertile women and men, which also affects relationship expectations [41].

Rejection of the child-free lifestyle and the need for parenthood have been expressed as predictors of marital satisfaction in infertile couples [108]. In other words, not having a child is one of the factors that makes the relationship negative and worrisome. In this regard, Chehreh et al., in line with the results of the present meta-analysis, reported that the mean concern score for not accepting the child-free lifestyle and the need for parenthood was higher in women than in men [108]. This may be due to the fact that women consider having children as a factor that stabilizes and consolidates married life, and therefore infertility creates more psychological anxiety for them [109].

Infertility stress is related to factors such as social concern, sexual concern, relationship concern, need for parenthood, and not accepting a child-free lifestyle [27]. Although infertile men also experience significant levels of stress [6], studies have paid more attention to the psychological distress of the women [110].

The psychological stress of infertility in men has a profound impact on their mental health and quality of life. Biologically, infertility-related stress disrupts the hypothalamus-gonadal hormonal axis and increases cortisol levels, leading to decreased testosterone and impaired sperm production [51]. Chronic stress further reduces sperm quality, count, and motility, while increasing the number of abnormal sperm [111, 112].

Social and familial pressures related to parenthood can threaten male identity, often resulting in sexual disorders such as erectile dysfunction and decreased libido [113]. Additionally, social and cultural factors-such as feelings of guilt, lowered self-esteem, and fear of judgment by others-intensify stress in infertile men [114].

These psychological and social pressures can strain marital relationships by reducing intimacy and increasing tension, creating a vicious cycle of stress and infertility that complicates treatment and recovery [114]. Men often face stigma and may struggle to express their emotions due to societal expectations of stoicism, which can worsen their psychological distress and hinder seeking support [103].

Overall, infertility in men is associated with significant emotional challenges including anxiety, depression, lowered self-esteem, and relationship difficulties, underscoring the need for comprehensive care that addresses both medical and psychological aspects [115, 116].

Although infertile men are also affected by psychological stress, such as the burden of the exorbitant cost of infertility treatments [117], in general, based on the combination of studies in the present analysis, the stress of infertility is more significant for women than for men in all dimensions of the instrument used. In addition to these concerns, there is no denying the impact of other psychological distresses, such as the feminization of infertility, i.e., the assumption that only women are responsible for fertility and that femininity is synonymous with motherhood [24, 109].

The findings indicate that while infertile women generally experience higher levels of stress than men, certain aspects of infertility-related stress-specifically concerns about relationships and negative views of a child-free lifestyle-may be similar for both genders. This similarity likely arises because infertility impacts the couple as a unit, leading both partners to share worries about their relationship and future family plans. Although men and women may use different coping strategies or focus on different stressors, they can experience comparable concerns regarding relationship stability and the consequences of living without children. Therefore, the absence of significant gender differences in these subscales reflects the shared impact of infertility on couples’ relationships and future outlooks, underscoring the importance of addressing these issues jointly in clinical practice [118–120].

The difference in infertility stress between developed and developing countries is largely due to cultural, social, and healthcare access factors. In developed countries, infertility treatment is more common because of better access to health services. Individuals in these countries receive more support and face less social stigma, resulting in lower levels of stress [121].

In contrast, in developing countries, the identity and marital challenges caused by infertility are more profound. Social stigma and family and societal expectations are much greater, particularly for women. Concerns about the need to become parents, social anxiety, and relationship stress are intensified by strong cultural and social norms that place high value on childbearing [122–124].

As a result, people in developing countries experience higher infertility-related stress, especially in domains such as need for parenthood, social concern, and relationship concern. These differences highlight the importance of addressing cultural attitudes and improving access to infertility care and psychosocial support in developing countries.

This study was conducted to highlight the psychological distress of infertile women and men, so that more attention is paid to their psychological dimension, because reducing psychological distress improves quality of life and also improves treatment outcome. While IRSS and FPI are robust for assessing infertility-related stress, future research should address cross-cultural factor variability and strengthen methodological rigor. Combining specialized and generic tools could enhance assessment precision in both clinical and research settings.

In this meta-analysis, the heterogeneity among studies was found to be substantial. The I² statistic represents the percentage of the total variation across studies that is due to heterogeneity rather than chance. Although all included studies assessed stress and its dimensions using the same instrument and same study designs, several factors could still contribute to this level of heterogeneity. The studies have included participants from different cultural backgrounds, socioeconomic contexts, or healthcare settings, which can influence stress perception and reporting. Stress levels can vary depending on how long individuals have been trying to conceive or what type of infertility they are experiencing. Despite the high I² value, the direction of effect across studies was consistent, with most studies showing higher stress in women. Thus, while statistical heterogeneity was present, the consistency in direction and use of the same instrument supports the validity of the pooled effect estimate.

Limitation

One limitation of the present study was the exclusion of studies that used different tools to measure stress, which made it impossible to combine all studies in a meta-analysis. Despite this, the meta-analysis was conducted using the valid and specific Fertility Problem Inventory (FPI) stress measurement tool and included an optimal number of studies (18 studies). Another limitation is that we only included articles published in English due to our limited proficiency in other languages.

Strengths

One of the main strengths of this study is that it is the first systematic review and meta-analysis to specifically examine infertility-related stress with a focus on gender differences. Other strengths of this study was the investigation of the psychological aspects of infertility, especially stress, with the aim of paying special attention to it in treatment and follow-up.

Conclusion

The meta-analysis results indicated that infertile women experience higher levels of stress compared to men. However, since infertility treatment inherently involves both partners, it is essential to consider women and men together and develop tailored stress reduction programs for each within infertility treatment clinics. Recognizing the differences in stress levels between women and men and understanding their underlying causes is crucial for designing appropriate interventions. Given the high prevalence of stress associated with infertility, community-based education, advocacy, and policymaking should also be prioritized to provide comprehensive support for affected couple.
