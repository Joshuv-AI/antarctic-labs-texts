Sentinel-2based dataset covering Indian regions for road detection: A dataset for infrastructure monitoring
Authors: Avishkar Karnik, Shilpa Pimpalkar, Manjusree Panchagnula, Subrat K Acharya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRoad extraction from satellite imagery plays a vital role in infrastructure monitoring applications such as transportation planning, autonomous navigation and disaster response. Unlike very high-resolution (VHR) imagery, Sentinel-2 data is publicly available, thereby being a strong contender for remote sensing problems such as road extraction. We present the Sentinel-2 Roads Dataset, a publicly available, open-access dataset designed for road segmentation tasks using Sentinel-2 satellite imagery. The dataset consists of Sentinel-2 images at 10 m spatial resolution acquired from seven diverse regions across India, covering urban, rural, and mountainous terrains. The corresponding binary road masks were generated using OpenStreetMap (OSM) data and were rasterized using QGIS software and Rasterio library. The dataset also offer a set of enhanced version of the Sentinel-2 images, which incorporates techniques such as gamma correction and CLAHE, for improved road visibility. For ease of reuse in different deep learning frameworks, the dataset is made available in TIF as well as PNG format. The dataset contains a total of 5634 high-quality samples, each of 256 × 256 pixels, obtained after manual refinement. As a baseline, several deep learning segmentation models were trained on both raw and enhanced images, which demonstrated that preprocessing techniques significantly improved segmentation performance . The proposed dataset serves as a valuable resource for computer vision and remote sensing researchers, offering a benchmark for road extraction tasks. It is publicly available for further research and development in road segmentation models using satellite imagery.Keywords: Binary mask, Road detection, Sentinel-2, Segmentation

Background

Road detection using remote sensing is having significant importance in city planning, disaster management, military surveillance, and defence. Sentinel-2 is very important data source due to its ability to provide global coverage, high resolution, and multi-spectral satellite imagery. Sentinel-2 supports road extraction through its relatively high spatial resolution and multispectral imaging capabilities, which help distinguish roads from surrounding land features. Its visible and near-infrared bands are particularly useful for identifying road surfaces, especially when leveraged with image processing and machine learning methods for improved accuracy. With reference to our recent finding [1], very few papers used Sentinel-2 as dataset for road extraction. Sentinel-2 annotated dataset for road extraction is not available for the researchers. Literature survey of 22 research papers included in this study on road extraction highlighted usage of Cartosat dataset [2,3] with a spatial resolution of <1 m, Sentinel [1] with a spatial resolution of 10 m, Deepglobe dataset [4] and Massachusetts Roads dataset [5] with spatial resolution of 0.5 m - 1 m. K. Suvarna Vani et al. [3] and C. Leena et al. [2] conducted significant studies leveraging Cartosat data. Y. Jia et al. [6] and C. Ayala [7] utilized Sentinel data in their respective research. Major existing research [[8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16]] collectively demonstrate the Massachusetts dataset's usage. Deepglobe dataset with detailed semantic annotations, making it ideal for training and testing deep learning models in complex environments presented in few findings [[17], [18], [19], [20], [21]]. G. Wang et.al. [22] and Chen et. al. [23] presented findings using both Massachusetts and Deepglobe dataset [4,5]. There are other findings which used Aerial and VHR imagery (spatial resolution 0.5 m - 1 m) [15,24], CasNet dataset (spatial resolution 0.8 m) [25], RoadTracer dataset (spatial resolution 0.6 m) [26], Diverse images captured using Google Earth Engine (having spatial resolution of 10m-30 m) [6,27].

The proposed research addresses the above mentioned gaps and presents systematic approach for dataset development. The significant outcome of the research is (i) open access Sentinel-2 dataset for road extraction (ii) dataset with 5634 images in .TIF format and dataset with 5634 images in .PNG format along with corresponding binary masks.

In addition to well-established datasets such as Massachusetts Roads dataset and DeepGlobe dataset, several recent benchmarks have incorporated OSM data for large-scale road and land-cover mapping. The OpenEarthMap [28] series provides a global very high-resolution (VHR) image dataset with manually annotated labels to support semantic segmentation across multiple geographies. OpenMapCD [29] focuses on change detection tasks and leverages multi-temporal imagery and OSM annotations to highlight the importance of temporal consistency in open-sourced data. The BRIGHT dataset [30] integrates Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) imagery and OSM to provide high-resolution building annotations on a global scale. While these datasets emphasize VHR imagery, imagery with spatial resolution of less than one meter and large-scale change detection, the Sentinel-2 Roads dataset fills a crucial gap by systematically pairing medium-resolution imagery with OSM masks across diverse Indian terrain.

The Sentinel-2 Roads dataset is created to provide an open, easily-accessible remote sensing resource for road extraction. This dataset aims to provide a ready-to-use dataset consisting of Sentinel-2 satellite images paired with corresponding binary road masks. These images and masks are available in both TIF and PNG formats, offering flexibility for a wide range of remote sensing applications. This dataset is especially valuable for deep learning and machine learning practitioners focused on remote sensing, as it opens up new avenues for training models with Sentinel-2 data. Furthermore, this research highlights how enhancing the original satellite images with pre-processing techniques, such as CLAHE and gamma correction, can significantly improve the performance of machine learning models in road extraction tasks.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

A systematic approach was adopted for the development of a robust dataset aimed at road extraction using deep learning techniques such as U-Net,Residual U-Net and Attention-Based U-Net. The process commenced with data collection, wherein Sentinel-2 images were acquired, and precise binary masks were generated to annotate road networks. Subsequent data processing involved image enhancement techniques such as resizing, normalization, and augmentation to ensure consistency and improve model learning. A thorough data filtering phase was implemented to eliminate low-quality, corrupted, or irrelevant samples, thereby refining the dataset for optimal performance. This was followed by a clear data description, organizing the dataset into structured directories containing paired image and mask files, divided into training, validation, and testing sets to support supervised learning. The proposed dataset specification further outlined key characteristics, including the type of imagery used, data collection methods, source locations, and data accessibility. This carefully curated and pre-processed dataset serves as a reliable foundation for training U-Net models, enabling accurate and efficient road segmentation. Its utility spans across critical applications such as urban planning, autonomous navigation, infrastructure monitoring, and geographic information systems (GIS), where precise road extraction is vital for decision-making and automation. Data collection

This section presents the details about method followed for acquiring images and corresponding binary masks.

Images

The images were acquired from seven areas of interest in India. The areas under consideration were the cities of Bengaluru (12.9716 ° N, 77.5946 ° E), Delhi (28.7041 ° N, 77.1025 ° E), Lucknow (26.8467 ° N, 80.9462 ° E), Pune (18.5204 ° N, 73.8567 ° E), Warangal (17.9693 ° N, 79.5926 ° E), Nagpur (21.1458 ° N, 79.0882 ° E), and the state of Gujarat (22.6708° N, 71.5724 ° E). The areas were chosen with the goal of making the dataset as diverse as possible, by balancing rural and urban regions, thereby incorporating various road types with different densities and sizes. Geographical diversity was also considered by ensuring the inclusion of hilly regions, water bodies and forest areas.

The shape files for the selected areas were obtained using GeoJson.io, which is an open-source web tool used for creating, editing and sharing spatial data. Then, entire Sentinel-2 scenes of 10 m spatial resolution of these shape files were acquired in TIF format using Google Earth Engine. The steps followed for the same are given in Algorithm 1. These Sentinel-2 scenes were then used for further processing.

Masks

Binary road masks for the areas of interest were obtained using the OpenStreetMap (OSM) plugin in QGIS. This plugin allows users to interact with OpenStreetMap data directly within QGIS. The rasterio library was used to rasterise the OSM data of the areas of interest and create road masks for the same. The generated road masks were binary, where white pixels represented roads and black pixels indicated non-road areas. The road masks acquired were in TIF format.

While generating masks from OSM data, potential spatial misalignments were accounted for by ensuring that both Sentinel-2 imagery and OSM vector data were projected into the same coordinate reference system (CRS). Rasterization was performed using the exact affine transform of the Sentinel-2 raster to minimize georeferencing offsets. To address temporal discrepancies between imagery acquisition and OSM updates, a manual inspection of all the samples was conducted and the masks with evident mismatches (for example, missing newly constructed roads or presence of roads no longer visible) were discarded. Thus, the final dataset ensured consistency between the Sentinel-2 imagery and the corresponding road masks. The steps for creation of road masks are given in Algorithm 2.

Data processing

The acquired Sentinel-2 scenes and their corresponding masks covered entire cities. Therefore, these large images were tiled into smaller, equally sized patches. For ease of use and implementation in machine learning applications, the Sentinel-2 scenes and their masks were divided into identical patches each of size 256 × 256 pixels. The patches were numbered sequentially. Each number representing an image patch matched with the corresponding mask patch number.

Although instances of TIF images being used as inputs and outputs is seen in remote sensing literature [32], the PNG format is often preferred over TIF. This is because PNG uses lossless compression which is more efficient for storage and at the same time maintains image quality. This reduces the storage requirements which is beneficial as deep learning models often work with large datasets. Moreover, PNG is universally supported across image processing libraries, deep learning frameworks, and web applications, and thus ensures smooth data handling. Thus, image patches as well as mask patches which were in TIF format were converted to PNG format, thereby supporting remote sensing applications which require larger inputs and want the processes to be less computationally intensive.

Studies [[33], [34]] have shown that preprocessing input images enhances model performance and improves accuracy. Image augmentation plays an important role in any segmentation task, as discussed by Nesteruk et al. [35]. In raw Sentinel-2 imagery, roads can sometimes blend in with the background, especially in darker regions. For road extraction tasks, it is necessary that the roads are distinguishable and clearly visible. Thus, techniques such as normalisation, gamma correction and Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE) were introduced to improve the road visibility in Sentinel-2 images, which should lead to better deep learning model performance (as discussed in Section 4.4 Utility of Dataset). In image enhancement pipeline, the first step was normalisation. Normalisation helps standardise intensity values, thereby reducing dataset inconsistencies.

where, xnormaliseddenotes the normalised value, xoriginal denotes the original value, xmin denotes the minimum value in the dataset and xmax denotes the maximum value in the dataset.

Gamma correction with a gamma value of γ = 0.4 was also applied. Gamma correction helps in enhancing darker regions in the image, thereby helping the machine learning models learn roads better in shadowed or low-contrast areas. Finally, CLAHE technique was applied per channel to the RGB images. CLAHE improves the local contrast in the image, which helps making the road boundaries sharper and more distinguishable. The enhanced images were converted to 8-bit PNG format, scaling pixel values to [0255] for compatibility with deep learning frameworks. Once all image and mask patches (TIF, PNG, and enhanced PNG) were prepared, they were organized into separate folders. The resulting dataset contained a total of 10,640 patches (each of size 256 × 256 pixels) per folder. This initial dataset was then subjected to manual inspection.

Data filtering

It is necessary for the dataset to be cleaned and to discard any unwanted and unnecessary entries. This step is crucial for achieving accurate model predictions, as model training highly depends on the quality of dataset [31]. The 10,640 samples acquired were manually reviewed and only the high-quality samples were retained. Some of the unwanted samples which were discarded are shown in Fig. 5. This included entries with incomplete or incorrect road masks.

Some of the discarded samples and the reason for their rejection.

A specific challenge during data curation was the presence of narrow roads in OSM annotations that were not visually detectable in Sentinel-2 imagery due to the 10 m spatial resolution. A width threshold of two pixels was applied during rasterization and samples where OSM-indicated roads were completely absent in the imagery were manually discarded. The dataset emphasizes major and clearly identifiable roads, improving its reliability for medium-resolution road extraction tasks.

After manual inspection and discarding of unwanted samples, the cleaned dataset contained a total of 5634 image patches and their corresponding mask patches. These samples were organised in their respective folders and the folders were compressed in ZIP format to reduce their storage size. This structured organization facilitates easy reuse by researchers testing deep learning models or developing new road extraction techniques. Some samples from the five folders are shown in Fig. 6.

Utility of dataset

To establish a baseline for the proposed dataset, an evaluation was conducted to assess the performance of multiple representative models. U-Net has proved to be an excellent baseline deep learning model for any image-related computer vision task for several years [8,22]. In addition to U-Net, recent and classic deep learning segmentation architectures specialized for road mapping such as Deep Residual U-Net [11], Deeplabv3+ [36], D-LinkNet [17], Rse-net [20], and CADUNet [8]. These models were selected as they cover both classical and recent approaches in semantic segmentation and road extraction, thereby providing a meaningful benchmark for future research. The dataset was divided into training, validation, and testing subsets, following an 80–20 train–test split and a subsequent 80–20 split of the training data into train and validation sets, resulting in 3605 training, 902 validation, and 1127 test samples. To maintain the dataset’s integrity as a benchmark, no preprocessing or postprocessing operations were applied beyond the enhancement procedure described earlier. All models were trained using the Adam optimiser with BCE-Dice loss, since Sentinel-2 road extraction is a problem depicting heavy class imbalance. A learning rate scheduler callback was employed to adjust the learning rate dynamically based on the validation F1 score. All the models were trained for 50 epochs, with a batch size of 32.

The aim of this demonstration was to compare the results of models trained on normal images as input, with the models trained on enhanced images as input, thereby highlighting the importance of data processing and image enhancement techniques. The model was trained twice, once on normal PNG training image samples without the enhancement, and then a second time on enhanced PNG training image samples. The results show that overall accuracy (OA) across all the models is consistently very high (greater than 0.99), while precision, recall, and F1-scores are more modest (approximately 0.40–0.55). This apparent disparity can be explained by the strong class imbalance in road segmentation, especially for Sentinel-2 imagery. Since Sentinel-2 imagery has a spatial resolution of 10 m, road pixels constitute only a small fraction of the image area and are observed to be around 1–2 pixels wide only. Thus, correctly classifying the dominant background pixels inflates overall accuracy, whereas metrics such as F1-score provide a more realistic measure of model performance. The heavy class imbalance (roads covering less than 5 % pixels per image tile) explains the high OA of 0.99 despite moderate F1 values. These findings highlight the necessity of reporting multiple evaluation metrics for class-imbalanced problems like road extraction. Moreover, the performance improvements observed when training with enhanced images, although numerically modest, consistently demonstrate the importance of preprocessing in improving model robustness. It should be noted that although recent studies show that techniques such as super resolution [6] and teacher-student models [37] can improve Sentinel-2 road extraction results to a range of 0.6–0.8 (depending on setting), no such pre-processing or post-processing techniques were carried out and vanilla implementation of the models were used in order to set a baseline for the dataset. Furthermore, it should also be noted that no data augmentation such as flips or rotation of images was carried out.

Table 1 confirms that training with enhanced images improves prediction results. In some cases such as Deeplabv3+, precision marginally decreases while recall improves, leading to overall F1 improvement. Enhancement and data preprocessing steps are crucial for any computer vision related task. Thus, an enhanced version of the original images is provided along with the original images, as a ready-to-use quick solution for improved road extraction and classification tasks.

Limitations

The dataset is constrained to specific geographical conditions within India, which may limit the generalizability of models to other regions. Although dataset covers as many diverse conditions and terrains as possible, the dataset can benefit from inclusion of regions from developed countries with more intricate road networks. Additionally, it should be noted that the dataset primarily focuses on the mapping of major roads, as the 10 m spatial resolution of Sentinel-2 imagery proves to be a challenge for detecting narrow roads such as alleyways. Narrow roads, alleyways, and footpaths are often below the detectability limit of Sentinel-2 imagery and hence are not comprehensively represented in the dataset. The dataset’s primary focus remains on capturing major and medium-scale roads, which align with the 10 m resolution capabilities of Sentinel-2. This dataset only provides binary road masks (road or non-road). The research can be extended to classify the roads based on different road types such as highways, streets, dirt roads, etc. for a more detailed road network analysis. Although Sentinel-2 imagery provides 13 spectral bands, the dataset released in this work is restricted to the RGB and NIR bands (B4, B3, B2, B8). This decision was made to ensure compatibility with standard deep learning pipelines, reduce storage requirements, and facilitate easy adoption by the remote sensing and computer vision research communities. The authors acknowledge that additional bands could provide complementary information which can further benefit road mapping tasks. Evaluating the relative contribution of individual bands and extending the dataset to multispectral inputs can prove to be a valuable contribution to future research.
