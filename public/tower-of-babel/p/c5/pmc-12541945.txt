Inflammatory biomarkers in alcohol use disorder: neutrophil/lymphocyte and monocyte/HDL-C ratios as potential diagnostic indicators
Authors: Omer Acat, Okan Imre
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlcohol use disorder (AUD) constitutes a major global public health concern due to its widespread biological, psychological, and social consequences. Although several biochemical markers—such as gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), carbohydrate-deficient transferrin (CDT), and mean corpuscular volume (MCV)—have been proposed for AUD diagnosis, their limited specificity can lead to diagnostic inaccuracies. This study investigates the relationship between AUD and novel inflammatory markers, including the monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), neutrophil-to-HDL cholesterol ratio (NHR), and monocyte-to-HDL cholesterol ratio (MHR).MethodsThis cross-sectional case–control study included 84 patients with clinically diagnosed AUD and 50 healthy controls (HC). The AUD group was subdivided into two categories: those with elevated liver enzyme levels (E-AUD) and those with normal liver function (NE-AUD). All participants underwent complete blood count and HDL-C measurement. Based on these values, NLR, MLR, NHR, and MHR were calculated. Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA and Kruskal–Wallis tests with SPSS version 25.0.ResultsNo significant differences were observed in neutrophil, monocyte, HDL-C, NLR, MHR, and NHR levels among the three groups (p > 0.05). However, HDL-C and NLR levels were significantly higher in the E-AUD group compared to controls. In the NE-AUD group, neutrophil, monocyte, and MHR levels were significantly elevated, despite normal liver enzyme profiles. These findings indicate early inflammatory activation in AUD patients without biochemical evidence of liver dysfunction.ConclusionsNeutrophil, monocyte, HDL-C, NLR, and MHR levels differ between individuals with AUD and healthy subjects. The elevated inflammatory markers in the NE-AUD group suggest their potential utility in early AUD detection. In contrast, increased HDL-C and NLR in the E-AUD group may reflect advanced disease. Further prospective studies are needed to validate these findings.

Background

Alcohol consumption is acknowledged globally as a significant public health concern due to its broad-spectrum biological, psychological, and societal effects. It negatively influences not only an individual’s physical health, but also their mental stability, interpersonal relationships, and overall life satisfaction. The harmful impact of alcohol is particularly prominent in critical organs like the brain, liver, and heart, resulting in multisystem dysfunction and a shortened lifespan [1–4]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), alcohol use accounted for nearly 3 million deaths worldwide in 2016 [5, 6]. When indirect causes—such as fatalities from accidents, suicides, and alcohol-related behavioral disorders—are taken into consideration, the actual mortality burden is presumed to be considerably greater [7].

Clinical studies have extensively documented the link between alcohol consumption and various mental and behavioral disorders [8]. Alcohol exerts direct neurotoxic effects on the central nervous system, impacting brain function beyond temporary cognitive disruptions [9]. These effects may contribute to lasting neurocognitive deficits over time. Additionally, alcohol use can trigger the development of psychiatric conditions such as depression, bipolar disorder, and psychosis, while also worsening the progression of existing mental health disorders [10–12]. Apart from its physiological and psychological consequences, alcohol consumption carries significant societal ramifications. It has been correlated with increased incidents of violence, family conflicts, and severe criminal activities, including acts of homicide [13, 14].

Given these widespread impacts, the diagnosis and timely treatment of alcohol use disorder (AUD) are critically important. However, current diagnostic approaches predominantly rely on clinical evaluation and patient self-reporting. Many individuals who consume alcohol fail to recognize or admit to having a problem and may consequently provide incomplete or misleading information during medical history-taking [15, 16]. This highlights the necessity of incorporating objective biomarkers into the diagnostic process. Although several biochemical parameters—such as gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), carbohydrate-deficient transferrin (CDT), and mean corpuscular volume (MCV)—have been proposed as supportive indicators in the diagnosis of AUD, these markers lack sufficient specificity and are prone to producing both false-positive and false-negative results [17].

At this point, evaluating alcohol-induced inflammatory processes through biochemical approaches has emerged as a promising strategy for both diagnostic and monitoring purposes. A substantial body of research indicates that alcohol consumption intensifies systemic inflammation and induces cellular-level changes within the immune system [18, 19]. In recent years, attention has turned to innovative inflammatory markers obtained from peripheral blood measurements. Among these, indices such as the monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), neutrophil-to-HDL cholesterol ratio (NHR), and monocyte-to-HDL cholesterol ratio (MHR) have shown correlations with inflammatory burden and prognostic outcomes across a range of clinical conditions [20–22]. Recent studies have further suggested that these indices may also hold diagnostic significance in diverse clinical contexts, including cardiovascular and hepatic diseases [23, 24].

Despite increasing attention, the relationship between these inflammatory indices and alcohol use disorder (AUD) has not been thoroughly investigated. Only a limited number of studies have specifically examined how markers such as the monocyte-to-HDL cholesterol ratio (MHR) are altered in individuals with AUD, reporting significant associations with subclinical inflammation in this population [25]. More broadly, reviews of alcohol-related biomarkers have highlighted the scarcity of evidence regarding the diagnostic and prognostic role of such indices in AUD and underscored the need for further focused research [26]. Accordingly, future studies evaluating markers such as the monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), neutrophil-to-HDL cholesterol ratio (NHR), and MHR in the context of AUD are warranted. Specifically, individuals diagnosed with AUD can be categorized into two subgroups based on liver function tests: those with elevated liver enzymes (ALT or AST) constitute the “Elevated” (E-AUD) group, while those with normal ALT and AST levels comprise the “Non-Elevated” (NE-AUD) group. This distinction is important for understanding how the effects of alcohol on inflammatory biomarkers may differ in the presence or absence of liver injury.

In this study, we investigated potential differences in MLR, NLR, NHR, and MHR values between individuals diagnosed with AUD and healthy controls, while also assessing the diagnostic relevance of these biomarkers by examining their variation across the E-AUD and NE-AUD subgroups. In addition, we explored whether these inflammatory parameters could serve as early diagnostic indicators of AUD, preceding the development of overt hepatic impairment. Distinct from prior studies, our work systematically evaluates not only the classical inflammatory indices (NLR, MLR), but also the more recently proposed HDL-based ratios (MHR, NHR) within AUD subgroups stratified by liver function status (E-AUD vs. NE-AUD). This comprehensive approach emphasizes the novelty of our study design and its potential to contribute meaningfully to the existing body of literature.

Methods

A case–control approach was utilized in this study to investigate the profiles of inflammatory biomarkers among individuals diagnosed with alcohol use disorder (AUD). Ethical approval for the study was granted by the Ethics Committee of Karamanoğlu Mehmetbey University, and all required institutional authorizations were obtained prior to data collection (Approval No: 05-2024/06). In accordance with ethical guidelines, all participants provided written informed consent, and the research adhered to the principles set forth in the Declaration of Helsinki, as revised in 2013.

The patient cohort included individuals aged 18–65 years admitted to Karaman Training and Research Hospital who had been clinically diagnosed with alcohol use disorder (AUD) and were hospitalized for the purpose of treatment planning. These patients were further stratified into two subgroups: one with elevated liver enzyme levels (E-AUD) and another with non-elevated liver function tests (NE-AUD). The control group (HC) consisted of healthy volunteers without a known history of alcohol use, who were undergoing routine pre-employment health evaluations. The diagnosis of AUD in the study was established solely based on clinical examination and DSM-5 criteria.

Prior to inclusion in the study, individuals with endocrine disorders, cardiovascular diseases, inflammatory conditions, neurological disorders, hyperlipidemia, metabolic syndrome, chronic systemic diseases other than liver pathology, known psychiatric disorders, and regular medication use (e.g., anti-inflammatory or immunomodulatory therapies), inability to cooperate, or lack of informed consent were excluded due to the presence of various systemic or localized health issues. Participants with severe cognitive impairment were also not enrolled. These stringent exclusion criteria were implemented to enhance the reliability and specificity of the study outcomes. After an overnight fasting period of approximately eight hours, venous blood samples were collected under standardized conditions. For serum samples, venous blood was allowed to clot for 30 min at room temperature and then centrifuged at 1800 × g for 10 min at 8 °C. Following serum separation, routine biochemical analyses, including aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), were immediately performed. Commercial kits were used, and analyses were conducted with a Beckman Coulter AU5800 auto-analyzer (Beckman Coulter, Indianapolis, IN, USA). Complete blood counts were performed using samples drawn into EDTA tubes and analyzed with an automated hematology analyzer (Beckman Coulter UniCel DxH 800, Beckman Coulter, Miami, FL, USA). Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software, version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). For continuous variables, descriptive statistics were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or as median with minimum and maximum values, depending on data distribution. Categorical variables were summarized using frequency counts and percentages. The distributional characteristics of continuous variables were examined through Q–Q plot evaluations and supported by skewness and kurtosis metrics to assess normality.

Group comparisons for categorical variables were carried out using the Chi-square (χ2) test. For continuous data, the selection of statistical tests was based on distribution properties. The Independent Samples t-test was utilized for comparing two groups when data exhibited normal distribution. In analyses involving three or more groups, one-way ANOVA was applied to normally distributed variables, whereas the Kruskal–Wallis test was used when normality assumptions were not met. For post hoc evaluations, Tukey’s test was employed in cases of equal variances, while the Games–Howell procedure was preferred when variances were unequal. For non-normally distributed pairwise comparisons, Dunn’s test was applied. Correlation tests were used to determine the relationship between continuous variables. The interpretation of correlation coefficients was as follows: weak = 0.01–0.49; moderate = 0.50–0.69; strong = 0.70–1.00 [27]. To assess the ability of inflammatory markers to differentiate between the E-AUD and NE-AUD subgroups, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted, with sensitivity, specificity, area under the curve (AUC), and balanced accuracy ((sensitivity + specificity)/2) values calculated. A significance threshold of p < 0.05 was adopted for all statistical assessments.

Post hoc power calculations

According to the maximum MHR results, a post hoc power analysis revealed that the statistical power of the study was calculated as 0.835. In this analysis, the effect size was determined to be 0.2835, and the significance level (α error probability) was set at 0.05. These findings indicate that the statistical test had sufficient power (83.5%) to detect a medium-sized effect based on the MHR outcomes.

Discussion

The findings of this study indicate that the inflammatory response is affected not only in the E-AUD group (elevated liver function tests in alcohol use disorder), but also in the NE-AUD group (non-elevated liver function tests in alcohol use disorder). This suggests that systemic inflammation may already be active during the early stages of alcohol use disorder (AUD), even when liver function tests remain within non-elevated ranges and the disease presents with no overt clinical symptoms. The observed changes in inflammatory markers—particularly HDL-C, NLR, neutrophil, monocyte, and MHR—appear to follow distinct patterns across AUD subgroups, potentially reflecting different phases or severities of the disorder.

The present findings indicate that both HDL-C and NLR levels were significantly elevated in individuals within the E-AUD subgroup when compared to healthy controls (HC). This pattern of increase is indicative of an enhanced systemic inflammatory state, which may reflect a more progressed stage of alcohol use disorder, potentially characterized by hepatic involvement. In light of these observations, HDL-C and NLR may serve as auxiliary biomarkers, contributing to the diagnostic evaluation of AUD in clinical settings.

Conversely, when comparing the NE-AUD subgroup to healthy controls (HC), significantly elevated levels of neutrophils, monocytes, and MHR were observed in the NE-AUD subgroup, despite the absence of liver enzyme abnormalities. This observation implies that systemic inflammation may be initiated in the early phases of alcohol use disorder, prior to the manifestation of overt hepatic dysfunction. Therefore, these inflammatory markers may hold promise as early diagnostic indicators, particularly in cases where routine liver function tests remain within reference limits.

Correlation analysis indicated that AST levels were modestly associated with inflammatory indices, whereas ALT showed no significant relationships. Specifically, the positive association between AST and NLR may reflect systemic immune activation accompanying early hepatic stress in individuals with AUD, while the inverse association between AST and MHR could be related to immunometabolic pathways in which reduced HDL-C activity and monocyte-driven inflammation contribute to hepatic vulnerability. These observations are consistent with recent studies reporting associations between inflammatory ratios such as NLR and MLR and transaminase levels in AUD populations [28], while inverse relationships between MHR and inflammatory pathophysiology have also been described in cardiovascular and metabolic disorders [25, 29]. Overall, these findings indicate that inflammatory indices may complement traditional liver enzymes in characterizing systemic alterations in AUD, although further studies are needed to clarify their clinical significance.

A direct comparison between the E-AUD and NE-AUD groups revealed statistically significant differences in several key biomarkers. In particular, the E-AUD group exhibited higher levels of high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) and lower levels of the monocyte-to-HDL-C ratio (MHR) when compared to the NE-AUD group. These biomarker variations may serve as valuable indirect indicators in the diagnostic process, especially in clinical scenarios where patients are unwilling or unable to accurately report their alcohol consumption. Such situations may involve individuals who minimize or deny their alcohol use, those who lack sufficient insight into the severity of their condition, or individuals who self-identify solely as casual or social drinkers. In these cases, objective biochemical differences such as those observed in HDL-C and MHR values may offer clinicians additional evidence to support early identification and appropriate intervention.

The present study also highlighted notable outcomes related to liver function tests, which remain fundamental in the clinical assessment of alcohol-associated conditions. Core hepatic enzymes—including aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT)—were significantly elevated in individuals diagnosed with alcohol use disorder (AUD), aligning with prior reports in the literature [30, 31]. Given the liver’s pivotal role in ethanol metabolism and detoxification, these elevations likely reflect both physiological adaptation and biochemical stress resulting from chronic alcohol exposure. Notably, in the NE-AUD group, enzyme levels—although within accepted clinical limits—were still elevated relative to those of the healthy control group. This finding suggests the presence of mild, subclinical hepatic stress that precedes overt biochemical evidence of liver injury. Mechanistically, ethanol is metabolized into acetaldehyde, a toxic and reactive intermediate that induces oxidative stress, ultimately promoting lipopolysaccharide (LPS) accumulation and initiating hepatocellular damage at the cellular level [32].

With regard to hematological parameters, our analysis revealed notable differences in neutrophil and monocyte counts among the study groups. Both the E-AUD and NE-AUD groups demonstrated elevated levels of these immune cells in comparison to the healthy control group. However, statistically significant differences were observed only in the NE-AUD subgroup. This finding lends support to the hypothesis that immune system activation may occur during the early stages of alcohol use disorder (AUD), even before liver dysfunction becomes clinically measurable through standard biochemical tests. It implies that systemic inflammation is not merely a consequence of advanced liver pathology, but rather an early component of the disease process. In contrast, the lack of statistical significance in neutrophil and monocyte elevations within the E-AUD group may be attributed to the activation of compensatory anti-inflammatory mechanisms, which could be triggered in response to more severe or prolonged hepatic injury. These homeostatic responses may serve to modulate or suppress further immune activation in the context of already existing liver damage, thus attenuating measurable changes in circulating inflammatory cell counts.

These results are generally in line with existing literature, which has reported elevated neutrophil and monocyte levels among individuals with chronic alcohol consumption [18, 33, 34]. In contrast, data on lymphocyte counts remain inconclusive, with studies showing inconsistent outcomes. Such variability may be attributed to differences in alcohol intake patterns, duration of use, comorbid physical or psychiatric conditions, or individual differences in immune responsiveness. The observed increase in neutrophil counts is of particular relevance, as neutrophils play a contributory role in alcohol-related liver injury through the release of cytotoxic mediators such as neutrophil elastase and myeloperoxidase—both of which promote inflammation and tissue destruction [35]. Similarly, chronic exposure to ethanol has been demonstrated to activate monocytes, leading to increased secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines including tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), thereby sustaining an inflammatory microenvironment and amplifying tissue injury over time [36]. Collectively, these immunopathological mechanisms support the concept that systemic inflammation likely precedes clinically apparent organ dysfunction in the natural course of alcohol use disorder. Accordingly, elevated neutrophil and monocyte counts observed in NE-AUD patients may serve as early markers of disease activity, preceding detectable hepatic impairment. Clinically, this raises the potential utility of early anti-inflammatory interventions to prevent or slow the progression toward overt liver damage—though this proposition warrants confirmation through large-scale, prospective, longitudinal research.

Inflammatory indices such as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR) have gained prominence as surrogate markers of systemic immune activation. In the present study, both NLR and MLR values were elevated in individuals from the E-AUD and NE-AUD groups relative to healthy controls; however, only the increase in NLR within the E-AUD subgroup reached statistical significance. The association between AUD and these ratios has been explored in a limited number of studies. One such investigation reported similar results, demonstrating significantly elevated NLR and MLR levels in patients with AUD, and further identified a specific link between NLR and hepatic injury [18]. These findings collectively suggest that NLR may hold promise as a prognostic indicator in AUD, although further studies are required to substantiate its clinical applicability.

Regarding high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), our study identified significantly elevated levels in the E-AUD group when compared to both the NE-AUD and healthy control groups. This observation is consistent with the findings of most prior studies in the field [37–39]. Furthermore, certain investigations have proposed that HDL-C concentrations may not only be associated with the presence of Alcohol Use Disorder but also reflect its severity [40, 41]. Supporting this notion, our data revealed that individuals with a prolonged history of alcohol consumption and evident hepatic involvement demonstrated higher HDL-C values. Conversely, a limited number of studies have reported decreased HDL-C levels in relation to alcohol intake [42]. Although HDL-C values appeared lower in the NE-AUD group in our cohort, this difference did not reach statistical significance. In addition, nutritional deficiencies (particularly malnutrition and micronutrient deficits), insulin resistance, and comorbid conditions such as metabolic syndrome may also influence HDL-C levels. Therefore, HDL-C should not be interpreted solely as a biomarker of advanced AUD, but rather in the context of alcohol consumption patterns (e.g., binge vs. chronic use), underlying metabolic background, and nutritional status. Fluctuations in HDL-C may be attributable to variability in alcohol consumption patterns, exposure duration, and individual metabolic responses. While traditionally labeled as “good cholesterol”, emerging evidence indicates that excessively elevated HDL-C may exert detrimental effects [43, 44]. In this context, markedly high HDL-C levels observed in some AUD cases may signal an unfavorable clinical trajectory. Recent studies [45, 46] have further suggested that the impact of HDL-C may be bidirectional, depending on alcohol intake levels and the underlying metabolic context, and that excessively high concentrations could potentially contribute to pro-inflammatory and atherogenic processes. Clarifying this potential relationship will require future prospective studies with broader lipid profiling and larger patient populations.

A particularly novel contribution of this study lies in the evaluation of the neutrophil-to-HDL-C ratio (NHR) and monocyte-to-HDL-C ratio (MHR), two emerging inflammatory indices. Although NHR values did not demonstrate significant differences across study groups, MHR levels were notably elevated in the NE-AUD group compared to both the E-AUD and healthy control cohorts. To the best of our knowledge, these markers have not yet been specifically examined in the context of alcohol use disorder. Existing literature has primarily focused on NHR and MHR within cardiovascular disease populations, where both have been linked to disease burden and severity [47, 48]. In our analysis, the isolated increase in MHR among NE-AUD participants—who exhibited normal liver enzyme levels and lacked overt clinical symptoms—may indicate an underlying subclinical inflammatory state. This early change could be explained by immune activation occurring before detectable elevations in liver enzymes. Chronic alcohol intake has been shown to increase intestinal permeability and facilitate lipopolysaccharide (LPS) translocation, which may activate monocytes and neutrophils via Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), subsequently promoting the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6. In addition, oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation byproducts generated during alcohol metabolism may reduce HDL concentrations and weaken its anti-inflammatory function. Together, these mechanisms could account for the rise in MHR observed prior to overt hepatocellular injury, potentially reflecting early immune alterations in AUD [49, 50]. As such, MHR has the potential to serve as a supplementary biomarker for the early identification of AUD. Future multicenter, prospective investigations are warranted to further assess its diagnostic performance and clinical utility in this setting.

According to the ROC analysis results evaluating the diagnostic value of inflammatory parameters used to differentiate between E-AUD and NE-AUD subgroups, MHR demonstrated statistically significant and balanced discriminative power. This finding suggests that MHR may not only reflect the presence of inflammation but also be clinically useful in distinguishing the biochemical stages of alcohol use disorder. While NHR showed limited diagnostic utility due to its high sensitivity but low specificity, the NLR parameter failed to provide a significant distinction between the two groups. These results indicate that MHR may serve as a potential early biomarker of inflammatory activity, particularly in individuals with normal liver function tests.

Limitations

This study has several important limitations. First, its cross-sectional design precludes the evaluation of temporal changes in inflammatory markers and the establishment of causal links with Alcohol Use Disorder (AUD). Although alcohol use histories were quantitatively recorded, detailed factors such as consumption patterns, duration, beverage type, age of onset, frequency, and severity were not included, potentially introducing heterogeneity in inflammatory profiles. The biomarker panel was relatively restricted to hematological and lipid-based indices, and direct measures of hepatic injury, such as liver biopsy or non-invasive imaging, were not incorporated. Moreover, potential confounders including smoking status, BMI, coexisting psychiatric disorders, and medication use were not accounted for. Finally, the single-center setting and modest sample size limit the generalizability of the findings. Larger multicenter prospective studies are warranted to validate these preliminary observations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that neutrophil, monocyte, HDL-C, NLR, and MHR levels significantly differed in individuals with alcohol use disorder (AUD) compared to healthy controls. Notably, in the NE-AUD (non-elevated liver function tests in alcohol use disorder) group—where liver function tests remained within normal limits—neutrophil, monocyte, and MHR levels were significantly elevated relative to the healthy group. This finding suggests that these inflammatory parameters may serve as potential early diagnostic biomarkers for AUD, even before hepatic impairment becomes apparent. Conversely, the significantly higher HDL-C and NLR levels observed in the E-AUD group may reflect advanced stages of AUD and could be associated with a poorer prognosis. Overall, our findings indicate that inflammatory markers may play a supportive role in both the diagnosis and prognostic assessment of AUD. However, further prospective studies with larger sample sizes are needed to validate these associations and clarify their clinical utility.
