Serum N‐Terminal Pro‐B‐Type Natriuretic Peptide Is Associated With Insulin Resistance in Chinese: Danyang Study
Authors: Ziwen Zheng, Junya Liang, Yun Gao, Mulian Hua, Siqi Zhang, Ming Liu, Zhuyuan Fang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe association of serum N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide (NT‐proBNP) with insulin resistance (IR), as measured by homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA‐IR), in the general population is unclear. Our study aimed to characterize its relationship in a large community‐based population. Subjects were recruited from the Danyang city between 2017 and 2019. Serum NT‐proBNP was measured using an enhanced chemiluminescence immunoassay. IR was defined by a HOMA‐IR in the highest sex‐specific quartile. Categorical and continuous analyses were performed with sex‐specific NT‐proBNP tertiles and naturally logarithmically transformed NT‐proBNP (lnNTproBNP), respectively. The 2945 participants (mean age 52.8 years) included 1728 (58.7%) women, 1167 (39.6%) hypertensive patients, 269 (9.1%) diabetic patients, and 736 (25.0%) patients with IR. In simple and multivariate‐adjusted regression analyses, serum lnNTproBNP were both negatively associated with HOMA‐IR (β = −0.19 to −0.25; p < 0.0001). Similar results were also obtained in multiple subgroup analyses. In multiple logistic regression analyses, elevated serum NT‐proBNP was associated with lower risks of IR (odds ratios: 0.68 and 0.39; 95% confidence intervals: 0.61–0.74 and 0.30–0.50 for lnNTproBNP and top vs. bottom tertiles, respectively; p < 0.0001). In conclusion, increased serum NT‐proBNP level was strongly associated with a lower risk of IR in Chinese.Keywords: homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance, insulin resistance, N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide

Introduction

Natriuretic peptides (NPs) are confirmed cardiac‐derived biomarkers of cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), especially heart failure (HF) [1, 2]. However, with an increased understanding of the physiology and pathology of the NP system during the past years, emerging evidence indicates that the effects of NPs extend beyond the cardiovascular system. NPs are also involved in the regulation of energy metabolism [3] and the development of diabetes [4]. These new findings open novel avenues in diabetes research, further studies on the mechanisms and clinical applications of NPs may provide new targets for therapeutic intervention in the fight against obesity and insulin resistance (IR).

IR underlies the pathogenesis of diabetes. The golden standard for measuring IR is the hyperinsulinemic euglycemic clamp (HEC), a technique by DeFronzo et al. [5], but its application is limited in clinical use because of its invasive procedure. Nonetheless, other useful surrogate indices such as homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA‐IR), homeostasis model assessment of β‐cell function, quantitative insulin sensitivity check index, triglyceride glucose index, and insulin‐to‐glucose ratio are more widely used for clinical purposes [6]. Of all these indices, HOMA‐IR was initially developed by Matthews et al. in 1985 [7]. Considering the strong correlation between HOMA‐IR and HEC [8], HOMA‐IR is currently used as a simple, validated model of the interaction dynamics between glucose and insulin to assess the degree of IR [9].

Two previous studies showed that circulating N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide (NT‐proBNP) was negatively associated with HOMA‐IR in high‐risk diabetic patients [10] and in non‐diabetic healthy American adults [11]. These results suggested that NT‐proBNP may play important roles in the pathogenesis in IR. However, previous studies relating serum NT‐proBNP to IR have focused on specific conditions either high‐risk diabetes [10] or non‐diabetes [11], rather than the general population, and did not broadly adjust for potential covariates, especially visceral fat and left ventricular mass index (LVMI) [10, 11]. Indeed, visceral fat and LVMI are the important determinants of HOMA‐IR (IR) [12] and NT‐proBNP [13], respectively. Therefore, in the current study, we aim to extend previous reports to investigate whether serum NT‐proBNP levels, independent of visceral fat and LVMI, are associated with the HOMA‐IR index and IR, in Chinese population.

Methods

Study Population

As described previously [14], we recruited subjects aged 18 years or older from Station Community in Danyang County investigate the comprehensive cardiovascular risk factors in Chinese. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Jiangsu Province Hospital of Chinese Medicine (2017NL‐045‐02) and followed the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Of the 3032 participants recruited between June 2017 and May 2019 in this study, 87 participants were excluded because of missing information on visceral fat (n = 7) or left ventricular mass index measurement (n = 59) or if there was not adequate blood sample for insulin (n = 20) or NT‐proBNP measurement (n = 1). Finally, the total number of subjects enrolled in the statistical analysis was 2945.

Anthropometric Measurements

Demographic and lifestyle information, including information on current smoking, alcohol intake, medical history, and use of medications, were self‐reported and collected by one experienced physician using a standardized questionnaire. The same observer also measured patients' blood pressure using Omron 7130 oscillometric blood pressure monitor three times consecutively after at least 5 minu of rest for each patient in the sitting position. Body weight, height, and waist circumference were also collected using standardized protocols. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as the body weight in kilograms divided by the body height in meters squared. All patients' venous blood samples were drawn after overnight fasting for the measurement of serum NT‐proBNP, insulin, and other biochemical indicators.

NT‐proBNP Measurements

We measured NT‐proBNP using an enhanced chemiluminescence immunoassay (VITROS Immunodiagnostic Products NT‐proBNP II Reagent Pact) on a VITROS 5600 integrated system (Ortho‐Clinical Diagnostics, Inc). The assay detection limits are 5 pg/mL for the lower and 30 000 pg/mL for the higher cutoff.

Glucose Homeostasis and Other Laboratory Measurements

Serum glucose was measured by the hexokinase method (Beckman AU5800). Insulin was measured by chemiluminescence immunoassay (Beckman DXI800). IR was estimated by the HOMA‐IR index: fasting plasma glucose (mmol/L) × fasting insulin (μIU/mL)/22.5 [7]. We also used the Beckman AU5800 automatic biochemical analyzer to measure serum total cholesterol, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, triglyceride, gamma‐glutamyl transferase, and so forth. The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated by the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD‐EPI) formula [15].

Visceral Fat Measurement

Visceral fat area was measured using dual bioelectrical impedance analysis (Omron HDS‐2000) according to the protocol provided by the manufacturer. Briefly, dual bioelectrical impedance analysis calculates cross‐sectional area of visceral fat at the umbilical level using two electric paths to measure truncal impedance and surface impedance [16].

Echocardiography Measurement

Echocardiographic measures were performed with the Philips CX50 device (Phillips, Bothell, WA) according to the standard protocol of the American Society of Echocardiography (ASE). Left ventricular (LV) mass was calculated by the ASE recommended formula [17], and standardized to body size by dividing the LV mass by body surface area, as LVMI. Left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) was defined as a LVMI > 115 g/m2 in men or a LVMI > 95 g/m2 in women, respectively [18]. For details on the methods of echocardiographic measurements, please see the Supporting Information.

Definitions of Metabolic Disease

Obesity was defined as a BMI of at least 28 kg/m2 according to Chinese criteria. Hypertension was defined as a systolic blood pressure of at least 140 mmHg or a diastolic blood pressure of at least 90 mmHg, or as the use of antihypertensive medications. Diabetes mellitus was defined as a fasting plasma glucose of at least 7.0 mmol/L or as the use of antidiabetic drugs. IR was defined by a HOMA‐IR in the highest sex‐specific quartile [19].

Statistical Methods

We used SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute) for database management and statistical analysis. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range for continuous variables and as frequency (n) and proportion (%) for categorized variables, respectively. Since NT‐proBNP was a skewed distribution and the range of NT‐proBNP values varied by sex, we carried out categorical analyses and used sex‐specific NT‐proBNP tertiles according to the methods proposed by Arnlöv J, et al. [20]. NT‐proBNP was further natural logarithmically transformed (lnNTproBNP) for all regression analyses as a continuous variable.

Differences between the tertile 1, 2, and 3 of serum NT‐proBNP groups for baseline characteristics, and HOMA‐IR index and the proportion of IR were compared using the 1‐way ANOVA. For categorical analyses, we also analyzed the difference and tested for trends of the HOMA‐IR index across NT‐proBNP tertiles in men and women individuals, respectively. For continuous analyses, we further explored the relationship between HOMA‐IR and lnNTproBNP using crude and multivariable‐adjusted (considered HbA1c as an important confounder) linear mixed‐effects models. Sensitive analyses were also performed in subjects (1) without hypertension, (2) without obesity, (3) without diabetes, and (4) without LVH. Finally, we performed simple and multiple logistic regression analyses to evaluate the odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of insulin resistance not only across NT‐proBNP tertiles and quintiles, but also for each 1‐unit increase in lnNTproBNP. A two‐sided value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of the Study Participants

The 2945 study participants (mean age 52.8 ± 11.9 years) included 1728 (58.7%) women, 1167 (39.6%) hypertensive patients, 269 (9.1%) diabetic patients, and 736 (25.0%) patients with IR. Median NT‐proBNP was 13 (25th−75th percentile 5−29) pg/mL in men and 29 (25th−75th percentile 14−53) pg/mL in women, respectively. Baseline characteristics of the study participants are presented by NT‐proBNP tertiles in Table 1. Individuals in the top tertile of NT‐proBNP were older, more likely to have hypertension (higher systolic/diastolic blood pressure), greater LVMI and higher rate of LVH, and lower eGFR, but lower proportions of obesity (lesser body mass index, waist circumference and visceral fat), dyslipidemia (lower triglyceride and LDL cholesterol) and IR (lower fasting insulin) (all p ≤ 0.003). However, the rate of diabetes mellitus was similar among three tertiles of NT‐proBNP groups (p = 0.30; Table 1).

Association of NT‐proBNP with HOMA‐IR and IR

Categorical Analyses

We further examined the association between serum NT‐proBNP and HOMA‐IR index in men and women participants, respectively. In the crude model, HOMA‐IR were both decreased across the tertiles of NT‐proBNP in men and women, respectively (p < 0.0001, data not shown). After adjustment for age, sex, body mass index, current smoking, and alcohol intake, the use of antihypertensive and antihyperglycemic, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, hypertension, diabetes, visceral fat, and LVMI, the similar trend of HOMA‐IR were observed across three groups in both men (2.26 vs. 1.89 vs. 1.98, p = 0.04; Figure 1A) and women individuals, respectively (2.34 vs. 1.98 vs. 1.79, p < 0.0001; Figure 1B).

Continuous Analyses

Because men and women had similar correlates of serum NT‐proBNP and HOMA‐IR, we combined men and women in further multivariable linear regression and logistic regression analyses. By using naturally log‐transformed NT‐proBNP as a continuous variable, serum lnNTproBNP showed negative association with the HOMA‐IR index in simple regression analyses, as well as after multivariable adjustment for the aforementioned confounders in the total cohort (β = −0.19 to −0.25, p < 0.0001; Table 2).

Subgroup Analyses

Subgroup analysis was then carried out to investigate the relationship between serum lnNTproBNP and HOMA‐IR in participants with normal blood pressure or weight or glucose or left ventricular mass index. Sensitivity analyses showed a consistent association when excluding patients with hypertension or obesity or diabetes or LVH before and broadly adjusted for covariates (β = −0.13 to −0.29, p < 0.0001; Table 2).

Discussion

The main finding of our study was that higher serum NT‐proBNP concentration was significantly and independently associated with a lower HOMA‐IR index and lower prevalence of IR. Among 2945 community‐based Chinese adults in our study, compared with the bottom tertile serum NT‐proBNP, subjects in the top tertile had a 61% decrease in the risk of IR. As HOMA‐IR or IR may be an independent predictor of diabetic cardiovascular complications [21, 22], our findings therefore may have potential clinical implications for cardiovascular prevention by increasing the production of NT‐proBNP and by improving insulin sensitivity.

Several previous studies have explored the association of serum NT‐proBNP with HOMA and/or IR in overweight/obese individuals [10], non‐diabetic healthy subjects [11, 23], and middle‐aged and elderly participants [24]. In line with our findings, in 3234 obesity/obese subjects recruited from the Diabetes Prevention Program, Walford GA et al. reported that circulating NT‐proBNP was positively associated with the reciprocal of HOMA‐IR only controlling for BMI or waist circumference. Other investigators reported similar associations of NT‐proBNP with IR in populations without diabetes [11, 23] and in adults aged 45−74 years [24] independent of traditional cardiovascular risk factors. However, previous studies relating NT‐proBNP and IR did not fully adjust for potentially important determinants such as visceral fat and LVMI. Indeed, evidence suggests that visceral fat not only positively correlates with insulin secretion (first‐ and second‐phase insulin secretion) but also with IR (HOMA‐IR), and worsens insulin sensitivity independent of BMI and subcutaneous fat in patients with diabetes [12]. Similarly, the release of NT‐proBNP is regulated by left ventricular expansion and pressure overload and influenced by LVMI, even in subjects without heart failure [13].

However, up to now, no previous study had examined the relationship between NT‐proBNP and IR simultaneously controlling for visceral fat and LVMI. In fact, increased LV mass (LVMI), which reflects compensatory remodeling in response to ventricular overload, is important contributor to the secretion of NT‐proBNP [25]. Therefore, our study further augments previous research [10, 11, 23, 24] by showing that increased NT‐proBNP is associated with decreased HOMA‐IR and lower risk of IR even adjusting for the abovementioned two important covariates.

The underlying pathophysiological mechanisms for the negative association of NT‐proBNP with IR are not well clear. First, an experimental study observed that increased insulin reduces the release of natriuretic peptides in isolated neonatal porcine hearts [26]. Second, there is at least one observational study [27] that suggested that IR patients (n = 206) have lower NT‐proBNP levels (17 vs. 31 pg/mL) compared with insulin‐sensitive subjects (n = 187), which may be attributed to a direct or indirect increase in glutathione production, suggesting oxidative stress appeared to mediate the association of IR with NT‐proBNP. Finally, one interventional study in heart failure patients with short‐term insulin infusion suggested that hyperinsulinemia may directly suppress NT‐proBNP secretion [28]. Notwithstanding this evidence, we could not completely rule out another possibility that lower NT‐proBNP causes IR. Indeed, animal studies reported that transgenic mice that overexpress BNP, compared with wild‐type mice, are protected from IR [29]. Another experimental and clinical study also suggested that natriuretic peptide stimulates adiponectin secretion from adipocytes and further regulates insulin sensitivity [30]. Although evidence supports an insulin‐sensitizing effect of natriuretic peptide, further investigations are still warranted to delineate the effects and mechanisms of serum NT‐proBNP in the development of IR.

Our study explored the relationship between NT‐proBNP and HOMA‐IR and the rate of IR in a relatively large sample of Chinese subjects. The information on medical history, anthropometric and blood biochemical measurements enables us to perform sensitivity analysis in individuals without obesity, hypertension, or diabetes, and the substantial information on covariates (smoke and alcohol, BMI, treatment of diabetes, and especially visceral fat and LVMI) allowed adjustment for a collection of potential confounders, albeit we cannot rule out residual variables. Notwithstanding these strengths, our study is limited by its cross‐sectional design, which enable us not to establish the temporality and causality of the observed associations. Future prospective study is warranted to explore the cause inference. In addition, we only measured IR using HOMA‐IR index but not HEC technique [5], considered as the golden standard for IR. Nevertheless, it has been reported that HOMA‐IR correlates well with HEC [8].

Conclusions

In conclusion, our large‐scale population study has demonstrated a significant and independent inverse association between serum NT‐proBNP level and HOMA‐IR index. Furthermore, we found that increased serum NT‐proBNP carried a lower risk of IR. Although prospective observational and interventional studies are warranted to further investigate whether increased NT‐proBNP formation may play a protective role in IR and subsequent cardiovascular complications, our findings may provide novel perspectives into the potential roles of NT‐proBNP in IR, and cardiovascular disease.
