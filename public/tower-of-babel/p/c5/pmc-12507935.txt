A phase I study of the OX40 agonist BGB-A445 with or without tislelizumab, an anti–PD-1 monoclonal antibody, in patients with advanced solid tumors: dose-escalation results
Authors: Jayesh Desai, Sanjeev Deva, Bo Gao, Kunyu Yang, Kenneth J. O’Byrne, Meili Sun, Tianshu Liu, Tarek Meniawy, Xinmin Yu, Mark Voskoboynik, Diwakar Davar, Marco Matos, Shiangjiin Leaw, Tahmina Rahman, Xiaofei Qu, Hugh Giovinazzo, Xin Chen, Yan Dong, Daphne Day
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeOX40 may stimulate T-cell activation, potentially enhanced with checkpointinhibition. Results are from the dose-escalation part of an ongoing, multicenter, open-label study (NCT04215978, registered 30 December 2019) investigating OX40 agonist BGB-A445 alone or with anti-PD-1 antibody tislelizumab in patients with advanced solid tumors.MethodsAdults ≥18 years with previously treated advanced solid tumors, measurable by RECIST v1.1, enrolled. Dose-escalation A: 8 cohorts received increasing doses of IV BGB-A445 as monotherapy (20, 60, 150, 300, 600, 1200, 2400, 3600 mg); dose-escalation B: 6 cohorts received increasing doses of IV BGB-A445 (150, 300, 600, 1200, 2400, 3600 mg) + IV tislelizumab 200 mg. Primary objectives were safety and tolerability; secondary objectives included overall response rate (ORR) and pharmacokinetics.Results112 patients were treated (A, n=63; B, n=49); 34/112 (30.4%) previously received checkpoint inhibitors. Treatment-related adverse events occurred in 36 (57.1%) (A) and 37 (75.5%) (B) patients, were grade ≥3 in 4 (6.3%) and 9 (18.4%), and caused treatment discontinuations in 1 (1.6%) and 1 (2.0%), respectively. Immune-mediated adverse events occurred in 8 (12.7%) (A) and 18 (36.7%) (B) patients, and infusion-related reactions in 9 (14.3%) (A) and 9 (18.4%) (B). No dose-limiting toxicities occurred. Unconfirmed ORR was 3.3% (unconfirmed partial response [PR], n=2) (A); confirmed was 21.3% (including PR, n=10) (B). BGB-A445 exposure increased dose-proportionally with a half-life of 8–13 days. OX40 receptor occupancy was saturated at ≥300 mg BGB-A445 in all cohorts.ConclusionBGB-A445 was well tolerated and demonstrated on-target immune activation at clinically relevant doses. Antitumor activity was observed across cohorts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00280-025-04809-1.

Introduction

Monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) that modulate immune checkpoint pathways to overcome tumor-mediated immunosuppression are an effective treatment paradigm in targeted cancer therapy [1]. Several antibody therapies that target the programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1)/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) axis have demonstrated clinical utility and have been approved for use as monotherapy or combination therapy for many cancer types [2]. Despite these therapeutic advances, treatment responses are still limited in a subset of patients, and relapse or resistance to monotherapy may develop [3, 4]. An important strategy in cancer therapy is to combine agents that have independent, nonoverlapping mechanisms of action, hence the continued effort to identify novel immune checkpoint pathways as potential drug targets [5].

OX40 (CD134) is a proinflammatory member of the tumor necrosis factor super-family that has been shown to be primarily expressed by activated CD4+ and CD8+ T cells and regulatory T (Treg) cells [6–12]. Together with its ligand, OX40L, OX40 plays an important role in the activation, potentiation, proliferation, and survival of T cells and the modulation of natural killer (NK) cell function [3, 5]. OX40 mAbs currently under study include BGB-A445 [13, 14], BMS-986178 [15], GSK3174998 [16], ivuxolimab [17], MEDI0562 [18], and MOXR0916 [19]. BGB-A445 is a humanized immunoglobulin (Ig)G1, Fc-competent mAb agonist that binds to the CRD4 region of OX40 [13]. Unlike some OX40 agents, BGB-A445 does not block the interaction between OX40 and its endogenous ligand [13, 14]. When OX40 is allowed to interact with its ligand, dendritic cells are able to fully activate T cells, which in turn are then able to enhance dendritic cell maturation and activation, leading to further T-cell activation, which causes a feed-forward loop (Fig. 1). With the addition of a PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor, this would lead to more T cells in the tumor microenvironment with the capacity to enhance tumor cell killing. Additionally, BGB-A445 targets the membrane-proximal end of OX40 while also binding the Fc gamma receptor (FcγR), which brings NK cells expressing FcγR and Tregs expressing OX40 into close proximity [14]. This allows NK cells to kill and, thus, deplete Treg cells, thereby reducing their ability to downregulate the antitumor response. Finally, unlike MOXR0916, BGB-A445 does not display a hook effect (antibody excess) dose response [13, 14]. This means that higher concentrations of BGB-A445 will not prevent binding of BGB-A445 to its target.

OX40 mAbs BMS-986178 [15], GSK3174998 [16], ivuxolimab [17], MEDI0562 [18], and MOXR0916 [19] have been evaluated in early-phase clinical studies and were shown to be safe when administered as monotherapy or in combination with other immune checkpoint inhibitors, including anti–PD-(L)1 agents. When BMS-986178 was combined with nivolumab (anti–PD-1) or ipilimumab (anti–cytotoxic T lymphocyte antigen-4) or with both nivolumab and ipilimumab, no efficacy was observed beyond that expected with nivolumab and/or ipilimumab [15]. Similarly, GSK3174998 added to pembrolizumab did not have an efficacy signal greater than expected for pembrolizumab alone [16]. However, these OX40 agonists may block the interaction of OX40 with its ligand [13], which could potentially limit the effect of OX40. In preclinical studies, OX40 agonists in combination with anti–PD-1 agents promoteT-cell activation, allowing an immune response against tumor cells. This combined therapy creates a more favorable tumor microenvironment, which leads to higher infiltration of activated immune cells and a decrease in immunosuppressive signals [20]. These early-phase and preclinical outcomes suggest that targeting the OX40 pathway may be a safe and effective option worthy of further exploration.

Preclinical results showed that BGB-A445 has significant immune-stimulating effects and therapeutic efficacy, either as a single agent or in combination with anti–PD-1 therapy. Tislelizumab is a humanized IgG4 mAb with high affinity and binding specificity for PD-1, and was specifically engineered to minimize FcγR binding on macrophages [21], thus limiting antibody-dependent phagocytosis [22], which is a potential mechanism of resistance to anti–PD-1 therapy [23]. Clinical studies have demonstrated that tislelizumab is generally well tolerated in patients with advanced solid tumors, both as a single agent and in combination with chemotherapy [24–28]. Tislelizumab has been approved in multiple indications and countries [29–32]. Here, we present results from the dose-escalation part of an ongoing multicenter phase Ia/b study (NCT04215978) investigating BGB-A445 alone or in combination with tislelizumab in patients with advanced solid tumors.

Methods

Study design and treatments

BGB-A317-A445-101 (NCT04215978) is an open-label phase Ia (dose escalation) and 1b (dose expansion) study. Phase Ia is being conducted in Australia, China, New Zealand, and the United States.

The study protocol and its amendments were approved by the appropriate local or national ethics body or institutional review boards for each participating center. The trial was conducted according to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the International Council for Harmonisation Good Clinical Practice guidelines. Patients provided written informed consent. The study was designed to assess BGB-A445 as monotherapy (Part A) and in combination with tislelizumab (Part B) in adult patients with advanced solid tumors for which there is clinical evidence of response to T-cell–based immuno-oncology agents (see Online Resource, Additional methods, for a list).

In phase Ia dose-escalation Part A, eligible patients were enrolled into 8 planned cohorts of increasing dose levels of intravenous (IV) BGB-A445 as monotherapy (20, 60, 150, 300, 600, 1200, 2400, and 3600 mg); in combination dose-escalation Part B, patients were enrolled into 6 planned cohorts of increasing dose levels of IV BGB-A445 (150, 300, 600, 1200, 2400, and 3600 mg) plus IV tislelizumab 200 mg. Dose escalation commenced with monotherapy BGB-A445 (referred to as Part A), whereas combination dose escalation with tislelizumab (Part B) commenced with the third dose level of BGB-A445 (i.e., 150 mg) once that dose level as a monotherapy met the criteria for dose escalation. At any given dose of combination therapy, in order for combination dose escalation to proceed (Part B), the next dose level of BGB-A445 monotherapy, as well as the current dose level of the combination, needed to meet the criteria for dose escalation. Dose-escalation decisions were made based on a review of real-time safety data and available preliminary pharmacokinetic (PK) and pharmacodynamic data, and in consultation with participating investigators as part of the safety monitoring committee. Dose escalation was guided by the Bayesian modified toxicity probability interval-2 method [33, 34]; the toxicity target rate for the maximum tolerated dose (MTD) was 30%, with the acceptable toxicity probability interval at 0.25–0.35. The planned enrollment was 52–91 patients (or more, depending on the dose-level increments required). Once responses were observed in the monotherapy cohorts, the safety monitoring committee advised to backfill the enrollment to collect signal-seeking antitumor activity and to complement the existing safety, tolerability, and PK data.

Treatments were administered on day 1 of each 21-day cycle; in Part B, tislelizumab was administered first, followed by BGB-A445. Patients remained on treatment until unacceptable toxicity, progressive disease (PD), or withdrawal of consent. Those who experienced disease progression in Part A were given the option to switch to either BGB-A445 in combination with tislelizumab or tislelizumab monotherapy. The phase Ib dose-expansion part of this study is ongoing and is briefly described in the Online Resource.

Patients

Eligible patients were aged ≥ 18 years, with histologically or cytologically confirmed advanced or metastatic unresectable solid tumors with measurable disease (defined by Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors version 1.1 [RECIST v1.1]), who had previously received standard systemic therapy or for whom treatment was not available, not tolerated, or refused. Eligible patients had an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance score of 0–1 and were required to have adequate organ function and a life expectancy of ≥ 6 months. Patients who had active leptomeningeal disease, uncontrolled brain metastasis, active autoimmune disease, any active malignancy during the previous ≤ 2 years, or had prior therapy (chemotherapy, immunotherapy, or investigational therapy) ≤ 14 days before the first dose of BGB-A445 were ineligible (see Online Resource for full inclusion and exclusion criteria).

Objectives, assessments, and statistical analyses

The primary objective of the dose-escalation part was to determine the safety and tolerability, as well as the MTD, of BGB-A445 administered as monotherapy and in combination with tislelizumab. Safety assessments included treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs), treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs), and serious adverse events, as graded by National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5.0. Immune-mediated adverse events were identified by investigators based on predefined protocol criteria, including exclusion of alternative causes, consistency with an immune-mediated mechanism, and requirement for systemic corticosteroids, immunosuppressants, or endocrine therapy.

Secondary objectives included the assessment of preliminary antitumor activity (i.e., overall response rate [ORR], duration of response, and disease control rate [DCR; defined as stable disease (SD) or better] by investigator assessment per RECIST v1.1). Complete response and partial response (PR) were defined as the disappearance of target lesions and ≥ 30% decrease from baseline in the sum of diameters of target lesions, respectively. Durable SD was defined as SD that persisted for at least 24 weeks. Clinical benefit rate (CBR) was defined as the sum of complete response, PR, and durable SD. Immunogenicity of BGB-A445 and tislelizumab (evaluated through detection of antidrug antibodies [ADAs] in serum samples) and the BGB-A445 PK profile were also assessed. Pharmacodynamics for both OX40 receptor occupancy (RO) and soluble OX40 data were assessed as part of the exploratory objectives. Safety and data on antitumor activity were summarized descriptively.

The safety analysis set included patients who received ≥ 1 dose of study treatment. The efficacy-evaluable analysis set included all patients who received ≥ 1 dose of study treatment, had evaluable disease at baseline, and had ≥ 1 evaluable postbaseline tumor response assessment (unless clinical PD or death occurred first). The dose-limiting toxicity (DLT)-evaluable analysis set included patients who received ≥ 80% of their assigned study treatments, remained on study during the DLT observation period, and had sufficient safety evaluation, or patients who experienced a DLT within the DLT observation period. The PK analysis set included all patients who received ≥ 1 dose of study treatment and had ≥ 1 derivable PK parameter. The ADA analysis set included all patients who received the study treatment and had both baseline and ≥ 1 postbaseline ADA result.

Results

Patients

At the data cutoff of 1 March 2024, 112 patients were enrolled (Fig. S1), with 63 receiving BGB-A445 alone in Part A (Fig. S1a) and 49 receiving BGB-A445 in combination with tislelizumab in Part B (Fig. S1b). In Part A, the most common tumor types (2 or more patients) were colorectal (n = 8); head and neck and intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (n = 4 each); breast, cervical, endometrial, gastric or gastroesophageal, hepatic, nasopharyngeal, non-squamous non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), ovarian, and sarcoma (n = 3 each); and adenocarcinoma, anal, cholangiocarcinoma, esophageal, neuroendocrine carcinoma, squamous NSCLC, and uterine leiomyosarcoma (n = 2 each, Table S1). The median age of the study population was 59 years (range, 27–80), 35 (55.6%) were female, and 42 (66.7%) were White (Table 1). Median time from initial diagnosis to first dose of study treatment was 1.9 years (range, 0.3–22.2). Overall, 96.8% of patients in Part A received prior anticancer treatments; the median number of prior lines of systemic anticancer therapies was 2 (range, 1–7). Nineteen (30.2%) patients had received prior immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy.

In Part B, tumor types occurring in 2 or more patients were non-squamous NSCLC (n = 9); colorectal (n = 6); gynecological (n = 5); bladder and cervical (n = 4 each); and head and neck, kidney, and squamous NSCLC (n = 3 each, Table S2). The median age of the study population was 61 years (range, 22–75), 27 (55.1%) were female, and 28 (57.1%) were White (Table 1). The median time from initial diagnosis to first dose was 2.2 years (range, 0.3–9.7). All patients in Part B had received prior anticancer treatments; the median number of prior lines of systemic anticancer therapies was 2 (range, 1–9). In Part B, 15 (30.6%) patients had received prior immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy.

Safety

In Part A, the median duration of exposure to BGB-A445 was 2.1 months (range, 0.7–31.0). The most common TEAEs were nausea (25.4%), fatigue (23.8%), and constipation (22.2%); TEAEs with a total incidence of ≥ 10% are shown in Table S3. TRAEs occurred in 36 (57.1%) patients (Table 2). Nine (14.3%) patients experienced infusion-related adverse events related to BGB-A445; the most common events (incidence ≥ 2 patients) were chills and dyspnea (n = 3 each), and back pain, chest discomfort, and pyrexia (n = 2 each). Most infusion-related adverse events (19/22, 86.4%) were grade 1–2. A TRAE leading to treatment discontinuation (troponin I increased) occurred in 1 (1.6%) patient, and there were no TRAEs leading to death. No DLTs occurred in Part A.

PK and pharmacodynamics

PK data are based on samples for 63 patients (Part A) and 48 patients (Part B), collected from study treatment cycle 1. BGB-A445 exposure in the monotherapy cohorts was similar to that in the combination cohorts. Serumconcentrations of BGB-A445 decreased in a biexponential manner after IV administration (Fig. S3). The arithmetic mean elimination half-life based on cycle 1 data ranged from 8 to 13 days. BGB-A445 exposures (maximum plasma concentration [Cmax] and area under the concentration–time curve [AUC]) across days 0–21 increased dose proportionally from 20 to 3600 mg (Fig. S3). Summaries of cycle 1 PK parameters of BGB-A445 as monotherapy and in combination with tislelizumab are presented in Tables S8 and S9. OX40 RO was saturated at dose levels of ≥ 300 mg in both the BGB-A445 monotherapy and the combination BGB-A445 plus tislelizumab cohorts (Fig. S4). Serum levels of soluble OX40 receptor (an indirect indicator of OX40 RO in the tumor microenvironment) show a stronger trend of soluble OX40 receptor induction after long-term treatment at higher doses (≥ 1200 mg) in both monotherapy and combination therapy settings (Fig. S4c, d).

Immunogenicity

A total of 89 patients (50 in Part A and 39 in Part B) had serum samples that were assessed for ADAs. ADAs were detected in 16 (18.0%) patients (7 [14.0%] from Part A and 9 [23.1%] from Part B). All ADAs detected were treatment emergent and treatment induced, and none were treatment boosted (pre-existing ADAs that were boosted to a higher level following BGB-A445 administration) [35]. Most ADAs (in 6 of 7 patients from Part A and 8 of 9 patients from Part B) were persistent, with ADAs in 1 patient from each part being transient. Preliminary analyses suggest that, although BGB-A445 exposures were slightly lower in patients with ADAs, the range of exposures was comparable to and overlapped that of patients without ADAs. Immunogenicity evaluations are ongoing.

Discussion

In this open-label multicenter phase Ia dose-escalation study, the novel OX40 agonist BGB-A445 was shown to be generally safe and well tolerated in patients with advanced solid tumors when administered as monotherapy and in combination with tislelizumab. No DLTs were observed in all tested doses of BGB-A445 when administered as monotherapy and in combination with tislelizumab. The rates of immune-mediated adverse events and infusion-related TRAEs were lower with BGB-A445 monotherapy compared with the combination with tislelizumab. No new safety signals for tislelizumab were observed when administered in combination with BGB-A445. The MTD was not reached, and the maximum administered dose of 3600 mg every 3 weeks was well tolerated. These safety findings were similar to reports from other early-phase clinical trials in which OX40 agents were administered as monotherapy and in combination with anti–PD-(L)1 agents [15–19].

The ORR of BGB-A445 monotherapy was modest (3.3%) but improved when tislelizumab was added to BGB-A445 (21.3%, all PR); a high percentage of patients in both groups of this heterogeneous and typical phase I trial experienced SD (32.8% and 31.9%, respectively). Given the modest response to BGB-A445 monotherapy, we anticipate that tislelizumab may be contributing more strongly to the response seen with the combination therapy. Durable SD was observed in 5 (8.2%) patients who received BGB-A445 monotherapy and in 6 (12.8%) patients who received combination therapy. In the BGB-A445 monotherapy group, responses were observed in patients with uterine leiomyosarcoma (with 1200 mg) and lung adenocarcinoma (with 2400 mg); in the combination group, responses were observed in patients with colorectal cancer (150 mg, 1200 mg, 2400 mg), anal cancer (150 mg), cervical cancer (300 mg), bladder cancer and urothelial carcinoma of the upper tract (600 mg), small cell lung cancer (1200 mg), non-squamous NSCLC (1200 mg), and adenocarcinoma (3600 mg). It should be noted that the patient in the monotherapy group with lung adenocarcinoma had previous exposure to checkpoint inhibitors. Additionally, in the combination group, 1 patient with colorectal cancer (1200 mg) experienced tumor shrinkage of nearly 90% (this patient’s tumor had a K-RAS G13D mutation), and a few others experienced shrinkage of 50–70%, highlighting the strong response of these patients. The mutation status of the 6 other tumors with tumor shrinkage of greater than 50% were 3 with unknown mutational status, 1 with high microsatellite instability, 1 with mismatch repair deficiency, and 1 with mismatch repair deficiency and BRAF V600E mutation.

There is a possibility that BGB-A445 may be able to overcome resistance to PD-1/PD-L1 monotherapy, as we observed a response in an immune checkpoint inhibitor-experienced patient with lung adenocarcinoma treated with BGB-A445 monotherapy, as well as SD in 2 patients previously treated with checkpoint inhibitors in this group. However, these observations would benefit from confirmation in larger numbers of patients in future trials. Published data from other early-phase studies of OX40 agents administered as monotherapy were similar, with zero to few observed instances of PR and the best response being SD [15–19]. In comparison, the ORR for the OX40 compound BMS-986178 in combination with the anti–PD-1 mAb nivolumab ranged from 6 to 12%, depending on the dosing schedule, with responses observed in patients with bladder cancer, cervical cancer, endometrial cancer, and renal cell carcinoma; however, the authors concluded that this level of efficacy was not above that expected from nivolumab alone [15]. In another phase I study, the ORR was 8% for patients treated with OX40 mAb GSK3174998 plus pembrolizumab; responses were observed in patients with bladder cancer, colorectal carcinoma displaying high microsatellite instability, melanoma, and NSCLC; similarly, the response was considered to be no different than that expected from pembrolizumab alone [16]. The data reported in the present study, however, support further exploration of the OX40 BGB-A445 plus anti–PD-(L)1 combination in patients with advanced solid tumors. The phase Ib portion of this study will be a dedicated expansion trial of BGB-A445 monotherapy, as well as BGB-A445 in combination with tislelizumab.

Exposure of BGB-A445 was similar across the monotherapy and combination therapy cohorts. Cmax and AUC for BGB-A445 were linear and dose proportional. While OX40 receptors on T cells in peripheral blood were saturated at dose levels of ≥ 300 mg, peripheral serum levels of soluble OX40 receptor showed a stronger trend of induction after long-term treatment at higher doses (≥ 1200 mg) in both the monotherapy and combination therapy settings, which may suggest greater intratumor biological activity at high doses. With BGB-A445 monotherapy treatment, 2 unconfirmed PRs occurred at 1200 mg and 2400 mg dose levels, while for combination therapy, confirmed PRs occurred in patients receiving doses of BGB-A445 ranging from 150 mg to 3600 mg. There were no safety and tolerability concerns for either monotherapy or combination therapy from 20 mg to 3600 mg of BGB-A445.

Unlike some OX40 agents, BGB-A445 does not block the interaction between OX40 and its ligand [13]. This may, in part, help to explain why some, albeit limited, activity was observed for BGB-A445 as monotherapy, as well as in combination with tislelizumab. Other differences in the mechanism of action between BGB-A445 and other OX40 agents may further explain the antitumor activity seen with BGB-A445, including the ability of BGB-A445 to facilitate interaction with NK cells and Tregs, leading to Treg depletion, and the fact that BGB-A445 is not limited by the hook effect. The clinical effect will be explored in future studies. BGB-A445 induced treatment-emergent ADAs with both monotherapy and combination therapy, and BGB-A445 exposure ranges for patients with and without ADAs were similar; further immunogenicity studies are ongoing.

Cardiac events observed in our study, including troponin elevation (Part A) and myocarditis (Part B), warrant attention. These findings, along with similar reports for other OX40 agonists [36, 37], suggest a potential class effect requiring further investigation. Previous studies have shown that OX40 blockade could reduce inflammation in viral-induced or autoimmune myocarditis [36, 37]. The occurrence of cardiac events in both monotherapy and combination arms necessitates more detailed exploration of the relationship between OX40 agonism and cardiac toxicities. Future studies should include rigorous cardiac monitoring to better understand this potential risk.

There were a few notable limitations of this study. The patient population in the dose-escalation Part A of this phase Ia/Ib study was heterogeneous, with varying advanced solid tumors that were not selected for OX40 and/or PD-L1 expression; however, there is clinical evidence of response to T-cell–based immuno-oncology agents in these tumor types. Also, it is not clear if the antitumor activity derived from the combination treatment is due to tislelizumab, BGB-A445, or both, as the contribution of each component was not determined. Finally, it is difficult to draw specific conclusions for patients with certain tumor types due to small sample sizes; however, the findings from this study will help to guide future research. It is important to evaluate the performance of BGB-A445 in a more homogeneous patient population and,thus, phase II studies of BGB-A445 with tislelizumab are underway for advanced or metastatic urothelial carcinoma, renal cell carcinoma, melanoma, and NSCLC (NCT05661955; NCT06029127; NCT05635708).

Conclusions

BGB-A445 was well tolerated. No DLTs were observed when BGB-A445 was administered either as monotherapy or in combination with tislelizumab in patients with advanced solid tumors. There were some indications of activity as a single agent; however, these were modest. Antitumor activity was more pronounced when BGB-A445 was used in combination with tislelizumab, even in this heterogeneous phase I population, and therefore further development with BGB-A445 is recommended in combination with tislelizumab or with other agents. The phase Ib dose expansion of this study is ongoing in patients with NSCLC, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, and nasopharyngeal carcinoma. BGB-A445 is also being evaluated with tislelizumab in patients with melanoma, renal cell carcinoma, urothelial carcinoma, and NSCLC in phase II studies.
