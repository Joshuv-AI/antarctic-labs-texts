US new playbook for global health: balancing national interest and global responsibility
Authors: Stephen Olaide Aremu, Adamu Ishaku Akyala, Fortune Barituka Dugbor, Umbochun Ladan Zamani, Onuche Noah John, Sarah Onyinoyi Seriki, Aishat Temitope Kasali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe recently unveiled America First Global Health Strategy represents a fundamental reorientation of US engagement in global health, framed as a ‘new playbook’ designed to safeguard US lives, prosperity and influence. Built around pillars of security, sovereignty and economic self-interest, the strategy emphasises bilateral agreements, co-investment and the global promotion of US health innovation. While positioned as a corrective to inefficiency and dependency in past aid programmes, this shift raises profound questions about equity, solidarity and the future of multilateralism in health governance. This analysis critically examines the implications of the US first approach through four inter-related lenses. First, the strategy’s security-first framing risks privileging outbreak containment over collaboration, potentially reinforcing a fortress mentality rather than fostering collective preparedness. Second, its critique of ‘dependency’ obscures the documented contributions of US programmes such as the President’s Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief and the President’s Malaria Initiative to health system strengthening, raising concerns that abrupt transitions could dismantle fragile gains. Third, the prioritisation of US innovation in commodity procurement highlights tensions between economic diplomacy and moral legitimacy, with the risk of crowding out local innovation ecosystems. Finally, the privileging of bilateralism over multilateralism may deliver short-term accountability but risks fragmenting global health coordination and undermining shared responsibility. At its core, global health security is indivisible; no nation can insulate itself indefinitely from cross-border threats. A strategy that prioritises national interests while relegating equity to the margins risks eroding US credibility and weakening global solidarity. We argue that only by integrating equity, reciprocity and multilateral collaboration into its ‘new playbook’ can the US safeguard both its own people and global health security.Keywords: Health policy, Health economics, Disease Outbreaks, Public Health, Pandemic Preparedness

Introduction

The unveiling of the America First Global Health Strategy (AFGHS) marks a pivotal transformation in how the USA engages with global health.1 While the authors refer to ‘over two decades’ of US leadership as the largest bilateral donor, it is important to recognise that the United States Agency for International Development (USAID), established in 1961, has long served as the primary instrument for delivering foreign assistance in global health and as the backbone of US global health expertise and implementation capacity. For decades, USAID, alongside flagship initiatives such as the President’s Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief (PEPFAR) and the President’s Malaria Initiative (PMI), has saved millions of lives, strengthened health systems and shaped global health governance through its accumulated successes, failures and lessons learnt.2,5 Its sudden dismantling represents a significant omission in the framing of the AFGHS, as the new strategy not only pivots towards US security, prosperity and innovation but also lacks the institutional foundation and expert workforce that previously enabled effective action. This raises a critical and unresolved question at the heart of the AFGHS: without USAID, who will carry out the strategy?5

At its core, the AFGHS emphasises bilateral partnerships, co-investment requirements and the advancement of US innovation in health.1 While this approach promises greater accountability and efficiency responding to domestic calls for measurable returns, it marks a departure from the traditional ethos of multilateral cooperation and global solidarity that once underpinned US global health assistance. The strategy reframes global health as a component of national security and economic policy, resonating with electorates sceptical of foreign aid and policymakers focused on strategic self-interest.6,10

This strategic pivot must be contextualised within the global political economy of health. The COVID-19 pandemic revealed both the interdependence and fragility of global health systems, as vaccine inequities and resurgent nationalism exposed the limitations of multilateralism.11 12 The AFGHS, therefore, emerges amid a broader trend in which health is instrumentalised as a tool of geopolitics, aligning with similar nationalist turns observed worldwide.8 Given the US disproportionate influence, this recalibration could reshape the architecture of global health governance, influencing donor behaviour, institutional relevance and the balance between cooperation and competition.

The declared priorities of the AFGHS, keeping USA safe, making USA stronger and making USA more prosperous, underscore its transactional orientation.1 By focusing on preventing outbreaks abroad before they reach US borders, the strategy reinforces a security-first framing of global health. Its emphasis on bilateral agreements and performance-based co-investment aligns assistance with US interests, while its promotion of US innovation links foreign aid with domestic economic growth.8 Though these priorities may enhance efficiency and transparency, they risk shifting global health from a model of shared responsibility towards one of strategic conditionality.6

Critically, the AFGHS’s bilateral emphasis may weaken multilateral institutions like the WHO, Gavi and the Global Fund, which have historically pooled risks and coordinated global responses.10 Moreover, its co-investment model could inadvertently penalise low-income countries unable to meet financial benchmarks, exacerbating inequities. The strategy’s underlying ‘dependency’ narrative also overlooks how initiatives like PEPFAR helped build resilient surveillance, laboratory and workforce systems across sub-Saharan Africa. Additionally, by intertwining global health aid with the promotion of US products, the strategy risks commodifying humanitarianism, potentially crowding out local innovation and diminishing US legitimacy as a cooperative leader.

Ultimately, the AFGHS raises a central question: can the USA reconcile national interests with global solidarity? While the strategy may deliver short-term efficiencies and political appeal, its long-term effects on equity, multilateralism and shared resilience remain uncertain (table 1). If the world’s largest donor normalises an approach that equates global health with national security and commercial gain, it could redefine global health diplomacy, potentially fragmenting cooperation and undermining collective preparedness for future threats. The durability of global health security will depend not merely on US strength but on its commitment to the principles of equity, solidarity and shared responsibility that sustain the health of all. The security-first lens: containment over collaboration

The security-first orientation of the AFGHS foregrounds containment as the principal mode of engagement with global health crises.13 Central to this framing is the pledge to ‘keep America safe’ by enhancing global disease surveillance systems, screening travellers at points of entry and ensuring outbreaks are swiftly contained at their source. At first glance, such measures appear both practical and logical, especially in a world where pathogens can cross borders within hours. Yet this framing, which positions infectious disease control predominantly as a national security imperative, risks narrowing the scope of global health to issues that directly threaten US safety while sidelining broader questions of equity, solidarity and shared vulnerability. Although the AFGHS highlights surveillance and data exchange, it fails to acknowledge the historical role of the USA in supporting comprehensive, population-based data systems, most notably the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS), that have long underpinned global health monitoring and informed national policy. The absence of DHS-style data, which offer comparable indicators across time, countries and regions, represents a critical gap that weakens both global decision-making and the US ability to track progress in countries where it invests.

The reduction of global health to a security problem is not entirely new. In the aftermath of the September 11 attacks, the securitisation of health gained momentum, with pandemics increasingly described as existential threats akin to terrorism.14 15 This lens justified massive investments in biodefence infrastructure, global surveillance and emergency preparedness. The AFGHS builds on this legacy but pushes it further, framing containment and unilateral surge capacity as the cornerstones of global health engagement.1 By privileging these tools, the USA risks reinforcing a fortress mentality: a belief that its borders can be shielded from global risks through technology, rapid response teams and advanced biomedical innovation, even if global systems remain fragmented.14 Without the population-level insights previously supplied by DHS, used for decades by countries, multilateral institutions and US agencies to assess trends and allocate resources, the strategy risks relying heavily on threat-based surveillance while ignoring the foundational demographic and epidemiological data that guide effective long-term planning.

Historical experience, however, demonstrates the limits of containment without collaboration. The 2014–2016 West African Ebola epidemic illustrated how delayed international engagement, driven in part by a perception that Ebola was a distant, regional problem, allowed the crisis to spiral into a global emergency. Similarly, the COVID-19 pandemic revealed the futility of unilateral measures such as travel bans and export restrictions when viruses are already widely circulating.16 17 Trust between governments, open channels for data sharing and equitable access to vaccines and therapeutics proved to be the true determinants of global resilience. Countries that embraced cooperative approaches, such as early genome sharing or pooled procurement, fared better than those that hoarded resources in the name of national security.18,21 DHS data historically reinforced such cooperation by enabling coordinated, evidence-based responses across countries; without comparable datasets, future crises risk being met with fragmented, unevenly informed action.

By emphasising national safety over global solidarity, the AFGHS risks undermining these lessons. Surveillance without trust may yield incomplete or distorted data, as countries may hesitate to report outbreaks for fear of stigma or punitive travel restrictions. Containment without equitable access to countermeasures breeds resentment, as seen during the vaccine nationalism of 2021, when wealthy nations secured doses far in excess of their needs while low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) were left behind.22 23 If the USA doubles down on unilateral surge capacity, stockpiling vaccines, medicines and protective equipment for its own population, it could exacerbate the very inequities that fuel the spread of disease. The loss of DHS, which helped countries accurately measure maternal mortality, childhood vaccination coverage, nutrition trends and numerous other indicators, compounds these inequities by depriving governments and multilateral institutions of the empirical foundation needed to design fair and effective interventions.

Furthermore, a purely security-first framing risks distorting research priorities. Funding pipelines may increasingly favour pathogens deemed ‘high threat’ to US interests, such as influenza, coronaviruses or bioterrorism agents, while chronic conditions, neglected tropical diseases and health system strengthening receive less attention. This selective investment pattern perpetuates a cycle where diseases that predominantly affect poorer populations remain underfunded, undermining global health equity. Over time, this could erode US credibility as a genuine partner in health, reducing its soft power and influence in global governance forums.10 24 The absence of DHS-like longitudinal data further limits the ability to track the broader impacts of such skewed investments, making it more difficult for both the USA and global actors to identify emerging disparities and recalibrate strategies.

Ultimately, infectious diseases do not respect borders and neither do their social, economic and political consequences.25 26 A strategy that treats global health threats primarily as national security challenges may provide short-term reassurance but risks long-term fragility. What is required is not just the capacity to contain outbreaks, but the willingness to build inclusive, resilient systems through trust, transparency and fair distribution of resources. Containment and collaboration must be complementary, not mutually exclusive. Without this balance and without the comprehensive population-level data historically supplied by DHS, which enabled countries and donors alike to see beyond immediate threats, the US new Global Health playbook could end up securing less safety, not more.27 28

Bilateralism and the waning of multilateralism: the new diplomacy of health

A central feature of the AFGHS is its embrace of bilateralism as the preferred model for global health engagement. The strategy promotes ‘direct, mutually beneficial’ partnerships with individual countries while downplaying multilateral platforms such as the WHO, Gavi and the Global Fund.10 This reorientation, framed as an effort to improve accountability, streamline aid and safeguard US investments, represents a significant restructuring of global health diplomacy. While bilateral agreements may deliver short-term efficiencies and visibility, they risk fragmenting global health governance, undermining collective action and weakening multilateral institutions vital for managing cross-border threats. Against this backdrop, a broader question emerges alongside the authors’ earlier query about reconciling national interests with global solidarity: will the USA, by doubling down on conditional, transactional bilateralism, inadvertently render itself less relevant in a competitive geopolitical landscape increasingly shaped by China and Russia?

At its core, bilateralism offers the USA greater control and visibility. Through one-on-one agreements, the USA can set terms of cooperation, ensure alignment with its strategic interests and promote US innovation. For recipient countries, such arrangements may facilitate direct access to US funding, technical expertise and biomedical technologies.3 29 The appeal is clear: fewer intermediaries, faster disbursement and tangible results.30 31 The PEPFAR illustrates both the strengths and weaknesses of this approach, saving millions of lives and boosting US soft power, yet also creating parallel systems that bypassed national health ministries and hampered integration within broader health systems.32 33 Expanding such bilateral approaches risks reproducing these inefficiencies, reinforcing dependence and sustaining donor-driven agendas rather than country ownership. Furthermore, by privileging transaction-based influence at the expense of broader coalitions, the USA may be narrowing the diplomatic footprint that once amplified its global presence.

By contrast, multilateralism provides mechanisms for resource pooling, norm-setting and collective coordination. Institutions such as WHO, Gavi and the Global Fund have aligned donor efforts, reduced duplication and given LMICs a platform in global decision-making.10 The experience of COVAX, despite its limitations, demonstrated the importance of collective mechanisms for equitable vaccine distribution during global crises. A continued US shift away from these platforms could erode global solidarity at a time when health threats, such as pandemics, antimicrobial resistance and climate-related diseases, require cooperation rather than competition. Historically, multilateral global health engagement has strengthened US diplomatic influence and extended its reach beyond narrow security goals, an advantage that the AFGHS now risks diminishing.

The strategic rationale behind bilateralism is also geopolitical. Direct deals enable the USA to sidestep contentious debates in multilateral forums, counter rivals such as China and achieve visible diplomatic wins. Yet, this transactional model risks alienating allies, signalling that US engagement is conditional and interest-driven. In regions where China and Russia are expanding their health diplomacy, perceptions of US unilateralism could diminish trust and weaken long-term influence. More importantly, if the USA retreats from multilateral leadership, it leaves a vacuum that competitors are eager to fill, potentially accelerating a shift in global power dynamics and reducing the US ability to shape international norms and agendas through health diplomacy.

The implications for global health equity are equally serious.34 35 Bilateral partnerships often target countries of geostrategic importance or those able to co-invest, sidelining fragile states and marginalised populations.36 Multilateral frameworks, conversely, distribute resources based on vulnerability, not political relevance. As the USA leans into bilateralism, gaps in global health financing may widen, exacerbating inequalities and leaving the poorest countries at greater risk. The long-term consequence is not only a weakening of equity but also a deterioration in the US reputation as a reliable and principled global health partner—further eroding the soft power that has traditionally bolstered its foreign policy.

Sustainability also poses a challenge. Bilateral initiatives are vulnerable to domestic political shifts and changing foreign policy priorities.36 A new administration could abruptly withdraw or redirect funds, destabilising dependent programmes. Multilateral systems, by pooling contributions and spreading risk, provide greater continuity and resilience. Thus, privileging bilateralism introduces volatility into global health governance precisely when stability is most needed. Over time, such unpredictability could make the USA a less attractive partner, pushing countries towards more consistent actors and accelerating the decline of US influence in global health diplomacy.

Ultimately, global health challenges are transnational and demand collective responses.37,42 While bilateralism enhances US visibility and control, it undermines the cooperative infrastructure essential for shared security. The choice between bilateralism and multilateralism is not merely technical but moral, determining whether global health will be guided by shared responsibility or transactional diplomacy. The long-term legacy of the AFGHS will hinge on whether it strengthens or erodes the foundations of equity, solidarity and collective resilience on which global health depends and whether the USA preserves or forfeits the global influence it has long exercised through health engagement.

Dependency or development? Rhetoric versus reality

The AFGHS casts a critical light on prior US health assistance, arguing that programmes like the PEPFAR and the PMI created a ‘culture of dependency’ among recipient countries. This rhetoric is not new; donors have long worried that aid can disincentivise domestic investment in health.1 10 Yet, the empirical record offers a more complex picture. Far from fostering passivity, US global health programmes have catalysed meaningful institutional and system-wide gains in many LMICs.

PEPFAR, for instance, is widely credited with saving over 25 million lives, but its contributions extend beyond HIV treatment. Evaluations show that PEPFAR investments helped build laboratory networks capable of diagnosing multiple infectious diseases, improved supply chain management systems and supported the training of over 300 000 healthcare workers across sub-Saharan Africa.43 Similarly, PMI-supported programmes strengthened entomological surveillance and provided platforms for integrated vector control that benefit broader public health beyond malaria.44 These structural gains illustrate that US investments, when long-term and programmatic, can serve as development enablers rather than dependency traps.

The new strategy’s emphasis on rapid transition to government ownership and private-sector engagement, while appealing in theory, risks undermining these fragile gains if pursued hastily. Many recipient governments still face constrained fiscal space: according to WHO, over 75% of low-income countries allocate less than 5% of Gross Domestic Product (GDP) to health, well below the Abuja Declaration target of 15%.45 Abruptly shifting financial and technical responsibilities to such governments risks service disruptions, workforce attrition and erosion of public trust.

Sustainability requires careful sequencing, gradual capacity building, domestic resource mobilisation and realistic co-financing arrangements. Without these safeguards, the push to ‘end dependency’ risks becoming an exercise in retrenchment, where progress painstakingly achieved over two decades is dismantled under the banner of efficiency. The challenge, then, is not dependency versus autonomy, but how to design partnerships that nurture durable self-reliance without abandoning vulnerable populations.

The commodities conundrum: who benefits?

The AFGHS pledges that 100% of US foreign assistance allocated for commodities and frontline healthcare workers will be preserved, signalling an effort to protect the most visible and politically defensible aspects of aid.1 On the surface, this appears commendable, ensuring a steady flow of diagnostics, therapeutics, vaccines and essential workforce support directly sustains lives. Yet, embedded within this commitment is an explicit ambition to promote US health innovation abroad, raising critical questions about who ultimately benefits from this procurement-driven model. This debate is further complicated by the long-standing ‘source and origin’ regulations that govern US foreign assistance and typically require the procurement of medicines and medical devices from US manufacturers, a legal framework that shapes how foreign assistance is operationalised and one whose relationship to the AFGHS remains uncertain.

Tying global health assistance to US economic interests reflects a shift from solidarity-driven aid to market-centred diplomacy. By privileging US-based companies in the procurement of commodities, the strategy risks turning health aid into a vehicle for export promotion. While such an approach may deliver short-term economic gains for US industries, it risks undermining the moral legitimacy of US leadership in global health by blurring the line between altruism and self-interest.10 This conflation of global public goods with domestic market expansion is particularly problematic in health, a sector where equity, trust and solidarity are foundational. Although procurement regulations were temporarily waived during the COVID-19 pandemic and in specific emergency contexts, the AFGHS does not clarify whether it intends to maintain, modify or further institutionalise these sourcing requirements, leaving open important questions about its future alignment with global equity goals.

Moreover, a US-centric procurement model could have unintended consequences for recipient countries. Over-reliance on US-supplied commodities may inhibit the growth of local manufacturing sectors and innovation ecosystems. Recent experiences with COVID-19 vaccine nationalism highlight the dangers of such dependency: when supply chains tightened, many LMICs were left vulnerable, underscoring the importance of building local production capacity. If US assistance crowds out local suppliers by tying aid to US exports, it risks perpetuating structural dependency rather than fostering resilience. Without clarity on whether the AFGHS will preserve or reform current procurement rules, recipient countries may face continued constraints on developing autonomous, regionally grounded production systems.

The global health community has increasingly called for investment in regional manufacturing hubs, technology transfer and intellectual property sharing to ensure equitable access and long-term sustainability. Yet, this new approach appears to run counter to this momentum, prioritising US innovation pipelines over local empowerment. While promoting US products is politically expedient, it risks reinforcing a perception of aid as transactional rather than transformational. The absence of guidance on how existing source and origin regulations will interface with the AFGHS’s broader ambitions further compounds these concerns, signalling a potential disconnect between the strategy’s stated goals and its operational realities.

Ultimately, the commodities conundrum is not simply about procurement; it is about legitimacy. If the USA positions its global health assistance as an extension of industrial policy, its moral authority as a leader in global solidarity may erode. To balance domestic interests with global equity, the USA must ensure that its procurement practices also invest in strengthening local innovation ecosystems, supporting technology transfer and diversifying supply chains. Clarifying how the AFGHS intends to navigate long-standing sourcing requirements would be an important step towards rebuilding trust. Without such efforts, the promise of safeguarding lives could be overshadowed by the perception that US new playbook is more about profit than partnership.

Relevance of the 2024 HHS global health strategy in comparing US policy shifts

The Global Health Strategy of the US Department of Health and Human Services (HHS), which was published about a year ago, offers an essential and timely benchmark for evaluating the AFGHS.46 Although USAID is institutionally distinct from HHS, the two agencies frequently collaborate across multiple countries, jointly supporting disease control programmes, health security initiatives and health systems strengthening efforts. Because of this close operational alignment, the 2024 HHS strategy provides valuable insight into the broader US Government’s global health philosophy and priorities. Its release less than a year before the AFGHS also makes it a particularly relevant comparator for understanding the magnitude of the strategic shift that the AFGHS represents.

In sharp contrast to the AFGHS’s focus on bilateralism, national interest and short-term outcome-driven investments, the HHS strategy emphasises multilateral cooperation, global solidarity, equity and long-term capacity building. It reinforces US commitments to WHO-led systems, global disease surveillance, research partnerships and programmes targeting vulnerable populations, areas where the AFGHS adopts a more selective, US-centric approach. Reviewing the HHS strategy alongside the AFGHS highlights not only policy differences but also divergent ethical foundations, with the former grounded in collective responsibility and the latter in strategic self-interest. Global health equity in the balance

At its core, global health has been driven by the pursuit of equity, solidarity and shared vulnerability, principles now challenged by AFGHS. By prioritising sovereignty, bilateral leverage and economic self-interest, the AFGHS represents a decisive shift from collective responsibility to transactional engagement. While such an approach may yield short-term efficiencies and visible returns for US taxpayers, it risks deepening inequities and fracturing the moral foundations of effective global health cooperation.

Framing aid as a means to ‘make America safer, stronger and more prosperous’ redefines foreign assistance as an extension of national interest rather than solidarity. This reorientation contrasts sharply with multilateral initiatives like PEPFAR, the Global Fund and Gavi, which were built on risk-sharing and collective stewardship.2,5 By linking aid to performance metrics and procurement of US goods, bilateralism may reduce recipient nations to clients instead of equal partners.

Moreover, the notion that the USA can shield itself from global health threats reflects a dangerous illusion of self-sufficiency.47 48 49 The COVID-19 pandemic proved that no country, regardless of wealth or power, is immune to transnational pathogens.50 50 51 Neglecting global equity not only undermines solidarity but also endangers US security, as inequities create reservoirs for future outbreaks.

Equity is thus a pragmatic imperative, not merely a moral one. To truly safeguard both US and global health, the AFGHS must restore equity and shared responsibility as central pillars of its vision, ensuring durable, legitimate and universally beneficial global health security.

Conclusion

US new playbook in global health reflects the political realities of an era shaped by surging nationalism, fiscal scrutiny and shifting global power dynamics. While this recalibration may satisfy immediate domestic pressures, its long-term impact will depend on whether the USA can strike a delicate balance between safeguarding national interests and sustaining the global conditions that ultimately underpin its own security and prosperity. A turn towards bilateralism and securitised framing may deliver short-term influence, but it risks weakening trust, eroding multilateral cooperation and fragmenting global responses to shared challenges such as pandemics, antimicrobial resistance and climate-related health crises. Rather than retreating inward, the USA could seize this moment to reaffirm its global leadership by demonstrating that smart global health investments, particularly through multilateral institutions like the WHO and through co-investment in countries’ public health systems, directly serve US strategic interests. Strengthening essential health services, improving equitable access to vaccines and supporting sustainable systems of pandemic preparedness reduce instability, forced migration and the economic shocks that inevitably reverberate back to the USA. By fostering reciprocal partnerships, especially with LMICs, the USA can move beyond paternalistic models of aid towards approaches grounded not only in mutual benefit but also in the pragmatic recognition that healthier, more stable populations contribute to local prosperity and open markets for US goods. Championing a strategically aligned vision of global health security would enhance collective resilience while reinforcing US credibility and influence in an increasingly multipolar world. Anything less risks transforming global health from a cooperative endeavour into a zero-sum geopolitical contest, an outcome that would ultimately leave all nations, including the USA itself, more vulnerable.
