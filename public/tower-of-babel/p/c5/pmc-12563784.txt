Gram-Negative Uropathogens in Diabetic Patients: A Study of Ampicillinase Cephalosporinase, Extended-Spectrum Beta-Lactamases, and Metallo Beta-Lactamases Producing Phenotypes
Authors: Satish R Patil, G. S. Karande, Ravindra V. Shinde, Satyajeet K. Pawar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAim:This study aimed to identify Gram-negative uropathogens (GN-UP)-producing extended-spectrum beta-lactamases (ESBL), metallo-beta-lactamases (MBL), and AmpC enzymes in diabetic patients and to analyze their antibiotic resistance profiles.Materials and Methods:Urine samples were cultured, and isolates were tested using the Kirby-Bauer disc diffusion method.Results:E. coli (43%) was the most common isolate, followed by Klebsiella spp. (25%). Among GN-UP, 13.26% were ESBL producers, 63.26% MBL producers, and 17.34% AmpC producers. MBL-producing isolates showed lower resistance to fosfomycin (22.58%), tigecycline (32.25%), and nitrofurantoin (33.87%). The high prevalence of MBL, ESBL, and AmpC producers in diabetic urinary tract infection (UTI) patients complicates effective antibiotic therapy.Conclusion:These findings underscore the need for regular antimicrobial surveillance and tailored treatment strategies in this vulnerable group.

INTRODUCTION

Urinary tract infections (UTIs) are among the most common bacterial infections, with Escherichia coli being the predominant causative agent, especially in diabetic patients who are at higher risk due to impaired immune responses.[1] Other frequently isolated organisms include Klebsiella spp., Acinetobacter spp., Proteus mirabilis, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Enterococcus, and Staphylococcus spp. Diabetes mellitus (DM), a growing global health concern, predisposes individuals to recurrent and complicated UTIs while also influencing the spectrum of uropathogens.[2] The rise of antimicrobial resistance (AMR), particularly to beta-lactam antibiotics, complicates empirical therapy and has rendered several previously effective antibiotics less useful. Extended-spectrum beta-lactamases (ESBL), metallo-beta-lactamases (MBL), and AmpC beta-lactamases (AmpC) represent key resistance mechanisms among Gram-negative bacilli, limiting treatment options.[3] These enzymes, often plasmid-mediated, facilitate horizontal gene transfer and contribute to widespread resistance, especially in hospital settings.[4] Understanding the prevalence and resistance patterns of ESBL, MBL, and AmpC-producing uropathogens in diabetic patients is essential for guiding appropriate therapeutic strategies and infection control practices.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Midstream urine samples were collected from diabetic patients and processed within two hours using standard microbiological methods. A semi-quantitative culture technique was employed for bacterial isolation, and identification was followed by antimicrobial susceptibility testing using the Kirby-Bauer disc diffusion method on Mueller-Hinton agar, according to CLSI 2021 guidelines with a 0.5 McFarland standard inoculum.

Phenotypic detection of beta-lactamase enzymes

ESBL: Double-disc synergy test was performed using ceftazidime (30 μg) and ceftazidime + clavulanic acid (30 μg + 10 μg) placed 20 mm apart. An increase of ≥5 mm in the inhibition zone around the combination disc indicated ESBL production.MBL: Imipenem (10 μg) and imipenem + EDTA discs were placed 20 mm apart. A ≥7 mm increase in the inhibition zone around the EDTA disc compared to imipenem alone indicated MBL production.AmpC: Cefoxitin and cefoxitin + cloxacillin discs were placed 20 mm apart. An increase of ≥4 mm in the zone around the combination disc compared to cefoxitin alone was interpreted as AmpC production.

RESULTS

Out of 98 Gram-negative uropathogens isolated from diabetic patients, phenotypic testing revealed that 13.26% were ESBL producers, 63.26% were MBL producers, and 17.34% were AmpC producers. MBL-producing organisms exhibited higher resistance to commonly used antibiotics compared to non-producers.

Table 1 shows the distribution of 98 isolates, with E. coli (7.14%) being the most common ESBL producer, followed by Klebsiella spp. (5.10%). No ESBL production was observed in K. oxytoca, P. aeruginosa, or P. mirabilis. Table 2 highlights that MBL production was highest in E. coli (22.44%), followed by Klebsiella spp. (18.36%) and P. aeruginosa (11.22%), with an overall MBL production rate of 63.26%. Table 3 indicates that E. coli exhibited the maximum AmpC production (6.12%), while K. oxytoca and A. baumannii showed the least (1.02% each), resulting in an overall AmpC production rate of 17.34%. Table 4 demonstrates that in MBL-positive uropathogens, the highest resistance was seen with cefotaxime (93.54%), ceftazidime (91.93%), and nalidixic acid (90.32%), whereas the lowest resistance was observed for fosfomycin (22.58%) and tigecycline (32.25%).

DISCUSSION

The present study highlights a considerable prevalence of multidrug-resistant mechanisms among Gram-negative uropathogens in diabetic patients.[5] ESBL production was observed in 13.26% of isolates, with E. coli being the most common producer. A significantly higher proportion (63.26%) of the isolates exhibited MBL production, raising concern due to its association with extensive antimicrobial resistance.[6] AmpC beta-lactamase production was seen in 17.34% of isolates, again predominantly in E. coli and Klebsiella spp.[7] MBL producers showed markedly higher resistance to third-generation cephalosporins and fluoroquinolones compared to non-producers.[8] This indicates possible selection pressure due to the overuse of beta-lactam antibiotics. The findings emphasize the urgent need for antimicrobial stewardship and surveillance strategies to curb the spread of resistant pathogens in vulnerable diabetic populations.

CONCLUSION

The high prevalence of ESBL, MBL, and AmpC-producing Gram-negative uropathogens among diabetic patients underscores the urgent need for routine screening and targeted antibiotic therapy. Early detection and rational antibiotic use are crucial to control the spread of multidrug-resistant infections in this vulnerable population.
